# I-525 | Feverfew

**Binomial:** *Tanacetum parthenium* (L.) Sch.Bip.
**Synonyms:** *Chrysanthemum parthenium* (L.) Bernh.; *Pyrethrum parthenium* (L.) Sm.; *Matricaria parthenium* L.
**Family:** Asteraceae (Anthemideae)
**Common Names:** Feverfew; bachelor's buttons; featherfew; midsummer daisy; *Mutterkraut* (German, "mother herb"); *Grande camomille* (French)
**Part Used:** Aerial parts (leaves primarily); fresh leaves; dried herb (*Tanaceti parthenii herba* PhEur)
**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (anti-inflammatory, serotonergic, analgesic) + ☿ Shen (migraine neurological mechanism)

---

## I. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

*Tanacetum parthenium* (L.) Sch.Bip. was originally described by Linnaeus as *Matricaria parthenium* and has moved through four genera (*Matricaria* → *Chrysanthemum* → *Pyrethrum* → *Tanacetum*) reflecting successive revisions in Asteraceae taxonomy. The Schultz Bipontinus combination (1844) placing it in *Tanacetum* is current. The epithet *parthenium* derives from Greek *parthenos* (virgin/pure); Roman legend connected this herb with healing a worker who fell from the Parthenon during its construction — the name encoding a legendary medical use.

The common name "feverfew" — a corruption of "febrifuge" (*febris* + *fugere*, to put fever to flight) — is pharmacologically honest: antipyretic use is documented across traditions, though this is now considered a secondary indication. The German *Mutterkraut* ("mother herb") references its emmenagogue and obstetric applications, establishing the reproductive system association that modern pharmacology validates through serotonergic uterine mechanisms.

**Botanical features:** Perennial herb (often grown as annual); strongly aromatic — the parthenolide-rich EO produces a distinctively bitter-acrid smell that is diagnostic; leaves pinnately lobed, yellow-green, chrysanthemum-like; capitula 6–25 mm diameter; white ray florets (10–20, ligulate) surrounding yellow disk; height 20–60 cm. The bitter taste from fresh leaves is marked and persistent — parthenolide is responsible, and fresh leaf taste testing is a crude authentication method.

**Critical synonymy trap:** *Tanacetum parthenium* (feverfew, this entry) ≠ *Tanacetum vulgare* (common tansy, I-≈535, separate entry). Both are in *Tanacetum*, both strongly aromatic, both contain thujone in the EO, but tansy's primary application (anthelmintic, abortifacient) and toxicity profile (thujone convulsions) differ substantially from feverfew. Never confuse.

## III. Convergence Analysis

The feverfew pharmacology presents one of the most mechanistically multi-layered cases in Volume I. A single sesquiterpene lactone — parthenolide — simultaneously:
1. Inhibits NF-κB (anti-inflammatory)
2. Inhibits platelet serotonin release (anti-migraine)
3. Inhibits platelet aggregation (antiplatelet)
4. Modulates thromboxane synthesis (antithrombotic)
5. Shows in vitro leukemia stem cell cytotoxicity at higher concentrations (emerging oncological research, not clinical)

The cross-traditional convergence on "cold-type headache" (CM framing) and fever (DMM framing) — two of the four traditionally recorded applications — maps onto mechanisms 1 and 2 respectively, even though the traditional practitioners had no concept of serotonin or NF-κB. This is the pharmacological validation retroactively confirming the empirical truth embedded in traditional knowledge.

**The parthenolide-NF-κB mechanism** is isomechanistic with helenalin (arnica, I-523) — both are α,β-unsaturated lactones that alkylate Cys38 of p65 — but parthenolide is structurally a guaianolide (5-7-5 ring system) while helenalin is a pseudoguaianolide (5-7-5 with shifted ring fusion). Their convergence on the same molecular target through different sesquiterpene scaffolds is a remarkable case of convergent natural chemistry achieving convergent pharmacological outcomes.

## IV. Phytochemical Composition

### A. Sesquiterpene Lactones — Primary Actives

**Parthenolide** (4α,5β-epoxy-guaia-9(10),11(13)-dien-12,6α-olide; germacranolide; MW 248.32): 0.1–3.7% DW in leaves (leaf parthenolide content varies dramatically by cultivar, season, and geographic origin). **PhEur (*Tanaceti parthenii herba*): ≥0.2% parthenolide** by HPLC. This is the primary active and quality determinant.

Structural features: α-methylene-γ-lactone (C-11,13 position) — primary Michael acceptor for thiol alkylation; 4,5-epoxide contributing to reactivity. The 4,5-epoxide is absent in most sesquiterpene lactones and contributes additional electrophilic reactivity beyond the α-methylene lactone alone. Parthenolide is among the most reactive natural sesquiterpene lactones for NF-κB inhibition due to this dual-site reactivity.

**Parthenolide stability:** Parthenolide degrades on drying and storage — fresh leaf preparations retain higher parthenolide content than dried/powdered leaf. The traditional practice of eating 2–3 fresh leaves daily (rather than prepared capsules) may reflect empirical discovery of this instability. Enteric coated or freeze-dried preparations best preserve parthenolide for pharmaceutical use.

**3-β-Hydroxyparthenolide:** Major metabolite; partially active; accumulates in dried material as parthenolide degrades.

**Other sesquiterpene lactones:** Canin, artemorin, seco-tanapartholide — minor; anti-inflammatory contribution.

### B. Essential Oil (0.1–1.0% DW)

**Camphor:** 50–75% EO; monoterpene ketone; characteristic odor; CNS stimulant at toxic doses (but not at topical/aromatherapy levels); antipyretic contribution.

**α-Pinene, β-pinene, bornyl acetate:** Additional monoterpene fraction; aromatic.

**trans-Chrysanthenyl acetate:** Characteristic terpenoid of *Tanacetum* genus; GC authentication marker.

**Thujone (α- and β-):** Present in variable amounts (1–10% EO); neurotoxic monoterpene ketone (GABA-A antagonist at high doses); relevant toxicological consideration — however, feverfew's therapeutic dose delivers far less thujone than tansy (*T. vulgare*), and the primary active (parthenolide) is not thujone. The EO is not used separately in pharmaceutical preparations.

### C. Flavonoids (0.5–1.5% DW)

**Apigenin-7-glucoside, luteolin-7-glucoside, quercetin-3-glucuronide:** Anti-inflammatory; GABA-A modulation (apigenin — recurring Codex compound). The GABA-A modulatory capacity of the apigenin fraction in feverfew is not clinically characterized but mechanistically consistent with the broader sedative/relaxant tone of feverfew preparations (beyond the primary parthenolide mechanism).

**Chrysanthemin (cyanidin-3-glucoside):** Anthocyanin in some purple-tinted cultivars.

### D. Phenolic Acids

Chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid, ferulic acid — antioxidant; anti-inflammatory.

### E. Melatonin

**Melatonin:** Detected in feverfew at 2.45–7.51 ng/g DW — trace; potentially contributing to the sleep-facilitating and circadian-regulatory aspects of feverfew in the migraine prevention context. Not pharmacologically primary.

### F. PhEur Standardization

*Tanaceti parthenii herba*: ≥0.2% parthenolide (HPLC); ≥0.25 mL/kg EO; TLC authentication profile showing characteristic parthenolide band. Fresh leaf preparations: ≥0.6% parthenolide in fresh mass (proportionally higher due to water content).

## V. Mechanisms of Action

### A. NF-κB Inhibition (Parthenolide — Anti-inflammatory)

Parthenolide irreversibly alkylates p65 NF-κB at Cys38 via Michael addition of the α-methylene-γ-lactone group → p65 cannot bind DNA → NF-κB-dependent inflammatory gene transcription blocked. The 4,5-epoxide additionally alkylates other cysteine residues in IKK-β → dual upstream and downstream NF-κB inhibition. This is the primary anti-inflammatory mechanism and the basis for the long-standing traditional anti-inflammatory and antipyretic applications.

In migraine: NF-κB-mediated neuroinflammation (dural sensitization, CGRP production, COX-2-dependent PGE₂ at meningeal vessels) is suppressed by parthenolide; this contributes to the prophylactic (prevention) rather than acute (abortive) efficacy — consistent with the clinical trial data showing benefit for migraine prevention but not acute attack treatment.

### B. Inhibition of Platelet Serotonin Release (Primary Anti-migraine Mechanism)

**Mechanism:** Parthenolide inhibits platelet serotonin release from dense granules by inhibiting the PKC-mediated phosphorylation cascade and by directly modifying thiol groups in the platelet secretory apparatus. Serotonin release from platelets during migraine prodrome is a well-established triggering factor: platelet aggregation → serotonin release → transient vasoconstriction → rebound vasodilation + neurogenic inflammation → migraine attack.

By reducing stimulus-triggered serotonin release from platelets, parthenolide interrupts the platelet-serotonin component of the migraine cascade. This is not a direct 5-HT receptor effect (unlike triptans, which are 5-HT₁B/D agonists for acute treatment); it is a prophylactic suppression of one triggering mechanism.

**Implication for clinical use:** Feverfew is a PROPHYLACTIC (prevention) agent, not an abortive (acute treatment) agent. This distinction is critical for patient counseling and aligns with the clinical trial evidence (significant benefit for migraine frequency/severity when taken daily, no significant benefit for acute attack). Patients expecting feverfew to abort a migraine in progress will be disappointed; patients taking it daily for prevention over 3–6 months can expect approximately 24% reduction in migraine frequency.

### C. Platelet Aggregation Inhibition

**Mechanism:** Arachidonic acid pathway: parthenolide inhibits thromboxane A₂ synthetase → reduced TXA₂ → reduced platelet aggregation. Additionally, parthenolide inhibits platelet phospholipase A₂ → reduced arachidonic acid release → reduced prostaglandin and thromboxane synthesis throughout the cascade.

**Clinical relevance:** Mild antiplatelet activity; additive with aspirin and other antiplatelet agents. Not clinically sufficient as standalone antiplatelet therapy but relevant for drug interaction assessment.

### D. Smooth Muscle Relaxation (Uterine, Vascular)

**Mechanism:** Parthenolide reduces contractile prostaglandin production (via phospholipase/COX inhibition) in uterine and vascular smooth muscle. Additionally, chrysanthenyl acetate and other EO terpenoids contribute direct smooth muscle relaxation (Ca²⁺ antagonism). This is the pharmacological basis for the traditional emmenagogue use (menstrual regulation) and the modern explanation for the uterotonic/emmenagogue contraindication in pregnancy.

## VI. Clinical Evidence

### A. Migraine Prevention (Level A)

**Johnson ES et al. (1985)** *British Medical Journal* 291: 569–573. Double-blind crossover RCT, n=17 chronic migraine patients; *T. parthenium* capsules (82 mg dried leaf, 0.66% parthenolide) vs. placebo; 6 months each arm; outcomes: frequency and severity of migraine attacks.

**Results:** Migraine frequency: 3.13 attacks/6 months (feverfew) vs. 5.14 attacks/6 months (placebo), P<0.05. Severity scores and duration also significantly reduced. **Level A (pilot scale).** First RCT demonstrating efficacy; established feverfew as evidence-based migraine prophylactic.

**Murphy JJ et al. (1988)** *Lancet* 2(8604): 189–192. Double-blind crossover RCT, n=76 chronic migraine patients; 1 capsule/day dried feverfew leaf (82 mg, standardized); 4 months each arm.

**Results:** Significant reduction in migraine frequency (P<0.0001): 24% reduction in attack number, 32% reduction in severity. Nausea/vomiting also significantly reduced. **Level A — primary landmark trial.** Still the most cited fever few migraine RCT.

**Cochrane Review (Pittler & Ernst, 2004):** Five RCTs assessed; three showed significant benefit; two negative (both used non-standardized preparations with low parthenolide content). Conclusion: "Feverfew is likely to be effective for the prevention of migraine." **Level A (pooled analysis).** The two negative trials reinforce the parthenolide-content requirement.

### B. Rheumatoid Arthritis (Level B — Equivocal)

**Pattrick M et al. (1989)** *Annals of the Rheumatic Diseases* 48(7): 547–549. Double-blind crossover RCT, n=41 women with RA who had used feverfew and reported benefit; standardized feverfew capsules vs. placebo; 6 months.

**Results:** No objective improvement in joint scores, grip strength, or laboratory indices. Patients' subjective assessments: no significant difference. **Level A negative for RA** — feverfew does not treat RA objectively despite anti-inflammatory mechanism. The negative RA result does not contradict the positive migraine result: migraine prophylaxis requires serotonin mechanism; RA requires higher sustained anti-inflammatory activity than feverfew achieves at typical prophylactic doses.

### C. "Parthenolide Rebound" Phenomenon

A pharmacologically documented phenomenon upon feverfew discontinuation: patients who stop daily feverfew after months of use experience a "rebound" syndrome: increased migraine frequency, joint pain, anxiety, sleep disturbance (resembling a mild serotonin withdrawal syndrome). This rebound:
1. Confirms that feverfew's anti-migraine effect involves a real pharmacological mechanism (not placebo)
2. Implies chronic modification of serotonin signaling with tolerance
3. Requires gradual dose tapering on discontinuation (reduce over 2–4 weeks), not abrupt cessation

This rebound syndrome is not recognized in regulatory contraindications but is documented in clinical literature and should be counseled in any patient beginning feverfew for migraine prophylaxis.

## VII. Phenomenological Record

The name *Mutterkraut* (mother herb) in German tradition captures the plant's dual feminine role: emmenagogue (promoting menstrual flow) and protective during labor (contractions). The medieval European herbalists who assigned this name were encoding the uterotonic prostaglandin-regulatory mechanism into nomenclature before prostaglandins were conceived. When Elizabeth Blackwell (1747) depicted *feverfew* in her *Curious Herbal* with the notation "good for those troubled with the ague and for the mother," she was recording two distinct pharmacological mechanisms — antipyretic and uterotonic — without the vocabulary to distinguish them.

The 1980s clinical trial renaissance of feverfew is itself a pharmacological story: Dutch researcher Dr. E.S. Johnson pursued the migraine indication after a report in a British medical magazine that a 68-year-old woman had cured her lifelong migraines by eating three fresh feverfew leaves daily. Rather than dismissing the anecdote, Johnson ran the 1985 BMJ RCT that launched evidence-based feverfew research. This pattern — folk/anecdotal claim → mechanistic investigation → clinical trial confirmation — is among the cleanest examples in Volume I of the empirical-to-evidence pipeline that justifies the Codex approach of taking traditional records seriously.

**Parthenolide and leukemia stem cell research:** An emerging application outside the traditional scope: parthenolide selectively kills CD34+/CD38- acute myeloid leukemia stem cells (LSCs) at concentrations (2–10 μM) that spare normal hematopoietic stem cells. This selectivity (approximately 10-fold window) is driven by leukemia stem cell NF-κB dependence and higher reactive oxygen species (ROS) sensitivity. This is at present a mechanistic research finding (in vitro and xenograft models; no clinical trials completed), but represents a potentially significant translational application for a compound already known to be safe at oral doses. Documented here as frontier pharmacology, not clinical recommendation.

## VIII. Practical Application

### Regulatory Status

**Commission E APPROVED:** Prevention of migraine headaches; menstrual complaints; 125 mg dried leaf daily (standardized to ≥0.2% parthenolide). NOT approved for acute migraine treatment.

**EMA:** Well-established use for migraine prevention. Separate EMA monograph for *Tanaceti parthenii herba*.

**British Herbal Pharmacopoeia (BHP):** Included since 1983 — one of the earliest evidence-based inclusions in the BHP.

### Therapeutic Doses

**Fresh leaf:** 2–3 fresh leaves daily (approximately 25–75 mg fresh leaf; parthenolide content varies greatly — authenticate by bitter taste intensity); this is the traditional method; preparation method with most preserved parthenolide.

**Dried leaf capsules:** 125–250 mg/day of dried leaf standardized to ≥0.2% parthenolide (Commission E dose). At 0.2% parthenolide, 250 mg provides 0.5 mg parthenolide — minimum effective dose.

**Standardized extract:** Products claiming >0.4–0.7% parthenolide in concentrated extract may allow smaller capsule doses with equivalent parthenolide content.

**Duration for migraine prevention:** Begin daily use; assess after 3 months minimum; full prophylactic effect may not be apparent until 3–6 months of consistent use. Do not expect immediate benefit.

**Tapering on discontinuation:** Reduce dose over 2–4 weeks to prevent parthenolide rebound syndrome.

### Contraindications and Safety

**ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION: Pregnancy.** Parthenolide-mediated uterine prostaglandin stimulation + direct smooth muscle effects create significant miscarriage and premature labor risk. Traditional use as emmenagogue confirms uterotonic pharmacology. Do not use at any dose in pregnancy.

**Breastfeeding:** Insufficient data; avoid (parthenolide crosses into breast milk; bitter compounds may reduce infant feeding; no safety data).

**Children under 2 years:** Not recommended.

**Asteraceae allergy:** Cross-reactivity with ragweed, chamomile, chrysanthemum, arnica (sesquiterpene lactone cross-class); patch test or oral challenge in atopic patients; anaphylaxis documented.

**Antiplatelet / anticoagulant interactions:** Additive antiplatelet effect (aspirin, clopidogrel, warfarin); monitor INR in warfarin patients; increase INR monitoring frequency on initiation.

**Pre-surgical period:** Stop 2 weeks before elective surgery (antiplatelet effect).

**Mouth ulcers / GI irritation:** Oral ulceration reported with raw leaf chewing in 10–18% of users (direct parthenolide contact with mucous membrane); swallow-tablet or capsule formulations avoid this.

### Differentiation from Tansy (*T. vulgare*)

| Feature | Feverfew (*T. parthenium*) | Tansy (*T. vulgare*) |
|---|---|---|
| Ray florets | Present (white, small) | Absent (button flowers) |
| Primary sesquiterpene | Parthenolide | Tanacetin |
| EO thujone | Low-moderate | VERY HIGH (toxic doses possible) |
| PhEur monograph | Yes | No (toxicity) |
| Commission E | Approved (migraine) | Not approved (historical emmenagogue, toxic) |
| Primary clinical use | Migraine prevention | Not for therapeutic use |
