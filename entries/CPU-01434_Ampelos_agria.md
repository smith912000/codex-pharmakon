*4-183 AMPELOS AGRIA* (heading at line 9677; SUGGESTED line 9678: "Ampelos agria, Labrusca [Pliny], Vitis labrusca — Wild Vine, Fox Grape"; text at line 9679, in Book Four). The whole chapter reads: "The wild vine sends out long sprigs like the vine, woody and rough, with the bark chapped. The leaves are like garden strychnos, but broader and longer. The mossy flower has filaments; and the fruit is like little grape clusters that ripen and grow red. The shape of the seeds is round. The root of this (boiled in wine and taken as a drink with two cups of seawater) purges out watery matter. It is also given for dropsy. The clusters [of fruit] clean away sunburn and every spot. The new shoots are preserved in salt to eat with meat [vegetable]."

**What the text gives.** A description (long, woody, rough sprigs with chapped bark; leaves broader and longer than the garden strychnos; a mossy, filamentous flower; red grape-like clusters with round seeds), one internal use (the root, as a purge for watery matter and for dropsy), one external use (the fruit clusters, for sunburn and spots) and one food use (the new shoots preserved in salt).

**Identity doubts.** The translators' last-listed binomial, *Vitis labrusca*, is the name of a North American fox grape by general knowledge, which cannot be the plant of a Greek text. The Greek phrase means "wild vine" and the text describes a climbing plant with grape-like fruit. Which of several wild vines of the Mediterranean region is meant is not decided by the chapter. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The chapter records a root purge taken in wine and seawater, a use for dropsy, a fruit wash for sunburn and spots, and salted shoots as a vegetable. The identity is not secure, and the root purge is an unmeasured preparation. The Codex gives no protocol for it.

### Preparation & Dosing

Line 9679 gives the root "boiled in wine and taken as a drink with two cups of seawater"; the fruit clusters applied to the skin; and "the new shoots ... preserved in salt to eat with meat". **Practitioner's note.** Nothing here gives a cultivator a practice. A wild vine, a purging root, a skin wash and a salted vegetable are recorded, and its identity is unresolved. The abstentive answer is the correct one.

### Contraindications

The root is recorded as a purge, and purgatives can cause dehydration and electrolyte loss. Drinking seawater, as the chapter directs with the root, is harmful. Dropsy needs a clinician. The plant is not securely identified, and wild vines should not be confused with poisonous climbers such as the white bryony of the next chapter. None of the uses above is advice.
