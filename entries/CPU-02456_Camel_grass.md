# Camel grass — idhkhir (إذخر), with its flower-heads (fuqqāḥ, فقاحه)

## *Cymbopogon schoenanthus* (L.) Spreng., camel grass, the fragrant Arabian sedge-like grass; the identification is the modern one, made from the Arabic name and Avicenna's description, and is of fairly high confidence at genus level* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** CM, TBN

---

### Origin & Tradition

Among the texts on disk, *idhkhir* is named by exactly two: Avicenna's *Canon* (CM) and Ibn Qayyim's *al-Ṭibb al-Nabawī* (TBN). It does not appear on the spine from any other tradition. (Dioscorides has a fragrant rush, *schoinos*, DMM 1-16, which the translator glosses with *Juncus* and *Schoenus* names; it is a cross-reference only and is not tagged here. Avicenna himself quotes Dioscorides on the plant, below.)

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** The *Canon* (Book II, entry *idhkhir wa-fuqqāḥuhu*, Arabic text line 10072) describes several kinds. "Of it is the Bedouin (*aʿrābī*), sweet of scent, and of it the reed-bed kind (*ājāmī*), and of it a fine kind, which is harder, and a coarse kind, which is softer and has no scent." He then quotes Dioscorides: "*idhkhir* is of two kinds, one without fruit and the other with black fruit" (قال ديسقوريدوس: إن الإذخر نوعان أحدهما لا ثمر له والآخر له ثمر أسود). The choice paragraph says the best is the Bedouin, the red, the most pungent in scent; the flower-heads tend to red and, when split, turn purple, and are fine, "like the rose in the sweetness of their scent when crumbled by hand." It "stings the tongue." Most of its benefit is in the flower, the flower-heads, the root and the stalks.

**Prophetic medicine (TBN).** Ibn Qayyim's entry (*al-Ṭibb al-Nabawī*, letter *hamza*, entry *idhkhir*, Arabic text line 5019) opens with a hadith and no medicine: "It is established in the *Ṣaḥīḥ* that he, peace be upon him, said of Mecca: 'its fresh grass is not to be cut.' Al-ʿAbbās said to him: 'Except the *idhkhir*, O Messenger of God, for it is for their smiths (*qayn*) and their houses.' So he said: 'Except the *idhkhir*.'" The phrase "established in the *Ṣaḥīḥ*" is his verdict of soundness. I checked the same hadith in the collections on disk. Muslim, *Ṣaḥīḥ*, no. 1353 (Ibn ʿAbbās, on the sanctity of Mecca; `Muslim_Sahih_OpenITI_JK000109.txt`, line 17543) reads "…and its fresh grass is not cut. Al-ʿAbbās said: O Messenger of God, except the *idhkhir*, for it is for their smiths and their houses. He said: except the *idhkhir*." Bukhārī (Kitāb al-Janāʾiz, chapter 75, *bāb al-idhkhir wa'l-ḥashīsh fī'l-qabr*, "chapter of the *idhkhir* and grass in the grave"; `Bukhari_Sahih_OpenITI_JK000110.txt`, line 8094) has the same exchange with "for our goldsmiths and our graves." Muslim no. 940 (line 11610) records that, when Muṣʿab ibn ʿUmayr's shroud was too short at Uḥud, the Prophet said to cover the head with the cloth "and place *idhkhir* on his feet." These are the hadith that give the plant its standing in Islamic practice: a grass with uses for smiths, roofing and graves, spared from the sanctuary's ban on cutting.

### Active Components

The *Cymbopogon* grasses are aromatic. Their fragrance comes from essential oils, mainly monoterpenoid compounds, held in the leaves and flower-heads, and the plants also carry the usual grass tissues (fibre, silica). The Arabic texts describe the scent and the sting on the tongue, which agree with a volatile-oil plant. No further constituent is stated in either text, and none is asserted here. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

This is a historical record of two texts, not a recommended practice.

- **Avicenna's uses.** Astringent and dissolving, for spitting of blood (flowers), for itch (oil), for hot swellings (decoction), for pains of the womb, for the stomach and liver, for stone, for ascites and for stings of vermin (details under Preparation).
- **Ibn Qayyim's uses.** Opening obstructions; moving urine and the menses; breaking stone; dissolving hard swellings of stomach, liver and kidneys; fortifying the gums and stomach.
- **Everyday uses.** The hadith show a grass used for roofing, smithing and burial.

A modern reader should treat the womb, menses and "stone" statements as historical medicine only. The gathering rule of Mecca's sanctuary still applies to the sanctuary itself.

### Preparation & Dosing

Only what the two texts give:

- **Quarter-mithqāl with pepper (CM).** "It benefits the muscles, and it benefits spasm if a quarter mithqāl of it is drunk with pepper" (Arabic text line 10088); the oil "removes fatigue."
- **One mithqāl (CM).** "Its root a mithqāl, especially with its weight of pepper, calms nausea" (line 10091). For ascites: "if a mithqāl of its root is drunk with pepper it benefits dropsy" (line 10096).
- **Decoction as bath (CM).** "Sitting in its decoction" for hot swellings of the womb; also as drops or sipped water (line 10093).
- **Poultice (CM).** For the coarse kind, "the fresh leaf next to the root, applied as a poultice, benefits the sting of vermin" (line 10097).
- **Drink or poultice (TBN).** Ibn Qayyim: "drunk or as a poultice" for hard swellings of stomach, liver and kidneys (line 5023). He gives no weight or duration.

### Scientific Correlation

*Cymbopogon* species contain volatile oils that are used in perfumery and flavouring, and aromatic grasses of the genus are used as fodder and thatch in arid regions. Whether the substance relieves any of the conditions named in the two Arabic texts has not been tested in sources retrieved for this entry. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **CM (Avicenna).** Temperament varies by kind and by informant. The reed-bed kind has "a cooling power"; "according to Ibn Jurayj all of it is cold, its root more astringent"; the flower-heads "warm a little, their astringency being less than their warming"; and the Bedouin kind "is nearly hot in the second [degree] in its nature" (line 10080-10082). He therefore gives no single degree for the plant as a whole.
- **TBN (Ibn Qayyim).** "Hot in the second [degree], dry in the first, fine (*laṭīf*), opening obstructions and the mouths of the vessels" (line 5023).
- The two readings differ on cold and heat for the reed-bed kind; Avicenna reports the disagreement and does not settle it.

### Cultivation Register

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent.* The *Canon* entry is organised by organ and complaint: head, stomach, womb, joints, poisons. It assigns the plant no standing in the care of the innate heat or radical moisture, and it makes no claim about the *ruh*. Where it says the plant "strengthens" something it is the gums, and the claim is local. No cultivation framing is attested for *idhkhir* in the *Canon*.

**Prophetic medicine (TBN).** *(b) Framed.* The text sets the plant first inside a legal and practical exception (the sanctuary's rule against cutting grass; smiths, houses, graves), and then, in Ibn Qayyim's own words, gives it a medical profile. The hadith is graded sound by him ("established in the *Ṣaḥīḥ*"), and I found the same hadith in Muslim and Bukhārī on disk. There is no *sunna* of taking *idhkhir* as medicine in these texts, and Ibn Qayyim does not name it a means of keeping health among the things he lists for the regimen of health (food and drink, clothing, dwelling, air, sleep and waking, movement and rest, intercourse, evacuation and retention; line 3795-3797). Elsewhere (line 845-847) he names it among the pasture of the milch camels whose milk and urine were prescribed for dropsy, which is a link to the camel remedy and not a cultivation status. No cultivation grade is assigned.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator has nothing to do with this grass beyond what the texts show: it is cut for roof, forge and grave, it is spared in the sanctuary by the Prophet's own exception, and it is a minor aromatic digestive and urinary medicine in two texts. If used at all, use it as these texts use it, for a stated complaint and for a short course.

### Contraindications

- **Sanctuary.** The hadith above forbids cutting fresh grass in the sanctuary of Mecca, with this plant excepted; follow local rules on gathering.
- **Pregnancy and the womb.** Both texts name the plant as moving the menses and, in Avicenna, for the womb and for stopping women's flux. It should not be used in pregnancy. This is a precaution drawn from the stated actions and not from a statement in either text. [USER: verify]
- **Identification.** Plant material sold as *idhkhir* may be a different aromatic grass.
- **Stone and dropsy.** The stone and ascites uses are historical record only; these conditions need medical care.
