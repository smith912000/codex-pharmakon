# Lilac — *laylak* (Arabic/Persian, "bluish"); *syringa* (Greek, "pipe/panpipe"); common lilac

## *Syringa vulgaris* L. | Family: Oleaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Cold & dry (second degree), bitter — a cooling astringent tonic bitter in the Galenic-Unani reading
**Training Phase:** Clarify (bitter-tonic phase — the pruning of stagnant heat and the sharpening of appetite/attention)

---

### Origin & Tradition

The common lilac is a comparative latecomer to the written materia medica of the West — it is native to the Balkan Peninsula and reached Ottoman gardens and, via Constantinople, the herbals of Renaissance Europe only in the sixteenth century. This matters for an honest entry: much of what is claimed for "lilac" in older texts is genuinely claimed for the wider Oleaceae — the olive (*Olea*), ash (*Fraxinus*), privet (*Ligustrum*) and manna-ash — a family whose members share the same bitter secoiridoid chemistry. The Codex records lilac here as the horticulturally familiar face of a very old pharmacological lineage, and grades the tradition accordingly: **data-sparse, largely analogical, and honest about it.**

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (AT).** The cuneiform medical corpus (the *materia medica* of the Nineveh tablets and the pharmacological series *URU.AN.NA = maštakal*) lists a broad class of bitter, aromatic "tree drugs" used as febrifuges, vermifuges and stomach-strengthening bitters, prepared as decoctions and steeped in beer or wine. Whether any single logogram maps specifically onto *Syringa* rather than onto the ash/olive kin of the region cannot be established from the tablets with confidence. **[USER: verify any specific Akkadian binomial before print — do not assign a cuneiform plant-name to lilac without a philological source.]** What is defensible in general terms: the Mesopotamian tradition possessed and used the bitter-astringent tree-drug category into which lilac chemically falls.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM).** In the Galenic-Unani system that Ibn Sīnā codified in the *Canon (al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb)*, the Oleaceae bitters are treated as cold-and-dry, appetite-restoring, liver-directed correctives — *muqawwī al-maʿida* (stomach-strengthener) and gentle *muḥallil* (resolvent) agents given for intermittent fevers and sluggish digestion. Lilac itself enters this framework by family resemblance rather than by a securely attested chapter of its own; the *doctrine* it inherits is the bitter-tonic, humour-cooling one.

**Dioscorides / Graeco-Roman (DMM).** The name is the load-bearing survival here. *Syringa* is Greek *syrinx*, "pipe" or "panpipe" — a direct botanical signature of the plant's hollow-pithed young stems, which were cut and cleared to make reed-pipes. Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* does not describe the modern lilac, but the naming logic (a plant named for the flute made from its hollow stem) is a genuine classical fingerprint later fixed to *Syringa* by Linnaeus. The associated doctrinal category is that of the cooling bitter aromatics used to "cut" thick humours and febrile heat.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The old doctrine has one reproducible claim buried in it: *bitterness is a therapeutic virtue*, and the specific virtue attributed to these tree-bitters is the resolution of "corrupt" or stagnant humours — febrile heat, worms, a torpid stomach. Read chemically, the doctrine is pointing at the **secoiridoid glycosides** (oleuropein-type compounds, and the phenylpropanoid glycoside **syringin/eleutheroside B**) that give the bark and leaf their intense bitterness. In the Oleaceae the secoiridoids *are* the bitter principle, and they *are* the family's documented antioxidant and anti-inflammatory pharmacophore. So the symbolic reading — "the bitter tree-drug cools inflamed, stagnant tissue" — is not empty poetry; it is a folk-encoding of a real class of redox-active, NF-κB–modulating phenolics. Where the doctrine over-reaches is in the *febrifuge/vermifuge* specificity: there is no modern controlled evidence that lilac reduces fever or expels helminths in humans, and the Codex marks that arm of the doctrine **unvindicated**, not merely unproven. The "pipe/panpipe" symbolism (hollowness, breath, the reed that carries air) is decorative rather than pharmacological and should be read as etymology, not doctrine.

### Active Components

Retrieved chemistry, from HPLC-DAD-ESI-TOF/MS profiling of *S. vulgaris* bark and leaf (Varga et al., 2018):

- **Secoiridoids (15 identified)** — the family's signature bitter glycosides, including **oleuropein**-type structures.
- **Syringin** (a phenylpropanoid glycoside; = eleutheroside B) — the **major** phenolic of the bark at ~2.52% of extract.
- **Rutin** — the major phenolic of the leaf at ~1.13%; plus other flavonoids.
- **Phenylpropanoids (6), lignans (3), low-molecular-weight phenols (6).**

In the wider Oleaceae these secoiridoids (chiefly **oleuropein**) are the best-characterised bioactives.

A word on the chemistry that carries the doctrine. Secoiridoids are a subclass of monoterpenoid glycosides in which the cyclopentane ring of the iridoid skeleton has been oxidatively cleaved ("seco-"); the resulting open-chain enol-ether esters are chemically reactive and intensely bitter, and it is this reactivity that underlies their redox and enzyme-modulating behaviour. Oleuropein and its relatives readily hydrolyse to the aglycone and to *elenolic acid* derivatives, and the ortho-diphenol (catechol) moiety they carry is a classic radical-quenching pharmacophore — the same structural feature that makes olive-leaf and olive-oil polyphenols antioxidant. **Syringin**, by contrast, is a phenylpropanoid (a sinapyl-alcohol glucoside) rather than a secoiridoid proper; it is the bark's dominant marker and is the compound shared with *Eleutherococcus* ("Siberian ginseng"), where it is catalogued as eleutheroside B. The leaf's **rutin** is a quercetin rhamnoglucoside — a well-mapped vascular-protective flavonoid. So the plant offers three overlapping antioxidant chemistries (secoiridoid, phenylpropanoid, flavonoid), which is exactly why the crude extracts scavenge radicals in vitro.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Lilac sits in the **Clarify** phase as a *tonic bitter*: a cooling, appetite-restoring, mildly astringent agent whose plausible, evidence-supported action is antioxidant and anti-inflammatory (via its secoiridoid/phenylpropanoid load) with a documented mild antinociceptive effect in animal models. It is **not** positioned as a febrifuge or anthelmintic in Kenetica, because the human evidence for those traditional claims does not exist. Its role is the gentle "reset the palate, cool the surface heat" function that bitters classically serve.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:

- **Varga et al. (2018), *Natural Product Research*** — methanolic bark and leaf extracts of *S. vulgaris* were profiled (33 phenolics: 15 secoiridoids, 6 phenylpropanoids, 3 flavonoids, 3 lignans, 6 low-MW phenols), with syringin (2.52%, bark) and rutin (1.13%, leaf) as majors. Both extracts showed radical-scavenging activity (DPPH, ABTS) and **antinociceptive activity in the mouse hot-plate test**. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2018.1425855). This is the single most direct pharmacological study on the species and is a small preclinical (in vitro + rodent) study — hypothesis-generating, not clinical.
- **Yu & Zhu (2026), *Molecular Biology Reports*** — review of **oleuropein**, the dominant Oleaceae secoiridoid, documenting antioxidant, anti-inflammatory (NF-κB suppression, Nrf2 activation), anti-tumour and anti-apoptotic mechanisms. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11033-026-12185-z). This supports the *class* pharmacology the lilac shares, not lilac-specific efficacy.

**Evidence grade: LOW.** One small preclinical study on the species itself; the rest is read-across from oleuropein/Oleaceae chemistry. The antioxidant/anti-inflammatory and mild analgesic directions are supported at a mechanistic/preclinical level. The classical **febrifuge and vermifuge claims are not supported by any retrieved human evidence** and are deflated here.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani (attested by family):** cold & dry, bitter; stomach-strengthening resolvent bitter.
- **TCM:** not attested for *Syringa vulgaris* as such (the TCM lilac-relatives — e.g. *Syringa reticulata* bark, "暴马丁香" — carry their own separate use and should not be conflated). Marked **not attested** for this species.
- **Ayurveda (rasa/guṇa/vīrya/vipāka):** not attested — lilac is not a classical dravya. Marked **not attested** rather than invented.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** Lilac falls under the standing blocking warning for Assyrian identifications, and the problem is compounded by chronology and geography: *Syringa vulgaris* is a Balkan native that reached Ottoman gardens only in the sixteenth century, so no cuneiform logogram can securely name it, and the entry rightly declines to assign one. What is defensible is only that Mesopotamian medicine possessed a bitter tree-drug *category* into which the plant's chemistry would fall — a category, not this species, and certainly not a cultivation status. **(c) absent** — and the species-level tag is anachronistic (see flag).

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides does not describe the modern lilac at all; the *De Materia Medica* offers only the naming logic — *syrinx*, the hollow-stemmed reed-pipe — that Linnaeus later fixed to *Syringa*. There is therefore no Dioscoridean substance-account here to carry either a therapeutic or, since he holds no subtle-body doctrine, a cultivation status. What the tradition genuinely offers is its discipline of attention to name, provenance and sensory character — and, applied here, the honesty to see that the classical fingerprint is etymological, not pharmacological. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status; the DMM tag is name-only (see flag).

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** Lilac enters the Galenic-Unani frame as a cold-and-dry bitter *muqawwī al-maʿida* and gentle *muḥallil* — but, as the entry states, by family resemblance to the Oleaceae bitters rather than through any securely attested chapter of its own, and Ibn Sīnā (d. 1037) predates the plant's arrival in the Islamic world by centuries. The corpus's strongest cultivation idea — conservation of innate heat and radical moisture — is a frame of the whole regimen, not a status assigned to this analogically-placed bitter, and no *mufarrih* or *Canon* chapter is claimed for it. Its inherited standing is therapeutic-tonic, not a cultivation status. **(b) framed:** attested by family analogy, no cultivation status sourced.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator uses lilac, if at all, as a cooling tonic bitter in a clarifying phase — a bitter bark decoction to sharpen a torpid appetite and cool surface heat, on the strength of its secoiridoid and phenylpropanoid load, not as a febrifuge or vermifuge, for which no human evidence exists. No tradition here grants it a cultivation or longevity status, and none honestly could: all three classical tags rest on family resemblance or on a name, since the plant itself was unknown to them. Work it gently, expect an antioxidant bitter, and read the classical lineage as chemistry inherited through the Oleaceae rather than a status attested for the lilac.

### Contraindications

Lilac is not a well-characterised internal medicine and should be treated with corresponding caution. Bitter secoiridoid/phenylpropanoid extracts may irritate the gastric mucosa at high dose. No safety data exist for pregnancy, lactation, or paediatric use — **avoid in pregnancy and lactation** on the general principle applied to unstudied bitters. Oleaceae cross-reactivity is possible in those with olive/ash/privet/lilac pollen sensitisation. Because no validated human dose exists, internal use should be conservative and is best reserved to informed practitioner supervision. Not a substitute for antipyretic or anthelmintic therapy.
