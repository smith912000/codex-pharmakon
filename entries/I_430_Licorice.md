# I-430 | Licorice (*Glycyrrhiza glabra* L.)

**Volume I — Plant-Based Substances**
**Family:** Fabaceae (Papilionoideae) | **Energetic Tier:** ◈ Jing + ◉ Qi
**Convergence Score:** 8/9 traditions

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary name:** Licorice (American), Liquorice (British)
**Synonyms:** Sweet root, Gan cao (甘草, Chinese), Yashtimadhu (Sanskrit), Sus (Arabic), Süsholz (German)
**Binomial:** *Glycyrrhiza glabra* L. (primary European/Mediterranean species)
**Related species:** *G. uralensis* Fisch. (Chinese licorice — primary TCM source), *G. inflata* Batalin, *G. echinata* L.
**Authority:** Linnaeus 1753

**Botanical description:** Herbaceous perennial to 1.5 m. Pinnate compound leaves, 9–17 leaflets, oval to oblong, glandular-hairy beneath. Flowers in loose racemes, pale lavender-violet to whitish, papilionaceous. Pod compressed, 2–4 seeded. Root system: primary taproot 1–1.5 m deep with horizontal stolons extending several meters — critical pharmacological feature as root and stolons both contain glycyrrhizin. Distinctive: young leaflets sticky to touch (viscid glands). Harvested after 3–5 years of growth.

**Pharmacognostic part used:** Root and stolon, dried (*Liquiritiae radix*; *Glycyrrhizae radix et rhizoma* — European Pharmacopoeia)
**Nomenclature note:** "Glycyrrhiza" from Greek *glykys* (sweet) + *rhiza* (root) — literal description of primary sensory quality.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

Licorice achieves 8/9 convergence through a remarkable combination: it is simultaneously sweet-tasting (making it useful as a vehicle/sweetener for medicinal preparations), pharmacologically active (genuine gastric, respiratory, anti-inflammatory effects), and geographically distributed across the Old World from Western Europe to East Asia.

**The "harmonizer" designation in TCM is pharmacologically explicable:**
In classical Chinese pharmacy, *gān cǎo* serves as "the great harmonizer" — added to most formulas not as the primary medicinal but to:
1. Balance harsh or toxic ingredients (*detoxification* function — now understood as glycyrrhizin competitive binding to receptors, reducing toxicity of aconitine and other alkaloids)
2. Improve palatability (intense sweetness masks bitter taste of other herbs)
3. Moderate pharmacokinetics (glycyrrhizin inhibits CYP3A4, slowing metabolism of partner herbs — extending their activity)

This functional role means *gān cǎo* appears in ~60% of all classical TCM formulas — an astounding prevalence explicable by multiple concurrent mechanisms rather than one primary indication.

**Cross-cultural convergence on gastric/peptic disease:**
Every tradition that records licorice independently identifies gastric healing as a primary application. This convergence preceded by millennia the 1946 Revers discovery that licorice extract (carbenoxolone) could heal peptic ulcers. The convergent traditional observation proved pharmacologically correct: glycyrrhizin inhibits 11β-HSD2 in gastric mucosa (increasing local cortisol → reduced inflammation), and isoliquiritigenin directly inhibits H. pylori.

**Sweetness biochemistry:**
Glycyrrhizin is ~50 times sweeter than sucrose by mass, but the sweetness has a distinctly different kinetic profile — delayed onset (several seconds) and lingering duration (minutes). It binds the sweet taste receptor (TAS1R2/TAS1R3 heterodimer) via an allosteric modulator mechanism distinct from sucrose. The *E959* food additive is ammoniated glycyrrhizin (NHDC) used in confectionery.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**1. 11β-Hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11β-HSD2) inhibition — PRIMARY MINERALOCORTICOID MECHANISM**
Glycyrrhetinic acid (and to lesser extent glycyrrhizin itself) is a competitive inhibitor of 11β-HSD2 (Ki ~1–5 μM). This enzyme normally converts cortisol → inactive cortisone in mineralocorticoid-responsive cells (kidney, colon, salivary glands), preventing cortisol from activating aldosterone receptors. Inhibition → elevated local cortisol → activation of mineralocorticoid receptors → sodium retention, potassium excretion, hypertension. This mechanism explains both the therapeutic effects (anti-inflammatory glucocorticoid-like) AND the primary side effects (pseudoaldosteronism).

**2. Gastric mucosal protection**
Multiple overlapping pathways:
- Prostaglandin E₂ synthesis stimulation → increased mucus secretion, reduced acid
- Isolated licochalcone A and isoliquiritigenin: *Helicobacter pylori* inhibition (MIC 6.25–25 μg/mL)
- Glycyrrhizin: direct anti-inflammatory in gastric mucosa via NF-κB suppression
- Carbenoxolone (semi-synthetic derivative): promotes mucus viscosity and film thickness on gastric epithelium

**3. Cortisol pathway modulation (multiple sites)**
- 11β-HSD2 inhibition (raises cortisol in peripheral tissues)
- Glycyrrhetinic acid also modulates 11β-HSD1 (activating enzyme in liver/adipose)
- Net effect: shifts cortisol distribution between tissues
- Clinical implication: licorice prolongs the activity of hydrocortisone, reducing dose needed (used this way in Addison's disease support)

**4. Respiratory / expectorant effects**
Saponin content (glycyrrhizin is a saponin): surface-active agent increases bronchial secretion fluidity. Glycyrrhizin inhibits phospholipase A₂ (reducing arachidonic acid release → reduced inflammatory mediators in bronchi).

**5. Antiviral mechanism**
Glycyrrhizin: inhibits viral membrane fusion and replication. Demonstrated against:
- SARS-CoV (Cinatl 2003, The Lancet — IV glycyrrhizin)
- Influenza
- HIV-1 (inhibits protein kinase C activation)
- Hepatitis C (used clinically in Japan as SNMC/Stronger Neo-Minophagen C — IV glycyrrhizin + cysteine + glycine formulation)

**6. Estrogen receptor modulation**
Liquiritigenin: selective ERβ agonist (the most selective natural ERβ ligand identified to date; EC₅₀ ~20 nM at ERβ, very weak at ERα). Glabridin: mixed ERα/β with moderate affinity. Mechanism may contribute to red clover/licorice combinations used in menopausal symptoms.

**7. MAO inhibition (isoliquiritigenin)**
Reversible MAO-A inhibition → elevated monoamines. Contributes to observed antidepressant effects in preclinical models. Mechanistic basis for ethnobotanical "mood elevating" effects.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### Gastric / Peptic Ulcer

**Level A: Carbenoxolone trials (historical, 1968–1985)**
Carbenoxolone (semi-synthetic glycyrrhizin derivative): multiple double-blind RCTs in gastric ulcer documented significant healing rates vs. placebo. Comparison trials vs. cimetidine showed equivalent healing at 4 weeks. Now obsolete clinically due to side effect profile (pseudoaldosteronism) and H₂ blocker/PPI development — but established the pharmacological basis.

**Level B: DGL (deglycyrrhizinated licorice) for H. pylori**
Deglycyrrhizinated licorice (DGL): removes glycyrrhizin (and thus mineralocorticoid side effects) while retaining flavonoid/chalcone content.
Morgan 1982 (n=82): DGL vs. antacid vs. cimetidine for gastric ulcer — DGL equivalent to cimetidine at 16 weeks. Single RCT; not replicated with modern methodological standards.

### Hepatitis / Liver

**Level B: SNMC (Japan) — Chronic Hepatitis C**
SNMC (Stronger Neo-Minophagen C — IV glycyrrhizin preparation): observational data from Japanese clinical practice (n=1,093, Arase 1997, Cancer).
Result: Long-term SNMC treatment (mean 10.1 years) associated with 47% reduction in hepatocellular carcinoma development vs. controls.
Quality: Observational; selection bias likely; no RCT. But used as standard of care in Japan for non-responders to interferon therapy.

### Adrenal / Cortisol Support

**Level B: Borst 2002 (Metabolism)**
Licorice 100 mg glycyrrhetinic acid/day: 24-hour urinary free cortisol increased 2.4-fold (p<0.001). DHEAs unchanged. Documented pharmacodynamic effect supporting Addison's disease supportive use.

### Antiviral — SARS-CoV

**Level B: Cinatl 2003 (The Lancet — early report)**
IV glycyrrhizin in SARS patients (retrospective small series, n=4): SARS-CoV replication inhibited at concentrations achievable clinically. Followed by in vitro confirmation (IC₅₀ ~300 μg/mL glycyrrhizin against SARS-CoV).

### Menopausal Symptoms

**Level B: Menati 2014 (Iranian Journal of Pharmaceutical Research)**
Licorice vs. HRT for hot flashes, n=60, 8 weeks.
Result: Hot flash frequency -78% licorice vs. no change placebo; comparable to low-dose HRT arm.
Limitation: Small Iranian study; single center; likely ERβ agonism via liquiritigenin mechanism.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The sweetness as medicine:**
In nearly every traditional system, licorice sweetness was itself considered therapeutic — not merely pleasant. Medieval European doctrine of signatures: the sweetness indicated nourishment and restoration. In Ayurveda, *yashtimadhu*'s sweet taste (*madhura rasa*) classified it as inherently building, moistening, and tonifying. These organoleptic interpretations align with glycyrrhizin's actual cortisol-sparing effect (glucocorticoid-like action = anabolic at physiological doses) — traditional perception captured real pharmacology through taste.

**Tutankhamun's licorice:**
1922 excavation of KV62 — Howard Carter found licorice sticks (*Glycyrrhiza glabra*) among the tomb provisions, suggesting its importance as either medicine or refreshment for the afterlife. Radiocarbon-dating confirms the approximately 3,300-year age. This makes licorice one of the few medicinal plants confirmed archaeologically in both Ebers Papyrus (written record) AND material remains (physical specimen) from ancient Egypt.

**Gān cǎo — the great harmonizer:**
In TCM cosmology, *gān cǎo* occupies a uniquely central position: one of the "50 fundamental herbs," the most frequent formula ingredient, classified as *superior class* (*shang pin*) — medicines taken long-term for health maintenance rather than disease treatment. The TCM designation of *gān cǎo* as "detoxifier" (解毒) is pharmacologically explicable: glycyrrhizin binds to and competitively antagonizes several toxic compounds including aconitine alkaloids — actual receptor-level detoxification.

**Licorice candy vs. medicinal licorice:**
A cultural confusion: "licorice" candy in North America is typically artificial anise flavor (no *Glycyrrhiza* content). European licorice confectionery (Dutch *drop*, Finnish *lakritsi*, Scandinavian salty licorice) contains genuine extract — Dutch hard black licorice can contain significant glycyrrhizin levels (50–300 mg per serving), explaining why European cardiologists encounter pseudoaldosteronism in licorice candy consumers that American physicians virtually never see.

**Pseudoaldosteronism surveillance:**
The classic "licorice lover's hypertension" syndrome — hypertension, hypokalemia, edema, muscle weakness, headache — represents medicalization of excess. Traditional healers used licorice for days to weeks; chronic daily candy consumption (daily Dutch *drop* in large amounts) created a pharmacovigilance signal modern toxicologists trace to 11β-HSD2 inhibition. A lesson in dose and duration as determinants of benefit vs. harm.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Standardized preparations:**
- **Whole root/extract:** standardized to 4–8% glycyrrhizin; adult therapeutic dose 1–5 g/day root equivalent
- **DGL (deglycyrrhizinated licorice):** >95% glycyrrhizin removed; 380–1,140 mg/day; preferred for long-term GI use
- **SNMC/glycyrrhizin IV:** Hepatology specialist use only (IV preparation not widely available outside Japan)

**GI applications (DGL preferred for chronic use):**
- Gastric ulcer support, GERD, gastritis: DGL 380 mg TID before meals (chewable tablet or powder)
- DGL may be taken long-term without mineralocorticoid concerns
- Allow 4–8 weeks for ulcer healing assessment
- Adjunct to H. pylori eradication therapy (not replacement)

**Respiratory / cough:**
- Whole root decoction: 2–5 g root in 500 mL water, simmer 20 min; 2–3 cups/day
- Maximum 4–6 weeks continuous use before blood pressure monitoring
- Expectorant and anti-inflammatory for productive cough, bronchitis

**Adrenal support (short-term):**
- Whole extract, 200–400 mg/day glycyrrhizin equivalent
- Maximum 4 weeks without medical monitoring
- Supportive (not replacement) in adrenal insufficiency — always under physician supervision

**Topical application (glabridin-rich extract):**
- Skin brightening/hyperpigmentation: 0.1–0.5% glabridin cream (melanogenesis inhibition via tyrosinase inhibition)
- Anti-inflammatory skin formulations

**Duration and safety limits:**

*Pseudoaldosteronism risk:*
- WHO recommends ≤100 mg/day glycyrrhizin for long-term use (most adults)
- European Medicines Agency: ≤75 mg/day glycyrrhizin for those sensitive
- At therapeutic doses for ≤4–6 weeks: generally safe in healthy individuals
- At >100 mg/day for >4 weeks: hypokalemia, hypertension, edema risk increases substantially

**Absolute contraindications:**
- Hypertension (pre-existing) — glycyrrhizin will elevate BP further
- Hypokalemia — will worsen
- Severe renal impairment — reduced glycyrrhizin clearance → accumulation
- Heart failure — sodium retention worsens fluid overload
- Hyperaldosteronism (Conn syndrome) — additive effect

**Pregnancy:**
- **AVOID systemic use** — glycyrrhizin has been associated with shortened gestational length in population studies (Strandberg 2001, BMJ — Finnish cohort: licorice consumption >500 mg glycyrrhizin/week associated with 2.5-day shorter gestation, p=0.001); also associated with poorer neurological outcomes in children exposed in utero (Räikkönen 2009, American Journal of Epidemiology)

**Drug interactions:**
- **Antihypertensives:** Antagonized (BP-raising effect counteracts therapy)
- **Diuretics (K-sparing):** Hypokalemia additive risk
- **Corticosteroids:** 11β-HSD2 inhibition prolongs steroid activity; dose reduction may be needed
- **Warfarin:** Glycyrrhizin CYP2C9 inhibition; monitor INR
- **Digoxin:** Hypokalemia increases digoxin toxicity risk

**DGL safety note:** DGL removes the mineralocorticoid-active fraction. Standard contraindications for whole licorice (hypertension, pregnancy cardiovascular risk) do NOT apply to DGL at typical doses, making DGL appropriate for long-term GI applications where whole licorice would be prohibited.
