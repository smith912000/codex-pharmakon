**Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *The names in the chapters.* The chapters print "Mudga" (including compounds such as Vana-Mudga) on about ninety lines of the chapter files, the most in Sutra 46 (twenty-three lines), the chapter on food and drink. A search for Hari-mudga found no occurrence. The passages below were read.

*The pulses (Sutra 46, lines 195 to 230).* The chapter lists the pulses (*Vaidala*) "commonly used as food": "the Mudga, Vana-Mudga, Kalaya, Makushtha, Masura, Mangalya, Chanaka, Satina, Triputa, Harenu, and Adhaki, etc." It says of them together: "They are generally cool and pungent of digestion, and have a sweet and astringent taste. They generate the Vayu, arrest the flow of urine and the evacuation of stool, and subdue the Pittam and Kapham." Of Mudga in particular: "The species known as the Mudga does not excessively generate Vayu in the system but tends to purify and invigorate the organ of vision. The green species is the best of all the varieties of Mudga pulse. The properties of Vanya (Mudga) are similar to those of the common variety." And: "All the varieties of pulse except the Mudga and the Masura tend to produce a distension of the abdomen caused by gas." Further on (lines 2157 to 2160) the text ranks "the pulses such as the Mudga, the Adhaki, and the Masura" with Shashtika rice, barley, wheat and Rakta-shali as "the best and by far the most nutritious" of the cereals of the paddy group.

*The soup (Sutra 46, lines 2395 to 2480).* "Mudga soup subdues the Kapham, and is appetising and agreeable. It forms the most wholesome diet to persons whose systems have been cleansed with the aid of purgative and emetic remedies, as well as to those suffering from ulcers." The *Raga-Shadava* is given as "another name for Mudga soup prepared with grapes and expressed pomegranate-juice", light and relishing. The soup of Masura, Mudga, wheat or Kulattha made with salt is "specially recommended in nervous diseases". Mudga soup with Patola or Nimba reduces fat and Kapha and is curative in Kushtha and parasitic disease; with Mulaka it removes dyspnoea, cough, catarrh and fever; cooked with Amalaka it is astringent and helps derangements of Kapha and Pitta. Other lines of the same chapter treat Mudga pulse as confectionery (lines 2630 to 2642), as a boiled or fried preparation (lines 2264 and 2675), and as a meal after which sour gruel or milk is taken (lines 2771, 2784, 2834).

*Other chapters.* The ulcer-patient's diet takes "Mudga soup" (Sutra 19, lines 215 to 221). The rules of hygiene say that yusha (Mudga soup, and the like) should be taken in the rainy season (Chikitsa 24, lines 746 to 748) and that curd should not be taken without being saturated with Mudga soup or honey or Amalaka juice (lines 696 to 698). In the chapter on diseases of the mouth the patient takes "the soup of Mudga boiled in alkaline water" (Chikitsa 22, lines 360 and 361). In the chapter on swelling the patient takes "Mudga pulse fried with the clarified butter prepared by cooking it with the decoction of Trikatu and Varshabhu" (Chikitsa 23, lines 148 to 151). In the chapter on injections, a plug "of the size of a Mudga-pulse" is a measure (Chikitsa 37, line 735).

*The rasayana chapter (Chikitsa 27).* Mudga is named in the chapter on elixirs and rejuvenators three times, always as the food that goes with the medicine. After the powdered Vidanga seeds taken for a month in one of five vehicles, "a meal of boiled rice with a copious quantity of clarified butter should be taken with the soup of Amalaka and Mudga pulse unseasoned with salt and cooked with only a small quantity of Sneha (clarified butter) after the medicine has been well digested" (lines 61 to 66; the text then says of "these (Rasayana) remedies" that "their use for every month increases the life-time of the user by one hundred years"). After the *Vidanga-Kalpa*, "the diet should consist of cooked rice and clarified butter mixed with a copious quantity of the soup of Mudga pulse" cooked with a little Sneha and salt (lines 94 to 96). After a Masha-pulse preparation taken for longevity and keener sight, the meal "should be taken with unsalted Mudga, and Amalaka soup or with milk alone" (lines 221 to 223). In all three the longevity is credited to the medicine; the pulse is its accompanying diet.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. As a general fact, the pulses are the seeds of plants of the pea family (Fabaceae), and a dried pulse is a source of protein and starch. Nothing more is stated here about the composition of the drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Sushruta text records Mudga as a food pulse and soup that it rates as light, relishing and wholesome after cleansing and in ulcers, as the best of the pulses for the eyes, and as the diet that goes with the rasayana medicines of Chikitsa 27. These are the text's statements about a Sanskrit name, and the Latin name the appendix attaches to it is the translator's, by position. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these preparations, each with its locus. Soup, plain or with grapes and pomegranate juice, or with Patola, Nimba, Mulaka or Amalaka (Sutra 46, lines 2395 to 2480). Soup unseasoned with salt and cooked with little Sneha, with boiled rice and clarified butter, after a rasayana medicine (Chikitsa 27, lines 61 to 66, 94 to 96, 221 to 223). Soup boiled in alkaline water (Chikitsa 22, lines 360 and 361). Pulse fried in ghrita cooked with Trikatu and Varshabhu (Chikitsa 23, lines 148 to 151). No quantity of pulse per meal is given in the passages read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Sushruta terms (the Vayu, Pittam and Kapham, *Vata-vyadhi*, *Kushtha*) are not modern diagnoses, so no modern finding can be set beside them. It is a general, well-established fact that cooked pulses are a food and that undercooked or raw seeds of many legumes can upset the gut. The identity rests on a positional pairing in a translator's appendix, so a study of one *Phaseolus* or *Vigna* species would not bear directly on the Sushruta pulse.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SS:** the passages read print for the pulses together that they are "generally cool and pungent of digestion, and have a sweet and astringent taste" and "subdue the Pittam and Kapham" (Sutra 46, lines 198 to 203); for Mudga alone that it "does not excessively generate Vayu", "tends to purify and invigorate the organ of vision", and that the green species is the best (lines 203 to 212). Mudga soup "subdues the Kapham". No *virya* or *vipaka* is printed for Mudga alone in the lines read.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Sushruta text names Mudga as a food pulse and soup with stated dietary virtues. Its nearest approach to a cultivation setting is Chikitsa 27, a rasayana chapter, where Mudga soup is the meal prescribed after three of the rasayana medicines; the text credits the added years to the medicines (for the Vidanga remedies, "their use for every month increases the life-time of the user by one hundred years") and does not grade the pulse itself as a rejuvenative. A search of the vajikarana chapter (Chikitsa 26) found no occurrence. The chapters say nothing of *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* for Mudga. The parent may judge that the three naming loci in Chikitsa 27 justify (a); the entry records them as an accompanying diet and grades (b). The Latin identification is the appendix's. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** The Sushruta text gives a cultivator a plain thing to do with this pulse and nothing more: it is cooked as a soup and eaten, and in the rasayana chapter it is the unsalted soup that follows the medicine. No special status is claimed for it, and nothing should be taken as an elixir on the strength of a Latin name the chapters do not give.

### Contraindications

The Sushruta passages read print no warning for the pulse itself, and the text counts Mudga among the pulses that do not distend the abdomen (Sutra 46, lines 228 to 230). As a general fact, a person with a legume allergy should avoid pulses, and raw or undercooked seeds are not a safe food. The identity is open, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
