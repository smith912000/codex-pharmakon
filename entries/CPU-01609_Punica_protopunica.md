# Punica protopunica — Balaustion (Dioscorides 1-154); balaustium, wild pomegranate flower

## *Punica protopunica, the second name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 1-154 ("Balaustion [Bedevian], Punica protopunica [Mabberley]"), glossed "Wild Pomegranate Tree"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), who attribute it to Mabberley, and is not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 4651) prints a Greek-derived name credited to Bedevian and one binomial credited to Mabberley, and one English name. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *1-154. BALAUSTION*, heading at line 4649, SUGGESTED line at 4651, text at line 4652. It stands in the section on fruit trees, after *1-153. SIDIA* (heading at line 4645) and before *1-155. MURSINE* (heading at line 4653). The translators' index gives *Balaustion* and *Balaustium* at page 158 and *Punica protopunica* at page 158.

The whole chapter reads: "Balaustium is the flower of wild pomegranate of which there are many kinds — some white, some red and some a rosy colour. It is similar to cytini and is juiced in the same way as hypocistis. It is astringent and good for the same purposes as hypocistis and cytini [flowers of pomegranate]."

The chapter is three sentences long and describes no leaf, fruit, root or place. It says what the drug is (the flower of a wild pomegranate), that there are "many kinds" distinguished by flower colour (white, red, "a rosy colour"), that it is similar to *cytini*, that it is prepared like hypocistis, and that it is astringent and "good for the same purposes as hypocistis and cytini". It gives no name other than *balaustium*.
*The comparison chapters.* *1-152. KUTINOI* (heading at line 4636), whose SUGGESTED line is "Cytini, Punica granatum — calyx of Pomegranate Flowers", says the flowers "are astringent, drying and restringent, and agglutinate bloody wounds, being good for the same purposes as pomegranates", that a decoction makes a mouth rinse "for moist flagging gums and loose teeth", and that it is put into poultices "as a glue for teeth which are broken". It adds "Some relate that whoever swallows down three cytini (though ever so little) shall not be troubled with eye sores all that year", and ends: "They are juiced like hypocistis [1-127]." *1-127. UPOKUSTIS, UPOKISTIS ETERA* (heading at line 4463, SUGGESTED "Cytinus hypocistis — Hypocistis") says hypocistis "is juiced like acacia, but some dry and break it, then steep and boil it", that it is "similar in strength to acacia, yet more astringent and drying", and that it is "good for those troubled with colic, dysentery, blood-spitting, or women's excessive discharges — taken either as a drink or infused". So the three clauses of 1-154 point to two earlier chapters for their detail. What 1-154 itself says is only that the uses are the same as theirs, and the uses named in those chapters (wounds, gums and teeth, colic, dysentery, blood-spitting, women's discharges) are Dioscorides' words there and are not repeated in 1-154.

*The preceding pomegranate chapters.* *1-151. RHOA* (heading at line 4629) is the fruit, with the kernel and the juice of the kernels; *1-153. SIDIA* (heading at line 4645) is the rind and, in its second sentence, the roots ("A decoction of the roots expels and kills worms hidden in the intestines"). The balaustion chapter closes this run of four pomegranate chapters.

**Identity.** The SUGGESTED line names the plant as a "Wild Pomegranate Tree", and the chapter calls it "wild pomegranate". The Latin binomial is a modern name the translators credit to Mabberley. It is a general botanical fact that *Punica protopunica* is a species restricted to the island of Socotra, and the chapter names no place; whether the translators mean that species, or *Punica granatum* in a wild form, is not stated in the lines read. **Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter number, *Balaustion*, *balaustium* and *Punica protopunica* found no existing entry on 1-154 itself. Three entries touch the same chapter. I_190 (Pomegranate, *Punica granatum*) names the flowers as "balaustion" and says they are used "specifically for excessive menstruation and as a wound astringent"; the 1-154 text prints neither of those uses. I_191 (Pomegranate rind and bark) uses "balaustion" in its cultivation paragraph as one of three medicinal fractions of the pomegranate (root bark, rind, flower). I_876 (Myrtle fungal growth, 1-156) cites 1-154 and 1-155 as neighbouring chapters. Entries I_190 and I_191 treat the flower as part of the cultivated pomegranate, whereas 1-154 describes it as the flower of a wild pomegranate; the Codex does not merge them.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. It is a general fact that pomegranate flowers and rinds are generally known to contain tannins, which are astringent. The chapter does not mention it, and it is not known which species the chapter's plant is. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records the flower of a wild pomegranate as an astringent, to be used for the same purposes as hypocistis and the flowers of the pomegranate. These are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for any of them, and the identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation: the flower "is juiced in the same way as hypocistis", and chapter 1-127 on hypocistis says it "is juiced like acacia, but some dry and break it, then steep and boil it, and do other things to it in the same way as lycium [1-132]". Chapter 1-154 gives no quantity, frequency or duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The one general fact is the one stated under Active Components: astringent plant parts are generally known to contain tannins, and the identity of the species is uncertain.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one quality: it is "astringent". It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. The chapter on its comparison plant, *cytini* (1-152), prints "astringent, drying and restringent", and the chapter on hypocistis (1-127) prints "more astringent and drying" than acacia. These are those chapters' words and are not attributed to 1-154.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use. What it offers is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: a drug named by the part used (the flower), sorted into kinds by colour (white, red, a rosy colour), and taught by comparison to a drug the reader already knows (*cytini*) and a method already learned (juicing like hypocistis). The plant is given no status beyond astringency. The identification is the translators'.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It offers one comparison, one preparation and one quality, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. A drug described only as astringent and for the same purposes as other drugs gives no guidance on safety, and the Codex gives none. Bleeding, dysentery, mouth sores and other complaints that persist need medical assessment. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion that may name a species Dioscorides did not know, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
