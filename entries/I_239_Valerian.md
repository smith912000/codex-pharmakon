# I-239 | Valeriana officinalis — Valerian / All-Heal / Garden Heliotrope

## Section 1 | Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Valeriana officinalis* L.  
**Family:** Valerianaceae (now included in Caprifoliaceae by APG system)  
**Common names:** Valerian (English — from Latin *valere* "to be strong/healthy"), All-Heal (English medieval name), Garden Heliotrope (English — not related to true Heliotrope), Phu (Greek — Dioscorides' name, possibly from Arabic/Persian), Baldrian (German), Valériane (French), Valeriana (Spanish/Italian/Portuguese), Valerijana (Serbian/Croatian), Kozliatnik (Russian — "goat's herb"), Spikenard (English confusion with *Nardostachys jatamansi* — DISTINCT PLANT), Tagara (Sanskrit — WARNING: shared with Valeriana wallichii; see below), Fu Ping (tentative Chinese identification), Indian Valerian (*V. wallichii*), Katzenkraut (German — "cat herb" — for feline attraction to dried root odor)

**CRITICAL NAMING WARNINGS:**
1. **"Spikenard" confusion:** "True spikenard" = *Nardostachys jatamansi* (Himalayan spikenard — I-xxx) — different species; related to Valerian family but distinct pharmacology; "Spikenard" in ancient texts may refer to either; context essential
2. **Indian Valerian:** *Valeriana wallichii* DC. = "Indian Valerian" or "Tagara" in Ayurvedic texts — distinct species from *V. officinalis*; similar but non-identical chemical profile; some Indian Tagara products are *V. wallichii* (sometimes preferable for Ayurvedic formulations)
3. **"Nard" in ancient texts:** Classical "nard" references in DMM, Song of Solomon, etc. may refer to Valeriana spp., *Nardostachys*, or multiple plants traded under the same name

**Botanical description:** Perennial herb 60–150cm; pinnate compound leaves; clusters of small pale-pink to white flowers in terminal corymbs; rhizome and roots (primary medicinal part) with characteristic earthy, pungent odor (isovaleric acid — intensifies on drying due to esterase release). The fresh plant has a lighter fragrance; the distinctive "dirty socks" or "cat attractant" odor develops specifically in dried root preparations through enzymatic conversion. Cats, rats, and earthworms are strongly attracted to dried valerian root.

**Distribution:** Native to Europe and temperate Asia; naturalized widely in North America, Australia, New Zealand. Widely cultivated: Netherlands, Germany, Belgium, France, United Kingdom, Eastern Europe, Mexico, United States (Oregon). Multiple ecotypes with varying valerenic acid content. Belgian cultivation known for highest valerenic acid content; Eastern European for highest valepotriate content.

**Authenticated part(s):** Root and rhizome (2-year plant minimum); dried or as fresh-plant tincture. Complication: active compound profile changes dramatically during drying and extraction:
- Fresh plant: valepotriates (iridoid esters) primary; unstable
- Dried root: valerenic acid + isovaleric acid predominate (valepotriates decompose to baldrinal during drying — baldrinal is active but different)
- Aqueous extraction: polyphenols, GABA, glutamine primary; volatile compounds lost
- Ethanol extraction: valerenic acid preserved; GABA/glutamine variable

European Pharmacopoeia: minimum 0.17% valerenic acid (as standardization marker).

## Section 3 | Convergence Analysis

**Primary convergence axis — nervous system sedation:** DMM (calming, diuretic), SS (Tagara — calming, sleep, anti-convulsant), FMT (sedative, nervous disorders), and the strong European medieval tradition all converge on nervous system calming as the defining therapeutic property. This is the most specific and consistent cross-traditional indication in this entry, and it is precisely the use that modern RCT evidence has most rigorously tested.

**Valeriana family pharmacological convergence (SA):** South American Andean cultures independently developed use of local *Valeriana* species (several dozen species are Andean) for sedation, anxiety, and headache — the same indications documented in European and Ayurvedic traditions for *V. officinalis*. This represents a genuine family-level pharmacological convergence suggesting that the Valerianaceae/Caprifoliaceae chemical profile (valerenic acid-type terpenoids, isovaleric acid) reliably produces sedative effects across species — and these effects were independently discovered in both Old and New Worlds. The SA convergence at family level is among the strongest in the Codex for sedative plants.

**The ancient "antidote" function:** DMM documents Valerian as an antidote to poisons — a cross-traditional pattern seen in strong-smelling, aromatic plants (also documented for garlic, black pepper, myrrh). The antimicrobial and GABA-modulating properties may have generated observable "antidote" effects against some microbial toxins or CNS toxins (GABA-A modulation protects against some excitotoxins). The antidote claim is historically interesting as a primitive recognition of CNS-protective pharmacology.

**Sedative plant convergence across traditions:** Valerian, Passionflower (I-240), Lavender (I-241), Lemon Balm (I-242) — four consecutively documented entries all share GABA-A positive allosteric modulation as a primary mechanism. This cluster of entries reflects a distinct pharmacological class (GABA-ergic botanicals) that multiple traditions independently discovered, each using locally available plants with convergent mechanisms. The Codex systematically documents all GABA-ergic traditions.

## Section 4 | Phytochemical Composition

**Valerenic acid and derivatives (primary standardization markers):**
- **Valerenic acid:** sesquiterpene carboxylic acid; 0.05–0.67% in dried root; primary GABA-A positive allosteric modulator; does NOT appear in fresh root (forms during drying/storage from precursors); non-benzodiazepine GABA-A modulation at β₃ subunit
- **Acetoxyvalerenic acid:** acetylated derivative; similar GABA-A activity
- **Hydroxyvalerenic acid:** metabolite; GABA-A active
- **Valerenal:** aldehyde form; CNS-active; partial adenosine A1 receptor agonist

**Valepotriates (iridoid monoterpenes — fresh root primary compounds):**
- **Valtrate, isovaltrate, acevaltrate, didrovaltrate:** epoxy iridoid esters; present in fresh plant (unstable)
- **Stability:** Degrade to baldrinol/baldrinal during drying and extraction; baldrinal is pharmacologically active (antispasmodic, sedative) but different from valepotriates
- **Cytotoxic potential:** Valepotriates are cytotoxic in cell assays (alkylating agents); raised safety concerns; however, instability means oral preparations contain minimal intact valepotriates; risk profile debated

**GABA and glutamine:**
- Free GABA: 0.01–0.04 μg/g in aqueous extracts; contributes to pharmacological activity (though CNS penetration of free GABA is limited)
- Glutamine: GABA precursor; may be converted to GABA by gut bacteria → increases available GABA peripherally
- Homoorientin: flavonoid; GABA-A modulator

**Flavonoids:**
- Linarin (6-methylapigenin acacetin): binds GABA-A benzodiazepine site; anxiolytic
- Hesperidin: flavonoid with sedative properties
- 6-Methylapigenin: GABA-A modulator; most characterized flavonoid anxiolytic in Valerian

**Isovaleric acid:**
- Gives dried root its characteristic pungent odor; CNS depressant at high concentrations; pharmacologically active

**Lignans:**
- Hydroxypinoresinol: sedative; GABA-A modulating

## Section 5 | Mechanisms of Action

**1. GABA-A Positive Allosteric Modulation (Primary)**
- **Valerenic acid:** binds GABA-A receptor at β₃ subunit (distinct from benzodiazepine binding site on γ subunit); enhances GABA-mediated Cl⁻ influx without displacing benzodiazepines (confirmed binding: Khom et al. 2007, J Ethnopharmacol)
- **6-Methylapigenin + linarin:** bind GABA-A benzodiazepine site; potentiate GABA action via classical benzodiazepine binding site
- **Mechanism combination:** valerenic acid acts on β₃ (non-BZD site) + flavonoids on benzodiazepine site = dual-site GABA-A potentiation; together produces more robust effect than either alone; may explain why whole extract > isolated compounds in clinical studies

**2. GABA-T Inhibition (Reduces GABA Breakdown)**
- Valerenic acid inhibits GABA transaminase (GABA-T): the enzyme that degrades GABA in the synaptic cleft
- Inhibiting GABA catabolism → sustained GABA availability → prolonged inhibitory neurotransmission
- This mechanism works synergistically with GABA-A modulation: more GABA available AND enhanced receptor response to available GABA

**3. Adenosine A1 Receptor Agonism**
- Valerenal (valerenic acid aldehyde): partial A1 adenosine receptor agonist
- A1 adenosine receptor activation: sleep-promoting; anxiolytic; neuroprotective
- Mechanism: A1 activation reduces cAMP → reduces neuronal excitability → sedation/sleep promotion
- This mechanism is distinct from GABA-A and explains sleep-promoting effects independent of pure GABA enhancement

**4. 5-HT₅a Receptor Agonism**
- Valerinic acid: partial agonist at 5-HT₅a serotonin receptor
- 5-HT₅a: poorly characterized; expressed in hippocampus, locus coeruleus; linked to circadian rhythm regulation
- This mechanism may contribute to sleep architecture improvement beyond simple sedation

**5. Glutamate Inhibition / Excitotoxicity Prevention**
- Reduced glutamate release from presynaptic terminals
- NMDA receptor modulation
- Together with GABA enhancement: shifts CNS toward inhibitory/excitatory balance toward inhibition

## Section 6 | Clinical Evidence

**Insomnia / Sleep Quality (Level A — Multiple RCTs)**
*Leathwood et al. (1982) — RCT (first major):* N=128 adults; aqueous valerian extract 400mg vs. placebo; single-night acute dose and 2-week repeated dose. Sleep latency significantly reduced in poor sleepers; subjective sleep quality significantly improved. Landmark early trial.

*Donath et al. (2000) — RCT (PSG-controlled):* N=16 insomnia patients; standardized valerian extract vs. placebo; 2-week repeated dose. Polysomnographic (PSG) objective: slow-wave sleep (deep sleep) significantly increased; sleep efficiency improved; wake after sleep onset reduced. First objective PSG-confirmed insomnia RCT for valerian.

*Oxman et al. (2007) — RCT:* N=405 (largest valerian insomnia RCT); valerian extract vs. placebo × 28 days. Primary outcome (PSQI sleep quality index): significant improvement in sleep quality; sleep latency reduced; next-day functioning improved. Well-powered trial.

*Fernández-San-Martín et al. (2010) — Systematic review and meta-analysis:* 16 RCTs; conclusion: valerian "may improve sleep quality without side effects" — mostly positive evidence but methodological heterogeneity limits definitive conclusion.

*Bent et al. (2006) — Systematic review:* 16 randomized trials; 9/16 showed significant sleep improvement; overall conclusion: "may improve sleep quality"; safe. The inconsistency across trials reflects preparation variation (valepotriate vs. valerenic acid standardized vs. aqueous).

**Anxiety (Level B)**
*Andreatini et al. (2002) — RCT:* N=36 GAD patients; valepotriate preparation vs. diazepam vs. placebo; 4 weeks. Psychic anxiety subscale (HAM-A): valepotriate and diazepam equivalent improvements; vegetative subscale: diazepam superior. Valepotriate equal to diazepam for mental anxiety in GAD.

*Kennedy et al. (2006) — RCT:* N=54; valerian combined with lemon balm vs. placebo; mood and anxiety scales. Both valerian single and valerian+lemon balm significantly reduced anxiety (Spielberger State Anxiety Inventory) at 600mg single dose. Acute anxiolytic effect confirmed.

**Menopausal Symptoms (Level B)**
*Mirabi et al. (2011) — RCT:* N=68 menopausal women; valerian capsule vs. placebo × 8 weeks. Hot flash frequency and severity significantly reduced (p<0.05).

**Restless Leg Syndrome (Level C)**
*Cuellar & Ratcliffe (2009) — pilot RCT:* N=37 RLS patients; valerian × 8 weeks. Symptom severity, daytime sleepiness: significantly improved. Small trial; needs replication.

## Section 7 | Phenomenological Record

**The "pungent root" and the pharmacological encoding of odor:** The dried valerian root odor — intense, somewhat unpleasant (isovaleric acid — the same compound that makes sweaty feet smell), distinctly earthy and pungent — is itself pharmacologically informative. The smell intensifies during drying (enzymatic release of isovaleric acid from bound forms) — meaning the pharmacological activation and the olfactory signature develop simultaneously. Traditional apothecaries would judge valerian quality by odor intensity: strong smell = good medicine. This is not merely a quality marker — isovaleric acid itself has CNS depressant activity.

**The cat-attractant property:** Domestic cats and rats are powerfully attracted to dried valerian root — a phenomenon documented since ancient Rome (the "cat-call herb"). The attraction is attributed to isovaleric acid and possibly to valepotriate metabolites. The attraction of carnivores (animals whose CNS physiology broadly resembles human neuropharmacology in relevant ways) to a plant with GABA-A activity creates a striking example of cross-species pharmacological recognition. The cats' attraction to valerian parallels their attraction to catnip (nepetalactone acts via feline TRPA1 receptors) — different mechanism, same animal recognition.

**The medieval "pied piper" legend:** Some historians of folk medicine propose that the Pied Piper of Hamelin story (1284 CE) involved a person using valerian root to lead rats away (and possibly children following the same plant odor). Whether or not historically accurate, the legend reflects genuine folk knowledge of valerian's rat-attractant property — an application that predates modern neuropharmacology by several centuries.

**Wartime sedative use:** During World War II, valerian was used by British civilians to manage anxiety caused by air raid stress — a period when pharmaceutical sedatives were diverted to medical military use. The official government endorsement of valerian for "shell shock" anxiety in both WWI and WWII civilian populations constitutes a population-level pharmacological experiment with consistently positive outcomes, conducted under conditions of real existential stress. This represents one of the most pragmatic historical validations of valerian's anxiolytic properties.

**The fresh vs. dried dichotomy:** Herbalists who work with fresh valerian frequently note that fresh root preparations feel qualitatively different from dried — the fresh root is often described as more "vitalizing" or "warming" (valepotriates predominant), while dried is more "sedating" and "earthy" (valerenic acid predominant). This phenomenological distinction is pharmacologically real: the two preparation types have genuinely different active compound profiles (iridoid esters vs. sesquiterpenes) with different receptor targets. Traditional practitioners who distinguished these effects without knowing the chemistry were responding to genuine pharmacological differences.

## Section 8 | Practical Application

**Standardized preparations:**
- **Standardized dry extract (0.17–0.8% valerenic acid; European Pharmacopoeia standard):** 300–600mg; 30–60 min before bedtime for sleep; 300mg 2–3×/day for anxiety; most evidence-based form
- **Dried root powder:** 2–3g in capsule; traditional dose; full-spectrum
- **Aqueous decoction:** 3–5g root steeped 10 min in boiling water; polyphenol/GABA/glutamine dominant fraction; milder effect
- **Tincture (1:5, 40–60% ethanol):** 4–8 mL before bedtime; alcohol-preserved valerenic acid
- **Fresh plant tincture (1:2, 90% ethanol):** Valepotriate-containing; unstable; use fresh (within 6 months)
- **Combination preparations:** Valerian + Lemon Balm (I-242) — most-studied combination for sleep/anxiety; enhanced GABA-T inhibition + rosmarinic acid synergy

**Timing protocols:**
- *Sleep:* 300–600mg standardized extract taken 30 min–1 hour before bedtime; onset may be slow first 1–2 weeks; full sleep effect often requires 2–4 weeks of nightly use
- *Anxiety:* 200–400mg during the day, 3× daily; consistent daily use more effective than as-needed
- *Acute calming (single dose):* 600mg 60 min before anticipated stressful event (Kennedy 2006 dose protocol)

**Clinical protocols:**
- *Chronic insomnia:* 600mg standardized extract nightly × 4 weeks (minimum); do not expect full effect before 2 weeks; combine with sleep hygiene; taper gradually if used >4 weeks
- *GAD (adjunct):* 300mg 3×/day × 4–8 weeks; GABA mechanism compatible with CBT; monitor with HAM-A or GAD-7
- *Menopausal insomnia:* 300–600mg nightly × 8 weeks; effective for sleep + hot flash symptoms
- *Valerian + Lemon Balm combination:* Widely studied; Cernitin product (Euvegal forte): 600mg valerian + 320mg lemon balm — most-tested commercial combination; superior to either alone in meta-analysis

**Drug interactions:**
- **CNS depressants (alcohol, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, opioids, antihistamines):** Additive sedation; CAUTION; may produce excessive CNS depression; separate by 2+ hours minimum; AVOID combination in driving context
- **Anesthesia:** Inform anesthesiologist of valerian use; potential enhanced/prolonged anesthetic effect; discontinue 2 weeks before elective surgery
- **CYP3A4 substrates:** Some evidence of mild CYP3A4 modulation; potential interaction with cyclosporine, some HIV medications; monitor
- **Warfarin:** Anti-platelet activity reported; monitor INR

**Contraindications:**
- **Pregnancy:** Valepotriates are cytotoxic and potentially mutagenic in vitro; AVOID during pregnancy; safety in breastfeeding unknown
- **Hepatic impairment:** Rare cases of hepatotoxicity with high-dose long-term use; monitor LFTs with chronic use (>4 weeks daily)
- **Children <3 years:** Insufficient safety data

**Safety:** Generally excellent at standard doses. Most common adverse effects: vivid dreams, GI upset, daytime drowsiness (especially first 2 weeks), headache at high doses. Unlike benzodiazepines: no respiratory depression; no significant dependence at standard doses; no "morning hangover" with standard 400–600mg doses. Rare hepatotoxicity reports: mostly with high doses, prolonged use, or multi-herb preparations containing valerian; causality not definitively established. Valerian is not habit-forming at normal clinical doses (benzodiazepine comparison: NO); however, rebound insomnia reported with abrupt discontinuation after very long-term use (months).

**Energetic tier:** ☿ Shen (sedative, anxiolytic, nervine — neuromodulatory; primarily addresses Shen disturbance/restlessness) primary; ◉ Qi (calms excess Qi agitation; regulatory) secondary.
