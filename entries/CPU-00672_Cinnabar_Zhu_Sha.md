**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 5-109 KINNABARI (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 10394), translator's heading "red crystalline Mercuric Sulphide — Cinnabar". "Some think cinnabar is the same as that which is called ammium, but are deceived in this." Ammium is made in Spain from a stone mixed with *Arguritidic* sand; in the furnace it has "a choking smell, and therefore the workmen there put bladders around their faces that they may see but not inhale the vapour"; painters use it for walls. The cinnabar of the chapter is "brought from Africa", so scarce that it barely suffices for the painters, and "a heavy or deep colour", so that "some thought it was the blood of dragons". "Cinnabar has the same properties as haematite, good for eye medicines and even more effectively, for it is more astringent and blood staunching. Taken with wax ointments it heals burns and pustules that break out." The next chapter, 5-110 UDRAGUROS (line 10398, "TOXIC"), gives quicksilver as made from ammion "which is also incorrectly called cinnabar", distilled on an iron spoon in a sealed pot.

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** Dan Sha is 丹砂 in `_sources/shennong.csv` line 3 and in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt` lines 869-876, p.22), the second entry (after Yu Quan, jade) under "Jades and Stones: Superior Class": "Dan Sha (Cinnabar) is sweet and slightly cold. It treats hundreds of diseases of the five viscera and the body. It nurtures the essence spirit, quiets the ethereal and corporeal souls, boosts the qi, brightens the eyes, and kills spirit demons and evil malign ghosts. Protracted taking may enable one to communicate with the spirit light and prevent senility. It is capable of transforming into mercury." Yang's notes: 34 gives the modern use (confused spirit, fright palpitations, insomnia; heavy and downbearing, it also suppresses retching); 35 explains that demonology was one of three models of disease current when the book was compiled; 36 calls "communication with the spirit light" a supernatural ability sought by Daoist adepts through self-cultivation and elixirs (lines 890-905). ### Active Components

Cinnabar is mercury(II) sulphide, a red mineral. It is far less soluble than many mercury compounds, but it is not harmless: heating it in air (roasting, "sublimating", distilling it to quicksilver as 5-110 describes) releases elemental mercury vapour. Mercury is a cumulative neurotoxin.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Chinese classic promises that long ingestion "prevents senility"; Greek and Tibetan texts use it on the skin, wounds and fevers. Ingesting a mercury compound in the hope of cultivation is a recognised route to mercury poisoning, and the Codex gives no protocol, dose or preparation. The "essence spirit" and "spirit light" lines are recorded in the classic's own terms and are not translated into any other tradition's vocabulary.

### Preparation & Dosing

DMM 5-109 says only "taken with wax ointments" for burns and pustules. The Greek furnace and distillation procedures belong to ammium and quicksilver (5-109, 5-110). SBJ gives no preparation. Clark records that cinnabar may be "prepared artificially from silver orpiment" (line 836) without method. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Mercury and its compounds are toxic to the nervous system and kidney; this is well established, as is the release of mercury vapour on heating cinnabar. No clinical studies were retrieved for the traditional uses. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No Egyptian locus for cinnabar was found (the one "Vermilion" is Bryan's word for an unnamed ingredient of a salve for suppleness). The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. No deity is attributed.

**Assyrian (AT).** *(c) Absent, no locus.* Nothing was read. The blocking warning is in force. [USER: verify]

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. What 5-109 and 5-110 offer a cultivator is the discipline of attention: telling cinnabar from ammium, knowing the source, the cost and the scarcity, and, for ammium, the workers' bladders against the fumes. No cultivation status is assigned. The chapter on its derivative quicksilver is marked TOXIC.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent, and the attestation rests on a spine row.* No cinnabar row or text was found. Innate heat and radical moisture cannot be tied to cinnabar without a source and are not tied to it here. [USER: verify]

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: upper grade.* Dan Sha stands under "Jades and Stones: Superior Class" (Yang p.22). The preface (lines 348-400) defines the superior class as those that "mainly nourish life", "nontoxic" and not harmful "in large amounts and for a long time", the ones to rely on "to make one's body light, boost the qi, prevent aging, and prolong life". The entry itself credits nurturing the "essence spirit" and quieting the "ethereal and corporeal souls", together with boosting the qi, brightening the eyes, killing demons and preventing senility. Yang's own note 36 sets "communicating with the spirit light" in the Daoist elixir context. Yang prints these terms in English; the Chinese was not checked. [USER: verify] The preface's "nontoxic" does not match the mineral's chemistry, so this grade records what the classic says and is no safety statement. Neidan (inner alchemy) is an overlay later cultivators bring, not part of the classic, and is not used here.

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(c) Absent.* No Sushruta locus for cinnabar was found (above). Nothing is said about *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* for it.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* Cinnabar is a therapeutic stone medicine of ch.20: it "maintain[s] the channels and the bone resin" (Clark line 836) and *mtshal* heals wounds and cures fevers (line 848). Medicine is the third of the four treatment branches and supports practice; no *bcud len* use and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice was found for it.

**Practitioner's note.** The one tradition that grades cinnabar highly, the Chinese classic, does so for a poisonous mineral, and its own preface calls the grade nontoxic. Nothing sourced supports taking cinnabar for cultivation, and the Greek text shows workers masking their faces against its fumes. For a cultivator the honest use is none: the entry is a record of what was written. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Cinnabar is a mercury compound; heating it releases toxic mercury vapour. Do not take it by mouth, burn it or sublimate it. Mercury poisoning damages the nervous system and kidneys, and the unborn child is especially at risk. Sale and use of mercury-containing medicines are restricted in many jurisdictions. "Sublimated" or "roasted" preparations named in the old texts are not standardised.
