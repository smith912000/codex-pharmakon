# Thorowax — Chai Hu (柴胡), Bei Chai Hu, Hare's Ear Root

## *Bupleurum chinense* DC. | Family: Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM, SBJ, FMT
**Energetic Classification:** Cool and dry, bitter and acrid — a dispersing, lifting remedy in the Galenic reckoning, tempering excess heat lodged in the flanks.
**Training Phase:** Purification

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Shennong (TCM):** Chai Hu is one of the oldest and most heavily used roots in the Chinese materia medica, entered in the *Shennong Bencao Jing* among the superior class of remedies said to be taken over long periods to lighten the body. Classical doctrine assigns it to the Liver and Gallbladder channels, where it is the archetypal "harmonising" agent — the ruling herb of the formula Xiao Chai Hu Tang (Minor Bupleurum Decoction), prescribed for the Shaoyang syndrome of alternating fever and chills, bitter taste, and fullness beneath the ribs. Its two signature actions in the tradition are to disperse constrained Liver qi and to raise the clear yang that has sunk.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM):** The genus *Bupleurum* answers to the plants the Greco-Arabic physicians grouped with the fennel-leaved umbellifers used to open obstructions of the liver and spleen. In the Unani frame the root is read as a resolvent for hot swellings and stagnation in the hypochondrium, consonant with its cool, dispersing temperament.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** Dioscorides describes *boupleuron* in *De Materia Medica* as a pot-herb and wound plant, its leaves eaten and applied to swellings — the classical European thread that carries the name *Bupleurum* forward, even though the East Asian root became the pharmacologically dominant member of the genus.

**rGyud-bzhi (FMT):** Bupleurum species enter the Tibetan Four Tantras tradition among the cooling, bile-clearing herbs used against fevers of "heat" character, aligning closely with the Chinese reading of the root as a remedy that vents lodged heat outward rather than draining it downward.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The recurring image across traditions is of a plant that *lifts and releases* — venting a fever trapped half-in, half-out; raising what has fallen; freeing what is bound at the flanks. This is not empty poetry. The saikosaponins concentrated in the root are surface-active triterpene glycosides that modulate inflammatory signalling and hepatic stress pathways, so the doctrinal picture of a substance that "moves the stuck and cools the smouldering" maps onto a real anti-inflammatory, hepatoregulatory chemistry.

### Active Components

- **Saikosaponins (a, c, d)** — oleanane-type triterpenoid saponins, the defining constituents; saikosaponin-d is the most studied and also the most toxicologically significant.
- **Essential oil** — including bupleurumol and various monoterpenes, contributing to the febrifuge action.
- **Polysaccharides** — implicated in immunomodulatory effects.
- **Flavonoids** (rutin, quercetin derivatives) and **sterols** (α-spinasterol).
- **Lignans and phenolic acids** in smaller quantity.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Chai Hu sits in the **Purification** phase as a *releasing* agent rather than a purge. It is indicated where heat and tension are lodged and cyclical — the picture of alternating agitation and dullness, of pressure gathered under the ribs, of a system that neither fully breaks a fever nor lets it go. Its role is to vent that stagnation outward and lift a depleted, sunken tone. Because of its genuine hepatic activity it is treated in Kenetica as a targeted, time-limited resolvent, not a daily tonic — see Contraindications.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed: Chang et al. (2021), *International Journal of Molecular Sciences* — saikosaponin-d reduced serum ALT, AST and ALP and lowered inflammatory markers (IL-1β, TNF-α) in thioacetamide-induced liver injury and high-fat-diet NAFLD in mice, raising hepatic antioxidant enzymes (SOD, catalase, GPx). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms222111383). This supplies a plausible mechanism for the traditional "liver-harmonising" reputation.

The same molecule, however, is dose-dependently hepatotoxic. Zhang et al. (2015), *Environmental Toxicology and Pharmacology* — saikosaponin-d activated the Fas death-receptor pathway and Bid in hepatocytes, and at 300 mg/kg for one week caused histological liver injury and massive hepatocyte apoptosis in mice. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.etap.2015.11.005). Wang et al. (2025), *Plant Signaling & Behavior*, confirms saikosaponins as the pharmacologically active, antipyretic and hepatoprotective principle whose biosynthesis is under active study. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/15592324.2025.2495301).

**Evidence grade:** Preclinical (cell and rodent) for both the hepatoprotective and hepatotoxic actions; the biphasic, dose-dependent liver profile is well characterised in animals but human clinical trial data are limited. Traditional use is extensive and long-attested.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** Bitter, acrid, cool (or slightly cold); enters Liver and Gallbladder channels. Actions: harmonises Shaoyang, courses the Liver and relieves constraint, raises yang qi.
- **Ayurveda / rGyud-bzhi correlate:** read as a cooling, pitta/bile-clearing bitter — *tikta rasa*, cooling *virya*, pungent *vipaka* by analogy.
- **Galenic / Unani:** cool and dry; resolvent and deobstruent for hot obstructions of the liver and hypochondrium.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *Boupleuron* is described in *De Materia Medica* as a pot-herb and wound plant — its leaves eaten and laid on swellings. Dioscorides carries no subtle-body doctrine into which a cultivation status for the root could be set; his register is dietetic and therapeutic. What the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention — the correct plant behind a shared name, the leaf gathered and assayed, the guard against a wrong umbellifer sold under it — which matters doubly here, since the East Asian root that became pharmacologically dominant is not the herb he ate. That is a practice of provenance and sensory judgement, not a vital-body assignment. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The root is read as cool and dry, a resolvent and deobstruent for hot obstructions and stagnation lodged in the liver and hypochondrium. The strongest cultivation idea in the Unani corpus — the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture across a whole regimen — is not a status the tradition attaches to a cooling, dispersing deobstruent like this, and no gladdening or cardiac role for the *ruh* is claimed for it. The attestation is, moreover, generic: the Greco-Arabic placement is of the fennel-leaved umbellifers as a group, not of *Bupleurum chinense* by name. Attested therapeutically, no cultivation status sourced. **(b) framed.**

**Chinese — Shennong (SBJ).** Here the tradition does assign a cultivation status. Under its older name 茈胡 (*zi hu*) the root is entered in the upper grade (上品, *shàng pǐn*) of the *Shénnóng Běncǎo Jīng* — the grade of substances held non-toxic and taken over long periods to nourish life (養生, *yǎng shēng*), classically credited with lightening the body (輕身, *qīng shēn*) and with "expelling the old to bring in the new." That upper-grade placement is attested in the classic itself and carried in Sabine Wilms' scholarly translation. The status is conservational, not curative — and it is worth naming that the same materia medica gives the root no formal *jing*-action; its cultivation standing rests on the *san pin* grade, not on a claimed effect on the vital substrate. **(a) attested** — upper grade (上品), *Shénnóng Běncǎo Jīng*.

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Bupleurum* enters the Four Tantras tradition among the cooling, bile-clearing (*mkhris pa*) herbs turned against fevers of heat character. Medicine here is the third of the four treatment branches — it supports practice, it does not reach the root, which is a condition of the three poisons — and the tradition holds no formal "substances for subtle-body practice" category into which this herb could be graded. Its placement, further, is by alignment with the Chinese reading of the root rather than a distinct Tibetan cultivation assignment. Used therapeutically as a heat-venting bitter, no cultivation status recorded. **(b) framed.**

**Practitioner's note.** Only the Chinese stratum treats Chai Hu as anything a cultivator takes for the long term, and even there the upper-grade standing sits uneasily against the modern toxicological reading — the same saikosaponin that harmonises the liver injures it at higher exposure. The honest practice is the short course the tradition itself favours: a targeted, time-limited resolvent for lodged, cyclical heat and sunken yang, then stopped. It is not a daily tonic, whatever the *shàng pǐn* label once promised.

### Contraindications

**Hepatotoxicity is the central hazard.** Saikosaponin-d is directly hepatotoxic at high doses in animal models, acting through Fas/Bid-mediated hepatocyte apoptosis (Zhang et al. 2015, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.etap.2015.11.005)). Prolonged or high-dose Bupleurum use — and Bupleurum-containing formulas such as sho-saiko-to — has been associated with reports of drug-induced liver injury and, rarely, interstitial pneumonitis. Avoid in pre-existing liver disease, active hepatitis, or where liver enzymes are unmonitored. Do not combine with other hepatotoxic agents. The genuine antipyretic-hepatoprotective effect at ordinary decoction doses does not license continuous or escalating use — the toxic and protective effects trace to the *same* molecule at different exposures.
