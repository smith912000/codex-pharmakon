# Melimela — MELIMELA (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 1-161); "glycymela" by some

## *A sweet kind of apple fruit, in the translator's heading "Honey Apples, Must Apples, Cider Apples"; Dioscorides says that some call it glycymela, "as we should say, sweet apples". The identification is ours: a sweet-fruited kind of the apple tree (Malus), species and variety unresolved, at low confidence. The translation prints no binomial of its own for this chapter, and the Codex does not equate it with the ordinary apple or the crab apple, which have their own chapters* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder from an extraction of the Dioscorides chapter headings; the 30 October check pass kept it as a distinct substance. A search of the other texts on disk was not made for the Greek name. The spine's existing apple and crab-apple entries state that this chapter is a separate "sweet kind" and is not their row (`Volume_I_Plant_Based/entries/I_893_Crabapple.md` line 18).

**Dioscorides (DMM).** The chapter is 1-161 MELIMELA, in Book One, in the section headed FRUIT TREES (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 4715; the name index prints "Melimela 166"). The translator's gloss line reads "SUGGESTED: Melimela — Honey Apples, Must Apples, Cider Apples". The whole chapter (lines 4715 to 4718) is: "Melimela soften the intestines and drive living creatures from there [worms]. They are bad for the stomach and cause a burning heat. They are called glycymela by some — as we should say, sweet apples."

That is the full text. The bracketed "[worms]" is the translator's, not Dioscorides's. Dioscorides gives no description of the tree or fruit, no place, no season, no part other than the fruit, and no preparation. The Greek name is a compound of "honey" (*meli*) and "apple" (*melon*) in the ordinary reading, and the gloss "glycymela", "sweet apples", is Dioscorides's own explanation of it. **Neighbouring chapters, for context only.** The neighbouring chapters give the other apples and the quince. 1-159 MELEA covers the leaves, blossoms and sprigs of "all sorts of melea trees" ("astringent, especially those of the quince tree"; line 4687) and says the apples ripe in spring "encourage bile, are hurtful to all that is sinewy, and are flatulent". 1-160 KUDONIA is the quince. 1-162 MELA EPEIROTIKA is a stomach and urine fruit, "yet less effective than quinces", and 1-163 AGRIOMELA the astringent wild apple. Melimela is the only one of the apple chapters that is called soft on the bowels and bad for the stomach.

A sweet cultivar would be expected to carry a high ratio of sugar to acid, which agrees in kind with the name. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** To soften the intestines and drive out intestinal creatures ("worms" in the translator's bracket).
- **What the text says against it.** Bad for the stomach; causes a burning heat.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no preparation, no number or amount, no season, no cultivar.

The Codex does not translate the classic's terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend using fruit as a vermifuge.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Ripe apples are a common food. Sweet fruit and its fibre and sugars can loosen the stool in some people when eaten in quantity. That is a general dietary fact and is not offered as a confirmation of Dioscorides's claim that the fruit drives out worms, which is not supported by anything retrieved. ### Cultivation Register

**Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured here. The chapter is a short note on an everyday fruit with a mixed report: softening to the bowels and expelling worms, bad for the stomach and heating. What Dioscorides offers a cultivator in general is a discipline of attention (provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, adulteration detection), but this chapter practises none of it: it gives no choice of fruit, no season and no test.

**Practitioner's note.** A sweet apple that Dioscorides reports as loosening, vermifuge, hard on the stomach and heating. Nothing in the chapter supports any cultivation use, and a cultivator should treat the fruit as food and nothing more.

### Contraindications

- **Stomach.** The text itself calls the fruit bad for the stomach; anyone with a weak stomach would do well to heed it.
- **Identity.** The species and cultivar are unresolved; do not assume modern dessert apples match the ancient "honey apple".
- **Worms.** Intestinal parasites are a medical condition; use proper care and not fruit.
- **Fruit-tree seed.** Apple seeds contain a small amount of cyanogenic glycoside; this is a general fact and is not in the text.
- **Heating.** The text's "burning heat" is the only quality printed; no more is said.
