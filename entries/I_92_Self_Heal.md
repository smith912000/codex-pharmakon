### Botanical Identity
Perennial 10–30cm; ovate leaves; distinctive dense cylindrical spikes of violet-purple flowers with brown bracts (giving a pinecone-like appearance); cosmopolitan weed of lawns, meadows, roadsides, woodland margins; grows in partial shade and disturbed ground worldwide. The flowering spike (the whole aerial part at flowering) is the drug material. One of the most globally distributed medicinal weeds — present on every inhabited continent. The *Xia Ku Cao* (夏枯草) designation in Chinese medicine specifically refers to the dried flower spike harvested in summer (after summer solstice).

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ)

*Xià Kū Cǎo*:
> *"Bitter, acrid; cold. Enters Liver, Gallbladder. Clears Liver Fire, dissipates nodules and masses, brightens the eyes. Primary herb for scrofula (tuberculosis of lymph nodes), goitre, and all hard swellings and lumps. For Liver Yang rising (headache, dizziness, eye redness)."*

The Chinese application — clearing nodules, dissipating masses, treating scrofula — is distinct from the European wound/throat focus. Together they constitute the two poles of self-heal's pharmacology: topical/wound/throat (European) and lymphatic/oncological/thyroid (Chinese).

### 2.2 De Materia Medica (DMM)

European herbal tradition:
> *"Prunella: good for wounds of all kinds. Applied fresh to wounds it staunches blood and promotes healing. The decoction gargled relieves quinsy and sore throats. Good for 'mouth thrush' and gum infections."*

### 2.3 Canon of Medicine (CM)

Ibn Sīnā on *brūnilla* (the European throat herb):
> *"Cooling and drying. Good for throat inflammation, quinsy, and oral infections. Heals wounds when applied. Reduces heat in the Liver."*

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)

Wound-healing aromatic herb preparations consistent with self-heal's application.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Antiviral (Primary — Emerging Modern Application)

**Prunellin + polysaccharides + rosmarinic acid + caffeic acid**: *P. vulgaris* has one of the most intensively studied antiviral profiles in the Lamiaceae family:

- **HSV-1 and HSV-2 inhibition**: Multiple mechanisms — prunellin sulphated polysaccharide directly binds viral glycoproteins → prevents cell entry; caffeic acid inhibits HSV replication; rosmarinic acid inhibits viral integrase. *P. vulgaris* extract has IC50 of 5–50µg/ml for HSV in vitro
- **HIV inhibition**: Polysaccharide fraction inhibits HIV-1 reverse transcriptase and integrase; prunellin blocks HIV binding to CD4 receptor
- **SARS-CoV-2**: Rosmarinic acid and luteolin have demonstrated Mpro inhibitory activity (shared mechanism with baicalein, I_82)
- **Influenza**: Ursolic acid inhibits neuraminidase

The antiviral spectrum of self-heal (HSV, HIV, influenza, coronavirus) makes it one of the most pharmacologically interesting plants in the Codex for infectious disease applications — yet it is one of the most widely available common weeds.

### 5.2 Lymphadenopathy / Anti-Tumour

**Ursolic acid + oleanolic acid + rosmarinic acid**: The Chinese *xia ku cao* application for "dissipating masses and nodules" (including thyroid nodules, scrofula, lymphoma) is now mechanistically interpretable:
- Ursolic acid: induces apoptosis in lymphoma cells (Bcl-2 inhibition, caspase activation); anti-angiogenic (VEGF inhibition)
- Oleanolic acid: inhibits NF-κB in tumour cells → reduces survival signalling
- Rosmarinic acid: MAO inhibition; NF-κB inhibition; pro-apoptotic in cancer cells

### 5.3 Anti-Inflammatory

**Rosmarinic acid + luteolin + ursolic acid**: Triple COX/LOX/NF-κB inhibition — broad anti-inflammatory that explains the wound, throat, and lymphatic applications.

### 5.4 Hypotensive / Liver Yang (TCM)

**Rosmarinic acid + luteolin**: Vasodilatory via eNOS upregulation (similar to the mechanism of rutin and quercetin). The TCM "Liver Yang rising" pattern (hypertension, headache, eye redness) and the modern antihypertensive mechanism converge: rosmarinic acid inhibits ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) → reduces angiotensin II-driven vasoconstriction → reduces blood pressure.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**HSV (herpes)**: Koytchev (1999): *P. vulgaris* lip cream significantly reduced HSV lesion area and healing time vs placebo in herpes labialis (cold sores) — statistically and clinically significant. The most robust clinical trial for this herb.

**Hypertension (China)**: Multiple Chinese clinical studies report *xia ku cao* preparations reduce blood pressure in hypertensive patients — consistent with the ACE-inhibitory mechanism of rosmarinic acid. Evidence quality variable but mechanistic support strong.

**Cancer (pilot — lymphoma/thyroid)**: Traditional Chinese oncology uses *xia ku cao* extensively as an adjunct for lymphomas and thyroid nodules. Clinical trial data limited; case series consistent with the ursolic acid apoptotic mechanism.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The plant named for what it does**: "Self-Heal" — a plant whose English name encodes its entire therapeutic category in two words. Carpenter's Herb, Woundwort, Heal-All — all English common names for the same plant point to the same application: you apply it to wounds and they heal. The naming tradition across European languages (from German *Brunella* for throat-healing through English *Self-Heal* for wound-healing) is a record of folk clinical observation — each name a one-word trial report. The Chinese name *Xia Ku Cao* (summer-withering herb) is pharmacological in a different way: the plant's timing (dying back after summer solstice) and its association with excess Fire in TCM theory led to its application for Fire-excess conditions (hot nodules, red eyes, headache) — the plant's life cycle encoded its therapeutic category.

**The common weed and the uncommon pharmacology**: Self-heal is one of the most pharmacologically interesting plants in the Codex relative to its botanical status. It grows in every lawn that hasn't been herbicided, on every roadside in temperate zones worldwide. It is the herb most likely to be growing within 500 metres of any reader of this Codex in a temperate country. Yet it has documented activity against HSV-1, HSV-2, HIV, influenza, and SARS-CoV-2 in vitro; anti-tumour activity in lymphoma and thyroid cancer models; antihypertensive activity via ACE inhibition; anti-inflammatory activity equivalent to commercial anti-inflammatory herbs. The pharmacological distance between "common lawn weed" and "multi-target antiviral anti-cancer antihypertensive" is zero — they are the same plant. This is perhaps the clearest illustration that traditional herbal medicine's most important herbs are not rare, exotic, or expensive — they are the weeds underfoot.

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Bitter, acrid; cold. Channels: Liver (肝), Gallbladder (膽). Clears Liver Fire, dissipates nodules, brightens eyes, reduces Liver Yang rising. One of the most important *qing re xiao zhong* (heat-clearing, swelling-dispersing) herbs.

**Ayurvedic (cross-reference)**: Bitter, astringent; cold. Reduces Pitta ↓↓↓. *Jvaraghna* (febrifuge), *Kuṣṭhāghna* (skin diseases), *Viṣaghna* (anti-infective). Not a classical Ayurvedic plant.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Autoimmune conditions**: Immunomodulatory polysaccharides — theoretically stimulatory; use with caution if on immunosuppressive therapy; discuss with specialist
- **Hypothyroidism / thyroid medications**: The traditional Chinese use for thyroid nodules includes potential thyroid-modulating effects; monitor thyroid function if using therapeutically
- **Pregnancy**: Limited safety data; avoid therapeutic doses
- **General safety**: Exceptional safety record; the herb is consumed as a food and condiment in parts of Asia (young leaves in salads, soups) — dietary amounts universally safe
