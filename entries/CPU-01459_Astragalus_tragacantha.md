# Gum Tragacanth Plant / Astragalus tragacantha — Tragakanthe (Dioscorides 3-23)

## *Astragalus tragacantha (the Dioscorides translators' suggestion for chapter 3-23; "Gum Tragacanth Plant, Goat's Thorn")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). **Named only by Dioscorides in this row.** The binomial is the translators' suggestion, not Dioscorides' own identification.

*3-23 TRAGAKANTHE* (heading at line 6720; text at lines 6722 to 6727). The SUGGESTED line reads "Astragalus tragacantha — Gum Tragacanth Plant, Goat's Thorn". The chapter sits in the section headed "ROOTS OF AKANTHODA or PRICKLY PLANTS". The description: "Tragacantha has a root that is broad and woody appearing above the earth. From this low strong branches emerge, spreading out. On them are many small thin leaves with prickles between hidden in the leaves — white, strong, upright. There is also a tragacanth gum adhering to the root when it is cut."

The text then speaks of the gum: "The best is transparent, smooth, slender, clean, and somewhat sweet. It is able like [other] gums to close the pores. It is used for eye medicines, coughs, roughness of the arteries; and for dripping fluids in a linctus [syrup] with honey. It melts when put under the tongue. A teaspoonful steeped in passum [raisin wine] is taken as a drink for pain of the kidneys and erosion of the bladder. Hart's horn that has been burned and washed (or a little allom scissile [5-123]) is also mixed with it."

The chapter prints no alternative names. The chapters on either side (3-22 LEUKAKANTHA, 3-24 ERUNGION) are different plants and are not read into this row.

### Active Components

Gum tragacanth is generally known as a plant exudate made mostly of complex polysaccharides, which swells in water to form a gel or thick mucilage. This agrees in general with the text's gum that "closes the pores" and "melts when put under the tongue". The composition of the gum Dioscorides meant was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records the gum as a quality-graded material used in eye medicines, for coughs, for "roughness of the arteries", for dripping fluids in a honey linctus, and in a raisin-wine drink for kidney and bladder pain. These are the text's own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for kidney or bladder disease, which need medical care.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these preparations. The gum in a linctus with honey (line 6726). The gum placed under the tongue, where it melts. "A teaspoonful steeped in passum [raisin wine] is taken as a drink" for kidney pain and bladder erosion (line 6726). Burnt, washed hart's horn, or "a little allom scissile [5-123]", mixed with it. The teaspoonful is the one quantity printed. No further dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Gum tragacanth is a known, long-used thickening and suspending agent, and a bulk mucilage can soothe by coating. This is a general statement about the material and no study of the kidney or bladder use was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** no warming, cooling or drying quality is printed for 3-23. The text says only that the gum is "able like [other] gums to close the pores".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and the chapter assigns the plant no status. What it offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention, and here the discipline is a sensory assay of the gum: the best is "transparent, smooth, slender, clean, and somewhat sweet", and it melts under the tongue. The chapter also teaches how to know the plant (a broad, woody root above ground, small leaves hiding prickles) and where the gum comes from (it adheres to the cut root). Provenance, quality grading and assay are the practice recorded. The identification with *Astragalus tragacantha* is the translators' suggestion. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator learns from this chapter how a gum is judged and nothing else. It does not support taking the gum as a vitality practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Gum tragacanth is generally known to be a bulk-forming material that can cause an allergic reaction in some people, and a dry swallowed gum can cause blockage in the throat or gut if taken without enough fluid. Pain of the kidneys or erosion of the bladder, the conditions the text names, need medical care. Many *Astragalus* species are known to be poisonous to livestock, and the identity here is the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
