# Antispodium — Antispoda (ἀντισποδία), "ashes used instead of spodium" (Dioscorides 5-86)
## *Plant and other ashes burned in sealed jars as a substitute for spodium (the calcined powder of 5-85); the translator's note reads "Ashes used instead of Spodium [5-85]"* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek Tradition (DMM).** Antispodium is the subject of one short chapter, *De Materia Medica* 5-86 *ANTISPODA* in the 2000 English translation (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10242-10248; name index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 859; book index "ANTISPODA 784", line 12014). It opens: "Since spodium is often not available and antispodia are very good to use, it is necessary both to show what they are, which of them are of equal strength, and how they should be taken." The chapter is a manufacturing note and a list of materials. It states no medical indication and no temperament of its own; the reader is sent back to spodium.

**What spodium is, in Dioscorides.** The reference is to 5-85, the chapter on *Pompholux* (the file's heading *Pompholyx*, suggested "Zinc Oxide"), which contrasts spodium with pompholyx: "the spodos is somewhat black and for the most part heavier as it is full of lumps, hairs and earth, because it is the scrapings and shavings from the floors and hearths in the brass refiners' shops" (line 10230). The same chapter says "Some think that the previously mentioned spodos is only made this way" (line 10234), tells how to roast the powder into lozenges (line 10241), and adds "spodium is also made out of gold and silver, and furthermore also from lead; that from lead is the best, then that from copper" (line 10241). The translator's gloss throughout is "calcinated powder". The lines read give the preparation of spodium but no medicinal action of it. (Pompholyx is called "astringent, cooling, filling, purging, and somewhat drying" in line 10241; that is said of pompholyx, not of spodium or antispodium.)

**The recipe.** "Take therefore myrtle leaves with the flowers and unripe myrtle berries, put them into an unfired clay jar, and having sealed the cover and pierced it through with many holes, put it into a potter's furnace. When the jar is well baked, put [the myrtle] into another unfired jar, and when this second jar is thoroughly baked, take it out, wash it and use it" (line 10248; the myrtle cross-references are 1-155 and 4-146). The same method is then said to apply to: a branch of the wild olive, or failing that the cultivated olive with its flowers; quinces cut in pieces with the pith taken out; oak galls; linen rags; unripe white mulberries dried in the sun beforehand; lentisk (1-90); terminthos shoots or buds (1-91); the tender leaves of bramble; the filaments of box; "that which is called false cypress together with its flowers"; branches of the fig tree dried in the sun; bulls' glue; and rough unwashed wool moistened with pitch or honey. The chapter closes: "All the previously mentioned ashes are useful in that they may serve instead of spodium [calcinated powder]" (line 10248). Though the chapter announces that it will show "which of them are of equal strength", no ranking among the materials is printed in the translation read. [USER: verify]

**A second route to a substitute.** In the preceding cadmia chapter (5-84) the burnt cadmia is beaten, mixed with wine and burned a third time "until it is formed perfectly into ashes without any roughness, and they use this instead of spodium [calcinated powder]" (line 10227). That is a different material used for the same purpose, and it is noted here only as a cross-reference.

**Identity.** The plural *antispodia* names a class of substitutes, not one substance. Several of the items (bramble, box, olive, myrtle, mulberry, fig) are plants, and the translator's notes give no identification beyond cross-references. The Codex files the row as "mineral" because it substitutes for a mineral-derived calcine. Whether every item listed is the plant a modern reader would assume was not checked. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Stated generally. Ashes of plant matter are mainly mineral salts: carbonates (including potassium and calcium carbonate), silica and oxides of the metals the plant took up, with carbon where the burning was incomplete. Sealed, pierced-lid roasting in a closed jar is a way of charring material with little air, so the product would be a mix of charcoal and ash, and the proportions would depend on the roasting. Dioscorides' spodium is a metal-bearing residue from the working of copper and brass, so it would carry oxides of zinc, copper or lead; the plant ashes would not. No quantities were read in the sources retrieved and none are given.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved give antispodia as a stand-in for a calcine, with no indication and no application of their own, and the thing they stand in for contains metal oxides that are not safe for a cultivator to prepare or take. No modern protocol is supplied. The only transferable matter is the historical habit: Dioscorides names a substitute when the authentic drug is unavailable.

The text gives one method (the double roasting in sealed, perforated unfired jars; washing; use) and a long list of starting materials (line 10248 above). ### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention. 5-86 is an example of it: a short, practical statement of what to do when the proper material is missing, with the materials named and the method fixed (sealed jar, two bakings, washing). Nothing in the chapter assigns a cultivation status, and nothing in it is a rite or an amulet.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing here for a cultivator to take. The chapter belongs to the craft of the physician's workshop: how to make one thing when you lack another. The lesson that carries over is attention to substitution and provenance, not the ashes themselves. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Toxicity of the material replaced:** spodium as Dioscorides describes it is a residue from metal working, with lead and copper variants; the substance it is a substitute for is not suitable for internal use.
- **Preparation hazard:** roasting in sealed jars produces carbon monoxide and smoke; the practice is a furnace operation, not a kitchen one.
- **Wool, glue and pitch:** burning animal glue, pitch-moistened wool or linen rags produces irritant and sooty products.
- **Ashes are alkaline:** wood and plant ash can irritate skin and mucous membranes.
- **Identity:** the plants named are given by Dioscorides' ancient names and were not independently identified here.
