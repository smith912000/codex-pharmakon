### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Sushruta text (SS); this entry rests on one text, Bhishagratna's English translation of the Sushruta Samhita, and on a Latin name that comes from the translator's appendix and not from the chapters.** The chapters are in `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/` and the appendix is in `Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`. The chapters name the drug only by Sanskrit name (*Karkaru*, and *Karkaruka* in one place). A search of the Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Tibetan text folders on disk for *Cucumis* found no hit. The Dioscorides text prints *Cucumis* only in the translator's SUGGESTED lines for other Greek drugs (lines 5937, 5952, 5956 and 9402, for colocynth, cucumber, melon and wild cucumber), and none is read as this plant. The row was added from the printed Latin (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP, confidence "medium") and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. **Row key.** The row has three source rows in `Sources/SS_Sushruta/extract/names.csv`, all keyed by a **Sanskrit name** as the OCR prints it: line 490, "Karkaru" and line 491, "Karka'ruka/" (appendix line 31926, Latin line 32011, "Cucumis utilatissimus ; Convolvulus"), and line 493, "Karkati" (appendix line 31932, Latin line 32015, "Cucumis utilatissimus."). The spine's binomial is the Latin common to the three, in the spelling that the batch note records as merged ("Printed forms merged: Cucumis utilatissimus"). "Karkaru" is the first of the names in the file. The extraction's note says each pairing was proposed by an agent from the two OCR columns and checked by a script. The Latin form "Cucumis utilatissimus" is printed the same way twice, so the spelling is the appendix's; the spine's "utilissimus" is a correction by whoever keyed the row.

**Identity doubt.** The Sushruta chapters give no Latin name, and the appendix's Latin is a translator's identification from 1907 to 1916 that the Codex has not checked. It is the Codex's understanding, not checked against POWO or a flora, that *Cucumis utilissimus* Roxb. is now usually treated as a long-fruited form of the melon, *Cucumis melo*. The *Karkaru* line prints a second Latin name, "Convolvulus Panicutatus", and a fragment "Mello."; the Codex does not know whether the second name is a second identification of the same drug or belongs to a name that the OCR dropped. It is also the Codex's understanding (unchecked) that *Convolvulus paniculatus* and *Batatas paniculata*, the subject of I_1368, are names of one morning-glory-family plant, so the same Latin name may stand on two entries of this batch. The appendix also prints "Cucumis Sativus" (line 33143) directly after "Trapusha" (line 33141), and, by the same counting as above, "Cucumis utillissimus" (line 31696, a third spelling) against the Sanskrit "Ervaruka" (OCR "Errairuka", line 31652, between "Eranda" at 31651 and "Gairika" at 31655, with "Elettaiia Cardamomum" at 31694 and "Ricinus communis" at 31695 before it). These two lines are not part of the row's loci. They show that Bhishagratna places the cucumber *Trapusha* under *Cucumis sativus* and, by the Codex's own count, the cucumber *Ervaruka* under the same *utilissimus* as *Karkaru*; the Codex does not draw a conclusion from them. **Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *The names in the chapters.* "Karkati" is not in any chapter file: a search of the chapter files for "karkat" found *Karkata-S'ringi* (for example Sutra 38 line 261; Chikitsa 17 line 140; Uttara 21 line 274), *Karkataka* (Kalpa 2, Uttara 58), a crab (*Karkata*, Chikitsa 25 line 110) and *Karkatika* (Kalpa 5 line 411, in a list of ingredients of an antidote compound), and none is read as the appendix's *Karkati*. "Karkaru" is in the chapters in these places.

*Sutra 45, the liquids chapter ("Drava-Dravya-Vidhi-madhyaryam", lines 2 to 5), line 983 to 989 (page 446).* "The Oils obtained from the Kirata-tiktaka, Atimuktaka, Vibhitaka, Narikela, Kola, Akshoda, Jivanti, Piyala, Karvudara, Surjavalli, Trapusa, Ervaruka, Karkaru, and Kushmanda seeds, etc. are sweet in taste, potency and digestion, and tend to pacify the deranged Vayu and Pittam. Cooling in their potency, they increase the slimy secretions of the organs, impair digestion, and help the copious evacuation of stool and urine." (The text prints "sweet ... in potency" and then "cooling in their potency"; the Codex quotes both as printed and does not reconcile them.)

*Sutra 46, the chapter on food and drink ("Annapana-Vidhi-madhyayam", lines 2 to 5), the group of potherbs, lines 1394 to 1409 (page 509, continuing on page 510).* The group opens with the fruits of creepers (*Pushpaphala*, *Alavu*, *Kalindak*; lines 1370 to 1375). Then: "The fruits such as the Trapusha, Ervaruka, Karkaru, and Shirna-Vrinta are heavy in digestion and long retained in the stomach in an undigested state. They are palatable, cooling in their potency, generate Kapham, tend to facilitate the discharge of stool and urine, contain a little alkaline matter, and are sweet in taste. A green and tender cucumber subdues the Pittam, while one which is of a pale yellow colour acts as a stomachic. A ripe and yellow Trapusham tends to subdue the Pittam only in the event of its possessing an acid taste. Similarly, ripe Ervarukas and Karkarus generate the Vayu and Kapham, have a sweet taste, and are found to be charged with alkaline matter. They are appetising, relishing and do not inordinately generate the Pittam."

*Chikitsa 31, the chapter on oleaginous substances ("Snehaupayogika-Chikitsita", lines 8 to 10), line 57 (page 547).* "The expressed oils of Trapusha, Ervaruka, Karkaruka, Tumbi and of Kushmanda are used in cases of Mutra-sanga (for diuretic purposes)." ("Karkdruka", with a diacritic, is the OCR's spelling; "(for diuretic purposes)" is Bhishagratna's gloss in brackets.)

*Also read.* Sutra 9 (line 20) names "Ervaruka" with the gourd, the watermelon and the cucumber among the fruits on which the incisions are practised; Sutra 42 (line 293) lists "Trapusha, Ervaruka" with the sweet-group drugs, and does not list *Karkaru*. Both lines are about the neighbouring name *Ervaruka*, which this entry does not claim.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Cucumis utilissimus*, *utilatissimus*, *Karkaru*, *Karkati* and *Karkaruka* found no entry on this drug. **I_489** (Cucumber, *Cucumis sativus*; its Sushruta paragraph speaks of *trapusha* and *ervaru* and gives them a framed (b) register) is the entry for the cucumber under another Latin name, and does not name *Karkaru*. **I_915** (Kasha) quotes Chikitsa 7 (line 56), which names *Trapusha* and *Ervaruka* seeds as an ingredient. **I_1368** (*Batatas paniculata*) is the sibling entry of this batch, and the possible shared Latin name noted above. The Dioscorides cucumber and melon chapters have no entry here. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Cucumis* belongs to the gourd family (Cucurbitaceae), that the fruit of the cultivated cucumbers and melons is mostly water, and that bitter wild or spoiled forms of the family can contain toxic cucurbitacins. Which of these the Sushruta drug resembles is not shown by the lines read. The chapters' "a little alkaline matter" and "charged with alkaline matter" are the text's own descriptions and are not a composition. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Sushruta text records *Karkaru* as a cooling, sweet fruit among the potherbs that is heavy to digest, that generates Kapha and eases the passage of stool and urine, and its seed oil among oils that pacify Vayu and Pitta; *Karkaruka* oil is said to be used for urinary obstruction (*Mutra-sanga*). These are the text's claims about a Sanskrit name, and the Latin name that the appendix attaches to it is the translator's. They are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives the following, each with its locus. Sutra 46, lines 1394 to 1409: the fruit eaten green and tender or ripe and yellow, with the properties above. Sutra 45, lines 983 to 989: the oil of the seeds. Chikitsa 31, line 57: the expressed oil, for urinary obstruction. No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SS:** Sutra 46 (lines 1394 to 1409) prints for the fruits *Trapusha*, *Ervaruka* and *Karkaru* together: heavy in digestion, cooling in potency, sweet in taste, generating Kapha, facilitating the discharge of stool and urine, with "a little alkaline matter"; and, for ripe *Ervarukas* and *Karkarus*, generating Vayu and Kapha, sweet in taste, "charged with alkaline matter", and not inordinately generating Pitta. Sutra 45 (lines 983 to 989) prints for the seed oils (including *Karkaru*) sweet in taste, potency and digestion, pacifying deranged Vayu and Pitta, and cooling in potency. No individual *vipaka* is printed for *Karkaru*.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* *Karkaru* is named in the food chapter (Sutra 46) as a cooling, sweet fruit of the potherb group, in the liquids chapter (Sutra 45) among seed oils, and in the chapter on oleaginous substances (Chikitsa 31, as *Karkaruka*) as an oil for urinary obstruction. A search of the chapter files for *Karkaru* and *Karkaruka* found no hit in the rasayana or vajikarana chapters (Chikitsa 26 to 30), and none of the lines read says anything of *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* for the drug. The frame is dietetic and therapeutic (*ahara* and a diuretic oil), and the sattvic-rajasic-tamasic food lists are Samkhya and later material and are not Sushruta's, so none is imported. *Karkati* is not in the chapters, so no claim is made for it.

**Practitioner's note.** In the Sushruta scheme *Karkaru* is a food and an oil for a defined complaint, with no rejuvenation or vitality status in the chapters read. A cultivator does nothing special with it. The Latin identification is the translator's appendix pairing and the chapters do not confirm it.

### Contraindications

The Sushruta passages read print no warning beyond the qualities above: the fruit is "heavy in digestion", "long retained in the stomach in an undigested state", and generates Kapha. Urinary obstruction is a condition that needs medical care. The identity is a translator's pairing, so no fruit should be gathered or eaten on the strength of this entry; it is a general fact that bitter wild members of the gourd family can be toxic.
