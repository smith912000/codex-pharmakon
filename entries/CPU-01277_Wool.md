# Wool — Eria (ERIA), "Wool"; burnt wool, Eria kekaumena (ERIA KEKAUMENA)
## *The fleece of the sheep, unwashed and still carrying its grease; the translator's heading to Dioscorides 2-82 is "Wool", and no species is named in the chapter text* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Wool is chapter 82 of Book Two, "Living Creatures", of the *De Materia Medica*, headed ERIA with the translator's gloss "SUGGESTED: Wool" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 5272-5273; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` carries the chapter as 2-82 (line 5272), and the book's page index prints "Wool 211"). The whole chapter is short (line 5274): "The best, unwashed wool is softest, like that from the neck and from the thighs. It is good (moistened in vinegar and oil or wine) as first treatment for wounds, bruises, peeling, black and blue bruises, and broken bones. For it easily drinks up the liquors into which it is dipped, and by reason of the oesypum [lanolin] that it contains it is softening. It is good applied with vinegar and rosaceum [1-53] for headaches and pains in the stomach or any other place."

Three things follow from that text. First, the wool meant is *unwashed* wool, the fleece with its grease still in it, and the translator's bracket identifies the grease as oesypum, "lanolin", the subject of the next-but-one chapter (2-84, a separate entry of this Codex). Second, the grade of the material is stated by a touch test and a place on the animal: the best is the softest, and the softest is that from the neck and the thighs. Third, the stated reason it works is physical: it "easily drinks up the liquors into which it is dipped", so it is a carrier and a pad. The uses are external dressings: first treatment for wounds, bruises, peeling skin, black-and-blue bruises and broken bones, wet with vinegar and oil or wine; and, applied with vinegar and rosaceum (oil of roses, per the translator's heading at 1-53), for headache and for pains of the stomach "or any other place". No quantity, duration or method of fixing it is given.

*Burnt wool (2-83).* The next chapter, ERIA KEKAUMENA, "Burnt Wool" (lines 5275-5277), treats the burnt material as a different drug. "Burnt wool has the properties of scab forming, repressing abnormal growths of flesh, and drawing ulcers to a scar." The text then gives the method: burn it in "an unfired clay jar", "after being cleaned and carded", and also burn locks dyed with sea purple. Some card the wool with the lanolin left on, moisten it with honey and burn it; some set skewers in a broad-mouthed jar, lay thin chips of pitch pine on them, and set the carded wool, moistened with oil, on top, layering chips and wool and lighting the chips from underneath. The ash is washed for eye medicines in a ceramic jar, with water poured on and stirred "lustily with the hands", changed repeatedly "until it does not bite if applied to the tongue but is somewhat astringent". This is a preparation of wool, not a variety of it, and is recorded under Preparation & Dosing.

*Wool as a vehicle elsewhere in the book.* Wool recurs through the *De Materia Medica* as a pad or pessary carrier, not as a drug in itself. Three examples that were opened: egg white "lessens inflammation of the eyes applied with wool and with rosaceum [1-53], wine and honey mixed with it" (2-55, line 5157); dried goat dung "applied in wool with frankincense" stops women's flows (2-98, line 5372); and vinegar "applied with unwashed wool or sponges keeps inflammation away" (5-21, line 9875). These show wool in its supporting role. They are not claims about wool.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, sheep wool is a protein fibre, keratin, built of long-chain proteins rich in sulfur-containing amino acids; in the fleece it is coated with a wax-and-fat secretion of the skin (the grease the text calls oesypum), together with sweat salts and dirt. Washing and carding remove most of the grease. Burning wool leaves an ash; nothing in the text says what the ash contains.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records unwashed wool as a soft absorbent dressing for wounds, bruises and fractures and as a carrier for vinegar, oil, wine and rose oil. The Codex does not recommend using raw fleece on wounds: it is not sterile, it can hold dirt and spores, and wounds and broken bones need clinical care. Burnt wool and its ash are recorded in the same spirit. The entry exists to record what the text says about a plain and humble material, and what the text says is that its virtue is softness and absorbency.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives two forms. Unwashed wool, moistened in vinegar and oil or wine, is laid on wounds and bruises, or applied with vinegar and rosaceum to pains (2-82, line 5274). Burnt wool is made as described in 2-83: cleaned and carded wool burnt in an unfired clay jar, with the variants given above, the ash washed in water until it no longer bites on the tongue but is somewhat astringent (lines 5276-5277). For the ash the text says it is washed "for eye medicines". No weight, volume or duration is given for either form. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Wool fibre absorbs a large amount of water relative to its weight and has long been used for padding; fleece with its grease still in it contains lanolin, a wax used in skin preparations (see the entry for wool fat). Raw fleece is not sterile and can carry bacteria and spores, including those that cause tetanus and anthrax, and wool-handling has occupational infection risks. Burning an animal fibre produces smoke and ash with irritant compounds. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** The single quality printed for wool in 2-82 is that it is "softening", and the text gives its cause as the oesypum it contains. For burnt wool, 2-83 prints the properties "scab forming, repressing abnormal growths of flesh, and drawing ulcers to a scar", and for the washed ash that it should be "somewhat astringent" on the tongue. No warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality is printed for either.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is asserted here. What the chapter offers a practitioner is a discipline of attention: it names a grade ("the best, unwashed wool is softest"), a place on the animal ("the neck and the thighs"), and a test by touch, and in 2-83 it gives a test by taste for the washed ash (it must not "bite" the tongue and must be "somewhat astringent"). The chapter makes no claim about the person's constitution, vitality or long life, so the substance carries a therapeutic status only. A cultivation reading of wool would be an imposition.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. Wool is a dressing, a pad and a carrier in a Greek materia medica. The only lesson the chapter teaches is the habit it models: judge the material by touch and by taste before using it, and choose the softest. The Codex does not recommend raw fleece on wounds. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Wounds and fractures:** raw fleece is not sterile; open wounds, deep injuries and broken bones need clinical care and clean dressings.
- **Eyes:** ash of burnt wool is not a safe eye preparation; do not apply it to the eye.
- **Allergy and skin reactions:** wool and wool grease can cause contact reactions in some people.
- **Smoke:** burning wool produces irritant smoke; do not burn it indoors.
- **Identity:** the chapter names no species, and its heading is simply "Wool"; reading it as sheep's wool is an inference from ordinary usage and not a statement of the text. [USER: verify]
