# Amomum cardamom — Kardamomon KARDAMOMON, cardamomum (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 1-5)

## *The spice Dioscorides calls kardamomon, for which the English edition prints "Amomum cardamom — Cardamom" with the cross-reference "see 1-14"; the identification is the edition's suggestion, the binomial is an old label (Amomum belongs to the ginger family, and the species most often called cardamom today is placed in Elettaria), and the plant is therefore of low confidence at species level: what the text describes is a seed-spice from Comagene, Armenia, Bosporus, India and Arabia, judged by hardness, fullness, closure, smell and taste* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named by Dioscorides among the texts on disk, in chapter 1-5.** This is a single-text entry. The row was added on 13 October as an unverified placeholder from the Dioscorides name list (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 6). A first pass proposed merging it with CPU-00005; the audit disagreed, because *Amomum* is a different genus from *Elettaria*, and the row is kept apart. The chapter was read in full on 30 October, together with 1-14 (AMOMON), which the edition cross-refers, and the name "cardamomum" was then searched in the whole text.

**Dioscorides, De Materia Medica (DMM).** The chapter is 1-5, KARDAMOMON, in Book One (aromatics), `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading at line 3576. The editor's line reads "Amomum cardamom — Cardamom", with "see 1-14". The text:

"The best cardamomum is brought out of Comagene, Armenia and Bosporus. It grows too in India and Arabia. Choose that which is hard to break, full, tightly shut (for that which is not is out of date), and which also has an offensive smell, and is sharp to the taste and somewhat bitter." (line 3579)

"A decoction (taken as a drink with water) is able to heat. It is good for those who have illness comitralis [possibly from comites — veins, arteries adjacent to nerves — mitralism — lesions on the heart], coughs, sciatica, paralysis, hernias, convulsions and griping, and it expels rectal worms. Taken as a drink with wine it is good for those who have defective kidneys and difficulty meiendi [urination]. It is also good for one who has been stricken by a scorpion and for all those hurt by the venom of other creatures. A teaspoonful (taken as a drink with bark from the roots of bay) breaks stones [kidney, urinary]. Taken as inhalations of smoke or fumes it is an abortifacient, and daubed on with vinegar it takes away parasitic skin diseases. It is also mixed in thick ointments and other antidotes." (line 3580)

The chapter gives no description of the plant, only of the dried product and where it comes from. The text names no plant part, but its "hard to break, full, tightly shut" test points to a seed capsule. [USER: verify] That reading is ours.

**Related chapter, 1-14, AMOMON** (line 3671). The edition's suggestions there read "Amomum repens, Elettaria cardamomum, Alpina cardamom — Bastard Cardamom, Lesser Cardamom; Amomum aromaticum [Mabberley] — Bengal Cardamom", with "see 1-5" pointing back. The two chapters describe different drugs in the Greek (kardamomon and amomon). The edition's cross-references mean that the botanical labels of the two overlap, and that is the reason this row is of low confidence: the text describes no plant, and the same label ("cardamom") is spread across two chapters. The text of 1-14 is not used here for the properties of 1-5.

**Other places in the text.** The spice is named as a substitute in the oil of fenugreek, 1-57 (TELINON, line 3997): "There are some who use cardamomum [1-5] instead of calamus [1-17], and opobalsamum [1-18] instead of cyprus, and steep them together." That is a recipe note and adds no property of its own.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** A hot drink for coughs, sciatica, paralysis, hernias, convulsions, griping and rectal worms; with wine for the kidneys and urination; against scorpion and other venom; with bay-root bark to break stones; as fumes an abortifacient; with vinegar for skin parasites; and as an ingredient of thick ointments and antidotes.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose of the decoction, and no length of use.

The Codex does not translate the classic's terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend a use of the spice on this basis. The fumes are said to be an abortifacient. That is recorded and is not a use to follow.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations in the text are: a decoction drunk with water or with wine (line 3580); the spice "with bark from the roots of bay" in "a teaspoonful"; inhalation of smoke or fumes; and an application "daubed on with vinegar". "A teaspoonful" is the only measure given, and it is for the stone-breaking drink. For the other uses no dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any dose from this entry.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

General, well-established facts only. Cardamom is a widely used culinary spice from the ginger family, valued for its aroma, and has long been added to foods and drinks in Asia and the Mediterranean. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** In 1-5 Dioscorides prints "able to heat" for the decoction and, for the choice of the product, "sharp to the taste and somewhat bitter" with an "offensive smell". No other quality is printed, and none is imported.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and 1-5 offers none. What the chapter does give is the clearest example in this batch of his discipline of attention. It names the best origins (Comagene, Armenia, Bosporus), and then gives a sensory assay: choose what is "hard to break, full, tightly shut", with an "offensive smell", "sharp to the taste and somewhat bitter", and refuse the product that is not tightly shut, "for that which is not is out of date". That is provenance, freshness testing and sensory assay. It is not a cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports a cultivation use. What a cultivator can take from the chapter is the test: check the origin, check the freshness by the closure and the hardness, check the smell and the taste, and do not buy a spice that fails them.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy.** The text lists the fumes as an abortifacient. Do not use fumes or concentrated preparations of this spice in pregnancy on the strength of this entry.
- **Venom, kidney stones, paralysis, hernia, convulsions.** These are medical conditions that need proper care.
- **Identity.** The binomial is the edition's suggestion and the plant behind the Greek name is uncertain. [USER: verify]
