# Fumaria bulbosa — Aristolochia Stroggole, the round aristolochia (Dioscorides 3-4)

## *Fumaria bulbosa [Bauhin, Linnaeus], the second form on the translators' SUGGESTED line ("Pistolochia [Fuchs], Fumaria bulbosa [Bauhin, Linnaeus], Corydalis cava [in Sprague] — Fumitory"); the chapter itself is headed and described as an aristolochia* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed under the chapter heading, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. Here the doubt is large. The chapter heading is *ARISTOLOCHIA STROGGOLE*, "round aristolochia", and the text calls the plant "Aristolochia". The translators' SUGGESTED line gives a Fuchs herbal name (*Pistolochia*), two fumitory binomials (*Fumaria bulbosa* of Bauhin and Linnaeus, *Corydalis cava* "in Sprague") and the English name "Fumitory". The sister chapters 3-5 and 3-6 carry *Aristolochia* binomials. So the entry's title is the translators' gloss and not the chapter's own subject, and a reader should not take it to mean that Dioscorides describes a fumitory. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *3-4. ARISTOLOCHIA STROGGOLE* at line 6548, SUGGESTED line at 6549, text at line 6550. The chapter stands in Book Three, "Roots", after 3-3 *GENTIANE* and before 3-5 *ARISTOLOCHIA MAKRA*. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 410.

The whole chapter reads: "Aristolochia is called this because it is thought to help women in childbirth exceedingly well. The round type is called female; it has leaves similar to cissus — sweet smelling, with sharpness, somewhat round, tender, with many shoots on one root. The branches are very long, the white flowers are similar to little hats, and the red (part) in them has a bad scent."

So the chapter itself gives a reason for the name (it is "thought to help women in childbirth exceedingly well"), the name of the round kind (female), and a description of the leaves, branches and flowers. It gives no habitat, no root, no use and no preparation. It does not carry the *POISONOUS* label that the next chapter has.

*What the neighbouring chapters say of the round kind.* The next chapter, *3-5. ARISTOLOCHIA MAKRA* (heading line 6551, text line 6554), is for the long kind, called male, and is labelled *POISONOUS*. It describes the roots of both: "The root of the round aristolochia is like a turnip, but the root of the long kind is the thickness of a finger, being twenty centimetres long or more. Both of them are mostly of wood colour, bitter to taste, and poisonous." The third chapter, *3-6. ARISTOLOCHIA KLEMATITIS* (heading line 6555), carries the uses of the three kinds together and names the round one in two places: "One teaspoonful of the round one (taken in a drink with wine, and also applied) is indeed good for poisons" (line 6570), and, after the uses taken with pepper and myrrh and applied as a pessary, "the round is effective for the things we have mentioned". Most of the uses in 3-6 are given for the aristolochias together and not for the round kind alone, and this entry does not assign them to it. The chapters 3-5 and 3-6 are read in I_1012 and I_1011, and are not merged here. [USER: verify]

*The fumitory chapter.* The translators' fumitory suggestion leads to the chapter *4-110. KAPNOS* (line 9016), whose SUGGESTED line gives *Fumaria officinalis*. That chapter describes "a very tender shrubby little herb similar to coriander" with a purple flower, and is the chapter on which the existing fumitory entries rest. It is a different chapter and a different description, and nothing from it is read into this entry. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

No analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. The chapter describes an *Aristolochia*, and the genus is well known to contain aristolochic acids, which are toxic to the kidney and carcinogenic. The translators' alternative, *Fumaria bulbosa* (now commonly placed in *Corydalis*), belongs to a different family that contains isoquinoline alkaloids. Which of the two, if either, is the plant of the chapter is not settled here. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records only that the plant is thought to help women in childbirth, and gives no preparation. The uses printed for the round kind in the neighbouring chapter (a poison remedy, a drink with wine) are not a protocol. The Codex gives none. If the plant is an *Aristolochia*, it is hazardous, and the text's own neighbouring chapter calls both roots "poisonous".

### Preparation & Dosing

Chapter 3-4 gives no preparation, no quantity and no use. The only quantity that names the round kind is in 3-6 (line 6570): "One teaspoonful of the round one (taken in a drink with wine, and also applied)", for poisons. No dose is given in chapter 3-4 itself. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identification is the translators' suggestion and conflicts with the chapter's heading, so a modern fact about a fumitory would not by itself bear on Dioscorides' plant. It is a general, well-established fact that herbal products containing aristolochic acid have caused kidney failure and urinary tract cancers, and many countries have banned them.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 3-4 prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality and no taste. It prints that the leaves are "sweet smelling, with sharpness" and that the red part of the flower "has a bad scent". The next chapter prints that the roots of both kinds are "bitter to taste, and poisonous".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a description for recognition (leaves like cissus, many shoots from one root, very long branches, white hat-shaped flowers with a foul-smelling red part), together with a note that the name comes from the plant's reputation in childbirth, and a use of smell as a mark. It is not a cultivation status. The identification is the translators', and conflicts with the heading. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The only transferable point is the habit of identifying a plant by its leaves, flowers and smell, and of keeping the author's own heading apart from a later gloss. The text offers nothing more, and the abstentive answer is the right one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 3-4 prints no warning, but the next chapter calls the roots of both round and long aristolochia "poisonous". If the plant is an *Aristolochia*, aristolochic acid is a recognised kidney toxin and carcinogen, and the chapter's reputation as a help in childbirth is not a reason for use. It is not suitable in pregnancy or labour on the strength of this entry. The identification is only the translators' suggestion and conflicts with the heading, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
