# Plum — ijjāṣ (الإجاص), the fruit, its gum and its leaf

## *Plum,* Prunus domestica *L. (the garden and Damascene plums); the identification is the modern one, supported by Avicenna's own citation of Dioscorides and Galen on the "Damascene" kind, and is of fairly high confidence* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** CM

---

### Origin & Tradition

The plum is named only by Avicenna's *Canon* among the texts on disk, under the name *ijjāṣ*. It is a single-text entry. (Dioscorides has the plum tree, *kokkumelia*, DMM 1-174, on the spine from another source; Avicenna cites Dioscorides within this entry, and the two are not merged here. The translator glosses DMM 1-174 *Prunus domestica*, "Plum Tree", and says of the fruit that those from Syria and Damascus soften the bowels. That supports reading the *Canon*'s "Damascene" plum as a plum.) In some modern Arabic dialects *ijjāṣ* names the pear, so the plum reading rests on the Dioscorides citation and the "Damascene" kind. [USER: verify]

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** The *Canon* (Book II, entry *al-ijjāṣ*, Arabic text line 10529) begins: "the plum is known." For the choice it ranks kinds: the garden (*bustī*) is stronger than the black; the yellow stronger than the red; the dull white is heavy and purges little; "the Armenian is the sweetest of all and the strongest in purging, and the best are the large, fat ones" (lines 10530-10532). It says that "the gum is subtilising, cutting and gluey (*mughrin*)," and that "in the Damascene there is binding and astringency according to Dioscorides, but not according to Galen" (line 10533). At the end Avicenna sides with Galen: "Galen said that Dioscorides erred in saying that the Damascene binds; rather it loosens the bowels" (قال جالينوس: إن ديسقوريدوس أخطأ في قوله أن الدمشقي يقبض بل يسهل, line 10542).

### Active Components

Plums contain sugars (notably sorbitol), organic acids, fibre and pectin, polyphenols, and the stone holds a kernel. Dried plums (prunes) are a familiar mild laxative, with sorbitol and fibre as the usual explanations. The plum tree exudes a gum, like other stone-fruit trees. The *Canon* names no constituent. All of this is general knowledge. The stone kernel contains cyanogenic glycosides, like other *Prunus* kernels; this is general knowledge and is not in the entry.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record. The plum is a food and a mild laxative, and the entry reads like a physician's note on both.

- **As food and laxative.** Dried plum and fresh differ: "the sweet one is stronger in purging bile; the fresh is stronger in purging than the dry" (line 10540). The unripe fruit "has binding, and little nourishment" (line 10534).
- **Gum.** "Knits wounds" (يلحم القروح); with vinegar it cuts ringworm (*qawbāʾ*), especially with honey or sugar and especially in children (lines 10535-10536). It "breaks the stone of the bladder" (line 10543) and "strengthens the sight as a kohl" (line 10538).
- **Leaf.** Gargled, it "prevents the descents (*nawāzil*) to the tonsils and uvula" (line 10537).
- **Water of the fruit** moves the menses (line 10543).

This is not offered as a recommended practice beyond ordinary eating.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives usage directions rather than doses: "let the raw fruit be eaten before the meal, and let those who are moistened drink honey-water and wine after it" (lines 10534-10535). The gum is applied with vinegar (line 10536), used as kohl (line 10538); the leaf is gargled (line 10537). The smaller the fruit the less it purges (line 10543). No weight or duration is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Prunes and plum juice have long been used as mild laxatives, and the laxative effect is attributed generally to sorbitol and fibre. That is general knowledge. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry on the gum, leaf-gargle or other uses named by Avicenna. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **CM (Avicenna), line 10532.** "Cold at the beginning of the second [degree], moist at the end of the second."
- **Stomach (line 10539).** The sweet fruit "relaxes the stomach by moistening it and cools it, and on the whole does not suit it" (line 10539). A clause just before it, "المزمنة أشد نفعا للصفراء", is ambiguous in the printed text and is not translated here (line 10538).

### Cultivation Register

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent.* The plum is a cool, moist fruit and a laxative for bile in the *Canon*. The entry is concerned with the choice of fruit and with the dispute between Dioscorides and Galen over whether the Damascene plum binds or loosens. It says nothing of the vital faculties, the innate heat or the radical moisture, and no cultivation status is attested.

**Practitioner's note.** Eat the plum as a food, before the meal, as the *Canon* advises, and expect a loosening of the bowel; the large and fat kind purges more. The one lesson from the entry that bears on practice is its habit of recording a disagreement between authorities and choosing between them by what the fruit does.

### Contraindications

- **Stone kernel.** Plum kernels contain cyanogenic compounds and should not be eaten in quantity. General knowledge.
- **Bowel.** Large amounts loosen the bowel; avoid in diarrhoea.
- **Stomach.** The *Canon* itself says the sweet plum "does not suit" the stomach (line 10539).
- **Gum and womb uses.** Historical record only. Menses-moving is stated for the fruit's water (line 10543); avoid in pregnancy as a precaution. [USER: verify]
