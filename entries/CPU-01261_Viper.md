# Viper — *Echidne* (ΕΧΙΔΝΗ), "Viper" (Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica* 2-18)
## *A venomous snake. The translator's gloss prints "Vipera communis, Coluber berus, Pelias berus — Viper"; the Greek name is* echidne *and the species is the translator's suggestion* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is Dioscorides. In the English text on disk (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`) the chapter is headed "2-18. ECHIDNE" (line 4985), with the gloss "Vipera communis, Coluber berus, Pelias berus — Viper" (line 4986). The name index (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 208) carries the same gloss and the locus "2-18 (line 4985)". The three Latin names are the translator's; the text itself says only "viper". The chapter sits in Book Two, which Dioscorides opens by saying it will deal with "living creatures and of honey and of milk and of animal fat", and which is headed "LIVING CREATURES" in this edition.

*The flesh.* The chapter opens (line 4987): "The flesh of the viper (boiled and eaten) makes the eyes quick-sighted and is also good for disorders of the nerves. It represses enlarging scrofulous tumours [glandular swelling, goitres]." The bracket is the translator's gloss.

*Preparation of the flesh (lines 4987-4992).* "You must (when you strip it) cut off the head and the tail because they are without flesh. Cutting off the extreme parts by a certain measure is but a tale. Then, the intestines having been taken out, wash that which is left and cut it in pieces. Boil it with oil and wine and a little salt and dill."

*Two rumours, one called a lie.* Dioscorides reports: "Some say that from feeding on them lice are bred in those who eat them but it is a lie. Some again say that those who eat them are longlived." The first he rejects in plain words, and he calls the practice of cutting off the extremities "by a certain measure" a "tale". The second, the claim of long life, he records and neither endorses nor rejects. That is a record of what was said, with his own judgement attached only to the first.

*The salts of the viper.* "Salts are made of them for the same uses, but they do not work as well. The living viper is put into a new pot, and with it a pint of salt and [the same of] well-pounded dry figs with six cups of honey. The cover of the pot is tightly corked with clay and it is baked in an oven until the salt has turned to coals. After this it is pounded into small pieces and stored. Sometimes it may agree better with the stomach if some spikenard [1-6, 1-7, 1-8, 1-10] or phullon [3-140] or a little malabathrum [1-11] is mixed in." The quantities are those of the ingredients of the preparation (a pint of salt, an equal amount of figs, six cups of honey) and not a dose for the patient. The viper "salts" are given as a weaker substitute, "for the same uses".

*Near neighbours.* The next chapter, 2-19 *Opheos geras*, the sloughed skin of snakes, is separate material: "boiled in wine is a remedy for pain in the ears if it is poured into them, and for toothache used as a mouth rinse. They mix it (especially that of the sea viper) with eye medicines" (lines 4994-4996). A "sea viper" is named there but is not the 2-18 viper. Elsewhere the text treats the viper as the cause of a bite, and gives remedies for viper bite in the plant chapters and others, for example a cedria plaster applied with salt that "helps the bite of the horned viper" (1-105, line 4329), "the genitals of a male hart" taken in wine for the bitten (line 5113), and a man's own urine as an antidote (line 5379). Those are antidotes and not uses of the viper as a medicine, and are not folded in. The salted tunny in a chapter at line 5071 is named for "those bitten by the viper called *prester*"; again an antidote.

The Dioscorides list has the row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 199, "Echidne,,plant,DMM II,yes". Its class column says "plant", which is wrong for a chapter of Book Two, "Living creatures" (the spine files it as an animal), and the last field flags the identification as uncertain. The loci for this entry are the text lines above, the name-index row and that list row. A search for "viper" in the Sushruta chapter files and the Assyrian text returned no hit. A snake's flesh appears in the Tibetan materia medica, but that text names a different animal, and is not folded in.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, the flesh of a snake is muscle, protein, water and a little fat. A viper's venom is a mixture of enzymes and peptides that affect blood clotting and tissue, and it is held in glands in the head; These are general facts, not statements of the text, which names no constituent and says nothing of venom in this chapter.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The record is that boiled viper flesh was eaten for sight and for "disorders of the nerves", and that a salt made of the animal was used for the same, less well. The Codex does not recommend either. A viper is a venomous animal whose handling can cause serious injury or death; many viper species are protected; and nothing in the sources supports the claimed effects. No protocol is offered.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations are those quoted: the flesh stripped of head and tail, gutted, cut in pieces and boiled with oil, wine, a little salt and dill; and the "salt" made by baking the living animal in a sealed pot with salt, figs and honey, then pounding it and storing it, optionally softened with spikenard, *phullon* or malabathrum. The quantities given belong to the salt-making (a pint of salt, the same of figs, six cups of honey). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Viper bites are medical emergencies and are treated with antivenom; the venom causes pain, swelling, bleeding disorders and sometimes shock. Eating cooked snake meat is not known to improve eyesight or nerve disorders, and wild snake meat can carry bacteria and parasites. Eyesight loss and nerve disorders need medical assessment. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** None. The chapter prints no quality (warming, cooling, drying, astringent) for the viper. The only effects named are the three in the text: it "makes the eyes quick-sighted", it is "good for disorders of the nerves", and it "represses enlarging scrofulous tumours". No other tradition's classification is available for this material.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. What he characteristically offers a practitioner is a discipline of attention (provenance, preparation, discrimination between what is observed and what is told), and this chapter shows the last of these. He gives a method of preparation, rejects the story of lice bred in those who eat the animal as "a lie", dismisses the measure by which the extremities are cut as "a tale", and records, without endorsing or rejecting it, the saying that those who eat the viper "are longlived". That last is a reported saying, and it is not a cultivation status. Recording is not endorsing. No amuletic use is recorded in the chapter.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing here for a cultivator to take up. The one lesson is Dioscorides' own: he separates what he can prescribe (a way of dressing and boiling the flesh), what he calls false (the lice, the measured cutting), and what he only repeats (long life), and a cultivator who reads him should do the same. A rumour of long life recorded for a venomous snake is not a reason to eat it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Venom and handling:** a viper bites; do not catch, kill or process one. Treat any bite as an emergency.
- **Infection and hygiene:** wild snake meat can carry bacteria and parasites; the "salt" made from a living animal baked with honey and figs is an unprocessed animal product of unknown safety.
- **Eyes and nerves:** loss of sight and nerve disorders need medical assessment; do not rely on a food or a salt for them.
- **Law and conservation:** many viper species are protected in law; the Codex does not recommend taking any animal for these uses.
- **Identification:** the species is a translator's suggestion; no product can be taken to be the *echidne* of the text.
