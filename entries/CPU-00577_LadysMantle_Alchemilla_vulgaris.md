# Lady's Mantle / Alchemilla / Sinau

## *Alchemilla vulgaris* L. | Family: Rosaceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Bitter, markedly astringent, cool, drying. Contracting and consolidating. Acts on the tissue interface — mucosa, endometrium, skin — rather than on the nervous system.

**Training Phase:** Foundation — Sealing. The herb of closure: bleeding that will not stop, tissue that will not knit, boundaries that will not hold. A structural herb, not a state herb.

---

### Origin & Tradition

A low, unassuming rosaceous perennial of European meadows and uplands, with round pleated fan-shaped leaves — *palmately lobed and radially folded like a closing parasol* — and a froth of tiny yellow-green apetalous flowers. Nothing about it looks important. It is, nevertheless, the only plant in the entire European materia medica **named after alchemy**.

**Alchemical Tradition (Europe, mediaeval–early modern) — *Alchemilla*, "the little alchemical one"**

The genus name derives from the Arabic-rooted vocabulary of alchemy (*al-kīmiyā*), and the reason is specific and physical, not decorative. Each morning, the plant's pleated leaves are found holding a perfect bead of water at the centre of the leaf-cup, and further droplets arrayed along the toothed margin — brilliant, spherical, and appearing *from the leaf itself*.

Mediaeval and early modern alchemical practitioners collected this water. It was regarded as a *celestial* or heavenly water — a dew that had descended from above, been received and purified by the plant, and was therefore fit as a solvent or menstruum in the Great Work, the operations aimed at the Philosophers' Stone. Dew-collection in general is a well-attested alchemical practice; *Alchemilla* was prized because it appeared to **produce** rather than merely **catch** the water, and to hold it in a form that was clean, discrete, and gatherable. That the plant was named for the operation rather than the operation for the plant tells you how strong the association was. **Western Folk & Wise-Woman Medicine — Lady's Mantle, Sinau, Frauenmantel, Marienmantel**

The vernacular names are Marian: the pleated leaf was read as the mantle or cloak of the Virgin, and the plant assigned to women. Its folk use is one of the most stable in European herbalism, and it is narrow: **a woman's astringent and a wound herb.** The classical indications are heavy menstrual bleeding (menorrhagia), intermenstrual and postpartum bleeding, leucorrhoea, diarrhoea, and the topical treatment of wounds, ulcers and inflamed mucosa — as a wash, a compress, or a gargle. It is a plant of *stopping* and *closing*. Ethnomedicinal records specifically and repeatedly attach *A. vulgaris* to gynaecological conditions and diseases of the reproductive system across Europe and the Balkans (as summarised in Vlaisavljević et al., *Molecules*, 2022 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27238113); Jurišić Grubešić et al., *Pharmaceuticals*, 2024 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ph17030286)).

The Serbian/Balkan name *virak* and the German *Frauenmantel* both persist in living use; the plant is still a standard European women's-tea ingredient.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

Two doctrines, and they map onto real phenomena with unusual precision — one beautifully, one straightforwardly.

**1. The celestial water is real, and it is not dew.** This is the entry's best doctrine↔botany link, and it deserves stating exactly.

Dew is atmospheric water vapour condensing onto a surface cooled below the dew point. It is *external* — it lands on the leaf. But the water in the centre of an *Alchemilla* leaf on a still morning is frequently **guttation fluid**: liquid water forced out of the leaf from inside, through specialised pores called **hydathodes** at the leaf margins and tips, driven by **root pressure** when soil moisture is high and transpiration is suppressed (humid night, closed stomata). It is *xylem sap*. It has come up through the root and stem and been extruded — not condensed — and the pleated, water-repellent leaf surface then rolls the droplets to the centre of the cup and holds them.

So the alchemists were **empirically right about the strange part and wrong about the direction.** They observed correctly that this water was not ordinary dew, that it seemed to issue from the plant, and that it was therefore in some sense *the plant's own water*. They were right. They then read it as descending — celestial, from above, condensed heaven — when it is in fact ascending: terrestrial groundwater pushed up under root pressure through a living column and out of a pore. The alchemical instinct ("this water is different, it has passed through the plant, therefore it is transformed") is a correct observation of a real botanical process, wearing an inverted cosmology. **The doctrine is a true perception with a reversed vector.**

The Kenetica reading: the leaf is a *demonstration object*. It shows, physically and every morning, a substance being drawn from below, conducted through a living structure, and delivered at the centre in purified droplet form. Whether or not one accepts any alchemical claim, that is what the plant does. Note also that guttation fluid is xylem sap, dilute but not pure — it carries dissolved minerals and can leave a mineral residue as it evaporates. It is not distilled water, and it is not medicine. **Deflate: there is no evidence whatsoever that Alchemilla guttation fluid has any pharmacological property.** No study of it was retrieved in this run. The link is symbolic and botanical, not therapeutic.

**2. The mantle that closes is the tannin.** The second doctrine is the astringent one, and it needs almost no translation. "Lady's mantle draws together, closes, seals, stops the flow" is what an herbalist says. "The extract is rich in hydrolysable tannins which precipitate surface proteins and constrict tissue" is what a chemist says. These are the same sentence. Astringency is one of the very few traditional pharmacological categories that survives contact with modern chemistry essentially intact, because **astringency is a directly observable physicochemical event** — you can feel it in your mouth. A tradition does not need a microscope to notice that a plant makes tissue contract.

Where the doctrine over-reaches: the leap from *local astringency on contactable tissue* to *systemic haemostasis in the uterus after oral ingestion*. A tea that puckers the mouth demonstrably astringes the mouth. It does not follow that its tannins reach the endometrium in astringent concentration — tannins are large, poorly absorbed, and extensively metabolised by gut bacteria before anything crosses (see Scientific Correlation, where this is now directly demonstrated for this species). **The topical/mucosal case is mechanistically clean. The systemic menorrhagia case is not, and it is untested in humans.** Hold that line.

### Active Components

*Alchemilla vulgaris* is a **tannin plant** first and everything else second. Note taxonomic caution: *A. vulgaris* is an aggregate (*A. vulgaris* auct., *A. vulgaris* L. s.l.) comprising many apomictic microspecies; a great deal of the literature does not resolve which. Composition varies accordingly.

- **Hydrolysable tannins (ellagitannins)** — the dominant and defining fraction; the astringent principle. Agrimoniin and pedunculagin are the compounds standardly named for the genus in the phytochemical literature. **The direct evidence retrieved this run is metabolic rather than analytical**: incubating lady's mantle herb extract with human faecal slurries from three healthy donors produced 33 detectable LC-MS metabolites, one identified as **urolithin C** (Kolodziejski et al., *Front Pharmacol*, 2025 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2025.1504787)). Urolithins are the signature gut-microbial catabolites of ellagitannins/ellagic acid. **This is the strongest available confirmation that the plant is ellagitannin-rich — the gut told us so.**
- **Flavonoids** — quercetin glycosides are standardly reported for the genus; RP-HPLC flavonoid profiling of aqueous *Alchemilla* infusion was performed by Jurišić Grubešić et al. (*Food Technol Biotechnol*, 2024 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.17113/ftb.62.01.24.8175)). - **Total phenolics** — high. In the four-plant aqueous-infusion comparison above, the highest-phenolic infusion reached 2061 ± 42 mg/L GAE with radical-scavenging IC50 1.9 mg/mL. [USER: verify which of the four species that figure belongs to — the retrieved abstract's species names were stripped.]

**The root is a distinct drug and is under-studied.** Methanolic root extract has its own phytochemical profile and activity (Aboul-Soud et al., *Plants*, 2022 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants11162140)); the authors note explicitly that root data are scarce relative to aerial parts. Do not assume aerial-part data apply to root.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

The use is **sealing**, and the honest form of it is largely topical.

**Primary — topical and mucosal astringent.** Wash, compress, sitz bath, gargle, mouthwash. Wounds that weep, ulcers, inflamed gums, sore throat, minor bleeding from contactable surfaces. This is the use where mechanism, tradition, and observable effect all agree, and it needs no absorption to work.

**Secondary — oral astringent for the gut.** Diarrhoea and loose stool. Same mechanism, same non-absorbed tannins, applied to the mucosa the tea actually reaches. Traditionally supported; mechanistically plausible; no human trial retrieved.

**Tertiary — the women's tea.** The traditional menorrhagia/leucorrhoea/postpartum use. This is what the plant is *famous* for and it is the use with the **weakest** evidence. Kenetica records it, honours the length and consistency of the tradition, and grades it as unproven. See Scientific Correlation. It is not a contraceptive, not an emmenagogue, and not a hormonal agent; nothing retrieved supports any endocrine action.

**Symbolic — the guttation practice.** Observing the morning droplet is a legitimate contemplative object and a genuinely instructive piece of plant physiology. It is not a preparation, it is not a dose, and it is not medicine. Kenetica keeps this in the practice column, not the pharmacy column.

**Not for:** anything requiring systemic drug action. Not an antitumour agent (see below — this is important). Not a cardioprotective. Not a state-changer of any kind; there is no reported psychoactivity and none should be sought.

All doses below are traditional-range and **not validated by any human clinical trial**. There is no established therapeutic dose for this plant.

**Cultivator's guide.** *A. vulgaris* is genuinely easy and one of the better plants to actually grow rather than buy. Hardy perennial (roughly USDA 3–8), it prefers **cool, moist, semi-shaded** conditions — a meadow-edge or north-facing border — and dislikes hot dry sun, which is precisely where it stops guttating. Propagate by division in spring or autumn, or by seed; note that most *Alchemilla* reproduce **apomictically** (seed set without fertilisation), meaning **seedlings are clones of the mother plant** — a gift for the cultivator, since a good plant breeds true, and a warning for anyone hoping to select or improve. It self-sows freely to the point of nuisance in a damp garden.

To see guttation you must supply the conditions the physiology requires: **wet soil, still air, high humidity, cool night, no wind.** Water the plant well in the evening; look at dawn before the sun hits it. In a dry spell you will see nothing — the root pressure isn't there. A grower who understands this understands the doctrine better than a reader who doesn't.

Harvest aerial parts at flowering for maximum tannin; dry in shade with airflow. Tannin content is the point, so harvest from unstressed, well-grown plants and store dry and dark. There is a useful non-medical fact too: as an understorey **living mulch** in an organic apple orchard, *A. vulgaris* (alongside *Fragaria vesca* and *Mentha × piperita*) did not deplete soil nutrient levels and differentially modified the abundance of 213 soil bacterial genera over four years (Manici et al., *Sci Rep*, 2025 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-06246-9)). It is a good, non-competitive ground layer.

### Scientific Correlation

Graded honestly. **There is no retrieved human clinical trial of *Alchemilla vulgaris* for any indication. Not for menorrhagia, not for wounds, not for anything.** Every finding below is in vitro, in animals, or in onion.

**Astringency / topical wound use — MECHANISTICALLY SOUND, CLINICALLY UNTESTED.** The traditional use rests on tannin–protein precipitation, which is a well-established general chemistry of hydrolysable tannins and is not in dispute as a phenomenon. The plant is demonstrably ellagitannin-rich (Kolodziejski et al., 2025, via urolithin C formation — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2025.1504787)). **But no wound-healing study of this species was retrieved in this run.** The most-attested use of the plant is supported by mechanism and centuries of practice, and by zero retrieved trials. That is the honest position.

**Menorrhagia / gynaecological — UNSUPPORTED (NULL RESULT FOR THE SEARCH).** The plant's headline traditional indication has **no retrieved supporting study of any kind** — no trial, no animal model, no mechanism specific to endometrial bleeding. Papers repeatedly *cite* the gynaecological ethnomedicine as their motivation (Jurišić Grubešić et al., 2024 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ph17030286); Vlaisavljević et al., 2022) and then go on to test something else entirely (cancer cells). **Nobody has tested the actual traditional claim.** Furthermore, the gut-metabolism study directly undermines the naive systemic model: the extract's constituents are *extensively biotransformed* by gut microbiota into 33 metabolites before anything reaches circulation (Kolodziejski et al., 2025) — so whatever an oral dose does systemically, it is not "tannins arriving intact at the uterus."

**Antioxidant — STRONG (in vitro), LOW CLINICAL MEANING.** Significant radical-scavenging in DPPH/FRAP/Rancimat (Jurišić Grubešić et al., *Food Technol Biotechnol*, 2024 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.17113/ftb.62.01.24.8175)); significant free-radical scavenging by the ethanolic extract (Vlaisavljević et al., 2022 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27238113)). A high-phenolic plant scavenges radicals in a cuvette. This is close to a tautology and predicts very little in a person.

**Genoprotection / cytoprotection — WEAK-to-MODERATE, MODEL-LIMITED.** Ethanolic extract showed an in vitro protective effect against chromosome aberrations (micronucleus assay) in peripheral human lymphocytes (Vlaisavljević et al., 2022). Extract at 250 and 500 mg/L was cytoprotective against methyl methanesulfonate-induced toxicity in *Allium cepa* bulbs across physiological and cytogenetic endpoints (Sci Rep, 2026 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-34304-9)). **The second study is in onion.** Onion root-tip assays are a screening tool, not evidence of human effect.

**Cardioprotection — WEAK (single animal study, and read the correction).** In mice, oral AV at 100 and 200 mg/kg mitigated isoproterenol-induced myocardial damage, raising SOD and catalase and modulating inflammation, autophagy and apoptosis (Alruhaimi et al., *Front Pharmacol*, 2024 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2024.1394557)). Note that this paper has since been **corrected** (*Front Pharmacol*, 2025 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2025.1650419)); the correction notice was retrieved but its content was not, so the nature and extent of the correction is unknown to this entry. **Treat with additional caution until the correction is read.** Single study, single lab, acute chemical-injury model, no traditional basis whatsoever — the tradition never claimed a heart action.

**Antitumour — LOUD IN VITRO, AND YOU SHOULD DISCOUNT IT.** This is now the largest body of work on the plant, and it is the least useful. Ethanolic extract strongly suppressed viability of B16F1, B16F10, 518A2 and Fem-X melanoma lines and showed in vivo activity in a syngeneic murine melanoma model with evidence of dendritic-cell and CD4/CD8 T-cell activation (*Diseases*, 2024 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/diseases12060125)); it reduced 4T1 murine breast cancer viability in vitro and in vivo via caspase-dependent apoptosis and autophagic death with reduced metastatic potential and immune reactivation (Jurišić Grubešić et al., *Pharmaceuticals*, 2024 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ph17030286)); ethanolic extract suppressed growth of multiple human tumour lines (Vlaisavljević et al., 2022); aqueous infusions were antiproliferative against MDA-MB-231, T24 and A549 (*Food Technol Biotechnol*, 2024); methanolic **root** extract was strongly antiproliferative against PC-3, MCF-7 and Caco-2 with cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis (Aboul-Soud et al., *Plants*, 2022 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants11162140)). Gut-microbiota-derived metabolites of the extract were also assessed against HT-29 versus normal CCD 841 CoN cells (Kolodziejski et al., 2025).

**Deflate this hard.** (i) Polyphenol-rich extracts are near-universally cytotoxic in vitro; this is a property of the assay class more than of the plant. (ii) The root study explicitly reports dose-dependent antiproliferative activity **against normal Vero cells too** — i.e. it is toxic, not selective, even though a selectivity index was calculable. (iii) **Not one of these studies tests the traditional use.** The ethnomedicine is being used as a funding-narrative preface for oncology screening. (iv) There are **no human data.** Nobody should take lady's mantle for cancer, and this entry should not be read as suggesting it.

**Antimicrobial — MODERATE (in vitro).** Root extract showed antimicrobial activity (Aboul-Soud et al., 2022). Separately, *A. vulgaris* extract was used to biosynthesise silver and gold nanoparticles which inhibited MRSA and multiresistant staphylococci/mammaliicocci at 50 µg/mL, suppressed biofilm at 25 µg/mL, and potentiated sub-inhibitory ampicillin (*Antibiotics*, 2026 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antibiotics15030250)). **⚠️ Read that second one correctly: the antibacterial agent there is the silver, not the plant.** The extract is a reducing agent in the synthesis. It is a nanotechnology paper, not a phytotherapy paper, and it is not evidence for lady's mantle.

**Veterinary/aquaculture — one positive.** Dietary *A. vulgaris* powder at increasing levels improved growth, feed conversion, haematology, immune status and antioxidant capacity, and resistance to a virulent columnaris challenge, in Nile tilapia over 60 days (*Antioxidants*, 2022 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox11081494)). A real in vivo whole-animal result. In fish.

### Contraindications

- **No human clinical safety data exist for this plant at any dose.** All safety statements below are inference from tannin chemistry and general herbal practice, not from trials.
- **Pregnancy — avoid.** The plant's entire traditional identity is uterine and haemostatic; the folk record is inconsistent as to whether it is safe or contraindicated in pregnancy. Absent any data, avoid. [USER: verify against your preferred source — traditions differ here and some explicitly recommend it in late pregnancy.]
- **Lactation — insufficient data; avoid at medicinal dose.**
- **Undiagnosed abnormal uterine bleeding — do not self-treat.** This is the important one. Heavy or irregular bleeding can indicate fibroids, polyps, endometrial pathology, or malignancy. Reaching for an unproven astringent tea instead of investigation is the real harm this plant can do, and it is not a hypothetical harm.
- **Tannin load, oral.** Chronic high-tannin intake can cause gastric irritation and nausea, and tannins **chelate iron and reduce its absorption**. In a person taking this herb *for heavy menstrual bleeding* — i.e. someone at elevated risk of iron-deficiency anaemia — this is a directly counterproductive interaction and deserves to be stated plainly. Take away from meals and away from iron supplements.
- **Drug interactions** — tannins may bind alkaloidal and other drugs in the gut and reduce absorption. Separate from medication by ≥2 hours.
- **Root extract — not for internal use.** Cytotoxic to normal cells in vitro (Aboul-Soud et al., 2022). Research material.
- **Taxonomic uncertainty.** *A. vulgaris* is an apomictic aggregate of many microspecies. Material identified only to "*A. vulgaris*" is of uncertain composition, and the literature does not resolve this.
- **Not an antitumour therapy.** No human data. Do not substitute for oncological care.
- **Guttation water is not a medicine** and has never been studied. Do not drink pooled leaf water as a therapy; it is xylem sap sitting in an open cup outdoors overnight and is microbiologically whatever the environment made it.
