### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Shennong text (SBJ); this entry rests on one text, the Shennong Bencao Jing in Yang Shou-zhong's 1998 translation, and on one entry in it.** The text is `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`. A search of the Dioscorides, Ebers, Assyrian, Sushruta and Tibetan text on disk for *Pulsatilla* found three lines, none for this plant: Dioscorides prints "Pulsatilla vulgaris" in the translators' line for chapter 2-207, *Anemone* (line 6378), which is a European species given for a Greek drug; Clark's rGyud-bzhi prints "Pulsatilla patens" as one of three binomials for the Tibetan drug *srub.ka* (line 1118), a different drug; and the Tibetan index repeats it (line 1632). None is read as Bai Tou Gong. The Latin name is the translator's pharmaceutical Latin and was matched to the binomial by stem in the extraction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv`, line 184: "pharmaceutical Latin 'Radix Pulsatillae Chinensis' matched by stem to on-disk binomial 'Pulsatilla chinensis'"). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** The row is keyed by the name as Yang prints it, *Bai Tou Gong*, which is the only name the extraction records for the entry; the entry's two other names (below) have no row of their own.

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *Bai Tou Gong, PDF page 97.* The page marker `[[p97]]` is at line 3609 and the running head "Herbs: Inferior Class" is printed at line 3610. The entry is the second on the page (the first, Lian Qiao, is at line 3611), at lines 3617 to 3622:

"Bai Tou Gong (Radix Pulsatillae Chinensis)225 is bitter and warm. It is nontoxic, treating mainly warm malaria, mania, cold and heat, concretions and conglomerations, accumulations and gatherings, and goiter qi. It expels blood [stasis], relieves pain, and heals incised wound. Its other name is Ye Zhang Ren (Secluded Old Man). Yet another name is Hu Wang Shi Zhe (Envoy of the Nomad King). It grows in rivers and valleys."

The square brackets are the translator's, as printed. The entry gives the taste and nature, a toxicity statement, indications, two other names with Yang's glosses, and the habitat. The Codex does not translate the names further.

Yang's note 225 (lines 3633 to 3636) is the translator's modern commentary and not part of the classic. The OCR of this note is badly damaged (for example "ahlt'" for "able", "haldll.'ss" for a word the Codex reads as "baldness", and "..lid" for "and", and the last sentence breaks off after "mainly used to"). What can be read is: "This medicinal is currently called Bai Tau Weng [sic, as the OCR prints it]. Besides what is discussed in the text, Pulsatilla is able to treat all wind troubles, cold pain of the knees, nosebleed, baldness, and swollen testicles. In modern clinical practice, it is mainly used to ..." The note puts these uses in the present and in "modern clinical practice". It does not say they are the classic's. The word read as "baldness" and the name "Bai Tau Weng" rest on damaged text. [USER: verify]

*The class headings.* Yang's table of contents prints "Herbs: Inferior Class" (line 729). The section's opening heading is printed at line 3157 (`[[p85]]`, line 3156), and the same running head recurs on later pages, including the one at line 3610. The next section heading, "Woods: Superior Class", is at line 3809 (`[[p103]]`, line 3808). No other class heading intervenes between lines 3157 and 3809 (the running heads in this range all read "Herbs: Inferior Class", lines 3221, 3288, 3374, 3452, 3534, 3610 and 3675). The preface (lines 391 to 395) says of the inferior class: "There are 125 inferior class medicinals which are used as assistants and envoys. They mainly treat disease and correspond to earth. They are usually toxic and cannot be taken for a long time. If one intends to eliminate cold and heat and evil qi, break accumulations and gatherings, and cure disease, one should base [one's efforts] on the inferior class." Yang's commentary on it (lines 362 to 364) says: "Inferior class medicinals specifically treat disease. They are usually at least slightly toxic. This means that they cannot be taken in large amounts or for prolonged periods of time without developing negative side effects." The count printed in the preface (125) and the count in Yang's commentary (122, lines 365 to 366) differ. The commentary does not say why, and this entry does not rely on either count.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Pulsatilla* belongs to the buttercup family (Ranunculaceae), and that many plants of that family contain ranunculin, a glycoside that breaks down, when the fresh plant is crushed, to protoanemonin, which irritates the skin and mucous membranes; this is a statement about the family and not a finding about this plant. Which compounds the root named by the classic held is not shown by the lines read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record, with a continuing use of the root in Chinese medicine under the name Bai Tou Weng, if Yang's note 225 is read as the OCR prints it. The Shennong entry lists indications in its own terms (warm malaria, mania, concretions and conglomerations, accumulations and gatherings, goiter qi) which are not modern diagnoses, and these conditions need medical care. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. The entry's own "nontoxic" sits against the preface's "usually toxic" for the class (see the Cultivation Register), and a fresh plant of this family can irritate, so nothing here supports gathering or using it.

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: the inferior class (下品), by position.* Bai Tou Gong stands in the section headed "Herbs: Inferior Class" (heading at line 3157; running head at line 3610 on the same page; next section heading at line 3809). It is **not** the first entry under the running head on its page, so the grade rests on its position within the section and not on standing directly beneath a heading. In the preface (lines 391 to 395) the inferior class "mainly treat disease and correspond to earth", is "usually toxic and cannot be taken for a long time", and is the class on which one bases the effort "to eliminate cold and heat and evil qi, break accumulations and gatherings, and cure disease". Yang's commentary (lines 362 to 364) says the same of this class. So the grade is the one the spec describes as short-course and disease-attacking, not the nourishing one. The entry's own indications ("cold and heat", "concretions and conglomerations", "accumulations and gatherings") are close to the preface's wording for the class. The entry also says "It is nontoxic", which sits uneasily against the preface's "usually toxic"; the preface's word is "usually", so the text does not contradict itself, and this entry does not resolve the tension. The entry says nothing of long-term use, "making the body light", nourishing life or any change of jing, qi or shen, and none is asserted here. The class position rests on the OCR text of Yang's headings and has not been checked against a page image. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** In the Chinese scheme this is a lower-grade drug, taken for a defined complaint and then stopped, and the tradition's own grading is the warning. The entry's "nontoxic" is recorded and not endorsed, because the preface sets the class's usual character against it and the identity rests on a name matched by stem. Nothing here supports long-term use.

### Contraindications

The Shennong entry prints no warning beyond "It is nontoxic". The inferior class is described in the preface as "usually toxic" and not to be taken for a long time, and this is a general statement about the class and not about this plant. Plants of the family are generally known to irritate the skin and mucous membranes when fresh. The identity is a name matched by stem, so no plant or root should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Malaria, mania, goitre and tumour-like swellings need medical care.
