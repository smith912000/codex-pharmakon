### Botanical Identity
Perennial 20–60cm; hairy stems and leaves; distinctive spikes of bright magenta-pink tubular flowers (June–September); grows in woodland edges, hedgerows, meadows; native to Europe and western Asia; common in ancient woodland — its presence indicates old, undisturbed sites. The aerial parts (leaf, stem, flower) harvested at full bloom are the drug material. Wood betony carries the distinction of having been the most highly regarded medicinal plant in medieval European medicine — a Roman saying attributed to the physician Musa (physician to Augustus Caesar): *"Sell your coat and buy betony."*

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 De Materia Medica (DMM)

Dioscorides (*Book IV, Chapter 1*):
> *"Kestron (Betony): warming and mildly drying. Drunk in wine, it helps those suffering from diseases of the chest, liver, and spleen. For headaches — the root in wine. For uterine pain. Drives out intestinal worms. The leaves drunk in honey-wine benefit those who cough up blood. Good for those bitten by snakes if eaten before the bite."*

Antonio Musa, physician to Emperor Augustus, wrote a comprehensive treatise on betony alone — *De herba vettonica* — listing 47 separate diseases for which betony was prescribed. This treatise, likely written in the 1st century BCE/CE, established betony as the supreme medicinal plant of Roman and post-Roman European medicine for over a millennium.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine (CM)

Ibn Sīnā on *ḥashshā al-dhahab* (betony group):
> *"Warm in the second degree, somewhat dry. Opens obstructions of the head and brain. Relieves headache and vertigo. Benefits the liver and spleen when their functions are obstructed. Expels intestinal worms. Relieves uterine obstructions. Good for chest conditions with thick phlegm."*

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (EP)

Bitter aromatic herbs for head pain and liver conditions in Egyptian preparations — consistent with betony's primary applications.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)

Head pain plants in Babylonian texts; anthelmintic (anti-parasitic) bitter herbs consistent with betony's dual application.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Cerebral Circulation / Nervine (Betonicine)

**Betonicine (stachydrine)**: The primary active alkaloid of wood betony:
- Improves cerebral microcirculation (vasodilatory effect on cerebral arterioles)
- Anti-ischaemic — reduces neuronal damage in hypoxic conditions (animal models)
- Hepatoprotective — reduces hepatocellular oxidative damage
- The traditional "nervine tonic" application for headache, vertigo, and "fullness in the head" is mechanistically consistent with cerebral vasodilatory activity

### 5.2 Anti-Inflammatory / Neuroprotective

**Rosmarinic acid + acteoside**: 
- Rosmarinic acid (shared with lemon balm I_51, rosemary): COX-2 inhibition; 5-LOX inhibition; NF-κB inhibition; MAO-A/B mild inhibition
- Acteoside (verbascoside): Potent antioxidant; NF-κB inhibition; protects against LPS-induced inflammation; neuroprotective against oxidative stress
- Combined: broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory with neuroprotective overlay — explains the head pain, liver, and nervous system applications

### 5.3 Digestive / Hepatic

**Betonyosides (iridoids) + choline + tannins**:
- Iridoids: bitter stimulants → stimulate gastric acid and bile secretion → cholagogue + choleretic → improved fat digestion and liver function
- Choline: hepatoprotective (methyl donor for phosphatidylcholine synthesis → maintains hepatocyte membrane integrity)
- Tannins: antimicrobial in gut → reduces pathogenic load

### 5.4 Anthelmintic

**Tannins + alkaloids**: Protein precipitation (tannins) creates an inhospitable environment for intestinal parasites; alkaloid fractions directly toxic to helminths. The traditional anthelmintic application (Dioscorides, Ibn Sīnā, AT) is pharmacologically coherent.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Modern clinical research on *Betonica officinalis* specifically is sparse** — the plant fell out of mainstream pharmaceutical research despite its ancient prominence. The available evidence is primarily:

- **Betonicine pharmacology**: In vitro and animal studies confirm cerebrovascular and hepatoprotective activity of stachydrine/betonicine (the compound is shared with several other plants and has been studied across species)
- **Rosmarinic acid**: Extensively studied across multiple plant sources; anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and MAO-inhibitory effects well-characterised (see lemon balm, I_51)
- **Acteoside**: Significant pharmacological research confirming anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective mechanisms
- **Traditional evidence**: The Materia Medica of medieval Europe (Hildegard von Bingen, Culpeper, the German herbals) consistently records betony for head pain, liver, and nervous conditions across 1,500 years of clinical tradition

The discrepancy between betony's historical pre-eminence (first herb in medieval pharmacopoeias) and its current research neglect is one of the clearest examples of the gap between traditional pharmacological knowledge and modern research priority.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The first herb of Europe**: The Herbarium of Pseudo-Apuleius (a popular compendium of c. 400–700 CE that was the primary herbal reference of early medieval Europe) begins with betony — not as the alphabetical first, but as the most important. The ordering was deliberate: betony opens the canon because it was the supreme herb. Antonio Musa's treatise listing 47 diseases cured by betony was widely copied and circulated through medieval monasteries. For approximately 1,000 years (c. 500–1500 CE), wood betony held the position that echinacea or turmeric holds in the modern public imagination — the universally known, universally recommended plant medicine. It appears in monastery herbals (the *Hortulus* of Walahfrid Strabo, 840 CE), in the medical curriculum at Salerno, in Hildegard von Bingen, in Culpeper. Then it fell out of fashion in the early modern period, replaced by pharmaceutical specifics, and is now little known outside specialist herbalist circles. The rise and fall of betony's reputation is a complete arc of a medicinal plant's cultural life.

**The monasteries and the medicine**: Wood betony (*Bishopswort*) was cultivated in every medieval monastery garden — both as medicine and as a plant with protective-spiritual associations. The Christianisation of European folk medicine transferred the protective functions of indigenous plant magic to saints and the Church, with betony carrying the protective associations of its pre-Christian use. Every Anglo-Saxon *lacnunga* (healing book) includes betony for protection against evil, disease, and poison — the same plant was simultaneously a well-documented medicine and a magical amulet. The pharmacology (cerebrovascular, liver-protective, anti-inflammatory) and the spiritual attributions (protection of the head, the vitality organs) mapped onto each other imperfectly but persistently across traditions.

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Bitter, acrid; slightly warm. Channels: Liver (肝), Lung (肺), Heart (心). Clears Liver Heat, disperses Wind, relieves pain, calms Shen, promotes Qi circulation.

**Ayurvedic (cross-reference)**: Bitter, astringent; slightly warming. Reduces Pitta ↓↓, reduces Kapha ↓. *Śūlaghna* (pain-relieving), *Medhya* (nervine tonic), *Yakṛt* (hepatic). Not a classical Ayurvedic plant.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Pregnancy**: Emmenagogue and uterine-stimulating history — avoid therapeutic doses
- **Hypotension**: Cerebrovascular vasodilatory effect — caution in patients with already low blood pressure; possible additive effect with antihypertensive medications
- **Astringent tannin content**: High tannin (5–15%) — reduce dose in sensitive GI patients; space from iron supplementation (tannins bind iron)
- **Generally safe**: 1,500+ years of continuous therapeutic use with no documented serious adverse effects at traditional doses
- Moderate dose, limited duration — standard approach given limited modern safety pharmacology data
