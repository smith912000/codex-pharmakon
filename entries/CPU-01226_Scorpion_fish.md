# Scorpion fish — *Skorpios thalassios* (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 2-14), "sea scorpion"
## *"Scorpaena, Scorpaenidae — Scorpion Fish; Cottus scorpius — Sculpin", the translator's identification; the text calls it "the sea scorpion"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica*, in the 2000 English edition (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`; "text" below). The chapter is 2-14, headed *SKORPIOS THALASSIOS*, at line 4973 (the spine's locus, `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`: "2-14 (line 4973)", translator's identification "Scorpaena, Scorpaenidae — Scorpion Fish Cottus scorpius — Sculpin", class animal). The Greek heading means "sea scorpion", and the translator gives two identifications that are different fishes, a scorpionfish and a sculpin; the text itself describes nothing of the animal that would choose between them. [USER: verify]

The whole chapter, line 4975, reads: "The gall of the sea scorpion is good for bathing eyes, white spots on the cornea, and excessive moisture in the eyes." That is one part (the gall, that is, the bile), one use (as an eye wash) and three complaints named together: the eyes generally, white spots on the cornea, and an excess of moisture in the eyes. The chapter gives no preparation and no quantity.

The chapter belongs to a short run of sea creatures at the start of Book 2. The chapter before it, 2-13 (*Skorpios chersaios*, the ground scorpion, line 4967), says that the land scorpion, "taken raw, pounded into small pieces, and so applied", is a remedy for the hurt it does, and that it is also roasted for the same purpose. The chapter after it, 2-15 (*Drakon thalassios*, "Trachinidae — Spiny-finned fishes, Weevers", line 4976), says: "The sea dragon (opened and applied) is a cure for the hurt done by his prickles." The sea scorpion's own chapter is not of that kind: it uses the bile for the eyes and says nothing of stings.

An English "sea-scorpion" also appears in the Sushruta in Bhishagratna's translation (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/kalpa/SS_Kalpa_ch03.txt`, lines 36-37; Bhishagratna Vol. 2, line 29794): "The venom of a scorpion, VisVambhara, Rdjiva-fish (OCR spelling), Uchchitinga (cricket) and a sea-scorpion lies in their saliva." This is Kalpasthana ch.3, on the places in which animal venom lies. It names a poisonous creature, gives no use of any part as a drug, and gives no Sanskrit name for the sea-scorpion in the rendering read. It does not attest the substance as a remedy, and it is not treated here as a second tradition; the identity of the Sushruta creature with the Dioscorides fish is unestablished. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Fishes of the scorpionfish family carry venomous spines, and many fishes have bile (gall) containing bile acids and the pigment bilirubin. These are general facts. The chapter names no constituent, and its remedy is the gall, not the spine; this entry names no further constituent.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The use recorded is an eye wash of fish bile for eye disease. The eye is a delicate organ, an animal bile is not a sterile preparation, and the complaints named (clouding of the cornea, excess discharge) are conditions for medical assessment. The Codex's application layer has nothing to add, and no protocol is offered.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives only "the gall ... is good for bathing eyes" (line 4975). Whether it is used neat or diluted, and how much, is not stated. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides.** No quality (warming, astringent, or the like) is printed for the sea scorpion's gall in chapter 2-14, and none is supplied here.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. Chapter 2-14 is a single sentence naming a part and a use. It records no provenance, season or assay such as makes the discipline-of-attention frame useful elsewhere in the work, and it assigns no status to the person taking the remedy.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing in the source for a cultivator to use. The chapter is a bile eye wash for named eye complaints; the one thing worth carrying away from it is that the two fish named as the "sea scorpion" differ, which is a reason to treat the identity as open and not a practice.

### Contraindications

Never put an unprocessed animal bile into the eyes: it is a contaminated and irritating fluid, and the eyes are easily damaged by it. Scorpionfish carry venomous spines that cause painful wounds, and handling them is itself a hazard. Eye disease should be assessed medically. These are general cautions and not claims from the text.
