# Tormentil / Potentilla

## *Potentilla erecta* (L.) Raeusch. | Family: Rosaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Cold & Dry (astringent) — inferred from the humoral literature; not a first-source attribution
**Training Phase:** Foundation — Signature Study

---

### Origin & Tradition

Tormentil is a low, wiry perennial of the rose family (Rosaceae), native across Europe, western Asia and North Africa. It favours acid, poor, damp ground — heaths, moorland, bog margins, upland pasture — where its slender stems carry small four-petalled yellow flowers (unusual in a family whose flowers are overwhelmingly five-petalled; the quartered flower is one of the plant's field signatures). The medicinal organ is not the aerial part but the rhizome — *Tormentillae rhizoma* — a hard, knotty, reddish-brown rootstock that stains blood-red on cutting, a colour the old herbalists read as a signature of its action on flux and haemorrhage. That red is tannin: the rhizome is one of the most tannin-dense drug plants in the European pharmacopoeia.

The plant carries two named-tradition tags:

- **DMM (Dioscorides / the Greek herbal stream):** the astringent "cinquefoil" complex of *De Materia Medica* runs through the *Potentilla* genus, and tormentil entered the European herbal canon as one of the strongest binding roots for flux of the belly, spitting of blood, and ulcerated mouth. **[USER: verify]** — the specific binomial *P. erecta* is a Linnaean-era identity; the Dioscoridean "cinquefoil/pentaphyllon" material cannot be pinned to this exact species with confidence, so treat the DMM attestation as genus-stream rather than a clean species match.
- **CM (Avicenna, *Canon of Medicine*, Unani):** the cold-dry astringent roots for diarrhoea, dysentery and mucosal laxity belong to the same Unani class Avicenna systematised. **[USER: verify]** for the specific species identity, for the same Linnaean-gap reason.

The English name itself carries the doctrine: "tormentil" ties to Latin *tormina* — griping, colic, the twisting pains of dysentery — the very complaint the root was reached for. In folk practice across Germany, Ukraine and Russia the rhizome was also steeped into alcoholic bitters (a use documented in the toxicology literature below), and used as a gargle for sore throat, a wash for haemorrhoids and slow-healing wounds, and a binding drink for acute diarrhoea.

### Active Components

- **Hydrolysable and condensed tannins** — the dominant fraction, reported at very high levels in the rhizome. The signature ellagitannin is **agrimoniin** (a dimeric ellagitannin also central to *Agrimonia*), alongside a spectrum of proanthocyanidins (condensed tannins) and catechin/epicatechin units.
- **Catechins and gallotannins** contributing to the astringent, protein-precipitating load.
- **Triterpenes** (e.g. tormentoside / related glycosides) and phenolic acids as minor constituents.

The pharmacological identity of the drug is essentially *the tannin*: a large, polyphenolic, protein-binding load delivered topically to the gut or mucosal surface rather than absorbed systemically (see Scientific Correlation — the clinical study could detect no tannin in serum).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica, tormentil is filed as one of the great European **astringent seals** — a Foundation-phase binding root whose whole action is *containment*: drawing lax, weeping, over-flowing tissue back to tone. The cultivator's read is mechanical and honest — the tannins precipitate surface proteins into a thin protective coagulum, tightening mucosa and reducing exudation and micro-bleeding. Primary use-cases: acute non-specific and infectious **diarrhoea**, historically dysentery; **sore-throat and mouth gargle** for inflamed or ulcerated mucosa; topical wash for **haemorrhoids** and minor wounds. It is the richer, more concentrated companion to Silverweed (*P. anserina*, CPU-00902) — same genus, same astringent grammar, higher tannin dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Form | Traditional preparation | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Decoction (rhizome) | Simmer chopped rhizome in water; drink or use as gargle/wash | Classic astringent delivery; bitter, strongly binding |
| Tincture / drops | Alcoholic extract; paediatric rotavirus RCT used "3 drops per year of life, 3×/day" of a standardised root extract | Do not extrapolate the trial's proprietary drops to arbitrary home tinctures |
| Adult UC study dose | 1,200–3,000 mg/day standardised extract, tolerated in the trial | Research setting, monitored; not a self-dosing recommendation |

Safety caveats: take with food — the high tannin load irritates an empty stomach. No fabricated "standard" gram doses are given here; traditional decoctions are self-limiting by bitterness, and the clinical figures above are study-specific, not general prescriptions.

### Scientific Correlation

- **Antidiarrhoeal (rotavirus, paediatric): GRADE — single small RCT, positive.** In a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 40 children (3 months–7 years) with rotavirus diarrhoea, tormentil root extract shortened diarrhoea duration to a median 3 days vs 5 days on placebo (P < 0.0001), with 40% vs 5% diarrhoea-free at 48 h. One trial, n=40, single centre — promising but not confirmatory.
- **Ulcerative colitis (adult): GRADE — open-label phase-I, uncontrolled, signal only.** In 16 patients with active UC, escalating doses to 3,000 mg/day were tolerated and clinical activity index fell during treatment and rose during washout; but this was open-label with no control arm, so efficacy is unproven. Mechanistically important finding: **no tannin, degraded or intact, was detectable in serum** — consistent with a purely luminal/topical astringent action, not systemic drug activity.
- **Acute toxicity: GRADE — preclinical, reassuring.** Aqueous rhizome extract showed no mortality or organ pathology in rats and mice at high single intragastric doses (2.5 and 6.8 g/kg), consistent with the long record of traditional and food use.
- **Tannin protein-precipitation / mucosal-sealing mechanism: GRADE — established general pharmacology, plausibly the mode of action here.** Directly supported for tormentil by the non-absorption finding above; the specific "antiviral in vitro" claim for this species was not substantiated in this retrieval — treat as **preclinical/NULL** pending a real cited source.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Cold and dry in the humoral schema — the archetypal astringent: binding, drying, tone-restoring, reached for wherever tissue is lax, weeping or in flux. The blood-red cut surface was read as a signature of its governance over haemorrhage and dysenteric flux. (Energetic attribution inferred from the Greco-Unani astringent class; not lifted verbatim from a single primary source.)

### Contraindications

- **High tannin load:** GI irritation and nausea if taken on an empty stomach; take with or after food.
- **Mineral/iron absorption:** tannins bind dietary iron and other minerals — caution with concurrent iron supplementation and in those at risk of iron-deficiency anaemia; separate dosing.
- **Gastritis / peptic irritation:** the astringent, bitter load may aggravate an already inflamed or ulcerated stomach lining.
- **Heavy/prolonged use:** theoretical hepatic caution with very heavy chronic tannin intake — not a demonstrated clinical harm at traditional doses, but a reason to keep courses short.
- **Constipation:** as a binding astringent, inappropriate where stool is already hard/dry.
- No adequate safety data in pregnancy/lactation for concentrated extracts — default to caution.
