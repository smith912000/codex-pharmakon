# Molubdaina — plumbago, "lead stone" (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 5-100)

## *A yellow, glittering, lead-bearing material that Dioscorides says is "made from silver and gold" and is also dug up; the 2000 English edition glosses it as plumbago ("Molybdos [Greek] — Lead") and as molybdomenite (selenite of lead), and its heading line begins "Silver Salts and Gold Salts". The identification is the translator's and is of low confidence, because the chapter describes a smelting product and a dug material together and the English glosses disagree with each other* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** The chapter is marked "TOXIC" in the 2000 English edition, and lead compounds are poisonous. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should apply or take any lead material.

This is a single-text entry. The row was entered on 8 November 2026 from a residual extraction key, "Silver salts", which does not match the chapter heading; the heading is MOLUBDAINA. This entry corrects the name and replaces the placeholder.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The chapter is 5-100 in the Book 5 section on metallic stones (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 10327 and following). The printed header lines are: "5-100. MOLUBDAINA / SUGGESTED: Silver Salts and Gold Salts Plumbago — Molybdos [Greek] — Lead — in this manuscript / Molybdomenite — Selenite of Lead / TOXIC. / Old English — Plumbago is native or mineralised carbon. Molybdenum is a metallic element — a silvery metal — symbol Mo. Molybdenite is disulphide of Molybdenum." The editor's note shows that the English names pull in different directions (plumbago as carbon, molybdenum, lead), and the text's own sense is lead.

The chapter reads: "The best plumbago looks like frothy silver — yellow, somewhat glittering, and a tawny yellow when pounded into small pieces. Boiled with oil it becomes like liver in colour. That which is an airy or lead colour is worthless. It is made from silver and gold, and there is some found which is dug out of the earth about Sebastia and Corycum. The best of this is neither like scoria [slag], nor stony, but yellow and glittering. It has properties like lithargyrum [5-102] and [slag] of lead, and is washed and burnt the same way. It is most effectively mixed with gentle plasters called lipara, and is effective with plasters that are not corrosive — promoting the growth of flesh in a wound or sore, and forming new skin. It is not suitable for those plasters that close open cuts and clean."

The neighbouring chapters give the context. 5-95 (molubdos peplumenos, "washed lead", also TOXIC; Codex row CPU-02598) describes washing lead in a lead mortar with a lead pestle (line 10296). 5-101 (skoria arguru, silver slag) says that slag "has the same strength as plumbago [5-100]" and is mixed with black plasters and those for forming new skin (line 10336). 5-102 (litharguros) is the oxide of lead and silver, for which the English text gives a long washing and whitening procedure.

### Active Components

The sources name no constituent. In general terms, litharge and related lead materials are lead oxides, and lead compounds are toxic by ingestion, inhalation and, to a lesser degree, through broken skin. Which of these the chapter's yellow glittering material was is not settled by the text. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Toxic.**

- **What the text says it is for.** As an ingredient of mild (non-corrosive) plasters of the lipara kind, for promoting the growth of flesh in a wound or sore and forming new skin; it is not suitable for plasters that close open cuts and clean.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no proportions in the plaster, no length of application, and no internal use.

The Codex recommends no lead preparation of any kind, including for wounds.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text says the material "is washed and burnt the same way" as litharge and lead slag, and that it is mixed with gentle plasters. The washing and burning methods are those of 5-102 and of the slag chapters, which the Codex does not reproduce. Boiling in oil is mentioned as a test of quality (it turns liver-coloured). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Lead and its oxides are cumulative poisons (nervous system, blood, kidney), and lead salts have long been used in plasters, which is the chapter's setting. None of this supports the text's claims for flesh growth. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM (2000 English).** No warming, cooling or astringent quality is printed for this chapter. The only qualifiers are the mild character of the plasters it suits and the editor's "TOXIC". None is imported.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is asserted. The chapter makes no claim about vitality or long life. What it offers a practitioner is the discipline of attention that Book 5 teaches throughout: tell the best from the worthless by colour and lustre, know the ore from the smelting product, know where it is dug (about Sebastia and Corycum), and match the strength of the material to the plaster it goes in.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. It is a toxic yellow lead material for mild wound plasters. The Codex does not recommend any use. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **TOXIC.** Lead is cumulatively poisonous. Do not ingest, inhale dust or fumes, or apply to wounds.
- **Pregnancy, children.** Lead is especially harmful. Avoid entirely.
- **Identity.** The English glosses (plumbago, molybdomenite, silver and gold salts) disagree; the identification is low-confidence. [USER: verify]
