# Iris pseudacorus — Oinos Akorites, "wine of akoron" (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 5-73); the binomial is the translator's suggestion

## *Wine made with akoron (acorus) and lycoris, Dioscorides 5-73; the 2000 English edition's SUGGESTED line offers Acorum officinarum, Gladiolus luteus, Acorus adulterinus and Iris pseudacorus (L.) (Yellow Flag, Water Flag) for the plant, so the Codex's identification of the plant as Iris pseudacorus is one of four translator's candidates and is of low confidence; the substance the text actually names is a medicated wine* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** *Iris pseudacorus* (yellow flag) is generally regarded as a poisonous plant, with an irritant rhizome. The Dioscorides line does not identify the plant with certainty, and this entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should make or drink this wine.

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk, and then as a wine, not as a plant.** This is a single-text entry. The text is the 2000 English edition of *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). The row came from the translator's SUGGESTED line at line 10167–10169, which lists *Iris pseudacorus* [Linnaeus] among its candidates for the Greek heading. The name-index row (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 846) gives the same line and class "other", and `_sources/dioscorides.csv` (line 800) lists the heading as "Oinos Akorites" with the plain identification *Acorum officinarum*. The Codex row was opened on 13 October as an unverified placeholder from the binomial.

**Dioscorides (DMM).** *Locus: Book 5, chapter 73, heading OINOS AKORITES (line 10167).* The chapter is one of the medicated wines of Book 5, and the whole text of the chapter (lines 10170–10171) reads: "Acorus and lycoris [?] wines are made in the same way [as those above] — steep eighty ounces of each of them in six gallons [of must] for three months, and then store it in another jar. They are effective for the side and chest, and for inducing urine." The bracketed "[?]" is the translator's, and so is "[as those above]". The wines just before it, in 5-68 to 5-72 (lines 10140–10160), are made by steeping a plant or resin in must; for example 5-68 asarabacca wine (three ounces to six pints of must; "diuretic, good for dropsy, jaundice, liver ailments, and sciatica") and 5-72 panax wine ("good for convulsions, hernias, bruises"). The chapter after it is 5-74, celery-seed wine. So the recipe is one of a series, and "the same way" refers to that series.

**The plant behind the wine: the akoron chapter.** The Greek name of the plant is given in Book 1, chapter 2, AKORON (lines 3547–3552). Its SUGGESTED line carries the same four candidates, then "[other usage] *Acorus calamus*, *Acorus aromaticus*, *Acorus odoratus* — Sweet Flag, Sweet Sedge, Myrtle Sedge". Dioscorides there describes it: "Acorum has leaves which resemble those of iris very much only narrower, and the roots are similar only one wrapped in the other, not growing downward but sidelong in the upper part of the earth. They are sharp to the taste, distinguished by pale white knots, and not unpleasant to smell. The best is thick and white, not worm-eaten, full and fragrant. Root such as this comes from Colchis and from Galatia and is called asplenium." Its uses (line 3552) include "The root is heating", a decoction "causes an urge to urinate", and it is "good for pain of the rectum, chest and liver". The chapter does not say that the wine of 5-73 is made from the root; it says only "acorus". That the wine is made from the root of 1-2 is the natural reading and is the Codex's inference. **Lycoris.** The text puts a question mark after "lycoris". No chapter headed *lycoris* was found by a search of the text for the word (the only hits for "lycori" are in this chapter). The second ingredient of the wine is therefore unidentified in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

**Identity.** The Codex's row name, *Iris pseudacorus*, is a candidate offered for the Greek *akoron* by the 2000 edition. The text describes leaves "resembling those of iris, only narrower" and a sharp, fragrant root, which fits a sweet-flag better than the yellow flag, but the text gives no flower, habitat or other character that decides it. The Codex already holds entries on sweet flag (`I_534_SweetFlag_Acorus_calamus.md`, `I_321_SweetFlag_Acorus_gramineus.md`); this entry is kept apart only because the row stands for the translator's *Iris* candidate. Overlap is flagged for the editor. A search of the other text folders on disk was not repeated for this entry beyond the Dioscorides text.

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, *Iris* rhizomes are known to contain irritant and poisonous compounds, and yellow flag is classed with the poisonous garden and wetland plants. The sweet flag (*Acorus*) is a different genus, rich in aromatic oil. Wine is a dilute ethanol solution. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the wine. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.** The text says the wine is "effective for the side and chest, and for inducing urine". That is all it says. The Codex gives no protocol and does not translate "side and chest" into a modern diagnosis. Because the plant behind the name is uncertain and one candidate is poisonous, the wine should not be reproduced.

### Preparation & Dosing

*Preparation (5-73, lines 10170–10171):* "steep eighty ounces of each of them in six gallons [of must] for three months, and then store it in another jar." These are batch quantities for the whole jar, and not a dose. *Dose:* No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Medicated wines are plant material steeped in fermenting grape juice; the alcohol extracts some plant compounds and itself acts on the body. *Iris* rhizomes are irritant; sweet flag chemistry varies by variety and its root has regulatory limits for food use in some countries [USER: verify]. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured here. The chapter records a recipe, uses, and nothing about long life or the person's vital constitution. What the book does offer a cultivator is a discipline of attention: in the akoron chapter it records provenance (Colchis, Galatia), a sensory assay (thick, white, not worm-eaten, full, fragrant) and the order of quality. The wine chapter carries none of this and names no source or season.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. The record is one line of a recipe for a medicated wine of uncertain content, one of whose possible ingredients is a poisonous plant, and no practice is attached.

### Contraindications

- **Poisoning:** the yellow flag is generally regarded as poisonous, and its rhizome can irritate skin and the gut. Do not gather, eat or drink it.
- **Identity:** the plant is not settled, and "lycoris" is unidentified. Do not substitute a plant on the strength of this entry.
- **Alcohol:** a wine carries the ordinary risks of alcohol, including in pregnancy and with medicines.
- **Urinary and chest complaints:** need proper medical assessment.
- **Pregnancy, children:** no statement in the sources retrieved. Avoid. [USER: verify]
