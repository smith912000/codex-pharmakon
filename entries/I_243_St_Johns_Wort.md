# I-243 | Hypericum perforatum — St. John's Wort / Klamath Weed

## Section 1 | Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Hypericum perforatum* L.  
**Family:** Hypericaceae (formerly in Clusiaceae/Guttiferae; now distinct family)  
**Common names:** St. John's Wort (English — flowers bloom traditionally around June 24th, the Feast of St. John the Baptist; "wort" = Old English for medicinal plant), Common St. John's Wort, Perforate St. John's Wort, Klamath Weed (invasive US designation), Goatweed, Tipton's Weed (English regional), Millepertuis (French — "thousand holes"), Johanniskraut (German — "St. John's herb"), Hierba de San Juan (Spanish), Erba di San Giovanni (Italian), Kantaron (Turkish), Iperico (Italian pharmaceutical name), Sarı Kantaron (Turkish — "yellow St. John's wort"), Binbirdelikotu (Turkish — "thousand holes herb"), Felzeleyen (Arabic/Persian — فلزلة), Zverboy (Russian — звероbой — "beast killer"), Svartemurken (Norwegian)

**"Perforate" designation:** The name *perforatum* ("perforated") refers to transparent glandular dots visible when the leaf is held to light — the glands contain essential oil and flavonoids. The "thousand holes" (Millepertuis, Binbirdelikotu) names across French and Turkish both independently document this same morphological feature — a case of visual pharmacological encoding in plant naming.

**Botanical description:** Perennial herb 30–90cm; erect branched stems; opposite elliptic-oval leaves (1–3cm) with translucent oil glands (appear as "holes" in light) and black glandular dots at margins; golden-yellow 5-petaled flowers (2–3cm) with numerous stamens; black glandular dots on petals contain hypericin (dark red pigment). Flowering period June–August (Northern Hemisphere). All *Hypericum* species can be distinguished by these black pigment glands on the petals.

**Distribution:** Native to Europe, temperate Asia, and North Africa; naturalized globally in temperate zones. Found in disturbed habitats — roadsides, meadows, forest edges. Now considered invasive weed in North America (California, Pacific Northwest), Australia, New Zealand, where it was accidentally introduced. Commercial cultivation: Germany (highest quality and largest pharmaceutical volume), Eastern Europe (Bulgaria, Romania, Hungary), Australia, Chile. Germany processes ~70% of global pharmaceutical-grade supply.

**Authenticated part(s):** Flowering tops — harvested at full bloom when hypericin + hyperforin content is maximal; quick-dried at <40°C to preserve volatile hyperforin (which degrades rapidly). European Pharmacopoeia: minimum 0.04% total hypericins (calculated as hypericin); German Commission E specifies minimum 0.2% hypericin AND minimum 2% hyperforin for antidepressant efficacy.

## Section 3 | Convergence Analysis

**Primary convergence axis — wound healing → nervous system:** DMM documents wound healing and nerve-related pain (sciatica). This early neurological-wound dual documentation preceded modern recognition that Hypericum's hypericin + hyperforin have both wound-healing (anti-inflammatory) and neuropharmacological (monoamine reuptake inhibition) properties. The same plant later became the world's most clinically studied antidepressant herb — representing a dramatic pharmacological discovery hidden within an ancient wound-healing herb.

**The Protestant/folk medicine anti-depression tradition:** European folk medicine of the 17th–20th centuries consistently documented St. John's Wort for "nervous exhaustion," "melancholy," "dark spirits," and "nerves" — a 300+ year continuous European observation predating controlled clinical research by centuries. The consistent cross-generational documentation of mood-improving effects in European folk medicine led to the German pharmacological research of the 1980s–1990s that generated over 30 RCTs and 7 systematic reviews. Folk pharmacovigilance (sustained cultural documentation of a consistent drug effect) served as the hypothesis for systematic clinical investigation.

**The sunflower-light tradition (Doctrine of Signatures meets pharmacology):** The traditional belief that St. John's Wort was "a plant of the sun" — expressed by its golden-yellow flowers, its midsummer blooming around summer solstice, and its traditional harvest on the summer solstice (June 24th) — led to its use for "darkness of the soul" (melancholy/depression). While Doctrine of Signatures is not pharmacologically valid as a principle, in this case it generated 300 years of folk clinical observation that turned out to be pharmacologically accurate. The plant's antidepressant properties were accurately identified through a non-pharmacological framework.

## Section 4 | Phytochemical Composition

**Primary antidepressant compounds:**
- **Hyperforin (2–5% in dried flowering tops):** phloroglucinol derivative; primary antidepressant active compound (CONFIRMED); volatile and unstable; responsible for the bulk of SERT/NET/DAT/GABA/glutamate reuptake inhibition; CO₂-stabilized extracts best preserve hyperforin; most RCTs use preparations standardized to ≥2% hyperforin; unique chemical structure not found in other plants
- **Adhyperforin (0.2–1.8%):** sister compound to hyperforin; similar reuptake inhibition mechanism; less extensively studied

**Naphthodianthrones (photosensitizing compounds):**
- **Hypericin (0.04–0.5%):** dianthrone pigment (responsible for red-purple juice when crushed); early proposed as primary antidepressant compound (now known to be secondary); MAO inhibition at high concentrations; STRONG photosensitizer (dermal/ocular toxicity with sun exposure); antiviral (HIV in vitro at high doses); also used in photodynamic therapy for cancer
- **Pseudohypericin (0.2–0.7%):** related compound; photosensitizing; antiviral

**Flavonoids:**
- **Amentoflavone:** biflavone; MAO-A inhibitor; minor contribution to antidepressant effect
- **Quercetin, isoquercitrin, rutin, hyperoside:** antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; weak GABA-A activity
- **Biapigenin:** benzodiazepine receptor binding (GABA-A BZD-site) at pharmacological concentrations

**Xanthones:** minor; possible MAO activity

**Phenolic acids:** Caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid — antioxidant

**Essential oil (trace, 0.05–0.3%):** methyl-2-octane, 2-methyloctane, α-pinene, limonene

## Section 5 | Mechanisms of Action

**1. Multimodal Monoamine Reuptake Inhibition (Primary Antidepressant Mechanism)**
**Hyperforin** is the most pharmacologically unique antidepressant compound in the Codex:
- **Sodium conductive mechanism (novel):** Unlike all known pharmaceutical antidepressants (which inhibit specific transporter proteins: SSRIs = SERT; SNRIs = SERT + NET; TCAs = SERT + NET; bupropion = DAT + NET), hyperforin elevates intracellular Na⁺ concentration via TRPC6 channel activation → elevated intracellular Na⁺ → reduces the Na⁺ gradient that drives monoamine reuptake transporters → ALL monoamine transporters simultaneously inhibited
- Specific transporters inhibited: SERT (serotonin transporter), NET (norepinephrine transporter), DAT (dopamine transporter), GABA transporter (GAT1/4), glutamate transporter — simultaneously
- This is the ONLY known natural compound that inhibits ALL of these transporters simultaneously via a single upstream mechanism (ionic gradient disruption)
- Consequence: simultaneous elevation of serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine, GABA, and glutamate in synaptic clefts
- This broad monoamine + GABA + glutamate simultaneous modulation explains both the antidepressant effect and the observation that SJW may work for anxiety as well as depression

**2. MAO Inhibition (Minor — Amentoflavone, Hypericin)**
- Amentoflavone: MAO-A inhibitor (reduces serotonin/norepinephrine catabolism)
- Hypericin: MAO-A and MAO-B inhibitor at pharmacological concentrations (higher concentrations required)
- Quantitative contribution: MAO inhibition was the originally proposed mechanism; now understood to be a minor contributory mechanism relative to hyperforin's reuptake inhibition

**3. GABA-A / Benzodiazepine Receptor (Minor)**
- Biapigenin: binds GABA-A benzodiazepine site (anxiolytic potential)
- Quercetin: mild GABA-A modulation
- Combined with GABA transporter inhibition (hyperforin): GABAergic anxiolytic component

**4. Sigma Receptor (Antidepressant/Neuroprotective)**
- Hyperforin: agonist at sigma-1 receptor (σ1R)
- σ1R activation: neuroplasticity enhancement; neuroprotection; antidepressant (Weng et al. 2012)

**5. TRPC6 Channel Activation (Neuroprotective)**
- Hyperforin directly activates TRPC6 (Transient Receptor Potential Canonical 6) channels
- TRPC6 activation: increases intracellular Ca²⁺ → BDNF/CREB pathway activation → neuroplasticity
- Also the direct mechanism for the Na⁺ elevation that drives monoamine reuptake inhibition (upstream)

**6. Anti-inflammatory / Antioxidant**
- Quercetin, hyperoside: NF-κB suppression; ROS scavenging
- Hyperforin: inhibits arachidonic acid release; reduces 5-LOX and COX activity
- Relevant to understanding SJW efficacy in somatoform/somatic anxiety disorders (inflammatory component)

## Section 6 | Clinical Evidence

**Major Depression / Mild-Moderate Depression (Level A — Extensive Evidence)**
*Linde et al. (2008) — Cochrane systematic review (definitive):* 29 RCTs (N=5,489); SJW vs. placebo and vs. standard antidepressants (SSRIs/TCAs). Key findings:
- vs. placebo: SJW SIGNIFICANTLY superior (OR 2.77; 95% CI 2.27–3.38)
- vs. TCAs and SSRIs: EQUIVALENT efficacy
- Adverse event profile: SJW significantly FEWER adverse events than both TCAs and SSRIs
- Subgroup: mild-moderate depression = clear benefit; severe depression = insufficient data
- Conclusion: "SJW is more effective than placebo for mild-moderate depression and is similarly effective as standard antidepressants with fewer side effects"

*Kasper et al. (2006) — RCT:* N=332; SJW WS 5570 (900mg/day) vs. paroxetine (20mg/day); 6 weeks. HAMD-17 response: SJW equivalent to paroxetine (51.7% vs 47.8% responders). SJW produced significantly fewer adverse events. Most direct SSRI comparison trial.

*Hypericum Depression Trial Study Group (2002) — multicenter RCT:* N=340; SJW (900–1500mg) vs. sertraline vs. placebo; 8 weeks. Neither SJW nor sertraline significantly outperformed placebo in this severe depression population. Important negative finding: confirms SJW is effective for MILD-MODERATE but not severe depression.

**Perimenopausal Depression (Level A)**
*Grube et al. (1999) — RCT:* N=111 perimenopausal women with psychological symptoms; SJW vs. placebo × 12 weeks. Anxiety, depression, insomnia, psychosomatic symptoms: all significantly improved. HAMD-17 reduced from 15.6 → 7.5 (SJW) vs. 15.5 → 12.4 (placebo).

**Anxiety (Level B)**
*Volz & Kieser (1997):* SJW LI 160 vs. placebo for somatoform disorder (anxiety + somatic symptoms); significantly better outcome. SJW's multi-transmitter effect (GABA + monoamine) provides mechanistic rationale for anxiety + depression comorbid presentations.

**OCD (Level C)**
*Taylor & Kobak (2000) — small RCT:* N=12 OCD patients; SJW vs. placebo × 12 weeks. Non-significant trend toward improvement. Case reports of benefit. Serotonergic mechanism suggests possible efficacy; insufficient evidence.

**Atopic Dermatitis / Wound Healing (Level B)**
*Schempp et al. (2003) — RCT:* N=21 atopic dermatitis patients; SJW cream vs. placebo × 4 weeks. Clinical score significantly improved; skin integrity measurements improved. Historical wound-healing use (DMM) partially validated.

## Section 7 | Phenomenological Record

**The summer solstice harvest and light symbolism:** The traditional harvest of St. John's Wort on June 24th (Feast of St. John) near summer solstice is one of the most precisely timed plant harvesting traditions in European folk medicine. This timing is not merely symbolic — it is pharmacologically optimal. Hypericin and hyperforin content peaks at full bloom in June/July (Northern Hemisphere); post-bloom and autumn-harvested plants have substantially lower active compound concentrations. The traditional harvest timing accidentally corresponds to the biological peak of pharmacological activity.

**The "anti-demon" tradition and its pharmacological interpretation:** Throughout European medieval medicine, St. John's Wort was used as a "demon-expelling" herb — burned to drive away spirits, worn in garlands for protection, placed above doorways. The "demons" in medieval European medicine were often what we would now classify as severe anxiety, depression, and psychosis. The plant's consistent use for these conditions through religious/folk frameworks that described them as "demonic possession" generated 1,000+ years of clinical observation of antidepressant/anxiolytic effect, expressed in the vocabulary of the prevailing cosmological framework.

**The TRPC6 discovery and its implications:** The 2000s discovery that hyperforin's primary mechanism is TRPC6 channel activation (not direct transporter inhibition as in SSRIs) represented a fundamental pharmacological innovation. A plant compound achieving the same downstream clinical outcome (antidepressant efficacy) through a completely novel molecular mechanism opened new possibilities for drug development. TRPC6 is now an active pharmaceutical target for depression, cancer, and neurological conditions — the lead compound for initial research was isolated from a 2,000-year-old European wound herb.

**Photosensitivity and the yellow-light paradox:** Hypericin causes photosensitization — enhanced UV sensitivity that can cause skin burns and eye damage in fair-skinned individuals taking high doses. The plant associated with light, summer, and the sun contains a compound that makes human skin more sensitive to sunlight. This paradox (light-associated herb causes UV sensitivity) is a pharmacological reality that generated the safety warning: avoid intense sunlight during SJW treatment. The medieval belief that "demons flee in sunlight" gains ironic pharmacological significance when the anti-demon herb makes its user sensitive to the very light that supposedly drives demons away.

**The German pharmaceutical transformation:** Germany's Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices (BfArM) Commission E approved St. John's Wort for depression in 1984 — years before any formal RCT had been conducted. This regulatory approval, based on traditional use evidence and preliminary trials, catalyzed the largest evidence base ever assembled for an antidepressant herb. The subsequent Cochrane review (29 RCTs, N=5,489) is the largest systematic review of any non-pharmaceutical antidepressant. Germany effectively transformed a European folk remedy into the most rigorously evaluated herbal antidepressant in history — a model for ethnopharmacology → regulatory approval → systematic clinical validation.

## Section 8 | Practical Application

**Standardized preparations:**
- **LI 160 (Jarsin® / Kira®):** 300mg tablet, 3×/day (900mg total); standardized to 0.3% hypericin; most-used preparation in RCTs; traditional first-line choice
- **WS 5570 (Perika®):** 300mg tablet, 3×/day; standardized to 0.3% hypericin + 5% hyperforin; used in paroxetine comparison trials; preferred for evidence-based practice
- **ZE 117 extract:** 500mg, 2×/day; standardized to low hyperforin (0.2%); designed for patients requiring reduced CYP3A4 induction (though still present)
- **Commercial supplements:** verify standardization to BOTH hypericin (≥0.3%) AND hyperforin (≥2%); single standardization to hypericin only is insufficient

**Clinical protocols:**
- *Mild-moderate depression (primary indication):* 900mg/day (300mg TID) standardized extract × 6–8 weeks minimum; assess HAMD-17 or PHQ-9 baseline/follow-up; full antidepressant effect develops over 4–6 weeks
- *Perimenopausal depression:* 900mg/day × 12 weeks; combined mood + menopausal symptom benefit; phytoestrogenic component minimal; primarily monoamine-mediated
- *Seasonal Affective Disorder:* Consider SJW + bright light therapy (mechanistically additive: SJW elevates monoamines; light therapy normalizes circadian serotonin rhythm)
- *Anxiety-depression comorbidity:* SJW broad-spectrum monoamine + GABA modulation is theoretically suitable; clinical evidence primarily in depression trials

**CRITICAL DRUG INTERACTIONS — MOST IMPORTANT HERBAL DRUG INTERACTIONS IN THE CODEX:**
St. John's Wort induces CYP3A4 (most potent herbal CYP3A4 inducer known) AND P-glycoprotein (P-gp):

**ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATIONS (life-threatening interaction potential):**
- **Cyclosporine (organ transplant):** CYP3A4 + P-gp induction → reduced cyclosporine levels → acute transplant rejection documented in multiple case reports (Journal of Liver Transplantation, Transplantation Proceedings); MULTIPLE DOCUMENTED TRANSPLANT REJECTIONS
- **Tacrolimus (organ transplant):** Same mechanism; documented acute rejections
- **HIV antiretrovirals (indinavir, efavirenz, lopinavir/ritonavir, nevirapine):** CYP3A4 induction → dramatically reduced antiretroviral blood levels → treatment failure → HIV drug resistance development; multiple documented cases
- **Combined oral contraceptives:** Reduced ethinyl estradiol + progestin levels → breakthrough bleeding, contraceptive failure → unintended pregnancy; multiple documented cases

**SERIOUS INTERACTIONS — SIGNIFICANT MANAGEMENT REQUIRED:**
- **Warfarin:** CYP2C9 + CYP3A4 induction → reduced warfarin levels → reduced anticoagulant effect → thromboembolic risk; frequent INR monitoring if combined; generally AVOID
- **Digoxin:** P-gp induction → reduced digoxin absorption and increased renal elimination → reduced efficacy → potential cardiac decompensation; AVOID
- **Statins (simvastatin, atorvastatin):** CYP3A4 induction → reduced statin levels → reduced lipid control
- **Antiepileptics (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital):** May alter levels; unpredictable direction; neurologist management essential
- **Chemotherapy (many agents — irinotecan, taxanes, imatinib, other CYP3A4-processed agents):** Reduced chemotherapy levels → treatment failure; AVOID in active chemotherapy

**SEROTONIN SYNDROME RISK:**
- **SSRIs/SNRIs (sertraline, paroxetine, fluoxetine, venlafaxine, duloxetine):** Combining SJW with pharmaceutical antidepressants → SEROTONIN SYNDROME risk; AVOID concurrent use; minimum 2-week washout switching between
- **MAO inhibitors:** ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION; serious serotonin syndrome; no washout adequate without physician management
- **Tramadol, meperidine (opioids with serotonergic activity):** Serotonin syndrome risk

**Other interactions:**
- **Anesthesia:** Tell anesthesiologist; may alter CYP-processed anesthetic agents; discontinue 2 weeks before elective surgery
- **5-HT₃ antagonists (ondansetron — antiemetics):** Reduced efficacy via CYP3A4 induction

**Patient counseling (MANDATORY when dispensing SJW):**
1. List ALL medications patient takes
2. Check for CYP3A4 substrates, P-gp substrates, serotonergic drugs
3. If transplant, HIV, anticoagulation, or active chemotherapy: CONTRAINDICATED
4. If on oral contraceptives: CONTRAINDICATED without additional contraception
5. Warn about photosensitization (avoid intense sun + UV exposure; use SPF 30+ sunscreen)

**Contraindications:**
- **Transplant patients:** ABSOLUTE (documented rejection risk)
- **HIV patients on antiretroviral therapy:** ABSOLUTE (treatment failure/resistance)
- **Pregnancy:** AVOID (insufficient safety data; some evidence of uterotonic effect)
- **Severe depression with suicidal ideation:** NOT SUFFICIENT as monotherapy; requires psychiatric evaluation and management
- **Bipolar disorder:** May trigger manic episode (serotonergic antidepressants carry this risk); psychiatric supervision essential

**Safety (at standard doses, no interactions):** Excellent. Most common adverse effects: photosensitization (1–3%; fair-skinned individuals most at risk), GI disturbance, fatigue, dizziness. Rare: mania induction in bipolar patients. NO hepatotoxicity at standard doses. NO significant dependence or withdrawal. The safety advantage over SSRIs is the most consistent finding across comparative RCTs.
