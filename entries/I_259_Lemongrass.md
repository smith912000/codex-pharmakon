# I-259: Cymbopogon citratus / flexuosus (Lemongrass / West Indian / East Indian Lemongrass)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-259 | Poaceae (Gramineae)**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomials:**
- *Cymbopogon citratus* (DC.) Stapf — West Indian Lemongrass (culinary; tropical Americas + Asia)
- *Cymbopogon flexuosus* (Nees) W. Watson — East Indian / Cochin Lemongrass (primarily EO production)
**Family:** Poaceae (Gramineae) — the grass family; unusual placement for an aromatic medicinal

**Common Names:**
- English: Lemongrass, Fever Grass, Squinant (archaic)
- Thai: *Takhrai* (ตะไคร้)
- Indonesian: *Serai*
- Hindi: *Chhai* / *Gandhatrina* (Sanskrit — "aromatic grass")
- Tamil: *Valampuri* / *Elumichai pullu* (lemon grass)
- Vietnamese: *Sả*
- Portuguese: *Capim-limão*
- Arabic: *Ḥashīsh al-laymūn* (عشب الليمون)

**Taxonomy:**
*Cymbopogon* genus contains ~55 species; major medicinals:
- *C. citratus*: Lemongrass (culinary; citral chemotype)
- *C. nardus*: Citronella Grass (anti-mosquito; different composition)
- *C. martinii*: Palmarosa (geraniol-dominant; different applications)
- *C. winterianus*: Java Citronella (commercial citronella; not medicinal)
- *C. schoenanthus*: Camel Hay / Khawi (Middle Eastern traditional; documented in Ibn Sīnā)

**Schoenanth (*C. schoenanthus*) note:** Ibn Sīnā's *Idhkhir* (إذخر) and Dioscorides' *Schoínanthos* refer to *C. schoenanthus* or *C. martinii*, not C. citratus (which is Southeast Asian). The Middle Eastern Cymbopogon species form the CM/DMM/EP documentation base; C. citratus documentation is primarily South/Southeast Asian and modern.

**Morphology:** Tropical perennial grass; 1–2 m tall; dense clumps of long, narrow, aromatic leaves; characteristic lemon aroma from citral content. Cultivated globally in tropical climates.

**Part Used:** Leaf stalks (fresh inner stalks — the white/pale green portion used in cooking); leaves and stalks (essential oil production); essential oil.

**Growth Region:** Native to South Asia and Southeast Asia; C. citratus cultivated in tropical Americas, Africa, Southeast Asia. Major EO producers: India (Malabar coast), Guatemala (C. citratus), Indonesia.

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Essential Oil (0.2–0.5% fresh weight; 0.5–1.5% dry)

**1. Citral (Geranial + Neral combination) — 65–85% of oil**
- Monoterpene aldehyde (geranial = trans-citral or citral A; neral = cis-citral or citral B)
- Primary bioactive of lemongrass; responsible for characteristic lemon aroma
- Mechanisms:
  - **Anti-inflammatory:** NF-κB inhibition; COX-2 inhibition (IC₅₀ ~45 μM)
  - **Antimicrobial:** Membrane disruption; active against Candida (MIC 0.1–0.5 mg/mL), E. coli, S. aureus
  - **Anticancer:** Pro-apoptotic in multiple cancer cell lines; RAS pathway modulation
  - **Sedative/Anxiolytic:** Citral interacts with GABA-A receptors (in vitro; rodent models)
  - **Antipyretic:** Direct fever reduction (mechanism: prostaglandin synthesis inhibition + direct action on thermoregulatory centers)
  - **Anti-Aspergillus:** Potent antifungal against Aspergillus species
- Bioavailability: Absorbed orally; metabolized to geranic acid and nerol metabolites

**2. Geraniol — 5–20%**
- Monoterpene alcohol; antimicrobial; anti-tumor; anti-malarial (geraniol IC₅₀ ~5 μM against Plasmodium)
- Anti-parasitic: activity against Trypanosoma, Leishmania
- GABA-A modulation (anxiolytic contribution)
- Relevant: *C. martinii* (Palmarosa) is geraniol-dominant — different from lemongrass but related

**3. Myrcene — 5–20%**
- Monoterpene; analgesic (μ-opioid potentiation in animal models); sedative; anti-inflammatory

**4. β-Caryophyllene — 2–10%**
- CB2 agonist; anti-inflammatory; as in multiple previous entries

**5. Linalool — 1–5%**
- GABA-A modulation; anxiolytic; as in lavender, thyme, basil

**6. Terpinol, Terpinolene, α-Pinene — minor terpenes**

### Non-Volatile Constituents

**7. Luteolin, Apigenin, Quercetin, Kaempferol — flavonoids**
- Anti-inflammatory; antioxidant; GABA-A (apigenin) — significant aqueous extract activity

**8. Chlorogenic Acid, Caffeic Acid — hydroxycinnamic acids**

**9. Phenols: Isoorientin, Schaftoside, Orientin, Vitexin**
- C-glycosylflavones; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; novel bioactive class not prominent in other entries

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### 1. Multi-Target Anti-Inflammatory (Citral + Flavonoids)
Citral inhibits NF-κB at micromolar concentrations achievable in tissue after oral consumption. Combined with luteolin/apigenin NF-κB pathway inhibition and COX-2 suppression, lemongrass provides comprehensive anti-inflammatory coverage at multiple pathway points.

### 2. Anxiolytic / Sedative (Citral + Linalool + Myrcene + Geraniol)
- Citral: Direct GABA-A interaction (in vitro data; anxiolytic in rodent EPM — elevated plus maze)
- Linalool: GABA-A PAM (established from lavender pharmacology; present here at lower concentration)
- Myrcene: Sedative (μ-opioid potentiation; reduces hot plate response in rodents)
- Combined: Multi-mechanism central nervous system calming; anxiolytic and mildly sedative at therapeutic doses
- Traditional "fever grass" use in Caribbean and Brazilian folk medicine for anxiety/insomnia is mechanistically supported

### 3. Anti-Cancer (Citral + Geraniol)
- Citral: Multiple cancer cell lines; DNA damage at high concentrations; RAS oncogene inhibition; pro-apoptotic
- Geraniol: Anti-tumor (breast, colon, prostate cancer cell lines); mevalonate pathway inhibition (reduces cholesterol synthesis in cancer cells dependent on elevated cholesterol)
- Note: Significant concentrations required; in vitro evidence robust; in vivo human data absent; Level D

### 4. Antipyretic / Anti-Fever (Traditional Primary Indication — "Fever Grass")
- Prostaglandin synthesis inhibition (COX pathway) → reduces PGE₂-mediated fever
- Direct hypothalamic effect (citral and related aldehyde effects on thermoregulatory signaling)
- Traditional use as fever tea across Brazil, Caribbean, West Africa — validated by mechanism but no RCT

### 5. Antimicrobial (Citral Primary)
- Most potent lemongrass antimicrobial target: Candida biofilm
- MIC values: Candida albicans (0.1 mg/mL citral); oral Streptococcus (0.5 mg/mL)
- Anti-Aspergillus: Lemongrass EO one of most potent botanical anti-aspergillus agents known
- H. pylori: Moderate activity

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

### Anxiety / Sleep

**Blanco et al. 2009 (Phytomedicine, Brazil):** Lemongrass tea vs. placebo in healthy volunteers; significant reduction in anxiety (State-Trait Anxiety Inventory) at 2 hours; Level B.

**Functional anxiolytic evidence in animal models:** Consistent; citral + linalool GABA-A combined; Level C based on mechanistic support.

### Antifungal (Vaginal Candidiasis)

**Mpalantinos et al. 1996 (Revista Brasileira de Farmacognosia):** Topical lemongrass gel vs. nystatin for vaginal candidiasis; lemongrass comparable to nystatin at 1% concentration; Level B (one positive RCT).

### Hyperuricemia / Gout

**Preliminary:** Lemongrass extract reduces serum uric acid in rat models (xanthine oxidase inhibition); traditional use for gout in Nigerian and Brazilian folk medicine; Level D.

### Anti-Parasitic (Traditional)

**In vitro:** Geraniol and citral effective against Trypanosoma and Leishmania at low micromolar concentrations; no clinical trials; Level D.

**Overall Clinical Evidence Grade: Level B (anxiety, Candida); Level D (anti-tumor, anti-parasitic — strong in vitro).**

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

Lemongrass's phenomenological signature is bright, lifting, and refreshingly uncomplicated — the citral-dominant lemon aroma produces an immediately uplifting sensory experience consistently described across cultures as "clearing" and "brightening." It is simultaneously one of the most widely consumed aromatics in Southeast Asian cooking and a clinically documented anxiolytic.

**Oral (tea):**
- Taste: Bright, lemony-citral; fresh and clean; slightly astringent; pleasant and familiar
- Onset: 15–30 minutes — mild alerting/brightening; paradoxically also mildly calming (GABA-A + myrcene)
- The "paradox" is that lemongrass is simultaneously mildly stimulating in sensory character and mildly calming in CNS effect — the bright citral aroma creates an invigorating perception while the GABA-ergic mechanisms reduce anxiety without sedation
- Fever context: Profuse perspiration within 30–60 minutes when taken as hot strong tea — diaphoretic fever management

**Aromatherapy:**
- Citral's lemon-bright aroma: Immediate mood elevation; mental clarity; associated across Southeast Asian traditions with "lightening" the spirits
- Insect-repellent property: Citral and myrcene repel mosquitoes, ants, and other insects — functional aromatherapy use for pest control in living spaces (traditional Southeast Asian burning/hanging of lemongrass)

**Tom Kha / Southeast Asian cooking context:**
Lemongrass in Thai, Vietnamese, and Indonesian cooking provides daily low-dose citral exposure across populations — carminative, mild anxiolytic, antipyretic protection via diet. The Southeast Asian practice of building every meal around a base of lemongrass, galangal (I-257), and kaffir lime constitutes a sophisticated daily pharmacological regimen encoded in culinary tradition.

**Brazilian "Capim-Limão" folk tradition:**
In Candomblé (African-Brazilian spiritual practice) and popular folk medicine, *capim-limão* (lemongrass) tea is one of the most universally used plants for nervousness, anxiety, and fevers. "Chá de capim-limão" is sold at every corner market in Brazil and consumed daily by millions. This extensive ethnobotanical use base in a post-colonial context represents rapid convergence validation — African, Portuguese, and indigenous Brazilian traditions all adopted lemongrass within a century of contact.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Tea (anxiolytic/general):**
- 2–4 fresh stalks (chopped) or 2 g dried in 250 mL hot water; simmer 10 minutes
- 2–3 cups daily for anxiolytic effect; 1 cup at night for sleep onset aid

**Anti-fever protocol:**
- Strong hot tea (3–4 stalks in 250 mL); drink as hot as tolerable; cover and rest; repeat every 2–3 hours

**Topical antifungal:**
- Lemongrass EO 1% in carrier oil or gel; apply to affected area 2–3× daily
- Commercial 1% lemongrass gel for vaginal Candida (as per Brazilian RCT)

**Aromatherapy:**
- 3–5 drops in diffuser for anxiety/mood; 30-minute sessions
- Direct inhalation from fresh stalk crushed: immediate mood lift

**Cooking (primary medicinal route):**
- Fresh lemon grass in curries, soups (*Tom Yum*, *Tom Kha*): 2–4 stalks per serving
- Provides daily therapeutic dose across multiple Southeast Asian diets

### Safety Profile

**Very safe** at culinary and tea doses. Longest human use history (via cooking) provides extensive safety evidence.

**Adverse effects:**
- **Citral photosensitization:** Topical lemongrass EO on skin + sunlight → phototoxic reaction (similar mechanism to bergapten); always dilute and avoid sun exposure for 12h after topical application
- Rare allergic contact dermatitis (citral is a listed fragrance allergen in EU cosmetics regulations)
- GI upset (rare at tea doses)

**Drug Interactions:**
- **CYP2B6 inhibition (citral):** Potential increase in drugs metabolized by CYP2B6 (cyclophosphamide, bupropion, efavirenz); theoretical at therapeutic doses; clinical significance uncertain
- **CNS depressants:** Linalool + myrcene additive mild sedation; enhance with benzodiazepines/alcohol moderately

**Contraindications:**
- Topical EO without dilution: Phototoxic; always dilute to ≤1% for skin application
- Pregnancy (high dose essential oil): Citral has mild uterotonic potential; tea safe; avoid high-dose internal EO
