# Patola (Pointed Gourd, Parwal) — Paṭola, Parval, Kulaka, Rājīphala, Bījagarbha
## *Trichosanthes dioica* Roxb. | Family: Cucurbitaceae
**Tradition Tags:** SS (Suśruta Saṃhitā) · CS (Caraka Saṃhitā) · Folk (pan-Gangetic ethnomedicine)
**Energetic Classification (Ayurvedic):** Tikta rasa · Laghu–Rūkṣa guṇa · Uṣṇa vīrya · Kaṭu vipāka · Tridoṣa-śāmaka (esp. Pitta-Kapha)
**Training Phase:** Purification · Bitter-tonic conditioning · Dietary-medicinal (āhāra-auṣadha)

### Origin & Tradition

**Suśruta, Caraka & the honoured bitter vegetable.** Paṭola is one of the rare *dravyas* that is simultaneously a daily **vegetable** and a classical **medicine** — the pointed gourd (parwal) eaten across the Gangetic plain and named repeatedly in both the *Suśruta* and *Caraka Saṃhitā*. Its standing in the corpus is high: it is a member of Suśruta's esteemed therapeutic groupings, and its leaves and fruit are cited for treating **alcoholism and jaundice** (Caraka), for oedema, subacute liver enlargement, alopecia, and as a general *tonic and febrifuge*. Ayurveda classes the leaf as antipyretic, diuretic, cardiotonic, laxative and antiulcer. As a bitter that also *nourishes* — an edible tikta — Paṭola occupies a prized niche: the medicine one can live on.

**The two Paṭolas — a source note.** Classical *Paṭola* is generally identified with *Trichosanthes dioica* (the cultivated pointed gourd), but the name also historically overlapped with the wild bitter *Trichosanthes cucumerina / lobata* (snake gourd relatives), whose intense bitterness suited more purgative use. This entry follows the standard modern identification with *T. dioica* while noting that the more sharply bitter classical Paṭola indications may draw on the wilder relative.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Bitter That Feeds:** Paṭola resolves an apparent contradiction at the heart of the bitter tradition. Bitterness (*tikta rasa*) is the taste of *renunciation* — it scrapes, dries, disillusions, reduces; it is the flavour the tongue does not crave, the anti-indulgence. Yet Paṭola is *food* — a vegetable eaten daily, sustaining, mild enough for the sick and the convalescent. In the symbolic grammar of Kenetica it therefore stands for **sustainable discipline** — the austerity that can be *lived*, not merely endured in a crisis-course. Where Kiratatikta is the fast, the shock of pure bitterness, Paṭola is the *diet* — the daily, quiet, feeding restraint that clears Pitta and Kapha without depleting the practitioner. It is the herb of the long path: the bitter you eat every day and are strengthened by.

### Active Components

Reported constituents of *T. dioica* include **cucurbitacins** (the bitter triterpenes characteristic of the Cucurbitaceae), other **tetra- and pentacyclic triterpenes**, **novel peptides and proteins**, **tannins, saponins, alkaloids, flavonoids**, and nutritionally significant **vitamin A and vitamin C**. The cucurbitacins and triterpenes are the plausible carriers of the hypoglycaemic, hypolipidaemic and hepatoprotective signals; the vitamins and peptide fraction reflect its dual identity as nourishing food.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Paṭola is Kenetica's **edible bitter — the daily Pitta-Kapha clearer**. It is the substance of the purification and bitter-tonic phases that can also be carried into ordinary diet: taken to clear heat, settle the liver, support digestion and blood, and hold metabolic sluggishness at bay *without* the depleting harshness of the pure febrifuges. In the training arc it corresponds to *maintained discipline* — the practitioner who has internalised austerity to the point that it nourishes rather than strains. Its classical jaundice/alcoholism and febrifuge uses fit a role as the gentle, sustainable liver-and-heat regulator across long conditioning.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the evidence for *T. dioica* is **preclinical (animal) and review-level**; the antihyperglycaemic signal is real but modest, and human data are essentially absent in the retrieved sources:

- Aqueous leaf extract of *T. dioica* (800 and 1600 mg/kg) significantly reduced blood glucose in glucose-loaded, normoglycaemic and streptozotocin-induced hyperglycaemic rats — confirming an **antihyperglycaemic action**, though it was **not as effective as glibenclamide** (Adiga et al., 2010, *International Journal of Diabetes in Developing Countries*) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4103/0973-3930.60011).
- A pharmacognostic overview catalogued its constituents (cucurbitacins, triterpenes, novel peptides, vitamins A and C, tannins, saponins) and its traditional antidiabetic, hepatoprotective, antipyretic and diuretic uses — including the Caraka references to jaundice and alcoholism — while noting several traditional claims remain scientifically unproven (Kumar et al., 2012, *Pharmacognosy Reviews*) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4103/0973-7847.95886).

**Evidence grade: LOW / preclinical.** The hypoglycaemic effect is reproducible in rodents but weaker than a standard drug; hepatoprotective and febrifuge claims rest largely on tradition and phytochemical plausibility, with no robust human trials in the retrieved literature. Its strongest evidence-backed identity is as a *nutritious dietary vegetable with mild metabolic benefit*, not a potent therapeutic. Science attributed to PubMed.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Rasa (taste):** Tikta (bitter) — with the mildness that permits its use as food.
- **Guṇa (quality):** Laghu (light), Rūkṣa (dry).
- **Vīrya (potency):** Uṣṇa (heating).
- **Vipāka (post-digestive):** Kaṭu (pungent).
- **Prabhāva (specific effect):** Its capacity to be simultaneously nourishing food and clearing medicine — an edible tikta — is treated as its distinctive virtue; classical hepatoprotective/jaundice affinity is ascribed at this level.
- **Doṣa action:** Tridoṣa-śāmaka, with primary Pitta and Kapha pacification (via bitter, light, dry qualities); its Uṣṇa vīrya and lightness keep it from aggravating Vāta as harshly as the dry febrifuges, and it is generally regarded as balancing across all three.
- **Karma (actions):** Jvaraghna (antipyretic), Dīpana-Pācana (digestive-kindling), Yakṛt-uttejaka / Kāmalāghna (hepatic-stimulant, anti-jaundice), Mūtrala (diuretic), Kṛmighna (anthelmintic), Rakta-śodhaka (blood-purifying), Hṛdya (cardiotonic, per classical claim).
- **Gaṇa membership:** Enumerated in Suśruta's esteemed therapeutic groupings among the bitter, febrifuge and blood-purifying drugs; a standard component of jvara, kāmalā (jaundice) and tvak-doṣa formulations.

### Contraindications

- Heating potency (Uṣṇa vīrya): moderate caution in high Pitta / hyperacidity despite its general Pitta-clearing use — leaf preparations are stronger than the fruit.
- Its drying, light qualities may aggravate severe Vāta / dryness states if used as concentrated leaf medicine over long periods.
- Antidiabetic use: mild effect only; not a substitute for prescribed glucose-lowering therapy, and additive hypoglycaemia is theoretically possible if combined — monitor.
- Jaundice and alcoholism are serious conditions; the classical indications do not license self-treatment in place of medical care.
