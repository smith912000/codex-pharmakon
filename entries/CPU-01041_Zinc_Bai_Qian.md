**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Two chapters in Book 5, "Metallic Stones" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). 5-84 KADMEIA, glossed "Cadmium Earth — Cadmia" (lines 10220-10227): the best is the Cyprian *botryites*, "thick and not very heavy ... with the outside in clusters, coloured like ashes"; the next an azure outside and whiter within with onyx-like veins; other types *placodes* (or *zonitis*) and *ostracitis*; the white is useless. It is dug from old mines, and also "comes from brass made red-hot in a furnace, from the soot sticking to the sides and top of the furnaces", from iron rods set to catch the rising particles, or from a burned stone called *purites*. Tests to tell it from false stones: it "is also discerned as follows. Cadmia (pounded into small pieces, made into a paste with vinegar and dried in the sun) comes together, but that from stone does not allow this." Action (line 10222): "It is binding — filling up sores, and cleaning their foulness. It stops discharges of the pores, and dries, draws to a scab, and represses abnormal growths of the flesh, and creates a new skin on malignant ulcers." *Botryitis* and *onychitis* are for eye medicines. Burning: covered in coals until transparent and sparking, quenched in Aminaean wine, or in vinegar for scabies. 5-85 POMPHOLUX, glossed "Zinc Oxide — Pompholyx" (lines 10228-10241): it is "fat, white and altogether very light, so that it can fly into the air", made when brass-workers sprinkle crumbled cadmia on the metal, or blown from cadmia in a purpose-built house with a chimney (the building is described); it can be counterfeited with bulls' glue, lambs' lungs, sea lungs or burnt green figs (line 10234); it is washed in rain water or in diluted Chian wine, and "Pompholyx is astringent, cooling, filling, purging, and somewhat drying" (line 10241). *Spodos*, the heavier scrapings from brass-workers' floors, is distinguished from it. Both chapters are in `extract/names.csv` (lines 10220, 10228). The metal zinc is not named. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Zinc is a bluish-white metal, an essential trace element for people, and the metal of galvanising and brass (an alloy with copper). Its oxide is a white, insoluble powder with mild astringent and antiseptic properties on the skin; its soluble salts (the sulfate, chloride) are irritant. Cadmia in the Greek sense is the furnace deposit or ore of brass-making, mainly zinc oxide and zinc carbonate, and can contain traces of other metals, cadmium and lead among them.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides' cadmia and pompholyx are drying, binding wound and eye medicines; Sushruta's zinc is a metal in a list, a foreign body that the body melts, and a covering for tumours before cautery. None is a cultivation substance. Zinc as a nutrient is a modern, dietary matter outside these sources, and the Codex gives no protocol, dose or preparation from them.

### Preparation & Dosing

DMM: cadmia is burnt in coals and quenched in wine or (for scabies) vinegar, sometimes burnt three times with wine and washed in a mortar until no filth remains (5-84, lines 10223-10227); pompholyx is washed in water or diluted wine (5-85, lines 10235-10241). SS: sheets of zinc, copper, lead or iron laid on a superficial tumour (Chikitsa ch.18, lines 411-413). No quantity of any zinc item for taking is given in the lines read. ### Scientific Correlation

Zinc oxide is a mild astringent and skin protectant, used today in skin preparations; soluble zinc salts and inhaled zinc fumes (as in brass-founding, the process in the Greek chapter) cause illness. Zinc is a nutrient, and deficiency and excess both have health effects. The Sanskrit statement that swallowed metals are melted and assimilated is not borne out; metals pass through the gut or are absorbed as salts. No clinical studies were retrieved for the traditional uses. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Zinc fumes from heating zinc or brass cause metal-fume fever, and cadmia of the Greek kind may contain cadmium and lead; heating such materials, as the chapters describe, is dangerous. Soluble zinc salts are irritant and emetic, and excess zinc interferes with copper uptake. Do not apply metals or "cadmia" preparations to the eye or to open sores. Purchase and use of traditional metal preparations are regulated in many places.
