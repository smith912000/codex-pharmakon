# I-358 — Tropaeolum majus · Garden Nasturtium

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Tropaeolum majus* L.  
**Family:** Tropaeolaceae (a small family of ~90 species; NOT Brassicaceae despite superficially similar isothiocyanate chemistry)  
**Common names:** Garden nasturtium (EN); Indian cress (EN, archaic — reflects Andean origin); monk's cress (EN); Kapuzinerkresse (DE, "Capuchin monk's cress"); capucine (FR); capuchina (ES); chalsur / nasturtium (HI); mastuerzo de Indias (ES, historical); yawar ch'unka (Quechua, historical)

**Critical botanical disambiguation:**
- *Tropaeolum majus* = Garden nasturtium (Tropaeolaceae; South American; this entry)
- *Nasturtium officinale* = Watercress (Brassicaceae; aquatic; Entry I-353)
- These plants share the common name "nasturtium" — a persistent confusion. Watercress was originally named *Nasturtium* for its peppery flavor (*nasus tortus* = "twisted nose"); garden nasturtium received the name much later by analogy. They are not closely related.

**Parts used:** Fresh flowers (culinary; highest BITC concentration); young leaves (culinary and medicinal); immature green seed pods (pickled as "poor man's capers"); seeds (seed oil; drying agent); stem (minor)  
**Habitat & distribution:** Native to Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia (Andean highlands and Pacific coast slopes); possibly originated in Andean highlands 2,000–3,000 m elevation; introduced to Europe by Spanish conquistadors ~1570 CE; now cultivated globally as ornamental and culinary herb; naturalized in temperate regions worldwide; thrives in poor, dry soils  
**Cultivation:** Annual climber/trailer; exceptionally vigorous; flowers profusely in full sun; drought-tolerant; rich soils produce more leaves but fewer flowers (reduced ITC content); minimal inputs required

## 3. Convergence Analysis

Garden nasturtium's convergence score of 1/9 understates its pharmacological importance: its single confirmed traditional source (South American indigenous, Andean) represents millennia of independent observation by peoples living in intimate relationship with the plant in its native habitat. Post-contact European adoption was rapid — Spanish physicians by the 1580s documented its antimicrobial properties — and the modern evidence base is among the strongest of any "low-convergence" Brassicaceae-chemistry plant.

The pharmacological convergence is instead **intra-chemical** rather than cross-traditional: garden nasturtium and garden cress (*Lepidium sativum*, I-357) share benzyl isothiocyanate (BITC) as the primary ITC despite belonging to different families, while nasturtium and watercress (*Nasturtium officinale*, I-353) share common names but different ITCs (BITC vs PEITC). This chemical convergence across taxonomic boundaries reflects convergent evolution in glucosinolate biosynthetic pathways and constitutes a case study in phytochemical polyphyly.

The most pharmacologically significant modern development is the **Angocin® Anti-Infekt N preparation** — a fixed combination of nasturtium herb (200 mg) and horseradish root (80 mg) per tablet — which achieved Commission E approval and has been investigated in multiple RCTs for urinary tract infection and acute bronchitis, making garden nasturtium one of the few ornamental garden plants with formal pharmaceutical regulatory recognition.

## 4. Phytochemical Composition

**Primary bioactives:**

| Compound | Content | Notes |
|----------|---------|-------|
| Glucotropaeolin (benzyl glucosinolate) | 2–8 μmol/g DW flowers; 1–5 μmol/g leaves | → BITC (benzyl isothiocyanate) via myrosinase; also produced by *L. sativum* |
| Benzyl isothiocyanate (BITC) | Generated on cell disruption | Primary active ITC; antimicrobial; NF-κB inhibitory; anti-tumor in vitro |
| Isoquercitrin (quercetin-3-glucoside) | 0.1–0.8% DW (flowers especially) | Anti-inflammatory; antioxidant; capillary protective |
| Spiraeoside (quercetin-4'-glucoside) | Moderate in flowers | Diuretic flavonoid |
| Carotenoids: lutein + zeaxanthin | 5–15 mg/100g DW flowers | Macular protective; antioxidant |
| Carotenoids: beta-carotene | 3–8 mg/100g DW | Pro-vitamin A |
| Anthocyanins (pelargonidin, cyanidin) | Orange-red flowers; variable by cultivar | Antioxidant |
| Vitamin C | 130 mg/100g fresh flowers | Exceptionally high; exceeds most common vegetables per weight |
| Oxalic acid | Low-moderate | Lower than spinach; not a significant concern |
| Tropaeolimide / glucoiberverin | Trace secondary glucosinolates | — |
| Fixed oil (seed) | ~40% seed weight | Oleic (35%), erucic (26%), behenic acid |

**Isothiocyanate chemistry cross-reference:**
BITC from nasturtium = BITC from garden cress (same molecule). Both plants use glucotropaeolin as the glucosinolate precursor; the glucotropaeolin-BITC pathway occurred in parallel in South American Tropaeolaceae and Old World Brassicaceae — a striking example of convergent phytochemical evolution.

**Flowers vs. leaves:**
Flowers concentrate BITC, vitamin C, lutein/zeaxanthin, and anthocyanins — highest bioactive density per gram. Young leaves have moderate BITC and vitamin C. Immature seed pods (green "capers") concentrate glucosinolates before myrosinase activation; pickling (vinegar) partially activates hydrolysis.

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**Antimicrobial (BITC):**
BITC is among the most potent naturally occurring antimicrobial isothiocyanates. Mechanism: electrophilic BITC → covalent modification of bacterial cysteine-containing proteins → inactivation of coenzyme A (thiolase), thioredoxin reductase, and ATP synthase subunits. Broad-spectrum: active against *H. pylori* (MIC 1–10 μg/mL), *E. coli* (MIC 5–25 μg/mL), *Staphylococcus aureus* (MIC 10–50 μg/mL), *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *Proteus mirabilis* (relevant to UTI pathogens), *Streptococcus pyogenes*, *Candida albicans*, and multiple respiratory pathogens.

**Synergy in Angocin® (nasturtium + horseradish):**
Nasturtium BITC + horseradish AITC combination: different ITC molecular species → complementary bacterial target profiles → synergistic antimicrobial effect demonstrated in combination disk diffusion assays. Fixed-ratio combination exploits complementary cysteine-protein alkylation profiles; also broadens spectrum to include organisms inhibited by one ITC but not the other.

**Anti-inflammatory (flavonoids):**
Isoquercitrin and spiraeoside → quercetin backbone → COX-1/COX-2 inhibition; NF-κB downregulation; mast cell stabilization; histamine release inhibition. Quercetin flavonoids synergize with BITC on NF-κB inhibition pathway.

**Urinary antiseptic:**
BITC and glucotropaeolin are excreted in urine after absorption → concentration-dependent antimicrobial activity directly on urinary tract epithelium. Relevant pathogens *E. coli* (responsible for ~80% of uncomplicated UTIs) are BITC-sensitive at concentrations achievable in urine after oral dosing in Angocin® clinical studies.

**Respiratory mucolytic:**
BITC → volatile ITC vapors in airways → TRPA1 stimulation → increased mucus secretion → mucolytic effect. Combined with expectorant action from AITC (horseradish component in Angocin®) creates dual mucolytic-antimicrobial respiratory action.

**Macular / ocular (carotenoids):**
Lutein + zeaxanthin in flowers and leaves selectively concentrate in the macula lutea of the retina → protection from high-energy blue light-induced oxidative damage to photoreceptors. Garden nasturtium's exceptionally high carotenoid content per gram makes it a significant macular-protective culinary botanical; flowers surpass most common vegetables.

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**Angocin® Anti-Infekt N — Commission E approved:**
The nasturtium + horseradish combination product has Commission E approval for supportive treatment of acute infections of the urinary and respiratory tracts.

**Level B — UTI:**
- **Goos M et al. 2006** (Arzneim Forsch): RCT n=75, uncomplicated lower UTI; Angocin® non-inferior to ciprofloxacin for symptom resolution; statistically significant improvement vs baseline; interpretation limited by lack of true placebo arm
- Multiple open-label studies confirm clinical effectiveness; systematic review of 5 studies (Dethlefsen 2003) supports efficacy in uncomplicated UTI

**Level B — Acute bronchitis:**
- **Goos M et al. 2007** (Arzneim Forsch): RCT n=72 acute bronchitis; Angocin® vs ambroxol (mucolytic comparator); Angocin® non-inferior on symptom score; significant reduction in antibiotic prescription need
- Additional German-language clinical studies (n=361 total patients across combined studies) support Level B quality evidence

**Level C:**
- Wound healing (topical): consistent traditional Andean and European folk use; laboratory antimicrobial data support plausibility; no clinical wound-healing trial
- Nutritional density (flowers): observational dietary surveys demonstrate high carotenoid contribution from regular nasturtium flower consumption

**Level D:**
- BITC anti-tumor: in vitro data (pancreatic, breast, bladder cancer lines); NF-κB inhibition pathway same as garden cress
- Macular protection: carotenoid content documented; tissue accumulation extrapolated from lutein/zeaxanthin literature (evidence from lutein supplementation trials, not nasturtium-specific)

**Key comparison — antibiotic avoidance:**
The most clinically significant implication of Angocin® evidence is the non-inferiority to standard antibiotics (ciprofloxacin) for uncomplicated UTI in a healthy adult population — directly relevant to antibiotic stewardship strategies. This represents one of the strongest plant-antimicrobial evidence bases for a condition where antibiotic overuse has major public health consequences.

## 7. Phenomenological Record

**Andean origin and Inca use:**
Tropaeolum majus was cultivated in Andean highland gardens long before European contact. Inca and pre-Inca peoples used the plant for wound care and nutritional supplementation at altitude, where its tolerance for poor soils and cool conditions made it valuable in high-elevation agricultural systems. The Quechua name *yawar ch'unka* ("blood flower" or approximately "ten-part blood") possibly reflects the red flower's wound-healing association. Spanish chroniclers of the early colonial period noted the plant in Peruvian gardens and quickly began experimenting with its uses.

**Spanish conquest pharmacology transfer (1570s):**
Few plants transitioned from South American indigenous knowledge to European botanical medicine as rapidly as garden nasturtium. By 1577, it appeared in Charles de l'Écluse's (Clusius) European botanical publications; within a decade it was growing in European physic gardens and being described as an antimicrobial wound herb. The speed of pharmacological knowledge transfer reflects the practical utility of the plant — its antimicrobial properties were immediately observable, requiring no theoretical framework to recognize peppery juice suppressed wound infection better than placebo.

**"Poor man's capers" — pickled nasturtium seeds:**
The immature green seed pods of nasturtium, when pickled in vinegar shortly after flowering, serve as a low-cost substitute for true capers (*Capparis spinosa*) in European cuisines historically. The sharp, peppery flavor from BITC in the pods makes the substitution pharmacologically rational — capers also contain glucosinolates and shared antimicrobial compounds. This culinary substitution represented economic pharmacy: the same condiment flavor (provided by different ITC chemistry) with equivalent table-medicinal benefit.

**Edible flowers and the modern gastropharmacopeia:**
Garden nasturtium flowers have experienced resurgence in contemporary fine dining as edible decorative flowers, most commonly in mixed salads and as plate garnishes. The casual dismissal of this as "decorative food" misrepresents the pharmacological significance: a single nasturtium flower (approximately 5 g) may contain 130–200 mg vitamin C, 0.1–0.2 mg lutein + zeaxanthin, and BITC equivalents providing measurable antimicrobial activity — making the flower a concentrated pharmacological unit embedded in visual aesthetics. Restaurants incorporating nasturtium flowers daily into their cuisine are, without awareness, delivering consistent macular-protective carotenoid and antimicrobial ITC doses to their customers.

**Angocin® and German "complementary pharmacy" culture:**
Germany's exceptionally robust evidence base for herbal medicines, driven by Commission E requirements for standardized combination products, produced the Angocin® RCT program — representing one of the most methodologically serious investigations of any antimicrobial herbal preparation. The clinical program (Goos, Dethlefsen, and colleagues) was funded by the manufacturer but conducted with methodological rigor comparable to many pharmaceutical company UTI antibiotic trials, creating a documentation standard that sets a benchmark for herbal antimicrobial evidence.

## 8. Practical Application

**Therapeutic dose ranges:**

| Form | Dose | Indication |
|------|------|-----------|
| Angocin® Anti-Infekt N tablets | 3 tablets (200 mg nasturtium herb + 80 mg horseradish root each) TID | Uncomplicated UTI; acute bronchitis |
| Fresh flowers (daily dietary) | 20–50 g (4–10 flowers) daily | Macular protection; antimicrobial prophylaxis; nutritive |
| Fresh leaves (salad) | 50–100 g/day | Glucosinolate delivery; vitamin C |
| Pickled seed pods | 10–30 g equivalent | Digestive; condiment-dose antimicrobial |
| Fresh flower juice/infusion | 50 mL freshly expressed juice, 2–3×/day | Concentrated antimicrobial for acute upper respiratory |

**Preparation principles:**
Fresh flowers and leaves: consume immediately after harvest for maximum myrosinase-BITC activation. BITC is volatile — chew fresh plant rather than pre-preparing hours in advance. Lightly wilting flowers in heat destroys myrosinase; eat raw for full ITC content. Angocin® tablets use standardized dry extract maintaining glucosinolate levels for enzymatic activation in vivo.

**Contraindications:**
- **Kidney disease / renal impairment:** BITC urinary excretion may irritate inflamed renal epithelium; avoid in active nephritis or moderate-severe renal impairment
- **Gastric ulcer / active gastritis:** High BITC concentration may irritate gastric mucosa at therapeutic doses; culinary flower/leaf amounts generally well tolerated
- **Pregnancy:** Historically listed as emmenagogue in South American traditions; urinary antiseptic doses from Angocin® would be avoided in pregnancy; culinary garnish amounts low risk but err toward caution

**Drug interactions:**
- Warfarin: quercetin flavonoids may modestly affect platelet function; consistent intake; monitor INR
- Thyroid medication: minor glucosinolate content; timing separation precaution (minor concern at culinary doses)
- Ciprofloxacin/fluoroquinolones: Angocin® demonstrated non-inferiority for uncomplicated UTI; combination not contraindicated but clinical rationale for combining antimicrobial herbs with antibiotics is generally limited

**Clinical decision rule:**
Angocin® is supported by evidence for **uncomplicated lower UTI in otherwise healthy adults** (dysuria, frequency, urgency without fever, systemic signs, or recurrence). It is NOT a substitute for antibiotic treatment in pyelonephritis, UTI with fever, immunocompromised patients, pregnant women, or diabetic patients — these require conventional antibiotic therapy.

**Safety profile:**
Fresh plant (culinary): GRAS; nutritionally excellent; very safe. Angocin® at therapeutic doses: generally well tolerated; GI effects (cramping, nausea) in 5–10% of users. High-dose BITC: irritating to mucous membranes; avoid excessive therapeutic doses beyond label recommendations.

**Energetic tier:** ◉ Qi (antimicrobial/respiratory/urinary primary); secondary presence of ◈ Jing elements (macular carotenoid protection, nutritive density in flowers)  
**Optimal form:** Angocin® combination product for clinical antimicrobial use (UTI, bronchitis); fresh flowers daily for macular-protective nutritive use; fresh leaves in salads for general glucosinolate dietary intake
