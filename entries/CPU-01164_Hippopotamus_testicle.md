# Hippopotamus testicle — Orchis Hippopotamou (ORCHIS HIPPOPOTAMOU), "the stones [testicles] of the hippopotamus"
## *The testicles of the hippopotamus, dried and pounded; the translation prints "Hippopotamus amphibious — Hippopotamus" under the chapter title* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 2-25, *Orchis Hippopotamou* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 5032-5034; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 215, "Hippopotamus amphibious — Hippopotamus", with the note "OCR spelling 'Hippopotamus amphibious' repaired to spine binomial 'Hippopotamus amphibius'"). The whole chapter, in the 2000 English edition, is one sentence: "The stones [testicles] of the hippopotamus are dried and pounded into small pieces and taken in a drink in wine against snakebite."

That is everything the chapter says. It gives no taste or quality, no dose, no source place, no instruction on choosing or testing the material and no warning. The Greek title is printed in this edition only in transliteration (ORCHIS HIPPOPOTAMOU); no Greek script is printed, and none is supplied here.

The chapter sits in Book Two, which Dioscorides announces as covering "living creatures" among other matters (line 4904, the Book Two preface). Its neighbours show the pattern. In 2-26, *Kastoros Orchis* (castoreum), the beaver's "stones [testicles] are good against the poisons of snakes" (line 5038). In 2-43 (the chapter headed "Testes of Deer", line 5112) "the genitals of a male hart (pounded into small pieces and taken in a drink with wine) help those bitten by vipers". The three entries carry the same use for the same kind of organ, in similar wording. Which of them borrows from which is not stated in the lines read. The 2000 edition's translator's gloss adds only the bracketed word "testicles" for "stones".

### Active Components

Testicular tissue is made of connective tissue, protein, lipid and the cells that make sperm and sex hormones (testosterone and related steroids). Dried and pounded, much of that would be denatured protein and fat. Nothing in the chapter or in the sources retrieved gives an assay of this material, and no composition figures are offered. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The text gives one use: a drink of the dried, pounded organ in wine against snakebite. The Codex's application layer has nothing to add to that, and nothing in it is a protocol.

It is not a recommended practice. Snakebite is an emergency treated with antivenom and urgent medical care, and no animal organ taken in wine is a treatment for it. The hippopotamus is a protected species, and sourcing its organs is conservation-sensitive. The entry stands as a record of what the Greek text states, and as one of several organs (beaver, deer, hippopotamus) that the book puts against the bites of venomous animals.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparation, as printed in 2-25: the testicles are dried and "pounded into small pieces", then taken "in a drink in wine". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] Quantity, timing and the amount of wine are all unstated.

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] General facts only: no animal tissue has been shown to neutralise snake venom when swallowed; venom is a mixture of proteins and peptides, and effective treatment is by specific antivenom given by injection. Swallowing dried tissue also carries the ordinary risks of untreated animal material (see Contraindications). The belief recorded in the chapter belongs to the ancient idea that an animal part could act on a harm of a related kind, and the sources retrieved do not say why Dioscorides or his sources chose the organ.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Greek / Dioscorides (DMM): no quality (warming, cooling, drying, astringent) is printed for this substance in 2-25, and none is assigned here. For comparison only, 2-26 says of castoreum that "generally it is warming" (line 5038); nothing similar is said of the hippopotamus.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. Nothing in 2-25 gives the substance a cultivation status; the chapter is a single therapeutic use against snakebite. What Dioscorides offers elsewhere in the book is a discipline of attention: provenance, season of gathering, a test by the senses, and the detection of adulteration. This chapter offers none of those for the hippopotamus organ, so even that frame is empty here. The Dioscoridean text does sometimes record an amuletic or ritual use while distancing itself; none is recorded in this chapter.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. There is no cultivation claim, no dose and no method, only a one-line treatment of snakebite that no longer applies. The honest use of the entry is as a record, and as a reminder that the right response to a snakebite is a hospital.

### Contraindications

- **Not a recommended practice.** This is a historical record of a Greek remedy. Nothing here is advice to use an animal organ.
- **Snakebite:** do not rely on any home or traditional remedy; seek emergency care at once.
- **Conservation and law:** the hippopotamus is a protected species, and the trade in its parts is regulated or unlawful in many places. [USER: verify the current status]
- **Hygiene and infection:** dried organ tissue from a wild animal can carry bacteria and parasites; taking it by mouth is unsafe.
- **Ethics:** the organ is obtained by killing a large wild animal.
