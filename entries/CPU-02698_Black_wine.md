# Oinos meletites (honeyed old wine; "Vinum melitites") — OINOS MELETITES (Dioscorides 5-15)

## *A wine made of hard wine with honey and a little salt, which the translator's SUGGESTED line renders "Old Wine with Honey"; the spine row's name "Black wine" does not match the chapter, which prints no colour, and the identification is corrected here; the grape link is through the wine, not the plant itself; medium confidence* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry.** Attested only in Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica*, book 5, chapter 15, OINOS MELETITES (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, line 9845). **Identification differs from the row's check line.** The row names it "Black wine", but the chapter prints no colour for this wine and its translator's heading is "Old Wine with Honey". Black wine is named elsewhere in book 5: chapter 5-14 ("Wine of Wild Grapes") begins "The black wine from the wild grape is astringent", and 5-11 speaks of "thick black wines". Neither is the subject of this entry.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The chapter reads: "Vinum melitites is given in long-lasting fevers to those who have a weak stomach because it gently loosens the bowels, induces urine, and purges the stomach. It is good for arthritis, kidney disease, and those who have a weak head. It is useful for women to drink with water as it has a fragrant smell and is nourishing. It differs from mead [honey wine] because that is made from hard old wine and a little honey, but for meletites five gallons of hard wine is added to four and a half litres of honey and one winecup of salt. You must make it in a large jar so that it may have room to ferment lustily, sprinkling on the salt little by little until it is quite fermented, and when it stops, pour it out into other ceramic jars." The bracketed gloss and the unit names are the translator's.

**Neighbouring chapters (context).** Chapter 5-16, OINOMELI (mulsum, honeyed wine), is a related but separate wine made "from old hard wine and good honey" (line 9852); it is on the spine as its own row (CPU-02636). Dioscorides distinguishes the two by the proportions and by the added salt: mead has "a little honey", while meletites has the honey measured against the wine and takes salt.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a recommended practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Long-lasting fevers in those with a weak stomach; loosening the bowels; inducing urine; purging the stomach; arthritis; kidney disease; a weak head; and as a fragrant, nourishing drink for women, taken with water.
- **What the Codex does.** It gives no protocol. A fever lasting long is a reason to see a clinician, and the wine is an alcoholic drink whose use for the sick the text itself hedges, since the preceding chapter on the weak secondary wine (5-13, *deuterias oinos*) says it is for those "to whom we are unsure about giving wine" (line 9836).

### Preparation & Dosing

One recipe is printed: five gallons of hard wine, four and a half litres of honey and one winecup of salt, in a large jar with room to ferment, the salt sprinkled in little by little until fermentation stops, then poured into other jars (line 9845). The chapter says women drink it with water but gives no amount to drink, no time and no duration. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Alcohol is a central nervous system depressant and a liver toxin in excess, and it interacts with many medicines. Fermentation, not the added materials, produces the alcohol. Honey adds sugar, and a high-sugar liquid can ferment further and become more alcoholic. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter shows a discipline of attention in its detail: it separates this wine from mead by proportion and salt, and it tells the maker to give the jar "room to ferment lustily" and to salt by degrees until the ferment stops, a sensory endpoint. That is craft and method, not a cultivation frame, and no status is attested.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to practise. A salted honey-wine from an indication-organised chapter, with no bearing on the vital constitution in the sources read. Historical only.

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

Per the entry's own Dioscorides (DMM) claims, Oinos meletites acts on the stomach, the bowels, the urinary passages, the kidneys, the joints and the head. The chapter says it is given in long-lasting fevers to those with a weak stomach, that it gently loosens the bowels, induces urine and purges the stomach, and that it is good for arthritis, kidney disease and those with a weak head. It is also described as fragrant and nourishing for women when drunk with water. In modern anatomy terms this maps to the gastric tract, the intestines, the renal and urinary system, the joints and the head as the text uses that term. The chapter prints no warm or cool quality, and no humoral or energetic frame is assigned to this wine, so none is mapped here. The entry notes that the text itself hedges on giving wine to the sick, and the Codex records these claims as historical only.

### Contraindications

- **Alcohol.** Avoid in pregnancy, breastfeeding, liver disease, alcohol-use disorder, diabetes (the honey), and with sedating or interacting medicines.
- **Fevers and kidney disease.** Medical conditions that need proper care; a wine is not a substitute.
- **Children.** Not for children.
- **Name.** The row's name "Black wine" is a misnomer for this chapter. [USER: verify the row name.]
