*4-30 AGROSTIS* (heading at line 8266; plate caption at line 8268, "Agrostis interrupta after FAGUET — 1894"; SUGGESTED lines 8270 and 8271: "Gramen [Fuchs], Stellaria holostea [Linnaeus], Caryophyllus arvensis glaber flore majore [Bauhin], — Greater Stitchwort [Mabberley] [other usage] Agrostis alba, Agrostis palustris — White Bent Grass, Fiorin Grass"; text at line 8272, in Book Four). The whole chapter reads: "Agrostis has little branches full of joints creeping on the earth and growing out from the stalks; sweet, knotty roots, the sharp leaves hard and broad like a little reed, nourishing for cows and labouring cattle. The root of this (pounded into small pieces) is applied to heal wounds. A decoction of it (taken as a drink) is good for griping, painful urination, and ulcers around the bladder, and it breaks urinary stones. It is also called aegicon, or amaxitist; the Egyptians say anuphi, the Romans, gramen, some say assefolium, sanguinalis, or uniola, the Spaniards, aparia, the Dacians, cotiata, and the Africans, jebal."

**What the text gives.** The description is of a creeping, jointed, rooting grass with sweet knotty roots and broad sharp leaves, grazed by cattle. The root is applied to wounds, and a decoction is drunk for griping, painful urination, bladder ulcers and stones. Seven alternative names are printed, from the Egyptians, Romans, Spaniards, Dacians and Africans.

**Identity.** Three different things are in the translators' apparatus: a plate caption (*Agrostis interrupta*, a bent grass), the preferred suggestion (*Stellaria holostea*, greater stitchwort, a non-grass), and an "other usage" (*Agrostis alba* and *A. palustris*, bent grasses). The chapter's description, "creeping on the earth" with "sweet, knotty roots", is that of a grass, and the Greek name means "grass". Which species is meant cannot be decided from the text.
