# Mimusops schimperi — Persea (Dioscorides 1-187)

## *Mimusops schimperi, the second of two names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 1-187 (which prints "Persea" first), with the English "Egyptian Lebekbaum [Bedevian]"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 4893) reads "Persea, Mimusops schimperi — Egyptian Lebekbaum [Bedevian]". The first item is the Greek name itself, so the only scientific suggestion is *Mimusops schimperi*, and the English name is credited to Bedevian, not to Dioscorides. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *1-187. PERSEA*, heading at line 4892, in Book One among the trees, the second-last chapter of Book One. The whole chapter (line 4894) reads: "Persea is a tree which grows in Egypt, especially in Thebes. It bears fruit fit to eat and good for the stomach, on which the spiders called cranocolopta are found. The dry leaves pounded into small pieces and applied are able to stop blood breaking out. Some have reported that this tree when it grew in Persis was deadly, but when transported into Egypt it was altered and became good to eat."

Dioscorides therefore gives the plant a place (Egypt, "especially in Thebes") and a fruit "fit to eat and good for the stomach", and says that "the spiders called cranocolopta" are found on the fruit. He gives one outward use, for the dry leaves, pounded and applied "to stop blood breaking out". He describes no leaf, flower, bark or size, so the chapter offers no way to identify the tree from its own lines. The last sentence is a report: "Some have reported" that in Persis the tree was deadly and that, transported into Egypt, it "was altered and became good to eat". The report is given as hearsay and is the only statement in the chapter about a change in the tree with the place.

**Neighbouring places in the same text.** The chapter after it, *1-188. IBERIS* (line 4895), is a bitter cress or candytuft plant (translators' lines) and is not this tree. The *cranocolopta* appear again in the translators' index: "Cranocolopta 182" (line 15496) and "Mimusops schimperi 182" (line 16611); "PERSEA 182" is in the chapter list (line 12423). A different "Persea" appears in the SUGGESTED line of 1-13, the cinnamon chapter, as "Persea cinnamomum" (line 3661); that is a name for cinnamon and not this tree. Entry III_188 (spider) already cites this chapter for the spiders on the fruit, as a pointer to its own subject. It treats the animal and not the tree, so it is not a duplicate. A search of the entries folder found no other entry on the Dioscorides chapter *Persea*.

### Active Components

The composition of the fruit and leaves Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, *Mimusops* is a genus of trees in the family Sapotaceae, many of which carry a milky latex and edible fruits. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records an Egyptian fruit tree whose fruit is edible and good for the stomach, whose pounded dry leaves are applied to stop bleeding, and that some reported to be deadly in its first home in Persis. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol, and a tree that the text itself says was reported as deadly in one place is not a tree to eat from on the strength of this chapter. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation: the dry leaves "pounded into small pieces and applied". It gives no quantity for the leaves, and no measure of how much fruit. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying, moistening or astringent quality and no taste for 1-187. It prints the fruit as "good for the stomach". The word "astringent" appears in neighbouring chapters, not in this one.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: where the tree grows (Egypt, especially Thebes), what lives on its fruit (the *cranocolopta* spiders), and the report that the same tree was said to be deadly in Persis and edible after transport to Egypt, a note on provenance of the kind the spec names. He reports the story with "Some have reported", and recording is not endorsing. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of an edible fruit and a leaf for bleeding. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this tree. It is an edible fruit and a styptic leaf with no stated quantity, and the text itself passes on a warning about where the tree grew. The useful lesson is Dioscorides' attention to provenance and to what is found on the fruit, and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Dioscorides reports that the tree was "deadly" in one place, so the fruit and leaves should not be used on the strength of this chapter. A fruit on which spiders are found should not be eaten without inspection. Bleeding that does not stop needs medical care, and a leaf poultice must not replace it. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no tree should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
