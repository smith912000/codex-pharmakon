# Whetstone — Akone (as printed in the 2000 English edition of Dioscorides)
## *"Whetstone: fine grained stone for sharpening blades", the translator's gloss under the heading 5-168 AKONE; the text itself names only the Naxian whetstone and says what comes off it when iron is rubbed on it* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The loci given for this entry are `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` ("5-168 (line 10703)", gloss "Whetstone fine grained stone for sharpening blades", class mineral) and `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 894 ("Akone", DMM V, identification marked yes). The chapter is *De Materia Medica* 5-168 *Akone* in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`: heading at line 10703, the translator's gloss at lines 10704-10705, the text at line 10706. It sits among the stones of Book 5, after 5-167 *Ammos* (sand, line 10700) and before 5-169 *Geodes Lithos* (geodes, line 10707).

The chapter reads in full (line 10706): "That which comes off the Naxian whetstone (when iron is rubbed on it) fills up bald patches with hair, and prevents maidens' breasts growing big. Taken as a drink with vinegar it reduces the spleen, and is good for epilepsy." The parenthesis is the translator's. So what the chapter treats is not the stone but the material that comes off it, the residue from rubbing iron on a Naxian whetstone, and it gives that residue four uses: on bald patches, to "fill up" with hair; on the breasts of young women, to prevent growth; as a drink with vinegar for an enlarged spleen; and as a drink, also for epilepsy. The first two are external, and the text does not say how the residue was applied (it does not say mixed, smeared, or bound on). The last two are internal and give the vehicle, vinegar, for the spleen; whether vinegar is also the vehicle for epilepsy is not stated, because the second clause (", and is good for epilepsy") follows the first without a new vehicle. The translator's gloss takes *akone* as a whetstone in general. The only kind specified is Naxian, from the Aegean island of Naxos, which is a general geographical fact and not stated by the chapter; the chapter says "Naxian" and no more.

The chapter gives no description of the stone, no colour, no grain, no test of a true Naxian, no price, no method of rubbing (how much iron, how long, wet or dry), no quantity of the residue, no dose of vinegar, and no duration. Some longer mineral chapters in Book 5 (for example 5-85 on *pompholyx*, line 10228) say how to choose a good piece; this one does not.

The whetstone appears elsewhere in the text only as an implement. 5-155 *Ioudaikos Lithos* (Judaean stone, line 10639) says that a quantity of that stone "like a cicer [2-126], dissolved like a cream on a whetstone and taken with three cupfuls of warm water as a drink can help painful urination, and break stones in the bladder": the whetstone is the surface on which another stone is ground to a cream. 5-172 *Samia* (line 10726) describes a second kind of Samian earth, called *aster*, "crusty and thick like whetstone". In neither is the whetstone itself treated as a drug, and neither adds to the properties given in 5-168.

**Identity.** The translator's gloss, "Whetstone: fine grained stone for sharpening blades", names a use, not a mineral; whetstones are made of various fine-grained stones, and the sources retrieved do not say what the Naxian stone was. The material that "comes off" is a mixture of the stone's fine particles and the iron worn from the blade. The chapter does not distinguish the two or say which carried the effect. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, the residue from rubbing iron on a fine-grained stone is a mixture of fine mineral grit from the stone, which may be silica or another hard mineral, and fine iron or iron oxide from the blade. Which stone the Naxian whetstone was is not stated in the sources read, so the grit's composition is not known from them. Iron oxide is poorly absorbed from the gut, and fine hard grit is an abrasive. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved say that Dioscorides recorded the residue from a Naxian whetstone, with iron rubbed on it, as a remedy for baldness, as something to stop the growth of young women's breasts, and, as a drink with vinegar, for an enlarged spleen and for epilepsy. The Codex does not recommend any of these. No whetstone residue has been shown to regrow hair, to alter breast development or to treat a spleen or epilepsy, and swallowing abrasive grit and metal filings is harmful. Epilepsy needs medical diagnosis and treatment. The use to prevent breast growth in girls is, in modern terms, an attempt to alter a young person's body, and the Codex records it as history only.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation is the residue itself: the material "that comes off the Naxian whetstone (when iron is rubbed on it)" (line 10706). The internal use is "as a drink with vinegar" for the spleen. The chapter gives no quantity of residue, no volume of vinegar, no frequency, no duration, no way of applying it to the scalp or the breast, and no preparation of the stone beyond the rubbing with iron. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Hair loss has many causes, and a mineral grit does not regrow hair. Breast development in girls is governed by hormones and cannot be prevented by an application. An enlarged spleen is a sign of disease (for example infection, liver disease or blood disorders) that needs diagnosis, and epilepsy is a brain disorder treated with prescribed medicines; neither is treated by iron and stone dust in vinegar. Vinegar is dilute acetic acid; swallowed with grit and iron it irritates the gut. Iron overdose is dangerous, particularly in children. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for the whetstone residue. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention, and this chapter offers a small instance: it names the kind of whetstone (Naxian) and the circumstance in which the material arises (iron rubbed on it) before saying what it does, and does not claim that any whetstone will serve. It records no amuletic use. The chapter is therapeutic and cosmetic, and assigns no cultivation status. The use to prevent maidens' breasts from growing is a statement about an effect on the body of young women, and it is recorded here only as part of the chapter.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The entry records that a Greek physician gave four uses for the residue of a Naxian whetstone: for baldness, to stop breast growth, and, with vinegar, for spleen and epilepsy. The Codex does not recommend repeating any of them. The thing a reader can take is the care of the naming: a particular whetstone, a particular way the material was obtained. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Epilepsy:** needs medical diagnosis and prescribed treatment; stopping prescribed medicine for a stone-dust drink risks seizures.
- **Enlarged spleen:** a sign of illness that needs diagnosis; pain in the upper left abdomen after injury is an emergency.
- **Ingestion of grit and iron:** abrasive and indigestible; iron in quantity is toxic, especially to children.
- **Breast development in girls:** not to be altered by any application; a concern about growth or development is a matter for a clinician.
- **Scalp application:** abrasive grit and metal filings on the skin irritate and can introduce infection.
- **Identification:** "whetstone" is the translator's gloss for a Greek word meaning a sharpening stone; the sources retrieved do not say what rock the Naxian stone was.
