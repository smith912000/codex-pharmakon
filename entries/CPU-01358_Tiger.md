# Tiger — "tiger" (Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, as a class of carnivorous animal); Panthera tigris L (Men-Tsee-Khang glossary on animals)
## *The tiger as an animal named in the Tibetan Four Tantras: a member of the carnivorous animals in the meat classification, with its bone as a separate medicine. Neither translation prints a Tibetan word for it, and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary gives the Latin name* Panthera tigris *L* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is the Tibetan *Four Tantras*. The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`; "MTK" below) has a glossary on animals in which "Tiger" is printed at line 7013 and the Latin "Panthera tigris L" at line 7015. The OCR has split the columns of that glossary, so the Tibetan and phonetic names are not paired to the English and Latin, and no Tibetan word for the tiger is available in the sources read. The Latin identification is the glossary's own.

The tiger does not have a flesh entry or a whole-animal entry in the materia medica of chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra. What the texts give is its place in the classification of meats and one part, the bone:

- **Meat classification.** In Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; "Clark" below), meats fall into nine sections, and group (e) reads: "Carnivorous animals include the tiger, leopard, bear (Selenarctos thibetanus), snow bear (Ursus arctos), snow leopard (Panthera pardus), wolf, lynx, fox, hill dog and weasel" (line 672). Group (d), the larger hill and game animals, separately lists a "bamboo forest tiger (Neofelis nebulosa)" (line 672), which is a different animal; Clark's index has the heading "Tiger, bamboo forest" (line 1652). MTK gives the parallel list at line 2912: "The tiger, leopard, black bear, Tibetan brown bear, snow leopard, wolf, lynx, fox, dhole (Tibetan wild-dog) and monkey are the group of canivorous wild animals."
- **The tiger's bone** is a separate substance with its own entry in this Codex. Clark line 1160: "Tiger bone: [has fifteen names]. It increases bone resin." MTK line 3523: "Tiger bone protects the spongy bone."
- **Qualities of the group's meat.** Clark attributes the qualities to the birds of prey ("coarse, light and sharp powers which increase the digestive heat, disperse tumors, increase flesh and eliminate all cold disorders", line 673). MTK says at line 2931: "The meat of birds, carnivorous animals and birds of prey that feed on raw meat has rough, light and sharp qualities, and therefore generates digestive heat, destroys tumors, increases weight and eliminates all cold disorders." So MTK extends the statement to carnivorous animals, and the tiger is in that group, while Clark's wording names only the birds of prey. The two translations differ, and I cannot say from the English which is closer to the Tibetan. Neither text says that tiger meat was eaten or used.
- **The tiger as a figure.** The tiger also appears outside the materia medica as a simile and as an omen. Clark line 379 says of a bile-natured person that they "have the characteristics of a tiger, monkey or 'harmer' spirit"; MTK line 1803 says of a person of *tripa* constitution that their "characteristic features are likened to those of tigers, monkeys and evil spirits". Clark line 402 lists "dreaming that one is riding a cat, monkey, tiger, fox or corpse" among the signs that a patient "has been seized by the Lord of Death", and MTK line 1856 has the parallel. These are descriptions and omens, not uses.
- **A homonym.** Clark prints a plant named *stag.sha* ("lit. 'tiger flesh'") at line 1017, with the Latin names *Potentilla discolor*, *Calophaca crassicaulis* and *Oxytropis chiliophylla*. It is a plant and is not this animal.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent of the tiger. In general terms, the tiger is a large carnivorous mammal, and its flesh and bone are protein, fat and mineral like those of other mammals. Nothing more specific can be said without a source.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts name the tiger as a carnivorous animal and give a group statement for the meat of such animals, and a separate action for its bone. The tiger is a threatened species and trade in its parts is prohibited, so the Codex cannot recommend, or describe a source for, any tiger product. Clark notes in his introduction that "nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57). This entry is a record of how the text classifies the animal.

### Preparation & Dosing

The texts give no preparation of the tiger itself and no quantity. The group statement in MTK line 2931 describes the meat's qualities; the bone is in its own entry. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. The qualities "rough, light and sharp" and the categories "cold disorders" and "tumors" belong to the Tibetan frame and have no direct counterpart in modern physiology. The tiger is a large wild carnivore; handling or eating wild carnivore meat carries parasite and infection risks. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** No taste, potency or post-digestive taste is printed for the tiger alone. For the meats of the animals in the classification, Clark line 673 says that all are "sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste". For the group of carnivorous animals, MTK line 2931 gives "rough, light and sharp qualities", which "generate" digestive heat, destroy tumors, increase weight and eliminate all cold disorders, as above. Clark gives the same powers for the birds of prey only. The tiger's bone has its own statement in the bone entry.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The tiger is named in the Explanatory Tantra as a carnivorous animal in the meat classification (Clark line 672; MTK line 2912) and as the source of a bone medicine (Clark line 1160), and the tiger-simile and the dream-omen appear in the discussion of constitutions and signs of death. None of this is a cultivation status. Nothing in the passages read places the tiger in any practice of essence extraction (*bcud len*), and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, so none is asserted. The wider frame is that the three humors are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration (Clark line 51), so disease is at root a spiritual condition, and that food, behaviour, medicine and accessory therapy are the four supports of treatment (line 1299), of which medicine is the third. The chapter on routine behaviour asks the practitioner to refrain from the ten negative actions, the first of which is "taking life" (line 616). The rejuvenation passage (lines 1266-1275) names no animal substance in the lines read.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The tradition classes the tiger among the carnivores and records its bone as a drug, and its own behavioural teaching begins with refraining from taking life. The Codex does not recommend any tiger product. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Conservation and law:** the tiger is an endangered species, and international trade in tiger parts is prohibited under CITES (Appendix I) and domestic law also restricts it; do not obtain any tiger product.
- **Hygiene:** meat from wild carnivores can carry parasites and infections.
- **Ethics:** the Codex does not recommend harming any animal for these uses, and certainly not an endangered one.
