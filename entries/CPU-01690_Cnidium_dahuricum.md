# Cnidium dahuricum — tang.kun, Tangkun (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Cnidium dahuricum, the second of three identifications Clark 1995 prints for tang.kun (after "a type of dill, Peucedanum sp. (white type)" and before Chaerophyllum reflexum, as printed); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text prints the name as "Tangkun" and its glossary sets a different binomial beside it* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under this binomial only by Clark.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. Both Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the line prints three identifications, the first of them a genus-level "Peucedanum sp.". The extraction lists all three and keys the row to the second (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 147: "a type of dill, Peucedanum sp. (white type); Cnidium dahuricum; Chaerophyllum reflexum", with the note "more than one Latin name given"). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1041).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads:

"tang.kun: a type of dill, Peucedanum sp. (white type): Cnidium dahuricum: Chaerophyllum reflexum: [is of two types. The white has long yellowish petioles, thin scattered leaves and a yellowish flower, with hot taste and pungent odor. The black type has leaves, stalk and root similar to Angelica and a yellow scented flower covered with black fur. Both types] cure heart fever, poisoning [and wind-phlegm combinations]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the two types, their petioles, leaves and flowers, the "hot taste and pungent odor", the words "Both types" and the closing "and wind-phlegm combinations". Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the three identifications, which are Clark's own and not the root text's, and the clause "cure heart fever, poisoning". The commentary's statement that the black type resembles *Angelica* is a plant description and not an identification. The line prints no part used, no preparation and no power. The entry before it (line 1040) is *tshar.bong* and the entry after it (line 1042) is *se.rgod*, both separate drugs.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3438).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20 (it ends at line 3583). The sentence reads: "Tangkun cures hot disorder of the heart and is an antidote against poison." The printed index (line 7508) lists "Tangkun 208". The text gives no Latin name in the passage. The Codex reads "Tangkun" as Clark's *tang.kun* from the spelling and from the agreement in action (heart, poison); the text does not say so. The neighbours in the sentence are also the same as in Clark: "Tsharbong" before it (Clark's *tshar.bong*, line 1040) and "Fruit of Saegoe" after it (Clark's *se.rgod*, line 1042). [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary.* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate blocks. In the "T" block (the letter at line 5858) the phonetic names include "Tangkun" (line 5869), the Tibetan transliterations include "tang kun" (line 5871), and the scientific names are in a separate run (lines 5928 to 5944). Counting the names in order, "Tangkun" is the fourth phonetic name (after "Tab seng", "Tak sha" and "Tamig") and the fourth scientific name is "Angelica sinensis" (line 5936), with the English "Hog fennel" (line 5938). The Codex therefore reads the glossary as pairing "Tangkun" with *Angelica sinensis*, by column position only, and not with *Cnidium dahuricum*. The glossary prints no *Cnidium dahuricum* (a search of the text for *Cnidium* finds only *Cnidium monnieri*, at line 5445). Hog fennel is the English name of a *Peucedanum*, which agrees with Clark's first identification and not with the glossary's binomial. The two Tibetan texts agree on the name and the actions and differ on the plant, and the Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Cnidium dahuricum*, *tang.kun* and *Tangkun* found no entry on this plant. I_1255 (*Cnidium monnieri*), written in this batch, is a sibling row of the same genus on a different Tibetan name. *Cnidium* otherwise appears in I_585 (*Kadenia dubia*, whose title gives "Dubious Cnidium"), I_987, I_1063 and I_1073 in passing (only the match was checked), and none is an entry on this plant. The Shennong text on disk names a different drug with a similar Latin stem, *Radix Angelicae Dahuricae* (Bai Zhi, Yang 1998, line 2835); the Codex does not link it to this drug. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Cnidium* and *Peucedanum* both belong to the carrot family (Apiaceae), and that the family is generally known to produce aromatic oils and coumarins. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained, because its Latin name is not settled. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug of pungent odor that cures "heart fever, poisoning" (Clark, line 1041) or "hot disorder of the heart" and works as "an antidote against poison" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3438). These are the texts' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation for this drug. Clark's commentary describes two types, white and black, and does not say which part is taken; the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence gives an action without a quantity, a frequency or a duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Tibetan terms (heart fever, hot disorder of the heart, poisoning) are not modern diagnoses or measured outcomes, so no modern finding can be set beside them here. The identity of the drug is unsettled between a *Peucedanum*, *Cnidium dahuricum*, *Chaerophyllum reflexum* and, in the glossary, *Angelica sinensis*, so a study of one plant would not bear directly on it.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** The only quality in the lines read is the commentary's "hot taste and pungent odor" for the white type (Clark, line 1041, inside the brackets). Clark's root-text clause prints no taste or power, and the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence (line 3438) prints none. For the drug as a whole no taste, power or post-digestive taste is printed outside the commentary.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes this drug by what it cures, which is heart fever and poisoning, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same ("hot disorder of the heart", an antidote against poison). The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this drug places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: a search of both Tibetan texts for *tang.kun* and "Tangkun" returned only the lines above and the glossary, index and name-list entries. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a heart-fever and poison drug in the Tibetan texts, its Latin name is not settled, and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary appears to pair its name with another plant. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug, although both texts class it as an antidote to poison, which is a use and not a safety statement. The identity is uncertain, and the carrot family includes poisonous members, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Poisoning and a fever that affects the heart need urgent medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
