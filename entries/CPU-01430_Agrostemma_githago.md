# Corn Cockle / Agrostemma githago — Aira (Dioscorides 2-122)

## *Agrostemma githago* (one of several binomials the Dioscorides translators suggest for 2-122, marked "[other usage]"; not Dioscorides' own) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry: named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** This row was added on 2026-10-09 by the plant-gap run (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP). The binomial appears on the SUGGESTED line of one chapter of the English *De Materia Medica* (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) and in no other text read. The identification is weak: the translators list three different suggestions for the same chapter, and this one is flagged "[other usage]". This is a short entry that says what the chapter says.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`.

*2-122 AIRA* (heading at line 5535; SUGGESTED lines 5536 and 5537: "Lolium [Fuchs], Lychnis segetum major [Bauhin], Agrostemma githago [Linnaeus] — Corn Cockle [other usage] Molinia caerulea, Aira coerulea — Purple Molinia, Lavender Grass, Blawing Grass"; cross-reference line 5538: "see 2-116"; text at line 5539, in the book on cereals). The whole chapter reads: "Aira (which grows among wheat) ground up has the power to remove the edges from nomae [grazer disease, eats away muscle, tissue and bones], rotten ulcers, and gangrene, applied as a poultice with radishes and salt. With natural sulphur and vinegar it cures wild lichenae [skin disease] and leprosy. Boiled in wine with pigeons' dung and flaxseed it dissolves scrofulous tumours [glandular swelling, goitres], and breaks open swellings that are hard to ripen. Boiled with honey and water and applied as a poultice it is good for those with sciatica. The smoke inhaled with polenta, myrrh [1-77, 1-73, 4-116], saffron, or frankincense helps conception. The Romans call it lolium, and it is also called thyaron."

**Identity doubt.** The chapter's only description of the plant is that it "grows among wheat". That is a weed of cereal fields, which fits the corn cockle suggestion but equally fits the other suggestions in the list (a ryegrass, *Lolium*, whose name the Romans give in the chapter's last sentence as "lolium", and a grass, *Molinia*/*Aira*). The translators' own list prefers none. The binomial *Agrostemma githago* is attributed to Linnaeus on the translators' line and is marked "other usage", which means a later author's use of the name and not a match made from the Greek text. [USER: verify] The chapter's cross-reference to 2-116 leads to the chapter headed BROMOS, which the translators identify as oats (*Avena*) with darnel as an alternative and marked NARCOTIC (lines 5485 to 5492). 2-116 is another plant and its uses are not attributed to this row.

### Active Components

Corn cockle (*Agrostemma githago*) is generally known to contain toxic saponins, and its seeds have been recorded as a contaminant of grain. Whether the plant Dioscorides called *aira* is corn cockle was not established, so no composition is attached to the chapter. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The chapter records a poultice for destructive ulcers, gangrene, skin disease and swellings, a decoction for sciatica, and a smoke inhaled to help conception. If the plant is corn cockle it is a toxic weed. The identification is unsettled, none of these uses is a recommended practice, and the Codex gives no protocol for it.

### Preparation & Dosing

Line 5539 gives the herb "ground up" and applied "as a poultice with radishes and salt"; with "natural sulphur and vinegar"; "boiled in wine with pigeons' dung and flaxseed"; "boiled with honey and water" as a poultice; and its smoke "inhaled with polenta, myrrh, saffron, or frankincense". No quantity, frequency or duration is printed. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** no warming, cooling, drying or astringent quality is printed for 2-122. The chapter says the ground herb "has the power to remove the edges" from destructive ulcers, which is a statement of action and is not a classification.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. The chapter gives only a habitat (among wheat) and two names. The smoke "helps conception" is a recorded use and not a cultivation status. No cultivation status or framing is attested.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here gives a cultivator a practice. A weed of the wheat field, a poultice and a smoke are recorded; if it is corn cockle, it is a plant to keep out of the grain, not into the body. The abstentive answer is the correct one.

### Contraindications

If the plant is corn cockle, its seeds are toxic when eaten and the plant should not be taken internally. The chapter's uses (ulcers and gangrene, leprosy, tumours, sciatica) are conditions needing a clinician, and inhaling smoke to help conception has no endorsement here. Do not use in pregnancy. None of the uses above is advice.
