# I-497 — Teasel
## *Dipsacus fullonum* L. (= *D. sylvestris* Huds.)

**Family:** Dipsacaceae (Caprifoliaceae s.l.) | **Volume:** I — Plant-Based | **Entry:** I-497

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted Name:** *Dipsacus fullonum* L. (1753); also *Dipsacus sylvestris* Huds.
**Common Names:** Wild teasel, fuller's teasel, teasel, Venus' basin, card thistle, brushes and combs
**Synonyms:** *Dipsacus sylvestris* Huds.; *Dipsacus fullonum* var. *sylvestris* (Huds.) Rouy & E.G.Camus; *Dipsacus fullonum* var. *strigosus* Wimm. & Grab.

**Etymology:** *Dipsacus* from Greek *dipsa* (thirst) — the leaf bases clasp the stem and hold rainwater, forming a natural water-collecting "cup" called the "Venus' basin"; this water was used in folk medicine for eye conditions (applied topically); *fullonum* from Latin *fullones* (fullers — cloth workers who used the dried seed heads to raise the nap on woolen cloth); "teasel" from the verb "to tease" (to card or comb fibers); "Fuller's teasel" = the cultivated variety used in textile processing

**Botanical Description:** Biennial herb, 70–200 cm; stem prickly, erect, branching in second year; leaves connate-perfoliate (united at base around stem, forming water-collecting cups); basal leaves large, oblong-lanceolate, wrinkled; stem leaves prickly on midrib; inflorescence: large ovoid to cylindrical head, 5–10 cm, with stiff spiny bracts longer than the florets; flowers small, lilac-purple, opening in a distinctive band that progresses both upward and downward from the center of the head simultaneously (unusual botany); bloom July–September; second-year plant is dramatically architectural in winter as dried seed heads persist on rigid stems

**Distribution:** Native to Europe, western Asia, North Africa; naturalized throughout North America, Australia, southern South America; common in disturbed habitats, roadsides, stream margins, chalk grasslands; often found near old mills and wool-processing sites (historical association with fulling industry)

**Plant Part Used:** Root (primary medicinal use); leaves (traditional topical); dried seed heads (historical textile use — non-medicinal)

**Adulterants/Substitutions:** *Dipsacus sativus* (fuller's teasel; cultivated with distinctly hooked bracts; not medicinal); *Dipsacus japonicus* (Japanese teasel; source of the TCM equivalent *xū duàn* 续断; distinct but closely related pharmacology)

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

### The Bone-Healer Across Two Independent Traditions

Teasel's most striking pharmacological claim — that it promotes bone and connective tissue healing — is supported by two entirely independent traditional observations separated by geography, language, and pharmacological framework. The Chinese designation 续断 (*xù duàn*, "reconnecting the broken") explicitly encodes the bone-repair application in the name itself. The Chinese tradition uses teasel root for bone fracture healing, joint pain, kidney toning (in the TCM framework where the kidney governs bone), uterine bleeding, and threatened miscarriage (again, kidney-qi governing fetal development in TCM).

The European tradition, beginning with Dioscorides, records more modest and primarily topical applications — warts and skin fissures — without the dramatic bone-healing claim. However, European folk herbalism (not the formal pharmacopoeia) accumulated references to teasel for joint pain, arthritis, and — in the 20th century — for what practitioners described as Lyme disease-associated joint and musculoskeletal symptoms.

### The Lyme Disease Controversy

In the early 2000s, herbalist Stephen Harrod Buhner's protocols for Lyme disease (*Lyme Disease: Alternative Therapies and Drug Treatments*, 2005) prominently featured teasel root (*Dipsacus* spp.) as a primary herb for spirochete-associated musculoskeletal symptoms. This triggered significant interest in teasel root in the herbal medicine community but no controlled clinical trials. The pharmacological rationale offered was anti-spirochetal activity from spennoside and related iridoids — a hypothesis supported by very limited in vitro data.

The pharmacological evaluation: spennoside (teasel's primary iridoid) has demonstrated modest anti-*Borrelia burgdorferi* activity in vitro (Shi et al. 2018, similar studies), but the concentrations required exceed what achievable in human tissue from herbal dosing. The anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory iridoid activity is real and may contribute to symptom relief in inflammatory arthropathy regardless of etiology. The specific anti-Lyme claim requires substantially stronger clinical evidence than currently exists. This case study illustrates the intersection of genuine pharmacological activity (iridoid anti-inflammatory), extrapolated specific claims (anti-Borrelia), and the clinical need that drives adoption of unvalidated herbal protocols.

### Venus' Basin — Water Pharmacognosy

The water-collecting leaf axils of *D. fullonum* — which gave the plant its genus name (*dipsa* = thirst) and the folk name "Venus' basin" — were historically used as a source of clean standing water for topical applications, particularly to the skin and eyes. The antibacterial properties of the teasel's own compounds leach into this naturally collected water, creating what amounts to a self-prepared antimicrobial botanical solution — a practical integration of plant morphology and pharmacological utility that folk medicine exploited without conceptual framework for the mechanism.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

### Iridoid Glycosides (Primary Bioactive Fraction)
**Spennoside:** major iridoid glycoside; anti-inflammatory; modest antimicrobial in vitro; the primary pharmacologically characterized constituent of *D. fullonum*

**Loganin:** iridoid precursor; also found in vervain (I-496); shared Lamiales/Dipsacales iridoid biosynthetic pathway

**Morroniside:** iridoid glycoside; hepatoprotective; found in Cornelian cherry (*Cornus mas*) — cross-order iridoid sharing

**Sweroside:** secoiridoid; bitter; anti-inflammatory; connects to gentian and related bitter iridoid tradition

**Cantleyoside, sylvestroside:** iridoid glycosides specific to *Dipsacus* spp.; minor

### Phenolic Fraction
**Chlorogenic acid:** hydroxycinnamic acid antioxidant; anti-inflammatory

**Caffeic acid:** antioxidant; antimicrobial

**3,5-di-O-caffeoylquinic acid (cynarin-related):** dicaffeoylquinic acid; choleretic; anti-inflammatory; shared structural class with artichoke's active constituents

**Kaempferol, quercetin glycosides:** flavonol antioxidants; anti-inflammatory; capillary-protective

**Luteolin glycosides:** flavone; anti-inflammatory; mild GABA-A modulatory

### Triterpenoids / Phytosterols
**Ursolic acid, oleanolic acid:** triterpene anti-inflammatories; recurring across Lamiales/Dipsacales entries

**Hederagenin:** triterpenoid saponin aglycone; anti-inflammatory; antiparasitic in animal models

**β-Sitosterol, daucosterol:** phytosterols; anti-inflammatory; cholesterol-modulating

### Alkaloids
**Stachydrine homologs (minor):** betaine alkaloid class; connects to Lamiaceae alkaloid pattern despite being in Dipsacaceae

### Polysaccharides
**Inulin (minor fraction):** prebiotics; present at modest levels (<10% vs elecampane's 44%); less pharmacologically significant than in Asteraceae entries

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Anti-Inflammatory / Immunomodulatory Mechanism

**Spennoside + iridoid complex → NF-κB inhibition:**
Teasel's iridoid complex demonstrates anti-inflammatory activity through NF-κB pathway suppression; reduces production of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and PGE₂; relevant to both the joint pain applications and the proposed Lyme-associated musculoskeletal symptom management

**Caffeic acid derivatives → COX-2 inhibition:**
Dicaffeoylquinic acids provide additional anti-inflammatory activity through COX-2 inhibition; these compounds' mechanism is similar to artichoke leaf's primary mechanism

### 5.2 Bone / Connective Tissue Mechanism

**Spennoside → osteoblast stimulation:**
Zhang et al. 2012 (*Molecules* 17(2):1486-1495): spennoside and related iridoids from *D. asperoides* stimulate osteoblast differentiation and proliferation in cell culture; increase alkaline phosphatase activity (marker of osteoblast function); reduce RANKL expression (marker of osteoclast activation); net effect: shift bone remodeling balance toward formation over resorption — directly relevant to the *xù duàn* bone-healing application

**Collagen synthesis stimulation:** iridoid-mediated TGF-β pathway activation promotes collagen type I synthesis by fibroblasts and osteoblasts; relevant to both bone matrix formation and tendon/ligament connective tissue repair

**Clinical validation in TCM:** Combination formulas containing *D. asperoides* are used in Chinese orthopedic medicine with extensive clinical tradition; the "bone-healing" effect is considered well-established in Chinese clinical practice even without Western RCT validation

### 5.3 Mild Antimicrobial Mechanism

**Spennoside → anti-*Borrelia* activity (in vitro, limited):**
In vitro studies showing modest activity against *Borrelia burgdorferi* at concentrations exceeding typical plasma levels from oral dosing; not clinically validated; anti-inflammatory mechanism more likely to explain symptom improvement in Lyme-associated arthritis than direct spirochetal killing

**Broad iridoid antimicrobial:** iridoids as a class demonstrate activity against various bacteria and fungi through membrane disruption and protein synthesis interference; concentrations achievable in vivo from herbal dosing provide mild antimicrobial activity relevant to topical applications

### 5.4 Hepatoprotective Mechanism

**Morroniside + chlorogenic acid → liver protection:**
Morroniside demonstrates hepatoprotective activity in CCl₄ animal models; chlorogenic acid antioxidant activity reduces hepatic oxidative stress; relevant to traditional liver applications in multiple traditions

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### 6.1 Joint Pain / Musculoskeletal

**Evidence Level: B-C (TCM tradition + pre-clinical; no Western RCTs)**

- Extensive Chinese clinical tradition for *xù duàn* (*D. asperoides*) in orthopedic conditions; multiple Chinese case series and observational studies; Level B-C
- Zhang 2012: osteoblast stimulation in vitro (Level D)
- No randomized controlled trials in Western literature for *D. fullonum* or *D. asperoides* for any musculoskeletal indication
- Buhner 2005 Lyme disease protocols: practitioner case series only; Level C-D

### 6.2 Bone Fracture (TCM — *Xù Duàn*)

**Evidence Level: B (TCM clinical evidence + pre-clinical)**

- Chinese Pharmacopoeia 2020: *Xù duàn* official monograph for trauma, bone fracture, joint pain, kidney deficiency patterns, uterine bleeding
- Multiple Chinese clinical studies (non-randomized) showing accelerated fracture healing with *Dipsacus* combination formulas
- Osteoblast mechanism provides Level D mechanistic support; clinical evidence Level B (TCM standard)

### 6.3 Regulatory Status

- **Commission E (Germany):** NOT monographed
- **ESCOP:** NOT monographed
- **EMA:** No herbal monograph
- **Chinese Pharmacopoeia 2020:** 续断 (*xù duàn*) official monograph — primary source of regulatory validation
- **Traditional Herbal Medicines Directory (UK):** Not included

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

### 7.1 The Textile Pharmacist

The use of *Dipsacus fullonum* in textile processing — the hooked bracts of the dried seed head were used to raise the nap on woolen cloth in a process called "fulling" — creates a rare case in which a pharmacologically active plant was domesticated primarily for an industrial rather than medicinal application. The textile industry's cultivation of teasel throughout medieval Europe (there are documented teasel fields in Somerset, England, from the 10th century onward) maintained large populations of the plant in proximity to human habitation, simultaneously keeping the medicinal tradition alive.

The textile application also explains why *Dipsacus* appears near wool mills, fulling mills, and cloth-processing centers throughout European geography — the plant's distribution is partly a map of the medieval textile economy. Natural teasel heads were used for raising woolen nap well into the 20th century; some Scottish tweed mills maintained teasel cultivation as late as the 1970s because no synthetic brush matched the performance of the natural hooked bracts for high-quality tweed finishing.

### 7.2 "Reconnecting the Broken" — The Chinese Name as Clinical Protocol

The Chinese name 续断 (*xù duàn*, "reconnecting what is broken/severed") is among the most direct and unambiguous pharmacological name-encodings in the Codex. The name describes both the clinical application (bone fracture repair) and the mechanism (reconnection of structural integrity). In Chinese orthopedic medicine, 续断 is used in combination formulas for fracture healing, torn tendons and ligaments, and joint degeneration — the entire spectrum of structural connective tissue pathology is encompassed by the single name "reconnect the broken." This specificity of the Chinese application — compared to the more general topical uses recorded by Dioscorides — suggests that Chinese medical practitioners conducted more extensive and systematic clinical observation of teasel's effects on the musculoskeletal system.

### 7.3 The Lyme Disease Window

The use of teasel in Lyme disease protocols in North American herbal medicine represents an instructive case study in how traditional plants acquire new applications in contemporary disease contexts. The pathway was: (1) traditional European topical wound/skin use; (2) TCM joint-healing tradition; (3) Western herbalist (Buhner) applies TCM-derived joint application to Lyme arthritis; (4) internet dissemination of protocols without clinical validation; (5) widespread use in Lyme patient communities. The genuine anti-inflammatory activity (iridoid NF-κB inhibition) likely provides real symptom relief for inflammatory arthropathy, regardless of whether Borrelia-specific killing occurs. The clinical validation gap — a real intervention with real symptom benefit but unknown mechanism specificity — illustrates the most common situation in herbal medicine.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Indications (Evidence-Based, Within Safety Parameters)

**Joint Pain / Musculoskeletal Inflammation (TCM-derived):**
Teasel root decoction or tincture; Level B-C evidence (TCM tradition); appropriate as supportive measure for joint pain, arthritis, musculoskeletal injury; not primary treatment; evidence strongest for TCM combination formulas rather than mono-herb

**Bone Fracture Support (TCM — Adjunctive):**
*Xù duàn* combination formulas per TCM practitioner guidance; Level B (TCM clinical evidence); adjunctive to conventional orthopedic management; osteoblast-stimulating mechanism supports use; not a substitute for fracture immobilization/surgical management

**Topical Wound Care:**
Traditional herb or the "Venus' basin" water for minor skin conditions; Level C; safe topical application

### 8.2 Preparations and Dosing

**Root Decoction:**
- 6–15 g dried teasel root in 400 mL water; simmer 20 minutes; divide into 2–3 doses daily (TCM Pharmacopoeia dose for *xù duàn*)
- Continuous use for orthopedic applications: 4–12 weeks minimum

**Tincture:**
- 1:5 in 50% ethanol; 2–4 mL three times daily
- Buhner Lyme protocol: 1–2 droppers teasel root tincture in water 1–2 times daily; titrate gradually; this represents practitioner-clinical guidance, not RCT-based dosing

**Combination TCM Formula:**
- *Xu duan* in combination with *Du zhong* (*Eucommia ulmoides*) and *Niu xi* (*Achyranthes bidentata*) for bone-joint conditions per TCM practitioner guidance

### 8.3 Contraindications and Safety

**CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- **Pregnancy:** Uterotonic action documented in TCM tradition for 续断; ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION throughout pregnancy
- **Active bleeding disorders:** TCM application includes stopping uterine bleeding (blood-stagnation clearing then astringing); effects on coagulation not fully characterized; monitor with anticoagulants

**SAFETY:** Generally well-tolerated; limited adverse event reporting; no established serious toxicity; TCM safety profile across centuries without significant toxicity documentation

**DRUG INTERACTIONS:**
- Anticoagulants: theoretical; monitor PT/INR
- NSAIDs: theoretical additive anti-inflammatory; generally safe combination

### 8.4 Energetics

**Energetic Tier:** ◈ Jing (structural/constitutional) + ◉ Qi
**Thermal Quality:** Warm, slightly bitter (Galenic); tonifying
**Directional Action:** Consolidating, reconnecting (connective tissue); descending (kidney toning)
**TCM Classification:** Bitter, pungent, slightly warm; liver and kidney channels; tonifies liver and kidney, strengthens tendons and bones, stops bleeding, calms the fetus

### 8.5 Quality and Authentication

**Botanical Authentication:**
- *D. fullonum*: prickly stem + connate leaf axils (water-collecting cups) + large ovoid-cylindrical flower heads with stiff spiny bracts
- TCM market: *xù duàn* (*D. asperoides*): cylindrical taproot, brown-black externally, grey-green internally; characteristic odor and slightly bitter taste; HPLC for spennoside content
- Distinguish from *D. sativus* (hooked bracts; fuller's teasel; not medicinal standard)
