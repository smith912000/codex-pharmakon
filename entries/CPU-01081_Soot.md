# Soot — Asbole (as printed in Dioscorides 5-182) / Soot-from-the-bet'a-pot (Bryan's Ebers) / Agara-dhuma, Griha-dhuma, "soot of a house" (Sushruta, Bhishagratna's English)
## *Soot, the black deposit of burnt wood, resin, pitch, oil or lamp flame: soot from glass-making (Dioscorides 5-182), soot of a room or house (Sushruta), and the Egyptian "Soot-from-the-bet'a-pot", Bryan's English* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The English text on disk is C. P. Bryan's 1930 *The Papyrus Ebers*, a translation with summaries (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`); the Egyptian words are not printed. The names row is `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` line 35: "Soot-from-the-bet'a-pot", "Bryan 1930, Ch. V Mineral Remedies (line 1205)", "Bryan's English identification". Bryan's Chapter V introduction says of the mineral remedies: "Outside of these the source of origin of the drugs is strictly laid down, or various preparations made from the parent drug are prescribed. Thus, the Iron must be Iron-from-Apollonopolis-parva; the Leather must be Leather-from-the-Sandal-maker; the Soot must be Soot-from-the-bet'a-pot; the Soda must be Soda-from-Upper-Egypt" (line 1137), and the same form heads his alphabetical list (line 1205). The one prescription found by searching the text for "soot" is in his summary of the salves for the "woodlike uha-disease": "An ointment of Hippopotamus Oil and Honey would perhaps be more sticky than picturesque. Another of Soot-from-the-bet'a-Pot with Honey and Sweet Myrrh would seem to be not only sticky but dirty" (line 3293). That is Bryan's paraphrase and comment, and the papyrus text, the column and the quantities were not read. [USER: verify] What a "bet'a pot" was is not stated in the lines read.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides treats soot in several chapters, and the spine's locus is the one at the end of Book Five. *De Materia Medica* 5-182, *Asbole*, "Soot from glass-making" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10772-10774; `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 907): "Fuligio pictoria [soot] that the painters use is taken from the glassmakers, as that is the best. It is putrifying and sharp, and with a waxy ointment of rosaceum [1-53] it heals fractures." The next chapter, 5-183 *Melan*, "Black Ink" (lines 10775-10777): "The ink with which we write is prepared from the soot gathered from torches, and to each ounce of gum is mixed three ounces of soot. It is also made from the soot of rosin, and the previously mentioned soot pictoria. Mix one pound of soot pictoria, half a pound of gum, half an ounce of bulls' glue, and half an ounce of chalcanthum [blue vitriol or copper sulphate]. It is good included in antiseptic medicines; and for burns it is rubbed on thickly with water and left alone until it forms new skins, for it falls off of its own accord when the sores have healed." Four chapters of Book One on the gums and resins describe how soot is made from them. 1-84 *Libanou Aithalie*, "Soot of Frankincense" (lines 4184-4190): a grain of frankincense is set alight on a lamp inside a ceramic jar covered with a perforated brass jar whose outside is kept moist with a sponge in cold water, "for all the soot adheres faster to it when it is not too hot"; "It has the ability to soothe inflammation of the eyes, repress discharges, clean ulcers, fill hollow sores, and repress diseases of the cornea." 1-85 *Lignuos Skeuasia* (lines 4191-4193): soot is made "in the same way" from myrrh, rosin and styrax, and "You can produce soot in the same way from other resins." 1-93 *Lignuos Retines* (lines 4246-4254) ends: "Soot is also taken from resins as well as thus [1-81] which is good mixed with medicines to make the eyelids pleasing, for gnawed-around eye corners, for the membranes of the eyelids, and for weeping eyes. Ink with which to write is also made from it." And 1-96 *Lignuos Ugras Pisses* (lines 4265-4267): "Soot is made from moist pitch. Light a new lamp, put a portion of pitch into it and cover the lamp with a new ceramic jar ... It is sharp and astringent and is used in medicines to make the eyelids pleasing, for rubbing, and when hair must be restored to eyelids that are filled with excessive watery fluids. It is good for weak, weeping, ulcerated eyes."

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The spine's locus is the English glossary in Bhishagratna's third volume, `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt` Appendix 3 (the entry "Agairadhama", paired by the OCR with ".. Soot." at line 31135; `Sources/SS_Sushruta/extract/names.csv` line 347). The OCR spelling is damaged; the plain forms in the text are *Agara-dhuma*, *Griha-dhuma*, "soot of a room" or "of a house". The text loci read are these. *Chikitsasthana* ch.9, on the treatment of cutaneous affections (*Kushtha*) (`SS_Chikitsa_ch09.txt` line 202): a mixture of *Svarjika*, sulphate of copper, sulphate of iron, *Vidanga*, "Agara-dhuma", *Chitraka*, *Katuka*, *Sudha*, turmeric and *Saindhava* "pounded together with the urine or bile of a cow should be applied to the diseased localities". Ch.18, on tumours (`SS_Chikitsa_ch18.txt` line 431): after a fat-originated tumour (*Medoja Arvuda*) is incised and sutured, it is plastered over with a compound of *Haridra*, "Griha-dhuma (soot of a room)", *Rodhra*, *Pattanga*, *Manahshila* and *Haritala* pounded together and mixed with honey. Ch.22, on affections of the mouth (`SS_Chikitsa_ch22.txt` line 404): "In the Kaphaja type of Rohini, the affected part should be rubbed with Katuka and Agara-dhuma (soot of a house — chimney-soot)." Ch.37, on the *Anuvasana* and *Uttara* enemas (`SS_Chikitsa_ch37.txt` lines 743-744): "a stick (Varti) made up of the pendant soot of a room (Agara-dhuma), Vrihati, Pippali, Madana fruit, Saindhava salt and Shunthi pasted with Shukta ... and cow's urine". *Kalpasthana* ch.8, on insect bites (`SS_Kalpa_ch08.txt` lines 293-295): "An anti-venomous compound (Agada) consisting of Kushtha, Chakra (Tagara), Vacha, Vilva roots, Patha, Suvarchika, house-soot and the two kinds of Haridra is efficacious in the case of a bite by a Trikantaka insect. An Agada consisting of house-soot, ..., Chakra, Kushtha and the seeds of Palasha destroys the poison of a Gala-goli insect." *Kalpasthana* ch.7 (`SS_Kalpa_ch07.txt` line 23) lists "Grihadhuma (soot of a room)" in a powder compound in the chapter on the medicated drum. *Uttaratantra* ch.14 (`SS_Uttara_ch14.txt` line 47) has a "warm collyrium made with the lamp-black collected from a burning lamp flame". Whether the lamp-black of that passage is the same material as the soot of a room is not stated. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Soot is mostly fine amorphous carbon from incomplete combustion, with tarry organic compounds and mineral ash that depend on what was burnt. Soot from wood, resin or pitch carries polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, some of which are carcinogens; chimney soot has long been associated with scrotal cancer in sweeps (general facts). The sources name no active component and give no quantities.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The texts record soot as a pigment and ink, as an eye ingredient (resin and pitch soots), as an external ingredient for fractures, burns and tumours, and, in Sushruta, as an ingredient in pastes for skin disease, mouth disease, insect-bite antidotes and an enema plug. The Codex has no protocol for any of this and offers none. Soot is a combustion residue with carcinogenic components; putting it into the eye, into wounds, into the bladder or the rectum risks serious injury and infection. The honest use today is none.

### Preparation & Dosing

Greek: soot gathered from a lamp-flame under a cooled lid (frankincense, myrrh, rosin, styrax, other resins, pitch); soot from torches or from glassmakers' furnaces ground with gum into ink (5-183); applied with rosaceum wax ointment on fractures (5-182); rubbed thickly with water on burns (5-183). Ayurvedic: pounded with other drugs in cow's urine or bile (ch.9), in honey (ch.18), rubbed on the part with *Katuka* (ch.22), pasted into a stick with *Shukta* and cow's urine (ch.37), in an *Agada* (*Kalpa* ch.8). Egyptian: with honey and sweet myrrh as a salve (Bryan's summary, line 3293). Only quantities for ink in Dioscorides are given ("to each ounce of gum ... three ounces of soot"). No dose is given for any medical use in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Carbon is inert and not absorbed, and carbon black is a standard ink pigment; "lamp-black" has been used as a pigment and kohl base. The soot of burnt organic material, particularly of resins, pitch and wood, contains polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (some carcinogenic) and is a skin and respiratory irritant, and chimney soot was the first occupational cause of cancer recorded. Activated carbon, a specially prepared form, adsorbs some poisons; ordinary soot is not activated carbon. Nothing read ties soot to a healing effect on fractures, burns, tumours, skin disease or eyes. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Soot is named as an ingredient for a complaint, in Bryan's summary of a salve for the "uha-disease". Nothing says what it does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. The Ebers material is indication-organised: the identity of each named substance is a modern hypothesis, and these forms are Bryan's. The one note on soot is a rule of provenance ("the Soot must be Soot-from-the-bet'a-pot"), which is a matter of choosing the right source for a drug, not of cultivation. Bryan reports no recitation with the soot salve; since he summarises, that is not proof that none existed. No deity is attributed. [USER: verify]

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. The chapters offer a craft of attention: which source gives the best soot (the glassmakers'), how to make it clean under a moist, cooled lid, which proportion goes into ink, and which soots he calls sharp, astringent or putrefying. The chapters are therapeutic and technical; none assigns soot a cultivation status.

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Soot of a house appears in the passages read in plasters, pastes, a plug and antidotes, in chapters on skin disease, tumours, mouth disease, enemas and insect bites. It is not named in a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* chapter in anything read here, and no *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* claim is made for it. *Shodhana* is not applied to it in the passages read. The sattvic, rajasic and tamasic labels are *Samkhya*, not Sushruta. [USER: verify] whether the *rasayana* chapters name it.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do. Soot earns its place in the Codex as a plain household and craft material that three traditions used in plasters, salves, ink and antidotes. None gives it a cultivation role, and its modern correlates are warnings. A cultivator can take from it the care with which Dioscorides made and sourced it, and leave it out of the body.

### Contraindications

- **Carcinogens and irritants.** Soot from wood, resin, pitch and chimneys contains polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and irritant particles; avoid skin contact, ingestion and breathing it in.
- **Eyes.** Soot in the eye is an irritant and a source of infection; traditional eye cosmetics and salves made from soot are not advised.
- **Wounds, burns and fractures.** Applying soot to burns or broken skin risks infection (tetanus included) and pigment tattooing; modern wound care is safer.
- **Enema and bladder use.** The Sushruta plug and enema are not advised.
- **Pregnancy and children.** No safety data from the sources read. [USER: verify]
- **Dose unknown.** No dose is given for any use in the sources read.
