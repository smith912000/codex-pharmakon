# Melilotus suaveolens — rgya.spos, Gyapoe (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Melilotus suaveolens ldb. (as Clark 1995 prints it), the first of two identifications Clark gives for rgya.spos, after which come Melilotus parviflora; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary sets Melilotus officinalis L. against Gyapoe by column position* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and on the first of two names in one line of one of them.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. Only Clark prints *Melilotus suaveolens* (line 1070 and the Latin-name index, line 1614). A search of Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 for "suaveolens" found nothing. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk for *Melilotus suaveolens* found nothing; for the epithet *suaveolens* alone it found only Dioscorides' translator's gloss *Tordylium suaveolens* (line 7098) and a Sushruta appendix line printed "Stereopermiim Suaveolens" (`Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`, line 32562), both different plants. The genus *Melilotus* occurs in Dioscorides in the *melilotos* chapter and its glosses (`Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 6907 to 6945, naming *Melilotus officinalis*, not *suaveolens*), which are another drug in another text and are not read as a mention of this one. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 169) the Tibetan name *rgya.spos* is recorded at Clark line 1070 with the identification "Melilotus suaveolens ldb.; Melilotus parviflora" and the note "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The key, *Melilotus suaveolens*, is therefore the **first** of the two binomials Clark prints, and not a later one. The second, *Melilotus parviflora*, has no row on the spine as far as the files read show. A separate row, *Melilotus officinalis* (names.csv line 285, from the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary line 5316), belongs to the same Tibetan name by position and is the spine's I_601 (see Overlap). Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance".

**Identity doubt.** The two editions name two different species of one genus: Clark's *Melilotus suaveolens* (and, second, *Melilotus parviflora*), the glossary's *Melilotus officinalis* L. The Codex cannot say which the Tibetan texts meant, or whether the Tibetan name covers more than one, and the entry stands for the Tibetan drug. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (lines 1070 and 1071).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry follows *brag.spos* (*Lepisorus waltonii*, line 1069; see I_1309) and is followed by *spangs.spos* (line 1071). It reads:

"rgya.spos: Melilotus suaveolens ldb.: Melilotus parviflora: [has ten names. It has long, thin petioles, yellow flower, sweet fragrance, bitter taste and cool power.]"

and the next line continues:

"spangs.spos: Indian Spikenard or Muskroot: Nardostachys jatamansi D.C.: [has twenty-five names and grows in shady meadows. It has a brown stalk, turquoise leaf and extremely fragrant red flower]. Both Melilotus and spikenard cure chronic fever and fever caused by poisoning [and dispel spirits]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The whole of line 1070 after the identifications is inside the brackets, that is, in the commentary layer: the ten names, the long thin petioles, the yellow flower, the sweet fragrance, the bitter taste and the cool power. The line therefore gives this drug no action of its own. The action sits in the next line, which treats the two drugs together: "Both Melilotus and spikenard cure chronic fever and fever caused by poisoning", with "and dispel spirits" in brackets. The word "Melilotus" in that sentence is Clark's. The Tibetan name, the two binomials (Clark's identifications, not the root text's) and the shared sentence are outside the brackets. The lines give no part used and no preparation. Clark's name index lists *rgya.spos* (line 1683).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428). Line 3448 reads, in a run of short sentences: "Gyapoe and Pangpoe cure chronic hot disorder and hot disorder associated with poisoning." The index lists "Gyapoe 209" (line 7382). The pairing of "Gyapoe" with "Pangpoe" follows Clark's pairing of the melilot with the spikenard: the glossary's "Pangpoe" is printed with the transliteration "spang spos" and "Nardostachys jatamansi" in consecutive lines (5586, 5588 and 5590), which is Clark's *spangs.spos*. The two editions therefore say the same thing of the same two drugs: chronic fever (Clark) or chronic hot disorder (Men-Tsee-Khang), and fever caused by poisoning or hot disorder associated with poisoning, in the same pairing. The words differ ("fever", "hot disorder") and the Codex does not treat them as one term. Clark's commentary adds "and dispel spirits"; Men-Tsee-Khang's sentence does not.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "G" block, lines 5303 to 5323).* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate runs. Eight phonetic names are printed (lines 5303 to 5306): "Gugul", "Gundrum", "Gurkum", "Guthup", "Gyakhur", "Gyapoe", "Gyasen", "Gyashuk". The transliterations (lines 5308 to 5311) include "rgya spos" (line 5309), the spelling of Clark's *rgya.spos*. The scientific names (lines 5313 to 5317) are, in order, *Commiphora mukul*, *Vitis vinifera*, *Carthamus tinctorius*, then on one line "Peucedanum praeruptorum Picris hieracioides Melilotus officinalis L." (5316), and "Dolichos bijlorus Thysanolaena maxima" (5317). The English names (lines 5319 to 5323) are "Guggul", "Grape", "Saffron", "Hog's funnel", "Hawkweed", "Melilot", "Chinese broad bean" and "Tiger grass". Counted in order, "Gyapoe" is the sixth phonetic name, *Melilotus officinalis* L. the sixth scientific name and "Melilot" the sixth English name. The count is consistent at the points where the English names allow a check: "Gugul" is first against *Commiphora mukul* and "Guggul", "Gundrum" second against *Vitis vinifera* and "Grape", "Gurkum" third against *Carthamus tinctorius* and "Saffron". The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position. [USER: verify] The glossary prints no *Melilotus suaveolens*.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Melilotus suaveolens*, *rgya.spos*, "Gyapoe" and "Pangpoe" found no entry on this plant or passage. I_601 (Yellow sweet clover, *Melilotus officinalis*, tags AT, CM, DMM) is the spine's entry for the glossary's binomial and carries no FMT tag; the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence (line 3448) and the glossary line (5316) are a candidate tag addition for it, not applied here. I_1212 (*Trifolium melilotus-coerulea*, Dioscorides) and I_661 (Red clover) are other plants. I_1309 (*Lepisorus waltonii*) treats the preceding Clark entry, *brag.spos* (line 1069). The spikenard half of the shared sentence (Clark line 1071) falls in the entries for *Nardostachys jatamansi* (I_137, tags EP, DMM, CM, SS, FMT, HEB) and I_1288 (*Gentiana jatamansi*, by position at "Pangpoe"). Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Melilotus* is a genus of the legume family (Fabaceae), whose sweet-clover species are known for the fragrance of coumarin and, in spoiled hay, for dicoumarol, an anticoagulant formed from it. Whether either applies to the plant behind the Tibetan name is not shown by the lines read: the identity is uncertain between two or three species, and Clark's commentary says only "sweet fragrance, bitter taste and cool power". No further statement about this drug's constituents is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that, with spikenard, "cure[s] chronic fever and fever caused by poisoning" (Clark, line 1071) or "cure[s] chronic hot disorder and hot disorder associated with poisoning" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3448). These are the texts' claims in their own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and does not translate "chronic fever", "hot disorder" or "poisoning" into modern diagnoses. Poisoning and chronic fever need medical assessment.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 1070) prints "sweet fragrance, bitter taste and cool power". No post-digestive taste is printed. Men-Tsee-Khang prints no taste or power for the name in the lines read. The actions printed are curing chronic fever and fever caused by poisoning, together with spikenard, and, in the commentary, dispelling spirits (Clark); and curing chronic hot disorder and hot disorder associated with poisoning (Men-Tsee-Khang).

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does, and by the company it keeps: with spikenard it cures chronic fever and fever caused by poisoning, and its commentary adds that the pair "dispel spirits". The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the chapter 20 lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes, and not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23 in both editions); no line read places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice, and "dispel spirits" is the commentary's therapeutic claim and not a practice instruction. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name, not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a chronic-fever and poisoning drug, paired with spikenard, in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and the two editions name two different species. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Poisoning and chronic fever need medical assessment; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Sweet-clover hay that has spoiled can be hazardous to animals (a general fact, not from the sources read). Because the identity is uncertain, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
