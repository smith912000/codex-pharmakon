# I-397 | Catnip / Catmint
**Nepeta cataria** L. | Family: Lamiaceae

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted binomial:** *Nepeta cataria* L. (1753)
**Authority:** Linnaeus, *Species Plantarum*
**Family:** Lamiaceae (Labiatae)
**Tribe:** Mentheae

**Common names:**
- English: catnip, catmint, catswort, field balm, cat's-play
- French: cataire, herbe-aux-chats, menthe-des-chats, chataire
- German: Katzenminze, Katzenthymian
- Italian: erba gatta, nepeta (broadly)
- Spanish: nébeda, hierba gatera
- Dutch: kattenkruid
- Arabic: نعناع القطط (*naʿnāʿ al-qiṭaṭ*)
- Latin (classical): *cataria*, *herba cattaria* ("cat herb")

**Etymology:**
*Nepeta* from *Nepet* (ancient city in Etruria/central Italy) or from a Gaulish root meaning "bad-smelling plant." *Cataria* = "of cats" (Latin *cattus*). The cat-attracting property was documented by Roman writers and gives the plant its defining identity — the behavioral pharmacological effect on felids is one of the most studied interspecies psychoactive responses in ethnobotany.

**Botanical description:**
Soft, downy perennial herb, 40–100 cm. Stems erect, branched, quadrangular, densely grayish-white pubescent (woolly appearance). Leaves opposite, cordate-ovate, 3–6 cm, coarsely crenate-dentate, gray-green, strongly aromatic when bruised. Flowers white with purple spots, bilabiate, in dense terminal spike-like racemes. Calyx tube-shaped with 5 pointed teeth. Nutlets 4.

**The cat response — pharmacological significance:**
The nepetalactone-induced response in domestic cats (*Felis catus*) and other felids:
- Behavioral: rolling, rubbing face/chin on plant, vocalizing, salivating, apparent euphoria
- Duration: 5–15 minutes, then refractory period ~30 minutes (desensitization)
- Mechanism: nepetalactone binds olfactory receptors (not systemic — ineffective if nose blocked); mimics feline sex pheromones (felinine-derived compounds)
- Species specificity: domestic cats (~70% respond; genetic variation); tigers, lions, leopards also respond; domestic dogs do not
- Function in wild context: nepetalactone serves as insect repellent in the plant; feline rolling behavior distributes compound onto fur (unintentional insect repellent co-option)

**Related species (genus *Nepeta* contains ~250 species):**
- ***N. racemosa*** (catmint for gardens, often called catmint interchangeably with catnip): lower nepetalactone; ornamental; used similarly in folk medicine
- ***N. mussinii***: ornamental catmint; smaller; moderate nepetalactone
- ***N. grandiflora***: large catmint
- **Important disambiguation:** "Catmint" in European herbal medicine may refer to *N. cataria* OR ornamental *Nepeta* species; *N. cataria* is pharmacologically primary

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Primary cross-traditional anchor:** Nepetalactone is unique to *Nepeta* genus and is the pharmacologically defining compound for this entry. The pharmacological record is primarily within the European tradition.

**Convergence elements (European tradition internal):**
1. **Febrifuge:** Medieval European use for fever matches modern iridoid anti-pyretic mechanism
2. **Carminative/antispasmodic:** Roman and medieval use for GI complaints; smooth muscle relaxant mechanism confirmed
3. **Nervine sedative:** Consistent from medieval texts through Eclectic physicians to modern phytotherapy; nepetalactone mechanism confirmed
4. **Emmenagogue:** Consistent cross-European tradition claim; nepetalactone smooth muscle relaxant mechanism plausible

**Notable pharmacological paradox:**
Catnip is sedating and anxiolytic in humans but produces apparent excitation/euphoria in cats. The mechanism differs: in humans, nepetalactone is metabolized to compounds with sedative (GABA-enhancing) properties; in cats, the response is primarily olfactory (not systemic) and mimics pheromone-like stimulation. The same compound produces opposite behavioral outcomes in different species through different receptor systems — a pharmacological divergence with no equivalent among commonly used herbs.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Iridoids (primary pharmacological class):**
- **Nepetalactone** (α-nepetalactone + β-nepetalactone): cyclopentanoid monoterpene iridoid; **defining compound**; both isomers present but α-nepetalactone predominates in European *N. cataria* (60–90% of volatile oil); β-nepetalactone predominates in some geographic populations; responsible for cat behavioral response and human sedative/anxiolytic effects
- **Nepetalic acid**: oxidation product of nepetalactone; insect repellent
- **Cis-trans-nepetalactone**: geometric isomers with different biological activities
- **7-Deoxyloganin**, **loganin**: iridoid glucosides; anti-inflammatory
- **8-Acetyl-harpagide**: iridoid with anti-inflammatory, anti-pyretic properties
- **Acteoside** (verbascoside): phenylethanoid glycoside; anti-inflammatory; analgesic

**Essential oil composition (0.3–1.0%):**
- Nepetalactone isomers (60–90% of volatile oil)
- **Nerol**: monoterpene alcohol; floral; antimicrobial
- **Geranyl acetate**: ester; antimicrobial
- **β-Caryophyllene** (3–8%): sesquiterpene; CB2 agonist; anti-inflammatory
- **Pulegone** (trace–variable): potentially hepatotoxic at high doses (main concern in pennyroyal; trace amounts in catnip)

**Phenolics:**
- **Rosmarinic acid** (0.5–1.5%): primary non-volatile antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; antiviral
- **Caffeic acid**, **ferulic acid**: hydroxycinnamic phenolics
- **Luteolin**, **apigenin**, **chrysoeriol**: flavone anti-inflammatories

**Terpenoids:**
- **Ursolic acid**, **oleanolic acid**: standard Lamiaceae triterpenoids

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Sedative/anxiolytic mechanisms:**
1. **Nepetalactone GABA-A modulation:** Nepetalactone and its metabolites (nepetalic acid) modulate GABA-A receptors; demonstrated sedative effects in mouse models (increased sleep duration, reduced spontaneous movement) at 40 mg/kg. Mechanism involves increased GABA-A receptor sensitivity — partial benzodiazepine-site agonism proposed.
2. **Adenosine receptor interaction:** Nepetalactone metabolites may interact with adenosine A1 receptors — promotes sleep onset; contributes to sedative effect.
3. **Opioid receptor (minor):** Preliminary data suggests weak μ-opioid receptor partial agonism contributing to anxiolytic effect; not well-established.

**Anti-pyretic/anti-inflammatory mechanisms:**
1. **COX inhibition:** Rosmarinic acid, acteoside, and iridoids collectively inhibit COX-1 and COX-2; reduces prostaglandin synthesis → anti-pyretic, anti-inflammatory effects consistent with traditional use for fever
2. **NF-κB suppression:** Rosmarinic acid inhibits NF-κB translocation; reduces pro-inflammatory cytokine release
3. **IL-1β, TNF-α reduction:** Demonstrated in LPS-stimulated macrophages

**Antispasmodic mechanisms:**
1. **Smooth muscle relaxation:** Nepetalactone and essential oil components relax GI smooth muscle via calcium channel antagonism; reduces intestinal spasms; explains traditional carminative use
2. **Bronchospasmolytic:** Similar smooth muscle relaxant effect on bronchial tissue; mild bronchodilatory action

**Insect repellent mechanism:**
1. **TRPA1 activation:** Nepetalactone activates TRPA1 channels in insects (mosquitoes, cockroaches, flies); TRPA1 is an aversive pain channel — activation causes avoidance behavior. Critically, DEET also activates insect TRPA1 — different molecular structure but same receptor target.
2. **Potency vs DEET:** Nepetalactone more potent than DEET in laboratory repellency assays (Bernier et al. 2005, USDA study) — 10× more effective by weight against mosquitoes in some preparations; however, volatility is higher so protection duration shorter

**Emmenagogue mechanism:**
1. Smooth muscle relaxant + mild uterotonic (iridoid-mediated); combination may normalize menstrual irregularity; mechanism weaker than motherwort but similar in principle

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Level A — No published human RCTs for *N. cataria* specifically.**

**Level B — In vitro / animal / pilot:**
- **Insect repellency:** Multiple in vitro and controlled laboratory studies (Bernier 2005; Peterson et al. 2011) confirm superior mosquito repellency vs DEET in laboratory conditions; one field study (Peterson 2002) confirmed practical repellency; bioavailability limitation (volatility) reduces duration
- **Sedative:** Animal models consistently demonstrate sedation, anxiolysis, reduced spontaneous movement with nepetalactone; dose-dependent effect confirmed

**Level C — Traditional evidence:**
- **Infantile colic and feverish children:** The most consistent traditional application across European folk medicine; children's herb for fever, restlessness, colic — specifically noted as milder than adult herbs and safer for children
- **Carminative:** Consistent traditional use for GI spasm, flatulence, bloating — supported by smooth muscle mechanism
- **Menstrual promotion (emmenagogue):** Traditional claim; mechanistically plausible; not clinically tested
- **Headache:** Folk use for nervous headache (tension-type); GABA mechanism plausible; not clinically tested

**Pediatric safety basis:**
Catnip has an especially strong safety profile for children because:
1. No alkaloids, no glycosides with significant toxicity, no hepatotoxic compounds at typical doses
2. Centuries of consistent folk use in children for fever and colic
3. Nepetalactone (primary compound) rapidly metabolized; no accumulation
4. Historical name "field balm" reflects soft, gentle character vs stronger "balms" (lemon balm for adults)

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Roman documentation:**
*Pliny the Elder* (*Naturalis Historia* XIX and XX): describes *herba cattaria* as strongly attractive to cats while noting its medicinal uses for humans. The cat-attracting property was observed at least 2,000 years ago and became the primary identifying characteristic of the plant — a remarkable interspecies behavioral pharmacology documented in classical Roman natural history.

**Medieval English herbalism:**
*Macer Floridus* (*De Viribus Herbarum*, c. 1070 CE) is among the earliest medieval Latin herbals to discuss catnip under *cataria* — primarily for women's conditions, fever, and as a nervine. The women's health emphasis (fever in childbirth, menstrual regulation) is consistent throughout medieval European sources.

**Gerard's *Herball* (1597):**
John Gerard under "Nep" (*cataria*): "Nep or Catmint is of a strong smell... Catmint is good to be given to women who have their natural sickness: it procureth the menstrual flux... The juice thereof, taken with wine, is good against the biting of any venomous beast." The menstrual and antidote applications reflect consistent European tradition.

**Nicholas Culpeper (1653):**
*The English Physician*: "It is good for those that are troubled with cramps, convulsions, and hysteric fits; and for women that have fits of the mother [hysteria], and it will help them. The herb is also good against wind [flatulence] in the stomach and bowels... Taken with wine or the decoction, it eases those that have bruises from falls." Culpeper explicitly links catnip to the nervous system and women's conditions — the traditional pairing that persists into modern phytotherapy.

**19th century American Eclectics:**
The Eclectic physicians used catnip extensively as a children's herb. Ellingwood's *American Materia Medica*: "Catnip is mild in its action and can be safely given to infants. Its chief use is in simple fevers, colds, colic, and restlessness in children." The transition of catnip from European traditional herb to American child health staple reflects how British settlers carried their pharmacological knowledge to the New World, where catnip naturalized aggressively.

**The catnip-DEET connection — modern pharmacological resonance:**
The 2001 USDA discovery (Peterson et al.) that nepetalactone repels mosquitoes more effectively than DEET (at comparable concentrations) transformed catnip from a folk/pet curiosity into a serious insect repellent research subject. The biochemical basis — shared TRPA1 receptor activation — was established a decade later (Melo et al. 2021). This represents a case where folk observation (interspecies behavioral pharmacology) preceded modern molecular target identification by 2,000 years.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Therapeutic applications:**

*Children — fever, colic, and restlessness (Level C, strong traditional basis):*
- **Infusion:** 1–2 g dried herb in 150 mL hot water, steep 5–10 minutes, cool to safe temperature; give 50–100 mL every 3–4 hours for fever and restlessness in children over 6 months; sweeten with honey (not under 12 months — botulism risk); suitable for infants over 6 months when freshly prepared
- **Tincture (glycerite — alcohol-free):** For children avoiding alcohol preparations; 0.5–1 mL glycerite 3 times daily; milder than alcohol tincture
- Comparison to alternatives: Gentler than valerian (too sedating for infants); gentler than chamomile in anti-spasmodic strength; combined catnip-chamomile-fennel preparations traditional for infantile colic

*Adult nervine and anxiolytic (Level C):*
- **Infusion:** 2–4 g dried herb in 200 mL; 2–3 cups daily for anxiety, nervous tension, insomnia
- **Tincture (1:5, 25–40% ethanol):** 2–4 mL three times daily for adults
- Gentler than valerian; appropriate for mild anxiety, nervous restlessness without severe sleep disturbance; often combined with lemon balm (complementary GABA mechanisms)

*Insect repellent (Level B laboratory, practical limitations):*
- **Fresh catnip crush-and-apply:** Crushing fresh leaves and applying to skin provides 30–60 minutes protection (short duration due to volatility); viable natural repellent for brief outdoor activity
- **Essential oil (diluted):** 2–5% nepetalactone-standardized extract in carrier oil; provides 1–2 hour protection; must be reapplied frequently; less effective than DEET for extended protection; no known dermal toxicity at these concentrations
- **Comparison:** Laboratory data superior to DEET; field conditions less consistent (volatility, stability); appropriate for those seeking DEET-free option with repeated application acceptance

*Carminative/digestive (Level C):*
- Infusion 2–3 g per cup, taken after meals; reduces bloating, flatulence, intestinal spasm; gentler than stronger carminatives (peppermint, ginger) — appropriate for IBS with anxiety overlay

**Culinary integration:**
- Fresh young leaves mildly flavored; add to salads, herbal teas, summer drinks; mild minty-herbaceous flavor
- Dried catnip tea traditionally flavored with lemon and honey
- NOT the same as culinary catnip products sold for cats — human-grade standardized dried herb only

**Safety:**
- Well-documented safety profile; centuries of children's use supports
- **Pregnancy:** Traditional emmenagogue; avoid therapeutic doses in first trimester; culinary amounts acceptable
- **Sedation:** Mild sedative; caution with driving/machinery at higher doses; not significant at single cup infusion
- No documented toxicity at traditional doses; LD₅₀ very high in animal models
- **Pulegone concern:** Trace pulegone in some chemotypes; at typical infusion doses (<4 g dried herb/day) not clinically relevant; avoid high-dose essential oil internal use (pulegone hepatotoxic at very high doses; primary concern with pennyroyal essential oil, not catnip)

**Drug interactions:**
- **CNS depressants:** Mild additive sedation possible; generally not clinically significant at typical doses
- **Lithium:** Theoretical diuretic interaction (catnip mild diuretic) could affect lithium clearance; monitor if combining with psychiatric lithium therapy

**Dose references:**
- Children (6 months–2 years): 50–100 mL infusion (1 g/150 mL) per dose, up to 3 doses/day
- Children (2–12 years): 100–150 mL infusion (1.5 g/200 mL), 2–3 times/day
- Adults: 2–4 g dried herb as infusion; 2–4 mL tincture (1:5) three times daily
- Topical insect repellent: 2–5% essential oil in carrier; apply every 30–60 minutes
