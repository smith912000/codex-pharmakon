# Medicinal Leech / Shui Zhi — Jalauka, "leeches" (Bhishagratna, Sutrasthana ch.13) / Leech, 水蛭 Shuǐ Zhì (project Shennong list)
## *The leech, the blood-sucking aquatic worm used for bleeding; Bhishagratna names twelve Sanskrit kinds and gives no binomial, and the Shennong row prints none* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM, SBJ, SS

The blocking warning is in force: no tablet numbers or deities are given. The tag rests on the spine row alone. [USER: verify]

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides has no chapter on the leech, and the word "leech" occurs in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` only as a hazard or a bleeding to be treated. In 2-127 (the Greek bean) the bean "is applied to discharges of blood caused by leeches, shelled and divided in two parts as it grew. The cut half closely pressed on suppresses it" (line 5591). In 3-94 (silphion) it "Gargled with vinegar ... casts off horseleeches that stick to the throat" (line 7395). In 5-21 (vinegar), "Sipped up, it expels leeches that were taken in a drink" (line 9878); in 5-23 (brine), "Sipped up and gargled it kills leeches" (line 9884). The index lists "leeches" and "horse leeches" (lines 13525, 13679). The tag rests on these mentions. The spine row's Dioscorides attestation is thus of leeches as something to be expelled, not as a drug. [USER: verify]

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** No Avicenna locus was found. `_sources/avicenna.csv` has no leech row (its animal rows are coral, locust, Spanish fly and musk) and no Avicenna text is on disk. No *Canon* chapter reference exists to give. The attestation rests on the spine row. [USER: verify]

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`) lists "Animals: Superior Class", "Middle Class" and "Inferior Class" in its contents (lines 770-776), but the text layer is readable only to PDF p.104 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/README.md`), so no animal monograph, and no leech monograph, could be read. `_sources/shennong.csv` has the row "Leech (水蛭 Shuǐ Zhì)" (line 343, no further data). **Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *Sutrasthana* ch.13 is wholly about leeches: "the Chapter which treats of leeches and of how and which to use (Jalaukavacharaniyam)" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch13.txt` lines 3-6). Leeches are to be applied to a patient who is "old or imbecile, or a woman, or an infant, or a person of an extremely timid disposition, or a person of a delicate constitution", unfit for surgery, "since this mode of bleeding is the gentlest that can be possibly devised" (lines 8-13). Blood vitiated by *Vayu*, *Pittam* and *Kapham* is to be drawn respectively by horn, by leeches, and by gourd (lines 14-20); the text says leeches, being born in water, "are possessed of Madhura (sweet or soothing) properties" and are used for blood vitiated by bile (lines 28-31; the OCR of the horn sentence at line 23 is garbled and not used). There are "twelve distinct species of which six are venomous, and six non-venomous" (line 56). The six venomous are *Krishna*, *Karvura*, *Alagarda*, *Indrayudha*, *Samudrika* and *Gochandana*, each described by body and colour; their bite brings itching, swelling, fever, burning, retching, drowsiness and delirium, the remedy is the antitoxic *Mahagada*, and "A bite by an Indrayudha usually proves fatal" (lines 56-87). The non-venomous are *Kapila*, *Pingala*, *Shankhamukhi*, *Musika*, *Pundarimukhi* and *Saravika* (lines 90-113). Habitats named are Yavana (Turkestan), Pandya (Deccan), Sahya (the Ghats) and Pautana (lines 115-121). Venomous leeches arise from the decomposed urine and faeces of toads and venomous fish, non-venomous from decayed aquatic plants (lines 123-134). Keeping: caught with wet leather and put in a new pitcher with pond water and ooze, fed on powdered zoophytes, dried meat and aquatic bulbs, water changed every second or third day and pitcher weekly (lines 143-154). Application: the skin is roughened with earth and powdered cow-dung, the leeches are sprinkled with water saturated with mustard and turmeric, and put on; if they will not take, a drop of milk or blood is used or a slight incision made; a horse-shoe mouth and arched neck show that they have taken; itching or drawing pain shows that fresh blood is being drawn and they are to be removed (lines 167-200). Rock salt is sprinkled on those that will not let go (lines 204-206); after, the leech is dusted with rice powder, its mouth oiled with salt, and made to vomit the blood, else it falls ill of the incurable *Indramada* (lines 208-241). The wound is dressed according to the bleeding (lines 243-262). Elsewhere leeches are a standard instrument: listed with the accessories of surgery in *Sutrasthana* ch.5 (line 52) and ch.8 (line 180), and applied to a swelling in *Chikitsasthana* ch.20 (lines 10-14).

### Active Components

The salivary secretion of the medicinal leech contains an anticoagulant protein (hirudin) and a number of other bioactive substances, including vasodilating and anti-inflammatory compounds and an enzyme that spreads fluid through tissue. The sources read here treat the animal, not its secretion; the Sanskrit chapter is about the blood it draws.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Sanskrit chapter is a procedure for bleeding a patient, with the leech as the gentlest instrument; it is not a substance to be taken. The Greek text records leeches only as something to be expelled. Nothing here supports applying a leech outside a clinic. Medical leeching survives as a regulated procedure in a few settings; the Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation of the leech for taking is given in the sources read. The only procedure is the application of living leeches in *Sutrasthana* ch.13 as above (selection, keeping, application, removal and dressing). No count of leeches or duration of application is given in the lines read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Hirudotherapy is used in modern medicine to relieve venous congestion in reattached tissue after reconstructive surgery, and the leech's anticoagulant is a recognised pharmacological finding. Infection with the bacterium that lives in the leech gut is a known complication. Beyond that, No clinical studies were retrieved for the traditional uses. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here for a cultivator to take. If the sources offer a discipline, it is the Sanskrit one: choose the right animal (non-venomous, from named places), keep it clean, apply it only where the patient cannot bear the knife, and make the animal disgorge so that it does not sicken. That discipline belongs to a physician, not to self-treatment. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Leech application carries risk of prolonged bleeding (especially in people on anticoagulants or with clotting disorders), anaemia, allergic reaction and wound infection, including by the gut bacteria of the leech; wild-caught leeches are not sterile. The Sanskrit text itself warns of venomous kinds and of the dying leech. Trade in and collection of wild medicinal leeches is regulated in many places, and the species used in medicine is believed to be protected under international wildlife-trade rules; the status of any given species has not been checked here.
