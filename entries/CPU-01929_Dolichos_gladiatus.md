# Dolichos gladiatus — Simbi (Shimbi, Sushruta Samhita Appendix 3)

## *Dolichos gladiatus (Jacq.), the sword bean, a climbing pea-family plant now usually placed in Canavalia as Canavalia gladiata (Jacq.) DC., as the translator's Latin gloss to the Sanskrit name read in the OCR as "I^imbi" (Shimbi, Simbi); the Latin is Bhishagratna's, the pairing with the Sanskrit name is ours, and the identification is of low to medium confidence* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** The raw seeds and pods of the sword bean are generally regarded as unsafe to eat unless thoroughly cooked. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should take this plant on the strength of it.


**Named in the *Sushruta Samhita* (SS) only through the translator's Appendix No. 3, "Names of drugs with Latin synonyms".** This is a single-text entry. The appendix is printed in two columns, Sanskrit names on the left and Bhishagratna's Latin or English glosses on the right, and the e-text `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt` splits the two columns into separate runs of lines. In that file the Sanskrit name stands at line 32892 as "I^imbi" (the OCR garbles diacritics) and the Latin gloss "Dolichos  gladiatus." at line 32974. The extraction file `Sources/SS_Sushruta/extract/names.csv` lists the Latin line as unpaired ("OCR column split"). The pairing here is ours, made by counting entries in the two columns from points where the pairs are unambiguous: Sigru to Moringa, Sikhari to Boswellia, Silajatu to "Bitumenous substance", then Simbi to "Dolichos gladiatus", Simsapa to Dalbergia Sissoo and Sindhuvara to Vitex Negundo, a run checked at its other end, where Sunthi falls on Ginger, Surana on Amorphophallus campanulatus and Suka-simbi on Mucuna pruriens. The count held at every checked anchor, but it remains a reconstruction from OCR and must be checked against the page scan. [USER: verify] 

**The name in the main text.** The form Simbi was not found in the chapter files of `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/`. The Sutrasthana chapter on pulses and beans (`SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` line 299) has the related "Shimva" in the plural: "The Shimvas (Beans) have an astringent taste and produce a condition of parchedness in the system. They are antitoxic, discutient, and reduce the Kapham and the power of sight. They are imperfectly digested and acquire a pungent taste in digestion, though ordinarily sweet in taste. They cause evacuation of the bowels and emission of flatus." The text then says there are "four varieties of Shimva such as, the white, the black, the yellow and the red, of which each preceding species is superior in virtues and qualities to the one immediately following" and that the Shimvas are "heat-making and pungent both in taste and digestion" (lines 311 to 315). Whether the Simbi of the appendix is the Shimva of this chapter is not stated in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] The main text also has "S'uka-s'imbi" (`SS_Uttara_ch44.txt` line 153), which the appendix, by the same column count, glosses as Mucuna pruriens; it is a separate name.

**A second tradition on disk.** The rGyud-bzhi text (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 949) lists, under *mkhal.zho.sha*, "Cowhage: Canavalia gladiata: Mucuna prurita". That is a Tibetan drug with a more than one Latin name and is not tagged on this row, so it is recorded here only as a cross-reference for the spine. [USER: verify whether FMT should be added]

In general terms, the seeds of the sword bean, like many pea-family beans, contain lectins and other anti-nutritional compounds that are reduced by thorough cooking. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Raw beans are unsafe.**

- **What the text says it is for.** For the related Shimva beans of the vegetable chapter: food qualities and effects on the humours, as quoted above. The appendix itself gives no use for Simbi.
- **What the text does not say.** No therapeutic use for this glossed species, no preparation, no dose.

The Codex gives no protocol and does not recommend this plant.

### Preparation & Dosing

None for this name. The vegetable chapter treats the Shimvas as foods (and, for unripe pulse beans, notes that they "can be digested only with the greatest difficulty", lines 324 to 328). **No dose is given in the sources retrieved.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. *Canavalia gladiata* is a cultivated bean of tropical Asia and Africa whose immature pods and cooked seeds are eaten, while the raw seeds are generally regarded as toxic. The Latin name *Dolichos gladiatus* is an old synonym. [USER: verify the accepted name.] No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SS (Sutra ch.46, lines 299 to 315).** For the Shimva beans: astringent in taste, parchedness-producing, "heat-making and pungent both in taste and digestion", sweet in taste in the ordinary way. These are the qualities of the Shimva group, not of a plant proved to be Simbi. [USER: verify]
- **SS (Appendix 3).** The glossary gives no qualities.
- No other tradition is tagged, and none is imported here.

### Contraindications

- **TOXIC when raw.** Raw or undercooked sword beans are generally regarded as unsafe. Do not eat them.
