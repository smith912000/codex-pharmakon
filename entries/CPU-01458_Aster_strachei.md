# Aster strachei — lug.chhung, Lugchhung (rGyud-bzhi); "Lesser starwort"

## *Aster strachei, the middle one of three binomials Clark 1995 prints for lug.chhung, and the only one in the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts in this row.** Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`.

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1105).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, line 1105: "lug.chhung: Aster diplostaphioides (D.C.) C.B. Clarke: Aster strachei: Aster poliothamnus: [has three names. It is slightly furry, has a pale blue flower, and its leaf and stalk resemble Aster flaccidus (lug.mig)]. It cures infectious fevers, poisoning, brown phlegm and channel fevers. [This type of Aster is also malodorous, has bitter taste and cool power, disperses spirits and (heals) infected wounds.]" The square brackets are as printed. Clark therefore prints three binomials for one Tibetan name, and *Aster strachei* is the second. The extraction took it as the row's name. The three are not shown to be one species, and this entry stands for the Tibetan drug *lug.chhung*, not for one botanical species. [USER: verify] The "Aster flaccidus (lug.mig)" the text compares it with is a different Tibetan name (me.tog.lug.mig, line 1077), which has its own spine row, CPU-01674 *Aster barbellatus*.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (lines 5437 to 5452).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate blocks. The phonetic names (line 5437) read "Lalaphue Langthangtse / Lishi / Looh dhue dorje Lugchhung Lugmur Lugnyal Lugru mukpo Lugru serpo". The scientific names (lines 5445 to 5448) read "Cnidium monnieri / Hyoscyamus niger L. Syzygium aromaticum Codonopsis thalictrifolia / Aster strachei / Phlomis younghusbandii Corydalis meifolia Pedicularis siphonantha Pedicularis longiflora". The English column (lines 5450 to 5454) reads "Celery / Henbane Clove / Lesser starwort / Mountain turkeycom / Lousewort Yellow lousewort". Read by position, nine phonetic names pair with nine scientific names, and "Lugchhung" stands against *Aster strachei* and "Lesser starwort". The pairing can be checked on one name: Clark line 1106 gives lug.ngal as *Corydalis meifolia*, and "Lugnyal" stands against *Corydalis meifolia* in the same way. The pairing is positional and was not printed in a single row. [USER: verify]

*Use in the Men-Tsee-Khang text.* Line 3470 to 3471: "Lugchung cures epidemic poisoning, baekan mukpo and hot disorder of the channels." The terms *baekan mukpo* and "hot disorder of the channels" are the translation's own and were not resolved further. A name list in Clark (line 1758) also prints lug.chhung among the names beginning *la* to *lo*, with no further information.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The Tibetan texts record a malodorous, bitter, cool-powered *Aster* given for infectious fevers, poisoning, brown phlegm and channel fevers, and for infected wounds. These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for infectious fever or poisoning, which need medical care.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes the plant by its taste and power (bitter, cool) and by what it cures: infectious fevers, poisoning, brown phlegm, channel fevers, infected wounds. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. Nothing read places *lug.chhung* in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. The line's mention that it "disperses spirits" is a therapeutic claim in the text and is not treated here as a cultivation status. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a bitter, cool-powered medicine for fevers and poisoning in the Tibetan texts and nothing more can be said from them. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. The Tibetan texts record the drug for poisoning and infectious fevers, which need medical care, and a home preparation must not replace it. The botanical identity is uncertain (three binomials in Clark), so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
