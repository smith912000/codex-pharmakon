### Origin & Tradition

Senna is the most potent botanical laxative in the ancient pharmacopoeia and remains the most widely used today — it is on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines as a stimulant laxative. The anthraquinone glycosides (sennosides A and B) provide a mechanism so reliable and dose-dependent that senna bridged traditional and modern medicine with minimal controversy. Its adoption across five traditions reflects both its geographic distribution (native to the Arabian peninsula and Nile Valley) and the universal medical requirement for effective evacuation therapy — a cornerstone of humoral purging doctrine in all pre-modern systems.

**EP — Egyptian (documented in later demotic medical papyri and trade records):** Senna preparations appear in later Egyptian medical documents and in trade records showing import from Arabia. The Egyptians used senna-type preparations as cathartics in the broader purgation protocols documented in the Ebers Papyrus, though the specific senna species identification in earlier papyri is debated. The spice trade route connecting Egypt to Arabia brought senna leaves as a medicinal commodity.

**AT — Assyrian (documented in Assyrian trade tablets as imported cathartic):** Senna appears in Assyrian medical tablets as an imported purging medicine from the Arabian south. Thompson (1924) documents it among the cathartic preparations. Its role in the Assyrian Materia Medica is primarily as a purgative in intestinal cleansing and "opening" (aperient) formulas.

**CM — Canon of Medicine (سنا — Sanā):** Ibn Sina's account of senna is the most comprehensive in pre-modern medicine. He distinguishes *Sanā Makkī* (from Mecca — the stronger *C. senna* Alexandrian type) from *Sanā Hindī* (Indian — *C. angustifolia*). Classification: hot in the first degree, dry in the second. Primary indication: purgation of phlegm and bile from the intestines — the classic humoral evacuation. He specifically recommends it combined with ginger and mastic to prevent griping (the cramping side effect). His preparation notes anticipate modern senna tea formulation almost exactly. He distinguishes it from stronger purgatives (scammony, hellebore) as suitable for repeated use.

**SS — Ayurveda (स्वर्णपत्री — Svarṇapatrī):** Senna is used in Ayurveda primarily as a *Virechana* (therapeutic purgation) herb. Classification: Tikta-Katu rasa, Ushna virya, Katu vipaka, Pitta-Kapha shamaka (through evacuation of excess). The *Bhavaprakasha Nighantu* documents senna specifically for constipation (*vibandha*), biliary conditions, and skin disorders (the Ayurvedic understanding that skin conditions reflect intestinal toxin load). Used in the therapeutic *Panchakarma* purgation phase.

**SBJ/TCM (番泻叶 — Fānxièyè — literally "foreign diarrhea leaf"):** Senna entered TCM pharmacopoeia via the Silk Road. Its TCM classification: bitter, cold; enters Large Intestine channel. Purges heat-stagnation; clears intestinal fire; used specifically for *rè bì* (hot constipation — hard dry stools with heat signs). The name literally describes its foreign origin and reliable action. Included in the modern Chinese Pharmacopoeia.

### Active Components

- **Sennoside A and B** — the primary anthraquinone glycosides; the main laxative actives; prodrugs converted by gut bacteria to rhein anthrone (the active form); rhein anthrone: (1) stimulates colonic peristalsis via ENS prostaglandin/5-HT mechanisms; (2) inhibits Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase in colonic epithelium → increased fluid secretion into colon; net effect: evacuant diarrhea within 6–12 hours
- **Sennoside C, D** — minor anthraquinone glycosides; similar mechanism, lower potency
- **Rhein, aloe-emodin** — free anthraquinones (small amounts); directly laxative but also mutagenic concerns at high doses
- **Kaempferol, isorhamnetin** — flavonoids; anti-inflammatory; antioxidant
- **Mucilage polysaccharides** — demulcent; modulate the harshness of the anthraquinone action
- **Tannins** — astringent; contribute to the constipating rebound effect after purgation

### Preparation & Dosing

**Senna tea (standard laxative preparation):**
1–2g dried senna leaf or pod in 150mL hot water; steep 10 minutes; drink before sleep; effect in 6–12 hours. The standard WHO-recommended preparation. **Single dose — not for daily use.**

**Senna compound (traditional Ibn Sina formula):**
Senna 2g + ginger 0.5g + mastic 0.5g in 250mL decoction; the ginger and mastic prevent griping while senna provides purgation. Historically the standard prescription form.

**Standardized sennoside supplement:**
12–36mg sennosides (standardized extract); equivalent to 1–2g dried leaf; used in modern pharmaceutical preparations (Ex-Lax, Senokot). Maximum single dose.

**Ayurvedic Virechana (supervised protocol only):**
Senna combined with *haritaki* (Terminalia chebula) and *trivrit* (Operculina turpethum) in the classical Triphala-senna purgation preparation; requires Ayurvedic practitioner guidance for proper Panchakarma integration.

### Scientific Correlation

- **Laxative efficacy (sennosides):** Müller-Lissner S et al. (1993): standardized senna preparations reliably effective for constipation; onset 6–12 hours; dose-dependent response; considered standard of care in opioid-induced constipation
- **Mechanism (rhein anthrone):** Beubler E, Kollar G (1985): rhein anthrone stimulates prostaglandin E2 release in colon, driving peristalsis; Mascolo N et al. (1988): ENS-mediated 5-HT3 pathway confirmed
- **Melanosis coli concern:** Chronic senna use (>1 year) causes melanosis coli (brown discoloration of colon mucosa from anthraquinone accumulation); reversible on cessation; not associated with increased cancer risk per current evidence (Nusko G et al. 2000)
- **Electrolyte effects:** Prolonged use causes hypokalaemia (potassium loss with fluid); must not be used continuously
- **Safety in single-dose use:** Single or short-course use is safe per WHO Essential Medicines documentation; chronic laxative abuse is the concerning pattern

### Contraindications

- **Chronic/daily use** — the most important contraindication: habituation, electrolyte imbalance (hypokalaemia), melanosis coli, potential laxative dependency; use for maximum 5–7 consecutive days only
- **Inflammatory bowel disease** — senna is contraindicated in active Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, intestinal inflammation; the anthraquinone stimulation of an inflamed mucosa worsens the condition
- **Intestinal obstruction** — absolute contraindication; the stimulant laxative action against mechanical obstruction creates dangerous pressure
- **Pregnancy** — anthraquinone glycosides cross the placenta and pass into breast milk; avoid during pregnancy and lactation
- **Electrolyte depletion** — do not use if already hypokalaemic; the added potassium loss can cause cardiac arrhythmia
- **Children under 12** — not recommended without medical supervision
