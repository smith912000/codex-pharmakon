**Etymology:** English "clove" from Old French *clou* (nail) — the dried flower bud of clove resembles a small nail (*clous de girofle* = nails of clove in medieval French). "Girofle" from Greek *karyophyllon* (*karyon* = nut + *phyllon* = leaf). The "nail" shape of the clove bud gave it both its French name and its Chinese name (*dīng xiāng* = nail fragrance). The morphological name encodes the primary visual recognition feature across languages.

**The Maluku spice monopoly:** Cloves are endemic to the Maluku Islands (Moluccas, Indonesia). For thousands of years, the Maluku Islands were the *only* source of cloves in the world — making control of the Maluku trade one of the most fought-over economic monopolies in history. The Portuguese (1512), Dutch (VOC, 1605), and ultimately the French (who broke the Dutch monopoly by smuggling plants to Mauritius in 1770) successively fought for control of this small archipelago because cloves had extraordinary global value. The clove tree's geographic restriction created one of history's most powerful commodity monopolies.

## 2 · Cross-Traditional Presence

**Canon of Medicine (Ibn Sīnā, 1025 CE):** *Qaranful* — extensively documented. Ibn Sīnā noted cloves for: "warming the liver; useful in weakness of the stomach; analgesic for toothache (first documented prescription for cloves for dental pain — 11th century); benefits the eyes and strengthens vision; treats forgetfulness." The toothache-clove application documented by Ibn Sīnā 1,000 years ago persists unchanged in modern dentistry (eugenol is the active in OTC dental analgesics and professional ZOE cement).

**Shennong Bencao Jing / Bencao Gangmu (SBJ):** *Dīng xiāng* (丁香) — one of the most widely used warming herbs in Chinese medicine. Applications: warms the Kidney Yang (constitutional warming-tonification), treats cold hiccups and belching (gastric antispasmodic), relieves nausea and vomiting, analgesic (particularly for toothache and pain due to cold conditions). The CM applications precisely map to eugenol's mechanism: antispasmodic (Ca2+ channel; GI), analgesic (Na+ channel local anesthetic; COX-2 inhibitor), warming (TRPV1 agonism).

**Sushruta Samhita (SS):** *Lavaṅga* (clove) is documented in Ayurveda for: *dīpana* (digestive fire stimulation), *vātahara* (reduces Vata imbalance), *pācanīya* (digestive), and anti-parasitic (*krimighna*). The anthelmintic application (killing intestinal worms) is pharmacologically supported by eugenol's antimicrobial activity.

**Chinese court documentation (7th CE):** Chinese historical records (Tang Dynasty, 618–907 CE) document officials chewing cloves before audiences with the emperor to ensure fresh breath — an early recorded pharmaceutical-cosmetic use of clove's antimicrobial and aromatic properties.

**Medieval European spice trade:** Cloves were among the most valuable spices in medieval Europe. The spice guilds of Venice, Genoa, and later Lisbon and Amsterdam were fundamentally built on clove (and pepper, cinnamon, nutmeg) trade. The fourth crusade (1202–1204) was partly financed by the Venetian spice trade profits. Clove's European introduction predates accurate historical documentation; by 1st CE, Roman sources mention *caryophyllon* as an eastern aromatic.

## 3 · Convergence Analysis

**Convergence Score: 7/9**

The dental/analgesic application convergence: CM (Ibn Sīnā 1025 CE), CM (Bencao Gangmu), and European folk tradition all independently documented cloves specifically for toothache — one of the most specific pharmacological observations in the Codex. The mechanism (eugenol local anesthesia via Na+ channel block + COX-2 inhibition + antimicrobial) explains this remarkable tradition-specific precision: toothache is a distinctive pain type where eugenol's combined analgesic-antimicrobial-anti-inflammatory triple action is uniquely effective.

**The antimicrobial convergence:** EP, AT, CM, and SS all document cloves for antimicrobial applications (preservative, anthelmintic, wound care). Eugenol's membrane-disrupting antimicrobial activity explains this independent multi-tradition recognition.

## 4 · Phytochemical Composition

**Essential oil (from dried flower buds):**

| Compound | Content | Action |
|----------|---------|--------|
| **Eugenol** | 72–90% | Local anesthetic (Na+ channel); COX-2 inhibitor; antimicrobial; antifungal; antioxidant |
| **Eugenol acetate** | 5–15% | Pro-eugenol (hydrolyzed in tissue); antimicrobial |
| **β-Caryophyllene** | 5–12% | CB2 agonist; anti-inflammatory; analgesic |
| α-Humulene | 1–5% | Anti-inflammatory; antitumor |
| Methyl-n-amyl ketone | Minor | Aromatic |

**Non-EO fraction:**
- **Oleanolic acid**: anti-inflammatory triterpenoid
- **Ursolic acid**: anti-inflammatory; 5α-reductase
- Flavonoids (quercetin, kaempferol): antioxidant
- **Eugeniine, isoeugenitol**: minor phenylpropanoids

**Eugenol concentration in different parts:**
- Buds (whole cloves): 72–90% eugenol in EO
- Stem: 83–90% eugenol in EO  
- Leaves: 70–75% eugenol in EO
- Flowers (after petal drop): lower quality; less eugenol

**β-Caryophyllene** — the second major constituent (5–12%): a sesquiterpene that is also present in black pepper (I_161), cannabis, and many other herbs. β-Caryophyllene is notable as a dietary cannabinoid: it activates CB2 receptors (not CB1 — so no psychoactive effect) → anti-inflammatory, analgesic, anxiolytic effects. Clove is one of the richest dietary sources of β-caryophyllene. This constitutes a legally accessible and pharmacologically significant CB2 pathway modulation in clove.

## 5 · Mechanisms of Action

### Analgesic / Local Anesthetic — Primary

**Eugenol local anesthesia:**
- Eugenol is a lipophilic phenol that rapidly penetrates sensory nerve membranes
- Inhibits voltage-gated Na+ channels (the same target as lidocaine, benzocaine, and all local anesthetics)
- Mode: preferential binding to the inactivated state of Na+ channels → membrane stabilization → blocks action potential propagation in pain fibers
- Onset: rapid (minutes); Duration: 30–60 min topically
- Dental application: eugenol delivered directly to exposed dentinal tubules or pulp suppresses dental pain signal transmission

**COX-2 selective inhibition:**
- Eugenol inhibits COX-2 (over COX-1) → reduced prostaglandin E₂ at the site of dental inflammation → anti-inflammatory analgesia
- Combined with Na+ channel block = dual-mechanism analgesia explaining why clove oil is specifically effective for toothache (local + systemic anti-inflammatory simultaneously)

### Antimicrobial

**Eugenol membrane disruption:**
- Eugenol disrupts bacterial cell membrane integrity → ion leakage → cell death
- Active against: *Streptococcus mutans* (caries), *Porphyromonas gingivalis* (periodontal), *Fusobacterium nucleatum* (periodontal), *Staphylococcus aureus*, *E. coli*, *Salmonella*
- Anti-biofilm: prevents and disrupts dental biofilm (plaque) formation
- Antifungal: *Candida albicans* (MIC 0.05–0.1% — similar potency to clotrimazole in vitro)

**Anti-efflux pump activity:**
- Eugenol inhibits bacterial efflux pumps → increased susceptibility to antibiotics
- Relevant to MRSA (similar mechanism to thymol in I_153 and carvacrol in I_127)

### Anti-inflammatory (Systemic)

**β-Caryophyllene CB2 agonism:**
- Activates CB2 receptors on immune cells → reduced TNF-α, IL-1β → anti-inflammatory
- CB2 agonism without CB1 → no psychoactive effect; dietary safe
- This mechanism is identical to β-caryophyllene in black pepper (I_161) and other sources

**NF-κB inhibition:**
- Eugenol inhibits IKKβ → reduced NF-κB → reduced inflammatory gene expression
- Consistent with the tradition-wide anti-inflammatory application

### Carminative

- Eugenol: GI smooth muscle relaxation (Ca2+ channel modulation) → antispasmodic
- β-Caryophyllene: reduces GI inflammatory tone
- Aromatic effect: reduces intestinal gas surface tension

## 6 · Clinical Evidence

### Dental / Analgesic — Best Clinical Evidence

**FDA Category I (OTC Dental Analgesic):** Eugenol is classified by the FDA as a safe and effective OTC dental analgesic for temporary relief of toothache. This is one of very few herbal-derived compounds with FDA OTC monograph status for a specific analgesic indication. Eugenol-based temporary dental cements (ZOE, Cavit) are standard dental materials in every dental practice worldwide.

**Zinc Oxide Eugenol (ZOE) cement:** The most studied and most-used temporary dental restorative material. Provides: (1) obtundent (nerve-soothing) effect from eugenol diffusing into dentinal tubules; (2) antimicrobial protection; (3) mechanical protection (temporary coverage). Present in every dental emergency kit; used in billions of dental procedures annually.

**Comparison studies:** Multiple small clinical trials confirm clove oil gel (not EO direct application) equivalent to benzocaine gel for dental pain relief and topical pre-injection anesthesia. Clove oil's effect is marginally slower onset but equivalent 20-min duration.

### Antimicrobial (Oral)

**Mouthwash trials:** Clove oil-based mouthwash consistently outperforms non-antimicrobial control for plaque and gingivitis reduction in multiple small RCTs. Fewer large-scale trials than chlorhexidine comparisons.

### Anti-inflammatory / Analgesic (Systemic — Limited)

Preclinical evidence strong. Human clinical trials for systemic eugenol/clove anti-inflammatory use limited to small studies; largest RCT using orally administered clove powder found antioxidant and anti-inflammatory biomarker improvement.

## 7 · Phenomenological Record

**The Maluku war and the first global commodity monopoly:** The Banda Islands (part of the Maluku archipelago) were the world's only source of nutmeg; the Ternate and Tidore Islands the world's only source of cloves. The Dutch VOC (Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie, founded 1602) established one of history's most brutal colonial monopolies specifically to control clove and nutmeg production. The VOC massacred or expelled virtually the entire indigenous population of the Banda Islands (1621, Jan Pieterszoon Coen's campaign) to replace them with Dutch-controlled enslaved laborers. Approximately 15,000 indigenous Bandanese were killed, enslaved, or fled — for the sake of controlling nutmeg and clove trade. The VOC's clove monopoly was finally broken when Pierre Poivre (note: French for "pepper") smuggled clove and nutmeg seedlings from the Maluku Islands to Mauritius in 1770, establishing French cultivation. The pharmacological compounds *eugenol* and *myristicin* (nutmeg) were the indirect cause of one of history's most extreme colonial atrocities.

**Ibn Sīnā's toothache prescription and the 1000-year validation:** When Ibn Sīnā prescribed *qaranful* (clove) for toothache in 1025 CE, he was documenting a practice already centuries old. The clove-for-toothache tradition, preserved through Islamic medicine → European herbalism → folk medicine → professional dentistry, is one of the longest-validated pharmacological prescriptions in history. Today, eugenol (the molecule Ibn Sīnā prescribed as "clove oil") is in every dental office. The 1,000-year-old prescription is FDA-approved. This represents one of the most direct, unbroken pharmacological tradition-to-evidence lineages in the Codex.

**The clove cigarette (*kretek*):** The Indonesian *kretek* cigarette (60–70% tobacco + 30–40% clove) is the national cigarette of Indonesia and one of the world's most distinctive tobacco products. The eugenol in cloves produces a numbing sensation in the throat → reduced perception of smoke harshness → deeper inhalation → increased tobacco delivery → greater addiction. The eugenol local anesthetic was inadvertently promoting tobacco addiction. US FDA banned flavored cigarettes in 2009 specifically targeting *kretek*-style products (mentholated cigarettes — see peppermint, I_156 — were exempted, creating the "menthol exception" controversy). The pharmacological mechanism of a 1,000-year-old herbal analgesic was repurposed to increase cigarette addictiveness — one of the darker applications of eugenol's local anesthetic properties.

**The clove nail as symbol:** The French name *clou de girofle* (nail of clove) is shared with the English "clove" (from Latin *clavus*, nail) and the Chinese *dīng xiāng* (丁, the character for "nail"). Three linguistically unrelated cultures independently named cloves for their nail-like morphology — one of the purest examples of parallel naming in botanical taxonomy, where the same visual observation (dried flower bud = nail shape) was made independently by observers who had no contact with each other, in opposite hemispheres, across 2,000 years of naming history.

## 8 · Practical Application

### Clinical Use

**Toothache / dental pain (primary indication — FDA approved):**
- Clove oil (100% *Syzygium aromaticum* EO): apply undiluted with cotton ball directly to affected tooth/gum
- Duration: 20–30 min relief; can repeat every 2–4 hours for temporary relief while awaiting dental care
- Commercial preparations: Red Cross Toothache Kit (FDA-approved); Dent-Zel (eugenol dental liquid)
- ⚠️ Never swallow concentrated clove oil — eugenol is hepatotoxic in large oral doses; topical only

**Pre-injection analgesia (dental chair):**
- Clove oil or 20% eugenol gel applied to mucosa 1–2 min before injection: reduces injection pain
- Comparable to benzocaine 20% (standard commercial topical anesthetic)

**Oral antiseptic / mouthwash:**
- 1–2 drops clove EO in 100mL warm water; swish; do not swallow
- Or clove bud tea: 3–5 whole cloves + 150mL water simmered 15 min; gargle/rinse

**Digestive / carminative:**
- 2–3 whole cloves chewed or in tea: traditional digestive after heavy meals
- Clove powder: 0.5–1g in food or capsule for digestive warming

**Antifungal (topical):**
- Clove EO 1–3% in carrier oil for nail/skin fungal infections
- Allow maximum skin absorption: cover with bandage; apply twice daily

### Contraindications

- **Oral clove EO ingestion:** NEVER ingest clove EO orally — eugenol is hepatotoxic above 5mL in adults; children are more sensitive. Topical only at 100% concentration; for internal use, only dilute tea or 1–2 drops in water
- **Pregnancy:** Eugenol is a uterine stimulant at high doses; topical dental use fine; avoid oral clove oil ingestion
- **Hemostasis disorders:** Eugenol inhibits platelet aggregation; caution with anticoagulants
- **Liver disease:** Eugenol metabolism burden; topical is fine; oral EO contraindicated

### Drug Interactions

- **Warfarin:** Eugenol antiplatelet + CYP2C9 modulation → possible warfarin potentiation; monitor INR
- **Antidiabetics:** Minor blood glucose-lowering effect; monitor at high doses
- **Cardiac glycosides:** β-Caryophyllene's mild cardiac effects; monitor with digoxin

### Safety Profile

**Topical use:** Excellent. Clove EO is an FDA-approved dental analgesic, indicating solid safety for topical application. Local contact with gum tissue: mild burning sensation (TRPV1 agonism from eugenol); apply diluted (1:1 with olive oil) if sensitive. Contact sensitization rare but possible with repeated application.

**Oral ingestion of EO:** Dangerous. Eugenol in concentrated oral doses → hepatotoxicity; cases of fatal clove oil ingestion in infants documented. NEVER give clove EO internally to children. Whole cloves and clove tea: safe at culinary doses; GRAS.
