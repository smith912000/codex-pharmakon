**Etymology:** Greek *glykyrrhiza* = *glykys* (sweet) + *rhiza* (root) = sweet root. The sweetness (50–100× sweeter than sucrose for glycyrrhizin) is the most immediate pharmacological property of licorice root — documented identically in every tradition that encountered it. Latin *liquiritia* → French *réglisse* → English "licorice." German *Süßholz* = sweet wood (direct translation). Every culture that encountered licorice named it primarily for its sweetness — an unusual convergence of naming-by-taste.

**Three-species clarification:**
- *G. glabra* (Mediterranean/European): Primary pharmacopoeial species; *Liquiritiae radix* (European Ph.)
- *G. uralensis* (Chinese): *Gān cǎo* (甘草); primary CM species; slightly different saponin profile but equivalent glycyrrhizin content
- *G. inflata* (Chinese desert): High licochalcone A content; antimicrobial emphasis

## 2 · Cross-Traditional Presence

**De Materia Medica (Dioscorides, ~77 CE):** *Glykyrrhiza* (DMM Book III.5) receives one of the longest monographs in DMM. Dioscorides: "The root is sweet; being masticated or the juice of it sucked up, it quenches thirst; it is good for those that have shortness of breath, dropsical cough, pains in the chest and liver, and kidneys; it soothes the roughness of the windpipe." The respiratory, hepatic, renal, and anti-thirst applications are directly documented. The anti-thirst application (noted across multiple ancient traditions) reflects glycyrrhizin's mineralocorticoid-like sodium retention — licorice increases plasma volume, reducing the osmotic drive for thirst.

**Canon of Medicine (Ibn Sīnā, 1025 CE):** *ʿIrq al-sūs* receives extensive treatment in Ibn Sīnā's Qānūn — he considered it "the king of herbs for the chest and lungs." Applications: cough, chest pain, hoarseness, gastric ulcer, liver protection, thirst reduction, wound healing, and — uniquely for Ibn Sīnā — as a base herb to be combined with stronger medicines to "harmonize" and reduce their toxicity. This harmonizing function directly parallels Chinese medicine's *gān cǎo* role.

**Shennong Bencao Jing / Bencao Gangmu (SBJ):** *Gān cǎo* (甘草) is classified as a Superior (上品) herb in the Shennong Bencao Jing — the highest category, indicating safety and tonifying properties. It appears in more classical Chinese formulas than any other single herb. Its CM functions:
- Tonifies the Spleen and Qi (adaptogenic, tonic)
- Clears Heat and relieves toxicity (anti-inflammatory; antidote to toxic herbs)
- "Harmonizes" all herbs in a formula (reduces side effects of other ingredients; glycyrrhizin's CYP enzyme modulation provides partial pharmacological explanation)
- Stops cough; expectorant
- Soothes pain; antispasmodic

The role of *gān cǎo* as "mediator/harmonizer" in Chinese formulary has no direct parallel in Western herbal practice — it represents a unique pharmacological niche of one herb modulating the pharmacokinetics and effects of all other herbs in a formula.

**Sushruta Samhita (SS):** *Yashtimadhu* (यष्टिमधु) — "rod of honey" — is classified among the rasayana (rejuvenating/longevity) herbs in Ayurveda. Applications: cough, voice disorders, gastric inflammation, wound healing, and as an ingredient in countless Ayurvedic compound preparations. The Charaka Samhita places licorice root in the *Balya* group (strength-promoting) and *Jivaniya* group (life-promoting).

**Ebers Papyrus (~1550 BCE):** ~50 references to licorice (*qnqn*) — making it one of the most mentioned plants in the entire Ebers Papyrus. Used for respiratory, GI, and liver conditions. The Egyptian documentation predates Dioscorides by 1,500 years and represents the earliest written record of licorice as medicine.

**Assyrian Medical Tablets:** *Suādu* (sweet wood, licorice) is extensively documented in cuneiform medical tablets from Nineveh and Assur — one of the most frequently mentioned medicinal plants in the entire Assyrian pharmacopoeia. Applications: respiratory, GI, urinary, and as a base for compound preparations (parallel to *gān cǎo*'s harmonizing role in CM).

## 3 · Convergence Analysis

**Convergence Score: 8/9** — Highest in this session; exceeded only by garlic, olive, and saffron in the full Codex.

Confirmed: DMM · CM · SBJ · SS · EP · AT  
Probable: BM · FMT  
Absent: SA

**Why 8/9?** Licorice has been found in virtually every Old World pharmacopoeial tradition because:
1. It grows across a geographic range spanning the entire Old World (Mediterranean → Central Asia → China)
2. Its pharmacological properties (sweetness, anti-inflammatory, respiratory, GI) are immediately apparent from first use
3. Its root is extremely shelf-stable (glycyrrhizin acts as a preservative) — making it tradeable across long distances
4. It has genuine efficacy for common conditions (respiratory, GI) that all traditions needed to treat

**The "harmonizer" convergence:** Both Ibn Sīnā (CM) and Chinese *gān cǎo* tradition explicitly documented licorice as a harmonizing herb that modifies the effects of other herbs in compound preparations. This convergent recognition of a single herb as a pharmacokinetic modifier — documented independently in Islamic and Chinese medical traditions — represents one of the most sophisticated observations in pre-modern pharmacology. Glycyrrhizin's CYP enzyme inhibition and its role in delaying drug metabolism partially explains this "harmonizing" quality.

## 4 · Phytochemical Composition

**Triterpenoid saponins (primary active class):**

| Compound | Content | Action |
|----------|---------|--------|
| **Glycyrrhizin** (glycyrrhizic acid) | 2–9% dry root | 50–100× sweeter than sucrose; anti-inflammatory; 11β-HSD2 inhibitor; antiviral; immune-modulating |
| **18β-Glycyrrhetinic acid** (GZA aglycone) | After gut hydrolysis | Direct 11β-HSD2 inhibitor; primary mineralocorticoid-excess metabolite |
| Liquiritic acid | Minor | Anti-inflammatory |

**Flavonoids:**

| Compound | Content | Action |
|----------|---------|--------|
| **Liquiritigenin** | 0.5–2% | ERβ selective agonist (phytoestrogen); anti-inflammatory |
| **Isoliquiritigenin** | Significant | PDE inhibition; anti-tumor; estrogenic |
| **Glabridin** | 0.1–1% | 5α-reductase inhibition; anti-inflammatory; tyrosinase inhibition (skin whitening) |
| Licochalcone A (*G. inflata*) | Significant | Antimicrobial; anti-inflammatory; antileishmanial |
| Formononetin | Minor | Phytoestrogen (weaker than liquiritigenin) |
| Kaempferol, quercetin | Minor | General anti-inflammatory |

**Polysaccharides:**
- Glycyrrhizan GA, GB, GC, GD: immunostimulant polysaccharides; macrophage activation

**Coumarins:**
- Liqcoumarin, Glycycoumarin: minor; anticoagulant (relevant to drug interactions)

**Glycyrrhizin → 18β-Glycyrrhetinic acid:** Intestinal bacteria (predominantly *Eubacterium* sp.) hydrolyze glycyrrhizin to 18β-glycyrrhetinic acid (GZA) in the colon. GZA is the compound responsible for the mineralocorticoid-excess syndrome (pseudohyperaldosteronism) — it directly inhibits 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11β-HSD2), which normally inactivates cortisol in kidney tubular cells. Inhibition → cortisol accumulates at mineralocorticoid receptor → sodium retention, hypokalemia, hypertension. This is the primary safety concern with licorice.

## 5 · Mechanisms of Action

### Anti-inflammatory — Primary Mechanism

**Glycyrrhizin:**
- Inhibits phospholipase A2 (PLA2) → reduced arachidonic acid release → reduced prostaglandin/leukotriene synthesis
- Inhibits 11β-HSD2 → local cortisol potentiation → endogenous anti-inflammatory enhancement
- Inhibits NF-κB pathway → reduced TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 synthesis
- Direct membrane-stabilizing effect (saponin membrane interaction)

**Isoliquiritigenin:**
- PDE4 inhibition → increased cAMP → anti-inflammatory in mast cells and neutrophils
- NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition

### Antiviral

**Glycyrrhizin — direct antiviral mechanisms:**
- Inhibits viral replication by interfering with viral protein binding to host cell receptors
- Inhibits PKC (protein kinase C) activity required for viral assembly
- Documented activity against: HIV, HSV-1/2, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, SARS-CoV-1, SARS-CoV-2 (IC₅₀ in the μM range for several)
- Japan approved glycyrrhizin IV preparation (Stronger Neo-Minophagen C, SNMC) for chronic hepatitis C — one of the very few herbal-derived compounds with a regulatory antiviral indication

### Expectorant/Mucolytic (Respiratory)

- Glycyrrhizin + polysaccharides → soothing effect on bronchial mucosa (demulcent)
- Saponin-class surface activity → reduced mucus viscosity → easier expectoration
- Anti-inflammatory in bronchial epithelium → reduced irritative cough

### Adrenocortical (Cortisol Potentiation)

**11β-HSD2 inhibition (the safety-relevant mechanism):**
- 18β-Glycyrrhetinic acid blocks 11β-HSD2 → cortisol not converted to cortisone in kidney → cortisol activates mineralocorticoid receptors
- Effect: sodium retention, potassium loss, blood pressure elevation, edema
- This is the "pseudohyperaldosteronism" syndrome
- Same mechanism used therapeutically in Addison's disease (adrenal insufficiency) — licorice as adrenal support has a genuine mechanism
- Dose-dependent: threshold for adverse effects typically >100mg glycyrrhizin/day (~50g whole root/day)

**DGL (deglycyrrhizinated licorice):** Licorice extract with glycyrrhizin removed. DGL retains anti-inflammatory flavonoids and mucilaginous compounds for GI applications WITHOUT the mineralocorticoid risk. This is the safety-optimized form for GI use.

### Estrogenic (Phytoestrogenic)

- Liquiritigenin: selective ERβ agonist (ERβ > ERα) — distinct from most phytoestrogens which prefer ERα
- ERβ selective agonism → potentially protective in hormone-sensitive cancers (ERβ has anti-proliferative effects in breast tissue)
- Contributes to traditional use in menopausal symptoms

## 6 · Clinical Evidence

### Gastric Ulcer / GI (DGL) — Best Evidence

**Carbenoxolone era (pre-DGL):** Carbenoxolone (synthetic glycyrrhetinic acid derivative) demonstrated significant efficacy for gastric ulcer in multiple RCTs (1960s–1970s) — before PPI era. Demonstrated that licorice chemistry heals gastric ulcers. However, the mineralocorticoid-excess side effects of carbenoxolone limited its use. Development of DGL was intended to retain efficacy without the adverse effects.

**DGL (Deglycyrrhizinated Licorice):**
- Rees 1979 (*Gut*, N=82): DGL vs. antacid vs. DGL + antacid in chronic gastric ulcer. All DGL groups showed superior healing rate at 12 weeks vs. antacid alone. Established DGL as a gastroprotective agent.
- Mechanism: flavonoid-driven prostaglandin E2 upregulation → increased mucosal protective mucus secretion; anti-*H. pylori* (licorice flavonoids show in vitro MIC activity against *H. pylori*).
- Modern application: DGL 380–760mg (chewed, not swallowed whole) 20 min before meals — standard protocol for gastric/duodenal ulcer, gastritis, GERD

### Hepatitis C (IV Glycyrrhizin — Japan)

**Stronger Neo-Minophagen C (SNMC):** Glycyrrhizin IV preparation approved in Japan for chronic hepatitis C. Multiple Japanese clinical studies (Arase 1997; Tsubota 1999): IV glycyrrhizin reduces liver enzyme elevation, delays fibrosis progression, and reduces hepatocellular carcinoma incidence in chronic hepatitis C. This is the only licorice-derived compound with a regulatory viral hepatitis indication.

### Adrenal Support

No large RCTs. Traditional Ayurvedic and herbal medicine application for adrenal fatigue/Addison's disease adjunct. Mechanism is clear (11β-HSD2 inhibition → cortisol potentiation); clinical evidence for this specific indication is limited to case series and traditional documentation.

### Menopausal Symptoms

**Mehrpooya 2018 (*Journal of Complementary & Integrative Medicine*):** Licorice vs. HRT for hot flashes. Licorice significantly reduced hot flash frequency and severity; comparable to HRT effect on some secondary measures. Mechanism: liquiritigenin ERβ agonism.

## 7 · Phenomenological Record

**The oldest-documented sweetener and medicine:** Licorice appears in Egyptian medical documents (Ebers Papyrus, c. 1550 BCE) and in cuneiform Assyrian tablets from Nineveh (c. 1300 BCE). It was discovered in the tomb of Tutankhamun (1325 BCE) among the provisions for the afterlife — suggesting its importance was sufficient to include it in royal funerary preparation. The Alexandrian pharmacy (4th–3rd CE BCE) used licorice as a base sweetener for bitter medicines — a direct parallel to its role as a pharmaceutical excipient today. The 3,500-year continuous use record makes licorice one of the longest-continuously-used medicines in human history.

**The "Gān cǎo" paradox — the herb in every formula:** Chinese medicine's concept of *gān cǎo* as the "harmonizer" present in an estimated 60–70% of classical Chinese formulas (some scholars estimate higher) represents a pharmacological sophistication not matched anywhere else in traditional medicine. The claim that licorice "moderates the actions of other herbs, prevents side effects, and guides other herbs to their destination" describes, in pre-modern functional language:
1. CYP enzyme inhibition by glycyrrhizin → delayed metabolism of co-administered herbs → prolonged action
2. P-glycoprotein inhibition → increased absorption of co-administered compounds
3. Direct anti-inflammatory buffering of harsh/toxic herbs (anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective effects)
4. Sweet taste masking of bitter/unpleasant herbs (enabling compliance)

A Chinese physician 2,000 years ago who described *gān cǎo* as "harmonizing" was observing real pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic interactions that we now understand mechanistically. This is one of the most striking examples in the Codex of traditional clinical observation that required 2,000 years of science to explain.

**The pseudohyperaldosteronism crisis (20th century):** The first published case of licorice-induced pseudohyperaldosteronism was reported in 1946 (Revers, Netherlands). The mechanism was not understood until the 1980s (Stewart 1987: 11β-HSD2 inhibition by glycyrrhetinic acid). Between 1946 and 1987, there was a 40-year gap between the clinical observation (licorice causes high blood pressure, edema, low potassium) and the mechanistic explanation. This gap produced a public health problem: Dutch licorice (*drop*), which has extremely high glycyrrhizin content, caused documented cases of hypertension and hospitalization in heavy consumers. The ECDC and EU have established glycyrrhizin limits for food. The crisis illustrates that traditional medicines require dose-aware consumption — the same mechanism that made licorice a "harmonizer" and adrenal support creates a dangerous mineralocorticoid excess at high continuous doses.

## 8 · Practical Application

### Clinical Use

**Gastrointestinal (gastric ulcer, gastritis, GERD, IBS):**
- **DGL (deglycyrrhizinated licorice):** 380–760mg chewable tablet 3–4 times daily before meals
- Must be chewed (not swallowed whole) — salivary enzyme contact activates mucosal protective pathway
- Duration: 4–8 weeks for ulcer healing; ongoing for functional GI complaints
- Safety: DGL has glycyrrhizin removed → no mineralocorticoid risk → safe for long-term use and hypertension patients

**Anti-inflammatory / adrenal support:**
- Whole licorice root: 1–5g decoction or tincture daily
- Limit: <100mg glycyrrhizin/day (typically 5–15g dried root maximum) to avoid pseudohyperaldosteronism
- Duration: maximum 4–6 weeks continuous; then 2-week break minimum
- Monitor BP and potassium

**Respiratory (cough, bronchitis):**
- Licorice root tea: 1–3g dried root per cup (decoction, simmer 10–15 min)
- EMA THMP approved for cough and throat irritation
- 2–3 cups daily; maximum 4–6 weeks

**Chinese formulary:** *Gān cǎo* used as harmonizing component in virtually all classical CM compound preparations; dosing per formula (typically 1.5–9g per formula).

### Contraindications

- **Hypertension:** Whole licorice contraindicated (glycyrrhizin → mineralocorticoid excess → BP elevation); use DGL only
- **Hypokalemia:** Contraindicated; glycyrrhizin worsens potassium loss
- **Cardiac conditions (heart failure, arrhythmia):** Potassium loss from glycyrrhizin can precipitate arrhythmia; contraindicated with digoxin
- **Pregnancy:** Avoid; glycyrrhizin crosses placenta and has been associated with preterm delivery and lower neonatal birth weight (Finnish cohort study, Räikkönen 2010)
- **Chronic kidney disease:** Sodium retention risk; avoid whole licorice
- **Corticosteroid therapy:** Additive mineralocorticoid effect

### Drug Interactions

- **Digoxin:** Hypokalemia from glycyrrhizin increases digoxin toxicity — SIGNIFICANT INTERACTION
- **Antihypertensives:** Glycyrrhizin antagonizes antihypertensive drugs; use DGL with hypertensive patients
- **Diuretics:** Potassium-wasting diuretics + glycyrrhizin = additive hypokalemia risk
- **Corticosteroids:** Additive mineralocorticoid effect; glycyrrhizin inhibits metabolism of methylprednisolone → increased steroid levels
- **Oral contraceptives:** Potentiate mineralocorticoid effect; monitor BP

### Safety Profile

DGL: Excellent safety profile. No mineralocorticoid concerns.

Whole licorice: The mineralocorticoid concern is real and dose-dependent. EC has set guidance on daily glycyrrhizin intake from food sources. Doses producing symptoms typically require >100mg glycyrrhizin/day for weeks to months. For short-term use (2–4 weeks) at standard therapeutic doses in otherwise healthy adults with normal BP and potassium, whole licorice is generally safe. For patients with hypertension, cardiac conditions, or who are pregnant: use DGL only or avoid.
