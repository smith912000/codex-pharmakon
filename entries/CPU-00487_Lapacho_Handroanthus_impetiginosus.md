# Lapacho / Pau d'Arco / Taheebo

## *Handroanthus impetiginosus* (Mart. ex DC.) Mattos (syn. *Tabebuia impetiginosa*, *Tabebuia avellanedae*) | Family: Bignoniaceae

**Tradition Tags:** SA
**Energetic Classification:** No classical humoral attribution. South American indigenous use is not organised by a Galenic hot/cold system; the "cooling, drying, blood-cleansing" language attached to pau d'arco in modern Western herbal catalogues is an **inferred retrofit** by 20th-century commercial herbalism, not an indigenous doctrine. ### Origin & Tradition

*Handroanthus impetiginosus* is a large deciduous canopy tree of the Bignoniaceae, native to a broad neotropical band running from northern Mexico through Central America and into Brazil, Paraguay, Bolivia and northern Argentina. In Brazil it is *ipê-roxo* — the purple ipê — and it is one of the most conspicuous trees in the South American landscape, because it flowers on bare wood: the tree drops its leaves and then erupts into a dense magenta-violet crown before the new foliage appears. The timber is dense, oily and rot-resistant, which is why the Portuguese trade name *pau d'arco* ("bow wood") attached to it; indigenous groups used the heartwood for bows and tool handles long before it was a supplement.

The **SA** tag here denotes South American indigenous ethnobotany rather than a single codified tradition. The medicinal material is not the timber but the **inner bark** (the phloem layer, stripped from beneath the outer cork), decocted as a bitter reddish-brown tea. Ethnobotanical reports across Brazilian, Paraguayan and Argentine sources describe inner-bark preparations used for fevers, infections, inflammatory complaints, gastric ulceration and skin conditions including fungal infection and slow-healing wounds. The peer-reviewed literature retrieved for this entry consistently characterises the plant in exactly this folk-medical frame: the *Phytotherapy Research* study below states plainly that the bark "has been used in folk medicine as anti-inflammatory and in the treatment of ulcers, bacterial and fungal infections."

**A caution about the tradition claim.** Much of what circulates in English-language supplement marketing as ancient Inca or Amazonian "taheebo" lore is poorly sourced, and the specific attributions to named peoples vary between retellings. The general folk-medical use of the inner bark is well attested in the botanical literature. Specific claims about pre-Columbian anticancer use are **not** supportable from anything retrieved here and should be treated as commercial mythmaking. **[USER: verify]** any named-people attribution against a primary ethnobotanical source before it is printed.

The modern chapter matters more than the ancient one. In the 1960s Brazilian press coverage of ipê-roxo bark as a cancer remedy triggered enormous demand; the isolated naphthoquinone **lapachol** entered US National Cancer Institute screening; and by the 1980s "pau d'arco" / "taheebo" was one of the flagship alternative anticancer supplements of the North American health-food trade. That arc — real chemistry, real early signal, failed translation, surviving commercial afterlife — is the reason this entry exists.

### Active Components

The pharmacologically interesting fraction is the **quinone** fraction of the heartwood and inner bark:

- **Lapachol** — a prenylated 1,4-naphthoquinone (*para*-quinone); the historically famous constituent, and the one that carried the antitumour screening programme.
- **β-Lapachone** — an *ortho*-naphthoquinone. According to the *Phytochemistry* review retrieved below, β-lapachone was "originally isolated from the heartwood of *Handroanthus impetiginosus*" and can also be obtained synthetically from lapachol. This is the constituent with live modern oncology interest.
- **α-Lapachone** — a related naphthoquinone, less studied.
- **Naphtho[2,3-b]furan-4,9-diones** — a furanonaphthoquinone series; the scaffold from which the antimicrobial derivatives below were built.
- **Veratric acid** (3,4-dimethoxybenzoic acid) — a phenolic acid, notable not for activity but because it has been proposed as an analytical **QC marker** for commercial extracts.
- **Iridoid-related compounds** — including 1-dehydroxy-3,4-dihydroaucubigenin, isolated as the anti-obesity principle of a butanol fraction in the mouse study below.

Cultivator's note: quinone content is a heartwood/inner-bark phenomenon, concentrating in the tissue the tree uses to resist fungal and insect attack. The outer bark and the leaves are not the target material, and a product listing "pau d'arco bark" without specifying inner bark is already a quality signal.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Lapacho is filed as a **Foundation — Signature Study** substance, and its assignment is deliberately not therapeutic. It is here as the Codex's worked example of **the deflation**: how a genuine bioactive can be real at the bench, fail in the body, and still be sold for forty years on the strength of the bench result.

The practitioner's use case is threefold:

1. **A quinone-chemistry study specimen.** Redox-cycling quinones are a recurring motif across this volume. Lapacho gives a clean case of the mechanism: quinones generate reactive oxygen species and form adducts with thiol groups. That is exactly why they kill tumour cells in a dish — and exactly why they are hard to dose in a person.
2. **A supportive traditional preparation, at food-herb strength, for inflammatory and mucosal complaints** — with the honest caveat that the human evidence is one small open-label trial (below).
3. **A refusal exercise.** The practitioner should be able to state, without hedging, that pau d'arco is not a cancer therapy.

**Refusals of the received doctrine, entered here as corrections:**

- **"Pau d'arco is an anticancer herb."** REFUSED. No retrieved evidence supports human antineoplastic efficacy of the bark preparation. Bench activity of an isolated constituent is not efficacy of a tea.
- **"β-Lapachone is a proven cancer treatment."** REFUSED. The 2021 *Phytochemistry* review describes **phase II clinical trials being conducted** for pancreatic cancer — i.e. investigational, not established. Grade: experimental.
- **"Drinking the tea delivers β-lapachone."** REFUSED as unsupported. Nothing retrieved establishes meaningful systemic exposure to β-lapachone from a domestic bark decoction.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Form | Material | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Inner-bark decoction | Inner bark, simmered | The traditional form. Long simmer, not an infusion — the actives are poorly water-soluble, which is itself a limitation on the traditional route. No verified dose. |
| Encapsulated dried bark | Whole bark powder | **1050 mg/day for 8 weeks** is the only human-trial regimen retrieved (McClure et al. 2022, n=12, open-label, healthy women with primary dysmenorrhea). This is a *safety/tolerability* figure from one small trial, not a validated therapeutic dose. |
| Ethanolic extract | Inner bark | Used in the rodent gastric-ulcer work at 100 and 300 mg/kg. **Animal doses; do not extrapolate to humans.** |
| Topical | Extract/decoction | Traditional for skin complaints. Preclinical wound-healing support only. |

**No fabricated doses are given here.** Outside the single 1050 mg/day trial figure, no human dose for this plant is established in the retrieved literature.

**Quality control is a live problem.** Commercial pau d'arco products are widely reported to vary in composition, and the analytical literature reflects the need: Jin et al. developed and validated the "first simple and reliable quantitative method that could serve for the QC of taheebo," measuring veratric acid, and found 14.92–15.58 mg/g across three extract batches. The existence of that paper is direct evidence that the trade lacked a routine QC assay. **Specific claims that a given percentage of retail products are adulterated or lapachol-free are NOT verifiable from the retrieved literature and are omitted.**

### Scientific Correlation

**Grading each claim:**

- **Antimicrobial activity of the naphthoquinone scaffold — PRECLINICAL, well supported (congener data).** Yamashita et al. synthesised naphtho[2,3-b]furan-4,9-dione **derivatives** based on *Tabebuia avellanedae* constituents. The lead compound **NQ008 is a fluorine-containing synthetic derivative, explicitly a congener and NOT a plant constituent** — it showed broad activity including against MRSA, was bactericidal by MBC/MIC ratio and time-kill, and showed preliminary antiviral activity. This says the chemical scaffold is druggable. It says nothing about a bark tea.
- **Gastric ulcer healing — PRECLINICAL (rat).** Ethanolic bark extract reduced acetic-acid-induced gastric lesions by 44% and 36% (100 and 300 mg/kg), with increased mucus and cell proliferation.
- **Wound healing — PRECLINICAL (cell + mouse).** α- and β-lapachone accelerated healing and increased Sirt3 and VEGF expression; β-lapachone effective at lower concentrations.
- **Anti-obesity — PRECLINICAL (ovariectomised mouse).** 0.5% butanol extract reduced body weight and fat mass; active compound identified as 1-dehydroxy-3,4-dihydroaucubigenin.
- **Human efficacy for any indication — WEAK / SINGLE SMALL TRIAL.** The only human trial retrieved is McClure et al. 2022: **n=12, single-arm, open-label, no control group, 25% dropout.** Pain intensity fell significantly from baseline — but an uncontrolled open-label pain trial cannot separate drug effect from placebo and regression to the mean. Graded: suggestive, not evidence of efficacy.
- **Human antineoplastic efficacy — NULL.** No trial retrieved. No evidence.
- **Anticoagulant effect at supplement doses — NOT DEMONSTRATED in the retrieved human data, and this partially deflates our own hook.** The McClure trial deliberately measured **prothrombin times** — the investigators clearly shared the concern — and reported that most laboratory markers "stayed within normal limits throughout the study period with a few clinically mild abnormalities," with no FDA-serious adverse events. **HONEST CORRECTION:** the standard warning that pau d'arco prolongs prothrombin time is *not* confirmed by the one human trial that looked, at 1050 mg/day for 8 weeks in 12 healthy women. The theoretical basis (naphthoquinone/vitamin-K structural analogy) remains, and the trial is far too small to detect uncommon events, so the caution is retained on precautionary grounds — but it is retained as **plausible and unconfirmed**, not as demonstrated fact.
- **The NCI lapachol clinical-development history — NOT VERIFIED THIS RUN.** Searches for a primary paper documenting the abandonment of lapachol's clinical development due to dose-limiting toxicity and prothrombin prolongation returned **zero results** in PubMed. The account is widely repeated in secondary literature and is very likely true, but **the Codex will not print it as sourced fact.** **[USER: verify]** against the primary NCI-era clinical pharmacology literature (much of which predates comprehensive PubMed indexing) before this passage is treated as established. What *is* supported: reviews retrieved here place lapachol among naphthoquinones of anticancer interest while noting the class kills cells via ROS generation, uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation and adduct formation with -SH enzyme groups — a mechanism with no tumour selectivity, which is precisely the profile that fails in a whole organism.
- **β-Lapachone toxicity — CONTESTED.** The 2021 review states β-lapachone "presents low toxicity, with no signs of toxicity against alveolar macrophages, dermal fibroblast cells, hepatocytes, or kidney cells." That is a cell-culture statement, and it sits uneasily beside the general naphthoquinone toxicity described in the 2022 review. Treat "low toxicity" as an in-vitro finding, not a clinical safety claim.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Lapacho sits **outside the humoral systems**. It has no Ayurvedic dosha assignment, no TCM temperature/flavour designation, and no Galenic degree of heat or dryness, because it entered European and Asian awareness only after those systems were closed. Where modern Western herbal texts assign it "cool and dry" or class it as an alterative or "blood purifier," that is an inferred back-formation applied to a South American plant by practitioners working in a borrowed vocabulary. The Codex records it as such and does not treat it as traditional testimony.

The one energetic observation that *is* native to the material: the bitter, astringent, resinous quality of the inner-bark decoction is real and organoleptically obvious, and bitterness is the honest sensory correlate of the quinone load.

### Contraindications

⚠️ **This substance must NOT be used as, or in place of, oncological treatment.** There is no human evidence of antineoplastic efficacy. Substituting pau d'arco for chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgery or immunotherapy risks disease progression and death. Any practitioner presenting it as a cancer therapy is committing harm.

- **Anticoagulant / antiplatelet co-medication (warfarin and others) — avoid.** Precautionary. The naphthoquinone/vitamin-K structural relationship gives a plausible interaction mechanism. Note honestly that the one small human trial that measured prothrombin time did not find a significant effect; the caution stands on plausibility and inadequate power, not on demonstrated interaction.
- **Bleeding disorders, and the perioperative period** — avoid, on the same precautionary basis. Discontinue well in advance of surgery.
- **Pregnancy and lactation — contraindicated.** No safety data. Cytotoxic redox-active constituents in a developing organism is an unacceptable unknown.
- **Gastrointestinal intolerance is the common real-world effect.** Nausea and GI upset are the dose-limiting complaints in practice; in the McClure trial, 9 of 12 participants reported an adverse event, most characterised as mild, and 3 of 12 did not complete the study.
- **Hepatic or renal impairment** — avoid; no clearance data.
- **Children** — no data; do not use.
- **Product identity risk.** Bignoniaceae bark on the retail market is not reliably authenticated to species, and QC assays were only recently developed. Unverified material should not be dosed.
- **Isolated β-lapachone is not a self-administration substance.** It is an investigational agent in trials; it is not the tea, and it must not be self-sourced.
