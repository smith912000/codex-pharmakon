# Assian stone — Asios lithos (printed in the translation as "Asian stone"), Dioscorides 5-142
## *A pumice-coloured, spongy, light, brittle stone with a yellowish salt "flower" on its outside, used for ulcers, growths, gout, the spleen and lung disease; the translator gives no identification ("UNKNOWN")* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-142, *Asios lithos* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 10572; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 915, "ASIOS LITHOS", translator identification "UNKNOWN", class guess mineral; list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 868, "Asios Lithos", mineral, flagged as an uncertain identification; the book index sends the reader to page 816, where the English index entry reads "Asian stone"). The whole chapter was read. The Codex entry name "Assian" is not the spelling the translation prints: the chapter text says "Asian stone", and the Greek heading is ASIOS. The chapter reads (lines 10575-10579; the page break at 816 falls inside it):

"Asian stone should be taken which is like pumice stone in colour — spongy, light and brittle, with yellowish partitions at the bottom. The flower [salt] of it is the yellowish salt fluid which gathers on the outside of the stones, which is thin in consistency and sometimes white in colour, sometimes like pumice stone, inclining to a yellow. Applied to the tongue it bites a little. Both of them are astringent, corrode gently, and dissolve small swellings used with terminthos [1-91] rosin or liquid pitch, and the flower [salt] is considered the strongest. Furthermore, dried, it heals old hard-to-close ulcers, represses abnormal fleshy growths, and with honey it cleans those like mushrooms, and wild ulcers. It fills the hollow sores of ulcers and cleans them with honey, and mixed with wax ointment it stops feeding ulcers. For gout it is used in plasters with bean meal, and for the spleen with vinegar and unslaked lime [quicklime — calx viva]. The flower [salt] (licked in with honey) is good for tuberculosis of the lungs. Stone baths are made from this into which the gouty put in their feet and are helped, and coffins are made of it that dissolve flesh. It relieves very fleshy and thick bodies [weight loss] smeared on instead of saltpetre [potassium nitrate] in a bath. If you want to wash it do it in the same way as cadmia [5-84]."

The chapter thus describes two products from one source: the stone itself and its "flower [salt]", a yellowish fluid that collects on the outside of the stones. It gives a colour and texture for the stone (pumice colour; spongy, light, brittle; yellowish partitions), a sensory test for the flower (it "bites a little" on the tongue), a statement of relative strength (the flower is "considered the strongest"), and a long list of uses. It does not say where the stone is found: the name carries the adjective, but the chapter names no place.

**Other chapters that refer to it.** Chapter 5-137 on *adarkes* says that the material "is like the flower [salt] of the stone of Asia in colour" (line 10549). That chapter is about another substance and is cited here only to show that Dioscorides used the "flower of the stone of Asia" as a known comparison for colour. The translator's index also lists the stone only under "Asian stone 816" (index, line 14795) and the Greek name under ASIOS LITHOS 816 (line 12039); no other chapter was found that treats it.

**Identity.** The translator gives no identification ("UNKNOWN"), and the Codex list marks the identification uncertain. The text does not say what mineral this is. The chapter's remark that coffins made of it "dissolve flesh" is quoted exactly as printed and is not interpreted here. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None can be stated from the chapter. The stone is unidentified, so no composition is asserted. In general terms, a yellowish salt fluid that gathers on the surface of a porous stone and that bites the tongue is an evaporative mineral deposit, a mixture of water-soluble salts whose makeup depends on the stone and the site; this is a general statement about such deposits, not a reading of the chapter, and no composition is claimed for the Asian stone.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records an unidentified, gently corroding stone and its salt flower as a multipurpose external material (ulcers, growths, small swellings, gout), with the flower licked in honey for lung disease and the stone smeared in a bath for "very fleshy and thick bodies". The identification is uncertain, the composition unknown, and the text itself calls it corrosive and says the stone-coffins "dissolve flesh". No modern protocol is supplied, and the Codex does not recommend internal use of an unidentified salt.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations given in 5-142 are, in the chapter's order:

- the stone or its flower used "with terminthos [1-91] rosin or liquid pitch" for small swellings;
- the stone "dried" for old ulcers and abnormal growths; "with honey" for mushroom-like and "wild" ulcers; "with honey" to clean and fill hollow sores; "mixed with wax ointment" for "feeding ulcers";
- "in plasters with bean meal" for gout; "with vinegar and unslaked lime [quicklime — calx viva]" for the spleen;
- the flower "licked in with honey" for tuberculosis of the lungs;
- "stone baths" in which the gouty put their feet; and stone coffins;
- the stone "smeared on instead of saltpetre [potassium nitrate] in a bath" for the very fleshy.

Washing: "If you want to wash it do it in the same way as cadmia [5-84]." Chapter 5-84 describes washing cadmia: "Being pounded with water in a mortar, the water then poured away until no filth remains on it washes it; then it is made into balls and put in jars" (line 10227). The Asian-stone chapter only points to that method and does not itself describe it.

No quantity, weight or duration is given for any of these. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. What the herbal offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, adulteration detection), and this chapter shows part of it: it says what to select (a pumice-coloured, spongy, light, brittle stone with yellowish partitions), gives a sensory assay for the flower (it "bites a little" on the tongue), distinguishes the stone from its flower and ranks the flower stronger, and refers the reader to the washing method of another chapter. It gives no place of origin, no season of gathering and no adulterant. The chapter is therapeutic and carries no cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to take from it for cultivation beyond the author's habit of selection and sensory test: say what the good material looks like and what it does to the tongue. The material itself is an unidentified, corrosive salt-bearing stone, and the text's own wording (it "corrodes") is the caution. It is not a recommended practice. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Corrosive:** the text says it "corrodes gently"; salts of this kind burn broken skin, the eyes and the mouth.
- **Unidentified material:** the identification is uncertain; a stone of unknown composition should not be swallowed, licked or applied to ulcers.
- **Quicklime:** the spleen preparation calls for unslaked lime, which burns skin and eyes.
- **Serious conditions:** gout, chronic ulcers, abnormal growths, spleen complaints and lung disease (including tuberculosis) need medical assessment.
