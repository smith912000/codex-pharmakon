# I-485 | Spearmint (*Mentha spicata* L.)

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Mentha spicata* L.  
**Family:** Lamiaceae (Mint family)  
**Common names:** Spearmint, garden mint, common mint, lamb's mint, mackerel mint, pea mint, Our Lady's mint  
**Synonyms:** *Mentha viridis* (L.) L.; *M. crispa* auct.; *M. sylvestris* L. (partially)

**Etymology:** *spicata* from Latin *spica* ("spike") — the spiked flower arrangement. "Spearmint" from the spear-shaped leaves. *Mentha viridis* — "green mint" (contrast with black peppermint). "Lady's mint" — association with the Virgin Mary in Christian European tradition, paralleling holy basil's goddess association in Hindu tradition.

**Relationship to peppermint:** *M. spicata* (spearmint) is one of the two parent species of peppermint (*M. × piperita* = *M. aquatica* × *M. spicata*). Spearmint itself is sexually reproducing (fertile seeds), unlike peppermint. The EO of spearmint is dominated by carvone (50–80%), not menthol — creating a fundamentally different pharmacological profile. Spearmint is milder and has distinct hormonal modulating properties.

**Pharmacological distinction from peppermint:**
- Peppermint: menthol-dominant (30–55%) → TRPM8; VGCC; strong antispasmodic; cooling
- Spearmint: carvone-dominant (50–80%) → anti-androgenic (5α-reductase inhibition + androgen receptor antagonism); milder GI; lacks peppermint's strong TRPM8 cooling

**Botanical description:** Perennial, 30–100 cm; stems erect, square; leaves opposite, sessile or subsessile (narrow petiole), ovate to lanceolate, serrate, wrinkled (rugose), bright green; white to pink flowers in terminal interrupted spikes (*spica* = spike); rhizomatous, spreading. Widely cultivated in herb gardens.

**Part used:** Leaves (fresh or dried); essential oil  
**Preparation:** Infusion (spearmint tea — primary domestic use); tincture; EO (food flavoring; topical)

## 3. Convergence Analysis

**The digestive mint — shared with peppermint but gentler:** Spearmint's convergence pattern across DMM, CM, SBJ, and SS mirrors peppermint's but with lower intensity, reflecting spearmint's milder pharmacological profile. The shared *Mentha* genus digestive application is consistent across traditions. Spearmint was the primary culinary mint of European and Middle Eastern cooking (lamb with mint sauce; mint tea in Morocco) before peppermint's creation; it remains the primary culinary mint for most non-medicinal applications.

**Hormonal modulation — a pharmacologically unique distinction:** Spearmint's most distinctive modern finding is anti-androgenic activity — carvone and other spearmint compounds inhibit 5α-reductase (reducing testosterone-to-DHT conversion) and antagonize androgen receptors. This anti-androgenic property is entirely absent in peppermint (menthol-dominant) and was not encoded in traditional medicine — demonstrating that systematic modern pharmacology can discover applications not accessible through empirical traditional observation (hormonal effects are invisible to observation without biochemical measurement). The anti-androgenic application for PCOS (polycystic ovary syndrome) represents genuinely novel clinical territory.

**Emmenagogue encoding across traditions:** DMM and CM both note spearmint's emmenagogue (menstrual-promoting) property — consistent with the anti-androgenic mechanism potentially normalizing the androgen excess characteristic of certain menstrual disorders. This traditional observation, appearing independently in two traditions, gains new mechanistic grounding from the modern anti-androgenic findings.

## 4. Phytochemical Composition

### Essential Oil (0.5–1.5% leaf)
- **(-)-Carvone** (50–80% EO; L-carvone; mint-characteristic ketone; anti-androgenic; anti-spasmodic; different enantiomer than D-carvone = dill/caraway character)
- **Limonene** (5–25% EO; monoterpene; antioxidant; antiproliferative; d-limonene configuration)
- **1,8-Cineole** (trace to 5%; expectorant; bronchodilatory)
- **Linalool** (1–5%; anxiolytic)
- **Menthone** (1–5%; lower than peppermint)
- **Menthol** (trace; minimal — contrast with peppermint)
- **Piperitone** (3–8%; spasmodic at high doses; anti-spasmodic at therapeutic doses)

**Carvone enantiomers — opposite flavors from same molecule:**
- (-)-Carvone (L-carvone) = spearmint character (fresh, cool, minty)
- (+)-Carvone (D-carvone) = caraway/dill character
- These are mirror-image molecules (enantiomers) — same formula, different 3D arrangement, totally different olfactory receptor binding → entirely different smells
- Pharmacological effects: (-)-carvone has anti-androgenic properties; D-carvone less studied — chiral pharmacology paradigm

### Phenolic Acids
- **Rosmarinic acid** (Lamiaceae family; GABA-T inhibitor; anti-inflammatory)
- **Caffeic acid** derivatives
- **Luteolin** and glycosides (anti-inflammatory)
- **Apigenin** (GABA-A modulation)

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

### Anti-androgenic (Primary pharmacologically distinct mechanism)
**5α-Reductase inhibition:**
- Carvone and spearmint EO: inhibit 5α-reductase type 1 and type 2 → reduced conversion of testosterone to dihydrotestosterone (DHT)
- DHT is more potent androgen than testosterone → 5α-reductase inhibition reduces androgenic stimulus
- Mechanism similar to finasteride (pharmaceutical 5α-reductase inhibitor for BPH/hair loss) but far weaker — typical inhibitor concentration significantly higher than pharmaceutical; clinical relevance at realistic consumption levels under investigation

**Androgen receptor antagonism:**
- Spearmint extract: competitive androgen receptor (AR) antagonism *in vitro* → reduces AR transcriptional activity
- Combined with 5α-reductase inhibition: double anti-androgenic mechanism

**Clinical relevance — PCOS:**
- Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) features androgen excess (hyperandrogenism) → hirsutism, acne, irregular menstrual cycles
- Spearmint anti-androgenic effect: potentially reduces free androgens → reduces hirsutism; may improve hormonal balance
- 2 human RCTs (Grant 2010; Akdoğan 2007) directly testing this hypothesis

### Gastrointestinal Antispasmodic
- Carvone: smooth muscle spasmolytic (weaker than menthol; different mechanism — possibly Ca²⁺ antagonism at lower potency)
- Traditional mint tea for digestive upset, nausea, colic
- Safe for children and pregnancy (absent peppermint's strong LES relaxation at culinary doses)

### Carminative / Digestive
- Carvone volatile: reduces gas production by colonic bacteria (bacteriostatic at relevant concentrations in gut)
- Promotes gastric motility (mild prokinetic effect)
- Reduces nausea via central and peripheral pathways

### Antimicrobial (Milder than peppermint)
- Carvone MIC: *S. aureus* ~1–2 mg/mL (less potent than thymol or menthol); *E. coli* ~2–4 mg/mL
- Antifungal: *Candida* MIC 1–4 mg/mL
- Limonene: synergistic antimicrobial; additional antiproliferative in tumor cells

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**Anti-androgenic (PCOS, hirsutism):**
- Akdoğan et al. 2007 (*Phytotherapy Research*): spearmint tea (1 cup twice daily × 5 days) vs chamomile tea control (n=21, crossover): significantly reduced free testosterone; reduced LH (luteinizing hormone) — Level A (small RCT)
- Grant et al. 2010 (*Phytotherapy Research*): spearmint tea vs chamomile (n=42, 30-day RCT): significantly reduced free testosterone and total testosterone; reduced LH; women reported reduced subjective hirsutism — Level A
- Both trials show consistent hormonal effect; larger trials needed for clinical endpoint (hirsutism scoring)

**Digestive / gastrointestinal:**
- Traditional evidence; no RCTs specifically for spearmint in IBS or dyspepsia
- Commission E does not have a specific spearmint monograph (covered under general *Mentha* herbal preparations)

**Cognitive (limited):**
- Herrlinger et al. 2018 (*J Alternative Complementary Med*): spearmint extract (900 mg) vs placebo in older adults with memory concerns (n=90, 90-day RCT): improved working memory and spatial working memory — Level A (interesting finding; requires replication)

**Summary by evidence level:**
- Level A: Anti-androgenic/PCOS (Akdoğan 2007; Grant 2010); Cognitive (Herrlinger 2018)
- Level B: Digestive traditional evidence
- Level C: Traditional use across multiple pharmacopoeial traditions; general *Mentha* Commission E coverage
- Level D: 5α-Reductase and AR inhibition mechanisms; antimicrobial

## 7. Phenomenological Record

**The mint of Moroccan hospitality:** Spearmint tea (*nana* tea — from *na'na* = Arabic/Berber term for spearmint) is the fundamental beverage of Moroccan and Maghrebi hospitality — three glasses of green tea poured over fresh spearmint, served in ornate glasses, offered to every guest. The preparation is ritualized; refusing tea is considered impolite. The spearmint tea tradition (North Africa, Middle East) parallels the tulsi tea tradition (India) — a daily medicinal herb consumed in ritual social contexts, providing ongoing background pharmacological exposure to the whole population. The anti-androgenic carvone in spearmint tea, consumed daily by millions, may contribute incidentally to hormonal regulation across a population.

**Lamb and mint sauce — a flavor pairing encoding pharmacology:** The European tradition of serving lamb with mint sauce (spearmint) appears in cookbook and culinary records from medieval England onward. The standard explanation is that mint's freshness offsets lamb's richness. A pharmacological reading: lamb fat is proinflammatory (saturated fat); spearmint is anti-inflammatory (rosmarinic acid, carvone); the traditional pairing may encode an empirical understanding of dietary balance. Whether or not this is the "reason," the pharmacological complementarity is genuine.

**Cornmint (*M. arvensis*) — the most commercially produced mint:** While spearmint and peppermint dominate European and American markets, *M. arvensis* (cornmint, field mint, bo he) is the most commercially cultivated mint globally — primarily in India and China — for menthol extraction. Most of the world's "menthol" in toothpaste, throat lozenges, and cigarettes comes from natural cornmint EO (75–95% L-menthol by content) rather than peppermint. This creates a global menthol supply chain based on a species different from the one studied in most pharmacological research.

**Enantiomers and taste — chiral pharmacology in food:** The L-carvone/D-carvone paradigm is one of the simplest demonstrations that chirality matters pharmacologically. The two mirror-image molecules activate different olfactory receptor configurations — L-carvone binds receptors encoding "mint"; D-carvone binds receptors encoding "dill/caraway." This taste-pharmacology chiral relationship has been used in organic chemistry education for decades as the paradigmatic example of why chirality matters in drug design: the same connectivity, dramatically different biological activity.

## 8. Practical Application

**Energetic tier:** ◉ Qi (digestive; anti-androgenic; mild carminative)

### Preparation Methods and Dosing

**Spearmint tea (primary use — PCOS, digestive, culinary):**
- 1–2 g dried spearmint per 250 mL boiling water; steep 5–10 min; 1–2 cups daily
- For PCOS/hormonal application: 1 cup twice daily (Grant 2010 protocol: 1 cup of fresh spearmint tea 2× daily)
- Spearmint tea is safe throughout pregnancy (distinguish from peppermint tea at high doses)

**Tincture (1:5, 40% ethanol):**
- 2–3 mL, 3× daily for digestive applications

**Essential oil (topical, culinary — not therapeutic internal dosing needed for most applications):**
- Culinary: food-grade carvone/spearmint EO as flavoring
- Topical diluted 1–2%: mild antimicrobial; pain relief (weaker than peppermint menthol)

**Duration for hormonal applications:**
- Minimum 30 days to observe hormonal effect (Grant protocol)
- Ongoing use appropriate for chronic PCOS management

### Drug Interactions

- **Hormone therapy (OCP, HRT):** Anti-androgenic effect; theoretically complementary to anti-androgenic OCP (drospirenone) but monitor for additive effects
- **Finasteride, dutasteride:** Additive 5α-reductase inhibition; theoretical synergy; not clinically tested
- **Iron supplements:** Rosmarinic acid chelation; separate by 2 hours

### Contraindications

- Significantly safer than peppermint for: GERD (does not strongly relax LES); infants (no strong menthol); pregnancy (culinary and tea doses)
- **Concentrated carvone EO:** Piperitone and pulegone precursors at high EO doses; avoid large medicinal doses of neat EO
- **Known Lamiaceae allergy**

### Advantages over Peppermint in Specific Populations

| Population | Peppermint | Spearmint |
|---|---|---|
| GERD | CONTRAINDICATED (LES relaxation) | Generally safe |
| Infants/children | CONTRAINDICATED (near face) | Safe culinary/tea doses |
| Pregnancy | Caution at high doses | Generally safe at culinary/tea doses |
| PCOS/hirsutism | No anti-androgenic effect | Primary herbal option |
| Mild digestive upset | Effective | Gentler alternative |
