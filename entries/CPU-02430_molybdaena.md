# Molybdaena (lead ore) — Molubdaina (MOLUBDAINA), Dioscorides 5-100

## *A yellow, glittering material "like frothy silver" that Dioscorides says is made from silver and gold and is also found dug out of the earth about Sebastia and Corycum, with properties like litharge and lead slag; the translator marks it TOXIC and glosses it in several incompatible ways. The Codex first-pass guess of galena (lead sulphide) is not supported by the chapter, so the identification is unresolved and of low confidence* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** The translator marks this chapter TOXIC. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should take or apply molybdaina.

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder ("confidence probable") with a first-pass check of galena (PbS). The chapter does not support that check, as set out under Identity below. A search of `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` for "molubdaina", "plumbago" and "molybd" found this chapter, back-references to it in the silver-slag chapter (5-101), the name index, and nothing in another tradition on disk.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-100, *Molubdaina* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 10327; name-index row in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` at row 873, translator gloss quoted below, class guess mineral; the list row in `_sources/dioscorides.csv` is at line 826, where the entry is mis-labelled as a plant; the book index lists "Plumbago 793"). The whole chapter was read. It reads (line 10332):

"The best plumbago looks like frothy silver — yellow, somewhat glittering, and a tawny yellow when pounded into small pieces. Boiled with oil it becomes like liver in colour. That which is an airy or lead colour is worthless. It is made from silver and gold, and there is some found which is dug out of the earth about Sebastia and Corycum. The best of this is neither like scoria [slag], nor stony, but yellow and glittering. It has properties like lithargyrum [5-102] and [slag] of lead, and is washed and burnt the same way. It is most effectively mixed with gentle plasters called lipara, and is effective with plasters that are not corrosive — promoting the growth of flesh in a wound or sore, and forming new skin. It is not suitable for those plasters that close open cuts and clean."

The chapter thus gives: tests for the best (it looks like frothy silver, is yellow and glittering, tawny when powdered, and turns liver-coloured when boiled in oil; an "airy or lead" colour is worthless); two sources (it is "made from silver and gold", and some is "dug out of the earth about Sebastia and Corycum", where the best is "neither like scoria nor stony"); a comparison (properties like litharge and lead slag; washed and burned the same way); and uses (mixed with gentle, non-corrosive plasters called *lipara* to promote flesh and new skin in wounds or sores, but not suitable for plasters that close and clean open cuts).

**The translator's gloss.** The name-index row, which this entry follows, reads: "Silver Salts and Gold Salts Plumbago — Molybdos [Greek] — Lead — in this manuscript Molybdomenite — Selenite of Lead TOXIC." The text of the chapter carries a further note (line 10331): "Old English — Plumbago is native or mineralised carbon. Molybdenum is a metallic element — a silvery metal — symbol Mo. Molybdenite is disulphide of Molybdenum." The translator uses "plumbago" as the name of the substance in the chapter's text and notes that in this manuscript it means lead, not graphite. Those glosses disagree with each other; the book index lists the item as "Plumbago".

**Related chapters.** Silver slag (5-101, *Skoria arguroy*, line 10338) is said to have "the same strength as plumbago [5-100]" and to be mixed with black plasters and plasters for forming new skin. Litharge (5-102, CPU-02428) is the main comparison. The lead slag (5-97, CPU-02424) and the lead stone (5-98, CPU-02425) are separate entries and are not folded in.

**Identity.** The chapter's description (a yellow glittering product made from silver and gold, also dug at certain places, resembling litharge and washed and burned like it) fits a lead-bearing material or a by-product of silver and gold refining, with a natural variant. It does not describe galena, a grey metallic lead sulphide, and does not say the material is an ore. The first-pass check "galena (lead ore)" and the Codex working title "lead ore" come from the translator's "lead" and are not confirmed by the chapter. Our identification is unresolved. ### Active Components

None can be stated from the chapter. The chapter ties the material to litharge and to lead slag, and the translator glosses it as a lead material, so lead compounds are the plausible main constituent, as for litharge (lead monoxide). This is a general inference from the comparisons and is not asserted as the composition of the material.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Toxic.** Dioscorides records molybdaina as a component of gentle, non-corrosive plasters that promote the growth of flesh and new skin in wounds and sores. Plasters made with lead compounds put lead on open wounds, from which it can be absorbed. The material is unidentified. The Codex supplies no protocol and does not recommend this material.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter says the material "is washed and burnt the same way" as litharge and lead slag (line 10332), so the methods are in 5-102 (litharge: broken to the size of carya, blown on coals, washed like cadmia, quenched with vinegar or wine) and 5-97 (lead slag: washed in a mortar, settled, made into tablets). It is mixed with *lipara* plasters. No quantities are given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any quantity from this entry.** [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. What the herbal offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, adulteration detection), and this chapter shows it: colour and glitter as the mark of the best, an oil boil that turns the best liver-coloured, rejection of an "airy or lead" colour, and two named places of origin. The chapter is therapeutic and technical and carries no cultivation status; the translator's TOXIC mark is the caution.

### Contraindications

- **TOXIC.** Lead compounds are poisonous. Do not ingest, inhale or put this material on wounds.
- **Wounds and sores:** lead absorbs through open wounds; the chapter's use in plasters is not safe.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children:** no statement in the sources retrieved. Lead is especially harmful in pregnancy and childhood. [USER: verify]
- **Identity:** the material is unresolved; do not assume galena, graphite, molybdenite or any other mineral is meant.
