# Scilla amoena — Bolbos emetikos (Dioscorides 2-201)

## *Scilla amoena, the first of two names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 2-201 ("Scilla amoena — Nodding Squill"), with Ornithogalum arabicum ("Great-flowered Star of Bethlehem") printed beside it* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 6311) prints two names with no choice between them, and the row takes the first. It was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *2-201. BOLBOS EMETIKOS*, heading at line 6309, SUGGESTED line at 6311, text at line 6312. It stands in Book Two, after *2-200. BOLBOS EDODIMOS* (heading at line 6301) and before *2-202. SKILLA* (heading at line 6314). The translators' index gives *Scilla amoena* at page 336 (lines 17430 and 17532) and *Bulbus vomitorius* at page 336 (line 15115).

The whole chapter reads: "Bulbus vomitorius has more flexible leaves — similar to a bridle and far bigger than the edible; and a root with a black bark similar to the bulbus [above]. The root eaten alone (as well as a decoction of it taken as a drink) cures disorders of the bladder and encourages vomiting."

The chapter is two sentences. The first describes by comparison only: the leaves are "more flexible", "similar to a bridle" (the Greek image is not explained in the text) and "far bigger than the edible", and the root has "a black bark" like that of "the bulbus [above]". The comparisons rest on the preceding chapter, 2-200, whose edible bulbus is "the edible" here; this entry does not read that chapter's description into 2-201. The second sentence gives the use: the root eaten alone, and a decoction of it drunk, "cures disorders of the bladder and encourages vomiting". The Latin name printed, *bulbus vomitorius*, is the "vomitory" bulb, which agrees with the Greek title *emetikos* and with the vomiting action. The chapter prints no other names.

**Neighbouring chapters.** 2-200 (*BOLBOS EDODIMOS*) is the subject of the existing entry I_1147 (*Ornithogalum luteum*), which quotes the 2-201 chapter only as a neighbour and says it does not carry 2-201's statements over. 2-202 (*SKILLA*, the medicinal squill, SUGGESTED *Scilla maritima*) is a different chapter, labelled "VERY POISONOUS", and is not read into this entry.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read, and the chapter prints none. It is a general fact that *Scilla* is a bulb-forming genus of the asparagus family. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the translators' *Scilla amoena* or of the *Ornithogalum* printed beside it. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides reports that the root eaten alone, or a decoction of it drunk, cures disorders of the bladder and encourages vomiting. These are the text's reports. The Codex gives no protocol, and an emetic root is not something the Codex recommends. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

2-201 gives two preparations: the root "eaten alone", and a decoction of it "taken as a drink". It gives no quantity, frequency or duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No study of the plant was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that inducing vomiting is not a safe or recommended way to treat a bladder complaint, and that bulbs of this family can be toxic. A modern fact about one species would not by itself bear on what Dioscorides says, because the identity rests on a translators' suggestion between two names in different genera.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. It prints the leaf comparison (more flexible, bridle-like, far bigger than the edible) and the black bark of the root.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use. The part of the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition that is present here is the distinction of one bulb from another by leaf and by root bark, which is how the chapter separates the emetic bulb from the edible one. The plant is given no status beyond that of an emetic root with a use for bladder disorders. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a two-sentence record of an emetic bulb distinguished from an edible one, without amounts, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning, though the root is an emetic by the chapter's own account. Vomiting induced for a bladder complaint is not advice, and bladder disorders need medical assessment. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion between two plants, so no wild bulb should be gathered or eaten on the strength of this entry, and the bulb of the next chapter's squill is marked "VERY POISONOUS" in the same text.
