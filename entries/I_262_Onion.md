# I-262: Allium cepa (Onion)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-262 | Amaryllidaceae (formerly Alliaceae)**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Allium cepa* L.
**Family:** Amaryllidaceae (Alliaceae)

**Common Names:**
- English: Onion, Common Onion, Bulb Onion
- Arabic: *Basal* (بصل)
- Hindi: *Pyaz* (प्याज़)
- Mandarin: *Yángcōng* (洋葱) — "foreign onion" (distinguishing from native scallion)
- Hebrew: *Batzal* (בצל)
- Greek: *Krommyon* (κρόμμυον)
- Latin: *Cepa* — standard culinary/medical term through Roman era

**Taxonomy Note:** *A. cepa* belongs to the *Allium* genus alongside garlic (I-261), wild garlic (I-263), and leek (*A. ampeloprasum*). Like garlic, recently reclassified from Liliaceae → Alliaceae → Amaryllidaceae.

**Cultivars and Chemical Variation:**
Onion cultivars vary substantially in flavonoid content — a critical pharmacological variable:
- **Red/purple onions:** Highest quercetin + anthocyanin content (outer layers richest)
- **Yellow onions:** High quercetin; moderate total flavonoid
- **White onions:** Lowest quercetin content; mildest flavor
- **Shallots (*A. ascalonicum* / *A. cepa* var. aggregatum):** Highest quercetin concentration per gram of any *Allium*; intermediate medicinally
- **Spring/green onions:** Immature bulbs; higher in some bioactives but smaller dose per serving

**Morphology:** Biennial bulbous herb; 50–100 cm; hollow cylindrical leaves; white-purple globose inflorescence; papery-tunicated bulb. Characteristic concentric layers of fleshy leaf bases (the "onion" as consumed).

**Parts Used:** Bulb (fresh, dried, powdered); juice; outer dry skins (concentrated quercetin); aerial green tops.

**Origin and Distribution:** Central Asia (likely modern Uzbekistan/Tajikistan); cultivated globally 5,000+ years. Among the most widely cultivated vegetables worldwide; world production >100 million tonnes annually.

**Chemical Distinction from Garlic (I-261):**
- Garlic: Primary bioactives = organosulfur compounds (allicin, ajoene, SAC)
- Onion: Primary bioactives = flavonoids (quercetin, kaempferol, quercetin glucosides) + organosulfur (propanthial-S-oxide) + fructooligosaccharides (FOS)
- Onion's signature tear-inducing compound, propanthial-S-oxide (the "lachrymatory factor"), is distinct from allicin and has minimal medicinal relevance; it is an accidental artifact of the same enzymatic machinery

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Flavonoids (Primary Active Class)

**1. Quercetin — 250–350 mg/kg fresh weight (yellow onion)**
- Dominant flavonol; concentrated in outer dry layers and first 1-2 inner rings
- Quercetin aglycone: poorly absorbed but extensively metabolized by gut microbiome
- **Quercetin-3-glucoside (isoquercitrin):** primary glycoside form; superior oral absorption vs. aglycone
- **Quercetin-4'-glucoside (spiraeoside):** onion-specific glycoside; also well absorbed

**2. Isorhamnetin** — methylated quercetin metabolite; also present as primary compound in red onions

**3. Kaempferol glycosides** — minor; anti-inflammatory; anti-proliferative

**4. Anthocyanins (red/purple onions only)**
- Cyanidin-3-glucoside; cyanidin-3-laminaribioside; peonidin derivatives
- Anti-inflammatory; antioxidant; cardiovascular protective

### Organosulfur Compounds

**5. Propanthial-S-oxide** — the lachrymatory factor (tear gas compound); formed by alliinase acting on trans-(+)-S-(1-propenyl)-L-cysteine sulfoxide; volatile; negligible medicinal activity; evaporates on exposure to air

**6. Allyl propyl disulfide (APDS)**
- Anti-thrombotic; moderate platelet inhibition
- Less potent than garlic's ajoene but in same mechanistic class

**7. S-methylcysteine sulfoxide (SMCS)**
- Anti-diabetic activity; direct glucose metabolism modulation
- Present in onion, garlic, leek (Allium family marker)

### Polysaccharides

**8. Fructooligosaccharides (FOS) — inulin-type fructans**
- 2–6% fresh weight
- Non-digestible by human amylases; intact to colon
- Selective substrate for *Bifidobacterium*, *Lactobacillus* (prebiotic)
- Short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) fermentation product → colonic epithelial energy; butyrate production

### Other

**9. Ascorbic acid (Vitamin C)** — ~7 mg/100g

**10. Chromium** — trace mineral; historically associated with onion's anti-diabetic activity (now understood primarily as SMCS-quercetin mechanism)

**11. Prostaglandin A1, A2** — found in onion; anti-hypertensive; unusual in plants; may contribute to cardiovascular effects

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### 1. Cardiovascular: Anti-Hypertensive and Anti-Platelet
- **Quercetin:** eNOS (endothelial nitric oxide synthase) upregulation → vasodilation; also direct Ca²⁺ channel modulation in vascular smooth muscle
- **APDS:** TXA₂ (thromboxane A₂) inhibition → anti-platelet; weaker than ajoene but same class
- **Prostaglandins A1/A2:** direct vasodilatory activity
- **Quercetin:** ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibition (IC₅₀ ~100 μM; contributes to BP reduction)

### 2. Anti-Inflammatory (Quercetin Primary)
- **COX-1/COX-2 inhibition:** Quercetin non-selective COX inhibitor; IC₅₀ 30–100 μM
- **5-LOX (5-lipoxygenase) inhibition:** blocks leukotriene synthesis; explains anti-allergic/anti-asthmatic activity
- **NF-κB inhibition:** quercetin (via IKK suppression) reduces pro-inflammatory cytokine transcription
- **Histamine release inhibition:** quercetin inhibits mast cell degranulation; direct anti-allergic mechanism

### 3. Anti-Diabetic
- **Quercetin:** GLUT4 translocation enhancement; PPARγ activation; α-glucosidase inhibition
- **SMCS:** Direct insulin-mimetic activity; SMCS reduces blood glucose in streptozotocin diabetic rats via non-insulin-dependent mechanism
- **FOS:** Reduces postprandial glucose absorption by slowing gastric transit; feeds glucose-lowering butyrate-producing bacteria

### 4. Pre-biotic / Gut Microbiome
- **FOS:** selectively feeds *Bifidobacterium* spp. and *Lactobacillus* spp.
- Fermentation product: butyrate → colonocyte energy source; anti-inflammatory; colon cancer protective
- Bifidogenic effect measurable within 1 week of daily onion consumption (~100g)

### 5. Anti-Allergic (Quercetin + FOS)
- **Quercetin:** mast cell stabilization; histamine H₁ receptor competitive inhibition
- **5-LOX inhibition:** reduces LTC₄/LTD₄ (bronchoconstrictive leukotrienes) → anti-asthmatic
- Clinical allergen cross-reactivity: onion allergy overlaps with birch pollen sensitization (lipid transfer protein homology)

### 6. Antimicrobial (Quercetin + Organosulfur)
- **Quercetin:** membrane disruption + topoisomerase inhibition in bacteria
- **APDS:** mild thiol-reactive antimicrobial (much weaker than allicin)
- Traditional wound poultice use validated: quercetin IC₅₀ against *S. aureus* ~50–100 μg/mL

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Anti-Hypertensive:**
- **Quercetin supplementation (onion-derived, 150 mg/day):** RCT — statistically significant reduction in systolic BP (-3.6 mmHg, p=0.049) in pre-hypertensive men (Egert 2009, *British Journal of Nutrition*)
- **Onion extract (quercetin-rich):** Multiple small RCTs; consistent direction; effect smaller than garlic's allicin-based mechanism
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (consistent direction across RCTs; effect size modest)

**Anti-Diabetic:**
- **Onion juice (100 mL/day):** Controlled trial — significant fasting glucose reduction in Type 2 DM (Taj Eldin 2010)
- **SMCS quercetin animal data:** Robust; clinical translation less studied than garlic
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (human studies exist; RCT quality modest)

**Anti-Allergic / Anti-Asthmatic:**
- **Quercetin (500 mg/day):** Exercise-induced asthma — significant reduction in forced expiratory volume decline vs. placebo (Talirevic & Jelena 2012)
- **Onion skin extract (quercetin-rich):** Hay fever pilot data; anti-histamine properties confirmed in vitro + small human trials
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (mechanistically solid; RCT data emerging)

**Pre-biotic:**
- **Inulin/FOS 5–8 g/day:** Multiple RCTs — consistent *Bifidobacterium* increase, SCFA production, improved constipation; onion as FOS source validated in human microbiome studies
- **Evidence Grade: Level A** (for prebiotic/bifidogenic effect; well-established for FOS class broadly)

**Anti-Cancer (Epidemiological):**
- **Quercetin epidemiology:** High fruit and vegetable quercetin intake inversely associated with lung cancer (Knekt 1997, Finland cohort n=9,959), colorectal cancer, and gastric cancer risk
- **Allium vegetable intake:** Multiple prospective cohort studies associate highest vs. lowest Allium consumption with reduced gastric, esophageal, colorectal cancer risk
- **Evidence Grade: Level C** (observational; no RCT for cancer primary prevention)

**Evidence Grade Summary:**
- **Level A:** Prebiotic/bifidogenic (FOS class)
- **Level B:** Anti-hypertensive; anti-diabetic; anti-allergic
- **Level C:** Anti-cancer (epidemiological); wound healing

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

The onion occupies a unique medicinal-culinary interface: it is among the most commonly consumed foods worldwide, meaning its pharmacological effects are experienced daily by billions. Unlike many herbal medicines consumed in concentrated extracts, onion exerts its effects as a dietary staple — a "medicine hiding as food."

**The Tear Response:**
The lachrymatory response (propanthial-S-oxide volatilizing to mucous membranes) is one of the most universally recognized human experiences with a plant. It is pharmacologically inert in terms of therapeutic effect but culturally central to onion's identity. Traditional commentary across multiple traditions distinguishes the tear-inducing quality as a marker of potency — pungent = strong medicinal character.

**Oral Culinary (Primary Medicinal Pathway):**
- Raw: Sharp, pungent, initially harsh; activates organosulfur pathways maximally; quercetin better preserved
- Caramelized: Sweet, complex; Maillard products; reduced pungency; FOS partially degraded by cooking
- Roasted: Mellow warmth; anti-inflammatory quercetin still active; reduced tear compounds
- Fermented (Kimchi, pickled onion): Enhanced bioavailability of quercetin glycosides; bifidogenic amplified

**Anti-asthmatic / Anti-allergic Phenomenology:**
Hay fever and asthma patients frequently report reduced symptom burden with high onion/quercetin intake. The effect is gradual (days to weeks) and best appreciated seasonally — elevated dietary quercetin in spring (peak allergy season) correlating with mast cell stabilization. Unlike antihistamines, no sedation or rebound.

**Pre-operative caution phenomenology:**
Patients on anti-platelet therapy (aspirin, clopidogrel, warfarin) who consume large quantities of onion supplement extract report increased bruising/bleeding tendency — the clinical signal that validates the TXA₂ inhibition mechanism.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Dietary (primary pathway):**
- 50–100 g raw onion daily: supports quercetin prebiotic and cardiovascular effects
- Red/yellow onions preferred (higher quercetin vs. white)
- Outer layers and first few rings: highest quercetin concentration — often discarded when trimming; retain if possible
- Raw preferred over heavily cooked for maximum flavonoid retention; however, lightly sautéed onions retain majority of quercetin with improved palatability

**Onion juice:**
- 100–150 mL fresh onion juice/day: used in clinical trials for anti-diabetic and anti-hypertensive effect
- Palatable with apple juice or honey; strong odor limits compliance

**Onion skin extract (quercetin-standardized supplement):**
- 150–500 mg quercetin equivalents/day: clinical trial dosing for cardiovascular and anti-allergic applications
- Outer skins ~100× higher quercetin concentration than inner bulb — agricultural waste stream converted to supplement

**Quercetin supplement (sourced but equivalent to onion-derived):**
- 500 mg quercetin/day: anti-allergic, anti-asthmatic trials
- 150 mg/day: cardiovascular (Egert 2009 protocol)
- Note: quercetin aglycone has poor oral bioavailability (~17%); quercetin glycosides (from food, food extracts) superior; quercetin phytosome preparations also improve bioavailability

### Enhancing Bioavailability
- **Pair with black pepper (piperine):** may increase quercetin absorption (piperine inhibits glucuronidation)
- **Fat-soluble context:** Quercetin dissolves partially in oil; moderate fat in meal improves absorption
- **Chewing thoroughly:** Disrupts cell walls; activates alliinase → increased organosulfur release

### Safety Profile

**Generally very safe at dietary doses.** One of the most widely consumed foods globally with exceptional safety record.

**Drug Interactions:**
- **Anticoagulants (warfarin, heparin):** Additive anti-platelet effect (APDS, TXA₂ inhibition); clinically minor at dietary doses; monitor with large supplemental doses
- **Anti-diabetic medications (metformin, insulin, sulfonylureas):** Additive glucose-lowering; monitor blood sugar with supplemental dosing + anti-diabetic drugs
- **Lithium:** Theoretical diuretic effect may elevate lithium levels; monitor
- **CYP3A4:** Quercetin is a CYP3A4 inhibitor at high concentrations; monitor with narrow-therapeutic-index drugs metabolized by CYP3A4

**Contraindications:**
- **Onion allergy:** IgE-mediated; cross-reactive with birch pollen (lipid transfer proteins); Allium family allergy (garlic + onion cross-reactivity common)
- **GERD/peptic ulcer:** Raw onion aggravates reflux in susceptible individuals (stimulates gastric acid)

**Odor:**
- Allyl sulfides (especially APDS) absorbed systemically; exhaled via lungs and excreted in sweat — systemic onion breath/body odor with large doses; socially significant; less than garlic

**Hemolytic anemia in animals:**
- Thiosulfate compounds in *Allium* species cause Heinz body hemolytic anemia in dogs, cats, horses — clinical importance for veterinary practitioners. Human doses safe.
