# Rose Hip — Dog Rose / Cynosbatos / Hipberry / Nasturi

## *Rosa canina* L. | Family: Rosaceae

**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry (Galenic/Unani); astringent, nutritive-sour
**Training Phase:** Restoration & Fortification (astringent-nutritive rebuild)

### Origin & Tradition

The rose hip is the swollen, scarlet false-fruit (hypanthium) left on the wild dog rose after the petals fall. Across the traditions it is the archetypal **astringent-yet-nourishing** remedy: sour, tightening, and — as the moderns would later discover — extraordinarily rich in vitamin C. This is one of the codex's cleanest **doctrine-to-evidence** cases.

**Dioscorides (*De Materia Medica*, kynosbatos).** The Greek herbal names the dog rose *kynosbatos* and describes the hips as astringent, given for **fluxes of the belly** (diarrhoea) — the classic astringent indication of a sour, tannin-bearing fruit. The name itself ("dog-bramble") records the plant the tradition meant.

**Avicenna / Unani.** The *Canon* and the wider Unani tradition class rose and its hips as **cold, dry and astringent (qabid)**, used to bind loose bowels, strengthen the stomach, stem haemorrhage and "flux," and as a gentle nutritive tonic. The astringency is the doctrinal core: a remedy that *tightens* lax tissue.

**Sushruta / Ayurveda.** Roses (and by extension the hips of wild *Rosa* species) enter the Indian corpus as *kashaya* (astringent) and cooling, used for bleeding disorders, digestive laxity, and as a mild rejuvenative — again the astringent-plus-nutritive pairing.

**Ebers Papyrus / Egyptian & Assyrian.** Roses are attested in the Egyptian and Mesopotamian materia medica; specific identification of *R. canina* hips to an exact Ebers or Akkadian entry should be treated as tentative [USER: verify the precise native name against a primary translation], though the genus's medicinal use in the region is genuine.

**The wartime rose-hip-syrup story.** The most famous chapter is modern and well documented: during the Second World War, with citrus imports to Britain cut off, wild rose hips were **collected nationally and made into rose-hip syrup** as a domestic vitamin-C source, especially for children — a real public-health programme that turned a hedgerow astringent into a scurvy-preventing nutritive.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The traditions read the hip by two signatures, and modern chemistry answers both. First, the **sour taste and tightening astringency** — the doctrinal "cold, dry, binding" quality — corresponds to a genuine load of **organic acids, ascorbate and polyphenols/tannins**: the fruit really does bind (astringe) tissue and really is sour because it is acid-rich. The astringent, tissue-tightening action the physicians used against "flux" is the tannin/proanthocyanidin fraction precipitating surface proteins — a real, local, physical effect, not a humoral abstraction.

Second, the **"restores strength / stems flux / good for the debilitated"** nutritive reputation maps onto the same **vitamin C** that the wartime programme exploited — ascorbate being precisely the nutrient whose absence produces the bleeding-gums, poor-healing, capillary-fragile picture that the old "flux and weakness" language circles around. Ascorbate is a required cofactor for prolyl/lysyl hydroxylase in **collagen** synthesis; a fruit that visibly "strengthens the debilitated and stops bleeding" is, in modern terms, supplying the exact cofactor whose lack collapses connective tissue and capillary integrity. The classical "cold and dry astringent" is therefore not overturned but *decoded*: the binding is tannin/acid, the nourishing is ascorbate, and the two doctrinal virtues sit on two distinct, real chemical fractions of the same hip.

Where the doctrine is genuinely *extended* by modernity is the joint indication — the folk use for aching joints turns out to rest on a fat-soluble **galactolipid (GOPO)** the ancients could not have isolated, yet whose anti-inflammatory action the modern trials confirm. This is the rare case where tradition pointed at an effect (soothing stiff, aching joints) whose *molecule* had to wait for twentieth-century lipid chemistry to be named — a doctrine running ahead of the mechanism, then caught up by it. The honest qualifier is scale: the mechanism is real, the clinical effect modest.

### Active Components

- **L-ascorbic acid (vitamin C)** — exceptionally high, one of the richest plant sources; concentrated in the fleshy hip wall.
- **GOPO** — a specific mono/digalactosyl-diacylglycerol galactolipid, the characterised anti-inflammatory principle of standardised hip powder.
- **Polyphenols & flavonoids** — including catechins, quercetin glycosides and proanthocyanidins (astringency + antioxidant capacity).
- **Carotenoids** (lycopene, β-carotene) and **tocopherols**.
- **Seed oil** — rich in linoleic acid (a separate cosmetic/nutritive fraction).
- **Fruit acids**, pectin, and — a genuine hazard — the fine **irritant seed hairs**.

A note on the **Galenic dose-scale**, which the rose hip illustrates well. Classical practice never treated "cold and dry" as a fixed verdict but as a graded intensity to be matched to the patient and corrected by preparation — the syrup (sweetened, moistened) softens the hip's dry astringency for a debilitated patient, while the plain decoction preserves full binding power for acute flux. This is the same logic that modern standardisation formalises: the osteoarthritis trials did not use "rose hips" generically but a **defined powder** processed to retain the labile phytochemicals, at a fixed daily dose. The tradition's instinct — that *how* the hip is prepared decides whether you get nutrition, astringency or anti-inflammatory action — is vindicated by the fact that the clinical effect depends on a specific, controlled preparation rather than the raw fruit.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Deployed as a **restorative-astringent nutritive**: an ascorbate- and polyphenol-dense whole-fruit preparation for rebuilding after depletion, supporting connective tissue and healing, and — using *standardised hip powder specifically* — as an adjunct for osteoarthritic joint pain. The astringent role (loose bowel, minor flux) is retained from tradition; the joint-pain role is the modern, trial-backed addition. Positioned as a modest, well-tolerated adjunct, not a replacement for analgesics.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:

- **The osteoarthritis doctrine-to-evidence win.** Christensen et al. conducted a meta-analysis of three RCTs (287 patients, ~3-month median duration) of standardised *Rosa canina* hip powder for osteoarthritis pain. Rose-hip powder reduced pain versus placebo with an effect size of **0.37 (95% CI 0.13–0.60, P = 0.002)**, homogeneous across trials (I² = 0%), with patients roughly **twice as likely to respond** (OR 2.19) and a **number-needed-to-treat of six** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joca.2008.03.001)). This is a genuine, statistically significant, traditional-use-vindicating result.
- **Mechanism.** Cohen's review attributes the anti-inflammatory action to the hip's characteristic **galactolipid (GOPO)** and notes standardised rose-hip powder shows antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity with clinical benefit in osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis and IBD — importantly **without** the ulcerogenic and platelet effects of NSAIDs (PubMed PMID 22762068; no DOI registered).
- **The vitamin-C/nutritive basis.** Tafreshi et al. confirm *Rosa canina* pericarp as a substantial source of **vitamin C**, phenolic acids, flavonoids and carotenoids (elicitation raised vitamin-C content by ~50%) — the chemical substrate of the classical nutritive reputation ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-025-06251-0)).

A note of scientific honesty on the joint result: all three pooled trials were **supported by the powder's manufacturer**, and the total evidence base is small (287 patients). Christensen et al. themselves frame the finding as promising but requiring "independent replication in a future large-scale/long-term trial." The effect size of 0.37 is, by convention, **small-to-moderate** — clinically real but not dramatic, and well below what a first-line analgesic delivers. The correct framing is a low-risk adjunct with a favourable safety profile (no ulcerogenic or antiplatelet action, per Cohen), not a replacement therapy.

**Evidence grade.** Vitamin-C richness: **strong/established**. Osteoarthritis pain reduction: **moderate and real but the effect is small** (ES 0.37), based on a limited, largely manufacturer-supported evidence base needing independent large-scale replication — honestly, a modest adjunct, not a cure. GOPO mechanism: **plausible, moderately supported**. Astringent/anti-diarrhoeal traditional use: **consistent with tannin chemistry** but not formally trialled.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic/Unani (Avicenna):** cold, dry, astringent (qabid); binds flux, strengthens stomach, stems haemorrhage; nutritive tonic.
- **Ayurveda (Sushruta lineage):** rasa — kashaya/amla (astringent-sour); virya — cooling (sheeta); used in bleeding/laxity, mild rejuvenative.
- **Dioscorides (Graeco-Roman):** kynosbatos, astringent, for belly-flux.
- **TCM:** *R. canina* itself not a classical Chinese drug — **not attested** (the Chinese materia medica uses *Rosa laevigata* hips, jin ying zi, a distinct species; do not conflate).

### Contraindications

- **Seed-hair irritant:** the fine hairs surrounding the seeds are a mechanical irritant — the traditional "itching powder." Always strain syrups/infusions; avoid inhaling powdered raw hips.
- **High vitamin-C load:** large supplemental doses may cause GI upset and, in predisposed individuals, raise oxalate/urinary-stone considerations.
- **Iron overload caution:** ascorbate enhances non-haem iron absorption — relevant in haemochromatosis.
- **Adjunct, not analgesic replacement:** the osteoarthritis effect is modest; do not discontinue prescribed therapy on its basis.
- Possible additive effect with anticoagulant/antiplatelet regimens is not established but warrants caution given high polyphenol/vitamin-C intake.
