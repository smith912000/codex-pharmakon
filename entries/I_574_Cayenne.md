## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary names:**
- English: Cayenne, Chili Pepper, Red Pepper, Hot Pepper
- Latin: *Capsicum annuum* (annuum = "annual," a misnomer — it is perennial in tropics)
- Spanish: *Chile*, *Ají* (from Nahuatl *chīlli*)
- Nahuatl: *Chīlli* (the original name; all European words derive from this)
- Hindi: *Lāl Mirch* (red pepper), *Mirchi*
- Sanskrit: *Kaṭuvī* (pungent), *Raktamaricha* (red pepper — post-Columbian usage)
- Arabic: *Filfil Ahmar* (red pepper)
- German: *Cayennepfeffer*, *Paprika* (for mild varieties)
- French: *Piment*, *Poivre de Cayenne*
- Chinese: *Làjiāo* (辣椒, "pungent pepper")
- Japanese: *Tōgarashi* (唐辛子, "Tang dynasty spice" — though named for China as conduit)

**Botanical identity:**
*Capsicum annuum* is a branching herbaceous to woody perennial (grown as annual in temperate climates), 0.5–1.5 m height, with white pentamerous flowers and characteristic fleshy berry fruits (botanically a berry, culinarily a vegetable/spice). The genus *Capsicum* contains approximately 30–40 species, five of which are domesticated.

**Key species disambiguation:**

| Species | Common Name | Scoville Range | Notes |
|---------|-------------|----------------|-------|
| *C. annuum* | Bell, Cayenne, Jalapeño, Paprika | 0–200,000 SHU | Most diversity; culinary backbone |
| *C. frutescens* | Tabasco, Malagueta | 30,000–225,000 SHU | Bushy perennial; Tabasco sauce |
| *C. chinense* | Habanero, Scotch Bonnet, Carolina Reaper | 100,000–2,200,000 SHU | Highest capsaicin; fruity aroma |
| *C. baccatum* | Ají Amarillo, Bishop's Crown | 30,000–50,000 SHU | South American culinary |
| *C. pubescens* | Rocoto, Manzano | 30,000–100,000 SHU | Black seeds; cloud forest adapted |

**Scoville Heat Unit (SHU) scale:**
- Pure capsaicin: 16,000,000 SHU
- Carolina Reaper (world's hottest, Guinness 2023): 2,200,000 SHU
- Ghost Pepper (*Bhut jolokia*): 1,000,000 SHU
- Habanero: 100,000–350,000 SHU
- Cayenne: 30,000–50,000 SHU
- Jalapeño: 2,500–8,000 SHU
- Bell pepper: 0 SHU (no capsaicin; *pun1* gene mutation)

**Wilbur Scoville** (1865–1942): American pharmacist at Parke-Davis; devised the organoleptic Scoville Organoleptic Test (1912) using trained human tasters. Modern measurement uses HPLC (High Performance Liquid Chromatography) converted to SHU equivalents.

**Identifying morphological features:**
- Flowers white (not yellow-green as in *Solanum* spp. or purple as in toxic nightshades)
- Berries attached at calyx, dangling or upright depending on variety
- Seeds white/cream (black seeds = *C. pubescens* only)
- Distinctive peppery-capsaicin odor when fruit crushed
- No milky sap (distinguishes from spurges/euphorbias)

**Note on post-Columbian medical adoption:**
*Capsicum* reached Europe ca. 1493 (Columbus second voyage brings seeds), spread to Africa and Asia via Portuguese trade routes by 1540s–1560s. Li Shizhen's *Bencao Gangmu* (1578) describes it. By 1700, chili had fully integrated into Ayurvedic, Unani, and Chinese pharmacopeias — making it one of the most rapidly adopted substances in medical history. Post-Columbian medical text citations are marked ~ rather than ✓ per tradition's original corpus.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Universal convergence point — thermogenic stimulation:**
Every tradition that encountered *Capsicum* rapidly adopted it as a digestive stimulant and circulatory activator. The subjective heating experience is cross-culturally consistent: Nahuatl *chilli* heat; Ayurvedic *ushna* (hot quality); Chinese *la* (辣, pungent-hot *qi* activating); European "warming" classification. The TRPV1 receptor mechanism explains this pharmacological universalism — the burning sensation is a genuine sensory phenomenon, not learned cultural response.

**Pre-Columbian pharmacological analog pattern:**
The niches *Capsicum* filled post-Columbian adoption were previously held by:
- *Piper nigrum* (black pepper): piperine-mediated GI stimulation
- *Zingiber officinale* (ginger): gingerol/shogaol TRPV1 agonism (weaker)
- *Piper longum* (long pepper): piperine + piperlongumine; Ayurvedic *trikatu*
- *Zanthoxylum* spp. (Sichuan pepper): hydroxy-alpha-sanshool; TRPV1/4 agonism
The fact that *Capsicum* rapidly displaced or supplemented these substances confirms its superior TRPV1 agonist activity.

**Wound management convergence:**
Badianus Manuscript and South American indigenous traditions both document topical chili application to wounds — apparently counterintuitive given the burning sensation. The mechanism (substance P depletion producing analgesia after initial hyperalgesia) was not understood until the 20th century, but empirical observation of pain relief after repeated application was accurate.

**Divergence point — extreme heat cultivation:**
Traditional use consistently employed moderate-heat varieties for medicine. The extreme-heat *C. chinense* cultivars (habanero, ghost pepper, Carolina Reaper) are primarily modern selections without traditional medical precedent. Extreme SHU levels approach systemic toxicity: anaphylaxis, respiratory compromise, and documented deaths from competitive "chili eating" at >1,000,000 SHU (typically cardiac events from capsaicin-induced coronary vasospasm).

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Primary bioactive compounds:**

**Capsaicinoids (0.1–1.0% dry weight of fruit; up to 1.5% in extreme cultivars):**
- **Capsaicin** (*trans*-8-methyl-N-vanillyl-6-nonenamide): primary capsaicinoid; 69% of total in typical cayenne; TRPV1 agonist with EC₅₀ ~1 nM
- **Dihydrocapsaicin**: 22% of total capsaicinoids; similar potency to capsaicin; differs in saturated C9 chain
- **Nordihydrocapsaicin**: 7%; shorter chain; slightly less potent
- **Homodihydrocapsaicin**: 1%; longer chain
- **Homocapsaicin**: <1%; longest chain
- Total capsaicinoids = "capsaicin content" for commercial purposes

**Capsaicin molecular structure:**
- Vanillyl head group (4-hydroxy-3-methoxybenzyl) linked to fatty acid tail (8-methylnonenamide)
- Lipophilic compound: logP ≈ 3.5; crosses skin, mucous membranes readily
- Molecular weight: 305.4 g/mol
- The vanillyl moiety = shared with vanillin, eugenol (structural basis for TRPV1 recognition)

**Carotenoids (responsible for red color):**
- **Capsanthin**: signature red carotenoid of *Capsicum*; antioxidant; 6–9 mg/100g in red cayenne
- **Capsorubin**: deep red carotenoid; photosensitizer applications
- **Beta-carotene**: provitamin A; 534 μg/100g cayenne
- **Lutein + zeaxanthin**: macular protection

**Vitamins:**
- **Vitamin C**: 143 mg/100g in red chili (2.4x RDA per 100g); highest of common vegetables
- **Vitamin B6**: 0.51 mg/100g (39% RDA)
- **Vitamin K1**: 14 μg/100g (low; no clinical warfarin interaction unlike parsley)
- **Vitamin A**: 952 μg RAE/100g from carotenoid conversion

**Phenolic compounds:**
- Quercetin, luteolin, apigenin (flavonoids)
- Ferulic acid, caffeic acid (phenolic acids)
- Capsaicinol: non-pungent capsaicin analog; anti-inflammatory

**Volatile aroma compounds (particularly *C. chinense*):**
- 2-Methoxy-3-isobutylpyrazine: green bell pepper aroma
- Various aldehydes and esters contributing fruity notes of habanero/Scotch bonnet

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Primary mechanism: TRPV1 (Transient Receptor Potential Vanilloid 1) agonism**

TRPV1 is a non-selective cation channel activated by:
- Heat > 43°C (physiological heat detection threshold)
- Extracellular acidosis (pH < 6.0)
- Capsaicin (exogenous ligand)
- Endocannabinoids (anandamide, N-arachidonoyl-dopamine)

**Capsaicin binding:**
Capsaicin binds the intracellular domain of TRPV1 (the S4–S5 linker region), not the pore itself. Binding → channel opens → Ca²⁺ influx → membrane depolarization → action potential generation in Aδ and C fibers → burning sensation transmitted to dorsal horn → spinothalamic tract → somatosensory cortex (pain) and hypothalamus (thermoregulation).

The subjective "heat" of capsaicin is neurologically identical to contact with hot liquid — the same fibers, same processing pathway. Capsaicin does not cause thermal damage; it generates a false thermal signal.

**Tachyphylaxis and substance P depletion mechanism (basis of analgesic use):**

Phase 1 (excitation, seconds to minutes): TRPV1 activation → intense burning
Phase 2 (desensitization, minutes to hours): Repeated or sustained TRPV1 activation → receptor internalization → calcium-dependent desensitization → reduced response to subsequent stimuli
Phase 3 (defunctionalization, days to weeks): With high-dose repeated application → depletion of **substance P** (neuropeptide Y) from peripheral C-fiber terminals → loss of nociceptive transmission from that region → prolonged analgesia

**Substance P:**
- Neuropeptide stored in C-fiber vesicles
- Released upon TRPV1 activation → amplifies pain signal via NK1 receptors in dorsal horn
- Also mediates neurogenic inflammation (plasma extravasation, mast cell degranulation)
- After capsaicin-induced depletion: nociceptive inputs fail to transmit (analgesic state)
- Replenishment takes weeks → explains prolonged effect of capsaicin cream

**TRPV1 expression sites (explaining clinical applications):**
- Skin and mucous membranes (topical pain applications)
- Bronchial C-fibers (cough reflex; capsaicin cough challenge test in research)
- Gut enteric nervous system (GI motility effects; dyspepsia)
- Bladder urothelium (intravesical capsaicin for overactive bladder)
- Cardiovascular: coronary and peripheral vasculature
- Trigeminal ganglia (migraine; nasal capsaicin for cluster headaches)

**Thermogenic mechanism:**
- TRPV1 activation in hypothalamus → perceived heat → vasodilation + sweating
- Peripheral vasodilation (not central fever) → heat dissipation
- Mild increase in metabolic rate: 50–100 kcal/day (Brown 2010, meta-analysis); sympathetic nervous system stimulation → β-adrenergic fat oxidation
- Gastric acid secretion stimulation (gastrin release via enteric TRPV1)

**Anti-cancer mechanism (preclinical):**
- Capsaicin induces apoptosis in cancer cell lines via TRPV1-mediated Ca²⁺ overload → mitochondrial membrane potential collapse → cytochrome c release → caspase-3 activation
- ROS generation
- EGFR pathway inhibition
- Proliferation inhibition in prostate, breast, colon, hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines (in vitro)
- Paradox: epidemiological data from chili-consuming populations shows reduced cancer rates in some (Mexican, South Korean) but not all studies

**Cardiovascular effects:**
- Acute: vasodilation (TRPV1-mediated release of CGRP and SP from perivascular nerve endings) → transient blood pressure reduction
- Capsaicin → triglycerides reduction; platelet aggregation inhibition; fibrinolysis enhancement
- Extreme doses: capsaicin-induced coronary artery vasospasm → Kounis syndrome (anaphylactic myocardial infarction, rare)

**Antimicrobial mechanism:**
- Capsaicin disrupts bacterial cell membrane integrity
- Effective against *H. pylori* at MIC 25–50 μg/mL (concentration not achievable through dietary intake alone)
- Inhibits *Staphylococcus aureus* biofilm formation

**Analgesic mechanism summary — clinical relevance:**
The substance P depletion mechanism is biphasic: initial application causes burning/hyperalgesia; continued use (3–4x daily for 3–6 weeks) produces genuine analgesia that persists for weeks after cessation. This counterintuitive pattern requires patient education for compliance.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Level A — Well-designed RCTs and meta-analyses:**

**Topical capsaicin for neuropathic pain:**
- **Capsaicin 0.075% cream for post-herpetic neuralgia**: Multiple RCTs; pooled analysis Mason 2004 (Cochrane) n=656; NNT = 5.7 for ≥50% pain reduction; significant vs placebo.
- **Capsaicin 8% patch (Qutenza / NGX-4010)**: Single 60-minute application by healthcare professional; FDA approved 2009; Backonja 2008 RCT n=402; 30% of patients achieved ≥30% pain reduction at week 8 vs 20% placebo; effect persists 3 months from single application. Approved indications: post-herpetic neuralgia, HIV neuropathy, diabetic peripheral neuropathy.
- **Capsaicin 0.025% cream for osteoarthritis**: Altman 1994 Arthritis & Rheumatism RCT n=70; significant pain reduction vs placebo at 4 weeks; knee OA validated population.
- **Meta-analysis topical capsaicin for chronic pain**: Derry 2017 (Cochrane); 25 RCTs n=4,402; moderate-quality evidence for efficacy in peripheral neuropathic pain; NNT approximately 6.

**Intranasal capsaicin for cluster headaches:**
- Fusco 1994: intranasal capsaicin 300 μg ipsilateral nostril; significant reduction in episodic cluster headache frequency vs placebo; 7-day treatment produced 2-week sustained effect.
- Civamide (zucapsaicin, *cis*-capsaicin) nasal spray: Phase II RCT n=28 (Diamond 2000); significant headache reduction.
- Mechanism: TRPV1 desensitization in trigeminal fibers → reduced CGRP release → interrupted cluster headache pathophysiology.

**Capsaicin for weight management (modest effect):**
- Whiting 2012 (meta-analysis, Appetite): capsaicin 2–6 mg/day reduces caloric intake by ~74 kcal/meal on average; effect size small but consistent; reduced appetite via satiety signaling.
- Lejeune 2003 (IJPO): capsaicin 135 mg/day + caffeine: increased fat oxidation; thermogenic effect confirmed calorimetrically.

**Level B — Observational/pilot/mechanistic:**

**Gastrointestinal:**
- Rodriguez-Stanley 2000: capsaicin 0.025% in gelatin capsules for functional dyspepsia; mixed results; some studies show improvement, some exacerbation — highly dependent on dose and administration route.
- Epidemiological: Choung 2011 (Am J Gastroenterol): spicy food intake protective for gastric ulcer in Korean population paradox (raw capsaicin vs. cooked; frequency effects).

**Cardiovascular:**
- Cholesterol/triglyceride: Small RCTs n=20–40 showing triglyceride reduction with 5 mg capsaicin supplementation; not replicated at scale.
- Qin 2015 (BMJ): Chinese prospective cohort n=487,375; regular spicy food consumption (vs never) → 14% reduced mortality; HR 0.86 (95% CI 0.82–0.90) for all-cause mortality; cardioprotective trend.

**Anti-obesity mechanism:**
- Kim 2014: Capsaicin activates brown adipose tissue (BAT) via TRPV1 → UCP1 upregulation → uncoupled mitochondrial respiration → heat production without ATP synthesis.

**Level C — Traditional evidence:**
- Badianus Manuscript 1552: *Chīlli* for gum pain (topical), stomach ailments, fever, ritual purification
- Ayurvedic texts (post-Columbian adoption): *Raktamaricha* classified *ushna* (hot), *laghu* (light), *tikshna* (sharp); digestive, carminative, stimulates Agni

**Level D — In vitro/animal only:**
- Anticancer: Multiple cell line studies; in vivo mouse xenograft inhibition; not translated to human RCTs.
- *H. pylori* antimicrobial: in vitro MIC data; clinical dosing not achievable through food.
- Neuroprotection: TRPV1 activation → BDNF expression in animal models.

**Approved pharmaceutical products:**
| Product | Active | Concentration | Indication | Approval |
|---------|--------|---------------|------------|---------|
| Qutenza patch | Capsaicin | 8% (179 mg) | PHN, HIV neuropathy | FDA 2009; EMA 2009 |
| Zostrix cream | Capsaicin | 0.025–0.075% | OA, PHN, diabetic neuropathy | FDA OTC |
| Axsain cream | Capsaicin | 0.075% | Neuropathic pain | UK approved |
| Civamide nasal | Zucapsaicin (*cis*) | 0.01% | Cluster headache | Phase III |

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The Jamestown Pepper Paradox — Columbus naming error:**
Columbus called *Capsicum* "pepper" (pimienta in Spanish) when he encountered it in the Caribbean in 1493 because he was looking for *Piper nigrum* to justify the voyage economically. The name stuck despite no botanical relationship. Black pepper (Piperaceae) and chili pepper (Solanaceae) are unrelated; the shared name remains one of history's most durable botanical misnomers. Columbus reportedly brought seeds back to Spain; within 50 years, chili had spread via Portuguese trade routes through Africa, India, Southeast Asia, and China faster than almost any other Columbian Exchange plant.

**Nahuatl taxonomy — pre-existing medical classification:**
Aztec medicine had detailed *Capsicum* taxonomy predating European contact. The Badianus Manuscript (1552, compiled by Martín de la Cruz, an Aztec physician at Tlatelolco) documents *Chīlli* preparations for multiple conditions. Bernardino de Sahagún's *Florentine Codex* (1576) lists at least eight varieties of *chīlli* with distinct culinary and medicinal applications — evidence of sophisticated botanical classification matching modern varietal understanding.

**Bhut Jolokia and the weaponization trajectory:**
The ghost pepper (*B. jolokia*, *Capsicum chinense* × *C. frutescens* hybrid, Assam/Nagaland) was certified as the world's hottest pepper by Guinness in 2007 at 1,000,000 SHU. The Indian Defense Research and Development Organization subsequently investigated ghost pepper grenades as a non-lethal weapon to incapacitate crowds — essentially creating a chemical weapon from food. The US OC (Oleoresin Capsicum) pepper spray used by law enforcement since the 1980s operates on the same principle at 500,000–5,000,000 SHU equivalent concentration. Capsaicin is one of few food compounds that has both pharmaceutical and weapons applications.

**Capsaicin anaphylaxis — the paradoxical cardiac event:**
Several documented fatalities following extreme hot pepper consumption (Carolina Reaper eating contests) have been attributed to capsaicin-induced coronary vasospasm — Kounis syndrome type I, where no pre-existing coronary disease exists but an allergic/chemical mediator triggers coronary spasm. A case report in BMJ 2019 documented multifocal reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS) following a single Carolina Reaper consumption — severe headache, thunderclap onset, CT showing cerebral vasospasm. Recovered fully. The neurological mechanism: TRPV1 on cerebral vasculature → CGRP release → then depletion → vasospasm in predisposed individuals.

**Schönlein-Henoch and TRPV1:**
Interstitial cystitis treatment with intravesical capsaicin (0.01% solution instilled directly into bladder via catheter) exploits TRPV1 desensitization on bladder urothelial C-fibers. Initial instillation is intensely painful (requiring premedication with intravesical lidocaine); subsequent treatments produce TRPV1 desensitization → reduced urgency, frequency, and pain. A compelling demonstration of the biphasic excitation/desensitization mechanism in a controlled clinical context.

**The endorphin hypothesis:**
The popular belief that spicy food triggers endorphin release creating a "chili high" was widely repeated in the 1980s–1990s. The actual mechanism: TRPV1 activation → pain signal → endogenous opioid release in dorsal horn as pain modulation response. The "high" is not from capsaicin directly but from the endogenous analgesic response to capsaicin-induced pain. Capsaicin itself has no direct opioid receptor activity. The practical implication: opioid antagonist naloxone does partially blunt the pleasure of spicy food — confirming the mechanism.

**Bell pepper genetic basis:**
The *pun1* (Pungency 1) gene encodes capsaicin synthase (CS/BADH). A deletion/frame-shift mutation in *pun1* → non-functional enzyme → no capsaicinoid synthesis → bell pepper (0 SHU). This is a naturally occurring loss-of-function mutation, selected during domestication for sweet pepper varieties. Bell peppers retain all other *Capsicum* nutrients (exceptional vitamin C; carotenoids) without the TRPV1 pharmacology.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Standardized preparations:**

**Topical capsaicin (pharmaceutical):**
- **OTC cream 0.025–0.075%**: Apply to affected area 3–4x daily; must use consistently for 3–6 weeks to achieve substance P depletion analgesia; initial burning expected (patient education critical); wash hands after (avoid eye contact)
- **High-concentration 8% patch (Qutenza)**: Healthcare-professional application only; 30–60 minute application under supervision; analgesia onset 1 week; duration 2–3 months; pre-treat with topical anesthetic
- **Protective measures**: Nitrile gloves mandatory during application; avoid mucous membranes; capsaicin dissolves in oil/dairy — NOT water (water spreads capsaicin; milk's casein neutralizes)

**Dietary/supplemental:**
- Cayenne standardized extract: 30,000–40,000 SHU capsaicin units; 1–3 capsules per day
- Culinary dose: ¼–½ tsp cayenne (1–2g) in food; provides thermogenic/carminative effects without clinical-level analgesia
- Capsaicin supplement 2–6 mg/day: weight management support; evidence modest (Level B)

**Gastric precautions:**
- Enteric-coated capsules reduce esophageal irritation
- Take with food
- Avoid in active peptic ulcer, GERD, IBS with diarrhea predominance (TRPV1 hypersensitization in IBS → symptom exacerbation)
- Paradox: low culinary doses may protect gastric mucosa; high acute doses → irritation

**Clinical decision framework:**

| Indication | Evidence Level | Preparation | Notes |
|------------|---------------|-------------|-------|
| Post-herpetic neuralgia | A | 8% patch or 0.075% cream | 8% patch preferred for compliance |
| Osteoarthritis pain | A | 0.025–0.075% cream | 3–4 weeks consistent use |
| Diabetic neuropathy | A | 0.075% cream | |
| Cluster headache (acute) | B | Intranasal (provider) | Not widely available |
| Weight management | B | 2–6 mg/day supplement | Modest effect; adjunct only |
| Functional dyspepsia | B | Low oral dose; enteric-coated | Dose-dependent; start low |
| *H. pylori* | D | Diet; no clinical data | Pharmacological dose unachievable dietarily |

**Pediatric / special populations:**
- Topical use in children: generally avoid <2 years; 0.025% with caution in older children
- Pregnancy: culinary use safe; therapeutic topical appears safe; no RCT data
- Lactation: culinary safe; limited topical absorption; no adverse infant reports
- Renal impairment: no dose adjustment required for topical

**First aid for capsaicin exposure:**
- Skin: Wash with soap and water; whole milk or yogurt (casein binds capsaicin); vegetable oil to emulsify then wash
- Eyes: Copious water irrigation minimum 15 minutes; capsaicin not water-soluble (irritation persists longer than most compounds); ophthalmology evaluation if severe
- Ingestion (extreme dose): Supportive care; milk/dairy; do NOT induce vomiting; IV fluids if severe GI symptoms; capsaicin is self-limiting in most cases
- OC pepper spray exposure: Same as above; fresh air; time resolves most cases

**Pharmacokinetics:**
- Absorption: Rapid across GI mucosa and skin (lipophilic); oral bioavailability ~35%
- Distribution: Crosses BBB (TRPV1 present centrally); Vd large (lipophilic tissue distribution)
- Metabolism: Hepatic via CYP2E1 and CYP3A4 → 16-hydroxycapsaicin, 17-hydroxycapsaicin, 16,17-dihydroxycapsaicin (all inactive)
- Elimination: Urine and feces; t½ plasma ~24 minutes; local tissue t½ longer
- No significant DDI documented at dietary doses; potential CYP2E1 interaction with high supplemental doses

**Drug interactions:**
- Warfarin: No clinically significant interaction at dietary doses
- Antihypertensives: Additive vasodilation theoretically possible; not clinically documented
- ACE inhibitors: Capsaicin-induced cough (via bronchial TRPV1) may worsen ACE inhibitor cough — clinical relevance uncertain
- Aspirin/NSAIDs: No significant interaction; both may address pain via complementary mechanisms

**Contraindications:**
- Active esophagitis, gastric ulcer (high-dose oral)
- Application to broken/irritated skin (topical)
- Known capsaicin hypersensitivity (rare; IgE-mediated)
- Concurrent topical anticoagulant use on same skin area

**Safety considerations:**
Capsaicin is GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) by FDA for dietary use. The LD₅₀ in rodents is ~47.2 mg/kg IV (capsaicin) — a dose not achievable through dietary or therapeutic topical use. Oral LD₅₀ is much higher. No documented human fatality from pharmaceutical capsaicin preparations. Deaths associated with extreme pepper consumption are via TRPV1-mediated vasospasm mechanisms rather than direct toxicity. OC pepper spray exposure at law enforcement concentrations causes temporary incapacitation; in enclosed spaces with repeated exposure, fatalities have occurred (asphyxiation via bronchospasm in vulnerable individuals).
