# Scrophularia aquatica — Galiopsis (Dioscorides 4-95)

## *Scrophularia aquatica [Linnaeus], "Water Figwort", the fourth of the names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-95 ("Galeopsis, Scrophularia maior, Ficaria [Fuchs], Scrophularia aquatica [Linnaeus] — Water Figwort"), with Galeopsis tetrahit ("Galeopsis, Holy Hemp, Common Hempnettle") printed as an "other usage"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 8890) prints four names, and a second line (8891) gives *Galeopsis tetrahit* as an "other usage"; the row takes the one that reads *Scrophularia aquatica*, which the translators attribute to Linnaeus. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-95. GALIOPSIS*, heading at line 8889, SUGGESTED lines at 8890 and 8891, text at line 8892. It stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), before *4-96. GALLION* (heading at line 8893). The translators' index gives *Galiopsis* at page 647 (line 15807).

The whole chapter reads: "Galiopsis — the whole little shrub with the stalk and leaves is similar to the nettle, but the leaves are smoother, and smell strongly if bruised. The thin flowers are nearly purple. It grows in hedges, byways and house courtyards everywhere. The leaves, juice, stalk and seed are able to dissolve hard lumps, cancers, tumours [possibly goitre], inflammation of the parotid gland, and pannus [opaque thickening of cornea with veins]. They must lay on a poultice of this twice a day, making the poultice lukewarm, and a decoction of this used in a warm pack is of benefit. It is also good applied with salt for nomae [grazer disease, eats away muscle, tissue and bones], other gangrenes, and rotten ulcers. It is also called guleobdolon, or galephos, the Egyptians call it aethopi, and the Romans, urtica labeonis."

The description is of a "little shrub", like the nettle in stalk and leaves but with smoother leaves that "smell strongly if bruised", and thin, nearly purple flowers; it grows "in hedges, byways and house courtyards everywhere". The parts used are leaves, juice, stalk and seed. They "are able to dissolve" hard lumps, cancers, tumours, inflammation of the parotid gland and *pannus* of the cornea. The method given is a poultice laid on twice a day and made lukewarm, and a decoction used in a warm pack; applied with salt, the plant is "good" for *nomae*, other gangrenes and rotten ulcers. The bracketed glosses (goitre, parotid, pannus, nomae) are the translators', not Dioscorides'. Three alternative names are printed: *guleobdolon*, *galephos*, and, from the Egyptians and Romans, *aethopi* and *urtica labeonis*.

**Overlap, and why the identity is doubtful.** The same chapter is the basis of the existing entry I_812 (*Galeopsis segetum*, Downy Hemp-nettle), which uses it as the Dioscoridean attestation for that plant and says the translators gloss it first as *Scrophularia aquatica* and only secondly as *Galeopsis tetrahit*. The two entries are not merged here. The chapter's "nettle" likeness, its purple flowers and its place of growth do not by themselves decide between a figwort and a hemp-nettle, and the Codex does not decide.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read, and the chapter prints none. It is a general fact that *Scrophularia* (the figworts) belongs to the figwort family, and *Galeopsis* to the mint family. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the translators' *Scrophularia aquatica*. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides reports a hedgerow plant whose leaves, juice, stalk and seed dissolve hard lumps, cancers, tumours, parotid inflammation and corneal *pannus*, and which is good with salt for *nomae*, gangrene and rotten ulcers. These are the text's reports, and a "cancer" or "gangrene" in the translators' glosses is not a modern diagnosis. The Codex gives no protocol for any of them, and the identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-95 gives these preparations: a poultice of the plant, laid on twice a day and made lukewarm; a decoction used in a warm pack; and the plant applied with salt. The poultice frequency (twice a day) is the only measure given; no quantity or duration is stated. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. It prints the plant's smell ("smell strongly if bruised") and the lukewarmth of the poultice, which is the preparation and not a quality of the plant.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use; it prints the Egyptian name *aethopi* and the Roman *urtica labeonis* as names only. The part of the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition that is present here is the description of the plant (nettle-like, smoother leaves, a strong smell when bruised, nearly purple flowers, where it grows) and the care taken over the preparation (a lukewarm poultice, twice a day). The plant is given no status beyond that of a remedial herb. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It offers a description, a poultice and a list of conditions that no modern reader should treat at home, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. A hard lump, a tumour, a gangrenous or rotting wound, or a change in the eye needs medical assessment, and a poultice must not replace it. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion that competes with *Galeopsis tetrahit*, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
