# I-341: Cassia senna (Senna)

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary name:** Senna  
**Botanical name:** *Senna alexandrina* Mill. (syn. *Cassia senna* L.; *Cassia acutifolia* Delile; *Cassia angustifolia* Vahl)  
**Family:** Fabaceae (Leguminosae), subfamily Caesalpinioideae  
**Synonyms:** Alexandrian senna (*C. acutifolia*), Tinnevelly senna (*C. angustifolia*), Indian senna, Khartoum senna  
**Traditional names:** Sana (Arabic سنا — origin of all European names); Sanay (Persian سنه); Svarnapatri (Sanskrit स्वर्णपत्री — "golden-leaved"); Nilavakkai (Tamil); Sen'ya (Japanese センナ); Cassia sene (Italian — origin of the genus common name)

**Botanical description:** Shrub or subshrub 0.5–1 m; pinnate leaves with 4–8 pairs of oblong-lanceolate leaflets, pale green; flowers yellow, 5-petaled, racemose; fruit a flat, leathery pod 4–6 cm containing 4–7 seeds.

**Taxonomic history:** The genus *Cassia* L. has been taxonomically revised; *Senna alexandrina* now encompasses both *C. acutifolia* (Alexandrian/Khartoum senna — leaflets narrower, higher sennoside) and *C. angustifolia* (Tinnevelly/Indian senna — leaflets broader, slightly lower sennoside). The pharmacopoeial monographs (USP, EP, BP) accept both sources.

**Commercial forms:**
- **Senna leaf:** dried leaflets; USP specifies ≥2.5% sennosides A+B
- **Senna pod (fruit):** higher sennoside content; longer-acting; preferred by some practitioners
- **Standardized extracts:** sennoside content 8.8 mg per tablet (Senokot®), 17.2 mg (Ex-Lax®)

## 3. Convergence Analysis

**Pattern:** Senna's convergence trajectory follows the Islamic trade network: originating in Sudan/Yemen, reaching the Unani-Tibb tradition via the Islamic medical revolution (7th–12th century CE), then spreading to Ayurveda (svarnapatri) via Indian Ocean trade and into Chinese medicine (fan xie ye) via the Silk Road.

Ibn Sīnā's account in the *Qānūn* is the most pharmacologically sophisticated pre-modern analysis: he distinguishes senna from harsher purgatives (scammony, colocynth) as gentler and suitable for elderly patients and children, recommends leaves over pods as milder, identifies interactions with colicky patients (add carminatives), and notes the 6–12 hour onset aligns with colonic transit time. This pharmacodynamic characterization is essentially correct by modern standards.

The Islamic pharmacopoeia's preference for senna over rhubarb for most patients (gentler, cleaner purge without astringent biphasic effect) has been validated by modern comparative studies: senna is more predictable, has fewer drug interactions, and lower oxalate content than rhubarb. However, rhubarb's broader set of constituents (tannins, stilbenes) gives it advantages in specific indications (GI bleeding, inflammatory conditions) where senna lacks effect.

## 4. Phytochemical Composition

### Anthraquinone Glycosides (primary active fraction)

| Compound | Leaf content | Pod content | Notes |
|----------|-------------|-------------|-------|
| Sennoside A | 0.5–2.0% | 1.0–3.5% | DIANTHRONE glycoside; prodrug; PRIMARY |
| Sennoside B | 0.5–2.0% | 1.0–3.0% | Stereoisomer of A; similar potency |
| Sennoside C | 0.1–0.3% | 0.2–0.5% | Heterodianthrone (rhein-aloe-emodin) |
| Sennoside D | Trace | 0.1–0.2% | Stereoisomer of C |
| Free anthraquinones (emodin, rhein, aloe-emodin) | <0.05% (ideally) | <0.05% | Free anthraquinones irritate stomach; quality specification |
| **Total sennosides** | **≥2.5% (USP)** | **≥3.4% (EP for pods)** | **Pharmacopoeial minimum standard** |

**Critical quality consideration:** High-quality senna should have LOW free anthraquinones (which cause cramping) and HIGH dianthrone glycosides (prodrugs that activate in colon only). Poor-quality senna with high free anthraquinones causes more GI cramping.

### Other constituents
- Mucilage (polysaccharides): 5–15%; provides demulcent buffering of anthraquinone irritation
- Flavonoids: kaempferol, quercetin, isorhamnetin glycosides
- Naphthalene glycosides (6-hydroxymusizin): minor contribution
- Resin fraction: minimized in pharmaceutical-grade preparations (causes cramping)

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

### Sennoside Mechanism — The Most Studied Anthraquinone Laxative

**Identical class mechanism as rhubarb (I-339)** but with higher specificity for the colon and better-characterized prodrug activation:

**Step-by-step activation:**
1. Sennosides A+B ingested → pass through stomach and small intestine UNCHANGED (stable to gastric acid; not absorbed; not hydrolyzed by human enzymes)
2. Reach colon (6–12 hours transit time)
3. Colonic bacteria (*Bacteroides ovatus*, *Bifidobacterium*): reduce the anthraquinone dianthrone moiety → sennidins → **rheinanthrone** (the active form)
4. Rheinanthrone acts on colonic enterocytes:
   - **Secretory mechanism:** stimulates Cl⁻ and water secretion into lumen (via prostaglandin E2 + cAMP elevation)
   - **Absorptive inhibition:** inhibits Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase → reduced water reabsorption
   - **Motility stimulation:** increased large intestine peristalsis (ricinoleic acid mechanism simultaneously active at serotonin 5-HT4 receptors)
5. Net result: increased luminal fluid volume → softer stool + increased propulsive motility → defecation 6–12 hours after dosing

**Colonic selectivity:** The prodrug design gives senna its pharmacological advantage — unlike bisacodyl or free anthraquinones, sennosides cause minimal upper GI effects (no nausea, no cramps from stomach/small intestine activation).

**Tolerance and dependence:** Prolonged use (>2 weeks) → downregulation of colonic sensitivity and motility; "cathartic colon" (historical term); now considered potentially overstated but chronic stimulant laxative use should be monitored. Dependency is psychological as much as physiological for most patients.

## 6. Clinical Evidence

### Constipation — Level A

**Cochrane review (Ford 2014; updated Johanson/Stewart 2010):** Stimulant laxatives (primarily senna and bisacodyl) significantly superior to placebo for constipation; senna specifically:
- Multiple RCTs (Ramkumar 2005 systematic review; 20 RCTs): senna superior to placebo for all constipation types
- Comparison vs. lactulose: similar efficacy; senna faster onset; lactulose better tolerated long-term
- Comparison vs. polyethylene glycol (PEG): PEG superior for chronic idiopathic constipation in multiple RCTs; senna appropriate for acute/short-term use

**Opioid-induced constipation:**
- Senna + docusate (Senokot-S®): standard prophylactic recommendation for all opioid prescriptions in palliative care and pain management; RCTs confirm benefit (Freedman 2006)
- Naloxegol, methylnaltrexone (mu-opioid antagonists peripherally) are superior in opioid-induced constipation but more expensive; senna remains first-line for most patients

**Bowel prep (procedural) — Level A:**
- Senna-based bowel preparation (e.g., X-Prep®): used for colonoscopy prep in some protocols; comparable to PEG-based prep for mucosal visualization; less fluid required

**Palliative care constipation — Level A:**
- WHO Essential Medicines List: senna is a listed essential medicine for palliative care constipation; evidence-based recommendation by ESMO (European Society for Medical Oncology) palliative care guidelines

### Pediatric Constipation — Level B
- Multiple studies: senna at 8.8 mg sennosides/day in children aged 2–12 — efficacy demonstrated; generally safe; osmotic agents (macrogol) preferred first-line but senna appropriate second-line

## 7. Phenomenological Record

### Islamic Medical Golden Age and Senna
The Islamic medical tradition's role in standardizing senna use is without parallel in any other single plant. During the 8th–12th century Islamic medical golden age — Al-Razi (Rhazes), Al-Zahrawi (Albucasis), Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna) — senna was systematically evaluated, compared to other purgatives, dosed, and classified in terms of humoural theory (preferred for bilious/Phlegmatic constipation; not for Melancholic). These texts entered European medicine via translations at Toledo (11th–12th century Spain) and became the foundation of European pharmacological practice for 400 years.

The commercial name "Alexandrian senna" reflects the trade route through Alexandria, Egypt — the primary Egyptian export point for Sudanese-origin senna. The Arab traders who controlled this route made significant profit; control of the senna trade was a geopolitical consideration in 9th–13th century Eastern Mediterranean commerce.

### The WHO Essential Medicines Designation
Senna's inclusion on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines (EML) since 1977 reflects global consensus on its importance, safety, and cost-effectiveness for a condition (constipation) that, while not life-threatening in most cases, profoundly affects quality of life and is a major burden in palliative care. The EML designation is particularly significant given the global burden of opioid-induced constipation as palliative opioids become more widely available in lower-income countries.

### Senna Tea and "Detox" Culture
Senna tea has become a prominent feature of the "detox" and "teatox" supplement market (promoted on social media, 2015–present). Products marketed as "flat tummy tea," "skinny tea," and similar — often containing senna as the active ingredient — are sold with weight loss claims. These claims are misleading: senna produces water weight loss via laxative effect, not fat loss; diarrhea from excessive senna use can cause dangerous dehydration and electrolyte depletion; cases of severe hypokalemia (life-threatening arrhythmia) from "detox tea" overconsumption have been reported (FDA adverse event reports). The FDA has issued multiple warnings against excessive use.

### Dependency and the Cathartic Colon Controversy
The concept of "cathartic colon" — structural colonic damage from chronic stimulant laxative use, producing an atonic, dilated colon — was prominent in gastroenterology literature from 1960s–1990s, based on barium enema radiological findings. Subsequent analysis has challenged this: melanosis coli (the classic barium enema finding) is a benign pigment change; the true structural colonic damage attributed to anthraquinones may have been overstated. Current guidelines (NICE, ACG) acknowledge potential for functional dependence with prolonged use but recommend risk-benefit individualization rather than categorical prohibition.

## 8. Practical Application

### Clinical Dosing Protocol

**Constipation (adult):**
- Standardized tablet (8.8 mg sennosides): 2 tablets at bedtime → bowel movement morning (~8–12 hours)
- Standardized tablet (17.2 mg sennosides, Ex-Lax® equivalent): 1 tablet at bedtime
- Liquid preparation (15 mg sennosides/5mL): 10–15 mL at bedtime
- Maximum: 70 mg sennosides/day (short-term); standard dose is much lower

**Opioid-induced constipation prophylaxis:**
- Senna 8.8 mg + docusate 50 mg (Senokot-S® equivalent): 2 tablets BID — start when opioid started; do not wait for constipation to develop
- Titrate up to 4 tablets BID if needed

**Children (2–12 years):**
- 3.75–7.5 mg sennosides daily, adjusted by weight; AVOID in < 2 years

**Bowel preparation:**
- Per individual protocol; specialist supervision; high fluid intake essential

### Carminative Combination (for cramping)
Traditional Unani-Tibb practice: add carminatives to senna preparation to reduce cramping:
- Fennel seed (Foeniculum vulgare): 2 g with senna preparation
- Ginger (Zingiber officinale): 1 g with senna
- Caraway (Carum carvi): 1 g
- This combination reduces cramping by addressing the minor upper GI sensitization from imperfect sennoside extraction and residual free anthraquinones

### Contraindications
- **Intestinal obstruction:** ABSOLUTE
- **Appendicitis:** ABSOLUTE
- **Inflammatory bowel disease (active flare):** AVOID
- **Pregnancy:** AVOID first trimester; use ONLY if necessary in second/third trimester (low sennoside placental transfer; breast milk transfer minor); osmotic agents preferred in pregnancy
- **Children < 2 years:** AVOID
- **Abdominal pain of unknown origin:** INVESTIGATE before laxative use

### Drug Interactions
- **Cardiac glycosides (digoxin):** hypokalemia from chronic senna → increased digoxin toxicity (critical in elderly taking both)
- **Thiazide diuretics + corticosteroids:** additive potassium depletion
- **Anticoagulants (warfarin):** reduced intestinal transit time → unpredictably reduced absorption of some drugs; indirect vitamin K effects
- **CYP3A4:** minimal interaction vs. rhubarb (sennosides have no direct CYP inhibition)

### Electrolyte Monitoring
For any patient using senna > 1 week regularly:
- Monitor serum potassium (K⁺): hypokalemia risk (increased with high doses, elderly, concurrent diuretics)
- Consider oral potassium supplementation if K⁺ < 3.5 mmol/L

### Energetic Classification
**Primary:** ◉ Qi (functional) — colonic Qi movement; "downward draining" function  
**Secondary:** None — senna is highly specific in its colonic mechanism with limited systemic effects beyond electrolyte management
