# Bermuda grass — ثيل (thīl), also given as يندكنا (yandakanā) and Tabaristan بنداوش (bandāwash)

## *A creeping, jointed grass, usually identified as* Cynodon dactylon *(modern identification); the Canon's quotation of Dioscorides adds kinds that are not grasses* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** CM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Avicenna (CM).** Bermuda grass is named only by Avicenna's *Canon* among the texts on disk (the word *thīl* also occurs in passing elsewhere in the text, line 10101, as a comparison for another plant, and line 11778 in a list; these are not used). It is read in the Arabic (`Sources/CM_Avicenna/text/IbnSina_Qanun_OpenITI_Shamela0010706.txt`, Book II, entry *thīl*, heading at Arabic text line 18757).

*Al-māhiyya*: "it is said that it is *yandakanā*, and the people of Tabaristan call it *bandāwash*; it is a known plant with jointed branches that run on the face of the earth, from whose branches roots strike into the ground, sweet in taste, with broad leaves, sharp at the ends, stiff like the leaves of the small reed; cattle and all beasts graze it" (نبات معروف وله أغصان ذات عقد يسعى على وجه الأرض ...).

**Avicenna quotes Dioscorides** ("Dioscorides said", قال ديسقوريدوس) for further kinds. As the Arabic text reports him: another kind has more leaves, branches and roots, is useful in medicine, and kills the herds that eat it, especially that growing on the roads of Babylon. A second kind grows "in the land of Orsus" with ivy-like leaves, a sweet-smelling white flower, small useful fruit, and five or six white, soft, sweet, stinking roots as thick as a finger. Its juice, boiled with wine or honey in equal amount, with half a part of myrrh and a third each of pepper and frankincense, "is a useful medicine" kept "for diverse diseases" in a copper box. A third kind grows in Qālīqiyā (Cilicia) and is called *nabt*: a beast eating it fresh is quickly sated, and cattle that eat much of it swell. I did not check these passages against the Dioscorides text on disk, so they are reported as Avicenna gives them.

**Identity.** The first description (jointed creeping stems that root at the nodes, sweet roots, reed-like stiff leaves, grazed by stock) fits the grass *Cynodon dactylon*, which is the usual modern identification of *thīl*. The Dioscorides kinds are not grasses (ivy-like leaves, white flowers, fruit), so they show that the Arabic name covered more than one plant in this passage. The modern identification applies to the first description only. [USER: verify against the Dioscorides chapter before relying on the other kinds.]

### Active Components

Bermuda grass, stated generally, is a perennial grass with creeping stems (stolons) and rhizomes, and its rhizomes store carbohydrate. The astringent action the text reports is of the kind usually attributed to tannin-bearing plants, but no composition is asserted for the plant from the text. No numbers.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Uses reported by Avicenna: as a poultice, especially the fresh root, on fresh bad wounds to close them and bring them to scar (*idmāl*); the juice, "prevents the flow of matter to the viscera"; "it prevents all the catarrhs" (*al-nawāzil*) of the head; the seed and root stop vomiting and check the flow to the stomach; the seed is a licked diuretic that breaks stone, "because of dryness joined to bitterness", the root likewise, and a decoction of both helps bladder ulcers; "drinking its decoction is good for colic and difficulty of urine and ulcers arising in the bladder"; in the eye, the juice cooked in wine and honey in equal parts, with half a part of myrrh-and-frankincense and a quarter of aloe, goes into "a good eye medicine". None of this is offered as a recommended practice.

### Preparation & Dosing

Preparations given: root as a poultice; juice; seed as a lick (*laʿūq*); decoction of seed and root; the juice cooked in wine and honey, with myrrh, frankincense and aloe, as an eye medicine; and the Dioscorides recipe above for the second kind. The proportions are given as parts, not as weights. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It may be said generally that *Cynodon dactylon* is a widespread forage and lawn grass, and that its pollen is a common cause of hay fever; this is a general fact, not drawn from the text.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Avicenna (CM).** *Al-ṭabʿ*: "cold and dry in the first degree, especially its fresh root" (بارد يابس في الأولى خصوصا أصله الطري). *Al-afʿāl wa-l-khawāṣṣ*: "its strength is astringent, and there is a sting in it" (قوته قابضة وفيه لذع). Its juice "prevents the pouring of matters to the viscera."

### Cultivation Register

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent.* The entry is an astringent and wound-closing drug, organised by temperament, then by the organ treated. It carries no cultivation status, and nothing is said of innate heat, radical moisture or the *ruḥ*. The Dioscorides material that Avicenna quotes is likewise a plain agricultural and medicinal description.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to cultivate: a cooling, astringent, wound-binding plant and diuretic. The nearest thing in the text to a discipline is Dioscorides' observation, relayed by Avicenna, that a plant which heals may kill the herds that graze it (the road-side kind from Babylon). Provenance matters.

### Contraindications

Historical record only. The entry reports that one Dioscorides kind kills the livestock that eat it and that cattle swell from eating another kind in quantity; no toxicity of the first, medicinal kind is stated. The recipes mix the plant with myrrh, frankincense, pepper and wine; no safety information on any is given. Do not treat any use here as medical advice.
