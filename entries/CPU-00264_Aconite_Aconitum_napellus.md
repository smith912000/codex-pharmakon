### Origin & Tradition

If antiquity crowned any plant the **"queen of poisons,"** it was aconite. *Aconitum napellus* — wolfsbane for its use to poison wolves and other predators, monkshood for the hooded shape of its violet-blue flower — appears in *De Materia Medica* Book IV, where Dioscorides records both its lethality and its use as a tipped-arrow and bait poison. The Greeks derived its mythology from the underworld itself: aconite was said to have sprung from the slaver of Cerberus dragged up from Hades by Heracles, and Medea was reputed to have used it against Theseus. The name *akoniton* and its associations with rocky, cliff-side growth and with death run through the whole classical tradition.

In Greco-Roman law and lore aconite was the assassin's and the executioner's plant. So feared was it that, by tradition, its mere possession could be treated as evidence of intent to murder under Roman law. It was the poison of legend for arrows, for baited carcasses set against wolves and leopards (hence *lycoctonum*, "wolf-killer," for a related species), and for human murder. Its reputation for swift, certain lethality persisted unbroken from antiquity through the Renaissance herbals to the modern era, where it remains a recurrent agent in homicide and suicide and a classic of the forensic toxicology literature.

The Eastern traditions tell a strikingly different story — not of poison alone, but of poison transformed into one of the most valued of all medicines. In **Chinese Medicine**, the lateral (daughter) roots of *Aconitum carmichaelii*, when subjected to elaborate detoxifying processing (**paozhi**), become **Fuzi** (附子), the supreme warming agent for "rescuing devastated yang" — used in classical formulae for cold limbs, collapse, and failing vitality. The unprocessed mother root, **Chuan Wu** (川烏), and the related **Cao Wu** (草烏) are far more toxic and used with extreme caution. In **Ayurveda**, related aconites (Vatsanabha, *Aconitum ferox* and allies) are likewise subjected to rigorous **shodhana** (purification, e.g. in cow's urine or milk) before use. In both systems the entire art lies in the processing: the raw drug is a deadly poison, the processed drug a powerful warming medicine, and the difference between them is a chemical transformation of the alkaloids.

That chemistry is now understood. The lethal principle is **aconitine** and its congeners — diterpenoid ester alkaloids that act on the voltage-gated sodium channel, holding it open and producing intractable cardiac arrhythmia. Traditional processing (prolonged soaking, boiling, steaming) **hydrolyses the highly toxic diester alkaloids to far less toxic mono-ester and aconine derivatives** — a genuine detoxification that the empirical traditions arrived at long before the chemistry was known. This entry stands in the Codex as the paramount example of a botanical whose toxicity and therapeutic value are separated only by processing, and of the absolute necessity of that processing.

### Active Components

- **Aconitine** — the principal toxic diterpenoid (C19-norditerpenoid) **diester alkaloid**. It binds voltage-gated sodium channels and holds them in the open state, causing persistent depolarisation of cardiac and nerve membranes. The result is the hallmark of aconite poisoning: paraesthesiae and numbness, then refractory ventricular arrhythmias (including ventricular tachycardia/fibrillation), and death. Extremely potent — among the most cardiotoxic of plant alkaloids.
- **Mesaconitine, hypaconitine, jesaconitine** — related diester diterpenoid alkaloids sharing the sodium-channel mechanism and contributing to overall toxicity; their relative proportions vary by species.
- **Benzoylaconine / aconine (mono-ester and amine alkaloids)** — the **hydrolysis products** formed during traditional processing and boiling. These are **markedly less toxic** than the parent diester alkaloids and account for the detoxification achieved by paozhi/shodhana, while some retain pharmacological (e.g. cardiotonic, analgesic) activity. This hydrolytic conversion is the chemical basis of traditional processing.
- **General class:** C19 norditerpenoid alkaloids. Toxicity tracks with the **diester** alkaloids; safety with their conversion to mono-ester and amine forms.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Recorded as **historical and specialist material.** Raw aconite is never self-administered. Processed-aconite (Fuzi) use belongs strictly to qualified practitioners of the relevant tradition.

1. **Yang-rescuing agent — processed Fuzi (Chinese Medicine).** The pre-eminent use of the *processed* lateral root: warming the interior, restoring "devastated yang," treating cold-pattern collapse and severe interior cold. Used only after correct paozhi processing and typically decocted long.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM / Galenic humoral degree:** Hot and Dry in the **fourth (lethal) degree** — the highest, frankly poisonous degree in the Galenic scale. The Western tradition classed aconite essentially as a poison rather than a routine medicine.
- **Chinese Medicine mapping:** Processed **Fuzi** (附子) — **acrid, sweet, very hot; toxic.** Enters Heart, Kidney and Spleen channels. Actions: restores devastated yang, augments fire, dispels cold, relieves pain. Raw **Chuan Wu / Cao Wu** — more toxic, used externally or with great caution internally. A premier yang tonic precisely because it is so hot.
- **Ayurvedic mapping:** Vatsanabha (related aconites) — *ushna virya* (hot potency), purified by shodhana; used in minute, processed quantities in classical formulations.
- **Kenetica Qi/Shen tier:** ◉ **Qi** — at the extreme of the warming/yang-moving axis (processed Fuzi is the archetypal "restore-yang" agent), with a ☿ **Shen** ascription reflecting its life-or-death, "rescue from collapse" action on the vital spark. The most paradoxical entry in the volume: deadliest poison and supreme warming medicine in one root.

### Contraindications

- **Raw/unprocessed root — absolute prohibition.** Never to be ingested in any unsupervised context. Lethal dose is small.
- **Cardiac disease — absolute.** Arrhythmogenic; can cause refractory ventricular arrhythmia and death.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding — absolute.** Toxic; traditionally contraindicated.
- **Heat patterns / yin deficiency (CM).** Even processed Fuzi is contraindicated in heat patterns and yin-deficiency with heat — its extreme heat aggravates them.
- **Under-processing / under-decoction.** Documented cause of poisoning even with "processed" material; the processing and pre-decoction are non-negotiable safety steps.
- **Combination rules (CM).** Classically incompatible with several herbs under traditional pairing rules (the aconite incompatibilities — e.g. with Ban Xia, Gua Lou, Bei Mu, Bai Lian, Bai Ji per the "eighteen incompatibilities"). [USER: verify the exact incompatibility list.]
- **Skin contact.** Alkaloids absorb through skin and mucous membranes; handle raw plant with protection.
- **General:** any suspected ingestion is a life-threatening emergency with no field antidote — supportive intensive care only.
