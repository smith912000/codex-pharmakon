# Plantago coronopis — Koronopous (Dioscorides 2-158)

## *Plantago coronopis, as it stands in "Plantago coronopis var β [Linnaeus]" among the names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 2-158 ("Buckshorn Plantain, Star of the Earth")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (lines 5895 and 5896) prints a run of names: "Coronopus, Cornu cervium [Fuchs], Coronopus hortensis [Bauhin], Plantago coronopis var β [Linnaeus], Coronopus, Cornus cervicum, Herba stella — Buckshorn Plantain, Star of the Earth". The binomial is the Linnaean name in the middle of that run. The row was added from the line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *2-158. KORONOPOUS*, heading at line 5893, text at line 5897. It stands in Book Two, after *2-157. KRITHMON* and before *2-159*, the sowthistle chapter. The translators' index gives *Coronopus* and *Coronopus hortensis* at page 284.

The whole chapter reads: "Coronopus is a little herb that is somewhat long, with indented leaves, spread on the ground. It is eaten (boiled) as a vegetable. It has a thin astringent root that is eaten for coeliac [intestinal complaints]. It grows in untilled places, on hillocks and by highways. It is also called ammonos, or astrion, the Africans call it atirsipte, the Latins caciatrix, some, stilago, or sanguinaria."

Dioscorides gives a description, a habitat and two uses, both of them foods. The herb is "somewhat long", with "indented leaves", spread on the ground. It "is eaten (boiled) as a vegetable". The root is "thin" and "astringent" and "is eaten for coeliac", which the translators bracket as "[intestinal complaints]". He says it grows "in untilled places, on hillocks and by highways". The chapter prints five further names: the Greek *ammonos* and *astrion*, the African *atirsipte*, the Latin *caciatrix*, and, from "some", *stilago* and *sanguinaria*. They are names only, with no use or belief attached.

**The plant as a leaf comparison.** The chapter is cited in other chapters as a reference leaf. In 4-70 *PSULLION* the leaf is said to be "similar to coronopis [2-158] only longer" (line 8643). In 4-153 *SESAMOEIDES MIKRON* the leaves are "similar to coronopis, yet rougher and smaller" (line 9396). The translators' cross-reference in 4-70 is bracketed as 2-158, and the 4-153 text does not cite a chapter. Another chapter, treated in I_1102 (*Holosteum umbellatum*), likewise compares its tendrils to those of "coronopus [2-158]" (line 8102). The entry I_886 (Hare's Ear) lists 2-158 among comparison plants in a note. None of these is an entry on 2-158 itself.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter number, *Koronopous*, *coronopus* and *Buckshorn* found no existing entry on this chapter. The plantain entries I_189 and I_469 (*Plantago major*) and I_676 (*Plantago lanceolata*) rest on other chapters (the plantain chapter is 2-153 *ARNOGLOSSON*) and are not on this plant.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a boiled vegetable and a thin astringent root eaten for "coeliac" complaints, which the translators gloss as intestinal. These are the text's own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for intestinal complaints, and wild plants from roadsides and untilled ground are not recommended foods. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation: the herb is "eaten (boiled) as a vegetable". The root is "eaten" for the complaint. No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints the root as "astringent". It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality for the herb and no taste.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers is the small discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: a form (long, indented leaves, spread on the ground), a root checked by taste (astringent) and a place (untilled ground, hillocks, roadsides). The plant is a food and a gut remedy in the text, and no status is recorded beyond that. The chapter records no amuletic use. The identification is the translators'.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It offers a description, a habitat, a boiled vegetable and a root eaten for intestinal complaints, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Plants growing "by highways" and in untilled ground may be contaminated, and persistent intestinal complaints need medical assessment. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion among several names, so no wild plant should be gathered or eaten on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
