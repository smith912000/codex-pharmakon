# I-255: Cinnamomum cassia / aromaticum (Cassia Cinnamon / Chinese Cinnamon)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-255 | Lauraceae**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Cinnamomum cassia* (L.) J.Presl (syn. *C. aromaticum* Nees)
**Family:** Lauraceae

**Common Names:**
- English: Cassia Cinnamon, Chinese Cinnamon, Bastard Cinnamon (pejorative trade term)
- Mandarin: *Ròu guì* (肉桂) — the primary TCM cinnamon; literally "flesh/meat cassia"
- Japanese: *Nikkei* (肉桂)
- Vietnamese: *Quế* (dominant producer; Vietnamese cinnamon C. loureiroi is related)
- Arabic: *Saleekha* / *Dār-sīnī* — used interchangeably with true cinnamon in some historical sources; distinguished as inferior in quality by Ibn Sīnā
- Sanskrit: *Tejapatra* (primarily refers to bay leaf species C. tamala in Indian context)
- Greek: *Kassia* (κασσία) — Dioscorides distinguishes *kassia* from *kinnamōmon* (I-254)

**Critical Note on Cassia Varieties:**
"Cassia cinnamon" encompasses multiple *Cinnamomum* species:
- *C. cassia* (Chinese Cinnamon, true cassia) — China origin; 1–5% coumarin
- *C. loureiroi* (Vietnamese/Saigon Cinnamon) — Vietnam; up to 6.97% coumarin; most pungent
- *C. burmanni* (Korintje/Indonesian Cinnamon) — Indonesia; intermediate coumarin
- *C. tamala* (Indian Bay Leaf) — South Asian; *tejapatra*; leaves used differently

All share high coumarin content relative to C. verum; coumarin content distinguishes them from true cinnamon for safety purposes. This entry primarily covers *C. cassia* (Chinese) and notes where C. loureiroi differs.

**Physical Distinction from C. verum:**
- Bark: Thick single-layer rolls (vs. Ceylon's thin nested quills)
- Color: Reddish-brown (vs. Ceylon's tan-brown)
- Aroma: Pungent, spicy, sharp (vs. Ceylon's sweet, delicate)
- Taste: More intense, slightly bitter (coumarin contribution)

**Part Used:** Bark (dried; ground); twigs (*Gui Zhi*, distinct from the bark *Rou Gui*); essential oil.

**TCM Distinction — Gui Zhi vs. Rou Gui:**
In TCM, two distinct preparations from *C. cassia* are used differently:
- *Rou Gui* (肉桂): Bark of mature branches; warming Yang; tonifying Kidney Yang; dispelling cold from interior
- *Gui Zhi* (桂枝): Young twigs; dispersing cold from exterior; promoting circulation; used in Gui Zhi Tang (Cinnamon Twig Decoction) — the foundational Shang Han Lun formula

This is a pharmacologically meaningful distinction: twig vs. bark have different constituent ratios (Gui Zhi has lower cinnamaldehyde relative to other cinnamon terpenes; Rou Gui is cinnamaldehyde-richer).

**TCM primacy:**
For TCM specifically, *C. cassia* is THE cinnamon — *C. verum* is the imported secondary. The Shang Han Lun's *Gui Zhi Tang* (Cinnamon Twig Decoction: Gui Zhi + Bai Shao + Zhi Gan Cao + Sheng Jiang + Da Zao) is arguably the most fundamental TCM formula for treatment of *Tai Yang* disease (exterior cold patterns); it represents 2,000 years of continuous clinical use as a core foundational prescription.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domain:** Warming Yang-tonifying / digestive — TCM primary; convergent with CM, SS, FMT.

**Cassia-Specific TCM Indications (beyond general cinnamon):**
1. **Yang deficiency patterns:** Kidney Yang deficiency (cold limbs, impotence, chronic diarrhea) — Rou Gui is first-line in *Jingui Shenqi Wan* (Kidney Qi Formula)
2. **Exterior cold dispersal:** Gui Zhi in Tai Yang wind-cold invasions — the original indication of the *Shang Han Lun*
3. **Blood stasis with cold:** Gui Zhi in formulas addressing cold-induced gynecological stagnation
4. **Fire-bringing down:** Unique TCM concept — Rou Gui in very small doses "leads fire back to its source" (Yuan Yang Zi Wei concept)

**Energetic Classification:**
- ◈ Jing (constitutional): Primary for Rou Gui — Kidney Yang tonification touches the constitutional level more deeply than C. verum
- ◉ Qi (functional): Primary for Gui Zhi — exterior wind-cold dispersal; circulation activation
- ⊕ Yuan (constitutional/deepest): For Kidney Yang deficiency patterns — Yuan Qi warming

**Primary Tier: ◈ Jing + ⊕ Yuan** (deeper constitutional/constitutional root warming than C. verum which is more ◉ Qi)

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Essential Oil (1.0–3.5% bark; higher than C. verum)

**1. (E)-Cinnamaldehyde — 55–95% of bark oil**
- Identical mechanisms to C. verum (I-254): anti-diabetic, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory
- Concentration generally slightly higher in C. cassia than C. verum

**2. Coumarin — 0.45–6.97% (CRITICAL DIFFERENCE FROM C. VERUM)**
- Not present as essential oil constituent but as secondary metabolite
- Mechanism: Coumarin is a precursor to dicoumarol (warfarin-type anticoagulant)
- Metabolism: CYP2A6 → 3-hydroxycoumarin → catechol → reactive o-quinone → hepatotoxic
- Daily tolerable intake (EFSA): 0.1 mg/kg body weight/day = ~7 mg/day for 70 kg adult
- *C. loureiroi* (Vietnamese): Up to 6.97% coumarin = ~700 mg/10 g powder = 100× over daily limit at high medicinal doses
- *C. cassia*: Typically 1–5% = 100–500 mg/10 g — exceeds EFSA limit at doses used in T2DM trials (6 g/day Khan 2003)
- **HEPATOTOXICITY:** Chronic high-dose cassia → hepatic injury (case reports in European supplement market; EU supplement coumarin limit: 12 mg/day)

**3. Methyl Eugenol — trace to 1%**
- Genotoxic potential (EFSA regulated); lower than in some other spices

**4. Cinnamyl Alcohol, Cinnamic Acid — minor**

**5. *Gui Zhi* (twig) — distinctive additional components:**
- Lower cinnamaldehyde than bark; higher cinnamic acid and cinnamyl acetate
- Diaphoretic action (promotes perspiration); anti-viral compounds; different pharmacological profile from bark

### Non-Volatile Constituents

**6. Procyanidins (Type A) — anti-diabetic (as in C. verum; similar)**

**7. Coumarin glycosides** — release coumarin on hydrolysis

**8. Catechin, Epicatechin, Procyanidin B-type** — antioxidants

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### 1. Identical to C. verum for Anti-diabetic Action
All three mechanisms documented for C. verum (PTP1B inhibition; IR-TK activation; AMPK):
- Cinnamaldehyde (same compound): identical molecular targets
- Procyanidins (same class): identical insulin signaling enhancement
- The majority of clinical T2DM trials (including the landmark Khan 2003 paper) used C. cassia

### 2. Coumarin-Specific Mechanisms (Cassia-Unique)
- **Anticoagulant:** Coumarin → dicoumarol (natural warfarin) — reduces vitamin K-dependent clotting factors
- **Hepatotoxicity (CYP2A6-mediated):** 3-hydroxycoumarin reactive quinone → hepatocellular damage
- **Anti-inflammatory (paradoxical low-dose):** Coumarin at low concentrations inhibits PGE₂; anti-edema
- Net: Coumarin creates a therapeutic window/toxicity profile absent from C. verum

### 3. TCM Yang-Tonification Mechanism (Rou Gui)
- Cinnamaldehyde vasodilation (peripheral warming effect) → subjective and measurable warmth restoration in cold-pattern deficiency
- Adrenal hormone support (preclinical): cinnamon extracts modulate adrenal steroid synthesis in animal models
- HPA axis modulation at high doses: possible cortisol/DHEA ratio effects consistent with TCM "Yang-supporting" clinical outcomes

### 4. Gui Zhi Diaphoretic Mechanism
- Cinnamyl alcohol + cinnamic acid in twigs → peripheral vasodilation → promotes perspiration
- Anti-viral immune mechanism: promotes fever resolution via sweating (TCM theory of releasing the exterior)
- Different from bark's deeper warming action

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

### Type 2 Diabetes

**Khan et al. 2003 (Diabetes Care) — NOTE: Used C. cassia at 1, 3, 6 g/day:**
- Same study as referenced in C. verum entry (I-254) — most cited cinnamon T2DM paper used C. cassia
- At 6 g/day for 40 days: fasting glucose −29%; triglycerides −30%; LDL −27%
- At 1 g/day: fasting glucose −18%; lipids similarly improved
- Safety at 6g/day C. cassia for 40 days: no hepatotoxicity reported in short trial; BUT long-term 6g/day would approach hepatotoxic coumarin doses

**Clinical implication:** Khan's positive data was from C. cassia, not C. verum. C. verum has equivalent mechanism but much safer coumarin profile. For long-term anti-diabetic use, C. verum at 3–6 g/day is safer than C. cassia at same dose.

### TCM Formula Evidence (Gui Zhi Tang)

**Classical clinical evidence:** 2,000+ years of TCM clinical documentation for Gui Zhi Tang in *Tai Yang* wind-cold patterns. Modern TCM RCTs (Chinese literature) show consistent benefit for mild viral upper respiratory illness treated early (within 24 hours). Level B in Chinese medical literature context.

**Systematic review of TCM Gui Zhi formulas (multiple Chinese meta-analyses 2010–2020):** Consistent therapeutic benefit for specific cold-pattern syndromes; heterogeneous preparations make Western-standard meta-analysis impossible; Level C by Western standards.

### Coumarin Safety Evidence

**EFSA 2008 Scientific Opinion on Coumarin:** Established 0.1 mg/kg/day tolerable daily intake; identified C. loureiroi and C. cassia as primary exposure sources in food/supplements. EU supplement coumarin limit set at 12 mg/day (equivalent to ~0.25 g C. cassia high-coumarin variety or ~3 g C. cassia average variety).

**Case reports of cassia hepatotoxicity:** At least 12 published cases of hepatic injury attributed to cinnamon supplement use; likely C. cassia high-coumarin; jaundice, elevated liver enzymes; resolved with discontinuation.

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

Cassia's phenomenological signature is more pungent, intense, and immediately heating than Ceylon cinnamon — the archetypal "cinnamon" aroma of American cuisine (where cassia dominates the retail market) is actually cassia's more aggressive profile rather than the delicate sweet warmth of C. verum.

**TCM clinical phenomenology:**
The Yang-tonification effect of Rou Gui in cold-pattern deficiency (chronic cold extremities, fatigue, reduced libido, chronic diarrhea) is described by TCM practitioners as a gradual restoration of "inner fire" — not a stimulant rush but a progressive warming of constitution over weeks. This aligns with the slow-onset adrenal/hormonal modulation proposed mechanistically.

**Gui Zhi Tang phenomenology (acute Tai Yang invasion):**
Classical description: The onset of *Tai Yang* disease (early viral infection with chills, mild fever, stiff neck, floating pulse) treated with Gui Zhi Tang produces perspiration and resolution of the exterior pattern within 1–3 doses. The diaphoretic-mediated resolution of early viral illness corresponds to the modern understanding of fever as host defense — promoting perspiration at this stage assists immune clearance without suppressing the fever response.

**Cassia aroma vs. Ceylon:**
Cassia is more immediately recognizable (the American "red-hot candy" cinnamon aroma); Ceylon is more refined and complex. The pungency difference corresponds to cinnamaldehyde concentration differences and the coumarin contribution to bitterness.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**CRITICAL SAFETY PREFERENCE:**
**For therapeutic/medicinal anti-diabetic use: Always specify C. verum (Ceylon Cinnamon).** The clinical benefits are equivalent; the coumarin hepatotoxicity risk is essentially absent with C. verum. Use C. cassia only if C. verum unavailable, and at doses not exceeding 1 g/day for long-term use.

**C. cassia dosing safety limits:**
- Short-term (≤4 weeks): Up to 6 g/day per Khan 2003 protocol — short-term hepatotoxicity uncommon at this dose
- Long-term (>4 weeks): Limit to ≤1–2 g/day C. cassia (average coumarin content); monitor liver function
- C. loureiroi (Vietnamese): More restrictive — ≤0.5 g/day for chronic use due to very high coumarin

**TCM Gui Zhi Tang (practitioner-guided):**
- Gui Zhi: 9–12 g per decoction (full formula includes Bai Shao, Zhi Gan Cao, Sheng Jiang, Da Zao)
- Decocted 20–30 minutes; drink warm; take 3× daily for 3–5 days maximum for exterior patterns
- Requires TCM practitioner guidance for accurate pattern diagnosis

**Rou Gui (TCM Yang deficiency):**
- 3–6 g per decoction; added in last 5 minutes of cooking (volatile cinnamaldehyde preserved)
- In compound formulas (Jingui Shenqi Wan, You Gui Wan): standard TCM prescribing practice

### Dosing Summary

| Indication | Form | C. cassia dose | C. verum alternative |
|------------|------|----------------|----------------------|
| T2DM short-term | Powder/capsule | 1–3 g/day ≤4 weeks | 3–6 g/day indefinitely |
| T2DM long-term | Powder/capsule | ≤1 g/day | 3–6 g/day |
| TCM Gui Zhi (exterior cold) | Decoction | 9–12 g (formula) | N/A (different tradition) |
| Digestive/carminative | Tea/culinary | Culinary amounts safe | Preferred |

### Safety Profile

**Hepatotoxicity (PRIMARY C. cassia concern):**
- Risk at doses >3 g/day C. cassia long-term (high coumarin)
- Monitor: Baseline liver enzymes; LFTs at 4–8 weeks if using therapeutic doses
- Contraindicated in hepatic disease

**Drug Interactions:**
- **Anticoagulants:** Coumarin anticoagulant effect + cinnamaldehyde antiplatelet → significant additive warfarin/anticoagulant interaction; **monitor INR more carefully with cassia than Ceylon**
- **Antidiabetics:** Identical additive hypoglycemia risk as C. verum
- **Hepatotoxic drugs:** Additive hepatotoxicity risk (coumarin)

**Contraindications:**
- Hepatic disease (relative/absolute depending on severity)
- Long-term high-dose use (>3 g/day beyond 4 weeks without LFT monitoring)
- Anticoagulant use at therapeutic doses (relative; monitoring required)
- Pregnancy (high dose, as with all Cinnamomum species)

**Consumer guidance:**
At culinary amounts in cooking — cassia is entirely safe (normal food use); safety concerns apply to medicinal/supplement doses. The European Food Safety Authority's coumarin guidelines are for supplement doses, not the amounts consumed in normal cooking.
