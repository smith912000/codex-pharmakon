# Pompholyx (zinc oxide) — Pompholux / pompholyx (as printed in the 2000 English edition of Dioscorides)
## *The light white furnace deposit from brass-working, glossed by the translator as "Zinc Oxide — Pompholyx" and elsewhere in the same edition as "crude zinc oxide"; the chapter itself defines it by how it is made, not by a mineral name* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus for this entry is *De Materia Medica* 5-85 *Pompholux* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 10228-10241; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row "5-85 (line 10228)"; `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 812, class mineral). The translator's gloss under the heading is "Zinc Oxide — Pompholyx" (line 10229). The chapter follows 5-84 *Kadmeia*, cadmia (line 10220), from which pompholyx is made, and precedes 5-86 *Antispoda*, "Ashes used instead of Spodium [5-85]" (line 10242). A further mention occurs in 5-132 *Trux*, the sediment of wine or vinegar, which "is washed the same as pompholyx [crude zinc oxide]" (line 10524; the bracket is the translator's).

The chapter is mostly a description of how the substance is made, chosen and cleaned. Its parts, in order:

*What it is, against spodium (line 10230).* "Pompholyx differs from spodium [calcinated powder] specifically." Spodos, the text says, is "somewhat black and for the most part heavier as it is full of lumps, hairs and earth, because it is the scrapings and shavings from the floors and hearths in the brass refiners' shops." Pompholyx "is fat, white and altogether very light, so that it can fly into the air." There are "two kinds of this; one is the colour of the air and somewhat fat. The other is very white and the most light."

*How it forms (lines 10230-10234).* It becomes white "during the working and finishing of the brass when the brass refiners sprinkle on crumbled cadmia thickly, wanting to have it better; for the smoke that is carried up from this is very white and is turned into pompholyx." It is also made on purpose "from cadmia intentionally blown with the bellows". The text then describes the workshop: a room with a ceiling, a chimney, a window near the loft, a small hole through the wall to the furnace for the bellows, a door, and a second room for the bellows blower. Cadmia pounded small is sprinkled into a coal fire from above; the "thin light part" is carried up and sticks to the walls and roof, at first "like bubbles standing on water", later "like wool fleeces", while the heavier particles fall to the floor and are "thought to be worse... because it is earthy and full of filth."

*Which is best, and how to test it (line 10234).* "It is thought that the Cyprian (which, worked into a paste with vinegar, has the smell of brass) is the best. The colour is pitchy, and furthermore it is like dirt to the taste. When pure it boils if it is put on a burning coal, becoming an airy colour." The sentence follows the remark that some think spodos is made only this way, and the text does not say whether "pitchy" and "like dirt to the taste" describe the spodos or the pompholyx; the passage should be read with that doubt. [USER: verify against the Greek] The text then warns that it "is counterfeited with bulls' glue, or lambs' lungs, with lungs of the sea [jellyfish], or with burnt green figs and some other similar things. These are easily perceived for none of the previously mentioned tests work on them."

*How to wash it (lines 10235-10241).* Three methods are given. In the first, the material, dry or made into a paste, is bound in a thin clean linen cloth and plunged about in rain water so that "the slimy matter and the stuff which is good can run out, but all the refuse will remain in the cloth"; it is allowed to settle, the water is strained off with the ashes, and the pouring and rolling are repeated "until no sandy matter subsides"; it is then dried and put in jars (lines 10235-10239). In the second, it is dried hard, squeezed in the hands with water to a honey-like consistency, strained through linen over a jar, more water poured on, the frothy stuff skimmed with a spoon into a new ceramic jar, and the settled part strained again into other jars "until the ashes become clean and without sand" (line 10240). In the third, a whole piece is wetted gradually so that the sand and stone sink and the filaments float, and the ashes in the middle are washed "like cadmia" (line 10241). A fourth note is that it can be washed with "Chian wine diluted with seawater", and "this is more astringent than that washed with water" (line 10241).

*Its qualities (line 10241).* "Pompholyx is astringent, cooling, filling, purging, and somewhat drying." The chapter then turns back to spodium: it "should be roasted after first being carefully and finely pounded", sprinkled with water, made into lozenges, put in a new ceramic jar on a small, gentle coal fire, and turned "until they become red and dry". It adds that spodium "is also made out of gold and silver, and furthermore also from lead; that from lead is the best, then that from copper."

Unlike most Dioscorides chapters, this one prints no list of diseases treated. The medicinal indications for pompholyx, in the sources read, are limited to the qualities in line 10241. The following chapter, 5-86, is on ashes that serve in place of spodium and does not mention pompholyx by name. No other tradition is tagged for this entry.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, the fume that rises from heating brass (an alloy of copper and zinc) in air condenses as fine particles of zinc oxide, a white powder; the translator's gloss rests on that, not on any statement in the text. The darker, heavier material swept from the floors would be mixed with ash, copper compounds and dirt, which fits Dioscorides' account of spodos. Zinc oxide is insoluble in water and dissolves in acids. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved say that Dioscorides described pompholyx, with its qualities of being astringent, cooling, filling, purging and somewhat drying, but the chapter read gives no disease for which it was used. The Codex does not recommend swallowing or inhaling metal-working fume or any "furnace deposit". The material was made in a workshop where fume was the product, and nothing in the sources supports a modern use. What the chapter does preserve is a working example of how a Greek physician handled a manufactured mineral: by provenance, by tests and by repeated washing.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations the text gives are for cleaning, not for use: washing in a cloth in rain water, washing by skimming, washing by letting the whole piece fall apart in water, and washing with Chian wine diluted with seawater (lines 10235-10241). Drying and storing in jars are mentioned (lines 10239-10240). No quantity of material, no number of washings beyond "repeat" and "often", no weight and no medicinal dose is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Zinc oxide is a white powder used today in skin protectants and ointments as a mild astringent and sun-blocking agent; that is a modern fact and not something this chapter says. Inhaling zinc oxide fume causes metal fume fever, a flu-like illness, and fume from heated brass can also contain copper and lead. Zinc oxide made in a metal workshop is not pure. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** the qualities printed for pompholyx are in line 10241: "astringent, cooling, filling, purging, and somewhat drying". The wine-washed form is "more astringent than that washed with water". No degree is given, no taste beyond "like dirt to the taste" (which may refer to spodos; see above), and no moisture rating beyond "somewhat drying". No other tradition is tagged, so no other classification is offered.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention, and this chapter is one of the fullest examples in Book 5: it separates pompholyx from the heavier, dirtier spodos by look and weight; it says where the material forms and how to make it deliberately; it names a place (Cyprus) as giving the best; it gives sensory tests (smell of brass on mixing with vinegar, behaviour on a burning coal); it names counterfeits and says that the tests defeat them; and it gives three washing methods that depend on watching the material settle. Those are practices of looking, testing and cleaning. The chapter gives pompholyx no cultivation status and prints no effect on the vital constitution of the person; its only statement of effect is a list of qualities. No amuletic use is recorded.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator beyond the attention it shows: know how a material is made, test it before trusting it, and clean it before use. The Codex does not recommend using pompholyx. A reader who wants the lesson of the chapter can take its method, not its substance. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Fume and dust:** do not make or breathe metal-working fume; zinc oxide fume causes metal fume fever, and brass fume can carry lead.
- **Ingestion:** a crude furnace deposit may contain copper, lead and other metals; do not swallow it. The text calls it "purging", and a purge is a poisoning risk here.
- **Eyes and wounds:** crude material is not sterile; keep it away from the eye and from broken skin.
- **Pregnancy and children:** unsuitable.
- **Adulteration:** the text itself records counterfeits (bulls' glue, lungs, burnt figs); any material called pompholyx today is a different, usually refined, product and cannot be assumed to match the ancient one.
- **Identification:** the equation with zinc oxide is the translator's gloss; the text defines the substance by its making.
