# Goby — Kobios (as printed in the Osbaldeston and Wood Dioscorides; the translators' suggested identification is "Sea Gudgeon")
## *A small sea fish; the translators give "Gobiidae, Gobius species — Sea Gudgeon"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The goby is chapter 2-32 of *De Materia Medica*, headed "KOBIOS", in the Osbaldeston and Wood English (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, line 5066; the entry is also in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`). The translators add a suggested identification line (line 5067): "SUGGESTED: Gobiidae, Gobius species — Sea Gudgeon". That line is the editors' gloss, not Dioscorides' text; the Greek name *kobios* is the only identification the author gives, and the English text itself calls the fish a "sea gudgeon". "Goby" is the entry name used by this Codex. The chapter reads (line 5068):

"Place a freshly caught sea gudgeon in a swine's stomach and sew it up. Boil it with twelve pints of water until it is reduced to two pints and then strain and cool it in the open air. If you give it to someone to drink you shall in this way bring down his bowels without any disturbance. Applied it helps those bitten by dogs or snakes."

So there are two uses in the chapter: a drink of the reduced broth as a purgative that works "without any disturbance", and an external application ("applied") for the bites of dogs and snakes. Whether "applied" refers to the broth or to the fish itself is not stated. [USER: verify]

The chapter sits among the fish chapters of Book Two, "Living Creatures". Neighbouring fish chapters give other uses. 2-30 *Smaris* (smelt) uses a salted fish's burnt head for abnormal growths of flesh; 2-31 *Mainis* uses a small sea fish's burnt head and its *garum* sauce; 2-29 *Ailouros* (the sheath fish, "Silurus") eaten fresh "is nourishing and good for the stomach, but salted it yields no nourishment"; and 2-33 *Omotarichos* is salted tunny for viper bites. They are separate substances and are not folded into this entry.

### Active Components

The goby family (Gobiidae) is a large group of small, mostly bottom-living fishes. Fish flesh is water and protein with some fat, and contains minerals; bones and skin add gelatin (collagen) when boiled in water. The preparation in the chapter, a long boil of a whole fish in a pig's stomach, would extract water-soluble proteins and gelatin and some fat into a broth. No composition specific to this entry or to the broth was read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is a first-century purgative broth and an external application for bites. The Codex gives no protocol. A purgative is not a safe self-treatment, bites from dogs or snakes are medical emergencies (infection, rabies and envenomation call for prompt professional care), and the recipe requires a pig's stomach and a whole fish, which makes a clean, safe preparation difficult. The text is recorded for what it shows of the Book Two fish chapters.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparation is given (line 5068): a freshly caught fish is put into a pig's stomach and sewn up; boiled with twelve pints of water until the water is reduced to two pints; strained; cooled in the open air. The measure given is the water ("twelve pints" reduced to "two pints"); no quantity of the broth per dose, number of doses or timing is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] Route: oral (as a drink) for the purgative effect; "applied" for bites.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. A broth of fish simmered in water is a food-like liquid containing protein, gelatin and fat. It is not an established medical laxative. Modern wound care for animal bites relies on cleaning, medical assessment and, where indicated, tetanus and rabies prophylaxis and antivenom; no fish preparation is part of it. Raw or undercooked animal organ casings can carry bacteria. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** No quality (warming, cooling, astringent, softening or otherwise) is printed in the goby chapter. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied here. What he can offer a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, season, sensory assay, detection of adulteration). This chapter has one small element of it: the fish is to be "freshly caught", and the broth cooled "in the open air", so the recipe has instructions about freshness and handling. It has no assay for telling a true goby from another small fish, no place of origin and no season. There is no cultivation status in the chapter, and no amuletic use.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. The only transferable lesson is the one Dioscorides states by example: fresh material, handled with care. The recipe is a historical record, not something to cook, and the bite use is no substitute for medical care.

### Contraindications

- **Bites from dogs or snakes:** need urgent medical care; do not rely on this text.
- **Purgatives:** a strong purgative can cause dehydration, especially in children, the elderly, pregnant women and people with bowel disease; a clinician should advise.
- **Food safety:** raw fish and animal stomach casings can carry bacteria and parasites; boiling reduces but does not remove every risk, and this recipe has no stated safety step.
- **Fish allergy:** fish is a common food allergen.
- **Legal and conservation status:** gobies are not generally protected as a group, but some species are, and fishing rules vary. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics and religion:** some traditions restrict animal products from pigs; the recipe uses a swine's stomach. This entry takes no position beyond noting it.
