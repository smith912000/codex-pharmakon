# I-623: Pennyroyal

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Family:** Lamiaceae | **Tribe:** Mentheae | **Entry Type:** TOXICOLOGICAL REFERENCE
**⚠ WARNING: NO INTERNAL USE OF ESSENTIAL OIL. HEPATOTOXIC. ABORTIFACIENT. HISTORICAL FATALITIES DOCUMENTED.**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Designation:** Pennyroyal

**Binomials:**
- *Mentha pulegium* L. (1753) — European pennyroyal; primary toxicological species
- *Hedeoma pulegioides* (L.) Pers. — American pennyroyal; identical toxicological profile; same toxic constituent (pulegone)

**Taxonomic Position:**
- Kingdom: Plantaria → Division: Magnoliophyta → Class: Magnoliopsida
- Order: Lamiales → Family: Lamiaceae → Tribe: Mentheae → Genus: *Mentha* (*M. pulegium*) / *Hedeoma* (*H. pulegioides*)
- *M. pulegium*: diploid (2n = 20), unlike most polyploid *Mentha* species
- Note: despite same family and superficially similar odor, *M. pulegium* is a distinct species from *M. spicata* and *M. × piperita* with a dramatically different chemotype

**Vernacular Names:**
- English: Pennyroyal ("puny/penny royal" — corrupted from *Pulegium regale*; Pulegium from Latin *pulex* = flea); Fleabane; Lurk-in-the-ditch; Pudding Grass (England; used in black pudding/haggis flavoring)
- Latin: *Pulegium*; *Mentha pulegium*
- Arabic: فليفلة (*fulayfila*); نعنع بري (*na'na' barrī*)
- Chinese: 美国薄荷 (American pennyroyal); 胡薄荷 (*hú bòhé*)
- Sanskrit: गंधपत्र (*gandhapattra*); related to *पुदीना* cluster
- Greek: Βληχώ (*blēchō*) — Dioscorides designation
- French: Pouliot; Menthe pouliot
- German: Poleiminze; Flohminze (flea-mint)
- Spanish: Poleo; Menta poleo
- Italian: Puleggio; Menta pulegio

**Etymology — "Pulegium":**
From Latin *pulex* (flea): pennyroyal was strewn in dwellings and rubbed on animals to repel fleas. This accounts for the alternative English name "fleabane." The insecticidal properties of pulegone (the primary toxic constituent) are relevant to this traditional use — DEET-like repellency at concentrations safe for external use, but hepatotoxic at consumed doses.

**Botanical Description (*M. pulegium*):**
Creeping or ascending herbaceous perennial, 10–40 cm height; strongly aromatic (distinctive peppermint-adjacent but sharper, camphoraceous odor with camphor and mint overlay). Leaves small, oval, 1–3 cm, slightly pubescent, short-petiolate. Flowers small, lilac-purple, in dense axillary whorls (not terminal spikes as in spearmint). Grows in damp meadows, stream margins, waste ground across southern Europe and North Africa; prefers seasonally wet, compacted soils.

*H. pulegioides* (American pennyroyal): similar habit; native to eastern North America; Algonquin/Cherokee traditional use for colds, headaches — externally for insect repulsion; EO composition virtually identical.

**Critical Confusion Risk:**
Fresh pennyroyal herb superficially resembles spearmint or other *Mentha* species. Fatalities have occurred from ingestion of pennyroyal EO obtained online under the mistaken belief that "natural" = "safe" or that it was equivalent to peppermint oil. **These are categorically different substances.**

**Core Traditional Function (both confirmed traditions):**
Emmenagogue → abortifacient. This is the single most consistent cross-traditional application. The hepatotoxic mechanism underlying pennyroyal's abortifacient action was not understood in antiquity; the clinical result (menstrual induction at sublethal doses) was observed and pharmacopoeial.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Convergence Pattern: Abortifacient/Emmenagogue Axis — Toxic Mechanism**

The 2/9 convergence score reflects genuine geographic restriction (North American pennyroyal absent from Old World systems; European pennyroyal absent from East Asian systems), not lack of pharmacological activity. Where the plant was available (Mediterranean, Near East), it was consistently used as an emmenagogue — the most direct traditional route to abortion before modern medicine.

**Historical Context of Abortifacient Use:**
Pennyroyal is among the oldest documented abortifacient plants in Western herbal literature:
- Dioscorides (65 CE): explicitly states it "expels the fetus"
- Pliny the Elder *NH* XX: "pulegium applied to the uterus... brings on the menses with great force"
- Roman satirist Juvenal (*Satires* VI, 595): references "herba" given to induce abortion — likely pennyroyal
- Medieval gynecological texts (Trotula, *Physica*): mention pulegium for suppressed menstruation
- Colonial America: Native American women (Cherokee) and European settler women both used pennyroyal tea for "bringing on menses"

**Modern Relevance:**
The ongoing availability of pennyroyal EO online, combined with historical memory of its use and misinformation about "natural" herbal abortifacients, creates an ongoing public health toxicology concern. Multiple modern fatalities (see Section 6) occurred in the context of self-managed pregnancy termination attempts.

**Energetic Tier:**
- Primary: ◈ **Jing** (constitutional; uterine/reproductive; hepatic)
- Acute toxic: System disruption (does not map to therapeutic tier framework)

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Essential Oil Composition — PRIMARY TOXIC FRACTION

| Compound | % Range | Toxicological Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Pulegone** | **40–90%** | **Primary hepatotoxin; CYP450-bioactivated to menthofuran and p-cresol** |
| (+)-Isomenthone | 5–15% | Precursor; partially converted to pulegone |
| Pulegyl acetate | 1–5% | Pulegone ester; hydrolyzed to pulegone |
| Menthol | 0–4% | Trace (vs. peppermint 35–55%) |
| Piperitone | 2–8% | Ketone; irritant |
| Piperitenone | 1–5% | Ketone; precursor |
| 1,8-Cineole | 1–5% | Non-toxic terpene |
| Isopulegol | 1–4% | Non-toxic monoterpene alcohol |
| Octanol | 0.5–2% | Minor |
| **(−)-Menthofuran** | **trace–5%** | **Primary reactive metabolite; formed by CYP2E1/3A4 from pulegone** |

**For comparison — Spearmint EO pulegone content:** 0.1–1.0% (EP limit)
**Pennyroyal EO pulegone content:** 40–90% — 40–900× higher than spearmint

### The Toxic Biosynthetic Branch — Pulegone Metabolism

The entire toxicological profile of pennyroyal derives from a single branch point in monoterpene metabolism:

```
Menthol-dominant pathway (peppermint M. × piperita):
  Pulegone → Menthone → Menthol [safe]

Pulegone-accumulating pathway (M. pulegium):
  Pulegone ACCUMULATES (lacks efficient menthone reductase activity)
  ↓ [CYP2E1 / CYP3A4 in liver]
  Menthofuran [REACTIVE METABOLITE — hepatotoxin]
  ↓ [further oxidation]
  Mint lactone + Hydroxymethylfuran + p-Cresol [reactive electrophiles]
  ↓
  Glutathione depletion → Protein adduct formation → Hepatocellular necrosis
```

This pathway explains:
1. Why peppermint (low pulegone, efficient menthone reductase) is safe at therapeutic doses
2. Why spearmint (pulegone <1%) is safe at food/tea doses
3. Why pennyroyal EO (pulegone 40–90%) is a primary hepatotoxin
4. Why NAC (N-acetylcysteine; glutathione replenisher) is the antidote of choice

### Non-Volatile Fraction (Minimal Therapeutic Relevance)
Flavonoids (luteolin, apigenin — shared with other Lamiaceae) and rosmarinic acid present at levels similar to spearmint. No clinically meaningful distinction from safe *Mentha* species. The danger is entirely in the concentrated EO or large amounts of fresh herb consumed for abortifacient effect.

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Hepatotoxicity — Pulegone Bioactivation Cascade (PRIMARY)

**Phase 1: Cytochrome P450 Oxidation**

CYP2E1 (primarily; 40–60% contribution) and CYP3A4 (secondary) oxidize pulegone via two pathways:

Pathway A — Menthofuran formation (primary toxic pathway):
- Pulegone → (via CYP2E1) → Menthofuran (volatile metabolite; detectable in breath of poisoned patients)
- Menthofuran → (via CYP2E1/CYP3A4) → γ-(Hydroxymethylfuranyl)butanone → Mint lactone + Hydroxypulegone
- Hydroxypulegone spontaneously rearranges to electrophilic pulegone oxide
- These reactive electrophiles alkylate protein sulfhydryl groups → enzyme inactivation + mitochondrial dysfunction

Pathway B — Direct p-Cresol formation (secondary):
- Pulegone → (via CYP2E1 aromatic hydroxylation) → p-Cresol [known hepatotoxin]

**Phase 2: Glutathione Depletion**

Reactive menthofuran metabolites and pulegone oxides conjugate with glutathione (GSH) via GSH-S-transferase — primary detoxification route. When GSH is depleted (within 1–2 hours of toxic dose):
- Unreacted electrophiles bind covalently to hepatocellular proteins (Michael addition to cysteine residues)
- Mitochondrial membrane potential collapses (uncoupling; NLRP3 inflammasome activation)
- Zone 3 (centrilobular) hepatic necrosis — same pattern as acetaminophen overdose
- ALT/AST elevation (often >10,000 U/L in severe cases)

**CYP2E1 induction risk:** Fasting, chronic alcohol use, and isoniazid all induce CYP2E1 → dramatically increased menthofuran production at any given pulegone dose → increased hepatotoxicity risk in these populations (mirrors acetaminophen toxicity risk factors exactly).

**NAC as antidote rationale:** N-acetylcysteine replenishes glutathione by providing cysteine for GSH synthesis → restores hepatic scavenging capacity for reactive intermediates. The mechanism is identical to NAC use in acetaminophen overdose. Efficacy window: most effective within 8–10 hours of ingestion; partial benefit up to 24 hours.

### 5.2 Abortifacient Mechanisms

Three distinct mechanisms contribute (dose-dependent):

1. **Uterine smooth muscle stimulation (direct):**
   Pulegone and isomenthone stimulate oxytocin receptor activity → uterine contractions. Dose-response: sublethal doses may induce uterine cramping/abortion; full abortifacient dose overlaps with hepatotoxic dose.

2. **Uterine irritation (indirect):**
   Reactive metabolites circulate and produce local inflammatory effects in uterine vasculature; prostaglandin release (COX activation) → uterine contractions.

3. **Hepatic failure → coagulopathy:**
   Severe hepatotoxicity impairs synthesis of all clotting factors (Factor I, II, V, VII, IX, X) → coagulopathy → uterine hemorrhage → fetal demise. This is the mechanism in lethal cases where abortion occurred.

**Critical clinical point:** The dose required for reliable abortion approximates or exceeds the hepatotoxic threshold. There is no therapeutic window for safe abortifacient use of pennyroyal oil. This has been recognized in toxicology literature since the 1990s.

### 5.3 Insecticidal / Repellent Mechanisms

Pulegone acts as:
- Acetylcholinesterase inhibitor (insect nervous system) → paralysis at contact concentrations
- Deterrent via olfactory irritation for fleas, mosquitoes, ants
- Effective flea repellent at topical concentrations too low to penetrate skin significantly

**Safety for topical insecticidal use:** External application to skin or pet fur at diluted concentrations (<1% in carrier) does not result in significant systemic absorption. However, concentrated pennyroyal EO should not be applied to cats (feline CYP2E1/CYP1A2 polymorphisms increase hepatotoxicity risk), dogs (hepatotoxicity risk with mucous membrane contact), or infants.

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

### Toxicology Case Reports (Level C — Case Evidence)

| Case | Description | Dose | Outcome | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gordon 1987 (JAMA) | 18-year-old woman, self-induced abortion with pennyroyal oil | ~30 mL EO orally | Severe hepatotoxicity; disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC); multi-organ failure; died after 8 days | Gordon 1987 JAMA 258(6):814 |
| Sullivan 1979 | Two siblings, 6-week-old infant given pennyroyal tea for colic | Herbal tea preparation | Hepatotoxicity; both recovered; EO not used — tea preparation caused milder toxicity | Sullivan 1979 |
| Bakerink 1996 (Pediatrics) | Infant given pennyroyal oil (not tea) for colic | Small volume EO | Hepatic failure; seizures; died | Bakerink 1996 Pediatrics 98(5):944 |
| Anderson 1996 | Multiple cases from poison control registry | Various | ALT/AST elevations across multiple cases; severity correlated with EO vs. tea form | Anderson 1996 Clin Toxicol |
| Rivera 1997 | 24-year-old woman, self-induced abortion | ~30 mL EO | Hepatorenal failure; spontaneous abortion; survived after NAC + dialysis | Rivera 1997 Vet Hum Toxicol |

**Pattern across all cases:**
- EO ingestion → severe toxicity; deaths documented
- Herbal tea at normal concentrations → mild to moderate toxicity (rarely fatal)
- Infants disproportionately vulnerable (lower body weight; less glutathione reserve; CYP2E1 developing)
- NAC administration critical; most survivors received aggressive NAC therapy

### Insect Repellency (Efficacy Data)

| Study | Intervention | Outcome | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Barnard 1999 | Pennyroyal EO vs. DEET (skin-applied) | Pennyroyal provided 30 min protection vs. DEET's 6+ hours; pennyroyal inferior but active | C |
| Kücükbay 2019 | Pennyroyal EO patch for flea repellency | Effective; comparable to citronella | C |

**Clinical assessment on insect repellency:** Pennyroyal has genuine repellent activity but inferior duration compared to DEET, picaridin, or IR3535. Given the toxicity risk of concentrated EO preparation, safer alternatives are strongly preferred for routine repellency.

### No Therapeutic Clinical Trials

No controlled clinical trials have been conducted on pennyroyal for any therapeutic indication. The toxicity profile precludes ethical trial design for internal use. External insecticidal/repellency use studies are observational only.

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Historical Abortifacient Use

**Classical Antiquity:**
Pennyroyal occupies a central position in ancient gynecological pharmacology precisely because abortion and menstrual induction were routine medical needs in antiquity. Key sources:

- **Aristophanes** *Peace* (421 BCE): mentions pennyroyal for menstrual issues — one of the earliest written references
- **Dioscorides** *De Materia Medica* (65 CE): "Pulegion... drunk with wine and honey, it moves menstruation and expels the fetus"
- **Pliny the Elder** *Naturalis Historia* (77 CE): "To move the courses, pennyroyal applied with salt kills the embryo"
- **Soranus of Ephesus** *Gynecology* (ca. 100 CE): The most systematic ancient gynecological text distinguishes contraceptives from abortifacients; recommends pennyroyal as abortifacient; advises patients to jump and kick their heels (mechanical action to supplement pharmacological — demonstrating understanding that the herb worked incompletely)

**Medieval and Renaissance:**
- **Hildegard von Bingen** *Physica* (ca. 1150 CE): Uses pennyroyal (*polei*) for menstrual disorders; aware of its strength
- **Trotula** gynecological manuscript (12th century Salerno): Pennyroyal preparations for retained menses
- **Turner** *A New Herball* (1551): "Penerial [pennyroyal] provoketh the termes [menses]"
- **Gerard** *Herball* (1597): "Pennyroyal provoketh the monthly courses... causeth the expulsion of the dead childe and after-birth"
- **Culpeper** *Complete Herbal* (1653): emmenagogue properties explicitly stated

**American Colonial and 19th Century:**
- Cherokee, Chippewa, Ojibwe: *H. pulegioides* used for colds, fevers — and explicitly as emmenagogue by women
- Colonial American "kitchen medicine": pennyroyal tea widely known and used as abortifacient among settler women
- 19th-century American Eclectic physicians (King's American Dispensatory): pennyroyal listed as emmenagogue; internal use of oil explicitly warned as dangerous
- British Pharmacopoeia 1867–1914: pennyroyal oil listed as pharmaceutical product; withdrawn as evidence of fatalities accumulated

**20th Century — Fatal Cases and Pharmacological Elucidation:**
- 1960s–1980s: Poison control centers begin accumulating pennyroyal cases; systematic review by Anderson (1996) documents the fatality pattern
- Gordon 1987 JAMA case — defining modern toxicology report; CYP2E1 bioactivation mechanism clarified by Thomassen et al. (1988) and Nelson et al. (1990)
- 1990s: Internet-era recirculation of historical herbal "abortifacient" information → renewed fatalities in context of limited abortion access
- 2000s–present: Pennyroyal EO available on natural product/essential oil websites; ongoing poison control cases

### Cultural Representation

**"Penny Lane" etymology speculation (unverified):** Some ethnobotanists have proposed that British street names incorporating "penny" derive from pennyroyal markets; widely disputed.

**Black Pudding / Haggis flavoring:** European pennyroyal (*M. pulegium*) was historically used as a flavoring herb in British blood puddings and Scottish haggis — the "Pudding Grass" name. At culinary concentrations in cooked food, the pulegone content is insufficient to cause toxicity. Cooking also reduces pulegone by 30–50% through volatilization. This culinary use is distinct from medicinal tea or EO use.

**Flea-mint identity:** Medieval European herb strewing on floors included pennyroyal specifically for flea control, which was genuinely effective at insect-repelling concentrations safe for residents walking on dried herb. This is a legitimate historical application.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### ⚠ CLINICAL RISK SUMMARY ⚠

**PENNYROYAL ESSENTIAL OIL:**
- NO SAFE INTERNAL DOSE EXISTS
- MINIMUM FATAL DOSE: as little as 10 mL (one tablespoon) in adults; lower in children
- MECHANISM: Hepatic zone 3 necrosis via CYP2E1-mediated pulegone → menthofuran bioactivation
- ANTIDOTE: N-acetylcysteine (NAC); call Poison Control (1-800-222-1222 US) immediately
- NAC PROTOCOL: Same as acetaminophen overdose (loading dose 140 mg/kg IV or oral; then maintenance)
- MAXIMUM WINDOW FOR NAC EFFICACY: 8–10 hours; partial benefit to 24 hours

**PENNYROYAL TEA (dried herb):**
- LOW-LEVEL risk at 1–2 cups occasionally for adults
- NOT SAFE for infants, children, pregnant women, nursing mothers
- NOT SAFE for persons with hepatic impairment, chronic alcohol use, fasting state (increased CYP2E1 activity)
- THERAPEUTIC WINDOW for emmenagogue effect (traditional use) OVERLAPS WITH TOXIC DOSE — no safety margin
- NOT recommended for any internal use by contemporary standards

### Legitimate Clinical Uses (External Only)

| Use | Form | Dose | Safety |
|---|---|---|---|
| Insect repellent (skin) | Diluted EO in carrier oil (0.5–1%) | Apply to exposed skin | Safe for adult skin application; avoid mucous membranes; do not use on infants or cats |
| Pet flea repellent | Diluted EO in water spray (0.5–1%) | Spray coat; do not apply to cats | Safe on dog coat at low dilution; toxic to cats |
| Household flea deterrent | Dried herb sachets | Place in bedding, drawers | Safe; non-toxic concentrations in air |
| Aromatherapy (diffusion) | EO in diffuser | 3–5 drops; limited duration | Low risk from inhalation at normal room air concentrations |

### Drug Interactions (Toxicologically Relevant)

| Substance | Interaction | Mechanism | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ethanol (chronic use) | Massively increased hepatotoxicity | CYP2E1 induction → increased menthofuran production | Severe — high fatality risk |
| Isoniazid | Increased hepatotoxicity | CYP2E1 induction | Severe |
| Acetaminophen | Additive hepatotoxicity (shared CYP2E1/GSH depletion pathway) | Synergistic | Severe — potentially additive hepatic necrosis |
| Rifampin | Increased CYP3A4 → increased pulegone oxidation | Enzyme induction | Moderate — increased toxicity |
| Disulfiram | Reduced pulegone clearance | CYP inhibition | Uncertain — do not combine |

### Differential Diagnosis Notes for Clinicians

**Presentation consistent with pennyroyal poisoning:**
- Right upper quadrant pain ± nausea/vomiting 2–6 hours post-ingestion
- Elevated ALT/AST (often dramatically — >1000 U/L; may exceed 10,000 U/L in severe cases)
- Coagulopathy (PT/INR elevation) if severe
- Possible spontaneous vaginal bleeding in pregnant patients
- History: ask specifically about use of "natural abortifacient," "herbal abortion," or "mint/pennyroyal oil"
- Odor: breath may smell of menthol/mint due to volatile metabolites (menthofuran expired in breath)

**Confirm with:**
- Serum pulegone (specialized toxicology laboratory)
- Urinary metabolites (menthofuran, mint lactone)
- Standard LFTs, PT/INR, BMP (hepatorenal syndrome risk)

**Immediate management:**
1. Call Poison Control immediately
2. NAC per acetaminophen overdose protocol
3. IV fluids; hepatic function monitoring
4. OB/GYN consultation if pregnant
5. ICU if severe hepatotoxicity (King's College criteria for fulminant hepatic failure)

### Regulatory and Legal Status

- **US:** Pennyroyal EO sold as "natural product"; no FDA regulation of EO sales; herbal abortifacient information freely distributed online
- **EU:** Banned as food additive; pulegone limits restrict its use; EO controlled as cosmetic/fragrance ingredient in some jurisdictions
- **ESCOP/Commission E:** No positive monograph; not recommended for any therapeutic indication
- **ACOG (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists):** Advises against all pennyroyal preparations; explicitly warns of hepatotoxic risk
- **AAP (American Academy of Pediatrics):** Pennyroyal listed as dangerous herb for infants and children
