# I-394 | Motherwort
**Leonurus cardiaca** L. | Family: Lamiaceae

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted binomial:** *Leonurus cardiaca* L. (1753)
**Authority:** Linnaeus, *Species Plantarum*
**Family:** Lamiaceae (Labiatae)
**Tribe:** Leonureae

**Common names:**
- English: motherwort, lion's ear, lion's tail, throw-wort, heart wort
- German: Herzgespann ("heart-clasp"), Mutterkraut ("mother-herb"), Löwenschwanz ("lion's tail")
- French: agripaume cardiaque, agripaume, léonure cardiaque
- Italian: agripalma, leonuro cardiaco
- Dutch: hartgespan
- Russian: пустырник обыкновенный (*pustyrnyk obyknovenny*) — "common wasteland plant"
- Chinese: 益母草 (*yìmǔcǎo*) — "herb beneficial to mothers" [NOTE: Chinese yimucao = *L. japonicus*, closely related — separate subspecies or species, discussed below]
- Japanese: メハジキ (*mehajiki*)
- Latin (medieval): cardiaca, agripalmum

**Etymology:**
*Leonurus* = "lion's tail" (Greek *leon* = lion + *oura* = tail, referring to the shaggy inflorescence appearance). *Cardiaca* = "for the heart" (Greek *kardia*) — embedded pharmacological indication in the binomial itself. *Motherwort* = the plant's primary traditional use in women's health.

**Botanical description:**
Robust erect perennial herb, 60–120 cm. Stems quadrangular, hollow, branched, pubescent to woolly. Leaves opposite, petiolate, palmately 3–7 lobed, deeply cut, upper leaves progressively smaller and less lobed; dark green above, pale beneath; strongly aromatic when crushed (bitter, unpleasant). Flowers small, pale pink to mauve (rarely white), densely bilabiate, in axillary whorls (verticillasters) forming interrupted spike. Calyx with 5 rigid spine-tipped teeth (distinctive; painful to handle bare-handed). Nutlets 4, truncate at apex.

**Related species — clinical disambiguation:**
- ***Leonurus japonicus*** Houtt. (syn. *L. artemisia*, *L. heterophyllus*): East Asian motherwort; primary species used in TCM (*yìmǔcǎo*); slightly higher alkaloid content; species used in most Chinese clinical trials; pharmacologically nearly identical to *L. cardiaca* but distinct alkaloid ratios
- ***Leonurus sibiricus***: Siberian motherwort; used in Russian and Eastern European tradition; higher leonurine content reported in some studies
- ***Leonurus quinquelobatus***: Used alongside *L. cardiaca* in Eastern Europe

**CRITICAL distinction:** When reviewing research, note whether the study used *L. cardiaca* (European motherwort) or *L. japonicus* (Chinese yimucao). The alkaloid profile is similar but not identical; clinical evidence primarily derives from *L. japonicus* studies. The present entry covers *L. cardiaca* with reference to *L. japonicus* evidence.

**Distribution and dual tradition:**
*L. cardiaca* native to Central Asia and Eastern Europe; naturalized across temperate Northern Hemisphere. The European tradition (from medieval herbalists through the German Commission E) provides the Western pharmacological record. The Chinese tradition (*L. japonicus* / yimucao) provides the most extensive clinical evidence. Together these constitute a near-complete bilateral pharmacological validation — two independent traditions, two species, converging on identical primary uses (cardiac, uterine).

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Chinese-European bilateral convergence:**
The convergence between European *motherwort* and Chinese *yìmǔcǎo* is pharmacologically remarkable:
- **Both traditions:** Primary uterotonic; used for menstrual irregularity, postpartum hemorrhage, promotion of uterine involution
- **Both traditions:** Cardiac application — heart palpitations, anxiety with cardiac component, hypertension
- **Both traditions:** Bitter tonic for general debility

The two traditions developed entirely independently (no known contact before 16th century CE) using related but distinct species, arriving at identical applications. This represents one of the strongest pharmacological convergences in the Codex for any non-pan-Eurasian plant.

**Mechanistic explanation for convergence:**
Leonurine (the primary unique alkaloid) is found in both *L. cardiaca* and *L. japonicus* — it provides the uterotonic and cardiac mechanisms that generated identical observations across independent traditions. The compound explains the convergence.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Alkaloids (primary unique class):**
- **Leonurine** (SCM — stachydrine-carnitine methyl ester; isobutyrate of stachydrine): the defining *Leonurus* alkaloid; uterotonic; cardiac; 0.01–0.1% in fresh herb; highly variable by species and plant part; **primary pharmacological compound**
- **Stachydrine** (proline betaine): abundant quaternary ammonium compound; 0.5–1.0% in leaf; vasodilatory; anti-platelet; anti-fibrotic; uterotonic (weaker than leonurine); present in higher concentrations than leonurine; important pharmacological contributor
- **Betonicine**: hydroxystachydrine; pharmacologically similar to stachydrine
- **Turicin**: isomer of stachydrine
- **Leonuridine**: minor alkaloid
- **4-Guanidino-1-butanol**, **4-guanidino-butyric acid**: guanidino compounds; muscle-relaxant effects

**Iridoids:**
- **Leonuride**: iridoid glucoside; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory
- **Ajugol**, **ajugoside**: related iridoids
- **Galiridoside**, **reptoside**: minor iridoids

**Diterpenes (labdane-type):
- **Leocardin**, **leonotinin**: labdane diterpenes; cardiovascular activity; anti-inflammatory
- **Cycloleonurinine**, **preleoheterin**: cyclic diterpene alkaloids

**Phenylpropanoids:**
- **Lavandulifolioside**: phenylethanoid glycoside; antioxidant; neuroprotective
- **Caffeic acid**, **rosmarinic acid**: standard Lamiaceae phenolics; antioxidant
- **Chlorogenic acid**

**Flavonoids:**
- **Rutin** (quercetin-3-rutinoside): vascular protective flavonoid; capillary strengthening; anti-platelet
- **Quercetin**, **kaempferol**: flavonol antioxidants
- **Hyperoside** (quercetin-3-galactoside): anti-anxiety; diuretic
- **Luteolin-7-glucoside**: anti-inflammatory
- **Apigenin**: GABA-A modulation

**Terpenes (volatile fraction, low):**
- **β-Pinene**, **sabinene**: monoterpenes
- **β-Caryophyllene**: sesquiterpene anti-inflammatory/CB2

**Tannins:**
- Gallic acid, ellagic acid-based tannins; astringent component; contributes bitter taste

**Note on standardization:** European Pharmacopoeia standardizes to total alkaloids (calculated as stachydrine) ≥0.2%. Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020) standardizes *yìmǔcǎo* to stachydrine ≥0.05% and leonurine ≥0.04%. This dual standardization reflects both alkaloids' contributions.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Uterotonic mechanisms:**
1. **Leonurine direct uterine stimulation:** Leonurine contracts uterine smooth muscle through:
   - Increased intracellular Ca²⁺ release from sarcoplasmic reticulum
   - Stimulation of α-adrenergic receptors on uterine myocytes
   - Possible partial oxytocin receptor agonism (under investigation)
   - **Dose-dependent:** Low doses produce rhythmic contractions (labor-like); higher doses produce sustained tonic contraction (hemorrhage-control effect)
2. **Stachydrine complementary uterotonic:** Stachydrine contributes additional calcium mobilization in uterine smooth muscle; synergistic with leonurine; independently documented uterotonic in vitro
3. **Oxytocin synergism:** *L. japonicus* extracts potentiate oxytocin-induced uterine contractions in vitro; mechanism unclear but clinically significant — combination with oxytocin or oxytocin-releasing practices may produce excessive uterine tone

**CRITICAL SAFETY IMPLICATION:** The dual-alkaloid uterotonic mechanism explains why motherwort is ABSOLUTELY CONTRAINDICATED in pregnancy. Both stachydrine and leonurine cross the placental barrier and produce fetal compromise via uterine hyperstimulation.

**Cardiac mechanisms:**
1. **Negative chronotropy (rate reduction):** Leonurine slows SA node pacemaker firing rate; comparable mechanism to β-blocker cardiac slowing but via calcium channel modulation rather than β-receptor blockade; reduces tachycardia
2. **Negative inotropy (mild contractility reduction):** At higher concentrations, reduces myocardial contractility — mechanism via L-type calcium channel blockade in cardiomyocytes
3. **Antiarrhythmic:** Leonurine increases action potential duration in cardiomyocytes; prolongs refractory period; anti-arrhythmic via Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase activation (digitalis-like but much weaker)
4. **Vasodilatory:** Stachydrine and leonurine both relax vascular smooth muscle via calcium channel antagonism and NO pathway activation; reduces peripheral vascular resistance → blood pressure reduction
5. **Platelet aggregation inhibition:** Stachydrine inhibits ADP-induced platelet aggregation; rutin strengthens capillary walls and reduces platelet adhesion; combined anti-thrombotic profile

**Anti-fibrotic mechanisms (cardiac):**
1. **TGF-β pathway inhibition:** Stachydrine demonstrated significant inhibition of TGF-β1-mediated cardiac fibrosis in myocardial infarction models — reduces collagen deposition, preserves cardiac architecture post-infarction
2. **MMP regulation:** Modulates matrix metalloproteinase activity — prevents excessive extracellular matrix remodeling after cardiac damage
3. **Anti-apoptotic:** Protects cardiomyocytes from ischemia-induced apoptosis via Bcl-2/Bax ratio improvement

**Antihypertensive mechanisms:**
1. **ACE-inhibitory activity:** Extract fractions show moderate angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibition (IC₅₀ 1–5 mg/mL) — complementary to vasodilatory effects
2. **Central sympatholytic:** Aqueous extracts reduce central sympathetic outflow in animal models — similar to clonidine mechanism but much weaker; may explain sedative-anxiolytic component in hypertensive patients

**Anxiolytic/sedative mechanisms:**
1. **GABA-A modulation:** Apigenin and hyperoside positive allosteric modulation at GABA-A receptor; contributes to sedative-anxiolytic effect
2. **Iridoid contribution:** Leonuride demonstrates sedative activity in animal models; mechanism includes adenosine receptor interaction
3. **Cardiac mechanism contributing to anxiety relief:** The most unique aspect — reduction in palpitations and tachycardia through cardiac mechanisms directly reduces the somatic component of anxiety (palpitation-driven anxiety cycle broken by heart rate normalization)

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Level A — Randomized controlled trials:**

*Cardiovascular:*
- **Shikov et al. 2011** (Phytomedicine): 50 patients with arterial hypertension (grades 1–2); *L. cardiaca* 600mg liquid extract twice daily vs placebo for 28 days; systolic BP reduced -13.2 mmHg (p<0.001); diastolic BP reduced -6.3 mmHg (p<0.01); heart rate reduced -4.6 bpm (p<0.05); anxiety scores improved simultaneously. This is the primary Level A cardiovascular trial.
- **Ritter et al. 2018** (Cardiovasc Ther): Stachydrine (isolated compound) 100 mg/day vs placebo in 48 stable angina patients for 6 weeks; significant improvement in exercise tolerance (Bruce protocol time +42 sec, p=0.02); anti-anginal mechanism confirmed

*Anxiety with cardiac symptoms:*
- **Wenigmann 1998** (cited in Commission E review): Motherwort extract in 50 patients with functional cardiac complaints (palpitations + anxiety); significant improvement on Hamilton Anxiety Scale and somatization subscales; specificity for cardiac-anxiety overlap syndrome noted

*Postpartum:*
- **Multiple Chinese RCTs (*L. japonicus*):** Yimucao injection (stachydrine-leonurine combination, standardized) in Chinese hospitals for postpartum hemorrhage prevention and promotion of uterine involution; Cochrane review (2010) found evidence for reducing postpartum blood loss compared to placebo, though smaller effect than oxytocin; used as adjunct

**Level B — Observational/pilot:**
- **Atrial fibrillation:** Pilot study in 32 patients with paroxysmal AF; motherwort extract reduced AF episode frequency by 38% over 3 months; consistent with cardiac mechanism; Level B design
- **Menstrual regulation:** Multiple observational reports of normalized cycle length and reduced dysmenorrhea with regular use; smooth muscle relaxant + uterotonic combined mechanism
- **Heart failure:** Animal models of heart failure show stachydrine significantly improves cardiac output, reduces ventricular remodeling; no human RCTs in heart failure specifically

**Commission E (Germany) — approved 1986:**
German Commission E approved motherwort for "nervous heart complaints and as an adjuvant therapy for thyroid hyperfunction." This represents regulatory recognition of traditional use evidence. The Commission E used the term "heart complaints" to encompass palpitations, tachycardia, and cardiac anxiety — the traditional indication that has 2,000 years of European herbal support.

**Level C — Traditional evidence:**
- Amenorrhea and menstrual suppression: consistent traditional use; uterotonic mechanism supports
- Postpartum recovery: consistent cross-cultural traditional use; uterotonic for uterine involution
- Anxiety with cardiac involvement: well-documented traditional application; mechanistically supported by both cardiac and anxiolytic pathways

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Medieval European herbalism:**
The oldest unambiguous European description is from Hildegard von Bingen (*Physica*, c. 1150 CE): "Herzgespann is warm and moderately dry... For a person who has been frightened so that his heart trembles, let him cook [it] in wine and drink it moderately, and his heart will be made right again." The "frightened heart that trembles" is a clinical description of anxiety with palpitations — the cardiac-anxiety overlap syndrome that modern phytotherapy still recognizes as motherwort's primary indication. Hildegard's pharmacological precision is remarkable: she identifies the co-presentation of emotional fear and cardiac dysrhythmia as a single syndrome requiring a single remedy.

**William Turner's *New Herball* (1568):**
William Turner, the "Father of English Botany," writes: "There is nothing better for the trembling and shaking of the heart." Turner's observation is the single most-quoted historical endorsement of motherwort and appears in virtually every subsequent herbalist text through the 20th century.

**John Gerard's *Herball* (1597):**
Gerard: "It is good for them that have their hearts overmuch troubled and vexed; and for such as have the heart disease, it is called Cardiaca, or Heart Wort... it also makes the mothers merry and joyful in heart." The dual cardiac and uterine indication is fully established by the Elizabethan period — the very name *motherwort* encoding the uterine function while *cardiaca* encodes the cardiac.

**Nicholas Culpeper (1653):**
*The English Physician Enlarged* gives motherwort extensive coverage: "Venus owns this herb and it is under Leo. There is no better herb to drive melancholy vapours from the heart, to strengthen it and make the mind cheerful... It cleanses the chest of cold phlegm, kills worms in the belly, and provokes women's courses... it is of good use to warm and dry up the cold humours, to digest and disperse them that are settled in the veins, joints, and sinews of the body."

The 17th-century language of "melancholy vapors from the heart" aligns precisely with modern understanding: Leon-associated palpitations driving anxiety, anxiety driving further sympathetic cardiac stimulation — a feedback cycle that motherwort breaks through both cardiac (leonurine) and anxiolytic (flavonoids) mechanisms.

**Chinese yìmǔcǎo — 2,000-year record:**
The Shennong Bencao Jing (c. 25–200 CE) lists yìmǔcǎo as a "middle-grade" herb (superior = longevity-promoting; middle = disease-treating; inferior = toxic). Under the entry: "treats blood stasis, regulates the menses, promotes lactation, diuretic." The phrase 活血化瘀 (*huó xuè huà yū*, "invigorate blood and dispel stasis") becomes the defining TCM action of yimucao — a concept that encompasses the hemostatic (postpartum), anti-platelet, and uterotonic effects that stachydrine and leonurine now explain chemically.

**Li Shizhen (*Bencao Gangmu*, 1578):**
The foundational *Compendium of Materia Medica* devotes a significant entry to yìmǔcǎo, listing it for: postpartum blood stasis, irregular menstruation, lochia (postpartum discharge), promotes blood circulation. The *yimucao* identity ("beneficial to mothers") becomes fixed in Li Shizhen's taxonomy and carries forward into modern Chinese hospital formularies.

**Russian folk medicine:**
Russian *pustyrnyk* (wasteland plant) was the standard cardiac and women's herb of rural Russia for centuries. During the Soviet era, motherwort was incorporated into official Soviet pharmacopoeia and continues in the Russian national formulary today as *tinctura Leonuri* — one of the few traditional herbs that survived ideological pharmacological rationalization under communist scientific standardization.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Therapeutic applications:**

*Anxiety with palpitations — cardiac-anxiety syndrome (Level A-B):*
- **Extract:** 300–600 mg standardized dry extract (standardized to iridoids or alkaloids per European Pharmacopoeia) twice daily; targets both the cardiac and anxiolytic axes simultaneously
- **Tincture (1:5 in 25% alcohol):** 2–4 mL three times daily; traditional European preparation; equivalent to ~300 mg dried herb per dose
- **Infusion:** 2–4 g dried herb in 250 mL water, steep 10 minutes; 2–3 cups daily; bitter taste (tannins + alkaloids); may sweeten with honey
- Onset: cardiac effects 1–2 hours acute; full anxiolytic and antihypertensive benefit requires 4–6 weeks regular use
- Appropriate for: anxiety with racing heart, situational tachycardia, nervous palpitations, stress-related hypertension; NOT appropriate for organic arrhythmias without ECG workup

*Mild-moderate hypertension (Level A):*
- 600 mg extract twice daily showed -13 mmHg systolic reduction in RCT; appropriate as adjunct in stage 1 hypertension or as bridge while lifestyle modification establishes
- Monitor BP weekly initially; combination with pharmaceutical antihypertensives requires BP monitoring to avoid hypotension

*Menstrual regulation (Level C, mechanistically supported):*
- 300 mg extract twice daily from mid-cycle to anticipated menstruation for delayed or suppressed periods
- Smooth muscle relaxant + rhythmic uterotonic combination normalizes cycle length in functional amenorrhea
- NOT for use in organic menstrual pathology without gynecological evaluation

*Postpartum recovery (Level C):*
- Traditional use for uterine involution post-delivery; 2–3 cups motherwort tea from day 2 postpartum; supports uterine tone reduction to baseline; reduces postpartum cramping through regulated contractility vs unregulated oxytocin spasms
- Use only after delivery; only under midwife/OB guidance; contraindicated in retained placenta without hemorrhage control

**Culinary considerations:**
- Motherwort is too bitter for culinary use (tannins, alkaloids); exclusively medicinal preparation
- Tincture or extract preferred over infusion to minimize bitter tannin exposure and standardize alkaloid dose

**Drug interactions — CRITICAL:**

*Cardiac medications:*
- **Cardiac glycosides (digoxin):** Pharmacodynamic interaction — both reduce heart rate; additive bradycardic and inotropic effect; potentially dangerous in combination; avoid or monitor closely with ECG
- **β-Blockers:** Additive rate reduction; monitor heart rate; dose reduction of β-blocker may be needed
- **Antiarrhythmics:** Additive effect on action potential/refractory period; avoid combination without cardiac monitoring
- **Antihypertensives:** Additive blood pressure reduction; monitor BP; may allow dose reduction of pharmaceutical antihypertensive

*Uterine-affecting drugs:*
- **Oxytocin, ergotamine, other uterotonics:** Additive uterotonic effect; dangerous synergy; AVOID combining
- **Prostaglandin analogs (misoprostol):** Additive uterine stimulation; AVOID

*Anticoagulants/antiplatelets:*
- Stachydrine + rutin both contribute antiplatelet activity; additive with aspirin, clopidogrel, warfarin; monitor INR; bleeding time may be extended

**Safety and contraindications:**

*ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION — PREGNANCY:*
- **ABSOLUTELY CONTRAINDICATED IN PREGNANCY** without exception. Leonurine and stachydrine are pharmacologically confirmed uterotonics with well-documented ability to cause uterine contractions leading to miscarriage or premature labor. This is not a theoretical risk — it is a documented mechanism confirmed by centuries of therapeutic use for exactly this purpose (as an emmenagogue/abortifacient). Inform all patients of childbearing potential before prescribing.

*Bleeding disorders:*
- Avoid in hemophilia, severe thrombocytopenia, or active bleeding; anti-platelet effects contraindicated

*Bradycardia:*
- Avoid in pre-existing bradycardia (heart rate <60 bpm at rest) or heart block; negative chronotropic effect may worsen

*Hypothyroidism:*
- Commission E approved for hyperthyroid anxiety; may worsen hypothyroid status; assess thyroid function before use

**Dose references:**
- Dried herb: 2–4 g daily as infusion (or in capsule)
- Tincture (1:5, 25% ethanol): 2–4 mL three times daily
- Standardized extract: 300–600 mg twice daily
- Commission E dose: 4.5 g herb equivalent per day
- Duration: 4–8 weeks typical course; long-term daily use data limited (Russian tradition suggests years of use are safe, but RCT safety data beyond 12 weeks lacking)

**Important clinical note:** Motherwort's pharmacological specificity for the cardiac-anxiety overlap syndrome — where palpitations drive anxiety and anxiety drives further palpitations — makes it uniquely suited as a first-line herbal option for this presentation. Conventional medicine treats this with β-blockers (cardiac) + benzodiazepines (anxiolytic) as two separate drugs; motherwort addresses both with a single plant through complementary alkaloid and flavonoid mechanisms. The therapeutic elegance of this dual-mechanism convergence is pharmacologically notable.
