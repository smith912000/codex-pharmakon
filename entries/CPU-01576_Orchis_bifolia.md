# Orchis bifolia — Saturion, the satyrion (Dioscorides 3-143)

## *Orchis bifolia [Linnaeus], the third of the names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-143 (which also prints Satyrion-trifolium [Fuchs], Orchis trifolia major [Bauhin] and Habenaria bifolia [Brunfels], with the other usage Satyrium hircinum, Orchis hircina — "Lizard Orchis")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (lines 7776 to 7778) offers four names for the chapter, then an "other usage" with two more, and the English name "Lizard Orchis" stands at the end of the whole line and so goes with the other usage (*Satyrium hircinum*) as printed, not with *Orchis bifolia*. The translators themselves add, for the chapter after this one, that "Modern satyrium species are only found at the Cape of Good Hope" (3-144). The row has not been verified against POWO. **Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-143. SATURION*, heading at line 7775, in Book Three among the roots of the *akanthoda*. The whole chapter (lines 7779 and 7783, divided by a page break) reads: "Satyrium some call trifolium because it bears leaves in threes (as it were) bending down to the earth, similar to rumex [2-141] or lily, yet smaller and reddish. It has a naked stalk a foot long, a white flower similar to a lily, a bulbous root as big as an apple — red, but white within, similar to an egg, sweet to the taste and pleasant in the mouth. One ought to drink it in black hard wine for severe spasms, and use it if he wishes to lay with a woman. For they say that this also is an aphrodisiac."

Dioscorides therefore names the plant for its leaves in threes (the Latin *trifolium*), compares the leaves to *rumex* or lily, and describes a naked stalk a foot long, a white flower like a lily, and a root "as big as an apple", red outside, white within, egg-like, sweet and "pleasant in the mouth". Two uses are given for the root: it is drunk "in black hard wine for severe spasms", and it is used by a man "if he wishes to lay with a woman". The second is introduced by "For they say that this also is an aphrodisiac", so Dioscorides reports it as a saying, in the same words as the statement of use, and the Codex records it as the text's report and does not endorse it. The chapter gives no place and no season, and no names other than *trifolium*.

**The chapters around it.** 2-141 *HIPPOLAPATHON*, to which the leaves are compared via *rumex*, is the translators' water dock; the chapter says it is "a great olus ... growing in marshes" and has "the same properties as those mentioned above". The neighbouring Orchis chapters are different plants: 3-141 *ORCHIS* (line 7761), the translators' military orchid, whose root "is eaten (boiled) like bulbus" and which is said to determine the sex of offspring; 3-142 *ORCHIS ETEROS* (line 7770), whose root applied "is able to dissipate oedema, clean ulcers, and repress herpes" and which carries the note "There is a similar story told of this as there is of the dog's stone [cyanosorchis 3-141]"; and 3-144 *SATURION ERUTHRONION* (line 7784), the translators' gnat-like or early purple orchid, "said that it is an aphrodisiac, like scincus", whose root "taken into the hand" is also said to encourage venereal diseases. The Codex does not carry any statement of 3-141, 3-142 or 3-144 over to 3-143. The common ground is the aphrodisiac report, which each chapter gives in its own terms.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter id 3-143 and for the Greek name *saturion* found no existing entry on this chapter. The only entries that matched the words *orchis* or *satyrion* are about other plants, and none cites 3-143.

### Active Components

The composition of the root Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, the terrestrial orchids (Orchidaceae) of Europe bear underground tubers, and orchid tubers are known to be rich in a mucilaginous starch-like carbohydrate. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a three-leaved plant with a sweet apple-sized root, drunk in black hard wine for severe spasms and used, as people said, as an aphrodisiac. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol for spasms or for sexual use. Many orchids are protected, and wild orchid tubers are not to be dug on the strength of this chapter. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation: "One ought to drink it in black hard wine for severe spasms". It gives no quantity, and it does not say whether the root is raw, dried or boiled. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that a plant's reputation as an aphrodisiac is not evidence of an effect. The identification is a translators' suggestion among four names and an "other usage", and the species named *Orchis bifolia* in the modern literature (which the translators’ own line also gives as *Habenaria bifolia*) is not shown by the chapter to be the plant described.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one taste, that the root is "sweet to the taste and pleasant in the mouth". It prints no warming, cooling, drying, moistening or astringent quality for 3-143.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant (leaves in threes bending to the earth, reddish and smaller than lily or dock leaves; a naked foot-long stalk; a white lily-like flower; an apple-sized root, red outside and white within, sweet to taste) and how it stands among its sister chapters (3-141, 3-142, 3-144), each of which describes the root of a differently-leaved orchid. He reports the aphrodisiac use with "they say", and recording is not endorsing. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a root drunk for spasms and an aphrodisiac of report. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a sweet orchid root drunk in wine, with no stated quantity, and a reputation Dioscorides passes on with "they say". The useful lesson is Dioscorides' method of telling a root by colour, size and taste, and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Wine with a root of unknown dose is not harmless, and severe spasms need medical assessment. Many wild orchids are protected by law in many places and their tubers must not be dug; the particular status was not researched. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
