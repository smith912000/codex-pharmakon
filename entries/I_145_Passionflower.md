# I_145 — Passiflora incarnata | Passionflower / Maypop / Wild Apricot / Holy Trinity Flower

**Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry:** I_145
**Convergence Score:** 3/9 traditions
**Energetic Tier:** ☿ Shen (anxiolytic/sedative/anticonvulsant) | ◉ Qi (antispasmodic)
**Safety Class:** Good; drowsiness dose-dependent; avoid concurrent CNS depressants; generally well-tolerated; one of the few anxiolytics appropriate in pregnancy at low doses (traditional use — verify with practitioner)

## Section 1 — Nomenclature

| Register | Name |
|----------|------|
| Scientific | *Passiflora incarnata* L. (Passifloraceae) |
| BM (Aztec) | Not in Badianus Manuscript (*P. incarnata* is North American, not Central American; Aztec traditions used *P. subpeltata* and other species for topical use) |
| SA (South American) | *Passiflora* species used by multiple Amazonian and Andean indigenous cultures; *P. incarnata* specifically is eastern North American |
| Native American | Cherokee, Creek, Natchez, and other southeastern North American nations documented use of *P. incarnata* for anxiety, epilepsy, wounds |
| European folk | Post-contact; 17th century introduction to Europe via Spanish missionaries |
| Spanish missionary | "Flor de la Pasión" — the morphological elements of the flower were reinterpreted as symbols of Christ's Passion (the tendrils = whips; the three styles = nails; the filaments = crown of thorns; the 5 petals + 5 sepals = 10 faithful disciples; the flower's 3-day life = resurrection); this Catholic interpretation spread the plant throughout European herbalism |
| German folk | Passionsblume; Christusblume |
| English | Maypop (the hollow fruits make a popping sound when stepped on); Wild Apricot; Holy Trinity Flower; Passion Vine |

**Botanical identity:** Perennial climbing vine to 8m (temperate zone) or larger (tropical); extraordinary flower morphology (20–30 filaments, corona of purple-white; 5 petals, 5 sepals; 3 styles capped with round stigmas; 5 stamens; single large ovary); leaves deeply 3-lobed; edible fruit (maypop) containing fragrant pulp. Native to southeastern United States; naturalized in the south; hardy to Zone 6. Considered an invasive agricultural weed in some regions.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Score: 3/9** (Native American · SA tradition · European folk post-contact confirmed)

Passionflower earns Codex entry despite 3/9 convergence because it represents the South American (SA) and North American (BM adjacent) traditions which otherwise contribute fewer entries to the first 145 entries of Volume I. It also provides the most RCT-validated natural anxiolytic for comparison with valerian (I_137), lemon balm (I_142), and skullcap (I_143).

**The morphological conversion to Christian symbolism** represents one of the most dramatic examples of cultural pharmacological adoption through religious reinterpretation: Spanish missionaries encountering the extraordinary passionflower structure in the Americas saw in it the entire passion narrative of Christ, which gave the plant immediate spiritual value and accelerated its adoption into European medicine. A pharmacologically active plant was incorporated into two medical traditions (indigenous American + European) through two entirely different hermeneutic systems simultaneously.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Flavonoids (primary active class in aerial parts):**
- **Chrysin** (5,7-dihydroxyflavone) — anxiolytic (GABA-A modulator; binds benzodiazepine receptor site); anti-inflammatory; aromatase inhibitor (testosterone-sparing effect)
- **Vitexin** (apigenin-8-C-glucoside) — GABA-A modulator; anti-inflammatory; antispasmodic; sedative
- **Isovitexin** — similar to vitexin; anxiolytic
- **Orientin, isoorientin** — anti-inflammatory; antioxidant
- **Apigenin** — GABA-A modulator (already documented in melissa I_142, chamomile, and other Lamiaceae)
- **Luteolin** — anti-inflammatory; mast cell stabilizer

**Alkaloids:**
- **Harmane, harmaline, harmalol** (β-carboline MAO inhibitors) — present in trace amounts; anxiolytic; antidepressant (weak MAO inhibition); antispasmodic; the β-carboline chemistry overlaps with Syrian rue (I_133 family) and ayahuasca additive herbs
- **Note:** β-carboline content is very low in *P. incarnata*; not sufficient for psychedelic effects at normal doses; however, MAO inhibition contribution to anxiolytic effect is pharmacologically real

**Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA):**
- Free GABA present in plant tissue (rare for plants); contributes to GABAergic effects directly

**Passiflorine:**
- Alkaloid specific to *Passiflora*; sedative; antispasmodic

**Flavone glycosides:**
- Multiple C-glycosyl flavones (2.5% of aerial part dry weight total) — primary commercial standardization marker

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Anxiolytic / Sedative (primary — multiple mechanisms):**
- **Chrysin:** high-affinity ligand at benzodiazepine receptor on GABA-A (Kd in nanomolar range); positive allosteric modulator → Cl⁻ influx → anxiolytic; sedative at higher doses
- **Vitexin + isovitexin:** GABA-A modulation at different subunit sites from chrysin (possibly α1 subunit) → synergistic GABA potentiation
- **Free GABA content:** Direct GABA supplementation (BBB permeability of free GABA is limited but non-zero, especially with active transport)
- **β-Carbolines (harmane/harmaline):** reversible MAO-A inhibition → increased serotonin and melatonin → enhanced sedation and mood; weak benzodiazepine receptor affinity
- **Combined:** GABA-A multi-subunit engagement + direct GABA + MAO inhibition = one of the broadest natural anxiolytic profiles

**Anticonvulsant:**
- Chrysin + vitexin: GABA-A mediated anticonvulsant (same mechanism as traditional use for epilepsy)
- Native American anticonvulsant application is pharmacologically consistent

**Antispasmodic (smooth muscle):**
- Passiflorine: direct smooth muscle relaxant; reduces GI spasm
- Chrysin: inhibits Ca²⁺-dependent smooth muscle contraction
- Explains traditional use for GI cramping accompanying anxiety

**Anti-inflammatory:**
- Orientin, isoorientin, luteolin: NF-κB inhibition → anti-inflammatory; useful for inflammatory pain accompanying anxious tension

**MAO inhibition implications:**
- Reversible MAO-A inhibition: mild antidepressant; increased serotonin availability
- Reversible MAO-B inhibition: mild dopaminergic; possible movement-related benefits (theoretical in Parkinson's context — no clinical evidence)
- **Interaction consideration:** MAO inhibition at trace levels — unlikely to produce clinically significant tyramine interaction at normal passionflower doses, but monitor in patients on dietary tyramine restriction

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Regulatory:**
- Commission E (Germany): APPROVED for nervousness; sleep disorders
- EMA: THMP listing for anxiety and sleep
- ESCOP: Anxiety; sleep disorders

**Clinical trials:**
- **Akhondzadeh et al. (2001) *Journal of Clinical Pharmacy and Therapeutics*:** RCT; passionflower extract vs. oxazepam (benzodiazepine) for Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD); 18-day trial; passionflower EQUIVALENT to oxazepam for anxiety reduction (HAM-A scores); significantly less impairment of job performance with passionflower vs. oxazepam. N=36. This single RCT comparing passionflower directly to a benzodiazepine is the most significant evidence for an anxiolytic herb in the Codex.
- **Ngan & Conduit (2011) *Phytotherapy Research*:** RCT; passionflower tea vs. parsley (control) for sleep; passionflower significantly improved subjective sleep quality scores. N=41. Cross-over design.
- **Preoperative anxiety:** Multiple small RCTs for passionflower for preoperative anxiety; generally favorable compared to controls
- **Pediatric ADHD:** Small pilot studies (2010s); passionflower extract comparable to methylphenidate for hyperactivity reduction in children; not replicated in large trials; requires further investigation

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The passion narrative flower — Catholic ethnobotany:**
When Spanish missionaries in the 1560s–1580s encountered the passionflower in Mexico and Florida, they saw in it a botanical illustration of Christ's Passion. The botanical identification of spiritual significance was documented by Giacomo Bosio in his 1610 treatise on the cross: the corona filaments = crown of thorns (72–80 filaments = 72 disciples or the instruments of passion), the 3 styles = nails, the 5 anthers = wounds, the 3-lobed leaves = the Holy Trinity, the tendrils = whips. This elaborate symbolic reading created one of the most culturally productive misreadings in botanical history: the plant was named "passion flower" for Christ's passion, received immediate reverence throughout Catholic Europe, was cultivated in monastery gardens and shipped to European botanic gardens, and its medicinal use was actively promoted by the same theological framework that its morphology had inspired.

**The Eclectic physicians — the American antianxiety tradition:**
19th-century American Eclectic medicine (a medical tradition using primarily botanical medicines) adopted passionflower as their primary anxiolytic after observing Cherokee and other southeastern American Indian uses. Samuel Felter, John Lloyd, and Finley Ellingwood documented passionflower extensively for "nervous irritability," "sleeplessness," and "convulsive disorders" in King's American Dispensatory (1898) — creating the scientific documentation of traditional use that enabled Commission E review a century later.

**"Maypop" — the acoustic folk name:**
The hollow fruit of *P. incarnata*, when ripe and stepped on, produces a sharp popping sound. The name "maypop" (or "may-apple" in some dialects) encodes this acoustic experience. The edible fruit — passionately sour-sweet — was a food source for Cherokee and other nations and was consumed fresh, dried, or as pulp. The same plant that provided anxiolytic medicine also provided nutrition — the complete botanical pharmacopoeia/food-system overlap.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Primary modern applications:**

1. **Generalized Anxiety Disorder / Situational anxiety (RCT-validated):** 400–600mg standardized extract (2.5% flavonoids as vitexin) 3× daily; OR passionflower tea (2g dried aerial parts per cup, steep 10 min, 3–4 cups daily); comparable to low-dose benzodiazepine with significantly better occupational performance preservation; onset within 1–2 hours

2. **Insomnia (sleep quality):** 300–400mg extract before bed; combine with valerian (I_137) + hops (Commission E-approved trio); Ngan & Conduit trial supports sleep quality improvement

3. **Anxiety with GI component (IBS, nervous stomach):** Passionflower + peppermint (I_002) + fennel (I_043) combination; antispasmodic + anxiolytic addresses both branches of anxiety-driven GI dysfunction

4. **Preoperative anxiety:** 400–600mg extract 60–90 minutes before procedure; small RCT evidence; low risk; surgeon/anesthetist should be informed

5. **Children's anxiety (off-label, traditional context):** Lower doses (100–200mg equivalent); historical Native American pediatric use; one small methylphenidate-comparison trial; NOT standard pediatric practice; consultation required

**Dose:**
- Dried aerial parts infusion: 2g per cup, steep 10 min; 3–4 cups daily
- Standardized extract: 300–600mg (standardized 2.5% total flavonoids as isovitexin or vitexin)
- Tincture (1:5 in 25%): 2–4mL 3× daily
- Commission E dose: 4–8g daily of herb equivalent

**Contraindications:**
- **Concurrent CNS depressants:** additive sedation; reduce dose or monitor; avoid with alcohol
- **MAO inhibitor medications (pharmaceutical):** β-carboline content; potential additive MAO inhibition → tyramine reaction; avoid combining
- **Anticoagulants:** limited evidence for interaction; monitor
- **Pregnancy:** traditionally used for pregnancy-related anxiety in Native American medicine; no documented teratogenicity in human observational data; avoid high doses; specialist consultation recommended

**Tolerance and dependence:** No evidence of tolerance or dependence with passionflower (unlike benzodiazepines); appears suitable for long-term management of chronic anxiety. This is a significant therapeutic advantage over pharmaceutical anxiolytics.
