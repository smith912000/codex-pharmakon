### Origin & Tradition

Rue is the great ambivalent herb of the old world — simultaneously the "herb of grace" that warded evil and consecrated holy water, and one of the most feared abortifacients and poisons in the classical dispensary. Native to the Balkans and the Mediterranean, its bitter, acrid, almost repellent smell (*graveolens* = "strong/heavy-smelling") is exactly what made it potent in the ancient imagination: a plant whose very odour seemed to drive things away — insects, infection, the evil eye, and, at higher dose, pregnancy and life itself. Its cross-tradition convergence is a convergence on *power and danger*: everywhere it appears, it is classed as extremely hot, extremely drying, sharply "moving," and to be handled with respect.

**Greco-Roman (Dioscorides):** Rue (*peganon*) receives a substantial treatment in Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* (Book III). He distinguishes wild and garden rue, warns that wild rue is the more violent, and lists its uses: as an antidote to poisons and venomous bites (a famous ingredient of mithridatic antidotes), a digestive and carminative, an emmenagogue and abortifacient, a treatment for chest and joint complaints, and a suppressant of libido. He also records its harms — that it "burns," inflames the body, and is dangerous for pregnant women. Pliny devotes extensive attention to rue as antidote and medicine; the herb was a Roman kitchen and pharmacy staple.

**Islamic / Unani (Avicenna):** As *sadhab* / *fijan*, rue is a fixed member of the Arabic materia medica and Avicenna's *Canon*, classed as intensely hot and dry, a *muḥallil* (resolvent) and deobstruent used against colic, "cold" nervous complaints, worms, and as an emmenagogue — with the same explicit warnings about its heat and its danger in pregnancy. It retained a place in Unani and folk practice across the Islamic world for menstrual regulation and as an antispasmodic, always at the edge of safety.

**Assyrian / Egyptian & Near-Eastern repertoire (hedged):** Aromatic bitter herbs of rue's type belong to the Near-Eastern medicinal-and-apotropaic tradition, where strongly smelling plants were used in fumigation and protective ritual as well as medicine. Specific ancient identifications with *R. graveolens* are uncertain, so the honest statement is that rue sits within the region's shared repertoire of powerful aromatic "driving-out" herbs rather than resting on a securely dated single reference. `[USER: verify any specific Ebers/cuneiform citation before asserting it]`

**Later European & apothecary tradition:** Rue became the "herb of grace" and "herb of repentance," strewn in courtrooms and churches against plague-"miasma," carried against contagion, and used by apothecaries as an emmenagogue and antispasmodic well into the early-modern period — even as its dangers were universally acknowledged.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — Grace, Repentance, and the Edge of the Poison:**
Rue's double identity — sacred protector and dangerous poison — is the humoral doctrine of *extreme heat and dryness* worn on the surface. A plant graded "hot and dry in the third degree" is, in the old system, a plant operating near the ceiling of tolerable force; and rue's real chemistry justifies it. Its **furanocoumarins** (bergapten, psoralen, xanthotoxin) are genuine phototoxins that, activated by sunlight, cross-link DNA in skin cells — the biochemical truth beneath the folklore that rue "burns." Its alkaloids and the uterine-active fraction underlie the abortifacient reputation the classical texts state outright. The symbolism of *grace* and *repentance* is the culture's way of encoding a herb that stands at the threshold between healing and harm — a plant of purification precisely because it is a plant of danger. In Kenetica it is recorded strictly as a **`[RESET]`-category historical entry**: a monument to the old pharmacy's respect for potent, edge-of-poison medicines, not a substance to be self-administered.

### Active Components
- **Furanocoumarins** — bergapten, psoralen (xanthotoxin), and related — the **phototoxic** DNA-intercalating fraction responsible for phytophotodermatitis.
- **Quinoline & acridone alkaloids** — including arborinine, skimmianine, and others; contribute to toxicity and bioactivity.
- **Rutin (quercetin-3-rutinoside)** — the flavonoid glycoside *named after this plant*; antioxidant, vasoactive, and notably **not** the source of rue's toxicity or its antifertility action (see below).
- **Essential oil (2-undecanone / methyl nonyl ketone dominant)** — source of the pungent odour and irritant/abortifacient potential.
- **Coumarins & furoquinolines** — additional bioactive/toxic constituents.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica
**No self-directed internal-use case is offered.** Recorded historical actions: emmenagogue/abortifacient, antispasmodic, carminative, anthelmintic, antidote/resolvent, and topical antiseptic — all at the cost of significant toxicity. In the Kenetica frame rue is catalogued as a **cautionary `[RESET]`-tier historical botanical**: studied for lineage and doctrine, contraindicated for practice. Any legitimate modern interest (e.g. rutin as an isolated flavonoid) is met through purified compounds, not the whole plant.

### Scientific Correlation
According to PubMed, the traditional reputations of rue are borne out in two specific, verifiable ways. **Gandhi et al. (1991), *Journal of Ethnopharmacology*** demonstrated **post-coital antifertility (anticonceptive) activity** of *Ruta graveolens* preparations in female rats given root, aerial parts, and aqueous/solvent extracts orally on post-coital days — directly corroborating the classical abortifacient/emmenagogue use; the study also found that the benzene and chloroform fractions were **toxic**, and, importantly, that **isolated rutin was inactive**, localising the antifertility effect to other constituents. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0378-8741(91)90188-j)

Separately, **Bernardo et al. (2002), *Cellular and Molecular Biology (Noisy-le-Grand)*** tested rutin — the flavonoid first isolated from rue — and found that at the concentrations examined it **did not damage plasmid DNA nor inactivate *E. coli***, i.e. rutin itself showed no toxic activity in those systems, consistent with rue's toxicity residing in its furanocoumarins and alkaloids rather than its flavonoid. (PubMed PMID 12146706 — no DOI issued for this article; cite by PMID.)

**Evidence grade:** *Preclinical/animal support for the abortifacient action is solid and matches the traditional record; human clinical evidence for any therapeutic benefit is absent, and toxicity/phototoxicity are well documented.* The net reading is a plant of confirmed biological potency and confirmed danger, not a recommendable remedy.

### Traditional Energetic Classification
- **TCM (analogue):** acrid, bitter, warm, toxic; strongly moving — invigorates and "drives," disperses cold-stagnation; classed with the potent, restricted movers.
- **Ayurveda / regional:** rasa — *tikta*, *kaṭu*; vīrya — intensely *uṣṇa*; a *tīkṣṇa* (sharp/piercing), potentially *garaviṣa* (toxic) herb; strongly *pitta*-aggravating; *kapha*/*vāta*-reducing in the humoral sense but hazardous.
- **Galenic / Unani:** Hot & Dry in the 3rd degree; resolvent, deobstruent, emmenagogue, antidote — flagged as dangerous and abortifacient.
- **Dantian mapping (Kenetica):** lower dantian — a violent mover of the pelvic/reproductive sphere; recorded, not prescribed.

### Contraindications
- **PREGNANCY — absolute:** documented abortifacient and uterine stimulant; potentially fatal to mother and fetus.
- **Phototoxicity:** skin contact plus UV → severe phytophotodermatitis (blistering, hyperpigmentation). Avoid handling fresh plant in sun.
- **Overdose toxicity:** gastrointestinal, hepatic, renal and neurological toxicity; convulsions reported in poisoning.
- **Fertility / hormone-sensitive states, hepatic or renal impairment, children:** avoid.
- **Drug interactions:** furanocoumarins can affect drug metabolism (CYP interactions) analogous to other psoralen-bearing plants.
