# Saussurea uniflora — lug.rtsi (rGyud-bzhi, Clark 1995 line 1001)

## *Saussurea uniflora, the only binomial Clark 1995 prints on line 1001 for the Tibetan name lug.rtsi; no English name is printed, and the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints no Saussurea uniflora and no matching Tibetan name* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, and on one passage in one of its two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions, and for this drug only Clark's edition prints the name and the binomial. A search of the Shennong (Yang 1998), Sushruta, Ebers and Assyrian text folders on disk for *Saussurea* found no hit in the primary texts. The Dioscorides text prints *Saussurea costus* once, in a translator's SUGGESTED line for the Greek drug *kostos* (line 3681), which is a different species and is not read as this plant. The row was added from a printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 126) the row is keyed by the **Tibetan name** *lug.rtsi*, which is the first name Clark prints, and not by a binomial. Its locus is "Clark 1995 (line 1001)", its translator identification is the single name "Saussurea uniflora", and its note is "Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch.20 materia medica style entry" with no remark about further Latin names. The spine binomial is therefore the only Latin name the line prints, and the question whether the row key is the first of several names does not arise.

**Identity doubt.** The line prints one Latin name and no author. The cluster of lines in which it stands, however, is about a group of plants: the entry above it is *spang.rtsi.do.wo* (line 999), whose commentary-layer description speaks of "three types", and the bracket on this line begins "All three types". Which three the commentary means is not stated on the line, and the Codex does not say. The Men-Tsee-Khang edition has no glossary row for *Saussurea uniflora* (a search of its text for *Saussurea* finds only *Saussurea lappa* on line 5676), and no Tibetan name spelt *lug rtsi* or *Lugtsi*. Its text does print a name "La tsi", which the Codex sets out below and does not pair. Clark's preface says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" (line 83) and that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect" (line 68). [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1001).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry follows two lines that Clark prints on lines 999 and 1000: *spang.rtsi.do.wo* ("Pterocephalus hookeri Hook: Saussurea nepalensis", line 999) and the line "[The second type,] (sbrang.rtsi.)hbyar.bag.(chan), [is similar and cures urinary disorders, polyuria, chronic diseases and fever of the urinary bladder.]" (line 1000). It is followed by *re.skon* (line 1002). It reads:

"lug.rtsi: Saussurea uniflora: [has dark fragrant flowers. All three types] cure infectious fever, poisoning and chronic fevers."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The description "has dark fragrant flowers" and the phrase "All three types" are therefore the commentary layer. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the Latin name, which is Clark's identification, and the statement "cure infectious fever, poisoning and chronic fevers". The verb is printed in the plural ("cure"), and its plural subject is supplied only by the commentary's "All three types"; read on its own the root-text layer has the Tibetan name followed by a plural verb, and the Codex does not repair the grammar. The line gives no part used, no taste, no power and no preparation. Clark's name index lists *lug.rtsi* (line 1758), and the Latin index lists *Saussurea uniflora* (line 1644).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, the nearest name (not paired).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` prints a name "La tsi" in four places and in its index. It is in the group of "bitter" substances in chapter 19 (line 3213: "Nimpa, Tikta, Bongnga karpo, Honglen, Sergyi metok, Dhungmo nyung, La tsi, Tripa, Kyerpa, Bhashaka, Drak shhun, Kyiche, Rekon and other similar substances are the group of medicinal substances having a bit¬ ..."), in the run of efficacies in chapter 20 (line 3384: "La tsi is an antidote against poison and it cures disorders caused by ein and cures disorder ofthe kidney, liver and infectious diseases."), at the head of the group that cures poisoning in chapter 21 (lines 3604 and 3605) and at the head of another group on line 3638. The index lists "Latsi 187,201,220,222" (line 7415). The glossary on medicinal ingredients has no row for it that the Codex found. The sound of "La tsi" is near that of *lug.rtsi*, and the claims overlap in kind (poison and infectious disease on both sides), but neither text pairs the two names and the Codex does not pair them. This entry is written from Clark's line 1001 only. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Saussurea uniflora*, *lug.rtsi*, *Lugtsi* and *La tsi* found no entry on this drug. **I_1172** (*Pterocephalus hookeri*, *spang.rtsi.do.wo*, tag FMT) covers Clark's lines 999 and 1000, the two lines directly above this one, and **I_1257** (*Corydalis edulis*, *re.skon*, tag FMT) covers the entry that follows it (line 1002); neither covers line 1001. **I_279** (*Saussurea laniceps*, "Snow Lotus", tags SS, FMT), **I_080** (*Saussurea costus*, "Costus", tags DMM, CM, SBJ, SS, FMT) and **I_551** (*Costus speciosus*) are entries on other species of the genus or on the costus root and are not merged. I_080 and I_279 carry an FMT tag; which Tibetan names it rests on (Clark's *ru.rta*, line 959, prints *Saussurea lappa*) was not checked for this entry. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Saussurea* is a large genus of the daisy family (Asteraceae) of mountain herbs, and that some of its species, such as *S. costus* (costus root) and *S. laniceps* ("snow lotus"), are used in several medical traditions; that is a statement about the genus and not about this species. The line read gives no composition for the Tibetan drug, and nothing is stated here about what it contains. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text records a drug that will "cure infectious fever, poisoning and chronic fevers" (Clark, line 1001). That is the text's claim, attached to this binomial through Clark's identification alone, and it is not a Codex protocol. Fever, infection and poisoning need medical care, and the Codex gives no protocol and no dose for them. High-altitude *Saussurea* species are gathered from the wild, and several are under conservation pressure in the Himalaya; the sources read say nothing about this species. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it cures infectious fever, poisoning and chronic fevers, a role it shares in the text with the neighbouring *spang.rtsi* group. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the name *lug.rtsi* and the binomial found it only in the line cited above and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, colophon at line 3950), and the herb entries of Clark's chapter 20 that mention essence extraction (lines 971, 1043, 1078, 1103, 1121 and 1144) do not include it. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the line read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a fever and poison drug in a single short line, with no preparation and no dose, and the pairing of its Latin name with any Men-Tsee-Khang name is open. The abstentive answer is the honest one.
