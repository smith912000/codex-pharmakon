# Dark Mullein / Black Mullein

## *Verbascum nigrum* L. | Family: Scrophulariaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Cooling, moistening, relaxing to the respiratory mucosa — **inferred by genus analogy** from the classical treatment of mullein generally (see *Traditional Energetic Classification*). No source consulted for this entry assigns an energetic grade to *V. nigrum* specifically.
**Training Phase:** Foundation — Signature Study

---

### Origin & Tradition

Dark mullein is a biennial-to-perennial Scrophulariaceae of European native flora, reaching one to one and a half metres, with a dense terminal raceme of bright yellow flowers whose stamens carry conspicuous purple-violet wool — the feature that separates it at a glance from the pale-felted, grey-green *V. thapsus*. Its leaves are dark green, crenate, and far less densely tomentose than those of great mullein. It favours disturbed calcareous ground, roadsides, woodland edge and river gravels, and it is grown ornamentally in low-maintenance planting; a 2013 Italian plant-pathology report describes *V. nigrum* in exactly that garden context in Biella Province (Garibaldi et al., *Plant Disease*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1094/PDIS-05-13-0557-PDN)).

**The honesty spine of this entry is taxonomic.** The mulleins of the historical pharmacopoeias — the ones that carry the demulcent-expectorant reputation into modern herbals — are *Verbascum thapsus*, *V. densiflorum*, *V. phlomoides*, and (for the verbascoside literature) *V. sinuatum*. Two of these are already held in the codex. *V. nigrum* is a genuine European mullein and shares the genus chemistry, but the overwhelming majority of "mullein pharmacology" you will meet in secondary sources is **genus-level**, and in this entry it is labelled **congener data** unless a *V. nigrum*-specific source is named. Cultivators should treat that label as load-bearing rather than pedantic: the *Verbascum* species differ measurably in what they accumulate (see *Active Components*).

On the tradition tags: **DMM** (the Dioscoridean/Greek herbal stream) and **CM** (Avicenna's *Canon of Medicine*, the Unani transmission) both carry a mullein — Greek *phlomos* and its Arabic-Persian descendants — as a plant of cough, catarrh, and eye and ear complaints. What neither stream can be made to deliver is a clean species identification. Pre-Linnaean *phlomos* is a genus-level or even a form-level category ("male" and "female" mulleins, distinguished by leaf and habit), and mapping it onto *V. nigrum* rather than *V. thapsus* is not something the primary texts support. **[USER: verify]** any claim that Dioscorides or Avicenna named specifically the dark-stamened black mullein; write it as genus attestation or drop it. The one *V. nigrum*-specific folk claim that is documented in the modern literature is Slavic/Russian: use for kidney complaints on the basis of a presumed diuretic action, reported as the ethnomedical premise of the Kalinina study cited below.

### Active Components

Documented for the genus, with the *V. nigrum*-specific findings marked:

- **Iridoid glycosides** — aucubin and catalpol. *V. nigrum*-specific: both are present in the foliage at concentrations high enough that *Cionus* weevils sequester them from the plant, quantified by HPLC in leaf tissue (Baden et al., *J Chem Ecol*, 2013).
- **Harpagoside** — an iridoid better known from devil's claw. *V. nigrum*-specific: NMR metabolomics placed *V. nigrum* in the higher-accumulating group of five mulleins surveyed, at approximately 0.5% dry weight (Georgiev et al., *Phytochemistry*, 2011).
- **Phenylethanoid glycosides** — verbascoside (acteoside), forsythoside B, leucosceptoside B. *V. nigrum*-specific: the same NMR study reported these totalling 5.6–5.8% dry weight in *V. nigrum* and *V. xanthophoeniceum*, versus lower levels in *V. phlomoides* and *V. densiflorum*. **This is the single most important chemotaxonomic fact in the entry** — it says that the pharmacopoeial mulleins and *V. nigrum* are not interchangeable, and that on phenylethanoids *V. nigrum* is the richer plant.
- **Flavonoids** — implicated as the diuretic principal in *V. nigrum* aerial parts, with the highest content, and the strongest effect, in the **stems** rather than the leaf or flower (Kalinina et al., 2013).
- **Mucilage and saponins** — the classical basis of the demulcent flower infusion. Genus-level; not independently verified for *V. nigrum* in the sources retrieved for this entry.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Filed as a **Signature Study** plant, and the signature being studied is *taxonomic discipline*, not a therapeutic effect. Dark mullein earns its place in the Foundation phase because it is the clearest available demonstration that a common name, a genus, and a drug are three different things.

The practical cultivator's uses are three:

1. **A demulcent flower infusion** for dry, unproductive, tickling cough and upper-airway catarrh — a *genus* tradition, resting on mucilage and saponins, with the mucilage coating irritated mucosa and the saponins traditionally credited with loosening tenacious secretion. Evidence grade: traditional. There is no controlled human trial of any *Verbascum* flower infusion for cough retrieved here, and none for *V. nigrum* at all.
2. **A diuretic aerial-part preparation** — the one indication where *V. nigrum* has its own animal data rather than borrowed data. Grade: preclinical, rat.
3. **Mullein flower oil for earache** — retained here only so that its limits can be stated, because this is the use most likely to cause harm. See *Contraindications*.

Deflation, stated plainly: the codex does not present dark mullein as a respiratory medicine of established efficacy. It presents it as a soothing traditional beverage-strength infusion with a rich and interesting phenylethanoid chemistry that has not been taken into human trials.

### Preparation & Dosing

No human dose for *V. nigrum* is established in any source retrieved for this entry. What follows is traditional practice and preparation *method*, not a validated posology.

| Preparation | Part | Method | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flower infusion | Fresh or dried corollas | Hot water, covered, 10–15 min | **Must be strained through fine cloth or a coffee filter.** No established dose. |
| Leaf infusion | Dried leaf | Hot water, covered | Same straining requirement; leaf is the hairiest part. |
| Flower oil (macerate) | Dried corollas | Cold or warm oil macerate, olive oil | Topical/external only; see contraindications before any auricular use. |
| Diuretic aerial extract | Herb/stem | Ethanol extract (research preparation) | Rat study only; **no human dose exists.** Do not extrapolate. |

**The straining rule is a real safety step, not folklore.** Mullein leaf and flower carry stellate hairs that are mechanically irritant to the throat and gut. Traditional practice strains mullein infusions specifically to remove them, and the codex treats unstrained mullein tea as a preparation error.

Flowers are harvested corolla-by-corolla as they open through the season, dried fast and in shade — mullein flowers brown readily and browned material is discarded.

### Scientific Correlation

Claim-by-claim, graded.

- **Diuretic activity, *V. nigrum*, aerial parts — PRECLINICAL (rat), species-specific.** Ethanol extracts of *V. nigrum* herb, flowers, leaves and stems at a single 50 mg/kg oral dose increased urine output in rats over 3 h; the **stem** extract was most active (14.6 ± 0.8 vs 5.2 ± 1.4 ml/kg BW control) and showed saluretic effect. The root extract did not. Activity tracked flavonoid content. Acute toxicity was assessed in mice. This is a single animal study; it does not establish human efficacy or a human dose.
- **Harpagoside and phenylethanoid accumulation, *V. nigrum* — CHARACTERISED (analytical), species-specific.** ~0.5% harpagoside and 5.6–5.8% total phenylethanoid glycosides, dry weight, by ¹H-NMR metabolomics with PCA across five *Verbascum* species. Analytical chemistry only — no activity claim attaches.
- **Iridoid glycosides in *V. nigrum* foliage — CHARACTERISED, species-specific.** Aucubin and catalpol quantified in leaf via an insect-sequestration study.
- **Verbascoside pharmacology — PRECLINICAL, CONGENER/COMPOUND-LEVEL.** Verbascoside is anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and neuroprotective in cell and rodent models; the compound was first isolated from mullein and is reviewed comprehensively by Alipieva et al. A representative primary study (Wang et al.) shows neuroprotection in Aβ-exposed U251 cells and APP/PS1 mice, with verbascoside sourced from *V. sinuatum* — **a congener, not *V. nigrum***. Isolated-compound activity at research doses says nothing about a strained flower infusion.
- **Antimicrobial activity of *Verbascum* isolates — WEAK, CONGENER.** In *V. bugulifolium*, iridoid and phenylethanoid isolates including verbascoside showed antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity but **"weak antimicrobial activity against tested microorganisms."** Congener data, and negative-leaning. No *V. nigrum*-specific antimicrobial work was retrieved.
- **Antioxidant activity of mullein flower extract — PRECLINICAL/FOOD-TECHNOLOGY, CONGENER.** Mullein flower extract raised the oxidative stability of cold-pressed oils. Relevant to preservation, not to therapy.
- **Cough, catarrh, bronchitis in humans — NULL.** No human clinical trial of *V. nigrum* for any respiratory indication was retrieved. Grade: traditional only.
- **Earache — NULL for *V. nigrum*; congener-only, and the congener trial does not isolate mullein.** One RCT (Sarrell et al., n=103 children 6–18) found a naturopathic ear-drop containing *Allium sativum*, ***Verbascum thapsus***, *Calendula* and *Hypericum* in olive oil as effective as an ametocaine/phenazone anaesthetic drop for otalgia in acute otitis media. This is (a) a **congener**, *V. thapsus*, not *V. nigrum*; (b) a **four-herb combination**, so no effect can be attributed to mullein; (c) an active-comparator design without placebo. It is honestly graded as weak and non-attributable.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

In the Greek stream carried through Dioscorides (DMM) and into Avicenna's Unani transmission (CM), mullein sits among the cooling, softening, moistening remedies of the chest and throat — the class whose action is to relax and lubricate rather than to heat or drive. The mucilage is the material correlate of that reading: a plant whose visible property is slipperiness under water is filed with the *demulcentia*. The dark-stamened *V. nigrum* is folded into this class **by genus inference only**, and the codex marks it as inference rather than attestation. Any grade of degree assigned to *V. nigrum* in a modern secondary source should be traced to its primary text before it is repeated.

### Contraindications

- **Never instil oil, or any liquid, into an ear with a perforated or possibly perforated eardrum.** This is absolute. Ear pain is a symptom requiring otoscopic examination before anything enters the canal; instillation through a perforation risks middle-ear and ototoxic injury. If the drum has not been visualised by a clinician, no mullein oil goes in the ear.
- **Ear pain in a child, ear pain with discharge, ear pain with fever, hearing loss, vertigo, or pain persisting beyond 48 hours needs medical assessment**, not a home macerate.
- **Irritant hairs.** Mullein leaf and flower hairs are mechanically irritant to the mouth, throat, oesophagus and gut, and to skin and eyes during harvest and handling. All internal preparations must be strained through fine cloth or filter paper. Handle dried material with care; the dust is irritant to the airway.
- **Do not ingest the seeds.** *Verbascum* seeds contain saponins and have a documented historical use as a fish poison (piscicide). Seed is not a food or medicine part and is excluded from all preparations in this codex.
- **No established human dose, no human safety data.** The toxicity data for *V. nigrum* is acute-toxicity screening in mice attached to a single rat diuretic study. That is not a human safety profile.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, paediatric internal use: no data — avoid.**
- **Diuretic caution.** If the aerial-part diuretic tradition is being followed, note that the rat study showed saluretic effect with both natriuretic and kaliuretic indices raised. In a human taking loop or thiazide diuretics, digoxin, lithium, or in anyone with renal impairment, an unquantified additive diuretic and potassium-losing effect is a real interaction risk. Avoid.
- **Renal complaints** — the folk indication that motivated the diuretic research — are precisely the situation in which self-treatment is least appropriate. Investigate the kidney, do not brew for it.
