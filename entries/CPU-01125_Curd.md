# Curd — Dadhi (the "Curd-group" of Sushruta's chapter on liquids); with the Takra (whey, buttermilk) group
## *Milk curd, the soured and set form of milk. Bhishagratna prints "Curd" for the group and "Dadhi" for the curd in a later line (Sutrasthana ch.45, line 726); no binomial applies* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The English is Bhishagratna's translation of the *Sushruta Samhita*. Curd is treated in Sutrasthana ch.45, "the Chapter, which deals with the rules to be observed in respect of liquid substances in general (Drava-Dravya-Vidhi-madhyayam)" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch45.txt` lines 2-6). The chapter runs through water, milk, curd, whey, butter and other liquids as groups. The curd group begins at line 555: "The Curd-group :— There are three kinds of curd such as, the sweet, the acid, and the extremely acid curd. Milk curd generally leaves an astringent after-taste. It is demulcent and heat-making in its potency, as well as spermatopoietic, vitalising and auspicious. It proves curative in Pinasa (nasal catarrh), intermittent fever (Vishama Jvara), dysentery, non-relish for food, difficult urination, and general cachexia" (lines 555-562).

The metrical text that follows (lines 564-573) separates the three kinds by their effect on the humours: sweet curd "greatly increases the slimy secretions of the organs and the quantity of fat and Kapham"; acid curd "deranges the Pittam and the Kapham"; extremely acid curd "vitiates the blood"; and curd "not perfectly curdled (Mandajatam)" is "acid in its (digestive) chemical reaction, acts as an inordinately strong purgative and diuretic agent, and deranges the three fundamental humours".

Curd is then taken by the milk it comes from (lines 575-621). Curdled cow's milk "is demulcent, sweet in digestion, appetising, strength-increasing and acrid. It subdues the bodily Vayu". Goat's curd is "light" and "subdues the deranged Pittam and Kapham". Buffalo's curd is "sweet in digestion, and spermatopoietic". Camel's curd is "pungent in digestion", "heavy and a purgative". Ewe's curd "proves aggravating in derangements of the Vayu and Kapham, as well as in cases of piles". Mare's curd "proves injurious to the eyes" and is "hot in its potency". Woman's curd "is demulcent, sweet in digestion, tonic, pleasant, heavy, and specially beneficial to the eyes" and "is the best of all kinds of curd, and of all emollient remedies (Santarpanam)". She-elephant's curd is "light in digestion, subdues Kapham". Then the text ranks them: "Of all the preceding kinds of curd, the one prepared with cow's milk should be regarded as the best in virtue and quality." Cloth-filtered curd "imparts a relish to the food"; curd of boiled milk "subdues the deranged Vayu and Pittam" and "increases the strength and the root principle of life"; the cream of curd "is heavy and spermatopoietic... phlegmagogic and aphrodisiac"; curd made without cream "is parchifying, astringent and arrests stool and urine" (lines 621-641). I note the apparent conflict in the translation, which calls the woman's curd "the best of all kinds of curd" at one point and the cow's curd "the best in virtue and quality" at another. The sentence about the cow's curd follows the list of the preceding kinds and I read it as the later ranking, but the translation does not say how the two are to be reconciled. [USER: verify]

The season rule is in lines 642-653: "The use of curd is generally prohibited in (Vasanta) spring, (Grishma) summer, and (Sharat) autumn, whereas it is recommended during the rains (Varsha) and in the forepart of winter (Hemanta), and in the cold season proper (Shishira)." The "residuary sediment of curd (Mastu)" is "frigorific and refrigerant, light and purifying to the internal channels of the body", "sweet and astringent", "anti-aphrodisiac", and "a speedy purgative" (lines 654-666). The chapter sums up "the virtues of the seven kinds of curd" (sweet, acid, extremely acid, incompletely curdled, curd of boiled milk, curd cream, creamless curd), together with the sediment (lines 661-666).

Curd appears elsewhere in Sushruta as an ingredient or a food, which I opened. In Sutrasthana ch.35, in a case of voracious appetite, "a diet consisting of buffalo-milk, or its curd (Dadhi) and liquid buffalo-butter should be prescribed" (`SS_Sutra_ch35.txt` lines 462-464). In ch.46, meat "cooked and prepared with clarified butter, curd, sour gruel (Kanjika), acid fruits... should be considered as a very wholesome diet, though heavy of digestion" (`SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` lines 2275-2282). In ch.20, in the passage on foods that do not go together, "The pot-herbs known as the Nadima and Siddhi should not be simultaneously eaten with curd, and the flesh of a cock", and the Lakucha fruit "should not be taken with milk, curd or meat soup" (`SS_Sutra_ch20.txt` lines 148-150, 169-171). In ch.19 and ch.21 curd is among the articles a patient with an ulcer should avoid, or that may aggravate one (`SS_Sutra_ch19.txt` lines 76-85; `SS_Sutra_ch21.txt` lines 303-306, 330-331).

### Varieties

**Takra (whey; also Takram)** (SS; Sutrasthana ch.45, line 668). "The Takra Group :— The Takra (whey) is sweet and acid in taste, and leaves an astringent after-taste. It is light, appetising and heat-making in its potency, and has a parchifying effect upon the organism." It is named curative in "chemical or combinative poisoning, oedema, dysentery, diarrhoea, jaundice, piles, enlarged spleen, abdominal glands, non-relish for food, intermittent fever, thirst, vomiting, water-brash, colic and obesity", and it "subdues the deranged Vayu and Kapham, and is non-aphrodisiac" (lines 668-686). A metrical passage defines it: "A compound made of equal parts of curd and water and subsequently churned so as to have the contained cream or butter completely skimmed off, and which is neither too thick nor too thin, is called Takram", with "a taste blended of the sweet, acid and astringent" (lines 688-692). "Waterless curd, churned with the entire butter or creamy substance inherent in it, is called Gholam (a kind of whey)" (lines 693-695). Its use "is prohibited in the hot season, nor should it be given to a weak person, nor to one suffering from an ulcer, or laid up with an attack of haemoptysis, or to one suffering from epileptic fits, vertigo (Bhrama), or from a burning sensation in the body", and is "recommended during the cold months" and in disorders of Kapham and Vayu (lines 695-703). Sweetened Takram "soothes the deranged Pittam and aggravates the Kapham"; acid Takram "subdues the Vayu and produces Pittam" (lines 706-708); acid Takram is drunk with rock-salt for Vayu, with sugar for Pittam, and with "Yavakshara and the powders of the drugs known as Vyosha" for Kapham (lines 710-722). The same passage names related products, *Kurchika* (inspissated milk), *Manda*, *Kilata*, *Moratha* and *Piyusha*, with a line each (lines 723-730); they are listed here because the text gives them and are not otherwise developed.

*Synthesis of the varieties.* Sushruta separates the curd group by the kind of curd (sweet, acid, extremely acid, incompletely set), by the milk it comes from (cow, goat, buffalo, camel, ewe, mare, woman, she-elephant), and by preparation (filtered, from boiled milk, creamed, creamless), and ranks cow's curd first for "virtue and quality". Takra is a separate group that the text defines by churning curd with water, and it differs from curd on the points the text itself prints: it is "light" where curd is a demulcent food, it is "non-aphrodisiac" where curd is called spermatopoietic, and it is prescribed for the humours of Vayu and Kapham where curd is said to subdue Vayu in most forms and to aggravate Kapham in the sweet form. I have placed the two side by side and have not equated either with a food or drug in another tradition.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, curd is milk acidified and set by lactic acid bacteria, which convert lactose to lactic acid; it contains milk protein (casein), fat in proportion to the milk used, calcium, and live bacterial cultures if it has not been heated afterwards. Whey or buttermilk is the watery part left after churning, and carries less fat.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record, with a limited food use. Curd is an ordinary dairy food, and the Sushruta text treats it as a food and as an adjunct in diet, hedged with seasonal and humoral rules (prohibited in spring, summer and autumn, in the text's own words; recommended in the rains, early winter and the cold season). The Codex gives no protocol. A reader with a dairy allergy or lactose intolerance, or with a condition that the text itself lists (an ulcer, haemoptysis), should take the text's cautions as a record and not as advice.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations the text gives: curd of each milk (lines 575-621); cloth-filtered curd, curd of boiled milk, curd cream and creamless curd (lines 621-641); the residuary sediment (*Mastu*, lines 654-666); and Takram, "equal parts of curd and water" churned and skimmed (lines 688-692), with the additions of rock-salt, sugar, or *Yavakshara* and *Vyosha* powders for Vayu, Pittam and Kapham respectively (lines 710-722). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Curd is a fermented dairy food; its protein and calcium content follows from the milk. Fermented milk contains lactic acid bacteria, and many people with lactose intolerance tolerate it better than milk, though not all do. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS):** Sutrasthana ch.45 prints, for milk curd in general, an astringent after-taste, a "demulcent and heat-making" potency, and the descriptors "spermatopoietic, vitalising and auspicious" (lines 556-559). It gives sweet curd as increasing the "slimy secretions" and *Kapham*, acid curd as deranging *Pittam* and *Kapham*, and extremely acid curd as vitiating the blood (lines 564-573). Cow's curd is "sweet in digestion" and subdues *Vayu* (lines 575-577). Takram is "sweet and acid in taste", with an astringent after-taste, "light, appetising and heat-making in its potency", and "sweet in its digestive reaction" (lines 668-683). The translator does not print the words *rasa*, *virya* and *vipaka* in these lines, so I have used his wording and not those terms.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed.* Curd is claimed in Sutrasthana ch.45 as a liquid food and drug, and the text calls milk curd "spermatopoietic, vitalising and auspicious" (line 559), the curd of boiled milk one that "increases the strength and the root principle of life" (line 633), and the cream of curd "aphrodisiac" (line 636). These are qualities of the substance, listed in a chapter on liquids. In the lines read, the chapter is not a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* chapter and does not classify curd as a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* substance, and I did not find curd assigned to either in Chikitsasthana ch.26 to ch.30 (a search of those chapters for "curd" and "Dadhi" returned one hit, in ch.26 line 103, where the clarified butter for a goat-testes recipe is said to be "prepared from churning milk (and not from curd)"). *Ojas* is not named for curd in any line read. So curd carries therapeutic and dietary qualities in this text and no attested cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a cultivation practice. Curd is a food whose use the text governs by season and by humour: avoided in spring, summer and autumn, allowed in the rains and the cold months, and avoided in an ulcer. The honest use of the entry is as a record of those rules. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Dairy allergy and lactose intolerance:** curd retains milk protein and some lactose.
- **Season and condition, as the text says:** curd "generally prohibited" in spring, summer and autumn (lines 642-646); Takram prohibited in the hot season and in weakness, ulcer, haemoptysis, epilepsy, vertigo and a burning sensation (lines 695-699).
- **Incompatible combinations the text lists:** curd with certain pot-herbs, with cock's flesh, and with the Lakucha fruit (Sutrasthana ch.20); the Codex treats these as a record and not as tested advice.
- **Hygiene:** unpasteurised or poorly stored curd can carry pathogens; keep it chilled.
- **Ethics and law:** none specific; curd is a common food. The Codex notes only that the sourced milk is an animal product.
