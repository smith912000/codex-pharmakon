### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The note is theirs, not Dioscorides', and no source for it was opened. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-84. DELPHINION*, heading at line 7271, SUGGESTED line at 7272, note at 7273, text from line 7274 to line 7288 (it runs across two pages of illustrations). It stands in Book Three, "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants".

The whole chapter reads: "Delphinium sends out shoots two feet long (or more) from one root, around which are little cut-in leaves — thin, somewhat long, similar to dolphins (from which they are named). The flower is similar to the white violet, with a purple colour. The seed in the pods resembles milium [3-158], and (taken as a drink in wine) helps those bitten by scorpions like nothing else can. They also say that scorpions grow faint and become inactive and numb when the herb is applied to them, and when it is taken away they are restored to their former state. It grows in rough sunny places. It is also called diachysis, diachytos, paralysis, camaros, hyacinthus, delphinias, nerion, nereadium, sosacros, or cronios; the Romans call it buccinus minor."

So Dioscorides describes shoots two feet long or more from one root, with thin, somewhat long, cut-in leaves "similar to dolphins" (the text derives the name from them), a flower like a white violet but purple, and seed in pods like *milium*. The one use is against scorpion stings: the seed in wine "helps those bitten by scorpions like nothing else can". He adds a second claim with a distancing phrase, "They also say", that scorpions become faint, inactive and numb when the herb is applied to them and recover when it is taken away. It grows in rough sunny places. The many other names are *diachysis, diachytos, paralysis, camaros, hyacinthus, delphinias, nerion, nereadium, sosacros, cronios*, and the Roman *buccinus minor*.

**Neighbouring chapters.** The next chapter, 3-85 *DELPHINION ETERON* (line 7289), is "the other delphinium" with "the same properties", and it is the basis of the spine's entry CPU-01501 *Delphinium elatum* (I_1065 in this batch). Chapter 3-86 is *PURETHRON* (pyrethrum), a different plant, and the spine row for *Delphinium peregrinum* points to it only because of an illustration caption (see I_1067). The Codex's existing larkspur entries, I_560 (*Delphinium consolida*) and I_225 (*Delphinium staphisagria*), were not checked for their chapters, but no entry cites 3-84 (chapter-level grep); the translators' *Delphinium consolida* suggestion sits near line 7850 and *Delphinium staphisagria* at 4-156 *STAPHISAGRIA* (line 9430), both different chapters. The translators' index lists *Delphinium oxysepalum* at page 460, the page of this chapter's second half. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a seed drunk in wine for scorpion stings, and a tale about scorpions numbed by the herb. These are the text's claims, the second hedged by "they also say". The Codex gives no protocol: a scorpion sting needs medical care, and the plant is, on the translators' own note, a poisonous genus.

### Preparation & Dosing

3-84 gives one preparation: the seed "taken as a drink in wine". The herb is also "applied" to scorpions in the second claim, which is a report and not a treatment. No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: habitat (rough sunny places), a description to recognise it (shoots from one root, cut-in leaves, a purple flower like a white violet, seed in pods), and the origin of the name (the leaf shape compared with dolphins). He records a numbing-of-scorpions report while distancing himself with "They also say"; recording is not endorsing. It assigns the plant no status. The identification is the translators'. **Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a single stated use against scorpion stings and a reported tale, with no amounts, so it is history and not a practice. The poisonous-genus note is the translators' and is the sole safety statement.

### Contraindications

The genus is marked poisonous by the translators and is generally known as toxic, so no part of any larkspur should be eaten or drunk on the strength of this entry. A scorpion sting needs medical care. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no wild plant should be gathered or used. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
