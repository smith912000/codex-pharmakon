# Pig — Hog (Bryan's Ebers) / Pig, Pork (rGyud-bzhi; Men-Tsee-Khang glossary)
## *The domestic pig; the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints* Sus scrofa domestica *Brisson, and the boar as* Sus scrofa *L.* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP, FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

This entry is the pig as an animal source: its flesh and its smaller parts (tooth, eyes, tongue, bone, bile, stool). Two products have their own entries and are not repeated: pig blood is in `III_23_Pig_blood.md`, and rendered pig fat (lard) is in `III_37_Lard.md`, which gives the Greek and Tibetan fat passages. The fat is mentioned below only where it completes the list of parts.

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The English text on disk is C. P. Bryan's 1930 *The Papyrus Ebers*, a translation with summaries; the Egyptian words are not printed. In Chapter VII, "Organic Remedies", Bryan lists the animals "whose identity is not in doubt, and who were called upon to sacrifice themselves in the cause of the Healing Art", and "Hog" stands in that list between "Ass" and "Dog" (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1404; `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` row "Hog", line 115, marked "Bryan's English identification; no Egyptian word or binomial given in this list"). The spine also carries `_sources/ebers.csv` line 141, "Pig", whose source field cites Ghalioungui's 1987 translation (Glossary I, Medicaments); that translation is not on disk and was not read. Bryan says that "in the case of the Hog" the physicians seem "to have used a wise discretion" in what they drew from him, and that although they "used most of his body from his teeth to his testicles", nowhere is his brain prescribed (lines 1419-1425). The prescriptions found:

- *Indigestion.* "ANOTHER REMEDY FOR INDIGESTION: A Hog's Tooth. Crush to powder, put inside four Sugar-cakes, and eat for four days." (line 1869). Bryan's comment compares it to modern pancreas preparations from the hog (line 1873); that comparison is his, not the papyrus's.
- *Hardenings.* "SALVE TO MAKE SUPPLE THE HARDENINGS: Hog's Fat, Oil-of-Worms, Oil-of-the-abxersu-animal, Mouse Oil, Cat's Oil. Gather into one and anoint therewith." (line 2191). Immediately after, "A Hog's Tooth, Cat's dung, Dog's dung, aau-of-samu-oil, Berries-of-the-wet-plant. Pound and apply as a Poultice." (line 2688).
- *A wound to be dried.* "Mix in Fat-of-the-Hippopotamus, or of the Hog, and apply as a poultice" (lines 2371-2374).
- *The vulva.* A douche of "Fresh Dates and Hog's Bile" after the cow's bile has failed (line 2557).
- *Blindness.* "ANOTHER AGAINST BLINDNESS": the OCR displaces the words, giving "The Two Eyes therefrom ... True Collyrium, Red-lead, Wild Honey ... of a Pig; remove the water. Crush, powder, make into one, and inject into the Ear of the Patient. Thereby he will at once recover. When thou hast seen properly to this mixing, repeat this Magic Formula: I HAVE BROUGHT THIS THING AND PUT IT IN ITS PLACE. [T]HE CROCODILE IS WEAK AND POWERLESS. (Twice)" (lines 2962-2975). The reading "the two eyes of a pig" rests on that scrambled line and is marked [USER: verify]. The formula is Bryan's rendering; the bracketed letter is the OCR's.
- *Other mentions.* "Hog's dung" is among the ingredients of the thirty-seven-item poultice (line 3185); "Hog's Blood" appears only as a comparison for the colour of what a remedy drives out (line 3511); pig's blood as one of five bloods is at line 2886 (see III_23).

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The spine's locus is the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 "Glossary on Animals" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` lines 7054-7058), which prints "Pig / Sus scrofa domestica Brisson" and "Boar / Sus scrofa L."; the Tibetan names beside them are not legible in the OCR. In the running text of the Explanatory Tantra the pig is a domestic animal in the meats classification (Clark 1995, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 672: "(g) Domestic animals include the dzo, yak, camel, horse, donkey, bull, skom.po ..., goat, sheep, dog, pig, chicken, cat, etc."; the wild boar is in group (d)). Of the meats, Clark line 677: "Pork is cool and light, heals wounds and eliminates brown phlegm." Men-Tsee-Khang line 2931: "Pork is cool and light in quality and heals wounds and baekan mukpo." The two translations render the same disorder differently ("brown phlegm", *baekan mukpo*); I did not check the Tibetan. The parts: pig bone "[has nine names, for example gtig.mug.] It cures brown phlegm" (Clark line 1160; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3523, "Pig bone cures baekan mukpo"); pig tongue "removes bone warts" (Clark line 1170) or "removes fragmented bones from comminuted fractures" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3536); pig blood and pig fat are at Clark lines 1176 and 1180 (see III_23, III_37); pig's stool "cures indigestion, septic disorders, infectious fevers and bile tumors" (Clark line 1199; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3568 gives "indigestion, epidemic and cholelithiasis"); and the bile duct of a bull, pig, etc. is named as a common source of animal bile (Clark line 880). The tradition also names pork as a dietary cause of a wind disorder: "(the immediate) causes of a wind disorder are light, coarse diet and behavioral patterns (e.g., tea, pork, goat's meat and fasting)" (Clark line 164). In the section on building the body the Men-Tsee-Khang text recommends eating the meat of carnivorous animals "to develop a fatty body like a pig" (line 4396); this is a simile and not a statement about pork.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, pork is muscle protein with fat, B vitamins (notably thiamine), iron and zinc; pig fat is mostly triglycerides; bile contains bile acids and bilirubin; teeth and bone are mostly calcium phosphate (hydroxyapatite) with collagen; the tongue is muscle. The pig is an omnivore and a host to several parasites that pass to humans through undercooked meat.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Egyptian prescriptions use the tooth, fat, bile and eyes for indigestion, hardenings, wounds, the vulva and blindness, and the Tibetan texts use the flesh, bone, tongue and stool for wounds, phlegm disorders and indigestion. The sources give no outcomes. Cooked pork is a food in many cultures and needs no protocol from this Codex. Parts such as stool, bile and eyes are not used: the practice is unhygienic and without evidence, and pork is forbidden by dietary law in some religious traditions.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparations the texts give: Ebers, a hog's tooth crushed to powder and eaten in four sugar-cakes for four days (line 1869); a salve of hog's fat with worm, mouse and cat oils (line 2191); a poultice of tooth with cat's and dog's dung and oil (line 2688); a douche of fresh dates with hog's bile (line 2557); and a mixture of eyes, collyrium, red lead and honey injected into the ear with a spoken formula (lines 2962-2975). The Tibetan passages give no preparation. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Pork is a source of complete protein, thiamine and iron. Undercooked pork can transmit *Trichinella* and the tapeworm *Taenia solium*, and hepatitis E has been linked to pig products. Pig-derived pancreatic enzymes are used in modern medicine (pancrelipase), which is the comparison Bryan draws, but that is a different preparation from a ground tooth. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Egyptian (EP):** none printed. The Ebers material is organised by complaint and gives no qualities for its ingredients.

**Tibetan (FMT):** Clark line 677 and Men-Tsee-Khang line 2931 print pork as cool and light, and healing wounds. Clark lines 672-673 place the pig among the domestic animals of the meats classification and say that all the meats are "sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste" and that the flesh of dry-land animals has "cool, light and coarse powers"; the text does not say how the general statement applies to the pig beyond the pork line above. No quality is printed for the bone, tongue or stool.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* The hog is named in the Ebers list of animal sources and in prescriptions for indigestion, hardenings, a wound, the vulva and blindness, which record what was done for a complaint and not what the animal does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. The one remedy read that carries a spoken formula (the blindness remedy, lines 2962-2975) shows the Egyptian pairing of spell and drug in a single intervention (*heka*): the formula is to be repeated after the mixing, twice, and dropping it would lose half the prescription. That is a statement about the form of the remedy, not a cultivation status. Substance identity in an indication-organised corpus is a modern hypothesis. No deity is attributed.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* The pig sits inside the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica of meats and animal parts (Clark 1995, lines 672-677 and 1160-1199), as a therapeutic substance with a stated quality (pork is cool and light) and indications (wounds, brown phlegm), and also as a dietary cause of wind disorder (line 164). No cultivation status is attributed to it: the Tibetan frame is that pathology is at root a spiritual condition and medicine is one of four treatment branches that supports practice but does not reach the root, and nothing read ties pork to that. No *bcud len* use was found. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here makes the pig a substance for cultivation. The Egyptian texts use parts of it as a drug for a complaint, one with a spoken formula, and the Tibetan texts cool and soothe with the meat while naming it a dietary cause of wind disorder. A cultivator who eats pork eats it cooked, as food, and takes no practice from this entry. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** raw or undercooked pork carries trichinosis, *Taenia solium* and bacterial risks; stool, bile, eyes and unwashed teeth are infection sources and are not to be used.
- **Dietary law and ethics:** pork is prohibited in Judaism and Islam and avoided in other communities; the Codex recommends no practice that conflicts with these.
- **Wind disorders (Tibetan):** Clark line 164 lists pork among the dietary causes of a wind disorder; people described as having that constitution should note the caution.
- **Cardiovascular and metabolic disease:** processed pork is high in salt and saturated fat; ordinary dietary caution applies.
- **Allergy:** red-meat (alpha-gal) allergy after tick bites can include pork.
- **Pregnancy:** undercooked pork should be avoided.
