### Origin & Tradition

The iris of the classical pharmacy is the fragrant rhizome — **orris root** — of the bearded irises, above all *Iris germanica* and the white-flowered *Iris florentina* (Florentine iris). Dioscorides gives iris a prominent place at the very opening of *De Materia Medica* Book I, a sign of its esteem; the genus is named for the goddess Iris of the rainbow, for the flower's many colours. The prized part is the rhizome, which when **freshly dug has little scent** but, after **drying and ageing for months to years**, develops the celebrated sweet, violet-like fragrance that has made orris one of the most important materials in perfumery for millennia — the classic violet note and a fixative that binds and prolongs other scents.

Medicinally the classical and later traditions valued orris as a warming, drying **expectorant and resolvent**: an aromatic to clear thick phlegm from the chest, to provoke expectoration in coughs and bronchial congestion, to act as a mild purgative and diuretic, and — applied externally — as a resolvent for swellings and an ingredient of perfumed powders and unguents. The dried rhizome was also given to teething infants to chew (a use better avoided today) and powdered orris served as a sweetening agent for tooth powders, sachets and the scenting of linens and hair.

A point of caution and clarity: the aromatic **orris** of *Iris germanica/florentina* must be distinguished from the more acrid, drastically purgative rhizomes of other irises (the Old-World *Iris pseudacorus*, "yellow flag," and the New-World "blue flag," *Iris versicolor*), which are harsher and more toxic. The Codex entry concerns the perfumer's orris; the acrid blue/yellow flags belong to a more cautious register.

### Active Components

- **Irones (α-, β-, γ-irone)** — the violet-scented ketones that develop **during drying/ageing** by oxidative breakdown of triterpenoid precursors (iridals); the source of orris's prized fragrance and its perfumery value. - **Iridal-type triterpenoids** — the precursors from which irones form on ageing.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. **Aromatic expectorant.** The defining medicinal use: warming and drying to loosen and expel thick chest phlegm in coughs and bronchial congestion.
2. **Resolvent / aromatic (external).** In perfumed powders and unguents; applied to disperse swellings in the older resolvent tradition.
3. **Perfumer's fixative and violet note.** The dominant modern use — orris butter as one of perfumery's finest and costliest fixatives. [Non-medical but central to the substance's identity.]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Unani/folk mapping:** hot-dry; mukhrij-e-balgham (expels phlegm), deobstruent, aromatic; cosmetic and perfumery aromatic.

### Cultivation Register

**Ancient Egyptian (EP).** Iris/orris survives in the Egyptian record as an aromatic and cosmetic material rather than as a substance with a constitutional action, and the corpus is indication-organised; no cultivation framing is attested — **(c) absent**. The tradition records what a fragrant rhizome was *used for* (scenting, cosmetic powder), not what it does to *ka*, *ba* or *ib*, and its substance-identity in the Egyptian material is a locational tag, not a subtle-body assignment.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (AT).** With the blocking warning in force for every Assyrian substance identification and tablet reference, orris carries **(c) absent**: it can be located in the aromatic-materia trade but no cultivation grade or vital-force role is securely attributable to it in this corpus.

**Dioscorides / Greco-Roman (DMM).** Dioscorides opens *De Materia Medica* Book I with iris, a mark of esteem, and treats it as a warming-drying expectorant and resolvent — a *therapeutic* profile, with no subtle-body system behind it: **(c) absent** as to any cultivation status. What the tradition genuinely offers a cultivator is its discipline of attention, and orris is almost a paradigm of it — the rhizome is scentless when fresh and must be dried and aged for months to years before the violet note develops, so provenance, patience and sensory assay *are* the practice.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** The Unani/folk mapping is hot-dry, *mukhrij-e-balgham* (expels phlegm), deobstruent and aromatic — a qualitative therapeutic assignment, not a cultivation status: **(b) framed**. The tradition's cultivation idea proper (conservation of innate heat and radical moisture) is not attached to orris in the sources retrieved, so none is claimed for it.

**Sushruta / Ayurveda (SS).** Orris appears, at most, as an imported aromatic; no *rasa/guna* profile and, critically, no *rasayana* or *vajikarana* status is attested for it in a classical source — **(c) absent**. Iris is not a plant of the classical Sushrutan pharmacopoeia, so a rejuvenative grade would be an import, and none is asserted here.

**Practitioner's note.** Orris is a craft material before it is a medicine: what a cultivator "does" with it is chiefly the discipline of *making* it — the long ageing that turns an odourless rhizome into the perfumer's violet fixative — plus its bounded medicinal use as a warming expectorant. No tradition here grades it as something taken to nourish life, and its allergen status and the sharper toxicity of the acrid blue/yellow-flag look-alikes are the practical warnings that keep it in the aromatic-and-therapeutic register, not a constitutional one.

### Contraindications

- **Allergy / sensitisation.** Orris is a recognised contact and respiratory allergen; avoid in known iris/orris sensitivity; caution in cosmetic use.
- **Acrid iris species.** Do not substitute *Iris versicolor* (blue flag) or *I. pseudacorus* (yellow flag) for orris — they are more irritant, emeto-cathartic and toxic.
- **Fresh rhizome.** Irritant; the medicinal/aromatic material is the aged rhizome.
- **Pregnancy — caution.** Aperient/emmenagogue reputation of iris rhizomes; avoid medicinal doses. [USER: verify.]
- **Infants.** Do not use the rhizome as a teething aid (choking/irritation).
