# Tutsan

## *Hypericum androsaemum* L. | Family: Hypericaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Cool–Dry; astringent-vulnerary, with a downward hepatobiliary and diuretic drainage

**Training Phase:** Consolidation (wound-closing and drainage work); *not* a mood or Opening-phase agent — see the deflation below

---

### Origin & Tradition

The plant carries two names and both are claims. **Tutsan** is an English mangling of the Old French ***toute-saine*** — "all-heal," "heals-everything" — a vulnerary boast attached to the leaf. ***Androsaemum*** is Greek: *andros* + *haima*, **"man's blood"**, for the deep blood-red juice that bleeds from the ripening berries when they are crushed. One name asserts the effect; the other asserts the reason. Put them together and you have a complete pre-modern argument: *this plant bleeds red, therefore it governs blood, therefore it closes wounds, therefore it heals everything.* It is one of the tidiest signature-doctrines in the European corpus — and it is also a good place to watch a signature doctrine do exactly half of what it promises.

**Greco-Roman and Classical Materia Medica (AT / DMM).** *Androsaemum* enters the Western record as a name in the classical botanical vocabulary, and its transmission through the mediaeval herbals is where the wound doctrine hardens. The classical reading is straightforward sympathetic reasoning of the blood-signature type: a plant whose fruit exudes what looks like human blood is assigned to **wounds, cuts, bleeding, and the closing of breached flesh**. The doctrine's internal logic is that like calls to like — the plant that carries blood knows blood, and what knows blood can command it to stop.

Note carefully that this is a *different* doctrine from the one attached to its famous cousin *H. perforatum* (St John's Wort), whose perforate-leaf signature (leaves pinholed with translucent glands, read as puncture-wounds already sustained and survived) generated an overlapping but distinct wound claim, and whose red oil was read as *Christ's* blood rather than *man's*. The two plants were never the same remedy. The modern conflation of them is a marketing artefact, not a traditional one — and it is the single most important thing this entry has to say.

**Mediaeval and Early-Modern European Herbal Tradition (DMM).** As *toute-saine* / *tutsan* / **Sweet-Amber** (for the ambergris-like scent of the dried leaf), the plant occupies the vulnerary shelf: **leaves laid on cuts, burns and open wounds**; **ointments made from the flowering tops** to heal breaches of the skin. The "all-heal" name is a genuine claim of breadth, but its documented centre of gravity is narrow and physical — it is a wound plant, not a panacea in practice. The doctrine here is *closure*: the herb draws the lips of a wound together, dries the weeping, and prevents the corruption that enters through an open surface.

**Iberian Folk Continuum (CM) — the living tradition.** This is where tutsan actually survives as medicine, and it is *not* as a wound herb. In **Portugal and Spain**, *H. androsaemum* is prepared as a **herbal tea (a hepatobiliary and diuretic infusion)** — taken for the liver and gallbladder, and for the flow of urine. The Portuguese use is documented in the modern literature as a digestive-tract tea: *"Several species of the genus Hypericum are used in Portugal to prepare herbal teas to which digestive tract effects are attributed"* (Xavier et al., 2012). The doctrine has shifted register entirely — from closing an external breach to opening an internal channel. The plant that was a binder outside became a drainer inside.

The berry tradition persists alongside: the red fruits used to prepare **ointments for cuts and wounds**, the practice explicitly documented as the ethnopharmacological rationale for modern skin-care investigation of the berries (Antognoni et al., 2016).

#### Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer

**Doctrine 1 — "man's blood governs blood" (the signature).** The berry bleeds red; therefore the plant closes wounds. Map this onto real chemistry and something interesting happens: **the signature is chemically honest about its pigment and dishonest about its inference.**

The red is real and it *is* a naphthodianthrone/polyphenol matter — the *Hypericum* genus is defined chemically by its **hypericins** (naphthodianthrones), the red pigments, and by its **acylphloroglucinols** (the hyperforin class). So the doctrine correctly identified that this plant carries an unusual red chromophore class. Where it fails is the inference: **the compounds doing the wound work are not the red ones.** The berry extracts that demonstrably drive fibroblast migration and inhibit collagenase are dominated by **shikimic acid** (108,143–115,901 mg/kg) and **chlorogenic acid** (45,781–57,003 mg/kg) — a colourless phenolic-acid fraction. The doctrine pointed at the pigment and the activity was in the acid.

This is the signature-doctrine failure mode in its purest form: *the signature correctly flagged that the plant was chemically unusual, then attributed the virtue to the wrong molecule for the right reason.* The red juice was a marker of a metabolically interesting plant. It was not the medicine.

And the wound doctrine itself? **Partly vindicated, by the wrong compounds.** Both polar berry extracts significantly increased **human fibroblast migration** — the actual cellular event of wound closure — and both inhibited **collagenase**, the enzyme that degrades the matrix a healing wound needs. *Toute-saine* was not wrong that this plant does something to a wound. It was wrong about why, and it was wildly overstated about how much.

**Doctrine 2 — "the drainer" (the Iberian tea).** The liver/gallbladder/urine doctrine has no signature to justify it and no classical pedigree — it is empirical folk practice, and it is the branch with the *least* mechanistic explanation and the *most* continuous use. The chemistry offers a partial handle: the water extract is chlorogenic-acid-dominated, and shows genuine activity on the colorectal cell lines that model the tissue the tea is drunk for. But there is a sharp honesty problem here, addressed in the Scientific Correlation: **the chlorogenic acid alone did not reproduce the extract's effect.** The tradition's active principle is, at present, unidentified.

**Doctrine 3 — the one that isn't there.** No tradition retrieved here assigns *H. androsaemum* to **melancholy, low spirits, or the mind**. The plant's modern reputation as an antidepressant is entirely borrowed from *H. perforatum* by genus-association. See the deflation.

### Active Components

- **(−)-Epicatechin** — the dominant leaf-methanol constituent at 6538 ± 235.36 µg/g (Yazici Bektaş et al., 2021).
- **Chlorogenic acid / 5-O- and 3-O-caffeoylquinic acids** — the most abundant phenolics of the ethanol:water and water extracts (Jabeur et al., 2016); 45,781.1–57,002.7 mg/kg in the polar berry extracts (Antognoni et al., 2016).
- **Shikimic acid** — 108,143.7–115,901.3 mg/kg in the polar berry extracts; the single largest identified constituent (Antognoni et al., 2016).
- **Caffeic acid** — 3313 ± 79.51 µg/g in the leaf-water extract (Yazici Bektaş et al., 2021).
- **Quillaic acid** (630 ± 18.9 µg/g) and **hederagenin** (762 ± 70.10 µg/g) — triterpenoids; quillaic acid is the constituent the authors credit for the fruit-methanol extract's apoptosis induction.
- **(−)-Epigallocatechin gallate** — 758 ± 20.46 µg/g in fruit methanol.
- **Herniarin** (167 ± 1.50 µg/g, fruit dichloromethane) — a coumarin; relevant to the photosensitisation caution.
- **Polycyclic polyprenylated acylphloroglucinols (PPAPs)** — **androforin A** and **hyperandrone A**, both first described from this species, alongside twelve known compounds (Wang et al., 2012).
- **Benzophenone synthase** — *H. androsaemum* is the source organism for the crystallographically characterised benzoyl-CoA-specific type III polyketide synthase; the species is chemically distinguished by its **benzophenone** pathway (Stewart et al., 2017). This is a real, structural, species-level chemical difference from *H. perforatum*.
- **Low naphthodianthrone/flavonoid content** — see the deflation; *H. androsaemum* was found to contain **the lowest flavonoid amount** among thirty *Hypericum* taxa (0.13%) (Pilepić et al., 2013).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Tutsan is a **Consolidation-phase agent** with two legitimate registers, neither of which is psychoactive:

1. **Topical wound and skin work** — the berry-derived polar extracts have the best mechanistic support in this entry: fibroblast migration, collagenase inhibition, UVA/B photostability, and protection of red cells against peroxidation. This is a *skin-repair* profile, and it is the one register where the traditional claim and the laboratory result point the same way.
2. **The Iberian hepatobiliary/diuretic tea** — retained as a living tradition with modest, unmechanised support. This is a drainage register: downward, opening, channel-clearing.

**Tutsan is not a mood agent and has no place in Opening-phase or affective work.** Practitioners arriving at this entry expecting a St John's Wort analogue should read the deflation and then leave. The corpus keeps this entry partly as a **control case**: it is what a *Hypericum* looks like when you strip away the one species that made the genus famous.

### Scientific Correlation

*According to PubMed:*

**The wound / vulnerary doctrine — graded MODERATE (in vitro, mechanistically apt, no clinical data).**

- Antognoni F, Lianza M, Poli F, Buccioni M, Santinelli C, Caprioli G, Iannarelli R, Lupidi G, Damiani E, Beghelli D, Alunno A, Maggi F. "Polar extracts from the berry-like fruits of *Hypericum androsaemum* L. as a promising ingredient in skin care formulations." *J Ethnopharmacol* 2016;195:255-265. PMID 27864112. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2016.11.029) — **The key wound-doctrine paper**, and it explicitly frames itself against the tutsan ointment tradition. Shikimic and chlorogenic acids dominant; significant increase in **human fibroblast migration**; both extracts inhibited **collagenase** (aqueous IC50 88.1 µg/mL, uncompetitive kinetics, comparable to chlorogenic acid alone); methanolic extract modulated IL-6 in human PBMCs; both photostable across UVA/B; both protected erythrocytes from AAPH-induced peroxidation at low concentrations. **Grade: this is the strongest correlate in the entry.** Fibroblast migration is the correct cellular proxy for the traditional claim. It is still a dish, not a patient.

- Jabeur I, Tobaldini F, Martins N, Barros L, Martins I, Calhelha RC, Henriques M, Silva S, Achour L, Santos-Buelga C, Ferreira ICFR. "Bioactive properties and functional constituents of *Hypericum androsaemum* L.: A focus on the phenolic profile." *Food Res Int* 2016;89(Pt 1):422-431. PMID 28460934. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodres.2016.08.040) — lipid peroxidation inhibition, radical scavenging, nitric oxide inhibition; **anti-*Candida* and >90% anti-biofilm effect against *C. glabrata* and *C. tropicalis***; notably **no toxicity to non-tumour porcine liver cells above 400 µg/mL**. **Grade: MODERATE for the antimicrobial/anti-inflammatory side of the wound doctrine.** The anti-*Candida* result is a genuine, unanticipated support for a topical use the tradition never articulated in those terms. The liver-cell non-toxicity is a relevant reassurance for a plant drunk as a *liver* tea.

**The Iberian hepatobiliary / digestive tea — graded WEAK (species-specific effect real; active principle unidentified).**

- Xavier CPR, Lima CF, Fernandes-Ferreira M, Pereira-Wilson C. "*Hypericum androsaemum* water extract inhibits proliferation in human colorectal cancer cells through effects on MAP kinases and PI3K/Akt pathway." *Food Funct* 2012;3(8):844-52. PMID 22596086. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1039/c2fo10226a) — the paper that names the Portuguese digestive-tea tradition as its rationale. **Contrarily to *H. perforatum*, *H. androsaemum* significantly inhibited proliferation and induced apoptosis** in HCT15 and CO115 colon carcinoma lines; decreased BRAF and phospho-ERK in the BRAF-mutant line specifically; caspase-dependent apoptosis; S and G2/M arrest. **And the crucial honest finding: chlorogenic acid, the main phenolic of the extract, showed *none* of those effects on its own.** **Grade: WEAK but scientifically interesting.** It is a real species-specific effect — *androsaemum* did what *perforatum* did not — attached to an unknown compound, in cancer cell lines that are not what the tea is drunk for. It corroborates that the tea does *something* to gut tissue. It does not corroborate the liver claim at all.

Fruit-methanol cytotoxic at IC50 73.23 ± 3.06 µg/mL (PC-3) and 31.64 ± 2.75 µg/mL (Hep G2); 64.75% apoptosis induction in PC-3 at 50 µg/mL, credited to quillaic acid. **Grade: compositional data STRONG; the cytotoxicity is cell-line screening at concentrations with no established in-vivo relevance. - Wang K, Wang YY, Gao X, Chen XQ, Peng LY, Li Y, Xu G, Zhao QS. "Polycyclic polyprenylated acylphloroglucinols and cytotoxic constituents of *Hypericum androsaemum*." *Chem Biodivers* 2012;9(6):1213-20. PMID 22700239. "Molecular architectures of benzoic acid-specific type III polyketide synthases." *Acta Crystallogr D Struct Biol* 2017;73(Pt 12):1007-1019. PMID 29199980. **⚠️ THE CENTRAL DEFLATION — the antidepressant evidence is NOT this plant's.**

The entire clinical literature on *Hypericum* for depression belongs to ***H. perforatum***. Not one retrieved study tests *H. androsaemum* for mood, affect, or depression. Worse, the compositional data actively argue *against* transferring the claim:

- Napoli E, Siracusa L, Ruberto G, Carrubba A, Lazzara S, Speciale A, Cimino F, Saija A, Cristani M. "Phytochemical profiles, phototoxic and antioxidant properties of eleven *Hypericum* species — A comparative study." *Phytochemistry* 2018;152:162-173. PMID 29775867. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phytochem.2018.05.003) — eleven species compared by HPLC-DAD-MS for polyphenols, acylphloroglucinols and naphthodianthrones. The authors nominate *H. tetrapterum*, *H. pubescens* and *H. montanum* as species of interest **alongside** *H. perforatum*, and nominate *H. perfoliatum* and *H. tetrapterum* as the phototoxic candidates. ***H. androsaemum* is named in neither shortlist.**
- Pilepić KH, Males Z, Crkvencić M. "Quantitative analysis of total flavonoids and total phenolic acids in thirty *Hypericum* taxa." *Nat Prod Commun* 2013;8(3):347-9. PMID 23678809. — *H. perforatum* among the highest flavonoid taxa (1.5%) and highest in phenolic acids (10.4%); ***H. androsaemum* among the LOWEST flavonoid taxa at 0.13%*** — roughly an order of magnitude below *perforatum*.
- Brunáková K, Bálintová M, Henzelyová J, Kolarčik V, Kimáková A, Petijová L, Čellárová E. "Phytochemical profiling of several *Hypericum* species identified using genetic markers." *Phytochemistry* 2021;187:112742. PMID 33965834. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phytochem.2021.112742) — confirms **strong genetically predetermined interspecific variability** in phenolic content across sections including Androsaemum. Section membership predicts chemistry. *Androsaemum* is its own section, and it is chemically its own plant.

**Grade for the antidepressant claim as applied to tutsan: UNSUPPORTED — and the burden of the compositional evidence runs against it.** This is not "not yet studied." This is a plant in a different chemical section, with an order-of-magnitude lower flavonoid content, a distinct benzophenone pathway, and a documented failure to reproduce its cousin's behaviour in the one head-to-head assay retrieved (Xavier 2012, where *androsaemum* worked and *perforatum* did not — showing the two are genuinely non-interchangeable in *both* directions).

**Also deflated:** the "all-heal" name. *Toute-saine* is a marketing claim from the twelfth century. The plant's documented competence is narrow: skin repair, an unmechanised gut/liver tea, and some antimicrobial breadth. Nothing retrieved supports "heals everything," and the name should be read as advertising, not as data.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

| Axis | Classification | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Cool | Vulnerary/astringent assignment; the cooling of an inflamed breach |
| Moisture | Dry | Drying of the weeping wound; the astringent register |
| Taste | Astringent, faintly bitter, aromatic when dried (*Sweet-Amber*) | Catechins/epicatechin (astringent), triterpenoids (bitter), volatiles (the amber scent) |
| Direction | **Split** — centripetal/closing when applied outside; downward-draining when drunk inside | The Iberian tea and the *toute-saine* ointment move in opposite directions. The tradition never reconciled this |
| Tissue affinity | Skin and breached surface (topical); liver, gallbladder, urinary channel, gut (internal) | Vulnerary tradition + living Iberian folk practice |
| Signature | **"Man's blood"** — the red berry juice | Genus-level naphthodianthrone pigmentation. The signature is real; its inference is wrong |

### Contraindications

- **⚠️ Photosensitisation — genus-level caution.** *Hypericum* species are the archetypal photosensitising genus, and phototoxicity is documented as a species-variable property across the genus (Napoli et al., 2018, which found *H. perfoliatum* and *H. tetrapterum* the phototoxic candidates and assayed all eleven species by photocytotoxicity in NIH/3T3 fibroblasts). *H. androsaemum* berry extracts were separately found **photostable across UVA/B** (Antognoni et al., 2016) — a genuinely reassuring result for the topical use. **Nonetheless, treat photosensitisation as a live genus caution and avoid strong sun exposure during internal use.** The presence of herniarin (a coumarin) in the fruit is an additional, if minor, reason for that caution.
- **⚠️ CYP450 and P-glycoprotein induction — genus-level caution, unquantified for this species.** *Hypericum* is the genus whose clinical significance rests substantially on drug-interaction risk via enzyme and transporter induction. **No study retrieved here measured CYP or P-gp induction by *H. androsaemum*, and the well-characterised induction data belong to *H. perforatum* and its hyperforin content.** This entry therefore carries the caution *as a genus caution with an unmeasured species-level magnitude*: do not assume tutsan is safe with concurrent medication merely because the induction data name a different species [USER: verify — a species-specific CYP/P-gp study for *H. androsaemum*, if one exists, should be sourced and cited before this caution is softened or removed]. Of particular concern by class analogy: anticoagulants, hormonal contraceptives, immunosuppressants, antiretrovirals, and any narrow-therapeutic-index drug.
- **⚠️ Do not substitute for *H. perforatum*, and do not substitute *H. perforatum* for this.** They are chemically distinct, they behave differently in the one head-to-head assay available, and they carry different risk profiles. Species-level identification is mandatory. "Shrubby St John's Wort" as a common name for tutsan is an active hazard.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, paediatric use — no data. Avoid.**
- **No established human dose** for any preparation, including the Iberian tea [USER: verify].
- **Biliary obstruction / gallstones.** A tradition of hepatobiliary drainage is a contraindication in obstruction, by the tradition's own logic. Do not stimulate flow through a blocked channel.
- **Depression is not an indication for this plant.** Anyone self-treating a mood disorder with tutsan on the strength of the genus name is taking an unstudied plant for an unsupported indication while forgoing care. State this to any practitioner who reaches for it.
