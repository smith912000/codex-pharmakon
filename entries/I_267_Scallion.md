# I-267: Allium fistulosum (Scallion / Welsh Onion / Green Onion / Spring Onion)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-267 | Amaryllidaceae (formerly Alliaceae)**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Allium fistulosum* L.
**Family:** Amaryllidaceae

**Common Names:**
- English: Scallion, Welsh Onion, Green Onion, Spring Onion, Bunching Onion, Japanese Bunching Onion
- Mandarin: *Cōng* (葱) — the primary Chinese Allium; one of four essential seasonings in Chinese cuisine (alongside garlic, ginger, and Sichuan pepper)
- Japanese: *Negi* (ネギ)
- Korean: *Pa* (파)
- Hindi: *Hara pyaz* (green onion)
- Arabic: *Basal akhdar* (بصل أخضر) — green onion

**Naming Confusion — Critical Clarification:**
The English common names for *A. fistulosum* are among the most confused in botanical English:
- **Welsh onion:** The plant is NOT from Wales. "Welsh" derives from Old English *wælisc* meaning "foreign" — so "foreign onion" (same naming logic as the German *Welschkraut* for Italian herbs). *A. fistulosum* is native to China/Siberia.
- **Spring onion:** In British English, "spring onion" = immature *A. cepa* (bulbing onion before bulb forms) OR *A. fistulosum*; used interchangeably causing confusion
- **Scallion:** American English typically = *A. fistulosum* or young *A. cepa*; from the Latin *cepa Ascalonia* (onion of Ascalon/Ashkelon, a city in ancient Canaan)
- **Japanese bunching onion:** Most accurate common name; specifically indicates *A. fistulosum*

**Key Distinction from Bulbing Onion (I-262, *A. cepa*):**
*A. fistulosum* does NOT form a true bulb — it forms loose clusters of green hollow stems from a base. The "bunching" habit (multiple green stems from each cluster) is distinctive. It is perennial and regrows indefinitely from the base when cut.

**Morphology:**
Perennial; 30–60 cm; hollow cylindrical green leaves; thin white base (not a true bulb); multiple stems per clump; white globose flowers (2nd year). The entire plant (green tops and white base) is edible.

**Origin:** Siberia/Central China (naturalized throughout East Asia); not native to Europe. Cultivation in China extends ~3,000+ years; major crop in Japan, Korea, China, SE Asia.

**Parts Used:** Whole plant — green tops (higher Vitamin C, chlorophyll) and white base (higher organosulfur).

**Critical TCM Application — *Cōng Bái Tāng* (Scallion Bulb Decoction):**
*Cōng bái* (the white base of the scallion, 葱白) is one of the most immediately actionable herbs in Chinese medicine:
- **Indication:** Early wind-cold invasion; chills predominant; no sweating; early URI within first 24–48 hours
- **Mechanism:** Diaphoretic — promotes sweating to "release the exterior"; allicin + allyl compounds promote perspiration
- **Formula:** *Cōng Chǐ Tāng* (Scallion and Prepared Soybean Decoction): 3–5 white scallion bases + prepared soybeans (*Dòu Chǐ*); simmer 10–15 min; drink hot; rest under covers
- This is among the simplest Chinese herbal formulas and among the most widely used home remedies across Chinese culture

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domains:**
1. **Diaphoretic / exterior-releasing** — SBJ, FMT, SS: promotes sweating; clears early-stage respiratory infections; unique among Allium for this primary emphasis
2. **Anti-cold / respiratory** — Universal within accessible range: URI; cough; nasal congestion
3. **Warming digestive** — All traditions: carminative; warming Stomach Qi
4. **Antimicrobial** — Allium class: allicin; wound application; topical

**Unique Pharmacological Position:**
*A. fistulosum* holds a unique niche in the Allium pharmacological spectrum:
- **Primary diaphoretic** (diaphoretic = sweating promoter): no other common Allium is primarily categorized as diaphoretic in TCM; this is scallion's signature therapeutic role
- Garlic: primarily cardiovascular, antimicrobial
- Onion: primarily flavonoid/anti-inflammatory
- Leek: primarily diuretic, prebiotic
- Scallion: primarily diaphoretic (exterior-releasing), respiratory

The diaphoretic mechanism likely involves volatile organosulfur compounds absorbed systemically and excreted via sweat glands + skin capillary vasodilation (autonomic thermoregulatory mechanism mediated by allyl compounds).

**Energetic Classification:**
- ◉ Qi (functional): Primary — Lung Qi (exterior-releasing); respiratory-digestive Qi
- ☿ Shen (neuromodulatory): Minor — warming, revitalizing effect on mood/energy in cold presentations

**Primary Tier: ◉ Qi** — exterior-releasing Lung Qi; surface-level defensive Qi activation

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Organosulfur Compounds

**1. Alliin** — ~0.5–1.0% fresh weight; higher in white base than green tops
**2. Allicin** — formed on crushing; characteristic pungent odor; antimicrobial; diaphoretic (systemic volatilization)
**3. Methyl allyl trisulfide (MATS)** — volatile; excreted via skin + lungs
**4. Allyl mercaptan** — end-product of allicin metabolism; responsible for the characteristic perspiration odor in high-dose Allium consumption

### Volatile Diaphoretic Compounds

**5. Diallyl sulfide (DAS)** — volatile; absorbed; excreted via lungs and skin; promotes sweating via cutaneous vasodilation

### Flavonoids

**6. Quercetin-3,4'-diglucoside** — primary flavonoid
**7. Isorhamnetin glycosides** — anti-inflammatory

### Vitamins

**8. Vitamin C — 18.8 mg/100g** (modest; green tops higher than white base)
**9. Vitamin K₁ — 207 μg/100g** (comparable to chives; green tops concentrate K₁)
**10. Folate — 64 μg/100g**

### Other

**11. Polysaccharides (scallion mucilage)** — prebiotic activity; minor

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### 1. Diaphoretic (Primary Unique Mechanism)
- **Allicin + volatile sulfides:** Absorbed through GI tract → systemic circulation → excreted via sweat glands and lungs; increase surface perspiration
- **Cutaneous vasodilation:** Allyl sulfides stimulate cutaneous blood flow via NO pathway → increased heat loss from skin → sweating induction
- **Autonomic thermoregulation:** Warm scallion decoction (hot temperature) + volatiles → combined thermal + pharmacological diaphoretic synergy
- The mechanism explains why the formula is always taken **hot** and followed by **rest under warm covers** — the thermal input + pharmacological vasodilation together produce the sweating response that "releases the exterior"

### 2. Anti-Viral (URI) — Emerging Evidence
- Allicin: inhibits viral neuraminidase enzymes (influenza mechanism; lower potency than oseltamivir but relevant)
- Quercetin: broad anti-viral activity (inhibits viral replication; RNA polymerase inhibition)
- Thermal steam from hot scallion preparation: humidifies nasal passages; reduces viral adhesion

### 3. Antimicrobial (Standard Allium Class)
- Allicin thiol-reactive mechanism: same as garlic
- Lower concentration per gram than garlic but consumed in greater quantity (daily staple food)

### 4. Digestive Warming
- Volatile organosulfur: gastric motility stimulation; carminative; reduces GI Cold presentations (TCM)
- Warmth perception in GI: TRP channel activation (TRPV1, TRPA1) by allyl compounds → warming sensation

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

***Cōng* as the Daily Foundation of Chinese Cooking:**
Chinese culinary tradition has four foundational aromatics: *cōng* (scallion 葱), *jiāng* (ginger, I-256), *suàn* (garlic, I-261), and *jiǔ* (rice wine). Every Chinese kitchen maintains scallions continuously. The phenomenological experience of Chinese cooking is inseparable from the scallion's characteristic aroma — the moment scallion hits hot oil (葱爆, *cōng bào* — "scallion explosion") creates one of the most recognizable olfactory signatures in Chinese cuisine.

**The Hot Decoction Experience (Wind-Cold Remedy):**
The *Cōng Chǐ Tāng* experience — taken at the onset of a cold:
- Three scallion white bases crushed + prepared soybean decoction; taken very hot
- Immediately: pungent, warming, slightly bitter broth; intense heat spreading through throat and chest
- 20–30 minutes: warmth spreading to limbs; mild diaphoresis (sweating) begins; patient rests under blankets
- 1–2 hours: subjective feeling of "release" — the cold sensation lifting, the body less heavy, nasal congestion beginning to clear
- This phenomenological arc — pungent stimulus → warmth → sweat → release — is precisely what "releasing the exterior" means in TCM clinical language; the patient and the tradition use the same qualitative descriptors across 3,000 years

**Japanese Negi Culture:**
In Japan, *negi* (A. fistulosum) is similarly fundamental — ramen broth, soba, miso soup all feature *negi*; the grilled *Yakitori* (chicken skewers) always includes *negi* alternating with chicken (the fat from chicken + the allyl sulfides from *negi* create a synergistic flavor compound). Japanese folk medicine: green onion compresses (poulticed) applied to throat for *kazegoe* (cold-strained voice) — warm scallion wrapped in cloth on throat; both warmth and volatile organosulfur provide relief.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Dietary (primary):**
- 20–50 g daily: standard culinary use in East Asian cooking
- White base (cōng bái): higher alliin; more medicinal
- Green tops: higher Vitamin K₁, Vitamin C; more nutritional

**Wind-Cold Decoction (*Cōng Chǐ Tāng*):**
- 3–5 scallion white bases (7–10 cm sections) + 10–15 g prepared soybeans (*Dòu Chǐ*)
- Simmer in 300 mL water 10–15 minutes; strain; drink hot
- Rest under blankets; 1–2 servings in first 12–24 hours of wind-cold URI symptoms
- Repeat in 4–6 hours if symptoms persist
- Particularly effective at first sign of illness, before fever is established

**Topical (scallion compress):**
- Crushed scallion white base wrapped in cloth; applied warm to throat, chest, or affected area
- Volatile allicin provides local antimicrobial + warming vasodilatory effect

**Scallion oil (confit):**
- Scallions cooked slowly in oil until golden-soft (Chinese condiment *cōng yóu* 葱油)
- Preserves fat-soluble organosulfur compounds in oil medium; prolonged stability; daily condiment

### Storage
- Refrigerate 1–2 weeks; stand-upright in water (counter) for short term
- Hollow tubes can develop mold if kept too long; use fresh
- Roots can be placed in water on a windowsill to regrow indefinitely (cut-and-grow)

### Safety Profile

**Very safe at dietary doses.** One of the most widely consumed vegetables in East Asian diet.

**Drug Interactions:**
- **Anticoagulants:** Minor anti-platelet (organosulfur class); negligible at culinary doses
- **Warfarin:** Vitamin K₁ (207 μg/100g green tops) — same as chives (I-265); consistency of intake key

**Contraindications:**
- Allium family allergy
- Large doses in combination with diaphoretic pharmaceutical drugs: additive sweating; monitor in elderly

**Animal Toxicity:**
All *Allium* species: thiosulfate toxic to dogs, cats, horses. Human amounts safe.
