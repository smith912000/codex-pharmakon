# Tittiri flesh — Tittiri / Tittira (as printed in Bhishagratna's English, Sutrasthana ch.46 and Uttaratantra ch.39); "partridge" (list row)
## *The flesh of the Tittiri, a bird of the Vishkira group (birds that scatter their food before eating) in Sushruta's meat chapter. The list row glosses it "partridge"; the translation prints the Sanskrit name without a gloss* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The text is Bhishagratna's 1907 English *Sushruta Samhita*, Sutrasthana ch.46, "the Chapter which treats of food and drink (Annapana-Vidhi-madhyayam)" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt`). The list row is `_sources/sushruta.csv` line 155, "Tittiri (partridge)", with the source "Sushruta Samhita, Sutrasthana ch.46 (Annapanavidhi); Bhishagratna trans. vol.1". The gloss "partridge" is the row's; the translation prints no English word for the bird in ch.46. The chapter opens (lines 9-12) with the claim that food "determines the origin of beings and forms the chief source of their bodily strength and complexion, as well as of the albuminous (Ojas) principle in their organisms". That is said of food in general.

The meat section begins at line 385: "Now I shall describe the properties of the different species of edible meats". The high-ground (Janghala) animals are divided into eight sub-species (lines 403-410), and the Tittiri is placed in the second, the Vishkira. Lines 450-454: "Birds such as the Lava, Tittiri, Kapinjala, Vartira, Vartika, Vartaka, Naptrika, Vatika, Chakora, Kalavinka, Mayura, Krakara, Upachakra, Kukkuta, Saranga, Shata-Patraka, Kutittiri, Kuruvahuka and Yavalaka belong to the Vishkira species. [They are so called from the fact of their picking up their food after scattering it first with their bills and claws (Skr. kira, to scatter)]." The list also has a *Kutittiri*, a different name that the text does not explain.

The group's properties (lines 463-466): "The flesh of a bird of this group is light, cooling, sweet and astringent in taste and tends to pacify the deranged humours of the body." Then the Tittiri is described (lines 472-478): "The flesh of the Tittiri is slightly heavy, heat-making and sweet in taste. It is spermatopoietic, appetising and astringent. It improves the intellect and complexion, and subdues the three deranged humours. The flesh of the yellow (Gaura) Tittiri proves curative in hic-cough and dyspnoea, and subdues the deranged Vayu." So the Tittiri differs from its group in being "slightly heavy" and "heat-making" where the group is "light" and "cooling", and a yellow kind is named with its own action. Bhishagratna does not comment on either point.

The bird appears in other passages:

- *Sutrasthana* ch.46, lines 2161-2165, in the closing verses: "The flesh of the Lava, Tittiri, Saranga, Kuranga, Ena, Kapinjala, Mayura (peacock), Varmi and Kurma (tortoise) should be regarded as the best of those of all other animals respectively belonging to their own species." The Tittiri is among the nine named as best of their kind.
- *Sutrasthana* ch.20 (the effects of regimen), lines 43-56: the Tittiri is among the game and birds whose flesh "may be safely included within the food stuffs of all human beings". The same chapter's list of incompatible preparations (lines 176-183) says that "the flesh of a Kapinjala, Myura (peacock), Lava, Tittira, and Godha, boiled with castor oil and on a fire of the twigs of castor plants, should not be eaten".
- *Chikitsasthana* ch.39 (distressing symptoms), lines 237-242: a diet of rice, mudga pulse and "(the soup of the flesh of) an Ena, Lava, hare, peacock, Tittiri, or deer, and such other light food" for a patient "after the exhibition of emetics and purgatives".
- *Uttaratantra* ch.39 (the treatment of fever), lines 904-918, which both recommends and restricts it: meat of the Lava, Kapinjala and various deer, or hare, "may be prescribed as diet for a fever-patient accustomed to the use of animal food"; but "several authorities, however, do not recommend the use of the meat of Sarasa, Krouncha, Mayura (peacock), Kukkuta (cock) and of Tittira in cases of fever, owing to its heaviness (as regards digestion), as well as to its heat-making potency. (We, too, subscribe to this opinion with a certain limitation.) The use of the flesh of these animals may, however, be recommended in a case of fever in moderate quantity and at proper time provided the fever is marked by a preponderance of the deranged bodily Vayu." That is the text's own caution, and it matches the "slightly heavy, heat-making" description in ch.46.
- The translator's introduction (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Vol1_frontmatter.txt`, line 3095) says that the Vaidyas prescribe "Tittira's flesh in fever &c."; that is Bhishagratna's remark, not the text's.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, partridge flesh, like other lean game bird meat, is mainly water and protein with little fat and some iron. No more specific statement can be made without a source.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Sushruta treats the Tittiri's flesh as a food and a light diet for the sick, with stated effects on the *doshas*, and with a caution in fever. The Codex does not prescribe it. Wild-bird meat carries hygiene risks, the taking of wild game is regulated, and the bird is not securely identified beyond the list row's gloss. The entry's honest use is as a record of how the text classifies the meat.

### Preparation & Dosing

The texts give two uses of the flesh. A soup of the flesh is part of the light diet after emetics and purgatives (*Chikitsasthana* ch.39, line 240). Prepared with castor oil over a fire of castor twigs, it is named as an incompatible preparation (*Sutrasthana* ch.20, lines 179-183). No quantity is given, except that in fever the flesh may be used "in moderate quantity and at proper time" when *vayu* predominates (*Uttaratantra* ch.39, lines 915-918). ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. The *dosha* terms (*vayu*, *pittam*, *kapham*) and the qualities "heat-making" and "spermatopoietic" belong to the Ayurvedic frame and have no direct counterpart in modern physiology. Game birds are lean meat, and wild birds can carry infections that pass to people through handling and undercooked meat. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Ayurveda (SS):** From *Sutrasthana* ch.46 (lines 472-478), for the flesh of the Tittiri: taste, "sweet" and "astringent"; potency (*virya*), "heat-making"; quality, "slightly heavy"; actions, spermatopoietic, appetising, improving the intellect and complexion, and subduing "the three deranged humours". The yellow (Gaura) Tittiri: curative in hiccough and dyspnoea and subduing deranged *vayu*. No *vipaka* is printed for it. For its group, the Vishkira (lines 463-466): light, cooling, sweet and astringent, pacifying the humours. The two statements differ, as noted under Origin & Tradition.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The Tittiri's flesh is described in a food-and-drug chapter (*Sutrasthana* ch.46), which opens by saying that food is the chief source of strength, complexion and *ojas* in beings (lines 9-12). That is a statement about food in general. The Tittiri's own sentence calls it "spermatopoietic" and says that it "improves the intellect and complexion", which are properties listed for a food and not a placement in a *vajikarana* or *rasayana* chapter; they are not read as such a status. I searched the *Chikitsasthana* chapter files for the name: the only occurrence is the light diet after emetics and purgatives (ch.39, line 240), and the *Uttaratantra* fever chapter restricts the bird. The closing verses of ch.46 name it among the best of its kind as a food, and not as a medicine. No passage read says that it builds *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana*. The sattvic and rajasic food lists are *Samkhya*-era material and not Sushruta, and are not used.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The text records the partridge-like Tittiri as a sweet, astringent, slightly heavy and heat-making bird meat, ranked among the best of its kind for food, taken in the diet after purgation, and used with care in fever. The Codex does not recommend it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** wild-bird meat can carry bacteria, parasites and viruses (including avian influenza); thorough cooking is essential, and the Codex does not recommend sourcing it from the wild.
- **Ayurvedic caution:** in fever the text itself sets the flesh aside as heavy and heat-making, and permits it only "in moderate quantity and at proper time" where *vayu* predominates (*Uttaratantra* ch.39, lines 908-918); and it forbids the flesh boiled in castor oil on a castor-wood fire (*Sutrasthana* ch.20, lines 179-183).
- **Legal:** the taking and sale of wild game birds are regulated and seasonal in most places.
- **Ethics:** the Codex does not recommend taking an animal's life for these uses.
