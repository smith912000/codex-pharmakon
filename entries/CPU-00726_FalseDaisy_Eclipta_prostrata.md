# False Daisy

## *Eclipta prostrata* (L.) L. | Family: Asteraceae

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ, SS

**Energetic Classification:** Cool, sweet-sour, moistening; descending and consolidating. Ayurvedic *rasa* bitter/astringent, *virya* cooling, *vipaka* pungent, *kapha–pitta* reducing. TCM: sweet, sour, cool; Liver and Kidney channels.

**Training Phase:** Foundation — permitted from early practice as a food-adjacent nutritive and topical oil. Internal use at therapeutic dose is a Consolidation-phase decision.

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Eclipta prostrata* (syn. *E. alba* (L.) Hassk.) is a sprawling, weedy annual of wet ground — paddy bunds, ditch margins, irrigation channels — across the tropical and subtropical world. It is unimpressive: small white ray florets around a green-yellow disc, a lax prostrate habit, roots at the nodes. It is also, by a wide margin, one of the most heavily used medicinal plants in Asia, and it is the subject of one of the Codex's most interesting convergences.

**Ayurveda — भृङ्गराज (Bhṛṅgarāja), "king of the bees" / Kesharaja, "king of hair"**
Bhringraj is *the* classical Ayurvedic hair drug. The indication is not vague: it is said to **blacken the hair, thicken it, prevent its fall, and delay greying** — and it is the base of an entire genre of medicated oils (*Bhringraj taila*, *Neelibhringadi taila*) in which the herb's juice is repeatedly cooked into a sesame or coconut oil base. The second name, *Kesharaja*, states the indication outright. Ayurveda also classifies it as a *rasayana* (rejuvenative) and — importantly for what follows — as a principal **liver** herb, used for *kamala* (a jaundice-cluster), hepatic and splenic enlargement, and as a *deepana-pachana* digestive-kindling agent. It is *pitta*-cooling despite its bitter-pungent trajectory.

**Traditional Chinese Medicine — 旱莲草 (Hàn Lián Cǎo) / 墨旱莲 (Mò Hàn Lián), "ink drought-lotus"**
The Chinese name carries the plant's most distinctive physical sign: cut or bruise it and the sap oxidises to **black**. *Mò* means ink. Han Lian Cao is classified as sweet, sour, cool, entering Liver and Kidney. Its classical function is stated as: **nourishes Liver and Kidney yin; cools the blood and stops bleeding; and — explicitly — blackens the hair.** It is a standard component of *Er Zhi Wan* (Two Ultimates Pill), paired with *Ligustrum lucidum* (Nü Zhen Zi), for yin deficiency presenting with dizziness, tinnitus, low back and knee soreness, and **premature greying**. It is also used for haemorrhagic patterns from blood heat: haematuria, haemoptysis, uterine bleeding.

**Siddha / Tamil — கரிசாலாங்கண்ணி (Karisalankanni)**
The Siddha tradition holds the plant in unusually high regard, again with a hair emphasis and, characteristically, a strong liver emphasis — used for jaundice and as a general *kayakalpa* (body-rejuvenation) article. The name embeds *kari* (black/dark). The convergence on darkness is now three-for-three across naming conventions.

**Vernacular and wider Asian use**
Recorded across India, Nepal, Bangladesh and beyond for diseases of **skin, liver and stomach** — a triad noted explicitly in the Timalsina & Devkota (2021) review of the species (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/biom11111738)). In Vietnamese traditional practice it is used for hepatoprotection, wound healing, and "blood cooling"/bleeding disorders (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ph18111653)) — a description that could be lifted verbatim from a TCM materia medica, which tells you something about diffusion in the region.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

Here is what makes this entry worth writing.

Two large, textually independent medical systems — Ayurveda and TCM — arrived at the same weedy paddy-margin plant and assigned it the same *strange and highly specific* claim: **it blackens the hair.** Not "it is a tonic." Not "it strengthens." It darkens hair. And both systems, separately, also made it a **liver** drug. Ayurveda for *kamala* and hepatic enlargement; TCM for Liver-yin. Two claims, two traditions, one plant, and the pairing is the same in both.

Convergence of this kind is evidence of *something*. The question is what.

**The deflationary reading, which must be put first.** The plant's sap turns black on exposure to air. Its Chinese name is literally "ink." Its Tamil name embeds "black." Its Sanskrit name references a black bee. A juice that stains everything it touches black, rubbed into hair, will darken hair — mechanically, as a dye, immediately, and reversibly. This is a signature so glaring that both traditions could have arrived at "blackens the hair" independently *without either of them ever observing a single follicle change*. The doctrine of signatures does not require diffusion to produce convergence; it only requires that two cultures look at the same obvious visual fact. Two traditions agreeing here is much weaker evidence than two traditions agreeing about something invisible would be. **The hair-blackening claim is confounded at its root by the plant being a dye.** Any honest treatment must say so before saying anything else.

**Where the doctrine does have chemical purchase.** The plant's characteristic constituents are **coumestans** — wedelolactone and demethylwedelolactone — and coumestans are a recognised phytoestrogen class. Androgenetic hair loss is androgen-driven; hair follicle cycling is hormonally modulated; and oestrogenic signalling prolongs anagen. A coumestan-rich plant with genuine effects on the hair follicle is not a chemically absurd proposition, and the preclinical literature does report hair-growth-promoting activity for the species (Timalsina & Devkota 2021, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/biom11111738)). So the doctrine is not *empty* — it is **confounded**, which is a different and more interesting condition. The traditions may have been right for a reason they could not have known, having arrived at the belief for a reason that does not support it.

**Where the doctrine maps cleanly and deserves respect: the liver.** This is the underrated half of the entry. Both traditions made this a liver herb, and the liver claim is *not* signature-driven — nothing about a small white daisy says "liver." It had to come from observation. And the chemistry cooperates: wedelolactone is a genuinely well-characterised hepatoprotective coumestan with a defined mechanism (NF-κB suppression). The tradition's *unglamorous* claim is the one that survives scrutiny; its *famous* claim is the one that is compromised. That inversion is a recurring lesson in this Codex and this entry is a clean instance of it.

**The Liver–hair link, taken seriously.** TCM does not treat "nourishes Liver/Kidney yin" and "blackens the hair" as two claims. It treats the second as a *consequence* of the first: hair is the "surplus of the blood," the Kidney "governs the hair of the head," and greying is a yin-depletion sign. So the system is making a structural assertion — that hair colour is downstream of hepatic/renal substrate — and then selecting a plant that is genuinely hepatoactive. Ayurveda's pairing of *rasayana*, *kesha* and *yakrit* indications does something similar. The modern reading need not accept the metaphysics to notice that both systems independently linked hair status to hepatic status and then reached for a real hepatoprotectant. That is a more sophisticated convergence than the dye story, and it is the part worth preserving.

**Cultivator's note.** This is one of the easiest plants in the volume. It wants wet feet, full sun to part shade, and warmth; it roots at the nodes, so propagation is a matter of pressing a cut stem into mud. It self-seeds prolifically and behaves as a weed of rice culture — which is precisely why it was available to peasant medicine across two continents. Grow it in a saucer-standing pot or a boggy bed edge; harvest the whole aerial part at flowering. It is frost-tender. It is also a heavy-metal accumulator from wet ground — **do not harvest from roadside ditches, tannery outflows, or urban drainage**, which is where it grows best and most abundantly. This is the single most important practical warning in the entry and it is routinely ignored.

### Active Components

**Coumestans (the defining class).** **Wedelolactone (WEL)** and **demethylwedelolactone (DWEL)** are the marker compounds and carry most of the demonstrated hepatic activity. Lopes et al. (2022) used ¹³C feeding experiments in *E. prostrata* hairy root cultures to establish the biosynthesis: the coumestans WEL and DWEL derive from **combined acetate and shikimate pathways**, whereas the phenylpropanoid 3,5-di-O-caffeoylquinic acid comes exclusively from shikimate (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11240-022-02342-0)). Also reported: 1,3,8,9-tetrahydroxycoumestan 3-sulfate.

**Thiophenes — the Asteraceae signature.** *E. prostrata* produces an unusual richness of **bithiophenes and terthiophenes**. Giang et al. (2024) isolated one new bithiophene, 5-(but-3-en-1-yn-1-yl)-5′-(methoxymethyl)-2,2′-bithiophene, plus α-terthiophene and nine other thiophene derivatives alongside wedelolactone and demethylwedelolactone (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/10286020.2024.2364912)). Thiophenes are phototoxic photosensitisers as a class — relevant to a plant used in topical oils applied to a scalp that then goes outdoors. **[USER: verify]** whether any documented phototoxicity incident is attributable to Bhringraj oil; none was retrieved this run and it should be flagged as mechanistic concern, not as a reported event.

**Triterpenoid and steroid saponins.** The **ecliptasaponins / ecalbasaponins** (oleanane-type). Named as an active class in the species review ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/biom11111738)). Their contribution to the hair claim is asserted in secondary literature more often than it is demonstrated.

**Flavonoids and phenolic acids.** **Luteolin, apigenin, quercetin**, and **diosmin** — the last isolated from the species for the first time by Van Anh et al. (2025) (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ph18111653)) — plus caffeoylquinic acids including 3,5-diCQA.

**The black pigment.** The sap darkens on air exposure via polyphenol oxidase acting on the phenolic pool. **[USER: verify]** the specific pigment chemistry before asserting a named compound; the mechanism as stated here is a reasonable inference from the phenolic content, not a retrieved finding.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

*E. prostrata* enters Kenetica in three distinct capacities, which should not be confused with one another because their evidential standing differs enormously.

**1. Topical hair oil (Foundation phase, permitted, evidentially weak, low-risk).**
The traditional medicated oil is a low-stakes practice: a cooling scalp application with a plausible-but-unproven follicular rationale, a certain and immediate cosmetic darkening effect from the dye fraction, and the mechanical benefit of scalp massage in an oil base. Practitioners should use it *knowing* that the visible darkening is probably staining, and that any follicular effect is a preclinical hypothesis. Framed that way, it is honest, pleasant, and cheap. Framed as a hair-regrowth treatment, it is an overclaim.

**2. Hepatic support (Consolidation phase, internal, moderate evidence, real caution).**
This is the strongest indication and the one the traditions arguably got right for the right reasons. The mechanistic and animal data on wedelolactone are substantive (see below). The Kenetica use case is *adjunctive support in a metabolic-hepatic context* — never as a replacement for diagnosis, never in the presence of an undiagnosed jaundice, and never as a licence to continue a hepatotoxic exposure. A hepatoprotectant is not a permit.

**3. Cooling/haemostatic (traditional, unassessed here).**
The TCM blood-cooling and bleeding indications are the least investigated of the plant's uses in the literature retrieved this run. **Data-sparse.** Recorded, not endorsed.

**The Kenetica teaching point.** This entry belongs in the curriculum as the counterweight to the Codex's hazard entries. Where *Echium plantagineum* shows a tradition failing because feedback was absent, *Eclipta* shows a tradition succeeding on a claim it could not verify (liver) while being led astray on a claim it could see (hair). **The visibility of an effect is inversely related to the reliability of the doctrine attached to it.** A tradition's confident, famous, easily-demonstrated claim deserves *more* scepticism than its quiet, unglamorous one.

### Scientific Correlation

**Graded honestly. Attribution: all findings below are from PubMed-retrieved articles; DOIs linked.**

**MODERATE-TO-STRONG (preclinical, in vivo) — hepatoprotection.** This is the best-supported claim in the entry.
- Luo et al. (2018): wedelolactone pretreatment in the Concanavalin A-induced hepatitis mouse model markedly reduced serum transaminases and liver damage severity; attenuated TNF-α, IFN-γ and IL-6 rises; lowered hepatic CXCL10 and ICAM1 expression; reduced leukocyte infiltration and T-cell activation; mechanism traced to NF-κB suppression via limited IκBα and p65 phosphorylation ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1142/S0192415X1850043X)). A defined mechanism in a standard immune-hepatitis model.
- Chen et al. (2023): WEL and DWEL in a thioacetamide-induced zebrafish liver injury/NAFLD model significantly improved liver function and reduced hepatic fat accumulation; integrated spatial metabolomics + liver-specific transcriptomics attributed WEL's effect (but **not** DWEL's) principally to steroid biosynthesis and fatty acid metabolism ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpha.2023.11.017)). Note the useful negative-within-positive: the two closely related coumestans do **not** work the same way.
- Giang et al. (2024): among 13 isolated compounds tested for protection of HepG2 cells, the most active reached only **38.68–48.54% cell viability at 100 μM** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/10286020.2024.2364912)). Read this soberly — it is a modest in vitro result at a high concentration, and it tempers rather than reinforces the enthusiasm of the in vivo work. Bithiophenes outperformed terthiophenes.
- **The gap: no human hepatic trial was retrieved this run.** Two animal models and one cell assay do not make a clinical indication.

**MODERATE (preclinical) — anti-inflammatory.** Van Anh et al. (2025) isolated 13 compounds from Vietnamese material and showed **wedelolactone, luteolin, apigenin and quercetin significantly inhibited TNF-α and IL-6** in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages; network pharmacology identified TNF, IL6, AKT1, NFKB1, EGFR and PTGS2 as hub targets with TNF and IL-17 pathway enrichment ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ph18111653)). Caveat: three of the four active compounds (luteolin, apigenin, quercetin) are ubiquitous flavonoids that do this in almost any plant extract — so this result supports the *extract* but says little that is **specific to this species**. Wedelolactone is the exception and the one that matters.

**WEAK — hair growth. This is the honest grade and it should disappoint.**
- Timalsina & Devkota (2021) list **hair growth promoting activity** among the species' reported biological activities, alongside antimicrobial, anticancer, hepatoprotective and neuroprotective ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/biom11111738)) — and the same activity list is echoed by Lopes et al. (2022) ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11240-022-02342-0)). But note what these are: **review-level assertions**, not primary trials retrieved here.
- **No primary hair-growth study — animal or human — was retrieved in this run.** A targeted PubMed search for *Eclipta prostrata/alba* + hair growth returned a single record, and that record is a general phytochemistry review.
- **There is no controlled human hair trial supporting Bhringraj.** None was retrieved and the reviewer literature does not claim one.
- The dye confound (above) means that even a positive uncontrolled human observation would be near-worthless, because the plant *visibly darkens hair without doing anything to it*. Any future trial must control for staining — e.g. by measuring follicle counts and anagen ratio, not perceived colour.

Verdict: the two traditions' most famous, most convergent claim is supported by review-level mentions of preclinical work and by a mechanism that is chemically *plausible* (coumestan phytoestrogens, androgen-driven alopecia) — and by nothing else. **The convergence is impressive and it is not evidence.** Both traditions had access to the same black sap.

**UNASSESSED / DATA-SPARSE.** Blood-cooling and haemostatic (TCM); *rasayana*/anti-ageing; premature greying as distinct from hair growth; the *kamala* indication as distinct from generic hepatoprotection; digestive (*deepana-pachana*). No relevant primary data retrieved. Recorded, not endorsed.

**Congener warning.** Wedelolactone is *not* unique to this genus — it occurs in *Wedelia* (whence its name) and elsewhere. **Any wedelolactone finding sourced from *Wedelia* or another producer must not be read as *Eclipta prostrata* data.** Every wedelolactone study cited above is explicitly framed by its authors around *Eclipta prostrata*; that is why they are here.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Ayurveda:** *Rasa* — tikta (bitter), kashaya (astringent). *Guna* — laghu (light), ruksha (dry). *Virya* — ushna in some sources, cooling in effect on *pitta* in others (**the sources genuinely conflict; [USER: verify] against a primary nighantu**). *Vipaka* — katu (pungent). *Doshaghnata* — kapha–pitta hara. *Karma* — kesya (hair-benefiting), rasayana, yakrit-uttejaka (liver-stimulant), deepana-pachana.

**TCM:** Sweet (甘), sour (酸), cool (凉). Channels: Liver (肝), Kidney (肾). Functions: nourishes Liver and Kidney yin; cools blood, stops bleeding; blackens hair. Category: yin tonic / blood-cooling haemostatic.

**Kenetica synthesis.** Both systems classify the plant as **cool, moistening, and consolidating** — a substrate-builder rather than a stimulant, acting on depth rather than surface, on the slow tissues (hair, liver, marrow-analogues) rather than the fast ones. Both route it through the liver. Both assign it to the *replenishing* rather than the *dispersing* end of their taxonomies, which is unusual for an Asteraceae weed and is a real point of agreement not explicable by signature.

Kenetica classifies *Eclipta prostrata* as a **Substrate Herb**: acting on the body's slowly-turning-over compartments, requiring long timescales to demonstrate anything, and — critically — offering a *visible short-term cosmetic effect that mimics its claimed long-term structural effect*. This last property is what makes it beloved and what makes it untestable by ordinary experience. **Filed additionally under *Confounded Signatures*:** substances where a doctrine's apparent empirical confirmation is generated by an unrelated mechanism.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy — avoid. This is the entry's principal flag.** *E. prostrata* is coumestan-rich, and coumestans are phytoestrogens; the plant additionally carries traditional emmenagogue associations across South Asian usage. **[USER: verify]** — no primary reproductive-toxicity study for this species was retrieved this run, so this is a precautionary contraindication grounded in compound class and traditional caution rather than in retrieved data. That is exactly why it is stated as a prohibition rather than a caution: the absence of safety data in a phytoestrogenic plant is not reassurance.
- **Lactation — avoid** internally, on the same precautionary basis. **[USER: verify]**
- **Hormone-sensitive conditions** (oestrogen-receptor-positive breast cancer, endometriosis, uterine fibroids) — avoid internal use. Coumestan class effect, precautionary. **[USER: verify]**
- **Photosensitivity — a real and under-discussed concern.** The plant's thiophenes and terthiophenes are a phototoxic compound class ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/10286020.2024.2364912) documents their abundance). A thiophene-rich oil left on a scalp before sun exposure is a mechanistically coherent phototoxicity risk. Recommend washing out before sun. **[USER: verify]** — mechanism-based inference; no incident report retrieved.
- **Asteraceae sensitivity** — cross-reactivity risk with ragweed, chrysanthemum, chamomile, feverfew. Patch-test topical oils.
- **Undiagnosed jaundice — do not self-treat.** Jaundice has causes that a hepatoprotectant does not address and can mask. The traditional *kamala* indication predates the possibility of differential diagnosis; the practitioner does not have that excuse.
- **Heavy metal accumulation — the sourcing hazard.** This plant thrives in exactly the wet, disturbed, often contaminated ground where you should not harvest food. Wild-harvest from paddy margins downstream of agriculture, from urban drains, or from industrial outflow is a genuine exposure route for cadmium, lead and arsenic. **Grow it or source it from tested cultivation. [USER: verify]** whether species-specific accumulation data exist; none was retrieved this run, and the warning here is generic to wetland weeds.
- **Anticoagulant/antiplatelet co-use** — the TCM haemostatic claim and the coumestan structure (coumestans are structurally coumarin-adjacent, though *not* vitamin-K antagonists) create a theoretical interaction in both directions. **[USER: verify]** — no interaction data retrieved. Flagged for awareness, not established.
- **Hepatic drug metabolism** — a plant with demonstrated effects on hepatic steroid and fatty-acid pathways ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpha.2023.11.017)) is a plausible source of pharmacokinetic interaction in polypharmacy. **[USER: verify]**
- **Children** — topical oil is traditionally used broadly, including in children. Internal therapeutic dosing in children: no basis retrieved; do not extrapolate.
- **Staining** — trivial but real: the sap permanently marks fabric, porcelain and pale hair. Not a safety issue; a compliance one.
