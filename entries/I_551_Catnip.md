**The Catnip Response — Nepetalactone:**
The feline behavioral response to catnip (rolling, rubbing, vocalizing, euphoria) is pharmacologically distinct from the herb's human therapeutic applications:
- **Compound:** Nepetalactone (cis-trans and trans-cis isomers of 4a α,7α,7aβ-nepetalactone)
- **Mechanism in cats:** TRPA1 receptor agonism in the nasal mucosa → activation of the µ-opioid reward pathway → euphoria; domestic cats + lions + jaguars + leopards respond; tigers do not; effect is genetic (dominant allele; ~50-70% of domestic cats respond)
- **Human mechanism:** Completely DIFFERENT — nepetalactone has sedative/anxiolytic properties in humans through GABA-A modulation; does NOT cause opioid-pathway euphoria in humans at therapeutic doses
- **Insect repellent:** Nepetalactone is approximately 10× more effective than DEET (N,N-Diethyl-meta-toluamide) as a mosquito repellent in some studies — a pharmacological application with growing research interest

**Botanical Identification:**
Perennial herb, 40-100 cm; leaves broadly ovate-cordate, grey-green, densely white-woolly below, strongly aromatic; white or pale lavender flowers with purple spots; native to Europe, Central Asia, and the Middle East; widely naturalized in North America (introduced by European settlers).

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Sedative/Anxiolytic (Nepetalactone in Humans):**
Nepetalactone → GABA-A receptor positive allosteric modulation (mechanism similar to other Lamiaceae monoterpenes; not via BZD-site); reduced neuronal excitability → sedation and anxiolysis. Animal studies (mice): nepetalactone at 25-50 mg/kg produced significant sedation in open-field test; potentiated pentobarbital sleep time (GABA-A mechanism confirmed by picrotoxin reversal).

**Diaphoretic:**
Hot nepetalactone vapors + hot liquid intake → peripheral vasodilation → sweat gland activation → diaphoresis; same mechanism as boneset (I-537) diaphoretic action; traditional fever management use.

**Antispasmodic (GI):**
Nepetalactone + flavonoids → GI smooth muscle relaxation; antispasmodic in infant colic; mechanism: Ca²⁺ channel modulation (weak) + direct smooth muscle relaxation.

**Insect Repellent:**
Nepetalactone → TRPA1 activation in mosquitoes → aversive response; repels *Aedes aegypti* (dengue/yellow fever), *Culex pipiens* (West Nile virus), and *Anopheles* spp. (malaria) at concentrations comparable to or exceeding DEET. Mechanism: mosquito TRPA1 (expressed in olfactory neurons) is activated by nepetalactone → noxious chemical detection signal → avoidance behavior.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Overall Evidence Level: C/D** — traditional; in vitro/animal for insect repellent; no RCTs.

**Level D — Insect Repellent:**
- Bernier UR et al. (2005, *Journal of Medical Entomology*): Nepetalactone from catnip EO repelled *A. aegypti* mosquitoes in wind tunnel assay; 10× more effective than DEET at equivalent airspace concentration. Mechanism via TRPA1.
- Zhu JJ et al. (2011): Catnip EO field effectiveness against blood-feeding insects; promising results but duration shorter than DEET formulations.

**Level C — Pediatric Colic:**
- Catnip + fennel + chamomile combination (similar to I-520 chamomile infant colic data): traditional household remedy for infant colic; limited controlled data; historical record consistent across European and North American household medicine.

**Commission E:** Not evaluated. No EMA/HMPC monograph for catnip specifically.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The Cat Response and Human Medicine:**
The dramatic, unmistakable feline response to catnip has given the plant an outsized cultural prominence compared to its modest human pharmacological applications. The cat's rolling, rubbing, and apparent ecstasy with catnip was observed by humans for millennia before the nepetalactone-TRPA1-µ-opioid mechanism was elucidated in 2021. The herb's association with pleasure and intoxication in cats colored human perception — catnip was always suspected to have psychoactive properties in humans despite the pharmacological reality that the human experience is sedative, not euphoriant.

**Diaphoretic Fevers — American Household Medicine:**
Catnip tea was a staple of 18th and 19th-century American household medicine for infant fever and colic. It occupied the same ecological niche as boneset (I-537) for adults — a hot-infusion diaphoretic given to induce sweating in febrile states. The specific use for INFANTS (who cannot receive aspirin due to Reye's syndrome risk, and for whom pharmaceutical fever management is complicated in early life) gave catnip lasting utility in household practice. Catnip tea remains in use in rural American communities as an infant colic and fever remedy.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Indications (Traditional):**
- Infant colic and digestive discomfort (mild antispasmodic; primary modern indication)
- Fever management in children (diaphoretic)
- Anxiety / sleep disorders in adults (mild sedative)
- Insect repellent (topical EO, emerging application)

**Preparation:**
- Infant colic: 1 tsp dried herb in 150 mL water; steep 5 min; cooled; small amounts (5-15 mL) given as needed
- Adult anxiety/sleep: 2-3 g dried herb as infusion; bedtime dose
- Insect repellent: diluted EO in carrier (5-10% nepetalactone) applied topically; shorter duration than DEET

**Contraindications:**
- Pregnancy: emmenagogue; AVOID therapeutic doses
- Infants <6 months: introduce cautiously; start with very small amounts

**Drug Interactions:**
- Sedatives: mild additive effect; caution with CNS depressants
