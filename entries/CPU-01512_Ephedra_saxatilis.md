## *Ephedra saxatilis Royle, the first of three binomials Clark 1995 prints for mtshe.ldum (with Ephedra intermedia and Ephedra sinica Stapf), and the one the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts in this row.** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this Tibetan name. Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. *Mtshe.ldum* is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark prints three *Ephedra* binomials for it, and the row takes the first. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and the three are not shown to be one species. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed names (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 (lines 1100 to 1103).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads: "mtshe.ldum: Ephedra saxatilis Royle: Ephedra intermedia: Ephedra sinica Stapf.: [has two names and is a type of jointed grass growing in rocky areas. It is green with red flowers and rounded petals and grows about fifteen centimeters high. The lug.mtshe type is seed-bearing, whilst ra.mtshe has no seed]. Ephedra [has bitter taste and] stops bleeding from the veins and arteries, cures liver fever, [is an expectorant, disperses tumors, reduces swellings and is a recommended ingredient in essence-extraction compounds]." A printed page number, 109, falls inside the entry at line 1101.

The square brackets matter. Clark's preface (line 82) says "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". So the part of the entry outside the brackets, that *Ephedra* "stops bleeding from the veins and arteries" and "cures liver fever", is the root-text layer as Clark gives it. The description, the bitter taste, the expectorant action, the dispersal of tumours, the reduction of swellings and the essence-extraction role are in the bracketed commentary layer. The plant described is a green, red-flowered "jointed grass" about fifteen centimetres high in rocky places, with two names, *lug.mtshe* (seed-bearing) and *ra.mtshe* (without seed). Clark's name index (line 1583) also prints *Ephedra intermedia*, *E. saxatilis* and *E. sinica* with no further information.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (Explanatory Tantra, glossary).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The materia medica summary of chapter 20 says, at line 3468: "Tshe dhum stops bleeding and cures hot disorder of the liver." This is the only statement of use there, and it follows the same two actions Clark gives outside his brackets. The text calls the disorder "hot", where Clark says "liver fever". The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate blocks. The phonetic name "Tshe dhum" is at line 5910, the Tibetan "mtshe ldum" at line 5914, the scientific name "Ephedra saxatilis" at line 5989 and the English names "Little dragon mugword Ephedra" at line 5992 (the printed "mugword" is an apparent misspelling; the neighbouring "Artemesia dracunculus" belongs by position to the entry before it, *Tshar bong*). The pairing is by position and was not printed in one row, and the same sequence of names (red and white sandalwood, *Tshar bong*, *Tshe dhum*) runs in both columns. ### Active Components

Plants of the genus *Ephedra* are jointed, nearly leafless shrubs. Some species, notably the Chinese *E. sinica*, contain ephedrine-type alkaloids, which are well-known stimulants of the sympathetic nervous system. Clark names three species for this drug and the alkaloid content of *E. saxatilis* was not read, so nothing is stated here about this plant's composition. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a bitter jointed plant used to stop bleeding, to treat "liver fever" or "hot disorder of the liver" and, in the commentary layer, to loosen phlegm and shrink swellings. The Codex gives no protocol. Ephedrine-containing plants are controlled or restricted as supplements in many places and carry real cardiovascular risk, and the Tibetan drug's identity is itself uncertain.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation and no quantity for *mtshe.ldum*. Clark gives only the plant's description, taste and effects, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text only its two actions. The one pharmaceutical phrase is that it is a "recommended ingredient in essence-extraction compounds", with no compound named. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identity of the Tibetan drug is uncertain across three species, so a modern study of one *Ephedra* would not bear directly on it. It is a general, well-established fact that ephedrine raises heart rate and blood pressure.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(a) Attested, in the commentary layer.* The bracketed text printed by Clark at line 1103 says that *Ephedra* "is a recommended ingredient in essence-extraction compounds", that is, in *bcud len*, which the cultivation spec places inside the Four Tantras. Clark's preface (line 82) says the square brackets are taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, so this is a later layer of the tradition than the root text, whose own words (stops bleeding, cures liver fever) say nothing of it. Clark's preface (lines 69 to 70) says that essence-extraction compounds, "apart from their invigorating qualities enable meditators and yogis to live without intake of gross food and thus engage in spiritual practices with enhanced mental clarity, sharpness and pliancy". That passage names other substances as examples (sundew, angelica, *Polygonatum*, salep, certain flowers, calcite and mineral exudate) and does not name *Ephedra*, so it is the setting and not a second attestation. The cultivation spec adds that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. If the commentary layer is held not to count as the tradition's own status, this paragraph falls to (b). [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** The only cultivation role the sources give is as one ingredient of compounds made by specialists, with none named and no dose. Nothing here supports a cultivator taking *Ephedra* alone for that purpose, and a stimulant-bearing shrub of uncertain identity is a poor candidate. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. Ephedrine-type alkaloids can raise blood pressure and heart rate, and *Ephedra* products are restricted in many countries. Bleeding and liver disease need medical care and must not be left to a home preparation. The botanical identity is uncertain (three species in Clark), so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
