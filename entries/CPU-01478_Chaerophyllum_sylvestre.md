# Chaerophyllum sylvestre — Murris (Dioscorides 4-116)

## *Chaerophyllum sylvestre (the translators' suggestion; "Wild Chervil, Cow Parsley, Cow Weed")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Overlap and identity doubt.** The same SUGGESTED line prints a run of names for one plant: "Scandix [Pliny], Myrrhis, Cicutaria [Fuchs], Myrrhis sylvestris seminibus laevibus [Bauhin], Anthriscus sylvestris [in Sprague], Chaerophyllum sylvestre [Linnaeus] — Wild Chervil, Cow Parsley, Cow Weed", then "[other usage] Myrrhis odorata, Scandix odorata, Chaerophyllum odoratum — Myrrh, Sweet Cicely, British Myrrh, Sweet Fern", then "see 2-168". Chapter 2-168 (Skandux) is already on the spine as I_1005 *Anthriscus sylvestris*, whose entry reports 4-116 only as the translators' cross-reference. The two rows therefore stand on one gloss line, with the English name "Wild Chervil, Cow Parsley, Cow Weed" shared by both, and this row may duplicate I_1005 in all but chapter. The text read does not decide it. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-116. MURRIS* at line 9072, SUGGESTED lines 9073-9077, text at line 9078.

The chapter reads: "Myrrhis is similar to hemlock in its stalk and leaves, but it has a long root — tender, round, sweetsmelling and pleasant to eat. A decoction (taken as a drink with wine) helps those bitten by harvest spiders, and it purges out the menstrual flow and afterbirth. Boiled in liquid (to be sipped) it is given for pulmonary consumption. Some say that it is a prophylactic against infection (taken as a drink with wine, twice or three times a day) in pestilential seasons. It is also called conila, or myrrha."

Dioscorides thus compares the plant with hemlock in stalk and leaf and singles out the root as the useful part: tender, round, sweet-smelling, pleasant to eat. He gives four uses: for the bite of "harvest spiders" (the translators' wording), for expelling menses and afterbirth, for pulmonary consumption, and, on others' report ("some say"), as a drink against infection in pestilential seasons. Two alternative names are printed, conila and myrrha. The chapter heading sits in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"). The neighbouring chapters are 4-115 Muriophullon (heading at line 9058, not read for this entry) and 4-117 Muagros, glossed *Camelina sativa*, an unrelated plant. The earlier heading *1-116. MURIKE, MURRIS* (line 4395) carries a similar name but is a different chapter and was not read for this entry.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. The wild chervil group belongs to the carrot family (Apiaceae), in which edible herbs and deadly poisonous ones, hemlock among them, can closely resemble each other. Dioscorides himself likens this plant to hemlock. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a sweet root drunk in wine for spider bites, for bringing on the menses and afterbirth, for consumption and, on hearsay, against pestilential infection. These are the text's claims. The preparation is also a historical emmenagogue, and the Codex does not recommend foraging or using the plant: the identification is the translators', with sweet cicely as an alternative, and carrot-family look-alikes can be fatal.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-116 gives a decoction "taken as a drink with wine", a root "boiled in liquid (to be sipped)" and, for the prophylactic use, a drink with wine "twice or three times a day" in pestilential seasons. No amount of root or volume of liquid is given in the chapter. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No study of the plant was opened. There is no established treatment effect of a wild chervil root against spider bite, consumption or infection that is known to this entry. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints "tender, round, sweetsmelling" for the root and no warming, cooling or other quality.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention the spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant (hemlock-like stalk and leaf, long tender round root), what its root smells and tastes like, what it is called, and what is claimed for it, one claim being explicitly hearsay ("some say"). It assigns the plant no status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The text's own comparison with hemlock is the practical lesson: do not use any wild carrot-family root without certain identification.

### Contraindications

Wild carrot-family plants are easily confused with poison hemlock and other deadly relatives. A preparation that brings on the menses and afterbirth is an abortifacient risk and must not be used in pregnancy. Do not forage from this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
