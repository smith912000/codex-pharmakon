# Arabian Jasmine

## *Jasminum sambac* (L.) Aiton | Family: Oleaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM, SBJ, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Cooling · Moistening-to-Drying (paradoxical; scent cools, topical flower dries) · Relaxing to the animal spirits
**Training Phase:** Calmative & Aromatic — Phase II (Nervine / Sensory Regulation)

---

### Origin & Tradition

Arabian jasmine — *mogra* in the Indian subcontinent, *sampaguita* in the Philippines, *mo li hua* (茉莉花) in China, *full/yasmin* in the Arab world — is a small evergreen shrub of the olive family whose intensely fragrant white flowers open at dusk. It must be distinguished at the outset from **common jasmine, *Jasminum officinale*** (poet's jasmine, the classic *yasmīn* of Persian gardens and the source of much commercial "jasmine absolute"). The two share the folk pharmacology of scent but differ botanically and in volatile profile; entries and studies that say only "jasmine oil" frequently blur them, and that ambiguity is flagged wherever it bears on a claim below.

**Ebers / Egyptian stream (EP):** Fragrant white flowers of the *yasmin* type circulated through Egyptian and wider Near-Eastern perfumery and unguent-making, where aromatic oils were pressed into service as calmatives, sleep aids and components of scented fat cones. The specific attribution of *J. sambac* (an Asian native) to pharaonic Egypt is better read as the later Arab-Egyptian materia medica than as a dynastic-period drug; treat the deep-Egyptian claim as **[USER: verify]** and rely on the Islamic-Egyptian continuity, where jasmine (*full*) is a documented perfume and nervine.

**Assyrian / cuneiform stream (AT):** Aromatic flowers and scented oils appear in Mesopotamian fumigation and unguent practice, but a clean cuneiform identification of *J. sambac* specifically is not something this entry can source. **[USER: verify]** — retain as a tradition tag pending a philological citation; do not assert a native Akkadian name.

**Dioscorides / Greek stream (DMM):** Dioscorides describes *iasme / oleum sampsuchinum* and Persian "jasmine oil" (* oleum jasminum*) as a warming perfumed oil used by the Persians in banquets and, medicinally, as a relaxant and emmenagogue-adjacent unguent — noting, tellingly, that its heavy sweetness "oppresses the head," i.e. it was recognised as a sedative-to-narcotic *scent*. This is the classical seed of the sedative-scent doctrine.

**Avicenna / Unani stream (CM):** In the *Canon*, jasmine (*yāsamīn*) is classed as warming and its oil (*duhn al-yāsamīn*) used to relax the nerves, relieve cold headaches, and as an aphrodisiac and "gladdening" agent — the *mufarriḥ* (exhilarant) category. Unani practice also carries the topical folk use of the fresh flowers.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The governing doctrine across all four streams is that jasmine's power *is its scent* — a volatile, near-immaterial virtue that acts on the *animal spirits* / *rūḥ* (the pneuma of the nervous system) rather than through gross matter. Classical writers split on temperament: Greek and Unani authors called the oil *warming* (it excites, gladdens, is aphrodisiac), yet simultaneously *sedating* ("oppresses the head," induces sleep). This is not incoherence — it maps cleanly onto modern pharmacology. The characteristic jasmine volatiles are **linalool, benzyl acetate, benzyl benzoate, methyl jasmonate, indole and *cis*-jasmone**. Inhaled linalool is a genuine CNS-active anxiolytic/sedative acting on autonomic tone; the same molecular signal read as "gladdening exhilarant" (mood elevation) and "sleep-bringer" (parasympathetic shift) is exactly the biphasic, dose-and-context-dependent behaviour of a low-dose olfactory anxiolytic. The doctrine of a virtue that "rises to the head" through smell is, in this one case, a fair pre-modern description of transnasal/olfactory neuroactivity — the pneuma *is* the volatile terpene reaching the CNS.

A second, entirely separate folk doctrine attaches to the **fresh flower applied to the body**: garlands of jasmine bound over the breasts of women who wished to *stop* milk after weaning or stillbirth. Here the doctrine is not "scent to the spirits" but a cooling, drying, astringent topical action on the glandular flesh — and this is the claim modern data most sharply illuminates.

### Active Components

- **Linalool** (esp. (R)-(−)-linalool) — monoterpene alcohol; the principal CNS-active volatile.
- **Benzyl acetate, benzyl benzoate, benzyl alcohol** — sweet floral esters, major odour contributors.
- **Methyl jasmonate, *cis*-jasmone, jasmine lactone** — signature jasmonoid volatiles.
- **Indole** — the heavy, "narcotic" facet of the scent at high concentration.
- **Non-volatile fraction:** secoiridoid glycosides (sambacins/sambacosides, oleuropein-type — an Oleaceae hallmark), flavonoids (rutin, quercetin glycosides), phenolics. The 2025 review catalogues **>200 phytochemicals**, dominated by terpenes and aromatic compounds.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Kenetica uses *J. sambac* in two clearly separated registers, and conflating them is the classic error.

1. **As an olfactory calmative (Phase II nervine).** The flower or a genuine jasmine volatile, inhaled at *low, pleasant* intensity, is deployed for pre-sleep settling, acute anxiety, and mood lift without sedation heavy enough to impair — a scent-level intervention, not an ingested drug. This is the register with the best mechanistic and human evidence.

2. **As a topical lactation suppressant (folk-clinical).** Fresh crushed flowers bound over the breasts during weaning, mastitis-avoidance, or after fetal loss. This is a real, studied use — *not* to be confused with galactagogue herbs; jasmine here does the opposite of fenugreek.

Traditional internal/aphrodisiac ("gladdening") use is retained as classical context but graded weakest for want of controlled human data.

### Scientific Correlation

*According to PubMed:*

- **Lactation suppression — GRADE: MODERATE (one real comparative clinical trial, old, single-centre).** Shrivastav et al. (1988) directly compared jasmine flowers (*J. sambac*) applied to the breasts against bromocriptine for suppressing puerperal lactation. Both lowered serum prolactin (bromocriptine more so), but on *clinical* endpoints — breast engorgement, milk production, analgesic use — the two were **equally effective**, and jasmine avoided the rebound lactation seen in some bromocriptine patients. The authors concluded jasmine is an effective, inexpensive alternative. This is a genuine positive human result and vindicates the specific folk doctrine of the topical drying flower. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1479-828x.1988.tb01614.x) (PMID 3214386). *Caveat: n-limited, 1988, not replicated at scale; mechanism of the prolactin drop is not established.*

- **Sedative scent / autonomic effect — GRADE: MODERATE-for-scent, mechanistically clean.** Kuroda et al. (2005) showed that low-intensity jasmine (tea) odour and, critically, its component **(R)-(−)-linalool** significantly *decreased heart rate* and *increased high-frequency HRV* (a parasympathetic/vagal shift), producing "calm and vigorous" mood states — while the (S)-(+) enantiomer did not. This is direct human evidence that the sedative-scent doctrine reflects real, stereospecific autonomic pharmacology, not suggestion alone. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-005-1402-8) (PMID 15976995). *Caveat: acute autonomic/mood endpoints, small n, not a clinical anxiety trial.*

- **General phytochemistry & pharmacology — GRADE: PRELIMINARY.** Wang et al. (2025), reviewing *J. sambac*, catalogue >200 compounds and antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, cardiotonic and anti-cancer *signals* — but explicitly note that most pharmacological work is limited to preliminary cell and simple animal experiments, with mechanisms "not thoroughly researched." Traditional uses reviewed include insomnia, headache and difficult labour. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2025.120478) (PMID 40882791).

**Deflation.** The internal "aphrodisiac/gladdening" and the sweeping antimicrobial/anticancer claims rest on preclinical data only and should not be presented as established human effects. **Null/negative honesty:** no robust controlled trial supports ingested jasmine as an anxiolytic; the human anxiolytic evidence is *olfactory/autonomic and acute*, and the single lactation trial, though positive, is decades old and unreplicated. The strongest, most doctrine-faithful claims are exactly the two most modest ones — scent-level autonomic calming, and topical lactation suppression.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Classically split: the **oil/scent** is warming, gladdening, aphrodisiac (Greek–Unani *mufarriḥ*), yet sleep-inducing and "head-oppressing" at strength — a biphasic nervine. The **fresh flower topically** is cooling, drying, astringent (the lactation-suppressing face). Kenetica records both poles rather than forcing one temperament.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides records the Persian perfumed oil (*oleum jasminum* / the warming aromatic unguent) as a relaxant whose heavy sweetness "oppresses the head" — an early note of a sedative *scent*. He carries no subtle-body system through which to assign jasmine a cultivation status; *pneuma* here is context, and the Galenic three spirits are later than his text. What the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention — knowing the oil's provenance, judging the sedative heaviness of the odour by the nose, and guarding against the species blur (the *J. sambac* / *J. officinale* confusion that dogs "jasmine oil" to this day). That is a real practice of assay, not a vital-body doctrine. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** In the *Canon*, *yāsamīn* is classed warming, its oil *duhn al-yāsamīn* used to relax the nerves, relieve cold headaches, and act as an aphrodisiac and gladdening agent — the tradition's *mufarriḥ* (exhilarant) idea of a drug that strengthens the *rūḥ*. That gladdening/cardiac-drug notion is the nearest thing here to a cultivation frame, but it belongs to the *Kitāb al-Adwiya al-Qalbiyya* literature rather than the *Canon* proper, and no sourced *mufarriḥ* status for *J. sambac* specifically is retrieved; the deeper Unani cultivation idea — conservation of innate heat and radical moisture — is a longevity frame of the whole regimen, not a status the tradition assigns to this perfumed oil. **(b) framed:** claimed and used, no cultivation status sourced.

**Chinese — Bencao (SBJ).** Jasmine enters the Chinese materia medica as *mo li hua* 茉莉花, an Asian ornamental absorbed into the *bencao* well after the classical Shennong stratum, worked as a warming aromatic that moves and regulates *qi* 氣 and scents tea. No *san pin* 三品 grade — upper 上品, the grade of substances taken long-term to nourish life — is recorded for it in the sources retrieved, and the earliest Shennong stratum does not carry the plant at all. Within *yang sheng* 養生 it therefore holds an aromatic/therapeutic, not a cultivation, status. **(b) framed.**

**Ayurveda (SS).** As *mallikā*, jasmine's flowers are used aromatically and topically within the tradition, but no *rasāyana* (rejuvenation) or *vājīkaraṇa* (generative vitality) assignment for *Jasminum sambac* is recorded in the sources retrieved — those are formal Sushrutan divisions and are not to be asserted for a species without attestation. Its standing here is therapeutic and dietetic, not a formal rejuvenative cultivation status. **(b) framed.**

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in these four traditions makes jasmine a substance a cultivator takes to build vitality from within. It is worked as *scent* — an olfactory calmative inhaled at low, pleasant intensity, where heavy dosing reverses into the head-oppressing effect the classical authors already named — and as a cooling, drying topical flower for suppressing lactation. The "cultivation" jasmine offers is therefore behavioural and abstentive: keep the dose light, keep the flower external for the drying job, and do not mistake a gladdening perfume for a longevity tonic. No sourced long-term internal use supports otherwise.

### Contraindications

- **Lactation:** the topical flower *suppresses* milk — do **not** use on a breastfeeding mother who intends to continue nursing. Correspondingly useful, and correspondingly hazardous if misapplied.
- **Pregnancy:** classical emmenagogue/"difficult labour" associations warrant caution; avoid therapeutic doses in pregnancy without professional guidance.
- **Fragrance sensitivity / asthma:** concentrated jasmine scent can trigger headache, nausea or bronchial irritation in sensitive individuals — the "oppresses the head" effect is real at high dose.
- **Essential oil / absolute:** for external, diluted use only; not for ingestion. Patch-test for contact sensitisation (benzyl esters, indole).
- **Species substitution:** confirm *J. sambac* vs *J. officinale*; do not assume equivalence of "jasmine oil" products.
