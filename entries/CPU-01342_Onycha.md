# Onycha (freshwater mussel) — ONUX, Onyx or Unguis, the "covering of a shellfish" (De Materia Medica 2-10)
## *The covering (nail-like shell piece) of a shellfish similar to the purpura, from nardus-bearing lakes in India; the translators' suggested identification is "Unionaceae, Onycha — Freshwater Mussels"; the Greek text itself says "shellfish similar to the purpura"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-10 *Onux* sits in Book Two, "Living Creatures", among the shellfish chapters, in the Osbaldeston and Wood English version (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 4952-4955; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` row 200). The editors' heading line reads "SUGGESTED: Unionaceae, Onycha — Freshwater Mussels" (line 4954). The chapter in full: "Onyx (or unguis) is the covering of a shellfish similar to that of the purpura [shellfish] found in India in the nardus-bearing lakes. As a result it smells sweet — the shellfish feeding on the nardus. It is gathered after the lakes are dried up by extreme drought. The best is brought from the Red Sea. It is somewhat white and fat. The Babylonian is black and smaller. Both of them (put on coals) have a sweet smell somewhat resembling castor [2-26] in scent. The inhaled smoke restores women troubled with constrictions of the uterus, and those who have falling sickness. Taken as a drink they trouble the bowels. The burnt shellfish itself does the same things as purpura and buccinum [above]" (line 4955).

The chapter thus holds three kinds of statement. The first is provenance and an assay by the senses: where it is found, when it is gathered, the Red Sea kind as best ("somewhat white and fat"), the Babylonian kind "black and smaller", and the test of burning on coals for a sweet smell compared with that of castor (the translators' bracket sends *castor* to 2-26, *Kastoros orchis*, line 5035). The second is use: the smoke inhaled for "constrictions of the uterus" and for "falling sickness", and a warning that, taken as a drink, "they trouble the bowels". The third is a cross-reference: the burnt shellfish "does the same things as purpura and buccinum". Those chapters read: 2-4 *Porphura*, "Burnt purpura dry and clean teeth, repress excrescent flesh, and draw boils and heal them" (line 4929); 2-5 *Kurekes*, "Burnt buccina do the same as the above but they are more caustic by nature. If anyone fills them with salt and burns them in an unfired clay jar they are good as toothpastes and rubbed on burns" (line 4934), with quicklime made from them (same line).

The name recurs in other chapters in other senses, none of them the shell. *Onychinum* is named in 1-64 *Krokinon* as the same as *butyrinum* and *styracinum* "differing only in name but having the same preparation and effect" as crocinum, an ointment (line 4047). *Onyx* is also a synonym for the plant astragalus in 4-62, line 8546, an onyx-banded stone in 5-153 (line 10632), and a mineral-stone name in the cadmia chapter (line 10222). The index gives "Onycha 186; Onyx 186, 821" (lines 16791). No Egyptian, Tibetan, Sushruta or other text is attached to this entry; the tag is DMM only.

**Identity.** The editors' suggestion "Unionaceae ... Freshwater Mussels" (line 4954, and the index "Unionaceae 186", line 17946) is a modern identification. The text itself compares the covering with that of the *purpura*, a sea shellfish whose chapter the translators gloss "Shellfish - yields Tyrian purple dye" (line 4928), and it says the best comes from the Red Sea; whether a freshwater mussel is what Dioscorides meant is not settled by the lines read, and what the "covering" (the Latin *unguis*, nail) is, a shell or another part of the animal, is not stated. ### Active Components

Shellfish shell material is mostly calcium carbonate in a protein matrix (conchiolin). The chapter says the product gives a sweet smell on burning; no analysis of that scent was read. No composition specific to this entry was read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced uses are inhaled smoke for uterine constriction and for falling sickness (epilepsy), with a bowel-disturbing drink. The Codex does not recommend fumigation with burnt shell as a therapy for either, and gives no protocol: the species is uncertain, and inhaled smoke from burnt animal material is an irritant and a hazard. The text is useful here as a record of an aromatic that traded from the Red Sea and Babylon, and of the assay by burning.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text's preparations: put the covering on coals and inhale the smoke (for uterine constriction and falling sickness); take it as a drink (the text warns this troubles the bowels); burn the shellfish itself, which "does the same things as purpura and buccinum" (2-4, 2-5, the tooth-cleaning, flesh-reducing and burn uses above). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Burnt shell yields calcium oxide (quicklime) with heat, which is a caustic, and smoke from burning organic material is an irritant to the lungs. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Dioscorides (DMM).** The chapter gives no quality in the terms of "warming" or "astringent". What it prints about the substance is sensory: "somewhat white and fat" for the Red Sea kind, "black and smaller" for the Babylonian, a sweet smell on burning "somewhat resembling castor". The effect on the bowels, "they trouble", is the only functional quality (line 4955). The related chapters call the burnt buccina "more caustic" than the purpura (line 4934). No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is the discipline of attention that runs through the whole book: provenance (India, the Red Sea, Babylon), season of gathering (after the lakes dry in extreme drought), sensory assay (colour, size, the smell on coals), and grading of kinds (the best, the Babylonian). The chapter is therapeutic and does not assign a cultivation status. It records the use of the smoke for uterine constriction and falling sickness without commenting on any cause, and it does not claim an amuletic or spiritual use.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice. The animal is of uncertain identity, the use is a fumigation, and the one honest takeaway is the method: ask where a material comes from, when it was gathered, and what it does on the coals. A cultivator who wants an aromatic has many better-defined ones elsewhere in the Codex.

### Contraindications

- **Inhaled smoke:** smoke from burning animal material is an irritant to the airways and is hazardous in enclosed spaces; the text's own warning is that the drink "troubles the bowels".
- **Falling sickness:** epilepsy needs medical care; this entry does not endorse any fumigation for it.
- **Pregnancy and uterine conditions:** the text recommends the smoke for uterine constriction; no safety information is given, and the entry advises none. [USER: verify]
- **Identity:** the animal is not settled (a freshwater mussel in the editors' suggestion, a purpura-like shellfish in the text); do not substitute a species on this basis. [USER: verify]
- **Conservation and trade:** collection of wild molluscs is regulated in many places, and freshwater mussels are among the most threatened animal groups; check local law before gathering any shell. [USER: verify]
