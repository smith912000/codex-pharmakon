# Cynanchum paniculatum — Shi Xia Chang Qing 石下長卿 (herb), also Xu Chang Qing "Senior Minister Xu" (Shennong Bencao Jing)

## *Cynanchum paniculatum, as the pharmaceutical Latin "Herba Seu Radix Cynanchi Paniculati" that Yang 1998 prints for the entry "Shi Xia Chang Qing"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Shennong text (SBJ).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The entry is in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`). The Latin name is the translator's pharmaceutical Latin and was matched to the binomial by stem in the extraction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv`, line 202). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The characters 石下長卿 come from the Codex's own list of Shennong names (`_sources/shennong.csv`, line 305), which prints them alone, and not from Yang's English text. They follow Yang's romanisation "Shi Xia Chang Qing". The entry's other name, "Xu Chang Qing", is printed by Yang in English only, and its characters are not printed in any primary text read for this entry. [USER: verify]

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *Shi Xia Chang Qing (Herba Seu Radix Cynanchi Paniculati), PDF page 102.* The page marker `[[p102]]` is at line 3781, and under it only the running title "The Divine Farmer's Materia Medica Classic" is printed (line 3782). The entry runs from line 3783 to line 3789. The entry that follows, Qiao Gen (*Radix Forsythiae Suspensae*, line 3790), is a separate drug. The text file's OCR has damaged one word and broken one line (it prints "cryin~," and splits "sadness, and / abstraction"); they are read here as crying, and as sadness and abstraction. The entry reads:

"Shi Xia Chang Qing (Herba Seu Radix Cynanchi Paniculati)239 is salty and balanced. It mainly treats demonic influx, spiritual matters, and evil and malign qi. It kills hundreds of spirits, gu toxins, and old demonic influx and transformation [manifesting as] running about, crying, sadness, and abstraction. Its other name is Xu Chang Qing (Senior Minister Xu). It grows in pools and swamps."

Yang's note 239 (line 3798) reads in full: "This medicinal is currently called Xu Chang Qing." It is the translator's remark and gives no further account of the drug. The terms "demonic influx" and "gu toxins" are explained by Yang in his preface note 15 (lines 512 to 524), which is the translator's modern commentary and not part of the classic. It says that "demonic influx usually refers to an infectious disease of sudden onset" manifesting acute abdominal pain, cold and heat, masses in the rib-side region and aching pain everywhere, and that "Gu toxins refer to disease caused by imaginary or real poisonous worms". The entry prints neither explanation.

*The class heading.* Yang's table of contents prints "Herbs: Inferior Class" (line 729). On the page of this entry no heading is printed under the page marker: the page opens with the running title only. The heading "Herbs: Inferior Class" is printed at the top of page 101 (line 3749, under `[[p101]]`), the page before, and the next heading in the file is "Woods: Superior Class" at the top of page 103 (line 3809, under `[[p103]]`). The entry therefore stands under the inferior-class heading by position, between the last herb heading and the first wood heading, and not directly beneath a heading. The preface (lines 391 to 395) says of this class: "There are 125 inferior class medicinals which are used as assistants and envoys. They mainly treat disease and correspond to earth. They are usually toxic and cannot be taken for a long time." The entry itself does not say "toxic" and prints no warning.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Cynanchum paniculatum*, "Xu Chang Qing" and "Shi Xia Chang Qing" found no entry on this plant. Yang prints two other *Cynanchum* drugs, which the Codex does not treat as this plant: Bai Wei (*Radix Cynanchi Baiwei*, line 2904) and Bai Tu Huo (*Herba Cynanchi Caudati*, line 2206); both are genus-level rows in `names.csv` (lines 129 and 92). A search of both Tibetan texts for *Cynanchum paniculatum* found no line (Clark line 904 prints *Cynanchum sibiricum*, a different species, as the second Latin name for *dug.mo.nyung*). The Codex's compiled table `Volume_IX_Master_Register/MASTER_SUBSTANCE_TABLE.csv` (line 1287) carries a row for the binomial with a "[RESET]" label and a constituent claim with no locus; it was not used here. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Cynanchum* is a genus of the dogbane family (Apocynaceae, in the milkweed subfamily). Nothing here says what the Shennong drug contained, and the Latin name is the translator's. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Shennong text records, in its own terms, a salty and balanced drug that "mainly treats demonic influx, spiritual matters, and evil and malign qi", and that "kills hundreds of spirits, gu toxins, and old demonic influx". These are the text's claims, framed in its own vocabulary of spirits and toxins, and not modern diagnoses, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The entry gives no preparation, no part of the plant, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. The translator's Latin name says "Herba Seu Radix" (herb or root), and the Codex takes that as the translator's, not as the classic's. The preface gives one general rule for toxic medicinals, not for this drug: "When treating disease with toxic medicinals, one should first use a sorghum grain-sized amount. Once the disease is gone, one should stop using it. If the disease is yet to leave, double the amount. If it is still there, increase the amount 10 times. The amount is measured by the removal of the disease" (lines 487 to 488 and 503 to 505; the OCR prints "Hthe" for "If the"). The entry does not say whether this drug is toxic, so the rule is recorded as the preface's, and no amount is given for this entry. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SBJ:** "salty and balanced" (line 3783). No toxicity is printed in the entry, and no meridian or other system of later Chinese medicine is printed in it. The preface describes the inferior class as usually toxic (line 393), which is a statement about the class and not about this entry.

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: the inferior class (下品), by position after the last herb heading.* The class is assigned by the printed heading "Herbs: Inferior Class", at line 3749 (`[[p101]]`), and the entry stands on page 102 with no heading of its own, before the first wood heading at line 3809. It is a printed class and not a promise of cultivation use. The preface (lines 391 to 395) gives the inferior class a purpose: it mainly treats disease, it is usually toxic and it "cannot be taken for a long time". The Codex reads the class as the classic's grade for a drug used against a complaint and then stopped, and not as a *yang sheng* ("nourishing life") drug.

The entry carries no sentence on protracted taking, no mention of making the body light and no mention of prolonging life. It asserts no *jing*, *shen* or *neidan* action, and none is added here.

**Practitioner's note.** An inferior-class entry in the Chinese scheme, listed for demonic influx, spirits and gu toxins, with no preparation and no dose. The class warns against long use. Nothing here supports taking this plant as a cultivation practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The Shennong entry prints no warning, and its silence is not a statement of safety; the preface says of the inferior class that it is "usually toxic and cannot be taken for a long time". The identity rests on a translator's Latin name, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Persistent distress, sudden abdominal pain or fever need medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
