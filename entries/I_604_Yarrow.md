# I-604 | Yarrow

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary designation:** *Achillea millefolium* L.

**Taxonomic position:** Asteraceae; tribe Anthemideae — *Achillea* genus contains ~140 species across the Northern Hemisphere; *millefolium* = "thousand-leaved" (Latin) — for the highly dissected, feathery-appearing leaves; same tribe as Chamomile (I-602), Feverfew (I-603), Wormwood (*Artemisia*, I-605), and Sweet Wormwood (*Artemisia annua*, I-606)

**Nomenclature derivation:**
- **Achillea:** Named for Achilles, the Greek hero of the Iliad. According to myth, Achilles was taught by the centaur Chiron to use yarrow to stanch the wounds of his soldiers at Troy. This is one of the oldest documented connections between a plant name and a specific pharmacological application in Western culture — the hemostatic property embedded in a mythological origin story. Dioscorides records this Achillean connection explicitly.
- **Yarrow:** Old English *gearwe* → Middle English *yarowe*; Germanic origin; related to the root for "to heal"
- *millefolium:* The highly divided leaves appear to contain "a thousand" tiny leaflets — a visual description preserved in the binomial

**Vernacular names across traditions:**
- English: Yarrow, Common Yarrow, Milfoil, Nosebleed Plant, Soldier's Woundwort, Staunchweed, Carpenter's Weed — all the English synonyms reference the hemostatic/wound-healing use
- Greek: *Achillea millefolion* — Dioscorides
- Arabic: *Buqail al-wujûh* / *Uqhuwan barri*
- Chinese: *Yī zhī hāo* (蓍草 *Shī cǎo*) — used in I Ching divination (yarrow stalks as the original divination tool; numerical operations with 50 yarrow stalks)
- Sanskrit: *Gandana* / related species; Ayurvedic: blood-stopping herbs in the same functional class
- Assyrian: *Uknû* — referenced in cuneiform medical texts for wound treatment
- Welsh: *Llysiau Milwr* ("soldier's herb")
- French: *Herbe aux charpentiers* (carpenter's herb), *Mille-feuille*
- German: *Schafgarbe* (sheep-garb, for sheep grazing it); *Tausendblatt* (thousand-leaf)
- Native American: Over 20 North American tribes documented uses for yarrow — Ojibwe, Nez Perce, Cherokee, Potawatomi — for wound healing, fever, GI complaints, and ceremonial use; one of the most widely used plant medicines among Native American peoples

**Botanical description:** Perennial herb to 1 m; highly dissected alternate leaves (the "*millefolium*" feathery appearance); flat-topped dense corymbs of small white to pale pink flower heads (ray + disc florets); strongly aromatic (camphor, borneol scent); widespread naturalized weed globally; grows in disturbed ground, roadsides, meadows; flowers June–September in temperate regions

**Pharmaceutical parts:** 
- **Aerial parts** (*Herba Millefolii*): leaves and flowering tops — the pharmacopoeial material; harvested during flowering when essential oil and flavonoid content highest
- **Flower heads** particularly valued for highest azulene/chamazulene content
- Essential oil distilled from aerial parts

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

Yarrow's 7/9 convergence represents one of the most consistent cross-traditional pharmacological consensuses for a wound/hemostatic herb in the entire Codex. The Achilles mythology — preserving the hemostatic use in the plant's very name since classical antiquity — is matched by independent documentation in Chinese, Islamic, Assyrian, and Tibetan sources, all converging on the same fundamental property: yarrow stops bleeding.

**The I Ching connection:** The use of *Achillea* stalks (*shī cǎo*) as the traditional divination tool of the I Ching (preceding the later coin method) embedded yarrow into the foundations of Chinese intellectual culture. Fifty yarrow stalks, processed through a numerical algorithm, generate the hexagrams of the I Ching. This ritual use maintained yarrow plants in cultivation throughout Chinese history independently of its medicinal applications — ensuring availability and cultural familiarity. The Confucian canon includes the yarrow stalk I Ching method; Chinese medical texts confirm the same plant's hemostatic properties.

**The wound-war connection:** Achillea's association with warfare — through the Achilles mythology and the English vernacular "Soldier's Woundwort" and "Staunchweed" — reflects a pharmacological reality: yarrow was likely a battlefield medicine in multiple cultures. Native American warriors carried yarrow for wound treatment; Achilles (legendary) used it at Troy; the common folk names in Germanic languages reflect carpentry accidents (carpenter's weed). Plants that stop bleeding were literally life-saving before modern surgical hemostasis.

**Hemostasis vs. anticoagulation paradox within the Anthemideae:** In the same botanical tribe (Anthemideae), yarrow PROMOTES hemostasis and platelet aggregation (via achilleine), while feverfew (I-603, adjacent entry in the same tribe) INHIBITS platelet aggregation (via parthenolide). This represents a pharmacological mirror within a single botanical family — the same tribe contains compounds at opposite ends of the hemostatic pharmacological axis.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Essential oil (0.2–1.0% dried aerial parts):**
- **Chamazulene:** variable (0–30% of EO depending on chemotype); generated during distillation (same mechanism as chamomile I-602); anti-inflammatory; the blue color of high-chamazulene yarrow oil; *Achillea* is actually a commercially important source of chamazulene
- **Camphor:** major in many European chemotypes (10–30% EO); antimicrobial; stimulating; CNS excitant at high doses
- **Beta-pinene, sabinene:** major terpenes
- **Borneol, bornyl acetate:** characteristic ester; anti-inflammatory; spasmolytic
- **1,8-Cineole (eucalyptol):** bronchodilator; some chemotypes
- **Monoterpenes:** alpha-terpineol, terpinen-4-ol

**Chemical polymorphism — multiple chemotypes:**  
*A. millefolium* shows remarkable chemical polymorphism (different chemotypes with different EO compositions) reflecting both genetic variation and geographic/ecological adaptation:
- Proazulene-rich chemotype (blue oil, high chamazulene): Mediterranean
- Camphor-rich chemotype: Central European
- Sabinene hydrate chemotype
- Chrysanthemyl acetate chemotype: Eastern European
- This chemotypic diversity creates significant quality variation between commercial products

**Achilleine — the hemostatic compound:**
- **Achilleine:** betaine alkaloid (N-trimethyl-amino acid derivative); the specific styptic/hemostatic principle of yarrow; water-soluble
- Mechanism: platelet aggregation promotion (opposite to feverfew's parthenolide)
- Content: 0.01–0.08% dried herb; water-soluble (extracted in teas and aqueous preparations)
- Isolated 1954 by Bergamaschi; hemostatic activity confirmed in multiple animal and in vitro studies

**Flavonoids (0.3–2.0%):**
- **Luteolin-7-glucoside:** major; anti-inflammatory
- **Apigenin-7-glucoside:** same compound as chamomile; GABA-A modulation
- **Quercetin, kaempferol, isorhamnetin** and their glycosides
- **Rutin:** quercetin-3-rutinoside; antioxidant; capillary stabilizing

**Sesquiterpene lactones:**
- **Psilostachyin:** sesquiterpene lactone; anti-inflammatory; antitumor (in vitro)
- **Leucodin, achillin:** minor sesquiterpene lactones
- Lower content than feverfew's parthenolide; less NF-κB inhibitory potency per gram

**Polyacetylenes:** antimicrobial

**Alkaloids:**
- **Achilleine** (above): betaine type
- **Stachydrine** (proline betaine): minor; estrogenic activity claimed but weak

**Phenolics:**
- Chlorogenic acid; caffeic acid; cynarin (same as artichoke)

**Bitter sesquiterpenes:** absinthin-related; contribute to bitter tonic activity and GI stimulation

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5A. Hemostasis — Achilleine (Primary Traditional Validation)

**Mechanism:**
- Achilleine → promotes platelet aggregation (mechanism of action at platelet level not fully characterized; possibly ADP-dependent pathway enhancement or thromboxane A₂ promotion)
- In vivo: achilleine significantly shortens blood clotting time in mouse tail-cut model (Barić 2016)
- Topical application: aqueous extract (achilleine in solution) applied to wound → local platelet aggregation promotion → accelerated clot formation
- Vasoconstriction: essential oil terpenes (camphor, 1,8-cineole) cause local vasoconstriction when applied topically → reduced blood flow to wound → hemostatic synergy

**Critical CONTRAST with Feverfew (I-603):**
- Yarrow achilleine: PROMOTES platelet aggregation → hemostasis
- Feverfew parthenolide: INHIBITS platelet aggregation → antithrombotic
- Both plants are in tribe Anthemideae
- Clinical implication: the two plants have opposing effects on hemostasis; a patient taking feverfew for migraines should be aware of this interaction; combining them would create pharmacological opposition

### 5B. Anti-Inflammatory (Chamazulene, Flavonoids, Sesquiterpene Lactones)

- Chamazulene → COX/5-LOX inhibition (same mechanism as chamomile I-602)
- Luteolin, apigenin → NF-κB modulation
- Sesquiterpene lactones (psilostachyin) → NF-κB inhibition (weaker than parthenolide)
- Net anti-inflammatory profile comparable to chamomile but with different compound balance

### 5C. Antispasmodic (Flavonoids, Essential Oil)

- Apigenin → GABA-A modulation → smooth muscle relaxation
- Borneol/bornyl acetate → smooth muscle relaxant
- GI antispasmodic: functional dyspepsia, IBS, menstrual cramps
- Historical use for amenorrhea and dysmenorrhea → modern mechanistic explanation via smooth muscle relaxation

### 5D. Bitter Tonic / Choleretic

- Bitter sesquiterpenes → taste receptor (TAS2R) stimulation → vagal activation → gastric acid + bile secretion → improved digestion
- Hepatic bile flow increase → choleretic
- This mechanism underlies the traditional use of yarrow in "digestive bitters" formulas alongside wormwood (I-605) and gentian

### 5E. Antimicrobial

- Essential oil: effective against *S. aureus*, *E. coli*, *C. albicans* in vitro (MIC values relevant for topical concentrations)
- Relevant for wound-care applications; antimicrobial prevents infected wounds

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The wound in the palm:** Picking yarrow from a hedgerow and pressing the fresh leaves against a small cut — the most direct, unmediated form of wound treatment in the European tradition — is one of the most sensory immediate moments in herbal medicine. The leaves are soft despite their dissected appearance; the camphor-aromatic scent is released by crushing; the applied leaf stops a small cut noticeably faster than an untreated wound. This direct cause-and-effect relationship — plant to wound to hemostasis — is the experiential core of yarrow's 7,000+ year of use, from Neanderthal burial sites (Shanidar Cave, Iraq) to modern First Aid herb kits.

**Shanidar Cave and the flower burial:** Among the oldest evidence for intentional plant use is the discovery at Shanidar Cave (northern Iraq) of a Neanderthal burial (60,000–70,000 BCE) in which pollen from *Achillea*, *Senecio*, *Ephedra*, and five other medicinal plants was found concentrated around the skeleton. Ralph Solecki's 1975 analysis suggested intentional burial with flowers — though later analyses debate contamination by rodent burrow activity. If genuine, this represents the oldest known association between humans and medicinal plants, and *Achillea* — the hemostatic/wound herb — is among the species in the floral layer.

**The I Ching stalks:** The meditative, precise counting of 50 yarrow stalks to derive an I Ching hexagram — the original method described in the Ten Wings commentary — is an hours-long ritual that demands presence, attention, and mathematical precision. The yarrow stalk method preceded the coin method by centuries and was considered more refined; Confucius himself is said to have preferred it. This ritualized use of yarrow as an oracle tool is entirely independent of its medical uses and yet connects the plant to the highest levels of Chinese philosophical tradition.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Topical hemostasis (minor wounds, nosebleed):**
- Fresh leaves: crush and apply directly to wound with firm pressure; hold 5–10 minutes
- Dried herb poultice: reconstitute in warm water; apply as compress; gauze soaked in strong tea (1:10 w/v)
- Tincture 1:5 in 70% ethanol: apply on cotton wool; appropriate for small cuts, abrasions
- For nosebleed: yarrow leaf crushed and placed in nostril (traditional German and British practice); small evidence base but logical mechanism

**GI/digestive complaints:**
- Yarrow tea: 1–2 g dried herb per cup, steep 15 minutes; 3 cups daily between meals
- Bitter tonic: combine with wormwood (*Artemisia absinthium*) and gentian for classic European digestive bitters formula
- Commission E approved dosage: 4.5 g dried herb/day or equivalent extracts

**Dysmenorrhea:**
- 300–600 mg standardized extract or 3–4 cups yarrow tea beginning 2 days before expected menstrual onset; continue through days 1–3
- Mechanism: smooth muscle relaxant → uterine spasm reduction; mild analgesic

**Safety:**
- Generally very safe at standard doses
- **Pregnancy:** traditional use as emmenagogue (menstrual stimulant) → avoid in pregnancy; the bitter sesquiterpene and essential oil components could stimulate uterine contractions
- **Bleeding disorders:** achilleine promotes platelet aggregation → caution in patients prone to thrombosis; conversely, can be used if wound bleeding is the concern
- **Anticoagulants:** theoretical: achilleine may reduce anticoagulant effect; monitor INR if patient starts yarrow while on warfarin
- **Asteraceae allergy (ragweed, chrysanthemum, chamomile):** yarrow cross-reactivity — patch test for sensitive patients; contact dermatitis from sesquiterpene lactone component (similar mechanism to feverfew)
- **Photodermatitis:** furanocoumarins present; sun exposure after topical application may cause dermatitis; warn patients

**Cross-reference:** I-603 Feverfew (same Anthemideae tribe; OPPOSITE platelet effects — yarrow achilleine promotes hemostasis vs. feverfew parthenolide inhibits platelet aggregation; complementary when wound healing needed + migraine prophylaxis needed; do NOT combine when hemostatic urgency exists); I-602 Chamomile (same tribe; chamazulene from both; apigenin shared; similar anti-inflammatory and antispasmodic profile — chamomile for internal inflammation, yarrow for wound hemostasis as traditional complementary pair); I-601 Calendula (both wound healing herbs; different mechanisms — yarrow promotes hemostasis + antimicrobial vs. calendula promotes re-epithelialization; combine in wound care protocols); I-605 Wormwood (next Anthemideae entry — Artemisia; bitter tonic comparison; digestive bitters formula co-component)
