# Lard (pig fat; also bear grease) — STEAR HUEION KAI ARNEION (Dioscorides 2-87, "Fats from swine or bears") / Pig fat (Clark 1995, Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *Rendered fat of the pig, with bear fat named beside it in the Greek chapter; Osbaldeston prints "SUGGESTED: Lard and Bear Grease"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM, FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

This entry is the rendered fat of the pig, with the bear fat that Dioscorides names alongside it. Pig blood has its own entry (`III_23_Pig_blood.md`) and is not repeated; the other animal fats of the Greek Fats chapters (bull, goat, sheep, deer, goose, poultry) are noted only where they bear on lard.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-87 *STEAR HUEION KAI ARNEION* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 5299-5305, Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). The chapter is a method of rendering, not a list of uses: "Fats from swine or bears are prepared as follows. Take the new thick fat that grows around the kidneys, remove the skin, and put it into a good amount of very cold rain water, rubbing it carefully with the hands and (as it were) reviving it again." It is washed often in clean water, put in a ceramic jar of twice the capacity with water to cover, set "over a gentle fire of coals" and stirred "with a continuous splashing"; when melted it is strained into water and cooled; the water is removed, and it is melted again with water in a clean jar; the dregs are left to settle, and the fat is put in a mortar "moistened with a sponge"; when congealed "take off the filth lying in the bottom"; it is melted a third time without water, poured into the mortar, cleaned, and "store it in tightly corked ceramic jars and put the jars in a very cold place." The chapter before (2-86) renders goose and poultry fat, and the chapter after (2-88) renders goat, sheep and deer suet "like fat of swine".

The uses are given in the general chapter on fats, 2-94 *STEATON DUNAMIS*, "Medicinal uses of Fats" (line 5342ff): "All fat is warming, softening and purifying". For swine: "Swines' fat is applied for disorders of the womb and perineum, and is also good for those burnt by fire. The same (kept in salt and grown very old in there as it were) warms and soothes. Washed in wine it is good for pleurisy. The same (applied with ash or chalk) is good for oedema, inflammation and fistulas [ulcers]." For bears: "Bears' fat is thought to make hair that was destroyed by alopecia [baldness] grow again, and is good for chilblains." Elsewhere lard is a vehicle: goat dung "applied with swines' grease" helps the gouty (2-98, line 5372), and 2-91 (line 5330) ends the scenting of poultry and goose grease with "In the same way both swine fat and bear fat and other similar fats are given a fragrant smell." The translator's bracketed gloss in 2-94 ("alopecia [baldness]") is the edition's.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Explanatory Tantra ch.20, the thirteen kinds of animal medicine, under "Fat". Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 1180): "Pig fat: collects poison, heals psoriasis [and cures baldness]. Human fat: subdues wind disorders and heals psoriasis." The bracket is a commentary interpolation (Clark's note, line 82). Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 3546): "Pig fat neutralizes poison and cures eczema and pimples." The verb "collects" is the same one used for pig blood in Clark, and the two translations render the skin disease differently (psoriasis; eczema and pimples); I did not check the Tibetan. In the dietary chapter, fats in general are described in a passage on "Oils" (Clark lines 684-685: "Oils include butter, sesame oil, marrow and fat, each in turn heavier than the previous one. They have a sweet taste and cool, aperient and oily powers ... Oils are recommended [as beneficial] for the aged, for children, in debility, emaciation, coarse complexion, semen deficiency, leucorrhoea, following purgation, mental strain, and whenever there is the threat of a wind disorder"); Men-Tsee-Khang lines 2941 and 2958-2960 give the same, adding "Animal fats are effective against joint pain, burns and loong disorders as well as for problems related to the ears, brain and uterus. Those who regularly consume fat enjoy more inner body heat, clear internal disorders, instantly synthesize bodily constituents ... It is said to strengthen sensory organs, stops aging and prolongs life." That last passage is about fats in general and is not assigned to pig fat. Pork: "Pork is cool and light, heals wounds and eliminates brown phlegm" (Clark line 677). No bear fat was found in the Tibetan lines; the black bear and brown bear are named only among the carnivorous animals (Men-Tsee-Khang line 2912). The spine's locus for this tag is Clark line 1180; `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` has no pig-fat row.

**Egyptian (EP) is not on this entry's tag line but has hog's fat.** Bryan's *Papyrus Ebers* (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`) names "Hog's Fat" in a salve "to make supple the hardenings" with worm-oil, mouse oil and cat's oil (line 2191), and "Fat-of-the-Hippopotamus, or of the Hog" in a poultice (line 2374). ### Active Components

The sources name none. In general: lard is mostly triglycerides (a mixture of saturated, monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids), with a small amount of cholesterol, and vitamin D that varies with the pig's feed and sun exposure. Bear fat is a comparable animal fat. Rendering with water and straining, as Dioscorides describes, removes membrane and blood, which is why the chapter insists on washing and on removing "the filth lying in the bottom". No quantities are given here because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Greek record is a fat rendered with care and used on the skin and in the womb and perineum, for burns, oedema and pleurisy (washed in wine); bear fat for baldness and chilblains. The Tibetan record is pig fat for poison and a skin disease. The Codex has no protocol for any of it and offers none. The reasons are specific: lard on a burn traps heat and risks infection (burns need cooling and medical care); poison is an emergency; the sources give no dose; bears are protected in many places; and pork fat is forbidden under some dietary laws. Lard as a cooking fat is ordinary food, outside this entry's sources.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparation is the Greek one, in 2-87: wash, melt in water over a gentle fire, strain into water, cool, remove the water, melt again, settle, mould in a mortar, scrape off the dregs, melt a third time without water, and store in tightly corked jars in a very cold place. Variants in the neighbouring chapters are the salt-keeping of old swine fat (2-94), scenting (2-91) and storing in honey (2-93, for goose, poultry or calf fat). The Tibetan texts give actions only. ### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Lard is an energy-dense animal fat and a source of saturated and monounsaturated fat; dietary saturated fat is associated with raised LDL cholesterol. Fats are emollients and occlusives: they hold moisture in skin, which is why animal fats have long been used on dry skin, but they are not sterile and turn rancid. No effect of lard or bear fat on burns, pleurisy, oedema, fistulas, alopecia, chilblains, psoriasis or poisoning is established; fat does not neutralise a poison. No clinical studies of the traditional uses were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Fats are stated generally to be "warming, softening and purifying" (2-94, line 5344), with bull, cow and calf fat "somewhat astringent" and goat fat "more astringent"; old swine fat kept in salt "warms and soothes". These are the only qualities printed for swine fat.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Pig fat is given by action only ("collects poison", "neutralizes poison"). For fats as a group the text gives taste and power: oils are sweet in taste with cool, aperient, oily powers (Clark line 684); fats are "successively heavier and cooler" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 2941). Pork is "cool and light" (Clark line 677). None of this is assigned to pig fat in a separate line.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied. Lard and bear grease are a therapeutic and cosmetic material in 2-87 and 2-94. What the text gives a cultivator is a discipline of attention in making: take the new thick fat around the kidneys, strip the skin, wash it in very cold rain water, render it three times, and keep it cold in corked jars. That is a craft rule about purity and storage, not a claim about the person's constitution.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Pig fat is a medicine of the Explanatory Tantra's animal list, given for poison and a skin disease. The dietary passage on fats in general makes larger claims ("stops aging and prolongs life", Men-Tsee-Khang line 2960), but they attach to fats as a class and are not transferred to pig fat. No essence-extraction (*bcud len*) use of pig fat is attested in the lines read, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition; none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do for cultivation. Lard is a cooking fat; Dioscorides's value to a cultivator is the rendering craft, and the Tibetan text's value is its warning that "collecting" poison is a statement about disease. The class-level claims for fats in the Tibetan diet chapter are not claims about lard.

### Contraindications

- **Burns and wounds.** Do not apply lard or any fat to a burn; cool with water and seek care. Fat on open wounds risks infection.
- **Poisoning.** An emergency requiring medical care; fat does not neutralise poison.
- **Cardiovascular and lipid disorders.** Dietary saturated fat is a consideration; follow medical advice.
- **Religious and ethical.** Pork products are forbidden in Judaism and Islam and in some other dietary rules; use means slaughter. Bears are protected in many places and trade in bear fat or parts may be illegal.
- **Dose unknown.** No dose is given in the sources read.
