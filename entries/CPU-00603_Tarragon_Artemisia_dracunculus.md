# Tarragon / Dragon Herb / Estragon

## *Artemisia dracunculus* L. | Family: Asteraceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, EF
**Tags (previous line, retained for reference):** DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Warm, dry, aromatic; carminative and mildly stimulant to digestion — a low-toxicity kitchen herb with one flagged constituent

**Training Phase:** Foundational / Daily Use — culinary quantities; concentrated extracts are a separate matter

---

### Origin & Tradition

The name is the whole argument. *Dracunculus* — "little dragon." French *estragon*, Arabic *ṭarkhūn*, English *tarragon*, all descending from the same root through a chain of borrowings, and all of them meaning dragon. The plant is a slender, glossy, anise-scented perennial of the steppe, native across Siberia, Central Asia and western North America, and it is one of the few plants in this Volume whose *name* generated its *doctrine* rather than the other way round.

Two explanations for the name circulate, and both are old enough to be worth recording:

1. **The roots.** *A. dracunculus* forms a mat of thin, coiling, serpentine roots. Dig one up and it looks like a knot of small snakes. A serpentine root, in the logic of the doctrine of signatures, announces a serpent remedy.
2. **The serpent-bite tradition itself.** Mediaeval and early-modern European herbal literature records tarragon as a remedy for the bites of venomous beasts — serpents, mad dogs. Whether this belief generated the name or the name generated the belief is genuinely unclear and probably unrecoverable; the two reinforced each other for centuries.

**Western Herbal / Dispensatory Tradition (DMM)** — Beyond the dragon business, tarragon has a mundane and well-attested medicinal profile as a **carminative, appetite stimulant, and digestive**. It is the herb given for wind, sluggish appetite, and a heavy stomach, and this use is consistent across European herbal literature and survives into the culinary tradition — tarragon in a sauce is not only a flavour decision. A **toothache** use is also recorded: fresh leaf chewed or held against an aching tooth, reported to numb the area. There are scattered records of use as a mild sedative or sleep aid and as an emmenagogue. It is one of the four *fines herbes* of French cookery, and its status as food is the single most important fact about its safety profile.

**Persian / Central Asian & Russian usage (DMM, adjacent)** — *Ṭarkhūn* is a staple culinary and folk-digestive herb across Persia, the Caucasus and Central Asia — the plant's likely origin region and the source of the name that reached Europe. In Russian usage it is the basis of the *tarhun* flavouring. This is a real, continuous, food-centred tradition. **[USER: verify]** the specific medicinal indications before asserting anything beyond the digestive/carminative use, which is secure.

**Chinese usage — attribution caution.** *(⚠️ de-coded 2026-08-10: this block was lettered **(CM)**, which denotes a different tradition. The content is retained; it is not a tagged tradition on this entry.)* *Artemisia dracunculus* is not a mainstream Chinese materia medica drug and does not carry a standard function statement in the way *Artemisia annua* (Qing Hao) or *Artemisia argyi* (Ai Ye) do. The CM tag here is **doubtful** and rests at best on regional or minority-nationality usage in the plant's Central Asian range extending into western China. **[USER: verify] — this tag may need to be dropped.** Do not manufacture a Chinese function statement for this plant. Its genuine second tradition is Persian/Central Asian, not Chinese.

**Cultivar distinction — the most practically important fact in this entry.** *A. dracunculus* exists in two commercially distinct forms that are not interchangeable:

- **French tarragon** (*A. dracunculus* var. *sativa*) — the culinary plant. Sterile, sets no viable seed, propagated **only** by cutting or division. Intensely aromatic. This is the herb of the kitchen.
- **Russian tarragon** (*A. dracunculus* var. *dracunculoides* or the wild type) — seed-grown, vigorous, coarser, markedly less aromatic and often described as nearly flavourless. Widely sold as seed to disappointed gardeners.

**The modern glycaemic research is on Russian tarragon.** The culinary herb and the research plant are different cultivars with different chemistry. Nothing in this entry should be read as transferring one's data to the other.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

The dragon doctrine deserves to be taken apart carefully, because it is a clean case of a real observation mis-assigned.

**Deflate the snakebite claim first, without qualification.** There is no evidence — none retrieved, none plausible — that tarragon treats envenomation. Snakebite is a medical emergency requiring antivenom. The serpentine root is a morphological accident of a steppe perennial's growth habit and carries no information about pharmacology. If any part of this Codex is used to justify treating a snakebite with a kitchen herb, the Codex has done harm. **The dragon is a name, not a mechanism.**

But notice what happens if you ask *why* a numbing herb accumulated a bite-and-venom reputation. The plant genuinely does numb tissue on contact. A herb that visibly deadens pain in the mouth is a herb that looks, to a pre-modern observer, like it is *drawing out* or *neutralising* something. Bites hurt. A plant that stops hurt at the site is a plant that answers bites. The serpentine root then supplies the confirming signature, and the name locks it in. The doctrine is built on a real pharmacological observation — **local numbing** — extended by analogy far past what the chemistry can carry.

And the numbing is real. Tarragon's essential oil is dominated by **estragole** (methyl chavicol), with **eugenol** and **eugenol methyl ether** among the identified constituents. Eugenol is the numbing principle of clove oil and a genuine, long-used dental topical anaesthetic. According to PubMed, GC-MS analysis of tarragon essential oils identified estragole, cis- and trans-β-ocimene, limonene, **eugenol methyl ether, eugenol acetate, eugenol**, caryophyllene oxide and α-pinene among the highest-concentration compounds ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms26051860)). The toothache tradition maps onto a eugenol-type volatile fraction and lands. The carminative tradition maps onto the aromatic volatile oil — a standard, unremarkable, plausible mechanism shared with every anise-scented culinary herb.

So the doctrinal layer splits cleanly in two: **the local, sensory, verifiable claim survives** (it numbs; that is eugenol chemistry). **The grand, systemic, analogical claim collapses** (it does not treat venom; the dragon is etymology). This is the most common shape of failure in traditional materia medica — a true small observation inflated by resemblance into a false large one — and tarragon is the tidiest specimen of it in Volume I.

### Active Components

- **⚠️ ESTRAGOLE (methyl chavicol)** — the dominant volatile constituent; the anise note. **A rodent hepatocarcinogen and genotoxic at high dose; EU-restricted in food.** See Contraindications. **FLAGGED.**
- **Eugenol, eugenol methyl ether, eugenol acetate** — the numbing fraction; eugenol is an established dental topical anaesthetic. Identified in tarragon essential oil by GC-MS ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms26051860)).
- **cis- and trans-β-ocimene, limonene, α-pinene, caryophyllene oxide** — terpene fraction, aroma and plausible carminative contributors ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms26051860)).
- ***trans*-anethole** — estragole's isomer, present in the anise-scented herb group; measured alongside estragole and *para*-anisaldehyde in tarragon extracts by microextraction-HPLC ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpba.2016.01.003)).
- **Polyphenols / phenolic fraction** — the constituents implicated in the Russian-tarragon glycaemic work, as distinct from the volatile oil.

**Composition varies enormously by cultivar.** French tarragon is volatile-oil-rich; Russian tarragon is comparatively oil-poor. A statement about "tarragon" chemistry that does not name the cultivar is nearly uninformative.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Tarragon is a **Foundational-phase herb**, and it earns that placement by being food. Its use case in this system is unglamorous and real: an aromatic digestive taken in culinary quantity, supporting appetite and easing wind — the standard action of the aromatic Asteraceae and Apiaceae kitchen herbs. In a curriculum that spends most of Volume I on plants that will injure the careless, tarragon is a corrective: a reminder that the bulk of useful traditional materia medica is boring, dietary, and low-dose.

Its second use is **pedagogical**, and here it pairs directly with the Pasque Flower and Jack-in-the-Pulpit entries that precede it. Those two teach that *processing* controls toxicity. Tarragon teaches that **the traditional use-pattern itself controls toxicity**. Estragole is a flagged rodent hepatocarcinogen. Tarragon is safe. Both are true, and they are reconciled by dose: nobody eats tarragon by the bowlful, and the culinary tradition — which never conceived of estragole — established a de facto exposure ceiling that modern toxicology finds acceptable. The tradition did not know why sprinkling was the right verb. It sprinkled anyway.

The risk arrives precisely where tradition is left behind: **concentrated essential oil, high-dose extracts, and daily supplementation** are exposures no culinary tradition ever produced. The safety record of tarragon-as-food does not extend to tarragon-as-oil.

The third and most interesting note is the **Russian tarragon glycaemic work**, which is a genuine modern development with human trial data (see below) — and which uses the *less* traditional, less flavourful cultivar at a standardised extract dose. That is modern pharmacology borrowing a plant name, not a tradition being validated.

**Cultivator's note.** This is the entry's most actionable section, because tarragon cultivation is where most people go wrong.

- **Buy French tarragon as a plant, never as seed.** French tarragon (*var. sativa*) is sterile and sets no viable seed. **Any packet of "tarragon seed" is Russian tarragon**, and it will grow vigorously and taste of very little. This is the single most common disappointment in the herb garden and it is entirely avoidable.
- Propagate French tarragon by **cuttings, division, or root runners** in spring. It is easy from a division and impossible from seed.
- It wants **full sun, light, sharply drained, rather poor soil**. A steppe plant. Rich, wet ground gives soft growth, weak aroma, and winter rot — the plant dies of kindness more often than neglect.
- **Divide every 2–3 years.** Clumps decline and lose aroma with age; division rejuvenates.
- Cut back hard in autumn; it is hardy but resents winter wet. Container growth with gritty compost suits it well in wet climates.
- Harvest leaves through summer; aroma is highest before flowering. It dries poorly compared to most herbs — the volatile fraction is fugitive. Freeze it, or steep it in vinegar, which is the traditional preservation and a good one.
- **Adulteration/substitution note.** According to PubMed, *Tagetes lucida* Cav. ("Mexican tarragon") is cultivated commercially in Costa Rica and sold in supermarkets as a **substitute for *Artemisia dracunculus***; its essential oil is 95–97% **methyl chavicol (estragole)** ([PMID 17354394 — no DOI; *Rev Biol Trop* 52(4):853-7, 2004]). It is not this plant, it is not in this family's genus, and it is an even purer estragole source. Know what you bought.

### Scientific Correlation

Graded honestly. Tarragon is unusual in this Volume in having a genuine positive human RCT — and it is not for anything the tradition claimed.

**Snakebite / venom — NO EVIDENCE. REFUTED IN PRINCIPLE (Grade F).** No retrieved study. No plausible mechanism. The doctrine is etymological and analogical in origin. **Snakebite requires antivenom and emergency care.** This is the deflation the entry exists to perform.

**Toothache / local numbing — MECHANISTICALLY PLAUSIBLE, clinically untested for this plant (Grade C).** GC-MS confirms eugenol, eugenol acetate and eugenol methyl ether in tarragon essential oil ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms26051860)), and eugenol's local anaesthetic action is independently well established in dentistry. But that is a *constituent-level* inference. **No retrieved trial tests tarragon itself for dental pain.** The tradition is very likely reporting something real; it has not been demonstrated for this species. Graded C — plausible, unproven — and not higher.

**Carminative / digestive / appetite — PLAUSIBLE, UNTESTED (Grade C/D).** Standard aromatic-volatile-oil mechanism, shared across the anise-scented culinary herbs, consistent with a broad and continuous food tradition. No retrieved controlled trial in humans for *A. dracunculus* specifically. Reasonable; unvalidated.

**Glycaemic control — SUPPORTED by a small human RCT (Grade B).** This is the real modern finding and it deserves careful, unexaggerated statement. According to PubMed, a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in **24 patients with impaired glucose tolerance** gave *Artemisia dracunculus* 1000 mg before breakfast and dinner for 90 days (n=12) versus placebo (n=12). The *A. dracunculus* group showed significant decreases in systolic blood pressure (120.0±11.3 → 113.0±11.2 mmHg), HbA1c (5.8±0.3 → 5.6±0.4%), AUC of insulin (56,136±27,426 → 44,472±23,370 pmol/L) and total insulin secretion (0.45±0.23 → 0.35±0.18), with a significant increase in HDL-C (1.3±0.3 → 1.4±0.3 mmol/L). No significant differences after placebo. The paper's own keywords name **Russian tarragon** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1089/jmf.2016.0005)).

Now the caveats, which are substantial: **n=24 total, 12 per arm.** Ninety days. A single centre. The changes, while statistically significant, are small in absolute terms — an HbA1c drop of 0.2% in a non-diabetic prediabetic range. Reduced insulin AUC and total secretion with slightly lower HbA1c is *consistent with* improved insulin sensitivity, which is the mechanistically interesting reading, but this trial is far too small to establish clinical benefit. It is a promising signal that warrants replication at scale, and it is not a reason to take tarragon for blood sugar. Note also a 2021 narrative review of phenolic-rich plant extracts in prediabetes and its subgroups, which surveys this field and concludes that evidence on plant extracts across prediabetes subgroups **remains scarce and warrants further study** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu13113733)) — an appropriately deflationary frame for the whole area.

**Gestational diabetes — DO NOT ATTRIBUTE TO THIS PLANT.** A randomised trial found that "Artemisia extract" for 10 weeks improved insulin sensitivity and up-regulated adiponectin in women with gestational diabetes, reducing fasting plasma glucose, serum insulin, HOMA-IR and HOMA-B ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcph.755)). **The retrieved record does not establish that the species was *A. dracunculus*.** *Artemisia* is a large genus. This trial is named here so it is not silently absorbed into a tarragon evidence base, and **[USER: verify]** the species before it is cited for anything. Tarragon is in any case contraindicated in pregnancy at medicinal dose (below).

**Antibacterial / cytotoxic potential — COMPUTATIONAL PREDICTION ONLY (Grade E).** The 2025 *IJMS* study is explicitly an *in silico* exercise — ADMETlab3.0, admetSAR3.0, CLC-Pred2.0, AntiBac-Pred and molecular docking — predicting that tarragon oil constituents exert antibacterial activity against some species and show potential cytotoxicity toward several cancer cell lines, and noting that ADMET profiles are generally safe but that some constituents may cause skin sensitisation and respiratory toxicity and may inhibit OATP1/OATP2 transporters ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms26051860)). **These are software predictions, not experiments.** The authors say so, framing the work as a guide to further experimental study. No biological claim follows.

**Estragole exposure is measurable in humans after eating these herbs.** A microextraction-HPLC method was developed to determine *para*-anisaldehyde, *trans*-anethole and estragole in basil, fennel and tarragon extracts and subsequently **in human plasma and urine after consumption**, explicitly to assess absorption and dose-based toxicity levels ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpba.2016.01.003)). Estragole from tarragon does reach the circulation. That is exactly why the dose question below is not academic.

**Net:** the famous doctrine (dragons, venom) is empty; the modest doctrine (numbing) is chemically well-founded but untested; the digestive use is plausible and unstudied; and the one solid human trial is for a use no tradition ever claimed, in the cultivar nobody wants to cook with, at n=12 per arm.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Warm and dry. Aromatic, gently dispersing, carminative — the classic warming-digestive signature of the aromatic culinary herbs. Directed at the middle: stomach, appetite, wind, sluggish digestion. In the humoral reading it warms a cold, damp, torpid stomach and moves stagnant food. Secondarily emmenagogue (traditional claim; unvalidated) and mildly sedative (traditional claim; unvalidated).

Unlike Pasque Flower and Tian Nan Xing, tarragon's energetics require **no processing to be safe** — the plant is simply what it is, and the tradition's handling of it is culinary rather than pharmaceutical. That is the point of its placement here.

### Contraindications

⚠️ **ESTRAGOLE — FLAGGED CONSTITUENT.**

- **Estragole (methyl chavicol) is a rodent hepatocarcinogen and is genotoxic in animal studies at high doses.** The EU restricts estragole levels in foodstuffs, and European regulatory assessment has advised reduction of exposure from concentrated botanical preparations. Estragole is absorbed and detectable in human plasma and urine after consuming estragole-bearing herbs ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpba.2016.01.003)).
- **Culinary tarragon is not the concern; concentrated tarragon is.** Ordinary food use has a long safety record and the dose is self-limiting. **Tarragon essential oil taken internally, high-dose extracts, and prolonged daily supplementation** produce exposures with no traditional precedent, and are not advised.
- **Do not use tarragon essential oil internally.** Do not treat "it's just a kitchen herb" as covering the oil. It does not.
- **Pregnancy — avoid at medicinal dose.** Traditional emmenagogue use plus a genotoxic-at-dose constituent is sufficient reason. **Culinary amounts in food are not the issue; medicinal doses, extracts and oil are.** Do not extrapolate the "Artemisia extract" gestational-diabetes trial to tarragon — the species is unconfirmed ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcph.755)).
- **Lactation — avoid at medicinal dose.** No data.
- **Children — culinary use only.** No medicinal dosing data.
- **Diabetes / glycaemic medication** — the RCT signal, small as it is, means additive effects with hypoglycaemic drugs cannot be excluded. Anyone on glucose-lowering medication should not add tarragon extract without medical supervision. Note the trial also found a **systolic blood pressure reduction** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1089/jmf.2016.0005)) — relevant to anyone on antihypertensives.
- **Asteraceae sensitivity** — cross-reactivity with ragweed, chamomile, mugwort and relatives should be assumed in sensitised individuals. *In silico* work flags skin sensitisation and respiratory toxicity potential for some oil constituents, and possible OATP1/OATP2 inhibition — **predicted, not demonstrated**, but worth noting for anyone on OATP-substrate drugs ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms26051860)).
- ⚠️ **SNAKEBITE IS A MEDICAL EMERGENCY.** The traditional dragon/serpent-bite indication has no supporting evidence and no plausible mechanism. Envenomation requires antivenom and emergency medical care. **Nothing in this entry constitutes a snakebite treatment.** This warning is not decorative — the plant's name is a standing invitation to exactly this error.
- **Know your cultivar and your genus.** Russian ≠ French tarragon; *Tagetes lucida* ("Mexican tarragon") is a commercial substitute with 95–97% estragole oil (PMID 17354394, *Rev Biol Trop* 52(4):853-7, 2004) and is not *Artemisia dracunculus* at all.
