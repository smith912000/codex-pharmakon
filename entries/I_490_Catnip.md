# I-490 — Catnip
## *Nepeta cataria* L.

**Family:** Lamiaceae | **Volume:** I — Plant-Based | **Entry:** I-490

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted Name:** *Nepeta cataria* L. (1753)
**Common Names:** Catnip, catmint, catwort, field balm, catswort, nip
**Synonyms:** *Nepeta cataria* var. *citriodora* (lemon catnip); *Calamintha nepetaria* Poir.

**Etymology:** *Nepeta* from the ancient Etruscan city of Nepeti (modern Nepi, Lazio, Italy) where the plant was reportedly abundant; *cataria* from Latin *catus* (cat), documenting the famous feline behavioral response; "catnip" = cat + dialectal *nip* (variant of *nep*, Old English for catmint)

**Botanical Description:** Aromatic perennial, 40–100 cm; stems square, densely white-pubescent; leaves cordate-ovate, crenate-serrate, grey-green, tomentose, strongly aromatic; flowers white to pale lilac with purple spots in terminal spikes; bloom June–September; characteristic dusty, minty-herbaceous aroma distinctly different from *Mentha*

**Distribution:** Native to Europe, Asia, North Africa; naturalized throughout North America, Australia; common in disturbed habitats, roadsides, waste ground, old fields; strongly synanthropic (associated with human habitation and settlement)

**Plant Part Used:** Aerial parts (leaves, flowering tops); essential oil

**Adulterants/Substitutions:** *Nepeta × faassenii* (garden catmint; ornamental; lower nepetalactone content; reduced feline behavioral response); *Glechoma hederacea* (ground ivy; also Lamiaceae; sometimes confused in folk nomenclature)

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

### The Feline Pharmacologist's Paradox

Catnip occupies a unique position in the Codex as the substance most extensively studied for its effects on non-human subjects. The feline behavioral response to nepetalactone is arguably the most reproducible and dramatic botanical pharmacological effect in all of mammalian biology, yet the compound responsible shows no equivalent psychoactive effect in humans. This asymmetry is not a failure of catnip as a medicinal plant — it is a window into the extraordinary specificity of olfactory pharmacology and the divergence of mammalian receptor systems across evolution.

The human medicinal tradition, while modest by comparison to the dramatic feline response, is pharmacologically coherent. *Nepeta cataria* has been used consistently across European and Islamic medical traditions as a diaphoretic (fever facilitation), carminative, antispasmodic, and mild sedative. These applications map to the non-nepetalactone phenolic and flavonoid constituents — structurally similar to those of lemon balm (*Melissa officinalis*, I-478) and other Lamiaceae sedatives/carminatives, and activated by the same GABA-modulatory mechanisms.

### Why Cats and Not Humans?

The feline behavioral response requires the vomeronasal organ (VNO, Jacobson's organ), which in cats is functionally active and neurologically integrated with the limbic system in ways that adult humans' VNO is not. In cats, nepetalactone binds to feline olfactory receptors as a super-stimulus, triggering rolling, vocalizing, and euphoric behavior through what behavioral neuroscience now characterizes as a genuine reward-circuit activation. The compound structurally mimics feline pheromones in its binding characteristics. In humans, there is no equivalent VNO-mediated olfactory euphoriant pathway; mild CNS effects observed in human studies are mediated through different mechanisms at much higher concentrations.

The ~30% of cats that show no response to catnip represent a simple Mendelian recessive trait — the gene encoding the relevant receptor variant has been mapped to chromosome B3 (Haze et al. 2020, *Science Advances*). This is one of the clearest examples of a genetically defined pharmacological non-response in any species.

### The *Jing Jie* Connection

*Nepeta tenuifolia* Benth. (荆芥, *jīng jiè*) is a major herb in Traditional Chinese Medicine, used for wind-cold patterns, respiratory infections, and skin eruptions. It is closely related to *N. cataria* but pharmacologically distinct. The extensive TCM record for the genus provides indirect validation for some of *N. cataria*'s traditional applications, even though *cataria* itself is not prominent in formal Chinese texts. The distinction between species within a genus — critical for oregano vs marjoram (I-486/I-488) and peppermint vs spearmint (I-484/I-485) — applies equally here: *jīng jiè* and catnip are related, not interchangeable.

### Nepetalactone as Multifunctional Ecological Signal

Beyond its mammalian pharmacology, nepetalactone serves a critical ecological function as an insect deterrent produced by the plant as defense against aphid herbivory. Aphids contain nepetalactone alarm pheromone precursors, and nepetalactone at low concentrations triggers aphid alarm-scatter responses. The evolutionary pharmacology is elegant: the plant synthesizes a compound that simultaneously serves as herbivore deterrent (insects), mammalian cat attractant (cats disturb vegetation and spread seeds), and antimicrobial aromatic protection — a multifunctional secondary metabolite performing distinct functions at different concentration ranges and in different biological receivers.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

### Essential Oil (Primary Bioactive Fraction)
**Nepetalactone** (cis/trans isomers; Z,E and E,Z): 70–99% of EO; cyclopentanoid monoterpene iridoid; the primary feline-active compound; *cis*-nepetalactone (Z,E isomer) more potent feline stimulus than *trans*-nepetalactone (E,Z isomer); isomer ratios vary by chemotype and geography

**Nepetalic acid:** related monoterpene acid; minor constituent

**Nepetalic anhydride:** minor

**α-Citral, β-Citral (geranial/neral):** lemon catnip var. *citriodora* has high citral and reduced nepetalactone; distinct aroma profile with reduced feline behavioral activity

**β-Caryophyllene:** sesquiterpene; CB2 partial agonist (documented across multiple Lamiaceae entries: I-479 Holy Basil, I-480 Rosemary, I-491 Self-Heal); anti-inflammatory

**Thymol, carvacrol:** minor phenols in some chemotypes; shared with thyme (I-482) and oregano (I-486)

### Phenolic Fraction
**Rosmarinic acid:** standard Lamiaceae constituent; GABA-T inhibitor; anti-inflammatory via COX/LOX dual inhibition; antiviral; the defining Lamiaceae phenolic recurring from I-476 through I-492 and beyond

**Caffeic acid:** hydroxycinnamic acid antioxidant

**Chlorogenic acid:** antioxidant; glycemic modulation

**Luteolin:** flavone; NF-κB inhibition; anti-inflammatory; mild GABA-A modulatory activity

**Apigenin:** flavone; GABA-A positive allosteric modulation at BZD site (same mechanism as chamomile; contributes to mild human sedation)

**Rutin:** quercetin-3-rutinoside; capillary-protective; anti-inflammatory

**Ursolic acid:** triterpene; adaptogenic; anti-inflammatory; recurring Lamiaceae compound

### Iridoid Glycosides
**Nepetaside:** iridoid glycoside; minor; characteristic of genus

**8-Epiloganic acid:** iridoid precursor; part of nepetalactone biosynthetic pathway

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Feline Behavioral Mechanism (Non-Human)

**Nepetalactone → feline olfactory receptor → vomeronasal organ → hypothalamic-limbic circuit:**
Nepetalactone acts as a super-stimulus on olfactory receptors (OR52M1 and related receptors) in the cat's highly sensitive olfactory epithelium; VNO-mediated signal transduction reaches limbic reward circuits; observed behavior (rolling, vocalizing, salivating, rubbing) resembles but is pharmacologically distinct from opioid or benzodiazepine agonism; naloxone does not block the response; effect lasts 5–15 minutes then refractory period ~30 minutes (receptor desensitization mechanism)

**Genetic basis:** ~30% cats show no response; autosomal recessive trait; gene locus on chromosome B3; other felids (lions, tigers, leopards) show variable response rates depending on VNO architecture and receptor expression

### 5.2 Human Sedative/CNS Mechanism

**Rosmarinic acid → GABA-T inhibition:**
Shared Lamiaceae nervine mechanism; increases synaptic GABA availability by inhibiting GABA transaminase; mild anxiolytic effect at sufficient herb doses (see I-478 Lemon Balm for primary clinical documentation of this mechanism)

**Luteolin, apigenin → GABA-A PAM:**
Mild positive allosteric modulation at the benzodiazepine binding site of GABA-A receptor; same mechanism as chamomile apigenin; contributes to mild sedative effect at herb-tea concentrations

**Nepetalactone (human):** crosses the blood-brain barrier at sufficient concentrations; no clear receptor target in humans identified; weak sedative effect in rodent models; human CNS mechanism incompletely characterized; contribution to sedation at typical herb-tea doses likely minor compared to rosmarinic acid and flavone fractions

### 5.3 Diaphoretic Mechanism

**Volatile oil components → peripheral vasodilation + sweating facilitation:**
The diaphoretic effect is mediated through mild prostaglandin synthesis modulation and direct peripheral vasoactive effects; mechanism similar to elderflower (*Sambucus nigra*); used empirically for fever facilitation before antibiotic-era medicine; enables endogenous pyrexia resolution

### 5.4 Carminative/Antispasmodic Mechanism

**Nepetalactone + monoterpene complex → GI smooth muscle relaxation:**
Calcium antagonism at intestinal smooth muscle; same general class as peppermint menthol (I-484) but weaker and without the TRPM8/VGCC pharmacological specificity; relieves intestinal cramping, gas, and colic

**Traditional infant colic application:** Weak catnip tea was administered to colicky infants in European and North American folk medicine; the carminative mechanism is plausible; however, fennel (*Foeniculum vulgare*) has superior clinical evidence for this indication

### 5.5 Insect Repellent Mechanism

**Nepetalactone → insect olfactory deterrency:**
Nepetalactone repels mosquitoes (*Aedes aegypti*, *Culex pipiens*) and other insects in laboratory bioassays through olfactory receptor binding that triggers avoidance behavior; mechanism analogous to but distinct from DEET (which blocks insect olfactory receptor sensitivity rather than triggering avoidance); duration of action in field conditions substantially shorter than DEET; the same compound that attracts cats repels insects — distinct receptor systems and opposite behavioral responses

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### 6.1 Sedative/Anxiolytic

**Evidence Level: B-C (pre-clinical support; limited human data)**

- No formal RCTs for sedative indication in humans
- Hatch 1972 (*Economic Botany* 23(2):156-161): historical review of human psychoactive claims; most 1960s reports were based on misidentification or confusion with cannabis; isolated reports of effects not reproduced under controlled conditions
- Rosmarinic acid studies (see Lemon Balm I-478, Kennedy 2004) provide mechanistic Level B support for the Lamiaceae GABA-T inhibition nervine mechanism applicable to catnip's shared phenolic fraction

### 6.2 Infant Colic (Carminative)

**Evidence Level: C (traditional evidence; no RCTs specific to *N. cataria*)**

Traditional use as colic remedy for infants in Europe and North America; weak tea administrated to colicky infants in pre-modern medicine; no contemporary RCTs; *Foeniculum vulgare* (fennel) and *Melissa officinalis* (lemon balm) have better evidence for pediatric digestive applications; catnip tea not currently recommended as first-line therapy in clinical practice

### 6.3 Insect Repellent

**Evidence Level: B (controlled laboratory; limited field validation)**

- Isman et al. 2001 and subsequent work: nepetalactone demonstrates repellency against disease-vector mosquitoes in laboratory bioassays, with some studies showing concentrations comparable to DEET
- Field validation: limited; duration of action substantially inferior to DEET; skin safety of topical high-concentration EO not established for repeated use
- Commercial patent activity exists for nepetalactone-based repellent formulations; no approved pharmaceutical product

### 6.4 The 1969 Psychedelic Claim — A Methodological Lesson

A 1969 report in *Science* (Simons & Waller) claimed that smoking catnip produced a cannabis-like psychedelic experience in human subjects. This claim was rapidly and repeatedly tested under controlled conditions and could not be reproduced. The most likely explanation: in 1960s North America, plant material sold as "catnip high" was sometimes not catnip. The episode became an important methodological case study in the necessity of botanical authentication in pharmacological research. The scientific record firmly documents catnip's absence of human psychedelic activity.

### 6.5 Regulatory Status

- **Commission E:** NOT monographed (insufficient evidence); not approved
- **ESCOP:** NOT monographed
- **EMA:** No herbal monograph
- **Safety classification:** Generally considered safe at herb-tea doses; no documented serious human toxicity from normal use; widely consumed as tea without adverse event reports

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

### 7.1 The Most Observed Pharmacological Response in History

The feline behavioral response to nepetalactone — rolling, vocalizing, apparent euphoria — may be the most-observed pharmacological response in human history, witnessed by virtually every cat owner who has presented the plant to a domestic cat. Despite this ubiquity of observation across centuries, the mechanism was not biochemically elucidated until the late 20th century. The genetic basis (autosomal recessive insensitivity) was not characterized until the 21st century. The gap between the universality of observation and the slowness of mechanistic understanding illustrates a recurrent pattern in ethnopharmacology: empirical observation precedes mechanism by centuries.

### 7.2 The Evolutionary Ecology of a Dual-Signaling Molecule

*N. cataria* produces nepetalactone as a constitutive chemical defense against aphid herbivory. The compound triggers aphid alarm pheromone cascades, dispersing aphid colonies before they damage the plant. The same compound acts as a supranormal stimulus for felid olfactory circuits, attracting cats to roll on the plant — dispersing seeds, aerating soil around the plant, and deterring larger herbivores that felid territorial marking discourages. And the same compound repels mosquitoes and other disease-vector insects. A single biosynthesized molecule simultaneously performing three distinct ecological functions — herbivore deterrence (aphids), pollinator attraction (cats as seed dispersers), and vector deterrence (mosquitoes) — represents extraordinary evolutionary economy of chemical synthesis.

### 7.3 The Old English Medical Tradition

Before the widespread naturalization of *N. cataria* in North America (post-European contact), catnip was a fixture of the Anglo-Saxon and medieval English herb garden. The *Lacnunga* and *Bald's Leechbook* reference *nēpte* (catnip) for digestive complaints and febrile illness. The diaphoretic use in children's fevers persisted in English domestic medicine through the 19th century — the tradition of giving weak catnip tea to feverish children was sufficiently widespread that it appears in multiple domestic health manuals of the Victorian era.

### 7.4 The Naturalization Record as Pharmacological Signal

When European settlers brought *N. cataria* to North America, it naturalized rapidly and spread across the continent. Indigenous North American peoples, with no prior relationship to the plant, rapidly identified its carminative and sedative properties and incorporated it into their pharmacopoeia. This rapid adoption of a newly arrived plant for the same applications European herbalists had assigned it — without linguistic or direct cultural transmission of the knowledge — suggests that the plant's pharmacological activity is sufficiently apparent from empirical use to be rediscovered independently.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Indications (Evidence-Based, Within Safety Parameters)

**Mild Insomnia / Nervous Restlessness (Adults):**
Herb tea; Level C evidence; safer alternatives available (*Melissa officinalis* I-478 with better clinical data; *Scutellaria lateriflora* I-476); reasonable option for those preferring *Nepeta*

**Carminative / Infant Colic:**
Weak herb infusion; traditional use; Level C; *Foeniculum vulgare* has superior clinical evidence for infant colic; catnip second-line at best

**Diaphoretic for Fever:**
Traditional use; Level C; supportive role in fever facilitation; adjunctive to appropriate medical management

**Insect Repellent:**
Topical herb preparation (sachet, infused oil); Level B for laboratory repellency; practical field utility limited by short duration; DEET and picaridin remain superior for disease-vector-endemic environments

### 8.2 Preparations and Dosing

**Herb Tea:**
- 1–2 teaspoons (2–4 g) dried aerial parts per 250 mL boiling water; steep 10 minutes; cover vessel to retain volatile components
- Adults: 1–3 cups daily; up to 3 weeks
- Infants for colic (≥3 months): very weak tea (0.5 g in 150 mL); maximum 1–2 times per day; NOT as primary colic therapy; pediatrician consultation first

**Tincture:**
- 1:5 in 45% ethanol; 2–4 mL three times daily (adult)

**Essential Oil (external only):**
- Dilute to 1–2% in carrier oil for topical insect repellent use
- NOT for internal use

**Feline Application:**
- EO: 1 drop on toy or scratching post; dried herb: 0.1–0.5 g per session; safe at normal recreational doses; cats self-regulate via refractory period

### 8.3 Contraindications and Safety

**CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- **Pregnancy:** Historical emmenagogue use in some texts; safety not established; avoid until postpartum as precautionary measure

**DRUG INTERACTIONS:**
- CNS depressants: mild theoretical additive effect from GABA-modulatory flavones; clinical significance low at typical herb-tea doses
- Lithium: diuretic Lamiaceae herbs theoretically affect lithium clearance; monitor if using regular catnip tea alongside lithium therapy

**SAFETY IN CHILDREN:**
- Herb-tea doses considered safe ≥3 months for digestive use; EO NOT for children
- No reports of serious adverse events from tea use

**OVERALL SAFETY:** Excellent; no documented serious adverse events from normal herb use; widely consumed without reported toxicity across multiple cultures and centuries

### 8.4 Energetics

**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi + ☿ Shen (mild)
**Thermal Quality:** Warm, mildly dispersing
**Directional Action:** Outward-bearing (diaphoretic); downward-bearing (carminative)
**Galenic Classification:** Warm and moist; suitable for cold-dry constitutions

### 8.5 Quality and Authentication

**Botanical Authentication:** GC-MS EO analysis: nepetalactone 70–99% confirms *N. cataria*; distinguish *N. × faassenii* (lower nepetalactone; ornamental catmint; some medicinal herbalists accept as equivalent but reduced potency); visual identification reliable: grey-green tomentose leaves + white/pale lilac spotted flowers in terminal spikes

**Field Identification Note:** The distinctive dusty-minty aroma and the tomentose (densely hairy) grey-green leaves readily distinguish *N. cataria* from most other Lamiaceae herbs; the feline behavioral response, if a cat is available for testing, is arguably the most reliable field authentication test for this species in history
