# Peucedanum officinale — Peukedanon, hog's fennel, sulphur weed (Dioscorides 3-92)

## *Peucedanum officinale [Linnaeus], one of several names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-92 (which also prints Peucedanum germanicum [Bauhin], Selinum officinale and Selinum peucedanum — "Hog's Fennel, Sulphur Weed"); a fennel-like root plant whose liquid was collected* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter, and no other tradition on disk is tagged for it. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. Four names are printed and the row takes the Linnaean *Peucedanum officinale*. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-92. PEUKEDANON*, heading at line 7356, the SUGGESTED line at line 7357, a translators' cross-reference "see 3-80" at line 7358, the first part of the text at line 7359 and the end of it at line 7363, in Book Three among the roots (the running head near it reads "ROOTS of AKANTHODA or PRICKLY PLANTS"). A page break (translators' page 471, lines 7360 to 7362) cuts the text between "It removes blockages" and "of the womb". The whole chapter, joined, reads in three parts.

*The plant and its liquid.* "Peucedanum sends out a thin, slender stalk similar to marathrum [3-81]. It has thick hairs in abundance around the root. The flower is yellow; the root black, with a strong scent, very full of liquid. It grows on shady hills. The liquid is taken as follows: the root whilst still tender is cut with a knife, and that which flows from it is presently placed in the shade (for under direct sunlight it is coloured immediately). Gathering it causes headaches and brings on vertigo if you do not rub your nostrils beforehand with rosaceum [1-53], and also wet your head beforehand with it. The root becomes useless having lost its liquid. The stalks and the root have their liquid removed like mandrake and are juiced, but this liquid does not work as well and quickly becomes useless. Sometimes a fluid similar to frankincense is found, already congealed, sticking to the stalks and to the roots. The juice made in Sardinia and Samothracia is the best, with a strong scent, yellowish, warming to the taste."

*The uses.* "It is good rubbed on with vinegar and rosaceum [1-53] for lethargy, mental illness, vertigo, and epilepsy, for those who have suffered for a long time with headaches, for the paralytic, sciatica, and rubbed on with oil and vinegar for the convulsed. The scent is good in general for disorders of the strength. It should be inhaled for womb constriction, revives those who fall in a faint, and drives away snakes. It is good for earache dropped in with of oil of roses, and put into cavities for toothache. It is good (taken with an egg) for coughs. It is effective for hard breathing, griping and windy afflictions. It gently soothes the intestines, lessens the spleen, and wonderfully helps hard labour in childbirth. A decoction (taken as a drink) is effective for disorders and matters related to the bladder and kidneys. It removes blockages of the womb."

*The root, the choice and the names.* "The root is effective for the same purposes, working less effectively. A decoction of this (pounded into small pieces) is taken as a drink. Dried, it cleans foul ulcers, removes scales from bones, and heals old ulcers. It is mixed with stiff ointments and warm compresses. Choose roots that are new, uneaten [by worms], sound, full of scent. The liquid is dissolved in pills with bitter almonds, rue, and warm bread or dill [3-67]. It is also called agrion, or agriophyllon; the Magi call it bonus daemon, some, pinasgelum, and the Romans, stataria."

The chapter is a long one, and what is distinctive is its method rather than its uses: the liquid is a trade product (made best in Sardinia and Samothracia), it is drawn by cutting the tender root, it must be kept in the shade because sunlight colours it, the gatherer is warned of headache and vertigo and told how to guard against them with rose ointment (*rosaceum*, 1-53) on the nostrils and head, the lesser liquid of stalks and roots is said to spoil quickly, and a buyer is told how to choose roots (new, uneaten by worms, sound, full of scent). One use is by scent alone ("The scent is good in general for disorders of the strength", inhaled for womb constriction and fainting) and one is protective ("drives away snakes"). The names given at the end include two Magi names, *bonus daemon* and *pinasgelum*, and the Roman *stataria*. The text gives no deity and no amuletic ritual; the Magi names are printed as names only.

**What the cross-references show.** The line "see 3-80" at line 7358 is the translators' pointer to *3-80. ELAPHOBOSKON* (heading at line 7237), whose SUGGESTED line prints *Peucedanum ostrithium* ("Masterwort, Broad-leaved Hog's Fennel") and carries the translators' note "produces peucedanin — see 3-92". The Codex records the pointer and the note; they are the translators', not Dioscorides', and 3-80 is a separate chapter whose statements are not carried over to 3-92. The text itself also refers to marathrum (3-81) for the stalk's likeness, and to the rose ointment (1-53), mandrake (for the way the liquid is removed), and dill (3-67).

**The chapters around it.** 3-91 *NARTHEX* ends just before the heading (it names sagapenum, "see 3-95"); 3-93 *MELANTHION* follows at line 7364 and is a separate chapter with a "POISONOUS" label in the translators' print. Neither is carried over to this entry.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter id 3-92 and for the names *peucedan*, *peukedanon*, "hog's fennel" and "sulphur weed" found no existing entry on the same chapter. I_1024 (*Athamanta cervaria*) prints "Peucedanum cervaria" and "Hog Fennel, Wild Celery" on its translators' line (lines 7091 to 7092), a different chapter and a different plant that shares the English name "Hog Fennel"; I_136 (*Hedera helix*) names *Peucedanum* only in a TCM analogy (the Chinese drug Qian Hu), unrelated to this chapter. Neither is a duplicate. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The composition of the plant Dioscorides describes was not read. As a general fact, *Peucedanum* is a genus of the carrot family (Apiaceae), a family that commonly carries volatile oils and coumarins, including furanocoumarins that can sensitise the skin to sunlight; the translators' note at 3-80, "produces peucedanin", names one coumarin-type compound in connection with the related plant of that chapter and is not a statement about 3-92. The strong scent and the resin-like fluid that Dioscorides describes are consistent with an aromatic gum-resin, but the chapter names no constituent. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a liquid from a black, strongly scented root, rubbed on with vinegar and rose ointment for lethargy, mental illness, vertigo, epilepsy, long headache, paralysis, sciatica and convulsion; inhaled for womb constriction and faintness; dropped in the ear or put in a tooth cavity; taken with an egg for coughs; and drunk as a decoction for the bladder and kidneys. It also records its use in labour and for blocked wombs, a lesser root decoction and a dried root for ulcers. The Codex gives no protocol for any of these. Several of the conditions are serious, and several uses are on the womb and in childbirth; none is a suitable subject for a self-treatment protocol, and the chapter's own warning that gathering causes headache and vertigo is itself a reason for caution.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives many preparations, with locus (lines 7359 and 7363): the liquid drawn from the cut tender root and kept in the shade; the lesser liquid from stalks and root "juiced"; the liquid "rubbed on with vinegar and rosaceum" and "with oil and vinegar"; the scent inhaled; the liquid "dropped in with oil of roses"; "taken with an egg"; a decoction of the liquid drunk; the root decoction "pounded into small pieces" drunk; the dried root for ulcers; the root mixed into "stiff ointments and warm compresses"; and the liquid "dissolved in pills with bitter almonds, rue, and warm bread or dill". It prints no quantity for any of them. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that many plants of the carrot family contain furanocoumarins, which can cause burns and blistering of skin exposed to sunlight after contact, and that epilepsy, mental illness, paralysis, hard labour and kidney or bladder disease need medical care and not a rubbed-on liquid. The identification is a translators' suggestion among several names, so the chapter does not show that the plant it describes is *Peucedanum officinale*.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one quality of the best liquid: "warming to the taste", and a strong scent and a yellowish colour; it also says the scent is "good in general for disorders of the strength". It prints no cooling, drying or moistening quality for 3-92 and no degree.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. This chapter is a full example of the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: provenance (Sardinia and Samothracia are named as the best), the method and care of gathering (cut the tender root, keep the liquid in the shade, guard the nostrils and head against headache and vertigo), a sensory assay (strong scent, yellowish colour, warming taste), the loss of quality (the root is useless once drained, the lesser liquid spoils quickly), and a buyer's rule for choosing roots (new, uneaten by worms, sound, full of scent). He records two Magi names, *bonus daemon* and *pinasgelum*, without comment. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a medicinal root and liquid, the text gives no further doctrine, deity or subtle body, and the "scent for disorders of the strength" is a medical use. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The useful lesson is Dioscorides' method: to learn where a material is best made, to protect oneself in gathering it, to keep it out of sun, and to judge it by scent, colour and soundness before use. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter itself warns that gathering the liquid causes headaches and vertigo. Plants of this family can cause sun-sensitive skin reactions after contact. The chapter's uses on the womb and in labour are not something to attempt, and its emmenagogue-type and labour uses carry risk in pregnancy. Epilepsy, mental illness, paralysis and kidney or bladder disease need medical care. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion among several names, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
