# Whelk — KUREKES, "buccina", "buccinae" (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 2-5); IONIA, the columellae (2-6)
## *The shell of the whelk, which the translation's gloss gives as "Buccinum species — Whelks"; used burnt, and in one chapter made into quicklime* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Chapter 2-5 of *De Materia Medica*, headed KUREKES, stands in Book 2 among the "Living Creatures" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 4932-4934; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 195, translator's identification "Buccinum species — Whelks"). I read the whole chapter and its neighbours. It is two sentences long and refers back to the chapter before it:

"Burnt buccina do the same as the above but they are more caustic by nature. If anyone fills them with salt and burns them in an unfired clay jar they are good as toothpastes and rubbed on burns. The medicine must be left alone until it grows hard like a shell, for after the burn is healed it falls off of its own accord. Quicklime is made of them as we will show when we come to discuss calx."

"The above" is chapter 2-4, PORPHURA (*purpura*, the shellfish "that yields Tyrian purple dye"): "Burnt purpura dry and clean teeth, repress excrescent flesh, and draw boils and heal them" (lines 4926-4929). So the whelk is read against the purple shell: the same actions, but sharper. The promised discussion of calx is chapter 5-133, ASBESTOS, "Quicklime — Calx Viva" (line 10525; text lines 10526-10532): "Calx viva is made as follows. Take the shells of sea creatures called buccinae [whelks], cover them in the fire or place them into a thoroughly hot oven, and let them remain there all night and the next day. If they have turned very white take them out, but if not, repeat until they are white. Afterwards dip them in cold water and put them into a new pot, corked carefully with cloths. Leave them in there for one night and in the morning take them out, and after it has been refined, put it in jars." The chapter adds that lime is also made from burnt flints or pebble stones and from common marble, "which is also preferred"; that "all calx in common is fiery, biting, burning and scab forming"; that mixed with grease or oil it "becomes digestive, softening and dissipating, forming new skins"; and that "the newest and that which was never wet" is held the most effective. The whelk shell is thus one of three sources Dioscorides gives for lime, and the first he names.

The neighbouring chapter 2-7 (MUAKES, mussels) says that burnt mussels have an effect "similar to the buccina but more peculiar" (line 4940), so the burnt shells of three shellfish are set side by side (purple shell 2-4, whelk 2-5, mussel 2-7). The flesh is mentioned only once, in 2-6.

### Varieties

**Ionia — the columellae of whelks and purple shells** (DMM; chapter 2-6, lines 4935-4937). The translator glosses IONIA as "the columellae of Buccinae [Whelks] and Purpurae" (names.csv line 196). Dioscorides says: "Ionia are the middle parts in the buccinae and purpurae around which they turn in or wind around. It is burnt in the same way, but is more caustic than the buccinae and purpurae because by nature they adhere less. The flesh of the buccina has a good taste and is good for the stomach, but does not soften the bowels." So the variety is the central spiral column of the shell of two animals, the whelk and the purple shell, burnt as the whole shell is, and judged sharper than either. The sentence on the flesh of the whelk, the only mention of the animal as food in the chapters read, sits in this paragraph and not in 2-5. The variety is a part of the shell, not a separate animal. Its name is Greek (*Ionia*) in the text and the translator does not explain it.

*Synthesis of the varieties.* In Dioscorides' own terms the three burnt shell materials are ranked by how caustic they are: the purple shell (2-4) is the base case, the whelk (2-5) "more caustic by nature", and the columella (2-6) more caustic again than both, the reason given being that the columellae "adhere less". The ground for the difference is the part of the shell and, for the whelk against the purple shell, its nature, not colour, place or grade. The text separates whole shell from columella, but lists no other kinds of whelk and no whelk by place of origin. The whole-shell preparations (toothpaste, salve for burns) belong to 2-5; quicklime belongs to 5-133 in the mineral book. The same kind of statement, burnt shell as cleanser and caustic, is made for the mussel (2-7). Setting these side by side is a comparison within one book and not an identification of one material with another.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, a mollusc shell is mostly calcium carbonate with a small organic framework. Burning it strongly turns the carbonate into calcium oxide (quicklime), which is strongly alkaline and caustic and reacts with water to give calcium hydroxide, with heat; this is what Dioscorides' "fiery, biting, burning" and "caustic" describe, and his instruction to burn until "very white", dip in cold water and store sealed matches that chemistry. Filling the shells with salt (sodium chloride) before burning, as 2-5 directs for the toothpaste, adds salt to the ash. The flesh is mollusc protein.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records the burnt whelk shell as a tooth powder and a salve for burns and the burnt columella as a sharper form, and gives the shell as a source of quicklime. The Codex does not recommend any of them. Burnt shell can contain quicklime, which burns skin, mouth and eyes; putting it on a burn or on the teeth is a hazard, not a remedy; and shellfish are an allergen. The whelk's flesh as food is an ordinary matter of seafood safety and is outside this entry. The entry exists to record what Dioscorides says.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations are those quoted. For toothpaste and burns: fill the shells with salt and burn them in an unfired clay jar (2-5). For quicklime: burn the shells in a fire or very hot oven for a night and a day, repeating until very white; dip in cold water; leave a night in a new pot corked with cloths; refine and store in jars (5-133). The columella is burnt the same way (2-6). The only timing given is "all night and the next day". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Calcium carbonate is an antacid and a mild abrasive; calcium oxide is a strong alkali that causes chemical burns, and slaked lime is caustic to skin and eyes. Quicklime has long been made by burning limestone, marble or shell. Shellfish allergy is a common food allergy. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM):** Chapter 2-5 prints one quality for the burnt whelk: "more caustic by nature" than the purple shell. Chapter 5-133 prints for lime in general that it is "fiery, biting, burning and scab forming", and that mixed with grease or oil it is "digestive, softening and dissipating". Chapter 2-6 prints "more caustic" for the columella, and calls the flesh of the whelk "good for the stomach" without softening the bowels. No taste or warming or cooling is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. The Galenic three spirits are later than Dioscorides. What he offers a practitioner is a discipline of attention (provenance, season, sensory assay, adulteration detection), and the whelk chapters show a small part of it. The test for finished lime is by sight: the shells are taken from the fire only "if they have turned very white", and otherwise the burning is "repeated until they are white" (5-133). The best lime is "the newest and that which was never wet". The salt-filled shells in 2-5 are left until the medicine "grows hard like a shell" and falls off of its own accord. These are working assays; they give the whelk no cultivation status, and the uses are therapeutic.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The whelk is a burnt-shell tooth powder, a burn salve and a source of quicklime, and the Codex does not recommend any use. If a cultivator takes anything from the chapters it is the habit of testing a preparation by sight and repeating it until the test is met. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Quicklime and burnt shell:** caustic; burns skin, eyes, mouth and airways; do not put on burns or teeth.
- **Burns:** need cooling and medical assessment; an alkaline paste makes damage worse.
- **Shellfish allergy:** shellfish allergy can be severe; anyone with it should avoid whelk in any form.
- **Wild harvest:** shellfish can concentrate toxins and bacteria; collection is regulated in many places.
