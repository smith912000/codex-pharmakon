# Tanacetum mutellinum — Atong, a krong (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary by column position; Clark 1995 prints a.krong with other Latin names)

## *Tanacetum mutellinum, as the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints it on line 5076, the seventh scientific name of its "A" block, which column order sets against "Atong" (transliteration "akrong"); Clark 1995 prints the same Tibetan name as a.krong (line 1036) with five other Latin names and does not print Tanacetum mutellinum, so the identity is the glossary's alone and rests on position* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and on a binomial that only one of the two editions prints.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta text folders on disk for *Tanacetum* and *mutellinum* found no hit. The row was added from a printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 238) the row is keyed by the **scientific name** "Tanacetum mutellinum", with the locus "Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (line 5076)" and the note "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". The line prints a single binomial, so the key is also the first and only name on its line. The Tibetan name *Atong* is reached from this row by column position and by a transliteration, as set out below. Clark's entry for the same Tibetan name (as the Codex reads it) has **no row** under that name in `names.csv`: the file holds no *a.krong* row, so the Clark passage was found by following the name.

**Identity doubt.** This is a weak identification, and the reasons are in the texts. The glossary prints *Tanacetum mutellinum* by position only. Clark prints *a.krong* with *Arenaria capillaris* Poir, *Stellaria palustris* Ehrh. (common type), *Gypsophila dahurica*, "Artemesia spice" (so printed) and *Artemesia hedinii* (black type) (line 1036): a pink-family group and an *Artemisia* group, with no *Tanacetum*. Clark prints "Tanacetum sp." once, in the entry for a different drug, *a.byag* (line 1023), whose Men-Tsee-Khang counterpart "Ajak" the glossary sets against *Pyrethrum tatsienense* (the subject of I_1341). The Codex therefore cannot tell whether the glossary's *Tanacetum mutellinum* is a deliberate identification of *Atong*, a slip for a neighbouring name, or a second plant under a shared name. Clark's preface says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" (line 83) and that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect" (line 68). The Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note (line 686) says that its botanical terms rest on the information of its own Materia Medica Department. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "A" block).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (the headings "Phonetic", "Transliteration" and "Scientific Name" are at lines 5051 to 5055). The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. Eight phonetic names are printed (lines 5059 to 5063): "Abikha", "Adre", "Agaru", "Ajak", "Arura", "Asho genda", "Atong" and "Awa". The transliterations (lines 5066 to 5071) are "a bi Sha", "a'bras", "a ga ru", "abyag", "a ru ra", "a sho gan+d+ha", "akrong" and "aba" ("a sho gan+d+ha" and "akrong" share line 5070, and "aba" is on line 5071). The scientific names (lines 5073 to 5077) are eight: "Fritillaria delavayi", "Mangifera indica", "Aquilaria agallocha", "Pyrethrum tatsienense" (all four on line 5073), "Terminalia chebula" (5074), "Mirabilis himalaica" (5075), **"Tanacetum mutellinum"** (5076) and "Lloydia serotina" (5077). Counted in order, "Atong" is the seventh phonetic name, "akrong" the seventh transliteration and *Tanacetum mutellinum* the seventh scientific name. The count is checked at three points: "Adre" (second) falls on *Mangifera indica*, whose transliteration *a'bras* the English column renders "Mango"; "Ajak" (fourth) falls on *Pyrethrum tatsienense*, a close relative of the names Clark's *a.byag* line (1023) prints, *Chrysanthemum tatsiense* and *Pyrethrum barbigera*; and "Arura" (fifth) falls on *Terminalia chebula* and "Chebulic myrobalan". The English column (lines 5148 to 5151) prints seven names for the eight: "Snake's head lilies", "Mango", "Eagle or aloe wood", "Ragwort", "Chebulic myrobalan", "Ashwagandha" and "Lioydia" (so printed), the last of which by its spelling goes with *Lloydia serotina*, so *Atong* appears to have no English name. The sixth place is a known doubt (I_1320: *Mirabilis himalaica* against "Ashwagandha"). The transliteration "akrong" is Clark's *a.krong* in another scheme, and the Codex reads the phonetic "Atong" as the same name. The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position and transliteration. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3438).* Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225; colophon at line 3583), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428): "Atong cures hot disorder of the lungs." The sentence stands between "Gongthokpa cures meat poisoning and disturbed hot disorder" and "Tsharbong cures fever of the larynx and lung disorders". The index lists "Atong 208, 221" (line 7260). The sentence gives no part used, no preparation and no taste.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, chapter 21 (line 3611).* In the chapter "The Group and the Method of Compounding Medicine" (contents, line 229; chapter 20 closes at line 3583), the groups of medicines that cure specific disorders are listed. Line 3611, on page 221 (the page number 220 is printed at line 3607), reads: "Chugang, Shingngar, Gundrum, Tarbu, Ruta, Gadhur, Atong, Solo karpo and Sugdra are the group of medicines that cure disorders of the lungs." The two Men-Tsee-Khang passages agree: the drug is a lung drug.

*Clark 1995, the same Tibetan name (read through the name, not the binomial).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry stands after *sgong.thog.pa* (line 1035), and a page number (104) stands in the middle of it (line 1037), so its text is on lines 1036 to 1039:

"a.krong (white type): Arenaria capillaris Poir: Stellaria palustris Ehrh. (common type): Gypsophila dahurica: Artemesia spice: Artemesia hedinii (black type): [is of four types, i.e., mkhan.pa.a.krong, phur.mo.a.krong and two types of rtsa.a.krong explained below which are glossy with dewdrops. The black type has a small stalk, thin petioles and blue scattered leaves with small indentations. The white type has a thick leafstalk, large leaves and yellow flowers. The tree of the same name has a thin petiole and a profusion of small round scattered leaves. All types] cure lung fever."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The four types, the black and white descriptions and "the tree of the same name" are therefore the commentary layer. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the five Latin names with Clark's labels, which are his identifications and not the root text's, and "cure lung fever". Clark's name index lists *a.krong* (line 1772) and the four sub-names *mkhan.pa.a.krong* (line 1678), *phur.mo.a.krong* (line 1717) and *rtsa.a.krong* (line 1732). Clark's entry for *mkhan.pa*, mugwort, says the plant "is also known as a.krong (dkar.mo)" (line 1078). Clark's chapter 21 list of the medicines for lung disorders (line 1213) includes "Gypsophila dahurica (Artemesia)", which is one of the Latin names of this entry.

The editions agree in role and in the organ: "cure lung fever" in Clark and "cures hot disorder of the lungs" in Men-Tsee-Khang. The Codex reads *a.krong* and *Atong* as one drug from that agreement, the transliteration and the order; neither text says so. The two translators' words ("fever", "hot disorder") are not treated as one term. The Codex's own list of rGyud-bzhi names (`_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv`) has no *Tanacetum* row. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Tanacetum mutellinum*, *Atong*, *a.krong* and *akrong* found no entry on this drug. **I_276** (*Arenaria kansuensis*) reads *a.krong* only to say that *Arenaria capillaris*, the one *Arenaria* in the Tibetan texts, is one of its identifications and is a different species from its own. **I_1344** (*Rhodiola saera*, *sro.lo*) reads *Gypsophila dahurica* under another Tibetan name, and **I_1296** (*Geranium pratense*) quotes Clark's line 1213 that also names it. **I_1320** (*Mirabilis himalaica*), **I_1341** (*Pyrethrum tatsienense*) and **I_1283** (*Fritillaria delavayi*) read the same "A" block of the glossary for their own binomials. **I_759** (*Tanacetum parthenium*) and **I_652** (*Tanacetum vulgare*) are entries on other species of the genus from other traditions. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Tanacetum* is a genus of the daisy family (Asteraceae), that many of its species are aromatic and contain essential-oil terpenoids and sesquiterpene lactones, and that the pink-family and *Artemisia* names Clark prints belong to other plant groups; these are statements about the genera and not about the Tibetan drug. The lines read give no composition for it, and nothing is stated here about what it contains. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that cures "hot disorder of the lungs" (Men-Tsee-Khang, lines 3438 and 3611) and "lung fever" (Clark, line 1039). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only through a glossary count and a transliteration, and they are not Codex protocols. Lung disease and fever need medical care. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used (the commentary's "tree of the same name" is a description and not a part), no quantity, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it cures lung fever, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says it cures hot disorder of the lungs and places it in the group of lung medicines. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names (*a.krong*, "Atong", "akrong", *Tanacetum mutellinum*) found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in Men-Tsee-Khang's rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23, colophon at line 3950) or in Clark's chapter 23, "Normal health" (lines 1264 to 1276). Of the herb entries of Clark's chapter 20 that mention essence extraction (lines 971, 1043, 1078, 1103, 1121 and 1144), one, the entry for mugwort *mkhan.pa* (line 1078), says that its white subspecies "is recommended in essence-extraction compounds" and that mugwort "is also known as a.krong (dkar.mo)". That is a statement about *mkhan.pa*, one of the four types in the *a.krong* entry (line 1036), and the Codex does not carry it to *a.krong* or to *Atong*, because the entry for *a.krong* itself prints no such claim. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a lung-fever drug with no preparation and no dose, whose Latin name rests on a column count and differs from every name Clark prints for it. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Lung disease and fever need medical care; the identity is open, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. It is a general fact that some members of the daisy family cause allergic skin reactions in sensitive people, which is a further reason to leave the plant alone while its identity is unsettled. No conservation information and no safety information for pregnancy were read. [USER: verify]
