### Origin & Tradition

The fig is among the first plants domesticated by humanity — archaeological evidence from Jericho (9400–9200 BCE) shows domesticated fig cultivation predating cereal agriculture, making it potentially the first deliberately cultivated plant. The fig appears in the Garden of Eden narrative (Adam and Eve's covering), in Buddhist tradition as the Bodhi tree species related to fig (*Ficus religiosa*), and in Greek mythology as the tree of Demeter. Its consistent medicinal documentation across six traditions for 5,000+ years reflects genuine pharmacological value: ficin (proteolytic enzyme), fig fiber (prebiotic), polyphenols (anti-inflammatory), and high potassium-calcium content (post-exercise electrolyte).

**EP — Egyptian (Dbt — documented in Ebers Papyrus):** Figs (*dbt*) appear extensively in Egyptian medical papyri. The Ebers Papyrus documents fig preparations for: constipation and bowel regulation (the laxative fiber); wound healing (fresh fig sap — ficin enzyme); digestive conditions; skin conditions; and as a drug vehicle (the sweet, sticky fig paste). Egyptian doctors used fig compresses for abscesses — the ficin enzyme's digestive action on necrotic tissue providing debridement.

**AT — Assyrian (Tittu — 𒁾 — the fig tree; documented in cuneiform):** The fig tree (*tittu*) is one of the most mentioned fruit trees in Mesopotamian texts — both medical and agricultural. Nineveh tablets document fig preparations as nutritive tonics, laxative preparations, and as vehicles for other medicinals. Fig sap (*tittu ša irī*) appears in skin and wound preparations.

**DMM — De Materia Medica (Σῦκον — Sykon):** Dioscorides provides comprehensive fig coverage in Book I. He distinguishes the ripe fruit (nutritive, laxative, detoxifying); the unripe fruit (astringent, hemostatic); the bark (purging); the leaf (for skin conditions, insect stings); and the milky sap (*opós* — the latex) as a digestive/vermicide. He notes the fig as "good for old men" — the longevity food of antiquity. He documents its use with thyme and cumin for snake bites (the proteolytic enzyme destroying venom protein).

**CM — Canon of Medicine (تين — Tīn):** Ibn Sina classifies ripe fig as warm in the first degree and moist. Primary indications: nutritive tonic; laxative (fig and honey is his standard mild laxative); liver tonic (*kabd*); expectorant (the fig decoction for chest conditions); anthelmintic; and notably the fresh fig latex for removing warts and corns (the ficin proteolytic mechanism). He specifically recommends figs for "those doing physical labor who need rapid strength restoration" — a direct Kenetica parallel.

**SS — Ayurveda (उदुम्बर — Udumbara):** The fig and its Indian relatives (*Ficus glomerata/racemosa*, *Ficus benghalensis* — Banyan) are extensively documented. Classification for *Ficus carica*:
- **Rasa:** Madhura (sweet) + Kashaya (astringent)
- **Virya:** Shita (cooling)
- **Vipaka:** Madhura
- **Dosha action:** Pitta-Vata shamaka; Kapha-increasing
- **Primary actions:** Brinhana (nourishing/anabolic); Balya (strength-building); Stanya-janana (galactagogue); Vrana-ropana (wound-healing); Raktapitta-hara (anti-hemorrhagic via unripe form)
- *Pañcavalkala* (Five bark preparation) includes *Udumbara* bark — a classical Ayurvedic wound healing wash

**SBJ/TCM (无花果 — Wúhuāguǒ — "flower-less fruit"):** Fig entered TCM pharmacopoeia with the Silk Road. Classification: sweet, neutral-slightly cool; Lung, Stomach, Large Intestine channels. Primary actions: moistens Lung (dry cough, hoarse voice); tonifies Spleen-Stomach; laxative (lubricates intestines); anti-inflammatory (throat, mouth ulcers). The Chinese name *Wúhuāguǒ* ("fruit without flower") reflects the fig's botanical peculiarity — the flowers are enclosed within the developing fruit (syconium), invisible externally.

### Active Components

**Enzymes:**
- **Ficin** — the cysteine protease latex enzyme; primary bioactive in fresh fig sap; proteolytic (breaks down protein — debrides wounds, digests necrotic tissue, kills intestinal worms through protein digestion); anti-inflammatory (via direct proteolysis of inflammatory mediators); anthelmintic

**Polyphenols:**
- **Anthocyanins** — cyanidin-3-rutinoside (primary in dark-skinned varieties); antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; the deep purple-red color
- **Chlorogenic acid** — the primary phenolic acid; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; blood glucose modulating
- **Rutin** — the primary flavonoid; capillary-strengthening; anti-inflammatory; antioxidant
- **Quercetin, kaempferol, luteolin** — anti-inflammatory; antioxidant

**Fiber:**
- **Pectin** (soluble fiber) — 3–5% of dry weight; prebiotic; gel-forming; cholesterol-lowering; glucose-moderating
- **Cellulose, hemicellulose** (insoluble fiber) — laxative; bulking
- **Total fiber** — 10–12g/100g dried figs; among the highest dietary fiber of any fruit

**Minerals:**
- **Calcium** — 162mg/100g (dried); significant plant calcium source
- **Potassium** — 680mg/100g (dried); electrolyte replacement
- **Magnesium** — 68mg/100g (dried)
- **Iron** — 2mg/100g (dried)

**Other:**
- **Psoralen, bergapten** — furanocoumarins in fig latex; photosentizing (topical latex + UV causes phototoxic dermatitis — relevant contraindication); in ancient medicine used for vitiligo (psoralen is the basis for PUVA therapy)
- **Sugars** — 47–57% dried weight (primarily glucose, fructose); rapid energy

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**1. Post-training nutritive recovery food ([BUILD] — primary)**
Dried figs are among the densest whole-food sources of training-relevant micronutrients: 3–4 dried figs (50g) provide ~300mg calcium, ~340mg potassium, ~34mg magnesium, significant iron, 5g fiber, and 30g carbohydrate for glycogen replenishment. This makes the fig-date combination (see I_089) the classical dual-electrolyte recovery food of the ancient training world.

**2. Intestinal motility and gut reset ([RESET])**
The high pectin and insoluble fiber content provides both soluble (prebiotic, gut microbiome) and insoluble (laxative, transit time) fiber effects. The standard constipation remedy across all six traditions is the simple preparation of 5–10 soaked figs eaten on an empty stomach — the fiber-ficin combination both stimulating and facilitating transit.

**3. Throat and respiratory demulcent ([CYCLE-OFF] recovery)**
The sweet-moist action of fig in TCM and Ayurveda for dry cough and hoarse voice maps to the mucilaginous pectin coating of the mucous membranes. For practitioners recovering from competitions that involve vocalization, respiratory exertion (combat sports, rowing), or seasonal illness, fig decoction provides a food-based mucosal repair tool.

**4. Ficin wound care (topical)**
Fresh fig latex applied to warts, corns, and verrucae provides keratolytic (protein-digesting) debridement via ficin. The traditional application described in De Materia Medica and Canon of Medicine has scientific basis: ficin is a validated proteolytic enzyme, though the phototoxic furanocoumarin content requires caution in sun-exposed areas.

### Preparation & Dosing

**Soaked dried figs (standard daily Jing-building food):**
5–8 dried figs soaked overnight in water; eat in the morning on empty stomach; drink the soaking water. This is the Ayurvedic *anjeer* morning tonic — considered one of the best natural treatments for constipation and anemia.

**Fig decoction (therapeutic — respiratory/laxative):**
10–15 dried figs simmered in 750mL water 20–30 minutes; strain; drink the broth for dry cough, hoarse voice, mild constipation. Traditional Unani (*Arq-e-Anjeer*) preparation.

**Fig paste (compound preparation vehicle):**
Blended dried figs into paste; used as the base for compound electuaries (herbal formulas mixed in fruit paste) — the ancient *electuary* format. Mix with ground seeds, herbs, honey for complete recovery formulas.

**Fresh fig latex (topical for warts/corns):**
Apply fresh white latex from broken stem or unripe fig directly to wart; cover; apply daily for 2–4 weeks. Do not use in sun-exposed areas.

### Scientific Correlation

- **Antioxidant (polyphenols):** Vinson JA et al. (2005): dried figs showed high total phenolic content and antioxidant capacity; anthocyanin content in dark-skinned varieties significant; anti-inflammatory via COX inhibition
- **Laxative (fiber/ficin):** Degen LP, Phillips SF (1996): fig fiber accelerated intestinal transit time in clinical study; Dahl WJ et al. (2003): fig paste significantly improved bowel frequency and stool consistency in elderly subjects
- **Ficin anthelmintic:** Gonzalez EJ et al. (2015): ficin demonstrated significant anthelmintic activity against intestinal nematodes in vitro; proteolytic mechanism confirmed
- **Antidiabetic (chlorogenic acid):** Mopuri R et al. (2015): *Ficus carica* leaf extract significantly reduced blood glucose and improved insulin sensitivity in diabetic rodent model
- **Bone health:** Calcium-vitamin K combination in fig supports bone mineral density; Zohdi V et al. (2015): fig extract protected against osteoporosis in ovariectomized rats

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian (EP).** The Ebers Papyrus records fig (*dbt*) for constipation, wound care, and as a drug vehicle — an indication-organised corpus that says what was done, not what fig does to the *ka/ba/akh/ib* person. The fig's heavy sacred and mythic weight (the Eden covering, the tree of Demeter) is *symbolism*, and symbolism is not a cultivation status. **(c) absent** — no vital-constitution framing is attested.

**Assyrian (AT).** The fig tree (*tittu*) is among the most-mentioned fruit trees in the Mesopotamian record, documented as nutritive tonic, laxative and vehicle, with fig sap in skin preparations. Under the standing blocking warning on Assyrian identifications and tablet references, no cultivation status can be asserted beyond this therapeutic-dietary record. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, so no cultivation status is available to record. He calls the fig "good for old men" — a dietetic longevity observation, not a doctrine of vital refinement — and offers the cultivator a **discipline of attention**: ripe fruit (nutritive, laxative) versus unripe (astringent, hemostatic) versus the milky latex (*opós*, vermicide/keratolytic). The fruit/latex distinction is the practice here, not a vital grade. **(c) absent**, with the discipline of attention as the real offering.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** Ibn Sina classes ripe fig warm in the first degree and moist, a nutritive tonic and mild laxative, recommending it for "those doing physical labor who need rapid strength restoration." The Unani cultivation idea proper is the conservation of *innate heat* and *radical moisture*; a warm-moist nutritive food is broadly restorative in that frame, but Ibn Sina assigns no formal cultivation status — this is dietetics, not a graded vital assignment. **(b) framed** — claimed and nutritive, no cultivation status recorded.

**Ayurveda (SS).** Fig's guna profile is genuinely nourishing — *brinhana* (anabolic), *balya* (strength-building), *madhura vipaka* — which sits adjacent to the *rasayana* rejuvenation discipline. But no formal *rasayana* designation for *Ficus carica* is attested in the sources retrieved, and the classical *Udumbara* of the texts is generally the related Indian species (*F. racemosa*/*glomerata*, e.g. in *Pañcavalkala* as a bark wash), not the common fig. **(b) framed** — the *rasayana*/*ojas* frame and the nourishing profile are real, but a cultivation status for this species is not attested; do not read the related-species record onto *F. carica*.

**Chinese (TCM).** Fig reached China along the Silk Road, long after the *Shennong Bencao Jing* was closed — so it carries **no *san pin* 三品 grade in the classic**, there being no classical entry to grade. Its Chinese record is Ming-era (Li Shizhen's *Bencao Gangmu*, 1596), a late compilation documenting therapeutic actions — moistens Lung, tonifies Spleen-Stomach, lubricates the intestines — not a cultivation grade. **(c) absent** for the classic; **(b) framed** for the later text, therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator uses fig as a nutritive recovery food — the *brinhana*/*balya* profile and the mineral density are real and dietetic, the soaked-fig morning tonic above being the honest expression of it. But that is *food*, not a formal cultivation-status assignment in any tradition, and the fig's sacred symbolism carries no claim about the vital constitution. The latex is a topical keratolytic-vermicide, a therapeutic agent kept off sun-exposed skin — not an ingested cultivation substance. Distinguish the two: eat the fruit, respect the latex.

### Contraindications

- **Fig latex phototoxicity** — the furanocoumarin content (psoralen, bergapten) in fresh fig latex causes phototoxic dermatitis when applied topically and exposed to UV; avoid sun exposure after topical fig latex use; this reaction does not occur with ripe fruit consumption
- **Diabetes / high sugar load** — dried figs contain very high sugar content (47–57%); count in total carbohydrate load; consume whole (fiber matrix) to moderate glycemic impact
- **Kapha constitution** — the sweet, moist, heavy nature of figs aggravates Kapha; obesity, congestion, diabetes predisposition — moderate use
- **Oxalate kidney stones** — figs contain moderate oxalate; limit in those with recurrent calcium oxalate kidney stones
- **Ficin allergy** — rare; cross-reactivity with latex allergy (latex-fruit syndrome); screen if latex sensitive
