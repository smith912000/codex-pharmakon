### Origin & Tradition

Poison hemlock is the most literarily famous of all plant poisons in the Western tradition — the *koneion* that Athens used as a means of judicial execution and that, in 399 BCE, killed **Socrates.** Plato's *Phaedo* gives the account that fixed hemlock in the cultural imagination: the philosopher drinking the cup, walking until his legs grew heavy, lying down as a creeping numbness and coldness rose from the feet upward toward the heart, conscious to near the end. That clinical picture — an **ascending paralysis** with preserved consciousness, ending in respiratory failure — is pharmacologically accurate and remains one of antiquity's most precise poisoning descriptions.

Dioscorides places hemlock among the poisons of Book IV and is unambiguous about its lethality, while also recording the ancient physicians' cautious external and minute use of it as a powerful **cooling, sedating and pain-deadening** agent — for inflamed swellings, for "hot" tumours and indurations of the breast, and as an anaphrodisiac to quell sexual excitement. This double character — deadly internal poison, potent topical sedative-anodyne — defined hemlock's place in pharmacy. Through the medieval and into the early-modern period it persisted as a dangerous anodyne and "discutient" for hard, painful swellings and as an ingredient of the soporific sponges and witches'-salve traditions, always handled as a poison.

Hemlock must be sharply distinguished from **water hemlock** (*Cicuta*) — a different, even more violently convulsant umbellifer — and from the many edible umbellifers (parsley, chervil, wild carrot) it dangerously resembles. The purple-blotched ("spotted," *maculatum*) stem and the foetid, mousy smell when crushed are its classic field marks. Its identity as the lethal umbellifer of the cup is the anchor of this entry.

### Active Components

- **Coniine** — the principal toxic alkaloid, a simple piperidine alkaloid (and one of the first alkaloids to be chemically synthesised). It acts on **nicotinic acetylcholine receptors**, producing a depolarising neuromuscular block — initial stimulation then paralysis — which clinically manifests as the ascending muscular paralysis culminating in **respiratory failure** (paralysis of the diaphragm). Consciousness is preserved until hypoxia supervenes — the *Phaedo* picture.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Recorded as **historical and archival material only.** Hemlock has no place in self-directed practice; its lethality is too great and its margin too small for any casual use.

1. **Judicial poison (historical).** The defining role — the Athenian execution agent; the death of Socrates. Documented, not practised.
2. **Topical sedative-anodyne for hot swellings (historical/specialist).** Dioscorides and later physicians used hemlock externally on inflamed, painful indurations and "hot" tumours for its deadening, cooling action — a dangerous specialist use.
3. **Anaphrodisiac (historical).** Recorded to quell sexual excitement — part of the cooling-sedating reputation. Documented, not recommended.
4. **Antispasmodic / soporific (historical pharmacy).** A constituent of soporific and pain-deadening preparations; abandoned as far too dangerous.
5. **Neuromuscular pharmacology (historical interest).** Coniine's nicotinic action is of historical pharmacological significance (an early synthesised alkaloid and a classic neuromuscular toxin).

### Preparation & Dosing

**No dose of any kind is given. Hemlock is a lethal poison with a small margin and no field antidote.**

- **Internal use — none.** Historical anodyne tinctures and extracts existed and are entirely obsolete; no figures are reproduced.
- **Topical (historical/specialist only).** Even external use risks absorption; not a home preparation.
- **Identification safety.** The principal practical message is *avoidance and correct identification* — distinguish from edible umbellifers (parsley, chervil, wild carrot) and from water hemlock (*Cicuta*). Note the spotted stem and mousy odour.

Safety framing: hemlock poisoning is a medical emergency — ascending paralysis and respiratory failure; management is supportive/ventilatory intensive care. There is no specific antidote.

### Contraindications

- **All internal use — absolute prohibition.** Lethal; small margin; no antidote.
- **Pregnancy — absolute.** Teratogenic (skeletal malformation documented in livestock) and lethal.
- **Misidentification risk — paramount.** Resembles edible umbellifers and the even-more-toxic water hemlock; foraging error is the leading cause of human poisoning.
- **Skin/mucosal absorption.** Even handling and topical use risk absorption.
- **No unsupervised use of any kind.** Archival/historical framing throughout.
