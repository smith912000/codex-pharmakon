# Smelt — SMARIS (Dioscorides 2-30)
## *Osmerus eparlanus — Smelt, in the edition's own "suggested" gloss; the text calls the material "a salted smaris"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides treats the smaris in Book Two of *De Materia Medica*, the book that he introduces as covering "living creatures" and then honey, milk, animal fat, cereals and pot-herbs (the book's preface, line 4904), in the Osbaldeston and Wood 2000 English edition (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). The chapter is **2-30. SMARIS** (heading at line 5059), with the edition's gloss "SUGGESTED: Osmerus eparlanus — Smelt" (line 5060) and the text at line 5061. The chapter reads in full:

"The head of a salted smaris burnt stops abnormal growths of (protuberant) flesh, stops gangrenous ulceration; and removes corns and warty abnormal growths. The flesh of this as well as all salted meat is good for one touched by a scorpion, and for one bitten by a dog."

The edition indexes it as "Smelt 195" (line 17607) and "SMARIS 195" (line 12605), and the project's name list (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, locus "2-30 (line 5059)") carries the same gloss. The chapter sits among the fishes: the entry before it is the sheath fish (*Silurus*, 2-29) and the one after is *Mainis* (2-31), glossed "small sea-fish, eaten by the poor". The two sentences of 2-30 are the whole of what Dioscorides says. He gives no description of the fish, no place where it is caught, no season, and no way of telling a good one from a bad.

On the identity, the word "SUGGESTED" in the edition's own gloss marks it as the editor's guess, and the text cannot confirm it. A smelt in the modern sense (*Osmerus*) is a fish of northern and cool waters, and Dioscorides is writing in the Greek-speaking Mediterranean; whether the fish he calls *smaris* is what the gloss names is therefore open. The matter is stated here for the user to check and is not resolved. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The material is a fish head, burnt, and fish flesh preserved in salt. Fish flesh is mostly water and protein, with fat and, in small fish eaten with bones, calcium; salting adds a great deal of sodium chloride. A burnt head would leave mostly ash, which is mineral matter, mainly calcium phosphate from bone, and charred remains. No active principle is claimed by the text.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced uses are external: burnt salted fish head for excess flesh growths ("abnormal growths of (protuberant) flesh"), for "gangrenous ulceration", and for corns and warts, and the salted flesh for the sting of a scorpion and the bite of a dog. The Codex offers no protocol. Gangrene and the bites of animals are medical emergencies; warts and corns have simple, safe modern treatments.

### Preparation & Dosing

The one preparation in the chapter is that the head of a *salted* smaris is burnt (line 5061). The text does not say how the ash is applied (dry, in oil, or in a dressing), how often, or for how long. The flesh "of this as well as all salted meat" is used for the sting and the bite, again without a method. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Ash and salt are irritants and do not treat warts, corns or infected tissue; gangrene needs urgent surgical and antibiotic care. Salted fish flesh laid on a bite wound adds salt and bacteria and is not a treatment for a dog bite, which needs washing, medical assessment, and tetanus and rabies prevention. Eating heavily salted fish is a recognised dietary risk factor for high blood pressure. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** None is given. Chapter 2-30 prints no quality (warming, cooling, astringent or the like) for the smaris. The neighbouring chapter on the sheath fish (2-29) says that salted it "yields no nourishment" while it "cleans the arteries and makes the voice clear" (line 5053), but that is said of another fish and is not applied to this one. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied here. What he offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, the detection of adulteration. Chapter 2-30 gives none of these for the smaris. It gives two sentences, and the material is itself a preserved product (salted fish) whose preparation is not described. No Egyptian, Assyrian or other framing is imported.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a salted fish used for skin growths and bites, with no preparation beyond burning the head. If the chapter has any lesson it is the plain one that the physician of this tradition used ordinary, cheap foods as medicines, and that the identity of the fish is the first thing to question.

### Contraindications

- **Wounds, gangrene and bites:** gangrene, spreading redness, fever, and animal or scorpion bites need clinical care; do not rely on this chapter.
- **Skin lesions:** do not burn or apply caustic material to warts, corns or unexplained skin growths; a growing or changing lesion should be examined for skin cancer.
- **Salt:** preserved fish is very salty and is best avoided in hypertension, heart or kidney disease. [USER: verify]
- **Hygiene:** salted or dried fish of unknown handling can carry bacteria and parasites.
- **Identity, legal and conservation status:** the species is the edition's suggestion only, and some smelt species are protected in places; wild fisheries are regulated. [USER: verify]
