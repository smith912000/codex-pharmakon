# Sheep brain — rGyud-bzhi, "Sheep brain" (brain, one of the thirteen animal-derived medicines)
## *Brain of the sheep; the translator prints only "Sheep brain", with no Tibetan name and no binomial in the line read* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Sheep brain is entered in the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, the chapter on the powers of medicines, in Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). It falls in the section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), whose thirteen headings are "horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). Under the heading "Brain" (line 1181) the whole list, in line 1182, reads: "Goat brain: unites severed ligaments and tendons. Sheep brain: cures dizziness and vertigo. Brain of game animals (e.g. deer): halts diarrhoea. Rabbit brain: cures colic. Human brain: reduces swellings and cures lymph disorders." Sheep brain stands second of five, and it carries no bracketed comment, no count of names and no Tibetan name. Clark says in his introduction (line 80) that he has "interpolated liberally from the Blue Aquamarine commentary"; the bracketed passages elsewhere in the chapter look like such interpolations, but the lines read do not state the bracket convention outright. The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3548) has the same list: "Goat brain heals tom [torn] ligaments and tendons. Sheep brain cures dizziness and reeling. The brain of herbivorous wild animals stops diarrhea. Rabbit brain cures dysentery." The two translations agree on the sheep ("dizziness and vertigo" in Clark, "dizziness and reeling" in Men-Tsee-Khang) and differ over the rabbit ("colic" or "dysentery"), which is not this entry's concern. The Tibetan wording is not in the files read, so which rendering of the sheep line is closer cannot be said. (The file prints "tom", an OCR error for "torn".)

The sheep is named among the "Domestic animals" in the meat classification (Clark, line 672; Men-Tsee-Khang, line 2917). In the treatment of wind disorders Clark lists a soup "of one-year-old (finely-ground) sheep's head" (lines 183-186) and Men-Tsee-Khang a "broth from aged sheep head" (line 1116). The head is not the brain, neither text says the brain is included, and the soup is not applied to this entry.

### Active Components

Brain is soft nervous tissue. It is high in water and in lipids, including cholesterol and phospholipids (the lipids of cell membranes and myelin), with proteins and smaller amounts of vitamins and minerals. No composition specific to this entry was read, and no active principle is claimed by the sources.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is one indication in a materia medica list: sheep brain cures "dizziness and vertigo" (Clark) or "dizziness and reeling" (Men-Tsee-Khang). The Codex offers no protocol. Dizziness and vertigo can have causes that need prompt care (a stroke, an inner-ear disorder, a drop in blood pressure or blood sugar, some medicines), and eating animal brain carries real risk (see Contraindications). The Tantra gives no preparation, dose or route in these lines. The list pairs the brain of different animals with different complaints; that pairing is the tradition's own organising principle and is recorded as such, with no reason given in the lines read.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Vertigo (a false sense of movement) and dizziness are symptoms, not diagnoses, and the cause decides the treatment; eating the brain of an animal does not treat any of them. Sheep carry a prion disease, scrapie, in which an abnormal protein accumulates in the brain and spinal cord, and many countries classify sheep skull, brain and spinal cord as tissue that must be kept out of the human food chain. Raw or undercooked sheep offal can also carry bacteria and parasites. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* Neither translation read gives sheep brain any cultivation framing. It is a one-line indication in a list of animal brains, and the complaint named is dizziness. The frame around it is the tradition's own: the project's cultivation spec records that the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) are derived from the three poisons and that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root. That point comes from the spec and not from any line about sheep brain. No source read attaches sheep brain to *bcud len* (essence extraction), and there is no formal Tibetan category of substances for subtle-body practice. Clark's remark (line 57) that "only very few animal products are used" nowadays is a note on usage, not on status.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a single indication in a list of animal brains, with no preparation and no dose, and eating sheep brain carries risk with no supported benefit. The honest use of the entry is as a record of what the Tantra says.

### Contraindications

- **Not a treatment for dizziness or vertigo:** sudden vertigo with weakness, speech trouble, severe headache or double vision is an emergency; persistent dizziness needs assessment. Do not rely on this line.
- **Neural tissue and prion disease:** sheep brain and spinal tissue can carry scrapie, and many countries restrict their use as food; whether the Tantra's physicians knew of any such risk is not addressed in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Infection:** raw or undercooked offal can carry bacteria and parasites, including tapeworm cysts in sheep; do not handle sick animals.
- **Legal and animal-welfare status:** obtaining the brain requires slaughter; domestic sheep are livestock, but the sale and use of animal tissue is regulated in many places. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** killing an animal for a substance with no supported benefit is hard to justify, and Clark's omens section (line 431) values saving animals from slaughter; that passage is about merit and omens, not about medicine.
