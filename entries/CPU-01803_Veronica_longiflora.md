# Veronica longiflora — yu.gu.shing, Yugu shing (rGyud-bzhi; Clark 1995 prints it first of five Latin names; the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary sets Sambucus adnata against the name by position)

## *Veronica longiflora, the first of five Latin names Clark 1995 prints for the Tibetan name yu.gu.shing on line 1019 (then Veronica incana, Senecio dianthus Franch (white type), Cacalia quinquelobus and Sambucus adnata Wall (black type of elder)); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints only Sambucus adnata against "Yugu shing". The Codex keeps the first name because the spine row is keyed to it, and the entry stands for the Tibetan drug* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and on a binomial that only one of the two editions prints.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta text folders on disk for *Veronica* found no hit in the primary texts. The row was added from a printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 138) the row is keyed by the **Tibetan name** *yu.gu.shing*, with the identification "Veronica longiflora; Veronica incana; Senecio dianthus Franch (white type); Cacalia quinquelobus; Sambucus adnata Wall (black type of elder)", the locus "Clark 1995 (line 1019)" and the note "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The spine's binomial is therefore the **first** of five names, and *Veronica incana* and *Cacalia quinquelobus* are later names with no entry of their own. The other three names reach this Tibetan drug from other rows: *Senecio dianthus* and *Sambucus adnata* are the subjects of the sibling entries **I_1357** and **I_1353**, each keyed in `names.csv` by its Men-Tsee-Khang glossary binomial as well (lines 323 and 393).

**Identity doubt.** The first name has no type label in Clark's string. The commentary describes three kinds of the drug ("the brown type", "the white type", "the plains-growing black type"), and Clark labels only two Latin names with them: *Senecio dianthus* "(white type)" and *Sambucus adnata* "(black type of elder)". Nothing in the line says which of the three kinds, if any, is *Veronica longiflora*, or *Veronica incana*, or *Cacalia quinquelobus*; the brown kind is said to be *dar.ya.kan*, a different drug. So the five names range over speedwell (*Veronica*), ragwort (*Senecio*), *Cacalia* and elder (*Sambucus*), plants of several families as the Codex understands it (not checked against a flora). The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary names a fourth genus again, *Sambucus adnata*, for the same Tibetan name. Clark's preface says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" (line 83) and that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect" (line 68). The Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note (line 686) says that its botanical terms rest on the information of its own Materia Medica Department. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1019).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry follows *spang.rgyan.dkar.po* (line 1018) and is followed by *rtsad* (line 1020). It reads:

"yu.gu.shing: Veronica longiflora: Veronica incana: Senecio dianthus Franch (white type): Cacalia quinquelobus: Sambucus adnata Wall (black type of elder): [has nine names. The brown type is said to be dar.ya.kan, and it grows in very hot areas. The white type has a large lobed leaf, pale green on top and brown beneath. It has small, slightly funy flowers, bitter taste, cool power and subdues spirits by its pungent odor. The plains-growing black type has dandelion-like dark green leaves. Both types] heal wounds, cure fever caused by poisoning [and dispel spirits]."

("funy" is as the OCR prints it; the Codex does not correct it.) Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The nine names, the three types, the leaves, the flowers, the taste, the power and the action on spirits are therefore the commentary layer. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the five Latin names with Clark's "white type" and "black type" labels, which are his identifications and not the root text's, and "heal wounds, cure fever caused by poisoning". Clark's name index lists *yu.gu.shing* (line 1749) and his Latin index lists *Veronica longiflora* (line 1659), *Veronica incana* (line 1656) and, separately, *Veronica ciliata* Fisch (line 1656), which is the second name of an unrelated drug, *ba.sha.ka* (line 975, the subject of I_1224). A search of the text for the Tibetan name *yu.gu.shing* found line 1019 and the index only (the entry for *dar.ya.kan* is named inside line 1019 and not the other way round; see I_1307).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3433).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225; colophon at line 3583), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428): "Yugu shing joins cuts and cures hot disorder associated with poisoning." The sentence follows "Pang gyen karpo cures laryngitis and hot disorder associated with poisoning" and is followed by "Tsed, Guthup and Dhumtak are antidotes to poison". The index lists "Yugu shing 207" (line 7570). The sentence gives no part used, no preparation and no taste.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "Y" block).* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate runs. Six phonetic names are printed (lines 6036 to 6038): "Yakyima", "Yherma", and, on line 6038, "Yhershingpa Yugu shing Yumo dejin Yungwa". The transliterations are "gya' kyi ma" (6040), "gyer ma" (6041) and, on line 6042, "gyer shing pa yu gu shing yu mo mde 'byin yung ba". The scientific names (lines 6044 to 6046) are six: "Chrysoplenium camosum", "Zanthoxylum armatum", "Scrophularia decomposita", **"Sambucus adnata"** (line 6045), "Paraquilegia anemonoides" and "Curcuma longa". Counted in order, "Yugu shing" is the fourth phonetic name and *Sambucus adnata* the fourth scientific name. The count is checked at two points: "Yherma" (second) falls on *Zanthoxylum armatum* and the English "Guinea pepper", and "Yungwa" (sixth) on *Curcuma longa* and "Turmeric", which Clark prints for *yung.wa*. The glossary prints no *Veronica*. The pairing is by position. [USER: verify]

The two editions agree in kind: Clark's drug will "heal wounds, cure fever caused by poisoning", and Men-Tsee-Khang's *Yugu shing* "joins cuts and cures hot disorder associated with poisoning". The Codex reads *yu.gu.shing* and *Yugu shing* as one drug from that agreement and from the name; neither text says so, and "fever" and "hot disorder" are two translators' words that are not treated as one term. Clark's chapter 21 list of the medicines that neutralise poisons (line 1212) names *Senecio dianthus* but not *Veronica*; that is the reading of I_1357, and it is not repeated here as an attestation for the first name.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Veronica longiflora*, *Veronica incana*, *yu.gu.shing* and "Yugu shing" found no entry on this first name. **I_1353** (*Sambucus adnata*) and **I_1357** (*Senecio dianthus*) read the same line 1019 for their own binomials, and the Y-block entries I_1355 (*Scrophularia decomposita*) and I_1356 (*Scrophularia incisa*), I_973 (*Zanthoxylum armatum*) and I_1330 (*Peucedanum praeruptorum*, which quotes the sentence at line 3433 for the neighbouring drug) read the same glossary or running text. **I_1032** (*Cacalia verbascifolia*) is an entry on another *Cacalia* from another tradition. **I_1217** (*Veronica beccabunga*) is an entry on another speedwell from another tradition. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Veronica* belongs to the plantain family (Plantaginaceae, formerly placed in the figwort family) and that the genus is known in the pharmacological literature for iridoid glycosides; *Senecio* and *Cacalia* belong to the daisy family (Asteraceae) and *Sambucus* to the elder family (Adoxaceae). These are statements about the genera and not about the Tibetan drug. It is also a general fact that many *Senecio* species contain pyrrolizidine alkaloids, which are liver-toxic. The lines read give no composition for the drug, and which plant is meant is not settled. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that will "heal wounds, cure fever caused by poisoning" (Clark, line 1019) and "joins cuts and cures hot disorder associated with poisoning" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3433), and the commentary adds that it "subdues spirits by its pungent odor" and will "dispel spirits". These are the texts' claims, and they are attached to this binomial only through Clark's first Latin name. They are not Codex protocols. Wounds, fevers and poisoning need medical care. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's line 1019 prints "bitter taste, cool power" for the white type, inside the square brackets, so in the commentary layer. It prints no post-digestive taste. The Men-Tsee-Khang lines read (3433, 6038, 6042, 6045) print no taste, power or post-digestive taste for *Yugu shing*.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it heals wounds and cures fever caused by poisoning, and the commentary adds that it subdues and dispels spirits. The Men-Tsee-Khang text says it joins cuts and cures hot disorder associated with poisoning. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. The commentary's "dispel spirits" is a therapeutic frame about illness caused by spirits and is not a cultivation status. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names (*yu.gu.shing*, "Yugu shing", *Veronica longiflora*) found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in Men-Tsee-Khang's rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23, colophon at line 3950) or in Clark's chapter 23, "Normal health" (lines 1264 to 1276), and the herb entries of Clark's chapter 20 that mention essence extraction (lines 971, 1043, 1078, 1103, 1121 and 1144) do not include it. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a wound-and-poison-fever drug with no preparation and no dose, and its first Latin name is one of five, with no label saying which kind of the drug it is. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Wounds, fevers and poisoning need medical care; the identity is open among several genera, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. It is a general fact that some of the genera named, notably *Senecio*, can contain liver-toxic alkaloids, which is a further reason to leave the plant alone while its identity is unsettled. No conservation information and no safety information for pregnancy were read. [USER: verify]
