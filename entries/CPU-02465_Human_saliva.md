# Human saliva — buzāq (بزاق) and luʿāb (لعاب), the saliva of a fasting person

## *Human saliva, especially of the fasting; the identification is plain from the Arabic text, which speaks of "the saliva of the fasting person" (luʿāb al-ṣāʾim); the text is of two entries and the first (buzāq) does not name a human, so the human reading of that entry rests on the second* | *(Volume III — Animal)*

**Tradition Tags:** CM

---

### Origin & Tradition

Saliva is named only by Avicenna's *Canon* among the texts on disk, in two separate entries, so the entry is a single-text entry. (The Prophetic text on disk records a saliva formula in a charm for healing at *al-Ṭibb al-Nabawī* line 3289, "In the name of God, the dust of our earth with the spittle of some of us…", but it is not tagged on this row and is not used here. See the report for the tag-addition lead.)

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).**

*Entry one,* buzāq *(Book II, entry* buzāq*, Arabic text line 11455).* The first paragraph is on what it is: "the strong in action is that of the hungry, on an empty stomach, and especially of a hot temperament" (القوي الفعل هو الذي للجائع على الريق وخصوصا من مزاج حار). Under wounds: "beneficial for ringworm (*qawbāʾ*)". Under the eye: "it benefits the *ṭarfa* and the whiteness" (ينفع من الطرفة والبياض, line 11458); I read *ṭarfa* as a blood-spot or blow to the eye and *bayāḍ* as a white opacity, which is a modern reading. [USER: verify] Under poisons: "it kills all the vermin, the snake and the scorpion" (printed as يقل الهوام كلها والحية والعقرب, lines 11458-11459). The Arabic verb in the printed text is *yuqillu* (يقل), which can be read "lessens"; I follow the sense of the parallel passage below and give "kills" as uncertain. [USER: verify]

*Entry two,* luʿāb *(Book II, entry* luʿāb*, Arabic text line 14716).* "The properties differ according to the kinds [of animal] and the temperaments of persons; its strength on the whole is ripening and dissolving" (يختلف بحسب الأنواع وبحسب أمزجة الأشخاص وقوته بالجملة منضجة محللة). For cosmetics: "it cleans freckles (*kalaf*), lentigines (*namash*) and dead blood" (يجلو الكلف والنمش والدم الميت, line 14718), where "dead blood" I take to mean a bruise. For wounds: "rub the ringworm with the saliva of the fasting person and camphor" (line 14719). For the head: "the saliva of the fasting person, dropped in an ear troubled by worms, kills them and brings them out at once" (lines 14720-14721). For poisons: "saliva resists poisons; and if the fasting person spits on a scorpion several times, it dies" (lines 14721-14722). The second entry's first words treat saliva across species, so the text here is not limited to humans, but each use is attached to the saliva of a fasting person.

### Active Components

Human saliva is mostly water, with mucins, salivary amylase and lipase, antibacterial proteins (lysozyme, lactoferrin, IgA), electrolytes and bicarbonate. It also carries the mouth's bacteria and, in a sick person, any blood-borne or respiratory virus. General physiology. The *Canon* names no constituent.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, and not a recommended practice. The *Canon*'s uses are cosmetic (freckles), skin (ringworm), the ear (worms), the eye, and poisonous bites and stings. None can be recommended. Saliva is a transmission route for infections, and using it on another person's wound, eye or ear carries real risk. Spitting on a scorpion or snake is not a remedy.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives the conditions only: from a fasting person, on an empty stomach, preferably of hot temperament (line 11456-11457); applied directly, rubbed with camphor on ringworm (line 14719), dropped in the ear (line 14720), or spat on a scorpion repeatedly (line 14722). No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Saliva contains antibacterial and enzyme components, and wounds of the mouth heal quickly, but there is no support for saliva as a treatment of skin, eye, ear or bite injuries, and the mouth is one of the most bacteria-rich sites on the body. This is general knowledge and not a study. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **CM (Avicenna).** No temperament degree is given. The first entry names "a hot temperament" of the person as increasing the strength (line 11457); the second says the strength "on the whole is ripening and dissolving" (line 14718), and that it varies with species and persons. No other tradition is tagged.

### Cultivation Register

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent.* Both entries are lists of local uses. The only notable idea is that the saliva of the fasting person, whose body is empty of food, is the strongest, which is a claim about the body's state at the time and not about the innate heat or radical moisture, and the text makes no such link. No cultivation status is attested.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing is asked of a cultivator. The one observation worth keeping is the text's own: it ties the strength of a bodily substance to the state of the person (fasting, hot temperament, particular person). It does not turn this into a practice. Today the sensible course is not to apply saliva to anything.

### Contraindications

- **Infection.** Saliva carries mouth bacteria and may carry viruses; do not apply it to another person's wounds, eyes or ears, or to your own broken skin.
- **Bites and stings.** The poison uses are historical record only. Seek medical care for snakebite and scorpion stings.
- **Ears and eyes.** Do not put saliva in an ear or eye; ear and eye problems need examination.
