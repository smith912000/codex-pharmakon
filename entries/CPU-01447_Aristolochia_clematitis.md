# Aristolochia clematitis (common birthwort) — Aristolochia klematitis (Dioscorides 3-6)

## *Aristolochia clematitis (the Dioscorides translator's suggestion for chapter 3-6, with A. rotunda and A. longa as older names in the same line)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). The binomials below are the translator's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification. **Named only by Dioscorides in this row.** (The older entry I_035 Birthwort, spine botanical *Aristolochia debilis*, also carries a DMM tag and treats the three aristolochias of Book III together; see the overlap note below.)

*3-6 ARISTOLOCHIA KLEMATITIS* (heading at line 6555). The translator's line reads "Aristolochia rotunda [Fuchs], Aristolochia longa [Fuchs, Brunfels], Aristolochia clematitis [Linnaeus, Bauhin] — Round Aristoloch, Apple of Earth, Common Birthwort", marked POISONOUS. The chapter opens: "There is also a third long type which is called clematitis, with slender branches full of somewhat long leaves similar to the smaller sempervivum [4-89, 4-90, 4-91]. The flowers are similar to rue [3-52, 3-53, 4-98], bright yellow, in a terminal flattened inflorescence. The roots are longer, slender, with a thick bark and an aromatic smell. The ointment makers use them effectively for thickening ointments." (from line 6558; a page break and two plate captions fall inside the sentence).

The uses that follow (from line 6570) are printed for the aristolochias as a group and are not all tied to clematitis. The text says: "One teaspoonful of the round one (taken in a drink with wine, and also applied) is indeed good for poisons, but the long one is given for poisons of snakes and deadly poisons." It is "taken in a drink with pepper and myrrh [1-77, 1-73, 4-116]" to put out "all remaining bodily wastes and the menstrual flow", is "an abortifacient" and does the same "applied in a pessary"; "taken as a drink with water, it helps asthma, rickets, chills, the spleen, hernias, convulsions, and pains of the side". Applied, it "extracts splinters and prickles, and takes off scales on bones"; "with iris and honey" it cleans rotten and foul ulcers, and it "cleans gums and teeth". Then the clematitis sentence: "It is thought that all clematitis is good for these things. Yet this has less strength than those previously mentioned." Further lines say it grows "in mountainous or warm, level places or else in rough, rocky places", that the one with a serious fever should "inhale the smoke over coals and the fever will stop", that applied it "heals wounds", that with the seed of dracunculus [2-196b] and honey it helps "malignancies in the nostrils", and that boiled with oil or swine grease it cures chills. "(Crateuas the Herbalist and Gallus have said the same and that it is good for the gouty)." The names printed are arariza, melecaprum, ephesia, lestitis, pyxionyx, dardanus, iontitis; the Gauls call it theximon, the Egyptians sophoeth, the Sicilians chamaemelum, the Italians terrae malum and the Dacians absinthium rusticum.

The sister chapters 3-4 (round, "ARISTOLOCHIA STROGGOLE", lines 6548-6550, where the translator's line gives *Fumaria bulbosa* and *Corydalis cava*, not an *Aristolochia*) and 3-5 (long, see I_1012) describe the other two kinds. 3-4 gives the reason for the name: "it is thought to help women in childbirth exceedingly well".

**Overlap.** I_035 Birthwort already presents the three Dioscorides aristolochias; this row isolates the third, "clematitis" kind, because the translator's gloss names *A. clematitis* only there. Other passages that mention the plant by name (3-55 at line 7035, where the seed "with aristolochia [3-4, 3-5, 3-6]" serves for bites of poisonous beasts) do not say which kind.

### Active Components

*Aristolochia* species are generally known to contain aristolochic acids, nitrophenanthrene carboxylic acids that are nephrotoxic and carcinogenic. That is a well-established statement about the genus. What a given plant of Dioscorides' chapter contained was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records an ointment-maker's root and a long list of uses for the aristolochia group, among them an abortifacient and menstrual-flow use, with the clematitis kind said to have less strength. The Codex does not recommend the plant. *Aristolochia* plants contain aristolochic acids, which damage the kidney and cause cancer, and the use of such plants is banned or restricted in many countries. No protocol is given.

### Preparation & Dosing

The doses and modes printed in 3-6 are for the group: "One teaspoonful of the round one (taken in a drink with wine, and also applied)" for poisons (line 6570); a drink "with pepper and myrrh" for wastes and the menstrual flow; a pessary; a drink with water for asthma, spleen and other complaints; a salve with iris and honey for ulcers; smoke inhaled over coals for fever; the seed of dracunculus with honey for the nostrils; oil or swine grease boiled and rubbed on for chills. No dose is printed for the clematitis kind specifically beyond "less strength". [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Aristolochic acid nephropathy and urothelial cancer after consumption of *Aristolochia* products are well documented in the medical literature, and this is a general statement about the genus. No study of *A. clematitis* itself was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** no warming, cooling or other quality is printed in 3-6. The text prints "POISONOUS" (the translator's marker) and, for the roots, an "aromatic smell" and a "thick bark"; the sister chapter 3-5 calls the roots "bitter to taste".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers is the discipline of attention the cultivation spec describes: where the plant grows (mountain or warm level ground, rough rocky places), how to know it (slender branches, rue-like bright yellow flowers in a flattened terminal cluster, slender aromatic roots with thick bark), the many names peoples gave it, and the care the writer takes to say that this kind "has less strength" than the round and long. The chapter records a smoke inhaled for fever and does not say it is a practice for the person. It assigns the plant no status. The identification with *A. clematitis* is the translator's gloss. **Practitioner's note.** The Greek text gives a cultivator a lesson in telling three similar plants apart and in not over-claiming for the weakest of them. It gives no practice to take up. The plant is a recognised kidney poison and carcinogen, and the abstentive answer is the right one: do not use it.

### Contraindications

*Aristolochia* plants (aristolochic acid) are nephrotoxic and carcinogenic. Sale or use is banned or restricted in many countries. Do not use in pregnancy: Dioscorides himself records an abortifacient use. None of the traditional uses above is advice, and no home preparation is safe. Anyone who has taken *Aristolochia* material and has kidney symptoms needs medical care.
