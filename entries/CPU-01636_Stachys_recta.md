# Stachys recta — Sideritis (Dioscorides 4-33)

## *Stachys recta [Linnaeus], "Mountain Woundwort", on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-33 ("Sideritis-prima [Fuchs], Sideritis vulgaris hirsuta erecta [Bauhin], Betonica annua var hirsuta, Stachys recta [Linnaeus], Stachys procumbens, Stachys sideritis — Mountain Woundwort")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 8292) prints six names, three of them with namers, and the row takes the one attributed to Linnaeus, *Stachys recta*; the line then gives two more names of the same genus, *S. procumbens* and *S. sideritis*. It was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-33. SIDERITIS*, heading at line 8291, SUGGESTED line at 8292, text at line 8293. The text is interrupted by a page number (575, line 8294) and the running head "BOOK FOUR: OTHER HERBS & ROOTS" (line 8296) and resumes at line 8297, in the middle of the list of other names. It stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), after *4-32. AGROSTIS EN PARNASSO* (line 8288) and before *4-34. SIDERITIS ETERA* (line 8298), the second sideritis. The Codex's own name extraction lists it (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 613); the translators' general index prints "Stachys recta 575" (line 17713).

The whole chapter reads: "Sideritis is a herb with leaves similar to marrubium [3-38] but longer, similar to those of sage or oak, yet smaller and sharp. It sends out foursquare stalks twenty centimetres long or rather more — not unpleasant to the taste, and somewhat gently astringent — on which are round whorls at distances apart (similar to marrubium), and in them is black seed. It grows in places under rocks. The leaves (applied) are able to close open cuts and sore wounds, and reduce inflammation. It is also called Heraclea, the Magi call it genitura, some say the blood of Titan, or the tail of a scorpion; Pythagoras says parmiron, Acreas, xanthophanes; Osthenes says buphthalmum, the Egyptians, sendionor, the Romans, vertumnus, some, solaster, and the Africans, asterchillos."

The chapter gives a description (leaves like those of *marrubium* but longer, "similar to those of sage or oak, yet smaller and sharp"; foursquare stalks twenty centimetres long or rather more; round whorls set at distances apart, again like *marrubium*; black seed in them), a taste and quality of the stalks ("not unpleasant to the taste, and somewhat gently astringent"), a place ("places under rocks") and one use, of the leaves applied: "able to close open cuts and sore wounds, and reduce inflammation". The rest of the chapter is a list of other names given to the plant by various people.

**The names.** The text lists *Heraclea*; "the Magi call it *genitura*"; "some say the blood of Titan, or the tail of a scorpion"; "Pythagoras says *parmiron*, Acreas, *xanthophanes*"; "Osthenes says *buphthalmum*"; "the Egyptians, *sendionor*"; "the Romans, *vertumnus*"; "some, *solaster*"; and "the Africans, *asterchillos*". They are names and attributions of names. The chapter reports them in a list and adds no rite, amulet, spell or deity. "The blood of Titan" and the scorpion's tail are, as the text prints them, names that "some say". The chapter later in the same book, *4-35*, quotes the authority Crateuas for the name *heraclea*; that is a labelled neighbouring note and not part of this chapter. The name *buphthalmum* is also printed elsewhere; see Overlap.

**The cross-reference to marrubium.** The number 3-38 after "marrubium" points, in this edition, to *3-38. PSEUDODIKTAMNOS* (SUGGESTED *Marrubium pseudodictamnus*), while the text's own horehound chapter is *3-119. PRASION*. The existing entry I_1129 records this as "an odd cross-reference" shared by 4-33 and four other chapters and does not resolve it. This entry does not either. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A chapter-level search of the entries folder for 4-33 found six files. None treats 4-33 as its own plant. I_1129 (*Marrubium pseudodictamnus*) lists 4-33 among the chapters that compare a plant to marrubium. I_1154 (*Parnassia palustris*, chapter 4-32) names 4-33 as the chapter that follows and says it is "not carried over". I_1130 (*Matricaria inodora*) and I_1174 (*Ranunculus repens*) note that 4-33 prints the name *buphthalmum* ("Osthenes says buphthalmum") among its other names, a name that also heads chapter 3-156; neither draws a species identification from it. I_1153 (*Parietaria officinalis*) notes that the synonym list of its own chapter includes *sideritis*, the heading of 4-33, so the same name is printed in two chapters. No entry on 4-33 as its own plant was found. The next chapter, 4-34, is a different plant and has its own row in this batch (I_1198, *Stachys arvensis*).

### Active Components

The chapter prints none. It is a general fact that *Stachys* (the woundworts and betonies) is a genus of the mint family (Lamiaceae), whose members are often aromatic and have square stems; the chapter's "foursquare stalks" and "round whorls" are consistent with that family in general terms. The composition of the plant Dioscorides describes was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides reports leaves applied to close open cuts and sore wounds and to reduce inflammation. This is the text's report, the identification is the translators', and the Codex gives no protocol. A wound that is open, deep, dirty or slow to close needs medical care, and a plant from a rock face is not a dressing.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-33 gives one preparation: the leaves "(applied)". It gives no quantity, frequency or duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints a taste and a quality for the stalks: "not unpleasant to the taste, and somewhat gently astringent". The leaves are described as "smaller and sharp"; the text does not say whether "sharp" means a point or a quality, and the Codex does not read it as a quality. It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use. It does record a long list of names, including those the text gives to the Magi, to Pythagoras and to Osthenes, and the phrases "the blood of Titan" and "the tail of a scorpion" that "some say"; the cultivation spec says that Dioscorides sometimes records such material while distancing himself and that recording is not endorsing, and here the list is of names only and carries no instruction. What the chapter offers as a discipline of attention is the description by comparison (marrubium, sage, oak), the count of the stalk's sides and the stalk's length, the position of the whorls and the colour of the seed, the place, and the taste. The plant is given no status beyond that of a wound herb. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The honest use of the chapter is as an exercise in recognising a plant by its square stalk, whorls and seed, and as a reminder that a plant can carry many names for different people. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter states no caution. The identity of the plant rests on a translators' suggestion that lists several species, and three different plants share the name *sideritis* in the text, so no wild plant should be gathered or applied to a wound on the strength of this entry. Open wounds and inflammation need medical assessment, and the risk of infection and tetanus from a soiled plant dressing on an open wound is real. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
