# Oxytropis microphylla — Tak sha, stag sha, "tiger flesh" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Oxytropis microphylla, the one binomial the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints, with the English name "Crazyweed", in second place of its "T" scientific-name run, which the Codex reads by position against the phonetic name "Tak sha" and the transliteration "stag sha"; Clark 1995 prints Potentilla discolor, Calophaca crassicaulis and Oxytropis chiliophylla for stag.sha* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and on a species name that only one of them prints, set against the Tibetan name by column position and not on one printed line.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian, Badianus, Avicenna and Sushruta material on disk for *Oxytropis* found nothing. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 369) the key *Oxytropis microphylla* is taken from the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary line 5930 with the note "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". It is the **only** binomial the glossary prints at that place, so it is neither the first nor a later one of several. Clark does not print *Oxytropis microphylla*. Clark's line for the same Tibetan name, *stag.sha* (line 1017, `names.csv` line 136), prints three binomials, *Potentilla discolor*, *Calophaca crassicaulis* and *Oxytropis chiliophylla* Royle (white type), and the names.csv row keys the first. The spine has its own row for that first binomial (*Potentilla discolor*, CPU-01774, not yet written); this entry is the row for the glossary's.

**Identity doubt.** The two editions agree on the Tibetan name and on one genus (*Oxytropis*, Clark's third binomial and the glossary's) and differ on the species; Clark also gives two other genera. The glossary's English name for the slot, "Crazyweed", is on the same line as the binomial (line 5930). The Codex reads the glossary's "Tak sha" and Clark's *stag.sha* as one drug from the spelling and from the agreement in kind of the actions (below); neither text says so. It does not choose among the species. The Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note (line 686) says that the botanical terms in their text "are based on the information procured from the Materia Medica Department of this institute"; it names no flora or earlier work. **Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "T" block, lines 5858 to 5998).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (page 314 is printed at line 6000). The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. The first two phonetic names of the block are "Tab seng Tak sha" (line 5860), and the first two transliterations are "rtab seng" (line 5862) and "stag sha" (line 5863). The scientific-name run begins after the last phonetic name ("Tukar", line 5924): "Fraxinus rhynchophylla Chinese ash" (line 5928), then "Oxytropis microphylla Crazyweed" (line 5930), "Caltha palustris" with "Marsh marigold" (lines 5932 and 5934) and "Angelica sinensis" with "Hog fennel" (lines 5936 and 5938). The English name stands on the same line as the binomial for the first two, so the first two pairs are printed as pairs. The block has twenty-four phonetic names, as I_1282, I_1300 and I_1315 record. Counted in order, "Tak sha" is the second phonetic name, *stag sha* the second transliteration and *Oxytropis microphylla* the second scientific name. The first slot is checked by Clark: "Tab seng" (*rtab seng*) stands against *Fraxinus rhynchophylla*, and Clark's *stab.seng* (line 978) lists *Fraxinus rhynchophylla* as its second binomial. The pairing of "Tak sha" with *Oxytropis microphylla* is positional and was not printed in one row. It agrees with Clark in genus, because Clark's *stag.sha* includes *Oxytropis chiliophylla*. *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3432).* Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428): "Abikha joins fractured skulls and hot disorder associated with poisoning. Taksha heals wounds, is anti-inflammatory and acts as an antidote against poison. Pang gyen karpo cures laryngitis and hot disorder associated with poisoning." The printed index lists "Taksha 207, 220" (line 7506). The name is in no taste group of chapter 19, and the sentence gives no part used and no preparation.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, chapter 21 (lines 3604 and 3605).* In the chapter on the groups and the method of compounding medicine (contents, line 229), the name is split across a line break: "Tsad, Nampar Guthup, Tak- / sha, Havo, Yungwa, Gangga chhung, Rerel, Vombu, Pang gyen, Pang tsi dhowo, Saegoe and Kyerpai barshun are the group of medicines that cure poisoning." The Codex reads "Tak- / sha" as "Taksha" from the index, which lists "Taksha 207, 220" and no hyphenated form.

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1017).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). It reads: "stag.sha (lit. 'tiger flesh'): Potentilla discolor: Calophaca crassicaulis: Oxytropis chiliophylla Royle (white type): [has five names. It has slightly bitter taste and subdues spirits by its sweet fragrance. The white variety has a green leaf and stalk and a single root from which many stalks branch out. The flower is reddish-blue, slightly coarse and is in the form of three-sided ears (cff. corn). The black or blue type has a yellowish-green leaf, smaller than the white type, as well as a large pod. It has a strong odor]. It heals wounds, cures septic disorders and poisoning, [halts dysentery, reduces swellings and acts as an emetic]." Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The taste, the sweet fragrance, the description and the second set of actions are therefore the commentary layer; "It heals wounds, cures septic disorders and poisoning" is outside the brackets and is the root-text layer as the brackets mark it. The Tibetan name means "tiger flesh" in Clark's gloss; the drug is a plant, and the homonym with a tiger product is noted in the tiger entries (III_267). Clark's name index lists *stag.sha* (line 1701) and his Latin index *Oxytropis chiliophylla* (line 1621) and *Potentilla discolor* (line 1629); a search found no index line for *Calophaca crassicaulis*.

*Clark 1995, chapter 21 (line 1212).* In Clark's chapter 21 ("The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines", heading at lines 1205 and 1206), the class of poison-neutralizing medicines reads, in part: "... (yellow) Scutellaria baicalensis, Pleurospermum hookeri, Senecio dianthus, Scutellaria, Oxytropis chiliophylla, Corallodiscus kingianus, Turmeric, Gentiana urnula, Fern, Tibetan tamarisk, Gentiana algida, Pterocephalus hookeri, Wild Rose ...". *Oxytropis chiliophylla* stands in it by its Latin name, and the Men-Tsee-Khang poison group (line 3605) names "Taksha". The Codex records the two lists side by side and does not say that either copies the other.

I_1273 (*Epimedium grandiflorum*), I_1254 (*Cnidium dahuricum*), I_1300 (*Hippophae tibetana*) and I_1315 (*Marrubium incisum*) print neighbouring "T" block lines. ### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Oxytropis* is a genus of the legume family (Fabaceae), that some of its species and some *Astragalus* species are generally known as "locoweeds" because they poison livestock, and that "Crazyweed" is one of the English names generally given to such plants. The glossary prints "Crazyweed" beside this binomial. Nothing in the lines read says what the Tibetan drug contained, or that it is a poisonous plant. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "heals wounds, is anti-inflammatory and acts as an antidote against poison" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3432) and that "heals wounds, cures septic disorders and poisoning" (Clark, line 1017), and Men-Tsee-Khang lists it in the group of medicines that cure poisoning (line 3605). These are the texts' own claims, and the plant behind the name is uncertain among at least four species and three genera. They are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. A wound or a poisoning needs medical care, and, because plants of this genus are generally known as livestock poisons, no plant should be used as an antidote or a wound remedy on the strength of this entry.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes this drug by what it does: it heals wounds and is an antidote to poison, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text sets it in the group of medicines that cure poisoning. Clark's commentary adds that it "subdues spirits by its sweet fragrance", a statement about its effect on spirits that the Codex records and does not read as a cultivation status. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23 in both editions), and Clark's materia medica names other drugs as essence-extraction ingredients (for example lines 853, 971 and 1144), none of them this one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name and not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a wound and poison drug in the Tibetan texts, with a note on a fragrance that subdues spirits, no preparation and no dose, and the species is uncertain. The abstentive answer is the honest one.
