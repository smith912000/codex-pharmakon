### Origin & Tradition

Mugwort is the dream-herb and the woman's-herb of the northern hemisphere, and its cross-tradition footprint is one of the widest of any plant in this Codex. A vigorous, silver-backed roadside Artemisia native across Eurasia, it carries the name of a goddess in the West (*Artemisia*, for Artemis, protector of women and childbirth) and stands as one of the pillars of East-Asian medicine as the material of **moxibustion**. Its convergence is remarkably specific: almost every tradition that met it reached for the same three uses — regulating and warming menstruation, driving out cold, and touching the world of sleep, vision, and the threshold. (Note: the classic *moxa* of Chinese medicine is usually the closely related **_Artemisia argyi_ (ài yè)**; European mugwort is *A. vulgaris*. The two are near-kin and share the tradition, but are distinct species — this entry is *A. vulgaris*, with the East-Asian record noted as the sibling species where relevant.)

**Greco-Roman (Dioscorides):** Mugwort (*artemisia*) is described in Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* (Book III) as a warming herb of the womb — an emmenagogue promoting menstruation and easing childbirth, used in decoction, bath, and pessary for gynaecological complaints. Pliny records its protective and travel-lore associations (worn to ward fatigue and evil). The dedication to Artemis, goddess of women and the hunt, fixes its symbolic identity from the classical period on.

**Islamic / Unani (Avicenna):** The Artemisia group (*shīḥ* / *baranjāsif* for mugwort-type species) is treated in the Arabic materia medica and Avicenna's *Canon* as hot-dry, deobstruent and emmenagogue — opening obstructions, warming a cold womb and stomach, expelling worms, and moving stagnant menstruation. The gynaecological-warming reading of the Greco-Roman tradition is carried forward intact.

**Egyptian (Ebers tradition):** Artemisia-type bitter aromatics belong to the Egyptian repertoire of warming, worm-expelling and gynaecological herbs. As with other Compositae, precise ancient species identification is uncertain, so the honest claim is that the wormwood/mugwort group of bitter Artemisias sits within Egyptian practice for these purposes. `[USER: verify before asserting a specific Ebers passage as A. vulgaris]`

**Assyrian (cuneiform):** Bitter aromatic herbs of the Artemisia type appear in Mesopotamian medical and fumigation practice; identification at the genus/family level is defensible, specific species attribution is not — the attribution here is to the shared bitter-aromatic repertoire.

**Chinese (Shennong lineage):** This is mugwort's other heartland. *Ài yè* (艾葉) — moxa leaf, classically *A. argyi*, with *A. vulgaris* as a near sibling — is a foundational drug: bitter, acrid, warm; it warms the channels and the womb, stops bleeding (especially uterine bleeding and threatened miscarriage), disperses cold, and, burned as **moxa**, delivers penetrating warmth to acupoints. Moxibustion is one of the oldest and most characteristic therapies of Chinese medicine, and mugwort is its physical substance.

**Ayurvedic (Sushruta lineage):** Artemisia species (*nagadamani* / *dawana*-group) enter Indian practice as warming, bitter, *krimighna* (anthelmintic) and emmenagogue herbs, used to move stagnation, expel worms, and support menstrual regularity — the same hot-dry, womb-warming, parasite-clearing profile reached independently.

**Tibetan (rGyud-bzhi):** Artemisia species (including mugwort-type *mkhan pa* plants) are used in the Tibetan medical tradition codified in the *rGyud-bzhi* (Four Tantras) and are the material of Tibetan moxa (*me-btsa'*), applied for "cold" disorders, digestive weakness, and channel-cold — a Himalayan parallel to the Chinese moxibustion use, again converging on warmth-against-cold.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Goddess-Herb of the Threshold:**
Mugwort's symbolism clusters around two poles: **the womb** and **the dream/threshold** — and both map onto real pharmacology. As the herb of Artemis, of midwives, and of the moxa that warms the womb, its gynaecological-emmenagogue reputation reflects a genuine uterine-active, warming, circulation-moving plant. As "St. John's plant," the herb of midsummer, the pillow-herb said to bring vivid and prophetic dreams, it belongs to the folk practice of *oneirogens* — plants placed under the pillow or drunk before sleep to deepen and colour dreams. This threshold-symbolism (between waking and sleeping, between life and the crossing of birth) is the culture's reading of a mildly psychoactive bitter aromatic whose thujone-bearing oil and bitter principles act on the nervous system. In Kenetica mugwort's principal placement is a **`[SLEEP]` dream-and-relaxation herb** (pillow, mild evening infusion), with a secondary **`[RESET]` warming/menstrual** profile — always bounded by its thujone content and emmenagogue power.

### Active Components
- **Essential oil — thujone (α-/β-), 1,8-cineole, camphor, borneol** — the neuroactive, warming, and (via thujone) dose-limiting fraction.
- **Sesquiterpene lactones** — bitter principles (the "artemisinic" family in the genus); digestive-bitter and anthelmintic activity; also the main **contact-allergens** of the Compositae.
- **Flavonoids (eupatilin, jaceosidin, and others in the *argyi* sibling), phenolic acids (chlorogenic, caffeic)** — antioxidant/anti-inflammatory fraction.
- **Coumarins & inulin-type polysaccharides.**
- **Allergen Art v 1** — a defensin-like pollen protein of *A. vulgaris*, central to mugwort pollinosis and food cross-reactivity.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica
Primary **`[SLEEP]` dream-and-relaxation** herb: dried leaf in a dream-pillow, or a light evening infusion, in the folk *oneirogen* tradition for deeper, more vivid dream recall. Secondary **`[RESET]` warming/digestive** use: bitter digestive infusion before rich meals; traditional (professionally supervised) warming/menstrual and moxibustion use for "cold" patterns. Its warm-dry-bitter-moving signature makes it a *threshold and warming* herb, not a daytime stimulant or a builder.

### Preparation & Dosing
| Form | Traditional / practical range | Kenetica notes |
|---|---|---|

### Scientific Correlation
According to PubMed, the moxa Artemisias are the subject of active pharmacological review. A review by **Wang et al. (2024), *Naunyn-Schmiedeberg's Archives of Pharmacology***, on *Artemisia argyi* Folium (the principal moxibustion species, sibling to *A. vulgaris*), catalogued **136 identified compounds** and pharmacological activity that is chiefly **anti-inflammatory, anti-tumour, antioxidant and antibacterial**, with clinical application across respiratory, immune, digestive, nervous-system and gynaecological uses — a modern correlate of the traditional warming/moving/stop-bleeding profile shared by the mugwort group. (Species note: this evidence is primarily for *A. argyi*; *A. vulgaris* is closely related but distinct.) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00210-024-03122-7)

For *A. vulgaris* specifically, the best-characterised modern finding is its **allergenicity**: **Ukleja-Sokołowska et al. (2016), *International Journal of Immunopathology and Pharmacology*** document IgE sensitisation to *Artemisia vulgaris* and its major pollen allergen **Art v 1**, with clinically significant cross-reactivity (via lipid-transfer proteins and defensin-like proteins) to foods including sunflower seeds — the molecular basis of the "mugwort–food" allergy syndrome and a real contraindication. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0394632016651648)

**Evidence grade:** *Moderate/preclinical for the anti-inflammatory–antioxidant–antibacterial pharmacology (largely on the sibling A. argyi); well-documented for allergenicity of A. vulgaris; traditional for the emmenagogue/dream uses (limited controlled human data).* Claims should be phased accordingly, and the species distinction kept explicit.

### Traditional Energetic Classification
- **TCM (ài yè):** bitter, acrid, warm; enters Liver, Spleen, Kidney. Actions: warm the channels and the womb, stop bleeding (esp. uterine), disperse cold, calm a restless fetus (classical); moxa delivers penetrating warmth.
- **Ayurveda:** rasa — *tikta*, *kaṭu*; guṇa — light, dry, sharp; vīrya — *uṣṇa* (heating); vipāka — *kaṭu*; dosha — reduces *kapha*/*vāta*, increases *pitta*; *krimighna* (anthelmintic), emmenagogue.
- **Galenic / Unani:** Hot & Dry 2nd degree; deobstruent, emmenagogue, carminative, anthelmintic.
- **Tibetan (rGyud-bzhi):** warming moxa material for "cold" disorders and channel-cold.
- **Dantian mapping (Kenetica):** lower dantian (womb-warming, pelvic-moving) with an upper-dantian dream/threshold action.

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese (TCM).** Mugwort's genuine practice-level framing is **moxibustion** (*jiǔ* 灸): dried, refined leaf-punk (*ài róng*) burned at acupoints to drive penetrating warmth against cold and stagnation. This is one of the oldest characteristic therapies of Chinese medicine and is a real, attested technique — but it is a **therapy, not a cultivation grade**. Two cautions hold it below (a) for cultivation status: the classical moxa leaf is *Artemisia argyi* (*ài yè* 艾葉), the sibling species, not the *A. vulgaris* of this entry; and no *san pin* 三品 grade assigning the leaf a life-nourishing, long-term *yang sheng* 養生 role is recorded in the sources retrieved. Moxa-as-technique is (a); cultivation grade is (b), unattested.

**Tibetan (FMT).** Artemisia is the material of Tibetan moxa (*me-btsa'*), applied within the *rGyud-bzhi* framework for "cold" disorders and channel-cold — again a **practice**, and one that per the tradition's own structure is medicine as the third of four treatment branches: it supports practice, it does not reach the root (the *nyes pa* derived from the three poisons). No formal "substance for subtle-body practice" category exists to assign mugwort to, and none is asserted. (b) framed as a warming external therapy.

**Ayurveda (SS).** As *nagadamani*/*dawana*-group, Artemisia is a warming, bitter, *krimighna* (anthelmintic) and emmenagogue herb — a moving, stagnation-clearing drug. **No *rasayana* status is attested**; its profile is therapeutic and *doshic*, not rejuvenative. (b).

**Unani (CM).** The *shīḥ*/*baranjāsif* group is hot-dry, deobstruent, emmenagogue — opening obstruction and warming a cold womb. Avicennan cultivation is the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture, and a hot-dry emmenagogue is not framed as a conservational longevity agent; nothing ties mugwort specifically to that project. (b)/(c) for cultivation status.

**Greek (DMM).** Dioscorides (*De Materia Medica* III) treats *artemisia* as a warming womb-herb and emmenagogue; Pliny **records** its protective, fatigue-warding, travel-lore uses — amuletic material that the tradition notes without endorsing, and which is folk-apotropaic, not a subtle-body doctrine. The later European St John's Eve / midsummer protective-herb and dream-pillow *oneirogen* traditions are folk practice, not Dioscoridean physiology. No cultivation system is present to draw on; what remains is the discipline of attention. (c) absent for cultivation status; the amuletic and dream-threshold uses are recorded folk framings, not tradition-attested cultivation.

**Egyptian (EP) and Assyrian (EP).** Bitter aromatic Artemisias belong to both repertoires for warming, worm-expelling and fumigation use, but species identification to *A. vulgaris* is not secure (uncertain for EP; under the standing blocking-warning for AT). No cultivation framing is attested in either. (c) absent.

**Practitioner's note.** What a cultivator actually *does* with mugwort is **burn it or sleep on it** — moxa for penetrating warmth against cold patterns (professionally, and with the *argyi*/*vulgaris* species distinction kept honest), or a dream-pillow / light short-course evening infusion in the folk *oneirogen* tradition for dream recall. Both are bounded by its thujone content and emmenagogue power, and neither is a "nourishing-life" cultivation grade — mugwort is a threshold-and-warming working herb, not a tonic taken to build the person. Its most spiritually loaded uses (St John's Eve protection, the dream-pillow) are folk-apotropaic practice, not a subtle-body cultivation status attested by any of the tagged traditions.

### Contraindications
- **Pregnancy — avoid:** emmenagogue and uterine stimulant (classical "moves the menses / eases birth" herb); internal use contraindicated in pregnancy.
- **Compositae/ragweed/mugwort-pollen allergy:** significant contact-dermatitis and food-cross-reactivity risk (Art v 1, LTP); a major and well-documented caution.
- **Epilepsy/seizure disorders:** thujone is a convulsant — avoid concentrated/oil preparations and prolonged high doses.
- **Moxa smoke:** respiratory irritant — ventilate; caution in asthma.
- **Prolonged high-dose internal use:** thujone accumulation — keep to short courses / pillow use.
