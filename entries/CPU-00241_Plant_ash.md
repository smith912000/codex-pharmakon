# Plant ash — Dong Hui (冬灰) "Pulvis Fumi Carbonisati" (Yang 1998); other name Li Hui "Chenopodium Ash"
## *The ash of burnt plants in the Divine Farmer's Classic; Yang 1998 prints "Pulvis Fumi Carbonisati" and glosses it as "simply plant ash", the best being the ash of Hui Di Cai (Herba Chenopodii Serotini)* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** Dong Hui is printed in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`) on PDF page 35, the last entry of the run headed "Jades and Stones: Inferior Class" (heading at line 1311; the next heading, "Herbs: Superior Class", is at line 1383). The text (lines 1362-1364): "Dong Hui (Pulvis Fumi Carbonisati)78 is acrid and slightly warm. It mainly treats black moles and eliminates polyps and warts, flat abscesses, erosive [sores], and scabs. Its other name is Li Hui (Chenopodium Ash)." Yang's footnote 78 (lines 1376-1379) reads: "This is simply plant ash. However, the ash of Hui Di Cai (Herba Chenopodii Serotini) is regarded as the best of all. Therefore, at the end of this passage this medicinal is given the name of Chenopodium Ash." `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv` has the row (line 43) "Dong Hui, Jades and Stones: Inferior Class, PDF p.35 (line 1362), Pulvis Fumi Carbonisati, mineral". The list `_sources/shennong.csv` carries 冬灰 (line 248), in characters only, as the last mineral row before the herb rows. The match of the characters to Yang's pinyin is by position; the first character reads "winter" and Yang gives no gloss of the name. [USER: verify]

**Identity.** The spine's note is "low; id-uncertain", and its label for the row is the formula K2CO3 (potassium carbonate, potash). That label is the project's and is not in Yang. Yang says only that the drug is "simply plant ash", with the ash of a goosefoot-type herb (Hui Di Cai, glossed Herba Chenopodii Serotini) judged best; he names no wood, straw or fuel, and does not say how the ash was made or whether it was leached into lye. The Latin "Pulvis Fumi Carbonisati" is the translator's. Another entry of this Codex, II_64 Plant ash, is a Dioscorides chapter (5-135, twig ash) for a different tradition and is not part of this one; the Chinese Classic and the Greek chapter are not equated here.

### Active Components

Wood and plant ash is mostly calcium carbonate, with potassium salts (potassium carbonate is the main soluble part), silica, phosphates and small amounts of many other minerals. Its solution is strongly alkaline (lye). Composition varies with the plant and the burn. The sources read give no composition.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Classic records Dong Hui for black moles, polyps, warts, flat abscesses, erosive sores and scabs, all of them surface complaints, and the verbs ("eliminates") fit a caustic applied to the skin. The Codex does not endorse any of these uses and offers no protocol, dose or preparation. Applying wood ash or lye to the body can burn it, and nothing here supports using it.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation is printed in the monograph and no dose is given in the sources retrieved. The lines read do not say whether the ash was applied dry, in water or as lye. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Plant ash and the lye leached from it are alkaline and caustic; lye has long been used to make soap and to treat food. Alkali burns skin and eyes. Nothing in general chemistry supports plant ash as a treatment for moles, polyps or warts. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- SBJ: "acrid and slightly warm" (Yang line 1362). Class: Inferior (heading line 1311). Yang notes that in his source text flavor and qi were not distinguished and that he omits the word flavor (footnote, lines 849-855).

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: a lower-grade drug (下品).* Dong Hui is the last entry in the run headed "Jades and Stones: Inferior Class" (Yang line 1311; the run ends at line 1382). Yang's rendering of the Classic's preface says the inferior class "mainly treat disease and correspond to earth. They are usually toxic and cannot be taken for a long time. If one intends to eliminate cold and heat and evil qi, break accumulations and gatherings, and cure disease, one should base [one's efforts] on the inferior class" (lines 391-395), and his note says this class "specifically treat disease" and "cannot be taken in large amounts or for prolonged periods of time without developing negative side effects" (lines 362-366). The monograph prints no phrase about protracted taking, lightness of body or long life. The grade is a statement of the Classic as translated by Yang in 1998 and is not carried forward from later materia medica.

**Practitioner's note.** A lower-grade drug in the Chinese scheme: for a defined complaint and a short course, and the tradition's own grading is the warning. There is no cultivation use here. Ash is an everyday material, and the Classic's entry records only that it was placed among the drugs that remove growths. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Wood ash and lye are caustic; they burn skin, eyes and mouth, and swallowing them is dangerous. Ash from treated or painted wood, plastics or household waste may carry toxic metals and other contaminants. Moles, polyps and warts should be assessed by a doctor, because a changing mole can be a skin cancer and must not be burnt off at home. The Classic's grade for this class says it cannot be taken for long. [USER: verify]
