# Mayweed / Anthemis cotula — Parthenion, Amarakon (Dioscorides 3-155); the ointments Amarakinon, Megaleion, Hedychroon (1-68, 1-69, 1-70)

## *Anthemis cotula* (the Dioscorides translators' suggested identification, "Cotula foetida [Fuchs], Chamaemelum foetidum [Bauhin], Anthemis cotula [Linnaeus] — Mayweed [Mabberley]"; in 3-155 also "Stinking Chamomile") | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides.** This entry stands on one text, Dioscorides (DMM). It was added 2026-10-09 (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP).

**Identity note.** The binomial belongs to the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood) and not to Dioscorides. It is attached to four chapters, and in each the SUGGESTED line is shared with another identification. In 3-155 (the plant chapter) the translators give "[other usage] *Origanum amaracus* — Amaracus *Origanum dictamnus* ... Dittany of Crete, Burning Bush" beside it; in 1-68 they give the same "other usage" *Origanum amaracus*. The other three chapters (1-68, 1-69, 1-70) are about ointments, and the chapter 1-68 text itself says the ointment is "sweetened with amaracus", which is the herb marjoram or dittany in the translators' own other reading, so the link from the ointments to *Anthemis cotula* is thin. The same chapter 3-155 is also named in the existing entry I_759 (*Tanacetum parthenium*, feverfew), which records that the identification of *parthenion* is contested. **Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000).

*3-155 PARTHENION (AMARAKON)* (heading at line 7856; text line 7860). This is the plant chapter. SUGGESTED: "Amaracinum, Parthenium, Cotula foetida [Fuchs], Chamaemelum foetidum [Bauhin], Anthemis cotula [Linnaeus] — Stinking Chamomile, Mayweed [Mabberley]". The text: "Parthenium has thin leaves (similar to coriander); the white flowers are in a circle, their middle is yellow, and they are somewhat poisonous to smell and bitter to taste. Dried and taken as a drink with vinegar and honey (or with salt) it is able to drive phlegm and cholera downward and out, and to help the asthmatic and depressive. The herb (without its flower) is effective (given in drink) for urinary stones and the asthmatic. A decoction of it is fit for bathing a hardened and inflamed womb. It is applied (with its flowers) for skin inflammation and other inflammation." The synonyms printed are "amaracum, leucanthemon, anthemis, chamemelum, chrysocalis, melabathrum, or flos campestris"; Roman "solis oculus, or millefolium"; Etruscan "cautan"; African "thamacth".

*1-68 AMARAKINON* (heading at line 4070; text line 4074). The SUGGESTED line carries the same binomial and "Mayweed [Mabberley]". The chapter describes the ointment: "The best amaracinum is made in Cyzicum. It is made from the oils of unripe olives and balaninum [1-40, 4-160]; thickened with xylobalsamum [1-18], schoenus [4-52] and calamus [sweet flag] but sweetened with amaracus and costus [1-15], amomum [1-14], nard [1-6, 1-7, 1-8, 1-10], carpobalsam [fruit of opalobalsamum, 1-18] and myrrh". Its qualities: "It is warming, sleep-inducing, opening the blood vessels, softening, heating and diuretic", with uses on decaying flesh, fistulas, ulcers, difficult urination, haemorrhoids, menstrual flow, hardness of the vulva, and hurt tendons and muscles. It adds a kind of oil that "drops out of the rock", found in Arabia and Italy. The chapter does not describe a mayweed plant.

*1-69 MEGALEION* (line 4079; text line 4083): "That which is called megalium used to be made but is no longer ... The mixture of this is the same as amaracinum [1-68] but there is resin added to it". It is "gently softening". Resin is added "only to colour and thicken" the oils, and turpentine is mixed in and boiled until it stops smelling.

*1-70 HEDYCHROON* (line 4084; text line 4088): "That which is called hedychroon and which is made in Co has the same strength and the same method of preparation as amaracinum [1-68] but it smells sweeter."

### Active Components

Mayweed, as the species is generally known, is an aromatic daisy-family plant containing bitter sesquiterpene lactones and volatile oils, and its sap and foliage can irritate skin. This is a general statement about *Anthemis cotula*; the plants named in the chapters were not analysed in the sources. For the ointments, the aromatics listed in 1-68 (calamus, costus, amomum, nard, myrrh) are the named ingredients, not the plant. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records the herb *parthenion* as an emetic-cathartic drink, a remedy for asthma and urinary stones, and a bath and poultice for the womb and for inflammation; and records three ointments whose ingredients are fragrant oils. These are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

3-155: dried, taken as a drink with vinegar and honey or with salt; the herb without flower, given in drink; a decoction for bathing; applied with its flowers. 1-68: the ointment made with oils of unripe olives and balaninum, thickened and sweetened with the listed aromatics, with honey and wine used for rubbing the jars and steeping the aromatics. 1-69 and 1-70 refer to 1-68. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Plants of the daisy family can cause contact dermatitis in sensitive people, and mayweed in particular is generally known as an irritant. This is a general statement and no study was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** 3-155 prints "bitter to taste" and "somewhat poisonous to smell". 1-68 prints the ointment as "warming, sleep-inducing, opening the blood vessels, softening, heating and diuretic"; 1-69 "gently softening". These qualities belong to the ointment, not to the herb.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. The chapters offer a discipline of attention: how to know the plant (thin leaves like coriander, white flowers in a circle with a yellow middle, a smell "somewhat poisonous"), where the best ointment is made (Cyzicum), and which ingredients make it. They record no status for the person and the binomial is the translators'.

**Practitioner's note.** The practice in these chapters is of assay and provenance: the smell of the herb, the place the best ointment is made. Nothing in them gives a cultivator a practice for the person, and the identification is doubtful, so no use is recommended.

### Contraindications

Mayweed is generally known to irritate skin, and the text itself calls the smell "somewhat poisonous". The ointments contain several strong aromatics. Do not use in pregnancy, since the text names menstrual-flow and womb uses. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
