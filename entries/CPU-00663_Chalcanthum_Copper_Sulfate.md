**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** 5-114 CHALKANTHON (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10419-10422), in Book 5. The text: "There is a single type of calcanthum formed by moisture into a solid. It has three different forms." One is the moisture that drips in caves and solidifies, called stillatitium by the Cyprian metal-workers, pinarium by Petesius, "stalacticon" by some; another "runs secretly in hollows" into ditches and thickens, called pecton; "The third sort is called coctile and is made in Spain", by dissolving it in boiling water and letting it stand until it "divides into many cubic forms hanging together like grape bunches", which is "useless and the weakest". The best is "azure coloured [blue], heavy, compact and transparent"; coctile is fittest for dyeing and making colours black but weakest for medicine. The edition's header gloss is "SUGGESTED: Calcanthum — Limestone", but a later chapter (the ink recipe, line 10777) glosses the word as "[blue vitriol or copper sulphate]", which fits the text's blue crystals. The chapter ends "It is burnt, as we will show [below] in calcitis"; see the separate Chalcitis entry. The chapter is not in `extract/names.csv`.

### Active Components

Copper(II) sulfate (usually the blue pentahydrate) is a water-soluble salt of copper. In solution the copper ion is astringent and a strong irritant of the gut lining, and in sufficient quantity it is an emetic and a poison. It is also a fungicide and algicide. The Greek text's "stalactite" and "coctile" forms are the natural salt and the one re-crystallised from solution.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Copper sulfate is a poison when swallowed, and the sources themselves flag the danger: Dioscorides makes it an emetic, and Yang's note calls it "toxic and erosive". The Chinese text's promise that sublimated Shi Dan might make one "an immortal" is recorded as the tradition's claim and is not something this Codex endorses. The Codex offers no protocol, dose or preparation.

### Preparation & Dosing

DMM 5-114 (line 10422): "two teaspoonfuls are swallowed or licked with honey to warm, form scabs, and to kill broadworms"; it "causes vomiting and helps those who have eaten mushrooms, taken as a drink with water"; "Diluted with water and dropped into the nostrils through wool it purges the head". The coctile form is made by dissolving in boiling water and letting it crystallise; it is burnt as described under calcitis. SBJ gives "taking it after it is sublimated" with no method and no quantity (lines 1102-1103). The Ebers lines give only the ingredient list of an eye salve (line 2734). No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Copper sulfate ingestion causes nausea, vomiting, gastrointestinal bleeding, haemolysis and liver and kidney injury, and deaths are recorded; it was once used as an emetic and is a known caustic on the eye and skin. It is used today as an agricultural fungicide and in chemistry. The old uses against mushroom poisoning and worms have no accepted modern basis. No clinical studies were retrieved for the traditional uses. [USER: verify]

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: middle class, with an immortality clause.* Shi Dan stands under the printed heading "Jades and Stones: Middle Class" (Yang 1998, line 1097). Yang's rendering of the preface says the middle class "mainly nurture personality ... may or may not be toxic, and [therefore,] one should weigh and ponder before putting them to their appropriate use. If one intends to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion, one should base [one's efforts] on the middle class" (lines 385-390), and Yang's own note on the preface says "Since this class of medicinals may be toxic, their prescription requires care" (lines 361-362). The entry adds its own line: sublimated, it "may prevent senility", and "protrated taking may increase longevity and make one an immortal" (lines 1102-1104). This is the *san pin* hierarchy, in which the middle grade is a mixed one, and it is the only cultivation claim in the pages read; the same line also says it may turn iron to copper, gold or silver. No *jing*, *qi* or *shen* claim is printed for Shi Dan in the lines read. The ancient Daoist elixir-seeking that the preface ties to the early materia medica (Yang's note, lines 374-379) is the context, and Yang's footnote 52 gives the practical verdict that the drug is toxic and erosive and was used only for tooth decay, polyps, sores and abscesses, and occasionally eye disease.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here for a cultivator to take. The Chinese text puts sublimated copper sulfate in the middle class with a promise of long life and immortality, and its own translator's note says the drug is toxic and erosive. The Greek text treats it as an emetic and a dye-stuff. The honest lesson is the one in the grading: a mixed-grade drug needs weighing and pondering, and for this one the weighing comes out against swallowing it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Contraindications

Copper sulfate is toxic by mouth and corrosive to the eye, skin and nose; it must not be swallowed, licked, or dropped into the nostrils or eyes. Poisoning needs urgent medical care. Preparations labelled "sublimated" or "calcined" in old texts are not standardised and may carry other metals. It is sold as a pesticide and chemical, not as a medicine, and its sale and use are regulated in many places. Pregnancy is a particular concern, though the Chinese text's line that it "makes pregnancy possible" is recorded here as the tradition's claim, not as advice.
