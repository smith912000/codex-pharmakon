# Salsoda (bul.tog) — bul.tog / mdzi.bu.ka / "Tibetan salt" (Clark 1995); Bultog (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *An earth medicine and salt of the Explanatory Tantra, used in tea; Clark glosses the word "Salsoda" and, in a neighbouring list, "sodium bicarbonate", while his index sets it beside the plant name Salsola collina, so the identity is not settled in the sources retrieved* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The loci given for this entry are `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` ("Clark 1995 (line 862)") and `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 164, where the name is spelled "Bultog", class mineral, identification marked uncertain. Line 862 of `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` reads: "bul.tog: Salsoda: [has five names: mdzi.bu.ka, Tibetan salt, etc., and has a bitter taste similar to earth ma.nu. It is used in tea and so there is little chance of mistaking it.] It halts necrosis, digests [undigested roasted grain] flour [and cures poisoning]." It is in chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (heading at line 792), in "(3) The Exposition of the Power of Earth Medicines" (line 857), where it follows golden sand (line 858) and potash (line 861) and precedes sulphur (line 863). The square brackets are commentary, by Clark's own statement (preface, line 82); the plain statement of use is "halts necrosis, digests flour", and the clauses "undigested roasted grain" and "and cures poisoning" are in brackets of their own. "Earth *ma.nu*" is not explained in the lines read; the Tibetan name *ma.nu* occurs in Clark's name index (line 1727) but is not defined in the body that was read. [USER: verify]

Chapter 20 is not the only place. In the group of tastes (chapter 19), "The group of medicines having salty taste includes rock salt, wood salt, horn salt, black 'sanchal' salt, white mineral salt, mirabilite, soda salt (saltpetre), white rock salt, ash salt, aragonite (Tartar salt) and salsoda (sodium bicarbonate)." (Clark line 767). Here Clark glosses salsoda as sodium bicarbonate. Chapter 20's own entry gives it a "bitter taste". The two statements do not agree, and the sources read do not explain it. A third mention is in the entry for bamboo concretion (line 881), where the mineral kind "is white, like salsoda".

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`) has the same material in a shorter form. In the salty group: "Gyam tsha, Gya tsha, Che nyang tsha, Ra tsha, Kharu tsha, Tsabru tsha, Ze tsha, Wa tsha, Zae tsha, Thai tsha, Shing tsha, Yabak khyara, Bultog and other similar substances are the group of medicinal substances having a salty taste." (line 3211). In the efficacies of the soil medicines: "Bultog heals gangrene and helps in the digestion of tsampa." (line 3373). Its index cites pages 187 and 200 (line 7281). Tsampa is roasted grain flour, which matches Clark's bracket "undigested roasted grain flour". The Men-Tsee-Khang text agrees with Clark on the salty group and with Clark on the flour; it renders "halts necrosis" as "heals gangrene" and does not repeat "cures poisoning".

Identity needs a caution. Clark's index (line 1641) prints "Salsoda" followed by "Salsola collina" (a plant). The index is a flat alphabetical list of terms, so the adjacency need not mean that salsoda is *Salsola collina*; in the body, *Salsola collina* appears only as a name for a different substance, *ga.dra*, "stag.tsher (Salsola collina)" (line 973). The `names.csv` row carries class "plant", but the spine and `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` class the entry as mineral, and the text places it among earth medicines. The word *salsoda* is also the Latin-form name of soda ash in general usage, and the only gloss in Clark's text is sodium bicarbonate (line 767); neither identification is established by the sources read. ### Active Components

No text read names a constituent beyond the gloss "sodium bicarbonate" in the list of salty substances (line 767). In general terms, sodium bicarbonate and the related sodium carbonate are alkaline salts; they neutralise acid and are used as raising agents and cleansers. If the plant identification were right, the composition would be different (plant ash salts). [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved say that the Tibetan Explanatory Tantra lists bul.tog among the earth medicines and the salts, as a substance used in tea that halts necrosis (gangrene) and helps digest flour. The identity is uncertain, the ailments (gangrene, poisoning) are emergencies for a clinician, and the Codex does not recommend taking an alkaline salt for them. Salt in tea is a custom and not a treatment in these sources.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources retrieved say only that the substance "is used in tea" (Clark line 862). They do not say how much, how often, how it was prepared, whether it was taken for the stated complaints or as an everyday addition, or in what compound it appears. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Sodium bicarbonate is an antacid and a sodium load; taken in quantity it can cause alkalosis, fluid retention and raised blood pressure, and it should be avoided in sodium-restricted conditions. Gangrene is tissue death from loss of blood supply or infection and is treated surgically and with antimicrobials; no salt in tea treats it. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** two different tastes are printed. Clark's chapter 19 puts salsoda in the salty group (line 767) and the Men-Tsee-Khang text puts Bultog there too (line 3211); Clark's chapter 20 entry says the taste is "bitter" (line 862). The sources read do not reconcile these. No potency (hot, cool and the like, in the sense of chapter 20, line 794) and no post-digestive taste is printed for it. Clark's general statement on the salty taste is that it "toughens the body and removes whorls (of wind, etc.) and blockages [of the channels, etc.]" and that excess "causes falling hair and greying of the hair, increases wrinkles, decreases strength and produces thirst, leprosy, erysipelas and blood and bile disorders" (line 779); this is a statement about the taste in general and is not applied to salsoda here. No other tradition is tagged, so no other classification is offered.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for bul.tog in the lines read. The entry is a statement of use for necrosis and for digesting flour, with a note that it is used in tea. Clark records elsewhere in chapter 20 which substances are named for essence-extraction (calcite, line 853; heart-leaved moonseed, line 971; mineral exudate, line 870); bul.tog is not among them. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted. That the substance is "used in tea" marks it as an ordinary household item, and the text does not turn that into a practice.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The record is that a salt used in Tibetan tea is also listed in the materia medica for gangrene and for digesting flour, and that the text and its translators disagree about whether its taste is salty or bitter and whether it is a mineral or a plant. Any cultivator's use is ordinary seasoning at ordinary quantities, with the general restraint on salt that the same text states for the salty taste. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Contraindications

- **Hypertension, heart, kidney and liver disease, oedema:** sodium salts, including bicarbonate, add to the sodium load and can worsen these conditions.
- **Alkali load:** repeated bicarbonate or carbonate causes metabolic alkalosis and interferes with some drugs.
- **Gangrene, necrosis, poisoning:** emergencies; seek medical care at once; do not rely on a salt.
- **Pregnancy and children:** avoid medicinal quantities.
- **Identification:** "Salsoda" is a translator's English form; the sources read leave open whether the substance is a mineral salt or a plant, and the taste is given as both salty and bitter.
