# Juniperus sabina — Brathus (Dioscorides 1-104, "savin"); "Savin, Sabin"

## *Juniperus sabina [Linnaeus], one of six names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for chapter 1-104 (with Sabina [Fuchs], Sabina folio tamarasci Dioscoridis [Bauhin], Brathys, Brathus [Latin] and Herba sabina), glossed "Savin, Sabin"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk names this plant under this name. The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The SUGGESTED line gives older herbal labels (Fuchs, Bauhin), Latinisations of the Greek name and the Linnaean binomial, and the entry takes the Linnaean one. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *1-104. BRATHUS* at line 4317, SUGGESTED line at 4318 and 4319, text at line 4320. The chapter stands in Book One, after 1-103 *ARKEOTHOS MEGALE, ARKEOTHOS MIKRA* (the junipers) and before 1-105 *KEDROS* (cedar), both different chapters. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 106.

The whole chapter reads: "There are two kinds of savin. One has leaves like cypress, but is more prickly with a strong smell, and sharp with a hot nature. The tree is short and extends itself mostly in breadth. Some use the leaves for perfume. The other kind has leaves like myrica [1-116]. Applied as a poultice the leaves of either of them close stomas [openings] and alleviate inflammation. Similarly, applied as a plaster with honey, they take away all blackness and foulness, and they break up carbuncles [infected boils]. Taken as a drink with wine (as well as applied or by inhalation) they draw out blood through the urine, and drive out the birth. They are mixed with hot ointments and in particular with gleucinum [1-67]. Some call it barathrum, some baryton, and the Romans call it herba sabina." The square brackets are the translators'.

So the chapter distinguishes two kinds of savin. The first has leaves like cypress, more prickly, with a strong smell and a hot, sharp nature; the tree is short and spreads in breadth; its leaves are used by some for perfume. The second has leaves like *myrica* (chapter 1-116). The uses apply to "either of them": the leaves as a poultice to close "stomas [openings]" and ease inflammation; as a plaster with honey to take away "all blackness and foulness" and to break up carbuncles; the drink in wine, "as well as applied or by inhalation", to "draw out blood through the urine, and drive out the birth"; and a place in hot ointments, particularly *gleucinum* (chapter 1-67). The other names are *barathrum*, *baryton* and the Roman *herba sabina*.

**Overlap.** Chapter 1-104 is not covered by an existing entry as a chapter. The juniper entry I_139 (tags EP, AT, DMM, CM, FMT, EF) treats *arkeuthos* in its Dioscorides paragraph (line 22), which corresponds to the neighbouring chapter 1-103 and not to this one, and its line 159 mentions *Juniperus sabina* only as a toxic species that has historically been confused with common juniper. The two Dioscorides chapters are separate, and nothing is merged. The cedar chapter 1-105 follows. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent. It is a general, well-established fact that *Juniperus sabina* is a poisonous conifer shrub whose volatile oil is toxic, and that savin has long been known as a dangerous plant. The existing entry I_139 (line 159) says the same of the species in a safety line. Whether the plant of this chapter is that species is the translators' suggestion, and the two kinds the chapter distinguishes cannot be assigned to species from the text. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a leaf poultice for inflammation, a honey plaster for carbuncles and discolouration, a wine drink that "draws out blood through the urine" and "drives out the birth", and use in hot ointments. These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. The drink recorded here is an abortifacient claim and a bleeding claim, and the plant is generally known to be poisonous, so the Codex gives no protocol and advises against any use of savin.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations and no quantities: the leaves "applied as a poultice"; "as a plaster with honey"; "taken as a drink with wine"; applied or inhaled; mixed into "hot ointments" and "gleucinum". It prints no dose, frequency or duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that savin is toxic when taken internally, that its oil can cause severe poisoning, and that it has been implicated in deaths when used to end pregnancy. The identity of the Dioscorides plant is a translators' suggestion.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 1-104 prints, for the first kind, "a strong smell, and sharp with a hot nature". That is the only quality printed in the chapter. It prints no taste and no moist or dry quality.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a division into two kinds by leaf (cypress-like and prickly against *myrica*-like), a habit of the tree (short and spreading in breadth), a sensory mark (a strong smell and a sharp, hot nature) and a note that the two kinds share their uses. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant, and the plant is poisonous. The transferable point is the habit of telling two near kinds apart by leaf. The abstentive answer is the right one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 1-104 prints no warning, although it records that the drink "drive[s] out the birth". Savin is a poisonous plant and must not be taken by mouth, and must not be used in pregnancy or to end one. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Carbuncles, inflammation and blood in the urine need medical care. The text gives no dose. [USER: verify]
