# camel's milk — "milk of a she-camel" (Sushruta Samhita, Bhishagratna 1907); Sanskrit word not printed in the lines read

## *The milk of the female camel (*Camelus*, with the species not stated in the text). Bhishagratna prints only "she-camel". The domestic camel of the regions the Sushruta text knows would be the one-humped dromedary, but that is our inference, not a statement of the text. Identification: camel milk, species of camel not settled. Confidence: medium* | *(Volume III — Animal)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Sushruta Samhita among the texts on disk, under this English phrase.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 13 October as a curated non-plant addition from the milk group of chapter 45, one of several milk rows added together; this entry replaces its placeholder. The first-pass guess was "camel milk". That stands. The general entry on milk is `III_285_Milk.md` (CPU-00535), which is tagged Assyrian, Avicenna and Ebers, and does not rest on Sushruta. The entry for the meat is `III_56_Camel_flesh.md`.

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The English is Bhishagratna's translation of the *Sushruta Samhita*. The main text is *Sutrasthana* ch.45, "the Chapter, which deals with the rules to be observed in respect of liquid substances in general" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch45.txt`, lines 4 to 6).

1. **The milk group.** Lines 413 to 416: "The Milk Group: The milk of a cow, she-goat, she-camel, ewe, she-buffalo, mare, she-elephant, or of a woman, is what generally comes to the use of man." The camel is the third of eight. The text then speaks of milk in general (lines 419 to 462): "the white fluid essence of drugs and cereals ... heavy, sweet, slimy, cold, glossy, emollient, laxative and mild", beneficial in a long list of diseases, "a sacred, constructive, tonic, spermatopoietic, rejuvenating and aphrodisiac" that "increases the duration of life" and "augments the quality of bodily albumen (Ojah)". These sentences are about milk as a group. Note that the group description calls milk cold and slimy, and the camel's own line (next) gives it nearly the opposite qualities; the text does not say each animal's milk shares each group property.
2. **The camel's own line.** Lines 489 to 494: "The milk of a she-camel is parchifying, heating, light, palatable and possessed of a little saline taste. It proves curative in oedema, abdominal glands, ascites, piles, intestinal worms and Kushtha, and is a good antitoxic agent." ("Hght" in the OCR is read as "light"; "parchifying" is the translator's word for drying.) This is the text's only separate statement on the milk itself.
3. **The curd.** Lines 591 to 596: "Curd prepared with the milk of a she-camel is pungent in digestion. It is found to be charged with alkali, and is heavy and a purgative. A continued use of curdled camel's milk proves curative in Vata, piles, cutaneous affections (Kushtha), worms in the intestines, and abdominal dropsy."
4. **The clarified butter.** Lines 814 to 819: "Clarified butter made with the milk of a she-camel is anti-toxic, appetising and pungent in digestion. It subdues the deranged Vayu and Kapham, and proves curative in oedema, worms in the intestines, cutaneous affections, abdominal glands, and ascites."
5. **A regimen for abdominal dropsy.** *Chikitsasthana* ch.14, on *Udara* (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/chikitsa/SS_Chikitsa_ch14.txt` lines 140 to 148): after a regimen of dieting with no water, or with only buffalo urine and (cow's) milk, the physician may prescribe that the patient "live upon the milk of a she-camel alone, foregoing the use of rice and water and submit himself to a course of Pippali for one month in the manner described before (under the treatment of Maha-Vatavyadhi)". The Pippali course is a separate procedure not read here.
6. **A regimen for Kushtha.** The gap-fill file for the end of *Chikitsasthana* ch.9 (`SS_Chikitsa_ch09_GAPFILL_end_pp360-361.txt`, line 13), a passage added to the corpus on 12 October 2026 from the University of Toronto scan of Bhishagratna's Vol. II, ends a course of oils, clarified butters and purgatives for skin disease with: "As an alternative, camel's urine and after its full digestion, camel's milk should be taken. Even parasitic types of Kushtha are sure to disappear within six months (under the course of this treatment)." The camel's urine is a separate row and a separate material. [USER: verify the passage against the printed page.]
7. **Time and treatment of milk.** The chapter's general rules apply to all milk (morning milk heavy and cold; evening milk refrigerant and eye-invigorating; cold or unboiled milk "extremely heavy", boiling frees it of the injurious traits, except for woman's milk; spoiled milk is unwholesome; lines 524 to 553). The text does not apply them to the camel in so many words.

### Active Components

The text names only a "little saline taste" and, for the curd, that it is "charged with alkali". In general terms, milk is an emulsion of fat in water carrying protein, lactose, minerals and vitamins. Camel milk is generally described as differing from cow's milk in its protein profile and as lacking one of the whey proteins of cow's milk; this is a general statement and no figure is given because none was read from a source. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only; not a recommended practice.** Camel milk is an ordinary drink in the regions where camels are kept, and needs no protocol from this Codex.

- **What the text says.** Drying, heating, light, palatable, slightly saline; curative in oedema, abdominal glands, ascites, piles, worms and *Kushtha*, and antitoxic. Its curd and butter act in similar diseases. In *Chikitsasthana* ch.14 it is a milk-only regimen for abdominal dropsy, and in the ch.9 gap-fill an alternative course for *Kushtha*.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no quantity, no duration beyond "one month" for the Pippali course and "six months" for the skin course.

The Codex gives no regimen built on camel milk, and in particular does not recommend a milk-only diet or the waterless regimen named for dropsy. Those are historical records.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text's preparations of the milk are: drinking it alone (ch.14), taking it after camel's urine has been digested (ch.9 gap-fill), curdling it (ch.45 lines 591 to 596) and churning it to clarified butter (lines 814 to 819). It gives no dose. The periods named, one month and six months, belong to the whole course and not to a quantity of milk. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Camel milk is a mammalian milk, drunk in North Africa, the Middle East and Central Asia. Raw camel milk has been linked to brucellosis, and dromedary camels are the known animal reservoir of the MERS coronavirus, so raw camel milk from infected animals is a recognised infection risk; heat treatment reduces it. [USER: verify] People with milk-protein allergy may react. A regimen of camel milk alone with no water or other food would be nutritionally unsound. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Ayurveda (SS), ch.45 line 489.** Qualities: drying ("parchifying"), heating, light; taste: palatable with a little saline taste. The curd is pungent in digestion, heavy and alkali-charged and a purgative; the butter is antitoxic, appetising and pungent in digestion, and subdues *Vayu* and *Kapham*. The English prints no separate *virya* or *vipaka* for the milk itself beyond these words.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status of its own.* In *Sutrasthana* ch.45, a chapter on liquid substances and not a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* chapter, milk as a class is called "constructive, tonic, spermatopoietic, rejuvenating and aphrodisiac", increases the duration of life, and augments bodily albumen (*Ojah*) (lines 452 to 462). The camel's milk is listed among the milks (line 415), but its own line is therapeutic and describes it as drying, heating and light, curative of dropsy, piles, worms and skin disease, and antitoxic, with no claim about *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana*. The text does not say that the class description holds for the camel's milk alike. It is therefore a framed claim: the milk group carries the language of rejuvenation as a group; the camel's own line is that of a remedy. The popular *sattvic*/*rajasic*/*tamasic* food lists belong to *Samkhya*-era and later material, not to Sushruta, and are not used here.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here makes camel's milk a cultivation substance. Sushruta treats it as a drying, heating remedy for dropsy, worms and skin disease, which is the opposite of the "cold, slimy, nourishing" milk of the group description. A cultivator has no use for it beyond ordinary diet where it is an ordinary drink, and the milk-only regimen is a historical record.

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection.** Raw camel milk carries a risk of brucellosis and other infections, and dromedaries are the animal reservoir of MERS-CoV. Boil or pasteurise; avoid contact with sick animals.
- **Milk-only or waterless regimens.** The ch.14 regimen (milk alone, no rice and no water) is a historical record. Do not attempt it; it risks dehydration, malnutrition and delay of proper care for dropsy.
- **Allergy and intolerance.** Milk-protein allergy and lactose intolerance apply.
- **Dropsy, ascites, piles, worms, skin disease.** These are medical conditions; use proper care.
- **Ethics and law.** Domestic camels are farmed in several regions; the wild Bactrian camel is a separate, threatened species. Some communities have food rules about camel products.
