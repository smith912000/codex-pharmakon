# Gentiana umula — gang.ga.chhung, Gangga chhung (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Gentiana umula, as the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints it, apparently an OCR form of Gentiana urnula, which is how Clark 1995 prints the first of three binomials for gang.ga.chhung (then Gentiana depressa and Leonurus sibiricus)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); the spine row rests on one printed line of one text, and the Tibetan name comes from a second text.** The row's locus is the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary, line 5283 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, row 278: "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)"). The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. No other text read for this entry names the plant: a search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk (including the three Bhishagratna volumes) for the genus name *Gentiana* found Dioscorides 3-3 GENTIANE (line 6541, glosses *Gentiana lutea* and Yellow Gentian) and Yang 1998's Long Dan (line 1936) and Qin Jiao (line 2494). These are other species and are not attributed to this row. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**The spelling.** The spine name "umula" is a binomial that does not appear in Clark 1995. Clark prints "Gentiana urnula H.sim" for gang.ga.chhung (line 1011), "Gentiana urnula" in the list of medicines that neutralize poisons (line 1212) and in the index of Latin names (line 1590). The same Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints "Seabuckthom" for sea buckthorn (line 5950) and "Datura thomapple" (line 5958), forms in which "rn" has been read as "m". The Codex therefore reads "umula" as an OCR form of "urnula". The spine name and this entry's title have been left as filed; whether to correct them to *Gentiana urnula* is a decision for the Codex editor. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** The key is the Men-Tsee-Khang scientific name. In Clark's line the same Latin name is the first of three (*Gentiana urnula*, *Gentiana depressa*, *Leonurus sibiricus*), so the key is the first name there.

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (lines 5274 to 5297).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "G" block. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. Ten phonetic names are printed at lines 5274 to 5278 ("Gabur", "Gadra", "Gadur", then "Gakya Gangga chhung Gokya Goje Gongthokpa" and "Go nyoe Gudue"). Ten transliterations follow at line 5280 ("ga bur ga bra li ga dur sga skya gang ga chung sgog skya go bye gong thog pa go snyod rgu drs"). The scientific names (lines 5282 to 5287) run "Cinnamomum camphora Rubus ellipticus Bergenia stracheyi Hedychium spicatum", "Gentiana umula", "Allium sativum", "Senecarpus sp. Erysimum melicentaea", "Carum carvi", "Senecio scandens", ten in all. The English column (lines 5289 to 5297) runs "Camphor Raspberry", "-", "Wild ginger", "-", "Garlic", "-", "-", "Caraway", "-". Counted in order, the fifth phonetic name, "Gangga chhung" (transliteration "gang ga chung"), stands against the fifth scientific name, *Gentiana umula*, and against a blank in the English column. The count is consistent at the points where the English names can be checked ("Garlic" at sixth place against *Allium sativum* and "sgog skya"; "Caraway" at ninth against *Carum carvi*). The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* Line 3432 (page 207), in the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), reads: "Gangga chhung cures poison and dysentery." Line 3605 (page 220) lists "Gangga chhung" among the medicines "that cure poisoning". The index (line 7372) lists "Gangga chhung 207, 220, 306"; pages 207 and 220 were found, page 306 was not located. The row's locus (line 5283) is the glossary line, which gives a name and no action.

*Clark 1995, the same Tibetan name (line 1011).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry stands in a very long OCR line that holds many entries (it follows *gyah.kyi.ma* and precedes *bya.rgod.spos*). It reads:

"gang.ga.chhung: Gentiana urnula H.sim: Gentiana depressa: Leonurus sibiricus: [grows in rocky meadows and is a four-pointed, eight-sided, banner-shaped plant with a bell-shaped flower. It has bitter taste and cool power and] dispels poisoning and dysentery caused by fever."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the brackets (commentary layer) are the habitat, the description, the bitter taste and the cool power. Outside them (root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the three Latin names, which are Clark's identifications, and the clause "dispels poisoning and dysentery caused by fever". Clark's chapter 21 lists *Gentiana urnula* in "the class of medicines which neutralize poisons" (line 1212, among Musk, the aconites, Turmeric and *Gentiana algida*). The two Tibetan texts agree on the Tibetan name, the genus and the action. They differ in one word: Men-Tsee-Khang says "cures poison and dysentery", Clark adds "caused by fever". The Codex does not choose a species beyond what the texts print.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder found no entry on *Gentiana urnula*, "umula" or gang.ga.chhung as the subject. I_1264 (*Delphinium brunonianum*) quotes the same Clark line 1011 and the Men-Tsee-Khang line 3432 for its neighbour *bya.rgod.spos*. I_1243 (*Bergenia stracheyi*) quotes the same glossary block (line 5276). I_1286 (*Gentiana algida*) is a sibling entry on a different gentian in the same poison class. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Gentiana* is a genus of the gentian family (Gentianaceae), and that bitter secoiridoid glycosides, of which gentiopicroside is an example, are characteristic of the family; Clark's commentary does record a "bitter taste". Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained or which part was used.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "dispels poisoning and dysentery caused by fever" (Clark, line 1011) and "cures poison and dysentery" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3432), and that stands in the groups of medicines that cure poisoning. These are the texts' claims and not Codex protocols. Poisoning and dysentery are emergencies and the Codex offers no advice for them.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it dispels poisoning and dysentery caused by fever, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same in kind. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the sentences cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a cool, bitter drug for poisoning and dysentery in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Poisoning and dysentery need medical care, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
