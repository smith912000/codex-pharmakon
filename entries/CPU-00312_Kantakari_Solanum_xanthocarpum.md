# Kantakari (Yellow-berried Nightshade) — कण्टकारी (Kaṇṭakārī), Nidigdhikā, Dhāvani, Kṣudrā, Duḥsparśā, Vyāghrī

## *Solanum xanthocarpum* Schrad. & Wendl. (≡ *S. virginianum* L.) | Family: Solanaceae

**Tradition Tags:** Suśruta Saṃhitā (Daśamūla component); Caraka Saṃhitā (kāsahara, śvāsahara groups); Ayurvedic (dravyaguṇa); Siddha (respiratory materia medica); folk medicine

**Energetic Classification (Ayurvedic):** Kapha-Vāta-śāmaka (chiefly clears Kapha from the respiratory channels and pacifies Vāta); heating.

**Training Phase:** Respiratory-opening / Breath-work · Kapha-clearing · Acute congestion support

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda (Suśruta & the classical corpus).** Kaṇṭakārī — "the thorny one," from *kaṇṭaka*, thorn — is one of the two nightshades (the "lesser" and the "greater") that anchor the Ayurvedic treatment of the breath. It is the **Kṣudrā** ("the small/lesser") of the classical pair *Bṛhatī–Kaṇṭakārī*, and both are among the **Daśamūla**, the "ten roots" — the single most important root-formula complex in Ayurveda, used across fever, respiratory disease, Vāta disorders and convalescence. Within the Daśamūla, Kaṇṭakārī and Bṛhatī form the two *kṣudra-mūla* (lesser roots), paired against the five great trees and the other lesser roots. Suśruta and Caraka classify Kaṇṭakārī among the **kāsahara** (anti-tussive) and **śvāsahara** (anti-dyspnoeic) dravyas; it is the go-to nightshade for kāsa (cough), śvāsa (dyspnoea/asthma), and hikkā (hiccough), and it is used for jvara (fever), pārśvaśūla (chest/flank pain) and heart conditions.

The Sanskrit synonym-cluster encodes its character: **Nidigdhikā** and **Duḥsparśā** ("hard to touch," for its thorns), **Vyāghrī** ("tigress," for its fierceness), **Kṣudrā** ("the lesser," distinguishing it from Bṛhatī). It is the yellow-berried, spreading, spiny herb of wasteland and roadside — a low, defended, sun-loving plant, its bright yellow fruit the diagnostic marker.

**Siddha and folk practice.** In the Tamil Siddha tradition (as *kaṇṭaṅkattiri*) the whole plant is a first-line remedy for respiratory disease, and the Govindan clinical studies (below) explicitly frame *S. xanthocarpum* as "widely used to treat respiratory diseases in southern Indian traditional medicine (Siddha)." The smoke of the burning seeds and fruit has been used folk-medically for toothache and cough.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Thorn that Opens the Breath:** Kaṇṭakārī is the defended, low, fierce plant — thorned, called "tigress" — and in the codex its doctrine is that *what clears the obstructed channel is itself sharp and heating*. The breath, in the Ayurvedic reading, is obstructed by kapha: cold, heavy, sticky mucus lodged in the srotas of the chest. The remedy is not soothing but penetrating — a hot, bitter, pungent, thorn-natured drug that cuts through the stagnation and reopens the passage. The tigress-plant is the principle of therapeutic fierceness: the gentle, cooling approach fails against deep kapha congestion; the channel is opened by heat and edge. Paired with its "greater" sibling Bṛhatī, it forms the doctrinal dyad of *lesser and greater* — the same fierceness in two magnitudes.

### Active Components

The genus is defined by its **steroidal alkaloids** — chiefly **solasodine** and its glycoside **solasonine**, together with **solamargine** — alongside steroidal saponins, the sterol **carpesterol**, flavonoids (apigenin, luteolin glycosides) and coumarins (e.g. scopoletin). Solasodine is the principal marker compound and the presumed carrier of much of the respiratory and anti-inflammatory activity. The classification within the *Solanaceous alkaloids* is noted in the MeSH indexing of the clinical trials below.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Kaṇṭakārī is the codex's **respiratory-opening / breath-work** substance for *cold-wet (kapha) obstruction of the chest*: productive cough, wheeze, a sense of heaviness and congestion, breath that feels blocked rather than merely shallow. Its action is bronchodilatory and expectorant — it opens the airway and mobilises secretions — with an anti-allergic/anti-histaminic dimension that makes it relevant where the obstruction has a reactive component. It is used in the acute-congestion and breath-work phases, and it pairs with Bṛhatī as the lesser member of the Daśamūla root-dyad.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, Kaṇṭakārī has an unusually direct clinical evidence base for a classical Ayurvedic dravya. In a pilot study, Govindan et al. (*Journal of Ethnopharmacology*, 1999) gave 300 mg of whole-plant powder of *S. xanthocarpum* (and of *S. trilobatum*) to patients with mild-to-moderate bronchial asthma and measured spirometry (FVC, FEV1, PEFR, FEF25–75%) before and 2 h after dosing; both herbs significantly improved pulmonary function, though the effect was smaller than salbutamol or deriphylline, with no untoward effects reported. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0378-8741(98)00160-3)

A follow-up randomised controlled trial (Govindan et al., *Phytotherapy Research*, 2004) gave 300 mg three times daily for 3 days and found progressive improvement in PEFR and reductions in rhonchi, cough, breathlessness and sputum — a response "equivalent to that of deriphylline but less than salbutamol," interpreted as bronchodilation plus reduction of airway oedema and secretions. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.1555) A review of botanical anti-allergy agents (Saini & Dhiman, *Anti-inflammatory & Anti-allergy Agents in Medicinal Chemistry*, 2022) lists *S. xanthocarpum* among herbs with anti-histaminic/anti-allergic activity useful in respiratory-tract allergic disease. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2174/1871523021666220411111743)

**Evidence grade: MODERATE — includes small human trials (one RCT).** The bronchodilator/expectorant signal is supported by controlled human data, but the trials are small and short. Claims are attributed to PubMed-indexed sources; efficacy is real but modest and not equivalent to standard bronchodilators.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Rasa (taste):** *Tikta* (bitter), *Kaṭu* (pungent).
- **Guṇa (qualities):** *Laghu* (light), *Rūkṣa* (dry), *Tīkṣṇa* (sharp/penetrating).
- **Vīrya (potency):** *Uṣṇa* (heating).
- **Vipāka (post-digestive effect):** *Kaṭu* (pungent).
- **Prabhāva (specific action):** *Śvāsahara / kāsahara* — a near-specific opening of the obstructed respiratory channel.
- **Doṣa action:** *Kapha-Vāta-śāmaka*; can aggravate Pitta if overused because of its heat and sharpness.
- **Karma (actions):** *Kāsahara* (anti-tussive), *Śvāsahara* (anti-asthmatic/anti-dyspnoeic), *Dīpana–Pācana* (kindles and digests), *Hṛdya* (cardiac tonic in classical use), *Jvaraghna* (antipyretic), *Vātānulomana* (regulates downward Vāta).
- **Srotas (channels):** primarily *prāṇavaha* (respiratory); secondarily *annavaha*.
- **Gaṇa membership:** one of the two *kṣudra-mūla* (lesser roots) of the **Daśamūla**; classed among Caraka's kāsahara and śvāsahara dravyas.

### Contraindications

- Heating and sharp — use with caution in Pitta-predominant states, hyperacidity, and active inflammation of the GI tract.
- Solanaceous steroidal alkaloids (solasodine/solasonine/solamargine) are potentially toxic in excess; overdosage risks gastrointestinal and neurotoxic effects. Do not exceed traditional doses.
- Avoid in pregnancy without qualified supervision.
- Not a replacement for rescue bronchodilators in acute or severe asthma — the trial effect was smaller than salbutamol; do not substitute for emergency asthma treatment.
- Distinguish carefully from Bṛhatī (*S. indicum*) and from *S. trilobatum*; commercial adulteration between the nightshades is common.
