# Water rat flesh — chhu.byi (colloquial chhu.yer), "Water rat (amphibious rodent with long snout and tail)" (Clark 1995)
## *The flesh of the water rat, an amphibious rodent; Clark prints no binomial for it, and glosses the bird named beside it as Cinclus cinclus* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The Explanatory Tantra (*bshad rgyud*), chapter 20, "Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", has a section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" listing thirteen kinds of animal product (Clark 1995, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` lines 1147-1148). The water rat comes near the end of that section, in a run of small whole creatures (beetle, slug, water rat, lizard) after the droppings; the OCR text prints no sub-heading for the run, and I take it to belong to the last of the thirteen kinds, "whole body". [USER: verify] The line (1202) reads: "chhu.byi (colloquial chhu.yer): Water rat (amphibious rodent with long snout and tail): rba.byi: Cinclus cinclus (a white-throated aquatic bird): Its flesh and that of water rat cure meat poisoning." After that the same line identifies a lizard (*rmigs.pa*, *Eremias argus*, which "is anthelmintic and neutralizes poisons") and ends the list of powers with the "medicinal horses", the vehicles raw cane sugar, crystal sugar and honey.

Two points about this line need stating plainly. First, the sentence is attached to the bird: "Its flesh" follows the entry for *rba.byi*, the white-throated aquatic bird (given as *Cinclus cinclus*), and "that of water rat" is added to it. So the water rat's flesh is credited with the same action, meat-poisoning, but as the second item in a two-item statement. Second, the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` lines 3572-3574) reads: "The meat of water birds and white-throated dippers cure meat poisoning." It has "water birds" where Clark has "water rat". I cannot tell from either English text what the Tibetan carries, or whether Clark's gloss "water rat" and the Men-Tsee-Khang "water birds" are two readings of one word. [USER: verify] This is the reason this entry's identity is flagged: the Tibetan name *chhu.byi* and Clark's "water rat" support the tag, and the Men-Tsee-Khang reading does not.

The name list `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` has no row for water rat; the attestation here rests on Clark's line alone. Clark's own preface (line 83) warns that "a great deal of Tibetan materia medica has remained either totally unidentified or only tentatively identified", and that where doubt remains about animal products he has given the Tibetan name beside the probable English identification. The water rat is such a case, with the colloquial name *chhu.yer* printed beside the book name.

*Other texts on disk, not folded in.* I searched the Sushruta chapters, Dioscorides, Bryan's Ebers, Yang's Shennong and Thompson's Assyrian text for "water rat" and "water-rat" and found nothing. Rats and mice appear in other entries of this book; they are different materials.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, flesh is mostly water and muscle protein, with fat and small amounts of vitamins and minerals. Nothing links any constituent to the action the Tantra gives, which is against *meat poisoning*, a category the text does not define.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan line says the flesh of the water rat (and of the dipper) cures meat poisoning. The Codex does not recommend it. Meat poisoning in modern terms is food-borne illness or toxin ingestion and needs medical attention; eating a wild rodent for it would add risk. Water rodents can carry infections that pass to people, and the animal's identity in the text is itself uncertain. The entry exists to record what the Explanatory Tantra says.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither translation gives a preparation, vehicle or quantity for water rat flesh. The "medicinal horses" at the end of the chapter (Clark line 1202) are named for compounded medicines in general; the text does not say that this flesh was taken with any of them. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Wild rodents that live near water can carry bacteria such as *Leptospira* (which passes to people through contact with contaminated water or animal urine) and other zoonotic organisms, and handling or eating wild rodent meat is a recognised infection risk. Food poisoning is treated according to its cause. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** No taste, post-digestive taste or potency is printed for the water rat's flesh. The Tantra's general rule is that all meats have a sweet taste and a sweet post-digestive taste (Clark line 673; Men-Tsee-Khang line 2923), and it gives dry-land, aquatic and amphibious animals different powers; Clark says amphibians "have both varieties of power" (line 673). The water rat is described as "amphibious", but the text does not list it among the meats in its classification (line 672), so these qualities are not assigned to it here. Its only printed action is against meat poisoning.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Water rat flesh appears in the materia medica of animal products with a single therapeutic action (Clark line 1202), alongside a bird whose identity is better fixed. The wider frame is real but does not reach it. In Clark's account the three humors are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration (line 51) and these three are "the three causes" of wind, bile and phlegm disorders (line 147), so disease is at root a spiritual condition and medicine is one support; the chapter on routine behaviour asks the practitioner to refrain from the ten negative actions, the first of which is "taking life", and to "regard worms, insects and the like as being similar to oneself" (lines 616-617). The rejuvenation passage in chapter 23 (lines 1266-1275) does not name this substance, and the lines read give it no *bcud len* use. The tradition has no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The water rat is a minor antidote entry in a list of thirteen kinds of animal product, with an identity the two English translations disagree on, and the tradition's own first precept is against taking life. The Codex does not recommend it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Infection and hygiene:** wild water rodents carry bacteria and parasites; handling, skinning and eating them is a risk.
- **Food poisoning:** suspected poisoning, with vomiting, diarrhoea or any neurological sign, needs urgent medical assessment, not an animal remedy.
- **Identity:** the Tibetan text may mean a rodent or a water bird; do not infer which animal is safe to use.
- **Conservation, law and ethics:** wild-animal taking is regulated in most places, and the Tibetan source itself places "taking life" first among the ten negative actions (Clark line 616).
