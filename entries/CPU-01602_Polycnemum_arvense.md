# Polycnemum arvense — Poluknemon (Dioscorides 3-108)

## *Polycnemum arvense, the first of two names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-108 (with Polycnemum recurvum), which prints no English gloss* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 7509) reads "Polycnemum arvense, Polycnemum recurvum", with no English name and no choice between the two. The row takes the first. It was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-108. POLUKNEMON*, heading at line 7508, SUGGESTED line at 7509, text at line 7510. It stands in Book Three, before *3-109. KLINOPODION* (heading at line 7511). The translators' index gives *Polycnemum arvense* at page 492.

The whole chapter reads: "Polycnemon is a shrub full of sprigs, with leaves similar to origanum, and a stalk with many joints like pulegium [3-36]. It does not have a tuft but little clusters on the top with a certain sharp, pleasant smell. It is effective (applied green, or dried, with water) for closing open cuts and sore wounds. You must loosen it after it has been applied for five days. It is taken in a drink with wine for slow painful urination and hernia. (Experience has taught how the little branches bruised in white wine are a great help for those possessed with the so-called water delirium.) It is also called clinopodium, polygonatum, colus iovis, or echeonymon, and the Romans call it puteologonthria."

Dioscorides gives a description (a shrub "full of sprigs", leaves like *origanum*, a many-jointed stalk like *pulegium*, little clusters on the top, a "sharp, pleasant smell") and three uses. The herb, green or dried, applied with water, closes "open cuts and sore wounds", and one instruction goes with it: "You must loosen it after it has been applied for five days." Taken in a drink with wine it is for "slow painful urination and hernia". The third use sits in a parenthesis and is introduced by an appeal to experience: "Experience has taught how the little branches bruised in white wine are a great help for those possessed with the so-called water delirium." The chapter does not explain "water delirium", and the Codex does not gloss it. [USER: verify] The closing list gives four other names (*clinopodium*, *polygonatum*, *colus iovis*, *echeonymon*) and a Roman name, *puteologonthria*, with no use attached.

**Names shared with other chapters.** The next chapter, *3-109. KLINOPODION*, is a separate plant, a "little shrub full of shoots two feet high" with its own text, and the name *clinopodium* printed here as another name of *polycnemon* is the heading of that chapter. The Codex does not read the two chapters as one plant. The name *polygonatum* printed here is likewise a name only, and the Codex does not link it to any other chapter. Where two names match, the match is reported and is not settled.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter number, *Poluknemon*, *Polycnemum* and *polycnemon* found no existing entry on 3-108.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a sweet-smelling shrub applied to cuts and wounds, drunk in wine for painful urination and hernia, and, on the strength of "experience", used for "water delirium". These are the text's own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for wounds, hernia, urinary difficulty or delirium, and a hernia or a delirium is a medical matter. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations: the herb applied "green, or dried, with water" to cuts and wounds and removed after five days; the herb "taken in a drink with wine"; and the little branches "bruised in white wine". The five days is the only duration printed. No quantity or frequency is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. It prints a "sharp, pleasant smell" for the clusters on the top.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use. What it offers is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: leaf and stalk compared with two other herbs, a smell noted, the same herb used green or dried, and a timing rule for the dressing (loosen it after five days). It also marks one use as learned by experience. The plant is given no status beyond a list of remedies. The identification is the translators'.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It offers a description and three remedies, one marked as learned by experience, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Open wounds, hernia, painful or slow urination and delirium all need medical assessment, and a home remedy must not replace it. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion between two species, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
