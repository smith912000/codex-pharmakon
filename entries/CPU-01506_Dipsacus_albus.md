## *Dipsacus albus (the translators' first suggestion; "Fullers' Teazle, Draper's Teazle"), in a list that continues with Dipsacus fullonum and Dipsacus sylvestris, "Wild Teazle, Shepherd's Rod, Barber's Brushes"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant as a drug. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The SUGGESTED line (lines 6643 and 6644) reads "Dipsacus albus, Cardo fullonum, Carduus fullonius [Fuchs], Dipsacus sativus [Bauhin], Dipsacus fullonum — Fullers' Teazle, Draper's Teazle Dipsacus sylvestris — Wild Teazle, Shepherd's Rod, Barber's Brushes". The first-named binomial is the spine's; two other species are named on the same line.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-13. DIPSAKON*, heading at line 6642, text at line 6645. It stands in Book Three, "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants", after 3-12 *KROKODEILION* (whose last lines, at 6640 and 6641, belong to that chapter and not to this one).

The whole chapter reads: "Dipsacus is also a prickly plant. It has a high stalk full of prickles, with leaves enclosing the stalk similar to lettuce, two at every knot, tall and prickly, having (as it were) some prickly bladders on the middle of the back both within and without, and hollow places around the two (joining) parts of the leaves so that it gathers water from the dew and showers (which is how it got its name). On the top of the stalk at every shoot there is one head similar to a hedgehog, somewhat long and prickly. Dried it turns white, but the head (divided) has small worms around the middle of the pith. The root of this (boiled with wine and pounded until the thickness of a wax ointment) is put in to heal cracks and fistulas in the perineum. The medicine must be stored in a brass box. They say that it is a cure for protruding and hanging warts. The worms from the heads (bound up in a purse and hanged around the neck or the arm) are said to cure those who have fevers with recurrent paroxysms. It is also called crocodilium, chamaeleon, or onocardium, some call it the bath of Venus, the Romans call it the lip of Venus, some, the thistle of Venus, the Egyptians, seseneor, some, chir, or meleta, and the Dacians, sciare."

So this chapter is mostly a careful botanical description: a tall prickly stalk, lettuce-like leaves in pairs at each knot, prickly "bladders" on the leaf-backs, and hollows where the paired leaves join that collect dew and rain ("which is how it got its name"), and a hedgehog-like head on every shoot that turns white when dried and holds small worms in the pith. The uses are three: the boiled and pounded root, made to the thickness of a wax ointment, healing cracks and fistulas of the perineum; a statement ("They say") that it cures protruding and hanging warts; and the worms from the heads, bound in a purse and hung around the neck or arm, "said to cure" recurrent fevers. The ointment must be stored in a brass box. The other names printed are Greek (*crocodilium, chamaeleon, onocardium*), Roman ("the lip of Venus"), others' ("the bath of Venus", "the thistle of Venus"), Egyptian (*seseneor*, some *chir* or *meleta*) and Dacian (*sciare*).

The binomial *Dipsacus* is the translators' candidate. The statement about the "worms" is Dioscorides' own and the Codex does not explain it. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a wine-boiled root ointment for perineal cracks and fistulas, a report that it cures warts, and a charm-like use of the head's contents hung around the neck for fevers with recurrent paroxysms, hedged with "said to". The Codex gives no protocol: fistulas and recurrent fevers need medical care, and a fever is a symptom, not a condition to charm away. The charm is recorded, not endorsed.

### Preparation & Dosing

3-13 gives these preparations: the root "boiled with wine and pounded until the thickness of a wax ointment" and "put in"; the ointment "stored in a brass box"; and the worms from the heads "bound up in a purse and hanged around the neck or the arm". No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter is a good example of the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: it is the longest description in the batch, giving stalk, paired leaves, leaf-backs, the water-catching hollows, the head, the colour on drying and the contents of the head. It also records a hung-on-the-body use, "bound up in a purse and hanged around the neck or the arm", under "are said to cure", so he distances himself from it; recording is not endorsing. It assigns the plant no status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. Its uses are a perineal ointment, a wart remedy and a charm for fevers, with no amounts, so it is history and not a practice. Its lasting value is as a model of how to describe a plant closely enough to recognise it.

### Contraindications

Fistulas of the perineum, protruding warts and fevers with recurrent paroxysms need medical care, and a home preparation must not replace it. The charm of hanging a purse of insect contents on the body does not treat a fever. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion covering several species, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
