# Shalmali / Silk Cotton Tree

## *Bombax ceiba* L. | Family: Malvaceae (formerly Bombacaceae)

**Tradition Tags:** SS
**Energetic Classification:** Cooling, astringent (*kashaya*), drying — **attributed to the Ayurvedic stream via secondary transmission**; the primary Sushruta text was not consulted for this entry, and the specific *rasa/virya/vipaka* assignment is marked as requiring verification against a primary edition.
**Training Phase:** Foundation — Signature Study

### Origin & Tradition

The red silk-cotton tree is a large deciduous tree of the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia, described in the recent review literature as culturally, economically and medicinally significant and valued both for its fibre and for its place in traditional medicine (Kumari et al., *Chemistry & Biodiversity*, 2025). It is unmistakable: a buttressed trunk, young bark armed with stout conical prickles, and — before the leaves return — a canopy of large scarlet, five-petalled flowers held on bare grey branches. The fruit is a capsule that splits to release seeds embedded in the pale silky floss ("kapok"-type fibre) that gives the tree its English name. Older literature and some pharmacological papers use the synonym *Bombax malabaricum* DC.; a 1992 Taiwanese hepatoprotection study is published under that name, and cultivators should treat *B. malabaricum* citations as referring to this tree.

**SS — the Sushruta / Ayurvedic stream.** Shalmali enters the codex on the Ayurvedic tag, and the parts are distinguished sharply, which is the discipline this entry teaches:

- **The gum or dried exudate — *mocharasa* (semul gum)** — is the astringent article, traditionally given for diarrhoea, dysentery, and excessive menstrual bleeding (menorrhagia). This is the classical core use.
- **The root, particularly the young tuberous root — *semul musli*** — is the tonic/*vajikarana* (aphrodisiac) article.
- **Young roots and flowers** enter food as well as medicine; the flowers are eaten as a vegetable, and the same review documents them as a source of fibre, carbohydrate, vitamin C, and the minerals iron, sodium, phosphorus and calcium — a food-plant status that is independently supported.

The tree also carries a ritual and symbolic weight in Indian tradition, and it is grown and marked in ways that reflect that. The codex records the *existence* of that role on the strength of the review's statement that the tree "holds cultural, economic, and medicinal significance," and declines to specify any particular deity association, festival, or textual episode. **[USER: verify]** any specific mythological or ritual attribution — including any Puranic or epic reference — against a primary Indological source before it is written into the manuscript. Specific ritual claims are exactly the class of detail that gets invented.

### Active Components

Confirmed in the retrieved literature:

- **Mangiferin** — a C-glucosyl xanthone. This is the best-documented single constituent: it is obtained **directly from methanolic extracts of *B. ceiba* leaves in substantial amounts** (Dar et al., 2005), and it is named in the 2025 flower review as a principal bioactive.
- **Shamimin** — a C-flavonol glucoside isolated from *B. ceiba* leaves, and the compound behind the hypotensive claim (Saleem et al., 1999).
- **Flower phenolics and flavonoids** — quercetin, kaempferol, gallic acid, catechin, and the sterol β-sitosterol, all named in the flower review.
- **Fibre, carbohydrate, vitamin C, iron, sodium, phosphorus, calcium** in the flowers.

**Not confirmed here.** The hooks proposed lupeol, naphthoquinones, tannins and mucilage. Tannins and mucilage are entirely plausible for an astringent gum and are the obvious chemical rationale for *mocharasa*, and lupeol and naphthoquinones (e.g. the shamimicin/mansonone-type compounds reported for the genus in the wider literature) are commonly cited — but **none of these was verified against a retrieved primary source in this run**, and so none is asserted here. They are listed as **[USER: verify]** rather than written into the codex as fact. This matters practically: the astringent gum, the part with the strongest traditional indication, is the part with the *weakest* confirmed chemistry in front of us.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Shalmali is filed as a Signature Study for a specific lesson: **the part with the tradition and the part with the data are not the same part.**

The tradition's centre of gravity is the **gum**, used astringently. The pharmacology's centre of gravity is the **leaf** (mangiferin, shamimin) and increasingly the **flower** (phenolics, food/nutraceutical framing). Almost nothing retrieved here tests the gum. A cultivator who reads a mangiferin antioxidant paper and then takes it as validation of *mocharasa* for diarrhoea has committed a part-substitution error, and this entry exists partly to name that error.

Working uses, honestly framed:

1. **Flowers as a food** — the only use with a solid, non-controversial footing. Documented nutrient content, eaten as a vegetable, and a reasonable subject for nutraceutical development.
2. **Gum as a traditional astringent** for loose stool and heavy menstrual bleeding — traditional grade, no clinical evidence.
3. **Leaf as a research substrate** — the source of mangiferin and shamimin, of interest to the cultivator as a plant to *grow and study*, not a plant to dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

**There is no established human dose for any part of *Bombax ceiba* in the evidence retrieved for this entry.** No human clinical trial was found. The table records traditional form only.

| Part | Traditional form | Traditional indication | Dose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gum / exudate (*mocharasa*) | Dried tears; powder, or decoction | Diarrhoea, dysentery, menorrhagia | **None established** |
| Root (*semul musli*) | Dried root powder, often with milk | Tonic, *vajikarana* | **None established** |
| Flowers | Cooked as vegetable; dried | Food; traditional medicine | Food quantities only |
| Leaf | Research extracts (methanolic/aqueous) | — | **Research preparation only — not a home dose** |

Safety caveats that constrain preparation: the toxicology below establishes that a leaf-derived isolate can be lethal in rodents at an achievable dose, which forbids any casual scaling-up of leaf extracts. Floss must be handled with respiratory protection during harvest and processing.

### Scientific Correlation

Graded claim-by-claim.

- **Hypotensive activity — PRECLINICAL (rat), compound-level.** Shamimin, from *B. ceiba* leaves, showed significant hypotensive potency in Sprague-Dawley rats at 15, 3 and 1 mg/kg. This is a real, species-correct, dose-ranging animal finding, and the strongest single result in the file. It is not a human antihypertensive.
- **Hypoglycaemic activity — PRECLINICAL (rat), and note the dose gap.** Shamimin was hypoglycaemic in rats **at 500 mg/kg** — over thirty times the top hypotensive dose, and the same dose reported as lethal in rats. This is a poor therapeutic window and the codex records it as a negative signal, not a diabetes indication.
- **Toxicology — PRECLINICAL, and load-bearing.** Shamimin caused no mortality in mice at 1 g/kg, **but 500 mg/kg was a lethal dose in rats.** Species divergence of this size is a warning, not a reassurance.
- **Antioxidant activity — PRECLINICAL, compound-level.** Mangiferin from *B. ceiba* leaves showed strong DPPH radical-scavenging activity (EC₅₀ 5.8 ± 0.96 µg/ml, 13.74 µM), comparable to rutin, with structure-activity work showing free hydroxyls and the catechol moiety are essential.
- **Hepatoprotective activity — PRECLINICAL, and MIXED.** Positive: mangiferin protected against carbon tetrachloride-induced liver injury *in vivo*; and an aqueous extract of *Bombax malabarica* significantly reduced CCl₄-induced SGOT and SGPT in rats (p<0.001) with histopathological confirmation. Qualifying: in a 64-plant Cambodian screen against *t*-BHP-induced hepatotoxicity in HepG2 cells, *B. ceiba* was among 19 extracts with "high hepatoprotective activities" — but the authors carried forward only *Peliosanthes weberi* and *Tinospora crispa* for mechanism, so *B. ceiba* is a screening hit, not a validated agent.
- **Analgesic activity — PRECLINICAL (mouse).** Mangiferin showed significant analgesia in acetic-acid writhing and hot-plate tests, with naloxone work indicating peripheral opioid-receptor interaction. Notably, **plant extracts and mangiferin failed to show acute anti-inflammatory activity** — a NULL worth recording, since "anti-inflammatory" is routinely asserted for this tree.
- **Antimicrobial activity — NOT VERIFIED.** No *B. ceiba*-specific antimicrobial study was retrieved. The 2025 review mentions antimicrobial properties as a general attribute of flavonoids as a class, which is not a finding about this plant. Grade: unsubstantiated in this run.
- **Aphrodisiac / tonic action of the root (*semul musli*) — NULL. Deflated.** A targeted PubMed search for *Bombax ceiba* root and sexual behaviour in rodents returned **zero results**. There is, in the evidence retrieved here, **no preclinical and no clinical support whatsoever** for the aphrodisiac or *vajikarana* indication. It is recorded as a traditional attribution only, and the codex explicitly declines to lend it any scientific colour.
- **Astringent action of the gum for diarrhoea or menorrhagia — NULL.** No study of *mocharasa* was retrieved. Traditional grade only.
- **Human clinical evidence, any indication — NULL.** None retrieved.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Within the Ayurvedic frame carried under the SS tag, shalmali's gum is read as *kashaya* (astringent) in taste, cooling in potency, and drying in effect — the classification that groups it with the *stambhana* (arresting, binding) remedies used to check flux, whether of stool or of menstrual blood. That reading is coherent with a tannin-rich exudate, and the astringent classification is doing exactly the work an energetic classification should: it predicts the traditional indication from an observable property of the material. The root, by contrast, is placed in the *vajikarana* (virilising) category, a rasayana-adjacent classification whose logic is nutritive and building rather than binding — which is why a single tree yields two articles with opposite energetic readings.

The codex flags that these assignments here come through the modern secondary transmission of Ayurveda, not from a primary Sushruta Samhita edition read for this entry. **[USER: verify]** the exact *rasa*, *virya* and *vipaka* against a primary text before publication.

### Contraindications

- **The evidence base is preclinical only.** No human trial of any part of *Bombax ceiba* was retrieved. Every pharmacological claim here is rodent or cell-culture. Nothing in this entry establishes safety or efficacy in a person.
- **No established human dose exists for any part.** Do not derive one from the rodent figures.
- **Lethality signal, leaf isolate.** Shamimin at 500 mg/kg was a lethal dose in rats, while showing no mortality in mice at 1 g/kg. A compound with a >30-fold gap between its effective hypotensive dose and its lethal dose in the *same species*, and a large interspecies discrepancy, must not be scaled to humans. Concentrated leaf extracts are not a home preparation.
- **Blood pressure interaction.** Given demonstrated hypotensive activity in rats, additive hypotension with antihypertensive drugs is a plausible risk. Anyone on antihypertensives, or perioperative, should avoid leaf preparations.
- **Glycaemic interaction.** Hypoglycaemic activity was shown, though only at a near-lethal dose. Caution with insulin or oral hypoglycaemics.
- **The floss is a mechanical respiratory irritant.** Kapok-type fibre is fine, light and airborne; harvesting, ginning or stuffing exposes the airway. Use respiratory protection. This is an occupational hazard, not a pharmacological one, and it is the most likely real-world harm from the tree.
- **The tree is physically armed.** Trunk and young branch prickles cause puncture wounds during climbing or harvest.
- **Pregnancy and lactation: no data — avoid.** The traditional menorrhagia indication touches the reproductive system and is entirely unstudied; that combination warrants exclusion rather than experimentation.
- **Menorrhagia and persistent diarrhoea are diagnostic problems.** Heavy menstrual bleeding may signal fibroids, coagulopathy, thyroid or endometrial pathology; chronic diarrhoea may signal infection or inflammatory bowel disease. Neither should be self-managed with an untested astringent gum in place of investigation.
- **Do not treat the aphrodisiac reputation as pharmacologically grounded.** It has no supporting evidence in the literature retrieved here.
