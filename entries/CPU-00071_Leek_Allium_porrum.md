### Origin & Tradition

The leek is the mild, sweet member of the onion-garlic clan, a cultivated *Allium* grown since deep antiquity around the Mediterranean and the Near East. The Egyptians grew and ate leeks (they appear among the foods of the pyramid-builders and in tomb offerings), and the Greco-Roman world prized them both as food and medicine. The emperor Nero, by a famous anecdote, ate leeks in quantity to clear and strengthen his voice, earning the nickname *Porrophagus* ("leek-eater") — a memory of the leek's classical reputation as the **singer's and orator's herb**, eaten to clear the throat and improve the voice. The leek is, of course, the national emblem of Wales, with its own legendary military origin story.

Dioscorides treats the leek among the pot-herbs of Book II, assigning it the *Allium* family's warming, pungent, **expectorant and cleansing** virtues in a gentler form than garlic or onion. Its dominant medicinal uses across the tradition are **respiratory**: clearing the chest and throat of thick phlegm, easing coughs and hoarseness, and "cleansing" the airways — the rational basis of the voice-clearing reputation. Beyond this it served as a diuretic, a mild emmenagogue, a digestive stimulant, a vermifuge, and (the juice) as a remedy for the bites and stings and for nosebleed and earache in folk practice. As a food it was valued as a wholesome, warming vegetable, gentler on the stomach than its fiercer cousins.

Like all the cultivated alliums the leek shares the family's **organosulphur chemistry** — milder here than in garlic — which underlies both its characteristic flavour and its antimicrobial, expectorant and cardiovascular-supportive actions. It also contributes useful dietary fibre (including inulin-type fructans, a prebiotic) and a range of flavonoids.

### Contraindications

- **Allium allergy / intolerance.** Avoid in known allium sensitivity; the family's FODMAP fructans can aggravate IBS in sensitive individuals.
- **Bleeding risk (high medicinal intake).** The alliums have mild antiplatelet activity; relevant only at high concentrated intake with anticoagulants/surgery.
- **Heartburn / reflux.** The pungent alliums can aggravate reflux in some.
- **Pregnancy.** Ordinary food amounts are fine; concentrated medicinal juice/emmenagogue use warrants the usual moderate caution. [USER: verify.]
