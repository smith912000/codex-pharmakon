# Water — Udor (as printed in Dioscorides 5-18) / the Water Group, Antariksha Jalam, rain water (Sushruta, Bhishagratna's English)
## *Water, drinking and medicinal; the translator of Dioscorides glosses "Water" and, for the next chapter, "Seawater"; Bhishagratna's Water Group names rain, river, lake, well and other waters* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM, SS, TBN

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 5-18, *Udor*, "Water" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 9860-9862; the list row is `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 747). It sits in Book Five among the wines and honeyed drinks (5-17 *Melikraton*, 5-20 *Thalassomeli*). The chapter is short and is an essay on how to judge water, not a list of uses: "Water in general is difficult to analyse because of the individualities of places and their peculiar natures and the air and many other things. For the most part the best is pure and sincerely sweet, not having any other quality whatsoever, or staying in the digestive system very long, furthermore soon passing through without trouble, and not inflative nor spoiled." The next chapter, 5-19 *Udor Thalassion*, "Seawater" (lines 9863-9869), was also read: "Water from the sea is hot, sharp, and bad for the stomach. It troubles the bowels, expelling phlegm." It is used warm in cloths to "extract and dissolve", for "disorders of the nerves, and chilblains before ulceration", in plasters, as a lukewarm suppository, as a wash for itches, lichen and nits, in a hot bath "helpful for long-lasting disorders of the body and the strength", inhaled as steam for "dropsy, headaches, and hardness of hearing", and "given as a purge by itself (or with posca [hot drinks], wine or honey)", with a hen or fish broth afterwards "to alleviate the sharpness of the pangs of hunger". It adds that "Seawater put in jars alone and not mixed with drinkable water becomes unpoisonous". And 5-20 *Thalassomeli* (lines 9870-9872), seawater sweetened with honey, "is thought to purge very well", made from honey, rainwater and seawater in equal amounts, strained and set in the sun in a pitch-glazed jar in the hottest days; boiled seawater with honey is "more moderate and milder". Those two chapters belong to seawater rather than to drinking water, and are noted for completeness.

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The English is Bhishagratna's 1907 translation (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch45.txt`). *Sutrasthana* ch.45 is "the Chapter, which deals with the rules to be observed in respect of liquid substances in general (Drava-Dravya-Vidhimadhyayam)" (lines 4-7), and it opens with the Water Group (line 9 onward). The main points, in the order the text gives them, are these. Atmospheric or rain water "is possessed of a non-patent taste. It is ambrosial in its nature, pleasant and beneficial to life. It is enlivening, invigorating or strength-giving, refrigerent, frigorific, antipyrotic, anti-hypnotic, and conquers vertigo, drowsiness and fits of fainting. It is most wholesome to the human body" (lines 10-14). After falling on the earth it takes one of the six tastes from its receptacle (river, pond, tank, well, fountain and so on), but the text rejects the view that the colour of the soil sets the taste (red, brown, grey, yellow, blue or white giving sweet, acid, saline, pungent, bitter or astringent) and says it is the predominance of the five material principles in the soil (lines 40-62). Atmospheric water has four classes, "rain water, hail water, frost water or dew, and snow water, of which the first is the best for its lightness" (lines 66-68); rain is of two kinds, the Gangam and the Samudram (from the Ganges' or the sea's vapours), and a rice-in-a-silver-bowl test tells them apart, the sea kind being "extremely unwholesome" (lines 69-94). The rain is collected on a clean white linen cloth or from the waterspouts of a house, into a golden, silver or earthen vessel (lines 98-110). Terrestrial water has seven kinds (well, river, lake, tank, fountain, spring and Chunti), with the seasons to use each (lines 114-128). Defiled water is described at length, with six categories of defect (touch, sight, taste, smell, potency, digestion; lines 138-181), and is purified by boiling, heating in the sun, or by immersing red-hot iron, hot sand or stones (line 182). There are seven modes of purifying water by immersing an object such as a pearl or a crystal (line 207) and seven ways of cooling it. The text ranks rivers by where they rise and flow: rivers draining the Jangala countries and flowing to the western sea are "light, and therefore wholesome"; those from the Sahya, Vindhya, Malaya, Mahendra and Himalaya ranges are each said to cause diseases (skin, *kushtha* and jaundice, worms, elephantiasis and dropsy, and heart, head and goitre), while the water of the Paripatra rivers "is wholesome, strength-giving, and conducive to health" (lines 228-262). Then: "Atmospheric water, collected in a good and proper receptacle, has the virtue of subduing the three deranged humours of the body, and acts as a pure tonic and elixir, its virtue varying with the excellence of the vessel in which it is contained" (lines 287-290). The washings of the moonstone (*Chandrakanta*) are said to have "the mystic virtue of warding off the attacks of monsters and demons" and to subdue *Pittam* (line 293). Cold water is advised for epileptic fits, hot seasons, burning from *Pittam*, poisoning of the blood, haemoptysis, abuse of wine, fainting, exhaustion, vertigo and vomiting, and avoided in pain at the sides, catarrh, rheumatism, diseases of the larynx, distension, acute fever, after emetics and purgatives, in hiccup and after an oily drink (line 300 onward). Then each water's effect: river water produces *Vayu* and is light; lake water quenches thirst and is "strength-giving, light, sweet and astringent"; tank, Vapi, Chunti, well, fountain, spring, Vikira, field and Palvala waters each have their own qualities. "Sea-water has a fishy smell, and a saline taste; it aggravates all the three deranged humours of the body" (line 352). Warm water "subdues the deranged Vayu and Kapham. It is antifat, appetising, diuretic ... and febrifuge" (line 364); water boiled down to a quarter and cooled is "light and limpid"; water boiled overnight "should not be knowingly given to a thirsty person"; cocoanut water is "heavy, demulcent, cool, pleasant and appetising" (line 390); and water should be taken "as little as possible" in loss of relish for food, catarrh, oedema, wasting diseases, impaired digestion, abdominal dropsy, skin disease, fever, eye disease, ulcer and diabetes (line 398).

### Varieties

The four varieties below come from the Arabic texts and are the attestations of CM (snow) and TBN (all four); each is a named kind of water in the tradition that names it. Sushruta's own rain water (*antariksha jalam*) and its four classes of atmospheric water (rain, hail, frost or dew, and snow water) are treated above under Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS) and in the Cultivation Register; they are kept there and are not merged with the Arabic entries that follow.

**Snow and ice** — ثلج (*thalj*), ماء الثلج والبرد (water of snow and hail), جمد / جليد (ice) (CM, TBN; CM: *Canon* Book II, entry *thalj*, `Sources/CM_Avicenna/text/IbnSina_Qanun_OpenITI_Shamela0010706.txt`, heading at Arabic text line 18788, four lines; TBN: *al-Ṭibb al-Nabawī*, letter *thā'*, entry *thalj*, `Sources/TBN_Prophetic/text/IbnQayyim_TibbNabawi_OpenITI_Shamela0023649.txt`, line 5143, with a second passage, "the water of snow and hail" (*māʾ al-thalj wa-l-barad*), at line 6835, inside his long chapter on water (*māʾ*, line 6781)). Both texts are read in the Arabic; ice is mentioned by Ibn Qayyim only in passing. Avicenna (CM), under *al-khawāṣṣ* (actions): "bad for the old and for whoever generates cold humours" (رديء للمشايخ ولمن يتولد فيه الأخلاط الباردة). Head (*aʿḍāʾ al-raʾs*): "the water of snow calms hot toothache." Joints and nerves (*ālāt al-mafāṣil*): "snow is harmful to the nerve (*ʿaṣab*) because it shuts in the hot vapours moving through it and keeps them from dissolving." Digestive organs (*aʿḍāʾ al-ghidhāʾ*): "harmful to the stomach, especially that in which cold humours are generated, and it makes thirsty because it gathers heat" (وهو يعطش لجمع الحرارة). Avicenna gives no temperament line and no dose. Ibn Qayyim (TBN) opens with a hadith: "It is established in the Ṣaḥīḥ that the Prophet said: 'O God, wash me of my sins with water, snow and hail'" (ثبت في «الصحيح» ... «اللهم اغسلني من خطاياي بالماء والثلج والبرد»). Hadith grading: Ibn Qayyim calls it established (*thabata*) in the Ṣaḥīḥ and cites it again at line 6835 as established in "the two Ṣaḥīḥs". I found the supplication in Muslim (`Muslim_Sahih_OpenITI_JK000109.txt`, lines 7449 to 7454, no. 598, wording "with snow, water and hail") and in al-Bukhārī (`Bukhari_Sahih_OpenITI_JK000110.txt`, lines 4538 and 41258, wording "with water" at 4538 and "with the water of snow and hail" at 41258). The word order differs between the texts; Ibn Qayyim's quotation is his own. From the hadith he draws a rule of treatment: "the illness is treated by its opposite" (الداء يداوى بضده). Sins have "heat and burning" that snow, hail and cold water oppose. He answers the objection that hot water removes dirt better: cold water has "a hardening and strengthening of the body that hot water does not", and "sins bring two effects, defilement and slackening" (التدنيس والإرخاء), so the cure sought is what "cleans the heart and hardens it". At line 6835 he adds: "from this the principle of the medicine of bodies and hearts is learned: treating their illnesses by their opposites." On the nature of snow itself (line 5143 ff.): "snow is cold on the sounder view, and whoever said hot erred; his doubt is that animals are generated in it, which does not show heat, since they are generated in cold fruits and in vinegar. As for its causing thirst, that is because it stirs up heat, not because of heat in itself; it harms the stomach and the nerve, and if toothache is from excessive heat, it calms it." At line 6835: snow has "in itself a sharp, smoky quality, and its water likewise"; hail water is "subtler and more pleasant than snow water"; ice (*jamad*, that is *jalīd*) "depends on its origin"; snow "takes on the quality of the mountains and the ground on which it falls, in goodness and badness." Ibn Qayyim's treatment of when to avoid iced water is given under Preparation & Dosing and Contraindications below.

**Rain water** — غيث (*ghayth*), the beneficial rain (TBN; *al-Ṭibb al-Nabawī*, `Sources/TBN_Prophetic/text/IbnQayyim_TibbNabawi_OpenITI_Shamela0023649.txt`, letter *ghayn*, entry *ghayth*, line 6044, about sixteen lines, read in the Arabic). Ibn Qayyim names rain water, as the rain of mercy named in the Qur'an, under this heading; it is the only text on disk that does so, and it is distinct from Sushruta's *antariksha jalam* (SS, ch.45), which is given above and is not equated with it. The TBN chapter on water (line 6781 ff.) is a separate passage of the same book. The entry opens: "*Ghayth* is mentioned in the Qur'an in several places; the name is sweet to the ear, and what it names is sweet to the spirit (*ruḥ*) and the body; ears are gladdened by its mention and hearts by its coming; and its water is the best of waters, the subtlest, the most beneficial and the greatest in blessing (*baraka*), especially when it is from a thundering cloud and has gathered in the mountain pools." He reasons that it is moister than other water "because it has not stayed long on the earth, so it takes no dryness from it, and no dry substance has mixed with it; therefore it changes and putrefies quickly, for its subtlety and its speed of being acted upon." (The Arabic uses *ruḥ* in the sense of spirit; Ibn Qayyim's usage here is poetic and is not developed as a physiology.) He records a dispute and gives each side without deciding: is spring rain subtler than winter rain, or the reverse? "There are two opinions." Those who prefer winter rain say the sun's heat is then least, so it draws up only the subtlest of seawater, the air is clear and free of smoky vapours and dust. Those who prefer spring rain say the heat dissolves the heavy vapours and thins the air, so the water is lighter and has fewer earthy parts, and it falls when plants and trees are alive and the air is pleasant. He does not choose. Hadith: "Al-Shāfiʿī, may God have mercy on him, mentioned from Anas ibn Mālik that they were with the Messenger of God and rain fell; the Messenger of God uncovered his garment and said, 'It is new in its coming from its Lord'" (إنه حديث عهد بربه). Ibn Qayyim gives it without a grading, and refers to his earlier treatment of the rain-seeking prayer (*istisqāʾ*) in his account of the Prophet's practice (*hadyihi*), where the Prophet sought rain and took blessing (*tabarruk*) from the first rain. I found the report in Muslim (`Muslim_Sahih_OpenITI_JK000109.txt`, lines 11042 to 11045, no. 898), where it is told by Anas with the Prophet's reason given as "because it is new in its coming from its Lord the Exalted". Ibn Qayyim's own quotation, through al-Shāfiʿī, differs slightly in wording. Cross-reference not read: in the chapter on water (line 6834) Ibn Qayyim says rain water was dealt with "earlier, under the letter *ʿayn*". I did not find that passage and did not read it. **Zamzam water** — ماء زمزم (*māʾ zamzam*) (TBN; *al-Ṭibb al-Nabawī*, `Sources/TBN_Prophetic/text/IbnQayyim_TibbNabawi_OpenITI_Shamela0023649.txt`, letter *mīm*, entry *māʾ zamzam*, line 6852, about nineteen lines, inside his chapter on waters, read in the Arabic). Named only by Ibn Qayyim among the texts on disk. He opens: "the master of waters, the noblest and greatest in rank, the dearest to souls, the dearest in price and the most precious to people; it is the *hazma* of Jibrīl and the drink God gave to Ismāʿīl" (سيد المياه وأشرفها وأجلها قدرا ... وهو هزمة جبريل وسقيا الله إسماعيل). Hadith 1 (established): "It is established in the Ṣaḥīḥ that the Prophet said to Abū Dharr, who had stayed between the Kaʿba and its coverings forty days and nights with no food but it: 'It is a food of nourishment' (*ṭaʿām ṭuʿm*)." Ibn Qayyim adds that another source than Muslim extends the wording: "and a healing of sickness" (وشفاء سقم). Verdict: he calls the main report established (*thabata*). I found it in Muslim (`Muslim_Sahih_OpenITI_JK000109.txt`, lines 33486 to 33521, no. 2473), where Abū Dharr says he lived thirty days and nights on nothing but Zamzam, grew fat, and felt no hunger, and the Prophet says "it is blessed, it is a food of nourishment" (إنها مباركة إنها طعام طعم). Muslim has "thirty" (not Ibn Qayyim's "forty"), and the added "healing of sickness" is not in the Muslim text I read. Ibn Qayyim himself marks it as an addition by another transmitter. Hadith 2 (disputed): "In the *Sunan* of Ibn Māja, from the hadith of Jābir ibn ʿAbd Allāh, from the Prophet: 'The water of Zamzam is for what it is drunk for'" (ماء زمزم لما شرب له). Ibn Qayyim reports the verdict exactly: "A group have weakened this hadith because of ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Muʾammal, its transmitter from Muḥammad ibn al-Munkadir." He then relates that ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Mubārak, on pilgrimage, went to Zamzam and said that Ibn Abī al-Mawālī had narrated the hadith from Ibn al-Munkadir from Jābir, and that he drank it "for the thirst of the Day of Resurrection", adding "Ibn Abī al-Mawālī is trustworthy, so the hadith is then *ḥasan*". His summary: "some have declared it sound (*ṣaḥḥaḥahu baʿḍuhum*), and some made it fabricated (*mawḍūʿ*), and both opinions are reckless (*mujāzafa*)" (وقد صححه بعضهم، وجعله بعضهم موضوعا، وكلا القولين فيه مجازفة). Ibn Qayyim's own position is therefore a middle one that treats the hadith as acceptable (*ḥasan*) by the Ibn al-Mubārak route and rejects both the sound and the fabricated judgement as overstated. Experience: "I and others have tried healing with Zamzam water in marvellous ways; I sought healing with it for several illnesses and was cured by God's leave, and I saw one who fed on it for a number of days, close to half a month or more, and felt no hunger and walked round the Kaʿba with the people like any of them; he told me that sometimes it lasted forty days with him, and he had strength to lie with his wife, to fast and to circle the house many times" (line 6852 ff.). This is Ibn Qayyim's report of his own and a contemporary's experience, not a hadith, and it is reported here as such.

**Nile water** — ماء النيل (*māʾ al-nīl*) (TBN; *al-Ṭibb al-Nabawī*, `Sources/TBN_Prophetic/text/IbnQayyim_TibbNabawi_OpenITI_Shamela0023649.txt`, letter *mīm*, entry *māʾ al-nīl*, line 6871, ten lines, inside his chapter on waters, read in the Arabic). Named only by Ibn Qayyim among the texts on disk. The entry: "The water of the Nile is one of the rivers of Paradise; its source is beyond the Mountains of the Moon in the farthest land of Abyssinia, from rains gathered there and torrents that feed one another; God drives it to the barren land with no plants and brings forth crops by it, from which cattle and people eat." Because that land (the text calls it *ibliza*) is hard, "if it rained the usual rain it would not be watered and not made ready for plants, and if it rained beyond the usual it would harm dwellings and dwellers and stop livelihoods; so He rained on distant lands and drove that rain to this land in a great river, and made its rise at known times to the measure of the land's watering and sufficiency; when it has watered and covered the land, He allowed its fall and sinking so that the benefit of sowing is complete." The Nile "gathered in it the ten matters mentioned before, and was among the subtlest of waters, the lightest, the sweetest in taste and the most pleasant" (واجتمع في هذا الماء الأمور العشرة التي تقدم ذكرها، وكان من ألطف المياه وأخفها وأعذبها وأحلاها). The ten matters are the ten tests of good water he lists at the start of his chapter on water (line 6781 ff.): clear in colour; no smell; sweet in taste, "like the water of the Nile and the Euphrates"; light and thin in body; of good course; far source; exposed to sun and wind; swift in flow; plentiful, so that it carries away impurities; and flowing from north to south or west to east. He adds that "when these descriptions are weighed, they are found complete only in the four rivers: the Nile, the Euphrates, Sayḥūn and Jayḥūn." Hadith (established): he cites the Prophetic saying, from the two Ṣaḥīḥs by Abū Hurayra: "Sayḥān, Jayḥān, the Nile and the Euphrates, each is of the rivers of Paradise" (سيحان وجيحان والنيل والفرات كل من أنهار الجنة). Verdict: he cites it as established in the two Ṣaḥīḥs. I found it in Muslim (`Muslim_Sahih_OpenITI_JK000109.txt`, lines 37994 to 37998, no. 2839); I did not find it in the Bukhari file on disk by that wording, so only Muslim is confirmed here. The hadith is the basis for the entry's opening phrase, "one of the rivers of Paradise".

*Synthesis of the varieties.* The varieties are separated by the Arabic texts on three different grounds, and none is graded against another. Snow is the one variety that both Arabic texts name: Avicenna (CM) gives it a short entry of actions and harms (the old, the cold-humoured, the nerve, the stomach) with one help for hot toothache, and Ibn Qayyim (TBN) adds the hadith of the supplication, the rule that illness is treated by its opposite, and his own judgement that snow is cold. Rain, Zamzam and the Nile are named by Ibn Qayyim alone, each under its own heading in *al-Ṭibb al-Nabawī*: rain by its origin (*ghayth*, the best, subtlest and most blessed of waters, and quick to putrefy), Zamzam by place and rank ("the master of waters", with an established hadith, a disputed one, and Ibn Qayyim's own report of experience), and the Nile by the ten tests of good water, which it meets together with the Euphrates, Sayḥūn and Jayḥūn. Ibn Qayyim ranks Zamzam first among waters in his own words; he ranks the four rivers as the waters that fulfil the ten tests; he ranks rain as the best of waters by subtlety and blessing; the three rankings are his, and the entry does not reconcile them. Beside these, the Greek chapter (DMM 5-18) judges water by its freedom from any quality and by place and air, and Sushruta's chapter (SS ch.45) ranks atmospheric water first among waters and sorts terrestrial waters by source and season; both are given above in their own terms. The Arabic varieties are laid beside them and not equated with them: Sushruta's four classes of atmospheric water (rain, hail, frost or dew, snow) and Ibn Qayyim's snow, hail and rain are each the text's own category, and the Arabic hadith frame (the Prophet's supplication, the first rain "new from its Lord", Zamzam as "a food of nourishment", the Nile as a river of Paradise) has no counterpart in the Greek or the Ayurvedic text.

### Active Components

Water is H₂O, with whatever is dissolved in it. Rain water is nearly pure; spring, well and river waters carry dissolved minerals (calcium, magnesium, bicarbonate, sodium, chloride and others) and sometimes iron, sulphur compounds or dissolved gases; sea water is about three per cent salt, mostly sodium chloride (general facts). Surface water also carries microbes, and its content depends on the ground it crossed, which is the point that both texts make in their own terms. The sources name no active component and give no quantities.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only for the medicinal uses; drinking clean water is not a special practice and needs no protocol from this book. The texts record water as a thing to be judged by place, season and vessel, boiled or sunned to purify, drunk warm or cold according to the complaint, and, in Greece, seawater as a purge, a bath, a steam and a compress. The Codex offers no protocol for seawater as a purge, or for river waters by origin, or for the moonstone washings. Contaminated water causes serious disease, and drinking seawater is dangerous (see Contraindications).

### Preparation & Dosing

Greek: seawater kept in jars, alone and undiluted by drinking water, "becomes unpoisonous", or is boiled first; given lukewarm as a suppository, warm for griping; honeyed seawater (5-20). Ayurvedic (ch.45): rain water collected on white linen or from spouts and kept in gold, silver or earthen vessels; defiled water purified by boiling, sun-heating or plunging in red-hot iron, hot sand or stones; seven ways of purifying with objects such as a pearl or crystal; seven ways of cooling; warm water; water boiled down to a quarter and cooled; water boiled and cooled for dysentery, burning of the skin, haemoptysis, wine abuse, poison, thirst, vomiting, catarrh, vertigo and loss of consciousness (line 390ff). Prophetic (TBN, see Varieties): no preparation is given for snow beyond drinking or holding snow-water, and Ibn Qayyim lists times to avoid iced water: "after the bath and intercourse, exercise and hot food; for those with cough, chest pain, weak liver, and cold temperaments" (line 6835 ff.); none is given for rain water or the Nile; for Zamzam, none other than drinking the water "for what it is drunk for" (the intention-bound drinking of the second hadith). ### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. The body needs water; clean drinking water is the main public-health safeguard against waterborne infection (cholera, typhoid, dysentery, parasites). Boiling kills most waterborne microbes, which fits Sushruta's instruction to purify defiled water by boiling, but his text states its reasons in terms of the humours and the six defects. Drinking seawater dehydrates, because its salt load exceeds what the kidneys can excrete. Warm and cold baths and compresses affect comfort and circulation in ordinary ways. The claim that rivers from named mountain ranges cause the named diseases is not a statement to be checked here, and no study was read. Cold applied to a sore tooth numbs the pain (a general, well-established fact; sudden cold can also hurt an already sensitive tooth), which is the context for the Arabic texts' snow-water for hot toothache; Ibn Qayyim's old claim that snow is hot because animals appear in it is not a physiological argument and is not taken up here. Rain is soft, low-mineral water, distilled water carrying dissolved atmospheric gases and some dust and salts, and when collected can carry whatever is on the surface it runs over; Ibn Qayyim's reasoning that rain mixes no dry substance and so is light and quick to spoil is consistent with that. Zamzam is groundwater whose dissolved minerals depend on the aquifer; water alone does not supply the energy, protein, vitamins or most minerals the body needs, so living on it for weeks is not safe, which is the opposite of what Ibn Qayyim's informant reports. Surface river water is not safe to drink untreated; the summer flood of the Nile that the text describes as a regulated gift is a real seasonal pattern of the river, now altered by dams. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** For ordinary water the only quality is the standard of judgement: "pure and sincerely sweet, not having any other quality", and passing through the digestive system without trouble. Seawater (5-19) is "hot, sharp, and bad for the stomach".

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** Sushruta gives the qualities per water, as printed in ch.45: rain water has a "non-patent" taste, is refrigerant and strength-giving, subdues the three humours, and is the best water for lightness; river water produces *Vayu*, is light, stomachic and liquefacient; lake water is sweet and astringent, light and strength-giving; tank water produces *Vayu*; Vapi water subdues *Vayu* and *Kapham* and generates *Pittam*; Chunti water is sweet and digestive; well water generates *Pittam* and is appetising and light; fountain water is light, appetising and destroys *Kapham*; spring water is sweet and subdues *Pittam*; field water is heavy; sea water aggravates all three humours; warm water subdues *Vayu* and *Kapham*; boiled-and-cooled water is recommended for the *Pittam* group of complaints (lines 300-398 of the file).

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** For snow (*Canon* Book II, *thalj*, Arabic text line 18788) no temperament degree is printed. Action: bad for the old and for cold-humoured people; shuts in vapours (nerve); makes thirsty by gathering heat.

**Prophetic medicine (TBN).** Ibn Qayyim says water in general is "cold and moist, it restrains heat, preserves the body's moistures and replaces what has dissolved from it" (line 6781 ff.). Snow is "cold on the sounder view" (*bārid ʿalā al-aṣaḥḥ*), with a "sharp, smoky quality" of its own (line 6835); hail water is subtler than snow water; he states and refuses the opposite opinion (that snow is hot). Rain water is moister than other waters, subtle (*laṭīf*), and quick to change and putrefy. For Zamzam and the Nile no temperament is given: Zamzam's rank is "the master of waters", and the Nile is ranked among the subtlest, lightest, sweetest waters and counted with the Euphrates, Sayḥūn and Jayḥūn as one of the four rivers that meet the ten tests in full.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. 5-18 is a pure instance of what he does offer a cultivator: a discipline of attention. Water is hard to analyse because of place, air "and many other things"; judge it by what it does in the body (does it pass through quickly, without inflation, unspoiled) and by its lack of any quality of its own. The chapter assigns water no cultivation status, and the seawater chapters are therapeutic.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** The *Canon* entry for water (*Mā'*, whose heading is blank in the text) sends the reader to Book I for the excellent and praised waters, and names the bad ones as stagnant marsh water with a strange taste or smell, turbid, heavy, quick to turn to stone (*Canon* Book II, entry *Mā'*, Arabic text line 15030). Remedies for each fault are listed, and "والماء الرديء بالجملة يصلحه الخل" ("bad water in general is corrected by vinegar"). For temperament he gives kinds only: sea water "حريف حاد" ("sharp, acrid"), nitrous water warming and drying, copper and iron waters good for the viscera. Cold water harms the obstructed but helps those with looseness and flux, and "يقوي القوى كلها على أفعالها إذا كان باعتدال" ("strengthens all the faculties in their actions if taken in moderation"), meaning the digestive, attractive, retentive and expulsive. The organ headings follow: sea water and sulphur waters for skin, joints and nerve; lukewarm water for epileptics and throat; iron water for spleen and stomach; very cold water harms the chest, desire and the bowel; mineral waters hinder urine and birth; sulphurous and stagnant waters breed fevers; sea water is sat in after viper bite. Innate heat and radical moisture are not named. Avicenna's separate entry on snow (*thalj*, Arabic text line 18788; see Varieties) is a list of harms to the old, the nerve and the stomach, and one help for hot toothache; no cultivation status and no framing of the *ruḥ* or innate heat is attested for snow, so (c) absent for snow. **(b) — therapeutic actions attested (faculties, skin, joints, viscera), with stated harms; no cultivation status.**

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* *Sutrasthana* ch.45 is a chapter on liquids in general. It calls rain water "ambrosial in its nature, pleasant and beneficial to life", "enlivening, invigorating or strength-giving", and says that atmospheric water collected in a good vessel "acts as a pure tonic and elixir". These are Bhishagratna's English words in a dravya chapter on liquids. The chapter is not a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* chapter, and no *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* claim is made for water in it, so no status is read into "elixir". The cultivation-relevant content is the discipline: where the water fell or rose, when to use it, in what vessel, how to test, boil and cool it, and when to drink little. **Prophetic medicine (TBN).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The frame is *sunna* and Ibn Qayyim's reading of it, for four waters named in *al-Ṭibb al-Nabawī* (see Varieties). Snow: the frame is the hadith of the supplication to be washed of sins with water, snow and hail, which Ibn Qayyim reads as the principle that bodies and hearts are both treated by opposites (*ṭibb al-abdān wa-l-qulūb*). He does not give snow a cultivation status; he reads the supplication's wording as pointing at two effects of sin, defilement and slackening, which the cold and hardening of snow-water answer for the heart. The claim is Ibn Qayyim's reading of the hadith, not a statement of the hadith itself, and the hadith is a prayer, not a practice of drinking or washing in snow; this is *sunna* in the sense of what the Prophet is reported to have asked, not what he is reported to have done with snow. Rain water: the frame is *sunna* and blessing (*baraka*): the Prophet uncovered himself to the first rain and said it was new from its Lord (reported in Muslim, no. 898), and Ibn Qayyim calls rain water "the greatest in blessing". No cultivation status such as a life-lengthening grade is given to it. Ibn Qayyim elsewhere speaks of "the six necessary things" on which the health of bodies rests (line 6020, in the entry on agarwood); the rain entry does not assign rain to any of them, and the list itself was not read for this entry. Zamzam water: the frame is *sunna* and *ṭibb nabawī* together: the Prophet's words "it is a food of nourishment" (established, Muslim no. 2473), the reported addition "a healing of sickness", and the disputed "for what it is drunk for". Ibn Qayyim assigns Zamzam the highest rank among waters and reports sustained fasting on it, but no life-lengthening status or place in his six necessary things is attested in the Zamzam entry: its role is nourishment and healing in the sense of the hadith. The practice described (living on it for days) is Ibn Qayyim's testimony of an observed person, not a hadith, and the Prophetic text does not prescribe it. Nile water: the frame is *sunna* and Ibn Qayyim's account of providence (*ḥikma*): the Prophet's saying that the Nile is one of the rivers of Paradise (established, Muslim no. 2839), and Ibn Qayyim's reading of the flood as a divine arrangement for the land's good. The Nile is given high rank among waters; no life-lengthening or cultivation status is attested for drinking it, and Ibn Qayyim's six necessary things are not applied to it in the Nile entry.

**Practitioner's note.** Water is the plainest substance in the Codex and the most carefully handled. Both texts ask a cultivator to know what the water is before drinking it: its place, its season, its vessel, and its freedom from defilement. Dioscorides says it is hard to analyse; Sushruta lists the defects. Neither assigns water a cultivation grade. The practical use is to drink clean water, warm when the complaint is cold or phlegmatic as Sushruta advises, and little when the text says little. In the Prophetic text (TBN) snow is a supplication's image and a medical caution (not after bath, intercourse, exercise or hot food; not for the cough-ridden or cold-natured), and a cultivator does not use it as a practice; rain water supports an attitude and a caution, not a procedure: the Prophetic gesture towards the first rain, and the plain remark that rain water, being so light, spoils quickly; for Zamzam a cultivator may note that the Prophetic text calls it nourishing and blessed and that intention is part of the second hadith, but the text does not support a regimen, and prolonged water-only fasting is a medical risk that this entry should not be used to justify; with the Nile there is nothing to do as a practice, since the text teaches how to judge water by ten tests, the Nile is the example that passes, and the discipline is the assay, which one can apply to any water.

### Contraindications

- **Contaminated water.** Water that is turbid, smelly, stagnant or fouled by sewage, animals or decay causes infections and parasites; treat it (boil it, filter it) or avoid it. Sushruta says the same in his own terms: the person who drinks or bathes in contaminated water "incurs the risk of being speedily affected" with oedema, jaundice, skin disease, indigestion, dyspnoea and other diseases (lines 196-202).
- **Seawater.** Drinking seawater, or taking it as a purge, can cause dehydration, dangerous electrolyte disturbance and kidney injury; the Greek purge is not recommended.
- **Cold water.** Sushruta's list of conditions in which cold water is to be avoided (side pain, catarrh, rheumatism, distension, acute fever, after emetics and purgatives, hiccup) is traditional doctrine and is not tested here.
- **Iced and cold water (Prophetic).** Ibn Qayyim warns against iced water after the bath, intercourse, exercise and hot food, and for people with cough, chest pain, a weak liver or a cold temperament; Avicenna (CM) warns against snow for the old and for those who generate cold humours, and for the stomach and the nerve. Snow from polluted air or ground carries what it picked up; Ibn Qayyim says as much in his own terms (it takes on the quality of the ground it falls on).
- **Collected rain.** Ibn Qayyim notes that rain water putrefies quickly. Collected rain can be contaminated and should not be treated as safe to drink for that reason alone; this is a general caution, not a statement in the text.
- **Water-only fasting (Zamzam).** Water-only fasting for weeks is dangerous; the report of living on Zamzam is not advice. Water drawn from any well may carry contaminants and should be of known quality.
- **River water (Nile).** Untreated river water carries pathogens and parasites (bilharzia occurs in the Nile basin; a general fact, not in the text) and should not be drunk on the strength of this entry.
- **Water restriction.** Sushruta advises drinking "as little as possible" in oedema, diabetes and other named diseases; fluid restriction is a medical matter, and no one should cut fluids on this basis. [USER: verify]
- **Over-drinking.** Very large intakes of plain water in a short time can dilute blood sodium dangerously (a general fact; not in the sources).
- **Dose unknown.** No volume is given for any use in the sources read.
