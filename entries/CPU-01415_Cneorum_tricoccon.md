# Spurge olive, widow wail / Cneorum tricoccon — Chamelaia (Dioscorides 4-172); Oinos chamailaites (5-79)

## *Cneorum tricoccon* (the Dioscorides translators' suggested identification, "Chamelaea [Pliny], Cneorum tricoccon — Dwarf Olive"; also "Cneorum [Bedevian] — Spurge Olive, Widow Wail" in 4-172) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides.** This entry stands on one text, Dioscorides (DMM). It was added 2026-10-09 (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP).

**Identity note.** The binomial belongs to the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood) and not to Dioscorides. The plate caption printed in 4-172, "Cneorum tricoccum after Le MAOUT — 1888" (line 9607), shows that the translation's illustration is a *Cneorum*. The SUGGESTED line gives "Chamelaea [Pliny]" as well as the binomial, with the spelling *Cneorum tricoccon* in the SUGGESTED line and *tricoccum* in the plate caption and the index. **Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000).

*4-172 CHAMELAIA* (heading at line 9601; text line 9605). The whole chapter: "Chamelaea has branches twenty centimetres long. The shrub is sprigged, and it has leaves like the olive but more slender, thick and bitter, biting to the taste, and irritating to the throat. The leaves of this purge phlegm and bile downward, especially taken in a catapotium [pill] with twice as much wormwood [3-26] mixed to one part of the chamelaea, but let it be made with water or honey into a catapotium. They are not soluble for they pass through as big as they were taken. The leaves (pounded into small pieces and taken with honey) purge foul, crusted ulcers. It is also called pyros achne, acnestos, coccos cnidios, chamelaea nigra, heraclion, or bdelura, the Romans call it citocacium, and some, eleago, or oloastellum." The chapter gives a description, a purgative pill with wormwood, a use on ulcers and a list of names. The remark that the leaves "pass through as big as they were taken" is Dioscorides' own.

*5-79 OINOS CHAMAILAITES* (heading at line 10189; text line 10191). SUGGESTED: "Chamelaea [Pliny], Cneorum tricoccon — Chamailaites — Dwarf Olive". The whole chapter: "After the shrub flowers, beat and sift twelve teaspoons with the leaves and throw them into two thirds of a gallon of must. After two months pour it into another jar. It is good for dropsy, liver disorders, weariness, and women who are constipated after childbirth." The previous chapter (5-78) is the same sort of wine from *thymelaea*, which "purges watery matter and reduces the spleen".

The translation's index lists the binomial at pages 725 (or 727) and 778, which fall at these two chapters (index entries at lines 11640 and 15397).

### Active Components

The plants of the spurge-olive group are not described chemically in the sources read. As a general caution, the leaves in the text are drastic purgatives and the plant is "biting" and irritating to the throat, which suggests irritant compounds, but the text does not say what they are. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records a purgative shrub (the leaves taken in a pill with wormwood to purge phlegm and bile), a use on ulcers, and a wine of the flowering shrub for dropsy, liver disorders, weariness and post-partum constipation. These are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. A drastic purgative plant has no place in a recommended practice, and the Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-172: the leaves taken in a pill (*catapotium*) "with twice as much wormwood mixed to one part of the chamelaea", made up with water or honey; the leaves pounded and taken with honey for ulcers. 5-79: "twelve teaspoons" of the flowering shrub's leaves and flowers, beaten and sifted, thrown into "two thirds of a gallon of must", poured into another jar after two months. The 5-79 amounts are of the ingredients of the wine, not a dose for a person. No dose for a person is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Plants in the spurge-olive and daphne groups are generally known to include irritant and purgative species, and the plant is described by Dioscorides as irritating to the throat. This is a general statement and no study was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** 4-172 prints the leaves as "thick and bitter, biting to the taste, and irritating to the throat", and as purging "phlegm and bile downward". No warming, cooling or other Galenic quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. The chapters offer a discipline of attention: how to know the plant (a sprigged shrub twenty centimetres long, olive-like but slender, thick and bitter leaves that bite the throat), the observation that the leaves pass through the body undissolved, and the timing "after the shrub flowers" for the wine. They record no status for the person. The identification is a translators' gloss.

**Practitioner's note.** The practice in these chapters is taste and observation: bitterness, bite and the irritated throat as the assay of a purgative. Nothing gives a cultivator a practice for the person. A purgative shrub has nothing here to recommend.

### Contraindications

The plant is described as irritating and purgative, and the wine of 5-79 is a medicinal wine of the same shrub. Avoid in pregnancy and in any inflammatory disease of the bowel. Do not self-prescribe purgatives. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
