# Sheep bone — rGyud-bzhi, "Sheep bone" (bone, one of the thirteen animal-derived medicines)
## *Bone of the sheep; the translator prints "Sheep bone" and, separately, "Bone from the head of a sheep's femur (thigh bone)", with no Tibetan name and no binomial in the lines read* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Sheep bone is entered in the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, the chapter on the powers of medicines, in Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). It falls in the section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), whose thirteen headings are "horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). Under the heading "Bone" (line 1159) the list in line 1160 runs through human, dragon, tiger, cowrie, pig, sheep, porcupine, monkey, snail and mule bone, and the sheep entries read: "Sheep bone: cures (all types of) wind disorders. Bone from the head of a sheep's femur (thigh bone) dispels urinary retention." The entry carries no bracketed comment, no count of names and no Tibetan name, unlike several of its neighbours. Clark says in his introduction (line 80) that he has "interpolated liberally from the Blue Aquamarine commentary"; the bracketed passages elsewhere in the chapter look like such interpolations, but the lines read do not state the bracket convention outright. [USER: verify]

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3523) has the same item in its own words: "Sheep bone cures loong disorders. The spherical head of the femur cures obstruction of urine." The two translations agree on both indications. Clark's "wind" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "loong" are two renderings of the same disorder class; the Tibetan wording is not in the files read, so nothing finer can be said. The Men-Tsee-Khang sentence about the femur does not repeat the word "sheep", but it follows the sheep entry directly, and Clark's version names the sheep. [USER: verify]

Sheep bones also appear outside the materia medica list. In the treatment of wind disorders Clark lists soups "[e.g., of sheep's knee-bone, of the four essences - meat, grain beer, cane sugar and butter, or of one-year-old (finely-ground) sheep's head]" (lines 183-186), and Men-Tsee-Khang has "bone soup, four nutrient soup and broth from aged sheep head" (line 1116). These are foods used as a pacifying treatment, not entries in the list of bone medicines, and no dose or method is given. The sheep itself is named among the "Domestic animals" in the meat classification (Clark, line 672; Men-Tsee-Khang, line 2917).

### Active Components

Bone is mineralised connective tissue: mostly calcium phosphate in the form of hydroxyapatite, set in a framework of collagen, with marrow, fat and a little water. Boiling bone in water gives gelatin and dissolved fat and minerals. No active principle is claimed by the sources, and none specific to this entry was read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is two short indications in a materia medica list: sheep bone for wind disorders, and the head of the femur for retention of urine. The Codex offers no protocol. Urinary retention can be an emergency (a painful bladder that cannot empty needs prompt medical care), and nothing in the lines read describes a preparation, route or dose. The bone soups in the diet section are ordinary cooked food and are not recommended here as a treatment.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation, route or dose is given for sheep bone in the sources retrieved: Clark and Men-Tsee-Khang state the indication only. The only preparation named in connection with sheep bones is soup of the "sheep's knee-bone" in the treatment of wind disorders (Clark, lines 183-186), with no quantity or duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Bone broth contains protein from collagen (as gelatin) and some minerals, and it is food, not a treatment for urinary retention. Retention of urine has many causes, among them prostate enlargement, obstruction by a stone, infection, nerve damage and some medicines, and the treatment depends on the cause. The Tibetan "wind" disorders do not correspond to a single modern diagnosis, and no equivalence is claimed. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** None is printed for the bone. The Clark line (1160) and the Men-Tsee-Khang line (3523) give indications only: no taste, potency, post-digestive taste or humoral action. The qualities Clark gives for mutton ("oily and warm in power", line 674) and for meats in general (line 673) concern flesh and are not applied to bone. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* Neither translation read gives sheep bone any cultivation framing. It is a two-sentence indication in a list of animal bones, and the complaints named are a wind disorder and a urinary one. The frame around it is the tradition's own: the project's cultivation spec records that the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) are derived from the three poisons and that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root. That point comes from the spec and not from any line about sheep bone. No source read attaches sheep bone to *bcud len* (essence extraction), and there is no formal Tibetan category of substances for subtle-body practice. Clark's remark (line 57) that "only very few animal products are used" nowadays is a note on usage, not on status.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a short indication in a list, with no preparation or dose, and the cooked bone soup that the diet chapters mention is food. If the complaint is urinary retention, the answer is a clinician, not a bone.

### Contraindications

- **Urinary retention is not self-treatable:** inability to pass urine needs urgent assessment; do not rely on this line.
- **Infection and hygiene:** bone and hide from livestock of unknown health can carry spore-forming bacteria such as anthrax, and raw bone and marrow carry ordinary food-poisoning risks; cook bone thoroughly and do not handle material from sick or dead animals.
- **Legal status:** domestic sheep are livestock; the trade in animal parts is regulated in many places, and the lines read say nothing on whether the bone was raw, burnt or boiled. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** obtaining the bone is a by-product of slaughter, and the Tantra gives no reason why this bone, and no dose.
