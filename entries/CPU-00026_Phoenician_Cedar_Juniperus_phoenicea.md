### Origin & Tradition

The classical "cedars" are a tangle of botany and translation. The *kedros* of the Greeks, and the "cedar" of many ancient texts, frequently denotes not the true cedar (*Cedrus*) but resinous junipers — above all *Juniperus phoenicea*, the Phoenician cedar/juniper of the Mediterranean and North African littoral. Dioscorides in Book I treats *kedros* and its products, and the most important of these is **cedria** — **cedar pitch/oil**, the dark resinous tar obtained by destructive distillation or "burning out" of the wood. It is this *cedria*, derived from juniper-type "cedars," that the classical authors credit with a near-miraculous preserving power.

The tradition links cedar oil/pitch directly to **Egyptian embalming**: Greek writers (Herodotus among them) describe the use of "cedar oil" in mummification, and cedria's reputation as the substance that "kept bodies from corruption" is one of the most persistent claims in the ancient materia medica. Whether the embalmers' "cedar oil" was always *J. phoenicea* cedria, a *Cedrus* product, or a juniper tar in particular cases is genuinely uncertain — another of the great identity problems of ancient pharmacy — but the **preservative-antiseptic** association is the through-line, and it has a real chemical basis in the antimicrobial phenolics of the tar.

Beyond embalming, the resinous "cedar" products served as antiseptic dressings for wounds and ulcers, as a vermifuge and parasiticide (the tar killed lice and treated skin parasites and mange), as a preservative for hides, timber and manuscripts, and as a warming-drying remedy for chronic skin conditions. In North African and Mediterranean folk practice *J. phoenicea* (Arabic *arar*) supplies wood, berries and tar used for fumigation, antiseptic washes and the treatment of skin disease and parasites.

### Active Components

- **Wood-tar phenolics (guaiacol, cresols, and related phenols from destructive distillation)** — the antiseptic, antiparasitic and preservative principles of cedria/cedar pitch; phenolic tars denature microbial proteins (the same chemistry as other medicinal tars). These are also **irritant**, the basis of the toxicity caution.
- **Essential oil (monoterpenes — α-pinene and others; with characteristic juniper-type constituents)** — distilled from foliage/wood; antiseptic and aromatic.
- **Berry constituents (oil, sugars, tannins)** — the female cones ("berries") share the juniper-type aromatic-astringent profile.
- **Diterpenes/resin acids** — resinous wood constituents contributing to the preservative action.

### Contraindications

- **Tar irritancy.** The wood-tar is irritant to skin and mucous membranes; dilute and patch-test; avoid broken-barrier overuse.
- **Pregnancy — avoid.** Junipers carry an abortifacient/emmenagogue reputation; medicinal use of *J. phoenicea* products is contraindicated in pregnancy. [USER: verify.]
- **Renal irritation.** Juniper-type volatile oils can be renally irritant; internal use inappropriate in kidney disease.
- **Identity uncertainty.** Because "cedar" spans several genera, practical use must rest on the verified plant, not on an assumed equivalence with true *Cedrus*.
- **No casual internal use of the tar.** Specialist framing.
