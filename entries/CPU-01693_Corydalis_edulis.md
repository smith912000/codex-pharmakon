# Corydalis edulis — re.skon, Rekon (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Corydalis edulis, the first of three binomials Clark 1995 prints for re.skon (with Potentilla tanacetifolia and Potentilla supina, as printed); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text prints the name as "Rekon", and its glossary sets Corydalis nepalense beside it* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under this binomial only by Clark.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. Both Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the line prints three, of which the row takes the first (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 127). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1002).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads:

"re.skon: Corydalis edulis: Potentilla tanacetifolia: Potentilla supina: [has seven names, for example, rtsi.dmar and phug.ron.rkang.bchas. It grows in rocky meadows, has spreading leaves like Nelumbium nucifer (‘utpala’) and a single root with eight tendrils. The flower is yellow and retains dewdrops. It is well-scented and has extremely bitter taste]. Corydalis is hemostatic and cures channel fever caused by brown phlegm [and dysentery with fever]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the seven names, the habitat, the leaves, the single root with eight tendrils, the yellow flower, the scent, the "extremely bitter taste" and the closing "and dysentery with fever". Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the three Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the clause "Corydalis is hemostatic and cures channel fever caused by brown phlegm", in which the word "Corydalis" is Clark's own for the drug. The line gives no part used and no preparation. It names two *Potentilla* species beside *Corydalis edulis*, which is a different genus.

*Clark 1995, two other places.* Chapter 19, "Groups of Medicines Having the Same Taste" (heading at line 764), line 768, puts "Corydalis edulis" in the bitter group: "The group of medicines having bitter taste includes neem, Indian gentian, Aconitum balfouri, Picrorhiza kurroa, bitter cucumber, Conessi (Kurchi), musk, (animal) bile, Indian barberry, Malabar nut tree, mineral exudate, Gentiana straminea, Corydalis edulis, etc." Chapter 21, "Compounding of Medicines" (heading at line 1204), line 1210: "The class of medicines which cure blood disorders consists of Red Sandalwood, Caraganajubata, Catechu tree, Picrorhiza, Vasaka (Malabar Nut tree), Emblic Myrobalan, Corydalis edulis, Pterocephalus hookeri, Madder and Bastard Teak." So Clark uses the binomial *Corydalis edulis* for the drug in three places.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, the same Tibetan name.* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The text prints the name as "Rekon" in three passages. In chapter 19 it is in the bitter group (line 3213): "Nimpa, Tikta, Bongnga karpo, Honglen, Sergyi metok, Dhungmo nyung, La tsi, Tripa, Kyerpa, Bhashaka, Drak shhun, Kyiche, Rekon and other similar substances are the group of medicinal substances having a bitter taste" (the sentence ends on line 3214). In chapter 20 (line 3428), under "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines": "Rekon dries up blood, cures baekan mukpo and hot disorder of the channels." In chapter 21 (lines 3598 to 3600) it is in the blood group: "Tsendhen marpo, zhomo, Sengdeng, Honglen, Bhashaka, Kyurura, Rekon, Pang tsi dhowo, Tsod and Tshoe are the group of medicines that cure blood disorders." The index (line 7478) lists "Rekon 187,206,220". The text gives no Latin name in these passages. The Codex reads "Rekon" as Clark's *re.skon* from the spelling and from the agreement in taste (bitter), in action (blood) and in the "channel" disorder; the text does not say so. Clark's "brown phlegm" and the Men-Tsee-Khang "baekan mukpo" look parallel, and no line read says that they are one term. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary.* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate blocks. In the "R" block the phonetic names are "Ranye Rechagpa / Rekon Rerel Risho Ru-ta" (lines 5667 and 5668), the Tibetan transliterations include "re skon re ral ri sho" (line 5673), and the scientific names are on one line (5676): "Polygonatum cirhifolium Stellera chamaejasme Corydalis nepalense Polystichum squarros Ligularia cymbulifera Saussurea lappa". Counting in order, "Rekon" is the third phonetic name and the third scientific name is "Corydalis nepalense". The Codex therefore reads the glossary as pairing "Rekon" with *Corydalis nepalense*, by column position only, and not with *Corydalis edulis*. The count can be checked on neighbours: "Ranye" stands first against *Polygonatum* (Clark's *ra.mnye* entry, line 1144, lists Polygonatum species) and the last name "Ru-ta" against *Saussurea lappa* and the English "Costus" (line 5683). The glossary prints no *Corydalis edulis*. The two Tibetan texts agree on the name and the actions and differ on the species, and the Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Corydalis edulis*, *re.skon* and *Rekon* found no entry on this plant. I_1260 (*Corydalis nepalense*), written in this batch, is the glossary-only sibling row for the same Tibetan name. I_1172 (*Pterocephalus hookeri*), I_1224 (*Adhatoda vasica*) and I_1249 (*Caragana jubata*) quote Clark's line 1210 (the blood-disorders class) in their prose and are not entries on *Corydalis*. I_1225 (*Adiantum pedatum*) quotes the "R" block of the glossary for its neighbour "Rerel". Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Corydalis* is a genus of the poppy family (Papaveraceae, in its fumitory subfamily) and that the genus is generally known to contain isoquinoline alkaloids. *Potentilla* belongs to the rose family. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained, because its Latin name is not settled. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record an extremely bitter drug that is "hemostatic and cures channel fever caused by brown phlegm" (Clark, line 1002) and that "dries up blood, cures baekan mukpo and hot disorder of the channels" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3428). These are the texts' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation for this drug. Clark's commentary describes a single root with eight tendrils but does not say which part is taken, and no quantity, frequency or duration is given in either text. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Bitter, by the taste-group lists (Clark line 768; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3213) and, in Clark's commentary, "extremely bitter taste" (line 1002, inside the brackets). No power (hot or cool) and no post-digestive taste is printed for this drug in the lines read.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes this drug by taste (bitter) and by what it does: it is hemostatic and cures channel fever caused by brown phlegm, and it stands in the class of medicines for blood disorders. The Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same ("dries up blood"). The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this drug places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the passages cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a bitter blood and channel-fever drug in the Tibetan texts, its Latin name is not settled, and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary appears to pair its name with another *Corydalis*. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. The identity is uncertain and the genus is generally known to contain alkaloids, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Bleeding and fever need medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
