# Mushrooms (fungi) — Muketes (as printed in Dioscorides 4-83) / the Udbhida group: Palala, Ikshu, Karisha, Venu, Bhumija (as printed in Bhishagratna's Sushruta)
## *Fungi species, mushrooms edible and poisonous (the translator's suggestion for Dioscorides 4-83); Bhishagratna gives no binomial for the mushrooms of the Udbhida group* | *(Volume IV — Fungal/Algal, shelved in Volume I)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM, SS

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 4-83, *Muketes*, "Fungi species — Mushrooms etc." (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 8783-8786; the names row for MUKETES is in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, and the translation prints the heading "SOME SPECIES POISONOUS" under the title). The whole chapter is a warning, with a method for the cook. "Fungi have a double difference for they are either edible or poisonous, and come to be so on many occasions, for they grow among rusty nails, rotten rags, the holes of snakes, or among trees that bear harmful fruits. Such as these also have a viscous coalesced fluid, and stored after they are picked they quickly spoil, growing rotten. Those that are not harmful (boiled in broth) are sweet, yet for all that taken too much they hurt, being hard to digest, choking or breeding bile. All are helped; drenched with nitre [saltpetre] and oil, or soaked in a decoction of sharp brine or thymbra [3-45], or liquified with origanum, or hen dung with vinegar, or syruped with a quantity of honey. They are nourishing and hard to dissolve, and are put out whole (for the most part) with the excrement." Dioscorides returns to mushrooms in two other places. In 4-84 *Kolchikon* (meadow saffron, line 8795), the plant when "eaten, kills by choking, similar to mushrooms", and he directs the reader to "give them whatever helps those who eat mushrooms [above], and cow's milk (taken as a drink)". In the chapter on vinegar, 5-21 *Oxus* (line 9873), vinegar is "good for ... curdling in the stomach, and with salt against mushrooms, ixia [3-103] and smilax" (line 9878). The chapter on agaric, 3-1 *Agaricon* (line 6516), is also tied to mushrooms by the translator's gloss "Agaricus campestris — Common Mushroom" and by the report that agaric "grows in the stocks of trees that are rotten, like mushrooms", but Dioscorides calls agaric a root and gives it its own chapter, so it is not treated here. **Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The English is Bhishagratna's 1907 translation (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt`). The chapter is *Sutrasthana* ch.46, "the Chapter which treats of food and drink (Annapana-Vidhimadhyayam)" (lines 4-7). Its opening speech states the frame: "food determines the origin of beings and forms the chief source of their bodily strength and complexion, as well as of the albuminous (Ojas) principle in their organisms. Food primarily depends upon the six different tastes" (lines 9-14). The mushrooms are in the "Udbhida Group" (lines 1847-1875), which follows the passage on flowers and precedes the *Pinyaka* group (oil-cakes). The passage opens: "Mushrooms are generally found to grow on stacks of straw (Palala), or are seen vegetating on the stems of bamboo (Venu) or sugar-cane (Ikshu), or as sprouting up from beneath the surface of the ground (Udbhida), or growing on a heap of decomposed cow-dung (Karisha)" (lines 1847-1852), and then gives the properties of each kind (see Varieties). The group is a food group: nothing in the passage gives a medical preparation, a dose, or a use against a disease.

### Varieties

The five kinds are given as the kinds of the group in Bhishagratna's sentence (lines 1848-1852: straw, bamboo, sugar-cane, ground, cow-dung) and then described in a different order (straw, sugar-cane, cow-dung, bamboo, ground; lines 1858-1873). The paragraphs follow the order of the descriptions.

**Palalaja** (mushroom grown on straw; SS; Sutra ch.46, lines 1848, 1853-1860). "Those which grow on stacks of (decomposed) straw (Palalam) are sweet in taste and digestion and tend to produce a state of dryness in the organism. They subdue the three deranged humours of the body." The Codex's variety list calls this kind *palalaja*; Bhishagratna prints *Palalam*.

**Ikshuja** (mushroom grown on sugar-cane; SS; lines 1850, 1861-1863). "Those which vegetate on the stems of sugar-canes (Ikshujam) have a sweet pungent taste. They leave an astringent after-taste in the mouth and are cooling in their potency."

**Karishaja** (mushroom grown on cow-dung; SS; lines 1852, 1864-1868). "Mushrooms growing on decomposed cow-dung (Karisha) should be regarded as possessed of properties similar to those of the preceding class. They aggravate the Vayu, are heat-making in their potency, and have an astringent taste." Bhishagratna's sentence says they are "similar" to the sugar-cane class and then lists properties that differ from it (heat-making, not cooling); the wording is his and is kept as printed. **Venuja** (mushroom grown on bamboo; SS; lines 1849, 1868-1870). "Those which vegetate on the stems of bamboos (Venuja) have an astringent taste and tend to enrage or aggravate the bodily Vayu."

**Bhumija** (mushroom grown in the ground; SS; lines 1851, 1871-1875). "Mushrooms which grow on the ground (Bhumija) are heavy of digestion and do not inordinately generate the Vayu, their tastes varying according to the soil they grow on."

*Synthesis of the varieties.* Only Sushruta separates the mushrooms into kinds, and the ground he uses is the substrate they grow on: straw, cane, dung, bamboo, ground. It prints a taste for four of the five (straw sweet; cane sweet-pungent with an astringent after-taste; dung and bamboo astringent), a potency for two (cane cooling, dung heat-making), an effect on *Vayu* for three (dung and bamboo aggravate it, ground-grown does not inordinately generate it), and a digestibility for two (straw sweet in digestion, ground heavy). It names no first-ranked kind, and only the straw kind is said to subdue the three humours. Dioscorides separates mushrooms into two, edible and poisonous, on a different ground: what the mushroom grew among (rusty nails, rotten rags, snake holes, trees bearing harmful fruit). The two lists can be placed side by side. They are not equated, and no Sushruta kind is said here to be an edible or a poisonous kind of Dioscorides.

### Active Components

Mushrooms are the fruiting bodies of fungi. They are mostly water, with protein, fibre, chitin in the cell walls, and a range of small molecules that differ greatly by species; a few species carry potent toxins, and some species are safe only if cooked. The sources name no active component and give no quantities. This entry cannot say which species either text means, and it does not.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The two texts record mushrooms as food, with a warning (Dioscorides) and a table of tastes and effects on the humours (Sushruta). The Codex has no protocol for foraging or eating wild fungi and offers none. Dioscorides' own first sentence, that fungi are "either edible or poisonous" and come to be one or the other by where they grow, is the main finding, and both of his remedies (nitre and oil, brine, origanum, honey) are for the cook, not tested by anything read here. Deaths from wild mushrooms still occur, and a plate of mushrooms cannot be judged by the descriptions in these two texts.

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Some wild mushrooms contain toxins that cause liver or kidney failure, nerve effects or severe gastroenteritis; several poisonous species look like edible ones, and cooking does not always destroy the toxins. Cultivated species (such as the button mushroom) are widely eaten. Nothing about the substrates (dung, straw, cane, bamboo) is assumed to determine safety. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides prints "sweet" for the harmless kind boiled in broth, and "nourishing and hard to dissolve" for fungi in general; he prints no hot or cold. 

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** Rasa, virya and effects on the humours as printed per kind in Sutra ch.46 (see Varieties): straw, sweet in taste and digestion, drying, subdues the three humours; cane, sweet-pungent, astringent after-taste, cooling in potency; dung, astringent, heat-making, aggravates *Vayu*; bamboo, astringent, aggravates *Vayu*; ground, heavy of digestion, tastes vary with the soil. No *vipaka* is printed beyond "sweet in digestion" for the straw kind.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. The chapter is a discipline of attention in its purest form: look at where the fungus grew, how it keeps ("stored after they are picked they quickly spoil"), what it is like when cooked, and be careful about how much is eaten. It assigns the mushroom no cultivation status.

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The mushrooms sit in the *Annapana-vidhi* chapter (Sutra ch.46), which opens with the principle that food is the chief source of bodily strength, complexion and *ojas*. That is a statement about food in general, and the passage then gives the properties of each mushroom kind, not an effect on *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana*. The mushrooms are not named in a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* chapter in anything read here, and the passage read uses no sattvic, rajasic or tamasic labelling (those categories are *Samkhya*, not Sushruta). **Practitioner's note.** The only use here is care. Both texts treat mushrooms as a food that needs knowledge: Dioscorides separates the edible from the poisonous by where they grew and how they keep, and Sushruta lists the kinds by what they grew on and what each does to the humours. Neither assigns a cultivation role. Taking from them any wild mushroom as a medicine or as a "tonic" would be an invention, and a dangerous one.

### Contraindications

- **Poisoning.** Wild mushrooms can be fatal, and edible and poisonous species can look alike; do not eat any wild mushroom without expert identification, and never on the basis of either text. Seek urgent medical care for any mushroom ingestion with vomiting, abdominal pain or diarrhoea, particularly if the symptoms begin or return after a delay.
- **Quantity.** Dioscorides notes that even the harmless kinds "taken too much ... hurt, being hard to digest, choking or breeding bile."
- **Choking and indigestion.** Hard-to-digest fungi are a choking hazard for small children and the frail.
- **Allergy and drug interactions.** Some people react to fungi, and some species interact with alcohol; this is a general caution and is not in the sources. [USER: verify]
- **Dose unknown.** No dose is given in the sources read.
- **Ethics and law.** Foraging is restricted in many parks and reserves; collecting some species is limited by law. [USER: verify]
