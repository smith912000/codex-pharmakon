### Origin & Tradition

The sweet chestnut is one of the great food-trees of the Old World — the "bread tree" of the European mountains, whose starchy nuts, dried and ground into flour, sustained whole upland populations through winters where grain would not grow. Dioscorides records the chestnut (the "Sardian acorn," *Dios balanos*) in Book I, noting the nourishing but somewhat heavy, costive quality of the nut and the astringency of its parts. The classical and medieval pharmacy valued the tree in two distinct registers: the **nut** as a hearty, binding nourishment, and the **leaf and bark** as astringent, drying remedies.

The most enduring medicinal use is the **astringent leaf decoction for cough** — and specifically for the spasmodic, paroxysmal cough of whooping cough (pertussis), for which chestnut leaf was a standard European folk remedy well into the modern era, prized as a soothing antitussive and "anti-spasmodic" for irritable, convulsive coughing. The high tannin content of leaf and bark also recommended the tree for the usual astringent purposes: checking diarrhoea, as a gargle for relaxed or sore throats, and as a wash for lax tissues and minor bleeding.

The nut itself, being starchy, sweet and astringent, was read as nourishing and strengthening but "binding" — apt to constipate if eaten in quantity — and was a food-medicine for the depleted as much as a staple. Chestnut thus sits at the intersection of nutrition and pharmacy: a famine-food, a winter staple, and a respiratory and astringent remedy all at once.

### Contraindications

- **Constipation / dryness.** The binding nut and astringent decoctions can aggravate constipation and dry conditions.
- **Tannin–iron interaction.** High-tannin decoctions taken with meals can impair non-haem iron absorption; relevant in anaemia.
- **Nut allergy.** Tree-nut allergy can include chestnut.
- **Pregnancy.** The nut is ordinary food; concentrated astringent leaf/bark medicines warrant the usual moderate caution. [USER: verify.]
