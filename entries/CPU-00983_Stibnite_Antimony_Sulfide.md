## *Stibnite, antimony trisulphide (Sb2S3), the dark ore used as an eye-paint and eye medicine; the lists name it "antimony" or "kohl", and ancient eye-paints are not all stibnite* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** Bryan's Chapter V list (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1143; `extract/names.csv` row) has "Antimony", and "Collyrium" is listed separately (line 1154). Bryan's introduction says of the mineral class that "few of them are prescribed in their crude or native state. Antimony, Calamine, Granite, Ink, Indigo, and Sulphur almost exhaust the list" (line 1137). **Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** 5-99 STIMMI, "Stibium — Trisulphide of Antimony, Black Antimony" (line 10320 in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`): "The best stibium is the brightest — shining, crusty when broken, with no earthiness or foulness, and quickly broken. It is able to close the pores, and is astringent and cooling, repressing fleshy abnormal growths, forming new skins on ulcers, and cleaning filth and ulcers in the eyes. It stops discharges of blood that come from the meninx [brain and spinal cord]. Generally its properties are like burnt lead." It will not let burns form a scab "rubbed on with new grease", and heals scabbed sores with wax and a little cerussa. It "is roasted — wrapped around with dough and hidden in coals until the dough has turned to a coal. When taken out it is quenched with the milk of a woman that bears a male child or with old wine"; burnt more it "melts like lead"; it "is washed like cadmia and brass, but some wash it like lead scoria". "Some have called this stibi, platyophthalmon, larbason, gynoecion, or chalcedonium."

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** Bhishagratna glosses three Sanskrit names as antimony, and the project's Sushruta index (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/extract/names.csv`) records *Srotonjana* as "Antimony." (Vol. III Appendix 3 line 33027). *Srotonjana*: *Chikitsasthana* ch.24, "the rules of hygiene and the prophylactic measures in general" (`chikitsa/SS_Chikitsa_ch24.txt` lines 111-124): "Srotonjana, produced in the river Indus, is the best and purest of Collyriums. It alleviates the burning and itching sensations in the eyes, removes all local pains, secretions and impurities, increases the range of vision, enables the eyes to bear the blasts of the wind and the glare of the sun and guards against the inroads of occular affections. Hence the application of collyrium (along the eye-lids) is highly recommended; but its use is forbidden just after taking one's meal or bath ... and after the fatigue of vomiting or riding, etc., nor after keeping late hours and also not during an attack of fever." In the eye chapters it is soaked successively in meat-juice, milk and ghee, or kept a month "inside the mouth of a (dead) black cobra" (*Uttara* ch.17 lines 288-318), is in a powder stuffed in a ram's horn with metals (`SS_Uttara_ch15.txt` lines 144-146), and is in a *Lekhana Puta-paka* (`SS_Uttara_ch18.txt` lines 150-156). *Anjana*, which Bhishagratna glosses "black Antimony" in *Uttara* ch.11 (line 41), is the first drug of the *Anjanadi* group (*Sutrasthana* ch.38 line 301, "Anjana, Rasanjana, Nagapushpa, Priyangu, Nilotpala, Nalada, Nalina, Keshara and Madhuka"), whose metrical text says the group is "anti-toxic in its character and allays the internal burning sensation of the body" (lines 303-307), and ch.38 itself gives no identification. *Rasanjana* is glossed "(antimony)" in a footnote to *Sutrasthana* ch.1 that counts it among the "earthy substances" (`SS_Sutra_ch01.txt` lines 478-481). Whether the three names are one mineral is not settled by the glosses. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Stibnite is antimony trisulphide, Sb2S3, a grey-black mineral with a metallic lustre that is soft and easy to grind to a dark powder. "Kohl" is an older name for eye-black made from dark minerals, and the dark mineral behind a given ancient "kohl" may be stibnite, galena (lead sulphide) or something else, so the texts' identification as antimony is a hypothesis in each case. Antimony compounds are toxic, as arsenic's are, and cause vomiting; the traditional "roasted" or "burnt" preparations are not standardised and their composition is not given in the lines read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. In the sources the substance is an eye medicine and eye-paint, a dressing for ulcers and burns, and a prophylactic collyrium, always applied to the outside of the lids or skin. Antimony compounds are toxic and eye products carry infection risk, and the Codex offers no protocol, dose or preparation. Ritual elements (the cobra's mouth, the male child's milk) are recorded as ritual.

### Preparation & Dosing

Only preparations the texts give. DMM 5-99: roasted in a dough wrapper in coals and quenched in the milk of a woman who bore a boy, or in old wine; or burnt on coals until it flames; washed like cadmia and brass or like lead slag. SS: soaked in meat-juice, milk and ghee (*Uttara* ch.17); kept a month in a dead black cobra's mouth, then mixed with rock salt and ash (*Uttara* ch.17); applied along the lids (*Chikitsasthana* ch.24). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Antimony sulphide is a poorly soluble dark powder; the astringent and cooling action Dioscorides gives it is a traditional observation not checked here. Antimony compounds were used as emetics in European medicine (tartar emetic) and, in a different form, against leishmaniasis; they are toxic. Non-sterile eye-paints cause eye infection. No clinical studies were retrieved for the traditional uses. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here for a cultivator to take. What the sources teach is care of the eyes by a sound habit and timing (Sushruta) and care in the choice of the mineral (Dioscorides); neither supports a modern use of the powder, and antimony and lead eye-paints have caused harm. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Antimony compounds are toxic if swallowed and irritant to the eye; "kohl" and "surma" products of unknown origin may contain lead and have caused poisoning, especially in children. Non-sterile eye applications carry infection risk. The ritual uses (the cobra's mouth, the male child's milk) are ritual, not therapy.
