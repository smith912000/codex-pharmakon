### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk lists the plant under this name (the name indexes in `Sources/*/extract/names.csv` and the list files in `_sources/` were searched; `_sources/dioscorides.csv`, line 570, is the only list row). The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The three binomials on the line are all species of the genus *Xyris*, and the chapter itself is a plant that Dioscorides compares to the iris and that the text also calls *iris agria*, "wild iris". The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-22. XURIS* at line 8197, SUGGESTED line at 8199, and the text at line 8200. The text is cut in the middle of a sentence ("... is good for wounds in") by a page number (564, line 8201), two plate-only pages (565 and 566, lines 8205 and 8209) and a running header (line 8211), and it resumes at line 8213 ("the head and fractures; ..."). The two plates between the halves, "Campanula media after FAGUET — 1888" (line 8204) and "Gladiolus communis from ENGLER-PRANTL — 1897" (line 8208), are plates for other chapters (the first is a *Campanula* plate, and 4-18 *MEDION* is glossed *Campanula medium*; the second is a *Gladiolus* plate, and *Gladiolus communis* is one of the names on the SUGGESTED line of 4-20) and are not attributed to this chapter. The chapter stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), after *4-21. SPARGANION* (line 8190) and before *4-23. ANCHOUSA* (line 8215). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 602. The translators' indexes disagree on the page: one prints "Xyris indica 562" (line 11949) and the other prints "Xyris 564", "Xyris capensis 564" and "Xyris congensis 564" (line 18127). The page markers put the heading on page 564, and the Codex follows the markers.

The whole chapter reads: "Xyris has leaves similar to iris but broader and sharp at the top, with a stalk breaking out of the middle of the leaves — thick enough, one-foot long — on which are triangular pods. On them is a purple flower, and in the middle it is a Phoenician colour [red]. The seed (in little cases) is similar to beans — round, red and sharp. The long red root has many joints, and is good for wounds in the head and fractures; and mixed with one third part flour of brass [zinc oxide], a fifth part of the root of centaury and sufficient honey, it extracts prickles and all sorts of weapons without pain. Applied with vinegar it cures oedema and inflammation. The root (bruised with passum [raisin wine]) is taken as a drink for convulsion, hernia, sciatica, slow painful urination, and intestinal discharges. Thirty grains of a decoction of the seed (taken as a drink in wine) is most uretical. If a decoction is taken as a drink with vinegar, it also reduces the spleen. It is also called iris agria, or cactos, the Romans call it gladiolus, and some call it iris agrestis, while the Dacians call it aprus."

The chapter is more detailed on the plant than on any other part. It compares the leaves to the iris ("broader and sharp at the top"), describes a stalk "breaking out of the middle of the leaves", about a foot long, with "triangular pods" bearing a purple flower that is "Phoenician" (the translators' bracket says red) in the middle; the seed "in little cases" is "similar to beans — round, red and sharp"; and the root is "long", red and "many joints". The uses come in three groups. The root, applied, is "good for wounds in the head and fractures"; mixed with one third part of "flour of brass" (the translators' bracket says zinc oxide), a fifth part of the root of centaury and enough honey, it "extracts prickles and all sorts of weapons without pain"; applied with vinegar it "cures oedema and inflammation". The root bruised with raisin wine is drunk for "convulsion, hernia, sciatica, slow painful urination, and intestinal discharges". The seed, as a decoction in wine, is "most uretical" (that is, a strong diuretic), and a decoction drunk with vinegar "reduces the spleen". The names printed are *iris agria*, *cactos*, *iris agrestis*, the Roman *gladiolus* and the Dacian *aprus*.

**Overlap.** A chapter-level search of the entries folder for 4-22, the Greek heading, *Xyris* and *iris agria* found no entry on this chapter. I_1195 (*Sparganium ramosum*, chapter 4-21) is the chapter directly before this one and its line 12 notes the adjacency. I_1092 (*Gladiolus illyricus*, chapter 4-20 *XIPHION*) has, in its text (line 8189), "the Romans call it gladiolus", and the same Roman name is printed here. That is a shared name and the two chapters describe plants of different shape (4-20 has two bulb-like roots, one on the other). The Codex does not read them as one plant. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. It is a general fact that *Xyris* is a genus of monocotyledonous plants (family Xyridaceae) distinct from the iris family (Iridaceae),. The translators' three species are of the genus *Xyris*, while the text itself gives the names *iris agria* and *iris agrestis*; the Codex does not resolve the difference. The chapter's "red" root and seed are visible colours and not statements of composition. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the plant. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records an external root for wounds, fractures and the extraction of weapons and splinters, and an internal root and seed for convulsion, hernia, sciatica, painful urination, intestinal discharges and the spleen. These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. Fractures, wounds with embedded weapons, convulsions and hernia are conditions that need medical care, and the Codex gives no protocol. The chapter's recipe uses a metallic preparation ("flour of brass") that the Codex does not endorse.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives several preparations. External: the root applied; the root mixed "one third part flour of brass, a fifth part of the root of centaury and sufficient honey"; the root "applied with vinegar". Internal: the root "bruised with passum [raisin wine]" taken as a drink; "a decoction of the seed (taken as a drink in wine)"; and a decoction "taken as a drink with vinegar". The chapter gives one dose, "Thirty grains of a decoction of the seed (taken as a drink in wine)" (line 8213), and the proportions of the plaster. For the root drink and the vinegar decoction no dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 4-22 prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality and no taste. It prints the colours of the flower, seed and root and a shape for each.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a detailed recognition set: leaves compared with the iris (broader, sharp at the top), a one-foot stalk breaking out of the middle of the leaves, triangular pods, a purple flower with a red centre, round red sharp seed in little cases like beans, and a long red jointed root. It gives no habitat, no provenance, no smell or taste assay and no adulteration test. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The identification is the translators', and the text gives the names *iris agria* and *iris agrestis* while the translators' species are of a different genus. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is the part-by-part recognition (leaf, stalk, pod, flower, seed, root), which is a good method for any plant. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 4-22 prints no warning, though it includes an internal use of the root and a plaster that contains "flour of brass" (zinc oxide in the translators' bracket). The identification is only the translators' suggestion, and the text's own names (*iris agria*, *iris agrestis*) point to a wild iris while the translators' species are of another genus, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Wild irises and iris-like plants include poisonous ones, which the chapter does not address. Fractures, embedded weapons and splinters, convulsions and hernia are not conditions to treat with a herb. The text gives two proportions and one quantity and no safety information for pregnancy, children or any medicine taken at the same time. [USER: verify]
