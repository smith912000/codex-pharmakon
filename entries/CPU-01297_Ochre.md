# Ochre — Ochra (as printed in the 2000 English edition of Dioscorides)
## *Ochre, the translator's gloss under 5-108 OCHRA; from the description, a light yellow, brittle, stone-free earth pigment, with Athens named as a source; no mineral name is given in the text* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus for this entry is *De Materia Medica* 5-108 *Ochra* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, line 10385; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row "5-108 (line 10385)"; `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 834, class mineral). The translator's gloss is "Ochre" (line 10387). The chapter follows 5-107 *Indikon*, indigo, and precedes 5-109 *Kinnabari*, cinnabar (line 10394). It must not be confused with the plant *Ochra* ("Wild Ochra", 267, in the English index), which is a different, botanical entry; this entry is the earth.

The whole chapter reads (lines 10388-10393, with a page break in the middle): "Ochre must be chosen which is light and yellow, with a rich colour, unstony, and brittle. That from Athens is like this. We must burn and wash it like cadmia [5-84]. It is an astringent for putrefying sores, dissolves inflammation and small swellings, represses abnormal growths of flesh, fills up sores and wounds (used with wax ointments), and diminishes calluses." The bracket "[5-84]" is the translator's cross-reference to the chapter on cadmia (*Kadmeia*, line 10220).

This is a chapter of the kind the Codex values for its discipline of attention. It gives a test for choosing the stone by colour ("light and yellow, with a rich colour"), by absence of grit ("unstony") and by texture ("brittle"). It names one place, Athens, where the stone has these qualities. It gives a method, burning and washing "like cadmia", and the cadmia chapter spells that method out: the cadmia is burned in coals "until it becomes transparent and casts out flaming sparks as plates of iron do", then quenched in Aminaean wine, or in vinegar if it is for itchy skin medicines, and is later "pounded with water in a mortar, the water then poured away until no filth remains on it" and made into balls kept in jars (5-84, line 10226). The chapter on ochre does not itself repeat these steps, so whether Dioscorides meant every step for ochre, and whether wine or vinegar was to be used, is not stated.

Two related chapters in the same book deal with red earths. 5-111 *Miltos Sinopike*, "Red Ochre from Sinope" (line 10406), is described: "The best rubrica Sinopica is thick and heavy, with a bright colour, without stones, and all one colour", from Cappadocia, "drying and adhesive", mixed into wound plasters. 5-112 *Tektonike* (line 10409), "Red Ochre", says "Ochre is burnt and turns into red ochre" (line 10411). That sentence tells us that the Greek writer regarded burned ochre as the source of a red earth, and it is the only place where the text relates the two. The red ochres are separate chapters with their own headings and are not part of this entry's tag.

No other tradition is tagged for this entry. A search of the other texts on disk found candidate loci that have not been added to the tag line: Clark's 1995 Tibetan translation has "rdo.mkhris: Yellow Ochre" (line 840) and "btsag: Red Ochre" (line 852), and Bryan's Ebers text mentions "red ochre or hematite" as a modern identification of the word *didi* (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`, line 1229). Neither is shown to be the substance of 5-108.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, yellow ochre is a clay-like earth coloured by hydrated iron oxide (the mineral goethite, a form of iron oxyhydroxide), and the pigment turns red when heated because it loses water and changes to the red iron oxide haematite; that matches the sentence "Ochre is burnt and turns into red ochre" in 5-112, but the text does not state the chemistry and the identification of the Greek material with goethite-bearing earth is the translator's. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved say that Dioscorides recommended prepared ochre applied to sores, swellings, growths of flesh, wounds and calluses, in wax ointments. The Codex does not recommend putting a mineral pigment on open wounds or sores: natural earths are not sterile and may be contaminated with other minerals, including toxic metals. The sources support a historical entry and nothing more.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources retrieved give these preparation steps: choose the stone by the signs above; burn and wash it as cadmia is burned and washed (line 10393 with 5-84, line 10226); and use it with wax ointments to fill sores and wounds (line 10393). The chapter gives no quantity, no formula for the ointment, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Ochres are iron-oxide-bearing clays, used as pigments since prehistory. Iron oxides are insoluble and are not known to treat disease; natural earths may carry other minerals, grit and microbes. Clay and iron-oxide dusts applied to a sore are a risk of infection, and an astringent action on a wound, if any, would be a matter of surface drying only. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** one quality is printed for ochre in the chapter: it is "an astringent" (for putrefying sores). The chapter prints no taste, no degree of heat or cold, and no moisture rating. The related chapter on Sinopic red ochre calls it "drying and adhesive" (line 10408), which is that chapter's wording and is not transferred to this entry. No other tradition is tagged, so no other classification is offered.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: provenance, assay, preparation. This chapter has a good part of it in a few words: the stone is to be "chosen" by colour, grit and brittleness; Athens is the place named for the right kind; and the earth is burned and washed before use. That is a practice of looking, naming a source and not using the material raw. It is a therapeutic chapter, and it does not assign the earth a cultivation status or any effect on the person's vital constitution. No amuletic use is recorded for it.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator beyond the attention it shows: choose carefully, know where the material comes from, and do not use it raw. The Codex does not recommend using ochre on wounds. If the reader wants a lesson from the chapter, it is that Dioscorides tested the earth before he trusted it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Open wounds, sores and ulcers:** do not pack a natural earth into a wound; it can introduce infection, including tetanus, and a wound that is "putrefying" needs clinical care.
- **Growths and calluses:** a growth of flesh that is changing, bleeding or spreading needs a diagnosis, not an ointment.
- **Contamination:** natural ochres may contain arsenic, lead or other heavy metals; the sources read do not mention this and do not say what was in the Athenian earth.
- **Dust:** do not breathe the powder.
- **Identification:** "ochre" is the translator's gloss; the plant of the same Greek name is a different entry.
