# Creeping Woodsorrel — Suan Cao (酸草) · Ḥummāḍ · Oxys

## *Oxalis corniculata* L. | Family: Oxalidaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM, SBJ
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry (second degree) — a refrigerant, astringent-sour herb in the Galenic-Unani scheme
**Training Phase:** Cooling / Descent — the quenching phase, used to draw down heat and check flux

---

### Origin & Tradition

Creeping woodsorrel is a low, decumbent herb of warm-temperate and tropical ground, trifoliate with heart-shaped leaflets that fold at dusk and a sharp lemon tartness on the tongue. That sourness — oxalic acid and its salts — is the sensory signature every tradition seized upon, reading it as a cooling, heat-cutting quality.

**Dioscorides & the Greek line (DMM):** The Greeks knew a cluster of sour-leaved herbs as *oxys* / *oxalis*, the name itself meaning "sharp" or "acid." Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* treats such sour pot-herbs as cooling and binding, given for stomach heat, to quench thirst, and to check loose bowels — the classic profile later attached across Europe to the woodsorrels and sorrels. The genus name *Oxalis* preserves this Greek root directly.

**Avicenna & the Unani tradition (CM):** In the humoral pharmacy transmitted through Avicenna's *Qanun* and later Unani practice, sour cooling herbs of this type are classed as cold and dry, prescribed to temper hot bilious (safra) conditions — bilious fever, excessive thirst, hot dysenteries, and inflamed swellings. The sour taste is itself read as evidence of a cold, contracting temperament that opposes heat and putrefaction.

**Shennong / Chinese folk use:** In Chinese practice *Oxalis corniculata* is *suan jiang cao* / *cu jiang cao* (sour-pulp herb), a clearing-heat, cooling-blood, detoxifying herb applied to dysentery and diarrhoea, hot urinary complaints (dysuria, "heat" in the water passages), sore throat, and — crushed fresh — to burns, insect bites, boils, and slow-healing wounds. The energetic reading is sour and cold, entering to drain damp-heat.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Across all three lineages the plant's logic is coherent: a taste of acid = a temperament of cold. Modern chemistry vindicates the sensory reasoning, because the sourness is genuinely oxalic acid, and the same phenolic-flavonoid load that accompanies it carries real antioxidant and antimicrobial action. The nyctinastic leaf-fold (closing at night, opening to light) made it in folk symbolism a plant of rhythm and modesty — apt for a herb the Kenetica files under the cooling descent, where excess heat is folded away rather than fought.

### Active Components

- **Organic acids:** high **oxalic acid** and oxalates (the source of the sour taste and of the safety caveat below), plus tartaric and citric acids.
- **Flavonoids & polyphenols:** the phytochemical burden most associated with its bioactivity; congeneric *Oxalis* material analysed by HPLC shows **chlorogenic acid, gallic acid, caffeic acid, quercetin and salicylic acid** as major phenolics, according to PubMed ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/cbdv.202400883)).
- **Other classes reported for the species:** alkaloids, terpenoids, cardiac glycosides, saponins, phlobatannins and steroids, per a phyto-pharmacology review of *O. corniculata* on PubMed ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.2174/1386207324666210813121431)).
- Ascorbate and carotenoid pigments contribute to the antioxidant profile.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Woodsorrel is a **quencher**. It belongs to the cooling-descent phase where the working aim is to take heat *down and out* — to settle a hot, over-fluxing gut, to cool an inflamed surface, and to relieve the dry thirst of a heat state. Practically its traditional roles cluster around three actions: refrigerant (fever, thirst, hot irritability), astringent-antidysenteric (loose, hot stools; dysentery), and topical vulnerary-antiseptic (burns, bites, boils, slow wounds). It is a minor, supportive herb — a corrective and a cooler, not a heroic remedy — and is best used fresh and briefly.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, a phyto-pharmacology review of *Oxalis corniculata* documents antibacterial, antifungal, antioxidant, anti-cancer, anti-diabetic, cardioprotective and wound-healing activities attributed to its polyphenol, glycoside and flavonoid profile, while explicitly noting that the species has **not** been the subject of clinical trials and calls for isolation/characterisation and pre-clinical work ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.2174/1386207324666210813121431)). Supporting genus-level chemistry: an evaluation of *Oxalis corymbosa* (a congener) confirmed significant antioxidant and antibacterial activity against *E. coli* and *S. aureus* and identified chlorogenic/gallic/caffeic acids and quercetin as major phenolics ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/cbdv.202400883)); and a screening of *Oxalis*-family leaf extract found flavonoids, glycosides and tannins with measurable, if modest, inhibition zones against oral-cavity bacteria ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.22092/ARI.2022.357207.1996)).

**Evidence grade: Traditional-to-Preclinical.** The direction of the traditional claims (cooling, antimicrobial, wound-supportive) is consistent with in-vitro and animal data on the species and close congeners, but there are no human clinical trials. Claims should be presented as traditional use with preclinical support, not as proven therapy.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** sour (酸), cold (寒); clears heat, cools blood, resolves toxicity, disperses stasis-swelling; used for damp-heat dysentery and hot urinary/skin complaints.

### Contraindications

**⚠️ Oxalate caution — primary safety flag.** *Oxalis corniculata* is rich in **oxalic acid / soluble oxalates**. It should be **avoided or strictly limited in anyone with a history of calcium-oxalate kidney stones, hyperoxaluria, gout, or renal impairment**, and not consumed in large or habitual quantities by anyone. Oxalates bind calcium (reducing its absorption and potentially contributing to stone formation) and impose a load the kidneys must clear. Avoid concurrent large intake with other high-oxalate foods. Caution in pregnancy and in children given the oxalate load and absent safety data. Not a substitute for medical care in dysentery or significant burns. Correct plant identification matters, as several sour trifoliate herbs are confused in folk use.
