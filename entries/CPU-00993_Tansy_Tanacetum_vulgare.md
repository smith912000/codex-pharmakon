# Tansy — *athanasia* (Greek, "immortality/deathlessness"); common tansy, "bitter buttons," "golden buttons"

## *Tanacetum vulgare* L. | Family: Asteraceae (Compositae)

**Tradition Tags:** SS, AT, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Hot & dry (third degree), intensely bitter and acrid — a powerful, *lethal-margin* Galenic-Unani drug
**Training Phase:** Purge (the expulsion phase — worms, retained menses, corruption driven out — and therefore the phase where the Codex's hazard doctrine bites hardest)

---

> ⚠️ **LETHAL-MARGIN DRUG.** Tansy's active principle, **thujone**, is a GABA-A–antagonist neuroconvulsant that is also abortifacient and hepatotoxic. Fatal human poisonings from tansy oil are on record. Its historical anthelmintic and emmenagogue use ran on a knife-edge between therapeutic and toxic dose. **The essential oil must never be taken internally. Read the Contraindications section before any use whatsoever.**

---

### Origin & Tradition

Tansy is a hardy Eurasian composite with flat corymbs of button-like, rayless yellow flowerheads and a sharp camphoraceous-bitter scent. Across the ancient and medieval materia medica it occupies one dominant therapeutic slot: the **great vermifuge and emmenagogue** — the drug that "expels worms and brings on the menses." That double indication is not a coincidence; both uses exploit the same violent physiological irritation, and both live dangerously close to the plant's toxic dose.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (SS).** The cuneiform pharmacopoeia possessed a well-developed class of bitter, aromatic anthelmintic "worm drugs" administered in beer or with oil, reflecting a culture keenly aware of intestinal parasitism. Whether a specific tablet-name denotes *Tanacetum* rather than another bitter composite cannot be securely established. **[USER: verify — do not assign a specific Akkadian plant-name to tansy without a philological source.]** The general point stands: the bitter-anthelmintic drug category into which tansy falls was known and used.

**Avicenna / Unani (AT).** In the Galenic-Unani system, tansy-type bitter aromatics are classed **hot and dry** and used as *ṭārid al-dūd* (worm-expellers), *mudirr al-ṭamth* (emmenagogues to provoke menstruation), carminatives and antispasmodics for the sluggish, "cold and phlegmatic" gut. The doctrine explicitly recognised these as strong, heating, drying drugs to be dosed with care — the hot-and-dry-third-degree grading is itself an ancient hazard label.

**Dioscorides / Graeco-Roman & the name (DMM).** The Greek name *athanasia* — "deathlessness, immortality" — is the plant's most telling classical signature, and its origin is thoroughly practical: tansy's thujone-rich oil is a potent **insect repellent and preservative**, and the herb was strewn on floors, packed among stored goods, and later used in embalming and as a shroud-herb to keep corpses free of maggots and flies. "Immortality" here means *incorruptibility of dead flesh* — the herb that keeps the worm away from the body. The very chemistry that names it deathless (thujone's insect-neurotoxicity) is the chemistry that makes it dangerous to the living.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The tansy doctrine is one of the cleanest doctrine-to-chemistry mappings in the plant volume. The traditional claim — *"tansy expels worms and provokes the menses"* — maps directly onto **thujone as a nonspecific neuro-muscular excitant and GABA-A antagonist**. In the parasite, thujone (and the co-occurring sesquiterpene lactones) disrupts neuromuscular function and tegument integrity, paralysing and killing the worm; in the human host at sufficient dose it does the *same kind* of thing — antagonising inhibitory GABA-A transmission to produce excitation, uterine stimulation (hence emmenagogue → abortifacient) and, past threshold, convulsions. The "brings on the menses / brings down the dead flesh" symbolism and the *athanasia* embalming-name are two faces of one pharmacological fact: **thujone is a neurotoxin that kills small nervous systems (insects, helminths) efficiently and large ones (humans) only at doses uncomfortably near the therapeutic one.** The doctrine correctly encoded both the use *and* the danger — "hot and dry in the third degree" was the ancients' honest way of writing "narrow therapeutic index." The Codex therefore *vindicates* the mechanism of the doctrine while heavily *deflating* the safety of its application: the vermifuge worked, but it had a lethal-margin problem the tradition itself half-acknowledged.

### Active Components

- **α- and β-thujone** — bicyclic monoterpene ketone; the principal toxic/active constituent. Content is chemotype-dependent: some populations are **β-thujone chemotypes** (up to ~84% of the essential oil), others are **camphor chemotypes** where thujone is minor (~6%).
- **Camphor, chrysanthenyl acetate, verbenol, 1,8-cineole (eucalyptol), α-campholenal** — co-occurring oxygenated monoterpenes.
- **Sesquiterpene lactones** (e.g. of the germacranolide type) — bitter principles contributing anthelmintic and antifeedant activity and potential contact allergenicity.
- **Flavonoids and phenolic acids** — antioxidant fraction.

Chemotype is decisive: the *pharmacological identity and hazard* of a given tansy sample depend entirely on whether it is a thujone-dominant or camphor-dominant population.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Within the **Purge** phase, tansy is catalogued as the archetypal — and archetypally dangerous — **anthelmintic/emmenagogue bitter**. In Kenetica it is a *studied-not-administered* entry: its mechanism is instructive (GABA-A antagonism; neuro-muscular disruption of parasites), but internal self-use is contraindicated. Any legitimate contemporary role is topical/repellent (drawing on the insecticidal thujone) or purely doctrinal-historical. The vermifuge use is documented and mechanistically real, but obsolete and unsafe beside modern anthelmintics (praziquantel, albendazole) with vastly better therapeutic indices.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Preparation | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Essential oil (internal) | **None — do not ingest** | Thujone-rich oil has caused fatal human poisoning; convulsant and abortifacient. Prohibited. |
| Dried herb infusion (historical vermifuge) | Historically small quantities of dried flowering herb, brief course | **[USER: verify — no safe validated human dose exists; historically fatal at excess.]** Not recommended. |
| Topical insect-repellent / strewing herb | External use of dried herb or dilute oil | The defensible traditional use; still observe dermal-allergen caution. |

The regulatory best-estimate **allowable daily thujone intake via herbal preparations/diet is only ~3–7 mg/day** (Pelkonen et al., 2012) — a ceiling that a thujone-chemotype tansy preparation can breach easily and unpredictably. This alone forecloses casual internal use.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:

- **Godinho et al. (2014), *The Scientific World Journal*** — the essential oil of *T. vulgare* (GC/MS: **β-thujone 84.13%**) and crude extract killed 100% of adult *Schistosoma mansoni* worms in vitro at 200 µg/mL, with tegumental damage and reduced egg viability. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1155/2014/460342). Direct confirmation of the anthelmintic doctrine — in vitro, against a fluke — with thujone as the dominant oil constituent.
- **Pelkonen, Abass & Wiesner (2012), *Regulatory Toxicology and Pharmacology*** — toxicological review: thujone **inhibits the GABA-A receptor**, causing dose-dependent excitation and convulsions; neurotoxicity is the principal toxic outcome; equivocal carcinogenicity signal in rats; metabolised chiefly by **CYP2A6** (then CYP3A4, CYP2B6); best-estimate allowable intake ~3–7 mg/day. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.yrtph.2012.11.002). This is the mechanistic spine of the hazard doctrine.
- **Karcheva-Bahchevanska et al. (2023), *Molecules*** — a Bulgarian **camphor-chemotype** *T. vulgare* oil (camphor 25.2%, thujone only 6.06%) showed antioxidant activity (ORAC 605 µmol TE/mL) and, notably, an intraperitoneal **LD (mean lethal dose) of 14.9 g/kg** in Wistar rats with no oral acute toxicity at the tested dose. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules28166155). Demonstrates that a *low-thujone chemotype* is far less acutely toxic — reinforcing that chemotype, not species, governs danger.

**Evidence grade: MODERATE for mechanism, HIGH for hazard.** The anthelmintic action and the GABA-A convulsant mechanism are both directly supported; the lethal-margin problem is well documented. **NULL/negative note:** the low-thujone chemotype's low acute toxicity does *not* validate internal use of high-thujone material — it shows the opposite, that safety is a chemotype lottery. There are no controlled human efficacy trials for tansy as an anthelmintic; modern practice has abandoned it precisely because of the therapeutic-index problem.

### Contraindications

**⚠️ DEADLY-MARGIN / DO NOT SELF-ADMINISTER INTERNALLY.**

- **Thujone toxicity:** GABA-A antagonism produces excitation, tremor, and **convulsions/seizures** at excess dose; **fatal poisonings from tansy oil are documented.** The essential oil must never be ingested.
- **Abortifacient / uterine stimulant:** **absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy** — historically used to procure abortion, with maternal deaths recorded. Also avoid in those trying to conceive and during lactation.
- **Hepatotoxicity** and potential for CYP-mediated drug interactions (metabolised via CYP2A6/3A4/2B6).
- **Seizure disorders:** contraindicated — lowers the threshold via GABA-A blockade.
- **Chemotype uncertainty:** any wild or unstandardised sample may be a high-thujone chemotype; thujone content cannot be judged by smell or appearance.
- **Contact allergy:** sesquiterpene lactones can cause allergic contact dermatitis; caution even in topical/strewing use.
- Not a substitute for modern anthelmintics; the risk/benefit ratio for internal use is unacceptable.
