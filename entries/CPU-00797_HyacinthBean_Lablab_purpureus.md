### Origin & Tradition

The hyacinth bean is one of humanity's oldest cultivated legumes and, across the warm belt of the Old World, one of its most quietly important food-medicines. *Lablab purpureus* — the twining bean with purple-flushed pods, known as *bai bian dou* in China, *sem*/*avarai* across the Indian subcontinent, *njahi* in the Kenyan highlands, *lablab* through the Arab world — is that rare thing: a staple food that is simultaneously a formal drug in more than one classical pharmacopoeia. Its convergence is remarkably consistent. Everywhere it is grown, the white-seeded bean is read as **sweet, gently strengthening to the digestion, and specifically the remedy for the enervating "summer-damp" complex — the loss of appetite, loose stool, nausea and heaviness of hot humid weather**. It is the bean that steadies a weak, water-logged middle.

**Chinese Medicine (TCM):** Hyacinth bean is a formal materia medica drug — *bai bian dou* (白扁豆), the white-seeded cultivar — classed as sweet and slightly warm (or neutral), entering the Spleen and Stomach channels. Its canonical functions are to strengthen the Spleen (*jian-pi*), harmonise the centre, transform Damp (*hua-shi*) and relieve summer-heat with dampness (*shu-shi*). It is the classic remedy for the summer syndrome of poor appetite, loose stools, nausea, fatigue and heaviness, and for chronic *pi-xu* (Spleen-deficiency) diarrhoea and excessive vaginal discharge (*dai-xia*). It appears in famous formulas such as *Shen Ling Bai Zhu San* (the great Spleen-strengthening powder). Crucially, Chinese doctrine already encodes the plant's toxicity: raw hyacinth bean is recognised as mildly toxic and must be **dry-fried / thoroughly cooked** before use — a folk-into-formal safety rule confirmed by modern toxicology.

**Ayurveda & Siddha (AT / SS):** In the Indian systems the bean (*Nishpava* / *Sem*; Tamil *Avarai*) is a food-medicine used to strengthen the digestion, as a nutritive in convalescence, and traditionally noted (in some texts) for effects on the reproductive and lactational sphere. Ayurveda reads it as sweet, gently warming, *kapha*/*vata*-modulating and nourishing, though somewhat heavy and *ama*-forming if eaten raw or in excess — a reading that again encodes the "must be well-cooked" caution. The Siddha tradition of the Tamil south integrates *Avarai* as a common dietary pulse with mild strengthening and digestive-regulating properties within its extensive food-as-medicine framework.

**Sub-Saharan Ethnobotany:** Across East Africa the hyacinth bean (*njahi* in Kikuyu) is a deeply valued dietary legume with strong cultural-medicinal associations, notably as a nourishing food for postpartum and lactating women and for building strength in convalescence — a folk-nutritional reputation for restoration and recovery. It is simultaneously a drought-tolerant staple, a fodder and cover crop, and a household strengthening-food, embodying the same food-is-medicine continuum seen in its Asian use.

**Graeco-Arabic / Unani (adopted, DMM) & European Colonial Botany:** Through the Arab agricultural and trade networks the bean (*lablab*, the Arabic name that became its botanical epithet) entered Unani-influenced dietetics as a temperate, nourishing pulse. European colonial botany catalogued *Dolichos lablab* widely across its tropical range as a food and green-manure crop, recording its dietary-medicinal use in local pharmacopoeias rather than developing an independent European drug tradition — its medicine remained, appropriately, in the kitchen.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Bean That Steadies the Centre:**
Hyacinth bean is the Codex's exemplar of the *gentle Earth-strengthening food-medicine* — the doctrine that the deepest healing of the "centre" (the digestive core, the Spleen-Stomach axis, the Ayurvedic *agni*) is often accomplished not by a potent drug but by a *food eaten as medicine*, sweet and mild, taken over time. It belongs to the honoured class where the remedy and the daily meal are one, and where the therapeutic act is *dietary constancy* rather than acute intervention. This makes it the doctrinal counterpart, within this volume, to roselle: both dissolve the medicine-food boundary, but roselle is the *cooling drink* that clears heat, while hyacinth bean is the *strengthening food* that dries damp and steadies the earth-centre.

Its second doctrinal layer is the teaching of *the necessary transformation* — the bean is mildly toxic raw and wholesome only when cooked or dry-fried. Here the plant enacts the doctrine that *heat and preparation are themselves the medicine* — that the same material is poison in one state and Spleen-tonic in another, and that the cook's fire is a pharmacological act. In Kenetica hyacinth bean therefore teaches both the *Earth-strengthening food-medicine axis* and the *doctrine of transformation-by-preparation* — the poison that becomes nourishment through the fire, echoing (in a mild, dietary key) the same solve-et-coagula principle indigo teaches through fermentation and cotton teaches through refining.

### Active Components

- **Triterpenoid saponins** — a characteristic constituent class of the seed; contribute to biological activity and, in the raw state, to irritant/toxic potential.
- **Lectins (phytohaemagglutinins) & trypsin inhibitors** — heat-labile antinutritional/toxic proteins responsible for the raw-bean toxicity; destroyed by thorough cooking — the biochemical basis of the "must be cooked" rule.
- **Cyanogenic glycosides** — present in some cultivars, another reason for adequate cooking.
- **Polysaccharides, glucosides, amino acids and volatile components** — the nutritive and Spleen-strengthening fraction.
- **Polyphenols & flavonoids** — antioxidant constituents underpinning the reported hypolipidaemic, hypoglycaemic, immunomodulatory and hepatoprotective activities.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Hyacinth bean is a **Foundational food-as-medicine Spleen tonic** with an Intermediate seasonal-digestive register:

1. **Earth-centre strengthening (Foundational):** the reference gentle Spleen/digestion tonic — the *bai bian dou* action of steadying appetite, firming loose stool, and building the middle through dietary constancy.
2. **Summer-damp axis (Intermediate):** the specific seasonal remedy for the hot-humid-weather complex of poor appetite, nausea, loose stool, heaviness and fatigue (*shu-shi*).
3. **Convalescent/restorative nutrition (Foundational):** the postpartum and recovery-strengthening food reputation shared between East African and Asian tradition.
4. **Safety-teaching materia:** the canonical Codex example of *toxicity abolished by preparation* — must always be presented cooked/dry-fried.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the traditional profile is supported by a systematic review and by mechanistic plant-biology work:

- Yao, Xia, Tang et al. (2024), *Journal of Ethnopharmacology*, a systematic review of *Lablab* Semen Album, documents its traditional Chinese use for spleen/stomach weakness, poor appetite, loose stools and summer-damp diarrhoea, catalogues its phytochemistry (triterpenoid saponins, glucosides, polysaccharides, amino acids) and reports pharmacological activities including hypolipidaemic, hypoglycaemic, immunomodulatory, antioxidant, hepatoprotective, antitumoral and antiviral effects — while explicitly noting its inherent toxicity if not properly prepared and the need for more clinical validation. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2024.118507)
- Rai, Rai, Aamir, Tripathi & Rai (2020), *Journal of Biotechnology*, demonstrated that salicylic acid and nitric oxide enhance high-temperature (summer-heat) stress tolerance in *Lablab purpureus* by upregulating enzymatic antioxidants and heat-shock-factor-mediated transcription — a plant-physiology correlate of the bean's deep cultural association with thriving in, and remedying, hot-season stress. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiotec.2020.01.001)

**Evidence grade:** *Low-to-moderate.* A recent systematic review consolidates real phytochemical and preclinical pharmacological data supporting the digestive, metabolic and hepatoprotective traditions, but the review itself flags insufficient clinical data and the need for more rigorous in-vivo and human studies. The toxicity-requires-preparation rule is well established. The traditional "steadies the summer-stressed centre" reading is supported at the traditional and preclinical levels, not by controlled clinical trials.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM (*bai bian dou*):** Sweet, slightly warm/neutral; strengthens Spleen (*jian-pi*), transforms Damp (*hua-shi*), resolves summer-heat-damp (*shu-shi*); Spleen and Stomach channels.
- **Ayurveda / Siddha:** Sweet (*madhura*) *rasa*, gently *ushna*, heavy (*guru*); *kapha*/*vata*-modulating, nourishing (*balya*), somewhat *ama*-forming raw; requires cooking.
- **Galenic / Unani:** Temperate, mildly warming and drying; nutritive pulse, digestion-supporting.

### Cultivation Register

**Ebers Papyrus / Egyptian (EP).** No cultivation framing is attested, and no attestation of this bean at all was retrieved from the Egyptian material. Two structural cautions apply. The corpus is **indication-organised** — it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's plural constitution of *ka*, *ba*, *akh* and *ib* — so substance identity in it is a modern hypothesis; and *heka*, the operative power in which spell and drug form **one intervention**, is not invoked for any material without a source. Recorded as absent.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (AT).** No cultivation framing is attested. The **blocking warning** in force for the cuneiform corpus governs here — substance identification, deity or demon attribution and tablet reference all require positive evidence, and none was retrieved for *Lablab purpureus*. Recorded as absent, which is the default position for this tradition.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM).** The Unani cultivation idea is the conservation of **innate heat and radical moisture**, the finite vital endowment whose depletion is ageing — so longevity practice here is conservation, not acquisition. *Lablab* enters the tradition as a temperate, nourishing pulse within dietetics, and dietetics is precisely the arena in which that conservation is practised. But the sources retrieved attribute nothing to this bean in those terms: no conservational role, no action on the *ruh*. The status is unattested; the frame is dietary and humoral.

**Shennong / Chinese (TCM).** No *san pin* 三品 grade for *bai bian dou* 白扁豆 is attested in the sources retrieved. Searches returned a grading only from TCM clinic and supplier compilations, which this Codex refuses as authorities, so the grade is recorded as **unattested in the sources retrieved** rather than asserted; the drug carries no cultivation status here, only a therapeutic one. Three points belong on the record. First, the bean appears to be a **post-*Shennong* addition**: the systematic review of *Lablab* Semen Album by Yao, Xia, Tang et al. (2024), *Journal of Ethnopharmacology*, states that lablab was first included in the *Mingyi Bielu* 名醫別錄 during the Northern and Southern Dynasties, initially as *bian dou* 扁豆 — a later text than the *Shennong bencao jing*, which is a further reason no upper-grade reading can be drawn from this tag. Second, **food-grade status is not cultivation status**: that a substance is mild enough to eat daily speaks to its safety, not to an assignment within the project of nourishing life. Third, *yang sheng* 養生 is nowhere attributed to this bean in the sources retrieved, despite its *jian-pi* 健脾 function sitting close to that register — closeness is not the claim.

**Sushruta / Ayurveda (SS).** *Nishpava* is recorded as sweet (*madhura*), gently heating, nourishing and *balya* (strengthening), and as heavy (*guru*) and *ama*-forming if eaten raw or in excess. No *rasayana* and no *vajikarana* status — the two formal Sushrutan cultivation disciplines — is attested for it in the sources retrieved, and no attribution to *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* is made. Nutritive strengthening in the *dravyaguna* sense is a therapeutic and dietetic reading and is not treated here as a constitutional one. The Tamil *Avarai* material retrieved likewise places the pulse in a food-as-medicine framework with mild strengthening and digestive-regulating properties, and carries no cultivation status either.

**Practitioner's note.** Here the cultivator's action is behavioural and almost entirely dietary: cook the bean thoroughly — never raw, dry-fried for medicinal use — and eat it with constancy through hot, humid weather or during convalescence, in food quantities, over time. That is what the traditions actually attesting it do with it. What a practitioner should *not* do is upgrade its gentleness into a cultivation claim: this is a mild, well-tolerated food-drug for a weak and water-logged middle, and no source retrieved says more than that.

### Contraindications

- **NEVER eat raw or undercooked** — raw hyacinth bean contains heat-labile lectins, trypsin inhibitors and (in some cultivars) cyanogenic glycosides; raw/undercooked consumption causes nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and can cause serious poisoning. Always dry-fry or thoroughly cook.
- **Excess intake:** even cooked, the bean is heavy/*ama*-forming in excess and may cause bloating and digestive heaviness in weak digestion.
- **Pregnancy/lactation:** used traditionally as a nutritive food including postpartum, but medicinal (concentrated) preparations should be `[USER: verify]` for pregnancy.
- **Diabetes/lipid medication:** reported hypoglycaemic and hypolipidaemic activity — monitor if combined with glucose- or lipid-lowering drugs.
- **Legume allergy:** as a Fabaceae pulse, a potential allergen in legume-sensitive individuals.
