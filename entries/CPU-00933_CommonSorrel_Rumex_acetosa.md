### Origin & Tradition

*Rumex acetosa* is the sour salad dock — a slender meadow perennial whose arrow-shaped leaves carry an unmistakable lemony bite. That sourness is the whole doctrinal story: across four traditions the plant is read as a **cooling, refreshing refrigerant** for hot, bilious, feverish, or scorbutic states, and the classical eye correctly located its virtue in its taste. It must be distinguished from its blunter cousin **Curly Dock (*R. crispus*, treated separately in this Codex)**: curly dock is the bitter, anthraquinone-rich *laxative/alterative* root drug, whereas common sorrel is primarily the **sour, cooling, vitamin-C-bearing leaf** — same genus, overlapping chemistry, but a different medicinal centre of gravity. The plant's antiscorbutic (scurvy-preventing) reputation made it a valued cool-season green across Europe and Asia long before ascorbic acid was named.

**DMM (Dioscorides / Graeco-Roman tradition, *De Materia Medica*, c. 50–70 CE):** Dioscorides describes the *lápathon* (dock) group including the sour, cultivated and wild sorrels (*oxalís* / *oxylápathon*). The sour docks are recommended as cooling, appetite-restoring, and mildly astringent — eaten as a pot-herb, their decoction used for hot stomach complaints, dysentery, and to check bilious excess. The classical framing is refrigerant-and-binding: cool the heat, tighten the flux.

**CM (Avicenna / Unani tradition, *al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb*, c. 1025 CE):** In Unani medicine the sour docks (*ḥummāḍ*) are classed Cold & Dry and directed at hot, bilious (*ṣafrāwī*) conditions — bilious fevers, excessive thirst, hot palpitation, jaundice-type heat, and as a refrigerant that "cuts" and settles bilious humour. The sour taste is read directly as the marker of its cold, incisive quality; the seeds are noted as more astringent (anti-diarrhoeal). This is a textbook Galenic mapping of *sourness → coldness → correction of hot bile*.

**SBJ (Shennong / Chinese tradition, *Shénnóng Běncǎo Jīng* lineage and later materia medica):** Chinese medicine knows *suān mó* (酸模) as a cool, sour–bitter herb that clears heat, cools the blood, promotes urination, and is applied (root and leaf) to heat conditions, certain skin/itch complaints, and constipation — the last reflecting the genus's anthraquinone content. Its energetic slot is *qīng rè liáng xuè* (clear heat, cool the blood), the Chinese analogue of the Graeco-Unani refrigerant reading.

**AT (Assyrian / Mesopotamian cuneiform medicine):** The Mesopotamian medical corpus records sour and dock-type pot-herbs among cooling and digestive plants, and *Rumex* species grew across the region as edible weeds. Secure identification of *R. acetosa* specifically within the cuneiform plant lists is not possible from the surviving lexical material, and no secure Akkadian binomial should be asserted [USER: verify against a current Assyrian materia medica concordance before citing any specific tablet]. What is defensible is the general Mesopotamian use of sour/dock cooling greens.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The doctrine is *sourness = coldness = refrigerant correction of hot bile*, and here the ancient taste-physiology maps onto chemistry with rare cleanliness. The **sour taste is literally organic acid** — chiefly **oxalic acid** and its salts (the oxalate that gives the genus and the species their names: *Oxalis*, *acetosa* from *acetum*/vinegar), together with malate and ascorbate. So when the Galenic and Chinese physicians read the sour bite as the signature of a cooling, "cutting," thirst-quenching virtue, they were reading a real acid load on the palate; the "refreshing coolness" is the astringent-acidic sensation of organic acids plus the genuine tissue benefit of **vitamin C (ascorbic acid)** in a pre-industrial diet. The **antiscorbutic** reputation is the doctrine's strongest vindication: sorrel really does supply ascorbate, and scurvy really is ascorbate deficiency, so "the cooling green that cures the mouth-rot and lassitude of the sick" is, in modern terms, a correct empirical observation of vitamin-C repletion. A second chemical thread is the genus's **anthraquinones and tannins** — the same emodin/chrysophanol-type compounds and condensed tannins that make curly dock a laxative-astringent; in sorrel these underwrite the mild binding (tannin astringency) and the occasional laxative/blood-cooling uses, and they carry real antioxidant and antibacterial activity. Where the doctrine must be **deflated**: the very acid that "proves" the cooling virtue is also the entry's chief hazard. Oxalic acid is a nephrotoxic, calcium-binding anti-nutrient; the "harmless cooling salad" is, in quantity, a genuine oxalate load with kidney-stone and gout implications. The tradition celebrated the sourness; modern pharmacology respects it and fears it in the same molecule.

### Active Components

- **Oxalic acid and soluble oxalates (potassium/calcium oxalate)** — The dominant sour principle; source of the lemony taste, the "cooling" astringent sensation, and the **principal safety hazard** (renal oxalate load, calcium binding). The doctrinal and the toxicological centre of the plant are the *same molecule*.
- **Ascorbic acid (vitamin C)** — The basis of the antiscorbutic reputation; genuinely present in the fresh leaf and the real correction of the "scurvy" indication.
- **Additional organic acids (malic, and others)** — Contribute to the tart, refrigerant taste profile.
- **Anthraquinones (emodin, chrysophanol and relatives)** — Genus-characteristic Polygonaceae constituents (more concentrated in root); underwrite mild laxative and antibacterial/antioxidant activity, milder here than in *R. crispus*.
- **Flavonoids, tannins, and phenolic acids** — Astringent and antioxidant fraction (the genus contains flavonoids, naphthalenes, stilbenes and tannins); basis of the mild binding action and free-radical scavenging.
- **Note:** iron content of sorrel is often folklorically overstated; the high oxalate substantially **impairs** absorption of the iron and calcium the leaf contains — an anti-nutrient effect that undercuts the "iron tonic" folk claim.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. `[RESET]` **Cooling, thirst-quenching refresher in hot/over-heated states.** A small sorrel-leaf garnish or brief infusion as a refrigerant, appetite-sharpening green during hot-weather training or "bilious," over-heated, dry-mouthed conditions — the classical refrigerant use, in *culinary* quantity.
2. `[CYCLE-OFF]` **Micronutrient-dense cool-season green.** As a vitamin-C-bearing spring/autumn leaf, sorrel supports the antiscorbutic/repletion role in a whole-food diet during recovery phases — again in food, not supplement, amounts.
3. `[RESET]` **Mild astringent for loose, hot digestive states.** The tannin/organic-acid astringency underlies traditional use for mild bilious flux — low-dose, short-term, and never in anyone with oxalate/stone risk.

**Hard scope note:** sorrel's role in Kenetica is *culinary-adjacent*, not a dosed botanical medicine. The oxalate hazard means it is the rare Codex plant where the correct instruction is "eat modestly as a food," not "prepare as a remedy."

### Scientific Correlation

The honest grades: **antiscorbutic (vitamin C) — HIGH / mechanistically secure**; **antioxidant-antibacterial (phenolics/anthraquinones) — MODERATE, mostly in-vitro**; **oxalate renal/gout hazard — HIGH-confidence, well-established**; specific clinical trials of *R. acetosa* as a medicine — sparse.

According to PubMed, the genus-level phytochemistry and pharmacology are reviewed by Li JJ, Li YX, Li N, Zhu HT, Wang D, Zhang YJ (2022), "The genus *Rumex* (Polygonaceae): an ethnobotanical, phytochemical and pharmacological review," *Natural Products and Bioprospecting* 12(1):21 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13659-022-00346-z)). It documents that *Rumex* species (the "sorrels" and "docks") contain anthraquinones, flavonoids, naphthalenes, stilbenes and tannins, and that extracts and isolates show antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antitumour and other activities — the chemical basis for the mild astringent, blood-cooling, and antibacterial reputations. This supports the *anthraquinone/tannin* half of the doctrine at the level of real chemistry and largely preclinical bioactivity, while stopping short of validating specific classical disease claims.

The **antiscorbutic** claim — the doctrine's strongest — is directly supported by ascorbate quantification. Manoj P, Kumar SS, Giridhar P (2019), "In vitro shoot multiplication … and quantification of ascorbic acid and major phenolics …," *3 Biotech* 9(10):353 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13205-019-1877-3)), measured substantial **ascorbic acid** (on the order of ~80–90 mg/100 g fresh weight in leaf material) alongside a rich phenolic profile (chlorogenic, ferulic and related acids). Vitamin C at that order of magnitude is a genuine antiscorbutic dose in dietary context — a clean modern vindication of the "cures the scurvy of the cooling green" tradition.

The **oxalate hazard** — the necessary deflation — is well grounded. Mitchell T, Kumar P, Reddy T, Wood KD, Knight J, Assimos DG, Holmes RP (2018), "Dietary oxalate and kidney stone formation," *Am J Physiol Renal Physiol* 316(3):F409–F413 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajprenal.00373.2018)), establishes that dietary (plant-derived) oxalate meaningfully contributes to urinary oxalate and calcium-oxalate stone formation, that urinary oxalate is a continuous stone-risk variable, and that patients benefit from limiting oxalate-rich foods, maintaining hydration, and co-ingesting adequate calcium (to bind oxalate in the gut). Siener R (2021), "Nutrition and Kidney Stone Disease," *Nutrients* 13(6):1917 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu13061917)), reinforces oxalate (with fluid, protein, sodium) as a modifiable dietary determinant of stone risk. Sorrel is a recognised high-oxalate green; the classical "harmless refreshing salad" therefore carries a real, evidence-based renal caveat — and the same oxalate impairs the leaf's iron/calcium bioavailability, deflating the folk "iron tonic" reading. The net honest picture: the ancients were **right** that sourness marked the active virtue (organic acids + ascorbate) and right about the refrigerant/antiscorbutic benefit — and unaware that the marker molecule is also the dose-limiting toxin.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** *Ḥummāḍ* — Cold & Dry (second degree); refrigerant, astringent, corrective of hot bile (*ṣafrā*); seeds more binding. **Attested** (Avicenna, Dioscorides).
- **TCM:** *Suān mó* — cool (*liáng*); sour–bitter (*suān kǔ*); clears heat, cools the blood, promotes urination, mild laxative (*qīng rè liáng xuè*). **Attested.**
- **Ayurvedic:** *Rumex acetosa* is not a principal classical Ayurvedic dravya; sour docks appear folklorically but a canonical *rasa/guṇa/vīrya/vipāka* monograph for this species is **not attested** and is not invented here (predominant *amla rasa*/sour taste is self-evident but not asserted as a classical citation).

### Contraindications

- **CRITICAL — oxalate load: kidney stones, gout, and renal impairment.** *R. acetosa* is a high-oxalate plant. Contraindicated (or strictly limited to small culinary amounts) in anyone with a history of calcium-oxalate kidney stones, hyperoxaluria, gout/hyperuricaemia, or chronic kidney disease. Avoid concentrated juices/extracts entirely. Maintain hydration and pair with dietary calcium to bind oxalate.
- **Acute oxalate toxicity at extreme intake.** Very large ingestions of oxalate-rich sorrel can, rarely, cause serious oxalate poisoning (hypocalcaemia, renal injury); this is a food-quantity, not remedy-quantity, hazard but must be respected — do not use as a bulk green.
- **Anti-nutrient effect.** Oxalate binds calcium and iron; do not rely on sorrel as an iron or calcium source, and be cautious in those with osteoporosis risk or on marginal calcium intake.
- **Distinguish from Curly Dock (*R. crispus*).** Sorrel is the sour vitamin-C leaf; curly dock is the bitter anthraquinone-laxative root. Do not transfer laxative dosing to sorrel or refrigerant expectations to curly dock.
