# Vitex agnus-castus — Agnos, agnus, lygos (Dioscorides 1-135); "Agnus Castus, Hemp Tree, Chaste-tree"

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk lists the plant under this name (the name indexes in `Sources/*/extract/names.csv` and the list files in `_sources/` were searched). Two other rows contain the letters *Vitex* and are not this plant: `Sources/SS_Sushruta/extract/names.csv`, lines 604 and 781, give *Vitex negundo* from an appendix of Sushruta, and they are a different species and a different text. The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *1-135. AGNOS* at line 4521, SUGGESTED line at 4522, and the text at line 4523. The text is cut by a page break (page 137, line 4524) and a running header ("Gums from Trees", line 4526), and resumes at line 4527 ("travels) holds a rod of it in his hand ..."). The chapter stands in Book One, in the section "Gums from Trees" (from page 78), after *1-134. AMORGE* (line 4514) and before *1-136. ITEA* (line 4528). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 137, and the translators' index prints "Vitex agnus-castus 137" (line 18071) and "Chaste-tree 137" (line 15279). A shorter index prints "Vitex agnus-castus 127" (line 11942), which does not agree with the page markers; the Codex notes it and does not use it.

The whole chapter reads: "Agnus [vitex] is a shrub almost as high as a tree bearing long sprigs, growing near and in watery fields and in rough and uneven places. It is hard to break and the leaves are similar to those of the olive tree yet more tender and bigger. Some of them bear white flowers inclining to purple, others purple flowers; and the seed is like that of pepper. It is warming and astringent and the fruit (taken as a drink) helps those stung by snakes, the splenetic, and those with dropsy. A teaspoonful (taken as a drink in wine) brings down milk [breastfeeding] and expels the menstrual flow. It destroys generation [birth control] and is rubbed on the head bringing on a deep sleep. A decoction of the herb and seed is good as a hip bath for disorders and inflammation around the womb. The seed (taken in a drink with pulegium [3-36], or the smoke inhaled or applied) causes purgation. It dissolves headaches applied as a poultice, and is gently poured on the lethargic and mentally ill with vinegar and oil. The leaves either smoked and inhaled or scattered around drive away venomous creatures, and applied as a poultice they help those stung by such beasts. Applied with butter and vine leaves they soothe hardness of the testes [old English: ovaries]. The seeds smeared on with water lessen cracks in the perineum, and with the leaves it heals dislocated joints and wounds. It is thought to be a preventative for chafing and blisters if anyone (as he travels) holds a rod of it in his hand. It is called agnus because in the sacrifices to Ceres the chaste matrons used it for sprinkling under them; and it is called lygos (that is, vimen) because of the difficulty of breaking the stems. It is also called agonon (as we should say, unfruitful or barren), or lygon, a sort of vimen [producing long flexible shoots], amictomiaenon, or tridactylon; the Magi call it semnon, a sort of venerandum [to be respected]; it is also called sanguis ibis, the Egyptians call it sum, the Romans, salix marina, others call it piper agreste, and some, ligusticum."

The chapter has four parts. The first is the description: a shrub "almost as high as a tree", with long sprigs, in "watery fields" and rough ground, "hard to break", with olive-like leaves that are "more tender and bigger", white-to-purple or purple flowers, and a seed "like that of pepper". The second is the quality and the uses of the fruit and seed: "warming and astringent"; the fruit in a drink helps snake-stung, the splenetic and those with dropsy; "a teaspoonful (taken as a drink in wine) brings down milk and expels the menstrual flow"; it "destroys generation"; rubbed on the head it brings "a deep sleep"; the seed in a drink with pulegium, or its smoke, "causes purgation". The third is the uses of the herb, the leaves and the seeds: a decoction of the herb and seed in a hip bath "for disorders and inflammation around the womb"; a headache poultice; a vinegar-and-oil pouring "on the lethargic and mentally ill"; smoked or scattered leaves that "drive away venomous creatures"; a pack with butter and vine leaves for hardness of the testes; seeds with water for cracks in the perineum; and the herb with the leaves for dislocated joints and wounds. The fourth is a short passage on the name and on a belief: "It is thought to be a preventative for chafing and blisters if anyone (as he travels) holds a rod of it in his hand", then the explanation of the name *agnus* (the chaste matrons used it "in the sacrifices to Ceres" for sprinkling under them) and of *lygos* ("because of the difficulty of breaking the stems"), and the other names. The names printed include *agonon* ("unfruitful or barren"), *lygon*, *amictomiaenon*, *tridactylon*, the "Magi" name *semnon* ("to be respected"), *sanguis ibis*, the Egyptian *sum*, the Roman *salix marina*, *piper agreste* and *ligusticum*. The foreign names are names that Dioscorides records. They are not texts of those peoples.

**Overlap.** A chapter-level search of the entries folder for 1-135, the Greek heading, *Agnos*, *agnus castus*, *Vitex* and *Chaste-tree* found no entry on this chapter. Three entries touch it by name. I_1001 (*Anagyris foetida*) quotes 3-167, where the leaves and stems of anagyris are said to be "very similar to agnus castus [1-135]" (its lines 14 and 38). I_056 (*Castor oil*) says in its line 13 that the English name "castor oil" is most likely a confusion with *Vitex agnus-castus* (a statement that is not from this chapter). I_1182 (*Salix repens*, chapter 1-136 *ITEA*) is the chapter directly after this one, and its line 12 notes the adjacency. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. It is a general fact that *Vitex agnus-castus* is a shrub of the mint family (Lamiaceae, in older books the verbena family) with aromatic leaves and small fruits. The chapter's "warming and astringent" is Dioscorides' description of an effect and is not a statement of composition. Nothing else is stated here about the composition of the translators' chaste tree. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a long list of uses (milk and the menstrual flow, ending generation, sleep, the womb, headache, "the lethargic and mentally ill", venomous creatures, testes, perineum, joints and wounds, and a rod carried on a journey). These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. Several of them concern the reproductive organs, fertility, pregnancy or mental illness, and the Codex gives no protocol for any of them. The identification is also only a suggestion, so there is no known plant on which to build one.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives many preparations. Internal: the fruit "taken as a drink"; "a teaspoonful (taken as a drink in wine)" for milk and the menstrual flow; the seed in a drink with pulegium for purgation. External: a decoction of the herb and seed as a hip bath; a poultice for headaches; vinegar and oil "gently poured on" the lethargic and mentally ill; the smoke inhaled, or the leaves smoked or scattered; a pack with butter and vine leaves for the testes; seeds smeared on with water for cracks in the perineum; the leaves and seed for dislocated joints and wounds; and a rod of the wood held in the hand while travelling. The only quantity is the one teaspoonful in wine. No dose is given in the sources retrieved for the other preparations. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a habitat (watery fields and rough ground), a recognition set (the near-tree habit, the long sprigs, the stems that are "hard to break", olive-like leaves that are more tender and bigger, the flower colours, the pepper-like seed), and an explanation of two of the plant's names. Two further points are recorded and not endorsed. The first is that the chaste matrons used the plant in the sacrifices to Ceres; the translation names the goddess and the Codex adds nothing to the sentence. The second is that holding a rod of the wood is "thought" to prevent chafing and blisters on a journey; the sentence reports a belief and the author does not vouch for it. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status, and the passage on the name is not a cultivation claim. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is the recognition check from habitat, build, stem and leaf, and the habit of marking a belief as a belief ("It is thought"). The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 1-135 prints no warning, though it says that the plant brings down milk, expels the menstrual flow and "destroys generation", so pregnancy, breastfeeding, fertility and hormonal medicines are clear concerns that the text does not otherwise address. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. The uses for snakebite, dropsy, mental illness and lethargy are not treatments the Codex endorses. The text gives one quantity and no safety information for children or for any medicine taken at the same time. [USER: verify]
