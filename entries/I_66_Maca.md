### Botanical Identity
Low-growing biennial 12–20cm; rosette of lobed leaves; tiny cream-white flowers; the pharmacologically active part is the hypocotyl (the underground turnip-like storage organ, typically 4–8cm diameter, 3–5cm long); colours range from cream-yellow to red-purple to black — traditionally considered different preparations with different properties. Grows exclusively at 4,000–4,500m altitude in the Peruvian and Bolivian Andes — one of the highest altitude cultivated plants on Earth. Has been cultivated continuously for 2,000+ years; now a major Peruvian agricultural export.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 South American Indigenous Traditions (SA)

**Inca agricultural calendar**: Maca was cultivated in the Junín and Pasco regions of Peru at 4,000–4,500m altitude — the only cultivable crop at this altitude in many seasons. Inca warriors were reportedly given maca before battle to increase stamina and strength (though post-battle maca was restricted to protect the enemy women, according to Spanish colonial chronicles — the sexual energy concern already noted by indigenous cultures).

**Traditional applications (Quechua/Andean)**:
- Energy and stamina at high altitude (the primary traditional application)
- Fertility — both livestock and human (documented in colonial-era agricultural records)
- Menopausal symptoms (female herbal tradition)
- Cognitive function ("mental clarity at altitude")
- Aphrodisiac (secondary to fertility and energy applications)

### 2.2 Badianus Manuscript / Mesoamerican (BM — loose classification)

Within the broader pre-Columbian pharmacopoeia tradition (classified here within the same tradition cluster as the Badianus Manuscript for Mesoamerican/South American indigenous medicine).

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Endocannabinoid System (Macamides)

**Macamides**: Inhibit fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH) → increased endogenous anandamide (endocannabinoid) → CB1 and CB2 activation → mood elevation, reduced anxiety, increased motivation. This is the most credible proposed mechanism for the "energy" and "mood" effects of maca — not direct stimulation (maca contains no caffeine or conventional stimulants) but endocannabinoid potentiation.

### 5.2 Reproductive / Sexual

**Macaenes + zinc**: Increase luteinising hormone (LH) pulsatility → increase testosterone (men) and progesterone (women). Multiple mechanisms proposed including hypothalamic-pituitary axis effects, but molecular target not definitively established. Sperm motility and count improvement confirmed in multiple animal studies.

**Anti-SSRI sexual dysfunction**: Multiple RCTs confirm maca reverses SSRI-induced sexual dysfunction — proposed mechanism: upregulation of dopaminergic reward pathways partially suppressed by SSRIs.

### 5.3 Menopausal Symptom Relief

**Not via oestrogen**: Maca is **not oestrogenic** — it does not bind oestrogen receptors. The menopausal symptom relief (hot flushes, mood, libido) is proposed to occur via hypothalamic-pituitary axis normalisation during the perimenopausal transition — restoring LH/FSH pulsatility patterns toward pre-menopausal rhythm. This mechanism distinguishes maca from phytoestrogenic herbs (sage, soy, red clover) and makes it suitable for oestrogen-receptor-positive cancer survivors.

### 5.4 Altitude / Ergogenic

At 4,000–4,500m altitude: the historical Andean application as a high-altitude food. Proposed mechanisms: improved mitochondrial efficiency (macamides), antioxidant protection (vitamin C, glucosinolates), GABA anxiolytic (reduces altitude-associated anxiety).

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Male fertility**: Gonzales (2001, *Asian Journal of Andrology*): Maca (1.5 or 3g/day × 4 months) — significant increase in sperm count and motility vs placebo (RCT, 9 healthy men). Brooks et al. (2008): Maca 2.4g/day significantly increased subjective sexual desire in healthy men at 8 and 12 weeks.

**Menopausal symptoms**: Meissner (2006): Gelatinised maca (Macafem) significantly reduced hot flush frequency and intensity vs placebo over 4 months. Multiple subsequent studies confirm.

**SSRI-induced sexual dysfunction**: Dording et al. (2008, *CNS Neuroscience & Therapeutics*): Maca 3g/day significantly improved libido and sexual function in patients with SSRI-induced sexual dysfunction — clinically important application given widespread SSRI use.

**Cognitive (post-menopause)**: Stojanovska (2015): Maca 3.5g/day vs placebo — significantly improved cognitive function (Trail Making Test) in post-menopausal women.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The crop at the edge of the world**: Maca is cultivated at altitudes where virtually nothing else grows — 4,000–4,500m in the Peruvian Andes, where oxygen partial pressure is roughly 60% of sea-level. The plant that survives and thrives at the physiological edge of plant life was observed to confer similar resilience to the humans who consumed it. Inca populations surviving at these altitudes depended on maca as a staple food — its energy and fertility applications emerged from 2,000 years of daily consumption under extreme physiological stress. The traditional applications are not folk pharmacology invented by wishful thinking; they are careful observation under conditions where the difference between adequate nutrition and death by altitude sickness or reproductive failure was clearly visible.

**Gelatinisation and raw debate**: Traditional Andean consumption is **always cooked** — boiled, baked, fermented into fermented beverages or porridge. Raw maca powder was an invention of the modern supplement industry. The glucosinolates in raw maca hydrolyse to isothiocyanates during storage and digestion; these are potentially goitrogenic at high doses. Traditional cooking (boiling) deactivates the myrosinase enzyme responsible for glucosinolate hydrolysis. Gelatinised maca (starch removed, pre-cooked) is the closest commercial approximation to traditional preparation. The traditional warning against eating maca raw reflects accurate pharmacological observation preserved across 2,000 years.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

| Form | Dose | Application |
|------|------|-------------|
| Gelatinised powder | 1.5–3g/day (in food) | Energy; fertility; menopausal symptoms — preferred form |
| Raw powder | 3–5g/day | Lower efficacy for some; goitrogenic risk at very high doses |
| Standardised extract (macamide content) | 500–1000mg/day | Sexual function; SSRI dysfunction; cognitive |
| Coloured variants | — | Black: most studied for cognitive/fertility; Red: reportedly most oestrogenic; Yellow: standard |

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM (cross-referencing)**: Sweet, slightly bitter; warming. Would classify as: Channels: Kidney (腎), Spleen (脾). Tonifies Yang Jing, builds constitutional essence, warms the Life Gate Fire (*Ming Men*).

**Ayurvedic (cross-referencing)**: Sweet, slightly pungent; warming. *Vājīkaraṇa* (aphrodisiac/reproductive tonic), *Bṛmhaṇa* (building/nourishing), reduces Vata ↓.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Thyroid conditions (hypothyroid)**: Glucosinolates are goitrogenic — use gelatinised form only; monitor thyroid function; separate from iodine supplementation
- **Hormone-sensitive cancers**: Safety profile different from oestrogenic herbs (maca is not oestrogenic) — BUT its effect on LH/FSH pulsatility means it may affect hormonal balance — consult oncologist for hormone-sensitive cancers
- **Pregnancy**: Reproductive stimulant — avoid therapeutic doses during pregnancy
- Very large doses (>5g/day raw): Gastrointestinal discomfort; potential thyroid effects
- Generally very safe at 1.5–3g/day gelatinised
