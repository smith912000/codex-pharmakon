# False hellebore — Li Lu 藜芦 (Shennong bencao jing); aliases Cong Ran 葱苒 (Yang: "flourishing scallion"), and Shan Cong 山葱, "mountain scallion"

## *Veratrum nigrum L., black false hellebore, a tall perennial whose root and rhizome are the Chinese drug li lu; the identification is ours, from the Chinese name and the standard modern identification, and is of medium confidence at species level, since the classic describes no plant (its Wu Pu layer gives a partial description of leaf and root, lost in the digitised text) and other Veratrum species are used under the same name* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** *Veratrum* is a poisonous plant. Eating or applying it has caused severe and fatal poisoning, with vomiting, a slow pulse and a fall in blood pressure. This entry is a historical record of what a classic text said and is not a recommendation to use the plant in any form.

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts on disk, under this Chinese name.** The principal text read is Sun Xingyan's 1799 reconstruction, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt`. Its other edition on disk, Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`), also has the drug, as "Li Lu (Radix Et Rhizoma Veratri)" (line 3343), and is read here as a second witness, attributed to Yang. The row began as an unverified placeholder under that pharmaceutical-Latin name. The European and Near Eastern white hellebore, *Veratrum album*, is treated from other traditions in I_245 (tags EP, AT, DMM, CM, SS), which does not cite the Shennong text; I_039 (*Helleborus niger*) and I_745 and I_761 (other *Helleborus* species) are a different genus.

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** The entry is 藜芦 in the lower classic (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, lower classic, entry 藜芦 *li lu*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 3187). Sun notes under the name that the *Yulan* writes 梨芦 for it (line 3185). The Shennong paragraph reads, as printed: 味辛，寒 ("acrid, cold"); 主蛊毒 ("governs gu poison"), 咳逆 ("counterflow cough"), 泄葱苒; habitat 生山谷 ("grows in mountain valleys"). The clause 泄葱苒 does not read as a complaint: 葱苒 is a name of the plant itself (the *Guangya*, quoted in Sun's note). Yang's English has "diarrhea and dysentery, intestinal afflux, head sores, scabs, and malign sores" and "kills various worms and eliminates dead muscles" where Sun's text has this clause, so the two editions differ in wording, and the Codex cannot say which the classic had. It writes only what Sun's paragraph supports. [USER: verify] The paragraph does not itself print the word "toxic" (有毒); the lower classic's preface (line 2935) says its drugs are "mostly toxic, not to be taken long" (多毒，不可久服).

The Wu Pu layer (《吴普》曰, third century, line 3189) gives aliases 葱葵, 丰芦 and 蕙葵 (the *Yulan* adds 山葱 and 公苒) and the flavours as judged by five authorities, nearly all marked toxic: "Shennong and Lei Gong: acrid, toxic" (the *Yulan* adds that Xuan Huangdi says toxic); "Qi Bo: salty, toxic"; "Li shi: very cold, greatly toxic"; "Bian Que: bitter, toxic, very cold" (神农、雷公：辛，有毒；岐伯：咸，有毒；李氏：大寒，大毒；扁鹊：苦，有毒，大寒). A sentence on the leaf and root ("叶、根小相…") is cut off in the printed text.

The Mingyi bielu layer (line 3189) adds a name beginning 葱 (the next character lost; Sun's note supplies 葱苒), 山葱, the habitat 生太山 ("grows on Mount Tai") and the harvest 三月采根，阴干 ("the root is gathered in the third month and dried in the shade").

Sun Xingyan's note (案, line 3191) quotes older sources, all the commentators' and not the Shennong text's: the *Guangya* says li lu is 葱苒; the *Fanzi jiran* says it comes from Hedong and the yellow-white is good; and the *Erya* has an entry on "mountain scallion" which Sun doubts is this plant (疑非此).

Yang 1998 (line 3343) renders the paragraph as "Li Lu (Radix Et Rhizoma Veratri) is acrid and cold. It is toxic, treating mainly gu toxins, cough and counterflow, diarrhea and dysentery, intestinal afflux, head sores, scabs, and malign sores. It kills various worms and eliminates dead muscles. Its other name is Cong Ran (Flourishing Scallion). It grows in mountains and valleys." His footnote 206 (line 3356) is commentary, not the classic: "This medicinal is now used only as an emetic. If one is poisoned by it, Chinese Scallion can resolve it."

**Appendix.** An appendix of drug relations, later than the main text, says of it: 藜芦 黄连为使；反细辛、芍药、五参；恶大黄 ("li lu: huang lian is its envoy; it is opposed to xi xin, shao yao and the five shen; it dislikes da huang", line 4437). Li lu is named as the thing a long list of other drugs is "opposed to" or "fears" (for example ren shen, line 4297; sha shen, line 4319; ku shen, line 4379). In the vegetable list the same appendix says 葱实，解藜芦毒, "scallion seed resolves li lu poison" (line 4639). The preface (line 4117) says that drugs standing in "dislike" or "opposed" relations should not be combined. These are historical statements and not an antidote protocol.

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, *Veratrum* species contain steroidal alkaloids (for example jervine and veratridine) which are toxic: they act on nerve and heart tissue and cause vomiting, a slow heartbeat and low blood pressure. In some species (for example the western American corn lily) the alkaloid cyclopamine causes birth defects in grazing animals. Which alkaloids occur in a given root varies with species and plant part. [USER: verify any named constituent before adding it.]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice, and the plant is poisonous. The classic records an acrid, cold root for gu poison and cough, and, in its Wu Pu layer, notes five authorities who call it toxic. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose and recommends no use. The classic's "gu poison" is a historical category and is not translated into a modern diagnosis.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. *Veratrum* species are known poisonous plants. Their alkaloids have been studied as pharmacological tools, and the plants have caused accidental poisonings when mistaken for edible greens or roots. The classic's category of "gu poison" has no modern equivalent. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**SBJ:** flavour and nature 味辛，寒, "acrid, cold" (line 3187). The Wu Pu layer records "acrid, toxic" (Shennong and Lei Gong), "salty, toxic" (Qi Bo), "very cold, greatly toxic" (Li shi) and "bitter, toxic, very cold" (Bian Que) (line 3189); the Codex does not settle the disagreement on flavour, and all five agree that the drug is toxic.

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The paragraph for 藜芦 stands in the lower classic, whose preface says the lower medicines "treat disease, correspond to the earth; mostly toxic, not to be taken long; for those who wish to remove cold and heat evil qi, break accumulations and gatherings, and cure illness" (主治病以应地。多毒，不可久服。欲除寒热邪气，破积聚，愈疾者，本下经; line 2935). The paragraph has no 久服 clause. The register is (b): the lower grade is the opposite of the life-nourishing frame. (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, lower classic, entry 藜芦 *li lu*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 3187)

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. This is a toxic lower-class drug that the classic's own preface says is not for long use, and five authorities in the Wu Pu layer call it toxic. The honest use of the entry is as a record, and as a warning.

### Contraindications

- **Poisonous:** *Veratrum* can cause severe or fatal poisoning (vomiting, slow pulse, low blood pressure, collapse). Do not eat, drink or apply any form of it. Poisoning needs emergency medical care.
- **Mistaken identity:** the plant has been mistaken for edible plants; never gather wild plants of this kind for food or medicine.
- **Pregnancy:** some *Veratrum* alkaloids cause birth defects in animals; the plant must not be used in pregnancy. [USER: verify]
- **Appendix pairings:** the appendix lists many drugs as "opposed to" or fearing li lu (lines 4297, 4301, 4319, 4361, 4377, 4379, 4437). These are historical pairings and not a modern interaction list.
- **Quantities:** no dose is given and none should be inferred.
