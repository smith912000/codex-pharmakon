# Trifolium melilotus-coerulea — Triphullon, trifolium, oxyphyllon (Dioscorides 3-123); "Trefoil"

## *Trifolium melilotus-coerulea [Linnaeus], the fourth name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-123, glossed "Trefoil"; "other usage" lines give Trigonella corniculata and Trigonella eliator ("Wild Trefoil")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk lists the plant under this name (the name indexes in `Sources/*/extract/names.csv` and the list files in `_sources/` were searched). The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line prints five names for the one chapter, and two further species as "other usage". The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *3-123. TRIPHULLON* at line 7639. The SUGGESTED line runs over three lines (7640 to 7642): "Trifolium odoratum, Lotus sativa [Fuchs], Lotus hortensis odora [Bauhin], Trifolium melilotus-coerulea [Linnaeus], Trigonella coerulea [in Sprague] — Trefoil [other usage] Trigonella corniculata, Trigonella eliator — Wild Trefoil". The text is at line 7643, and the page number 508 follows at line 7644. The chapter stands in Book Three, after *3-122. PHALAGGION* (line 7631) and before *3-124. POLION* (line 7655). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 529, and the translators' index prints "Trifolium melilotus-coerulea 508 Trifolium odoratum 508" (lines 17904 to 17909). Woodcut captions from the Fuchs herbal of 1545 stand on the next pages ("Oenanthe, Filipendula" at line 7647 and "Alcea" at line 7651); they are for other chapters.

The whole chapter reads: "Trifolium is a shrub higher than a foot, with slender black stems like onion stalks with branches attached. These have are three leaves on every sprig (like the lotus tree). The smell of them when they emerge is like rue [3-52, 3-53, 4-98], but when grown it is like bitumen. It sends out a purple flower; the seed is somewhat broad and rough with at the one end (as it were), a horn. The root is thin, long and strong. The seeds and leaves (taken as a drink in water) help pleurisy, frequent painful urination, epilepsy, those beginning to have dropsy, and womb congestion. It expels the menstrual flow, but three teaspoonfuls of the seed or four teaspoonfuls of the leaves must be given. A decoction of the leaves (pounded into small pieces and taken as a drink with vinegar and honey) helps those bitten by venomous creatures. Some say that a decoction of the entire shrub with roots and leaves applied with hot cloths to those bitten by snakes soothes the pains, but if someone with an ulcer is applied with hot cloths from the water in which another was healed he feels the same pains as those bitten did. Some give three leaves in drink for fevers with recurrent paroxysms, or three seeds with wine for dissolving the circular flows of acute fevers. The root is also mixed with antidotes. It is also called oxyphyllon, menyanthes, asphaltium, orcnicinum, the Romans call it trifolium, and some, trifolium acutum odoratum." (The words "These have are three leaves" are as printed in the translators' text.)

The chapter has three parts. The description comes first: a shrub "higher than a foot", slender black stems "like onion stalks", three leaves to each sprig, a purple flower, a broad rough seed with a "horn" at one end, and a thin, long, strong root. The second part is use. The seeds and leaves taken in water "help pleurisy, frequent painful urination, epilepsy, those beginning to have dropsy, and womb congestion"; the plant "expels the menstrual flow"; a decoction of the leaves with vinegar and honey helps those bitten by venomous creatures; and "some say" that a decoction of the whole shrub applied with hot cloths soothes the pains of snakebite. The third part is a remark that Dioscorides attributes to others ("Some say", "Some give"): a person with an ulcer who is treated with hot cloths wrung from the water in which another person was healed "feels the same pains as those bitten did", and three leaves in a drink, or three seeds with wine, are given for recurrent fevers. The root "is also mixed with antidotes". The names printed include *oxyphyllon*, *menyanthes*, *asphaltium* and *orcnicinum*, the Roman *trifolium*, and *trifolium acutum odoratum*.

**Overlap.** A chapter-level search of the entries folder for 3-123, the Greek heading, *Triphullon*, *Trifolium melilotus-coerulea* and *Trefoil* found no entry on this chapter. I_1121 (*Lotus tetragonolobus*, chapter 4-111) notes, in its line 18, that the Renaissance name "Lotus sativa [Fuchs]" is printed on the SUGGESTED line of this chapter as well as on its own, and treats the two chapters as separate. I_1126 (*Malva alcea*, chapter 3-164) says that a plate captioned "Alcea from FUCHS — 1545" stands among the plates of the 3-123 to 3-124 run (its line 20). Other entries with *Trifolium* in the file name (red clover, white clover, I_661 and I_709) are different species from other traditions and were not read as the same plant. The name *trifolium* is also printed among the alternative names of 4-18 *MEDION* (line 8179: "the Romans call it trifolium odoratum"), a chapter the translators gloss as a different plant (*Campanula medium*). Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. It is a general fact that *Trifolium* and *Trigonella* are genera of the pea family (Fabaceae). The chapter's smells ("like rue" on emerging, "like bitumen" when grown) are sensory notes and not statements of composition. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the translators' blue melilot or of any other plant on the SUGGESTED line. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a long list of internal and external uses (pleurisy, painful urination, epilepsy, dropsy, womb congestion, the menstrual flow, venomous bites, recurrent fevers). These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. Epilepsy, pleurisy, venomous bites and fevers are conditions that need medical care, and the Codex gives no protocol for them. The identification is also only a suggestion, so there is no known plant on which to build one.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives several preparations. Internal: the seeds and leaves "taken as a drink in water"; a decoction of the leaves, pounded small, "taken as a drink with vinegar and honey"; three leaves in a drink, or three seeds with wine, for recurrent fevers (reported as "Some give"). External: a decoction of the entire shrub, with roots and leaves, applied with hot cloths to snakebite (reported as "Some say"). The root is mixed with antidotes. The chapter gives one dose for the menstrual flow: "three teaspoonfuls of the seed or four teaspoonfuls of the leaves must be given" (line 7643). For every other preparation no dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that pleurisy, epilepsy, dropsy (oedema) and venomous bites need medical assessment and treatment, and that herbs promoted to bring on the menstrual flow are unsafe in pregnancy. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 3-123 prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality and no taste. It prints two smells (like rue when the plant emerges, like bitumen when it is grown).

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a recognition set (height, black slender stems, three leaves to the sprig, purple flower, horned seed, thin strong root) and a smell assay that changes with the age of the plant (rue when it emerges, bitumen when grown). The chapter also keeps two kinds of statement apart, in the way the spec's frame allows: its own description and its own doses, and the reports it attributes to others ("Some say", "Some give"). That separation is a real part of the discipline. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The identification is the translators', and they print several names. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is the smell check at two stages of growth and the habit of marking which statements the author makes himself and which he reports from others. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 3-123 prints no warning, though it records a dose and says the plant "expels the menstrual flow", so pregnancy is an obvious question that the text does not answer. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, and the SUGGESTED line prints several plants, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. The uses for epilepsy, pleurisy, dropsy, venomous bites and fevers are not treatments the Codex endorses, and none of them should wait for a herb. The text gives no safety information for children or for any medicine taken at the same time. [USER: verify]
