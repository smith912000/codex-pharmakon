# Deuteria oinos — DEUTERIAS OINOS (Dioscorides 5-13); alias potimon; related adynamon

## *A weak "secondary wine" made by soaking pressed grape husks in water, boiling the liquid down and salting it, which the translator glosses "Deuteria or Secondary Wine"; the plant behind it is the wine grape, but the chapter names no species, so the identification is ours, from the husks of grapes in the recipe, and is of medium confidence* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry.** Deuteria is named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk. The text read is the English translation of *De Materia Medica* in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, chapter 5-13, DEUTERIAS OINOS, "SUGGESTED: Deuteria or Secondary Wine" (heading at line 9836, text on the lines that follow). The row began on 8 November as an unverified placeholder from an extraction residual (key "DEUTERIAS OINOS [DMM]"); this entry replaces it. A search of the other texts on disk was not needed to place it: the chapter stands inside Book 5, on wines, between 5-12 *Omphakites oinos* (Lesbian wine) and 5-14 wine of wild grapes.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The chapter reads, in the translation: "Deuteria which some call potimon is made as follows: into the husks of the grapes out of which you have pressed two hundred and seventy gallons of wine, put in thirty nine gallons of water. Mix it, trample it, strain it out, and boil it down to two thirds. To every four and a half litres of the liquid that is left add two pints of salt, and after winter pour it out into ceramic jars, but [do not] use it after a year, as it grows weak quickly." The bracketed "[do not]" is the translator's insertion and is printed in the text. The use follows: "It is fit for those to whom we are unsure about giving wine and yet feel compelled to satisfy the desire of the sick, and for those who have recovered from a sickness that lasted long."

The chapter then adds a second drink in the same breath: "A wine is made called adynamon [lack of vital powers], with the same strength as deuteria. Mix an equal measure of water and must [grape pulp], boil it gently over a soft fire until the water is used up, and after cooling it pour it out into a pitch glazed jar. Some mix an equal amount of sea water, rain water, honey and must, pour it out into another jar and set it in the sun for forty days. They use it for a year for the same purposes." Adynamon has no heading of its own in the printed text; it is folded into 5-13 and is read here as part of this entry, not as a separate substance.

**Setting in the wine chapters.** The general chapter before it, 5-11 "The Effects of Wines" (the passage that ends just before 5-12, lines near 9830), is where Dioscorides states his rule of use. As printed it says that wines of middle age are best for drinking, that the amount must be gauged "by the person's age, the time of the year, the customs of the drinker, and the quality of the wine", that "all drunkenness (especially that which is continual) is pernicious", and that after wine one must drink water. Deuteria is therefore a drink for the case where ordinary wine is in doubt; the text gives it no virtue beyond that.

### Active Components

The chapter names no constituent. In general terms, the husks (pomace) left after pressing grapes still carry sugars, acids and the colouring and tannin compounds of the skin, and soaking them in water gives a thin, weakly sweet extract. If any fermentation takes place, a low alcohol content results; whether the ancient drink fermented at all is not stated in the chapter. Salt is added, according to the recipe, in the proportion printed above. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify any named constituent before adding it.]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a recommended practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** A mild substitute for wine for those whom the physician hesitates to give wine, and for convalescents from a long illness (5-13).
- **What the text does not say.** It gives no action on any organ, no indication for a disease and no dose.
- **Why the Codex does not recommend it.** Home-made drinks of unknown alcohol content, kept in jars for a year, are a hygiene risk, and alcohol is a matter of individual medical advice. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparation is the one printed in 5-13 and quoted above: husks of grapes from which 270 gallons of wine were pressed, 39 gallons of water, trampling and straining, boiling down to two thirds, then two pints of salt to each "four and a half litres" of the liquid, stored in ceramic jars after winter and not used after a year because it "grows weak quickly". The units are those of the English translation; the Greek measures are not printed in the text on disk and the conversion is the translator's. [USER: verify] The adynamon variants are: equal water and must boiled down until the water is gone and stored in a pitch-glazed jar; or equal parts of sea water, rain water, honey and must set in the sun for forty days and used within a year. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Grape pomace is the solid residue of pressing and contains skins, seeds and stems; water extracts of it contain sugars, organic acids and polyphenols in small amounts. Boiling concentrates such an extract, and salt acts as a preservative at high concentration. Alcoholic drinks of any strength carry the known health risks of alcohol. ### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. Chapter 5-13 records a method and a use: it is a recipe for a cheap, weak drink that keeps for a year and is given to the sick and to the convalescent. It assigns no cultivation status and no life-nourishing property. What the chapter does offer a practitioner is the discipline of attention that runs through Book 5: the quantity of husk to water, the point at which the boiling stops, the time after which the drink is no longer to be used, and the observation that it loses strength quickly. That is a practice of provenance and of time, not a cultivation claim.

**Practitioner's note.** A plain household preparation for the weak and the convalescent, and nothing more is attested. A cultivator has no use for it as a practice; the nearest lesson in the text is Dioscorides' own: moderation, and water after wine.

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

Little to map: the entry states that deuteria, a weak secondary wine made from pressed grape husks, boiled down and salted, is for those to whom wine is doubtful, and for those who have recovered from a long sickness, to satisfy the desire of the sick. It states no action on any organ, tissue or system, no indication for a disease and no quality beyond its being weak. Dioscorides' rule is that it should not be used after a year because it grows weak quickly. The adynamon variant is folded into the same chapter, made from equal water and must boiled down, or from a mixture of sea water, rain water, honey and must sunned for forty days, and the text says it has the same strength. No humoral or elemental classification is printed. The chapter is cited here for its method and its use, and no organ mapping is given because the text supplies none. No dose is given.

### Contraindications

- **Alcohol.** Any fermented drink carries the risks of alcohol. Do not give it to children, in pregnancy or lactation, to those who should not drink, or before driving. The text itself warns that drunkenness is "pernicious" (5-11).
- **Spoilage.** A home-made drink stored for months can spoil. The text says the drink weakens within a year; the Codex adds that spoiled liquid should not be taken. [USER: verify]
- **Salt.** The recipe adds a large amount of salt; this matters for anyone on a salt-restricted diet.
- **Identity.** The grape is our reading from "husks of the grapes"; the species and variety are not named by the text.
