# Prishata flesh — Prishata (Prishata deer), venison of the Janghala group, Sushruta Samhita
## *The flesh of the animal Bhishagratna prints as "Prishata"; in Kalpa ch.1 he glosses it "a species of spotted deer", his Vol. 3 glossary reads "The porcine deer (?)", and the spine list flags the identity as uncertain* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The Prishata is named in the "Meat Group" of *Sutrasthana* ch.46, the chapter on food and drink (*Annapana-Vidhi-madhyaya*), in Bhishagratna's English (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt`; `_sources/sushruta.csv` line 150 lists "Prishata (spotted deer)", source "Sutrasthana ch.46 (Annapanavidhi); Bhishagratna trans. vol.1", flagged id_uncertain). The chapter says the flesh of animals that are aquatic, marsh-dwelling, village-dwelling, carnivorous, unbifurcated-hoofed or high-ground "is generally used as food", each succeeding kind superior to the one before it (lines 385-394), so the high-ground (*Jangala*) animals come last and highest. The *Jangala* animals fall into eight sub-groups, the first being the *Janghala* (lines 403-410). Its members are listed: "the Ena (black deer), the Harina (red deer), the Rishya (blue deer), the Kuranga (antelope), the Karala, the Kritamala, the Sharabha, the Shvadanstra, the Prishata, the Chitrila (Spotted deer), the Charushka, the Mriga-matrika, etc." (lines 413-426). The group text is: "These species of venison have a sweet and astringent taste, are light, keen, pleasant (palatable), laxative, and diuretic in their effect. They subdue the Vayu and the Pittam" (lines 426-430). The chapter then takes Ena, Harina, Kuranga and Mriga-matrika singly (lines 431-447) but gives nothing by name for the Prishata.

Other places. *Uttarasthana* ch.39 (fever): "Some authorities are of opinion that Ena, Prishata, S'arabha, Kala-puchchha, Kuranga, Mriga-matrika (different kinds of deer) or S'as'a (hare) may be prescribed as diet for a fever-patient accustomed to the use of animal food" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/uttara/SS_Uttara_ch39.txt` lines 904-908). *Kalpasthana* ch.1 (toxicology), in the signs by which poisoned food for a king is detected, has "A Prishata (a species of spotted deer) sheds tears and a monkey passes stools" (`SS_Kalpa_ch01.txt` line 180). The same chapter lists, among a king's wholesome cordials, "the meat and soup of the flesh of a peacock, mungoose, Godhi (a species of lizard), or Prishata deer" (lines 447-449). Parts of the animal other than flesh also occur: the bile of the Prishata among the biles in the antivenom called *Rishabhagada* (`SS_Kalpa_ch05.txt` line 419), and in oils cooked with the biles of several animals "a deer of the Prishata class" (`SS_Uttara_ch61.txt` line 122, in the chapter on *Apasmara*). These are separate materials and are not treated here.

**Identity.** Bhishagratna's English is not consistent. In Sutra ch.46 the parenthesis "(Spotted deer)" follows the next name, Chitrila (line 421), not Prishata; in Kalpa ch.1 it follows Prishata ("a species of spotted deer", line 180); and the Vol. 3 glossary reads "Prishata — The porcine deer (?); birds that roam together" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt` line 30862). The spine list gives "spotted deer" for both Prishata and Chitrila (`_sources/sushruta.csv` lines 150-151). Which deer is meant cannot be settled from the lines read. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Venison is muscle meat: protein, with fat that varies with the animal, and iron and B vitamins as in other red meats. Wild game can carry parasites, and a wounded or diseased animal is a poorer food than a healthy one. No analysis specific to this entry was read, and the animal's identity is not settled. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records Prishata venison as a food in the best-ranked class of flesh, as one of the meats some authorities allowed a fever patient who already ate meat, and as part of a king's poison-protective diet. Nothing in the sources supports taking it as a medicine, and the animal's identity is uncertain. The Codex does not recommend the flesh for any complaint and gives no protocol. Anyone who hunts or eats game follows local law and food-safety practice, which the text does not address.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation given for the Prishata by name is the soup for a king in *Kalpasthana* ch.1: the flesh of a Godha, mungoose or deer "should be cooked and spiced with pasted Palindi (Trivrit), Yashtimadhu and sugar", the peacock with sugar, Ativisha and Sunthi, "and that of a Prishata deer with Pippali and Sunthi" (`SS_Kalpa_ch01.txt` lines 451-466). The text adds that the soup of Simhi taken with honey and clarified butter is similarly beneficial as an antitoxic (same passage). In the after-drinks of Sutra ch.46, "The Asava (wine) of the Pippali should be used after a meal of the long thighed venisons" (lines 2835-2837). No quantity is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Venison is a lean red meat and must be cooked through; game animals are known carriers of foodborne parasites and bacteria. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Sushruta (SS).** Printed in *Sutrasthana* ch.46 for the *Janghala* group as a whole (lines 426-430): sweet and astringent in taste; light, keen, pleasant, laxative and diuretic; subduing *Vayu* and *Pittam*. The individual properties the chapter prints belong to Ena, Harina, Kuranga and Mriga-matrika (lines 431-447); none is printed for the Prishata by name. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The Prishata is named in the food-and-drink chapter, whose opening lines tie food as a class to bodily strength, complexion and the *ojas* principle (ch.46, lines 9-12). That is a claim about food in general, not about this flesh. The chapter ranks the high-ground animals highest among the classes of flesh (lines 385-394), a ranking of foods and not a cultivation grade. In the lines read the Prishata appears in a fever diet, in a toxicology chapter as an animal that shows distress at poisoned food, and in a king's protective diet; none is a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* passage. No *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* claim is made for it. *Sattvic*, *rajasic* and *tamasic* food lists are *Samkhya*-era material, not Sushruta, and are not used.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice. In Sushruta this is one of the deer whose flesh was ordinary good food, with a place in a fever diet and in a king's guarded table. A person who eats game may read the group classification as the tradition's description of the food; a person who does not has nothing to adopt.

### Contraindications

- **Identity:** the animal is not settled (spotted deer, "porcine deer (?)", or a deer unglossed in ch.46); do not substitute a species on this basis. [USER: verify]
- **Food safety:** wild game can carry parasites and bacteria; cook through, and do not eat the flesh of a sick animal.
- **The text's own cautions:** the fever diet is allowed only for a patient already accustomed to animal food, and by "some authorities" (Uttara ch.39). The group properties in ch.46 describe the food; they are not a safety clearance.
- **Legal and conservation status:** hunting and trade in deer are regulated in most countries, and several deer species are protected. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics and religion:** killing an animal for food is a moral question for many traditions, and meat is restricted in some; this entry takes no position beyond recording the text.
