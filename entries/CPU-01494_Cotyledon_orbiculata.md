# Cotyledon orbiculata — Kotuledon eteron (Dioscorides 4-93)

## *Cotyledon orbiculata (the translators' first suggestion; "Cotyledon"), with Cotyledon barbeyi also on the line* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The line reads "Cotyledon orbiculata, Cotyledon barbeyi — Cotyledon", and the row takes the first name. The translators' index lists *Cotyledon orbiculata* at page 644. **Identity doubt:** *Cotyledon orbiculata* is generally known as a southern African succulent, so it looks an unlikely match for a plant described by Dioscorides. The entry records it only because the translators printed it. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-93. KOTULEDON ETERON* at line 8868, SUGGESTED line at 8869, text at line 8870. The Greek heading reads, as printed, "kotuledon eteron", which the text itself renders as "another kind of cotyledon".

The whole chapter reads: "There is also another kind of cotyledon with broader, fat leaves similar to little tongues, thick around the root, describing (as it were) an eye in the middle, similar to the bigger sempervivum [4-89], astringent to the taste. It has a thin little stalk, and on it flowers and seeds similar to hypericum [3-171], but the root is bigger. It is good for the same uses as sempervivum. It is also called cymbalium."

So the chapter is a description and a cross-reference. The plant has broad, fat, tongue-like leaves thick around the root, with an "eye" in the middle like that of the bigger sempervivum (chapter 4-89), a taste that is astringent, a thin stalk bearing flowers and seeds like those of hypericum (chapter 3-171, which was not read for this entry) and a bigger root than the first cotyledon. It gives no use of its own. It says it is "good for the same uses as sempervivum" and does not say which sempervivum, since Book Four has three (4-89, 4-90 and 4-91). The one name printed is *cymbalium*, which chapter 4-92 also prints among its names.

*The uses by reference.* If the reference is to the bigger sempervivum (4-89, line 8827), which the description itself invokes, those uses are as follows (lines 8827 to 8835). The leaves, "applied by themselves or with polenta", are for erysipelas, herpes, gangrenous ulceration of the cheeks, inflammation of the eyes, burns and gout in the feet. The juice, poured on with polenta and rosaceum, is for headaches, and given in drink to those bitten by harvest spiders, those with diarrhoea and for dysentery. Taken with wine it "draws out roundworms", and in a pessary "it stops women's excessive discharges". The chapter calls the plant "cooling and astringent". Chapter 4-90 is said to have "the same strength as the previous one", and 4-91, the third kind, is "warming, sharp, and ulcerating". This chain of references is the reader's, not the text's, and the entry does not assign these uses to 4-93 as attested. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** The preceding chapter, 4-92 *KOTULEDON*, has its own row, CPU-01493 *Cotyledon lusitanica*. The sempervivum chapters have rows CPU-01624 (4-89, *Sempervivum tectorum*), CPU-01623 (4-90, *Sedum rupestre*) and CPU-01622 (4-91, *Sedum acre*). None is merged here.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. The genus *Cotyledon* belongs to the stonecrop family (Crassulaceae), whose members are succulent, and nothing is stated here about its composition. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Chapter 4-93 itself records only a description and a taste and refers its uses to sempervivum. The Codex gives no protocol and does not carry the sempervivum uses over as 4-93's own. The identification is uncertain.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-93 gives no preparation. It says only that the plant is "good for the same uses as sempervivum". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints "astringent to the taste" and no warming, cooling or drying quality for 4-93.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter is a lesson in the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: it identifies a plant by comparison with two others (sempervivum and hypericum), notes what differs (broader leaves, a bigger root) and states a taste. It assigns the plant no status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a recognition note that points to another remedy, so it is history and not a practice.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning and no use of its own. The remedies in the chapter it refers to include a pessary, a worm remedy and applications for ulceration, and these should not be self-treated. The identification is uncertain, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
