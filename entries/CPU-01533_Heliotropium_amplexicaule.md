# Heliotropium amplexicaule — Eliotropion mikron, the little heliotropium (Dioscorides 4-194)

## *Heliotropium amplexicaule (the first of two suggestions on the translators' SUGGESTED line: "Heliotropium amplexicaule — Blue Heliotrope", then "Heliotropium ciliatum — White Heliotrope")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk names this plant. The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain; the translators offer a second species, and the text itself gives few features. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-194. ELIOTROPION MIKRON* at line 9745, SUGGESTED line at 9746, text at line 9747. The chapter stands in Book Four, "Other Herbs & Roots", after 4-193 *ELIOTROPION MEGA* (the great heliotropium, the subject of the sister entry I_1098) and before 4-195 *SKORPIOIDES*. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 772.

The whole chapter reads: "The little heliotropium grows in marshy places and near lakes, with leaves like that mentioned above, only rounder; with round seed hanging like hanging warts. A decoction of the herb and seed (taken as a drink with saltpetre [potassium nitrate], hyssop [3-30], nasturtium [2-185], and water) is able to drive out broadworms and roundworms. Smeared on with salt it takes away hanging warts."

So the chapter is brief. It compares the leaves with those of the great heliotropium (4-193, "rounder"), describes the seed as round and "hanging like hanging warts", gives a habitat (marshy places and near lakes), and records two uses: a compound drink against worms and a salt smear against warts. It prints no other names. The cross-references to hyssop (3-30) and nasturtium (2-185) are the translators' and were not followed. The bracketed gloss "[potassium nitrate]" is theirs.

The sister entry I_1098 rests on chapter 4-193 and is a separate entry; the two chapters are not merged here, and the description of leaves, flowers and root in 4-193 is not read into this one.

### Active Components

No analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. The genus *Heliotropium* is generally known to contain pyrrolizidine alkaloids, which are toxic to the liver, but whether the plant of this chapter is a *Heliotropium* is only the translators' suggestion. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a compound drink to expel worms and a salt smear for warts. The Codex gives no protocol for either. The identity is a translators' suggestion, and a plant of a genus generally known to contain liver-toxic alkaloids is not something to take as a drink on the strength of a translated herbal.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives two preparations: a decoction of the herb and seed drunk with saltpetre, hyssop, nasturtium and water, and the herb smeared on with salt. No dose, quantity or duration is printed for either. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a comparison with another plant ("leaves like that mentioned above, only rounder"), a seed described by an image ("hanging like hanging warts") and a habitat (marshy places and near lakes). The chapter is short and assigns no cultivation status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is the habit of describing a plant by comparison with a neighbouring one (the great and the little heliotropium). The text offers nothing more, and the abstentive answer is the right one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 4-194 prints no warning. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Plants of the genus *Heliotropium* are generally known to contain liver-toxic alkaloids, and the drink is not advice. Intestinal worms call for medical treatment. [USER: verify]
