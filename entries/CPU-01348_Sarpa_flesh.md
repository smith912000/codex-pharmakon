# Sarpa flesh — Sarpa (snake), flesh of a hole-dwelling (Vileshaya) animal, Sushruta Samhita
## *The flesh of the animal Bhishagratna prints as "Sarpa", which he glosses only as "a species other than those specifically described"; the spine list reads "snake"; the Darvicara, taken singly in the same passage, is not glossed* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The Sarpa is named in the "Meat Group" of *Sutrasthana* ch.46, the chapter on food and drink (*Annapana-Vidhi-madhyaya*), in Bhishagratna's English (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt`; `_sources/sushruta.csv` line 226 lists "Sarpa (snake)", source "Sutrasthana ch.46 (Annapanavidhi); Bhishagratna trans. vol.1"). The chapter says the flesh of high-ground animals is "generally used as food", each succeeding kind superior to the one before (lines 385-394), and divides the high-ground (*Jangala*) animals into eight sub-groups, the seventh being the *Vileshaya* (lines 403-409). The group is defined: "Animals such as, the Shvavit, Shalyaka, Godha, Shasha, Vrishadansha, Lopaka, Lomasha-Karna, Kadali, Mriga-Priyaka, Ajagara, Sarpa, Mushika, Nakula and Maha-Vabhru belong to the group of Vileshaya (hole-dwellers)" (lines 574-578). The group text: "The general properties of the flesh of animals belonging to this species are to increase the consistency of stool and urine. They are heat-making in potency, sweet in digestion as those of the preceding group. They subdue the Vayu and generate the Pittam and Kapham. They are demulcent and beneficial in cough, dyspnoea and cachexia" (lines 580-587).

The Sarpa is then taken singly: "The flesh of a Sarpa (a species other than those specifically described) is curative in piles and derangements of the Vayu. It is a vermifuge and anti-toxic (a neutraliser of chemical or resulting poisons). It invigorates the eye-sight, is appetising and sweet and improves the intellect. Of these the flesh of the Darvicara is appetising, pungent in digestion, sweet in taste, and extremely efficacious in eye diseases. It is laxative and diuretic, and subdues the deranged Vayu" (lines 604-612). The parenthesis is Bhishagratna's; the Darvicara is named without a gloss in the passage, and the lines read do not say whether it is a kind of Sarpa. The after-drink for the group: "Phalasara wine should be taken after the flesh of any of the hole-dwelling (Vileshaya) animals" (lines 2847-2849).

Other places. The Sarpa is not otherwise named as food in the chapters on disk. Other uses of the snake in Sushruta are different materials: the black ashes of a well-burnt cobra (*Krishna-Sarpa*) pasted with *Vibhitaka* oil for *Svitra* (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/chikitsa/SS_Chikitsa_ch09.txt` lines 270-274), and, in a case of *Dushyodara*, which the text says is to be treated "without giving any hope of a positive cure", a *Krishna-Sarpa* "enraged to bite a sugarcane", the cane then being chewed by the patient (`SS_Chikitsa_ch14.txt` lines 100-112). *Kalpasthana* ch.4 and ch.5 are given to snake venom and the treatment of snake-bite (`SS_Kalpa_ch04.txt`, `SS_Kalpa_ch05.txt` line 6). None of these is a statement about the flesh.

**Identity.** "Sarpa" is the general Sanskrit word for snake and the spine list reads it so. Bhishagratna's gloss in the passage, "a species other than those specifically described", points to no species, and the Vol. 3 glossary has no entry for it among the lines read. The chapter does not say which snakes were eaten. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Snake flesh is muscle meat: protein, with little fat in most species. Snakes and other reptiles can carry *Salmonella* and parasites in the flesh, and a venomous snake presents a risk at the bite and, if the venom glands are not removed, in preparation. No analysis specific to this entry was read, and the identity is not settled. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records snake flesh as a food of the hole-dwelling group, with stated actions on piles, *Vayu* disorders, worms, poison, eyesight and the intellect. Nothing in the sources supports eating or sourcing it. Snakes are handled at the risk of a bite, many species are protected, and the flesh carries bacteria and parasites. The Codex does not recommend snake flesh for any complaint and gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation or dose is given for the Sarpa. The only related instruction is the general after-drink for the *Vileshaya* group: Phalasara wine after the flesh of any hole-dwelling animal (ch.46, lines 2847-2849). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Reptile flesh must be cooked through and handled hygienically; reptiles are recognised carriers of *Salmonella*, and some parasites are passed to people who eat raw or undercooked snake. The Sushruta claims of eyesight, intellect and anti-toxic action are traditional and unverified here. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Sushruta (SS).** Printed in *Sutrasthana* ch.46. For the *Vileshaya* group (lines 580-587): increases the consistency of stool and urine; heat-making in potency; sweet in digestion; subdues *Vayu*; generates *Pittam* and *Kapham*; demulcent; beneficial in cough, dyspnoea and cachexia. For the Sarpa singly (lines 604-612): curative in piles and *Vayu* derangements; vermifuge; anti-toxic; invigorates the eyesight; appetising; sweet; improves the intellect. For the Darvicara, taste sweet, pungent in digestion. The text says the Sarpa is sweet and gives no other taste (*rasa*) of its own. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The Sarpa is named in the food-and-drink chapter, whose opening lines tie food as a class to bodily strength, complexion and the *ojas* principle (ch.46, lines 9-12). That is a claim about food in general, not about this flesh. The one cognitive claim the text makes for the Sarpa, that it "improves the intellect" (line 609), is a property listed for a flesh, not a placement in a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* chapter, and no chapter of that kind read names the snake as food. No *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* claim is made for it. *Sharirasthana* ch.4 names a person-type "Sarpa-Sattva" in the *Rajasika* group of mental temperaments (`SS_Sharira_ch04.txt` lines 601-603); that concerns a human temperament and not the flesh, and the *sattva/rajas/tamas* scheme is *Samkhya* material used beside *dravyaguna*, so it is not applied to the substance.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice. In Sushruta this is one of the hole-dwelling animals, with a short list of stated uses and an unspecified species. A person who does not eat snakes has nothing to adopt, and one who does should treat the text as history and not as a protocol.

### Contraindications

- **Identity:** the snake is unspecified; do not substitute any species on this basis. [USER: verify]
- **Venom and bites:** the same text devotes two chapters to snake venom (Kalpa ch.4, ch.5); handling a live snake is a risk in itself.
- **Food safety:** reptile flesh can carry *Salmonella* and parasites; cook through.
- **The text's own cautions:** the *Vileshaya* group generates *Pittam* and *Kapham* (ch.46, lines 584-585).
- **Legal and conservation status:** many snakes are protected or regulated in many countries. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics and religion:** killing an animal for food is a moral question for many traditions, and meat is restricted in some; this entry takes no position beyond recording the text.
