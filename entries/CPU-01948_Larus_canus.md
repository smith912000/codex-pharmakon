# Larus canus — so.bya, feathers (rGyud-bzhi, Clark's "Heron", with Larus canus kamtschatschensis as one of two Latin names)

## *Feathers of a water bird; Clark prints the Tibetan name so.bya, the English "Heron", and two Latin names (Larus canus kamtschatschensis, the mew or common gull, and Phalacrocorax carbo sinensis, the great cormorant). The row's binomial is the first of the two. The bird that so.bya denotes is not settled by the texts read; the Men-Tsee-Khang translation renders it "cormorant". This row covers the same line as the Heron feather entry (III_105, CPU-01162); identification confidence is medium for "a water bird's feather", low for the gull* | *(Volume III — Animal)*

**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** This is a single-text entry in the sense that only the rGyud-bzhi tradition is attested in the files read, through two English translations. The feather is listed in the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, the chapter on the efficacy of medicinal substances, in Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). It is in section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), whose heading says that "the thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body", and the feather is under "Hair" (line 1192). Line 1194 reads: "so.bya: Heron: *Larus canus Kamtschatschensis*: *Phalacrocorax carbo sinensis*: [has fourteen names]. Its feathers relieve urinary retention [and dispel spirits]." The square brackets mark interpolations taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries (Clark, line 82), so "has fourteen names" and "and dispel spirits" are commentary material, and "relieve urinary retention" is the root-text wording as Clark gives it. The extraction row (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`) records the line, with class "plant", which is an error.

**The two Latin names.** Clark explains in his introduction (line 83) that he gives the Tibetan name first, then an English name, "in almost all cases ... followed by at least one scientific name", and that "often more than one identification is available". The Latin names printed here are not those of a heron: *Larus canus* is a gull, *Phalacrocorax carbo* a cormorant. The Codex's row follows the first of the two. The same Latin pair appears in Clark's list of meats (line 672), where "heron" is among the water creatures. [USER: verify]

**The Men-Tsee-Khang rendering.** The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, lines 3557–3558) has, in the corresponding passage: "A peacock's crown feather cures poisoning and lung abscesses. A cormarant's [sic] feather cures obstruction of urine. Owl feather cures ascites." The OCR prints "cormarant"; the sense is cormorant. So that translation renders the bird as cormorant, with the same indication (obstruction of urine). The same file's list of aquatic animals (line 2919) names "cormorant" and "heron" separately, and its glossary on animals (lines 6842–6934) lists "Heron" and "Cormorant" with *Phalacrocorax carbo*, in a scrambled OCR layout. The Tibetan wording is not in the files read, so which English word is right cannot be decided here. A gull is not named for this feather in the Men-Tsee-Khang text. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** This line is already the subject of `III_105_Heron_feather.md` (CPU-01162), which quotes the same Clark and Men-Tsee-Khang lines. The two rows differ only in the name chosen for the bird: "heron" there, *Larus canus* here. They are very probably one substance, and the editor should decide on a merge; nothing is merged here.

### Active Components

A feather is made of keratin, a tough structural protein rich in cysteine, arranged as a central shaft with barbs. No composition specific to this entry was read, and no preparation is given that would indicate whether the feather was burnt, powdered or used whole. [USER: verify] Wild birds' feathers can also carry feather mites, bacteria and fungi, and, in waterbirds, avian influenza virus.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a recommended practice.** The sourced content is one indication in a materia medica list: the feathers "relieve urinary retention" (Clark) or "cure obstruction of urine" (Men-Tsee-Khang). The Codex gives no protocol. Urinary retention is a medical emergency or a sign of an underlying problem that needs prompt assessment, and no source read supports the claim. Gulls and cormorants are wild birds, protected in many places, and collecting their feathers can be unlawful.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* Neither translation read assigns the feather any cultivation framing. Clark's commentary bracket attaches the feather to "dispel spirits", which is a protective or exorcistic use and not a cultivation status, and it comes from a commentary and not the root-text wording; the Men-Tsee-Khang rendering of the same passage does not carry it. No source read attaches the feather to *bcud len*, and no formal Tibetan category of "substances for subtle-body practice" exists. Clark notes of the whole section that "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57).

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a one-line indication in a list of animal medicines that the translator says are little used now, the identity of the bird is uncertain between translations, and no practice is attached. The "dispel spirits" gloss is a commentary's addition and is recorded, not endorsed.

### Contraindications

- **Urinary retention:** needs prompt medical assessment; do not rely on this line.
- **Infection:** wild-bird feathers can carry bacteria, fungi, mites and avian influenza virus.
- **Legal and conservation status:** gulls and cormorants are protected wild birds in many jurisdictions, and collecting feathers, or the birds, may be an offence. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** killing a wild bird for a substance with no supported benefit is hard to justify.
- **Identity uncertainty:** the bird named is not settled (heron, gull or cormorant, depending on the translation).
