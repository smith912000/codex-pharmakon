# Salted tunny — *Omotarichos* (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 2-33)
## *"Omotarichos is the flesh of the salted tunny"; the translator's identification is "Orcynus thynus — Tunny"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica*, in the 2000 English edition (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`; "text" below). The chapter is 2-33, headed *OMOTARICHOS*, at line 5069 (the spine's locus, `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`: "2-33 (line 5069)", translator's identification "Orcynus thynus — Tunny", class animal). The Greek heading is itself the name of the preparation: the first sentence defines it as "the flesh of the salted tunny" (line 5071), so the material is a salted fish, not the living animal.

The whole chapter, lines 5071-5075 (a page break falls inside it), reads: "Omotarichos is the flesh of the salted tunny. Those bitten by the viper called prester (a serpent, the bite of which was fabled to cause death by swelling) take this. Those who eat it must be compelled to drink a great amount of wine and then to vomit. It is excellent for the same purposes as eating sharp meats. It is also effective applied to the bites of dogs." The bracketed note on the *prester* is the translator's. The chapter gives two uses: eaten for the bite of the *prester* viper, with a forced drink of wine and then vomiting, and applied to dog bites. "The same purposes as eating sharp meats" refers to a remedy set that this chapter does not itself list.

The neighbouring chapters show that salted fish is a small group in Book 2. Chapter 2-30 (*Smaris*, line 5059) says of the smelt: "The flesh of this as well as all salted meat is good for one touched by a scorpion, and for one bitten by a dog." Chapter 2-29 (the sheatfish, line 5051) gives the salted flesh different uses, to draw out splinters. Chapter 2-34 (*Garron*, line 5076) follows, headed "Garum — Liquid from Salt Fish" (line 5077): the liquid that comes out of salted flesh or fish, applied, "heals those bitten by dogs" (line 5078), among other uses. That is the same dog-bite use as 2-33 and 2-30, here for the brine.

No other tradition on the tag line, and no text outside Dioscorides on disk, was found naming the salted tunny. A whole-word search of the Sushruta, Ebers, Tibetan and Assyrian texts on disk for "tunny" and "tuna" returned nothing.

### Active Components

Tunny is an oily fish, and salting preserves it by drawing water out of the flesh. Salted fish is therefore high in sodium chloride and rich in protein. These are general facts about preserved fish. The chapter names no constituent and this entry names none.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The recorded uses are an emergency remedy for a viper bite, a method that includes forced vomiting, and an application to dog bites. Neither is an indication for which salted fish has any support, and snakebite and animal bites need proper medical care. The Codex's application layer has nothing to add, and no protocol is offered.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives the preparation only as the salted flesh itself. For the viper bite the patient takes it and is "compelled to drink a great amount of wine and then to vomit" (lines 5072-5074); for dog bites it is applied (line 5075). No quantity of fish, volume of wine or dose is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The general facts are compositional (above). Salted fish is not an antidote for any venom, and induced vomiting is not an accepted treatment for snakebite.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides.** No quality (warming, astringent and so on) is printed for the salted tunny in chapter 2-33. The only characterisation is "excellent for the same purposes as eating sharp meats" (line 5074); this is a comparison, not a quality, and none is supplied here.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. Chapter 2-33 defines a preserved food, gives it two emergency uses, and ends. It records no provenance, season or assay such as makes the discipline-of-attention frame useful elsewhere in the work, and it assigns no status to the person taking it.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the source is for a cultivator. The remedy is an emergency measure for a bite, and its method (wine, then forced vomiting) is not something to repeat. The one practical lesson of the neighbouring chapters is that Dioscorides treats salted flesh as a distinct material from fresh, which is a small point of attention and not a practice.

### Contraindications

Salted tunny is a preserved food, and a heavy sodium load is unsuitable for anyone told to restrict salt. Forced vomiting after a large drink of wine, as the text prescribes, risks aspiration and alcohol toxicity and must not be copied. A venomous or infected bite needs medical care, not this. Tunny stocks are under pressure in some fisheries and mercury accumulates in large predatory fish; these are general cautions and not claims from the text.
