# Privet — Common Privet, Prim, Print, Wild Privet

## *Ligustrum vulgare* L. | Family: Oleaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry (second degree) — a drying, astringent, binding herb in the Galenic-Unani reckoning.
**Training Phase:** Sealing / Astringent Phase — used where tissue is lax, weeping, or ulcerated and needs to be drawn together, never for the toxic fruit.

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian (AT).** Cuneiform plant lists from the Mesopotamian herbal corpus record hedge shrubs of the olive kinship used as gargles and washes for the inflamed mouth and throat, a "binding" leaf-drug applied to sores. Privet grows across the Near East as wild scrub, and the astringent-leaf gargle tradition attributed to these tablets is consistent with the later, better-documented Greco-Roman use. The specifics of tablet attribution are traditional and general rather than pinned to a single securely translated line.

**Dioscorides & the Greek Materia Medica (DMM).** Dioscorides describes *kypros/ligustron* — an Oleaceae shrub — whose leaves and flowers were chewed or boiled into a decoction for ulcers of the mouth (aphthae), for a relaxed uvula, and as a cooling wash for hot, spreading sores and erysipelas-like inflammations of the skin. The plant appears again in Pliny under *ligustrum*, framed as a hedge and dye plant whose leaves serve the same astringent, mouth-healing purpose. This is the taproot of privet's Western medicinal identity: a topical astringent, not an internal remedy.

**Avicenna & the Unani synthesis (EP as received through the Canon).** The *Qanun* carries the Oleaceae astringent-gargle tradition forward, classing such leaf drugs as cold and dry, useful for fluxes of the mouth and throat, aphthous ulcers, and spongy gums. The internal use of the berry is not part of this therapeutic line — the medicinal object is consistently the leaf and flower.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** Privet reads in the old herbals as an emblem of *restraint and enclosure* — the hedge plant, the boundary shrub, the thing that binds a garden's edge. That symbolism maps cleanly onto its real pharmacology: the leaf is rich in secoiridoids and phenylethanoids that tan and tighten mucosal proteins, literally "binding" a weeping surface. But the same plant that binds also poisons: its glossy black berries are a genuine hazard, and the doctrine of the enclosing hedge doubles as a warning — this is a plant to keep at the boundary, not to take within.

### Active Components

- **Secoiridoids:** oleuropein (concentrated in the flowers), oleacein, oleocanthal — the same olive-oil phenolics privet leaf produces in quantity. According to PubMed, oleacein and oleocanthal reach roughly 23 mg/g and 8 mg/g of dried aqueous privet-leaf extract respectively ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2018.06.006)).
- **Phenylethanoid glycosides:** verbascoside (acteoside) and echinacoside — astringent, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory.
- **Flavonoids:** including quercetin and luteolin derivatives.
- **Toxic principle of the fruit:** secoiridoid glucosides plus the glycosides historically named **ligustrin** and **syringin**, concentrated in the ripe black berry and responsible for its emetic and purgative toxicity.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Within Kenetica, privet leaf sits squarely in the **Sealing / Astringent Phase** as a topical, mucosal tool: a gargle or mouthwash for aphthous ulcers, spongy or bleeding gums, sore throat, and a relaxed uvula; a compress or wash for weeping, superficial skin inflammation. Its secoiridoid content gives a plausible anti-inflammatory and antioxidant rationale for these traditional uses. It is deployed as an external astringent only — never as an internal remedy, and never involving the fruit.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, common privet leaf is a genuine reservoir of olive-type secoiridoids. Peyrot des Gachons and colleagues showed privet leaves yield both oleocanthal and oleacein — the prized anti-inflammatory phenolics of extra-virgin olive oil — at gram scale ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms252212020)). Litewski, Koss-Mikołajczyk and Kusznierewicz profiled the different morphological parts of *L. vulgare*, finding flowers richest in oleuropein and demonstrating antioxidant, COX-2-inhibitory, α-amylase-inhibitory and antiproliferative activity in the extracts ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules29020399)). Czerwińska and co-workers found an aqueous privet-leaf extract, characterised by its oleacein/oleocanthal/verbascoside content, reduced hyperalgesia and allodynia in a streptozotocin diabetic-neuropathy rat model ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2018.06.006)).

**Evidence grade: traditional, with mechanistic support.** The chemistry (secoiridoids, phenylethanoids) is well documented and biologically plausible, but the specific traditional uses — astringent gargle, mouth-ulcer wash — have not been tested in human clinical trials. There is no controlled evidence for internal therapeutic use, and the berry toxicity is well attested in the poisoning literature.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** cold and dry (second degree); astringent, binding, "cooling" to hot fluxes and inflamed mucosa.
- **TCM (by analogy, not classical attribution):** a bitter, cool, astringent leaf drug that "clears heat" and "restrains leakage" of the mucosa — an interpretive mapping, as *L. vulgare* is not a classical *ben cao* herb (distinct from *Ligustrum lucidum*, whose fruit *nü zhen zi* is a tonic — do not conflate the two species).
- **Ayurvedic (interpretive):** rasa — kashaya (astringent), tikta (bitter); virya — cooling (shita); vipaka — pungent (katu); pacifying to Pitta and Kapha at the surface. Interpretive, not a classical dravya.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** Privet reaches the Mesopotamian record only as a general astringent hedge-leaf of the olive kinship used in gargles and washes for the inflamed mouth, and the entry itself concedes that the tablet attribution is "traditional and general rather than pinned to a single securely translated line." Under the standing blocking warning that governs every Assyrian substance identification, deity attribution and tablet reference, no securely translated line assigns this leaf a place among the drugs that oppose a named causal agency — ghost, oath or divine hand — let alone a status a person takes to work upon the self. The *asû*'s gargle here is a therapeutic wash, not a cultivation act, and the identification itself is not secure enough to claim more. Cultivation status: **(c) absent** — the attestation is general, uncertain, and therapeutic.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The Oleaceae shrub *kypros/ligustron* is given a plainly topical account — leaves and flowers chewed or decocted for aphthous ulcers, a relaxed uvula, and cooling washes for hot spreading sores. Dioscorides carries no subtle-body doctrine into which such a leaf could be assigned a cultivation status; what he offers a cultivator instead is his discipline of attention — correct identification of the Oleaceae shrub, the season of gathering leaf and flower, the sensory assay of a genuine astringent decoction, and the guard against confusing a boundary hedge with a look-alike. That is a real practice of provenance and judgement, sharpened here by the fact that the same plant's berry is a genuine poison, so the discrimination of part from part is itself the skill. But it is a discipline of the gatherer's eye, not a doctrine of the vital body. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The *Qanun* carries the Oleaceae astringent-gargle line forward, classing such leaf drugs as cold and dry and directing them at fluxes of the mouth and throat, aphthae and spongy gums. The strongest cultivation idea in the Unani corpus — the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture, that finite vital endowment whose slow depletion *is* ageing — is a longevity frame belonging to the whole regimen, and nothing in this cooling, drying, boundary-holding astringent is assigned to it; no *mufarrih* or *ruh*-strengthening cardiac role is claimed for privet leaf, and none should be. The leaf is attested therapeutically within the tradition but carries no recorded cultivation status of its own. **(b) framed:** claimed by the tradition as a topical astringent, with no cultivation status sourced.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing here to cultivate. Across all three traditions privet is a spit-out astringent for a weeping mouth or a hot skin sore — a leaf worked topically and briefly, then stopped. It builds no vitality, conserves no innate heat, and belongs to no rejuvenative category; the only discipline it genuinely trains is the gatherer's — knowing the true Oleaceae leaf, gathering it in season, and above all keeping the toxic black berry firmly outside the body. The hedge that binds a garden's edge is the right emblem: this is a plant for the boundary, held at arm's length, not taken within.

### Contraindications

⚠️ **THE BERRIES ARE TOXIC — DO NOT INGEST.** The ripe black fruit of *Ligustrum vulgare* contains secoiridoid glycosides together with the glycosides historically named ligustrin and syringin. Ingestion causes nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, weakness and hypotension; poisonings are documented particularly in **children** attracted to the berries, and severe cases can involve significant fluid loss and cardiovascular depression. Livestock poisonings are also recorded. **Any suspected berry ingestion — especially by a child — is a medical emergency; contact poison control immediately.**

⚠️ **Leaves and sap are irritant.** The foliage can provoke contact irritation and gastrointestinal upset if swallowed in quantity. Privet leaf is for **topical, spit-out** use only; do not use as an internal remedy.

- **Do not confuse with *Ligustrum lucidum*** (glossy privet), whose fruit is a distinct TCM tonic — the species, parts and uses are not interchangeable.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** insufficient safety data — avoid internal use entirely.
- **Children:** keep berries out of reach; the fruit is the principal poisoning risk.
- Discontinue topical use if oral or skin irritation appears.
