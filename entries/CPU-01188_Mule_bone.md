# Mule bone — bshul.chhags, "bone of Packhorse/Mule" (Clark 1995, rGyud-bzhi Explanatory Tantra ch.20)
## *The bone of a pack animal that Clark glosses "Packhorse/Mule"; the Tibetan name is printed in the OCR text as bshul.chhags and no binomial is given* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** In the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`) lists the thirteen animal-origin medicines (about lines 1148-1149): horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body. Under the heading Bone (line 1160) the list runs through human skull and bone, dragon bone, tiger bone, cowrie shell, pig bone, sheep bone, porcupine bone, monkey bone and snail shell, and ends: "bshul.chhags: bone of Packhorse/Mule: cures lymph disorders." The same line 1160 holds the monkey bone item. The index at the back of the volume has an entry "Packhorse bone" (line 1623). A search of `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` and `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` for "mule", "packhorse" and "bshul" found no row, so the only locus for this entry is Clark line 1160 (the batch file points to that line alone).

"Lymph" here is Clark's English for a Tibetan pathological category; the Men-Tsee-Khang translation prints what looks like the same category as *chhuser* (for example "The bone of animals with rounded hooves cures disorders of chhuser", line 3524). I did not read a definition of either word in the lines used, and I do not equate it with the lymphatic system of modern anatomy. [USER: verify]

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, lines 3523-3524) does not print a mule or packhorse bone by name. Its run of bones reads "Pig bone cures baekan mukpo. Sheep bone cures loong disorders. The spherical head of the femur cures obstruction of urine. Porcupine bone stops bleeding. Monkey bone is an ecbolic. Snail shell cures ein ... disorders and drains ascites. The bone of animals with rounded hooves cures disorders of chhuser." The last sentence, "The bone of animals with rounded hooves cures disorders of chhuser", has the same action as Clark's packhorse/mule bone. Whether "animals with rounded hooves" is the Men-Tsee-Khang rendering of the same Tibetan item is not stated in either text; the correspondence is by position (last in the bone run) and by indication (*chhuser* and "lymph"), and is my reading. ### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, bone is mainly calcium phosphate (hydroxyapatite) in a collagen matrix, with marrow fat and small amounts of other minerals. Nothing in equine bone is known to act on the lymphatic system. A mule is the hybrid offspring of a male donkey and a female horse; its bone is ordinary mammalian bone.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text records the bone as a drug for a category of disorder that Clark calls "lymph". The Codex repeats that as a record and gives no indication from it. Bone from a working or dead animal is not a tested remedy; the swollen glands, masses and fluid collections that may lie behind the word "lymph" in a modern reading are conditions for diagnosis and treatment by a clinician. Mules and horses are domestic animals, so there is no conservation issue in the material itself, but burnt or ground bone from an unknown carcass can carry anthrax spores and other pathogens. No modern protocol is invented here.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither translation prints a preparation, form, vehicle or dose for this bone in the lines read (Clark line 1160; Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3523-3524). The weather-beaten scapula "in ash form" that Clark's line gives for a human bone (line 1160) is a different item and is not transferred to the mule. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Bone has no known effect on the lymphatic system, and enlarged lymph nodes have many causes, including infection and cancer, that need examination. Animal bone from carcasses can carry the spores of anthrax and other infectious agents. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** In the lines read, the bone has no taste, potency or post-digestive taste printed. The only action printed is that it "cures lymph disorders" (Clark line 1160). The humoral (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) classification of the item is not printed for it in those lines. Not found in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The substance is attested in the Explanatory Tantra ch.20 animal-origin list, among the bones, as a drug for "lymph" disorders; that is a therapeutic status only. The tradition's frame for illness is that the three humours are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, line 51), and medicine is one branch of treatment within that frame; no line read assigns the bone a place in any practice of essence extraction (*bcud len*) or any other cultivation scheme. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The mule's bone is one short line in a list of bones, with a single stated action, and the Codex records it and does not recommend it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Not a tested remedy:** do not substitute bone preparations for diagnosis of swollen glands, lumps or fluid collections.
- **Infection:** bone, ash or powder from carcasses can carry anthrax spores and other pathogens; do not handle, burn for inhalation or ingest.
- **Identification:** the Tibetan name is glossed "Packhorse/Mule" by Clark and printed differently by the Men-Tsee-Khang translation; the animal is not identified to a species.
- **Ethics:** the Codex does not recommend slaughtering or harvesting any animal for these uses.
