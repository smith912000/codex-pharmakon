# Barringtonia aculangula — Hijjala (Sushruta Samhita, Appendix; "Barringtonia aculangula" as printed by Bhishagratna)

## *Barringtonia acutangula (L.) Gaertn., the Indian oak or freshwater mangrove, a medium tree of wet ground in South Asia; "aculangula" is Bhishagratna's misprint of the species name; the identification is his, printed in the Appendix against a name the scan gives as "Hijjala", and is of high confidence on the Appendix pairing, but the name occurs nowhere else in the Sushruta chapters on disk, so the entry rests on one glossary line* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named by the *Sushruta Samhita* among the texts on disk, in the Appendix to Bhishagratna's Volume III only, under the Sanskrit name Hijjala.** This is a single-text, single-locus entry. The row was added on 13 October as an unverified placeholder from the Appendix; this entry replaces it. The attestation was confirmed on 30 October by opening the Appendix (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`, lines 31660-31750), and the name was then searched in every chapter file and in all three volumes of the translation.

**Sushruta Samhita, Appendix (SS).** The Appendix is a two-column glossary that the scan has split into a column of Sanskrit names and a column of identifications. On the "H" page the names column ends, in the scan's own spellings, "Hamsa-padi, Haridrsk, Hari-Mudga, Haritaki, Hasti-karna-Palabsa, Hasti-Fippali, Hasti-sundai, Haya-msiraka, Hijjala, Hila mochikai" (in the pairing below the names are given in plain spelling; the scan reads "Hijjala" at line 31689 and "Hila mochikai" at line 31691). ### Active Components

In general terms, *Barringtonia* is a genus of tropical trees of the family Lecythidaceae, and the seeds and bark of several species are used in the Indo-Pacific region as fish poisons because they contain saponins. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. *Barringtonia acutangula* is a tree of the wet lowlands of India and South-East Asia, often along streams and in swamps, and it is grown as an ornamental and for local uses. The genus is a known source of fish-poison plants. [USER: verify] No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry.
