### Origin & Tradition

The name *Hypericum* carries within it an entire worldview. From the Greek *hyper* (ὑπέρ, "above") and *eikon* (εἰκών, "image" or "icon"), the plant was literally understood as something placed *above* images — hung over doorways, windows, and religious icons to ward off demons, evil spirits, and the ill-fated forces that ancient Mediterranean cultures believed hovered at thresholds. The practice of suspending the herb at entrances was documented across Greece, Rome, and early Christian Europe, and it reflects the deepest layer of this plant's cultural biography: *Hypericum* was not primarily a digestive tonic or a joint herb in the classical imagination. It was a plant of the spirit, a guardian herb, understood to work on the soul and mind before the body.

The second layer of the name lies in the calendar. The feast of St. John the Baptist falls on June 24 — the summer solstice by the old Julian calendar — and across European folk medicine, this was the prescribed day for harvesting *Hypericum perforatum*. The timing is not arbitrary. The plant reaches peak flowering precisely at midsummer, and the red oil that bleeds from crushed flowers under the midsummer sun was interpreted by herbalists and folk practitioners as the blood of St. John himself — the martyr beheaded by Herod, whose spilled blood became a symbol of spiritual protection and healing in the Christian tradition. The old name *fuga daemonum* — "flight of demons" — appeared in medieval European herbals as an alternative designation, and the plant was classified alongside other strongly protective herbs used in exorcism and spiritual purification.

This is not superstition to be dismissed. What the medieval tradition was observing and encoding in religious language was the plant's profound action on the nervous system and mood — an action that was genuinely distinct from most other herbs in the European materia medica. Long before neurochemistry existed as a framework, European healers had correctly identified that this plant altered the interior emotional and psychological landscape in a way that other herbs did not. The language of "demonic oppression" and "melancholy" in pre-modern medicine is, in part, a cultural translation of clinical depression and anxiety — and *Hypericum*'s reputation as the specific remedy for these states was earned across centuries of empirical use before any randomized controlled trial confirmed it.

The traditional preparation that best captures this solar-spiritual pharmacology is the *oleum hyperici* — the oil of St. John. Made by packing fresh flowers into olive oil and exposing the mixture to sunlight for several weeks, the oil transforms from clear yellow-green to a deep blood-red as hypericin — the plant's signature naphthodianthrone pigment — leaches into the carrier. This red oil was applied to wounds, nerve injuries, burns, and bruised flesh. The wound-healing tradition is fully documented in Dioscorides (1st century CE), who describes *hyperikon* (ἑπερικόν) in *De Materia Medica* for "expelling bile," as a diuretic, and as a wound herb applied topically. These pharmacological actions — bile-expelling (mild hepatobiliary action), diuretic, and wound-healing — form the three pillars of the Dioscoridean account, and all three correspond to verifiable phytochemical actions now understood.

Avicenna (Ibn Sina, 980–1037 CE) incorporates *Hadariq* (هضاريق) — the Arabic designation for *Hypericum* species — into the *Canon of Medicine* (*Al-Qanun fi'l-Tibb*) with specific attention to its utility in nerve pain (*waja' al-asab*), melancholy (*waswas saudawi*), and wound healing. The nerve pain and melancholy indications represent an expansion of the Dioscoridean tradition in a more explicitly neuropsychiatric direction, and Avicenna's characterization of the herb's action on the spirit (ruhaniyyat) as brightening and elevating is pharmacologically prescient. The CM tradition uses the root preparation more frequently than the aerial parts, and the formulations are combined with other nervines for sustained mood support.

The Egyptian Pharaonic tradition (EP) did not use *Hypericum perforatum* specifically — the plant's native range is temperate Europe and Western Asia — but the solar flower principle embedded in Egyptian plant medicine provides the conceptual framework within which the plant's pharmacological character would have been interpreted. Yellow-to-red flowering plants were associated with Ra and the solar principle throughout Egyptian sacred botany; the progression from golden yellow flowers to blood-red oil specifically mirrors the mythological arc of the solar eye (Wadjet/Sekhmet), and the EP tradition's extensive use of sun-activated preparations (oils macerated under direct sunlight) is structurally identical to the *oleum hyperici* process. The EP tag here acknowledges this methodological and symbolic continuity rather than documenting specific Egyptian use of this species.

Across traditional European folk medicine (outside the formal Galenic tradition), St. John's Wort held a position of extraordinary importance for what was called the "nervous temperament" — what we would now identify as mood instability, seasonal affective patterns, anxiety with melancholy, and what 18th- and 19th-century physicians called "nerve weakness" or "neurasthenia." The plant's reputation specifically for seasonal mood disorders — it flowers at the maximum solar point of the year, and it was classically prescribed for the "winter darkness of the soul" — anticipates the modern clinical interest in seasonal affective disorder (SAD), for which *Hypericum* extracts have documented efficacy. This pattern, where the traditional indication precisely matches the modern clinical application without any direct pharmacological knowledge, is one of the most compelling arguments for the validity of traditional medicine's long empirical process.

### Active Components

- **Hyperforin** (phloroglucinol derivative, ~2–4% in standardized extracts): now considered the primary antidepressant compound in *Hypericum*. The mechanism is pharmacologically unique and has no equivalent among pharmaceutical antidepressants: hyperforin activates TRPC6 (Transient Receptor Potential Canonical 6) ion channels in neuronal membranes → increased intracellular Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ concentration → non-competitive inhibition of ALL five major monoamine uptake transporters simultaneously: serotonin (SERT), dopamine (DAT), norepinephrine (NET), GABA, and glutamate. This "pan-monoamine reuptake inhibition" profile is distinct from SSRIs (which block only SERT), SNRIs (SERT + NET), or NDRIs (DAT + NET). The simultaneous modulation of all five systems may explain the broader mood-elevating, anxiety-reducing, and sleep-normalizing effect observed clinically, compared to the more targeted and often side-effect-laden pharmaceutical alternatives. Hyperforin is also — critically — the primary inducer of CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein (see Contraindications). Its concentration in the plant varies significantly by growing conditions, season, and part used; standardized extracts specify hyperforin content (typically 3–5%) to ensure therapeutic consistency.

- **Hypericin** (naphthodianthrone, ~0.1–0.3% in standardized extracts): the compound responsible for the blood-red color of *oleum hyperici* and historically the presumed primary antidepressant. Early research suggested monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitory activity as hypericin's antidepressant mechanism, but subsequent careful investigation demonstrated that MAO inhibitory activity at therapeutically achievable concentrations of hypericin is insufficient to explain the clinical antidepressant effect. The MAO hypothesis has been largely abandoned in the current literature. What hypericin *does* possess in well-characterized form is: potent antiviral activity — documented inhibition of HIV-1 replication (inhibits protein kinase C and retroviral integrase; disrupts viral envelope fusion) and HSV (herpes simplex virus, both HSV-1 and HSV-2) at concentrations achievable with concentrated preparations; and photosensitizing activity via UVA light activation → singlet oxygen generation → oxidative membrane damage (the same mechanism exploited in photodynamic cancer therapy). The photosensitization is the basis for the UV-exposure contraindication. Hypericin and pseudohypericin are structurally related naphthodianthrones; both are active.

- **Pseudohypericin**: closely related to hypericin in structure and activity; antiviral; contributes to the total naphthodianthrone content; co-standardized with hypericin in quality extracts.

- **Amentoflavone** (biflavonoid): potent competitive inhibitor of benzodiazepine receptor binding (Ki ~1 μM), acting at the central benzodiazepine site on GABA-A receptors. This is one of the most potent natural compounds documented for benzodiazepine receptor affinity. The contribution of amentoflavone to *Hypericum*'s anxiolytic dimension is significant and mechanistically well-characterized; it may explain why clinical trials consistently show a combined antidepressant-plus-anxiolytic profile rather than pure mood elevation. Critically, however, amentoflavone has poor oral bioavailability in isolation; in whole plant extracts, co-extraction with hyperforin and other lipophilic compounds appears to improve CNS delivery.

- **Quercetin, rutin, kaempferol** (flavonoids): broad antioxidant activity; NF-κB inhibition with anti-inflammatory consequences; quercetin additionally inhibits CYP1A2 and CYP2C9 at high concentrations (clinical significance at food-equivalent intakes is low, but relevant at supplemental doses in polypharmacy contexts); rutin is a quercetin-3-O-rutinoside with documented capillary-protective and anti-inflammatory activity; kaempferol has documented anti-cancer and anti-inflammatory properties. This flavonoid matrix contributes to the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory dimensions of the herb's overall wound-healing and neuroprotective action.

- **Xanthones** (1,3,6,7-tetrahydroxyxanthone and others): anti-depressant activity in animal models; documented inhibition of MAO-A (weakly) and additional neurochemical actions; contribute to the anti-inflammatory profile.

- **Tannins** (catechins, proanthocyanidins, ~10–15%): astringent; wound-healing (tissue contraction and antimicrobial); contribute to the topical wound and burn applications of the *oleum hyperici*.

- **Essential oil** (2-methyloctane, pinene, myrcene, caryophyllene): antimicrobial; mild anti-inflammatory; contributes to the aroma of the fresh herb and the traditional wound-healing preparations.

- **Hyperforin's CYP3A4 induction mechanism (pharmacokinetic note)**: Hyperforin binds the pregnane X receptor (PXR), a nuclear transcription factor that acts as a master regulator of CYP3A4, CYP2C9, and the efflux pump P-glycoprotein (Pgp). When PXR is activated by hyperforin, it upregulates transcription of CYP3A4 (the single enzyme responsible for metabolizing approximately 50% of all pharmaceutical drugs in clinical use), CYP2C9 (warfarin, NSAIDs, sulfonylureas), and Pgp (the intestinal drug efflux pump). This is not a theoretical concern — it is the most clinically significant pharmacokinetic drug interaction in all of herbal medicine.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

St. John's Wort occupies two specific and clearly delineated phases in the Kenetica framework: `[RESET]` and `[CYCLE-OFF]`. These are the phases defined by the need for nervous system restoration, mood recalibration, and recovery from the sustained neurochemical demands of peak training and competition cycles. Understanding *why* this placement is precise — rather than simply convenient — requires understanding the neurochemical cost of sustained high-performance training.

Prolonged high-intensity athletic preparation imposes a specific neurochemical burden that is distinct from the physical burden of tissue damage and inflammation. Elevated cortisol suppresses serotonergic neurotransmission by increasing tryptophan metabolism via IDO (indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase) toward the kynurenine pathway rather than serotonin synthesis. Sustained sympathetic activation depletes dopaminergic reward signaling — the mechanism behind "burnout" and the post-competition affective flatness that many athletes recognize but cannot readily name. GABA depletion from chronic stress reduces the inhibitory tone of the nervous system, producing a residual irritability and sleep disruption that persists well into recovery phases. *Hypericum*'s pan-monoamine reuptake inhibition profile addresses all three of these simultaneously: serotonin restoration eases the melancholy and dysphoric dimension; dopamine and norepinephrine reuptake inhibition restores motivational tone and the reward response; GABA modulation (both via hyperforin's GABA reuptake inhibition and amentoflavone's benzodiazepine receptor affinity) reduces the residual nervous system hyperactivity.

The `[RESET]` application is not about treating clinical depression — it is about reversing the subclinical neurochemical depletion patterns that follow sustained peak performance demands, and returning the athlete to a stable baseline from which the next training cycle can be built. This distinction is clinically meaningful: the plant's evidence base shows consistent superiority to placebo for mild-to-moderate depression and comparable efficacy to standard antidepressants at equivalent severity — but it does *not* show adequate efficacy in severe depression (the Shelton 2001 JAMA trial established this ceiling definitively). For Kenetica purposes, this limitation is not a weakness: the target population is not severely depressed, but neurochemically depleted from performance demands, and for this profile, *Hypericum*'s gentler polymodal action is precisely appropriate.

The `[CYCLE-OFF]` application is more specific: many athletes transitioning off stimulant-heavy stacks (caffeinated compounds, adaptogens with strong sympathomimetic activity, training phases characterized by high stimulant load) experience a withdrawal-adjacent period of low mood, motivational deficit, and sleep disruption. *Hypericum* used in the first two to four weeks of stack transition provides a pharmacological bridge — restoring monoaminergic tone while stimulant-induced receptor adaptation gradually normalizes.

The nerve pain dimension — documented by Avicenna and supported by the hyperforin mechanism (TRPC6 channel modulation has been documented in nociceptive fiber function) — makes the plant relevant for athletes managing training-related neuropathic pain or post-injury nerve recovery. The traditional *oleum hyperici* topical application for wounds, bruises, and nerve injuries has both the longest historical track record and emerging mechanistic support.

One application that Kenetica specifically does NOT include: use during active polypharmacy protocols. The CYP3A4 and Pgp induction concern (see Contraindications) makes *Hypericum* incompatible with any stack that includes pharmaceutically active compounds metabolized by these pathways. This restriction is absolute.

### Preparation & Dosing

- **Standardized oral extract (primary clinical form):** 300 mg three times daily (900 mg/day total) of extract standardized to 0.3% hypericin and 3–5% hyperforin; this is the dose used in the majority of clinical trials showing efficacy for mild-moderate depression; WS 5570 (Neuroplant) and LI 160 (Jarsin/Kira) are the two most widely studied standardized preparations; onset of effect typically 2–4 weeks; full effect at 6–8 weeks.

- **Standardized oral extract (Kenetica `[RESET]` protocol):** 300–600 mg twice daily of standardized extract during the 4–8 week reset phase; timing with morning and midday meals reduces the mild gastric irritation occasionally reported; evening dosing is appropriate if the primary target is sleep normalization.

- **Traditional tea (lesser clinical potency but traditional and acceptable for mild use):** 2–4 g dried herb (aerial parts at peak flower) in 250 mL hot water (not boiling), steep 10 minutes; 2–3 cups daily; the tea preparation delivers significantly lower hyperforin concentrations than standardized ethanolic extracts, as hyperforin is lipophilic and poorly water-soluble; this preparation retains the flavonoid and tannin content more fully and is appropriate for mild mood support or topical wound use.

- **Traditional *oleum hyperici* (topical wound and nerve preparation):** Fresh flowers packed into olive oil in a glass jar, sealed, and exposed to direct sunlight for 3–6 weeks until the oil turns deep blood-red; strain; apply topically to wounds, bruises, nerve injuries, and burns; do NOT apply to UV-exposed skin (hypericin phototoxicity risk); excellent for nerve contusion injuries in athletes when sun exposure is controlled.

- **Tincture (1:5, 40–50% ethanol):** 4–6 mL three times daily; intermediate potency between tea and standardized extract; used in traditional European herbal practice.

**Timing:**
- Mood and Shen restoration: morning and afternoon doses with meals; evening dose for sleep normalization
- `[RESET]` phase: begin at end of competition cycle, continue for minimum 6 weeks for full neurochemical normalization
- `[CYCLE-OFF]` bridge: 300 mg twice daily during first 4 weeks of stack transition; taper dose in weeks 5–6

### Scientific Correlation

- **Pan-monoamine reuptake inhibition (hyperforin/TRPC6):** Müller WE et al. (2001, *Pharmacopsychiatry*, in vitro + animal): Hyperforin at therapeutically relevant concentrations (0.1–3 μM) produced dose-dependent inhibition of synaptosomal reuptake of serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine, GABA, and L-glutamate in rodent brain preparations, with IC50 values of 0.05–0.5 μM. The mechanism was demonstrated to be TRPC6-channel dependent via calcium imaging — distinguishing it from the membrane transporter-blocking mechanism of SSRIs. Wonnemann M et al. (2000, *Journal of Pharmacology and Experimental Therapeutics*) confirmed the polymodal reuptake inhibition and established the TRPC6 dependence.

- **Clinical efficacy — Cochrane review (mild-moderate depression):** Linde K et al. (2008, *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews*, meta-analysis of 29 RCTs, n=5,489): *Hypericum* extracts were superior to placebo (relative risk of non-response 0.72, 95% CI 0.62–0.84) and comparable to standard antidepressants (relative risk 1.00, 95% CI 0.90–1.11) for mild-to-moderate depression, with significantly fewer adverse effects and lower dropout rates. This is among the largest evidence bases for any herbal intervention in psychiatry.

- **Severity ceiling (severe depression):** Shelton RC et al. (2001, *JAMA*, n=200, 8 weeks): In severe MDD (DSM-IV criteria, Hamilton Depression Rating Scale ≥20), *Hypericum* extract LI 160 (900–1500 mg/day) was not superior to placebo (HAM-D response rate 14.3% vs. 5.0% placebo, p=ns). Established the clinical limitation for severe presentations.

- **Equivalence to paroxetine:** Kasper S et al. (2010, *Phytomedicine*, n=242, 6 weeks): Standardized extract WS 5570 (600–1800 mg/day) was equivalent to paroxetine 20–40 mg/day in the primary efficacy measure (HAM-D total score reduction) with superior tolerability and lower discontinuation rate.

- **CYP3A4 induction (mechanism and clinical consequences):** Durr D et al. (2000, *Clinical Pharmacology & Therapeutics*, n=8 healthy volunteers): *Hypericum* extract (900 mg/day, 14 days) reduced cyclosporine plasma levels by 52% (p<0.01), attributable to induction of intestinal CYP3A4 and Pgp. Multiple transplant rejection cases followed. Wang Z et al. (2001, *Clinical Pharmacology & Therapeutics*): demonstrated parallel induction of intestinal and hepatic CYP3A4, CYP2C9, and Pgp by hyperforin via PXR activation. Moore LB et al. (2000, *PNAS*) confirmed hyperforin as a potent PXR agonist at sub-micromolar concentrations.

- **Antiviral activity (hypericin):** Meruelo D et al. (1988, *PNAS*): Hypericin demonstrated antiviral activity against HIV and other retroviruses in vitro and in animal models; mechanism subsequently characterized as inhibition of viral protein kinase C and envelope fusion. Moraleda G et al. (1993, *Antimicrobial Agents and Chemotherapy*): hypericin inhibited HSV-1 and HSV-2 replication with IC50 values in the nanomolar range. UVA activation substantially enhanced antiviral potency, suggesting photodynamic mechanism.

- **Amentoflavone benzodiazepine receptor binding:** Baureithel KH et al. (1997, *Pharmaceutica Acta Helvetiae*, in vitro): Amentoflavone competitively inhibited [³H]flunitrazepam binding to central GABA-A/benzodiazepine receptor complex with Ki = 0.97 μM, classifying it among the most potent natural benzodiazepine site ligands characterized.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**European folk medicine / Galenic tradition:**
- Nature: Warm, dry (second degree in Galenic classification — gently warming without drying excess)
- Quality: Penetrating, nerve-specific, solar
- Primary indications: melancholy (black bile excess), nerve weakness, wound healing, malaria and tertian fever (the red oil tradition), spiritual protection
- Humoral classification: the plant's action was interpreted as resolving a cold, heavy, dark accumulation (black bile/melancholy = cold and dry excess) through its warm, light, solar action — the color of the oil (blood red, solar gold) encoded the humoral logic: warmth dissolves the cold of melancholy

**Avicenna / Canon of Medicine (CM) classification:**
- *Mizaj* (temperament): warm and dry in the second degree
- Primary actions: fortifies the nerves, dispels melancholy, resolves nerve pain, facilitates wound healing, promotes urine
- The nervine and psychological action is given greater specificity in Avicenna's account than in any earlier Galenic source — the CM tradition clearly identifies the CNS as the primary target organ

**Ayurvedic parallel:**
- The pharmacological profile maps to herbs acting on *Vata* disorders of the *Manovahasrotas* (channels governing mental function and emotional experience): the Shen-brightening, nerve-restoring, and melancholy-clearing action parallels *Shankhapushpi* (*Convolvulus pluricaulis*) and *Brahmi* (*Bacopa monnieri*) in their neuroprotective and anxiolytic dimensions, though the specific pan-monoamine reuptake inhibition mechanism is pharmacologically distinct

**TCM Energetic Parallel (for cross-reference, not a primary tradition):**
- The Shen-brightening and melancholy-clearing action parallels herbs acting on the Heart channel to settle disturbed Shen — specifically *He Huan Pi* (Mimosa bark, the "happiness bark"), which is used for "constrained emotions, sorrow, and worry that damage the heart"
- The wound-healing and antiviral dimensions parallel *Jin Yin Hua* (Lonicera) for clearing heat and toxins
- The seasonal SAD application parallels the understanding that Shen disorders worsen in the absence of solar Qi (winter months) — treated by herbs and foods that carry solar Yang Qi, which is the deepest layer of the Hypericum energetic profile

**Shen classification in Kenetica:**
- ☿ Shen primary: the plant's most direct and best-documented action is on the emotional and psychological interior — mood elevation, melancholy clearing, and nervous system restoration; the Shen application is not secondary or incidental but the defining characteristic of the herb's profile
- ◉ Qi secondary: wound healing, antiviral, diuretic, mild hepatobiliary action

### Contraindications

**THE CRITICAL DRUG INTERACTION — CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein induction (absolute contraindication with numerous pharmaceuticals):**
This is the most clinically significant pharmacokinetic herb-drug interaction documented in all of herbal medicine, and it must be treated as an absolute contraindication — not a theoretical concern — in any context where the following drug classes are in concurrent use:

- **Transplant immunosuppressants (ciclosporin, tacrolimus, sirolimus):** *Hypericum* use has caused documented transplant organ rejection due to cyclosporine level reductions of 40–60%. Multiple cases of acute rejection and patient death have been documented in the transplant literature. This is an absolute, life-threatening contraindication.
- **Antiretroviral medications (indinavir, saquinavir, lopinavir, efavirenz, nevirapine, and most ARVs):** *Hypericum* significantly reduces plasma levels of multiple antiretroviral agents via CYP3A4/Pgp induction. HIV treatment failure has been documented. Absolute contraindication.
- **Oral contraceptives (ethinyl estradiol, levonorgestrel, desogestrel):** CYP3A4 induction reduces plasma ethinyl estradiol levels, causing contraceptive failure. Multiple cases of unintended pregnancy have been documented. Absolute contraindication for contraceptive reliability.
- **Anticoagulants (warfarin):** CYP2C9 induction reduces warfarin levels, reducing anticoagulant effect. Risk of thromboembolic events in patients requiring therapeutic anticoagulation. Absolute contraindication without close INR monitoring.
- **Anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital):** bidirectional interaction (these drugs also induce CYP3A4); complex pharmacokinetic interaction; seizure control may be compromised. Contraindicated.
- **Digoxin:** Pgp induction reduces intestinal digoxin absorption; reduced digoxin levels documented (Johne et al. 1999); potentially dangerous in patients where digoxin therapeutic window is narrow. Contraindicated.
- **Anticancer medications (imatinib, irinotecan, docetaxel, and many others):** reduced chemotherapeutic drug levels may compromise cancer treatment. Oncology protocols universally exclude *Hypericum* during treatment.
- **Benzodiazepines, tricyclic antidepressants, many psychiatric medications**: CYP3A4 and CYP2C19 substrates — levels altered unpredictably.

**Serotonin syndrome risk:**
Combination with SSRIs (fluoxetine, paroxetine, sertraline, escitalopram), SNRIs (venlafaxine, duloxetine), tramadol, triptans (sumatriptan), linezolid, or other serotonergic agents carries a documented risk of serotonin syndrome (characterized by hyperthermia, agitation, clonus, diarrhea, and in severe cases, rhabdomyolysis and death). Multiple case reports exist. The combination of *Hypericum* with SSRI therapy is contraindicated without physician oversight.

**Phototoxicity (UV exposure):**
Hypericin is a photosensitizing agent activated by UVA radiation → generation of reactive oxygen species → cutaneous phototoxic burns, particularly in fair-skinned individuals. At standard therapeutic doses, the risk is low for most individuals with incidental sun exposure, but: high-dose supplementation + prolonged UV exposure (outdoor athletes, tanning bed use) increases risk substantially. Topical hypericin-containing preparations must never be applied to UV-exposed skin.

**Severity limitation:**
*Hypericum* is not adequate as a sole treatment for severe or melancholic depression. Its clinical evidence base is specifically for mild-to-moderate presentations. Use in severe depression as monotherapy may constitute under-treatment of a life-threatening condition.

**Pregnancy:**
Limited safety data; hyperforin has demonstrated uterotonic activity in animal studies; traditional classification as a herb that "moves blood" and the historical emmenagogue association suggest avoidance in the first trimester; no safe dose established for pregnancy.

**Cognitive caveat:**
Despite not producing significant sedation at standard doses, amentoflavone's benzodiazepine receptor activity may marginally impair reaction time in dose-sensitive individuals; this is generally not clinically significant but warrants awareness in sport contexts requiring maximum reaction speed.
