### Origin & Tradition

Nutmeg is the seed kernel of an evergreen tree native to the Banda Islands (the original "Spice Islands") of Indonesia, and one of the most historically consequential spices on earth — the nutmeg monopoly drove Portuguese, Dutch and English imperial competition, and the Banda Islands were the site of notorious colonial violence over its control. The single fruit yields **two spices**: the seed (**nutmeg**) and the lacy red aril surrounding it (**mace**), similar in chemistry and action. It entered the Mediterranean, Indian, Chinese and Arabic pharmacopoeias along the spice routes and is treated across all the major traditions.

Nutmeg's medicinal identity combines two threads. As a **warming, aromatic carminative** it eases digestive wind, colic, nausea and poor appetite — the everyday digestive spice. As a **warming astringent** it is, in Chinese Medicine especially (**Rou Dou Kou**, 肉豆蔻), a specific remedy to **warm the spleen/intestines and bind the bowel** in chronic cold diarrhoea and "daybreak diarrhoea" (the early-morning loose stool of kidney/spleen yang deficiency) — uniquely among the aromatic spices, it both warms and astringes the gut. In Ayurveda (**Jatiphala**) it is warming, *vata-kapha*-reducing, carminative, astringent (anti-diarrhoeal), and is also valued as a mild sedative/sleep aid and an aphrodisiac. The Unani tradition concurs.

Nutmeg carries a notorious dark side: in **larger-than-culinary doses it is a deliriant and toxin.** Its constituent **myristicin** (and related compounds) produces, at doses of a tablespoon or more, a delayed, unpleasant and dangerous anticholinergic-like delirium with hallucination, tachycardia, nausea and prolonged distress — a recurrent cause of poisoning, especially among those seeking a cheap intoxicant. The culinary spice is entirely safe; the danger is purely one of overdose, the central safety message of this entry.

### Preparation & Dosing

- **Culinary spice (grated):** ordinary, safe food use — a pinch to a small fraction of a nut.
- **Small medicinal dose (Ayurveda/CM):** a fraction of a seed (e.g. as Rou Dou Kou, often **dry-roasted/processed** to reduce oiliness and strengthen the binding action) within formulae. [USER: verify the standard small dose before stating a number; do NOT give large doses.]
- **Topical:** nutmeg butter/oil in warming liniments.

Safety framing: **culinary amounts are safe; doses of roughly a tablespoon (≈ one or more whole nuts) or more cause dangerous, delayed myristicin delirium and toxicity.** Never use nutmeg as an intoxicant. The narrow line is between spice and poison-by-quantity.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Chinese Medicine mapping:** **Rou Dou Kou** — acrid, warm; Spleen, Stomach, Large Intestine; warms the middle, moves qi, binds the intestines, stops (cold) diarrhoea (usually dry-roasted/processed).
- **Ayurvedic mapping:** Jatiphala — *katu-tikta-kashaya* (pungent-bitter-astringent), *ushna* (heating), *vata-kapha* reducing; carminative, astringent (*grahi*), nervine/sedative, aphrodisiac.
- **Unani mapping:** *jauz buwa* — hot-dry; carminative, astringent, nervine.
- **Kenetica Qi/Shen tier:** ◉ **Qi** for the warming, carminative and bowel-binding actions, with a ☿ **Shen** ascription reflecting its sedative effect in small doses and its deliriant action in overdose — a spice that touches the mind at both ends of the dose curve.

### Contraindications

- **Overdose — dangerous.** Doses around a tablespoon/one or more whole nuts cause delayed myristicin delirium, tachycardia and toxicity; never use as an intoxicant.
- **Pregnancy — avoid medicinal/large doses.** Reputed abortifacient/uterine effects in quantity; culinary amounts are food. [USER: verify.]
- **Heat patterns / yin-deficiency heat (CM).** A warming spice aggravates heat.
- **Psychiatric vulnerability / MAOI interaction.** Myristicin's weak MAOI activity and psychoactivity warrant caution.
- **Children.** Keep large quantities out of reach; toxic in overdose relative to body weight.
