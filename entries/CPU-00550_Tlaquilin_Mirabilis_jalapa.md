# Tlaquilin / Four O'Clock Flower / Marvel of Peru

## *Mirabilis jalapa* L. | Family: Nyctaginaceae

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ, BM

**Energetic Classification:** Cold, Dry (Third Degree) — Purgative, Downward-Draining, Cutting

**Training Phase:** Purgation — restricted. Not a beginner's plant. The root is a protein toxin carrier and is handled as such.

---

### Origin & Tradition

The plant is named for a lie. *Jalapa* attaches it to the true jalap purgative, *Ipomoea purga* of Xalapa — a Convolvulaceae resin drug with which it shares no chemistry and no family. Linnaeus took the folk name and fossilised the confusion in the binomial. *Marvel of Peru* misplaces it again; it is Mexican. The genus name *Mirabilis*, "wonderful," is the only honest part, and it refers to the one thing everyone actually observed.

**The flower opens at dusk.** Around four in the afternoon it unfolds, releases a strong sweet scent, works the night on hawkmoths, and closes by morning. On a single plant the flowers may be crimson, yellow, white, or streaked and sectored across one bloom — the pigment system is famously unstable, and this plant's mutable colour gave Carl Correns the material for the 1909 discovery of **non-Mendelian, cytoplasmic (maternal) inheritance**. It is one of the very few plants in this codex with a permanent place in the history of genetics, and it earned it by refusing to obey the rules of the nucleus.

**Aztec / Mesoamerican Practice (BM)**
The plant is Nahua, and the Nahuatl name recorded is *tlaquilin*. It appears in the **Badianus Manuscript (Libellus de Medicinalibus Indorum Herbis, 1552)** — the Aztec herbal composed by the native physician Martín de la Cruz and translated into Latin by Juan Badiano at the Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco. This is a genuinely post-conquest document written by an indigenous practitioner, and it is the plant's primary attestation.

Two indication clusters carry through the Mesoamerican sources. **Purgation** — the tuberous root taken internally as a violent cathartic, which is the whole reason the "jalap" name attached itself. And **skin** — poultice and wash of leaf and root applied to abscesses, boils, wounds, eruptions and inflamed swellings. The skin use is the safer and better-attested of the two; the purgative use is the dangerous one and is discussed under Contraindications.

**Post-Columbian Diffusion — and a chronology that must be stated plainly (SBJ)**
*M. jalapa* is a **New World plant**. It was carried to Europe in the sixteenth century as an ornamental, and from Europe by trade to India, Southeast Asia and China, where it naturalised aggressively and was absorbed into local practice under new names — in China as **紫茉莉 *zǐ mòlì*** ("purple jasmine"), where the tuberous root (*zǐ mòlì gēn*) is used for damp-heat conditions, urinary difficulty, and topically for swellings and abscesses.

Note what this means and do not let it be smoothed over. **The Chinese use of this plant cannot predate roughly 1500 CE.** Any source that places *M. jalapa* in the *Shennong Bencao Jing* (~200 CE) or any other pre-Columbian Chinese classic is wrong — not approximately, but categorically, because the plant was on the other side of an ocean. The same applies to claimed Ayurvedic classical attestation. This plant's Asian usage is real, is several centuries old, is clinically coherent, and is **early modern, not classical**. It is a folk adoption, not an ancient lineage. **[USER: verify]** any Chinese or Indian source that claims otherwise — the claim is the error, not the verification.

This matters beyond pedantry. A plant with a 400-year Asian folk record has a different evidential weight than one with a 2,000-year documented tradition, and readers reach for the second when handed the first.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

The doctrine of *tlaquilin* is **the inversion of the day**. It opens when everything else closes. Its whole signature is contrariety — the flower that works at night, the colour that will not stay fixed, the inheritance that ignores the father, the name that points at the wrong plant. It is a plant of reversal, and every tradition that used it used it to reverse something: to turn the bowel out, to bring the abscess up and open, to drive downward and outward what had settled and stuck.

The Aztec reading is the phase-appropriate one. This is not a plant of building or holding. It is a plant of *expulsion*, and the practitioner reaches for it at the moment when the correct action is to get something out.

Now map that onto the chemistry, and here the doctrine collides with something the tradition could not possibly have known.

The **purgative doctrine is chemically real and chemically terrifying.** The tradition observed that root causes violent evacuation and reasoned, correctly, that it drives downward. What it could not know is *why*. The root's principal characterised constituent is **Mirabilis antiviral protein (MAP)** — a **type 1 ribosome-inactivating protein**, an enzyme in the structural family of **ricin** and **abrin**. It works by cleaving the N-glycosidic bond at a single adenine in the sarcin-ricin loop of ribosomal RNA, depurinating the ribosome and shutting down protein synthesis in the cell. That is not an irritant laxative mechanism. That is a **protein-synthesis-inhibiting cytotoxin acting on the gut epithelium**, and the "purgation" is, at least in part, the observable consequence of killing enterocytes.

The tradition's phenomenology was accurate. Its inference — *this is a strong purge* — was the correct description at the level of observation and a catastrophic underestimate at the level of mechanism. This is the single most important doctrine-to-chemistry mapping in the entry, and it points the other way from most: the tradition was **not superstitious enough**. It treated as a cathartic what is more accurately described as a ribosome poison with a favourable oral-absorption profile.

The **abscess/skin doctrine is chemically plausible and better-founded than it looks.** RIPs in the *Mirabilis* genus are demonstrably **antifungal and antibacterial** — the closely related *M. expansa* proteins ME1 and ME2 are active against *Fusarium*, *Alternaria*, *Pythium*, *Pseudomonas syringae* and *Agrobacterium*. A protein that kills microbes on contact, applied to an infected swelling, is doing something. And the topical route keeps a type 1 RIP where a type 1 RIP is least dangerous — outside. The Aztecs found the plant's safest effective application, and it is the one this codex endorses.

The **"marvel"/mutability symbolism resolves to real biology** — betalain pigment genetics and cytoplasmic inheritance. Charming, and for once the wonder was pointing at something true.

### Active Components

- **Mirabilis antiviral protein (MAP)** — a **type 1 ribosome-inactivating protein**, 250 amino acids, heat-stable, rigid, with an intramolecular disulfide bond; an RNA N-glycosylase domain **with no carbohydrate-binding (lectin) domain**, which is what makes it "type 1" rather than a ricin-like type 2. Originally purified from the tuberous root. Structural work is extensive: crystallised (Miyano et al., *J Mol Biol*, 1992; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-2836(92)90142-7)) and the recombinant mutant structure solved at 2.1 Å (Nishida et al., *Toxins*, 2025; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/toxins17120575)).
- **MAP-4** — a second, distinct type 1 RIP isolated from seed (Bolognesi et al., *J Biol Chem*, 2002; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1074/jbc.M111514200)). The plant makes more than one of these.
- **Betalains** — betacyanins (red-violet) and betaxanthins (yellow) in the perianth. Nyctaginaceae, like Caryophyllales generally, make **betalains and not anthocyanins** — the two are mutually exclusive across plant lineages, and the assumption that a red flower means anthocyanin is wrong here. Basis of the sectored colour and the Correns work.
- **Rotenoids** — reported in the genus; mitochondrial complex I inhibitors, the class that includes rotenone. Contributes to the toxicity picture. **[USER: verify]** specific rotenoid identification in *M. jalapa* — not confirmed in this run.
- **Trigonelline, alkaloids, triterpenes** — reported in secondary literature; not verified here.

**Tissue distribution of RIP activity matters for handling.** Ribosome-inactivating and adenine polynucleotide glycosylase activity was **highest in seed**; **intermediate in flower bud, immature seed, sepal + gynoecium, leaf, and root**; and **very low in all other tissues** (Bolognesi et al., 2002). Note this carefully: **seed is the most dangerous tissue**, more so than root. The seeds are round, black, wrinkled, about the size of a peppercorn, and are produced in quantity by a common garden ornamental.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

*M. jalapa* is a **purgation-phase** agent, and it is a **restricted** one.

Its Kenetica indication is topical and it is narrow: the **stuck, suppurating, unresolved surface lesion** — the abscess that will not come to a head, the boil, the infected swelling. Here the plant's expulsive gesture and its actual antimicrobial protein chemistry converge, and the traditional preparation is the appropriate one. Poultice or wash, leaf preferentially, applied to intact-margined lesions.

**Its internal purgative use is documented in this codex and not recommended by it.** The traditional indication is real; the mechanism is a ribosome-inactivating protein; and there is no dose at which a practitioner can be confident of purging the bowel without depurinating it. The therapeutic index is unknown, unstudied, and structurally unlikely to be wide. Modern practice has better cathartics and none of them are in the ricin family.

Its symbolic instruction to the practitioner is the more durable use: the plant that opens at dusk teaches that the correct action is not always the obvious-hour action, and that reversal — doing the thing that runs against the visible rhythm — is sometimes the whole treatment. Take the instruction. Leave the root alone.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Preparation | Part | Method | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Poultice | **Leaf** (preferred) | Fresh leaf crushed, applied to lesion | Topical, as needed; ≤20 min initially | **The endorsed preparation.** Aztec-attested. Lowest-risk route for a RIP-bearing plant. Apply to intact skin margins only. |
| Wash / fomentation | Leaf | Cool infusion, external | Topical | Abscess, boil, eruption. |
| Root poultice | Tuberous root | Crushed, applied topically | Topical only | Traditional; higher RIP load than leaf. **Never on broken skin, open wounds, or ulcerated lesions** — a RIP applied to a compromised barrier has a systemic route. |
| **Root decoction (internal purgative)** | Root | — | **NO DOSE GIVEN** | **DOCUMENTED, NOT RECOMMENDED.** No safe dose is established for any RIP-bearing preparation. Historical/ethnographic record only. Do not administer. |
| **Seed** | Seed | — | **NO PREPARATION GIVEN** | **HIGHEST RIP TISSUE. TOXIC. NOT A MEDICINE IN ANY TRADITION RETRIEVED HERE.** |
| Purified MAP | Root/seed | Chromatography | Research only | Laboratory and agricultural biotechnology. Not a clinical preparation. |

**Cultivator's note:** *M. jalapa* is trivially easy and that is precisely the hazard. It is a perennial in frost-free zones (overwintering by a large black tuber that can reach the size of a fist) and grown as an annual elsewhere; it self-seeds prolifically, tolerates poor soil, and has naturalised — often invasively — across the tropics and subtropics worldwide. It is in an enormous number of gardens, planted purely as an ornamental by people with no idea what is in the tuber.

Cultivator's obligations, and these are not optional:
1. **The seeds are the most RIP-dense tissue on the plant, they are the size and colour of a peppercorn, and they fall on the ground where children are.** If children use the garden, deadhead before seed set. This is the single practical safety instruction of this entry.
2. **Label the tuber** if you lift and store it. A large anonymous tuber in a shed is a food-shaped object containing a ribosome-inactivating protein.
3. **Wear gloves** when lifting or cutting tubers, and do not handle with cuts on the hands.
4. **Check invasive status** before planting outside its native range.
5. Harvest leaf for the topical preparation, in the morning before the flowers open. Lift tubers in autumn if you must — but the codex's endorsed preparation does not require them.

The dusk-opening habit is worth cultivating for its own sake; it is a moth garden plant, and the scent at four o'clock is the whole argument for growing it.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the *M. jalapa* literature is **substantial but almost entirely about one protein**. There is a deep, high-quality structural biology and enzymology literature on MAP, and essentially **no clinical literature on the plant as a medicine**. Grade accordingly — this is a well-studied *molecule* attached to an unstudied *drug*.

**MAP is a type 1 ribosome-inactivating protein — GRADE: WELL-ESTABLISHED (definitively characterised).** Multiply confirmed across three decades and several independent groups. MAP consists of an RNA-glycosylase domain with no carbohydrate-binding domain, and — unusually among RIPs — it inactivates **both eukaryotic and prokaryotic ribosomes**, cleaving the N-glycosidic bond at **A2660 of 23S rRNA** in *E. coli*, where ricin and abrin A chains cannot (Nishida et al., *Toxins*, 2025; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/toxins17120575); Hartley et al., *FEBS Lett*, 1991; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0014-5793(91)81227-y)). The 2025 crystal structure at 2.1 Å establishes R171 as the key active-site residue for stable complex formation with the target adenine, and shows MAP binding the C-terminal domains of both eukaryotic P2-stalk and bacterial L12-stalk. MAP is immunologically distinct — it does not cross-react with other common RIPs (Ng et al., *Biochem Mol Biol Int*, 1993). This is not preliminary and not contested.

**Potency, quantified — GRADE: WELL-ESTABLISHED.** Native MAP inhibits rabbit reticulocyte protein synthesis with **IC₅₀ 3.5 nM**, roughly one-thirtieth the activity of ricin A chain; eliminating its disulfide bond by site-directed mutagenesis (C36/220S) raised activity ~22-fold to **IC₅₀ 0.16 nM** (Habuka et al., *J Biol Chem*, 1991). Read the comparator properly: "one-thirtieth of ricin A chain" is not reassurance. It places MAP squarely within the ricin family's order of magnitude.

**Enzymatic promiscuity — GRADE: WELL-SUPPORTED.** MAP depurinates not only rRNA in intact ribosomes but also poly(A), **DNA**, and tobacco mosaic virus RNA — and depurinated **autologous DNA more extensively than other polynucleotides**. The authors concluded the activity is better described as **adenine polynucleotide glycosylase** than rRNA N-glycosylase (Bolognesi et al., *J Biol Chem*, 2002; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1074/jbc.M111514200)). A protein that depurinates DNA is a genotoxicity question that no one has answered for this plant.

**⚠️ Abortifacient activity — GRADE: PRELIMINARY BUT SAFETY-CRITICAL.** Purified MAP demonstrated **abortifacient activity in pregnant mice**, alongside cell-free protein synthesis inhibition and antiproliferative effect on tumour cells (Wong et al., *Biochem Int*, 1992; PMID 1482397). This is a single older study and would ordinarily be graded weakly — but it is entirely consistent with the RIP class (trichosanthin, the α- and β-momorcharins and other type 1 RIPs are the classical abortifacient proteins), and the class-consistency raises the prior sharply. **Treat as a real hazard. This finding governs the pregnancy contraindication absolutely.**

**Antiviral activity (in plants) — GRADE: WELL-SUPPORTED, and note the species.** MAP-containing root extracts sprayed on test plants 24 h before inoculation inhibited **potato virus X, potato virus Y, potato leaf roll virus and potato spindle tuber viroid by almost 100%**, confirmed by infectivity assay and nucleic acid spot hybridisation; purified MAP matched the crude extract. Activity was seen against **mechanically transmitted viruses but not aphid-transmitted** ones (Vivanco et al., *Plant Dis*, 1999; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1094/PDIS.1999.83.12.1116)).

**Read this correctly, because the name invites the error.** "Mirabilis *antiviral* protein" is antiviral **in plants, applied to plant surfaces, against plant viruses, prophylactically**. It works because it inactivates ribosomes non-selectively at the site of entry. **This is not evidence that *M. jalapa* is an antiviral medicine for humans**, and the extremely large gap between "sprayed on a potato leaf" and "given to a person" is the whole distance a claim would have to travel. No such evidence was retrieved. **This is the entry's key null.**

**Antimicrobial (genus-level) — GRADE: PRELIMINARY, and it is the wrong species.** The RIPs ME1 and ME2 from *Mirabilis expansa* storage roots were active against *Pythium irregulare*, *Fusarium oxysporum solani*, *Alternaria solani*, *Trichoderma* spp., *Pseudomonas syringae*, *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* and others, with an additive antifungal effect; **ME2 showed high similarity to MAP** (Vivanco et al., *Plant Physiol*, 1999; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1104/pp.119.4.1447)). This is congener data, transferred on strong sequence homology, and it is the best mechanistic support available for the traditional topical abscess use. It is not a study of *M. jalapa*.

**Antiproliferative / anticancer — GRADE: PRELIMINARY, and beside the point.** MAP inhibits tumour cell proliferation (Wong et al., 1992). Every type 1 RIP does; killing dividing cells is what ribosome inactivation does. The RIP class has been pursued for decades as immunotoxin warheads — targeted, conjugated, delivered — which is a completely different proposition from a root decoction. **This is not an anticancer plant.**

**Human clinical evidence for any indication — GRADE: NONE. No trials. No human data.**

**Human toxicology — GRADE: NOT ESTABLISHED, which is itself the finding.** A dedicated PubMed search for *Mirabilis jalapa* root extract pharmacology toxicity returned **zero results**. There is no oral LD₅₀, no toxicokinetic profile, no characterisation of MAP's gastrointestinal absorption or systemic bioavailability in humans. For a plant containing a ricin-family protein and used traditionally as an internal purgative, **the absence of a toxicology literature is the most alarming fact in this entry.** No dose can be graded safe against no data.

**Purgative mechanism — GRADE: TRADITIONAL, mechanism unstudied.** No paper retrieved investigates the cathartic action. The RIP-mediated enterocyte hypothesis advanced in the Doctrinal Layer above is **inference from mechanism, clearly flagged as such, not a finding**. Rotenoid contribution is likewise unquantified.

**Cytoplasmic inheritance — GRADE: WELL-ESTABLISHED, historically.** Correns' 1909 variegation work. Not pharmacology; included because it is why this plant matters to science.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

| System | Classification |
|---|---|
| Aztec / Nahua (*tlaquilin*) | Cold; strongly evacuant; drawing and opening on the surface. Purgative and vulnerary |
| Chinese folk (*zǐ mòlì gēn*, post-1500 CE) | Sweet, bland, cool; clears damp-heat, promotes urination, disperses swelling and toxin. **Early modern adoption — NOT classical** |
| Indian folk (post-Columbian) | Cooling; purgative; topical for swelling. **No valid classical Ayurvedic attestation** |
| European (16th c. onward) | Adopted as ornamental; the "jalap" purgative name is a **misattribution** to *Ipomoea purga* |
| Kenetica | Cold, dry, third degree. Downward-draining, cutting, expelling. Purgation-phase, **restricted**. Endorsed topically only. |

The traditions agree, which is unusual and here unhelpful — they agree because they were all describing the same violent observable effect, and none of them knew what was producing it.

### Contraindications

**⚠️ CLASS WARNING: This plant contains a type 1 ribosome-inactivating protein in the structural family of ricin and abrin. Handle every recommendation below as governing, not advisory.**

- **PREGNANCY — ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION.** Purified MAP is **abortifacient in pregnant mice**, and the RIP class contains the classical abortifacient proteins. Do not use in any form, by any route, including topically, in pregnancy or in anyone who may become pregnant during treatment. This is not a precaution; it is a demonstrated activity.
- **INTERNAL USE — NOT RECOMMENDED AT ANY DOSE.** The traditional purgative use is documented here as history. There is no established human dose, no toxicology, no LD₅₀, and no reason to expect a wide therapeutic index for a ribosome-inactivating protein. The codex does not provide an internal dose and does not endorse one.
- **⚠️ SEEDS ARE THE HIGHEST-RIP TISSUE AND ARE A PAEDIATRIC HAZARD.** Highest ribosome-inactivating activity of any tissue on the plant. Peppercorn-sized, black, shed freely from a common garden ornamental. Deadhead before seed set where children have access. Suspected ingestion is a **medical emergency — seek immediate medical attention and name the plant**; RIP toxicity can be delayed in onset, and an initially well child is not a reassurance.
- **Broken skin, open wounds, ulcerated lesions — do not apply.** A RIP crossing a compromised barrier has a systemic route. Topical use is for intact-margined lesions.
- **Lactation — avoid.** No data.
- **Children — avoid therapeutically.** No data, and the seed hazard is the dominant paediatric concern.
- **Genotoxicity — unassessed.** MAP depurinates DNA, and depurinates autologous DNA more extensively than other polynucleotides. Nobody has followed this up in a mammalian system. Absence of evidence here is absence of study.
- **Handling** — MAP is **heat-stable and rigid**. Do not assume that cooking, drying or boiling destroys it. Glove when lifting or cutting tubers.
- **Do not confuse with *Ipomoea purga*** (true jalap, Convolvulaceae) despite the shared name. Different family, different chemistry, different drug.
- **Do not confuse with *Mirabilis expansa***, the Andean root crop *mauka* — which is **eaten**, and which also contains RIPs (ME1, ME2). The relationship between traditional processing and RIP inactivation in *M. expansa* is not addressed by any source retrieved here, and it should not be assumed that *M. jalapa* is edible by analogy. It is not.
- **Chronology contraindication (evidential):** do not accept, cite, or rely on any claimed pre-1500 CE Chinese or classical Ayurvedic source for this plant. The plant was in Mexico. The source is wrong.
