# Corydalis meifolia — lug.ngal, Lugnyal (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Corydalis meifolia, as the binomial printed in the scientific-name run of the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary against "Lugnyal" / "lug ngal" by column position, and as the first identification Clark 1995 prints for lug.ngal (printed "Corydalis, meifolia", with Linaria buriatica as the second)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. Both Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the line prints two. The extraction holds two rows: one keys Clark's line to the first identification (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 193, "Corydalis, meifolia; Linaria buriatica") and one keys the glossary's scientific-name column, with the note "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)" (line 313). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1106).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads:

"lug.ngal: Corydalis, meifolia: Linaria buriatica: [the white type grows on river banks and has a soft leaf and thin petiole. It is adhesive, with a green leaf and pale yellow flower. Corydalis has a sweet taste and cool power,] neutralizes poisons, reduces swellings of the limbs [and is outstanding in relieving pain caused by wind imbalances]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the white type, its habitat and leaf, the "sweet taste and cool power" and the closing "and is outstanding in relieving pain caused by wind imbalances". Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the two Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the clause "neutralizes poisons, reduces swellings of the limbs". The line gives no part used and no preparation. The word "Corydalis" in the commentary is Clark's own for the drug. *Linaria* belongs to a different family from *Corydalis*. Clark's name index prints "lug.ngal" (line 1758) among the *l* names.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (lines 5435 to 5454).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "L" block. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. The phonetic names are "Lalaphue Langthangtse / Lishi / Looh dhue dorje Lugchhung Lugmur Lugnyal Lugru mukpo Lugru serpo" (lines 5435 to 5437), and the Tibetan transliterations include "lug ngal" (line 5441), the same spelling as Clark's *lug.ngal*. The scientific names are "Cnidium monnieri" (line 5445), "Hyoscyamus niger L. Syzygium aromaticum Codonopsis thalictrifolia" (line 5446), "Aster strachei" (line 5447) and "Phlomis younghusbandii Corydalis meifolia Pedicularis siphonantha Pedicularis longiflora" (line 5448). Counting in order, "Lugnyal" is the seventh phonetic name and "Corydalis meifolia" is the seventh scientific name. The English column (lines 5450 to 5454) holds seven names against nine scientific names, and so cannot be matched place by place ("Mountain turkeycom" stands at line 5453, and the Codex does not assign it). The count can be checked on neighbours: "Lugchhung" stands fifth against *Aster strachei*, as Clark's *lug.chhung* line (1105) gives, and "Lalaphue" stands first against *Cnidium monnieri*, as Clark's *la.la.phud* line (896) gives. The pairing is positional and was not printed in a single row. [USER: verify]

*What the Men-Tsee-Khang running text does not say.* A search of the Men-Tsee-Khang text for "Lugnyal", "Lugngal" and "lug ngal" returned only the glossary lines. Its index lists "Lugmig", "Lugmur", "Lugru", "Lugru mukpo" and "Lugru serpo" (line 7428) and no "Lugnyal". The Men-Tsee-Khang text therefore gives this drug a name and a binomial and no taste, action or use, and Clark's line 1106 is the only line read that says what the drug does.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Corydalis meifolia*, *lug.ngal* and *Lugnyal* found no entry on this plant. I_1022 (*Aster strachei*) quotes the same glossary block and uses "Lugnyal" against *Corydalis meifolia* as a check on its count, and is not an entry on this plant. I_1257 (*Corydalis edulis*), I_1259 (*Corydalis melanochlora*) and I_1260 (*Corydalis nepalense*), written in this batch, are sibling rows of the same genus on other Tibetan names. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Corydalis* is a genus of the poppy family (Papaveraceae, in its fumitory subfamily) and that the genus is generally known to contain isoquinoline alkaloids. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained, because its Latin name is not settled. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Clark records a drug that "neutralizes poisons, reduces swellings of the limbs" and, in the commentary, "is outstanding in relieving pain caused by wind imbalances" (line 1106). These are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation for this drug. Clark's commentary describes a white type with soft leaf and thin petiole but does not say which part is taken, and no quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes this drug by what it does, which is to neutralize poisons and reduce swellings of the limbs. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this drug places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: Clark's line was read in full and the Men-Tsee-Khang text names the drug only in its glossary. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a poison and swelling drug in Clark, its Latin name is not settled, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text lists it by name only. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug, although Clark's line classes it as an antidote to poisons, which is a use and not a safety statement. The identity is uncertain and the genus *Corydalis* is generally known to contain alkaloids, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Poisoning needs urgent medical care.
