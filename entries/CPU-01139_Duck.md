# Duck — "Duck" (Bryan's English, 1930); "Duck's Food"
## *The duck as it appears in the Ebers Papyrus in C. P. Bryan's English. Bryan's list prints "Duck" with no Egyptian word and no binomial; the identification is Bryan's English, and the papyrus passages that name a duck are very few* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The text on disk is C. P. Bryan's 1930 English *The Papyrus Ebers* (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`), an OCR file in which Bryan translates and also summarises and comments. In his chapter VII, "Organic Remedies", Bryan says that a full list of the animal materia medica "would not be possible since (as with many of the plant and mineral substances) the identity of a considerable number of the animals mentioned is still beyond us. But even so the list of those animals whose identity is not in doubt, and who were called upon to sacrifice themselves in the cause of the Healing Art, is a formidable one" (lines 1388-1393). The list that follows includes, in the line printed at line 1398, "Goose Duck Stag Gazelle Ram Goat". That line is the whole locus of the batch: the row in `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` reads "Bryan 1930, Ch. VII Organic Remedies (animals) (line 1398)", with the note "Bryan's English identification; no Egyptian word or binomial given in this list".

Bryan then lists the parts the Egyptians took from animals in general (hair, feathers, horns, hoof, hide, eyes, ears, teeth, brain, blood, bile, bones, marrow, liver, spleen, guts, uterus, vulva, phallus, testicles, semen, fat, tallow, flesh, shell, wings, excrement, urine, eggs, milk, toes, tail; lines 1408-1418). That is a list for all the animals together, and it does not say which of these parts came from the duck. For the ass, and for the cow, Bryan goes on to name the parts each gave (line 1427; see III_77); he gives no such sentence for the duck.

The only other place in the file where I found the word "duck" is "Duck's Food" (line 3655), in a column of ingredients inside the passage on examining a person with an illness of the abdomen (lines 3615-3660). The column reads "Gum-drops / Fresh lead-earth ... Cook in Oil and Honey and let the patient take for four days. If it afterwards moves under thy fingers like grains of sand, and all his limbs burn with the itch— / Fermenting Bread / Some Horn / Duck's Food", and then a line ending "(uncertain)". The OCR has broken the layout of the two-column page, so I cannot tell which remedy the "Duck's Food" belongs to, or whether Bryan's "Duck's Food" is a plant or something taken from the animal; it is a name, with no gloss. I do not read it as a use of the duck's body.

The goose, which Bryan lists beside the duck, is a different matter: "Goose-grease", "Goose-oil", "Goose's eggs" and "Blood-of-a-Goose" occur repeatedly in the file (for example lines 2357, 2644, 3032, 3185, 3199). Those are the goose, and are not carried over to the duck.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms duck meat and fat are made of protein and fat (the fat is relatively abundant in domestic ducks), and feathers are keratin. Nothing more specific can be said without a source.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The papyrus, as Bryan gives it, names the duck among the animals "called upon to sacrifice themselves" and gives it no remedy of its own that I could read. The Codex gives no regimen built on the duck. Duck is an ordinary food in many places and needs no protocol from this Codex; wild waterfowl are protected in many places.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources retrieved give no preparation for the duck. The one ingredient name, "Duck's Food" (line 3655), has no stated preparation of its own; the surrounding remedy is to be cooked in oil and honey and taken for four days (line 3647), but the OCR does not show that "Duck's Food" belongs to that remedy. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Raw or undercooked duck carries bacterial risk (salmonella and campylobacter), and wild waterfowl can carry avian influenza viruses. No part of the duck is an established treatment for any complaint named in the papyrus. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Egyptian (EP):** None. The Ebers material is organised by complaint, and Bryan's English prints no qualities, tastes or temperaments for the duck.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. For the duck there is a name in a list of animals and one ambiguous ingredient name, and nothing more. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); the passages read give no recitation with the duck, but since Bryan summarises and the OCR is damaged, that is no proof the papyrus gives none. No deity is attributed.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The duck is a word in a list. The honest use of the entry is as a record of how little the retrieved text says. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** raw or undercooked duck carries bacterial risk; wild waterfowl can carry avian influenza and other infections, so avoid handling sick or dead birds.
- **Allergy:** some people are allergic to poultry meat, feathers or eggs.
- **Ethics and law:** wild ducks and other waterfowl are protected or regulated in many places, with closed seasons and licences. The Codex does not recommend harming any animal for these uses.
