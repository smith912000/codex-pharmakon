# Bursera gummifolia — Mastichelaion (Dioscorides 1-51), "oil of mastic"

## *Bursera gummifolia* (the name printed on the Faguet plate caption under chapter 1-51; the translators' SUGGESTED line for the chapter gives *Schinus molle*, *Pistacia lentiscus*, *Bursera gummifera* and *Sideroxylon mastichodendron*) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry.** This row is named only by Dioscorides (DMM). It was added 2026-10-09 from the extraction list (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) at the locus 1-51, line 3952 of the text file.

**Identity note (read before the rest).** The spine name *Bursera gummifolia* is not a binomial that the translators propose for this chapter. It is the caption of a plate, "Bursera gummifolia after FAGUET — 1878", which stands under the 1-51 heading at line 3954 and is printed a second time at line 4127, in the pages before chapter 1-89 (SCHINOS). The SUGGESTED line of 1-51 (line 3956) reads: "Schinus molle, Pistacia lentiscus, Bursera gummifera, Sideroxylon mastichodendron — Mastic Tree, Pepper Tree, Herb Mastic, Oil of Mastic". That line has *Bursera gummifera*, not *gummifolia*, and it is the translators' suggestion and not Dioscorides' identification. The neighbouring chapters 1-50, 1-89 and 1-90 carry the same SUGGESTED line, with the note "Schinus molle is now an American genus; Bursera gummifera is now a West Indian tree [Loudon]" (for example lines 3948 and 4218). The material the chapter actually describes is an oil made from mastic. No text read identifies the source plant as a *Bursera*, and the translators' own note calls the genus American, a reason to treat it as a later identification. **Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). The chapter is in Book One, in the section on ointments.

*1-51 MASTICHELAION* (heading at line 3952; text at line 3957). The whole chapter reads: "Mastichinum is made from mastic pounded into small pieces. It is good for disorders in the womb — gently warming, astringent, softening. It is also good laid on the stomach for hardened swellings, for the abdominal cavity and dysentery; and for cleaning away spots on the face, and causing a good colour. The best is compounded in the isle of Chios." The chapter gives no description of a plant and no method beyond "pounded into small pieces". It is a product chapter (an oil or ointment, *mastichinum*), whose material is mastic.

*Related chapters read.* 1-50 SCHINELAION (line 3945) describes the oil of the fruit of the same tree: "Schininum [lentiscinum] is made from ripe berries the same as oil of bay, and thickened before use. It heals parasitic skin diseases on beasts of burden and dogs [veterinary]. It is effective mixed with suppositories, remedies to remove fatigue, and medications for leprosy. It also stops sweating." 1-89 SCHINOS (line 4211) describes the tree: "a well-known tree that is wholly astringent in its fruit and leaves", with a boiled juice of bark, root and leaves taken for "throwing up blood, discharges from the stomach, and dysentery", for uterine bleeding and prolapse, "generally it may be used instead of acacia and hypocistis [1-127]". 1-90 SCHININE RETINE (line 4221) gives the resin: "A resin called lentiscina comes from lentiscum, and also some called mastic", taken for vomiting of blood and an old cough, mixed into tooth powders and ointments for the face, chewed for sweet breath, and, at the end of the chapter (line 4231), "The best and most abundant grows in Chios ... It is adulterated by frankincense and resin of pine cones being mixed with it." These are separate chapters, and are reported here only because 1-51 depends on the mastic they describe. They are not attributed to this row.

### Active Components

Mastic as a material is the resin of *Pistacia lentiscus*, which is an aromatic terpenoid resin (a gum-resin of the Anacardiaceae). *Bursera* species are generally known as resin-bearing trees of the family Burseraceae. Which plant, if any, the plate caption's *B. gummifolia* corresponds to, and its composition, was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records an oil of mastic as a warming, astringent, softening preparation for the womb, the stomach, hardened swellings, dysentery and the skin of the face. The Codex gives no protocol for it. The entry's botanical name is a plate caption, so no practice can be tied to a plant called *Bursera gummifolia*.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation is the one printed: mastic "pounded into small pieces" and made into *mastichinum* (1-51). The routes given are laid on the stomach, and used for womb disorders (the chapter does not say how). No quantity, vehicle or oil is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Mastic resin from *Pistacia lentiscus* is a long-used chewing resin, and resins in general are astringent and aromatic, which is a general statement. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. What the chapter offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention in the spec's sense: provenance ("The best is compounded in the isle of Chios") and, in the neighbouring 1-90, a sensory assay of the resin ("clear and similar in whiteness to Tyrrhenian wax — full, dry, crumbly and sweet-smelling") and a warning about adulteration. It assigns the material no status as a long-term or life-nourishing substance. The link to a plant called *Bursera gummifolia* is a plate caption and is not Dioscorides'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice. It records a topical and gynaecological ointment for a physician's use and a provenance check. The abstentive answer is the correct one, and the plant name on this row is in any case unreliable.

### Contraindications

No contraindication is printed in the chapter. As a general caution, resins and resin-bearing plants cause allergic contact dermatitis in some people, and a preparation "for disorders in the womb" is not advice for use in pregnancy. Do not infer from this entry that any *Bursera* species is a safe source of mastic.
