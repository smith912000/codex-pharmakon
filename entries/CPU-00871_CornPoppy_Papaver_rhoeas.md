# Corn Poppy — Field Poppy / Flanders Poppy / Coquelicot / Red Poppy
## *Papaver rhoeas* L. — Corn Rose, Flanders Poppy, Coquelicot, Corn Poppy; native names: Arabic *ḫashḫāsh aḥmar* / *shaqā'iq al-nu'mān* (the "anemone-poppy" of Nu'mān), Greek *mḗkōn rhoiás* (the "flowing" or "falling" poppy), Latin *papaver erraticum* / *papaver rhoeas* | Family: Papaveraceae

**Tradition Tags:** EP, AT, DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification (Galenic/Unani):** Cold in the first degree, Moist in the first-to-second degree (*bārid raṭb*) — a gentle refrigerant-relaxant, distinct from the opium poppy's far colder, narcotic action

### Origin & Tradition

*Papaver rhoeas* is the scarlet weed of disturbed ground — the poppy that follows the plough, that reddens fallow fields and, famously, the churned earth of the Western Front. It is a botanical cousin of the opium poppy (*P. somniferum*, treated separately in this Codex) but is a fundamentally different medicine: it does **not** contain morphine, codeine, or thebaine, and no traditional culture ever used it as a true narcotic. The classical record is remarkably consistent across four traditions: a *mild* soother — of coughs, of restlessness, of wakeful children — and a food-safe red colourant.

**BM (Assyrian / Mesopotamian cuneiform medicine):** The Mesopotamian materia medica records several poppies under names built on the sign for *ḫul* / poppy-like plants, and red field poppies of the *Papaver* group grew abundantly across the Fertile Crescent as arable weeds. Tablets from the Nineveh medical corpus prescribe poppy preparations for pain, sleeplessness, and "crying" (restless, wakeful) conditions. Which passages refer specifically to *P. rhoeas* versus *P. somniferum* versus *P. setigerum* cannot be resolved with certainty from the surviving lexical lists, and no secure Akkadian binomial for the field poppy should be asserted [USER: verify against a current Assyrian materia medica concordance before citing any specific tablet]. What is defensible is the general Mesopotamian recognition of a poppy-group of "quieting" plants.

**AT (Avicenna, *al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb*, c. 1025 CE):** Avicenna distinguishes multiple poppies (*khashkhāsh*) by colour and strength. The wild red poppy (*khashkhāsh aḥmar* / *barrī*) is classed as cooling and moistening but **markedly weaker** than the white/black opium poppy, which he places among the strong cold narcotics capable of dangerous stupefaction. The red poppy he directs toward gentle promotion of sleep, soothing of "hot" coughs and pleuritic irritation, and calming of restlessness — a *mulaṭṭif* (softening, demulcent) rather than a *mukhaddir* (true stupefacient). This graded distinction is the doctrinal heart of the entry: the tradition already knew that the red field poppy and the opium poppy were not the same drug.

**DMM (Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica*, c. 50–70 CE):** Dioscorides describes *mḗkōn rhoiás* — the poppy "that flows," so named for its readily shed scarlet petals — recommending a decoction or syrup of the petals and heads as a mild hypnotic and for soothing inflammation. He is explicit that its power is far below that of the opium-yielding poppy (*mḗkōn hēmeros*, from which *opós*/opium is drawn). His petal-syrup preparation is the direct ancestor of the European *sirop de coquelicot* / *syrupus rhoeados*, a pharmacy staple into the nineteenth century as a colourant and gentle pectoral for children's coughs.

**EP (Ebers Papyrus / Egyptian tradition, c. 1550 BCE):** The Egyptian medical papyri include poppy-group remedies (*špnn* is the debated candidate term) used for calming, for crying children, and as ingredients in compound sedative and analgesic preparations. As with the Mesopotamian record, secure species assignment between the field poppy and the opium poppy is not possible from the orthography alone, and the strong "quieting a screaming child" indication historically attached most firmly to opium-bearing material. The field poppy's Egyptian role is best stated as part of a poppy-group of cooling, calming plants rather than as a documented *P. rhoeas* monograph [USER: verify species attribution].

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The doctrine is *coldness without narcosis* — a refrigerant that quiets without extinguishing. Map this onto the chemistry and it holds with unusual precision. The opium poppy's cold, stupefying "first degree of poison" is the pharmacology of morphinan alkaloids acting at µ-opioid receptors; the field poppy contains **no morphinans at all**. Its characteristic alkaloids are *rhoeadine*-type isoquinolines (rhoeadine, rhoeagenine, and relatives) plus small amounts of others — a chemical class with, at most, mild non-opioid CNS-depressant activity in animal work and no addictive narcotic action. So the Galenic intuition that the red poppy is "the same kind of cold, but far gentler" is, at the molecular level, correct: same family, same broad sedative *gestalt*, entirely different and much weaker receptor pharmacology. The second doctrinal thread is the **redness** itself. The classical eye read the blood-scarlet petal as a signature of the blood and the heart; the modern reading is that the colour is cyanidin-based anthocyanin pigment (notably mecocyanin), a water-soluble flavonoid with antioxidant rather than sedative action — the pigment that made the petals a valued food and syrup colourant is pharmacologically distinct from whatever mild calming the plant offers. The Remembrance symbolism of the Flanders poppy (John McCrae's 1915 "In Flanders Fields", and the poppy's post-war adoption as a memorial emblem) is genuinely modern and cultural, not pharmacological — but it rhymes with the ancient signature of red-poppy-as-blood-of-the-fallen, a continuity worth noting without over-reading.

### Active Components

- **Rhoeadine and rhoeagenine (rhoeadine-type isoquinoline alkaloids)** — The alkaloids characteristic of *P. rhoeas*, concentrated in the latex, capsule, and green aerial parts rather than the mature petals. Chemically isoquinoline-derived but **not** morphinan; associated with mild, non-opioid CNS-depressant / sedative activity in animal models. They are the plausible basis for the plant's gentle "quieting" reputation, but the potency is low and the human evidence is essentially absent.
- **Additional minor alkaloids** — trace papaverrubines, coptisine, and related isoquinolines have been reported in the genus; none confer opioid activity.
- **Cyanidin-based anthocyanins (mecocyanin)** — Water-soluble red pigment of the petals; antioxidant; the constituent responsible for the classical petal-syrup colour and for food use. Not sedative.
- **Mucilage and demulcent polysaccharides** — Contribute to the soothing, film-forming "pectoral" action on an irritated throat that underlies the cough-syrup indication.
- **Meconic-acid-poor profile** — Notably, the field poppy lacks the morphine/meconic-acid load of opium latex; this is a defining *negative* feature and the single most important safety point of the entry.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. `[RESET]` **Gentle evening down-regulation.** A petal infusion or traditional syrup as a mild, non-narcotic aid to winding down after high-arousal training days or during over-reached, sympathetically "wired" states. The effect is subtle — think chamomile-tier, not hypnotic-drug-tier — and should be framed to the athlete as such to avoid disappointment or dose-escalation.
2. `[RESET]` **Dry, tickling cough and throat irritation.** The demulcent mucilage plus mild sedative fraction soothe an unproductive cough that disrupts sleep during illness or hard training blocks — the plant's most historically consistent use.
3. `[CYCLE-OFF]` **Restlessness and light sleep-onset support.** During recovery phases marked by irritability and shallow sleep, a low-dose petal preparation can serve as a benign, non-dependence-forming ritual. It is explicitly **not** a replacement for addressing sleep architecture, and not a sedative to lean on nightly at escalating doses (see Contraindications).

### Scientific Correlation

The honest evidence grade for *P. rhoeas* as a sedative is **LOW — one methodologically clean animal study, essentially no human trials, and a mechanism that is real but undefined.**

According to PubMed, the anchor study is Soulimani R, Younos C, Jarmouni-Idrissi S, Bousta D, Khallouki F, Laila A (2001), "Behavioral and pharmaco-toxicological study of *Papaver rhoeas* L. in mice," *Journal of Ethnopharmacology* 74(3):265–274 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0378-8741(00)00383-4)). A lyophilised ethanolic/aqueous **petal** extract reduced locomotor, exploratory and postural behaviour in mice (staircase and free-exploratory tests), confirming a genuine sedative effect at ~400 mg/kg. Three findings from this paper are decisive for the doctrine:
- **Thin-layer chromatography of the petals detected anthocyanins but no alkaloids** — i.e. the sedative petal fraction is not obviously alkaloid-driven, and the red pigment travels with the active material.
- The sedation was **not antagonised by flumazenil (benzodiazepine/GABA-A site), naloxone (opioid), sulpiride (dopaminergic D2), or atropine (cholinergic)** — so, critically, the mechanism is **not opioid** (reinforcing the opium contrast) and also not classical benzodiazepine-GABAergic; it remains undefined.
- The extract **did not potentiate an infra-hypnotic dose of pentobarbital**, further separating it from strong CNS depressants. Reported toxicity was low-to-moderate for a crude extract (LD10 ≈ 2000 mg/kg, LD50 ≈ 4000 mg/kg intraperitoneally in mice).

This is a near-ideal result for the Codex's purposes: it *confirms* the ancient "gentle quieting" claim, *refutes* any conflation with opium (naloxone-insensitive), and is *honest* that the responsible chemistry and receptor target are not yet pinned down. Rhoeadine-type alkaloids remain the leading candidate for the plant's sedative reputation on chemical grounds, but the petal data show the story is not that simple, and no human efficacy trial supports a defined dose. The Remembrance symbolism, by contrast, has no pharmacological content and is presented purely as cultural history.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Cold in the first degree, Moist in the first-to-second degree; *mulaṭṭif* (softening/demulcent) and mildly *munawwim* (sleep-promoting) — expressly graded weaker than opium poppy. **Attested** (Avicenna, Dioscorides).
- **TCM:** No classical Chinese monograph for *P. rhoeas* specifically; the opium poppy capsule (*yīng sù ké*, *P. somniferum*) is the genus member in the Chinese pharmacopoeia. **Not attested** for the field poppy — do not conflate.
- **Ayurvedic:** *P. rhoeas* is not a classical Ayurvedic dravya; the opium poppy (*ahiphena*) is. A *rasa/guṇa/vīrya/vipāka* profile for the field poppy is **not attested** and is not invented here.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian (EP).** The Egyptian medical corpus records poppy-group preparations (the debated *špnn*) for calming and for quieting restless, crying children, but the material is indication-organised: it registers what was done for a complaint, not what the plant does to a person's *ka*, *ib* or *akh*. No operative *heka* recitation is attested for the field poppy specifically, and — as the entry itself states — the field poppy cannot be securely separated from the opium poppy in the orthography, so any subtle-body or vitalising role assigned to *P. rhoeas* here would be a modern hypothesis dressed as attestation. There is no seat-of-the-heart doctrine or vital-endowment framing recorded for this species. Cultivation status: **(c) absent** — a cooling, calming plant of the poppy group, no cultivation framing attested.

**Assyrian (AT).** The Mesopotamian materia medica recognises a poppy-group of "quieting" plants, prescribed for pain, sleeplessness and restless wakefulness, and illness in this tradition is classified partly by the causal agency a substance opposes. But the standing blocking warning is in force for every Assyrian substance identification and tablet reference, and — as the entry notes — no secure Akkadian binomial for the field poppy can be asserted from the surviving lexical lists. Nothing in the retrieved record assigns *P. rhoeas* a status with respect to the person's vital constitution rather than a presenting complaint. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides gives *mḗkōn rhoiás* a precise therapeutic account — the "flowing" poppy of the readily shed scarlet petals, its petal decoction or syrup a mild hypnotic and demulcent, expressly graded far below the opium-yielding *mḗkōn hēmeros*. Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system into which a cultivation status could be assigned. What the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention: distinguishing this poppy from its narcotic cousin by petal and habit, knowing which part (petal, not latex or capsule) carries the food-safe gentle action, assaying the syrup. That graded discrimination is a real practice of provenance and sensory judgement, not a vital-body doctrine. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** Avicenna classes the wild red poppy (*khashkhāsh aḥmar* / *barrī*) as cold and moist and, decisively, as a *mulaṭṭif* (softening, demulcent) that gently promotes sleep and soothes hot coughs — markedly weaker than the *mukhaddir* (true stupefacient) white and black opium poppies he ranks among the dangerous cold narcotics. This graded distinction is a genuine doctrinal achievement, but it is a therapeutic grading, not a cultivation status. The strongest cultivation idea in the Unani corpus — the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture as the finite vital endowment whose depletion is ageing — is a frame of the whole regimen; it is not a role the tradition assigns to this gentle refrigerant, and no *mufarrih* (cardiac, *ruh*-strengthening) standing is claimed for it. **(b) framed:** attested and carefully graded therapeutically, with no cultivation status sourced.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does very little "cultivation" with the corn poppy. Across all four traditions it is a mild, short-term refrigerant-demulcent — an evening tisane to take the edge off a wired, over-reached state, or a petal syrup for a dry cough that spoils sleep. It builds nothing, tonifies nothing, and stores nothing; the honest framing to give an athlete is chamomile-tier, not drug-tier, and the toxicity gradient runs the wrong way (petals benign, latex and green capsules not), so the temptation to escalate dose or switch plant parts is precisely the move the tradition's own grading warns against. The one durable discipline here is Dioscorides': know that this is not opium, and know your plant part.

### Contraindications

- **CRITICAL — do not confuse with opium poppy (*Papaver somniferum*).** *P. rhoeas* contains **no morphine, codeine, or thebaine** and is not a narcotic or an opioid substitute. Equally, the reverse must be respected: this entry does **not** license use of *P. somniferum* material. Do not represent field-poppy preparations as delivering opioid analgesia; they do not, and the sedation is naloxone-insensitive.
- **Alkaloid-bearing parts (latex, unripe capsules, seedlings/foliage) are mildly toxic and should not be self-dosed.** The green plant and latex carry the rhoeadine-type alkaloid load; livestock poisoning from grazing large amounts of foliage is documented. Overuse of alkaloid-rich parts is unwise and has no established safe human dose [USER: verify].
- **Children:** although the traditional syrup was a paediatric cough remedy, modern practice is cautious with any poppy-group preparation in young children; defer to current paediatric guidance rather than historical dosing.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** insufficient safety data for medicinal doses; restrict to culinary/colourant quantities or avoid [USER: verify].
- **Sedative stacking:** the effect is mild, but do not combine medicinal doses with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other CNS depressants on the assumption of "herbal = inert."
- **Escalation caution:** because the effect is gentle, users may be tempted to increase dose or switch to stronger plant parts — precisely the wrong move given the toxicity gradient toward the alkaloid-rich tissues.
