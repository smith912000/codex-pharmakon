# Rheum spiciforme — chhu.rtsa, Chhu tsa, chu rtsa (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Rheum spiciforme, the first of two binomials Clark 1995 prints for chhu.rtsa (then Rheum officinale), with male, female and neuter types in the commentary; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text prints Chhu tsa and no Rheum spiciforme* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and under this binomial on Clark's edition alone.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the other texts on disk for *Rheum* found no species name for this plant. Yang 1998 prints "Da Huang (Radix Et Rhizoma Rhei)" at line 3222, a Shennong drug named at genus level, and footnote 228 (line 3670) offers "Radix Rhei Undulati" as a possible identity for a different Shennong medicinal; neither names *Rheum spiciforme*, and the Codex does not attribute the Shennong drug to this entry. The Dioscorides text prints *Rheum* only in translators' SUGGESTED lines for a Greek drug (lines 6524 to 6525, "Rheum officinale — Wild Rhubarb"), which is not read as this plant. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 216) the row is keyed by the Tibetan name *chhu.rtsa*, with the identification "Rheum spiciforme; Rheum officinale" and the locus "Clark 1995 (line 1140)". The spine row (CPU-01779) took the **first** binomial, *Rheum spiciforme*, and not the later one. Clark's second binomial, *Rheum officinale*, has its own entry in the folder (I_304) with a different tag line. Clark's Latin index prints *Rheum spiciforme* (line 1639) and, next to it in the alphabetical run, *Rheum webbianum*.

**Identity doubt.** Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" and (line 68) that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect". The Men-Tsee-Khang text prints a different species against the same sound, as set out below. The Tibetan name does not fix the species, and the Codex does not choose among them. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (lines 1137 to 1141).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). A page number ("112") separates the entry from the preceding drug, *lchum.rtsa* (line 1137). It reads: "chhu.rtsa: Rheum spiciforme: Rheum officinale: [has seven names. The male type has a hollow petiole, large, thick, green leaf, known as tha.chhu.ra, and a yellow, three-sided ear, or fruit. The female type has a red, solid petiole, small inflorescence and a leaf similar to the above, known as rdza.chhu.rar. The neuter type is of two (sub-species, one of which has a large leaf and short petiole, whilst the other has a short, red petiole and hard leaf)]. Rheum purges septic conditions, dries [accumulations of] fluid in wounds, [cures phlegm disorders and brown phlegm, and acts as a test for poison]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, so the seven names and the three types are the commentary layer, and so are "cures phlegm disorders and brown phlegm, and acts as a test for poison". Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the two Latin names and "Rheum purges septic conditions, dries [accumulations of] fluid in wounds". In chapter 21, "The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines" (heading at line 1205), line 1225 reads: "The category of medicines inducing purgation includes (long-tipped) Chebulic myrobalan, Castor Oil seed, Cassia pods, Euphorbia sp., Physic nut (Baliospermum montanum), Euphorbia pallassii, Rheum emodi (a type of rhubarb), Lactuca dissecta (a type of larkspur), Physalis peruviana, Rheum nobile, Stellera chamaejasme and chhu.rtsa (Rheum spiciforme)." Clark's name index lists *chhu.rtsa* (line 1692).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (read through the name, not the binomial).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225). Line 3495: "Chum tsa is a laxative that cleanses hot disorder combined with poisoning, hot disorder of the vessel organs and baekan disorders. Chhu tsa eliminates infection and helps to dry abscesses." In chapter 21 (colophon at line 3689), line 3661 lists both names among "the group of medicines that are purgatives": "Arura, Dhenda, Dhong ga, Shri khenda, Dhurji, Thamu, Chum tsa, Ngonbu, Tronbu, Tsi takmo, Rechagpa and Chhu tsa". The index lists "Chhutsa 211,223" and "Chum tsa 211,223" (line 7296). "Baekan" is a Tibetan term that the editors keep untranslated (note at line 686). The glossary (lines 5166 to 5177) prints the phonetic names "Chhu tsa Chum tsa" and "Cinshingnama", the transliterations "chu rtsa lcum rtsa" and "srin shing sna ma", the scientific names "Rheum emodi", "Rheum palmatum L." and "Jasminum officinale", and the English names "Rhubarb East asian rhubarb" and "White jasmine". Counted in order, "Chhu tsa" is first, against *Rheum emodi* and "Rhubarb", and "Chum tsa" second, against *Rheum palmatum*. That is the reverse of Clark, whose *lchum.rtsa* takes *Rheum emodi* first and whose *chhu.rtsa* takes *Rheum spiciforme*. The glossary does not print the pairing in one row and prints no *Rheum spiciforme*. [USER: verify]

The two editions agree in kind: Clark's drug "purges septic conditions" and "dries fluid in wounds", and the Men-Tsee-Khang text's *Chhu tsa* "eliminates infection and helps to dry abscesses", and both place it in a purgative list. The Codex reads Clark's *chhu.rtsa* and the glossary's *Chhu tsa* as one drug from the spelling and that agreement; neither text says so. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Rheum spiciforme* and *chhu.rtsa* found no entry on this plant. I_1175 (*Rheum emodi*, *lchum.rtsa*) quotes line 1140 to tell the two Tibetan names apart. I_1176 (*Rheum nobile*), I_1304 (*Jatropha glandulifera*), I_1306 (*Lactuca dissecta*) and I_1333 (*Physalis peruviana*) quote the purgation list of line 1225 for their own drugs. I_304 (Medicinal Rhubarb, *Rheum officinale*; tags DMM, FMT) and I_305 (Tangut Rhubarb, *Rheum tanguticum*; tag FMT) are entries on other species, and I_304 carries a general Tibetan paragraph on *chu-rtsa* with no line reference. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general, well-established fact that *Rheum* is a genus of the knotweed family (Polygonaceae) and that rhubarb roots of the medicinal species are known to contain anthraquinone compounds with a laxative action. The lines read do not say what *Rheum spiciforme* or the Tibetan drug contains, and nothing is stated here about its composition.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a purgative that "purges septic conditions, dries [accumulations of] fluid in wounds" (Clark, line 1140) and "eliminates infection and helps to dry abscesses" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3495), and they list it in a purgation category (Clark, line 1225; Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3661). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial through Clark's first of two names, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for infection, abscess or wounds, which need medical care, and no dose for a purgative.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary describes petioles, leaves and fruit of three types and does not say which part is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's line 1140 and the Men-Tsee-Khang lines read (3495, 3661, 5166 to 5177) print no taste, power or post-digestive taste for this drug. The neighbouring Clark entry, *lchum.rtsa* (line 1137), prints "sour taste and coarse power" for a different drug, and these are not carried over. What is printed is a place in the purgation categories (Clark, line 1225; Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3661) and the three types (male, female, neuter) of the commentary.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it purges septic conditions and dries fluid in wounds, and the chapter 21 lists place it among the purgatives. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names (*chhu.rtsa*, *Chhu tsa*, *Rheum*) found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, colophon at line 3950), and the herb entries of Clark's chapter 20 that mention essence extraction (lines 971, 1043, 1078, 1103, 1121 and 1144) do not include it. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name more securely than to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a purgative for septic conditions in the Tibetan lists, with no preparation and no dose, and a purge is a medical act and not a cultivation. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug beyond Clark's commentary note that it "acts as a test for poison", which is not a safety statement. It is a general fact that purgative rhubarb preparations cause cramping and diarrhoea, can disturb body salts if misused, and are not given in pregnancy or to children without medical advice; none of that is shown for this species in the lines read. Infection and abscess need medical care, the identity is open between species, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
