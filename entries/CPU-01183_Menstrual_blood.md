# Menstrual blood — "Womb blood" (Clark 1995, rGyud-bzhi Explanatory Tantra ch.20) / "Menstrual blood" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *Human menstrual blood; Clark prints it as "Womb blood", the Men-Tsee-Khang translation as "Menstrual blood"; neither prints the Tibetan word or a binomial in the lines read* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The substance is human, not animal in the strict sense; it is shelved in Volume III because the Tibetan materia medica files it with the animal-origin drugs. In the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`) opens the animal section with the line "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin. The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (about lines 1148-1149). Under the heading Blood, the text runs through deer, goat, wild yak and antelope, pig, donkey and rooster's-comb blood and then reads (line 1176): "Womb blood: constricts the mouths of the channels, regenerates flesh [around wounds, and is beneficial for warts]." The square brackets are Clark's own insertions, marking words he takes from the commentary and not from the root verse. The list row that created this entry, `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 360, reads "Menstrual blood" with the source "rGyud-bzhi, Explanatory Tantra ch.20 (Efficacy of Medicinal Substances)".

The second English translation on disk prints the same item by a different name. The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3544) reads: "Menstrual blood stops bleeding and facilitates the growth of normal tissue." The two translations therefore agree that the blood is a haemostatic of a kind and a promoter of tissue growth; Clark adds "constricts the mouths of the channels" and "beneficial for warts", the Men-Tsee-Khang text adds "stops bleeding". The equation "womb blood" = "menstrual blood" rests on the position of the item in the blood list and on the Men-Tsee-Khang rendering; I did not see the Tibetan, so the identification is the translators'. The same two books describe the blood in the body. In Clark's embryology (line 238) "In a woman between the ages of twelve and fifty, each month blood accumulates from the essential nutriment. For three days (each time) this dark, odorless blood is expelled by the downwards voiding wind through the door of the womb and it descends through two large channels (uterine tubes)." The Men-Tsee-Khang text (line 1269) says that for conception the menstrual blood "should be such that it resembles the sap of Tsoe and hare blood, whose stain can easily be cleaned with washing", and (line 1276) that conception does not take place during the three days of menstruation. These passages tell us how the tradition understood the fluid; they do not recommend ingesting or applying it.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, menstrual fluid is a mixture of blood and shed uterine lining, with cervical and vaginal secretions. Blood supplies water, red cells, plasma proteins (including clotting factors such as fibrinogen), iron in haemoglobin and electrolytes. Menstrual fluid can carry any blood-borne or genital-tract pathogen present in the person from whom it comes, among them hepatitis B and C viruses and HIV.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. This is not a recommended practice, and the Codex does not offer it as one. The Tibetan text records the item as an external or internal drug for bleeding and for flesh growth; that is what the line says and the Codex repeats it as a record of what the tradition wrote. Nothing here supports collecting, using or selling menstrual blood. The reasons are plain: it is a human body fluid that can carry blood-borne infection; its use on wounds invites infection; it is not a tested haemostatic; consent, privacy and dignity are engaged whenever a person's body fluid is handled by someone else; and in many jurisdictions the sale or transfer of human body material is regulated. A person with heavy or prolonged bleeding should seek medical care. No modern protocol is invented here.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Blood clots through platelets and the clotting cascade; haemostasis on a wound is a function of fresh clot and pressure, and material applied from another person adds infection risk without adding a known benefit. Menstrual fluid is not a recognised therapeutic agent in modern medicine. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The substance is attested in the Explanatory Tantra ch.20 animal-origin list as a drug for bleeding and tissue growth, which is a therapeutic status. The tradition's frame for illness is that the three humours are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, line 51), and medicine is one branch of treatment within that frame; no line read assigns this item a place in any practice of essence extraction (*bcud len*) or any other cultivation scheme. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted. The item is one of the human-derived materials in the chapter (alongside human bone, flesh, urine and excreta in the same lists); that these appear in a medical text is a record of what the compilers listed, not a statement that the Codex endorses the use.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator, and menstrual blood is not a substance to take or apply. The Tibetan text records it for bleeding and flesh growth; the Codex records that and stops. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Not a recommended practice:** do not ingest, inject, apply to wounds or otherwise use menstrual blood or any other person's blood or body fluid as a medicine.
- **Infection:** menstrual blood can transmit blood-borne viruses (hepatitis B and C, HIV) and bacteria; contact with broken skin, mucous membranes or wounds is a route of infection.
- **Bleeding:** heavy, prolonged or unexplained bleeding is a medical matter; seek care and use tested treatments.
- **Legal and ethical:** the handling, sale or transfer of human body material is regulated in many places, and the person whose body produced it has rights over it. The Codex does not recommend harvesting it.
