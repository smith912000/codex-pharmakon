# I-512 | Hawthorn (*Crataegus monogyna* Jacq. / *C. laevigata* (Poir.) DC.)

**Volume I — Plant-Based Substances**
**Family:** Rosaceae (subfamily Amygdaloideae)
**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (cardiovascular, cardiotonic) primary | ◈ Jing (vascular structural repair) secondary

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary binomials:**
- *Crataegus monogyna* Jacq. (1775) — single-style hawthorn; most common European species
- *Crataegus laevigata* (Poir.) DC. (1825) — two-style hawthorn (= *C. oxyacantha* of older literature); primary species in pharmacological studies
- European Pharmacopoeia accepts both species and their hybrids for *Crataegi folium cum flore* (leaf with flower) and *Crataegi fructus* (fruit)

**Synonyms & historical names:**
- *Crataegus oxyacantha* auct. (non L.) — widely used in phytomedicine literature; taxonomically superseded by *C. laevigata* and *C. monogyna*; clinical trial literature often uses this name
- Historical: whitethorn, quickthorn, May tree, May blossom, bread and cheese tree

**Common names:**
- English: Hawthorn, common hawthorn, whitethorn, May tree, hedgerow (planting use)
- German: *Weißdorn*, *Eingriffeliger Weißdorn* (single-style), *Zweigriffeliger Weißdorn* (two-style)
- French: *Aubépine*, *Épine blanche*
- Italian: *Biancospino*
- Spanish: *Espino blanco*
- Arabic: *Za'rūr* (hawthorn fruit)
- Chinese: 山楂 (*shān zhā*, "mountain hawthorn") — primarily *C. pinnatifida* Bge. = Chinese hawthorn; different species but pharmacologically related
- Japanese: *Sanzashi* (山査子)
- Hebrew: *Zarayat* (thorn bush)

**Etymology:** *Crataegus* = Greek *krataios* (strong, hardy) — the hard, dense wood; *monogyna* = one pistil/style; *laevigata* = smooth (leaf surface); "haw" = Old English word for hedge or berry (the fruits = "haws").

**Botanical authentication:**
- Deciduous shrub/small tree 3–8 m; dense branching with characteristic **thorns** (modified branches, 1–2 cm — diagnostic); alternate lobed leaves (3–5 lobes in *C. monogyna*, 3–7 in *C. laevigata*); inflorescence: dense corymbs of white 5-petaled flowers with pink anthers (May–June; "May blossom"); fruit ("haw"): small round drupe, deep red at maturity, 6–8 mm; 1 stone in *C. monogyna*, 2 stones in *C. laevigata*; fruit ripens September–November
- Authentication: thorns + lobed leaves + white/pink flower corymbs + red autumn berries = unmistakable; the European hawthorn complex is one of the most botanically recognizable shrubs in temperate Europe

**Chinese hawthorn:** *Crataegus pinnatifida* Bge. — Chinese Pharmacopoeia 2020 official (山楂 *shān zhā*); pinnately lobed leaves (more deeply divided than European species); larger fruits; higher oleanolic acid content; same OPC and flavonoid class but different ratio; distinct TCM application profile (digestive, food stagnation, blood stasis) compared to European focus on cardiovascular.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

Hawthorn represents an unusual Codex entry: a plant with deep European cultural roots and moderate cross-traditional pharmacological documentation, where the defining pharmacological indication (cardiovascular) was developed not from traditional use but through systematic early 20th-century German phytomedicine investigation — paralleling the St. John's Wort and horse chestnut patterns of European evidence development.

The cultural integration of hawthorn into European life — hedgerows defining the agricultural landscape for 1,000 years, the "May tree" of Celtic and Christian festival tradition, the thorny hedge of fairy-tale forest borders — created long-term intimate familiarity with the plant. Folk practitioners in Germanic and Celtic cultures documented the berries' cardiac-strengthening effects as far back as the 15th-century herbals (Johann Kügelgen, 1892, appears to be the first modern European physician to systematically investigate hawthorn for heart failure). The Eclectic American physicians (Green, Ellingwood) independently arrived at the cardiac tonic indication in the 1800s from US *Crataegus* species, providing cross-continental confirmation.

The OPC (oligomeric proanthocyanidin) mechanism discovered in hawthorn is now recognized as pharmacologically shared across multiple Rosaceae family members (grape seed, pine bark/Pycnogenol, elderberry, bilberry) — a Rosaceae-wide cardiovascular protective chemistry pattern. Hawthorn contributes the uniquely cardio-specific flavonoid hyperoside (quercetin-3-galactoside) and vitexin glycosides to this family pattern.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Oligomeric proanthocyanidins (OPCs) — primary cardiovascular active fraction:**
- **Procyanidins B-1, B-2, B-3, B-4, B-5** (catechin/epicatechin dimers): 1–3% DW in leaves+flowers; 0.5–2% in fruit
- **Procyanidin C-1** (catechin trimer): present
- **Total OPCs (condensed proanthocyanidins):** 1–4% DW in leaves+flowers; higher in flowers than fruits
- PhEur marker: *Crataegi folium cum flore* standardized to ≥1.5% procyanidins expressed as epicatechin
- WS 1442 extract (Schwabe): standardized to 18.75% OPCs; primary RCT preparation

**Flavonoids:**
- **Hyperoside** (quercetin-3-galactoside): 0.3–1.8% DW in flowers; primary flavone marker; positive chronotropic (heart rate) effect at low doses; anti-inflammatory; HPLC authentication marker
- **Vitexin-2''-O-rhamnoside** (acacetin-8-C-glucoside-2''-rhamnoside): 0.5–1.5% DW; cardiospecific flavone; reduces lipid peroxidation in myocardium; coronary vasodilatory
- **Vitexin**: 0.2–0.8% DW; same GABA-A PAM compound appearing across multiple Codex families (passionflower, barley, various Leguminosae)
- **Rutin, isovitexin, orientin, quercetin**: present at varying concentrations
- PhEur alternative marker: ≥1.5% total flavonoids expressed as hyperoside

**Triterpene acids:**
- **Ursolic acid**: 0.3–1.0% DW; anti-inflammatory; cardioprotective in ischemia-reperfusion models
- **Oleanolic acid**: 0.2–0.8% DW; similar activity; Chinese hawthorn higher concentration
- **Betulinic acid**, crataegolic acid: minor

**Phenolic acids:**
- **Chlorogenic acid**: 0.2–1.5% DW; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory
- **Caffeic acid, ferulic acid**: present

**Amines:**
- **Acetylcholine**: trace amounts in hawthorn flowers (0.03–0.07 mg/100g fresh); debated clinical significance; positive cholinergic effect might contribute to bradycardia effect
- **Choline**, histamine (trace): floral amines

**Sugars/polysaccharides:**
- Pectin, sorbitol, fructose in fruits: nutritive; mild laxative

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Cardiovascular mechanisms — multi-target pharmacology:**

1. **Positive inotropy (increased contractile force) without increased oxygen demand:**
- OPC and vitexin fraction: inhibit myocardial phosphodiesterase (PDE3) → increased cAMP → increased calcium availability for actin-myosin cross-bridging → positive inotropic effect (stronger contraction)
- Crucial distinction: unlike cardiac glycosides (digoxin), hawthorn does not increase myocardial oxygen demand proportionally to inotropy; may actually reduce oxygen demand via improved efficiency
- Mechanism: Koch E et al. 2011 (*Phytomedicine* 18:476-483) demonstrated WS 1442 increase in cAMP via PDE3 inhibition

2. **Coronary vasodilation:**
- Vitexin-2''-O-rhamnoside: relaxes coronary artery smooth muscle → increased coronary blood flow
- OPCs: enhance NO (nitric oxide) bioavailability → endothelium-dependent vasodilation
- Combined: improved myocardial perfusion; reduced anginal symptoms

3. **Antiarrhythmic:**
- Hyperoside: prolongs action potential duration and refractory period → class III-like antiarrhythmic effect; reduces premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) in animal models
- OPCs: reduce arrhythmias in ischemia-reperfusion models; mechanism involves potassium channel modulation

4. **Cardioprotective (ischemia-reperfusion):**
- OPCs + flavonoids: scavenge superoxide and hydroxyl radicals generated during reperfusion; reduce apoptotic cascade in ischemic myocytes; reduce infarct size in animal models

5. **Peripheral vasodilation:**
- OPCs reduce peripheral vascular resistance → afterload reduction → reduced cardiac workload
- Mild antihypertensive effect (5–10 mmHg systolic in clinical trials)

6. **Lipid-lowering (Chinese *C. pinnatifida* — stronger effect than European species):**
- Oleanolic acid + flavonoids: PCSK9 inhibition → increased LDL receptor expression; bile acid sequestration
- More prominent in Chinese pharmacopeial use than European

**Anti-inflammatory:**
- Quercetin, hyperoside: COX-2 inhibition; NF-κB pathway suppression
- Relevant to atherosclerotic plaque inflammatory component

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Chronic heart failure (CHF, NYHA classes I–III) — Level A:**

1. **Tauchert M et al. 2002** (*American Journal of Cardiology* 94:657-661) — n=209, CHF NYHA II; WS 1442 900 mg/day vs placebo, 16 weeks; significant improvement in maximum workload (ergometry), reduced symptoms; Level A

2. **Holubarsch CJF et al. 2008** (*European Journal of Heart Failure* 10:1255-1263) — "SPICE" trial; n=2,681, CHF NYHA II–III, EF 25–35%; WS 1442 900 mg/day vs placebo, 24 months; primary outcome: time to first cardiac event NOT significant in full population; positive in pre-specified subgroup (EF >35%); significant reduction in sudden cardiac death in subgroup; Level A (negative primary outcome; positive secondary analyses — complex interpretation)

3. **Pittler MH et al. 2003** (Cochrane Review): included 8 RCTs; WS 1442 significantly superior to placebo for exercise tolerance, reduction of symptoms (dyspnea, fatigue) in CHF; Level A (Cochrane)

4. **Multiple German-language trials** (Leuchtgens 1993, Weikl 1996, Schmidt 1994): consistent positive effects in NYHA II patients; maximum workload improved 10–15% vs placebo; Level A (smaller trials)

**Hypertension — Level B:**
- **Walker AF et al. 2002** (*Journal of Human Hypertension* 16:48-54) — hawthorn extract 1200 mg/day vs placebo, 16 weeks; n=36, type 2 diabetes + mild hypertension; significant reduction in resting diastolic BP (5 mmHg); Level B
- Multiple studies; modest antihypertensive effect; not primary antihypertensive monotherapy

**Atherosclerosis — Level D:**
- In vitro and animal studies; OPC-mediated LDL oxidation reduction; reduced plaque formation; insufficient human trial data

**Dyslipidemia (Chinese *C. pinnatifida*) — Level B:**
- Chinese RCTs on 山楂 preparations for hypercholesterolemia; consistent modest LDL-C reduction; Level B (Chinese trial quality variable)

**Commission E (1994, updated 2016):** APPROVED for:
- Cardiac insufficiency corresponding to functional capacity classes I and II as defined by NYHA
- Senile cardiac changes
Standard dose: WS 1442 extract (18.75% OPCs): 900 mg/day equivalent.

**EMA Well-established use (2016):** Symptoms of cardiac discomfort (palpitations, heaviness in chest, dyspnea during effort) in elderly patients with NYHA class I or II CHF.

**Limitation — the SPICE trial:**
The negative primary outcome of the SPICE trial (2008) dampened therapeutic enthusiasm for hawthorn in moderate CHF. However, the SPICE trial enrolled a more severe CHF population (EF 25–35%) than earlier positive trials (EF >35%), and a positive subgroup result for sudden cardiac death reduction remained. Current evidence supports hawthorn for mild CHF (NYHA I–II) as an adjunct, not as replacement for evidence-based pharmaceutical therapy (ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, diuretics, ARBs).

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The May tree and Celtic rites:**
Hawthorn is the "May tree" of European tradition — its dense white blossom arriving in May coinciding with Beltane (Celtic fire festival, May 1) and later Mayday celebrations. The blooming of the May tree signaled the transition from winter to summer, and the tree itself was considered the boundary marker between ordinary and fairy-tale worlds in Celtic folklore (*fairy thorns* in Ireland = solitary hawthorns protected from cutting for fear of disturbing fairy dwelling).

The traditional prohibition on bringing hawthorn blossom indoors (it was considered bad luck, even presaging death) may encode an observation about the smell of hawthorn flowers: the volatile amine trimethylamine (released as flowers begin to decay) has been identified in hawthorn blooms — the same compound released in human corpses during decomposition. The traditional "death-smell" association with indoor hawthorn blossom may represent an empirical observation about the flowers' volatile chemistry rather than pure superstition. This is a recurring Codex pattern: folk prohibition encoding genuine chemical observation.

**Hedgerow hawthorn and agricultural enclosure:**
The Enclosure Acts of 17th–19th century England drove the planting of hawthorn hedgerows across the British countryside — the thorny growth providing livestock containment. An estimated 200,000+ miles of hawthorn hedgerow now crisscross England, making hawthorn the defining visual character of the English countryside. The hedgerow provided berries for livestock winter supplementation, game cover, and — at the folk level — an accessible medicinal plant on every farm boundary. This intimate integration of *Crataegus* into agricultural practice created ideal conditions for systematic empirical observation of the berries' effects over generations.

**Johann Kügelgen and the founding observation (1896):**
The story of hawthorn entering formal European medicine through Dr. Johann Kügelgen — a late 19th-century Viennese physician — is documented but contested. The more firmly established attribution is that Dr. E. Green of Trinity, Newfoundland, and later Dr. Finlay Ellingwood in Chicago's Eclectic tradition (1898) systematically documented hawthorn's cardiac tonic effects in clinical case series. The parallel European and North American investigation of *Crataegus* for cardiac indications in the same decade (1890s) constitutes a genuine independent cross-cultural pharmacological confirmation — though both were drawing on European immigrant folk knowledge rather than completely independent traditions.

**Hawthorn as cardiac tonic in Chinese medicine:**
TCM 山楂 (*shān zhā*) use for cardiovascular conditions focuses on "resolving blood stasis" — a TCM conceptual category that includes atherosclerosis, thrombosis, and vascular stagnation. This is a direct functional parallel to the Western cardiovascular protective mechanism, despite the completely different conceptual framing. The modern Chinese pharmaceutical development of *C. pinnatifida*-based preparations for dyslipidemia and cardiovascular risk reduction follows the same botanical intuition.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Evidence-based protocol:**
- **WS 1442 (Crataegutt, 18.75% OPCs standardized):** 900 mg/day (450 mg b.i.d. or 300 mg TID); proven dose from RCTs; minimum 8 weeks before assessing response (onset gradual)
- **LI 132 extract (60% ethanol, standardized to flavonoids):** 100–300 mg flavonoids (as hyperoside)/day
- **Dried leaf/flower:** 4–6 g/day as tea (3× daily); Commission E traditional dose; less reliable bioavailability vs standardized extract
- **Dried fruit (haws):** 5–10 g/day; TCM dose 3–10 g/day for 山楂; appropriate for digestive/metabolic indications

**Commission E approved dose:** 900 mg WS 1442/day or equivalent providing ≥4.5 mg OPCs

**Duration:** Long-term maintenance acceptable; no toxicity established up to 6 months in clinical trials; continue as long as clinically indicated in CHF management

**Position in CHF management:**
- First-line CHF pharmaceuticals (ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, loop diuretics, spironolactone): continue as prescribed; hawthorn is ADJUNCTIVE, not replacement
- NYHA I–II: hawthorn may be used alongside conventional therapy; discuss with prescribing cardiologist
- NYHA III–IV: insufficient evidence; pharmaceutical optimization essential; hawthorn may be considered but evidence limited to NYHA I–II

**CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- Severe CHF (NYHA IV): insufficient evidence; unstable hemodynamics
- Pregnancy: alkaloids (trace amines) and vasodilatory effect; insufficient safety data; avoid
- Hypersensitivity to Rosaceae

**Drug interactions:**
- **Digoxin:** additive positive inotropy → potential for enhanced digoxin toxicity; monitor digoxin levels; reduce digoxin dose if combining
- **Antihypertensive medications:** additive blood pressure lowering; monitor and adjust doses
- **Nitrates (nitroglycerin):** additive coronary vasodilation → potential hypotension; monitor
- **Anti-arrhythmics (amiodarone, flecainide):** additive action potential duration effects; use with caution; cardiologist oversight required
- **Phosphodiesterase inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil):** additive vasodilation → hypotension; caution

**Chinese *C. pinnatifida* (山楂) for digestive use:**
- Standard TCM decoction: 9–15 g dried fruit per day
- Indication: food stagnation, meat/fat dyspepsia, blood stasis
- Commercial: 山楂丸 (*shān zhā wán* = hawthorn pills); widely available
- Gastric acid concern: 山楂 significantly increases gastric acid secretion → contraindicated in peptic ulcer, gastritis; separate category from European hawthorn cardiovascular use
