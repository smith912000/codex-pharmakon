# Woad — Da Qing Ye (leaf), Ban Lan Gen (root), Isatis, Glastum, Guado

## *Isatis tinctoria* L. | Family: Brassicaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM, SBJ, FMT

**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry (Galenic); bitter-cold, heat-clearing and toxin-resolving (TCM)

**Training Phase:** Cooling / Heat-Clearing (antiviral, anti-inflammatory support)

---

### Origin & Tradition

Woad is one of the oldest dual-purpose plants in human culture — simultaneously the great pre-indigo **blue dye** of the Old World and a heat-clearing medicine spanning the Near East, Europe, and China. Its indigo-yielding leaves and its bitter roots gave two distinct drugs from one Brassica.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (SBJ):** Blue-dyeing plants of the woad type are among the ancient dye and medicinal materials of the Near East, where indigo-bearing herbs were valued both for colouring textiles and for topical, cooling applications to inflamed skin — a genuine ancient association of the blue dye plant with cooling remedies.

**Dioscoridean / European Pharmacy (DMM):** Dioscorides describes *isatis* (woad) in *De Materia Medica*, distinguishing the cultivated dyer's woad from a wild form and recommending the leaves, externally, for wounds, ulcers, spreading sores, and inflammations (erysipelas-type heat) — a cooling, astringent, wound-consolidating use that maps precisely onto its later anti-inflammatory profile. Woad (*glastum*) was also famous to the Classical world as the body-paint of the northern Britons.

**Avicennan / Unani (AT):** In the Unani tradition woad is treated as cold and dry, an astringent and desiccant applied to hot swellings, ulcers, and haemorrhage — consistent with the Galenic reading absorbed through Arabic pharmacy.

**Shennong / Chinese Materia Medica (CM):** Woad is the plant behind two of the most important heat-clearing drugs of TCM. **Da Qing Ye** (大青葉, the leaf) and **Ban Lan Gen** (板藍根, the root) are classic *qing re jie du* — "clear heat and resolve toxicity" — remedies, used for febrile epidemic disease, sore throat, mumps, and eruptive fevers. **Qing Dai** (青黛, indigo naturalis), the processed indigo pigment, is a related preparation. Root of *Isatis* was formally admitted to the European Pharmacopoeia in 2011, bridging the Chinese and European traditions.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Woad is the Codex's blue-cold archetype — colour and coolness as one signature. The plant that dyes cloth the colour of deep water is read as drawing heat downward and out, "quenching the fever as it stains the cloth." The doctrine ties the visible blue (indigo/indirubin pigments) to the invisible cooling action, and the pharmacology substantiates it: the same indole alkaloids that give the blue also carry the anti-inflammatory and antiviral activity.

### Active Components

Woad is chemically rich. The characteristic constituents are **indole alkaloids** — **indirubin** and its isomer indigo (the blue pigments), **tryptanthrin** (an indoloquinazoline and the principal anti-inflammatory alkaloid), **indolinone**, and related indoles. Alongside these are **glucosinolates** (as befits a Brassica) and their breakdown products, **phenolic compounds and flavonoids**, **polysaccharides** (implicated in immune/antiviral activity of Ban Lan Gen), plus carotenoids, volatile constituents, and fatty acids (Speranza et al. 2020, review). Indirubin is notable pharmacologically as a cyclin-dependent-kinase inhibitor, the lead behind anti-leukaemic development.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica woad sits in the **Cooling / Heat-Clearing phase** as a heat-resolving, antiviral-leaning simple. Its documented actions are **anti-inflammatory** (dual COX-2 / 5-LOX inhibition via tryptanthrin), **antiviral and antimicrobial**, **antioxidant**, and **anti-tumour** (indirubin's kinase inhibition). The traditional use-cases it supports are: acute febrile and eruptive illness with sore throat (the Ban Lan Gen indication), and topical calming of hot, irritated, or inflamed skin (the Dioscoridean/European indication). It is used as leaf (Da Qing Ye), root (Ban Lan Gen), and topical extract.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, woad has a substantial modern evidence base. Speranza et al. (2020), a comprehensive review in *Plants*, documents anti-inflammatory, anti-tumour, antimicrobial, antiviral, analgesic, and antioxidant activities and confirms the phytochemistry (tryptanthrin, indirubin, indolinone, phenolics, glucosinolates, polysaccharides) ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants9030298)). In a randomised, single-blind **human clinical trial**, Heinemann et al. (2004, *Planta Medica*) showed that topical lipophilic *Isatis tinctoria* extracts significantly reduced sodium-lauryl-sulphate-induced irritant contact dermatitis (smaller visual scores and less transepidermal water loss versus untreated fields), identifying **tryptanthrin as a dual inhibitor of COX-2 and 5-LOX** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-2004-818963)); notably the extract outperformed pure tryptanthrin and no benefit was seen in the UVB-erythema model — an honest, mixed result.

**Evidence grade: MODERATE.** Strong phytochemistry and mechanistic pharmacology, one supportive controlled human dermatological trial, and extensive in-vitro antiviral/anti-inflammatory data; large-scale clinical antiviral efficacy in humans remains less firmly established.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** Ban Lan Gen / Da Qing Ye — **bitter, cold**; enters Heart, Stomach (and Lung) channels; **clears heat, cools blood, resolves toxicity**, benefits the throat.
- **Ayurvedic:** Not a classical Ayurvedic dravya; no authentic *rasa/guna/virya/vipaka* assignment exists and none is asserted.
- **Galenic / Unani:** Cold and dry; astringent and desiccant to hot swellings and ulcers.

### Contraindications

- **Spleen/Stomach cold-deficiency (TCM):** As a strongly bitter-cold drug, Ban Lan Gen/Da Qing Ye can injure a cold, weak digestion — avoid or use cautiously with abdominal cold, loose stools, and no genuine heat signs.
- **Prolonged/high-dose use:** Rare reports of gastrointestinal upset and, with heavy indigo-naturalis-type preparations, haematological concern; avoid unsupervised long courses.
- **Pregnancy & lactation:** Insufficient safety data for cold, blood-cooling herbs of this class; avoid unless directed by a qualified practitioner [USER: verify].
- **Allergy:** Discontinue topical use if contact sensitisation appears.
- **Not a substitute for antiviral or antibiotic care** in serious infection.
