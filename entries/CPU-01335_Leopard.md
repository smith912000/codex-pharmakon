# Leopard — the leopard named among the carnivorous wild animals, rGyud-bzhi (Explanatory Tantra)
## *The leopard, which the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary renders "Panthera pardus Linnaeus"; the Tibetan name is not paired reliably in the OCR of the glossary table* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The leopard is named only as a member of a group of animals. In the Explanatory Tantra's discussion of meats, the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation says: "The tiger, leopard, black bear, Tibetan brown bear, snow leopard, wolf, lynx, fox, dhole (Tibetan wild-dog) and monkey are the group of canivorous wild animals" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, lines 2911-2913; "canivorous" is the printed typo; the book's index has "leopard 162", line 7415). Clark 1995 gives the same group in its list of nine sections of meats: "(e) Carnivorous animals include the tiger, leopard, bear (Selenarctos thibetanus), snow bear (Ursus arctos), snow leopard (Panthera pardus), wolf, lynx, fox, hill dog and weasel" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, line 672). The Men-Tsee-Khang text counts eight groups, not nine (line 2893); Clark's text says "nine sections" and later "the above eight sections" (lines 672-673), and the difference is not explained in the lines read.

What the tradition says of the group's flesh is general. Both books give all the meats a sweet taste and a sweet post-digestive taste (Men-Tsee-Khang line 2923; Clark line 673). Men-Tsee-Khang: "The meat of birds, carnivorous animals and birds of prey that feed on raw meat has rough, light and sharp qualities, and therefore generates digestive heat, destroys tumors, increases weight and eliminates all cold disorders" (line 2931). Clark attaches the same sentence to birds of prey alone: "birds of prey ... live on raw flesh. Their flesh has coarse, light and sharp powers which increase the digestive heat, disperse tumors, increase flesh and eliminate all cold disorders" (line 673). So whether the carnivores share these powers depends on which translation is followed.

Two other passages name the carnivores' meat as a class. The diet for a *baekan* (phlegm) disorder includes "the meat of carnivores" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 1107; Clark line 176, "meat of carnivorous animals"). After over-application of slimming therapy, "eating the meat of carnivorous animals, administering mild enemas and sleeping after a heavy meal will help develop a fatty body like a pig" (Men-Tsee-Khang lines 4393-4395; Clark line 1405, with the added sentence "Once one has gained weight there is nothing more life-giving than meat"). The leopard is not named in either. No materia medica entry for any part of the leopard (skin, bone, fat, bile) was found in Clark's chapter 20 or in the Men-Tsee-Khang text; a name search for "leopard" returned only the carnivore lists above, and the identically named "stag.sha (lit. 'tiger flesh')" in Clark line 1017 is a plant.

**Identity.** The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary on animals reads "Leopard / Panthera pardus Linnaeus" (lines 7078-7080), in an OCR-scrambled table where names and Latin names sit in separate blocks; the pairing is by order. Clark's list gives "snow leopard (Panthera pardus)", which is the leopard's binomial attached to the snow leopard (the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary has its own "Snow Leopard" entry, line 7126, with a different, OCR-damaged Latin name). `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` and `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` hold no leopard row, so the spine's cited locus rests on the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary line alone. The Tibetan name is not given here because the glossary's Tibetan transliterations cannot be paired reliably. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Meat of a large carnivore is mostly water and muscle protein with fat, and supplies iron, zinc and B vitamins. Wild carnivores can carry parasites such as *Trichinella*. No analysis specific to this entry was read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources name the leopard as a member of a meat group and nothing more. The leopard is a protected and declining species, and nothing in the sources would justify killing, buying or consuming any part of one. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation or dose is given for the leopard in the sources retrieved. The tradition's rule that old meat is warmer and fresh meat cooler, and that dried and cooked meats are lighter than raw or burned (Clark line 682), is general to all meats and not specific to the leopard. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Meat from wild carnivores must be cooked through; it can carry parasites and zoonoses. The leopard is a protected species, and trade in its parts is restricted internationally. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The leopard has no entry of its own. The classes it belongs to, as printed: all meats sweet in taste and sweet in post-digestive taste (Men-Tsee-Khang line 2923; Clark line 673). The meat of dry-land animals is "cool, light and rough in quality" and "brings down hot disorders associated with *loong* and *baekan*" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 2923; Clark line 673 gives "cool, light and coarse powers which eliminate wind and phlegm fevers"). The group of carnivores and birds of prey that feed on raw flesh is "rough, light and sharp" and generates digestive heat (Men-Tsee-Khang line 2931). No taste or potency is printed for the leopard by name. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* The leopard has no entry of its own in the sources retrieved and appears only as a member of the carnivore group of meats. The group's flesh is credited with building weight after over-slimming and as part of a phlegm diet (see above), but no passage connects it with *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any other practice, and the tradition has no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice. The tradition roots the three humors in attachment, hatred and obscuration (Clark line 51); the lines read draw no connection between those roots and the leopard. No cultivation framing is attested for the leopard.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice. The leopard is named in a list of animals, and the only passages that touch a cultivator concern carnivore meat as a class, for phlegm disorders and for rebuilding weight. The animal is protected, so even the class reading offers no ground for using it.

### Contraindications

- **Conservation and law:** the leopard is a protected species in much of its range and subject to international trade restrictions (CITES). Possession of skin, bone or meat can be an offence. [USER: verify]
- **Food safety and zoonoses:** meat from wild carnivores can carry *Trichinella* and other parasites.
- **Identity:** the Latin name rests on a scrambled glossary table and on a translation that attaches the same binomial to the snow leopard; do not rely on either. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** killing a protected predator for food or medicine is not defensible on the strength of a list entry; this entry takes no position beyond recording the text.
