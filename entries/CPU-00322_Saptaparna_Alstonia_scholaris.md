### Origin & Tradition

**Suśruta & the Ayurvedic corpus.** Saptaparṇa — literally "seven-leaved," from the characteristic whorl of leaves radiating from each node — is a classical tree-drug of the Sanskrit tradition, cited in both the *Suśruta* and *Caraka Saṃhitā*. Its bark (*saptaparṇa tvak*) is the principal officinal part, valued as a bitter febrifuge, anthelmintic, galactagogue and skin-and-ulcer remedy. Suśruta groups it among bitter, *kṛmighna* (anthelmintic) and *tvak-doṣa* (skin-disorder) relevant drugs, and it is a recurrent component of *jvara* (fever) and *kuṣṭha* (dermatoses) formulations. Its bitter, drying, heating profile made it a natural antimalarial-antipyretic long before malaria was understood — a classical intuition the modern literature partly vindicates.

**The tree of the crossroads — folk and colonial names.** In vernacular India the tree accrued a striking cluster of names: **Devil's Tree**, **Blackboard Tree** (its soft even wood was cut for school slates), and *Saptaparni* / *Chhatiwan*. The "Devil" epithet attaches to its heavy, cloying night-scent and its association with liminal ground — cremation grounds, crossroads, boundary plantings — and to a folklore of spirits congregating in its crown. Its milky latex (the Apocynaceae signature) reinforced a reputation as a plant of hidden, double-edged potency.

**Dai / Yunnan ethnomedicine.** As with *Marsdenia*, southern Chinese (Dai) ethnopharmacy independently adopted *A. scholaris* leaf as a treatment for chronic respiratory disease — tracheitis, cough and cold — and the leaf extract was developed into an approved commercial respiratory formulation. This is the empirical seed of the modern antitussive/antiasthmatic research described below.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Seven-Leaved Whorl:** Saptaparṇa is the plant of *the sevenfold radiance from a single centre*. The whorl of seven leaves springing from one node is a natural mandala — one becoming seven, the axis and its rays — and the number seven carries its full symbolic freight: the seven *dhātus* (tissues) that the body builds outward from a single nourishing centre; the seven *cakras* strung along the axial channel; the seven notes, days, planets. In Kenetica the tree stands for **structured emanation** — the disciplined outward branching of a practice from one grounded root, seven expressions of a single vow. Its darker aspect — the Devil's Tree of the crossroads, the night-scent, the latex — supplies the necessary shadow: emanation is also exposure, and the plant that clears fever and worm also guards a threshold. It is fittingly a *purification* herb: what radiates cleanly from the centre must first be cleared of what festers.

### Active Components

The pharmacologically active fraction is a suite of **monoterpenoid indole alkaloids** — most notably **picrinine** (the principal antitussive/antiasthmatic marker), together with **scholaricine, vallesamine, echitamine, akuammidine** and related bases — concentrated in leaf and bark. Iridoids, triterpenoids, flavonoids and the characteristic latex constituents accompany them. Picrinine has been proposed as a quality-control marker for leaf-derived respiratory products.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Saptaparṇa is Kenetica's **bitter respiratory-and-febrile clearer** — the whorled tree deployed when the practitioner is feverish, congested, worm-burdened, or "damp-cold" in the chest. Its alkaloid fraction earns it a genuine place in respiratory conditioning: it belongs to the purification and fever-clearing phases, opening and drying the airways and settling cough. Symbolically it is used at points of *re-centring* — where a practice has branched chaotically and needs pruning back to its single root before it can radiate cleanly again. As a heating bitter it is a corrective course, not a daily tonic.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the respiratory tradition has real preclinical support, and amarogentin-style bitter chemistry is separate — here the alkaloids do the work:

- The **alkaloid fraction of *A. scholaris* leaf** showed significant antitussive, antiasthmatic and expectorant activity across ammonia-, sulfur-dioxide- and citric-acid-induced cough models and histamine-induced bronchoconstriction in guinea pigs; **picrinine** was identified as the main active antitussive/antiasthmatic alkaloid (Shang et al., 2010, *Journal of Ethnopharmacology*) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2010.03.029). The same work notes the leaf extract had been approved as a commercial respiratory formulation in China.

**Evidence grade: LOW–MODERATE / preclinical, with an ethnomedically-derived regulated product.** The respiratory activity is well-modelled in animals and the leaf extract has reached formulation status, which raises confidence above pure bench work; but the classical *antimalarial/febrifuge* and *anthelmintic* uses, and human efficacy generally, remain to be robustly demonstrated in the sources retrieved this run. Science attributed to PubMed.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Rasa (taste):** Tikta (bitter), Kaṣāya (astringent).
- **Guṇa (quality):** Laghu (light), Rūkṣa (dry) — some texts note Snigdha owing to the latex.
- **Vīrya (potency):** Uṣṇa (heating).
- **Vipāka (post-digestive):** Kaṭu (pungent).
- **Prabhāva (specific effect):** Marked antipyretic-anthelmintic action ascribed to the bark beyond its rasa profile; galactagogue reputation is a classical prabhāva-level claim.
- **Doṣa action:** Kapha-Vāta śāmaka (dries Kapha, warms and settles cold Vāta congestion); may aggravate Pitta if overused owing to Uṣṇa vīrya.
- **Karma (actions):** Jvaraghna (antipyretic), Kṛmighna (anthelmintic), Kāsahara / Śvāsahara (antitussive / antiasthmatic), Kuṣṭhaghna (dermatoses), Stanyajanana (galactagogue), Dīpana-Pācana (digestive-kindling).
- **Gaṇa membership:** Grouped in the Sanskrit corpus among bitter, kṛmighna and tvak-doṣa (skin-disorder) drugs; a standing component of jvara and kuṣṭha formulations.

### Cultivation Register

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** *Alstonia scholaris* is a South- and Southeast-Asian tree of the Apocynaceae; it stands outside the classical Greco-Arabic materia medica and has no place in the Avicennian *Canon*. Its bark entered regional Unani-Tibb practice in India (as *dita* / *chhatim* bark, an antiperiodic febrifuge) only in the colonial and modern era, and even there its role is therapeutic — a bitter fever-drug — not one of the corpus's cultivation ideas. No conservation-of-innate-heat-and-radical-moisture frame, and no *mufarrih* or *ruh*-strengthening role, is attested for it, and none is invented here. **(c) absent** — and the CM tag is doubtful for a tree the classical Canon never knew (see attestation note).

**Chinese — Shennong (SBJ).** There is no classical *Bencao* attestation for this tree. *A. scholaris* is not a herb of the *Shennong Bencao Jing* or the Han–Tang classical stratum; the Chinese use is Dai (Yunnan) ethnomedicine — the southern minority tradition that independently took the leaf for chronic cough, tracheitis and cold — later developed into a modern, regulated leaf-extract respiratory formulation. That is folk and modern-pharmaceutical attestation, not the classical *san pin* system: no upper/middle/lower grade is recorded and no *yang sheng* 養生 cultivation status attaches to it. **(c) absent** — and the SBJ tag misfiles a Dai/Yunnan folk drug under the classical Bencao stratum (see attestation and proposal notes).

**Ayurveda (SS).** Saptaparṇa (सप्तपर्ण) is genuinely attested in the *Suśruta* and *Caraka Saṃhitā*, where its bark is worked as a bitter febrifuge (*jvaraghna*), anthelmintic (*kṛmighna*) and skin-disorder drug (*kuṣṭhaghna*), grouped among the bitter *kṛmighna* and *tvak-doṣa* drugs with Tikta–Kaṣāya rasa and Uṣṇa vīrya. But this is a therapeutic and dietetic standing, not a formal cultivation status: no *rasayana* (rejuvenation) or *vajikarana* (generative-vitality) assignment is recorded for it in the sources retrieved, and no *ojas*-building action is claimed. The seven-leaved-whorl symbolism (the seven *dhātus* and *cakras*) that this entry develops is a Kenetica reading, not an attested Sushrutan assignment, and is not counted here as a cultivation status. **(b) framed** — attested in the classical Ayurvedic corpus, with no rejuvenative cultivation status recorded.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator uses Saptaparṇa as a corrective, not a cultivation substance: a heating bitter for a defined feverish, congested or worm-burdened episode — a purification and airway-clearing course — then stopped. Its own "Devil's Tree" reputation and its potent indole alkaloids are the caution; none of the three tagged traditions raises it to a rejuvenative or longevity status, and its symbolic role in Kenetica (pruning a scattered practice back to one root) is a discipline of attention, not a claim on the body's vitality. Clear what festers, then leave the tree at the crossroads.

### Contraindications

- Heating potency: caution in Pitta excess, hyperacidity, and inflammatory-heat conditions.
- Alkaloid content makes it inappropriate for casual high-dose or prolonged self-use; the "Devil's Tree" reputation encodes a real caution about potency.
- Pregnancy and lactation: bark preparations should not be self-administered despite the galactagogue tradition — practitioner supervision required.
- The respiratory data are preclinical; do not substitute for treatment of acute asthma or serious respiratory illness.
