# Calcareous spar (Ning Shui Shi) — Ning Shui Shi, "Calcareous Spar" (Yang 1998); other name Bai Shui Shi (White Water Stone); list row 凝水石
## *A white stone of the Divine Farmer's Classic that Yang glosses "Calcareous Spar"; his footnote 57 says the identification "is still under some debate" and that some authorities give Glauberitum, Gypsum or Calcitum* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** Ning Shui Shi is printed in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`) on PDF page 30, directly under the heading "Jades and Stones: Middle Class" (line 1168, the top of that page). The text (lines 1169-1174, with OCR damage on the word "generalized" and on "burning"): "Ning Shui Shi (Calcareous Spar)57 is acrid and cold. It mainly treats generalized fever, concretion and accumulation evil qi in the abdomen, burning heat within the skin, and vexatious fullness. It is taken with water. Protracted taking may make one free from hunger. Its other name is Bai Shui Shi (White Water Stone). It is produced in mountains and valleys."

Yang's footnote 57 (lines 1192-1194): "The pharmacological identification of this medicinal is still under some debate. Some authorities identify it as Glauberitum, Gypsum, or Calcitum." The translator himself gives "Calcareous Spar" as the working gloss, then lists three candidates, so the Latin glosses do not agree with each other. The spine row's note is "id-uncertain" and Yang's footnote bears it out. The list `_sources/shennong.csv` carries the row as 凝水石 (line 126), and `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv` has the row "Ning Shui Shi" (line 24) with the identification "Calcareous Spar". The spine's formula and Latin name (CaCO3, Calcitum) pick the third of Yang's three candidates; the sources retrieved do not say why. [USER: verify]

Two details of the text are worth setting down plainly. The line "It is taken with water" (line 1172) is the only mode of taking printed. The name literally contains "water", and the other name is "White Water Stone"; Yang's translation prints the names without comment, and the Codex does not interpret them.

### Active Components

The identification is uncertain, so the composition is stated for each of Yang's candidates and not for the stone. Calcite (Calcitum) is calcium carbonate, CaCO3, the commonest carbonate mineral; "calcareous spar" is an older name for clear, cleavable calcite. Gypsum is hydrated calcium sulfate, CaSO4·2H2O, soft enough to scratch with a fingernail. Glauberite is a sodium calcium sulfate, Na2Ca(SO4)2. The sources read give no composition for the stone Yang translates.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Classic records the stone, taken with water, for generalized fever, abdominal accumulations, burning heat within the skin and vexatious fullness, and says that long taking may leave one "free from hunger". The Codex does not endorse these claims and offers no protocol. Because the stone cannot be identified with confidence, even a safe-sounding candidate such as calcium carbonate cannot be assumed.

### Preparation & Dosing

The monograph prints one mode of taking: "It is taken with water" (line 1172). No further preparation is printed and no dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Calcium carbonate is an antacid and a dietary calcium source in general. Gypsum is the material of plaster and, calcined, plaster of Paris. Glauberite is a rare evaporite mineral. Which of these, if any, the Classic meant is the open question of footnote 57, and no property of the Classic's stone is established. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- SBJ: "acrid and cold" (Yang line 1169). Class: Middle (heading line 1168). Yang's footnote 32 (lines 849-855) explains that his rendering omits the word "flavor" and that "balanced" means neither cold nor hot; this entry is "cold". No other quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: a middle-grade drug (中品).* Ning Shui Shi stands under "Jades and Stones: Middle Class" (Yang line 1168). Yang's preface says the medium class medicinals "mainly nurture personality and correspond to humanity. They may or may not be toxic, and [therefore,] one should weigh and ponder before putting them to their appropriate use. If one intends to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion, one should base [one's efforts] on the middle class" (lines 385-390). Yang's own note (lines 356-366) says this class "may be toxic" and that "their prescription requires care". The monograph prints one line of the "protracted taking" type, "may make one free from hunger" (line 1173), and no longevity, lightness or immortality claim. The grade is a statement of the Classic as translated by Yang in 1998 and attaches to the entry as printed, not to any one candidate for what the stone was; it is not carried forward from later materia medica works.

**Practitioner's note.** A middle-grade drug in the Chinese scheme: not a "nourish life" drug for long use, but a drug for controlling disease, here a cold stone against fever. The stone's identity is itself in dispute, so nothing here supports taking anything sold under the name. For a cultivator the entry is a record of how the Classic ranked a white stone that cools. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Because the identity is uncertain, a powder sold under this name may be calcium carbonate, gypsum or another mineral, and any of these may be contaminated. Mineral medicines are unstandardised and their sale is regulated in many places. A cold stone given against fever is not a substitute for medical assessment of fever, which can signal serious infection.
