# Ativisha — Ativiṣā / Aruṇā / Śṛṅgī / Viṣā / Bhaṅgurā (Sanskrit); Atis, Ativish (Hindi); Indian Atees, Aconite (English — non-toxic species)
## *Aconitum heterophyllum* Wall. ex Royle | Family: Ranunculaceae
**Tradition Tags:** CM, SS, FMT
**Tags (previous line, retained for reference):** SS (Suśruta Saṃhitā — the "non-toxic" detoxified aconite; antidiarrhoeal, digestive, paediatric); CS (Caraka); AH
**Energetic Classification (Ayurvedic):** Tridoṣaghna, with primary Kapha–Pitta hara; heating (uṣṇa vīrya) yet — uniquely — dīpana without aggravating Pitta unduly
**Training Phase:** Recovery / Gut-restoration phase; Paediatric-analogue gentle-tonic tier; convalescent digestive-priming

> ⚠️ **Species-identity and conservation flag.** *Aconitum heterophyllum* is the ONLY non-poisonous member of the genus *Aconitum* and must not be confused with the highly toxic aconites (e.g. *A. napellus*, *A. ferox* / Vatsanābha). It is also a **critically endangered** Himalayan herb — sourcing must be sustainable/cultivated, and identity must be verified against toxic look-alikes before any use.

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda (Suśruta, Caraka, Vāgbhaṭa):** Ativiṣā holds a special and somewhat paradoxical place: its name means "beyond/most poison" (ati-viṣā), yet the classical tradition and modern pharmacology agree it is the *non-toxic* aconite — the safe member of an otherwise lethal genus. Suśruta and the nighaṇṭus classify it as **dīpana (digestive-kindling), pācana (toxin-digesting), grāhī/saṃgrāhī (absorbent, checking flux)** — making it the go-to drug for **atisāra (diarrhoea)**, **āma (undigested-toxin) conditions**, **jvara (fever)**, cough, and worms. Critically, it is regarded as **safe and appropriate for children** — a rare distinction that made it a cornerstone of Ayurvedic paediatrics (bāla-cikitsā), given for infantile diarrhoea, colic, indigestion and fever. It is a key ingredient in classical formulae such as **Bāla-cāturbhadra** and appears throughout digestive and antipyretic compounds. Its root is the officinal part.

**Regional & folk (Himalayan):** Across the northwestern Himalayas (Kashmir, Himachal, Uttarakhand) the bitter root ("Atis") is a household remedy for fever, diarrhoea, dyspepsia and general debility, and as a tonic during convalescence. According to PubMed, a critical review documents its status as the only non-poisonous *Aconitum*, its ancient use across traditional systems, and its harvesting primarily for the root (Wani et al., 2021, *J Integr Med*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joim.2021.12.004)). A second review (under the name Aruṇā) records traditional use in urinary infections, diarrhoea and inflammation, and as an expectorant and hepatoprotective (Paramanick et al., 2017, *J Pharmacopuncture*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3831/KPI.2017.20.011)).

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Tamed Poison:** Ativiṣā's doctrinal signature in Kenetica is *the venom that heals* — the paradox of a poison-named, poison-genus plant that is in fact the gentle, child-safe restorer. It embodies the alchemical doctrine that the boundary between medicine and poison is *identity, dose and preparation*, not category: within the most dangerous family sits the safest member, and knowing which is which is the whole of the art. Symbolically it teaches **discrimination (viveka)** — the refusal to treat a genus, a class, or a reputation as destiny; the demand to identify the specific thing in front of you. In the training vocabulary it is the *restorative astringent* of the recovery arc: the herb that consolidates a leaking, depleted, post-illness gut, that rebuilds digestive integrity gently enough for the most fragile system. It is the answer to "how do you rebuild what is depleted without further burdening it?" — heat that kindles without scorching, astringency that holds without blocking.

### Active Components

The genus is defined by **diterpenoid alkaloids** ("aconites"), but *A. heterophyllum*'s signature is the comparatively benign **atisine-type C20-diterpenoid alkaloids** (atisine, heteratisine, hetisine, F-dihydroatisine and related), which lack the acute cardiotoxicity of the aconitine-type alkaloids found in the toxic species. According to PubMed, the medicinal properties are attributed to these diverse diterpene alkaloids, alongside carbohydrates, proteins/amino acids, saponins, glycosides, quinones, flavonoids and terpenoids (Paramanick et al., 2017, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3831/KPI.2017.20.011); Wani et al., 2021, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joim.2021.12.004)).

- **Atisine, heteratisine, hetisine, F-dihydroatisine** (atisine-class diterpenoid alkaloids) — the characteristic, non-cardiotoxic actives
- **Flavonoids, saponins, glycosides** — antioxidant/anti-inflammatory fraction
- **Terpenoids, quinones** — reported bioactive constituents
- Notably **lacks aconitine-type alkaloids** in the quantities that make other aconites lethal (the basis of its safety) `[USER: verify chemotype of specific source]`

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. **Gut-restoration astringent (grāhī)** — flagship use: consolidation of diarrhoea and post-illness digestive leak; the recovery-phase agent for a depleted, over-loose gut.
2. **Convalescent digestive primer** — dīpana-pācana that re-kindles appetite and digests residual āma without the harsh Pitta-aggravation of the pungent warmers (Cavya, Hiṅgu) — suited to fragile, recovering systems.
3. **Antipyretic adjunct** — for fever with digestive involvement.
4. **Paediatric-analogue gentle tier** — its classical child-safe status marks it as the template for the "gentle enough for the fragile" category (clinical paediatric use requires qualified supervision and verified identity — not self-administration).

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the evidence is **preclinical/review-level and consistent with the traditional digestive-antipyretic profile**. A critical review reports in-vitro analgesic, anti-inflammatory, antiarrhythmic, antiparasitic, anticancer and CNS effects, and confirms *A. heterophyllum* as the only non-poisonous *Aconitum* while flagging its critically endangered status (Wani et al., 2021, *J Integr Med*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joim.2021.12.004)). A pharmacognosy review documents traditional and primary pharmacological use in diarrhoea, urinary infection, inflammation, as an expectorant and hepatoprotective, and catalogues its diterpene-alkaloid chemistry (Paramanick et al., 2017, *J Pharmacopuncture*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3831/KPI.2017.20.011)).

**Evidence grade:** Traditional use (including safety and paediatric use) is extensive and long-standing; scientific data are in-vitro/animal and review-level, without robust human clinical trials for the antidiarrhoeal indication. The distinctive *safety* relative to toxic aconites is supported by both tradition and its atisine-type (non-aconitine) alkaloid chemistry, but this safety is **species- and chemotype-specific** — it does not transfer to misidentified material.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Rasa (taste):** Tikta (bitter), Kaṭu (pungent)
- **Guṇa (quality):** Laghu (light), Rūkṣa (dry)
- **Vīrya (potency):** Uṣṇa (heating)
- **Vipāka (post-digestive):** Kaṭu (pungent)
- **Prabhāva (special effect):** The non-toxic aconite — dīpana + grāhī action that kindles digestion and checks flux while remaining gentle/child-safe, a profile atypical for a heating drug and not predicted by taste alone
- **Doṣa action:** Tridoṣaghna; primary Kapha-Pitta hara (its bitterness moderates the heat, so it pacifies Pitta rather than aggravating it as most uṣṇa drugs do)
- **Karma (actions):** Dīpana (digestive kindling), Pācana (digests āma), Grāhī/Saṃgrāhī (absorbent, antidiarrhoeal), Jvaraghna (antipyretic), Kṛmighna (anthelmintic), Kaphaghna (expectorant), Balya/Rasāyana (restorative in convalescence)
- **Gaṇa membership (Suśruta):** grouped with dīpana/grāhī antidiarrhoeal and antipyretic drugs; central to paediatric (bāla) formulations

### Cultivation Register

**Avicenna — Unani (CM).** Under the Unani tag Ativiṣā (Atees) would present as a bitter, warming digestive and antipyretic astringent — a corrective for a cold, flux-prone stomach — but the retrieved sources carry no actual Unani material for this species. Unani's strongest cultivation idea, the lifelong conservation of innate heat (*ḥarārat gharīziyya*) and radical moisture (*ruṭūbat aṣliyya*) — a finite vital endowment whose depletion *is* ageing — is a frame of the whole regimen, not a status the tradition assigns to this drug; no *mufarrih* or cardiac-*ruh*-strengthening role is recorded for it, and no *Canon* chapter attribution is available or claimed. Cultivation status: **(c) absent.**

**Ayurveda — Suśruta (SS).** Ativiṣā sits in the dīpana–pācana–grāhī class, worked to kindle digestion, digest *āma* and check *atisāra* while remaining gentle enough for the fragile paediatric constitution. It carries a *balya* (strengthening) and convalescent-restorative reputation, and this entry lists "Balya/Rasāyana" among its *karma* — but no formal *rasāyana* or *vājīkaraṇa* assignment for *A. heterophyllum* is traceable to Caraka, Suśruta or Meulenbeld in the sources retrieved, and no specific *ojas*-building action is attested for it. Its classical standing is therapeutic, digestive and paediatric — a recovery-phase corrective — not a formal rejuvenative cultivation status. **(b) framed:** claimed and used, but with no sourced *rasāyana* grade. (*Note: rasa/rajas/tamas are Sāṃkhya categories and are not invoked here.*)

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The FMT tag places the white-aconite material within Sowa Rigpa's fever-and-digestive repertoire, but medicine is only the third of the four branches of treatment: it supports practice and does not reach the root of the three *nyes pa* (rlung / mkhris pa / bad kan), which are themselves derived from the three poisons and are at root a spiritual condition. No *bcud len* (essence-extraction) role is attributed to this species in the retrieved sources, and Tibetan medicine carries no formal category of "substances for subtle-body practice" under which to file it. Cultivation status: **(c) absent.**

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a longevity or subtle-body substance. Across all three tagged traditions Ativiṣā is worked as a recovery-phase digestive astringent — kindling a depleted gut, checking flux, settling fever — taken for a defined convalescent course and then stopped. The one genuinely cultivational lesson it carries is *viveka*, discrimination: the whole of the art is identifying that this single non-toxic member sits inside a lethal genus and not mistaking it for its poisonous look-alikes. That is a discipline of attention exercised before use, not a vitality a cultivator takes the plant to acquire.

### Contraindications

- **⚠️ Misidentification is the primary hazard:** toxic aconites (*A. napellus*, *A. ferox*/Vatsanābha) are potentially lethal. Use only verified, correctly identified *A. heterophyllum* from a reputable source. When in doubt, do not use.
- **Conservation:** critically endangered — insist on sustainably cultivated material; wild-harvest contributes to extinction risk.
- **Pregnancy / lactation:** despite the non-toxic reputation, therapeutic doses should follow qualified supervision, not self-administration.
- **Paediatric use:** classically child-safe *within the tradition and at traditional doses*, but modern unsupervised dosing of any aconite-genus plant in children is inadvisable — supervised, verified material only.
- **Not a substitute** for rehydration and conventional care in acute or severe diarrhoea/fever.
