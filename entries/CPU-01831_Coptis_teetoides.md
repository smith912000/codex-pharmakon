# Coptis teetoides — myang.rtsi.spras, ser.po.khrag.rkang (rGyud-bzhi, Clark 1995 line 1072; Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary lines 5559 and 5974)

## *Coptis teetoides, the first of the two binomials Clark 1995 prints on line 1072 for the Tibetan name myang.rtsi.spras ("Gold Thread: Coptis teetoides: Adonis sibirica") and the binomial the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints on lines 5559 ("Coptis teetoides", English "Gold thread") and 5974 ("Coptis teetordes chinensis Mamira"). The glossary prints its Tibetan and scientific names in separate OCR runs, so the pairing of each Tibetan name with its binomial is positional and is re-checked here by counting* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, in two modern editions: Clark 1995 (a translation with commentary interpolations) and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (Root and Explanatory Tantra with a glossary). The two editions are translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions.** Both are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The row was added from a printed binomial (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP, confidence "medium") and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. **Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` the plant has two rows, and both are keyed by the **binomial**, not by a Tibetan name: row 328 ("Coptis teetoides", Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary, line 5559) and row 378 ("Coptis teetordes", glossary, line 5974), each noted "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". Neither row has a Clark line. The key is therefore not the first Tibetan name. Clark's entry for the plant (line 1072) opens with three names, "myang.rtsi.spras or ser.po.khrag.rkang or lo.mar.sngo.sprin", and the spine binomial is the first of Clark's two Latin names for it. By the counts below, the first glossary row stands against Clark's first Tibetan name (myang.rtsi.spras) and the second against his second name (ser.po.khrag.rkang, in the glossary khrag rkang).

**The first glossary row, checked by counting (line 5559).** The N block prints nine phonetic names (lines 5539 to 5541): Ngonbu, Nheu, Nimpa, Nyalo, **Nyangtse trae**, Nye shing, Nyiga, Nying shhosha and Nyiwa, with Tibetan transliterations (lines 5543 to 5546) "sngon bu, sne'u, nim pa, mya lo, **myang rtsi spras**, nye shing, nyi dga', snying zho sha, snyi ba". The scientific names (lines 5548 to 5565) are "Cyananthus lobatus", "Chenopodium album", "Azadirachta indica", "Aconogonom campanalatum", **"Coptis teetoides"** (line 5559), "Asparagus adscendens", "Malva botka", "Choerospondias axillaris" and "Codonopsis convolvulacea": nine. The transliteration *myang rtsi spras* is the fifth in the Tibetan run and the Coptis the fifth in the Latin run, and the spelling is Clark's first name for the plant. The neighbours fit: Nimpa with neem (*Azadirachta indica*, English "Neem") and Nyiwa with *Codonopsis convolvulacea*, which is Clark's *snyi.wa* (line 1061). [USER: verify]

**The second glossary row, checked by counting (line 5974).** The T block prints the phonetic names (lines 5860 to 5926) Tab seng, Tak sha, Tamig, Tangkun, Tangzil, Tapak, Tarbu, Thalka dorje, Thangtrom, Tharam, Thamu, Tikta, Tiyangku, **Traagkang** (printed on one line as "Tiyangku Traagkang", line 5898, with the transliteration "tig ta pri yang ku khrag rkang" at line 5901), Tronbu, Tsa tri, Tsendhen marpo, Tsendhen karpo, Tshar bong, Tshe dhum, Tsher ngon, Tsitaka, Tsoe and Tukar. The scientific names (lines 5928 to 5998) run, counting the Hyoscyamus and Datura lines as one slot, to twenty-four as well, and the fourteenth is **"Coptis teetordes chinensis Mamira"** (line 5974), against Traagkang as the fourteenth name. A shorter check fits: Tikta stands against *Swertia chirayta* (line 5968), Tiyangku against "Dracocephalum tanguticum Dragon head" (line 5972, Clark's *pri.yang.ku*, "Callicarpa macrophylla Vahl or Dracocephalum tangutium Maxim"), Tronbu against *Euphorbia stracheyi*, and Tsa tri against *Cicerbita macrorhiza* (Clark's *rtsa.mkhris*, "Ixeris gracilis ... Sonchus sp."). The extra words "chinensis Mamira" on the Coptis line are not explained by the glossary, and the Codex does not interpret them. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, line 1072.* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), section "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). Between *spangs.spos* (spikenard) and *rgud.drus*, it reads:

"myang.rtsi.spras or ser.po.khrag.rkang or lo.mar.sngo.sprin: Gold Thread: Coptis teetoides: Adonis sibirica: [has seven names. It has a glossy, thin, lobed leaf, slender, flexible petioles and a yellow, furry flower. Gold thread has bitter and astringent tastes,], dries fluid [accumulations, pus, etc.], cures infectious fever [and heals wounds. The inferior type is known as sngo.sprin.khrag.rkang or ldum.bu.lchags.kyu.chan and has a long, slightly yellow stalk, scattered green leaves and a slightly bitter taste. Apart from a lack of fluid-drying properties it has the same uses as the superior type above.]"

Clark says (line 82) that interpolations in square brackets are taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The description, the tastes and the "inferior type" are therefore the commentary layer. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan names, the English and Latin names (Clark's identifications), and the statements "dries fluid" and "cures infectious fever". Clark's preface says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" (line 83), which is the case here: the second Latin name, *Adonis sibirica*, is a different genus.

*Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra chapter 21 (line 1211).* In the chapter on the compounding of medicines (heading "21. The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines") the line reads: "The categories of medicines which cure infectious fevers includes Bile of elephant and so forth, Bitter Cucumber, White Aconite, Meadow Cranesbill (Geranium pratense), Fagonia cretica, Populus alba (tree), Primula sibirica (herb), Common Fumitory and Gold Thread."

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, chapter 21 (line 3601).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` lines 3596 to 3602 (printed page 220): "Giwam, Sergyi metok, Bongnga karpo, Gadhur, Ji tsher, Dhewa, Parpata and Traagkang are the group of medicines that cure contagious disorders." The group matches Clark's infectious-fever class name for name where Clark names them (Ji tsher with *Fagonia cretica*, Dhewa with *Primula sibirica* and *Populus alba*, Parpata with Common Fumitory, Bongnga karpo with White Aconite), and Traagkang falls where Clark has Gold Thread. This is a second, independent reason for reading Traagkang as the Coptis. The index prints "Traagkang 220" (line 7520). The Men-Tsee-Khang text has no individual line for Traagkang in its own chapter 20.

*Rejuvenation (Clark ch.23, lines 1266 to 1276).* The chapter on normal health and rejuvenation names its rejuvenating compounds (garlic and ghee, calcined metals with mineral exudate, leadwort with cow's urine, the three myrobalans), and does not name Gold Thread. No *bcud len* (essence extraction) mention of it was found.

*Other editions and traditions.* A search of the Dioscorides and Sushruta folders for *Coptis* found no hit. The Shennong text (Yang 1998, line 2044) prints Huang Lian, "Rhizoma Coptidis Chinensis", as a different species in a different tradition, and Clark's and the glossary's *Coptis teetoides* is not read as the same plant.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Coptis teetoides*, *myang.rtsi.spras*, *khrag.rkang*, *Traagkang* and "Gold Thread" found no entry on this plant. **I_302** (*Coptis chinensis*, Chinese goldthread) and **I_795** (*Coptis groenlandica*) are other species. **I_1303** (*Ixeris gracilis*) counts the same T-block lines (5897 to 5906, 5968 to 5982) and reads Traagkang against the Coptis, as here. **I_1236** (*Asparagus adscendens*) counts the N block (lines 5539 to 5566). **I_1296** (*Geranium pratense*) cites Clark's infectious-fever class (line 1211). **I_1257** (*Corydalis edulis*, *re.skon*, line 1002) is a Clark entry in the same section, about seventy lines above this one. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. As a general fact, *Coptis* is a genus of the buttercup family (Ranunculaceae), and the rhizomes of several *Coptis* species are known to contain isoquinoline alkaloids such as berberine. That is a statement about the genus and not about this species, and the line read gives no composition for the Tibetan drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text records a herb that "dries fluid" and "cures infectious fever" (Clark, line 1072), grouped with other drugs against "contagious disorders" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3601). That is the text's claim, attached to this binomial through two editions' identifications, and it is not a Codex protocol. Fever and infection need medical care, and the Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's line 1072, in its commentary layer, prints the tastes "bitter and astringent" for the superior type and "slightly bitter" for the inferior type; outside the brackets it prints the action "dries fluid". No post-digestive taste and no potency (hot or cool) is printed for it in the lines read. No Chinese classification is given, because the entry is not tagged for the Shennong text.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The rGyud-bzhi places Gold Thread inside the materia medica of the Explanatory Tantra (chapter 20, the herbs) and inside the class of medicines for infectious fever (Clark ch.21, line 1211; Men-Tsee-Khang ch.21, line 3601). The Root Tantra frames medicine as one of four remedies for disease, "diet, behavioral pattern, medicine and accessory therapy" (Clark, line 172). The chapter on rejuvenation (ch.23) does not name Gold Thread, and no *bcud len* use of it is attested in the lines read. It is therefore a therapeutic drug for fever in this text and carries no cultivation status. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. The rGyud-bzhi gives a cultivator no practice with Gold Thread; it is a fever drug in a class of fever drugs. Fever calls for a physician, and the identity of the Tibetan herb is open between two editions' Latin names.

### Contraindications

The lines read print no warning for this drug. The identity is open (Clark prints a second Latin name from another genus), so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Infectious fever needs medical care. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
