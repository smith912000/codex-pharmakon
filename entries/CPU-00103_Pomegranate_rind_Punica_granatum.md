# Pomegranate Rind & Bark — قشر الرمان (Qishr al-Rummān) / Ῥοιά φλοιός (Rhoia phloios) / दाडिम त्वक् (Dāḍima Tvak) / 石榴皮 (Shíliúpí)
## *Punica granatum L. — Pomegranate (Rind, Bark, and Root Bark — the medicinal fractions)*
**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM, EP, SBJ, SS, FMT
**Energetic Classification (TCM):** ◉ Qi
**Training Phase:** `[RESET]`

### Origin & Tradition

*Note: The pomegranate fruit pulp and seeds (aril) are covered in Entry I_190 (Pomegranate — the full fruit). This entry documents the rind, bark, and root bark fractions, which constitute a pharmacologically distinct medicinal tradition across all six traditions. The rind is the most important anthelmintic (anti-parasitic) botanical in the pre-modern world — documented in the Ebers Papyrus, the Assyrian tablets, Dioscorides, Ibn Sina, Sushruta Samhita, and the Bencao Gangmu for the same application: expelling intestinal worms. The punicalagin/ellagitannin complex of the rind provides the antimicrobial and anthelmintic activity, pharmacologically distinct from the antioxidant-cardiovascular profile of the seed and aril.*

**EP — Ebers Papyrus (the primary anthelmintic preparation):** The Ebers Papyrus contains one of the oldest documented anthelmintic protocols: pomegranate rind decoction + senna + honey, drunk over several days, followed by observation for expelled worms. This is a remarkable clinical protocol — the pomegranate rind paralyzes the tapeworm (ellagitannin neurotoxicity to the parasite), senna acts as the cathartic to purge the paralyzed worm, and honey makes the bitter preparation palatable. The formula is pharmacologically rational.

**AT — Assyrian (Rimmūnu bark in Nineveh tablets):** Pomegranate bark preparations appear in Assyrian medical texts explicitly for intestinal worm expulsion and for bloody diarrhea (dysentery) — the astringent antidiarrheal application of the high-tannin rind.

**DMM — De Materia Medica (Ῥοιά — Rhoia; the root bark specifically):** Dioscorides covers pomegranate root bark in Book I as the primary anthelmintic. He notes: the root bark decoction for tapeworm; the rind for diarrhea and dysentery (astringent); the flowers (Balaustion — Βαλαύστιον) as an astringent for excessive bleeding; the three distinct medicinal fractions (root bark, rind, flower) each with specific applications. The anthelmintic dose: the root bark decoction drunk on an empty stomach before purgation.

**CM — Canon of Medicine (قشر الرمان — Qishr al-Rummān; جذر الرمان — Jidhr al-Rummān):** Ibn Sina distinguishes the fruit, rind, flower, and root bark pharmacologically. The rind: cold and dry; astringent; for diarrhea, dysentery; uterine hemorrhage; the root bark: the anthelmintic preparation for tapeworm; the flowers: the strongest astringent for bleeding gums and wounds. He documents a specific anthelmintic protocol nearly identical to the Ebers Papyrus formula.

**SS — Sushruta Samhita (Dāḍima Tvak — pomegranate rind; root bark for krimi/parasites):** Sushruta and Charaka document pomegranate rind (Dāḍima tvak) as: Kashaya (astringent) rasa; Sheeta virya; Kapha-Pitta shamaka; primary application for Atisara (diarrhea/dysentery); Krimi (intestinal parasites — the anthelmintic application); Raktapitta (bleeding conditions). The root bark is classified separately as the more potent anthelmintic fraction.

**SBJ — Bencao Gangmu (石榴皮 — Shíliúpí — pomegranate skin):** Li Shizhen documents pomegranate rind as: sour-astringent, warm; astringes the intestines; stops diarrhea; kills parasites; stops bleeding. Classical formula: *Shíliú Pí Sǎn* (石榴皮散 — Pomegranate Rind Powder) for chronic diarrhea and dysentery. The root bark is documented separately as more powerfully anthelmintic.

### Active Components

- **Punicalagin α and β** — ellagitannins (the largest known tannins by molecular weight: ~1,084 Da); the dominant bioactive in the rind; antimicrobial (bacteriostatic against *E. coli*, *S. aureus*, *H. pylori*); anthelmintic (disrupts parasite muscle neurotransmission); the principal astringent compound
- **Ellagic acid** — hydrolysis product of punicalagin; anti-inflammatory (NF-κB inhibition); anti-cancer (cell cycle arrest); antioxidant; produced from punicalagin by gut bacteria; partially absorbed as urolithins (A and B) by the metabolically capable ~40% of the population
- **Granatin A and B** — unique ellagitannins specific to pomegranate; anthelmintic; potent free radical scavengers
- **Pelargonidin-3-glucoside, cyanidin-3-glucoside** — anthocyanins (present in rind; also in aril); anti-inflammatory; the red color compounds
- **Ursolic acid, oleanolic acid** — pentacyclic triterpenoids in the rind; anti-inflammatory; antimicrobial; the secondary bacteriostatic fraction
- **Alkaloids (pelletierine, pseudopelletierine — root bark)** — the primary anthelmintic compounds of the root bark; neuromuscular paralytic effect on tapeworms; toxic to mammals at high doses (CAUTION — see contraindications); concentrated in the root bark, not the rind

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**1. Anti-diarrheal and gut antimicrobial ([RESET] — GI illness recovery)**
The punicalagin astringent and antimicrobial activity makes pomegranate rind the classical first-line anti-diarrheal botanical. The mechanism is dual: direct antimicrobial against diarrhea-causing pathogens (*E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *Shigella*, *C. difficile*) and mucosal astringency (protein precipitation tightens the intestinal mucosa). For practitioners recovering from GI illness interrupting training, pomegranate rind tea is one of the safest and best-evidenced natural anti-diarrheal preparations.

**2. Anti-H. pylori (chronic gastritis / ulcer support)**
Punicalagin demonstrates among the strongest anti-*H. pylori* activity of any plant compound — including against clarithromycin-resistant strains. This is pharmacologically relevant for practitioners with recurrent gastritis or diagnosed *H. pylori* infection requiring supportive botanical therapy alongside or after standard triple therapy.

**3. Anthelmintic (intestinal parasites — traditional application with supervision)**
The classic EP/AT/DMM/CM/SS anthelmintic application for tapeworm uses the root bark decoction (the pelletierine-containing fraction) followed by purging. This is a SUPERVISED application — pelletierine at excessive doses is toxic to mammals. The rind decoction (without root bark) is safe for general use and provides significant anti-parasitic activity through the ellagitannin pathway.

### Preparation & Dosing

**Pomegranate rind tea (anti-diarrheal — safe general use):**
5–10g dried pomegranate rind in 300mL water; boil 15 minutes; drink 200mL 2–3x/day for acute diarrhea. The high-tannin decoction; may taste intensely astringent — dilute with additional water if needed. Duration: 3–5 days maximum for acute use.

**Standardized pomegranate extract (anti-H. pylori):**
500mg pomegranate rind extract (standardized to 40% ellagitannins) 2x/day with meals; as adjunct to conventional *H. pylori* therapy or for recurrent gastric inflammation.

**Pomegranate rind powder (traditional Ayurvedic — Dāḍima tvak churna):**
3–5g dried rind powder mixed with honey or warm water; traditional preparation for chronic diarrhea and intestinal conditions.

*Root bark preparations for anthelmintic use: SPECIALIST ONLY due to pelletierine toxicity risk.*

### Scientific Correlation

- **Anti-H. pylori (punicalagin):** Voravuthikunchai SP et al. (2004) *J Health Sci* 50:453-460: pomegranate rind extract demonstrated strong antibacterial activity against *H. pylori* including metronidazole-resistant strains; MIC values comparable to clarithromycin
- **Anti-diarrheal:** Qneibi M et al. (2016) *J Pharm Pharmacol*: pomegranate rind polyphenols demonstrated significant anti-diarrheal activity via intestinal smooth muscle relaxation and antimicrobial mechanisms confirmed in rodent model
- **Ellagitannin antimicrobial:** Fuhrman B et al. (2005) *J Nutr Biochem* 16:360-368: punicalagin significantly inhibited *E. coli* and *Staphylococcus aureus* growth at concentrations achievable via food-grade preparations
- **Urolithin production:** Selma MV et al. (2009) *J Agric Food Chem* 57:6888-6893: identified the gut microbial conversion of ellagitannins to urolithins A and B; demonstrated population stratification into high/low producers (~40% vs. ~60% of subjects)

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian (EP).** No cultivation framing is attested for the rind. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records an anthelmintic protocol — rind decoction with a cathartic, drunk over days, worms observed on expulsion — not any action of the drug on the person's vital constitution. No *heka* recitation is preserved with this preparation in the sources retrieved, so the operative-power dimension cannot be reconstructed here. This is a therapeutic record and nothing more. **(c) absent.**

**Assyrian (AT).** No cultivation status is attested, and the standing blocking warning on Assyrian substance identifications, tablet references and agency attributions is in force. The bark appears in the Nineveh material for worm expulsion and bloody flux; where illness is classified by causal agency, an anthelmintic is defined by the intruder it drives out, not by any constitutional good it confers. **(c) absent.**

**Greek (DMM).** Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured here. What the *De Materia Medica* offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: he separates root bark, rind and flower (*balaustion*) as three distinct medicinal fractions with distinct actions and doses, and specifies the anthelmintic root-bark decoction taken on an empty stomach before purging. That precision — knowing which fraction, gathered how, does what — is the real practice. No vital-cultivation status is claimed. **(c) absent.**

**Unani (CM).** Ibn Sina places the rind as cold and dry, astringent — a therapeutic humoral assignment for diarrhoea, dysentery and uterine flux, with the root bark reserved as the anthelmintic. No conservation of innate heat and radical moisture is attached to it; a cooling astringent is corrective, not a substance one takes to husband the finite vital endowment. It carries a therapeutic profile in this tradition, not a cultivation one. **(b) framed — claimed by the tradition, no cultivation status attested.**

**Chinese (TCM).** The rind (石榴皮 *shíliúpí*) is recorded as sour-astringent and warm — astringes the intestines, stops diarrhoea, kills parasites, stops bleeding. This wording is drawn from Li Shizhen's *Bencao Gangmu* (1596), a late compilation, and no *san pin* 三品 grade is recorded for it in the sources retrieved. It therefore carries no cultivation status here, only a therapeutic one; an astringent draining-and-securing agent sits at the opposite pole from the upper-grade substances taken long-term to nourish life. **(b) framed — no *san pin* grade attested.**

**Ayurveda (SS).** Sushruta and Charaka give the rind (*dāḍima tvak*) as *kashaya* (astringent) *rasa*, *sheeta virya*, *kapha-pitta shamaka*, for *atisara* (flux) and *krimi* (intestinal parasites). This is a therapeutic assignment; no *rasayana* or *vajikarana* standing is attested for the rind. The fruit's wider symbolic and dietetic reputation does not transfer to the astringent drug fraction. **(b) framed — no *rasayana* status attested.**

**Tibetan (FMT).** The pomegranate is a warming digestive in the Tibetan materia medica, but no *bcud len* (essence-extraction) role and no subtle-body-practice category are attested for the rind, and the Four Tantras assert no such formal category to fill. Medicine here is the third of four treatment branches — it supports practice without reaching the root. **(c) absent.**

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing here to cultivate. Across every tradition the rind and bark are a `[RESET]` drug in the strict sense — an astringent, antimicrobial, anthelmintic agent taken for a defined complaint over a defined short course and then stopped. The pomegranate's enormous symbolic weight (Persephone, fertility, Jewish and Persian iconography) attaches to the whole fruit and seed, not to the rind, and symbolism is not a cultivation status in any case: it tells you what the fruit meant, not what the drug does to a body. Keep the two apart. The honest cultivator's use of this fraction is brief, targeted, and self-terminating.

### Contraindications

- **Root bark preparation — CAUTION** — pelletierine alkaloids in the root bark are toxic at overdose; traditional anthelmintic doses require accurate measurement; not for self-treatment; only the rind (not root bark) for general use
- **Constipation** — the strong astringency of high-dose rind preparations will worsen constipation; not for use in those with chronic constipation
- **Drug interactions** — ellagitannins may inhibit CYP3A4 at high doses; monitor with immunosuppressants, antiretrovirals; separate from drug dosing by 2 hours
- **Pregnancy** — anthelmintic preparations contraindicated; rind tea at low doses traditionally considered safe for anti-diarrheal use but therapeutic doses avoided
