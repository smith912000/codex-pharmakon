# Orchis incarnata — dwang.po.lag.pa, Wangpo lagpa, "a type of Salep Orchid" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Orchis incarnata Linn., the only binomial Clark 1995 prints for dwang.po.lag.pa, after the English phrase "a type of Salep Orchid"; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints Dactylorhiza hatagirea against the name "Wangpo lagpa". This is the same Tibetan name and the same Clark line (1130) as I_1263* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and on a species name that only one of them prints.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian, Badianus, Avicenna and Sushruta material on disk for *Orchis* found nothing. Dioscorides prints orchid chapters (3-141 *Orchis*, line 7761, and the neighbouring chapters for other species) whose translators' lines name other species (for example *Orchis militaris*); they are the subject of other entries (I_1140 to I_1143 are among them) and are not read as this drug. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Same passage as an existing entry.** The Clark line this entry rests on, line 1130, is the line I_1263 (*Dactylorhiza hatagirea*, tag FMT) already quotes in full under the same Tibetan name. This entry and I_1263 are one Tibetan drug under two binomials, Clark's and the glossary's, and they are not merged (the spine carries a row for each). I_1263 is the entry that also treats the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary and running text for the name; this entry reads those lines again only to set the two editions side by side.

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 209) the row for *dwang.po.lag.pa* reads "a type of Salep Orchid; Orchis incarnata Linn." with no "key uses the first" note. The first item is an English phrase and not a Latin name, so the key *Orchis incarnata* is the **only** binomial Clark prints for the name and not one of several. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", which is not the case for this line. The glossary's binomial, *Dactylorhiza hatagirea*, has its own row (names.csv line 389) and its own entry (I_1263).

**Identity doubt.** Clark's own preface (line 68) says that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect" and that some specimens "remain to be identified", and the two editions here give two different genera for one Tibetan name. The English names agree in kind: Clark's "Salep Orchid" against the glossary's "Dactylorhiza" (an English-column entry, line 6048). The Codex reads Clark's *dwang.po.lag.pa* and the glossary's "Wangpo lagpa dbang po lag pa" as one drug from the near-identical spelling, the agreement of the English names, and the agreement in kind between "tonic, aphrodisiac" and "develops physical strength and increases seminal fluid"; neither text says so. The Codex does not choose between the two species. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1130).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry sits between *sog.ka.pa* ("Shepherd's Purse", line 1129) and *ri.sho* ("Hill Dockleaf", line 1131). It reads:

"dwang.po.lag.pa: a type of Salep Orchid: Orchis incarnata Linn.: [has twelve names, for example brgya.bying, etc., and grows in meadows and near springs. Its root resembles a man's hand (i.e., with five-fingered tubers), and the flowers are yellowish-blue. It has a green leaf and petiole and an inflorescence resembling the layered structure of a stupa. The six-fingered type is known as tsogs.bdag (Ganesh), whilst the five-fingered type is known as sbang.lag (Indrahanta), the fourfingered type as dri.zahi.lag.pa (Gandhamadna) and the single tuber as thehu.rang. The common type has a yellow flower, and all types having three or more tubers] are tonic, aphrodisiac [and collect poison]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the twelve names, the habitat, the root, the flowers, the types and their names, and the closing "and collect poison". Outside them (the root-text layer, as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the English phrase, the binomial, which is Clark's identification and not the root text's, and the words "are tonic, aphrodisiac". The line prints no taste, no power and no preparation. Clark's name index lists *dwang.po.lag.pa* (line 1722), *tsogs.bdag* (line 1734), *thehu.rang* (line 1703) and *dri.zahi.lag.pa* (line 1705); his Latin index lists *Orchis incarnata* (line 1621) and "Salep Orchid" (line 1641).

*Clark 1995, introduction (lines 69 and 72).* The preface, in Clark's own voice and not the root text's, says: "Some of these such as Round-leaf Sundew, and the roots of Angelica, Polygonatum and Salep orchid have exceptional fortifying and rejuvenating properties. The latter root was even popular in eighteenth-century London as a tonic infusion. These as well as certain flowers and types of calcite and mineral exudate have long been renowned also in the preparation of 'essence-extraction' compounds, which apart from their invigorating qualities enable meditators and yogis to live without intake of gross food and thus engage in spiritual practices with enhanced mental clarity, sharpness and pliancy." (The sentence runs across a page break, lines 69 to 72.) It names "Salep orchid" by its English name only and gives no Tibetan name, so the Codex reads it as bearing on this drug from Clark's use of the same English phrase at line 1130. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary and running text.* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The glossary's "W" block prints, on one line, "Wangpo lagpa dbang po lag pa Dactylorhiza hatagirea" (line 6026). The name is not in the taste groups of chapter 19 and not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23, lines 3900 to 3950). The text gives no part used and no preparation.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Orchis* is a genus of terrestrial orchids (Orchidaceae) growing from underground tubers, and that dried orchid tubers, the material called salep, are generally known to be rich in a mucilaginous polysaccharide. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained, and the species is uncertain.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Clark records that the drug is "tonic, aphrodisiac" (line 1130), and Men-Tsee-Khang that "Wangpo lagpa develops physical strength and increases seminal fluid" (line 3495). These are the texts' own claims, and the commentary layer adds that it collects poison. They are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. Wild orchid tubers are legally and ecologically sensitive (see Contraindications), and this entry does not recommend gathering or buying them.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary describes the tubers and the preface names "the roots", so the root is the part the sources speak of, but no line says how it is prepared for the Tibetan drug. The preface's remark that the root "was even popular in eighteenth-century London as a tonic infusion" describes London practice and not the Tibetan drug. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The materia medica line classes the drug by what it does: it is tonic and aphrodisiac (Clark, line 1130), and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says it develops physical strength and increases seminal fluid (line 3495). The only line in either edition that moves toward cultivation is Clark's introduction (lines 69 to 72), which names the roots of "Salep orchid" among herbs with "exceptional fortifying and rejuvenating properties" and says that these plants have "long been renowned also in the preparation of 'essence-extraction' compounds". That sentence is the translator's, it names the plant by an English phrase, and it has no root-text line behind it: Clark's materia medica marks essence-extraction ingredients drug by drug (for example lines 853, 971 and 1144) and does not do so at line 1130, and the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23 in both editions) does not name an orchid. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, that *bcud len* (essence extraction) is located inside the Four Tantras, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. The entry therefore records the introduction's claim as the translator's framing and attaches no cultivation status to the drug.

**Practitioner's note.** The Tibetan lines give a cultivator nothing to do with this plant beyond what a physician would: it is a tonic and aphrodisiac drug, with no preparation and no dose, and the species is uncertain between two genera. Clark's introduction links the salep orchid root to essence-extraction compounds, but as the translator's remark and not as a prescription. Wild orchid tubers should not be gathered on the strength of it. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. The identity is uncertain between two binomials, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. It is a general fact that all wild orchids are covered by CITES (Appendix II or I), which regulates trade, and that gathering wild orchid tubers can harm populations; the legal and conservation status of the species was not checked in the sources.
