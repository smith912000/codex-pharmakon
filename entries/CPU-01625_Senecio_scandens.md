# Senecio scandens — rgud.drus, Gudue, rgu drs (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Senecio scandens Buch.Ham, the first of three binomials Clark 1995 prints for rgud.drus (with Senecio frabri L. and Galium boreale L., spelled as printed), and the one the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints in its scientific-name column* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the line prints three. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed names (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1073).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads: "rgud.drus: Senecio scandens Buch.Ham: Senecio frabri L.: Galium boreale L.: [has a thin, smooth leaf, white flowers with a reddish hue growing from each joint, and an ovary containing seeds. It has hot and bitter tastes, and the flower may also be pale blue and slightly yellow. The inferior type has a long, slender, lobed leaf and yellow flower. Both types] heal wounds, unite severed channels, cure colic [and wound fever, and are recommended in fever, infectious fever and poisoning]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". So the bracketed parts of the entry (the description of the leaf, flowers and ovary, the "hot and bitter tastes", the inferior type, the wound fever and the use in fever, infectious fever and poisoning) are the commentary layer. What stands outside the brackets is the root-text layer as Clark gives it: the plant "heal[s] wounds, unite[s] severed channels, cure[s] colic". The text outside the brackets thus gives three actions and no taste or power; the taste ("hot and bitter") is inside the brackets. The second binomial is printed "Senecio frabri L."; it is reproduced as printed and the Codex does not say what it stands for. [USER: verify]

The Tibetan name appears also in Clark's index of Tibetan names (line 1680: "... rgu.thub. rgud.drus. rgun.hbrum ..."), and the English-Latin index prints *Senecio scandens* and *Senecio frabri* (line 1644) and *Galium boreale* (line 1590). No other line of Clark's text names the drug: a search of the file for *rgud* and *Senecio scandens* found line 1073 and these indexes only. A different drug, *yu.gu.shing* (line 1019, "Senecio dianthus Franch (white type)"), carries another *Senecio* and is not this drug.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents line 225; closing line 3583, "This is the twentieth chapter, which reveals the efficacy of medicinal substances"), prints, in a run of herbal drugs at line 3448: "Gudue treats open wounds, joins ruptured blood vessels and cures intestinal pains." The index gives "Gudue 209" (line 7380). The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate blocks: the phonetic name "Gudue" at line 5278 (in the run "Go nyoe Gudue"), the Tibetan "rgu drs" at line 5280 (in a row of Tibetan names ending "go snyod rgu drs"), and the scientific name "Senecio scandens" at line 5287 (last in a column that also holds *Cinnamomum camphora*, *Rubus ellipticus*, *Bergenia stracheyi* and *Hedychium spicatum*). The glossary does not print these on one row, and the columns hold different numbers of entries, so the pairing of "Gudue" with "Senecio scandens" is by the order of the columns and is not printed. The Codex does not state that the glossary pairs them. That *Gudue* is a form of *rgud.drus* is a reading from the sound of the name and from the agreement on wounds, and is not stated in the text. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *rgud.drus*, *Gudue* and *Senecio scandens* found no entry on this drug. One entry touches the same Clark line: I_906 (Cleavers / Goosegrass) says in its Tibetan paragraph that "a neighbouring entry, *rgud.drus* (line 1073), prints *Galium boreale* L. among its identifications and is a different Tibetan name; it is not attributed to this row." The genus-level *Senecio* entries I_843 (*Senecio jacobaea*) and I_1190 (*Senecio vulgaris*, this batch) are Dioscorides-based entries on other species and say nothing of this drug. None of them is an entry on *rgud.drus*.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Senecio* is a large genus of the daisy family (Asteraceae), and that many species of the genus are known to contain pyrrolizidine alkaloids, which are toxic to the liver. Whether the plant of the Tibetan drug is such a species, and which of the three binomials it is, is not settled by the lines read. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the Tibetan drug. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that heals wounds, unites severed channels and cures colic (Clark line 1073, outside the brackets), which the commentary layer extends to wound fever, fever, infectious fever and poisoning, and which the Men-Tsee-Khang text states as treating open wounds, joining ruptured blood vessels and curing intestinal pains (line 3448). These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for wounds or colic, and the botanical identity is uncertain.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark line 1073 prints "hot and bitter tastes" for the plant, inside the square brackets (the commentary layer). It prints no power outside the brackets. The Men-Tsee-Khang text (line 3448) prints no taste or power for *Gudue*. No other quality is printed in the lines read.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes the plant by what it does: healing wounds, uniting severed channels and curing colic, with fevers and poisoning added by the commentary layer. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this entry places *rgud.drus* in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. In Clark's herbs section the essence-extraction mention near this entry is for the white mugwort sub-species (the *mkhan.pa* entry at line 1078, inside the brackets), not for *rgud.drus*, which is not named there. Its framing is therapeutic only. "Channels" here are the text's own term for conduits in the wounded body, and the Codex does not read a subtle-body doctrine into it.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a wound-healing, channel-uniting and colic-curing drug in the Tibetan texts, and nothing more can be said from them. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. A wound that does not heal, abdominal pain that persists, and fever with poisoning need medical care. The botanical identity is uncertain (three binomials in Clark), and many *Senecio* species are generally known to be liver-toxic, so no plant should be gathered or taken on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
