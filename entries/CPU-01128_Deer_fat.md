# Deer fat — "Deer fat" under "Fat" (Clark's English); the same item in the Men-Tsee-Khang English
## *The fat of the deer as a remedy in the Tibetan Explanatory Tantra. Clark's translation prints "Deer fat" with no Tibetan headword and no binomial; the source list `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` (line 362) has a row "Deer fat", class animal, from "Explanatory Tantra ch.20 (Efficacy of Medicinal Substances)"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The text on disk is Barry Clark's 1995 *The Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). Chapter 20, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (line 792), says that "The power of medicines is twofold: (a) the power of taste and (b) the natural power [of the substance]" (line 794), and its "specific section" divides the individual substances into eight kinds, the eighth being "(8) animal products" (line 802). The animal products are arranged by body part, and "Fat" is one heading (line 1179). The line under it reads (line 1180):

"Snake fat: expels bullets, (shrapnel, etc.) [and cures anterior transposition of the calf muscle.] Deer fat: is anthelmintic and protects against poisoning. Pig fat: collects poison, heals psoriasis [and cures baldness]. Human fat: subdues wind disorders and heals psoriasis."

So the Tibetan text gives deer fat two actions, that it "is anthelmintic" and that it "protects against poisoning", and places it with the fats of the snake, the pig and the human. Clark's introduction says that "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary, whilst those in parentheses ( ) are derived from the oral instructions of Dr. Yeshe Donden and Dr. Pema Dorje" (line 82). The two actions of deer fat are not in brackets, so they are the text's own. The fats of the other animals on the line carry bracketed additions, but the deer-fat entry has none.

The second English text on disk, the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`), has the same sentence (line 3546): "Deer fat cures ein disorders and protects from poisoning." It keeps the Tibetan term *ein* in phonetic form, as its translators say it does for technical terms ("key technical terms of Tibetan medicine... have been kept in phonetic Tibetan", preface), and I do not define it. The same pairing occurs for other items. For "Deer blood", Clark has "is anthelmintic" (line 1172) and Men-Tsee-Khang has "cures ein disorders" (line 3539). From that parallel it appears that Clark's "anthelmintic" renders the same disorder as the Men-Tsee-Khang "ein disorders", but neither text states the equation, and I record it as an appearance only. The Men-Tsee-Khang text introduces the animal group with "There are thirteen medicinal substances derived from animals. They are the horn, bone, flesh, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, fur, urine, feces and the entire body of an animal" (line 3503). Fat is therefore one of the thirteen kinds, and deer fat is one of the fats named under it.

Clark's introduction says that "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57). The Men-Tsee-Khang preface says that "the use of animal products was negligible", that "animal products in Tibetan medicine have been completely substituted by equally effective herbal substances", and that their potencies are given "to preserve the understanding of the therapeutic values of animal products" (line 687).

The same chapter prints other deer products, which I read and list so that they are not lost: "sha.wa: Tibetan Deer: Cervus elaphus: [has three names]" with horns of "similar [action] to Rhino horn" (Clark lines 1153-1154), "Deer blood: is anthelmintic, halts bleeding from the womb [and prevents hangovers]" (line 1172), and "Brain of game animals (e.g. deer): halts diarrhoea" (line 1182). These are separate materials from the fat.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, animal fat is mainly triglycerides (fatty acids esterified to glycerol), with small amounts of cholesterol and fat-soluble vitamins, depending on the animal and its diet. Nothing more specific can be said without a source.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text records deer fat as an anthelmintic and a protection against poisoning. The Codex does not recommend sourcing it. Deer products are subject to hunting and trade controls in many places, the material cannot be checked by a buyer, a protective effect against poisoning is not something an unverified fat can offer, and the tradition's own present-day practice, as its translators describe it, has moved to herbal substitutes.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives no preparation for deer fat. It does not say whether it is rendered, taken by mouth, or applied, and the lines read give no quantity. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Animal fat is an energy-dense food and cannot treat an intestinal parasite or protect against a poison. A person who has swallowed a poison needs emergency care, and intestinal worms are treated with specific medicines. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** None printed for this item. Chapter 20 says that medicines act by taste and by natural power, and lists eight powers (heavy, oily, cool, blunt, light, coarse, hot, sharp; Clark line 796). The deer-fat entry (line 1180) prints no taste, power or post-digestive taste, and neither does the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence (line 3546). Clark's introduction says that the later *Crystal Orb* and *Crystal Rosary* give each medicine's tastes, potencies and qualities (line 57), but that text is not on disk, so none is supplied here.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for deer fat in the lines read. Chapter 20 assigns it two therapeutic actions, anthelmintic and protective against poisoning, and says nothing about essence extraction (*bcud len*) or any other practice. The Men-Tsee-Khang preface records a Buddhist objection to causing harm to any being as the reason animal products have been replaced by herbs (line 687), which is the tradition's own comment on animal products in general and not a cultivation status for this one.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. Deer fat is one clause in a line of four fats, and its tradition describes animal products as largely replaced. The honest use of the entry is as a record. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Poisoning:** a suspected poisoning is a medical emergency; do not rely on any fat or other animal product.
- **Intestinal worms:** see a clinician for diagnosis and proper treatment.
- **Hygiene:** raw or poorly rendered animal fat is an infection risk, and wild game can carry zoonotic disease.
- **Law and conservation:** deer are hunted under licence or protected in many jurisdictions, and trade in wild-animal products is restricted; the Codex does not recommend sourcing any.
- **Ethics:** the Codex does not recommend killing or harming wild animals for these uses, and the Tibetan translators themselves record a Buddhist objection to harming any being.
