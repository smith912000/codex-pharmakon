# Cistanche deserticola — Rou Song Rong 肉松容 (herb), also written Rou Cong Rang in the translator's note (Shennong Bencao Jing)

## *Cistanche deserticola, as the pharmaceutical Latin "Herba Cistanchis Deserticolae" that Yang 1998 prints for the entry "Rou Song Rong"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Shennong text (SBJ).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The entry is in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`). The Latin name is the translator's pharmaceutical Latin and was matched to the binomial by stem in the extraction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv`, line 98). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The characters 肉松容 come from the Codex's own list of Shennong names (`_sources/shennong.csv`, line 135), which prints them alone, and not from Yang's English text. They follow Yang's romanisation "Rou Song Rong". Yang's own note 138 (line 2335) spells the modern name "Rou Cong Rang", and the Codex's file `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/SBJ_Original_Chinese.md` (line 193) prints 肉苁蓉 for it; that file is a Codex compilation and not a primary text, and was not used for any claim below. The spelling and the characters are therefore not settled by the lines read. [USER: verify]

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *Rou Song Rong (Herba Cistanchis Deserticolae), PDF page 61.* The page marker `[[p61]]` is at line 2314, the heading "Herbs: Middle Class" is printed directly beneath it at line 2315, and the entry runs from line 2316 to line 2323. The entry that follows, Ji Li Zi (*Semen Astragali Complanati*, line 2324), is a separate drug. The text file's OCR has damaged four words (it prints "stren~hens", "con~omerations", "takin~" and a broken line "may make the / body light"); they are read here as strengthens, conglomerations and taking. The entry reads:

"Rou Song Rong (Herba Cistanchis Deserticolae)138 is salty. It mainly treats the five taxations and seven damages, supplements the center, eliminates cold and heat and pain in the penis, nourishes the five viscera, strengthens yin, and boosts the essence qi. In females, it makes pregnancy possible and [treats] concretions and conglomerations. Protracted taking may make the body light. It grows in mountains and valleys."

The entry prints no other name, no part of the plant and no preparation. Yang's note 138 (lines 2335 to 2339) is the translator's modern commentary and not part of the classic. It says: "Nowadays, this medicinal is called Rou Cong Rang. It supplements life gate ministerial fire, moistens the five viscera, and boosts the essence and blood." It adds that the drug "treats taxation damage, cold pain in the lumbus and knees, flooding, vaginal discharge, and seminal emission" and "is particularly good for infertility due to insufficiency and expiry of pure yang". Of these, the entry shares the five viscera, essence and the matter of conception; "life gate ministerial fire", "blood", "lumbus and knees" and "pure yang" are the note's words and are not in the entry.

*The class heading.* Yang's table of contents prints "Herbs: Middle Class" (line 716), and the heading is printed at the top of the page of this entry (line 2315, page 61). It is also printed at the top of page 59 (line 2263); page 60, between them (marker at line 2276), prints none. The preface (lines 385 to 390) says of this class: "There are 120 medium class medicinals which are used as ministers. They mainly nurture personality and correspond to humanity. They may or may not be toxic, and [therefore,] one should weigh and ponder before putting them to their appropriate use. If one intends to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion, one should base [one's efforts] on the middle class." The superscript numbers were lost in the OCR. A translator's note (lines 404 and 405) says: "It was believed that some medicinals are effective for treating disease and, at the same time, are good for the cultivation of various virtues in human beings." The OCR dropped its number, and the Codex reads it as the note on "nurture personality". Yang's commentary (lines 358 to 362) says that middle-class medicinals "are able to cultivate personality or modify temperament" and that "this class of medicinals may be toxic, their prescription requires care". The entry itself prints no warning.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Cistanche deserticola*, *Rou Song Rong* and *Rou Cong Rong* found two entries on the same drug under another spine row. I_070 (*Cistanche tubulosa*, spine CPU-00673, tags SBJ and FMT) names both *C. tubulosa* and *C. deserticola* in its identity line, and I_069 (Cistanche, tags SBJ and CM) is a second file on the genus; neither was merged and neither was read as a source here. A search of both Tibetan texts for *Cistanche* found no line, so the FMT tag on I_070 is not confirmed by the Tibetan text files on disk. I_002, I_126, I_161, I_614 and I_801 mention the Chinese name in passing and are not entries on this drug. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Cistanche* is a genus of fleshy root-parasitic plants of the broomrape family (Orobanchaceae) that carry no chlorophyll and live on the roots of host plants, and that the genus is generally known to contain phenylethanoid glycosides such as echinacoside. Nothing here says what the Shennong drug contained, and the Latin name is the translator's. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Shennong text records, in its own terms, a salty drug for the five taxations and seven damages, for supplementing the center, for cold and heat and pain in the penis, for the five viscera, yin and the essence qi, and for conception and concretions and conglomerations in women. It adds that protracted taking may make the body light. These are the text's claims and not modern diagnoses, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The entry gives no preparation, no part of the plant, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. The translator's Latin name says "Herba" (herb), and the Codex takes that as the translator's, not as the classic's. The phrase "protracted taking" implies repeated use over time and gives no number. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SBJ:** "salty" (line 2316). No temperature, no toxicity and no meridian or other system of later Chinese medicine is printed in the entry. The preface says of the middle class that it "may or may not be toxic" (line 387), which is a statement about the class and not about this entry.

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: the middle class (中品), by the printed heading, with a "protracted taking" sentence.* The class is assigned by the heading "Herbs: Middle Class", printed directly above the entry at line 2315 (`[[p61]]`). It is a printed class and not a promise of cultivation use. The preface (lines 385 to 390) gives the middle class a purpose: it mainly nurtures personality, it may or may not be toxic, and it is the class to base one's efforts on to "control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion". The Codex reads the class as the classic's middle grade, between the long-term class and the disease-treating class, and does not read it as a *yang sheng* ("nourishing life") drug in the sense the superior class carries.

The entry itself adds one sentence that touches cultivation: "Protracted taking may make the body light" (lines 2321 to 2323). The Codex quotes the English as Yang prints it and does not turn it into any pharmacological effect. The entry also says that the drug "nourishes the five viscera, strengthens yin, and boosts the essence qi" (lines 2317 to 2319). Those are the text's own words as the translator renders them, and the Codex does not restate them as a *jing*, *shen* or *neidan* action beyond that quotation.

**Practitioner's note.** A middle-class entry in the Chinese scheme, with a "may make the body light" sentence for protracted taking, no preparation and no dose. The class itself carries a caution about toxicity and about weighing the use, and the entry gives no number for "protracted". Nothing here supports taking this plant as a cultivation practice on the strength of the entry, and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The Shennong entry prints no warning, and its silence is not a statement of safety; the preface says of the middle class that it "may or may not be toxic" and that one "should weigh and ponder before putting them to their appropriate use". The identity rests on a translator's Latin name, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. The entry says the drug "makes pregnancy possible", which is an indication and not a safety statement for pregnancy; no safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
