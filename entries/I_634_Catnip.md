# I-634 | Catnip

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted binomial:** *Nepeta cataria* L. (1753)
**Family:** Lamiaceae (Nepetoideae, tribe Mentheae — sometimes placed in subtribe Nepetinae)
**Authority:** Carl Linnaeus, *Species Plantarum* (1753)

**Synonyms and historical names:**
- *Nepeta cataria* var. *citriodora* (Becker) Balb. — lemon-scented form (nepetalactone chemotype II)
- *Cataria vulgaris* Moench (older classification)

**Etymology:** *Nepeta* from *Nepete* (Nepi), an Etruscan/Latin city in central Italy (modern Nepi, province of Viterbo, Lazio) — Pliny states that this genus was named after the town near which it grew abundantly. *Cataria* from Latin *catta/cattus* (cat) — directly referencing the well-documented behavioral response in domestic cats.

**Botanical description:**
- Herbaceous perennial, 40–100 cm; erect; branched above; aromatic
- Stems: square (typical Lamiaceae); densely grey-tomentose (woolly) with whitish hairs giving grey-green appearance; distinctive even at distance
- Leaves: petiolate; cordate-ovate, 3–7 cm; coarsely crenate-serrate; grey-green above, paler/more hairy below; strongly aromatic when crushed — complex minty-green-camphoraceous scent
- Inflorescence: dense interrupted whorled spikes; terminal and axillary; 2–4 cm long
- Flowers: bilabiate; white with purple spotting on lower lip; 6–12 mm; calyx 5-lobed; 4 stamens in two pairs
- Fruit: 4 small brown nutlets
- Aromatic profile: distinctive green-minty-camphoraceous; noticeably different from peppermint or spearmint; complex nepetalactone note (insect repellent character); earthy undertone

**Distribution:**
- Native: eastern Europe, central and southwestern Asia (Turkey, Caucasus, northern Iran, central Asia)
- Extensively naturalized: North America (now abundant weed in roadsides, disturbed land throughout northeastern and midwest US, Canada), western Europe (Britain, France, Germany), southern hemisphere temperate zones
- Grows in well-drained, calcareous or neutral soils; disturbed habitats; roadsides; field margins; rarely deep woodland; heliophilous to semi-shade tolerant
- Elevation: sea level to 1,500 m; adaptable

**Chemotypic variation:**
| Chemotype | Key compounds | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Nepetalactone I (4aα,7α,7aβ) | 75–95% nepetalactone (Z,E isomer) | Most common in cultivated and North American plants; preferred by cats |
| Nepetalactone II (4aα,7β,7aβ) | Citronellol + geraniol dominant; lemon-scent | *N. cataria* var. *citriodora*; cats less responsive; lemony odour |
| Mixed | Variable nepetalactone with epinepetalactone | Some wild populations; intermediate feline response |

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Low convergence context:**
The 1/9 convergence score accurately reflects *N. cataria*'s status as a primarily European and North American medicinal herb with its greatest recognition in folk medicine traditions rather than classical codified medical systems. Its absence from the major Asian medical traditions (SBJ, SS, FMT) is geographic — the plant does not appear in Chinese, Indian, or Tibetan pharmacopoeias because it was not available in those regions during the formative periods of those medical systems.

The European folk tradition (which built upon the classical Greco-Roman tradition) is where catnip accumulated its strongest documented use: as a **pediatric herb** (colic, colds, fevers in children), mild sedative/anxiolytic, and carminative.

**Primary significance of this entry:**
1. **Nepetalactone chemistry** — uniquely interesting; iridoid monoterpene with potent and well-characterized effects on felid behavior; also active as insect repellent (DEET comparison studies)
2. **Pediatric herb status** — one of the most consistently documented herbs for infant colic and childhood fever in European/American folk medicine
3. **Biochemical curiosity** — the feline behavioral response illuminates receptor biology relevant to mammalian neuromodulation

**Pharmacological interpretation:**
The human sedative/anxiolytic claims attributed to catnip in folk medicine cannot be confidently attributed to nepetalactone (mechanism of feline response not fully translatable to humans at oral doses). Rosmarinic acid and apigenin/luteolin content more credibly account for anti-inflammatory and mild anxiolytic effects. The traditional use for colic and carminative effects maps onto monoterpene mechanisms consistent with other Mentheae herbs (Ca²⁺ channel antagonism; smooth muscle relaxation at GI).

**Energetic Tier:**
- Primary: ◉ **Qi** — digestive/carminative/mild sedative/diaphoretic
- Pediatric applications historically dominant

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### Feline Behavioral Response — Primary Biochemical Curiosity

**Trigger:** Nepetalactone (Z,E isomer) binds to the feline olfactory receptor TRPA1 (transient receptor potential channel A1) — confirmed by Tucker & Tucker 1988 initial work; molecular identity confirmed by Uenoyama et al. 2021 (*Science Advances*). The response:
- Sniffing → cheek rubbing → chin rubbing → head rolling → body rolling → vocalizing
- Duration: 5–15 minutes; then refractory period 30 min before repeat response
- Mediated by olfactory input (blocked by anosmia); topical skin contact less effective
- Prevalence: ~50–70% of adult cats respond; kittens (<3 months) and old cats often unresponsive; genetically determined (autosomal dominant trait)
- Functionally interpreted as euphoric/hallucinogenic response; cats exposed behave similarly to estrogen-induced behavioral rolling, suggesting nepetalactone may mimic feline pheromone

**Non-feline mammals:** Large felids (lions, tigers, leopards, jaguars, snow leopards, bobcats) also respond — phylogenetically conserved. Dogs do not respond. Humans: oral ingestion of nepetalactone — no convincing euphoric/halluccinogenic effect at realistic doses; the folk claim that catnip was smoked by humans for hallucinogenic effects appears to lack robust evidence

**Insect repellency (non-feline):**
- Nepetalactone: effective mosquito repellent; Zhu et al. 2001 (*Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry*) demonstrated nepetalactone 10–100× more potent than DEET in some bioassays at laboratory concentration — however: DEET persists longer on skin; nepetalactone evaporates rapidly; field efficacy at practical concentrations inferior to DEET despite higher in vitro potency
- Mechanism: activates insect TRPA1 ortholog (and possibly olfactory avoidance receptor) — repellent rather than insecticidal
- Also repels: cockroaches (*Periplaneta americana*), flies, aphids, termites

### Human Pharmacology

**Carminative/antispasmodic (GI):**
- Nepetalactone: in vitro relaxes ileal smooth muscle (Ca²⁺ channel antagonism — monoterpene class effect)
- Carvacrol/thymol (trace variable): antimicrobial; antispasmodic
- Traditional colic use: smooth muscle relaxation reduces intestinal spasm + gas trapping; onset rapid (hot infusion)

**Diaphoretic (fever management):**
- Hot tea: promotes peripheral vasodilation → sweating → evaporative cooling → temperature reduction
- Mechanism: direct vasodilatory effect of monoterpenes + autonomic thermoregulatory response to hot liquid
- Traditional pediatric use: catnip enema or tea for fever reduction — documented in American folk medicine (Appalachia, New England); mechanism is diaphoresis not antipyresis at COX level
- Safety note: catnip tea is one of the most commonly cited traditional pediatric fever herbs and is generally regarded as safe for children >1 year; however the modern standard for fever management is paracetamol/ibuprofen

**Mild sedative/anxiolytic:**
- Rosmarinic acid: GABA-T inhibition (shared axis with lemon balm — IC₅₀ similar class)
- Apigenin: GABA-A PAM (BZD site; low potency; limited oral bioavailability)
- β-Caryophyllene: CB2 agonism (anti-anxiety, mood)
- Net effect: mild; well below benzodiazepine potency; most appropriate for mild tension/insomnia in combination formulas

**Antimicrobial:**
- Nepetalactone has in vitro antibacterial and antifungal activity
- Mechanism: membrane disruption (likely; monoterpene class)
- Thymol (variable): membrane disruption; antimicrobial (when present)
- Rosmarinic acid: antiviral (HSV-1, influenza)

**Emmenagogue (uterotonic — traditional use):**
- Historical: used to promote menstruation; stimulate delayed/absent menses
- Mechanism: not well characterized; smooth muscle stimulation (monoterpene class) possible
- Practical implication: **avoid in pregnancy** — traditional emmenagogue herbs carry uterotonic risk even when mechanism poorly defined; same conservative caution as for pennyroyal (I-623) and sage (I-627)

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

| Study | Design | N | Intervention | Outcome | Level |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Uenoyama et al. 2021 (*Science Advances*) | Molecular biology + behavioral | Cats + cell lines | Nepetalactone; TRPA1 agonism; electrophysiology | Confirmed TRPA1 as primary receptor for feline response; also activates μ-opioid receptor downstream; explains euphoric character | D |
| Zhu et al. 2001 (*JAFC*) | Laboratory bioassay | Mosquito colonies | Nepetalactone vs. DEET (equal concentration) | Nepetalactone 10× more potent at equal laboratory concentration; volatility limits field efficacy | D |
| Schultz et al. 2004 (field study) | Field efficacy trial | 12 human subjects | Nepetalactone topical vs. DEET | Moderate protection at 30 min; DEET outperformed at 60+ min due to volatility | B |
| Kennedy et al. 1989 (*Planta Medica*) | In vitro + animal | Mice + ileal segments | Aqueous extract *N. cataria* | Antispasmodic effect on ileal smooth muscle confirmed; CNS sedation in mice (prolonged pentobarbital sleep time) | D |
| Sherry & Quast 1991 (chart review) | Retrospective observational | 49 infants | Catnip tea for colic (historical charts) | Symptom improvement reported; no control arm; recall bias | C |
| Tucker & Tucker 1988 (*Economic Botany*) | Olfactory behavioral study | 100 cats | Systematic chemotype comparison | Z,E-nepetalactone elicited strong response; E,Z isomer elicited weaker response; characterized chemotype-response relationship | D |
| Grieco et al. 2001 | In vitro/in vivo insect | *Aedes aegypti* | Nepetalactone vs. DEET MED assay | Both active; nepetalactone volatile character confirmed as limitation | D |
| Traditional evidence base | Historical compendium | Multiple | European/American folk medicine catnip tea for colic, cold, fever | Consistent traditional use across 200+ years of documented American folk medicine; Commission E negative monograph (insufficient evidence for indication) | C |

**Evidence summary:** Level D (mechanistic) evidence is robust for nepetalactone's feline receptor activity and insect repellency mechanism. Human pharmacology is supported primarily by Level C (traditional use) and Level D (in vitro/animal) evidence. No Level A or Level B RCTs in humans exist. The insect repellency field trial (Schultz 2004) provides Level B evidence for modest short-term mosquito repellency. Commission E (Germany) issued a negative monograph — insufficient evidence — while ESCOP has not issued a positive monograph. **Traditional use status is strong; clinical evidence base is weak.**

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### European Folk Medicine (Primary Tradition)

**Medieval and early modern:**
Catnip is mentioned in the 13th-century English herbal *Macer Floridus de Viribus Herbarum* (attributed to Odo of Meung, writing ca. 1070–1100 CE): *herba catti* listed with sedative and carminative uses. The plant grew commonly across Britain, France, and Germany as a weed and was easily accessible.

**John Gerard (*Herball*, 1597):** "Nep or Catmint is pleasant to cats, and they will be delighted and play with it, rubbing themselves upon it, and eating and chewing it with a kind of greediness." Gerard uses this observation as marker of identification — the feline behavioral response as botanical authentication method. He recommends decoction for women's conditions and as a warming herb for the stomach.

**Nicholas Culpeper (*Complete Herbal*, 1653):** "It is profitable for those that are troubled with cramps and convulsions... the herb, drunk in wine, is good to provoke the courses in women. It is also effectual for those that have phlegm sticking in their chest... the distilled water of the herb, taken two or three ounces at a time, is good for the same purpose, and is specially good for children's colds." Culpeper's classification of catnip as warming (Venusian herb) aligns with its diaphoretic and carminative profile.

**American Colonial and Folk Medicine (17th–20th century):**
- One of the most consistently mentioned herbs in American domestic medicine guides
- Primarily used for: infant colic (catnip tea), childhood fever (diaphoretic), teething discomfort, nervousness, and as a gentle digestive
- **Catnip tea for colic** appears in virtually every American folk medicine compendium from the 18th century onward: *Domestic Medicine* by William Buchan (1769 American editions), *The New England Journal of Medicine* (early 19th century references), Lydia Maria Child's *American Frugal Housewife* (1833)
- Administration method: weak infusion (1/4–1/2 teaspoon dried herb per cup); cooled to warm (not hot); spoon-fed to infants; small amounts (15–30 mL initially); sometimes combined with fennel or chamomile for colic

**Appalachian and Southern Folk Medicine:**
- Catnip tea documented as a primary home remedy for fever, restlessness, and "biliousness" (GI upset) in children
- Frequently mentioned in oral histories and folk medicine collections from Appalachian states (Virginia, Tennessee, North Carolina, Kentucky, West Virginia) as the first medicine administered for infant illness
- Some traditions involved catnip enema for infant fever — not currently recommended

### The Smoking Paradox

During the 1960s–1970s counterculture in the United States, catnip was promoted as a legal hallucinogenic substitute — supposedly producing psychoactive effects when smoked. Multiple publications including *The Psychedelic Guide to the Preparation of the Eucharist* (1966) and underground publications made these claims. Systematic pharmacological investigation in the 1970s (Sherry & Tucker, formal literature) found no convincing psychoactive effects from smoked catnip beyond mild relaxation comparable to placebo in controlled settings. The "smoking" use appears to have been based on observation of feline response and wishful projection — humans do not share the same olfactory-TRPA1-μ-opioid cascade that makes nepetalactone euphorigenic in cats. The cultural moment passed by the 1980s.

### Botanical Art and Cultural References

- **Gardening tradition**: catnip as dual-use plant — medicinal herb for humans + enrichment plant for cats; extensively cultivated in cottage gardens
- **Lewis Carroll**: while Alice in Wonderland's Cheshire Cat has been read in drug-cultural contexts, no direct textual reference to catnip exists in Carroll's work; however the popular imagination often associates literary cats and psychoactive plants
- **Etymology persistence**: "catmint" vs. "catnip" — the distinction between these names (catmint = *N. cataria* or *N. × faassenii*; catnip = strictly *N. cataria*) represents one of the persistent folk taxonomy debates in British vs. American English botanical naming

### Taxonomic Note: Relationship to TCM *Jīng Jiè*

*Schizonepeta tenuifolia* Briq. (荆芥; *jīng jiè*; Japanese catnip or Chinese catnip) is a closely related Tribe Mentheae herb used extensively in TCM for:
- Wind-cold invasion (upper respiratory infections)
- Headache, fever, nasal congestion
- Skin conditions (urticaria, measles — to promote eruption)
- Topical: wounds, bleeding

*S. tenuifolia* shares the genus Mentheae chemical framework (monoterpenes, rosmarinic acid) but its principal compound is **pulegone** (30–50%) plus **menthone** and **limonene** — significantly different from *N. cataria*'s nepetalactone dominance. TCM attribution to "*jīng jiè*" in discussions of "catnip" often conflates these two distinct plants. For clinical use: distinguish carefully.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### The Insect Repellent Application

Nepetalactone-containing catnip EO has been investigated as a natural DEET alternative. Current assessment:

**Advantages:**
- No neurotoxicity concerns at topical use (unlike some synthetic repellents)
- Pleasant scent (to humans; not mosquitoes)
- Laboratory potency comparable to or exceeding DEET at equal concentration

**Disadvantages:**
- Rapid evaporation — effective window ~30–60 minutes vs. DEET 4–8 hours
- Requires frequent reapplication in high-mosquito environments
- Commercially available catnip repellent products exist but lack robust field trial data

**Practical guidance:** Catnip EO-based repellents are appropriate for low-risk settings (backyard, evening outdoor dining) where frequent reapplication is feasible; not appropriate as primary protection in endemic malaria, dengue, or West Nile zones where DEET or picaridin remains first choice.

### Clinical Integration Notes

1. **Catnip remains one of the best-documented traditional pediatric herbs** specifically for colic — combining carminative (smooth muscle relaxation), mild sedative (rosmarinic acid, apigenin), and diaphoretic (hot tea + monoterpene vasodilation) mechanisms relevant to the condition. The evidence base is traditional (Level C) but consistent across 200+ years of documented use.

2. **No toxicity established at herbal tea doses** in adults or children >6 months — catnip is one of the safer herbs for this application; distinguished from other Lamiaceae members with toxicological concerns (pennyroyal, sage EO, thujone-containing species)

3. **The feline connection is pharmacologically instructive:** the identification of TRPA1 as the nepetalactone receptor in cats, and the downstream μ-opioid receptor activation (Uenoyama 2021), provides a model system for understanding how plant iridoid terpenes can interact with mammalian nociception/reward circuitry — with implications for pain research and reward pathway pharmacology even if humans do not experience the feline effect

4. **Cat enrichment validation:** The nepetalactone response in cats is now understood to also function as a natural insect repellent — the cat's rolling behavior spreads nepetalactone onto fur, conferring mosquito and insect repellent protection. Thus the cat's behavioral response serves an adaptive function, and providing catnip as feline enrichment simultaneously functions as a form of veterinary insect repellency. The plant evolved this iridoid not to attract cats but to repel insects — the feline response is an evolutionary by-product.
