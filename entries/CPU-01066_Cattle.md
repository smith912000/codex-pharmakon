# Cattle — Ox, Cow, Calf (Bryan's Ebers) / Cow, Bull, Ox in the rGyud-bzhi and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary
## *The domestic bovine as a source of medicine; the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary gives the cow as* Bos taurus domestica *Gmelin* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP, FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The English text on disk is C. P. Bryan's 1930 *The Papyrus Ebers*, a translation with summaries; the Egyptian words are not printed. In Chapter VII, "Organic Remedies", Bryan lists the animals "whose identity is not in doubt", among them "Crocodile Ox Cow Calf Mouse Porcupine" (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1392; `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` rows "Ox", "Cow", "Calf", each marked "Bryan's English identification; no Egyptian word or binomial given in this list"). Of the parts the physicians took, he says of the Cow: "she gave her bile, brain, blood, fat, flesh, horns, liver, milk, spleen, and tallow" (line 1429), and calls her, after the Ass, "a good second" among the animals (line 1427). The places in Bryan's text where the animal's parts appear include: Cow's Fat or Cow's Flesh placed on a wound "in order to ripen (i.e., decompose) it" (line 2322); "Cow-fat" with collyrium "to make the flesh grow" (line 2399); roasted Cow's Fat with wax and palm-fibres as a plaster "to use on the fourth day" (line 2265); "Honey, Cow's Brain, Mason's Clay" for wheals from blows (line 2385); Calamine, Cow's Brain and Wasp's dung plastered on a diseased breast for four days (line 2544); Horn-of-a-Cow with garlic in a douche to protect the vulva (line 2556); "Cow-horn" in a suppository for the diseased anus (line 2042); Cow's Blood, with Ass's, Pig's, Dog's and Stag's blood, ground with incense in lizard's dung and applied to the place where a hair had been pulled out so that it should not grow again (lines 2885-2894); "The Blood-of-a-Black-Calf, cooked in Oil", and the same of a Black Cow, in remedies for grey hair (line 4032); "Fat-of-an-Ox" (line 2919), and Fat-of-the-Ox with the fat of the ass and the ram, fruit of the dompalm, poppy and sea-salt "to refresh the met" (line 3151); "Flesh-of-a-Fat-Cow" for strengthening the limbs (line 3161); Bullock's fat in a liniment to make the *met* supple (line 3170); "Baked Ox-liver" as an eye-cooler (line 3017); and "Flesh-of-a-Live-Cow" with fresh bread, incense, lettuce and sweet beer, "ordered to be 'drunk' for four days" (line 3275). Bile and milk have their own entries in the Codex; for bile see III_14. [USER: verify] that Bryan's "Ox", "Cow" and "Calf" are one animal throughout.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The spine's two Men-Tsee-Khang glossary loci, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` rows "Bos Taurus" (line 7033) and "Bos domesticus" (line 7084), point into the glossary on animals at the end of `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. Line 7031 prints "Cow" and line 7033 its scientific name, "Bos Taurus domestica Gmelin". Line 7084 prints "Bos domesticus mutus" directly under "Yak", so it is the glossary's yak and not a second name for the cow; the OCR column split leaves the Tibetan names unpaired. [USER: verify] Where the text itself names cattle: the Explanatory Tantra ch.20 as Clark translates it lists the "bull" among the domestic animals of the meats classification (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 672; Men-Tsee-Khang line 2917 lists "zho, yak, camel, horse, donkey, cow, kompo, goat, sheep, dog, pig, hen and cat" as "the group of domesticated animals"). "Beef is cool and oily and dispels wind fever" (Clark line 674); "Beef is cool and oily in quality and cures hot disorder associated with loong" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 2931). Under the animal medicines: "Rhino hide and Ox hide: cure smallpox" (Clark line 1185; Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3554-3555); the bile duct of a bull as a common source of the resin *gi.wam*, animal bile (Clark line 880); "Cow urine (especially that of the superior red-brown cow): purges lymph disorders and chronic fevers" (Clark line 1197, see III_16). For bile disorders Clark's diet lists "fresh meat of she-yak-calf or of ox-dzo cross" (line 175) and "fresh beef" (line 1412). Cow's milk is "beneficial for punctured lung and consumption; it heals chronic infectious fever and frequent urination, and dispels lethargy" (Clark line 708), and in Men-Tsee-Khang "it also sharpens mental ability and is rejuvenating" (line 3059); milk has its own entry in the Codex.

### Varieties

**Ox** (EP; Bryan 1930, list at line 1392). Named in Bryan's list of animals. The prescriptions found that give the ox by name are "Fat-of-an-Ox" (lines 2919, 3151), "Baked Ox-liver" (line 3017), "Ox Bile" (see III_14) and "Ox fat" in a mixture with saffron seeds and coriander (line 3519). Bryan's "bullock" ("Bullock's fat", line 3170; "Bullock's Flesh", line 3170) may be the same animal; the translation does not say.

**Cow** (EP; Bryan 1930, line 1392). The female is the source of the longest run in the list: bile, brain, blood, fat, flesh, horns, liver, milk, spleen and tallow (line 1429). The prescriptions are the cow's fat or flesh on a wound, the fat with collyrium, the brain with honey and clay or with calamine and wasp's dung, the horn, the blood for hair, the flesh of a fat cow and of a live cow (lines above).

**Calf** (EP; Bryan 1930, line 1392). Named in the list. The one prescription found that names the calf is "The Blood-of-a-Black-Calf, cooked in Oil", one of the remedies that aim at once to preserve the hair and remove grey hairs (line 4032).

*Synthesis of the varieties.* Bryan's list gives Ox, Cow and Calf as three names, and the prescriptions separate them by the part used, not by the animal: the ox's fat and liver, the cow's brain, horn, fat and blood, the calf's blood (black calf and black cow are paired). The Tibetan texts do not separate the cow from the bull in this way but by product: beef, hide, bile duct, urine (with the red-brown cow as "superior"), milk. The two texts therefore do not list the same kinds of variety; the Egyptian list is of parts and sex or age (the translation does not make clear whether a bullock is an ox), the Tibetan of products and, in the case of urine, of colour.

### Active Components

Cattle (*Bos taurus*) are ruminants. In general their tissues are the source of beef (muscle protein, fat, iron, B vitamins), of milk and butter, of hide and collagen (gelatin and glue), of bile, and of horn (keratin). The flesh, liver, brain and spleen differ greatly in composition; the liver is rich in vitamin A, iron and B vitamins, and the brain in cholesterol. The texts do not name any active component, and none is claimed. No quantities are given here because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. This entry records what two traditions took from cattle. It does not give a protocol. Meat, milk and butter from cattle are ordinary foods in many cultures, and the Codex treats them elsewhere (milk and ghee have their own entries). The medicinal uses recorded here are of the raw parts: blood, brain, fat, flesh, liver, horn and hide on wounds, eyes, hair and the "met" in Egyptian compounds, and hide for smallpox in the Tibetan text. The Codex gives no instruction for them. Raw animal blood, brain and organ tissue carry infection and prion risks (see Contraindications), and no dose is given.

### Preparation & Dosing

Preparations given by the sources, with locus: cow's fat or flesh applied to a wound to ripen it, then dressed with fats, oil and fruits, and finally a plaster of powdered fresh ivory in honey (Bryan, line 2322); roasted cow's fat, wax and palm-fibres worked in uah-corn into a plaster (line 2265); honey, cow's brain and mason's clay (line 2385); blood of a black calf cooked in oil (line 4032); the flesh of a live cow with bread and beer, "drunk" for four days (line 3275). In the Tibetan texts hide and meat are named without method in the lines read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved for any of these. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Beef is a source of complete protein, iron and vitamin B12. Bovine collagen is the source of gelatin. Cattle products can transmit bovine spongiform encephalopathy (a prion disease) through brain and spinal tissue, and zoonoses such as brucellosis (raw milk), anthrax (hides) and tuberculosis. There is no established support for cow's blood or fat as a hair treatment, or for ox hide as a treatment for smallpox, which was eradicated by vaccination. No clinical studies of the traditional uses were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The Tibetan texts print qualities for beef: "cool and oily" and "dispels wind fever" (Clark line 674); "cool and oily in quality and cures hot disorder associated with loong" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 2931). Cow's milk and milk products are "cool and light" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3067; Clark line 711). Hide and urine are given by action only (above).

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** Bryan's translation prints no energetic quality for any part of the ox, cow or calf; the corpus is organised by complaint.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* The Ebers material read records what was done for a complaint with the animal's fat, flesh, brain, blood, horn, liver and bile: wounds, wheals, breast disease, the hair, the eyes, the *met*. It does not describe what the animal does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); Bryan reports no recitation with the cattle compounds read, and since he summarises, that is not proof the papyrus gives none. No deity is attributed to the animal in the passages read. Because Ebers identifications are modern hypotheses, "Ox", "Cow" and "Calf" are Bryan's English renderings.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status for the animal.* Cattle appear in the Explanatory Tantra's meats and animal medicines by their products: beef (cool, oily), hide (smallpox), bile duct (*gi.wam*), urine (the red-brown cow "superior"; and see III_16, where cow urine is named in the preliminary cleansing before rejuvenation). The Men-Tsee-Khang text calls cow's milk "rejuvenating" (line 3059); that is a statement about milk, which has its own entry, and is not carried over to the animal or to its flesh. The Clark lines read name no essence-extraction (*bcud len*) use of any bovine product, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition; none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing to cultivate with cattle as such in these sources. The Egyptian text uses the animal's parts as ingredients, and the Tibetan text uses its flesh, hide, bile and urine as foods and drugs for named disorders; the one word of rejuvenation attaches to milk, not to the animal. A cultivator who eats beef or dairy does so as food and should follow the cooling, oily qualities the Tibetan text prints, not look for a hidden tonic.

### Contraindications

- **Prion disease (BSE).** Brain, spinal cord and some other tissues of cattle can carry the agent of bovine spongiform encephalopathy; eating or applying them is unsafe where that is a risk.
- **Infection.** Raw blood, organs and unpasteurised milk can carry bacteria and parasites (brucellosis, tuberculosis, *E. coli*, *Salmonella*). Hides can carry anthrax. Never put raw animal tissue on a wound.
- **Allergy.** Beef and milk allergies exist, as does the tick-borne alpha-gal allergy to red meat.
- **Smallpox.** The Tibetan hide remedy is not a treatment; smallpox is eradicated and no one should use it.
- **Dose unknown.** No dose is given for any medicinal use in the texts read.
- **Religious and legal.** Cattle are sacred in Hindu practice and their slaughter is restricted by law in parts of South Asia; local rules apply. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics.** Using the parts means slaughter.
