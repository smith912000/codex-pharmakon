# Alfalfa — Lucerne, Buffalo Herb, "Father of All Foods" (al-faṣfaṣa), Purple Medick

## *Medicago sativa* L. | Family: Fabaceae (Leguminosae)

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM, SBJ, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Cool & Moist — a cooling, nourishing tonic (Galenic/Unani); sweet and neutral-to-cool in Eastern schemes
**Training Phase:** Nourishing Phase — a restorative, mineral- and vitamin-dense tonic for depletion and convalescence. Bounded by a hard safety flag (see Contraindications).

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian Tablets (AT):** Alfalfa is among the oldest cultivated forages of the ancient Near East. Mesopotamian agriculture prized it as fodder that fattened and strengthened livestock, and the Assyrian medical-agricultural record treats it as a strengthening, restorative feed — the seed of the folk title "father of all foods."

**Dioscorides — De Materia Medica (DMM):** Dioscorides describes *mēdikē* (the "Median herb," so named because it came to Greece from Media/Persia) as a fodder legume, noting its agricultural value and its use as a green forage and poultice herb. The classical placement is as a nourishing, cooling green rather than a potent drug.

**Chinese Materia Medica (SBJ):** *Medicago sativa* (and the closely related *M. polymorpha*, mùxu / 苜蓿) entered the Chinese materia medica as a cooling, sweet-bland vegetable and mild diuretic used to clear damp-heat, benefit the bladder and drain the intestines — a food-grade tonic-and-drainer rather than a strong medicinal.

**Sushruta Samhita (SS):** In the Ayurvedic tradition, lucerne/alfalfa is used as a cooling, nourishing green — a *balya* (strength-giving) and mildly *mutrala* (diuretic) fodder-and-food herb, given for depletion and to support lactation and recovery.

**Broader Materia Medica (BM):** The Arabic name *al-faṣfaṣa* gives the English "alfalfa." Greco-Arabic physicians classed it among cooling, moistening, nutritive legumes. In 19th–20th century Western herbalism alfalfa leaf and sprout became a popular "green tonic," vitamin source and galactagogue, and the seed a folk cholesterol remedy — the use that modern trials partly corroborate.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Alfalfa's symbolism is *deep nourishment from deep roots* — its taproot descends metres into the subsoil, and the folk doctrine reads this as the plant "drawing up" minerals to feed the depleted. Pharmacologically this maps onto a genuinely mineral- and vitamin-K-rich leaf. But the same doctrine of "concentrated vitality" carries the shadow: the non-protein amino acid L-canavanine, an arginine mimic, is the hidden hazard inside the tonic — potency and danger from the same source.

### Active Components

- **Saponins** — triterpenoid (medicagenic-acid) saponins, associated with the seed/leaf cholesterol-lowering effect via bile-acid/cholesterol binding in the gut.
- **Coumestrol** — a coumestan phytoestrogen; the principal estrogenic constituent, relevant to both traditional galactagogue use and estrogen-sensitivity cautions.
- **Vitamin K (phylloquinone)** — alfalfa leaf is notably vitamin-K-rich, the basis of the warfarin interaction.
- **L-canavanine** — a non-protein amino acid concentrated in seeds and sprouts; a structural analogue of L-arginine and the constituent implicated in lupus-like autoimmunity (see Contraindications).
- **Vitamins & minerals** — provitamin A carotenoids, vitamin C, B-group vitamins, and minerals (calcium, iron, potassium) underpin the "green tonic" reputation.
- **Isoflavones & other phytoestrogens** — additional estrogenic flavonoids beyond coumestrol.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Filed as a **Nourishing-Phase restorative tonic** — a mineral/vitamin-dense green for convalescence, depletion and poor nutrition, and traditionally as a galactagogue and mild diuretic. A secondary, evidence-supported use is adjunctive lipid-lowering (seed). It is positioned as a *food-grade tonic*, and its Kenetica placement is explicitly hazard-bounded: leaf and sprout in modest culinary amounts for most users, with the canavanine/autoimmune flag governing who may use it at all.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, alfalfa has a genuine — if double-edged — evidence base:

- Mölgaard, von Schenck & Olsson (1987), *Atherosclerosis*: 40 g heat-prepared alfalfa seeds three times daily for 8 weeks lowered total plasma cholesterol by a median 17% and LDL cholesterol by 18% (maximal 26% and 30%) with a 34% fall in apolipoprotein B in type-II hyperlipoproteinemia patients; values returned to baseline after cessation. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0021-9150(87)90019-0)
- Pozzo et al. (2014), *J Sci Food Agric*: a bioactive mixture including *Medicago sativa* reduced serum cholesterol and hepatic steatosis in high-fat-diet mice, upregulating CYP7A1 — mechanistic support for the hypolipidemic effect (multi-herb model, so not alfalfa-specific). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/jsfa.6972)
- Malinow et al. (1982), *Science*: cynomolgus macaques fed alfalfa sprouts developed an SLE-like syndrome; dietary L-canavanine sulfate **reactivated** the syndrome — the foundational evidence for the lupus hazard. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.7071589)
- Akaogi et al. (2006), *Autoimmunity Reviews*: reviews the role of L-canavanine in autoimmunity, noting the first human report of alfalfa-associated SLE in a hypercholesterolemia volunteer, corroboration in monkeys, and relapse on L-canavanine re-challenge; canavanine is mis-incorporated into proteins in place of arginine, disrupting structure and driving autoimmunity. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.autrev.2005.12.004)

**Evidence grade: Mixed — moderate for lipid-lowering, strong for the canavanine/autoimmune hazard.** The cholesterol effect is supported by a small controlled human trial plus mechanistic animal data; the lupus association is well-established mechanistically and in primates, with human case reports (epidemiological data remain sparse, and the association is described as controversial for pre-existing disease).

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM (mùxu):** Sweet, bland; cool/neutral. Clears damp-heat, benefits the bladder and small intestine, mildly diuretic and draining. (Attested.)
- **Ayurvedic (Sushruta lineage):** *Madhura* (sweet) rasa, cooling *virya*, *balya* (strengthening) and mildly *mutrala* (diuretic); kapha-neutral, pitta-pacifying green. (Attested at the food-tonic level.)
- **Galenic / Unani:** Cool and moist; a nutritive, cooling legume-green. (Attested.)

### Contraindications

- **⚠️ SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS / AUTOIMMUNE DISEASE — DO NOT USE.** The non-protein amino acid **L-canavanine** (concentrated in seeds and sprouts) can **trigger a lupus-like (SLE) syndrome and reactivate existing lupus**. This is demonstrated in primates (Malinow 1982) and reviewed with human case reports (Akaogi 2006). Alfalfa seed, sprout and high-dose leaf products are contraindicated in anyone with SLE, a history of lupus, or other autoimmune disease. This is the governing hazard of the entry.
- **⚠️ WARFARIN / ANTICOAGULANTS.** Alfalfa leaf is **rich in vitamin K**, which antagonises warfarin and can reduce anticoagulant effect and raise clot risk. Avoid therapeutic alfalfa (and keep dietary intake consistent) in anyone on warfarin or vitamin-K-antagonist anticoagulants.
- **⚠️ RAW SPROUTS — INFECTION RISK.** Raw alfalfa sprouts are a documented vehicle for *Salmonella* and *E. coli* outbreaks. Cook sprouts; the immunocompromised, pregnant, elderly and very young should avoid raw sprouts entirely.
- **Photosensitivity:** Alfalfa can be photosensitising; caution with sun exposure and in photosensitive individuals.
- **Hormone-sensitive conditions:** Coumestrol and other phytoestrogens mean caution in estrogen-sensitive conditions (some breast, uterine, ovarian conditions) and possible interference with hormonal therapy.
- **Pregnancy:** High-dose/therapeutic use not recommended (phytoestrogen content; raw-sprout infection risk); ordinary dietary amounts of cooked leaf are traditionally used as a galactagogue but the hazards above still apply.
