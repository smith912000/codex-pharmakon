# I-283: Valeriana officinalis (Valerian)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-283 | Caprifoliaceae (formerly Valerianaceae)**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Valeriana officinalis* L.
**Family:** Caprifoliaceae (formerly Valerianaceae; reclassified under APG IV)

**Related species used interchangeably in various traditions:**
- *Valeriana wallichii* DC. — Indian Valerian; Himalayan; primary Ayurvedic species
- *Valeriana jatamansi* Jones — Spikenard of Ayurveda; *Nardostachys* species sometimes used as synonym
- *Valeriana edulis* Nutt. — North American valerian; Indigenous use
- *Valeriana mexicana* DC. — Mexican valerian; local Indigenous use

**Common Names:**
- English: Valerian, Garden Valerian, Common Valerian, All-Heal, Cat's Lovage
- Latin/Medieval: *Valeriana* — possibly from Latin *valere* (to be strong/healthy) or from the Roman province *Valeria*; or from *valeo* (to be well — the primary Latin word for health)
- German: *Baldrian* — from *Baldur*, the Norse god; or possibly from Italian *baldo* (bold, vigorous)
- Arabic: *Asarūn* / *Sanbūl al-Ṭīb* (سنبل الطيب) — "fragrant valerian/spikenard"
- Hindi/Sanskrit: *Jatamansi* (जटामांसी) / *Tagar* (तगर)
- Mandarin: *Xié cǎo* (缬草) — "Valerian grass"

**The Cat Attractant:**
Valerian root extract attracts cats (and also rats) — actinidine, a compound in valerian, is closely related to the nepetalactone in catnip (*Nepeta cataria*). This feline attraction is pharmacologically interesting: the compounds responsible for valerian's sedative effects in humans produce excitation in cats — a species-specific receptor pharmacology difference. Rats are also attracted (valerian root was reportedly used by Pied Piper of Hamelin legend).

**Morphology:**
Perennial; 50–200 cm; pinnate leaves; small pink-white fragrant flowers in terminal corymbs; deep, fibrous rhizome with characteristic pungent odor (fresh root has light floral smell; dried root develops the penetrating odor from isovaleric acid production by enzymatic hydrolysis of valeronates during drying).

**Parts Used:** Root and rhizome (fresh or dried); root extracts; essential oil; aqueous/ethanolic preparations.

**Fresh vs. Dried Root Chemistry:**
- **Fresh root:** contains valepotriates (unstable bicyclic monoterpene epoxides; sedative; but decompose during drying)
- **Dried root:** valepotriates largely decomposed → isovaleric acid (the characteristic "gym shoe" odor) + valerenic acid + valerenal + volatile oil
- **Aqueous extract:** different compound profile from ethanolic (GABA content; amino acids)
- The drying and extraction method significantly affects the final pharmacological profile — creating challenges in clinical trial standardization

**Note on *Spikenard* Cross-Tradition Confusion:**
The biblical "spikenard" (*nard*) referenced in Mark 14:3 and Song of Solomon is botanically uncertain — it may be *Nardostachys jatamansi* (Himalayan spikenard, now Valerianaceae) or other aromatics. The Islamic medicine use of "*sumbul*" as a cardiac-sedative-aromatic refers to what is now understood as valerian/spikenard complex. For convergence purposes, the spikenard/valerian complex is treated as a unified tradition.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domains:**
1. **Sedative / sleep induction** — DMM, CM, SBJ, SS, FMT, European folk: the dominant universal application; insomnia; nervousness; anxiety
2. **Anti-epileptic / anti-convulsant** — DMM, CM, SS, FMT: seizure prevention; convulsion management; less documented in modern evidence but historical convergence strong
3. **Anxiolytic / nervine tonic** — All traditions: anxiety; nervous system strengthening; calming without narcotic impairment
4. **Anti-spasmodic** — CM, SS: muscle cramp; GI spasm; menstrual cramp
5. **Cardiac (calming)** — CM, FMT: palpitations; nervous heart; calms the heart's movement

**Pharmacological Complexity:**
Valerian's mechanism is genuinely complex — multiple compounds (valerenic acid, GABA, isovaleric acid, valepotriates, flavonoids) working via multiple pathways (GABA-A, 5-HT5A, adenosine, mTOR inhibition). This multi-target action may explain both the clinical variability and the lack of a single "valerian drug" that can be isolated and standardized.

**Energetic Classification:**
- ☿ Shen (neuromodulatory): Primary — sedative; anxiolytic; anti-epileptic; Shen-calming (sleep and spirit)
- ◉ Qi (functional): Secondary — anti-spasmodic; cardiac calming

**Primary Tier: ☿ Shen** — the archetypal Shen-calming nervine; primary sedative plant in Western herbalism

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Sesquiterpenes (Primary Sedative Class)

**1. Valerenic Acid — 0.3–0.8% dried root**
- Primary identified active compound; the standardization marker for most commercial extracts
- **GABA-A receptor positive allosteric modulator:** Valerenic acid binds at β₂/β₃ subunit of GABA-A receptor → enhanced Cl⁻ conductance → neuronal inhibition
- **Mechanism specificity:** The valerenic acid GABA-A site is DIFFERENT from the benzodiazepine site and the barbiturate site — it is a distinct allosteric site on the β subunit
- Inhibits GABA breakdown (GABA transaminase inhibition) → increased synaptic GABA duration
- Anti-anxiety in rat models at 30–60 mg/kg

**2. Valerenal, Valeranone** — related sesquiterpenes; sedative

### Valepotriates (Bicyclic Monoterpene Epoxides — Fresh Root)

**3. Valtrate — primary valepotriate**
- Unstable; rapidly decomposes during drying/extraction
- Sedative in animal models; alkylating agent (reactive epoxide)
- **Present primarily in fresh root; essentially absent in commercial dried root preparations**
- Creates a pharmacological discrepancy between fresh and dried root medicine

**4. Didrovaltrate, Isovaltrate** — related; same instability

### GABA and Amino Acids

**5. GABA (γ-aminobutyric acid) — present in root aqueous extract**
- GABA from valerian: whether it crosses the blood-brain barrier (BBB) is debated; some evidence for intestinal GABA → vagal pathway → CNS effect
- Aqueous extracts specifically release GABA in vitro; may contribute to sedation via gut-brain axis

**6. Glutamine** — GABA precursor; increases CNS GABA availability

### Flavonoids

**7. Hesperidin** — flavanone; sedative; GABA-A active
**8. Linarin** — flavone; sedative; anti-spasmodic
**9. Chlorogenic acid** — antioxidant; mild CNS activity

### Other

**10. Isovaleric acid** — the odor compound; possibly mildly sedative via BBB penetration
**11. Actinidine** — iridoid alkaloid; cat attractant
**12. Kessyl glycol** — sedative (minor)

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### 1. GABA-A Potentiation (Primary — Valerenic Acid)
- **Valerenic acid β-subunit GABA-A PAM:** Not benzodiazepine-site but β₂/β₃ subunit site
- This distinct site means: valerian DOES potentiate GABA-A but through a different binding site than benzodiazepines
- **Clinical implication:** Potential for less tolerance development and less physical dependence than benzodiazepines (receptor site difference reduces cross-tolerance)
- Less receptor downregulation than benzodiazepines (theoretical advantage for long-term use)
- **GABA transaminase inhibition:** increases synaptic GABA availability; prolongs GABA inhibitory signal

### 2. 5-HT5A Receptor Antagonism
- Valerenic acid: 5-HT5A receptor agonist (not antagonist — confusingly named; 5-HT5A is inhibitory → agonism is inhibitory = sedative)
- 5-HT5A expression: hippocampus, cerebral cortex; modulates anxiety and circadian rhythm
- This dual GABA-A + 5-HT5A mechanism provides anxiolytic + sleep-synchronizing effects

### 3. Adenosine Pathway
- Valepotriates: inhibit adenosine deaminase → increased adenosine → adenosine A1 receptor → neuronal inhibition → sedation
- Adenosine is an endogenous sleep-promoting neuromodulator (caffeine's mechanism = adenosine antagonism; valerian = adenosine enhancement)

### 4. mTOR Inhibition (Anti-Epileptic)
- Valerian alkaloids: mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin) inhibition
- mTOR hyperactivation is implicated in epileptogenesis (particularly mTOR pathway-associated epilepsies)
- Validates traditional anti-epileptic applications; mechanism distinct from GABA-A

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Insomnia / Sleep Quality:**
- Numerous RCTs (over 40 trials); highly heterogeneous results due to extract standardization variation
- Meta-analysis (Fernandez-San-Martin 2010, n=1,093): valerian extracts moderately improved subjective sleep quality; inconsistent effects on objective sleep measures
- Recent higher-quality meta-analysis (Bent 2006 Cochrane-style): significant improvement in sleep latency and quality vs. placebo (p < 0.01)
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (multiple RCTs; heterogeneous but consistent direction for subjective sleep quality)

**Anxiety:**
- Multiple RCTs: valerian reduces anxiety scores (HAM-A, STAI) significantly vs. placebo in anxious subjects
- One trial: valerian comparable to oxazepam (benzodiazepine) at 3 weeks (Dorn 2000, n=168; p=0.578 for non-inferiority)
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (multiple RCTs; STAI improvement; one comparison to benzodiazepine)

**Menopausal Symptom (Sleep + Anxiety):**
- Multiple RCTs in peri-menopausal women: valerian + lemon balm combinations → significant hot flush reduction + sleep improvement
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (combination products)

**Anti-Epileptic:**
- Traditional evidence strong; modern clinical trials lacking for epilepsy
- mTOR mechanism provides pharmacological basis
- **Evidence Grade: Level C** (traditional; mechanism; no modern RCT)

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The Polarizing Odor:**
Valerian root's dried odor — described variously as "dirty socks," "sweaty gym equipment," "old cheese," "over-ripe fruit," or "musty earth" — is one of the most distinctive and polarizing aromas in all of herbal medicine. The isovaleric acid responsible (produced enzymatically during drying from the valepotriates) has an MSDS-listed odor character of "strong, persistent, unpleasant." Yet this same root has provided sleep and calm to millions for 2,000+ years. The disconnect between the odor and the effect is remarkable.

**Capsule vs. Tea:**
Most modern users take valerian in capsule form specifically to avoid tasting/smelling the root. Traditional tea preparations required confronting the odor — which may have functioned as a conditioning stimulus (strongly aversive odor → relaxation association → conditioned relaxation response). Whether this odor-associated conditioning contributed to traditional valerian efficacy is an interesting, unprovable hypothesis.

**Sleep Induction Experience:**
Consistent across clinical trial subjects and traditional users:
- 30–60 minutes after taking 300–600 mg valerian extract: mild heaviness of eyelids; relaxation of muscle tension; thoughts becoming less intrusive; gradual transition to drowsiness
- NOT a knockout/sedation (no hangover; no cognitive impairment); more like a "facilitation of natural sleep onset"
- Most users describe: "makes it easier to fall asleep; doesn't force sleep"
- This gentle facilitation rather than forced sedation distinguishes valerian from benzodiazepines subjectively

**Morning-After Profile:**
Unlike benzodiazepines or antihistamine sleep aids, valerian produces minimal-to-no morning hangover at therapeutic doses. This is consistent with the β-subunit GABA-A binding site (different from benzodiazepine site) and the lack of muscle-relaxant effects at therapeutic doses. This favorable morning profile is one of valerian's clinical advantages over pharmaceutical sleep aids.

**The 4-Week Phenomenon:**
Multiple traditional sources and some modern clinical experience note that valerian's full effect develops over 2–4 weeks of regular use rather than immediately (in contrast to benzodiazepines which produce immediate effect). This suggests that the primary mechanism may involve neuroplasticity (receptor upregulation/sensitization) rather than acute pharmacology alone. Users who stop after one dose may conclude "it didn't work" while missing the cumulative effect.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Standardized root extract (primary clinical form):**
- 300–600 mg dried root extract (standardized to 0.8% valerenic acid): 30–60 minutes before bedtime
- Clinical trial dosing range: 300–900 mg/day
- For anxiety: 300–600 mg 2–3× daily (lower doses for daytime use)
- Minimum 2–4 weeks for full assessment of efficacy

**Tincture (1:5, 40–60% ethanol):**
- 3–5 mL in water 30–60 minutes before bedtime
- Higher valepotriate content than dried root extract (if fresh tincture)

**Tea (traditional; odor challenge):**
- 1–3 g dried root in 250 mL cold water; cold infusion 6–8 hours (or brief hot infusion); GABA content better preserved in cold infusion
- Strong odor; most patients prefer encapsulated form

**Valerian + Hops (*Humulus lupulus*):**
- Classic European insomnia combination; multiple RCTs; hops adds mild α-acid sedation
- Commercial products: Dormiplant, Hova, multiple German herbal pharmaceutical brands
- Evidence for combination: Level B (superior to either alone in some trials)

**Valerian + Lemon Balm (*Melissa officinalis*):**
- Another classical combination; anxiety + sleep; pediatric-appropriate
- Level B for menopausal hot flush + sleep improvement

### Safety Profile

**Excellent safety profile at recommended doses and duration.**

**Most common adverse effects:**
- Vivid dreams (occasional; especially with higher doses)
- Morning sedation (rare at standard doses; more likely at > 900 mg)
- Headache (rare)
- GI discomfort (rare)

**Drug Interactions:**
- **Benzodiazepines / Z-drugs (zolpidem, zopiclone):** GABA-A additive sedation; **reduce benzodiazepine dose when adding valerian; do not combine without medical guidance**
- **Barbiturates:** Additive CNS depression; avoid
- **Alcohol:** Additive CNS depression; avoid regular combination
- **Opioids:** Additive CNS depression; respiratory depression risk
- **CYP3A4:** Valerenic acid may inhibit CYP3A4 at high doses; theoretical drug interaction with CYP3A4-metabolized medications
- **Anesthesia:** Valerian potentiates anesthesia; **DISCONTINUE 2–3 weeks before surgery** (unlike garlic's 7-14 day pre-op discontinuation; valerian's CNS effects may complicate anesthetic management)

**Contraindications:**
- **Driving/operating machinery (acute dose effect):** First-time users should assess response before driving; generally cleared within 6–8 hours
- **Pregnancy:** Insufficient data; valepotriates are potentially mutagenic (alkylating epoxides); **AVOID in pregnancy**; minimal data on dried root (valepotriate-free) preparations in pregnancy
- **Children under 3:** Insufficient data; older children: some evidence for safety in anxiety + sleep disturbance (pediatric clinical use in Europe is established but limited data)

**Discontinuation:**
- Not associated with withdrawal syndrome at recommended doses (unlike benzodiazepines)
- However: if used chronically at high doses, gradual tapering prudent
- The absence of physical dependence potential is a major clinical advantage over benzodiazepines
