# Mercurialis annua — Linozostis (Dioscorides 4-191)

## *Mercurialis annua [Linnaeus], the second of two names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-191 (which also prints Mercurialis mas [Fuchs]); "Annual Mercury"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 9732) reads "Mercurialis mas [Fuchs], Mercurialis annua [Linnaeus] — Annual Mercury", followed by the translators' gloss of the Greek name, "lino — to smear; zoster — shingles". The gloss explains the name; the chapter itself prints no use for shingles. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-191. LINOZOSTIS*, heading at line 9731, in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"). The whole chapter (line 9734) reads: "Linozostis has leaves like basil, similar to those of helxine [4-39, 4-86] but smaller, and little branches with two joints and many thick wings. The seed of the female grows in abundant clusters. The male has branches that are small and round, as though it were, two little stones lying together. The whole shrub is twenty centimetres long or more, and both types (used as vegetables and eaten) induce movement of the bowels. Boiled in water (and the water taken as a drink) they expel bile and watery stuff. It is thought that the leaves of the female (pounded into small pieces and taken as a drink, as well as applied to the genitals after the menstrual flow cleansing) causes the conception of a female child, and that if the leaves of the male are used in the same way, it comes to pass that a male child is born. It is also called argyros, aritrillis, or chrysitis; the Egyptians call it aphlopho, the Romans, herba Mercurialis mascula, or testiculata, the Africans, asumes; and it is also called parthenium, or herbula Mercurii."

Dioscorides therefore treats the plant as two kinds, female and male, distinguished by the seed (clustered, on the female) and the branches (small, round and paired "like two little stones", on the male). He describes it by comparison with basil and with *helxine*. The uses printed are a bowel-mover (both kinds, eaten as vegetables), a drink of the boiling water that expels "bile and watery stuff", and a use for sex of the child: the leaves of the female, pounded and drunk and applied to the genitals after the menstrual flow, lead to a female child, and those of the male, used the same way, to a male child. Dioscorides opens that claim with "It is thought that", so he reports it as a belief and does not put it in his own voice. The Codex records it as the text's report and does not endorse it. The chapter prints other names as names only: the Greek alternatives *argyros*, *aritrillis* and *chrysitis*, the Egyptian *aphlopho*, the Roman *herba Mercurialis mascula* and *testiculata*, the African *asumes*, and *parthenium* and *herbula Mercurii*.

**The two chapters it points to.** The cross-references "[4-39, 4-86]" lead to two chapters that are not this plant. 4-39 *ELXINE KUSSAMPELOS* (heading at line 8338) is the translators' bindweed, "Helxine-cissampelos ... Convolvulus arvensis"; its juice "taken as a drink has a laxative effect on the bowels". 4-86 *ELXINE* (line 8799) is the translators' pellitory, "Helxine, Parietaria ... Parietaria officinalis", with "rough leaves similar to mercury [4-191]" and leaves "astringent and cooling". So 4-86 compares its own leaves to this plant, and this chapter compares its leaves to 4-39 and 4-86.

**Neighbouring places in the same text.** Mercury is named in other chapters. In 2-53 (line 5151), on parts of poultry, "There are some who boil sea colewort, mercury [4-191], cnicum [4-119, 4-190], or polypody [4-188] together with it"; the preparation there is a poultry dish, and the lines read do not say which of the broths it modifies. In 2-75 *GALA*, on milk (line 5236), "All milk overturns the stomach and the intestines where the pasture is scammonious or has hellebore, mercury [4-191] or clematis, as has been observed by us in the Vestin mountains" — a notice that the animal's pasture changes the milk. And 4-192 (line 9735), the next chapter, which calls its own plant "the wild male linozostis", is the sister plant *Kunokrambe* (see I_1133). The translators' index lists "Mercurialis annua 738" and "Mercurialis mas 738" (lines 16605 and 16639).

### Active Components

The composition of the plant Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, plants of the genus *Mercurialis* (Euphorbiaceae) are regarded as harmful when eaten in quantity, and Dioscorides' own note in 2-75 that mercury in a pasture upsets the milk of the animals fits that warning in direction only. This is a general statement and not a finding for the species of this chapter. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records an annual herb eaten as a vegetable or drunk as a boiled decoction to move the bowels and to expel bile and watery matter, and a belief that the leaves in a certain way determine the sex of a child. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol for any of them, and does not offer the sex-of-child claim as a method. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations: the plant "used as vegetables and eaten"; boiled in water, "the water taken as a drink"; and the leaves "pounded into small pieces and taken as a drink, as well as applied to the genitals after the menstrual flow cleansing". The chapter gives no dose or quantity for any of them (the only measure in it, twenty centimetres, is the height of the plant). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that the sex of a child is determined at conception by the father's chromosome and cannot be influenced by a herb, so the chapter's report on the male and female leaves has no pharmacological basis. Because the identity is a translators' suggestion between a "female" and a "male" kind, a modern fact about one *Mercurialis* species would not by itself bear on the remedies recorded.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying, moistening or astringent quality and no taste for 4-191. It prints only the actions (bowel-moving, expelling bile and watery stuff). The words "astringent and cooling" in 4-86 belong to that chapter's plant.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant and tell its two kinds apart (leaves like basil, branches with two joints, the female with clustered seed, the male with paired round branches, the whole twenty centimetres or more), and, through 2-75, that a pasture with this plant changes what the animal gives. The sex-of-child report is introduced with "It is thought that"; recording is not endorsing. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a vegetable and a drug for the bowels. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a bowel-moving herb with a reported folk belief attached, and no quantity. The useful lesson is Dioscorides' method of telling two kinds apart by their seed and branches, and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Dioscorides gives the plant as a bowel-mover that "expels bile and watery stuff", and records leaves applied to the genitals after the menstrual flow, so none of these should be self-treated, and no safety information for pregnancy was read. Species of *Mercurialis* are generally regarded as harmful when eaten in quantity, and the same author notes that mercury in a pasture upsets milk. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion that the text itself does not confirm, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
