# Rumex hepatonsis — sho.mang, Shomang, "Dockleaf" (rGyud-bzhi, Clark 1995 line 1109; Men-Tsee-Khang glossary by column position)

## *Rumex hepatonsis Spr., the first of three binomials Clark 1995 prints on line 1109 for sho.mang, after the English "Dockleaf" and before Rumex nepalensis and Rumex acetosa; the spelling "hepatonsis" is as printed, and the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary sets Rumex nepalensis ("Field sorrel") against "Shomang"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Shennong (Yang 1998), Sushruta, Ebers and Assyrian text folders on disk for *Rumex* found no hit in the primary texts. The Dioscorides text prints *Rumex* only in translators' SUGGESTED lines for Greek drugs (lines 5717 to 5718, 5726 and 5747: *Rumex obtusifolius*, *Rumex acetosa*, *Rumex alpinus*, *Rumex hydrolapathum*), which are European species given for a Greek drug and are not read as this plant. The row was added from a printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 195) the row is keyed by the **Tibetan name** *sho.mang*, which is the first name Clark prints, and not by a binomial. Its locus is "Clark 1995 (line 1109)", its translator identification reads "Dockleaf; Rumex hepatonsis Spr.; Rumex nepalensis; Rumex acetosa", and its note reads "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The spine binomial *Rumex hepatonsis* is therefore the **first** of Clark's three Latin names and the second item in the identification string, which opens with the English "Dockleaf". The other two Latin names are not lost: *Rumex nepalensis* has its own row (line 358, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary line 5804) and its own entry, I_301, and *Rumex acetosa* has I_699 in other traditions. The related hill dock *ri.sho* has its own row (line 210, Clark line 1131).

**Identity doubt.** The binomial is the least secure part of this entry. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and (line 68) that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect". The three names printed here are three different species. The spelling "hepatonsis" is printed so on line 1109 and again in Clark's Latin index (line 1639); the sources on disk contain no flora against which to check it, and the existing entry I_301 prints the same name as "Rumex hepaticus" (line 83 of that file). The Codex keeps the spelling as printed and does not correct it. The Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note (line 686) says that its botanical terms rest on the information of its own Materia Medica Department and names no earlier flora. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1109).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry follows *rnya.lo* or *snya.lo* (a *Polygonum*, line 1108) and is followed by *pa.yag* (line 1110). It reads:

"sho.mang: Dockleaf: Rumex hepatonsis Spr.: Rumex nepalensis: Rumex acetosa: [has three names and is generally classified according to five types, i.e., hill dock (ri.sho; see below), sheep dock (lug.sho) and plains dock (klung.sho). It has a large, red, jointed stalk, a large, glossy, flat, smooth, green leaf and coarse red or yellow flowers. It has sweet and bitter tastes. Chhu.sho ('waterdock') is also called ga.bra.ma or chhu.sha.wa. It is smaller than the above type, has slightly sour taste and no stalk]. Dockleaf cures wound fevers [and is beneficial in infectious fevers]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Almost all of the description is therefore the commentary layer: the three names, the "five types" (of which only three are named in the bracket as printed), the stalk, leaf and flower, and the tastes. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the English name, the three Latin names, which are Clark's identifications, and the sentence "Dockleaf cures wound fevers". "And is beneficial in infectious fevers" is inside the brackets. Clark's name indexes list *sho.mang* (line 1763) and the Latin index lists *Rumex hepatonsis* (line 1639).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "S" block).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs, and the columns do not hold the same number of entries. The phonetic names for the page that begins at line 5774 are seventeen, from "Shingngar" to "Sumchutik" ("Shomang" is the third), and the transliterations (lines 5787 to 5796) include "sho mang" (line 5788). The scientific names begin at line 5798: "Glycyrrhiza glabr" (so printed), "Cinnamomum zeylannicum", **"Rumex nepalensis"** (line 5804), "Rhus vemiciflua", "-" and so on, and the English names are interleaved: "Licorice", "Cinnamon", **"Field sorrel"** (line 5806). Counted in order, "Shomang" is the third phonetic name, "sho mang" the third transliteration, *Rumex nepalensis* the third scientific name and "Field sorrel" the third English name. The count is checked on "Shingngar" against *Glycyrrhiza* and "Licorice" (first), "Shudag" against *Acorus calamus* and "Sweet Flag" (fifth) and "Sumchutik" against *Elettaria cardamomum* and "Cardamom" (seventeenth). The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position only. The glossary therefore gives *Rumex nepalensis*, which is the **second** Latin name in Clark's string and not the first, so the two editions overlap on one binomial and differ on the spine's binomial. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225; colophon at line 3583), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), lines 3476 to 3480: "Zangtsi cures tripa disorders and jaundice. Nyalo cures hot disorder of the vessel organs and particularly the hot disorder of the small and large intestines. Shomang cures hot disorder of the wounds." (the printed line breaks are removed) The printed index lists "Shomang 210" (line 7493). "Hot disorder" is the translators' rendering and is kept as printed. The sentence gives no part used, no preparation and no taste. The editors' note at line 686 says that names of medicinal substances "have been kept in phonetic Tibetan".

The two editions agree in kind. Clark's drug "cures wound fevers" and, in the commentary layer, is "beneficial in infectious fevers"; the Men-Tsee-Khang *Shomang* "cures hot disorder of the wounds". The Codex reads *sho.mang* and *Shomang* as one drug from the spelling and that agreement; neither text says so. The Codex's own list of rGyud-bzhi names (`_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv`) carries the same drug twice: "Himalayan Dock, Rumex nepalensis Spreng." (line 54) and "Shomang" with no botanical name and the identity flagged uncertain (line 314). [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Rumex hepatonsis*, *sho.mang*, *Shomang* and *Dockleaf* found one entry on this drug, **I_301** (*Rumex nepalensis*, "Himalayan Dock", tag FMT). I_301 quotes the same Clark line (1109) and the same Men-Tsee-Khang glossary row for *Shomang*, and reads *Rumex nepalensis* as one of several identifications for the Tibetan name. This entry covers the same Tibetan passage under the first of Clark's binomials, so the two overlap on the passage and are not merged. I_301's heading names the Tibetan drug "Zangs-rtsi-sho-sha", which is not the name its own text cites (*sho.mang*); that is flagged for review in the report. I_699 (*Rumex acetosa*, tags AT, CM, DMM, SBJ), I_690 (*Rumex crispus*), I_720 (*Rumex obtusifolius*) and I_1180 (*Rumex alpinus*) are entries on other species in other traditions and are not merged. I_1280 (*Fragaria nilgeerensis*) quotes Clark's line 1224, which names "Hill Dockleaf" (*ri.sho*) among the emetics; that is a different Tibetan drug.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Rumex* is a genus of the knotweed family (Polygonaceae) and that the sorrels and docks are known for oxalic acid, which gives the leaves a sour taste, and for tannins in the roots. This is a statement about the genus and not a finding about the Tibetan drug. The lines read give no composition for *sho.mang*, and the three Latin names printed for it are three species, so nothing is stated here about what this drug contains. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "cures wound fevers" (Clark, line 1109) and "cures hot disorder of the wounds" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3480). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only through Clark's first Latin name and a glossary count, and they are not Codex protocols. A wound that is hot, swollen or discharging needs medical care, and the Codex gives no protocol and no dose for it. The English "Dockleaf" and "Field sorrel" are garden and meadow plants in other places, and the English names are not evidence that the Tibetan drug is one of them.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used (the commentary-layer description speaks of stalk, leaf and flower, and names no part as the drug), no quantity, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's line 1109 prints "sweet and bitter tastes" for the main type and a "slightly sour taste" for *chhu.sho*, and both statements are inside the brackets, so they belong to the commentary layer. It prints no power or post-digestive taste. The Men-Tsee-Khang lines read (3480, 5788, 5804, 5806) print no taste, power or post-digestive taste for *Shomang*.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it cures wound fevers, and, in the commentary layer, helps in infectious fevers. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names (*sho.mang*, *Shomang*, *Rumex*) found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, colophon at line 3950), and the herb entries of Clark's chapter 20 that mention essence extraction (lines 971, 1043, 1078, 1103, 1121 and 1144) do not include it. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a wound-fever drug with no preparation and no dose, and its Latin name is a positional choice among three species. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Wounds and fevers need medical care; the identity is open among three species, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. It is a general fact that sorrels and docks contain oxalates, which are a known concern in quantity for people prone to kidney stones, and this is a statement about the genus and not about the Tibetan drug. No conservation information and no safety information for pregnancy were read. [USER: verify]
