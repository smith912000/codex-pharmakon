# Crithmum maritimum — Krithmon (Dioscorides 2-157)

## *Crithmum maritimum (the translators' first suggestion; "Samphire, Sea Fennel, Peter's Cress"), with Cachrys maritimum as their second name* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The line reads "Crithmum maritimum, Cachrys maritimum — Samphire, Sea Fennel, Peter's Cress".

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *2-157. KRITHMON*, heading at line 5886, SUGGESTED line at 5887, text at line 5888. It stands in Book Two among the vegetables (*Lachana*).

The whole chapter reads: "Crithmon (also called critamon) is a shrubby little herb about a foot in height, with thick leaves, which grows in rocky maritime places. It is full of fat whitish leaves similar to those of purslane — yet thicker and longer and salty to the taste. It bears white flowers and a soft fruit similar to libanotis — sweet smelling, round. When dry it splits and has a seed within similar to wheat. The roots are the thickness of a finger, fragrant, and with a pleasant taste. The seed, root and leaves (boiled in wine and taken as a drink) are effective to help frequent painful urination and jaundice, and they induce the menstrual flow. It is used as a vegetable (eaten either boiled or raw), and is also preserved in brine."

So Dioscorides describes a small shrubby herb about a foot high, growing in rocky places by the sea, with fat whitish leaves like purslane but thicker, longer and salty to the taste; white flowers; a soft round sweet-smelling fruit like that of libanotis which, dry, splits and shows a seed "similar to wheat"; and finger-thick fragrant roots of pleasant taste. Seed, root and leaves boiled in wine and drunk are said to help "frequent painful urination" and jaundice and to bring on the menstrual flow. As food it is eaten boiled or raw and is kept in brine. The only other name printed is *critamon*; the chapter gives no Roman, African or other foreign names.

**Neighbouring places in the same text.** Two other places carry the Latin name. Chapter 2-108 *KRITHE* (barley) has, under its SUGGESTED lines, the cross-reference "see 2-157, Crithmum maritimum" (line 5435); the chapter itself concerns barley and its water and polenta, and why the translators placed the cross-reference there is not stated. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a coastal herb that was a table vegetable, eaten raw or boiled or kept in brine, and also a wine decoction of seed, root and leaves taken for painful, frequent urination, for jaundice and to bring on the menses. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol: jaundice and painful urination need medical assessment, and the identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

2-157 gives these preparations: seed, root and leaves "boiled in wine and taken as a drink"; the plant "eaten either boiled or raw" as a vegetable; and "preserved in brine". No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints the leaves as "salty to the taste", the fruit as "sweet smelling", and the roots as "fragrant, and with a pleasant taste". It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality for 2-157. (The word "warming" belongs to 3-88, a different chapter.)

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: where the plant grows (rocky maritime places), how to know it (a foot high, fat whitish leaves like purslane but thicker and salty, white flowers, a round fruit that splits to show a wheat-like seed, fragrant finger-thick roots), and what is done with it (a vegetable eaten raw or boiled, kept in brine). It assigns the plant no status beyond that of a food and a wine decoction. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a foraged coastal vegetable and a short list of urinary, jaundice and menstrual remedies with no stated amounts, so it is history and not a practice. The chapter's useful lesson is its method of recognition by place, leaf, fruit and root.

### Contraindications

Painful, frequent urination and jaundice are symptoms that need medical care, and a home preparation must not replace it. The use to induce the menstrual flow should not be self-treated, and no safety information for pregnancy was read. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion that is shared with another chapter (3-88), so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
