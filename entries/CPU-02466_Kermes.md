# Kermes — dūd al-qirmiz (دود القرمز), the dyers' worm, and the other "worms" of the entry dūd

## *Kermes, the scale insect* Kermes vermilio *(Planchon) that lives on the kermes oak and gives the crimson dye; the identification is the modern one, made from the Arabic* qirmiz *and Avicenna's "the worm of the dyers", and is of fairly high confidence for the first paragraph only; the entry also treats a many-legged worm of damp places, which is a different animal (probably a woodlouse)* | *(Volume III — Animal)*

**Tradition Tags:** CM

---

### Origin & Tradition

Kermes is named only by Avicenna's *Canon* among the texts on disk, under the general heading *dūd*, "worms". It is a single-text entry. The heading holds more than one creature, so this entry follows the text and keeps them apart.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** The *Canon* (Book II, entry *dūd*, Arabic text line 11941) begins: "the worm of *qirmiz*, which is the worm of the dyers; its strength is like the strength of *isfīdhāj* [ceruse, white lead, in the usual sense of the word; [USER: verify]], except that it is finer and penetrates more. Some said: this worm is picked up from many things, even from the oak" (دود القرمز وهي دودة الصباغين إن قوتها كقوة الأسفيذاج إلا أنها ألطف وأغوص ... حتى من البلوط, lines 11942-11943). That is the kermes. The oak detail agrees with the scale insect of the kermes oak.

The same entry then treats other creatures: "the worm of many legs" (*al-dūd al-kathīr al-arjul*) that is under the jars and doors, "the red worm that is under the water jars, which has many legs and curls round when touched" (lines 11947-11953). A creature with many legs that curls up when touched and lives under water jars fits a woodlouse, but the text gives no name, and that identification is a modern guess. [USER: verify] It is a different animal from the kermes and is listed here only because the *Canon* does not separate them.

### Active Components

The kermes insect holds a red anthraquinone-type pigment, kermesic acid, the dye. That is general chemistry. The *Canon* names no constituent. The woodlouse is a crustacean, with a calcium-carbonate shell; also general biology.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice.

- **Kermes (lines 11944-11946).** Fresh, cooling with a measure of dryness; "drying without stinging"; Galen: "a moderate astringency." For wounds of the nerve: powdered, with wine, or with vinegar and honey.
- **The many-legged worm (lines 11946-11954).** The text reports, with "it was said" (*qīla*), that "when a mithqāl of it is drunk it cures spasm and tetanus" (إذا شرب منه مثقال أبرأ التشنج والكزاز المؤذيين, line 11947); ground with pomegranate rind and rose oil and dropped into the ear, it calms ear pain (lines 11948-11949); rubbed on the palate with honey, it helps quinsy (*khawānīq*), and likewise when eaten, and helps asthma, and orthopnoea in grazing animals (lines 11949-11952); drunk with wine, it helps jaundice (lines 11952-11953) and difficult urination (lines 11953-11954).

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives one quantity: a mithqāl of the many-legged worm drunk, "it was said" (line 11947). For kermes it gives the forms "powdered with wine, or vinegar with honey" (lines 11946). All else is without weight. No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Kermes was the main source of crimson dye in the Mediterranean before the cochineal insect of the Americas replaced it. That is general history of dyes. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry on the medical uses. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **CM (Avicenna), lines 11944-11945.** Fresh kermes: "cooling, with a measure of dryness"; "drying without stinging"; Galen adds "a moderate astringency." The many-legged worm has no temperament in the entry. No other tradition is tagged.

### Cultivation Register

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent.* The entry treats dye insect and damp-place worm alike as external and internal remedies by complaint. It makes no statement about the vital faculties, the innate heat or the radical moisture, and gives no cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing is asked of a cultivator. The entry's lesson is about identification: one Arabic word, *dūd*, covers a scale insect, an unnamed crustacean and perhaps other creatures, and the text makes no attempt to unify them.

### Contraindications

- **Identification.** Do not mix the kermes insect with the other creatures of the entry; the text itself does not.
- **Infection and hygiene.** Eating or applying wild-caught invertebrates carries a risk of infection and parasites. Do not.
- **Tetanus.** The tetanus and spasm use is historical record only. Tetanus is a medical emergency; vaccination prevents it.
- **Allergy.** Insect-derived red dyes can cause allergy; general knowledge.
