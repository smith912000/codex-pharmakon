# Salvia aethiopis — Aithiopis (Dioscorides 4-105)

## *Salvia aethiopis, the binomial printed at the end of the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-105 ("[Pliny] Aethiopis — Ethiopian Sage — Salvia aethiopis")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line reads "[Pliny] Aethiopis — Ethiopian Sage — Salvia aethiopis": the first name carries a bracketed attribution to Pliny, the English name follows, and the binomial comes last. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-105. AITHIOPIS*, heading at line 8971, SUGGESTED line at 8972, text at line 8973. It stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), before *4-106. ARKTION* (heading at line 8974), and the page number 656 follows at line 8977. The translators' index gives *Aethiopis* and *Salvia aethiopis* at page 656 (lines 14502 and 17341).

The whole chapter reads: "Aethiopis has leaves similar to verbascum [4-104], very rough and thick, in a circle around the bottom of the root. The stalk is foursquare, thick and rugged, similar to apiastrum [3-118], or arction [4-106], putting out many wings. The seed is about the size of ervum [2-129, 2-131] with two in one capsule. There are many long thick roots from the very bottom, gluey to the taste, but dried they become black and hard like horns. It grows abundantly in Messenia and Ida. The root of this is called arcturum, and it has leaves similar to verbascum, only rougher and rounder. The root is tender, sweet and white; and the stalk is soft and long, similar to little cumin. The root and seed of this (boiled in wine) are held in the mouth to lessen toothache; and it is applied with hot cloths for burns and chilblains. It is taken as a drink in wine for sciatica and painful urination."

**The doubled passage.** The text from "The root of this is called arcturum" to the end repeats, almost word for word, the text of the next chapter, *4-106. ARKTION* (line 8976: "Arctium (which some call arcturum) has leaves similar to verbascum [4-104] but rougher and rounder; the tender root is sweet and white; and the soft stalk long like little cumin. The root and seed of this (soaked in wine) have the strength (held in the mouth) to soothe toothache. It is a poultice for burns and chilblains. It is also drunk in wine for sciatica and dysuria."). That chapter is the subject of the existing entry I_1010 (*Arctium minus*), which notes the same doubling. The Codex does not know whether the repeat is an intrusion in the translation or in the Greek, and does not say. The description of 4-105's own plant is therefore only the part before "The root of this is called arcturum": rough, thick leaves like verbascum in a circle at the base, a square, thick, rugged and winged stalk, a seed the size of *ervum* with two to a capsule, and many long, thick, gluey roots that turn black and hard like horns when dried. It "grows abundantly in Messenia and Ida" (two places named, not otherwise identified in the chapter). The uses (toothache, burns and chilblains, sciatica and painful urination) stand only in the repeated part, so whether they are claimed for *aithiopis* is not settled by the text. [USER: verify]

**Cross-references.** The chapter points to *4-104* (verbascum), *3-118* (apiastrum), *4-106* (arction) and *2-129, 2-131* (ervum) by note; none is read here beyond 4-106.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read, and the chapter prints none. It is a general fact that *Salvia* is a genus of the mint family (Lamiaceae). Nothing is stated here about the composition of the translators' *Salvia aethiopis*. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter as printed gives, after the repeated passage, uses for toothache (root and seed boiled in wine and held in the mouth), burns and chilblains (applied with hot cloths) and sciatica and painful urination (drunk in wine). These are the text's reports, doubtful in their attachment to this plant. The Codex gives no protocol, and the identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations, all in the repeated part: root and seed boiled in wine and held in the mouth; the root applied with hot cloths; and a drink in wine. It gives no quantity, frequency or duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality. The only taste printed is that the roots are "gluey to the taste" and, in the repeated part, that the root is "sweet". "Hot cloths" and "boiled" describe the preparation and not a quality of the plant.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use. The part of the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition that is present here is the description of the plant (rosette of rough leaves, square winged stalk, two-seeded capsule, gluey roots that blacken and harden on drying) and the naming of a place of growth (Messenia and Ida). The plant is given no status in the chapter beyond that of a described herb with a root-and-seed remedy. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It offers a careful description and a set of remedies without amounts that may belong to another chapter's plant, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Toothache that persists, sciatica and painful urination need medical assessment, and a home remedy must not replace it. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify] None of the traditional uses above is advice.
