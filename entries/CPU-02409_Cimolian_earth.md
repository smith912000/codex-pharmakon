# Cimolian earth — Kimolia (KIMOLIA), "Cimolian earth" (Dioscorides)

## *A soft white or purplish, fatty, cool-feeling earth from Kimolos, used diluted with vinegar on swellings and rubbed on burns; the translator's gloss reads "Cimolite — soft hydrous Alumina Silicate", a modern suggestion that the text does not support by any description beyond colour and feel, so the identification is of medium confidence* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** This is a single-text entry. The chapter is 5-176 KIMOLIA in Book Five of the *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10746-10749; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` carries it as 5-176, line 10746, translator's identification "Cimolite — soft hydrous Alumina Silicate", class mineral; `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 901 has "Kimolia", mineral). It stands in the run of earths under the page heading "METALLIC STONES". A search of the other texts on disk (Sushruta, Shennong, rGyud-bzhi, Ebers) for the name found no matching drug, and no attestation from them is claimed. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder; this entry replaces it.

The chapter is one paragraph (line 10749): "Some Cimolian earth is white, and some inclining to a purple with a certain fattiness to it, and somewhat cold to the touch. This is considered the best. Both sorts (diluted with vinegar) dissolve inflammation of the parotid gland, and the second type dissolves lesser tumours. They are good for burns, rubbed on by hand to keep the affected parts from blistering. They suppress hardness from stones [urinary, kidney], and inflammation on any part of the body; furthermore they withstand erysipela [streptococcal skin infection], and in brief the genuine are of much use if the bastard ones are not taken."

So the text names two sorts, a white one and one "inclining to a purple" with a fatty quality; says the purplish, fatty, cool-to-the-touch kind "is considered the best"; gives one preparation (diluted with vinegar) for swelling of the parotid and "lesser tumours"; gives a rub for burns; and warns that the genuine earth is useful only if the "bastard ones" (counterfeits) are not taken. It does not say where the earth was dug. The place is in the name only (the Greek *Kimolia*, the Latin "Cimolian"), and the island of Kimolos is not named in the lines read. [USER: verify]

*The run of earths around it.* The first chapter of the run, 5-170 GE, "Earth" (line 10711), sets the general rule: "All earth that falls within the scope of medicinal use is supremely useful for cooling, and for closing the pores. There are various kinds, some good for other things in some preparations." The chapter before this one, 5-175 SELINOUSIA (line 10739), says Selinusian earth "does the same things [as those above]" and gives its best kind as white, brittle and "very quickly dissolved if it gets wet". The chapter after it, 5-177 PNIGITIS (line 10750), says that earth has "the same properties as fuller's earth [ammonium silicate] except for its strength", which is the only place in the run that names a fuller's earth. The text does not say that Cimolian earth is a fuller's earth, and no claim is made here.

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. The translator's gloss, "Cimolite — soft hydrous Alumina Silicate", is a modern suggestion. In general terms, cimolite is a clay mineral (a hydrated aluminium silicate) and clays of this kind are fine-grained, absorbent and soft when wet. The chapter's description (white or purplish, "fatty", cool to the touch) is consistent with a fine clay but does not prove it. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a recommended practice.** The text records a fine, cool earth used as a diluted rub on swellings and burns. The Codex does not translate "inflammation of the parotid gland", "lesser tumours" or "erysipela" into modern diagnoses and gives no protocol. Burns and spreading skin infections need proper care. An unidentified earth dug from the ground can carry contaminants, and nothing in the text supports a modern use.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives one preparation: the earth "diluted with vinegar" for parotid inflammation and lesser tumours, and "rubbed on by hand" for burns (line 10749). It gives no ratio of earth to vinegar, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] The chapter gives no washing or roasting method for this earth; the washing and roasting recipe in the Eretrian earth chapter (5-171) belongs to that chapter and is not carried over.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Clay minerals are fine, hydrated aluminium silicates; powdered clays have a long record as drying, absorbent and protective dusts on skin, and as cooling poultices when wet. Vinegar is dilute acetic acid and is a mild irritant to broken skin. Clay applied to a burn is not a modern treatment, and a non-sterile earth on a broken burn risks infection (soil-borne spores include those of tetanus). No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** The text prints one quality for the earth: it is "somewhat cold to the touch" (line 10749). That describes the feel of the material in the hand and is not a graded temperament. The general statement in 5-170, that medicinal earth is "supremely useful for cooling, and for closing the pores" (line 10711), belongs to earth in general and is not printed in this chapter. No degree, no humour and no taste is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: how to tell the best kind (white or purplish, fatty, cool to the touch) and a warning that the genuine is of use "if the bastard ones are not taken" (line 10749). The chapter assigns no cultivation status, and the earth carries only a therapeutic one.

**Practitioner's note.** An ordinary cooling earth for swellings and burns in the ancient scheme. Nothing here supports ingestion or any cultivation use. The lesson that the chapter gives is the old one of knowing the real from the counterfeit before trusting a material, and the Codex does not turn that into a practice.

### Contraindications

- **Burns, skin infection, swellings.** These need proper medical care. Do not put earth on a burn or an open wound.
- **Contamination.** A dug earth may carry grit, microbes (including tetanus spores) and heavy metals, and the source of any commercial "Cimolian earth" is unknown.
- **Identity.** The cimolite identification is the translator's gloss, of medium confidence. The commercial name has been applied to other clays. [USER: verify]
- **Ingestion.** The chapter does not give a drink. Do not eat clay on the strength of this entry.
