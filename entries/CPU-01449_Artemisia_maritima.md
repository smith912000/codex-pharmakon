# Sea wormwood — Apsinthion thalassion, Absinthium marinum (Dioscorides 3-27)

## *Artemisia maritima (the Dioscorides translator's suggestion for chapter 3-27, with A. pauciflora; sea wormwood)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). The binomials below are the translator's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification. **Named only by Dioscorides in this row.**

*3-27 APSINTHION THALASSION* (heading at line 6767). The translator's line reads "Artemisia maritima, Artemisia pauciflora — Sea Wormwood, Garden Cypress, Sea Artemisia". The chapter (line 6769): "Absinthium marinum (also called seriphion) grows abundantly in the Taurus Mountains around Cappadocia and in Taphosiris, Egypt. The Isiaci use it instead of an olive branch. The herb has thin branches similar to the small abrotanum [3-29] with abundant little seeds, somewhat bitter, bad for the stomach, and with a strong smell. It is astringent with some heating, and boiled by itself (or with rice) and taken with honey it kills ascaridae [threadworms] and roundworms, loosening the bowels gently. It does the same things with sapa [new wine syrup] or boiled together with lentils. Cattle grow very fat feeding on it [fodder]. It is also called sandonion, or seriphum; the Romans call it santonicum."

The next chapter, 3-28, is the "third kind" of wormwood, santonicum (see I_1015), and says it can do "the same things as the Seriphian [3-27]". The ordinary wormwood chapter just before (3-26) is a different plant and was not used here.

**Identity.** The text prints *santonicum* as a Roman name in 3-27 and gives it again as the proper name of the next chapter. The translator assigns two different binomials to the two chapters. Whether Dioscorides meant two plants by that Latin name, or the names crossed in transmission, cannot be told from the text. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

*Artemisia* species are generally known to contain volatile oils and bitter sesquiterpene lactones. What the plant of this chapter contained was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records a bitter, astringent, slightly heating herb boiled for threadworms and roundworms and as cattle fodder, and says it is bad for the stomach. The Codex does not recommend it as a practice.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparations are those printed in 3-27: boiled by itself or with rice and taken with honey; or with sapa; or boiled with lentils (line 6769). No quantity is printed. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Wormwoods in general have a long history as bitter herbs against intestinal worms. That is a general historical statement. No study of *A. maritima* was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** 3-27 prints "somewhat bitter", "bad for the stomach", and "astringent with some heating".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers is the discipline of attention the cultivation spec describes: where the herb grows (the Taurus Mountains by Cappadocia, Taphosiris in Egypt), how to know it (thin branches like small abrotanum, abundant small seeds, strong smell), what the Isiaci do with it, and the writer's own caution that it is bad for the stomach. It records no use for the person's vital constitution and assigns no status. The identification is the translator's. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator takes from the chapter the habit of identification by habitat and sensory assay, and its caution. A bitter herb the text itself calls bad for the stomach is a short-course vermifuge in the record and nothing more.

### Contraindications

Dioscorides himself says the herb is bad for the stomach. Wormwood-group herbs in large amounts can cause toxicity, which is a general statement. Do not use in pregnancy. No traditional use above is advice, and no dose is given.
