# Lycium europaeum — Lukion (Dioscorides 1-132, "Lycium", also "pyxacantha"); "European Box Thorn" on the translators' line

## *Lycium europaeum, the first binomial on the translators' SUGGESTED line for chapter 1-132 (glossed "European Box Thorn"); a second line adds Lycium lanceolatum and Rhamnus infectorius [Matthiolus], "Spear-leaved Box Thorn"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk names this plant under this name. The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The SUGGESTED block lists two plants, *Lycium europaeum* and, after it, *Lycium lanceolatum* with *Rhamnus infectorius* [Matthiolus]. The entry takes the first. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The heading *1-132. LUKION* is at line 4494, the SUGGESTED block at line 4496 and the chapter text at lines 4497 to 4503, with a page break in the middle (page 130 is printed at line 4499, and the running head and the rest of the sentence resume at lines 4501 and 4502). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 134. The chapter stands in Book One (the running head near it reads "Gums from Trees"), after 1-131 *RHODIDES* (the rose pomanders, line 4490) and before 1-133 *AKAKIA* (acacia, line 4504).

The chapter has two parts. The first is about the plant and its prepared juice, and the second is about a different plant that is said to give the "Indian lycium".

*The plant.* "Lycium (also called pyxacantha) is a thorny tree with stems of three feet or longer around which the leaves grow thickly, similar to box. It has a black fruit similar to pepper — bitter, thick, smooth; a pale bark also similar when moistened, and many winding and woody roots. It grows abundantly in Cappadocia, Lycia and many other places. It loves rough places." The translators' glossary places Cappadocia in Asia Minor (line 3458) and Lycia on the south coast of Asia Minor (line 3507).

*The juice.* "The juice is drawn out of the leaves and shrubby stuff, pounded together and steeped for many days. Then it is boiled, the woody matter of it removed, and the liquid boiled again until it becomes the consistency of honey." Two more points follow. First, "It is counterfeited by the sediment of oil being mixed with it when boiled, or the juice of wormwood [3-26], or an ox gall." Second, "The froth which swims on top during boiling is taken off and bottled for eye medicines, but the rest is used for other purposes. Similarly, juice is made from the fruit pressed out and left in the sun."

*The test of a good juice.* "The best lycium takes fire and as it is quenched has a red foam. It is outwardly black but when it is cut yellow, without a stinking smell, astringent with bitterness, the colour of saffron — such as the Indian which is better than the rest and more effective."

*The uses.* "It is astringent and removes things that darken the pupils. It heals scabs on the eyelids, their itching and old discharges. Rubbed on it is good for pus-filled ears, tonsils, ulcerous gums, chapped lips, cracks in the perineum, and affritus [chafing] of the perineum. It is good for those troubled with colic and dysentery either taken as a drink or given as a suppository. It is given in water to those who spit-up blood, and those troubled with coughs, and is swallowed down like pills or taken as a drink with water for one bitten by a mad dog. It makes hair look yellow. It heals hangnails, whitlows, shingles and putrefying ulcers. It stops the menstrual flows of women. It helps those bitten by mad beasts, taken as a drink with milk or swallowed down like pills."

*The Indian lycium.* "It is said that the Indian lycium is made of a shrub called lonchitis [3-161, 3-162]. But it is a kind of thorn that has many upright stems, three feet long or more, growing out of the bottom, thicker than the bush, the broken bark looking red but the leaves similar to those of the olive tree. Boiled in vinegar and taken as a drink it is said to cure inflammation of the spleen and sickness of the head, and to expel women's flows. Given raw (pounded into small pieces) and taken in a drink it is said to have a similar effect. One half-wineglass of the seed (taken in drink) purges out watery matter, and is an antidote against deadly medicines." The chapter therefore sets the Indian kind beside the first as better, and names a different shrub, *lonchitis* (chapters 3-161 and 3-162), as the source that "is said" to be used. The text does not settle whether the Indian drug is the same plant. The Codex does not settle it either.

**Other places in the same text.** *Lycium* is the text's standard for a prepared juice, and eight other chapters use it as a comparison, each by the cross-reference [1-132]. 1-127 *UPOKISTIS* (hypocistis, line 4466) says the plant is dried, steeped and boiled "in the same way as lycium". 1-134 *AMORGE* (olive-oil sediment, lines 4517 to 4520) says amurca rubbed on with wine "is as effective as lycium for toothaches and wounds". 1-147 *ROUS* (sumac, line 4596) says the juice of its dried leaves boiled to the consistency of honey is "as useful for as many things as lycium". 3-3 *GENTIANE* (line 6544) says the plant is "applied like lycium" as a wound herb. 3-7 *GLUKORIZA* (liquorice, line 6579) is "juiced like lycium". 3-8 *KENTAURION MAKRON* (line 6600) says that "In Lycia they juice it and use it like lycium". A paragraph on the juicing of herbs in the text under the heading 3-9 *KENTAURION MIKRON* (line 6615) names "Lycium [1-132], wormwood [3-26], hypocistis [1-127]" as things boiled and stirred in the sun. 1-18 *BALSAMON* (line 3708) uses the tree as a comparison of size: the balsam tree is "similar in size to lycium [1-132] or pyracantha". None of these is attributed to this plant. They show that the juice was a standard preparation, which is the part of the chapter with the most weight.

**Overlap.** The chapter 1-132 is not the subject of any existing entry (chapter-level search for 1-132, *Lukion*, *Lycium europaeum* and *Lycium lanceolatum*). It is cited only as a cross-reference in I_1027 (*Balsamodendron gileadense*, the balsam tree, 1-18) and in I_1062 (*Cytinus hypocistis*, 1-127), and neither entry draws a use from it. The chapter 3-161 and 3-162 *lonchitis* is a separate matter and is not read here. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. As a general fact, a boiled-down plant juice of the kind described, with an astringent bitter taste, is the sort of preparation that commonly contains tannins, and the text itself calls it "astringent with bitterness". That is a general statement about astringent juices and not a finding about *Lycium europaeum*. Nothing is stated here about its composition. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records an eye, mouth, ear, bowel, skin and bite remedy made from a boiled-down juice, and a different remedy from an Indian kind. These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol. The list includes the stopping of the menstrual flow, a purge and an "antidote against deadly medicines", all of which are medical matters, and the identity of the plant is a suggestion between two species. The part of the chapter a cultivator can use as a model is the method of testing a prepared drug, set out below.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives the preparation in full (see Origin & Tradition): leaves and shrubby stuff pounded and steeped many days, boiled, strained of the woody matter, and boiled again to the consistency of honey; or a juice from the pressed fruit left in the sun. The froth is skimmed for eye medicines. The routes given for use are rubbing on, a drink (in water, or with milk), a suppository, and swallowing "like pills". No quantity is given for the first drug. The only quantity in the chapter is for the Indian kind: "One half-wineglass of the seed (taken in drink)". The translation prints "wineglass", and the text read does not define the measure. No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] As a general fact, the genus *Lycium* is in the nightshade family, and some species of it are known for berries used as food. The identity here is only the translators' suggestion, and the "black fruit similar to pepper" of the chapter is not shown to be one of those species, so no modern finding is carried over.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one quality for the drug, that it is "astringent" (line 4502) and, in the test of a good juice, "astringent with bitterness". It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality. The taste of the fruit is given as "bitter".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. What it gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is substantial: provenance (where the plant grows: Cappadocia, Lycia, and the Indian kind rated better), a method of preparation, a list of the ways the drug is adulterated (the sediment of oil, the juice of wormwood, ox gall), and a sensory and fire assay of a good juice (it "takes fire", gives a red foam when quenched, is black outside and yellow when cut, has no stinking smell, tastes astringent with bitterness and is the colour of saffron). That is a real practice of discrimination and it is the tradition's own. It is a discipline for the maker or buyer of a drug and not a vital or spiritual one. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** What a cultivator can take from the chapter is the habit of attention it models: know where a drug comes from, know how it is made, know how it is faked, and test it with the senses and with fire before relying on it. The chapter gives nothing else, and the preparation should not be copied from this entry, because the plant is not securely identified and the text gives no dose for it.

### Contraindications

Chapter 1-132 prints no warning. It records a use for stopping the menstrual flow, a purge for the Indian kind, and a swallowed preparation for the bite of a mad dog or "mad beasts", and each of those is a medical matter. A bite from a possibly rabid animal needs urgent medical care, and the chapter's remedy is not a substitute. The identification is only the translators' suggestion between two species, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. The chapter gives no dose for the first drug. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
