## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 De Materia Medica (Dioscorides, c. 50–70 CE)
DMM Book II.151: *Krommuon* — extensive entry. Dioscorides distinguishes: raw onion = sharp and pungent, produces tears, causes thirst, flatulence; cooked onion = milder, more digestible; green (spring) onion = intermediate. Properties documented:
- Diuretic ("promotes urine")
- Menstrual promoting ("moves menstruation")
- Topical: "removes dandruff applied with vinegar"; "draws out splinters"
- "Opens the passages" — expectorant/carminative application
- Appetite-stimulating
- Wound healing (topical preparations)
- Specific note on the lachrymatory property: "*The raw one, being applied to the eyes, causes tears*" — Dioscorides correctly identifies the compound as being in the raw (not cooked) form and specifically ocular in its irritation.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine (Ibn Sīnā, 1025 CE)
*Baṣal* — warming, carminative, diuretic. Ibn Sīnā distinguishes four preparations (raw/cooked/roasted/juice) with different indications:
- Raw juice: for hearing loss (poured in ear — antimicrobial application parallel to garlic)
- Cooked: for cough and respiratory secretions
- Roasted: applied externally for hemorrhoids and anal fissures
- Juice with honey: for ophthalmic conditions
Ibn Sīnā documents: "*Weakens eyesight if eaten much raw*" — an early observation possibly related to the lachrymatory response + allium compounds' effect on the lens (still under investigation).

### 2.3 Shennong Bencao Jing / Bencao Gangmu (SBJ)
*Cōng* (葱) and *Yángcōng* (洋葱): Spring onion/green onion deeply integrated in Chinese medicine. Li Shizhen documents cōng (spring onion): antimicrobial; warming; diaphoretic (sweat-inducing for fever/wind-cold invasion); "relieves qi stagnation and removes toxins." Common cold treatment (spring onion + ginger + brown sugar tea = Chinese household fever remedy). Bulb onion less historically documented in China (introduced later via Central Asia).

### 2.4 Sushruta Samhita / Charaka Samhita (SS)
*Palandu* — Ayurvedic classification: mostly warming, slightly sweet post-digestion. Charaka Samhita: *palandu* is a *balya* (strength-giving tonic); cardiac (hridya); digestive; improves complexion; treats *vata* disorders. Contrasted with garlic (*lashuna*): both Allium, but onion is "sweeter" and less rajasic — permissible in moderate quantities in some sattvic-adjacent contexts where garlic is excluded.

### 2.5 Ebers Papyrus (c. 1552 BCE)
*Ḥdw n ḥmw* — among the most frequently appearing plants. Pyramid builder rations documented alongside garlic (Herodotus: "radishes, onions, garlic"). Egyptian mummification: onion residues found in eye sockets, chest cavities, and wrapped with mummies — proposed explanation: antimicrobial preservation; possibly symbolic (onion's concentric layers representing eternal cyclical renewal); also, the pungent volatile compounds may have served as insect deterrents during embalming.

**Specific tomb finding:** In the mummy of Ramesses IV (c. 1149 BCE), onion bulbs were discovered in the eye sockets — the most iconic single archaeological find for Allium in ancient Egypt. The placement suggests deliberate anatomical positioning, possibly ritual (eyes that will see in the afterlife) combined with practical (antimicrobial, aromatic deterrent for body cavity pests).

### 2.6 Assyrian Medical Tablets (c. 700–600 BCE)
*Šapṣālu* — wound poultices; respiratory; GI conditions. In combination formulae alongside garlic.

### 2.7 Four Medical Tantras (FMT)
*Btsod* / *de* — warming digestive; treats cold-type stomach and respiratory conditions; anti-phlegm (Kapha-type parallel).

### 2.8 Badianus Manuscript (BM) — TENTATIVE
Wild *Allium* species native to Mexico were used by Nahua medicine. The BM reference to related plants is tentative; cultivated onion post-Columbian.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Convergence: 8/9** — second only to ginger, black pepper, and cinnamon in the Codex. Onion and garlic co-dominate as the most universally documented Allium complex across traditions.

**Universal applications:** Cardiovascular/circulatory (warming, diuretic), antimicrobial (wound, GI), respiratory (cough, secretions), digestive (carminative), topical wound healing.

**Uniquely onion vs. garlic:** Onion is universally documented as MORE tolerable, LESS pungent (in culinary use), and LESS offensively odorous than garlic — leading to broader social and culinary acceptance. The pharmacological consequence: onion's quercetin dominance vs. garlic's allicin/organosulfide dominance means onion has SUPERIOR flavonoid/antioxidant activity while garlic has SUPERIOR direct antimicrobial activity. These are complementary rather than redundant.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

### 4.1 The Lachrymatory Factor System (Onion-Specific)

Onion possesses **lachrymatory factor synthase (LFS)** — an enzyme absent in garlic — that diverts alliin metabolism away from allicin toward the **lachrymatory factor** (propanethial S-oxide, LF):

```
ONION CELL DISRUPTION:
  1-Propenyl-L-cysteine sulfoxide + alliinase
  → 1-Propenyl sulfenic acid
  → [WITHOUT LFS]: allicin (garlic pathway)
  → [WITH LFS]: Propanethial S-oxide (LACHRYMATORY FACTOR — volatile gas)
    ↓
  Reaches eyes → reacts with water in corneal film
    ↓
  Sulphuric acid analog → corneal pain → reflex lacrimation
```

Enzymatic basis of onion-induced crying: gas phase propanethial S-oxide reaches eye faster than the mouth can metabolize it. **Solutions:**
- Chill onion (LFS slows at 4°C)
- Cut under water (dissolves LF before eye contact)
- Ventilation (dilutes gas)
- Sharp knife (fewer cells ruptured → less LFS activation)

### 4.2 Quercetin (and Conjugates) — Primary Bioactive
**Quercetin-3-glucoside** (isoquercetin): 60–80% of total quercetin; water-soluble; better absorbed than free quercetin
**Quercetin-4'-glucoside**: 10–20%
**Quercetin** (free aglycone): 5–15%
**Total quercetin:** 25–300 mg/100g depending on variety (red > yellow >> white)

Quercetin mechanisms:
- **H1 antihistamine** (competitive inhibitor of histamine at H1 receptor) + mast cell stabilization (inhibits PKC-mediated degranulation) = dual anti-allergy mechanism
- **COMT inhibitor** (catechol-O-methyltransferase): prolongs dopamine/noradrenaline half-life; may contribute to mood effects
- **Xanthine oxidase inhibitor**: reduces uric acid formation (gout prevention/treatment)
- **Anti-inflammatory:** COX-1/2 inhibition; NF-κB inhibition; IL-6, IL-1β reduction
- **Antiviral:** Inhibits neuraminidase; reduces viral replication
- **Anticancer:** EGFR inhibition; PI3K/Akt inhibition; apoptosis induction; CYP1B1 activation of tamoxifen metabolites

### 4.3 Organosulfide Compounds
**Dipropyl disulfide** and **dipropyl trisulfide**: primary allyl sulfides in onion (propyl, not allyl chain as in garlic); milder antimicrobial activity than garlic's allicin; antiplatelet.
**Thiosulfinates** (trace allicin plus onion-specific thiosulfinates): antimicrobial, antiplatelet.

### 4.4 Fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS) and Inulin
4–8% dry weight; prebiotic; selectively stimulates *Bifidobacterium* and *Lactobacillus*. Highest FOS content in cooked vs. raw (cooking partially degrades to shorter-chain FOS that are more easily fermented).

### 4.5 Chromium Complex
Trace element organic chromium complex in onion; potentiates insulin receptor sensitivity. Contributes to antidiabetic activity of dietary onion.

### 4.6 Anthocyanins (Red Onion)
**Cyanidin-3-glucoside** and **cyanidin-3-galactoside**: responsible for red color; anti-inflammatory; additional antioxidant; the same compound found in elderberry (I_173) and berries — making red onion an unusually dense dietary source of berry-equivalent anthocyanins.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Anti-inflammatory and Antihistamine (Quercetin)
H1 receptor competitive antagonism (reversible): blocks histamine binding to smooth muscle and endothelium, reducing bronchoconstriction, vasodilatation, and mucus secretion in allergic reactions. Simultaneously inhibits IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation (PKC pathway) → reduced histamine, prostaglandin D2, leukotriene C4 release from mast cells. Two-pronged anti-allergy mechanism: blocks action of released histamine AND reduces the amount released.

### 5.2 Cardiovascular (Multiple)
- **Antiplatelet:** Quercetin + thiosulfinates → reduced TXA₂ synthesis (aspirin-like COX-1 inhibition) + direct platelet activation inhibition
- **Antihypertensive:** Quercetin → eNOS activation → NO production → vasodilation; also direct VGCC modulation
- **LDL oxidation prevention:** Quercetin's antioxidant activity in LDL particle → reduces oxidized LDL (oxLDL) → less foam cell formation → atherosclerosis protection
- **COMT inhibition** → prolonged noradrenaline → cardiac inotropy (clinical significance unclear at dietary doses)

### 5.3 Antidiabetic
**Quercetin:** Inhibits aldose reductase (prevents glucose → sorbitol conversion → diabetic neuropathy/retinopathy pathway); PPARγ agonism (insulin sensitization); SGLT2 inhibition (glucose reabsorption in kidney — same target as pharmaceutical gliflozins).
**Chromium complex:** Insulin receptor sensitization via chromodulin protein.
**FOS:** Prebiotic → GLP-1 stimulation from L-cells → insulin secretion enhancement.

### 5.4 Antimicrobial (Thiosulfinates + Quercetin)
Quercetin forms metal complexes that disrupt bacterial cell membranes; inhibits DNA gyrase (antibiotic parallel); anti-biofilm. Thiosulfinates: same mechanism family as garlic's allicin (thiol-trapping) but less potent. Together: moderate broad-spectrum activity adequate for topical wound care and GI antimicrobial support, inferior to garlic for systemic antimicrobial applications.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### 6.1 Quercetin — Hypertension
**Egert et al. 2009 (British Journal of Nutrition) RCT:** N=93; quercetin 150mg/day × 6 weeks; significant reduction in systolic BP (−2.6 mmHg, P=0.02) in hypertensive subgroup. Meta-analysis (Serban 2016, Phytomedicine): 7 RCTs, N=587; quercetin supplementation significantly reduced systolic BP (WMD −3.04 mmHg) and diastolic BP (WMD −2.63 mmHg) — modest but consistent.

### 6.2 Quercetin — Allergies
**Rogerio 2010:** Quercetin supplementation reduced eosinophil infiltration and IL-4/IL-5 in experimental allergic asthma. Multiple in vitro and animal studies confirm H1 antagonism and mast cell stabilization; clinical RCT data limited for dietary onion specifically vs. isolated quercetin.

### 6.3 Whole Onion — Glycemia
**Taj Eldin 2016 (Env Health Prev Med) RCT:** N=84 T2DM patients; raw red onion 100g/day vs. control × 8 weeks; fasting blood glucose significantly reduced (−40 mg/dL from baseline); consistent with aldose reductase inhibition + chromium + quercetin synergism.

### 6.4 Epidemiological
**Netherlands Cohort Study (Hertog 1993):** N=120,852; high quercetin intake (largely onion-derived) associated with significantly reduced lung cancer risk (RR 0.52 for highest vs. lowest quartile). This was the landmark study establishing quercetin/onion → lung cancer epidemiological relationship. Multiple subsequent cohort studies confirm onion intake inversely associated with gastric, colorectal, and lung cancer risk.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Ramesses IV and the Eye-Socket Onions (c. 1149 BCE):** The placement of onion bulbs in the eye sockets of the mummified pharaoh Ramesses IV during embalming (discovered by Grafton Elliot Smith, 1905) is the most anatomically specific and archaeologically documented application of an allium in antiquity. The interpretation remains contested between symbolic (onion = eternal eye of Horus/Ra cycle), antimicrobial (volatile compounds deterring embalming-period insects), and aromatic preservation. All three interpretations have pharmacological plausibility — onion's thiosulfinate volatiles are genuinely insecticidal and the compounds persist in dried plant material for months.

**The Lachrymatory Factor and the Chemistry of Crying:** The evolutionary purpose of propanethial S-oxide (LF) production in onion — as distinct from allicin production in garlic — is believed to be a defensive strategy against insects and herbivores that specifically attack Allium (especially the onion fly, *Delia antiqua*). The lachrymatory factor is more volatile and immediately aversive than allicin. The enzyme LFS represents a unique evolutionary specialization within *Allium* — a secondary metabolite enzyme acquired independently of garlic's defense chemistry. The human cultural consequence — onion-cutting = crying = comedy trope, emotional metaphor, cooking challenge — is a civilizational artifact of a fly deterrent.

**The "Medicine Chest" Attribution to Elder — Applied to Onion:** Hippocrates' attribution of "medicine chest" to elder (Sambucus) is widely cited; a parallel cultural attribution existed for onion in multiple traditions. The concept of a single widely available plant that addresses most common medical conditions (digestive, respiratory, cardiovascular, antimicrobial) appears in DMM, CM, Ayurveda, and Egyptian records simultaneously — reflecting onion's unique position as both global staple food and global medicine, accessible to all economic classes unlike imported spices.

**White vs. Red Onion — Invisible Pharmacology:** The quercetin content difference between red onion (100–300 mg/100g) and white onion (<5 mg/100g) is invisible to most consumers — both look like "onions." This quercetin gradient is one of the largest pharmacological variations between visually similar cultivars of any food plant in the Codex. White onion has been selected for mild flavor (low sulfur compounds) and visual appeal (pure white) at the cost of nearly all flavonoid pharmacological activity. Red and yellow onions retain the pharmacological heritage; Vidalia and other "sweet" onions are essentially medicinal blanks. The cultural preference for white onion in certain American and European cuisines represents an inadvertent pharmacological downgrade from traditional red/yellow varieties.

**Onion Syrup for Cough (Pan-European Folk Medicine):** Raw onion + sugar or honey, macerated overnight → viscous syrup. Administered for cough, bronchitis, childhood respiratory infections across France (sirop d'oignon), Germany, Poland, Russia, Italy. The mechanism: quercetin + thiosulfinates → mild antimicrobial + bronchial anti-inflammatory + mucolytic; quercetin mast cell stabilization → reduced mucus secretion. The folk remedy is pharmacologically coherent; no RCT exists but the mechanism supports efficacy for mild cough/cold conditions.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Dosing and Preparation
| Application | Form | Dose | Notes |
|-------------|------|------|-------|
| Cardiovascular/antidiabetic | Raw red/yellow onion | 1 medium onion (100g)/day | Eat raw or lightly cooked |
| Antihistamine/allergy | Quercetin extract | 250–500mg quercetin/day | Standardized for quercetin aglycone |
| BP reduction | Quercetin extract | 150mg/day | Per Egert 2009 |
| Folk cough remedy | Onion syrup | 1 tbsp × 3–4/day | Raw onion + honey maceration |
| Topical antimicrobial | Fresh onion slice | Direct application | Traditional wound/splinter use |

**Maximize pharmacological value:** Red or yellow (not white or sweet) onion; raw or lightly cooked (quercetin is heat-stable but thiosulfinates degrade); outer layers contain more quercetin than inner (outer skin and first few layers = highest concentration).

### 8.2 Safety
- Culinary amounts: fully safe; no significant toxicity documented
- **Antiplatelet**: Significant at high doses (>200g/day raw); additive with aspirin/clopidogrel — minor concern at culinary amounts
- **GERD**: Raw onion in quantity → lower esophageal sphincter relaxation → acid reflux (same as peppermint/onion LES relaxation mechanism)
- **Fabaceae allergy**: None (Amaryllidaceae, unrelated); but Allium-specific allergy (rare) exists — occupational asthma in onion handlers from LF volatiles
- **Pregnancy**: Culinary amounts safe; avoid concentrated medicinal doses (emmenagogue at very high doses — same tradition documented by Dioscorides)
- **FOS-related gas**: High onion consumption → significant flatulence/bloating from FOS fermentation; reduce dose gradually; GI tolerability varies

### 8.3 Tradition-Practice Concordance
Onion's universal cross-cultural medical record is validated most clearly in quercetin pharmacology (anti-inflammatory, antihistamine, antihypertensive) and prebiotic FOS (gut health). The traditional diuretic and respiratory applications map to quercetin's anti-inflammatory activity in airways and kidney. The universal preference for pungent (yellow/red) onion in traditional medical applications — in contrast to sweet/white onion — reflects accurate intuitive pharmacognosy: the pungent varieties carry the pharmacological activity. The exception to onion's universal positive assessment across traditions is notable: Ayurvedic sattvic exclusion of onion (alongside garlic) as *rajasic* (passion-inflaming) represents the only major cross-traditional pharmacological dissent, and it is paradigm-based (spiritual-energetic model) rather than empirically pharmacological.
