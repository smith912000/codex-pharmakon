# Agaric (larch bracket fungus) — Agaricon (AGARICON), "Agaricum"
## *A woody fungus taken by the ancients to be a root; the translator's heading to Dioscorides 3-1 suggests Fomes officinalis, Polyphorus officinalis, Boletus purgans, Polyphorus igniarius, Boletus laricis and Ungulina officinalis, and lists Agaricus aurantiacus and Agaricus campestris, the Orange and Common Mushroom, under "other usage"; the chapter text itself gives no binomial and calls it a root* | *(Volume IV — Fungal/Algal, shelved in Volume I)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Agaric is chapter 1 of Book Three, "Roots", of the *De Materia Medica*, headed AGARICON (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 6514-6522; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` carries it at line 407 as 3-1, line 6514, and the list `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 388 has "Agaricon, Fomes officinalis" as an identification not marked uncertain). It is the first chapter of the book. The translator's heading (lines 6515-6517) gives the long list of suggested names quoted above, which shows that the identification is a matter of the translator's and earlier botanists' opinion and not of the text. The entry's title, "larch bracket fungus", comes from the Codex's own naming of the material, not from Dioscorides, who does not mention a larch. [USER: verify]

*What the text says it is (line 6518).* "Agaricum is said to be a root similar to silphium [3-94], not thick on the outside like silphium, but all thin. Some of it is male and some female, of which the female excels, having straight veins within, but the male is round and grows the same on all sides. In taste both are the same, tasting sweet indeed at first; after dissolving it grows bitter. It grows in Agaria in the Sarmatian (country). Some say it is the root of a tree, some that it grows in the stocks of trees that are rotten, like mushrooms. It also grows in Galatia in Asia, and in Cilicia on the cedars but this is brittle and weak." So the chapter reports the nature of the material as uncertain ("said to be a root"; "some say it is the root of a tree, some that it grows in the stocks of trees that are rotten, like mushrooms"); it gives a male/female grading, with the female preferred for its straight internal veins; it records the taste (sweet at first, then bitter as it dissolves); and it names three sources: Agaria in the Sarmatian country, Galatia in Asia, and Cilicia, where it grows on cedars and is "brittle and weak".

*Properties and uses (lines 6518 and 6522).* "As for the properties of it: it is astringent and warming, good for griping, indigestion, hernias, and falls from on high." The chapter then gives the following. For liver ailments, asthma, jaundice, dysentery, nephritis, dysuria, womb constriction "and sickly looks" a teaspoonful is given. To those with "tuberculosis of the lungs" it is given with passum (raisin wine), and to the splenetic with vinegar and honey. For gastritis it is "chewed and swallowed down without moisture poured on it", and in a similar way for acidic vomiting. Thirty grains with water "stops the throwing-up of blood". An equal amount with vinegar and honey is good "for sore hips, sore joints, and epilepsy". It "encourages the menstrual flow", and the same weight is given to women with a suffocated womb. It "dissolves shivering, given before a fever fit". A teaspoonful or two as a drink with honey and water "purges the bowels". It is "an antidote for poison taken with one teaspoonful of diluted wine", and thirty grains with wine helps the strikes and bites of snakes. The chapter closes: "Finally, it is good for all internal disorders, given according to strength and age — to some with water, to others with wine, and to others with vinegar and honey, or honey and water."

*Rhubarb as a cross-reference.* The next chapter, 3-2, on rhubarb (RA), tells the reader: "You must give it as you do agaric [above] for every disorder — allowing the same amounts with liquids, using it with honeyed wine to those not feverish, but to the feverish give it with honey and water; for tuberculosis with passum; to the splenetic with vinegar and honey; for gastritis chewed as it is and swallowed down" (line 6528). That chapter treats rhubarb and is not folded in, but it confirms that agaric was a standard against which another root was dosed.

*Mushrooms, a different entry.* Dioscorides treats the fungi in general at 4-83, MUKETES, "Fungi species — Mushrooms etc.", marked "SOME SPECIES POISONOUS", which is a separate entry of this Codex (`I_868_Mushrooms.md`). The translator's inclusion of the Common Mushroom and the Orange Mushroom under "other usage" in 3-1 does not make agaric the same material as those.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, a bracket fungus is the fruiting body of a wood-rotting fungus; its tissue is built of chitin and glucans, and woody bracket fungi hold resinous material and a range of bitter secondary compounds. The identification is uncertain (see above), so no particular compound is assigned to the material the text describes. Nothing links any constituent to the actions the text gives.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides gives agaric as an all-round internal drug with a purgative effect in the larger amount, and records many uses. The Codex does not recommend any of them. The identification of the material is open, several of the conditions named (blood-spitting, tuberculosis, nephritis, epilepsy, jaundice, snakebite) are serious and need clinical care, and a bitter purgative taken for "all internal disorders" is not a safe general remedy. The entry exists to record what the text says, to keep its identification question open, and to place agaric as a named root-like drug in the Greek materia medica.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives quantities, in the translator's units, and the preparations that go with them (lines 6518 and 6522). It does not say how the material is prepared before use, whether powdered, grated or cut, and the instruction for gastritis is that it is "chewed and swallowed down without moisture poured on it". The doses as printed:

- "Twenty grains is given with honeyed wine to those not feverish, but it is given in honey and water to the feverish."
- "For liver ailments, asthma, jaundice, dysentery, nephritis, dysuria, womb constriction, and sickly looks a teaspoonful is given."
- "Thirty grains (taken with water) stops the throwing-up of blood."
- "A teaspoonful or two (taken as a drink with honey and water) purges the bowels."
- As an antidote for poison, "taken with one teaspoonful of diluted wine" (the English does not say in so many words what the teaspoonful measures, and it is read here as of the agaric), and "thirty grains (taken as a drink with wine)" for the strikes and bites of snakes.
- "Given according to strength and age."

The units "grains" and "teaspoonful" are the translator's renderings of the original measures; the original Greek weights are not printed in the English text. These doses are historical record and are not recommendations. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Bracket fungi are the fruiting bodies of wood-decaying fungi, and some species of the group have been used in folk medicine in several places; the translator's list for 3-1 includes bracket fungi (Fomes, Polyphorus, Ungulina and Boletus species, one of them named *laricis*), and the Codex's title reads the material as a bracket fungus of the larch, which has become rare in parts of its range. [USER: verify] Whether the Greek material was that fungus cannot be settled from the English text. Fungi in general are not interchangeable: some are edible, some are toxic, and misidentification of wild fungi is a leading cause of poisoning. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** The text prints that agaric is "astringent and warming" (line 6518), and gives its taste as "sweet indeed at first; after dissolving it grows bitter". It prints a male and female grade, with the female excelling. No cooling, drying or moistening quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is asserted here. What the chapter offers a practitioner is a discipline of attention: it grades the material (the female, with straight veins within, "excels"; the male is round), it gives the taste on the tongue (sweet, then bitter), it names the sources and calls the Cilician agaric, from cedars, "brittle and weak", and it ties the dose to the patient: "according to strength and age". That is a practice of provenance and assay and it is attested for this substance. It is not a cultivation status. The chapter makes no claim about the person's constitution, vitality or long life, and the drug is given for conditions, not for nourishing life.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. Agaric is a bitter-tasting drug, a purge in the larger amount, given for many conditions, with doses in the translator's units and an identification that is not settled. The habit the chapter teaches is to grade the material by sight and taste and to match the dose to the person's strength and age. The Codex does not recommend taking it, and nothing in the chapter supports long-term use. No modern dose is given here. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Identification:** the material the text describes is not securely identified; do not collect or consume wild fungi on the basis of this entry. Misidentified fungi can be fatally poisonous.
- **Purgative action:** the text says the larger dose "purges the bowels"; people with diarrhoea, dehydration, pregnancy or bowel disease should not take purgatives without medical advice.
- **Pregnancy:** the text says it "encourages the menstrual flow", and the Codex advises against any such substance in pregnancy.
- **Serious conditions:** the text names blood-spitting, tuberculosis, jaundice, epilepsy, kidney disease and snakebite; each needs medical care.
- **Conservation:** the larch bracket fungus is rare in parts of Europe and may be protected there; do not collect it. [USER: verify]
