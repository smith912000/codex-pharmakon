# Water Hemlock / Cowbane

## *Cicuta virosa* L. | Family: Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Cold, damp, violently acrid — a marsh poison of the "convulsive" class, not the "narcotic" class

**Training Phase:** None. This entry is **identification and avoidance only**. No phase of Kenetica training admits this plant internally, at any dose, by any preparation.

---

> ## ☠️ DEADLY — DO NOT INGEST, TASTE, OR TEST
>
> ***Cicuta virosa*** **is among the most violently toxic plants in the Northern Hemisphere.** Its principal toxin, **cicutoxin**, is a non-competitive antagonist at the GABA-A receptor-gated chloride channel. It does not sedate, dull, or intoxicate — it *removes the brain's brake*, producing unopposed neuronal depolarisation, and within minutes: vomiting, trismus, and violent tonic-clonic convulsions progressing to **status epilepticus**, rhabdomyolysis, renal failure, and death by anoxia or cardiac dysrhythmia.
>
> **Time to onset can be under 15 minutes. A fragment of tuber is enough.**
>
> **This entry contains no dose. There is no therapeutic window. There is no "careful" preparation.** Any figure quoted below is toxicological — a description of how people die, not a schedule for how anyone might live.
>
> **If ingestion is suspected: emergency services immediately.** Do not induce vomiting; seizure onset is fast enough that an emetic risks aspiration mid-convulsion. Definitive management is airway control and aggressive benzodiazepine-led seizure termination in hospital.

### Origin & Tradition

*Cicuta virosa* is a perennial umbellifer of the cold-temperate wetlands — ditches, fen margins, slow streams, marsh edges — across northern and central Europe, Russia, and northern Asia, with vicariant species (*C. maculata*, *C. douglasii*, *C. bulbifera*) occupying the same ecological niche across North America. It is a plant of the waterline: the chambered, aromatic rootstock sits half in the mud, and it is that rootstock — sweet-smelling, parsnip-like, transversely partitioned into hollow chambers oozing a yellow resinous juice on cut — that carries the killing dose.

The tradition around this plant is, almost uniquely in this codex, a tradition of **warning rather than use**. That is itself the datum worth recording.

**Dioscoridean / Graeco-Roman Materia Medica (DMM) — attested with a heavy caveat.**
Classical materia medica does carry a marsh-dwelling "hemlock" distinct from the dry-ground hemlock of the philosophers, and the wet-habitat umbellifers were grouped among the acrid, deadly, non-food umbels. But the classical taxonomy of the Apiaceae is a swamp of its own: "hemlock," "water hemlock," "hemlock dropwort," and "cowbane" have been applied inconsistently across two millennia and at least three genera (*Cicuta*, *Conium*, *Oenanthe*). **Any specific classical passage assigned to *Cicuta virosa* rather than to *Conium* or *Oenanthe* should be treated as uncertain attribution.** What survives reliably is the *external* register: the acrid umbel-juices appear as counter-irritant and vesicant plasters, applied to skin over swellings and joints, never taken by mouth. **[USER: verify the DMM tag against a primary-source concordance — the classical citation trail here is likely a *Conium*/*Oenanthe* conflation.]**

**Northern European folk medicine and veterinary lore — attested.**
The vernacular names are the doctrine. **Cowbane.** *Wasserschierling*. *Sprängört*. This is a plant known through its livestock kill-count, not its pharmacy. Farm lore in the fen countries is consistent and practical: it emerges early, before the palatable pasture greens up; cattle grazing wet margins in spring pull the tubers out of soft mud with the shoot; the animal is usually found dead at the water's edge with no observed illness in between. The North American record is identical in structure — a 2010 Utah case series in *Toxicon* documented **nine mature cows found dead from a herd of 81** after grazing water hemlock, with no clinical signs witnessed at all (Panter et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.toxicon.2010.11.009)). The folk use that does persist is **external and counter-irritant** — a bruised-root poultice as a vesicant on animals and, rarely and recklessly, humans. Even this is a bad idea; cicutoxin is lipophilic and dermally available.

**⚠️ THE FATAL CONFUSION — this is the whole practical content of the entry.**
*Cicuta* is repeatedly mistaken for edible Apiaceae. This is not a rare event; it is the *standard* mechanism of human poisoning. The confounders:

- **Water parsnip** (*Sium latifolium*, *Berula erecta*) — shares the habitat exactly.
- **Wild parsnip / wild carrot** — the root smells sweet and parsnip-like. A 14-year-old boy in Saskatchewan died 20 hours after eating what his family gathered as "wild carrot"; cicutoxin was recovered from the plant sample (Heath, *Vet Hum Toxicol* 2001, PMID 11205076 — no DOI assigned).
- **Wild celery** — foliage confusion.
- **Sweet flag / cattail root** and other foraged marsh rhizomes.

A 2021 *Pediatric Neurology* case is the archetype: a 9-year-old bit into the root of a water plant **in an inner-city park**, and presented an hour later in convulsive status epilepticus that resisted midazolam and lorazepam and was only broken with propofol. Cicutoxin and cicudiol were confirmed in blood (Arsenova et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pediatrneurol.2021.11.011)). He survived. Many do not.

**⚠️ DISTINGUISH FROM POISON HEMLOCK (*Conium maculatum*) — separate codex entry, different plant, different death.**
These are constantly conflated in popular writing and must never be conflated here:

| | **Water Hemlock (*Cicuta virosa*)** | **Poison Hemlock (*Conium maculatum*)** |
|---|---|---|
| Toxin class | **Cicutoxin** — C17 polyacetylene | **Coniine / γ-coniceine** — piperidine alkaloid |
| Mechanism | **Non-competitive GABA-A antagonist** | Nicotinic ACh receptor agonist→depolarising blockade |
| Clinical picture | **Violent convulsions, status epilepticus** | **Ascending flaccid paralysis, clear mind, respiratory arrest** |
| Death | Anoxia during seizure; dysrhythmia; rhabdomyolysis | Diaphragmatic paralysis while conscious |
| Habitat | Wet — marsh, ditch, streambank | Dry — waste ground, roadside, hedgerow |
| Smell | Sweet, parsnip-like root; hollow chambered rootstock | Mousy, foetid; purple-blotched stem, solid |
| The famous death | — | Socrates |

A 1988 USDA review put both side by side and called waterhemlock plainly "the most violently toxic poisonous plant known," noting signs appear **within 15 minutes** of a lethal dose (Panter, Keeler & Baker, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2527/jas1988.6692407x)).

#### **Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

The traditional signature-reading of *Cicuta* is unusually accurate, and it is worth saying exactly *why* — because the doctrine and the chemistry converge on the same point from opposite directions.

Folk classification split the poisons of the north into two families: those that **cool, slow, and silence** (the narcotics — hemlock proper, poppy, the nightshades' stupor) and those that **inflame, jerk, and tear** (the convulsants — cowbane, the dropworts). The convulsive poisons were read as *hot in action despite cold in habitat* — a fire arising out of standing water, a paradox that made cowbane a genuinely uncanny plant in fen folklore. The "sardonic" grimace associated in classical and medieval sources with the marsh umbels (the *risus sardonicus* of trismus) is not a fable; it is a rictus produced by masseter tetany, which is exactly what a GABA-A blockade of the brainstem and cortex produces.

Map that onto the receptor and the doctrine becomes literal. Cicutoxin is **not a stimulant** — it adds nothing. It is a *disinhibitor*. GABA is the CNS's principal inhibitory transmitter; cicutoxin blocks the chloride channel it gates, non-competitively, so the neuron cannot be quieted. The "fire from cold water" is precisely the phenomenology of removing a brake rather than pressing an accelerator: the excitation is the body's own, unopposed. The folk intuition that this plant *releases something already present* rather than *introducing something foreign* is, mechanistically, correct.

Where the doctrine must be deflated: any reading that positions cowbane as a **"powerful medicine at the right dose"**, or as a shamanic/visionary plant, or as a homeopathic-style "similar" for epilepsy on the reasoning that it *causes* convulsions. This last is the most seductive and the most dangerous — the *Cicuta virosa* homeopathic preparation exists precisely on that logic, and while ultramolecular dilutions are pharmacologically inert (and therefore only a waste of money), the same doctrine applied to a tincture of any real strength is lethal. There is no dose at which cicutoxin's GABA-A blockade becomes therapeutic. The blockade's steepness is the point: the margin between "nothing" and "status epilepticus" is not a therapeutic window, it is a cliff edge.

### Active Components

- **Cicutoxin** — the principal toxin. A **C17 conjugated polyacetylene** (long π-bond conjugation, terminal hydroxyl, allylic hydroxyl). Not an alkaloid. Highly lipophilic, concentrated in the chambered rootstock and the oleoresin.
- **Cicudiol, cicutol derivatives, virol A and B** — related C17 polyacetylenes, co-occurring. Cicudiol has been confirmed alongside cicutoxin in human blood post-ingestion (Arsenova et al., 2021, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pediatrneurol.2021.11.011)).
- **Structure–activity relationship** — a *J Med Chem* study on *C. virosa* itself synthesised cicutoxin and virol derivatives and found that **π-bond conjugation length and geometry, plus the O-functional groups, determine acute toxicity**, and that potency at displacing [³H]EBOB from GABA-gated Cl⁻ channels in rat cortex **correlated with acute lethality in mice** (Uwai et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/jm000185k)). This is the cleanest chemistry→mechanism→death chain available for the species.
- **Distribution within the plant** — the tuber/rootstock is by far the most concentrated. The 1988 USDA review notes evidence for other plant parts is inconclusive (Panter et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2527/jas1988.6692407x)); however, a 2010 outbreak implicated **immature seed heads** of *C. maculata*, which carried less cicutoxin but 9.5–22.5× more cicutol derivatives and were highly toxic on bioassay (Panter et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.toxicon.2010.11.009)). **Assume the whole plant is dangerous.**

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**There is no use case.** *Cicuta virosa* appears in this codex for four reasons, none of them therapeutic:

1. **Foraging safety.** Anyone gathering wetland Apiaceae — water parsnip, wild celery, marsh roots — is working in this plant's habitat. The cultivator's obligation is negative identification: not "is this water parsnip?" but "can I positively exclude *Cicuta*?"
2. **Disambiguation.** To hold the line against the *Conium* conflation that contaminates most popular herbal writing.
3. **Mechanistic anchor.** Cicutoxin is the codex's reference example of **GABA-A non-competitive antagonism**, useful for understanding by contrast the plants that act on the *other* side of that receptor (the sedative and anxiolytic entries).
4. **Doctrinal calibration.** It is a hard test case for the "everything is medicine at the right dose" premise. It isn't. Some plants are only ever a hazard.

**Field identification — the exclusion checklist.** Reject any marsh umbellifer showing: a **chambered, transversely-partitioned rootstock** (slice it lengthways — the horizontal air chambers are diagnostic and no edible confounder has them); **yellow-to-brownish oily juice** exuding from cut root or stem base, staining and resinous; a **sweet, parsnip-like or carroty root smell** in a plant standing in water; prominent **veins running to the leaf-tooth notches rather than the tips** (*Cicuta* runs to the notch — a genuinely useful umbellifer discriminator); umbel bracts sparse or absent. **Wear gloves. Wash hands. Do not handle then eat.** If any doubt remains at any point: leave it. There is no forageable prize in that habitat worth the downside.

**Toxicological reference figures (NOT a dosing schedule — these describe lethality):**
- A goat-model study established a lethal dose for *C. douglasii*, concluding **1–2 fresh tubers would be lethal to a goat** (Welch et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.toxicon.2020.02.010)). *Congener species — do not read this as a human figure.*
- A forensic review, in a survey of plant lethal doses, cites cicutoxin from *Cicuta virosa* as killing at approximately **500 mg** (Konopka, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5114/amsik.2020.104944)). **[USER: verify]** — this figure comes from a narrative literature review framed around a novel, not a primary toxicokinetic study; treat as order-of-magnitude only. The clinically relevant statement is the one from the *Clin Toxicol* review: **"ingestion of even a small amount of plant matter may result in severe intoxication"** (Schep et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/15563650902904332)).

**Emergency management (for information — this is hospital work, not home practice).** The *Clin Toxicol* review sets the standard: **prompt airway management and seizure control** are primary; decontamination only if early and after stabilisation; haemodialysis has been used successfully in renal failure; prognosis is good *if* aggressive supportive care arrives in time (Schep et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/15563650902904332)). A controlled goat study found **diazepam gave near-instant control of convulsions**, with complete control at 10 mg/kg, and outperformed phenobarbital — supporting benzodiazepines as first-line (Green et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.toxicon.2021.12.003)). Note the human case above nonetheless required escalation to propofol.

### Contraindications

**☠️ ABSOLUTE — THERE IS NO CONDITIONAL LIST.**

- **Contraindicated in all persons, all conditions, all doses, all routes, always.** This is not a risk–benefit statement. There is no benefit side.
- **Do not ingest, taste, chew, suck, or "test" any part.** Onset can precede any recognition that something is wrong. The Berlin paediatric case reached status epilepticus about one hour after a single bite of root ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pediatrneurol.2021.11.011)); the USDA review reports signs within **15 minutes** of a lethal dose in livestock ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.2527/jas1988.6692407x)).
- **Do not apply topically.** Cicutoxin is lipophilic; the traditional vesicant use risks systemic absorption through the blister it creates.
- **Do not handle without gloves, and do not eat, smoke, or touch the face after handling.** Wash thoroughly.
- **Keep out of forage.** Livestock deaths cluster in early spring when *Cicuta* greens up before palatable pasture and soft mud yields whole tubers to grazing.
- **Never gather wetland Apiaceae roots on positive identification alone.** Gather only on *exclusion* of *Cicuta* — and if exclusion is not certain, do not gather.
- **This entry must not be read as a preparation guide.** Its omission of dose is deliberate and permanent.

**⚠️ SUSPECTED INGESTION → EMERGENCY SERVICES NOW.** Do not wait for symptoms; do not induce vomiting (aspiration risk during imminent seizure); bring a plant sample if it can be done safely. Tell responders: *water hemlock, cicutoxin, GABA-A antagonist, expect status epilepticus, benzodiazepine-led seizure control, watch for rhabdomyolysis and renal failure.* Prognosis is good with prompt aggressive supportive care (Schep et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/15563650902904332)) — the variable is speed.
