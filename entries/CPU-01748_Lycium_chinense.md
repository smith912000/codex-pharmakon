# Lycium chinense — phang.ma, Phangma (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Lycium chinense, the only binomial the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints against Phangma, and the third of four binomials Clark 1995 prints for phang.ma (Eleagnus pungens, Lycium barbarium, Lycium chinense, Leonurus heterophyllus Sweet); the glossary's English name set against the same place by position is "Motherwort"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and on a binomial that is one of four candidates.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk for *Lycium chinense* found nothing. The genus *Lycium* occurs in Dioscorides only in the translators' glosses, where *Lycium europaeum* is suggested for the chapter on lycium (`Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 4496 and 4497; see I_1125) and where lycium is named as a comparison for the balsam tree (line 3708), and in the Sushruta botanical appendix as a bare "Lycium" (`Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`, line 31606). None is read as a mention of this drug. The Shennong overview files on disk list a *gou qi* "Lycium/Goji Berry" (`SBJ_Overview.md`, line 68), which belongs to the Chinese entry I_596 and is not read here. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` this row (line 337) is keyed to the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary line 5620, where *Lycium chinense* is the only binomial printed at that place, with the note "Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". The key is not the first name of Clark's line. Clark's own row for *phang.ma* (names.csv line 187, Clark line 1097) is keyed to the first of four names, *Eleagnus pungens*, and is the subject of I_1271. In Clark's line *Lycium chinense* is the **third** of the four binomials, after *Eleagnus pungens* and *Lycium barbarium* (Clark's spelling) and before *Leonurus heterophyllus* Sweet. The row key here is therefore a later name in Clark's line and the only name in the glossary's.

**Identity doubt.** The four names are of three genera as the Codex understands them: an oleaster (*Eleagnus*), a boxthorn (*Lycium*, a nightshade-family genus) and a motherwort (*Leonurus*, a mint-family genus); none was checked against a flora. The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary is itself divided: its scientific column prints *Lycium chinense* against Phangma, but the English name set against the same place by position is "Motherwort" (see below), which is the usual English name of *Leonurus*, Clark's fourth name. The Codex cannot say which plant the Tibetan texts meant. The entry stands for the Tibetan drug. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1097).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry stands between *mdzo.mo* (*Caragana jubata*, line 1096) and *srad.dkar.wa* (a type of gum tragacanth, line 1098). It reads:

"phang.ma: Eleagnus pungens: Lycium barbarium: Lycium chinense: Leonurus heterophyllus Sweet: [has thin leaves and a pale trunk from which the branches protrude horizontally. It has a sweet-tasting reddish-brown] seed, [which is of two types, black and white, and] cures heart fevers and gynecological disorders."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the thin leaves, the pale trunk with horizontal branches, the sweet taste and reddish-brown colour, and the two types, black and white. Outside them (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the four identifications, which are Clark's and not the root text's, the word "seed" and the clause "cures heart fevers and gynecological disorders". Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance". The part used, as Clark prints it, is the seed. The line gives no preparation. Clark's name index lists *phang.ma* (line 1717).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text on "Phangma" (line 3458).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428). The sentence, which ends a paragraph, reads: "The fruit of Phangma cures cardiac hot disorder and gynecological disorders." The index lists "Phangma 210" (line 7467). Clark's "seed" and "heart fevers" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "fruit" and "cardiac hot disorder" are the two translators' wordings; the part named differs (seed, fruit) and the Codex does not treat the two words as one. The gynecological disorders agree. The sentence gives no Latin name, no taste and no preparation. The Codex reads "Phangma" as the same name as *phang.ma* from the spelling, the agreement of the actions and the run of neighbouring names. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "P" block, lines 5576 to 5670).* The glossary prints this drug as a short run of three lines in turn: "Phangma" (line 5616), "phang ma" (line 5618) and "Lycium chinense" (line 5620). The row before it pairs "Pema gesar" with "Bombax ceiba L." (lines 5612 and 5614) and the row after it begins "Phowari" (line 5622), with "Piper nigrum" (line 5626). The English names for these places are one line (5661): "Tibetan lancea Silk-cotton tree Motherwort Black pepper Wormwood ashes". Counted in order from the row for "Payak" (*Lancea tibetica*, line 5610), the third English name, "Motherwort", falls on Phangma, the fourth, "Black pepper", on Phowari. The glossary's scientific column and its English column therefore point at different plants for this drug, and the English column agrees with Clark's fourth name. The English pairing is by position, not printed in one row. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** The same Tibetan drug and the same Clark line (1097), Men-Tsee-Khang running text (3458) and glossary line (5620) are treated in I_1271 (*Eleagnus pungens*, tag FMT), which takes Clark's first name. The two entries are one drug under two binomials, the first and the third of Clark's four; they are not merged, and each says so. I_596 (Goji, *Lycium barbarum*, tags SBJ and FMT) and I_116 (Goji, *Lycium barbarum*, tags SBJ, FMT and CM) carry an FMT paragraph that cites no Clark or Men-Tsee-Khang line; *Lycium barbarium*, as Clark prints it, is the second binomial of this same *phang.ma* line (1097), the only Clark line in which any *Lycium* occurs. Neither Goji entry names the name *phang.ma* (a search of I_116 for "phang" found nothing, and I_596 gives a different Tibetan name, "Dre-tsher-ma", in its title line). I_1125 is *Lycium europaeum* (Dioscorides) and does not concern the Tibetan texts. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Lycium* is a genus of the nightshade family (Solanaceae), whose berries are the goji of commerce, that *Eleagnus* belongs to the oleaster family and *Leonurus* to the mint family, and that these are unrelated families. Which of the four the Tibetan texts mean, and whether "seed" or "fruit" is the part, is not settled by the lines read, so no statement about this drug's constituents is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "cures heart fevers and gynecological disorders" (Clark, line 1097) and whose fruit "cures cardiac hot disorder and gynecological disorders" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3458). These are the texts' claims in their own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and does not translate "heart fevers" or "hot disorder" into modern diagnoses. Heart and gynecological complaints need medical assessment.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give the seed (Clark) or the fruit (Men-Tsee-Khang) as the part and no preparation, quantity, frequency or duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 1097) prints "a sweet-tasting reddish-brown seed". No power and no post-digestive taste is printed. Men-Tsee-Khang prints no taste or power for the name in the lines read. The actions printed are curing heart fevers and gynecological disorders (Clark) and curing cardiac hot disorder and gynecological disorders (Men-Tsee-Khang).

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: its seed cures heart fevers and gynecological disorders, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says its fruit cures cardiac hot disorder and gynecological disorders. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the chapter 20 lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes, and not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23 in both editions); no line read places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name, not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a heart-fever and gynecological drug in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and the plant behind the Tibetan name is uncertain between four candidates. The abstentive answer is the honest one; the goji berry's reputation as a tonic is not a Tibetan claim for this name in the lines read.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Heart complaints and gynecological disorders need medical assessment; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Because the identity is uncertain, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read, and the drug is recorded for gynecological disorders, so pregnancy is a question this entry cannot answer. [USER: verify]
