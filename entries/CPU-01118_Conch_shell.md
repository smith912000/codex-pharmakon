### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The conch is in the materia medica chapter of the Explanatory Tantra (ch.20, "Efficacy of Medicinal Substances"), in Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). The chapter's Specific Section says that the powers of individual natural substances are set out in eight sections: "(1) precious [metal] medicines (2) stone medicines (3) earth medicines (4) tree medicines (5) resins (6) plants (7) herbs (8) animal products" (line 802). The conch stands in the first section, "The Powers of Precious [Metals]" (line 803), after pearl and mother of pearl and before coral and lapis lazuli (line 815). So the Tibetan scheme counts conch shell among the precious medicines, not among the animal products; the Codex shelves it in Volume III as the shell of a sea animal, which the text does not itself say.

The line reads (line 815): "Conch shell: [conch shell has five names and is of superior and ordinary types. The superior type, g-yas.hkhyil (lit., 'right clockwise swirl'), incarnates five times as a conch and is lily white, with a clockwise swirl. Another superior type, gzi.dung, is red in color. The ordinary types are recognized as having an anti-clockwise swirl, and there is also a 'thorny' conch, tsher.ma.chan. All four types] dry pus, puncture whorls [of pus, etc.], cure bone fever [and are beneficial for the eyes]." Clark states his convention in the preface (line 82): text in square brackets is taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, and text in parentheses from the oral instructions of Dr. Yeshe Donden and Dr. Pema Dorje. So what is bare in this line, the actions "dry pus, puncture whorls, cure bone fever", is the translation of the root tantra, and the five names, the grades and the eye benefit are commentary. The word "whorls" is Clark's; the bracketed "[of pus, etc.]" is a commentary gloss, and I do not elaborate on what a pus whorl is. [USER: verify]

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`) gives the same chapter in different words (lines 3330-3333). It first lists the eight categories of natural substances, beginning with "Rinpoche men (precious medicine)" (line 3324), and then says of the precious ones: "Pearl stops cerebrospinal fluid discharge and acts as an antidote against poison. Shells has the same potency as that of pearl. Conch dries up pus, breaks swollen abscesses and cures hot disorders of the bone. Coral cures hot disorders of the liver, neuritis and fever associated with poisoning. Lapis lazuli cures poisoning, disorders of the chhuser and leprosy." Where Clark has "puncture whorls", Men-Tsee-Khang has "breaks swollen abscesses"; both translations agree on drying pus and on bone disorder (Clark: "bone fever"; Men-Tsee-Khang: "hot disorders of the bone"). The Men-Tsee-Khang text prints no grades of conch, no names and no eye use. The same text opens its list of precious medicines with gold, which it says "promotes longevity, is rejuvenating"; no such word is printed for the conch.

The conch also appears in both texts as a figure and not as a substance. In the passage on the physician's speech, the physician should "pronounce such in the manner of blowing a conch in the market place", openly (Clark line 1489; Men-Tsee-Khang line 4663: "declare it openly like blowing a conch in the marketplace"). That is a simile for plain speech, not a medicinal or ritual use of the shell.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, a conch shell is almost entirely calcium carbonate (as aragonite and calcite) in a small proteinaceous matrix, with trace elements. A powder of the shell is a calcium carbonate material. No quantities are given here because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text records conch shell as a precious-class medicine that dries pus (a drying action on suppuration), "punctures whorls" or "breaks swollen abscesses", and cures bone fever or hot disorders of the bone, with a commentary note that it helps the eyes. The Codex does not recommend taking powdered shell for these complaints. It records no compounded formula in the lines read, no preparation, and no processing; shells of unknown origin may carry contaminants, and the Tibetan tradition itself prepares its mineral medicines in ways this Codex does not reproduce.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives no preparation for the conch in the lines read: there is no calcining, no grinding, no vehicle and no compound. The materia medica names it among the single substances and lists its actions only. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Calcium carbonate is an antacid and a calcium supplement, and is the main component of shells. Nothing in that supports drying pus or curing bone fever. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** The materia medica chapter opens (lines 796-797) with the eight powers (heavy, oily, cool, blunt, light, coarse, hot, sharp), the seventeen secondary qualities, and the principle that hot-powered medicines cure cold disorders and cool-powered ones cure hot diseases. For the conch, however, Clark's line 815 prints no taste, no power and no post-digestive taste; it prints only the actions (dries pus, punctures whorls, cures bone fever). The Men-Tsee-Khang text prints "hot disorders of the bone" as a complaint treated, which is a statement of the disease and not of the shell's own quality. No quality of the conch itself is printed in the lines read, and none is supplied.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The conch is attested in the Explanatory Tantra ch.20 as a precious-class medicine, with only therapeutic actions printed (Clark line 815; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3333). The Tibetan frame is that the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) arise from the three poisons (attachment, aversion, delusion), so that pathology is at root a spiritual condition, and that medicine is the third of four treatment branches: it supports practice and does not reach the root. Within that frame the conch is a medicine of the third branch. No *bcud len* (essence extraction) use of the conch was found: a search of Clark and of the Men-Tsee-Khang text for "conch" returned only the materia medica line above, the simile of the physician's speech, and the Men-Tsee-Khang index. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted. The commentary's remark that the superior kind "incarnates five times as a conch" is a statement about the grade of the shell in a commentary; the lines read do not say what the "incarnation" means or that it confers a status in practice, and none is claimed. ### Contraindications

- **Contamination:** shell from unknown sources, or powdered shell not prepared to a recognised standard, can carry heavy metals and microbes; the Codex does not recommend taking shell material.
- **Calcium load:** large amounts of calcium carbonate can cause constipation, raise blood calcium, and add to kidney-stone risk; they interfere with the absorption of some medicines.
- **Not a wound dressing:** the "drying pus" and "breaking abscesses" actions are historical statements; a swollen abscess needs medical drainage and care.
- **Conservation and law:** some conch species are subject to harvest limits and trade controls (for example under CITES); shells should not be taken from living animals or from protected populations.
- **Cultural status:** the conch, and especially the right-turning kind, is a sacred object in Tibetan and other traditions; it is not an ingredient to be taken casually.
