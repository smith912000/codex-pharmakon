# I-486 | Oregano (*Origanum vulgare* L.)

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Origanum vulgare* L.  
**Family:** Lamiaceae (Mint family)  
**Common names:** Oregano, wild marjoram, pot marjoram, pizza herb, organy  
**Synonyms:** Multiple subspecies with distinct chemistry: *O. vulgare* subsp. *vulgare* (low phenol; common in northern Europe); *O. vulgare* subsp. *hirtum* (Link) Ietsw. (Greek oregano; high carvacrol + thymol; primary medicinal use); *O. vulgare* subsp. *virens* (Hoffmanns. & Link) Ietsw. (Iberian oregano); *O. compactum* Benth. (Moroccan oregano; very high carvacrol)

**Etymology:** *Origanum* from Greek *oros* ("mountain") + *ganos* ("brightness, joy") — "joy of the mountain" — the herb that brightens mountain slopes with its aromatic presence. Alternatively, from *orein* ("to grow on hills") + *ganos*. The "joy of the mountain" naming encodes both the plant's habitat (mountainous, rocky, well-drained Mediterranean slopes) and its sensory character (the heady aromatic intensity of dried oregano in sunshine). *vulgare* = "common."

**CRITICAL species/subspecies distinction — the "oregano" problem:**
The name "oregano" encompasses dramatically different plants with different chemistry:
- *O. vulgare* subsp. *vulgare* (northern Europe, UK): very low EO content; minimal thymol/carvacrol; essentially not medicinal as an antimicrobial agent
- *O. vulgare* subsp. *hirtum* (Greece, Turkey): 60–85% carvacrol + thymol in EO; strong antimicrobial; the medicinal oregano
- *Thymus capitatus* (Spanish oregano; head thyme): sometimes sold as oregano; carvacrol-dominant; similar profile to Greek oregano
- *Lippia graveolens* (Mexican oregano): entirely different genus (Verbenaceae); distinct chemistry; not botanically oregano

Commercial "oregano oil" may derive from any of these sources. The profound antimicrobial evidence is specific to high-carvacrol preparations (primarily Greek/Turkish subsp. *hirtum* or *O. compactum*). Low-carvacrol European oregano used in cooking provides flavor but minimal antimicrobial activity.

**Botanical description (*O. vulgare*):** Aromatic perennial, 20–80 cm; stems erect, often purple-tinged; leaves opposite, ovate, hairy, toothed or entire; small white to pink-purple flowers in branching paniculate inflorescences; calyx 5-lobed (not 2-lipped as in other Lamiaceae — diagnostic). Mediterranean/Asian native; naturalized in Europe, Americas.

**Part used:** Aerial parts (leaves and flowering tops); essential oil  
**Preparation:** Dried herb culinary; infusion; tincture; essential oil (high-carvacrol preparation — "oil of oregano")

## 3. Convergence Analysis

**Ancient Mediterranean antimicrobial consensus — carvacrol before chemistry:** DMM, CM, EP, and AT converge on oregano for antiseptic/wound care and digestive/respiratory conditions — exactly the applications predicted by carvacrol's membrane-disrupting antimicrobial mechanism. This is the same convergence pattern as thyme (I-482), reflecting that thyme and oregano share carvacrol/thymol chemistry and were used interchangeably or complementarily in Mediterranean traditions. The Greek word *origanon* distinguished from *thymian* (thyme) but the applications overlap substantially.

**Egyptian tomb documentation — botanical archaeology:** Botanical remains from Egyptian tombs (New Kingdom, ~1500–1000 BCE) include identifiable *Origanum* plant material — aromatic herbs placed in tombs for their preservative (antimicrobial) properties. The use of oregano (and thyme, hyssop) as tomb preservatives is consistent with their antimicrobial activity; the same mechanisms that prevent bacterial spoilage of food also slow decomposition of organic matter in burial contexts. This archaeological evidence provides independent validation of the EP's written documentation.

**Pizzaiolo and physician — culinary perpetuation of antimicrobial folk medicine:** Pizza's tomato sauce is virtually always seasoned with oregano (along with basil). This culinary association (oregano → pizza → Mediterranean diet) has perpetuated global oregano exposure long after traditional medicinal use declined. Whether the association of oregano with preserved/fermented tomato products was originally antimicrobial in function (oregano preserving tomato preparations from spoilage) or purely gustatory is debated but pharmacologically interesting: carvacrol does extend the shelf life of tomato-based foods.

## 4. Phytochemical Composition

### Essential Oil (high-carvacrol subspecies: 0.5–5% aerial parts)
- **Carvacrol** (5-isopropyl-2-methylphenol; 40–85% EO in *hirtum*; primary antimicrobial; TRPV1 agonist; NF-κB inhibitor; gut barrier protective; anti-cancer)
- **Thymol** (0–30% EO; isomer of carvacrol; same antimicrobial mechanism; less dominant in oregano vs thyme)
- **p-Cymene** (10–30% EO; carvacrol/thymol precursor; synergistic membrane penetration enhancer)
- **γ-Terpinene** (5–15%; antioxidant; synergistic)
- **β-Caryophyllene** (sesquiterpene; CB2 partial agonist; anti-inflammatory)
- **Linalool** (trace–5%; anxiolytic)
- **Terpinen-4-ol** (antimicrobial; same as in tea tree)

**Subspecies chemistry comparison:**
- *hirtum* (Greek): carvacrol 60–85%  
- *vulgare* (European): linalool + sabinene dominant; carvacrol <5% → negligible antimicrobial
- *O. compactum* (Moroccan): carvacrol 70–85%

### Phenolic Acids and Polyphenols
- **Rosmarinic acid** (Lamiaceae family; anti-inflammatory; GABA-T inhibitor; antiviral)
- **Caffeic acid** (anti-inflammatory)
- **Lithospermic acid** (high antioxidant; estrogenic-like; anti-thrombotic)

### Flavonoids
- **Luteolin** (COX/LOX; anti-inflammatory)
- **Apigenin** (GABA-A; anti-inflammatory)
- **Quercetin** (multi-mechanism anti-inflammatory; antiviral)
- **Naringenin** (antiviral; anti-inflammatory)
- **Eriodictyol** (antioxidant; anti-inflammatory)
- **Hispidulin** (GABA-A PAM; anticonvulsant; anti-tumor)

### Triterpenoids
- **Ursolic acid** (anti-inflammatory; adaptogenic; anti-tumor)
- **Oleanolic acid** (hepatoprotective; anti-inflammatory)

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

### Antimicrobial (Carvacrol — primary; same mechanism as thyme)
**Membrane disruption (identical mechanism to thymol in thyme):**
- Carvacrol integrates into bacterial/fungal phospholipid bilayer via phenolic OH group interaction
- Disrupts membrane potential → increases K⁺ and ATP efflux → impairs cellular homeostasis → bactericidal
- MIC values: *S. aureus* 0.1–0.5 mg/mL; *E. coli* 0.125–0.5 mg/mL; *L. monocytogenes* 0.1–0.25 mg/mL; *P. aeruginosa* 0.25–1 mg/mL; *C. albicans* 0.03–0.25 mg/mL

**Anti-MRSA and anti-biofilm:**
- Carvacrol: active against MRSA (methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*) MIC 0.1–0.25 mg/mL
- Biofilm disruption: eradicates pre-formed biofilms of *Staphylococcus*, *E. coli*, and oral *Streptococcus* at 2–4× MIC
- Synergistic with conventional antibiotics: carvacrol + oxacillin against MRSA shows synergy (FIC index <0.5)

**Gut antimicrobial:**
- *Clostridium difficile* inhibition: carvacrol MIC 0.5 mg/mL (clinically relevant for C. diff overgrowth)
- *Helicobacter pylori*: carvacrol MIC 0.25–1 mg/mL (relevant for peptic ulcer context)
- SIBO (small intestinal bacterial overgrowth): herbal antimicrobial protocols using high-carvacrol oregano oil

### Gut Barrier Protective (Carvacrol)
- Carvacrol activates TRPV1 in gut epithelium → increases tight junction protein expression (ZO-1, occludin, claudin-1)
- Reduced gut permeability ("leaky gut") → reduced lipopolysaccharide (LPS) translocation → reduced systemic inflammation
- Verified in intestinal epithelial cell models and in vivo rodent models

### Anti-inflammatory (Carvacrol + Polyphenols)
- **Carvacrol:** NF-κB inhibition via p65 subunit → ↓IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, COX-2; iNOS suppression
- **Rosmarinic acid:** COX-2, 5-LOX inhibition; NF-κB inhibition
- **Luteolin, quercetin:** COX/LOX dual inhibition
- **β-Caryophyllene:** CB2 partial agonism → peripheral anti-inflammatory

### Antifungal (Carvacrol — most potent natural antifungal)
- Carvacrol against *Candida albicans*: MIC 0.03–0.25 mg/mL (among lowest MIC of any natural compound)
- Mechanism: ergosterol disruption (same as amphotericin B target but different mechanism); disrupts hyphae-to-yeast transition (virulence morphotype inhibition)
- Active against fluconazole-resistant *Candida* strains
- Clinical application: intestinal candida overgrowth protocols; topical tinea pedis

### Antiparasitic
- Carvacrol: activity against *Giardia lamblia*, *Blastocystis hominis* *in vitro*
- Mechanism: cystocide activity (disrupts protozoan membrane)
- Support for traditional use in intestinal parasites across multiple cultures

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**Antimicrobial / gastrointestinal infections:**
- Chedid et al. 2014 (*Global Advances in Health and Medicine*): Emulsified oregano oil (200 mg with 200 mg of other herbal antimicrobials BID × 4 weeks) in SIBO patients: 46% elimination rate (comparable to rifaximin control arm) — Level A (this was a mixed formula; oregano-specific attribution uncertain)
- Bures et al. 2015: high-carvacrol oregano preparation in *H. pylori* eradication (pilot, n=14): some *H. pylori* clearance — Level B

**Anti-candida:**
- In vitro data: strong carvacrol MIC data — Level D
- No formal RCT for oregano in clinical candidiasis
- Functional medicine protocols use high-carvacrol oregano oil for intestinal candida; clinical trial evidence lacking

**Anti-inflammatory:**
- No clinical RCTs for oregano as anti-inflammatory specifically
- Mediterranean diet studies show oregano-including diets reduce inflammatory markers — Level B (diet, not oregano specifically)

**Summary by evidence level:**
- Level A: SIBO (combination formula including oregano oil; Chedid 2014)
- Level B: *H. pylori* pilot; Mediterranean diet association
- Level C: Traditional use across 5 traditions; EP documented archaeological evidence
- Level D: Extensive antimicrobial (MRSA, Candida, C. diff, H. pylori, Giardia); anti-inflammatory; gut barrier

## 7. Phenomenological Record

**Joy of the mountain — ecosystem pharmacology:** *Origanum vulgare* grows in abundance on calcareous, rocky mountain slopes throughout the Mediterranean — the thin, well-drained, alkaline soils that stress other plants are oregano's preferred habitat. The stress physiology of mountain oregano (UV radiation, temperature extremes, drought, poor soil) upregulates EO production — the carvacrol and thymol are defensive compounds produced under environmental stress. The mountain habitat that "joys" the plant (oros + ganos) also concentrates the pharmacological compounds. The richest medicinal oregano comes from the most stressed populations.

**The pizza paradox — most consumed medicinal herb globally:** Oregano may be the most widely consumed medicinal plant in the world by volume — not because of medicinal applications but because of pizza. Oregano is the defining herb of pizza (derived from Neapolitan *pizzaiolo* tradition, 19th century), and pizza is now one of the most globally consumed foods. If the average pizza has 0.5 g of oregano (mostly *vulgare* subsp. *vulgare*, low-carvacrol), then global pizza consumption delivers roughly millions of kilograms of oregano annually. The pharmacological exposure from pizza oregano is minimal (wrong subspecies, heating destroys EO), but the cultural penetration is extraordinary.

**SIBO and herbal antimicrobials — emerging clinical territory:** Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) is increasingly recognized as a clinical entity driving IBS symptoms, nutritional malabsorption, and systemic inflammation. Standard treatment is rifaximin (non-absorbable antibiotic). The Chedid 2014 paper comparing oregano oil-containing herbal formula to rifaximin in SIBO represents a collision between traditional antimicrobial herbalism and evidence-based functional medicine — with results suggesting herbal antimicrobials may be clinically competitive. Whether this holds in larger trials remains to be determined.

**Greek vs. European oregano — a quality failure encoded in etymology:** The Greek oregano (*O. vulgare* subsp. *hirtum*) that gave the world its pharmacological and culinary character is fundamentally different from the bland northern European common oregano (*subsp. vulgare*). When Greek and Italian immigrants brought oregano to northern Europe and North America, they attempted to recreate Mediterranean flavors with locally available *vulgare*-type plants — which had negligible carvacrol and therefore neither the flavor nor the pharmacology of the Mediterranean original. The "oregano" that became commercially dominant in northern markets (dried *vulgare* subsp. *vulgare*) is a shadow of the original. The medicinal "oil of oregano" market's success may partly reflect consumers' subconscious recognition that the standard commercial herb lacked something the label promised.

## 8. Practical Application

**Energetic tier:** ◉ Qi (antimicrobial; gut; anti-inflammatory; immune functional)

### Preparation Methods and Dosing

**CRITICAL — Species/subspecies specification:**
All medicinal applications require HIGH-CARVACROL oregano:
- *O. vulgare* subsp. *hirtum* (Greek oregano) OR
- *O. compactum* (Moroccan oregano) OR
- Commercial "oil of oregano" with documented carvacrol content ≥60%

**Oil of oregano (primary antimicrobial application):**
- Standard commercial: 600–900 mg high-carvacrol oregano oil per day (divided 2–3 doses)
- Dilute with olive oil or take in capsule form — neat oil irritating to oral mucosa
- With food; take 3–4 hours away from probiotics (antimicrobial activity will kill probiotic organisms)
- SIBO protocol: 200 mg oregano oil + complementary botanicals (berberine, garlic); 4–8 weeks
- Duration: maximum 4–6 weeks continuous; take 2-week break; re-evaluate

**Dried herb infusion (digestive, anti-inflammatory — milder):**
- 2–4 g dried Greek/Turkish oregano per 250 mL; steep 10 min; 3 cups daily
- Less antimicrobial than EO but gentler; appropriate for ongoing digestive support

**Tincture (1:5, 60% ethanol):**
- 3–5 mL, 3× daily (high-ethanol preserves carvacrol)

**Culinary use (preventive low-dose):**
- High-quality Greek/Turkish dried oregano in cooking (1–3 g per meal): provides ongoing carvacrol exposure
- Best preserved in dried form (carvacrol more stable than linalool-type volatiles)

### Timing Considerations
- **Probiotics:** separate from oregano oil by minimum 4 hours (ideally 8 hours or bedtime/morning schedule)
- **During antimicrobial courses:** if using for SIBO or infection clearance, probiotics given after course is complete, not during

### Drug Interactions

- **Anticoagulants:** Carvacrol antiplatelet activity; monitor INR
- **Diabetes medications:** Rosmarinic acid mild hypoglycemic effect; monitor blood glucose
- **Iron supplements:** Rosmarinic acid chelation; separate by 2 hours

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy:** Carvacrol and thymol uterotonic potential at high medicinal doses; culinary use safe; avoid medicinal EO doses
- **Known allergy to Lamiaceae** (cross-reactivity with thyme, sage, rosemary)
- **Bleeding disorders:** Anti-platelet carvacrol/thymol
- **Active antibiotic treatment for specific infections:** Discuss with physician — herbal antimicrobials may interfere with clinical assessment of antibiotic response

### Quality Markers

- Carvacrol + thymol combined ≥60% EO (HPLC; GC-MS) — mandatory for medicinal antimicrobial application
- Species: *O. vulgare* subsp. *hirtum* or *O. compactum* explicitly stated
- NOT *O. vulgare* subsp. *vulgare* (northern European; low carvacrol; not medicinal as antimicrobial)
- Fragrance: intensely pungent, sharp, warming (Greek oregano) vs. mild green (European oregano = wrong subspecies)
