# Malva verticillata — lcham.pa, Champa, nyi.dgah (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Malva verticillata Linn, the first binomial Clark 1995 prints for lcham.pa, after the English "a type of Mallow" and before Dalbergia lanceolaria; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints Malva botka, not this binomial, against Champa* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and chiefly on one line of one of them.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. Only Clark prints *Malva verticillata* (lines 1092 and 1223, and the Latin-name index, line 1614). A search of Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 for "verticillata" found nothing. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk for *Malva verticillata* found nothing; for the epithet *verticillata* alone it found only Dioscorides' translator's gloss *Convallaria verticillata* in the Solomon's seal chapter (`Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, line 8050), a different plant. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 182) the Tibetan name *lcham.pa* is recorded at Clark line 1092 with the identification "a type of Mallow; Malva verticillata Linn; Dalbergia lanceolaria" and the note "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The key, *Malva verticillata*, is therefore the **first of the two binomials** Clark prints, after the English phrase "a type of Mallow", and not a later one. The second, *Dalbergia lanceolaria*, has no row on the spine as far as the files read show. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" and that "sometimes the same name refers to different plants growing in unrelated geographical locations".

**Identity doubt.** The two binomials are of different families as the Codex understands it (a mallow, and a legume tree genus; not checked against a flora), and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary names a third, *Malva botka*, against the same Tibetan drug (I_1313). Both editions say "mallow" in English. The Codex cannot say which plant the Tibetan texts meant. The entry stands for the Tibetan drug. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1092).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry stands between *btsod* (*Rubia cordifolia*, line 1091) and *brag.lcham* (*Bergenia ciliata*, line 1093). It reads:

"lcham.pa: a type of Mallow: Malva verticillata Linn: Dalbergia lanceolaria: [has six names, for example nyi.dgah (name of the neuter type) and phor.mdog ldan. The male type is known as ha.mdog.ldan . It has a long petiole, blue leaf and white or pale brown flower. The female type is known as rgya.lcham.pa and has a round leaf, thin, long petiole and white or pale brown flower. Nyi.dgah, the neuter type, is also called a.dza.ka, i.e. the former name refers to the leaf and the latter one to the seed. It has sweet and astringent tastes], cures urinary retention, diarrhea, [kidney fever, quenches] thirst [and dries wound pus. The male type, or ha.lchi.me.tog (flower), halts nocturnal emission, whilst the root, known as pa.la, is beneficial in consumption and anorexia.]"

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the six names, the three types (male, female, neuter) with their leaves, petioles and flowers, the sweet and astringent tastes, kidney fever, quenching of thirst (the verb only), the drying of wound pus, the male type's flower and its use against nocturnal emission, and the root *pa.la* for consumption and anorexia. Outside them (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the English phrase and the two binomials, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the clauses "cures urinary retention, diarrhea" and the noun "thirst". The line names three parts: a leaf and a seed (for the neuter type's two names), the flower of the male type, and the root. It gives no preparation.

*Clark 1995, chapter 21 (line 1223).* The chapter on compounding (heading at line 1205) lists classes of medicines, and the line reads: "In the category of oedema medicines are included lake salt, rock salt, golden sand, crab, Smaller Cardamon and (a type of) Mallow leaf (Malva verticillata)." Here the binomial is attached to a part, the leaf. The Latin-name index lists *Malva verticillata* with "Mallow" and "Mallow leaf" (line 1614), and the Tibetan name index lists *rgya.lcham.pa* (line 1683) and, spelled "lchampa", the main name (line 1687). A third place, in the diet material, has "Stew of mallow and Plantago depressa increases digestive heat and stops diarrhoea" (line 698), with an English word and no binomial; the Codex records the likeness to the Men-Tsee-Khang diet sentence below and does not equate the two names.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428). Line 3456 reads: "Champa clears obstruction of urine, relieves thirst and stops diarrhea." The index lists "Champa 170, 210" (line 7290). Page 170 is the diet passage (line 3046): "Cooked Champa and Baekhur generate heat and stop diarrhea." A separate sentence names "Nyiga" in a list of diuretics (line 3652, page 222; index line 7454): "Gya tsha, Gyam tsha, Sergi Jema, Digsin, Sugmel and Nyiga are the group of medicines that are diuretics." Clark's "urinary retention", "diarrhea" and "thirst" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "obstruction of urine", "diarrhea" and "thirst" agree in kind; they are two translators' wordings. The Codex reads "Champa" as *lcham.pa* from the spelling, the agreement of the three actions and the glossary's English name "Blue mallow" set by position against it, and reads "Nyiga" as Clark's *nyi.dgah*, the neuter type, from the transliteration "nyi dga'" (glossary line 5546). [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary.* The glossary sets *Malva botka* against "Champa" (the "C" block, scientific name at line 5143, English "Blue mallow") and against "Nyiga" (the "N" block, scientific name at line 5564, English "Marsh mallow"), both by column position; the counts are set out in I_1313. It prints no *Malva verticillata*.

**Overlap.** I_1313 (*Malva botka*, tag FMT) treats the same Tibetan drug, the same Men-Tsee-Khang sentences and the glossary binomial, which the glossary prints at two places; the two entries are one drug under two binomials, Clark's and the glossary's, and they are not merged. A search of the entries folder for *Malva verticillata*, *lcham.pa*, "Champa" and "Nyiga" found no other entry on this drug. I_460 (Common mallow, *Malva sylvestris*, tags EP, DMM, CM, TCM, SS) and I_703 (Dwarf mallow, *Malva neglecta*, tags EP, AT, DMM, CM) are mallows of other species from other traditions and do not cite the Tibetan texts. *Dalbergia lanceolaria* has no entry. I_1242 (*Bergenia ciliata*) and I_1273 (*Epimedium grandiflorum*) are the neighbouring Clark entries *brag.lcham* and *rta.rmig* (lines 1093 and 1094) and are not this drug. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Malva* is a genus of the mallow family (Malvaceae), whose leaves and flowers are characteristically mucilaginous, and that *Dalbergia* is a genus of legume trees in another family. Which plant the Tibetan texts mean is not settled by the lines read, so no statement about this drug's constituents is made. Clark's commentary prints the tastes "sweet and astringent".

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "cures urinary retention, diarrhea" and thirst (Clark, line 1092), whose leaf is listed among oedema medicines (Clark, line 1223), and which Men-Tsee-Khang says "clears obstruction of urine, relieves thirst and stops diarrhea" (line 3456). These are the texts' claims in their own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and does not translate "kidney fever" or "nocturnal emission" into modern diagnoses. Urinary retention, oedema and diarrhea need medical assessment.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read name parts, not preparations: the leaf (Clark, line 1223), the seed and leaf as sources of the neuter type's two names, the flower of the male type, and the root *pa.la* (line 1092), and a cooked leaf vegetable for "Champa" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3046). No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary (line 1092) prints "sweet and astringent tastes". No power and no post-digestive taste is printed. Men-Tsee-Khang prints no taste or power for "Champa" in the lines read; the cooked vegetable is said to "generate heat" (line 3046). The actions printed are curing urinary retention and diarrhea, and thirst (Clark, root-text layer), with kidney fever, wound pus, nocturnal emission, consumption and anorexia in the commentary; and, in chapter 21, a place among oedema medicines.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it cures urinary retention, diarrhea and thirst, and its commentary gives the root for consumption and anorexia. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes, and not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23 in both editions); "consumption" in Clark's commentary is a disease name and not a practice, and no line read places the drug in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name, not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a urinary, diarrhea and thirst drug in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and the plant behind the Tibetan name is uncertain. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Urinary retention, oedema and diarrhea need medical assessment; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Because the identity is uncertain, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
