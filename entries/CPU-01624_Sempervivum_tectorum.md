# Sempervivum tectorum — Aeizoon to mega (Dioscorides 4-89)

## *Sempervivum tectorum [Linnaeus], "Houseleek, Hens and Chickens, Jupiter's Beard, Roof Houseleek", the last of the names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-89 ("Sedum maius [Fuchs], Sedum majus vulgare [Bauhin], Sempervivum tectorum [Linnaeus]")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (lines 8831 and 8832) prints three names with their namers, the last being Linnaeus, and the row takes the last. It was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-89. AEIZOON TO MEGA* ("the great aizoon"), heading at line 8827, a plate caption "Sempervivum tectorum after FAGUET — 1888" at line 8829, the SUGGESTED line at 8831 and 8832, a translators' note at 8833 ("The Greeks gave the name aizoon to sempervivum [Loudon]."), and the text at lines 8834 and 8835. It stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), before *4-90. AEIZOON TO MIXRON* (heading at line 8836). The translators' index gives *Aeizoon to mega* at page 640 (line 11975).

The whole chapter reads: "The great aizoon is called this because of its evergreen leaves. It sends out stalks a foot long or rather more, the thickness of a big finger, fat, very flourishing, with in-cuts (as it were) like the Characian tithymal [4-165a]. The leaves are fat, the size of a big finger, similar to a tongue towards the top, those below bending downwards, but those around the head set together one to another, describing an eye-like circle. It grows in hilly and tilled places. Some plant it on their houses. It is cooling and astringent; the leaves (applied by themselves or with polenta) are good for erysipela, herpes [viral skin infection], and gangrenous ulceration of the cheeks, inflammation of the eyes, burns, and gout in the feet. The juice is poured on with polenta and rosaceum [1-53] for headaches, and it is given in drink to those bitten by harvest spiders, those with diarrhoea, and for dysentery. Taken as a drink with wine it draws out roundworms, and in a pessary it stops women's excessive discharges [menstrual flow]. The juice is effective rubbed on weak eyes, by reason of blood. It is also called aeithales, ambrosion, chrysospermon, zoophthalmon, bouophthalmum, stergethron, aeonion aichryson, holochryson, chrysanthemom, protogonom, boros, or notios; the Magi call it paronychia, some, chrysitis, the Romans, ceriacuspia, some, Jovis caulis, leapetes, or sedum majus, and the Egyptians, pamphanes."

The chapter gives the reason for the name (the leaves stay green), a description (fat stalks over a foot long with in-cuts like the Characian tithymal, fat finger-sized leaves tongue-shaped towards the top, those at the head forming "an eye-like circle"), the place of growth (hilly and tilled places) and the remark "Some plant it on their houses". It then gives its quality, "cooling and astringent", and the uses: the leaves, alone or with polenta, for erysipelas, herpes, gangrenous ulceration of the cheeks, inflamed eyes, burns and gout in the feet; the juice with polenta and rosaceum on the head for headaches; the juice drunk for harvest-spider bites, diarrhoea and dysentery; the juice in wine to draw out roundworms; a pessary to stop women's excessive discharge; and the juice rubbed on weak eyes "by reason of blood". The bracketed glosses are the translators'.

The chapter prints many other names: Greek (*aeithales*, *ambrosion*, *chrysospermon*, *zoophthalmon*, *bouophthalmum*, *stergethron*, *aeonion aichryson*, *holochryson*, *chrysanthemom*, *protogonom*, *boros*, *notios*), the Magian *paronychia*, *chrysitis* (from "some"), the Roman *ceriacuspia*, *Jovis caulis*, *leapetes* and *sedum majus*, and the Egyptian *pamphanes*. They are names only, with no belief attached. That the plant is planted on houses is a report of a practice; the text gives no reason for it. [USER: verify]

**Cross-references.** The chapter points to *4-165a* (the Characian tithymal), *1-53* (rosaceum) and nothing else. None is read here beyond the labelled notes. The chapter after it (4-90) says its plant's leaves "have the same strength as the previous one", which a reader may take as a reference to this chapter; that is the reader's reading, not the text's. The existing entry I_1058 (*Cotyledon orbiculata*) quotes parts of this chapter (the roundworms, the pessary and "cooling and astringent") while reading a neighbouring cotyledon chapter, and does not treat 4-89 as an entry of its own. No entry is duplicated.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read, and the chapter prints none. It is a general fact that *Sempervivum* belongs to the stonecrop family (Crassulaceae), whose members store water in fleshy leaves. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the translators' *Sempervivum tectorum*. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides reports a cooling, astringent plant whose leaves are applied to skin inflammations, burns and gouty feet, whose juice is applied to the head and the eyes and taken for spider bites, diarrhoea, dysentery and worms, and which is used in a pessary for women's discharge. These are the text's reports. The Codex gives no protocol for any of them, and the identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-89 gives these preparations: leaves applied by themselves or with polenta; juice poured on with polenta and rosaceum; juice given in drink; juice drunk with wine; a pessary; and juice rubbed on the eyes. It gives no quantity, frequency or duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one quality for the plant: "cooling and astringent". It prints no warming or drying quality and no taste beyond "astringent".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use; it records the Magian name *paronychia*, the Egyptian *pamphanes* and the Roman *Jovis caulis* as names only, and the remark that some plant it on their houses without a reason. The part of the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition that is present here is the detailed description of the plant (fat stalks, fat leaves shaped like a tongue, the eye-like circle at the head), its place of growth and its stated qualities. The plant is given no status beyond that of a cooling, astringent remedial herb. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It offers a careful description, a quality and a list of remedies without amounts, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. A pessary, a bite from a spider, dysentery or bleeding from the vagina need medical assessment, and none of these remedies must replace it. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no wild or garden plant should be used on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify] None of the traditional uses above is advice.
