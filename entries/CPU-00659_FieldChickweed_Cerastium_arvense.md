# Field Chickweed

## *Cerastium arvense* L. | Family: Caryophyllaceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM

**Training Phase:** Foundation — Restoration & Tissue Repair (topical / low-stakes internal)

### Origin & Tradition

Field Chickweed, also called field mouse-ear chickweed or starry mouse-ear, is a low, mat-forming perennial of the pink family, circumboreal in distribution and abundant across Eurasia and North America — dry meadows, roadsides, montane slopes, and disturbed ground. The white, notched, five-petalled flowers give the "starry" epithet; the softly hairy, opposing leaves give the "mouse-ear."

Before anything else, this entry must state its own limits, because the honest reading of *Cerastium arvense* is that **it is a folk-tradition plant with almost no scientific literature of its own**. A search of PubMed for *Cerastium arvense* returns a mere handful of papers, and not one of them is a phytochemical or pharmacological study of the species. The literature that exists treats it as an *ecological object*: a component of pollinator networks, an agricultural broadleaf weed, and a colonist of contaminated ground. That absence is itself a finding, and this entry is written around it rather than over it.

**Doctrine of the Materia Medica (DMM)** — [USER: verify]
The chickweed tradition in European herbals is real and old, but it is overwhelmingly a tradition attached to **common chickweed, *Stellaria media*** — the cooling, demulcent, vulnerary "little star" of the hedgerow, applied to hot skin, itch, inflamed eyes, and irritated gut. *Cerastium* species have been folded into that tradition largely by **morphological adjacency**: they are chickweeds, they are Caryophyllaceae, they look and taste broadly similar, and they occupy the same disturbed ground. Whether *C. arvense* carries an independent, primary attestation in the classical materia medica — as opposed to being swept in under a generic "chickweed" heading by later compilers — this codex cannot confirm from retrievable sources. The DMM tag is therefore retained **provisionally and flagged**. A cultivator or scholar working from a primary herbal should treat "chickweed" in any pre-modern text as *Stellaria* unless the text says otherwise.

**Contemporary Folk Medicine (CM)** — [USER: verify]
Contemporary folk and forager practice treats field chickweed as a **lesser substitute** for common chickweed: a poultice herb for itch, minor abrasion, and hot inflamed skin; occasionally a spring potherb. The distinction folk practice actually enforces is culinary, not medical — *Stellaria media* is tender and mild; *Cerastium* is **hairy, tougher, and less palatable**, which is why foragers pass it over. The hairiness is diagnostic and it is the reason field chickweed never rose to the same standing.

**Distinguishing from *Stellaria media* (in codex)**
This matters more than usual, because the two are constantly conflated:

| | *Stellaria media* (common chickweed) | *Cerastium arvense* (field chickweed) |
|---|---|---|
| Habit | Sprawling annual, soft, succulent | Mat-forming perennial, wiry |
| Stem | **A single line of hairs** running up one side, switching sides at each node — the definitive field mark | Uniformly hairy/downy all round |
| Leaves | Smooth, ovate, glabrous | Softly hairy, narrow, "mouse-ear" |
| Petals | Deeply cleft, shorter than or equal to sepals | Notched, **conspicuously longer** than sepals — showy |
| Palatability | Mild, tender, eaten freely | Hairy, coarse, rarely eaten |

**Do not carry data across this line.** Everything published on the demulcency, saponin content, or nutritional profile of *Stellaria media* is data about *Stellaria media*. This codex refuses to launder it into a *Cerastium* entry.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

The chickweed doctrine is a *demulcent* doctrine, and demulcent doctrines are among the most chemically legible in the whole materia medica — which is exactly why the gap here is worth naming.

The doctrine says: *cool, moist, slippery, drawing.* A herb that is cool and moist is applied to what is hot and dry. In real chemistry, "cool and moist and slippery" almost always resolves to **mucilage** — long-chain polysaccharides that hydrate into a viscous colloid, form a physical film over irritated epithelium, reduce mechanical friction, and slow the diffusion of irritants to nerve endings. That is not a metaphor; it is a mechanism, and it is one of the few places where a Galenic quality maps almost one-to-one onto a molecular class. The "drawing" quality of a poultice likewise resolves to osmotics and simple occlusion.

The Caryophyllaceae *as a family* also carry a second, better-established chemical signature: **triterpenoid saponins** (the family includes the classic saponin sources *Saponaria officinalis* and *Gypsophila*). Saponins are surfactants. At low concentration on intact skin they are mild cleansers and wetting agents — which is a plausible chemical shadow of "softening" and "cleansing the blood." At higher concentration, or on mucosa, the same surfactancy becomes **irritant and haemolytic**. The doctrine's gentleness and the family's chemistry are in tension, and the tension is real, not rhetorical.

And here the doctrine must be deflated. The demulcent reading of field chickweed is an **inference from family and from a sibling species**, not from a measurement of this plant. The doctrine is coherent. It is chemically *plausible*. It is not *demonstrated*. Where a codex would normally say "the old classification anticipated the assay," here it must say: **there is no assay.** The tradition has made a bet on a look-alike, and nobody has cashed the ticket.

### Active Components

**Stated plainly: no phytochemical profile of *Cerastium arvense* was retrievable for this entry.** No quantified mucilage content, no saponin assay, no flavonoid characterisation, no essential-oil analysis. What follows is **family-level expectation**, labelled as such, and must not be read as species-specific data:

- **Triterpenoid saponins** — characteristic of Caryophyllaceae broadly; presence, identity, and concentration in *C. arvense* **unmeasured** [USER: verify]
- **Mucilaginous polysaccharides** — the presumed basis of any demulcent action; **unquantified** in this species
- **Flavonoids** — near-universal in the family (typically flavone C-glycosides in Caryophyllaceae); **not characterised** for *C. arvense*
- **Common plant acids and minerals** — assumed, unmeasured

**One thing IS measured, and it is not a virtue.** Kasowska and colleagues, studying plants colonising post-flotation copper tailings, found that *Cerastium arvense* growing on contaminated substrate accumulated copper in its shoots to the point of reaching the **critical toxicity level for the plant itself** — alongside enrichment in cadmium, cobalt, nickel and lead. (According to PubMed: Kasowska D, Gediga K, Spiak Z, *Environ Sci Pollut Res Int*, 2018;25(1):824–835 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-017-0451-y).)

This is the single most practically important chemical fact in this entry, and it is about **contamination, not constituents**. Field chickweed is a coloniser of disturbed ground — roadsides, spoil, tailings, industrial margins. That is precisely where a forager finds it, and precisely where it will load its tissues with heavy metals. The plant's ecology is a hazard vector.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Field chickweed sits in **Foundation phase** as a *restorative and tissue-repair* herb of the mildest possible order — and, candidly, as a **substitute of last resort**. Where *Stellaria media* is available, it is the better plant on every axis: better attested, better characterised, more palatable, and gentler.

The Kenetic use case for *C. arvense* is therefore narrow and honest:

- **Topical poultice** for minor abrasion, hot itch, or inflamed skin — the traditional indication, chemically plausible, evidentially unsupported for this species
- **A field-identification discipline.** Learning to tell *Cerastium* from *Stellaria* on the hairline of a stem is a real training in the attentional precision the whole Foundation phase asks for. The plant's best use may be as a teacher of discrimination rather than as a remedy.

**Cultivator's note.** Field chickweed needs no cultivation and resists none — it is a mat-forming perennial that spreads by rooting stems and seed, tolerant of poor, dry, and disturbed soil, full sun to light shade, and it will colonise gravel and thin turf without help. It is treated as an **agricultural weed** and appears as a target species in herbicide-screening work (According to PubMed: Chen CN et al., *Bioorg Med Chem*, 2010;18(14):4897–904 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bmc.2010.06.015)) — which is a way of saying it is hard to get rid of and easy to get. If a cultivator wants it, the task is containment, not propagation: grow it in a bed you are willing to lose, or in a container.

It is not, however, without ecological value. In Colorado ponderosa pine forest, *Cerastium arvense* was identified as one of four floral species acting as a **key connector in bee–flower interaction networks** — a hub species disproportionately important to pollinator network structure, and notably *not* one of the most abundant flowering plants there (According to PubMed: Davies CB, Davis TS, Griswold T, *Ecol Appl*, 2023;33(8):e2927 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/eap.2927)). A cultivator's guide should say this: the strongest evidence-backed reason to keep field chickweed in the ground is **for the bees**, not for the medicine chest.

### Preparation & Dosing

⚠️ **No validated dosing exists for this species.** Nothing below is derived from a clinical or pharmacological study of *Cerastium arvense*, because no such study was retrievable. These are traditional-practice ranges reported for chickweed generally, and every one is flagged.

| Preparation | Method | Traditional range | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh poultice | Bruised or macerated aerial parts, applied to intact-but-irritated skin | Applied to tolerance; renewed as it dries | [USER: verify] — traditional; no species data |
| Fresh infusion | Aerial parts steeped in just-boiled water, 10–15 min | 1–2 g dried / cup, up to 3× daily | [USER: verify] — extrapolated from generic chickweed practice; **not** *C. arvense* data |
| Fresh in food | Spring aerial parts | Small quantity | Discouraged — hairy and coarse; *Stellaria* is the food plant |
| Tincture | — | — | **No traditional or modern basis retrievable.** Do not construct one. |

**Harvest:** aerial parts, spring to early summer, before or at flowering, **from clean ground only** — see Contraindications. Reject any material from roadsides, spoil heaps, industrial margins, tailings, or former mine land.

### Scientific Correlation

**Grade: E — No species-specific evidence.**

This is the lowest grade this codex issues, and *C. arvense* earns it cleanly. It is not a case of negative findings; it is a case of **no findings at all**.

| Claim | Evidence for *C. arvense* | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Demulcent / soothes irritated tissue | **None.** No mucilage assay, no clinical or animal study | **E — untested** |
| Vulnerary / wound-healing | **None.** No in vitro, animal, or human data | **E — untested** |
| Anti-inflammatory | **None** | **E — untested** |
| Contains saponins | **Not measured in this species.** Family-level expectation only | **E — inferred, unverified** |
| Accumulates copper and other heavy metals from contaminated soil | **Yes — measured, species-specific.** Shoot Cu reached critical toxicity level on copper tailings; enriched in Cd, Co, Ni, Pb (Kasowska et al. 2018, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-017-0451-y)) | **B — direct field measurement** |
| Ecologically significant as pollinator-network connector | **Yes — measured, species-specific** (Davies et al. 2023, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/eap.2927)) | **B — direct field study** |

**The null is the headline.** Two decades of searchable biomedical literature contain no pharmacological investigation of this plant. Note what the pattern of the existing literature tells us: *C. arvense* is studied by ecologists, weed scientists, and soil remediation researchers — never by pharmacologists. Nobody has looked. That is not evidence of absence of activity; it is absence of evidence, and the two are different. But a practitioner is entitled to know which one they are standing in.

**On the tempting cross-species import.** There is a real literature on Caryophyllaceae saponins, and there is a folk-and-partial-scientific literature on *Stellaria media*. It would be trivially easy to decorate this entry with it and produce something that reads authoritative. This codex declines. *Cerastium arvense* is a different species in a different genus with a different growth habit, different pubescence, and — for all anyone has actually checked — a different chemistry. **Congener and confamilial data are not species data.**

**Anti-doctrinal note.** Any source that offers you confident percentages, specific saponin names, or clinical claims for *Cerastium arvense* is either importing *Stellaria* data without saying so, or inventing. Ask it for the primary reference on *C. arvense*. There isn't one.

The Kenetic reading is deliberately conservative. A cool-moist surface classification is what the plant *looks like* it should be. Until someone runs an assay, that is all it is.

### Contraindications

⚠️ **HEAVY-METAL ACCUMULATION — the primary practical hazard.** *Cerastium arvense* is a coloniser of disturbed and contaminated ground and has been measured accumulating copper to critical-toxicity concentrations in its shoots on post-flotation copper tailings, with concurrent enrichment in cadmium, cobalt, nickel, and lead (Kasowska et al. 2018, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-017-0451-y)). **Never harvest from roadside verges, spoil, tailings, brownfield, former mine land, railway margins, or any ground of unknown history.** This is not a theoretical caution — it is the best-established chemical fact about this plant, and it is a fact about poison.

**Saponin caution (inferred).** Caryophyllaceae characteristically produce triterpenoid saponins, which are surfactants and can be gastrointestinally irritant and haemolytic in quantity. *C. arvense* has not been assayed, so its saponin load is **unknown** — which means it cannot be assumed low. Treat internal use as unquantified risk. Do not use in quantity. Do not use as a staple food.

**Pregnancy and lactation:** avoid. Not from a known risk, but from a known ignorance — there is no safety data of any kind for this species.

**Paediatric:** avoid internal use.

**Identification risk:** the Caryophyllaceae contain both benign and saponin-rich, unpalatable members, and *Cerastium* is a large, taxonomically difficult genus with many similar species. Misidentification within the genus is easy. If you cannot key it confidently to species, do not use it.

**Allergy:** contact sensitivity to the pubescent foliage is plausible; patch-test before poulticing broadly.

**The overriding contraindication is evidential.** This plant has no safety profile, no efficacy profile, and no chemical profile. It has a folk reputation borrowed from a relative. Prefer *Stellaria media* — which at least has an independent tradition of its own — for every indication *Cerastium* is reputed to serve.
