# Spider — Dioscorides, ARACHNE (arachne; also "holcos" or "lycos")
## *Spiders and their webs; the translator's gloss prints "Arachneidae — Spiders", with no species given* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The spider is chapter 2-68 of the *De Materia Medica*, in Book Two, in the section "Living Creatures", in the Osbaldeston and Wood English version (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, line 5209; printed page 205). The chapter is headed "2-68. ARACHNE" and the translator's gloss reads "Arachneidae — Spiders". Its first sentence gives the creature's other names: "The spider — a creature also called holcos or lycos (that is, raptor, for example, lupus)". No species and no binomial are given in the chapter.

The chapter gives four uses, and all are external or minor except the last. (1) The spider itself, "worked into one piece with a plaster, spread on linen and applied to the forehead or temples, cures the periodical circuits of paroxysm every third day in acute fevers" (line 5211). The "every third day" fever is the text's own description of the pattern of attacks, which the translator does not gloss; a fever "with paroxysm every third day" is the tertian pattern in the author's terms and the Codex states no more than the text does. (2) "The cobwebs of spiders are applied to staunch blood, and prevent inflammation in ulcers that break out on the surface of the skin." (3) "There is another kind of spider which spins a white web, thin and thick, of which it is said that when put into a purse of leather and hanged around the arm it cures the flows of quartain [with paroxysms every fourth day] acute fevers." The words "it is said" mark this as a report that Dioscorides passes on and does not vouch for; it is a hung object, an amulet-like use, and the text records it without endorsing it. (4) "Boiled together with rosaceum [1-53] and poured in the ears it helps earaches." Rosaceum is the oil cross-referenced to chapter 1-53; its properties are in that chapter and not read here.

The spider appears again in the book in other roles that are not uses of the animal. In 2-24 (line 5020), the red mullet, "cut apart whilst it is raw and applied it heals the hurt caused by the sea dragon [2-15], scorpion, and the spider", so the spider there is a cause of injury. The persea tree of Egypt is said to bear fruit "on which the spiders called cranocolopta are found" (line 4894). Several plants are given for "those bitten by harvest spiders" (for example lines 4855, 5831, 7382); that is a separate creature in the translator's rendering and is not a remedy made from a spider.

### Active Components

Spiders are arthropods; the body is chitin and protein with some lipid. Spider silk is a protein fibre (fibroin-like) and cobweb is a tangle of silk with dust and whatever the web has caught. Spider venom, in species that have it, is a mixture of peptides and other small molecules, but the chapter does not use the venom or name a species. Nothing in the chapter names an active principle.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records three uses of the spider and its webs: a plaster on the forehead or temples for fevers, cobweb on bleeding or inflamed skin ulcers, and a spider boiled with rosaceum in the ear for earache. The Codex recommends none of them. Cobweb on a wound is a contamination risk and a fever or an earache needs assessment, and the Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations and no quantities: the whole spider "worked into one piece with a plaster, spread on linen" and applied to forehead or temples; the cobwebs applied to the skin; a second kind of spider in a leather purse hung around the arm; the spider "boiled together with rosaceum" and poured into the ears. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Cobweb is not sterile, and applying cobweb to a wound can introduce bacteria, including tetanus spores; it is not a recognised treatment. Fever pattern (every third or fourth day) is characteristic of some malarial infections, and the paroxysm descriptions in the chapter may reflect that, but the text does not say so and no identification of the disease is made here. A fever or a persistent earache is a reason for clinical care. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** None is printed. The chapter on the spider gives no warming, cooling, drying or other quality for the animal or its web, and no degree. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and the chapter attaches none to the spider. The pneuma and the three Galenic spirits belong to later writers and are not read in this chapter. What the chapter does offer, as the discipline of attention that the Codex finds in Dioscorides, is the habit of distinguishing kinds: it names the creature's other names (holcos, lycos), separates a "kind of spider which spins a white web" from the first, and marks the hung-purse use with "it is said". It records an amulet-like use (the spider in a purse hung on the arm) and in doing so reports it at one remove; recording is not endorsing.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a short chapter of external applications, one of them reported at second hand, and none is a practice for the cultivation of the person. The honest use of the entry is as a record of what Dioscorides says.

### Contraindications

- **Infection:** cobweb or a crushed spider on broken skin can infect the wound, including with tetanus; do not apply them.
- **Fever and earache:** a recurring fever needs assessment, since recurring fever can be malaria or another serious infection; ear pain with discharge or hearing loss needs examination, and nothing is poured into a possibly perforated ear.
- **Venom and allergy:** some spiders are medically significant (bites can be serious) and some people are allergic to spider material; the chapter names no species, so none can be called safe.
- **Ethics:** killing animals for a plaster with no supported benefit is hard to justify.
