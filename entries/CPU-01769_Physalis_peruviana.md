# Physalis peruviana — khron.bu, Tronbu (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Physalis peruviana, the first of three binomials Clark 1995 prints for khron.bu (before "Clerodendron fortunatum" and "Euphorbia sieboldiana", as printed), and the only one of the three that Clark's chapter 21 names in its purgative class; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text prints no Physalis and sets Euphorbia stracheyi against the name "Tronbu" by column position* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and under this binomial on Clark's edition alone.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Ebers, Assyrian, Sushruta and Shennong text on disk for *Physalis* found other species and other drugs: Dioscorides prints *Physalis alkekengi* and *Physalis francheti* in translators' lines for the *strychnos* (halicacabum) chapter (lines 8662 to 8665); the Shennong text (Yang 1998) has *Suan Jiang* (*Herba Physalis Alkekengi*, line 2951), a different species; and Bhishagratna's Sushruta volume 3 prints "Withania Somnifera; Physalis Flexuosa" in a list of plant names (lines 31340 to 31342), which is not this species. None of these is read as this drug. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 214) the row is keyed by the Tibetan name *khron.bu*, with the identification "Physalis peruviana; Clerodendron fortunatum; Euphorbia sieboldiana", the locus "Clark 1995 (line 1136)" and the note "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The spine row (CPU-01769) took the **first** of the three binomials.

**Identity doubt.** Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" and (line 68) that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect". Here three species of three genera are given for one Tibetan name, and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary sets a fourth species, in a fourth way, against the spelling "Tronbu" (below). The Tibetan name does not fix the species and *Physalis peruviana* is one candidate. The Codex does not choose among them. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1136).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997), which runs to "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147). The entry follows *sngon.bu* (*Lactuca*, line 1135) and *thar.nu* (line 1134), and reads:

"khron.bu: Physalis peruviana: Clerodendron fortunatum: Euphorbia sieboldiana [has three names. It has a small, round green leaf and a taproot and] purges bile disorders."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The count of names and the description are inside the brackets; "purges bile disorders" is outside them and is the root-text layer as Clark's marks stand. Clark's name index lists *khron.bu* (line 1678) and his Latin index lists *Physalis peruviana* (line 1626).

*Clark 1995, chapter 21 (line 1225).* In Clark's chapter 21 ("The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines", heading at lines 1205 and 1206), the class of purgatives reads: "The category of medicines inducing purgation includes (long-tipped) Chebulic myrobalan, Castor Oil seed, Cassia pods, Euphorbia sp., Physic nut (Baliospermum montanum), Euphorbia pallassii, Rheum emodi (a type of rhubarb), Lactuca dissecta (a type of larkspur), Physalis peruviana, Rheum nobile, Stellera chamaejasme and chhu.rtsa (Rheum spiciforme)." *Physalis peruviana* stands in it by its Latin name, and neither *Clerodendron fortunatum* nor *Euphorbia sieboldiana* does. The list agrees in kind with the "purges" clause of line 1136. The Codex records that and does not say that either line copies the other.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (read through the name, not the binomial).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The glossary's "T" block prints the phonetic name "Tronbu" (line 5899) and the transliteration "khron bu" (line 5902), so the spelling ties the Men-Tsee-Khang name to Clark's *khron.bu*. The scientific-name run puts *Euphorbia stracheyi* ("Spurge") against "Tronbu" by position (see I_1275); the glossary prints no *Physalis*. Chapter 20, within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), line 3495: "Dhurji and Tharnu purge all forms of hot and cold disorders. Ngonbu cleanses chhuser disorders, while Tronbu cleanses tripa disorders." "Chhuser" is a Tibetan term kept untranslated (the editors' note, line 686). Chapter 21 ("The Group and the Method of Compounding Medicine", contents, line 229), line 3661: "Arura, Dhenda, Dhong ga, Shri khenda, Dhurji, Thamu, Chum tsa, Ngonbu, Tronbu, Tsi takmo, Rechagpa and Chhu tsa are the group of medicines that are purgatives." The index lists "Tronbu 211,223" (line 7523). Clark renders the humour as "bile" and Men-Tsee-Khang transliterates it as "tripa"; the two clauses ("purges bile disorders", "cleanses tripa disorders") agree in kind, and the Codex does not claim more.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Physalis peruviana*, *khron.bu* and *Tronbu* found these. I_1275 (*Euphorbia stracheyi*, FMT) is the entry for the same Tibetan name through the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary and quotes Clark's line 1136 in full; the two are not merged, because the binomials differ. I_1304 (*Jatropha glandulifera*), I_1306 (*Lactuca dissecta*), I_1175 (*Rheum emodi*) and I_1176 (*Rheum nobile*) quote the same Clark purgative class (line 1225) for their own drugs. I_1274 (*Euphorbia helioscopia*) covers *Dhurji*, the neighbour named in the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence at line 3495. I_620 (*Physalis alkekengi*) is the Chinese-lantern entry of other traditions, a different species. No entry on this species was found. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Physalis* is a genus of the nightshade family (Solanaceae), that its fruit is enclosed in a papery husk (the calyx), and that *Physalis peruviana* is generally known in English as the Cape gooseberry. Which compounds the Tibetan drug holds, and which part of which plant it is, is not shown by the lines read, so no statement about its composition is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "purges bile disorders" (Clark, line 1136) and that "cleanses tripa disorders" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3495), and both editions place it among the purgatives (Clark, line 1225; Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3661). These are the texts' claims in their own terms, attached to this binomial only through the name, and they are not Codex protocols. A purgative is a drug that provokes evacuation of the bowel, and a strong one can dehydrate and harm. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and no plant should be gathered or used as a purgative on the strength of this entry.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary mentions a "taproot" and a "small, round green leaf" but does not say which part is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

The Men-Tsee-Khang lines read (3495, 3661, 5899) print none. The classes it stands in (purgatives, lines 1225 and 3661) are classes of action and not of taste or power.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The materia medica classes this drug by what it does: it purges, and it stands in the purgative group of both editions. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, lines 3900 to 3950; Clark's chapter 23 is headed "Normal health", lines 1262 to 1276), and Clark's materia medica names other drugs as essence-extraction ingredients (for example lines 853, 971 and 1144), none of them this one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name and not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. A purgative is a short, supervised intervention if it is anything, and the lines give no preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Purgatives can cause dehydration and loss of salts, and should not be taken without medical advice; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Because the species is one of several candidates, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read.
