# Chrysanthemum leucacanthemum — Leukakantha (Dioscorides 3-22)

## *Chrysanthemum leucacanthemum (the translators' suggestion; "Ox-eye Daisy, White Weed, Dog Daisy")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Identity doubt.** The SUGGESTED line (line 6718) reads "Chrysanthemum leucacanthemum, Leucanthemum, [Bedevian] — Ox-eye Daisy, White Weed, Dog Daisy". The chapter sits in the section headed "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants" (running head at the page break near line 6720), and the name *leukakantha* and the Roman name *alba spina* both carry the sense of a white thorn. The text read describes only the root and no thorn or flower, and the ox-eye daisy is not a thorny plant. Whether the translators' daisy fits the chapter is therefore uncertain. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *3-22. LEUKAKANTHA* at line 6717, text at line 6719.

The whole chapter reads: "Leucacantha has a root that is similar to cyprus [1-124] — bitter and strong — which is chewed to lessen toothache. Three cups of a decoction (taken as a drink with wine) helps lung congestion that has lasted long, sciatica, hernia, and convulsions. The juice from the root (taken as a drink) does the same. It is also called polygonatum, or phyllon, others call it ischias, the Romans call it gniacardus, and the Thuscans, alba spina."

So the text gives the root as the only part described, likened to the root of *cyprus* (chapter 1-124, which was not read) and "bitter and strong". It is chewed against toothache; a wine decoction, three cups, is drunk for long-lasting lung congestion, sciatica, hernia and convulsions, and the root juice drunk "does the same". Five further names are printed: *polygonatum*, *phyllon*, *ischias*, Roman *gniacardus* and Thuscan *alba spina*. The translators' index lists the binomial at page 387.

**Overlap.** No existing entry covers this chapter. The adjacent 3-23 Tragakanthe is I_1023 *Astragalus tragacantha*, which reports 3-22 as a different plant. I_783 Common Daisy (*Bellis perennis*) names the ox-eye daisy only as a look-alike. The name *leucacantha* also appears as an alternative name in two other chapters: 1-119 Ramnos trissus (line 4425) and 3-122 Phalaggion (line 7635, "while others call it leucacantha"). Dioscorides therefore applies the name to more than one plant, and the three chapters are not to be merged on the name.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. The daisy family (Asteraceae), to which the translators' ox-eye daisy belongs, is generally known to include species that cause contact skin allergy in some people. This is a statement about the family, not this species. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a bitter, strong root chewed for toothache and a wine decoction of it for long-standing chest congestion, sciatica, hernia and convulsions. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol: the identification is doubtful, and convulsions and hernia are conditions that need medical care.

### Preparation & Dosing

3-22 gives the root chewed, and "three cups of a decoction (taken as a drink with wine)". The root juice is "taken as a drink" with no quantity. The three cups are the only amount printed; the chapter does not say how much root, how long or how often. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes: it identifies the plant by its root, by comparison with another chapter's root and by taste, and records six names across Greek, Roman and Thuscan usage. It assigns the plant no status. The identification is the translators' and uncertain. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. A root described only by taste and a toothache use does not support one.

### Contraindications

Convulsions, hernia and persistent lung congestion need medical assessment and are not for self-treatment from a historical entry. The plant's identity is doubtful, so no wild plant should be gathered or drunk on this basis. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
