# Cowrie shell — hgron.bu (Clark: "Cowrie shell", "has eight names, for example karshapani etc."); Cowrie (Men-Tsee-Khang)
## *The shell of a cowrie (a marine snail). Clark's English prints "Cowrie shell" and gives no scientific name in the lines read; the source list `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` (line 336) records "Cowrie" with class "animal"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The cowrie is in the materia medica chapter of the Explanatory Tantra (ch.20, "Efficacy of Medicinal Substances"), in Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). It stands in the eighth of the chapter's eight sections, "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), which opens: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). The cowrie is filed under *Bone* (heading at line 1159), among human, dragon, tiger, pig, sheep, porcupine and monkey bones, a snail shell and a mule's bone. So the tradition classes the cowrie shell as a bone of the animal-product kind, not among the precious medicines where the conch stands.

The line reads (line 1160): "hgron.bu: Cowrie shell: [has eight names, for example karshapani etc.] It is hemostatic, dries pus, [oedema fluid and lymph (accumulations), disperses tumors and is beneficial in eye diseases]." Clark's preface states his convention (line 82): text in square brackets comes from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. So the bare actions of the root tantra, as Clark gives them, are "hemostatic" and "dries pus"; the further items (oedema fluid and lymph accumulations, dispersing tumors, the eye benefit) and the eight names are commentary. The name "karshapani" is printed as an example; the Tibetan name is Clark's "hgron.bu", and I did not check either against a lexicon. [USER: verify]

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`) gives the animal-products passage in the same order (line 3523): "Tiger bone protects the spongy bone. Cowrie stops bleeding and dries pus and chhuser. Thokgi saepai ruepa and Gyueser thoepa cure colitis. Pig bone cures baekan mukpo." So both translations agree that the cowrie stops bleeding and dries pus. Men-Tsee-Khang adds *chhuser*, a technical term that it deliberately leaves in phonetic Tibetan (line 686) and the only statement about it in the lines I read is that the nature of *ein* and *chhuser* "can be either hot or cold" (line 1041); the term is listed among the Tibetan terms of the glossary (line 4829) with no English gloss in the lines read. Clark's bracketed commentary reads "oedema fluid and lymph (accumulations)" in the same place; I do not equate *chhuser* with either English phrase. [USER: verify]

Both translators comment on animal medicines in general, and the comments bear on this entry. Clark writes (line 57): "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts." Men-Tsee-Khang writes (line 687): "Buddhism strictly denounces causing harm to any being. The demand for Tibetan medicinal substances in the past was very limited, and the use of animal products was negligible. Presently... animal products in Tibetan medicine have been completely substituted by equally effective herbal substances", and the translation gives their potencies "in order to... preserve the understanding of the therapeutic values of animal products". The cowrie line is therefore preserved as a record of what the Gyueshi says, and the translators do not describe it as in current use.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, a cowrie shell is almost entirely calcium carbonate in a small proteinaceous matrix, with a hard, glossy surface. The shell is not the animal's flesh. No quantities are given here because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text records the cowrie shell as an animal-product medicine that stops bleeding and dries pus, with a commentary note that it helps eye disease and disperses tumors. The Codex does not recommend taking powdered shell for these complaints: bleeding needs direct pressure and medical care, and a wound with pus needs cleaning and, often, antibiotics or drainage. The text gives no compound and no preparation, the translators themselves describe animal products as largely replaced, and the Codex adds no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives no preparation for the cowrie in the lines read: no burning, grinding, vehicle or compound. It lists the shell among single animal-product medicines, with actions only. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Calcium carbonate is the main component of shells, and is an antacid and a calcium supplement. Nothing in that supports stopping bleeding or drying pus. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** The materia medica chapter opens with the eight powers (heavy, oily, cool, blunt, light, coarse, hot, sharp) and the seventeen secondary qualities (Clark lines 796-797). For the cowrie, line 1160 prints no taste, no power and no post-digestive taste; it prints only actions (hemostatic, dries pus), with commentary actions in brackets. Men-Tsee-Khang line 3523 likewise prints actions only. No quality of the cowrie itself is printed in the lines read, and none is supplied.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The cowrie is attested in the Explanatory Tantra ch.20 as an animal-product medicine, one of the bones (Clark line 1160; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3523), with therapeutic actions only. The Tibetan frame is that the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) arise from the three poisons (attachment, aversion, delusion), so that pathology is at root a spiritual condition, and that medicine is the third of four treatment branches: it supports practice and does not reach the root. Within that frame the cowrie is a medicine of the third branch. No *bcud len* (essence extraction) use of the cowrie was found: a search of Clark and of the Men-Tsee-Khang text for "cowrie" and "cowry" returned only the materia medica lines above and index entries. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted. The modern translators' own statement that Buddhism "strictly denounces causing harm to any being", and that animal products have been substituted, belongs to the practitioner's side of the frame and is recorded in the note below.

**Practitioner's note.** The cowrie gives a cultivator nothing to do. The Tibetan text records the shell as a bone-class animal medicine for bleeding and pus, and its modern translators say that such products have been largely replaced by herbal substances because of the Buddhist rule against harming beings. The practical reading is abstention: for the complaints named, the tradition's own present practice is not to use the shell. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Contamination:** shell from unknown sources, or powdered shell not prepared to a recognised standard, can carry heavy metals and microbes; the Codex does not recommend taking shell material.
- **Not a treatment for bleeding or infection:** significant bleeding and any wound with pus need medical care; powdered shell applied to a wound is a route to infection.
- **Calcium load:** large amounts of calcium carbonate by mouth can cause constipation, raise blood calcium, and add to kidney-stone risk.
- **Ethics:** Men-Tsee-Khang records that the tradition's own present practice is to substitute herbal substances for animal products, on the Buddhist ground of not harming beings.
- **Conservation and law:** shell collecting is regulated in many coastal areas, and trade in some marine shells is controlled; take no shell from living animals or protected shores.
- **Cultural status:** cowrie shells have currency, ornament and ritual uses in many cultures; the Codex records only the medical line of the Tibetan text.
