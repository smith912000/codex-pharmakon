# Galium verum — Gallion (Dioscorides 4-96; also gallerium, galatium)

## *Galium verum [Linnaeus] (one of three names on the translators' SUGGESTED line: "Gallium [Fuchs], Galium luteum [Bauhin], Galium verum [Linnaeus] — Ladies' Bedstraw, Rennet")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk names this plant. The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line gives three forms: a Fuchs herbal name (*Gallium*), Bauhin's *Galium luteum* and Linnaeus's *Galium verum*, with the English names "Ladies' Bedstraw, Rennet". The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-96. GALLION* at line 8893, SUGGESTED line at 8894, text at line 8895 and, after a page break (page number 647 and a running head, lines 8896-8898), line 8899. The chapter stands in Book Four, "Other Herbs & Roots", after 4-95 *GALIOPSIS* and before 4-97 *ERIGERON*, both different plants. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 676.

The whole chapter reads: "Gallion is called this because coagulates milk instead of rennet. It has little branches, with the leaves very similar to aparina yet straight, and a thin yellowish flower on top, in thick abundance and smelling good. The flower is smeared on for burns from fire, and it stops flows of blood. It is also mixed with waxy rose ointment and placed in the sun till it turns white, and this is a medication for acopon. The root encourages sexual intercourse [aphrodisiac]. It grows in marshy places. It is also called gallerium, or, galatium."

So the chapter gives a reason for the name (the plant "coagulates milk instead of rennet"), a description (little branches; leaves like *aparina* but straight; a thin yellowish flower, abundant and sweet-smelling), two uses of the flower, one use of the root, a habitat and two further names. *Aparina* is the plant of chapter 3-104 *APARINE* (heading at line 7475), which the existing entry I_906 reads. The word *acopon* is printed untranslated by the translators, and this entry does not gloss it. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

No analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. Bedstraws (*Galium*) are generally known to contain iridoid glycosides and, in the roots of some species, anthraquinone pigments, but which species the chapter describes is not settled, and nothing in the text says what makes the flower coagulate milk. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a topical use of the flower on burns, a haemostatic use, a sun-bleached rose-ointment preparation for a condition the translators leave untranslated, and a reputation of the root as an aphrodisiac. The Codex gives no protocol for any of these. The identity is a translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives two preparations: the flower "smeared on" (no vehicle named), and the flower "mixed with waxy rose ointment and placed in the sun till it turns white". No dose, no duration and no quantity is printed for either, and the use of the root has no preparation. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that some bedstraw species have been used in cheese-making to curdle milk in several European traditions, but that practice is not read in any source here and does not by itself establish the identity of Dioscorides' plant.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 4-96 prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality and no taste. It prints only that the flower is "smelling good".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a name explained by an observed property (curdling milk), a description for recognition (leaves like *aparina* yet straight, yellowish abundant flower, sweet scent), a habitat (marshy places) and a preparation that depends on the sun (rose ointment "placed in the sun till it turns white"). It is not a cultivation status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The only transferable point is the habit of identifying a plant by leaf, flower, scent and habitat, and of reading the name as a record of what was observed. The text offers nothing more, and the abstentive answer is the right one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 4-96 prints no warning. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Burns and bleeding need proper first aid and medical care, not a herbal smear. The root is recorded as an aphrodisiac, with no dose, and that is not advice. [USER: verify]
