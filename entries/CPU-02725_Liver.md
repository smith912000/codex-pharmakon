**Avicenna, Canon (CM).** The entry is *kabid* (*Canon* Book II, Arabic text line 14316, to line 14326). It opens with the general quality: الدم المتوتد عن الأكباد غليظ وأصلحه كبد البط المسمن والدجاج المسمن (14317-14318): "the blood generated from livers is thick, and the soundest is the liver of the fattened duck and the fattened hen". Later in the passage: "the liver is slow to pass through the vessels, except the liver of the fattened duck" (14323-14324).

**Named animals.** Head (14318-14320): "the liver of the goat, and especially the he-goat, uncovers the case of the epileptic, and if the epileptic eats it, he falls (is thrown)" (يكشف أمر المصروع وإذا أكل صرع صاحب الصرع); "and the liver of the lizard (*wazagha*, species not stated) on eroded teeth quiets the pain". Next (14322): "the liver of the wolf helps all pains of the liver". Galen is cited (14322-14323): "as for me, I put them in the remedy of *ghāfit* (agrimony) and found no additional benefit over [the remedy] without them". Poisons (14324-14326): the liver of the rabid dog (the verb in the text, *yusammā*, is unclear; [USER: verify]) "helps the one bitten by it"; "they have mentioned that it prevents fear of water, and a people lived by it, who were also treated with other treatments". The section labels in this line are out of place in the text (the wolf's liver is under the eye heading); this is a feature of the text as retrieved.

### Active Components

In general terms, liver is rich in protein, iron, vitamin A and cholesterol. The passage names none of these. No analytical source was retrieved for the Canon's uses. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.** The Codex gives no protocol and does not recommend this material. The terms of the *Canon* (hot, cold, dry, moist, the humours) are the tradition's own and are not translated into modern diagnoses. The epilepsy, tooth and rabies uses are historical. Eating a rabid animal's organ is dangerous and is not advised.

### Preparation & Dosing

No dose is given in the passage read. [USER: verify] The preparations are the eaten liver (goat), the liver applied to teeth (lizard) and the liver of the rabid dog; the passage gives no method for them.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established fact: liver is a rich food source of iron and vitamin A (excess vitamin A is harmful, particularly in pregnancy). Rabies is a fatal viral disease, and no organ of an animal cures it. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

The Canon maps animal liver onto the liver itself, the teeth, epilepsy and rabies. Goat liver, and especially he-goat liver, is said to reveal the case of the epileptic, who falls if he eats it. A lizard's liver applied to eroded teeth quiets pain. Wolf liver is said to help all pains of the liver. A rabid dog's liver is said to help the one it has bitten and to prevent fear of water. The Canon describes liver as generating thick blood and as slow to pass through the vessels, except the fattened duck's, which it calls the soundest. Galen is cited as finding no added benefit from liver in an agrimony remedy. The passage gives no temperament, dose or method, and the entry adds no subtle-body or humoral reading beyond the Canon's own words.

### Contraindications

- **Rabies.** Never rely on an animal organ after a bite; seek proper medical care at once.
- **Epilepsy.** The "test" described is not a diagnostic method.
- **Hygiene.** Raw or undercooked organ meat carries infection risk.
- **Vitamin A.** Large amounts of liver are harmful in pregnancy.
- **Conservation and ethics.** Some of the named animals are protected in places. [USER: verify]
