# Garum (fish sauce) — Garron (GARRON), "garum"; the translator's heading is "Garum — Liquid from Salt Fish"
## *The liquid that runs from salted flesh or fish, which the translator glosses elsewhere as a "Roman sauce made of fermented fish"; Dioscorides' own definition is "the liquid that comes out of salted flesh or fish", and no fish is named for it in chapter 2-34* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Garum is chapter 34 of Book Two of the *De Materia Medica*, in the part headed "LIVING CREATURES", headed GARRON with the translator's gloss "SUGGESTED: Garum — Liquid from Salt Fish" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 5076-5078; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` has it at line 224 as 2-34, line 5076; the book's index prints "Garum 196"). It stands between the salted tunny (2-33, *omotarichos*) and fish soup (2-35). The whole chapter is a single paragraph (line 5078):

"Garum (the liquid that comes out of salted flesh or fish), applied, represses gangrenous ulcers in the cheeks, heals those bitten by dogs, and is sometimes given as a suppository for dysentery and sciatica. It is given to some so that it may repress ulcers (of the bowels), to others that it may encourage ulceration of the parts not ulcerated, and to remove fluids troubling the hips."

So the text gives five uses: applied to gangrenous ulcers of the cheeks; applied to dog bites; as a suppository for dysentery and for sciatica; and, with a purpose that depends on the patient, "to repress ulcers" of the bowels in some and "to encourage ulceration of the parts not ulcerated" in others; and "to remove fluids troubling the hips". It gives no method of making the liquid, no fish, no quality assay and no quantity.

*The gloss.* The only places where the translator explains the word are two brackets. In 2-31 (*Mainis*, the maena, a small sea-fish "eaten by the poor"; line 5065) the text says that "its garum [Roman sauce made of fermented fish] rolled up and down around the mouth lessens rotten ulcers in there". In 5-129 (*Alos Anthos*, flowers of salt; line 10506) the best flower of salt is said to have "a somewhat bad scent like garum [Roman sauce made from fermented fish], sometimes smelling worse". So the translator takes garum to be the Roman fermented fish sauce. Neither bracket is Dioscorides' own words.

*Garum in the neighbouring fish chapters.* The salted-fish chapters round it give related uses, which are not garum and are not folded in: the brine of the salted silurus (2-29) is "good in baths for dysentery at the first sign" and as a suppository "cures sciatica"; the flesh of salted fish and meat is "good for one touched by a scorpion, and for one bitten by a dog" (2-30); salted tunny is given to those bitten by the viper called *prester* (2-33). The match of the dysentery, sciatica and dog-bite uses with those of garum is noted here and not argued.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, fish sauce is the liquid from fish that have been salted and left to ferment and autolyse. It is a very salty solution of amino acids and small peptides (from the breakdown of fish protein), with sodium chloride in high concentration, some fat, and the products of fermenting bacteria, including biogenic amines such as histamine. Nothing links any constituent to the uses the text gives.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records garum as a topical and suppository medicine for gangrenous cheek ulcers, dog bites, dysentery, sciatica and fluids "troubling the hips". The Codex does not recommend any of these uses. Gangrene, a dog bite and dysentery need a clinician; a dog bite carries a risk of rabies and tetanus; and a very salty fermented fish liquid in the bowel is not a safe remedy. Fish sauce is today an ordinary seasoning and its use at table is not in question here. The entry is about the ancient medicinal use.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Fish sauce is high in salt, which in strong solution is antimicrobial and an irritant to raw tissue; a strong salt solution placed in the bowel can draw water into it and cause harm. Fermented fish products can contain histamine in amounts that cause reactions in some people. Dysentery, sciatica and infected wounds are medical conditions with their own treatments. ### Contraindications

- **Wounds and bites:** do not apply fish sauce to an open wound or a dog bite; a bite needs medical care, including assessment for rabies and tetanus.
- **Bowel use:** a salty fermented fish liquid should not be given by suppository or enema.
- **Fish allergy:** garum is a fish product and can cause severe reactions in people allergic to fish.
- **Histamine and sodium:** fermented fish sauce can cause histamine reactions in some people and is very high in salt, a concern in high blood pressure and kidney or heart disease.
- **Identity:** the chapter names no fish; the "Roman fermented fish sauce" reading is the translator's.
