# Sabina recurva — shug.pa.tsher.chan, Shugpa tsherchen (rGyud-bzhi, Clark 1995 line 1043; Men-Tsee-Khang running text and glossary by column position)

## *Sabina recurva (Harriet) Antoine, as the first of three binomials Clark 1995 prints on line 1043 for shug.pa.tsher.chan, followed by Juniperus recurva and Empetrum niger; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary sets Juniperus communis ("Juniper") against "Shugpa tserchen" and prints no Sabina recurva* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Shennong (Yang 1998), Sushruta, Ebers and Assyrian text folders on disk for *Sabina* and *Juniperus* found no hit in the primary texts. The Codex's own overview files for Ebers and Assyrian mention juniper (`EP_Original_Hieroglyphic.md` lines 660 and 821; `AT_Original_Cuneiform.md` lines 389 and 569), and these are not the primary texts and are not read here as this plant. The Dioscorides text prints *Juniperus* and *Sabina* in translators' SUGGESTED lines for Greek drugs (lines 4306 to 4307, 4318 to 4319 and 10015: *Juniperus communis*, *Juniperus excelsa*, *Juniperus sabina*), and these are not read as this plant. The row was added from a printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 149) the row is keyed by the **Tibetan name** *shug.pa.tsher.chan*, the first name Clark prints, and not by a binomial. Its locus is "Clark 1995 (line 1043)", its translator identification reads "Sabina recurva (Harriet) Antoine; Juniperus recurva; Empetrum niger", and its note reads "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The spine binomial *Sabina recurva* is therefore the **first** of Clark's three Latin names. *Juniperus recurva* and *Empetrum niger* have no row of their own. The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary row for *Juniperus communis* (line 361, glossary line 5816) is a different binomial for the same Tibetan name by column position, set out below, and is not part of this entry's title.

**Identity doubt.** Clark prints three genera for one Tibetan name: two junipers (the first under the genus name *Sabina*, the second under *Juniperus*) and a crowberry (*Empetrum*, a different family). The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints a third juniper, *Juniperus communis*, against the same Tibetan name. The two editions therefore agree on "juniper" and differ on the species, and the Codex does not choose. Clark's preface says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" (line 83) and that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect" (line 68). The author's name "(Harriet)" is printed so on line 1043 and is not corrected here. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1043).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry follows *se.rgod* (wild rose, line 1042) and is followed by *skyer.pa* (Indian barberry, line 1044). It reads:

"shug.pa.tsher.chan: Sabina recurva (Harriet) Antoine: Juniperus recurva: Empetrum niger: [has eight names and is of three types, rgya.sung (juniper), lha.shug and shug.tsher. It has a small, thorny trunk and] cures kidney fevers, infectious tissue degeneration, lymph disorders and heals wounds. The leaf is recommended for use in disorders of the lower intestine, whilst the fruit is said to be supreme in (curing) fever in the limbs, in drying lymph accumulations and as an essence-extraction ingredient."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries and that those in parentheses derive from the oral instructions of Dr. Yeshe Donden and Dr. Pema Dorje. The eight names, the three types and the "small, thorny trunk" are therefore the commentary layer, and the "(curing)" is the oral-instruction layer. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the three Latin names, which are Clark's identifications, and the whole of the therapeutic statement: "cures kidney fevers, infectious tissue degeneration, lymph disorders and heals wounds", the recommendation of the leaf "in disorders of the lower intestine", and the statement that the fruit "is said to be supreme in ... fever in the limbs, in drying lymph accumulations and as an essence-extraction ingredient". Clark's name indexes list *shug.pa.tsher.chan* and *shug.tsher* (line 1763) and *lha.shug* (line 1770), and the Latin index lists *Sabina recurva* (line 1641), *Juniperus recurva* (line 1602) and *Empetrum niger* (line 1583).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "S" block).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs, and the columns do not hold the same number of entries. The page that begins at line 5774 prints seventeen phonetic names, from "Shingngar" to "Sumchutik", and "Shugpa tserchen" is the sixth (line 5776). The transliterations (lines 5787 to 5796) include "shug pa tsher can" (line 5789). The scientific names begin at line 5798: "Glycyrrhiza glabr" (so printed), "Cinnamomum zeylannicum", "Rumex nepalensis", "Rhus vemiciflua", "-", "Acorus calamus L." and **"Juniperus communis"** (line 5816), and the English names include **"Juniper"** (line 5818). Counted in order, "Shugpa tserchen" is the sixth phonetic name, "shug pa tsher can" the sixth transliteration, *Juniperus communis* the sixth scientific name and "Juniper" the sixth English name. The count is checked on "Shudag" against *Acorus calamus* and "Sweet Flag" (fifth) and on "Shomang" against *Rumex nepalensis* and "Field sorrel" (third). The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position only. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3438).* Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225; colophon at line 3583), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428): "Shugpa tsherchen cure hot disorder of the kidneys and the inflammation of muscle tissues." (the verb is printed "cure"). The printed index lists "Shugpa tsherchen 208" (line 7493). The sentence gives no part used and no preparation, and does not mention essence extraction.

The two editions agree in kind: Clark's drug "cures kidney fevers" and the Men-Tsee-Khang *Shugpa tsherchen* cures "hot disorder of the kidneys". Only Clark gives the leaf, the fruit and the essence-extraction clause. The Codex reads *shug.pa.tsher.chan* and *Shugpa tsherchen* as one drug from the spelling and that agreement; neither text says so. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Sabina recurva*, *Juniperus recurva*, *shug.pa.tsher.chan*, *Shugpa* and *Empetrum niger* found these. **I_234** (*Sabina tibetica*, "Tibetan Juniper", tag FMT) quotes the same Clark line 1043, says the statement belongs to a differently identified species and does not transfer it, and grades its own species (c); this entry is the entry on the passage itself. **I_1075** (*Empetrum nigrum*, Dioscorides *empetron*, tag DMM) carries a "Tibetan note, not a tag" on the same line and is deliberately not tagged FMT. **I_139** (*Juniperus communis*, tags EP, AT, DMM, CM, FMT, EF) cross-refers to *J. recurva* as the Tibetan *Shukpa* and says that it is an incense-medicine (*bdug-pa*, *bsang*); a search of both Tibetan texts on disk for "bsang", "bdug", "incense" and "fumigat" found no passage tying any drug to those terms (the only hits are a description of the fragrance of incense in a city, Clark line 111 and Men-Tsee-Khang line 786, and personal names, Men-Tsee-Khang lines 507 and 588), so that statement is not found in the lines read for this entry. **I_1110** (*Juniperus sabina*, savin, tag DMM) is a European species given for a Greek drug. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Sabina* and *Juniperus* are genera of the cypress family (Cupressaceae), whose members carry aromatic volatile oils in the foliage and cones, and that *Empetrum*, the third genus printed, belongs to the heath family (Ericaceae). Clark's three names therefore point to plants of two families. The lines read give no composition for the Tibetan drug, and nothing is stated here about what it contains. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "cures kidney fevers, infectious tissue degeneration, lymph disorders and heals wounds", whose leaf "is recommended for use in disorders of the lower intestine" and whose fruit "is said to be supreme" in three roles, one of them essence extraction (Clark, line 1043), and a drug that cures "hot disorder of the kidneys and the inflammation of muscle tissues" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3438). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only through Clark's first Latin name, and they are not Codex protocols. Kidney, lymph and bowel disorders and wounds need medical care, and the Codex gives no protocol and no dose for them. The Dioscorides entry for savin (1-104, line 4320) records, for a European juniper in another tradition, that taken as a drink with wine the leaves "draw out blood through the urine, and drive out the birth"; that is a reason against gathering any juniper on this entry's strength, and it is not a statement about the Tibetan drug.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation for this drug beyond naming the leaf and the fruit as the parts recommended, and give no quantity, frequency or duration. The essence-extraction clause names the fruit as an ingredient and gives no compound, no method and no dose. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. It is a general fact that the aromatic oil of some junipers is an irritant and, in some species, toxic.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* This is the one drug of the batch whose root-text layer names it for essence extraction: Clark's line 1043 says the fruit "is said to be supreme ... as an essence-extraction ingredient". The cultivation spec records that essence extraction (*bcud len*) is located inside the Four Tantras (Gerke), that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. The clause is one of three roles given for the fruit, and no class heading in the lines read assigns this drug a status. Clark's preface (from line 69, across a page break) says that essence-extraction compounds "enable meditators and yogis to live without intake of gross food", but it names Round-leaf Sundew, Angelica, Polygonatum, Salep orchid, certain flowers, calcite and mineral exudate and does not name juniper. The drug is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, colophon at line 3950), and the Men-Tsee-Khang running text (line 3438) gives it a kidney role only. The framing is therefore therapeutic, with one essence-extraction clause that rests on Clark's rendering and on a Latin name that is uncertain.

**Practitioner's note.** The only cultivation-facing line is the fruit's essence-extraction clause, and it gives no compound, no method and no dose, so a cultivator cannot act on it. The plant's identity is a positional choice among three genera, and several junipers are poisonous. The abstentive answer is the honest one: nothing here supports gathering or taking this plant.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Kidney fevers, lymph disorders and wounds need medical care; the identity is open among species of two families, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. It is a general fact that the foliage of some junipers (savin, *Juniperus sabina*, is the usual example) is toxic, and Dioscorides' savin entry (1-104, line 4320) says a drink of it "drive[s] out the birth", so the genus is not a safe default in pregnancy; this is a statement about the genus and not about the Tibetan drug. No conservation information was read. [USER: verify]
