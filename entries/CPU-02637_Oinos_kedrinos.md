# Oinos kedrinos — ΟΙΝΟΣ ΚΕΔΡΙΝΟΣ (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 5-45); cedar wine, with the juniper, cypress, bay, pine and fir wines made the same way

## *A medicinal wine steeped with the cut wood of cedar, juniper, cypress, bay, pine or fir, with a second recipe from the fruit of the "bigger cedars". The translator's heading lists all six trees, "Cedar Wine, Juniper Wine, Cypress Wine, Bay Wine, Pine Wine, Fir Wine", and gives no single species. The tree is therefore not identified: the text itself says the six are made the same way, and the identification of the "cedar" is of low confidence. [USER: verify]* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

The chapter was opened and read for this entry.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-45, *Oinos Kedrinos* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading at line 10011, text at line 10013), in Book Five among the "Other Wines". The whole chapter reads:

"Cedar, juniper, cypress, bay, pine or fir wines are made the same way. Separate the newly cut wood when it gives out fruit, lay it in a bath in the sun or near the fire so that it may sweat, and then mix one pound of this to four and a half litres of wine. Mix it and leave it alone for two months. Then put it in another jar, and having placed in the sun for a while, put it in smaller jars. We must fill up the jars of wines made like this, for if we do not they grow sour. Nevertheless these medicinal wines are unfit for the healthy. They are all warming, urinary, and somewhat astringent. That of bay is the most warming. A wine is also made from the fruit of the bigger cedars. Mix half a pound of bruised cedar berries to four and a half litres of must and keep it in the sun for four days, and after all this strain it and pour it into another jar."

Read in order, the chapter gives: (1) the kinds, six trees made "the same way"; (2) the material, newly cut wood taken when the tree "gives out fruit", laid in a bath in the sun or near the fire so that it "sweats"; (3) the proportion, one pound of this to four and a half litres of wine; (4) the time, two months, then transfer to another jar, a time in the sun, and smaller jars; (5) a keeping rule, the jars must be kept full or the wine goes sour; (6) a judgement, "these medicinal wines are unfit for the healthy"; (7) the qualities, warming, urinary, somewhat astringent, bay the most warming; (8) a second method from the fruit of the "bigger cedars", half a pound of bruised cedar berries to four and a half litres of must, four days in the sun, then strained.

**Neighbouring chapters.** The three that follow carry related wines. 5-46 *Oinos Arkeuthidites* (juniper wine, translator: *Juniperus communis*) says that "Wine is also made from the berries of juniper trees, as well as from the fruit of the cedar, which has the same effects" (heading line 10014). 5-47 *Kedrites* (heading line 10017) is a cedar-rosin wine: "Wash the cedar rosin with sweet water, and place in each nine-litre container a winecupful apiece [of rosin], and then fill it with must" (translator's suggestions *Cedrus libani*, *Cedrus deodara* as printed); it is "warming and reduces the intensity of symptoms", good for a cough without fever, chest and side disorders, griping, stomach and intestinal ulcers, and a list of other complaints. 5-48 *Oinos Pissites* (heading line 10025, pitch wine) is made of moist pitch and must and is "warming, digesting, cleansing, and purging". Those wines have their own chapters and are not folded into this entry; they are named so a reader can see 5-45 sits at the head of a run of conifer and resin wines. The text does not say which species the "bigger cedars" are. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The text names no constituent. In general terms, the wood and fruit of conifers such as juniper, cedar, pine and fir, and of bay and cypress, contain volatile terpene oils and resins, and berries of juniper contain essential oil. Steeping them in wine would transfer some of these oils and the alcohol-soluble resin to the liquid. This is a general statement about the plants and about wine as a solvent, not a finding about the ancient drink; which species was used, and in what amount, is not known from the text. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a recommended practice.**

- **What the text says it does.** The wines are "warming, urinary, and somewhat astringent"; bay is "the most warming". They are "medicinal" and "unfit for the healthy".
- **What the text does not say.** No illness is named in this chapter (the illnesses are given in the next chapter, 5-47, for the cedar-rosin wine, and cannot be transferred to this one), no dose, and no duration of use.

The Codex does not translate the text's terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend an infused resinous wine. Conifer and bay infusions made at home can be strong and vary widely, and the text's own judgement that they are unfit for the healthy is the right note to keep.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparation is given in the chapter: wood "newly cut ... when it gives out fruit", sweated in the sun or by the fire; "one pound of this to four and a half litres of wine"; two months standing; moved to another jar, sunned briefly, then moved to smaller jars that must be kept full; and the cedar berry variant, "half a pound of bruised cedar berries to four and a half litres of must" for four days in the sun, strained (line 10013). The units are the translator's and no conversion is attempted. No dose for drinking is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any amount from this entry as a dose, and do not attempt the preparation.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Juniper berries, pine and fir needles and resin, bay leaf and cypress contain terpenes and resins that are used as flavourings and, in some preparations, as traditional diuretics or astringents. Several conifer and cypress-family oils are irritant or toxic in quantity, and the identity of the "cedar" is uncertain, since true cedars, junipers (sometimes called cedars) and cypress-family trees differ. The text's "urinary" is in kind with the diuretic reputation of juniper in general; the other terms have no modern equivalent and are not translated into one. ### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body or vital system, and nothing in 5-45 links these wines to long life, constitution or practice. The chapter says the opposite of a tonic: the wines are "medicinal" and "unfit for the healthy". What it offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention to making: the choice of wood freshly cut at fruiting, the sweating of the wood, the ratio, the standing time, and the care to fill the jars so that the wine does not turn sour. That is provenance and technique, and it is as far as the text goes.

**Practitioner's note.** A group of resinous wines with a plain warning attached: they are medicines, not drinks for the healthy. There is no cultivation status attested, and the Dioscoridean answer to "should I take this for wellbeing" is no.

### Contraindications

- **Healthy people.** The text states these wines are "unfit for the healthy" (line 10013).
- **Alcohol.** The wine is alcoholic: unsuitable for children, in pregnancy and lactation, or for anyone avoiding alcohol.
- **Conifer and juniper material.** Juniper, cypress-family and some conifer oils are irritant and are generally avoided in pregnancy and in kidney disease. This is a general safety point and not from the text. [USER: verify]
- **Identity.** The tree is not fixed by the text, which lists six. Do not substitute any plant for another on the strength of this entry.
- **Sourness and spoilage.** The text warns that unfilled jars grow sour.
