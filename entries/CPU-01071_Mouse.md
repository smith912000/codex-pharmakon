# Mouse — Mues (House Mouse) / Mouse, Tail-of-a-Mouse, Mouse Oil (Bryan's Ebers)
## *The house mouse,* Mus musculus *(the translator's suggestion for Dioscorides 2-74); Bryan gives only "Mouse"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP, DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The English text on disk is C. P. Bryan's 1930 *The Papyrus Ebers*, a translation with summaries, with an Introduction by Professor G. Elliot Smith (title page, `Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 52); the Egyptian words are not printed. The spine's list row (`_sources/ebers.csv` line 136, "Mouse", id uncertain: yes) cites "Ghalioungui 1987 trans., Glossary I (Medicaments)", which is not on disk and was not read. The text loci are these. Bryan's Chapter VII lists "Mouse" among the animals "whose identity is not in doubt" (line 1392; `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` row "Mouse"). Bryan's summary of the wilder prescriptions mentions "a draught composed of the one-thirty-second part of the Tail-of-a-Mouse with Honey, one-third, for cooling the anus" (line 1380). The prescription itself is under the anal complaints: "Remedy to cool the anus: Onion-meal, Tail-of-a-Mouse, Honey, Water: Strain and take for four days" (lines 2000-2020; the quantity figures in the OCR are garbled). A salve "to make supple the hardenings" is made of Hog's Fat, Oil-of-Worms, Oil-of-the-abxersu-animal, "Mouse Oil" and Cat's Oil, "Gather into one and anoint therewith" (line 2191). Two uses go the other way: "Cover the wall and the floor with Mice-excrement and with their urine. That will prevent the durra being eaten" in the granary, a remedy "to prevent the rodents eating durra" (lines 4236-4240), and a cat's fat smeared on clothes "to keep mice away from clothes" (line 4176; the cat's fat is the remedy there, not the mouse). The Introduction by Elliot Smith has a passage on the mouse (lines 592-610): in a Predynastic cemetery in Upper Egypt, he writes, he found "in the stomachs of small children who had died nearly sixty centuries ago the remains of mice which had been skinned and eaten just before their death"; "There is reason to believe that the faith in the mouse as a life-giving substance was due to the fact that after the inundation, when the mud left by the falling river became dried by the rays of the sun and mice were seen to emerge from these cracks, the Egyptians of sixty centuries ago believed, as their modern representatives believe to-day, that these mice were actually formed from the mud deposited by the river", and so "these mice were regarded as one of the most potent elixirs of life, and therefore appropriate remedies for saving life under desperate circumstances." That is Elliot Smith's 1930 argument, from burial finds and from what he reports of modern Egyptian belief. It is not a passage of the papyrus. [USER: verify] against current Egyptology.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-74, *Mues*, "Mus musculus — House Mouse" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 5232; the heading is printed inline). The whole chapter: "It is declared that cut-apart house mice are usefully applied to those touched by scorpions, and that eaten roasted they dry the spittle in the mouths of children." The previous chapter, 2-73, is the "Myogale species — Shrew Mouse": "The shrewmouse (cut apart and applied) is a remedy for its own bites" (line 5231; the shrew mouse also appears at line 6129). Mouse dung is in the chapter on dung, 2-98 (heading at line 5366; the mouse sentences are near its end): "The dung of mice (pounded into small pieces with vinegar and rubbed on) cures baldness. Taken as a drink with frankincense and honeyed wine it expels urinary stones. Mouse dung given to children [as a suppository] encourages the bowels to evacuate." Dioscorides's opening verb, "It is declared", marks the scorpion and the children's-spittle uses as report.

### Active Components

The mouse is a small rodent whose body, as eaten whole, is protein, fat and bone with some minerals. The tail is mostly skin, tendon and bone. Mouse oil is body fat, and mouse droppings are digested plant matter with gut bacteria. The texts name no active component. No quantities are given here because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources record the mouse (whole, tail, oil, dung) as an ingredient and Dioscorides records it as an application for scorpion stings and as a food for children's drooling. The Codex has no protocol for any of this and offers none: wild and house mice carry disease (see Contraindications); no dose is given; using the animal means killing it; and the use for children, and "a mouse to a sick child in extremis" as Elliot Smith puts it, is dangerous. A scorpion sting needs medical care in the places where scorpions are dangerous.

### Preparation & Dosing

Egyptian: the tail of a mouse, with onion-meal, honey and water, strained and taken for four days (Bryan lines 2000-2020; his summary at line 1380 gives the tail as "one-thirty-second" and the honey "one-third"; the proportions are as OCR'd and uncertain). Greek: the mice "cut apart" and applied to a scorpion sting; the mice "eaten roasted" for children's drooling; dung pounded with vinegar for baldness (2-74, 2-98). No dose is given in the sources retrieved beyond the Egyptian proportions. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. House mice carry and spread *Salmonella*, leptospirosis, hantaviruses (urine, droppings and nesting material), lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus, tapeworms and fleas that carry other pathogens. No effect of mouse tail, oil or dung on anal complaints, baldness, bladder stones or constipation is established. A scorpion sting is treated by local care and, where the species is dangerous, antivenom; applying a split mouse is not a treatment. No clinical studies of the traditional uses were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** Bryan's translation prints no energetic quality for the mouse; the corpus is organised by complaint. The prescription to "cool the anus" is a name for the complaint treated, and not a quality assigned to the tail.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** None is printed for the mouse in 2-74, which gives uses only.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(b) Framed; the status is a modern inference.* The papyrus passages read use the mouse as an ingredient for a defined complaint: the tail in a draught to cool the anus, the oil in a salve for hardenings. They say nothing about what it does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. The one cultivation-flavoured statement is Elliot Smith's, in the Introduction of 1930 (lines 592-610): that mice were "one of the most potent elixirs of life" to the Egyptians, because they were thought to arise from the Nile mud. He builds this on Predynastic burial finds and on belief he says modern Egyptians share. It is an inference by one scholar and is not a text of the papyrus, and it is a modern, early-twentieth-century reading; no source read states it for the Ebers prescriptions. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); Bryan reports no recitation with the mouse remedies read, and since he summarises, that is not proof the papyrus gives none. Elliot Smith himself calls the giving of a mouse "a magical procedure", while holding that it began on "rational grounds". No deity is attributed. [USER: verify]

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. The chapters are short and carry the hedge "It is declared"; they give a use for each form (cut-apart mouse on a sting, roasted mouse for children's drooling, dung for baldness and stones). Recording them is not endorsing them. The chapter assigns no status beyond the therapeutic.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do. The mouse is a sourced entry, but the only "life-giving" claim for it is a modern scholar's inference about Egypt, which is not in the papyrus text and not in Dioscorides. A cultivator can take from the entry the fact that two ancient traditions used the animal, and that one modern scholar reads the Egyptian use as a ritual of life; there is no practice to follow.

### Contraindications

- **Disease.** Mice carry *Salmonella*, leptospirosis, hantavirus and other infections; handling their bodies, droppings or urine is a health hazard. Never feed one to a child. [USER: verify specifics]
- **Scorpion stings.** Seek medical care; do not apply an animal.
- **Dose unknown.** No dose is given for most uses in the sources read.
- **Ethics and law.** Using the animal means killing it; laboratory and pet mice are protected by animal-welfare law in many places.
