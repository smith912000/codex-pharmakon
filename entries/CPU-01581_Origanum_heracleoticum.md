# Origanum heracleoticum — Origanos Erakleotike, the Heracleotic origanum, also called conila (Dioscorides 3-32)

## *Origanum heracleoticum [Loudon] — "Winter-sweet Marjoram", the single name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-32* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 6809) prints one name only, attributed to Loudon, with the English "Winter-sweet Marjoram". The chapter's own Greek name means "Heracleotic origanum", and the text gives no place for the epithet. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-32. ORIGANOS ERAKLEOTIKE*, heading at line 6808, text at line 6810, in Book Three among the roots of the *akanthoda*. The whole chapter reads: "Origanum heracleoticum (also called conila) has a leaf similar to hyssop [3-30], and a tuft not of a round shape but (as it were) divided, and on the tops of the sprigs, the seed, not thick. It is warming; as a result a decoction of it (taken as a drink with wine) is good for those bitten by poisonous beasts. It is given as an antidote with passum [raisin wine] for those who have taken a drink of hemlock or meconium [4-65], and with vinegar and honey for those who have taken a drink of gypsum or ephemerum [4-85]. For convulsions, hernia, and dropsy it is eaten with a fig. It is dried and the amount of an acetabulum [vinegar cruet] taken in a drink with honey and water to expel black (fluids) through the bowels. Licked in with honey it induces the menstrual flow and cures coughs. A decoction of it in a bath is good for prurigo [chronic itching], psoriasis and jaundice. The juice of the green herb cures tonsils, [inflammation of the] uvula, and apthae [aptha — infant thrush or candidiasis]. Dropped in with oil irinum [1-66] it purges through the nostrils. With milk it also soothes earache. A vomitory medicine is made from it with onions and rhus [1-147], all of them being sunned in the burning heat under the dog [in summer] in a brass copper jar for forty days. The herb scattered under[foot] expels snakes."

The chapter is a full one. It describes the plant briefly: a leaf like hyssop (chapter 3-30, *USSOPOS*, heading at line 6792), a tuft "not of a round shape but (as it were) divided", and a thin seed on the tops of the sprigs. It gives one quality, "It is warming", and draws from it a first use, "as a result", in a decoction drunk with wine for those bitten by poisonous beasts. It then gives, in its own order, an antidote (with passum, for hemlock or *meconium* poisoning; with vinegar and honey, for gypsum or *ephemerum*), a food-and-medicine use (eaten with a fig, for convulsions, hernia and dropsy), a purge (an *acetabulum* drunk in honey and water, "to expel black (fluids) through the bowels"), licked in with honey for the menstrual flow and coughs, a bath decoction for itching, psoriasis and jaundice, a juice for the tonsils, uvula and *apthae*, a nasal drop with *irinum* (chapter 1-66), and a milk preparation for earache. The bracketed glosses (for example "chronic itching", "infant thrush or candidiasis") are the translators'. The chapter ends with a long vomitory medicine made with onions and *rhus* (chapter 1-147) and sunned for forty days in a brass copper jar, and with a note that the herb "scattered under[foot] expels snakes". It prints one alternative name, *conila*, in its first words. The names that follow the previous chapter's text at line 6807 ("syncliopa, alcibiades, pancration or styphonia", the Egyptian "suphlo", the Magi's "oculus pythonis" and the Roman "schiolebina") belong to 3-31 *STOICHAS* (line 6796) and are not read into this row.

**The chapters around it.** 3-33 *ORIGANOS ONITIS* (line 6811) says of the next origanum that it "can do the same things as the Heracleotic [3-32]" but "is not altogether as effective". 3-34 *AGRIORIGANOS* (the translators' wild marjoram) and 3-35 *TRAGORIGANOS* are separate chapters, which are not read into this row.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter id 3-32 and for the names *heracleoticum*, *erakleotike* and *conila* found no existing entry on this chapter. I_433 and I_177 (both *Origanum vulgare*) and I_464 (*Origanum majorana*) speak of Dioscorides's origanum chapters in general terms and cite no chapter number, so whether either draws on 3-32 cannot be told from the entry text. I_1146 (*Origanum onitis*, chapter 3-33), written in the same batch, treats the next chapter. No entry is duplicated.

### Active Components

The composition of the plant Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, plants of the mint family (Lamiaceae) that are strongly aromatic, among them the marjorams and oreganos, owe their smell to volatile terpenoid oils, and the phenols carvacrol and thymol are well known constituents of some oreganos. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a warming herb given against poisonous bites and as an antidote to hemlock, *meconium*, gypsum and *ephemerum* poisoning, and many other uses. The Codex gives no protocol for any of these. The antidotes are statements of the text and are not to be relied on: poisoning is a medical emergency. The identification is the translators' single suggestion.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives many preparations, with locus (line 6810): a decoction drunk with wine; the herb with passum, or with vinegar and honey, as an antidote; the herb "eaten with a fig"; the dried herb, "the amount of an acetabulum [vinegar cruet] taken in a drink with honey and water"; the herb licked in with honey; a decoction used in a bath; the juice of the green herb; the juice dropped in with *irinum* oil; the herb with milk; and the vomitory medicine with onions and *rhus*, sunned for forty days. The only quantity printed is the *acetabulum* in the dried-herb purge; the translators' gloss is "vinegar cruet", and the modern equivalent of that measure was not looked up. ### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that a herb drunk with wine is not an antidote to hemlock or to other poisons, and that jaundice, hernia and dropsy call for medical assessment. The identification is a translators' suggestion of a single name, so the chapter does not show that the plant it describes is *Origanum heracleoticum* in the modern sense.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one quality, "It is warming", and draws its first use from it ("as a result"). It prints no taste, and no cooling, drying, moistening or astringent quality for 3-32.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to tell the plant (a hyssop-like leaf, a divided tuft, a thin seed on the sprig tops), its one named quality (warming), and a recipe that fixes a season and a time, as the vomitory medicine is sunned "under the dog [in summer]" for forty days. The herb scattered underfoot "expels snakes" is the nearest thing to a protective use; the text states it as a fact of the herb and gives no ritual, and the Codex records it and does not endorse it. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a warming medicinal herb. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a warming herb used as an antidote and for many ailments. The useful lesson is Dioscorides' habit of tying a use to a quality ("It is warming; as a result"), and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Poisoning (including hemlock) is an emergency; this chapter is no substitute for emergency care. The vomitory medicine and the purge are of unknown strength. Jaundice, hernia, dropsy and convulsions need medical assessment. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
