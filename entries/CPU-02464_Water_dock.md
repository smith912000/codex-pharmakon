# Water dock — barṭāniqī (برطانيقي), the "Britannic" herb

## *Water dock (the herb* Britannica *of the Greek herbals, perhaps* Rumex aquaticus *or* R. hydrolapathum*); the identification is the modern one, made from the name alone, and is of low confidence, because Avicenna's entry gives only two hearsay descriptions and treats a sour-leaved herb*  | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** CM

---

### Origin & Tradition

Water dock is named only by Avicenna's *Canon* among the texts on disk, under the name *barṭāniqī*. It is a single-text entry, and the text is five lines. The name is the Arabic form of a Greek herb name "of Britain". A quick search of the Dioscorides text on disk under three spellings of "Britannic" found nothing, so the connection with the spine's *Rumex* entries (for example I_690, I_720) is not shown and no merge is proposed.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** The *Canon* (Book II, entry *barṭāniqī*, Arabic text line 11249) gives the identity only as report: "it was said that it is *bustān afrūz*, and it was said that its leaf resembles the leaf of the wild sorrel (*ḥāmiḍ barrī*) but is nearer to black and finer" (قيل أنه بستان أفروز وقيل أن ورقه يشبه ورق الحامض البري لكنه أقرب إلى السواد وأحسن). I have not identified *bustān afrūz* with a plant. Its action is: "its leaf is extremely astringent" (قابض في غاية). It "heals wounds and ulcers." For the head: "its juice is the best thing for old ulcers of the mouth and for thrush (*qulāʿ*), and one should make a rob (*rubb*) of it that benefits thrush with the utmost benefit" (lines 11252-11254).

### Active Components

Docks (*Rumex*) contain tannins and anthraquinone glycosides in the root, and oxalates in the leaf of the sorrel-type species. This is general botany and is not stated by the *Canon*. If the *barṭāniqī* of the entry is a sorrel-leaved dock, the astringency noted by Avicenna fits tannin, but that identification is not shown. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The *Canon*'s two uses are as an astringent wound-healer and as a mouth medicine for chronic mouth ulcers and thrush, taken as a juice or a rob. It is not offered as a modern recommendation.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives one preparation: the juice (*ʿaṣāra*), made into a rob (a concentrated juice, *rubb*) for thrush (line 11253-11254). No dose or duration is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Tannin-rich astringents are known generally to tighten tissue and are used in mouth washes. That is general pharmacology. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **CM (Avicenna), line 11252.** Astringent "in the extreme" (*fī ghāyat*). No temperament degree is given. No other tradition is tagged.

### Cultivation Register

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent.* The entry is an identification by report and a mouth-ulcer remedy. It makes no statement about the vital faculties, the innate heat or the radical moisture, and gives no cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing is asked of a cultivator. The entry is honest about not knowing what the plant is ("it was said…, and it was said…"), and that is the lesson to keep: a plant known only by a transmitted name should be held as a name.

### Contraindications

- **Identification.** The plant is not secure. Do not take unidentified docks internally.
- **Oxalates.** Sorrel- and dock-type leaves contain oxalic acid; large amounts are harmful, especially to people prone to kidney stones. General knowledge, not in the text.
- **Mouth ulcers.** Chronic mouth ulcers need medical examination.
