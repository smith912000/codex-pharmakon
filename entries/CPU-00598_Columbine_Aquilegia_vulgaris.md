# Columbine — Granny's Bonnet, Culverwort, Aquileia (Latin herbals), European Columbine

## *Aquilegia vulgaris* L. | Family: Ranunculaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry in the second degree (Galenic), with a subtle penetrating, cleansing quality
**Training Phase:** Purification

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian (AT):** Cuneiform materia-medica tablets of the Nineveh/Kuyunjik tradition catalogue a large flora of the Mesopotamian and Zagros foothills, where wild *Aquilegia* species grow. The Assyrian herbal record is fragmentary and identifications are frequently uncertain, so columbine appears in the tradition-stream more as a regional mountain herb of the "cooling, cleansing" class than as a securely named single drug. It is included here as an attested regional plant of the cuneiform pharmacopoeia rather than as a precisely translated entry, and no specific therapeutic claim should be built on the Assyrian layer alone.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** Greek and later Byzantine commentators associated columbine with the cooling, astringent mountain herbs of the *De Materia Medica* tradition. The plant became firmly fixed in the European written record through the medieval and Renaissance herbals that transmitted Dioscoridean doctrine — Hildegard of Bingen, and later Gerard and Culpeper — who used the leaves and seeds cautiously for sore throats, swollen glands, and as a "cleansing" agent. These are late transmissions of the classical stream, and their claims are traditional, not clinically validated.

**Avicenna (CM):** The *Qanun* tradition of Unani medicine places herbs of this cooling-drying character in the management of hot, inflammatory swellings and eruptive fevers. Columbine's use in the Persianate stream is consistent with its Galenic classification as a cold-dry herb applied to "hot" complaints.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The flower's five nectar-spurs were read in medieval Europe as doves clustered around a cup (Latin *columba*, dove), giving both the common name and a Marian and Holy-Spirit symbolism. In the Kenetica frame this dove-and-cup image is read as *guarded descent*: a plant that offers a cooling, cleansing gift but conceals a poison in its seed and root. The symbolism ties directly to its real pharmacology — a herb whose surface beauty masks cyanogenic and irritant chemistry, and which therefore teaches the Purification-phase lesson that not every offered draught is safe to drink.

### Active Components

- **Cyanogenic glycosides** — the fresh plant, and especially the seeds and roots, can liberate hydrogen cyanide on tissue damage; this is the principal acute hazard.
- **Flavonoids and flavone C-glycosides** — congeneric *Aquilegia* species yield vitexin, isovitexin, orientin, isoorientin and related flavones (documented in *A. pubiflora*), the likely basis of antioxidant and antimicrobial activity.
- **Phenolic acids** — ferulic, sinapic and chlorogenic acids reported in the genus.
- **Isoquinoline alkaloids (e.g., magnoflorine)** — characteristic of Ranunculaceae, contributing to bitterness and toxicity.
- **Triterpenoid saponins** — contributing to the irritant, "cleansing" surfactant quality of leaf preparations.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Columbine is placed in the **Purification** phase strictly as a *doctrinal and topical-symbolic* teaching plant, not an ingested one. Its historical role — cooling hot swellings of the throat and glands, and cleansing the skin — maps onto the phase's theme of clearing surface heat and eruption. In practice the Kenetica protocol treats columbine as a **study specimen and external-reference herb only**: it is used to teach recognition of guarded/toxic flora and the humoral logic of cold-dry cleansers. No internal Kenetica preparation is advanced for general practitioners.

### Scientific Correlation

Direct pharmacological study of *Aquilegia vulgaris* itself is sparse; most data come from congeners and mixed screens.

- According to PubMed, Jan et al. (2021), *BMC Complementary Medicine and Therapies*, performed HPLC phytochemical analysis and in-vitro bioactivity screening of *Aquilegia pubiflora* leaf extracts, identifying vitexin, isovitexin, orientin, isoorientin, ferulic, sinapic and chlorogenic acids, and reporting antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and cytotoxic (HepG2) activity — evidence that the genus carries genuine flavonoid-driven bioactivity. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-021-03333-y
- According to PubMed, Giordani et al. (2019), *Natural Product Research*, screened Mongolian medicinal plant extracts (including an *Aquilegia* species) for antifungal activity against *Candida*, dermatophytes and *Malassezia*, attributing activity to flavones such as luteolin and apigenin and to rutin. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2019.1610960

**Evidence grade: Preclinical / Traditional.** In-vitro genus-level data support antimicrobial and antioxidant potential, but there are no clinical trials of *A. vulgaris*, and no toxicological work establishing a safe ingested dose. All internal-use claims remain traditional and unverified.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Cold and dry (second degree); cleansing, mildly astringent; indicated in humoral doctrine for hot, inflammatory swellings.
- **Ayurveda:** Not a classical Ayurvedic dravya; by analogy its bitter-astringent profile would read as *tikta/kashaya rasa*, *sheeta virya* (cooling potency), *katu vipaka* — offered only as a cross-system mapping, not an attested Ayurvedic use.
- **TCM:** No standard Zhong Yao identity; not to be confused with any Chinese official drug.

### Contraindications

⚠️ **TOXIC PLANT — SERIOUS HAZARD. This entry documents a historically poisonous herb; it is NOT a recommendation to ingest.**

- **Cyanogenic risk:** The fresh plant, and especially the **seeds and roots**, contain cyanogenic constituents that can release hydrogen cyanide. Ingestion has historically caused poisoning and can be fatal. **Do not eat any part of the fresh plant, seeds, or roots.**
- **Irritant alkaloids and saponins:** Ranunculaceae isoquinoline alkaloids and saponins can cause mucosal and gastrointestinal irritation, vomiting, and cardiovascular effects.
- **Absolutely contraindicated** in pregnancy, lactation, in children, and in anyone with cardiac or hepatic compromise.
- **No self-administration.** Handle dried material with gloves; keep seeds away from children and animals.
- If ingestion is suspected, treat as a cyanide/plant-poisoning emergency and seek immediate medical care.
