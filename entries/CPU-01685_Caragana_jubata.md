# Caragana jubata — mdzo.mo, Zhomo (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Caragana jubata Poir, the first of two binomials Clark 1995 prints for mdzo.mo (with Caragana gerardiana); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text prints the name only as "Zhomo", without a Latin name* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts, and under this binomial only by Clark.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. Both Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the line prints two, of which the row takes the first (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, row 186). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1096).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads:

"mdzo.mo: Caragana jubata Poir: Caragana gerardiana: [is a thorny tree, larger than gra.ma and covered with white ‘fur’. It has a yellow flower and heartwood similar to red sandalwood.] Caragana dissolves blood clots and cures blood fever."

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the description (thorny tree, fur, flower, heartwood) and the comparison with *gra.ma*. Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the two Latin names, which are Clark's own identifications and not the root text's, and the clause "dissolves blood clots and cures blood fever", in which the word "Caragana" is Clark's own for the drug. The line prints no taste, no power and no number of names.

*Clark 1995, the compounding chapter (line 1210).* In chapter 21 (heading at line 1205), among the classes of medicines that cure specific disorders, the text reads, with no square brackets: "The class of medicines which cure blood disorders consists of Red Sandalwood, Caraganajubata, Catechu tree, Picrorhiza, Vasaka (Malabar Nut tree), Emblic Myrobalan, Corydalis edulis, Pterocephalus hookeri, Madder and Bastard Teak." "Caraganajubata" is printed as one word. The Tibetan name is not given in the line, and the Codex reads it as *mdzo.mo* from the binomial. Clark's line 1096 and this one agree that the drug belongs to blood.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (lines 3457 and 3598).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. In chapter 20, in the section "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (it opens at line 3428), line 3457 says: "Zhomo is a blood thinner and cures hot disorder associated with the blood." In chapter 21 (the groups and method of compounding, which opens at about line 3585), line 3598 says: "Tsendhen marpo, zhomo, Sengdeng, Honglen, Bhashaka, Kyurura, Rekon, Pang tsi dhowo, Tsod and Tshoe are the group of medicines that cure blood disorders." The printed index lists "Zhomo 210" (line 7558). The Men-Tsee-Khang text gives no Latin name for "Zhomo". The Codex reads "Zhomo" as the same name as *mdzo.mo* from the sound, from the agreement with Clark's "blood fever" and "blood clots", and from the two lists of blood medicines, in which the second place of Clark's list (the Caragana) and the second place of the Men-Tsee-Khang list ("zhomo") correspond, and "Honglen" (Clark's *hong.len*, Picrorhiza) and "Pang tsi dhowo" (Clark's *spang.rtsi.do.wo*, *Pterocephalus hookeri*) recur in both. The text does not say so. The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints no "Zhomo", so the glossary gives it no Latin name. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Caragana*, *mdzo.mo*, *Zhomo* and "Pea shrub" found no entry on this plant. ### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Caragana* is a genus of thorny shrubs of the pea family (Fabaceae). Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained, which may be another species of the genus. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "dissolves blood clots and cures blood fever" (Clark, line 1096), that is placed among medicines that cure blood disorders (Clark, line 1210; Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3598), and that "is a blood thinner and cures hot disorder associated with the blood" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3457). These are the texts' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. It is a general, well-established fact that a medicine said to thin the blood or dissolve clots could interact with anticoagulant drugs and with bleeding disorders, and the Codex draws no conclusion for this drug from that.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's line 1096 prints no taste, no power and no post-digestive taste. The Men-Tsee-Khang sentences print no quality, only that the drug cures "hot disorder associated with the blood". For this drug no quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Tibetan texts class this drug by what it cures, which is blood fever and blood disorders, and it stands in the blood-disorder classes of chapter 21 in both texts. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this drug places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: a search of both Tibetan texts for *mdzo.mo*, "Zhomo" and *Caragana* returned only the lines above and the index and glossary entries. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a blood drug in the Tibetan texts, and its Latin name is not settled. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. The identity is uncertain, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. A drug said to thin the blood is not for use alongside anticoagulants or in bleeding disorders without medical advice, and fevers and blood disorders need medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
