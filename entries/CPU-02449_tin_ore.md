*Clark 1995* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). Chapter 20 lists the stone medicines in part (2) (line 816). The entry (line 841) reads:

By the bracket convention of Clark's introduction (line 82; square brackets carry the commentaries), the tantra's own words are the closing ones, "promotes flesh growth". **What is not given.** In the passages read, neither text gives a taste, potency or post-digestive taste for the ore by itself, a dose, a preparation (the bell-metal making is a metalworking use, not a medicine) or a formula containing it.

**Identity.** "Stannic" is the translator's word and means tin-bearing. The commentary's two clues (mixed with copper to make bell metal; looks like silver ore) are consistent with a tin ore, since bell metal is a copper-tin alloy (a general fact, not read in the texts). The usual tin ore is cassiterite, tin dioxide; the texts do not name it. The entry is filed as "tin ore, species unresolved". [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None is stated in the texts. As a general fact, cassiterite is tin dioxide, which is insoluble, and tin ores commonly occur with other metal minerals; tin metal and its oxides are of low toxicity, but ores may carry arsenic, lead and other metals. No composition is asserted for the ore the tantra means.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.** Both texts record the ore as promoting the growth or healing of flesh. A raw metal ore of unknown composition should not be swallowed or put on wounds, and wound healing needs proper care. The Codex supplies no modern protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources retrieved give no preparation for the ore as a medicine. The only process named is the mixing of the ore with copper to make bell metal (Clark's bracket, line 841), which is metalworking and not a medicine. No form, route, measure or duration is given.
