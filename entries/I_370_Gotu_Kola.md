# I-370 — Centella asiatica · Gotu Kola

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Centella asiatica* (L.) Urb.  
**Synonyms:** *Hydrocotyle asiatica* L.; *Trisanthus cochinchinensis* Lour.  
**Family:** Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)  
**Common names:** Gotu kola (EN — from Sinhalese *goṭu kola*); Indian pennywort (EN); Asiatic pennywort (EN); brahmi (EN/SA — **NOTE: significant naming confusion** — see below); fo-ti (EN colloquial — NOT the same as *Polygonum multiflorum* fo-ti; confusion persists); Wassernabel (DE); hydrocotyle asiatique (FR); hierba de clavo (ES); ji xue cao [積雪草] (TCM, "accumulated snow herb"); mandookaparni / mandukaparni [मण्डूकपर्णी] (SA/Ayurveda, "frog leaf"); brahmi (Ayurveda — shared with *Bacopa monnieri* in some regions; see note); pegagan (Malay/Indonesian); bua bok [บัวบก] (TH); gotukola (SI); thankuni (BN)

**CRITICAL NAMING DISAMBIGUATION — "brahmi":**
In Ayurvedic tradition, the term *brahmi* ("relating to Brahma" — supreme consciousness herb) applies to TWO DISTINCT PLANTS depending on geographic region:
- In South India and Sri Lanka: *brahmi* = *Centella asiatica* (gotu kola) — this entry
- In North India: *brahmi* = *Bacopa monnieri* (water hyssop; Plantaginaceae) — a different plant with partially overlapping but distinct phytochemistry

Both *C. asiatica* and *B. monnieri* are recognized as *medhya rasayana* (cognitive rejuvenating adaptogens) in Ayurveda, and both are sold under "brahmi" labeling in global herbal markets — creating widespread consumer and clinical confusion. They are pharmacologically complementary but not identical. When "brahmi" appears in research or traditional texts, species identification is required.

**Parts used:** Fresh or dried aerial parts (leaves and stems); standardized extracts (TECA — Titrated Extract of Centella asiatica; also TTFCA — Tri-Triterpene Fraction of Centella asiatica)  
**Habitat & distribution:** Perennial; native to tropical and subtropical regions of Asia, Africa, and the Pacific; grows in wetland margins, rice paddies, streambanks, and moist soils at low to moderate altitude; pantropical distribution; widely naturalized; India, Sri Lanka, Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, China, Madagascar are major growing regions  
**Physical characteristics:** Small creeping herb; distinctive round "pennywort" leaves (coin-shaped, hence "pennywort") on petioles 5–15 cm; purple-pink flowers in small umbels; stoloniferous growth along water margins

## 3. Convergence Analysis

Gotu kola's formal 2/9 convergence score understates its actual cross-cultural pharmacological consensus. The Chinese and Indian formal traditions both confirm it clearly, and African traditional medicine across multiple unconnected cultures independently arrived at the wound-healing and cognitive-enhancement applications. The total cross-cultural convergence, properly accounting for all documented traditional systems, approximates 5–6 independent traditions — all agreeing on wound healing as the primary application and cognitive support as secondary.

The pharmacological case for gotu kola is among the most mechanistically elegant in the Codex: **triterpene saponins (asiaticoside, madecassoside) stimulate TGF-β1-mediated collagen synthesis** — providing a direct molecular explanation for the wound-healing traditional use that would have been entirely unverifiable by any classical pharmacologist. The mechanism predicts the traditional observation perfectly.

Gotu kola's **pharmaceutical development arc** is directly parallel to celery/NBP and broccoli/sulforaphane:
- Traditional wound-healing use (Ayurveda, TCM, African medicine) → 20th century European phytotherapy interest → triterpene isolation → mechanism elucidation → TECA/TTFCA pharmaceutical preparations → Commission E and European regulatory approval → MADECASSOL® topical wound product (widely used in Europe and Asia for keloids, burns, and surgical wounds)

The cognitive/nootropic claim represents the second major application trajectory: *medhya rasayana* (Ayurvedic cognitive rejuvenating) → modern academic interest in BDNF enhancement, acetylcholinesterase inhibition, and HPA adaptogenic effects → multiple clinical trials in healthy volunteers and mild cognitive impairment populations. Evidence is Level B but consistent in direction.

The standardized pharmaceutical preparation used in most clinical trials. Standard specification: 40% asiaticoside + 29–30% asiatic acid + 29–30% madecassic acid + 1% madecasside (by HPLC). This standardization enables pharmacological reproducibility across studies.

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**Wound Healing — Primary Mechanism:**
Asiaticoside → upon hydrolysis: asiatic acid → (1) TGF-β1 (transforming growth factor beta-1) upregulation → fibroblast proliferation → collagen type I and III synthesis enhancement → wound matrix formation; (2) VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor) upregulation → angiogenesis → vascularization of wound bed; (3) Epithelialization acceleration → keratinocyte migration at wound margins. Madecassoside provides parallel anti-inflammatory suppression (TNF-α, IL-1β, COX-2 inhibition) reducing destructive inflammatory phase while asiaticoside promotes constructive reparative phase. The combined action (anti-inflammatory + pro-reparative) mirrors the pharmacological rationale for modern advanced wound care dressings.

**Antifibrotic (keloid prevention):**
At the specific concentrations in TECA preparations, asiaticoside → paradoxically normalizes collagen synthesis in keloid fibroblasts (which over-synthesize collagen) → prevents excessive scar formation. The same TGF-β1 pathway that promotes wound healing in normal tissue restrains overactive collagen deposition in keloid tissue — a concentration-dependent bidirectional regulation that explains TECA's use in BOTH wound healing (initiating repair) AND keloid/hypertrophic scar prevention (restraining excess repair).

**Venous Insufficiency (CVI) — Second Major Mechanism:**
Triterpene saponins → collagen synthesis and stability in vascular wall connective tissue → strengthening of capillary and venous wall → reduced capillary permeability → reduced edema. Asiatic acid → inhibits leukocyte-endothelial adhesion in inflamed venous wall → reduces venous inflammatory cycle. This mechanism explains TECA's well-evidenced effect on chronic venous insufficiency (CVI) symptoms: leg edema, heaviness, pain, fatigue, varicosities.

**Cognitive / Neuroprotective:**
Multiple parallel mechanisms:
1. Asiatic acid → NRF2 pathway activation → antioxidant enzyme upregulation (GPx, SOD, catalase) → neuronal oxidative stress reduction
2. Asiaticoside → BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) upregulation → neurotrophic support → dendritic arborization and synaptic density
3. Rosmarinic acid → acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibition → enhanced cholinergic transmission → learning and memory support
4. GABA modulation: possible anxiolytic pathway (consistent with traditional calming use)
5. HPA axis regulation: adaptogenic suppression of cortisol hypersecretion under chronic stress

**Anti-inflammatory:**
Madecassoside → IκBα stabilization → NF-κB nuclear translocation inhibition → TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 suppression. COX-2 inhibition. In parallel, asiaticoside-derived madecassic acid activates PPARγ → anti-inflammatory transcriptional program.

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**Commission E approved:**
Commission E has a monograph recognizing *Centella asiatica* extract (aerial parts) for supportive treatment of wounds and improvement of venous insufficiency.

**MADECASSOL® (TTFCA/TECA topical and oral):**
Widely used pharmaceutical formulation (Bayer and others) for wound healing, keloid prevention, and chronic venous insufficiency in Europe and Asia. One of the most commercially significant plant-based pharmaceutical products globally.

**Level A (meta-analysis — CVI):**
- **Pointel JP et al. 1987** (*Angiology*): multicenter RCT of TECA in CVI; significant reduction in edema, heaviness, pain vs placebo
- **Cochrane systematic review:** Incandela L et al. 2001 — TECA significantly reduced microangiopathy symptoms and edema in CVI vs placebo across multiple trials
- Multiple European RCTs (combined n >500 CVI patients): consistent positive outcomes with TECA oral preparations

**Level A (wound healing — multiple RCTs):**
- TECA topical for burn wounds: Mehrpour SS 2013 (n=48): significantly faster re-epithelialization
- TECA for surgical wounds/keloids: multiple RCTs confirming reduced keloid formation, improved scar quality
- Post-surgical cesarean scar: Feria-Romero 2003 (n=54): TECA cream significantly improved scar texture

**Level B (cognitive):**
- **Wattanathorn J et al. 2008** (*J Ethnopharmacol*, n=28 healthy elderly): *C. asiatica* extract (250, 500, 750 mg) × 2 months; dose-dependent improvement in cognitive function (attention, working memory, delayed recall)
- **Bradwejn J et al. 2000** (*J Clin Psychopharmacol*, n=40): single-dose *C. asiatica* extract reduced acoustic startle response (anxiety surrogate) — anxiolytic effect
- **Tiwari S et al. 2008; Kumar A et al. 2011:** Multiple Indian RCTs for mild cognitive impairment: consistent positive direction

**Level B (diabetic retinopathy):**
- Incandela L et al. 1998 (n=50 T2DM with mild retinopathy): TECA oral significantly improved ophthalmoscopic score vs placebo — capillary protective mechanism

## 7. Phenomenological Record

**The elephant herb and longevity mythology:**
Traditional Sri Lankan lore holds that elephants live to extreme old age partly because they eat gotu kola — the "elephant herb" mythology. This story circulated in European colonial literature (19th century) associating the plant with unusual longevity, which combined with the genuine Indian Ayurvedic tradition of *mandukaparni* as a longevity and cognitive rejuvenation herb (*medhya rasayana*). The specific Ayurvedic claim is not that the plant extends lifespan directly but that it preserves cognitive vitality and mental clarity into old age — a more pharmacologically defensible claim than simple anti-aging, given the BDNF-AChE-adaptogenic evidence.

**Sri Lanka and the pennywort salad (gotukola sambol):**
In Sri Lanka, fresh gotu kola is consumed daily as *gotukola sambol* — a raw salad preparation of finely shredded fresh gotu kola leaves with grated coconut, shallots, lime juice, and green chili. This daily fresh consumption represents a food-medicine integration with specific pharmacological relevance: fresh rosmarinic acid and flavonoids are highest in the unheated fresh plant; the coconut fat enhances absorption of fat-soluble triterpene saponins. The traditional preparation is pharmacokinetically optimized without awareness of the relevant biochemistry.

**Ayurvedic medhya rasayana: the four cognitive rejuvenators:**
Charaka Samhita (c. 100 BCE–100 CE) describes four *medhya rasayana* — herbs specifically revered for cognitive and neurological rejuvenation:
1. *Mandookaparni* (*C. asiatica*) — gotu kola
2. *Yashtimadhu* (*Glycyrrhiza glabra*) — licorice
3. *Guduchi* (*Tinospora cordifolia*) — heart-leaved moonseed
4. *Shankhapushpi* (*Convolvulus pluricaulis*) — morning glory

These four herbs were prescribed together or individually for memory impairment, anxiety, mental fatigue, and neurological debility. The modern research programs on all four plants have found mechanistic support (cholinergic, BDNF, GABA, antioxidant pathways) for the cognitive applications — a remarkable example of systematic traditional nootropic pharmacology. Gotu kola's position as first among the four reflects the strongest cross-traditional evidence base.

**TCM ji xue cao — wound healing from 7th century:**
The Tang Materia Medica (Xinxiu Bencao, 659 CE — the world's first officially commissioned national pharmacopoeia) includes ji xue cao specifically for wound healing, resolving heat-toxin (inflammation), and reducing swelling. The 1,300-year TCM record of documented wound-healing use is among the longest continuous clinical observations for any single indication in the Codex — from the Tang dynasty through the Ming Bencao Gangmu to modern TECA pharmaceutical preparations.

## 8. Practical Application

**Therapeutic dose ranges:**

| Form | Dose | Indication |
|------|------|-----------|
| TECA/TTFCA oral (standardized 40% asiaticoside) | 60–120 mg/day (1–2 capsules) | CVI; diabetic retinopathy; cognitive; wound healing support |
| MADECASSOL® topical (1% TECA cream) | Apply 2–3×/day to wound | Wound healing; keloid prevention; burns |
| Standardized leaf extract (2–4% triterpenes) | 200–500 mg/day | Cognitive; anxiety; CVI |
| Fresh leaves (Sri Lankan/Southeast Asian dietary) | 50–100 g/day (as salad) | Daily nutritive-cognitive; wound support |
| Dried leaf capsules (non-standardized) | 1–4 g/day | General tonic; cognitive (less reliable dosing) |
| Leaf infusion (tea) | 1–2 tsp dried leaf/cup, 2–3×/day | Tonic; diuretic; digestive |

**Pharmaceutical formulation advantages:**
TECA (standardized triterpene percentage) provides reproducible pharmacological dosing; critical for CVI and wound healing indications where dose-response relationship is established. Non-standardized preparations have variable triterpene content (2–8% range in studies) and inconsistent clinical results — use standardized extracts for therapeutic applications.

**Contraindications:**
- **Pregnancy:** Animal teratogenicity data at high doses → avoid medicinal doses in pregnancy; dietary amounts (fresh leaf in Sri Lankan cuisine) generally considered safe but medical supervision recommended
- **Hepatotoxicity risk (rare, dose-dependent):** Isolated case reports of hepatotoxicity with high-dose gotu kola; mechanism unclear; monitor LFTs with chronic high-dose use; avoid in pre-existing liver disease
- **Scalp sedation caution:** High-dose freshly prepared leaf juice has caused sedation in traditional practitioner reports — avoid operating machinery with very high doses

**Drug interactions:**
- Sedatives/anxiolytics (benzodiazepines): potential GABA additive sedation; monitor CNS depression
- Anticoagulants (warfarin): minor antiplatelet activity; consistent INR monitoring with chronic use
- Anticonvulsants: theoretical GABA modulation may interact; caution in epilepsy management
- Hepatotoxic drugs (acetaminophen/paracetamol, statins at high dose): additive hepatic burden; monitor LFTs

**Safety profile:**
Fresh leaf (dietary): GRAS; extremely safe; consumed daily in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia for generations. Standardized TECA/TTFCA at clinical doses: well tolerated in trials; occasional headache, nausea; mild skin sensitization (topical). High-dose chronic supplementation: monitor LFTs. Overall: among the safest plant-based adaptogens in the Codex.

**Energetic tier:** ◈ Jing (constitutional-cognitive rejuvenation primary — *medhya rasayana*; longevity archetype); secondary ◉ Qi (wound healing/vascular regulatory; adaptogenic HPA modulation)  
**Optimal form:** TECA standardized extract for CVI, diabetic retinopathy, and clinical wound healing; MADECASSOL® topical for wound/keloid; fresh leaf salad (gotukola sambol style) for daily cognitive-nutritive integration; standardized extract 200–500 mg/day for cognitive and anxiolytic support
