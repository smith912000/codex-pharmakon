# Abrotanum mas — Abrotonon (ABROTONON; Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 3-29); also called abutonon, absinthium, heraclion, cholopoeon, thelyphthorion

## *Artemisia abrotanum L., southernwood, a shrubby aromatic wormwood-relative; the identification is the English edition's, which prints "Abrotanum mas [Linnaeus], Artemisia abrotanum — Southernwood" among its suggestions, and is of high confidence at species level for the plant Dioscorides calls abrotonon, but the "male" kind that gives the row its name is, in the text, only the second of two kinds he mentions* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named by Dioscorides among the texts on disk, in chapter 3-29.** This is a single-text entry. The row was added on 13 October as an unverified placeholder from the Dioscorides name list (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 435); this entry replaces it. The chapter was read in full on 30 October, and the name "abrotanum" was then searched in the whole text.

**Dioscorides, De Materia Medica (DMM).** The chapter is 3-29, ABROTONON, in Book Three (roots, juices, herbs, seeds), `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading at line 6776, text at lines 6777-6778. The editor's line of suggestions reads: "Abrotonum foemina [Fuchs], Absinthium ponticum [Bauhin], Artemisia pontica, Abrotanum mas [Linnaeus], Artemisia abrotanum — Southernwood". Those names are the later botanists' labels, set by the editor above the chapter. They are not Dioscorides' words. The Latin "mas" and "foemina" in the list are therefore the editor's, and the entry name "Abrotanum mas" is a Linnaean-period label for the plant, not a separate drug.

The description in the text: "The female abrotanum is a tree-like shrub, somewhat white, the leaves with little in-cuts (like those of seriphium) around the branches, with a golden corymbus [flat or slightly convex inflorescence] on the top, full of flowers that display in the summer, with a sweet smell, and some strength, bitter in taste. This seems to be the Sicilian. The other (called male) is full of sprigs, with slender seeds like wormwood. It grows abundantly in Cappadocia, and Galatia in Asia, and Hierapolis in Syria." The text therefore names two kinds, "female" and "male"; the "male" is described only by its sprigs and its slender seeds like wormwood. The chapter's uses are then stated for "the seed of these", that is, of both kinds together.

The uses given are: "The seed of these (pounded raw, boiled, and taken in a drink of water) helps difficult breathing, hernia, convulsions, sciatica, difficult painful urination, and the stoppage of the menstrual flow. Taken in a drink of wine it is an antidote for deadly poisons. With oil it is an ointment for those who have chills. It drives away snakes scattered under [foot] or inhaled as smoke. Taken in a drink of wine it helps those who are bitten. It is particularly good for the strikes of the harvest spider and scorpions. It helps inflammation of the eyes applied with boiled quince or with bread. It dissolves pannus [opaque thickening of cornea with veins; eyes] pounded into small pieces and boiled with barley meal. It is also mixed in the composition of oil irinum [1-66]. It is also called abutonon, absinthium, heraclion, cholopoeon, thelyphthorion," and the list of names continues across the page break.

**Other places in the text.** The plant is the base of the oil in 1-60, ABROTONINON (line 4010): "Take eight pounds of the leaves of abrotanum [3-29] and eleven pounds five ounces of the oil aromatized with the ingredients that go into cyprinum [see 1-65], and having steeped them in oil a day and a night, strain it out. If you wish to make it last a long time throw away the first leaves, put in other leaves, steep them too, and strain it out. It is warming and good for closing up or hardness in the vulva, and expelling the menstrual flow and afterbirth" (lines 4012-4015). It is also one of the herbs in the oil of marjoram, 1-58 (SAMPSUCHINON, line 4000), and its wine appears in 5-62 (OINOS KALAMINTHITES, GLECHONITES, ABROTONITES, lines 10105-10113): "Wine from southernwood, glechium or calaminth is made like that of thyme. Because they are urinary they are good for stomach disorders, those who lack appetite, and jaundice." Those three chapters are cited for the plant, not as separate varieties.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** The seed, in drink: difficult breathing, hernia, convulsions, sciatica, painful urination, stopped menses, poisons, snake, spider and scorpion bites. The plant, applied: chills, eye inflammation, pannus. The leaf in oil: a warming oil for the vulva and for bringing down the menses and afterbirth (1-60).
- **What the text does not say.** The chapter gives no dose for the seed and no quantity for the ointments.

The Codex does not translate the classic's terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend this plant. Several of the uses, such as bringing on the menses and afterbirth, concern pregnancy and are not to be followed.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these preparations: seed pounded raw or boiled and drunk in water or wine (3-29, line 6778); seed or herb with oil as an ointment; seed pounded and boiled with barley meal, or the herb applied with boiled quince or bread, for the eyes; and the oil of 1-60, made from eight pounds of leaves to eleven pounds five ounces of aromatized oil, steeped "a day and a night" (lines 4012-4015). That last measure is the oil-maker's recipe quantity, not a dose. No dose is given in the sources retrieved for the seed or the ointments. **Do not use any dose from this entry.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** In 3-29 Dioscorides prints the flavour "bitter in taste" and a "sweet smell" with "some strength" for the female kind. In 1-60 the oil is "warming". No other quality is printed for the plant in 3-29, and none is imported.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and this chapter offers none. What it does offer a practitioner is a discipline of attention: it separates two kinds by habit (the "female" tree-like, whitish shrub with golden flower clusters, the "male" full of sprigs with slender seeds), it names where each grows (Cappadocia, Galatia, Hierapolis in Syria), it identifies the Sicilian kind, and in 1-60 it gives the oil-maker's care in replacing the first leaves with fresh ones to make the oil last. That is provenance, plant-recognition and preparation. It is not a cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports any cultivation use. What a cultivator can take from the chapter is the habit of knowing exactly which kind of a plant is in the hand, where it grew, and how it was prepared before it was used, which is the attention the chapter itself shows.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy.** The text lists the plant for bringing down the menses and afterbirth (3-29 and 1-60). Do not use any preparation of this plant in pregnancy.
- **Bites, poisons, eye disease, hernia, breathing difficulty.** These are medical conditions that need proper care; this entry is no guide to them.
- **Identity.** The text names two kinds, and the Linnaean labels given for them are the editor's; the species is the English edition's identification. [USER: verify]
