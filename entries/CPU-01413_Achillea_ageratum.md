# Sweet maudlin / Achillea ageratum — Achilleios (Dioscorides 4-36); Ageraton (4-59)

## *Achillea ageratum* (the Dioscorides translators' suggested identification in both chapters, "Milfoil, Sweet Maudlin"; also listed by them in 4-36 with *Achillea atrata* and *Achillea fragrantissima*, and in 4-59 with *Ageratum conyzoides*) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides.** This entry stands on one text, Dioscorides (DMM). It was added 2026-10-09 (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP).

**Identity note.** The binomial belongs to the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood) and not to Dioscorides. In 4-36 the SUGGESTED lines read "*Achillea ageratum* — Milfoil, Sweet Maudlin *Achillea atrata* — Black Milfoil / *Achillea fragrantissima*, *Santolina fragrantissima* — Lavender Cotton", three different plants for one chapter. In 4-59 they read "*Achillea ageratum* — Sweet Maudlin, Milfoil *Ageratum conyzoides* — Floss Flowers, Goat Weed, Bastard Agrimony, Celestine", where the second is a plant of another genus. The two chapters are not stated by Dioscorides to be the same plant, and the identification is doubtful. [USER: verify] The row has not been checked against a modern botanical authority. This is distinct from I_770 *Achillea millefolium* (yarrow), which is a separate entry. The 4-36 synonym list of this chapter prints "millefolium" as a Roman name (see below), so the two entries overlap in the old name.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000).

*4-36 ACHILLEIOS* (heading at line 8304; text from line 8307, interrupted by a page break and a plate caption, "Helxine cissampelos after FUCHS — 1545"). The text: "Achillea is also called achillea sideritis. It bears small rods a hand's width long (or rather more) in the shape of spindles, and about them thin little leaves having frequent in-cuts across like coriander — somewhat red, clammy, smelling considerably, not unpleasant but having a medicinal smell. There is a round tuft on the top; the flowers white, resembling gold. It grows in fertile places. The fibres of this (pounded) congeals bloody wounds, reduces inflammation, and stops bleeding, as also of that of the womb (in a pessary); and a decoction of this is a douche for the excessive menstrual flows of women. It is also drunk for dysentery." Synonyms: "myriomorphon, chiliophyllon, stratioticon, or Heracleon"; Roman "supercilium veneris"; also "acorus sylvaticus, militaris, or millefolium"; African "asterchillos". The chapter follows 4-35, the third *sideritis* chapter, whose herb "congeals bleeding and new wounds".

*4-59 AGERATON* (heading at line 8516; text line 8518). The text: "Ageratum is a low shrub twenty centimetres long, full of single sprigs, similar (especially) to origanum; with a tuft on which is a flower (like a protuberance) of a golden colour, smaller than helichrysum. It is called ageratum because the flower remains for a long time, keeping its colour. A decoction of it is burning [to take or use]. Smoke from the herb itself is inhaled to induce the movement of urine, and to soften hardness around the womb." The bracket is the translators'.

The translation's index lists the binomial at pages 576 and 599, the pages of these two chapters (index entry at line 14451).

### Active Components

Plants of the genus *Achillea* are generally known to contain volatile oils, sesquiterpene lactones and flavonoids, which accords with the strong "medicinal smell" in 4-36. The constituents of the plants named in the chapters, and of *Ageratum conyzoides*, were not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. For 4-36 Dioscorides records a wound and bleeding herb, with a pessary and a douche for excessive menstrual flows and a drink for dysentery. For 4-59 he records a smoke for urine and the womb. These are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-36: the fibres pounded and applied to wounds, used in a pessary, a decoction as a douche, and a drink for dysentery. 4-59: a decoction, described as "burning"; the smoke of the herb inhaled. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Yarrow-type *Achillea* plants are generally known as traditional wound and bleeding herbs, and as potential allergens in the daisy family. This is a general statement, with no study opened for *A. ageratum*. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** no warming, cooling or other quality is printed for 4-36. For 4-59 the decoction is "burning". 4-36 also prints a smell, "not unpleasant but having a medicinal smell".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. Both chapters offer a discipline of attention: how to know the plant (spindle-shaped rods, thin cut leaves like coriander, a round tuft of white-and-gold flowers; or a low shrub like origanum with a golden flower that keeps its colour), where it grows (fertile places), and what it is called. They record no status for the person. The identification with *A. ageratum* is a translators' gloss.

**Practitioner's note.** The practice in these chapters is recognition: telling a plant by its leaf, tuft and smell. Nothing gives a cultivator a practice for the person. The identification is doubtful, so no use is recommended.

### Contraindications

The 4-36 uses include menstrual-flow and womb applications, so avoid in pregnancy. The 4-59 decoction is called "burning". Members of the daisy family can cause skin reactions in sensitive people. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
