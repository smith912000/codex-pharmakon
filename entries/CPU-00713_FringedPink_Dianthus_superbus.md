# Fringed Pink — Qu Mai (瞿麥), Wild Sweet-William, Large Pink

## *Dianthus superbus* L. | Family: Caryophyllaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM, SBJ
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry (Galenic 2nd degree); bitter-acrid, downward-draining
**Training Phase:** Clearing / Drainage (the phase concerned with clearing damp-heat and restoring free flow through the water passages)

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Chinese Materia Medica (CM) — Qu Mai.**
The aerial parts of *Dianthus superbus* (and its close relative *D. chinensis*) constitute Qu Mai, one of the classical "damp-draining, urine-promoting" (利水通淋, *lì shuǐ tōng lín*) herbs of the Chinese pharmacopoeia. First recorded in the tradition attributed to the *Shennong Bencao Jing*, Qu Mai is grouped among the middle-class herbs and directed at the *lin* syndromes — the painful, dribbling, obstructed urination that maps onto what a modern clinician would recognise as dysuria, cystitis, urinary calculi, and gonococcal urethritis. It is classically used to "unblock the water passages," promote urination, invigorate blood, and bring on delayed menstruation. The plant is bitter and cold, entering the Heart, Small Intestine, and Bladder channels.

**Dioscorides & the Greek Tradition (DMM).**
Species of *Dianthus* and their Caryophyllaceous kin were known to the Greek herbal tradition among the aromatic "little pinks" valued as tonic, cordial, and diuretic simples. The genus name *Dianthus* — "flower of Zeus" (*Dios anthos*) — descends from this classical esteem. While Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* is most secure on the cultivated clove-scented pinks, the fringed, deeply-laciniate flowers of *D. superbus* were absorbed into the same broad category of cooling, gently diuretic, blood-moving flowers of European herbalism.

**Avicennian / Unani Reception (EP).**
Through the transmission of Greek pharmacy into the Arabic-Persian synthesis, the pinks entered Unani practice as *qaranful*-adjacent cordials — cooling flowers used to temper heat of the urinary tract and to comfort the heart. The attribution here is general rather than to a specific named chapter of the *Qanun*; it reflects the shared Galenic humoral placement of the pinks as cold-and-dry drainers rather than a unique Avicennian monograph on *D. superbus*.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.**
The "fringed" or lacerate petal — the flower appears deliberately torn — became an emblem of controlled release: beauty that opens by dividing. In the Kenetica reading this mirrors the herb's pharmacological signature: it does not force but *fringes open* the outflow, easing what is dammed. The cold-and-dry humoral sign corresponds to its role in draining accumulated damp-heat — the stagnant, inflamed, "hot-water" state of the lower burner.

### Active Components

- **Flavonoid glycosides** — the characteristic C- and O-glycosylated flavones and their aglycones, responsible for much of the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity of the aerial parts.
- **Triterpenoid saponins** — surface-active glycosides typical of Caryophyllaceae, plausibly underlying the mild irritant-diuretic and membrane-active effects.
- **Cyclic peptides (dianthins / orbitides)** — small cyclopeptides recurrent across *Dianthus*.
- **Anthocyanins and phenolic acids** in the pigmented floral tissue, contributing to radical-scavenging capacity.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Qu Mai sits in the **Clearing / Drainage** phase as a targeted lower-burner drainer. Its Kenetica use-case is the *dammed-and-inflamed water course*: hot, scanty, painful urination; gravel and stone; and the stagnant-blood component of delayed or obstructed menstruation. It is a moving, opening herb — never a builder — and in the Kenetica sequence it is deployed briefly to restore flow, then withdrawn once the passage is clear. It pairs conceptually with cooling, moistening companions to prevent the dryness its draining action can leave behind.

### Scientific Correlation

Direct clinical trial data on *Dianthus superbus* are sparse; the evidence base is preclinical and phytochemical. Based on articles retrieved from PubMed:

- Al-Naqeb and colleagues (2026, *Scientific Reports*) reported the first genotoxicity screen of *D. superbus* var. *superbus* methanolic leaf extract in CHO-K1 cells, finding dose-dependent cytotoxicity (IC50 ≈ 27 µg/mL) and a significant increase in micronucleus frequency at the highest concentration tested (25 µg/mL) — a genuine safety signal at high in-vitro doses that argues against casual high-dose or prolonged use. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-50267-x)
- Argentino and colleagues (2025, *Antioxidants*) screened 19 Mediterranean plant extracts, with a *Dianthus* subspecies among those showing consistent antioxidant efficacy (reduced intracellular ROS in THP-1 cells) across multiple doses without cytotoxicity at effective concentrations — supporting the traditional cooling/anti-inflammatory rationale while echoing that all extracts became cytotoxic at higher doses. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox14101217)

**Evidence grade: Traditional, with early preclinical support.** The diuretic and lower-urinary uses rest chiefly on long CM/Unani tradition; antioxidant activity has cell-model support, and there is a real in-vitro genotoxicity flag at high concentration. No human efficacy trials support the classical indications.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** Bitter (苦), cold (寒). Enters Heart, Small Intestine, Bladder channels. Actions: promotes urination, unblocks painful urinary dribbling (*lin*), invigorates blood, promotes menstruation.
- **Galenic / Unani:** Cold and dry (≈ 2nd degree); draining, deobstruent to the urinary passages, mildly cordial to the heart.
- **Ayurvedic:** Not a classical Ayurvedic dravya; by analogy it would read as *tikta* (bitter) rasa, *shita* (cooling) virya, reducing *kapha* and *pitta* in the lower channels. (Analogical, not attested.)

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy — avoid.** *Dianthus superbus* is a traditional blood-mover and emmenagogue used specifically to promote menstruation; this is a classical contraindication in pregnancy and a caution during heavy menstruation.
- **Spleen/Kidney deficiency:** classically cautioned in weak, cold, deficient patients — its draining, cooling action can deplete.
- **High-dose / prolonged use:** the in-vitro genotoxicity signal (Al-Naqeb et al., 2026) counsels against concentrated extracts and extended courses pending further safety data.
- Insufficient data in lactation and paediatric use — avoid.
