# Ferula narthex — shing.kun, Shingkun, "Asafoetida" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Ferula narthex, the only binomial Clark 1995 prints for shing.kun, under the English name "Asafoetida"; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints "Ferula asafetida" in the place that column order gives to "Shingkun", with the English "Asafoetida"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under the binomial *Ferula narthex* only by Clark.** The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`, and both name the drug. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. The spine row takes Clark's binomial (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 70). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Other texts on disk that bear on the name (not tagged on this row).* The word *narthex* is also the heading of Dioscorides 3-91, which prints "3-91. NARTHEX" with the translators' line "SUGGESTED: Ferula foetida, Ferula puberula, Narthex asafoeteda — Asafoetida, Assafoetida" (`Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 7341 to 7342). The chapter opens: "The pith of narthex (which the Romans call ferula) taken in a drink whilst it is green helps bloodspitting and stomach complaints." (line 7343). By its own words the chapter describes the pith of a giant-fennel-like plant, and the asafoetida identification is the translators' suggestion, so the Codex does not read it as an attestation of the Tibetan drug. The Sushruta text names asafoetida as "Hingu" (for example `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt`, lines 1419 and 1459). Neither text prints the binomial *Ferula narthex*. Yang 1998, Ebers and the Assyrian text name neither. These possible tag questions are reported to the parent and not decided here, and this entry writes no register paragraph for either text. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 918).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792). The chapter lists eight sections of individual natural substances at line 802. The text file prints the headings "(5) Resins" (line 879) and "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997), and this line falls between them, among the entries after "(5) Resins". The entry follows *karany.ja* (Indian beech, line 917) and precedes *byi.tang.ga* (*Embelia ribes*, line 919). It reads:

"shing.kun: Asafoetida: Ferula narthex: [has fourteen names and is of two types. One is the resin of the Pokka tree. A white thread rubbed against this tree will turn blue. The other type is radishlike and the root is half exposed above the ground. It is pale yellow and contains a milky sap.] It is a vermifuge and cures cold disorders and heart wind."

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the fourteen names, the two types, the thread test and the radish-like root with its milky sap. Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the English name "Asafoetida", the Latin name, which is Clark's identification and not the root text's, and the sentence "It is a vermifuge and cures cold disorders and heart wind." Clark's index of Latin names lists "Ferula narthex" (line 1585) and "Asafoetida" (line 1557).

*Other Clark lines that name asafoetida.* Four more Clark lines name it without a Tibetan name. In chapter 16, on diet (heading at line 659), line 703: "Ginger increases (digestive) heat and Asafoetida cures wind disorders." In chapter 19 (heading at line 747), line 769: "The group of medicines having hot taste includes black pepper, wild ginger, longpeper, fresh (moist) ginger, Asafoetida, a type of Anemone rivularis (srub.ka), che.tsha, Arisaema intermedium, onion, garlic, etc." In chapter 21, line 1216, Asafoetida is in the class of medicines used "to cure wind-phlegm combinations", and line 1221 names it first after Musk in the category of anthelmintics ("The category of anthelmintics contains Musk, Asafoetida, Garlic, ..."). In chapter 30, "Direct Therapeutic Techniques" (heading at line 1409), lines 1410 to 1411, for a wind disorder: "the ‘Asafoetida three’ decoction. The powders of nutmeg and Asafoetida should also be prescribed". Clark's identification of "Asafoetida" with *Ferula narthex* is made only at line 918; the other lines carry the English name.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text on "Shingkun".* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The name appears in four places and in the index. In chapter 16, "Dietetics" (contents, line 209), lines 3051 to 3053: "Ginger generates heat while Shingkun treats loong disorders." (the sentence runs over a page break, and the page number 170 and the running header "Explanatory Tantra" stand between "generates" and "heat" in the text file) In chapter 19, line 3215: "The group of medicinal substances having a hot taste includes Nalaesham, Chaga, Pipiling, Gasher, Shingkun, Subka, Che tshawa, Dhawa, onion, garlic and other similar substances." In chapter 21, line 3615: "Men ga, Gakya, Shingkun, Kharu tsha, onion and garlic are the group of medicines that cure combined disorders of baekan and loong." And in chapter 21, line 3638: "La tsi, Shingkun, Gokya, Soma raza, Thangtrom, Langthangtse, Jitanga, Dre dru, snail shell, Phur thal, Cinshingdru, Dhawa, Yherma and Tuma are the group of medicines that cures cm disorders" ("cm" as printed; the group has *Jitanga*, snail shell and *Thangtrom*, which Clark's anthelmintic list at line 1221 also has in the form *Embelia*, "Snail shell", "Datura" and "Henbane", so the Codex reads the group as the anthelmintics, from the members and not from the word). The index (line 7493) lists "Shingkun 171, 187, 202, 221, 222, 277". The sentences give no Latin name. The Codex reads the drug as the same as *shing.kun* from the identical spelling and from the agreement of the hot taste and the wind action with Clark's lines 703 and 769. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary.* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "S" block (the letter at line 5669), prints the phonetic name "Shingkun" (line 5719) and the transliteration "shing kun" (line 5725). The scientific names are in a later run, which ends with "Ferula asafetida" (line 5765) and, in the English column, "Asafoetida" (line 5768). Counting from the end of the run, "Ferula asafetida" is the last scientific name and "Shingkun" is the last phonetic name, and the English "Asafoetida" agrees with the name. The extraction repaired the OCR spelling "asafetida" to *asafoetida* (`names.csv`, line 355). The glossary therefore names *Ferula asafetida* for this drug, by column position and by the English name, and does not print *Ferula narthex*. The two Tibetan texts agree on the Tibetan name and the English name and differ on the binomial. The Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Ferula narthex*, *shing.kun* and *Shingkun* found no entry on this binomial. I_1178 (*Rosa macrophylla*), I_1251 (*Chaenomeles speciosa*) and I_1233 (*Arisaema intermedium*) quote lines of the same glossary block or the same taste and anthelmintic lists, and are entries on other plants. The existing asafoetida entries are I_677 (Asafoetida, *Ferula assafoetida*, tags AT, CM, DMM, SBJ, SS, FMT), I_015 (*Ferula assa-foetida*, tags AT, DMM, CM), I_406 (Hingu, *Ferula asafoetida*, SS) and I_251 (Xinjiang asafoetida, *Ferula sinkiangensis*). I_677's FMT paragraph (lines 27, 77 and 92) is on the Tibetan name *shing kun* and is the nearest overlap: it cites no Clark or Men-Tsee-Khang line, and it gives colic and indigestion, which are not in the lines read here (the lines read give wind, cold disorders, heart wind, worms, hot taste and baekan-and-loong disorders). The spine has no separate row for the glossary's spelling *Ferula asafetida*. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that asafoetida is the oleo-gum-resin of the roots of certain *Ferula* species (family Apiaceae), that its strong smell is due to sulfur-containing compounds, and that *Ferula* is a genus of large umbellifers. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained, and Clark's commentary layer gives two forms, a resin and a radish-like root. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "is a vermifuge and cures cold disorders and heart wind" (Clark, line 918), that "cures wind disorders" (Clark, line 703) and that "treats loong disorders" (Men-Tsee-Khang, lines 3051 to 3053). These are the texts' claims, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give two preparations in which asafoetida is named and no quantity for either. Clark's chapter 30 names an "Asafoetida three" decoction and says "The powders of nutmeg and Asafoetida should also be prescribed" (lines 1410 to 1411), for a wind disorder. The first is attached to the English name and the second to the drug by name only. Clark's commentary layer at line 918 describes the resin and the root and does not say which part is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Tibetan terms ("cold disorders", "heart wind", "wind disorders", "loong disorders") are not modern diagnoses or measured outcomes, so no modern finding can be set beside them here. The identity of the plant behind the drug is given as two *Ferula* binomials by two texts, so a study of one *Ferula* species would not bear directly on it. It is a general, well-established fact that asafoetida is used as a cooking spice in small amounts in South Asian and Iranian cooking.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** The Tibetan texts print a taste for this drug. Clark's chapter 19 lists "Asafoetida" in the group of medicines having hot taste (line 769), and the Men-Tsee-Khang text lists "Shingkun" in the group of medicinal substances having a hot taste (line 3215). No power or post-digestive taste is printed for it in the lines read. The root-text sentence at line 918 gives its action ("a vermifuge", "cold disorders and heart wind") and not its taste.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does and by its taste: hot in taste, a vermifuge, against cold disorders and heart wind, and against wind disorders in the diet and treatment chapters. The Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same in kind and also places it among medicines for combined baekan and loong disorders. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the lines cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic and dietary only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this drug beyond what the texts say of its place in food and in treatment: a hot-tasting spice that treats wind, cold disorders and worms. The texts give no dose, and the Latin name differs between the two Tibetan texts. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Asafoetida as a spice is a different matter from a medicinal preparation of unknown strength, and the identity of the plant is given differently by the two Tibetan texts, so no preparation should be made or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy, infants or any medicine interaction was read. [USER: verify]
