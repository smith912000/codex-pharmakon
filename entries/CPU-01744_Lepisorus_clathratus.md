# Lepisorus clathratus — Drakpoe, brag spos (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary, by column position)

## *Lepisorus clathratus, as the binomial printed in the scientific-name run of the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary's "D" block, with the English "Pyrrosia leaf"; the Tibetan name "Drakpoe" is set against it by column position only, and Clark 1995 prints other Lepisorus species for brag.spos* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, and on one printed binomial in one of its two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. Only Men-Tsee-Khang prints *Lepisorus clathratus* (line 5246). A search of Clark 1995 for "Lepisorus" found *Lepisorus waltonii* and *Lepisorus scolopendrium* (line 1069), the bare word "Lepisorus" in the same entry and at line 1084, and the index (line 1609), and no *clathratus*. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk for the genus name found nothing. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 267) the key is the glossary's own scientific name, *Lepisorus clathratus*, at "Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (line 5246)", with the note "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". Line 5246 prints two binomials, "Lepisorus clathratus Caragana gerardiana", and the key is the first of the two on that line. The glossary gives one binomial per entry, so this is not a first-of-several-names case as in Clark. Clark's *brag.spos* row (names.csv line 168) is keyed *Lepisorus waltonii*, and is the subject of I_1309, written in the same batch.

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "D" block, lines 5179 to 5261).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs, and the pairing of a Tibetan name with a binomial is not printed in one row. The phonetic names run from "Dali" (line 5181) to "Drita sazin" (line 5196). Counting each name separately (line 5183 holds two, line 5188 holds five, line 5189 holds two), there are twenty-two, and the sixteenth is "Drakpoe" (line 5190), after "Dra go" and "Drakcham" (line 5189) and before "Drama" (line 5191). The transliterations include "brag lcham brag spos" (line 5208), so the name "brag spos" is Clark's *brag.spos*. The scientific names begin at line 5215. Counted in the same way, the sixteenth is "Lepisorus clathratus" (line 5246), followed by "Caragana gerardiana" (seventeenth) and "Corallodiscus batalin" (eighteenth, line 5247). The English names include "Pyrrosia leaf" (line 5249), "Pea shrub" (line 5250) and "Corallodiscus fem" (line 5251, "fem" evidently a misprint of "fern"). So "Drakpoe" stands against *Lepisorus clathratus* and the English "Pyrrosia leaf", "Drama" against *Caragana gerardiana* and "Pea shrub", and "Drakya hawo" (eighteenth, line 5192) against *Corallodiscus*. The last pairing checks against Clark, whose *brag.skya.ha.wo* (line 1068) is a *Corallodiscus*, and *brag.spos* in Clark is a *Lepisorus*. The pairing of "Drakpoe" with *Lepisorus clathratus* is by position between those checks and is not printed in a row. [USER: verify] "Pyrrosia" is another fern genus; the glossary prints it as the English name and the Codex does not interpret it.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* In chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), line 3448 reads, in the middle of a longer passage: "Drakpoe treats wounds, dries up pus and protects spongy bone." The index lists "Drakpoe 209" (line 7347). The text gives no taste, power or part used for it. That the "Drakpoe" of this line is the glossary's "Drakpoe" rests on the identical spelling. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, the same Tibetan name.* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997), line 1069 reads:

"brag.spos: Lepisorus waltonii ching: Lepisorus scolopendrium: [has four names, for example, btsun.mo.re.ral, etc. It has a swordlike, narrow, yellow leaf with four lobes at the base covered with dewdrops. (At least two similar subtypes have now been classified.)] Lepisorus heals wounds, dries pus, sustains bone resin, [cures channel fevers and is beneficial for injuries and enlargement of the diaphragm]."

The commentary layer (square brackets) holds the names, the leaf, the "two similar subtypes" remark and the channel fevers and diaphragm; the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it, is "Lepisorus heals wounds, dries pus, sustains bone resin". At line 1084 a commentary gloss on the entry for *ldum.bu.re.ral* says that this drug "is said to be the 'king fern', whilst brags.spos (Lepisorus) is the 'queen fern' and brags.skya.ha.wo (Corallodiscus) is the 'minister fern'". Clark's "heals wounds, dries pus, sustains bone resin" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "treats wounds, dries up pus and protects spongy bone" agree on wounds and pus and are worded differently on the third ("bone resin" and "spongy bone"); the Codex does not treat the third pair as one term. The two Tibetan texts therefore agree on the Tibetan name and on the action in kind, and differ in the species: Clark's two are *waltonii* and *scolopendrium*, and the glossary's, by position, is *clathratus*. The Codex does not choose.

**Overlap.** The same Tibetan drug is treated in I_1309 (*Lepisorus waltonii*, Clark's first binomial for *brag.spos*, written in this batch). The two entries are one drug under two binomials, one drawn from each edition; they are not merged, and each says so. I_1270 (*Dryopteris fragrans*, tag FMT) uses "Drakpoe" standing sixteenth against *Lepisorus clathratus* as a check on its own count for the "minister fern", and is not an entry on this drug. I_1248 (*Caragana gerardiana*) quotes the same glossary line 5246 for its own name, which stands beside this one on that line. I_1269 (*Drynaria propinqua*) and I_1225 (*Adiantum pedatum*) quote the line 1084 gloss that ranks the three ferns. A search of the entries folder for *Lepisorus*, *brag.spos*, *Drakpoe* and "Pyrrosia" found no other entry on this drug. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Lepisorus* is a genus of ferns (family Polypodiaceae in the usual treatment, which the Codex has not checked against a flora), and that ferns bear neither flowers nor seeds. Clark's commentary describes the plant by its leaf only. Which part was used is not said, and no general statement about constituents is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "heals wounds, dries pus, sustains bone resin" (Clark, line 1069), whose commentary adds "cures channel fevers and is beneficial for injuries and enlargement of the diaphragm", and "treats wounds, dries up pus and protects spongy bone" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3448). These are the texts' claims in their own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and does not translate the Tibetan terms into modern diagnoses. Wounds with pus need medical care.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** No taste, power or post-digestive taste is printed for this drug in the lines read. Clark's commentary (line 1069) describes the leaf only, and Men-Tsee-Khang prints none. The actions printed are healing wounds, drying pus and sustaining bone resin (Clark) and treating wounds, drying up pus and protecting spongy bone (Men-Tsee-Khang). The "queen fern" rank (Clark, line 1084) is a ranking among three ferns and is not a quality.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it heals wounds, dries pus and sustains bone resin, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text gives a parallel set of actions. The "queen fern" in Clark's gloss (line 1084) names a place in a trio of ferns and is not a cultivation status. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes, and not in the rejuvenation material; no line read places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name, not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a wound and pus drug in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and the species behind the Tibetan name differs between the two editions. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Wounds, pus and fever need medical assessment; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Because the name-to-plant pairing rests on column position and the editions differ, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read.
