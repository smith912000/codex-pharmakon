# Earth Chestnut / Bunium bulbocastanum — Pseudobounion (Dioscorides 4-125)

## *Bunium bulbocastanum (the Dioscorides translators' suggestion for chapter 4-125; also Carum bulbocastanum — "Arnut, Earth Chestnut, Pig Nut, Tuberous Caraway")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). **Named only by Dioscorides in this row.** The binomial is the translators' suggestion, not Dioscorides' own identification. The text gives little, and this is a SHORT entry.

*4-125 PSEUDOBOUNION* (heading at line 9147). The SUGGESTED line (line 9148) reads "Bunium bulbocastanum, Carum bulbocastanum, Sium bulbocastanum — Arnut, Earth Chestnut, Pig Nut, Tuberous Caraway", with the cross-reference "see 2-136, 4-124".

The whole chapter (line 9150): "Pseudobunium is a small shrub, twenty centimetres long, found in Crete, with leaves similar to bunium [above], and a sharp taste. A decoction made with four small branches (taken as a drink with water) is able to heal griping, slow and painful urination, and pains of the side. Applied lukewarm (with salt and wine) it dissolves tumours [possibly goitre]."

The chapter describes the plant only by comparison with "bunium", gives a place (Crete), and names two uses, one by mouth and one external. It prints no alternative names. The translators' bracket "possibly goitre" is theirs. The description says nothing of a tuber, which is the feature of the modern earth chestnut, so the match with *Bunium bulbocastanum* rests on the translators' suggestion alone. [USER: verify]

The cross-referenced chapters are different. 4-124 BOUNION (heading at line 9144) is the plant the text compares this one to; its SUGGESTED line is "Meum bunius — Coriander-leaved Bawd-money". It is "urinary and warming, draws out the afterbirth, and is properly used for the spleen, kidneys and bladder", and it has its own names ("aton, actine, or anemosphoron ... scopa regia"). It is not attributed to this row. 2-136 BOUNIAS (line 5684) is, in the translators' line, a turnip or land cress kind of plant ("Brassica campestris var rapa ... Land Cress") whose seed "makes poisons ineffective", and is a different plant that shares only part of the name.

### Active Components

Nothing is read here. The text names no constituent. Plants of the carrot family (Apiaceae) are generally known to contain essential oils, but what the plant Dioscorides meant contains was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records a drink of four small branches for griping, slow and painful urination and pains of the side, and a lukewarm application with salt and wine to dissolve tumours. These are the text's own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for urinary difficulty or neck swellings, which need medical care.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives two preparations. "A decoction made with four small branches (taken as a drink with water)" (line 9150). And the plant "applied lukewarm (with salt and wine)". Four small branches is the only quantity printed, and no volume or duration is given. No further dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the plant has "a sharp taste" (line 9150). "Applied lukewarm" is a preparation and not a quality. No warming or cooling quality is printed for 4-125 itself (the related 4-124 is called "warming").

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and the chapter assigns the plant no status. What it offers a cultivator is a short discipline of attention: a small shrub of about twenty centimetres, found in Crete, leaves like *bunium*, a sharp taste, and the way the chapter tells it from the true *bunium* by name ("pseudo-"). The identification with *Bunium bulbocastanum* is the translators' suggestion and is not supported by the text's description. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in this chapter gives a cultivator a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. The identity of the plant is uncertain, and carrot-family plants include dangerous look-alikes, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Griping, painful urination and neck swellings can have serious causes and need medical assessment. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
