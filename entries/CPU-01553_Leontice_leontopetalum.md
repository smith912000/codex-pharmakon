# Leontice leontopetalum — Leontopetalon (Dioscorides 3-110)

## *Leontice leontopetalum, the only binomial the translators print for 3-110 ("Leontice, Lion's Leaf, Lion's Turnip")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the SUGGESTED line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The line (line 7528) reads "Leontice leontopetalum — Leontice, Lion's Leaf, Lion's Turnip".

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-110. LEONTOPETALON*, heading at line 7527, SUGGESTED line at 7528, text at line 7529. It stands in Book Three, in the section headed "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants", between 3-109 (clinopodium) and *3-111. TEUKRION*. The translators' index gives *Leontice leontopetalum* at page 495 (lines 16286 and 16287).

The whole chapter reads: "Leontopetalum sends out a stalk twenty centimetres long (or rather more) with many wings on whose tops are pods similar to cicer [2-126]. In these are two or three little seeds. The flowers are a Phoenician colour [red] (similar to anemone), but the leaves are similar to colewort [kale], cut-in like those of poppy. The root is black like rapum [turnip] with abnormal growths (as it were), some knotty. It grows in fields and among wheat. A decoction of the root (taken as a drink with wine) helps those bitten by snakes, quickly relieving their pain. It is also mixed with enemas or suppositories for sciatica. It is also called leontopodium, leontium, doricteris, lychnis sylvestris, doris, pardale, thorybethron, rapeium, papaver corniculatum, or anemone; the Romans call it papaverculum, and semen leoninum."

Dioscorides gives a full description for a plant of this length: a stalk twenty centimetres or somewhat more, with "many wings", pods like those of *cicer* holding two or three small seeds, red flowers like the anemone, leaves like kale but cut in like those of the poppy, and a black root like a turnip, with "abnormal growths" and some knots. It grows in fields and among wheat. Two uses are printed: a wine decoction of the root against snake bite, relieving pain quickly, and the root as an ingredient of enemas or suppositories for sciatica.

**Names shared with other chapters.** The chapter prints eleven further names, and two of them are the names of other chapters in the same text. *Leontopodium* is the title of *4-131. LEONTOPODION* (heading at line 9185), which describes a small whitish herb with black flowers and is a different chapter and a different row; *lychnis sylvestris* is also printed among the other names of *4-132. IPPOGLOSSON* (heading at line 9193, the name at line 9196). The Latin names *papaver corniculatum* and *anemone* are printed here as alternative names of this plant, while the flower is said to be "similar to anemone". The text does not say why the names overlap. [USER: verify] No text of 4-131 or 4-132 beyond these names is read into this entry.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a field plant with a black knotty root, drunk in wine as a decoction after a snake bite and used in enemas or suppositories for sciatica. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol. A snake bite is an emergency that needs medical care, and the identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

3-110 gives these preparations: a decoction of the root "taken as a drink with wine"; and the root "mixed with enemas or suppositories". No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a field plant with two uses and no stated amounts, so it is history and not a practice. The useful lesson is its detailed method of recognition.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. A snake bite and sciatica need medical care, and a home preparation must not replace it. A root taken as a decoction in wine, or placed in an enema or suppository, is not a safe home remedy when its identity is uncertain. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
