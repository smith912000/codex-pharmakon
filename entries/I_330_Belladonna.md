# I-330: Atropa belladonna (Belladonna / Deadly Nightshade)

**Entry I-330 | Solanaceae**

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Atropa belladonna* L.
**Family:** Solanaceae

**Common Names:**
- English: Belladonna, Deadly Nightshade, Devil's Cherries, Witch's Berry
- German: *Tollkirsche* ("mad/crazy cherry"), *Belladonna*
- French: *Belladone*, *Belladonne*
- Italian: *Belladonna* — "beautiful woman" — the pharmacological name that became the genus's popular designation
- Arabic: *Dhifra* (ضفرة) / *Luffāḥ* (لفاح) — "mandrake-class" — Ibn Sīnā associated it with mandrake family
- Sanskrit: *Parasikayavani*

**Etymology:**
*Atropa* — from Greek *Atropos*, one of the three Fates (Moirai) — the one who cuts the thread of life; encoding the plant's lethality. *belladonna* = "beautiful woman" (Italian) — Renaissance Italian women used belladonna berry extract as eye drops to dilate pupils (mydriasis from atropine) — large pupils were considered aesthetically attractive. The genus (Atropos/death) and species (beautiful woman) encode two entirely different aspects of the plant: its toxicity and its cosmetic use. *Atropine* — the primary alkaloid — takes its name from *Atropa*.

**The Mydriasis Cosmetic Tradition:**
Renaissance Italian women dropped dilute belladonna extract into their eyes to produce pupil dilation (*mydriasis* from atropine's muscarinic receptor antagonism at the iris sphincter). This created the "alluring large-eyed look" considered beautiful in Renaissance Italian culture — an effect captured in many contemporaneous portraits of women with strikingly large pupils. The practice carried real ophthalmological risks (cycloplegia, blurred vision, photophobia, tachycardia from systemic absorption through conjunctiva) but persisted as a cosmetic tradition. Modern ophthalmology uses atropine eye drops for diagnostic mydriasis — the pharmaceutical application of the same Renaissance cosmetic.

**CRITICAL TOXICITY:**
*Atropa belladonna* is among the most toxic plants in the European flora. The attractive black berries (resembling cherries) cause serious poisoning in children — 3–5 berries can be fatal in a child; 10–20 berries can be fatal in an adult. **ALL PARTS ARE TOXIC.** The plant is included in the Codex as a pharmaceutical source and important historical-clinical documentation, not as a self-treatment herb. Atropine and other alkaloids are extracted under pharmaceutical control only.

**Morphology:**
Perennial; 1–2 m; large ovate leaves (10–20 cm); dull green; dull purple bell-shaped flowers; shiny black berries (1–2 cm) with persistent purple star-shaped calyx — visually attractive; extremely toxic.

**Parts Used (Pharmaceutical):** Leaves and roots — source of atropine and hyoscyamine alkaloids; pharmaceutical extraction only.

**The Pharmaceutical-Poison Duality:**
Belladonna holds a unique position in the Codex as a plant where the dose-toxicity curve is so steep that:
- **Micro-dose:** Therapeutic (atropine for bradycardia, cycloplegia, antispasmodic)
- **Small excess:** Toxic (anticholinergic toxidrome)
- **Moderate excess:** Potentially lethal

This is the classic pharmacological principle of **sola dosis facit venenum** (Paracelsus: "the dose makes the poison") at its most extreme. Paracelsus himself is reported to have used the phrase while working with nightshade alkaloids. Every modern use of atropine — cardiac resuscitation (ACLS protocol), anesthesia premedication, organophosphate poisoning antidote, ophthalmological dilation — is a controlled pharmaceutical use of belladonna's primary alkaloid.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domains:**

1. **Anticholinergic / antispasmodic** — DMM, CM, SS, EP, AT; atropine mechanism; **pharmaceutical Level A**
2. **Surgical anesthetic adjunct** — DMM, CM, SS; scopolamine/hyoscine component; anti-nausea; antisecretory
3. **Ophthalmic (mydriasis)** — DMM, European Renaissance; atropine mechanism
4. **Narcotic / sedative (overdose mechanism)** — all traditions; alkaloid CNS depression at toxic doses
5. **Antidote utility** — atropine for organophosphate/nerve agent poisoning; **Level A**

**The Atropine Antidote:**
One of the pharmacological reversals in the Codex: the most toxic common European plant provides the primary antidote to an even more acutely toxic class of compounds — **organophosphate pesticides and nerve agents (sarin, VX, tabun)**. Organophosphates inhibit acetylcholinesterase (AChE) → excess ACh at all muscarinic and nicotinic synapses → cholinergic crisis. Atropine competitively blocks muscarinic ACh receptors → reverses the muscarinic excess (bronchospasm, excessive secretions, bradycardia) → life-saving in organophosphate poisoning. Military medics worldwide carry atropine autoinjectors (EpiPen-style) specifically for nerve agent exposure. The poison-as-antidote pharmacological relationship is one of the most dramatic in medicine.

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Tropane Alkaloids (0.3–1.0% leaf; 0.4–0.6% root)

**1. L-Hyoscyamine — PRIMARY compound (70–90% of total alkaloids)**
- Muscarinic acetylcholine receptor (mAChR) competitive antagonist — all muscarinic subtypes (M1–M5)
- **Therapeutic:** Anti-spasmodic (GI, bladder, bronchial); antisecretory; mydriasis; cycloplegia; anti-emetic
- **Racemizes to atropine** (racemic mixture of L + D hyoscyamine) during extraction and storage
- Naturally L-form is more potent; commercial atropine = racemic = lower potency

**2. Atropine (racemic Hyoscyamine)** — pharmaceutical standard; emerges during processing
- **Bradycardia:** FDA-approved IV atropine for cardiac resuscitation; ACLS protocol
- **Organophosphate antidote:** IV atropine (large doses) reverses muscarinic crisis
- **Preoperative antisecretory:** Reduces airway secretions before anesthesia
- **Ophthalmology:** 1% atropine drops for mydriasis; cycloplegia; amblyopia treatment in children

**3. Scopolamine (Hyoscine) — ~5–10% of alkaloids**
- Primary CNS-penetrant tropane alkaloid
- **Anti-emetic:** Transdermal scopolamine (Transderm Scōp®) for motion sickness; **FDA-approved**
- **Pre-anesthetic:** Antisecretory + anti-emetic in surgical setting
- Greater CNS effects than atropine (better blood-brain barrier penetration)

**4. Apoatropine, Belladonine** — minor alkaloids

### Non-Alkaloid Compounds

**5. Scopoletin** — coumarin; anti-inflammatory (minor)
**6. Chlorogenic Acid** — antioxidant
**7. Atropine-related esters** — minor

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### Anticholinergic (mAChR Antagonism)
- **L-Hyoscyamine/Atropine → competitive muscarinic receptor blockade (M1–M5)**
- **M2 cardiac:** Antagonism → increased heart rate → anti-bradycardia
- **M3 smooth muscle/secretory:** Antagonism → bronchodilation; reduced GI motility; reduced secretions (salivary, bronchial, GI, sweat)
- **M3 iris sphincter:** Antagonism → iris dilator dominance → mydriasis
- **M3 ciliary muscle:** Antagonism → ciliary relaxation → cycloplegia (inability to accommodate)
- **CNS (scopolamine):** M1/M4 in CNS → anti-emetic; sedation; cognitive impairment at high doses

### Anticholinergic Toxidrome (OVERDOSE)
Classic teaching mnemonic:
- **"Dry as a bone"** (reduced secretions — xerostomia, anhidrosis)
- **"Red as a beet"** (cutaneous vasodilation — flushing)
- **"Hot as a hare"** (hyperthermia from impaired sweating)
- **"Blind as a bat"** (mydriasis + cycloplegia — inability to focus)
- **"Mad as a hatter"** (CNS: agitation, hallucinations, delirium)
- **"Fast as a race horse"** (tachycardia)
At extreme doses: seizures, coma, death from respiratory failure

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Pharmaceutical Atropine/Hyoscyamine Indications:**
- **Cardiac resuscitation (bradycardia):** Atropine IV; ACLS protocol; **Level A** (standard of care)
- **Organophosphate poisoning antidote:** Atropine IV; **Level A** (standard of care)
- **Motion sickness (scopolamine patch):** Transderm Scōp® FDA-approved; multiple RCTs; **Level A**
- **Ophthalmology (mydriasis/cycloplegia):** Atropine 1% drops; **Level A** (standard of care)
- **Pediatric amblyopia (atropine penalization):** Atropine drops to penalize better eye; **Level A** (RCTs)
- **Overactive bladder (hyoscyamine):** Levsin® (L-hyoscyamine); FDA-approved; **Level A**

- All clinical evidence relates to pharmaceutical-grade isolated alkaloids under medical supervision

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The Witches' Salve:**
Historical accounts of medieval European witchcraft include descriptions of "flying ointments" — ungents applied to skin (broom handle or directly) containing belladonna, henbane, mandrake, and other Solanaceous alkaloids. Whether these accounts are literal or metaphorical, the pharmacology is coherent: transdermal absorption of tropane alkaloids → hallucinatory CNS effects → the sensation of flying/supernatural transport. Historian Michael Harner's analysis and ethnopharmacologist Erika Nansen's work on hallucinogenic fungi and plants in European witchcraft suggest that some "flying" experiences reported by accused witches were genuine hallucinogen experiences from self-administration. The broomstick as application device for vaginal application of the ointment (increasing mucosal absorption) has been proposed as a literal interpretation — whether accurate or historical fantasy remains contested.

**The Paracelsus Legacy:**
Paracelsus (1493–1541), the Swiss-German physician-alchemist, is credited with the foundational pharmacological principle *"sola dosis facit venenum"* (only the dose makes the poison). His work with belladonna alkaloids at therapeutic vs. toxic doses was central to his formulation of this principle — the same substance that cures at one dose kills at another. Every modern pharmacological concept of therapeutic index, LD50, and dose-response curves descends from this observation, first crystallized in working with *Atropa belladonna*.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### CONTEMPORARY USE: PHARMACEUTICAL EXTRACTION ONLY

**THIS PLANT IS NOT FOR SELF-PREPARATION OR HOME USE.**

**Pharmaceutical Products Derived from Belladonna:**
- **Atropine injection:** Bradycardia; anesthesia; organophosphate antidote; ophthalmology
- **Transderm Scōp® (scopolamine patch):** Motion sickness; post-operative nausea
- **Levsin® (L-hyoscyamine):** Antispasmodic; IBS; bladder disorders
- **Atropine eye drops (1%):** Ophthalmological dilation; amblyopia treatment

**Homeopathic Preparations:**
- *Belladonna 30C* (homeopathic): No pharmacologically active alkaloid at standard dilutions; safety/efficacy debates ongoing; completely distinct from herbal belladonna

### Safety Profile — EMERGENCY INFORMATION

**TOXIC PLANT — DO NOT HANDLE OR INGEST.**

**Poisoning Treatment:**
- Emergency: Call Poison Control immediately
- Hospital treatment: Physostigmine (cholinesterase inhibitor) — specific antidote for anticholinergic toxidrome
- Supportive: benzodiazepines for agitation/seizures; cooling for hyperthermia; catheter if urinary retention

**Child Safety:**
- Berries are black, shiny, sweet-tasting — attractive to children
- 3–5 berries potentially fatal in a child
- REMOVE or fence belladonna plants where children may be present

**Drug Interactions (Pharmaceutical Atropine):**
- Antihistamines (anticholinergic antihistamines): additive anticholinergic
- Tricyclic antidepressants: additive anticholinergic
- Clozapine, quetiapine: additive anticholinergic
