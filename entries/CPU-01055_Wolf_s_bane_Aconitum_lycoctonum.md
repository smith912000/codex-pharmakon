**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000).

*4-78 AKONITON ETERON* (heading at line 8734; text at line 8737). The translator's identification is "Aconitum luteum, Aconitum lycoctonum [Fuchs, Linnaeus], Aconitum pyrenaicum, Aconitum vulparia — Wolf's-bane, Dog's-bane", marked POISONOUS. The text reads: "Aconitum alterum grows plentifully in Italy on the hills called Vestini, differing from that above. It has leaves similar to those of the plane tree but more jagged and a great deal smaller and darker, with a bare stalk (like a stem of fern) the height of a foot or more. The seed is in pods, in a way somewhat long; the black roots are like the fringes of squills [sea onions]; these they use for hunting wolves, placing them into raw meat which, when eaten by the wolves, kills them. It is also called cynoctonon, lycoctonon, or white bean, while the Romans call it colomestrum." The chapter gives a habitat, a description of leaf, stalk, seed and root, and the hunting use. It gives no medicinal use for people. A plate caption above the heading (line 8732) reads "Aconitum lycoctonum after FAGUET — 1894".

*4-77 AKONITON* (heading at line 8723), the chapter "above" that 4-78 contrasts itself with. The translator's identification is *Aconitum pardalianches*, *Solanum quadrifolium bacciferum* and *Paris quadrifolia* (herb Paris), with "other usage" *A. napellus* (monk's hood). The text says of it that its root applied to a scorpion makes it insensible, that it is "mixed with pain-relieving medicines for eyes", and that "Put into lumps of meat and given to them, it kills panthers, sows, wolves, and all wild beasts". It is reported here only because 4-78 defines itself against it. It is not attributed to this row.

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).**

The neighbouring entries are bong.nga.dkar.po, White Aconite (line 965), which says of the four types of aconite "white, red, yellow and black" that "The black type is highly toxic, whilst the white one has bitter taste and neutralizes poison", and bong.nga.dmar.po, Red Aconite (line 968), "an antidote to poison". *Clark 1995, ch.21 (compounding).* Line 1212, in the chapter that opens at line 1204, lists the poison-neutralising class: "The class of medicines which neutralize poisons consists of Musk, Red, Yellow and White Aconites, White and Brown Uncaria ...".

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011.* `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, line 5120: "Bongnga serpo bong nga ser po Aconitum lycoctonum". The line prints the phonetic name, the transliteration and the scientific name together, so on this line the Tibetan name is paired to the binomial. (The extraction note on `names.csv` row 245 says the Tibetan name is not paired, and for this row that note is wrong.) The text also names the plant in the group that cures poisoning (line 3604: "La tsi, Bongnga karpo, Bongnga serpo, Bongnga marpo, Khungdher karpo and mukpo, Pawo karpo and serpo ... are the group of medicines that cure poisoning"), and a "Common Name" column of the glossary prints "Yellow wolfsbane" (line 5157, in a list that also reads "Indian aconite" and "Blue clover"; the OCR does not tie the name to a row). The glossary's index (line 7279) lists "Bongnga serpo 205, 220".

### Active Components

*Aconitum* species are generally known to contain toxic diterpenoid alkaloids, and the *lycoctonum* group is known for lycoctonine-type alkaloids. The ones in the plants named by Dioscorides and by the Tibetan texts were not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records the plant solely as a wolf-bait poison (4-78). The Tibetan texts record a bitter, cool-powered yellow aconite used in poison fevers and bile fever and against meat poisoning, as the texts' own claims and not as a Codex protocol. The plant is a deadly poison, the identification between the two texts is unsettled, and the Codex gives no protocol for it.

### Preparation & Dosing

Dioscorides (4-78) gives the roots placed "into raw meat" as bait for wolves, which is not a human preparation. Clark (line 969) gives the "roots" of red and yellow aconite for meat poisoning, and the Men-Tsee-Khang line 3604 lists the plant in a poison-curing group, with no preparation or quantity in the lines read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Aconite alkaloids are potent cardiac and neurological toxins, and poisoning from *Aconitum* plants is a well-documented medical emergency. This is a general statement about the genus and no study of *A. lycoctonum* was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. The chapter offers what the cultivation spec calls a discipline of attention: where the plant grows (the Vestini hills of Italy), how to know it (plane-like leaves, a fern-like bare stalk, pods, roots like the fringes of squills), what it is called, and what it does to wolves. It records no use for the person and assigns the plant no status. The identification with *A. lycoctonum* is a translator's gloss. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra classes the plant by its powers (ch.20) and by its place in compounded classes (ch.21): bitter taste and cool power, a medicine for poison fevers and bile fever and for meat poisoning, and a member of the class that neutralises poisons. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice and not reaching the root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. Nothing read assigns this plant a place in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Its framing is therapeutic and antidotal only. The identification with *A. lycoctonum* rests on a glossary line, while Clark prints *A. autumnale*. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the sources read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a wolf-bait poison in Dioscorides and an antidote-class medicine in the Tibetan texts, and by general knowledge a deadly poison. The abstentive answer is the correct one: do not use it.

### Contraindications

*Aconitum* plants are deadly poisons. The roots can cause fatal cardiac arrhythmia and paralysis, and handling the fresh plant can cause numbness through the skin. None of the traditional uses above is advice, and no home preparation is safe. Anyone who has eaten any part of an *Aconitum* plant needs urgent medical care. Do not use in pregnancy. The Tibetan tradition itself distinguishes the black aconite as the highly toxic type, and processing in that tradition is outside what was read.
