# Indian Rennet — Paneer Doda / Vegetable Rennet

## *Withania coagulans* (Stocks) Dunal | Family: Solanaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, SS

**Energetic Classification:** Bitter–pungent; heating (*uṣṇa vīrya*); pungent post-digestive (*kaṭu vipāka*); light and dry (*laghu, rūkṣa*)

**Training Phase:** Metabolic Regulation — Digestive

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Withania coagulans* is a rigid, greyish, hairy shrub of the arid zones — Rajasthan and Gujarat in India, Baluchistan and the Afghan borderlands, extending into Iran. It bears small orange-red berries enclosed in a papery calyx, and those berries do something that almost no other medicinal plant does, and that everyone who has ever handled them knows within minutes.

**They curdle milk.**

This is the whole entry in one fact. The binomial is not decorative: **Dunal named the plant for the function.** *Coagulans* — the coagulator. Every vernacular name is a translation of the same observation: Hindi **Paneer Doda** and **Paneer Bandh** ("cheese-tier"), Punjabi **Paneer**, English **Indian Rennet** and **Indian Cheese Maker**, Persian **Panir-band**. A plant whose name in six languages means "the thing that sets milk" is a plant whose primary property was never in doubt.

**A NECESSARY SEPARATION.** *Withania coagulans* is **not Ashwagandha.** Ashwagandha is *Withania somnifera* — a different species, a different part (root, not fruit), a different indication (adaptogen, *rasāyana*, sleep — the epithet *somnifera* names it), a different lead molecule (withaferin A dominant), and a vastly larger evidence base. They share a genus and a chemical class (withanolides) and nothing else that matters clinically. *W. coagulans* is a **fruit** drug for **milk and sugar**; *W. somnifera* is a **root** drug for **stress and vigour**. Treating them as interchangeable — which the supplement market does — is a substitution error, not a synonym. *W. coagulans* is also, per the retrieved literature, a **"Near-Threatened"** species with poor natural regeneration, which makes casual substitution an ecological problem as well as a pharmacological one.

**Ayurveda (SS).** Sanskrit *Rishyagandha*; the fruit is the drug. Ayurveda reads it as *tikta-kaṭu* (bitter–pungent) with heating potency, and assigns it to *prameha* — the classical polyuric/metabolic category that includes *madhumeha*, "honey-urine," the entity that maps most closely onto diabetes mellitus. This is the antidiabetic thread, and it is old. The fruit is also *dīpana-pācana* (kindling digestive fire and promoting digestion), a carminative and a *vātanāśaka*. The living folk practice is beautifully simple: a few berries are steeped overnight in water, and the water — **"paneer dodi ka pani,"** the fruit-water — is drunk in the morning for sugar. This is not a museum piece; it is current household practice across northwest India and Pakistan.

**Unani (CM).** Known as *Panir-band* / *Habb-ul-kaknaj*. Unani classes it as hot and dry, and uses it as a *muhallil* (resolvent), *musakkin* (sedative) and digestive. The Unani reading of the antidiabetic use is consistent with its humoral frame: a hot, dry drug against a cold, wet, flooding disease of excessive flux.

**Siddha.** Used within the Siddha materia medica as a digestive and for urinary/metabolic complaints, consistent with the *prameha* rubric. Ethnobotanical survey work in the Koh-e-Safaid range of the Pakistan–Afghan borderlands found *W. coagulans* to be the **single most familiar medicinal plant among 92 species recorded**, with a relative frequency of citation of 0.96 and the highest relative importance value (1.63) of any plant surveyed ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13002-018-0229-4)). In a separate ethnomedicinal survey of diabetic patients in Jodhpur, Rajasthan, *W. coagulans* scored a Disease Consensus Index of **0.54**, placing it fifth among 21 antidiabetic plants — behind *Momordica charantia* (0.71) and *Azadirachta indica* (0.64) but ahead of *Gymnema sylvestre* ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2015.08.047)). **This is real, quantified ethnographic consensus.** It is evidence that people use it and believe in it. It is not evidence that it works.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The doctrinal architecture here is unusually clean, and unusually seductive — which is why it needs careful handling.

*The coagulating doctrine.* The traditional logic is sympathetic and explicit: **this is the plant that binds what is loose.** It takes milk — a flowing, dispersed, unstable colloid — and sets it into a solid. Ayurveda's *prameha* is, in its own terms, a disease of **excessive flux**: too much urine, too much sweetness pouring out, tissue dissolving and running away through the bladder. Confronting a disease of flux with the plant that visibly, reproducibly, in your own kitchen, arrests flux and makes the liquid hold — that is a doctrine of *stambhana*, of binding, applied by direct analogy from a demonstrable physical act. Add the *dīpana* reading — a hot, digestive drug that "cooks" what is improperly dispersed — and the system is coherent. The same berry that ties milk ties the body's leaking sweetness.

*What the chemistry actually says about that analogy.* **The analogy is chemically false, and the falseness is instructive.**

The milk-clotting is real and it is enzymology. The active agent is an **aspartic protease** — purified from the fruit as a 31 kDa monomer, optimal at pH 5.5 and 65 °C, retaining 73% proteolytic activity after an hour at 65 °C, with mass spectrometry and inhibitor profiling confirming that **the aspartic protease was the only enzyme responsible for milk coagulation** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2017.02.034)). It works exactly as animal chymosin works: it clips κ-casein, destabilises the casein micelle, and the micelles aggregate. It is a genuine vegetable rennet.

The antidiabetic activity, insofar as it exists, is attributed to **withanolides** — steroidal lactones — acting on glucose handling. These are entirely different molecules doing entirely unrelated chemistry in an entirely different tissue.

So: **the berry does two real things, and the tradition connected them with a metaphor that does not hold.** The protease does not tie sugar. The withanolides do not clot milk. The plant is a genuine double-actor whose two actions have no mechanistic relationship whatsoever — the doctrine inferred a single principle where there are two. This is the entry's most valuable lesson: *the name being the function is what makes the wrong inference so persuasive.* A plant that visibly performs "binding" invites the belief that it binds everything. The visible act licenses the invisible claim. It should not.

*What survives.* The withanolide thread stands or falls on its own preclinical evidence — which is genuine, and modest, and reviewed honestly below. It does not inherit credibility from the cheese.

### Active Components

- **Aspartic protease (milk-clotting)** — 31 kDa monomer; Km 1.29 mg/mL and Vmax 0.035 µmol Tyr/min on casein; optimum pH 5.5, 65 °C; the sole coagulating enzyme.
- **Withanolides** — steroidal lactones; the medicinal class. Includes **Coagulansin-A**, **Coagulin L**, withanolide J, withaperuvin, 27-hydroxywithanolide I, coagulin E.
- **Withaferin A** — present in fruit (confirmed by HPLC), though this is the molecule that dominates *W. somnifera*.
- **Flavonoids, tannins, phenols, β-sterols, steroids, terpenoids** — reported across fruit extracts.
- **Trace minerals** — Mg and Ca, proposed (weakly) as contributors to glycaemic action.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

*W. coagulans* is filed as a **metabolic-regulation and digestive agent**, with a hard annotation: **its food-technology credentials are far better established than its medical ones.**

Its Kenetic value is partly as a **case study in doctrinal inference**. It is the cleanest example in the Codex of a tradition reading a visible, reproducible physical property (curdling) as a signature for an invisible internal one (binding sugar), building a coherent and widely-held practice on it, and being — as far as the mechanism goes — simply wrong about the connection while possibly being accidentally right about the outcome via a completely different route.

The antidiabetic use is filed as **ethnographically strong, preclinically supported, clinically unproven.** The gap between the confidence of use (RFC 0.96 — near-universal recognition in surveyed communities) and the strength of the human evidence (none retrieved) is one of the widest in this volume.

### Scientific Correlation

*Evidence below was retrieved from PubMed for this entry; DOIs link to the original reports.*

**Milk-clotting. Grade: STRONG — established, characterised, and the best-supported property in the file.**

The aspartic protease has been purified (ammonium sulfate fractionation + cation exchange), sized at 31 kDa, kinetically characterised, and shown by inhibitor profiling and mass spectrometry to be the sole coagulant; both NaCl and CaCl₂ reduced its activity, suggesting suitability for low-salt cheeses ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2017.02.034)). **This is the one claim in the entry that requires no hedging.**

Applied work confirms it while also puncturing any romance: in a factorial comparison against camel chymosin on camel and bovine milk, **pure *Withania* extract exhibited the *lower* coagulating effect**, producing cheeses with lower yield, hardness, fat, protein and total solids ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-92797-6)). It works; it works *worse* than the industrial enzyme. It has also been used to produce analog cheese from corn steep liquor ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/fsn3.3191)). Honest reading: a functional but second-rate rennet with a niche in low-salt and vegetarian cheese-making.

**Antidiabetic. Grade: WEAK–MODERATE preclinical; NO human trial evidence retrieved. This is the claim the plant is sold on, and it is the claim with the thinnest support.**

The animal data are real and directionally consistent:

- Aqueous fruit extract in normal, sub-, mild- and severely diabetic rats: 1000 mg/kg identified as most effective, reducing fasting blood glucose by 33.2% at 4 h in normal rats; glucose-tolerance reductions of 15.7%, 28.9% and 37.8% at 3 h in normal, sub- and mild-diabetic rats; and in severe diabetics, 52.9% (FBG) and 54.1% (post-prandial) reductions after 30 days ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12291-009-0015-0)). **Note the dose: 1000 mg/kg in a rat is enormous.** The paper's attribution of activity to trace Mg and Ca is not persuasive.
- Fruit extract (10 mg/kg, 3 weeks) in four-month streptozotocin-diabetic rats attenuated hyperglycaemia, reduced IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α in kidney, restored glutathione, inhibited lipid peroxidation and reduced kidney hypertrophy — the authors explicitly concluding that "translation of therapeutic efficacy in humans requires further studies" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1155/2014/201436)).
- A hydroalcoholic fruit fraction inhibited α-amylase (IC₅₀ 104.71 µg/mL) and α-glucosidase (IC₅₀ 70.79 µg/mL) in vitro and blunted the glycaemic peak in a starch tolerance test — though notably **none of the fractions delayed the time to peak glucose** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.4103/jpbs.JPBS_423_20)). Enzyme-inhibition IC₅₀s in the tens of µg/mL for a crude fraction are weak.

**And the deflation:** Coagulin L has been proposed as a DPP-4 inhibitor on the strength of molecular docking (−7.69 kcal/mol vs saxagliptin's −8.44) and 100 ns MD simulation ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/07391102.2020.1822917)). Similar in-silico work proposes *W. coagulans* withanolides as HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.36721/PJPS.2026.39.4.REG.14438.1)) and as VEGFR inhibitors for glioblastoma ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00894-024-06178-7)). **These are docking studies. They are computational hypotheses and they demonstrate nothing about efficacy in an organism.** A favourable docking score is a prediction that a molecule *might* fit a pocket. It is not pharmacology. This entry cites them explicitly in order to mark them as non-evidence, because they are routinely deployed in marketing as though they were results.

**Summary of the antidiabetic thread: consistent rodent signal at high doses, plausible enzyme-level mechanisms, zero human trials retrieved, and a supporting literature padded with in-silico work. The gap between RFC 0.96 and n=0 humans is the finding.**

**Hypolipidaemic. Grade: WEAK preclinical.** Aqueous fruit extract (250 mg/kg) in cholesterol-fed hyperlipidaemic rabbits reduced total cholesterol, triacylglycerol, LDL and tissue lipid, decreased acetyl-CoA carboxylase activity, reduced plasma lipid peroxides and raised glutathione and SOD ([PMID 25241586](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25241586/) — no DOI assigned). Single species, single lab, no human data.

**Coagulansin-A — the most interesting molecule. Grade: MODERATE preclinical, and genuinely promising.**

Coagulansin-A (Coag-A), a withanolide from *W. coagulans*, was compared head-to-head with dexamethasone in two rodent models:

- **Arthritis** (CFA-induced, 10 mg/kg i.p., 28 days): improved body and organ weights, haematology, serum biochemistry, survival and antioxidant status; significantly better histopathology than dexamethasone, with hepato- and nephroprotective rather than toxic effects ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.taap.2024.117008)).
- **Asthma** (OVA-induced, 0.1–10 mg/kg i.p.): improved airway inflammation, antioxidant status and physiological parameters while preserving liver and kidney structure — **whereas dexamethasone showed hepato- and nephrotoxicity** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10787-026-02238-x)).

Beating dexamethasone on safety in a rodent model is a meaningful preclinical result, and it lands on the traditional respiratory indication (the fruit and leaf are used for cough, bronchitis and asthma-like symptoms in South Asian and Middle Eastern practice). **But both studies come from the same research group, both are rodent, both are i.p. rather than oral, and neither has been replicated independently or advanced to human trial.** Filed as the plant's most promising lead, not as a conclusion.

**Other. Grade: WEAK to NEGLIGIBLE.** Ethanolic fruit extract mitigated silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis in rats via NF-κB-p65/NLRP3 and TXNIP modulation ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fitote.2025.106578)) — real mechanistic work, rodent-only. Methanolic fruit extract showed apoptotic activity against MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells (~50% viability at 40 µg/mL) but — importantly for the species separation — **activity was lower than *W. somnifera*** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaim.2017.01.001)). Silver-nanoparticle papers using *W. coagulans* as a reducing agent ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/cbdv.202402839); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.msec.2019.02.113)) are **green-chemistry publications, not pharmacology of the plant**, and carry no weight for any traditional claim. A broad review covers the phytochemistry and claimed activities ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules26226881)).

**Conservation note:** the plant is documented as "Near-Threatened" with poor natural regeneration, motivating micropropagation research ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-08274-7)) and transgenic withanolide-enrichment work ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsomega.3c07069)).

**Summary grade: The rennet is proven. The antidiabetic doctrine is ethnographically near-universal, preclinically plausible at high rodent doses, and clinically untested. Coagulansin-A is the real lead. The plant's fame rests on the claim its evidence supports least.**

### Contraindications

- **Hypoglycaemia with antidiabetic drugs — the principal risk.** The whole point of this plant's folk use is glucose-lowering, and rodent data show substantial FBG reductions. Anyone taking insulin, sulfonylureas, meglitinides, metformin or any glucose-lowering agent who also drinks paneer dodi water risks **additive hypoglycaemia**. This is not a theoretical interaction — it is the plant doing exactly what it is taken to do, twice. Blood glucose must be monitored; do not stack an unquantified folk preparation on a titrated drug.
- **Pregnancy — avoid.** No safety data retrieved. Solanaceae withanolide-bearing species warrant precaution, and the genus's better-studied member (*W. somnifera*) is conventionally avoided in pregnancy. Absence of retrieved data is not reassurance.
- **Lactation — avoid.** No data.
- **Surgery.** Discontinue in advance of scheduled surgery given the glycaemic effect and unquantified dose.
- **Species substitution — *W. somnifera*.** This entry does not transfer to Ashwagandha and Ashwagandha's evidence does not transfer here. Different species, part, indication, and lead molecule. Verify the binomial and the plant part on any commercial product.
- **Solanaceae membership.** Nightshade family; individuals with established Solanaceae sensitivity should exercise caution.
- **Dose is genuinely unknown.** "A few berries in water overnight" is not a dose. Berry size, withanolide content, steeping time and water volume are all uncontrolled, and withanolide content varies with growing conditions (micronutrient supplementation alone measurably alters it). **A hypoglycaemic agent with an uncontrolled dose is the specific hazard here.**
- **Conservation.** Near-Threatened with poor natural regeneration. Wild-harvested material carries an ethical cost.
- **No human efficacy evidence.** Nothing retrieved for this entry supports *W. coagulans* as a treatment for diabetes in humans. It must not replace glycaemic therapy.

**Cross-references:**
- ***Withania somnifera*** (Ashwagandha) — **the mandatory disambiguation.** Same genus, different drug entirely: root vs fruit, adaptogen vs antidiabetic, withaferin A-dominant, far larger evidence base. The most common substitution error in the Solanaceae.
- **CPU-01038** — *Wrightia tinctoria* (Indrajao): the milk-clotting cross-link. An unrelated Apocynaceae whose stem proteases hydrolyse κ-casein in the same order as commercial vegetable coagulants — convergent enzymology, and a mirror-image name problem (there, the vernacular misleads; here, the binomial tells the truth).
