# Coriander — كزبرة (Kuzbara) / Κορίανδρον (Koriandron) / धनिया (Dhanyā) / 芫荽 (Yánsui)
## *Coriandrum sativum L. — Coriander / Cilantro*
**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM, EP, SBJ, SS, FMT
**Energetic Classification (TCM):** ◉ Qi
**Training Phase:** `[PRIME]` `[RESET]`

---

### Origin & Tradition

Coriander is among the oldest documented culinary-medicinal plants — coriander seeds were found in the Nahal Hemar cave (Israel, ~8,000 BCE), in Egyptian New Kingdom tomb offerings, and in the Ebers Papyrus. The unique dual-identity of this plant — the seeds (spice, carminative, cooling) and the fresh herb/leaf (cilantro — cooling, aromatic, digestive) with divergent phytochemical profiles — creates two partially overlapping but distinct pharmacological identities documented across all six traditions. The seeds have been used consistently for their digestive-carminative properties across all traditions; the fresh herb for its cooling, aromatic properties; and an emerging modern research line on the seed's insulin-sensitizing and anxiolytic activities through linalool.

**EP — Ebers Papyrus (documented in digestive and wound preparations):** Coriander (grt in hieroglyphic notation) appears in the Ebers Papyrus in digestive preparations, wound dressing preparations, and as a component of multi-herb formulas. The carminative application for digestive discomfort is the primary documented use.

**AT — Assyrian (Kisibirru in Nineveh tablets):** Coriander is one of the most frequently mentioned plants in the Nineveh medical tablets. Applications: digestive carminative; anti-nausea; wound healing; combined with other spices in complex polyherbal formulas. The Assyrian palace herb garden records document coriander as a cultivated medical-culinary plant.

**DMM — De Materia Medica (Κορίανδρον — Koriandron):** Dioscorides covers coriander in Book III. He notes: the seed as carminative; against intestinal worms (anthelmintic — the aldehyde compounds); anti-nausea; the fresh herb as cooling, but notes excessive fresh leaf consumption causes confusion and stupefaction (a reference to the higher linalool concentration of fresh leaf causing mild CNS effects at large doses). He recommends the seed over the fresh herb for most medicinal applications.

**CM — Canon of Medicine (كزبرة — Kuzbara):** Ibn Sina classifies coriander as cold and dry in the second degree. Primary applications: digestive; carminative; reduces blood to the head (cooling cerebrovascular — relevant for his fever management); cooling for hot temperaments; the roasted seed as the mildest preparation; the seed with vinegar for reducing erysipelas (hot skin infection) — topical anti-inflammatory. He explicitly warns against excessive fresh coriander leaf consumption causing "diminished reason."

**SS — Sushruta Samhita (Dhanyā / Dhānā — coriander seed):** Coriander seed is classified in Ayurveda as: Tridoshic (one of the few spices that does not aggravate any dosha when used in culinary amounts); Madhura-Tikta rasa, Sheeta virya; Tridosha shamaka. Primary applications: Dīpana (digestive fire-kindling without excess heat); diuretic (cooling UTI remedy); anti-diabetic (seed infusion for reducing blood sugar — the AMPK mechanism now understood); the classic post-meal digestive preparation (Dhaniyā pāna — coriander seed water).

**SBJ — Bencao Gangmu (芫荽 — Yánsui — fresh herb; 胡荽子 — Húsuīzǐ — seed):** Li Shizhen covers both the herb and seed. Fresh herb: pungent, warm; promotes sweating; unblocks the pores; the earliest Wind-Cold dispersion preparation. Seed: promotes digestion; the classic digestive seed. Interestingly, TCM classifies the fresh herb as warm (in contrast to the cooling assignment in Ayurveda and Ibn Sina) — a genuine tradition-specific difference reflecting different primary applications and perhaps different varieties.

### Active Components

**Seeds:**
- **Linalool** — 60–80% of seed essential oil; primary GABA-A modulator (anxiolytic and mild sedative at high doses); anti-nociceptive (analgesic via TRPA1 agonism followed by desensitization); anti-fungal; the major bioactive monoterpene
- **α-Pinene, γ-Terpinene** — secondary monoterpenes; antimicrobial; bronchodilatory
- **Coriandrin, dihydrocoriandrin** — isocoumarins; the insulin secretagogue mechanism; stimulate insulin release from pancreatic β-cells; hypoglycemic
- **Petroselinic acid** — unusual omega-6 fatty acid (C18:1Δ6) in seed oil; anti-inflammatory; the dominant seed oil fatty acid
- **Flavonoids (quercetin, isoquercitrin, rutin)** — anti-inflammatory; antioxidant

**Fresh leaf (cilantro):**
- **Linalool, geraniol, β-phellandrene** — the fresh herb volatile profile; distinct from the seed profile; the unique "soapy" aroma to those with OR6A2 gene variant (cilantro taste polymorphism)
- **Dodecenal** — aldehyde; the primary antimicrobial compound of fresh cilantro; active against *Salmonella* (Wang IK et al. study)
- **β-Carotene** — 337μg/100g (significant for a herb); provitamin A
- **Vitamin K** — 310μg/100g (extraordinarily high; relevant for anticoagulant interaction — see contraindications)

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**1. Digestive carminative and insulin sensitization ([PRIME] / [BUILD])**
Coriander seed's linalool and coriandrin combination provides two complementary metabolic benefits: immediate carminative action (reduces gas, bloating, cramping after meals) and medium-term insulin sensitization (β-cell stimulation + PPAR-γ activation improving glucose uptake). For practitioners on high-carbohydrate BUILD phase diets, coriander seed infusion or powder with meals improves carbohydrate tolerance and reduces GI discomfort from high-volume eating.

**2. Anxiolytic and sleep support (linalool — [SLEEP] complementary)**
Linalool's GABA-A positive allosteric modulation (without the receptor downregulation of pharmaceutical benzodiazepines) provides gentle anxiolytic activity. Aromatherapy studies and oral studies both confirm reduced anxiety markers. At culinary doses this is a gentle nervous system tonic; at supplemental essential oil aromatherapy doses, it provides pre-sleep calming.

**3. Antimicrobial for food safety and respiratory ([RESET])**
Dodecenal from fresh cilantro is active against *Salmonella* at concentrations present in a normal serving — the fresh herb's traditional pairing with meats and fish in hot-climate cuisines reflects empirical antimicrobial food safety practice. The seed's terpene profile provides respiratory antimicrobial and bronchodilatory activity relevant during cold/cough recovery.

**4. Heavy metal chelation (experimental — cilantro fresh herb)**
A research line (not yet RCT-validated) suggests cilantro fresh herb facilitates mobilization of heavy metals (mercury, lead, arsenic) via organosulfur-like binding. The evidence base remains primarily animal studies and case reports — included here as an active research area, not a clinically validated application.

### Preparation & Dosing

**Coriander seed infusion (digestive / blood sugar — standard preparation):**
1 tsp (2–3g) lightly crushed coriander seeds in 250mL hot water; steep 10 minutes; drink 20–30 minutes before meals. The Ayurvedic Dhaniyā pāna preparation; the TCM/CM digestive seed infusion. Can also be consumed post-meal as a carminative.

**Coriander seed powder (metabolic — Ayurvedic dose):**
2–4g dried coriander seed powder in warm water 2x/day; the dose used in clinical studies for blood sugar management in pre-diabetic populations.

**Fresh cilantro (antimicrobial / heavy metal — food medicine):**
20–50g fresh cilantro leaves as regular daily component of salads, sauces, and prepared dishes. The food-dose provides antimicrobial dodecenal, carotenoids, and vitamin K. The heavy metal chelation application uses 400mg freeze-dried cilantro extract 2x/day — but evidence quality is insufficient for a firm recommendation.

### Scientific Correlation

- **Anxiolytic (linalool):** Linck VM et al. (2009) *Phytomedicine* 16:1130-1137: inhaled linalool significantly reduced anxiety-like behavior in mice in elevated plus maze; GABA-A benzodiazepine receptor mediation confirmed; comparable to diazepam at GABA-ergic binding
- **Hypoglycemic (seed):** Eidi M et al. (2009) *Phytother Res* 23:404-406: coriander seed extract significantly reduced blood glucose in streptozotocin-diabetic rats; increased insulin secretion and improved peripheral glucose uptake vs. control
- **Anti-Salmonella (dodecenal):** Wang IK et al. (2005) *J Agric Food Chem* 53:7186-7190: (E)-2-dodecenal isolated from cilantro demonstrated antibacterial activity against *Salmonella* significantly superior to gentamicin at equivalent concentrations; membrane disruption mechanism
- **Lipid-lowering:** Chithra V, Leelamma S (1997) *Plant Foods Hum Nutr* 51:167-172: coriander seed significantly reduced total cholesterol, LDL, and triglycerides in high-fat-diet rats; increased HDL; bile acid sequestration and lipid oxidation inhibition mechanisms
- **Heavy-metal chelation (cilantro fresh herb):** Aga M et al. (2001) — cilantro leaf extract reduced lead levels in rodent models; Uchikawa T et al. (2011) — cilantro + chlorella combination accelerated mercury excretion in human subjects (evidence base still limited; an active research area, not a validated protocol)

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian (EP).** No cultivation framing is attested, and one distinction has to be held firmly. Coriander is well established in Egyptian funerary contexts archaeobotanically — seeds recovered from tomb deposits — but **presence in a tomb is a deposition fact, not a cultivation status**. It tells us that coriander was available, valued enough to place with the dead, and part of the food economy. It does not tell us that the tradition held coriander to act on the *ka*, the *ba*, the *akh* or the *ib*. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint. No *heka* recitation is attested for coriander in the sources retrieved, and no deity attribution is made here.

**Assyrian (AT).** No cultivation status is attested. *Kisibirru* is frequent in the Nineveh tablets and coriander appears in the palace garden records as a cultivated plant — but that is horticulture, not cultivation in this section's sense, and the Codex's blocking warning over Assyrian substance identifications and tablet references applies in full. The corpus classifies illness by causal agency; nothing retrieved assigns coriander a role in relation to a person's vital constitution.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. What he gives a cultivator is a discipline of attention, and coriander is a good demonstration of it: he treats seed and fresh herb as two different materials with different actions, recommends the seed over the herb for most medicinal purposes, and records that the fresh leaf in quantity produces confusion and stupefaction. That is a dose-and-part judgement arrived at by observation — the practice is knowing which part you hold, in what quantity, and where the boundary of safe use sits.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** No cultivation status is attested; only the frame. The tradition's cultivation idea is the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture — a finite endowment whose depletion is ageing — so longevity here is conservation, not acquisition. Ibn Sina's rating of coriander as cold and dry in the second degree is a temperamental co-ordinate governing its use in hot complaints, not a claim that it conserves that endowment. His warning that excessive fresh leaf causes "diminished reason" is a *harm* recorded against a faculty, not a cultivation status; it is a reason for restraint, not a practice. No claim that coriander strengthens the *ruh* is attested, and none should be constructed from the *mufarrih* literature, which belongs to a different work.

**Ayurveda (SS).** Attested in the *dravyaguna* with unusually complete co-ordinates — *madhura-tikta rasa*, *sheeta virya*, *tridosha shamaka*, *dīpana*, *mūtrajana*, *prameha-ghna* — and one genuinely notable property: it is among the few spices that aggravate no *dosha* at culinary quantity. That tolerability is a real and useful fact. But *rasayana* and *vajikarana* are formal Sushrutan divisions, and coriander seed is not recorded in either in the sources retrieved; no *ojas* claim is attested for it. Status therefore unattested — a well-characterised therapeutic and dietary substance without a rejuvenative grade.

**Chinese (SBJ).** No *san pin* 三品 grade is attested for either 芫荽 (fresh herb) or 胡荽子 (seed) in the sources retrieved, and it is worth noting that this entry's Chinese material is drawn from the *Bencao Gangmu* — Li Shizhen, 1596 — a late compilation rather than the classics, so no grade should be back-projected onto the earlier tradition from it. The recorded actions are dispersing and digestive: the fresh herb pungent and warm, promoting sweating and unblocking the pores; the seed promoting digestion. Both are curative in character, and *yang sheng* 養生 is a conservational project, not a curative one. No 輕身 attribution is attested.

**Tibetan (FMT).** No cultivation framing is attested, and this entry supplies no Tibetan attestation for coriander at all — the tag stands without supporting material in the body of the entry. In the Four Tantras' own architecture, medicine is the third of four treatment branches and does not reach the root of the three *nyes pa*, which is the three poisons; there is no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice to place coriander in, and *bcud len* is not attested for it. The tradition tag should be re-examined against a source.

**Practitioner's note.** Coriander is a food that does an honest job. Seed infusion before or after a meal, at the quantity a cook would use, is a carminative with a real and reproducible effect, and the seed's blood-sugar research line is worth watching without being oversold. What a cultivator does with it is eat it — regularly, in ordinary amounts, as part of how meals are constructed. Six of the seven traditions here classify it therapeutically or dietetically and none assigns it a rejuvenative grade, which is the correct outcome for a kitchen spice and not a deficiency in the record.

### Contraindications

- **Vitamin K content (fresh herb — anticoagulant interaction)** — cilantro contains extraordinarily high vitamin K (310μg/100g); large quantities of fresh cilantro significantly increase vitamin K intake and can counteract warfarin anticoagulation; patients on warfarin should maintain consistent cilantro intake (not wildly variable) and monitor INR; those on VKA therapy should not start/stop heavy cilantro consumption abruptly
- **Hypoglycemic drugs — additive effect** — coriandrin insulin-secretagogue effect is additive with sulfonylureas and insulin; blood glucose monitoring required at therapeutic seed doses
- **Allergy (Apiaceae family)** — cross-reactivity with carrot, celery, parsley, fennel, dill, anise; Apiaceae allergy syndrome; oral allergy syndrome possible
- **OR6A2 polymorphism (cilantro aversion)** — the "soapy taste" experienced by ~17% of Northern Europeans is a genuine genetic variant (OR6A2 olfactory receptor gene); not a contraindication but explains patient non-compliance with cilantro-based preparations; substitute coriander seed preparations
