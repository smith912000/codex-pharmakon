# Teucrium chamaepitys — Chamaipitus (Dioscorides 3-175); Oinos chamaipituinos (5-80)

## *Teucrium chamaepitys* (the Dioscorides translators' suggested identification, citing Linnaeus; ground pine, yellow bugle) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Identity note.** This plant is named only by Dioscorides (DMM). The binomial is the suggestion printed on the SUGGESTED lines of the translation by Osbaldeston and Wood (2000), not Dioscorides' own: "Chamaepitys lutea vulgaris [Bauhin], Teucrium chamaepitys [Linnaeus], Ajuga chamaepitys [in Sprague] — Ground Pine, Yellow Bugle". The existing entry I_503 (*Ajuga iva*, "Ground Pine") also draws on the Dioscorides *chamaepitys* chapter, and other *Teucrium* entries exist (I_654, I_712, I_716, I_740). The overlap is flagged for editorial decision. [USER: verify] Source: `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).**

*3-175 CHAMAIPITUS* (heading at line 7977). "Chamaepitys is a bow-backed herb creeping on the ground, with leaves similar to the smaller sempervivum [4-89, 4-90, 4-91], but much thinner, fatter and rough, thick around the branches, with a smell of pine. The flowers are thin and yellow, (or white) and the root is like that of chicory." Uses: "A decoction of the leaves of this (taken as a drink with wine for seven days) cures jaundice. A decoction (taken as a drink with honey water for forty days) cures hip pains. It is given (effectively) for liver complaints, frequent painful urination and inflamed kidneys, and it is good for griping." Then: "In Heraclea, Pontus they use it as an antidote, giving a decoction to drink against aconitum [4-77, 4-78]." Polenta moistened with a decoction is applied for the same purposes; "Pounded into small pieces with figs (and given as a pill) it soothes the bowels"; taken with honey, "scales aeris" and rosin "it purges"; as a pessary with honey "it expels things from the womb"; smeared on with honey it "dissolves hard lumps in the breasts, heals wounds, and represses herpes". Other names: *pitusorusis*, *orizelon*, *holocyron* in Pontus ("wild bryony"), *ionia* (Athenians), *sideritis* (Euboea); the Magi *sanguis Minervae*; the Romans *cupripum*; the Dacians *dochela*.

*5-80 OINOS CHAMAIPITUINOS* (heading at line 10192). "Chamaepityinum is made in the same way, and is good for the same things as it is diuretic." "In the same way" refers to the wine of the preceding chapter (5-79, *chamelaea* wine), whose method is twelve teaspoons of the beaten, sifted shrub put into two thirds of a gallon of must and poured into another jar after two months. The 5-80 text itself gives no quantity.

### Active Components

*Teucrium* species (family Lamiaceae) are generally known to contain diterpenoids, and some species have been linked to liver injury. Whether this applies to the plant Dioscorides describes was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records the plant for jaundice, hip pain, liver and kidney complaints, as an antidote to aconite in Pontus, and in a wine; these are his claims and not a Codex protocol. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

*3-175:* a decoction of the leaves in wine "for seven days" (jaundice), in honey water "for forty days" (hip pains); polenta moistened with the decoction applied; a pill pounded with figs; a honey pessary. *5-80:* a wine made as in 5-79. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Some *Teucrium* species are generally known to be hepatotoxic, and germander preparations have been associated with liver injury. This is a general statement about the genus; no study of this species was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** no warming, cooling, taste or degree is printed in 3-175 or 5-80. The chapter prints a smell of pine.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. The chapter offers a discipline of attention: the creeping, bow-backed habit, the smell of pine, yellow or white flowers, a root like chicory's, and the long course (seven and forty days) for named complaints. No status is assigned. The binomial is the translators' gloss. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the source read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The abstentive answer is correct: the uses recorded include a forty-day course and a pessary, and the genus is associated with liver harm.

### Contraindications

*Teucrium* species are associated with liver injury, and the 3-175 uses include pessary and abortifacient-type actions ("expels things from the womb"). Do not use in pregnancy. The traditional uses are not advice; for jaundice or liver complaints seek qualified care.
