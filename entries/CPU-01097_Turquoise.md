# Turquoise — THUITES LITHOS, "Thyites" (Dioscorides 5-154) / Turquoise, g.yu (Clark; Men-Tsee-Khang)
## *Blue-green copper-aluminium phosphate gemstone; Osbaldeston prints "SUGGESTED: Thyites — Turquoise" for the Greek stone, and Clark prints "Turquoise" for the Tibetan* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM, FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

Turquoise enters the Codex through a Greek stone whose identification is a suggestion and a Tibetan precious substance that the translator calls turquoise outright. The two texts give it small and different uses, and the entry keeps them apart.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 5-154 *THUITES LITHOS* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10634-10637, Osbaldeston and Wood 2000; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` row 5-154, line 10634; `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 880 "Thuites Lithos, mineral, DMM V"). The whole chapter: "Thyites is found in Ethiopia. It is greenish like jasper [previously this was green chalcedony], and when washed produces a milky colour. It is extremely biting, and is able to clean away things that darken the pupils." The heading "SUGGESTED: Thyites — Turquoise" is the editors' identification and the bracketed gloss "[previously this was green chalcedony]" is theirs as well: the text itself describes a greenish stone from Ethiopia that turns milky in water and does not name turquoise. Whether Dioscorides meant turquoise is therefore an editor's hypothesis, and the entry's only Greek evidence for turquoise rests on that. [USER: verify] The chapter sits in Book 5, among the stones: the previous chapter (5-153) is the banded alabaster stone, "Alabastrites", and the next (5-155) is the Judaean stone, which is marked "UNKNOWN" by the editors and said to "break stones in the bladder". The chapter gives no dose, preparation or contraindication.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch.20, "(1) The Powers of Precious [Metals]" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` lines 810-811): "Turquoise: [turquoise is of two types, superior and ordinary. Of the superior types drug.dkar is pale blue and very bright, whilst drug.dmar is bright reddish-blue and greasy. The third, gyu.spyang, is brighter than the first two sub-types. Among the ordinary types bar.g-yu resembles drug.dmar and g-yu.sngon resembles drug.dkar. A Chinese turquoise also exists.] Turquoise cures poison-fever and liver-fever." It comes in the list straight after the metals (gold, silver, copper, iron: "Herewith is concluded the section on metals which can be melted") and before pearl. The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text lists it in the same group (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 3330): "First to reveal the efficacy of precious medicine: Gold promotes longevity, is rejuvenating and acts as an antidote against precious metal poisoning. Silver dries up chhuser, pus and impure blood. Copper dries up pus and cures hot disorders of the lungs and liver. Iron cures hepatotoxicity, eye disorders and generalised edema. Turquoise is an antidote against poison and cures hot disorders of the liver. Pearl stops cerebrospinal fluid discharge ..." The longevity and rejuvenation are said of gold, not of turquoise. The book index lists turquoise at page 197 (line 7549). `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 125 carries it as "Turquoise ... Explanatory Tantra ch.20". The "poison-fever" of Clark and the "antidote against poison" of Men-Tsee-Khang are the two translators' renderings of the same sentence; I did not check the Tibetan.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, turquoise is a hydrated phosphate of copper and aluminium, its blue-green colour coming from copper (with iron giving greener varieties); it is a soft stone (hardness about 5 to 6) and is mostly sold as a gem. Imitations are common: dyed howlite and magnesite, stabilised or reconstituted turquoise, and glass. The Greek stone, if it is not turquoise at all, may be a green chalcedony or another stone, as the editors' own bracket indicates.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Greek chapter describes a sharp stone that cleans what darkens the pupils, an eye application, with no instruction on how it was applied. The Tibetan texts list turquoise as a precious substance that cures poison-fever and liver-fever, and the stone is not recommended for internal use here. The Codex gives no protocol, and a copper-containing mineral swallowed or applied to the eye is a hazard.

### Preparation & Dosing

None is given. Dioscorides says only that it is "washed" (the milky colour test) and is "extremely biting"; the Tibetan text describes the types by colour and brightness and gives no preparation. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Copper compounds are irritant to the eye and mucous membranes and, swallowed in quantity, are toxic; copper-containing minerals can also carry arsenic, lead or other impurities, and aluminium phosphate is insoluble. There is no clinical evidence that turquoise treats fever, liver disease or poisoning, and none was retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** 5-154 prints "extremely biting" (acrid). No hot, cold, wet or dry quality is printed in the passage.

**Tibetan (FMT):** none is printed for turquoise in Clark's entry beyond its uses (cures poison-fever and liver-fever). Clark gives taste and power for the metals listed just before it (for example silver "has the same (astringent) taste and post-digestive taste as gold"), but not for turquoise, and the entry does not extend them to it. The Men-Tsee-Khang line prints the actions only.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What the chapter offers is a discipline of attention: the stone's provenance ("found in Ethiopia"), its colour ("greenish like jasper"), and a simple assay of what it does in water ("when washed produces a milky colour"), and then its action on the eye. The chapter is therapeutic, and no cultivation status is assigned to the stone. The identification as turquoise is the editors' suggestion.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* Turquoise sits in the Explanatory Tantra's section on precious substances next to gold, silver, copper, iron and pearl (Clark lines 810-811; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3330) and has a therapeutic status only: cures poison-fever and liver-fever, or is an antidote against poison and cures hot liver disorders. The same Men-Tsee-Khang sentence says gold "promotes longevity" and "is rejuvenating"; it says no such thing of turquoise. The Tibetan frame is that pathology is at root a spiritual condition and that medicine is one of four treatment branches, supporting practice but not reaching the root; nothing read ties turquoise to that. No *bcud len* use was found. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here makes turquoise a substance for cultivation. The Greek text uses a stone, probably not even turquoise, as a sharp eye remedy; the Tibetan texts list it as a precious antidote and liver-heat remedy and give the longevity and rejuvenation lines to gold. A cultivator takes no practice from this entry. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Toxicity:** turquoise contains copper; swallowing powdered stone or placing it in the eye risks irritation and copper poisoning, and unprocessed mineral may carry other metals.
- **Eye:** Dioscorides calls the stone "extremely biting"; abrasive and caustic material in the eye can scar the cornea.
- **Identity and adulteration:** the Greek stone's identification is only suggested, and commercial "turquoise" is often dyed or reconstituted stone.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** no evidence read; avoid.
- **Conservation and ethics:** gem-grade turquoise is a limited resource, and grinding it for a remedy of no demonstrated benefit is wasteful.
