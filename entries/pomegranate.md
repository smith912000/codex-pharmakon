# Pomegranate — *Punica granatum* L.

**Family:** Lythraceae | **Class:** plant | **Volume:** I · Plant | **Codex id:** CPU-00103
**Attested in:** rGyud-bzhi · Avicenna · Sushruta · Ebers Papyrus · Assyrian tablets

---

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

*Punica granatum* L. (Linnaeus, 1753) belongs to the family Lythraceae (formerly placed in the monogeneric family Punicaceae prior to APG III reclassification). Common names: pomegranate (English), anar (Persian, Urdu, Hindi), dadima (Sanskrit), sendru / སེན་དྲུ (Tibetan), rumman (Arabic). The genus name *Punica* derives from the Latin *malum punicum* — "Carthaginian apple" — reflecting the fruit's ancient association with North Africa in Roman botanical writing, though the plant's native range extends from the eastern Mediterranean through Iran to the northwestern Himalayas.

A deciduous or semi-evergreen shrub or small tree reaching 5–8 m, characterised by glossy lanceolate leaves, scarlet pentamerous flowers, and a persistent calyx atop a thick-skinned berry (botanically a balausta) containing 200–1,400 arils surrounded by juicy pulp. The rind (pericarp), root bark, flowers, seeds, and juice are all pharmacologically distinct and are used differentially across traditions.

## 2. Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2a. rGyud-bzhi (Four Medical Tantras) — Tibetan Medicine

In Sowa Rigpa (the Tibetan science of healing), the pomegranate is known as *sendru* (སེན་དྲུ) and is classified as warm in nature with sour taste, corresponding to the fire and earth elements. The rGyud-bzhi, the foundational four-volume Tibetan medical canon (composed c. 12th century CE, attributed in tradition to Yuthog Yonten Gonpo the Younger), employs sendru principally for disorders of digestive fire (*me drod*), including indigestion, gastric cold, and accumulated metabolic waste. Pomegranate appears in compound formulas such as *Sendru Dang Nay* (Pomegranate Digestive Essence), combined with cinnamon, cardamom, long pepper, and saffron, targeting stimulation of gastric warmth and appetite. Both seed and rind preparations are documented. The sour-warm pharmacological profile makes sendru an agent for augmenting *mkhris pa* (bile) and countering cold-phlegm (*bad kan*) accumulation. [Sources: 1, 6]

### 2b. Avicenna — *Al-Qanun fi'l-Tibb* (Canon of Medicine, completed c. 1025 CE)

Ibn Sina (Avicenna, 980–1037 CE) classified pomegranate across its morphological parts with distinct temperamental qualities. Sweet pomegranate was prescribed for chronic cough, throat roughness, and chest pain, functioning as a mucus-softening agent for the respiratory tract. Sour pomegranate — particularly the dried seeds and the mesocarp — was employed for its pronounced astringent quality (*qabid*), which Avicenna considered beneficial for "tanning" an overactive or humourally damp stomach, reducing excess humoral flow to the intestines. Pomegranate peel was noted for its drying and astringent properties, prescribed for uterine haemorrhage. The root bark preparation was used for intestinal parasites. Avicenna's framework placed different parts of the plant in different humoral registers: sweet varieties as more temperate and moist; sour varieties and the rind as cold and dry in the second or third degree, hence styptic and anti-diarrhoeic. [Sources: 3, 7]

### 2c. Sushruta — *Sushruta Samhita* (c. 600 BCE, compiled and redacted to c. 500 CE)

In Ayurvedic classical medicine, pomegranate is *dadima* (दाडिम). Acharya Sushruta listed dadima among the finest fruits alongside amalaka, draksha, and matulunga (*Sushruta Samhita*, Uttaratantra). Specific references appear at S.S. U. 47/38 and S.S. U. 49/20, where dyed dadima pulp is prescribed for aphthous ulcers of the oral cavity. The fruit rind (*dadima tvak*) is specially indicated for *grahani* (malabsorptive bowel disease), *atisara* (diarrhoea), and *pravahika* (dysentery with tenesmus) — applications corresponding directly to the rind's high tannin content. The seed with pulp is classified as *hridya* (cardiac tonic). Fresh flower juice is used as nasal drops for epistaxis. Doshically, dadima is considered to pacify all three doshas in balanced form, though the sour-astringent varieties have specific action on Pitta and Kapha. [Sources: 8, 9]

### 2d. Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE)

The Ebers Papyrus — the most extensive surviving Egyptian medical text, dating to the reign of Amenhotep I (18th Dynasty) and compiled from earlier sources — documents pomegranate root (*inr n šndm* in reconstructed rendering) strained with water as a treatment for intestinal worms, specifically those described as "snakes of the belly," a term interpreted by Egyptologists as referring to tapeworms (*Taenia* spp.). The alkaloids of the root bark (pelletierines) were understood empirically to cause the worms to release their intestinal attachment, after which purgatives were administered. Pomegranate and wormwood are described as the principal vermifuge pair in the Egyptian tradition, a pairing that persisted in regional folk medicine into the modern era. Exact prescription numbers within the papyrus are not reproducible from secondary literature without access to the Joachim (1890) or Ebbell (1937) translations directly; secondary sources confirm the anthelmintic indication. [Sources: 5, 10]

### 2e. Assyrian Cuneiform Tablets (Neo-Assyrian period, c. 8th–7th century BCE)

Approximately 660 medical cuneiform tablets from the library of Ashurbanipal (668–627 BCE) at Nineveh are preserved in the British Museum; approximately 420 further tablets survive from other Neo-Assyrian and Babylonian sites. Among the ca. 5,000 medical prescriptions recorded, pomegranate (*ḫašḫūru* in Sumero-Akkadian botanical nomenclature) is documented as a medicinal plant known in Mesopotamia for gastrointestinal applications. The cuneiform evidence testifies to a broad Assyrian and Babylonian familiarity with pomegranate as a botanical medicine, consistent with its contemporaneous use in Egypt. Direct transliteration of specific tablet prescriptions requires specialist Assyriology sources beyond this entry's verified reach; the general botanical attestation is confirmed in the secondary literature. [Sources: 11]

## 3. Convergence Analysis

Five geographically and conceptually independent traditions converge on three therapeutic nodes: (1) **gastrointestinal astringency** — the rind is employed for diarrhoea, dysentery, and malabsorptive conditions across the Ayurvedic, Unani, and Tibetan traditions; (2) **anthelmintic action** — root bark preparations for intestinal parasites appear in the Egyptian, Assyrian, and Unani traditions, with the active mechanism now identified as pelletierine alkaloid-induced nematode paralysis; (3) **cardiac and haemostatic support** — seed pulp as heart tonic (Ayurveda), peel for uterine bleeding (Unani/Avicenna), and flower juice for epistaxis (Ayurveda). The convergence on the rind for astringency and the bark/root for anthelmintic action is particularly striking given the chemical specificity: the rind concentrates ellagitannins (astringent), while the root bark concentrates piperidine alkaloids (anthelmintic). These are distinct phytochemical profiles in distinct anatomical parts, yet multiple traditions identified and differentiated them empirically.

## 4. Phytochemistry & Composition

Pomegranate chemistry is markedly part-specific.

**Rind (pericarp):** The dominant compounds are hydrolysable ellagitannins. Punicalagin — the characteristic and highest-molecular-weight polyphenol (MW >1,000 Da) found in pomegranate — constitutes approximately 80–85% (w/w) of pericarp ellagitannins, with ellagic acid at approximately 1.3% (w/w). Additional ellagitannins include punicalin, granatin A and B, casuarinin, corilagin, pedunculagin, and tellimagrandin I. Pomegranate juice pressed from whole fruit contains ellagitannins exceeding 2 g/L — higher than any commonly consumed fruit juice. [Source: 12, 13]

**Root and stem bark:** Contains the piperidine alkaloids pelletierine, pseudopelletierine, N-methylpelletierine, and isopelletierine. Quantified yields from raw bark: pelletierine 1.8 g/kg, pseudopelletierine 0.52 g/kg, methylpelletierine 0.20 g/kg, isopelletierine 0.01 g/kg. These alkaloids are the pharmacologically active anthelmintic constituents. [Source: 14]

**Seed (aril):** Rich in punicic acid (an omega-5 conjugated linolenic acid), constituting 74–85% of total seed oil fatty acids. Also contains ellagic acid, flavonoids (47 phenolic metabolites identified by Ambigaipalan et al., 2017), and anthocyanins (cyanidin, delphinidin, pelargonidin glycosides) predominantly in the juice. [Source: 15]

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**Ellagitannin / urolithin pathway:** Punicalagin and related ellagitannins are not absorbed intact; they are hydrolysed to ellagic acid in the intestine, then metabolised by gut microflora into urolithins A and B, which are conjugated hepatically and circulate in plasma within 12–56 hours. Urolithins reach target tissues and are the likely bioactive form for systemic effects. Mechanisms include: inhibition of NF-κB nuclear translocation (anti-inflammatory); inhibition of HIF-1α and VEGF expression (anti-angiogenic); induction of apoptosis via cell-cycle arrest; and inhibition of aromatase (relevant to oestrogen-dependent pathways). [Source: 13]

**Antihypertensive:** Attributed to polyphenol-mediated improvement in endothelial function, reduction of oxidative stress, and possible ACE-inhibitory activity.

**Anthelmintic (alkaloid):** Pelletierine alkaloids cause neuromuscular paralysis of intestinal nematodes and cestodes, causing worm detachment; at higher doses, direct toxic effects are observed. [Source: 14]

## 6. Clinical Evidence

Evidence quality is heterogeneous and frequently overstated in popular sources.

**Blood pressure (best-supported indication):** A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis (Ghaemi et al., *Phytotherapy Research*, DOI: 10.1002/ptr.7952, PMID: 37461211) pooled 14 RCTs (n = 573) and found pomegranate juice consumption reduced systolic BP by a weighted mean of −5.02 mmHg (95% CI: −7.55 to −2.48, p < 0.001). Effect was more pronounced at ≤300 mL/day and ≤2 months duration; longer-term data are limited. An earlier 2017 review (Sahebkar et al.) found evidence "weak" and noted inconsistency across trials. The overall effect is modest and population-specific. [Sources: 16, 17]

**Prostate cancer (PSA kinetics):** A Phase II study (Pantuck et al., 2006, *Clinical Cancer Research*) of 40 patients with rising PSA post-surgery found PSA doubling time extended from 15 to 54 months (p < 0.001) following daily pomegranate juice consumption; 85% showed decreased PSA rise rate. This is a single, uncontrolled phase II study with a surrogate endpoint; it does not demonstrate survival benefit. [Source: 13]

**Osteoarthritis / rheumatoid arthritis:** A systematic review (PMC8767078) on osteoarthritis and a 2021 systematic review (Malek Mahdavi et al., *International Journal of Clinical Practice*, DOI: 10.1111/ijcp.13999) on RA both concluded evidence is preliminary — small sample sizes, heterogeneous interventions, short follow-up, low-to-very-low GRADE certainty. Anti-inflammatory biomarker reductions are reported but clinical significance is uncertain. [Sources: 18, 19]

**Diabetes glycaemic control:** One RCT (Khajebishak et al., 2019, n = 52 obese T2D patients, 8 weeks) showed increased GLUT-4 gene expression; human glycaemic data overall are limited and inconsistent. [Source: 15]

**Bottom line:** The antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanistic data are strong; the clinical translation to hard outcomes in humans remains incompletely established across most indications.

## 7. Phenomenological Record

No classical tradition documents altered states or psychoactive properties attributable to pomegranate consumption in therapeutic doses. The phenomenological record is one of constitutional correction rather than experience modification: descriptions centre on sensations of digestive warmth (Tibetan), gastric settling (Unani), and post-dysenteric recovery. The pelletierine alkaloids in root bark preparations, at medicinal and toxic doses, produce neurological effects (giddiness, muscular weakness, confusion) that are adverse rather than sought; see Section 8 for toxicology.

## 8. Practical Application

**Forms and dosing (evidence-based or classical ranges):**
- *Juice (whole-fruit pressed):* 240–300 mL/day; the range used in most RCTs showing BP effects. Contains highest ellagitannin concentration of any commercially available juice.
- *Standardised extract (pericarp):* Typically standardised to ellagic acid content; dosing varies by product — no universally established therapeutic dose for humans.
- *Rind decoction (classical):* Used at 3–10 g dried rind per preparation in Ayurvedic and Unani practice for diarrhoea/dysentery.
- *Root bark (anthelmintic, historical use only):* 6–12 g recommended in historical pharmacopoeias; classical pelletierine dose 1–2 g. **Not recommended in modern clinical use** given toxicity profile (see below).

**Cautions:**

*Root and stem bark — significant toxicity risk.* The alkaloid-bearing bark/root is not a benign preparation. Published case reports document a young man admitted with complete heart block and left heart failure following ingestion of a decoction made with 40 g bark. Adverse effects of pelletierine absorption include giddiness, confusion, and severe muscular weakness. The therapeutic window is narrow; the anthelmintic dose approaches toxic doses. Modern anthelmintics should be substituted. [Source: 14]

*Drug interactions:* Pomegranate juice inhibits CYP3A4 and CYP2C9 in vitro, with in vitro inhibition comparable to grapefruit juice. However, a 2023 clinical pharmacokinetic review (PMC10003857) found that neither pomegranate juice nor extract produced clinically significant effects on CYP2C9-metabolised drugs (including warfarin) at usual dietary intake. One case report (Komperda, *Pharmacotherapy*, 2009, PMID: 19637955) described a potential warfarin interaction (subtherapeutic INR on juice cessation). Prudence is warranted with anticoagulants and narrow-therapeutic-index CYP3A4 substrates at high juice intake. [Sources: 20, 21]

*Pregnancy:* Concentrated rind and root bark preparations should be avoided in pregnancy; juice in dietary amounts is not contraindicated by evidence.

## 9. Spiritual & Esoteric Layer

The pomegranate carries one of the most cross-culturally consistent symbolic registers of any plant in this Codex.

**Graeco-Roman / Eleusinian:** The pomegranate is central to the myth of Persephone, whose consumption of six seeds in Hades bound her to cyclical underworld return — establishing the fruit as a symbol simultaneously of fertility, mortality, cyclic time, and the threshold between worlds. The Eleusinian Mysteries, which dramatised this myth, deployed the pomegranate as a sacred object. In Hera's iconography, the fruit represents marriage, fecundity, and queenly sovereignty.

**Abrahamic traditions:** In Judaism, the pomegranate (*rimon*) is one of the seven species of the Land of Israel (Deuteronomy 8:8) and is associated in rabbinic tradition with the 613 commandments (said to correspond to its seed count, though this is a folk numerical claim, not a verifiable botanical one). In Islam, the Quran (Surah 6:99, 6:141, 55:68) cites the pomegranate as one of the fruits of paradise. In Christian iconography, the pomegranate — particularly when depicted split open — represents the Resurrection and the abundance of the Church.

**Iranian / Achaemenid:** The pomegranate blossom (*Golnar*) symbolises peace and love in Iranian culture; pomegranate motifs appear in Achaemenid limestone bas-reliefs.

**Humoral framework (Unani):** Sweet pomegranate as temperate-moist (cardiac-restorative); sour pomegranate and rind as cold-dry (astringent, styptic, anti-putrefactive). The tripartite sweet/sour/bitter variety classification in Avicenna parallels the Ayurvedic dosha-specific prescribing.

**Doshic framework (Ayurveda):** Dadima is considered *tridoshahara* (pacifying all three doshas) in balanced forms, a status reserved for few fruits.

## 10. Functional–Anatomical Correlate

The phytochemical geography of *Punica granatum* maps onto a coherent functional anatomy:

- **Gut mucosa (rind ellagitannins):** The ellagitannin-rich pericarp has its primary demonstrable action at the gut lining — astringency, tannin-protein cross-linking at mucosal surfaces, anti-inflammatory NF-κB modulation — consistent with classical use for diarrhoea and dysentery.
- **Vascular endothelium (juice polyphenols, urolithins):** Systemic ellagitannin metabolites (urolithins) and anthocyanins act on endothelial nitric oxide production and oxidative stress pathways — correlating with observed modest antihypertensive and cardioprotective signals.
- **Intestinal lumen / helminth neuromuscular junction (root bark alkaloids):** Pelletierines act peripherally within the gut lumen, paralyzing worm musculature without requiring systemic absorption for primary efficacy — explaining the historical efficacy and the narrow therapeutic window.
- **Androgen / oestrogen axes (urolithins):** Urolithin-mediated aromatase inhibition and androgen-pathway modulation provide a mechanistic basis for the prostate and breast cancer lines of investigation.

The convergence of gastrointestinal, haemostatic, and anti-parasitic applications across five traditions is best understood through this part-specific phytochemical logic: each tradition, working from observation, identified the rind and bark as more medicinal than the juice, and further distinguished rind (astringent, haemostatic) from root bark (anthelmintic, toxic at high dose).
