# Goat blood — rGyud-bzhi, "Goat blood" (blood, one of the thirteen animal-derived medicines)
## *Blood of the domestic goat; the translator prints only "Goat blood", with no Tibetan name and no binomial* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Goat blood is listed in the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, the chapter on the efficacy of medicinal substances, in the English of Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). Section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147) says: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). Under the heading "Blood" the entry reads (line 1173): "Goat blood: cures venereal diseases, smallpox, [brown phlegm and leprosy]." The same list continues: "The blood of wild yak and of Hodgson's antelope (gtsod): halts diarrhoea. Pig blood: collects [scattered types of] poisoning and brown phlegm. Donkey blood: cures rheumatism and lymph disorders in the joints", then rooster's-comb blood and womb blood. Goat blood comes second in the blood list, after deer blood (line 1172, "Deer blood: is anthelmintic, halts bleeding from the womb [and prevents hangovers]").

Clark states his bracket convention in the introduction (line 82): interpolations in square brackets "are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary", and those in parentheses derive from oral instructions of Dr. Yeshe Donden and Dr. Pema Dorje. So "brown phlegm and leprosy" is commentary material, while "cures venereal diseases, smallpox" is the root-text wording. The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation of the Root and Explanatory Tantra (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3539) agrees on the core: "Goat blood cures venereal diseases and smallpox. The blood of wild yaks and antelopes stop diarrhea." That text lists the same thirteen substances (line 3503). No Tibetan name for goat blood is printed in either translation. The terms "venereal diseases" and "smallpox" are the translators' renderings of Tibetan disease names; which Tibetan terms they stand for is not given in the files read. [USER: verify]

Clark also states that, of the animal products documented in the traditional texts, "Nowadays only very few animal products are used" (line 57), and that the materia medica of plant and mineral drugs is where Tibetan medicine "really comes into its own". That is a statement about practice at the time of his writing (1995), not about what the Tantra says.

### Active Components

Blood is mostly water. Its solids are blood cells (red cells rich in haemoglobin, white cells, platelets) and plasma proteins (albumin, globulins, fibrinogen), with lipids, electrolytes, glucose and hormones in solution. The iron of haemoglobin is held in haem. Raw blood can carry any blood-borne microorganism the animal has. Goats are a recognised host of *Brucella*, for example, which causes brucellosis in people. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is a single line of indication in a materia medica list: goat blood "cures venereal diseases, smallpox" (with commentary additions). It is not a modern medicine for any condition, and smallpox has been eradicated worldwide. The Codex offers no protocol. The reasons are practical and plain: raw or fresh animal blood is a route for infection; obtaining it means slaughtering or bleeding an animal; its sale or medical use is regulated or prohibited in many jurisdictions (check local law [USER: verify]); and the Tibetan text itself gives no preparation, dose or administration route for it. Blood-based foods exist in many cuisines under food-hygiene regulation, but that is a food use and is not what the Tantra describes.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Smallpox (variola) was declared eradicated by the World Health Organization in 1980. Venereal (sexually transmitted) infections are today treated with specific antimicrobial medicines, and nothing in goat blood is an accepted treatment for any of them. Fresh animal blood can transmit infection. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The line in Clark ch.20 (line 1173) gives an indication only: no taste, potency (*nus pa*), post-digestive taste or humoral (*nyes pa*) action is printed for goat blood in the sources retrieved. Elsewhere Clark (line 673) says of meats generally that "all the different types of meats ... are sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste", and gives dry-land animal flesh "cool, light and coarse powers"; that statement is about flesh (*sha*), not blood, and is not applied here. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for goat blood in either translation read. The substance is a short entry in a list of animal medicines, with an indication for disease and nothing about a practitioner. The framework around it is the tradition's own. Clark's translation of the opening invocation (lines 100-102) is addressed to the "Master of Medicine" and "Awakened One" who "dispels the three poisons"; the project's cultivation spec records that the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) are derived from the three poisons (attachment, aversion, delusion), and that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root. Those two points come from the project spec, not from a line about goat blood. No source read assigns goat blood a role in practice or in *bcud len* (essence extraction), and there is no formal Tibetan category of "substances for subtle-body practice". Clark's section on omens (line 431) also advises that, for imminent signs of death, one "should save from death (animals, etc. that are due to be killed, e.g., by paying for their release)"; that line is about merit and omens, not about medicine, and is recorded here only because it bears on the ethics of a substance that requires an animal.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a one-line indication from a list of animal medicines, the tradition itself notes animal products are little used now, and the substance carries infection and ethical costs with no benefit supported by any source read. Reading the line is the whole of the practice.

### Contraindications

- **Infection:** raw animal blood can carry bacteria, viruses and parasites; goats are a recognised source of brucellosis.
- **Not a treatment for the listed conditions:** smallpox is eradicated and sexually transmitted infections need diagnosis and specific treatment; do not use goat blood in place of care.
- **Legal and animal-welfare status:** obtaining blood requires killing or bleeding a goat; slaughter, sale of blood products and medicinal claims are regulated in many places. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** in the Tibetan setting, the text read elsewhere (line 431) values saving animals from slaughter; a practitioner working within a Buddhist ethic would weigh that. This is a general note, not a Tantric rule about goat blood.
- **Religious or dietary rules:** some faiths prohibit consuming blood; this entry takes no position beyond noting it.
