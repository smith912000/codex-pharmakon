# Lloydia serotina — a.wa, Awa (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Lloydia serotina Reichb., the second of two names Clark 1995 prints for a.wa (the first is Carex sp.), and the one the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts in this row.** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this Tibetan name. Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. *A.wa* is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark prints two names for it: "Carex sp." first and "Lloydia serotina Reichb." second. The row takes the second, because that is the one the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and the two are not shown to be one plant. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed names (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 (lines 1050 to 1053).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). Clark's preface (line 81) says that "headings have been added", and his chapter 20 note 12 says that this section "does not appear as a separate section in the original text". So the heading is Clark's. The entry reads: "a.wa: Carex sp.: Lloydia serotina Reichb.: [is of three types. It (is a grass with long, round, hollow, brittle stalks) which grow on north-facing rocks and has no flower, fruit or pod. The inferior type, a.wa.dar.dpyangs, is threadlike and has no flower or fruit, whereas both are found on the female type. All types] are beneficial in chest sores and eye diseases, [and they stimulate tooth growth and dispel spirits]." A printed page number, 105, falls inside the entry at line 1051.

The square brackets matter. Clark's preface (line 82) says "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary", and that those in parentheses ( ) are derived from the oral instructions of Dr. Yeshe Donden and Dr. Pema Dorje. So the part of the entry outside the brackets, that the drug is "beneficial in chest sores and eye diseases", is the root-text layer as Clark gives it. The description (three types; a grass with long, round, hollow, brittle stalks on north-facing rocks, with no flower, fruit or pod; the threadlike inferior type *a.wa.dar.dpyangs*; the female type that has flower and fruit) and the last two actions, that the drug "stimulate[s] tooth growth and dispel[s] spirits", are in the bracketed commentary layer. The parenthesis "(is a grass with long, round, hollow, brittle stalks)" is in Clark's oral-instruction layer. Clark's index of Tibetan names (line 1772) prints *a.wa.* and *a.wa.dar.dpyangs.* with no further information. His English name index prints *Carex* (line 1567) but not *Lloydia*.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The materia medica summary says, at line 3442: "Awa is beneficial against wounds of the abdomen and eye problems." The printed page number is 208, and the index (line 7260) gives "Awa 208, 302". Where Clark says "chest sores", the Men-Tsee-Khang text says "wounds of the abdomen". The two English renderings do not agree, and the lines read do not say which Tibetan word each translates. ### Active Components

The constituents of the plant the Tibetan texts mean were not read, and nothing is stated here about the composition of *Lloydia serotina* or of any *Carex*. As a general fact, *Lloydia* is a genus of small bulbous plants of the lily family, while *Carex* is a genus of sedges (grass-like plants). Clark's bracketed description, of a flowerless, fruitless grass with hollow stalks, reads as a sedge or grass. The sources retrieved do not explain how it comes to be paired with *Lloydia*. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug for chest sores (Clark) or wounds of the abdomen (Men-Tsee-Khang) and for eye diseases, and, in the commentary layer, for stimulating tooth growth and dispelling spirits. These are the texts' claims. The Codex gives no protocol. Eye disease and sores need medical assessment, and the identity of the drug is itself uncertain.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The two texts claim the drug for sores and the eyes, and Clark's bracketed layer adds that it stimulates tooth growth and "dispel[s] spirits". That last phrase names an action against a class of disease-causing agents. It is a therapeutic claim and is not a cultivation or essence-extraction status. Neither line read assigns *a.wa* any such status: the words *bcud len* and "essence-extraction" do not occur in the entry or in the Men-Tsee-Khang line. Clark's preface (lines 69 to 70) names other substances as examples of essence-extraction ingredients (sundew, angelica, *Polygonatum*, salep, certain flowers, calcite and mineral exudate) and does not name *a.wa*, so that passage is the setting only. The cultivation spec adds that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists.

**Practitioner's note.** The sources give a cultivator no practice with this drug. It is a remedy for sores and eye disease with no stated preparation or amount, and its identity is uncertain between a sedge and a lily relative, so the honest answer is that there is nothing here to take up.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. Eye disease and sores need medical care and must not be left to a home preparation. The botanical identity is uncertain (two genera in Clark), so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
