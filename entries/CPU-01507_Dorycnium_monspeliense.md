# Dorycnium monspeliense — Doruknion (Dioscorides 4-75)

## *Dorycnium monspeliense (the translators' suggestion: it stands on the ALSO line of the SUGGESTED block, after "Dorycnium [Bedevian] — Venemous Trefoil"; "Leopard's Bane Groundsel" is given for another plant, Senecio doronicum)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed under the chapter heading, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED block reads "Dorycnium [Bedevian] — Venemous Trefoil ALSO: Dorycnium monspeliense, Dorycnium herbaceum Senecio doronicum — Leopard's Bane Groundsel", so the translators offer a genus, two species of it, and a plant of a different genus. The row took *D. monspeliense* from the ALSO line. It has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-75. DORUKNION* at line 8692, SUGGESTED block at line 8693, the label "POISONOUS" at line 8694 and the text at line 8695. The chapter stands in Book Four, "Other Herbs & Roots". The name appears in the translators' name index (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 655).

The whole chapter reads: "Dorycnium is a shrub similar to a newly-planted olive, with branches less than a foot long. It grows among rocks not far from the sea. It has leaves similar to the olive in colour but smaller, stronger and extremely coarse. The flower is white, on the top it has little pods as thick as those of cicer [2-126] in which are five or six little round seeds, about the amount of little ervum [2-129, 2-131], smooth, firm, and of various colours. The root is the thickness of a finger and the length of a foot, and this seems also to have a sleepy quality, and taken too much it kills. Some also say that the seed of it is taken for love medicines. Crateuas calls it halicacabum [a bad poison], or caleam."

So the chapter is a description followed by two statements about danger and use. The description gives a small shrub, olive-like in leaf colour, growing on rocks near the sea, with a white flower, small pods of five or six smooth round seeds in several colours, and a finger-thick root a foot long. The root is said to seem to have "a sleepy quality" and, "taken too much", to kill. The word "also" most naturally points back to the chapter before it, 4-74 (*STRUCHNON MANIKON*, line 8675), whose closing lines (about lines 8690 to 8691) describe a drink of the root that causes "not unpleasant fantasies", leaves one "beside himself for three days" at two teaspoonfuls and kills at four, with "honey and water, taken as a drink in copious amounts and vomited up again" as the remedy. That link is the reader's inference and 4-75 itself gives no antidote. [USER: verify] Dioscorides then reports, on the authority of unnamed others ("Some also say"), that the seed "is taken for love medicines". He records it and does not endorse it. Crateuas is named as calling the plant *halicacabum*, which the translation glosses "a bad poison", and *caleam*.

No medicinal use against disease is printed. The chapter is a recognition and warning chapter.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. The translators' genus *Dorycnium* belongs to the pea family (Fabaceae). Nothing is stated here about the composition of the plant or about what in it could produce the "sleepy quality" the text reports. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides labels the plant poisonous in the translation, says the root may be sleep-inducing and fatal in excess, and reports a love-medicine use of the seed on hearsay. The Codex gives no protocol, and a plant whose own text says "taken too much it kills" is not offered as a remedy or a cultivation aid.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality and no taste. The only qualities printed are of the leaf ("extremely coarse") and a "sleepy quality" attributed to the root.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition: where the shrub grows (rocks near the sea), the colour and coarseness of the leaf against the olive, the shape and number of the seeds, the length and thickness of the root, and a plain warning about the root. It records a love-medicine use of the seed and does not endorse it. It assigns the plant no status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. What the text teaches is recognition and restraint: a shrub to be known and not eaten. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Dioscorides' own text says the root "taken too much ... kills", and the translation labels the plant "POISONOUS". The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so nothing should be gathered or taken on the strength of this entry. Love-medicine use is reported only as hearsay.
