# Common Alder — Black Alder, European Alder, Aune Glutineux

## *Alnus glutinosa* (L.) Gaertn. | Family: Betulaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT (Assyrian), CM (Avicenna), DMM (Dioscorides)
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and Dry (second degree) — a drying, binding, astringent agent in the Galenic-Unani scheme
**Training Phase:** Tempering

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian (AT):** The riverine trees of Mesopotamia and the wider Near East supplied bark astringents to the cuneiform pharmacy, whose recipes repeatedly call for bitter, binding bark decoctions to "bind the bowels," staunch flux, and wash inflamed sores. Alder, a tree of wet margins across the region's northern reaches, belongs to this class of tannin-rich barks used as drying washes and anti-diarrhoeal drinks.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** *De Materia Medica* devotes sustained attention to astringent barks and their power to "contract and dry" — the operative virtue Dioscorides ascribes to oak, and to related bark medicines used against loose bowels, spitting of blood, and slack, weeping wounds. The alders were carried through this bark-astringent tradition by later herbalists, who used decoctions of the bark and leaves as gargles for sore throat and as washes for ulcers and swellings.

**Avicenna (CM):** In the *Qanun*, astringent (*qabid*) barks are classed as cold and dry, prescribed to arrest fluxes, tighten relaxed tissue, and cool inflamed surfaces. Alder bark fits this Unani category precisely — a cooling, contracting remedy for hot, weeping, or over-loose conditions, applied internally against diarrhoea and externally against inflamed mucous membranes.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Alder is the tree that stands in water yet resists rot — its felled timber famously hardens rather than decays when submerged, and its cut wood bleeds a startling orange-red. Folk imagination read this as a tree that "holds the boundary" between water and land, dissolution and endurance. In Kenetica this maps onto the Tempering phase: the plant of holding form under flux. The symbolism is underwritten by real chemistry — the same tannins and diarylheptanoids that make the bark a tissue-binding astringent also make it a genuine antioxidant and anti-inflammatory, i.e. a substance that resists oxidative "rot" at the biochemical level.

### Active Components

Alder bark and leaf are dominated by two overlapping chemistries: hydrolysable/condensed tannins and the characteristic *Alnus* diarylheptanoids.

- **Diarylheptanoids** — chiefly **oregonin**, the predominant compound of the stem bark (reported as the great majority of a characterised bark extract), together with hirsutanonol and related curcuminoid-like C7 diaryl scaffolds. These are the signature antioxidant/anti-inflammatory constituents of the genus.
- **Tannins** — condensed and gallo-tannins giving high total-phenol content (gallic acid equivalents), responsible for the astringent, tissue-binding action.
- **Phenolic acids and flavonoids** — gallic acid, and in the leaf, **shikimic acid** identified as a major bioactive.
- **Triterpenes and lignans** in minor quantity.
- **Emodin-type anthraquinones** in trace, historically linked to the mild cathartic folk use.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Common alder is a **Tempering-phase** astringent: its role is to tighten, dry, and settle tissue that has become slack, weeping, or over-loose. Traditional and evidence-supported applications converge on: astringent gargles and mouthwashes for inflamed throat and gums; external washes and compresses for inflamed skin, weeping sores, and minor wounds; and internal bark decoction as a folk anti-diarrhoeal and antipyretic. In the Kenetica frame it is used to consolidate after a Purification-phase cleanse — to firm and cool tissue rather than to move or stimulate. It is a topical and mucosal remedy first; internal use is traditional and should be conservative.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:

- **Smeriglio et al. (2022), *Plants (Basel)*** — LC-MS profiling of *A. glutinosa* stem-bark extract found a high total-phenol content with **diarylheptanoids as the predominant class and oregonin the single major compound**, and demonstrated strong concentration-dependent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity plus anti-angiogenic effects in chick chorioallantoic membrane and zebrafish models — a biochemical basis for the bark's traditional use against inflammatory conditions. DOI: [10.3390/plants11192499](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants11192499).
- **Altınyay et al. (2016), *Journal of Ethnopharmacology*** — methanol leaf extracts of *A. glutinosa* subsp. *glutinosa* showed wound-healing (increased wound tension and contraction), anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity in rodent models, with **shikimic acid** isolated by bioassay-guided fractionation as the major active compound. DOI: [10.1016/j.jep.2016.07.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2016.07.007).
- **Bikiaris et al. (2025), *Antioxidants (Basel)*** — black alder extracts incorporated into cosmetic emulsions showed measurable antioxidant (DPPH) and photoprotective activity, the first report on alder-enriched emulsions for skin protection. DOI: [10.3390/antiox14111275](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox14111275).

**Evidence grade:** The antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions are supported by **preclinical in-vitro and animal data of moderate quality**; there is essentially no human clinical trial evidence. The astringent, antipyretic, and anti-diarrhoeal uses remain **traditional**. Claims should be framed accordingly.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Cold and Dry, second degree. Astringent (*qabid*), styptic, refrigerant; used to arrest flux and tighten relaxed tissue.
- **TCM (analogical):** cool, bitter/astringent; drains damp-heat and astringes — no classical *materia medica* entry for *Alnus glutinosa*, applied by property analogy to other astringent barks.
- **Ayurvedic (analogical):** *rasa* astringent (kashaya) and bitter (tikta); *virya* cooling (shita); *vipaka* pungent; kapha- and pitta-moderating, potentially vata-aggravating in excess. Analogy only; not a classical dravya.

### Contraindications

- **Astringent load / constipation:** high-tannin decoctions can cause constipation, nausea, and gastric irritation; avoid prolonged or high-dose internal use.
- **Mineral and drug absorption:** tannins chelate iron and other minerals and can bind co-administered alkaloidal drugs — separate from meals, supplements, and medication.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** internal medicinal use not established as safe — [USER: verify]; avoid beyond culinary/trivial exposure.
- **Allergy:** *Alnus* pollen is a well-known aeroallergen (birch-family cross-reactivity); those with birch/alder pollen allergy should use topical preparations cautiously.
- **Not a substitute for rehydration:** in significant diarrhoea, fluid/electrolyte management and medical assessment take priority over any astringent folk remedy.
