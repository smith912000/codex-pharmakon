# Pyrethrum tatsienense — a.byag, Ajak, a byag, gzer.hjoms (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Pyrethrum tatsienense, the fourth of four binomials on line 5073 of the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary, which column order sets against "Ajak" (a byag); Clark 1995 prints a.byag with Chrysanthemum tatsiense first and Pyrethrum barbigera fourth, and no Pyrethrum tatsienense* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Shennong (Yang 1998), Sushruta, Ebers and Assyrian texts on disk for *Pyrethrum* found no hit. The Dioscorides text prints "Pyrethrum" only in the translators' SUGGESTED lines for Greek drugs (for example lines 6202 and 7298, the second for a drug the translators call "Pellitory of Spain"), which are not read as this plant. Yang 1998 prints "Ju Hua (Flos Chrysanthemi Morifolii)" at line 1612, a drug of a different species, and it is not read as this one either. The row was added from a printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 235) the row is keyed by the **scientific name** "Pyrethrum tatsienense", with the locus "Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (line 5073)" and the note "Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". Line 5073 prints **four** binomials on one line ("Fritillaria delavayi Mangifera indica Aquilaria agallocha Pyrethrum tatsienense"). The key is the **fourth**, a later name on the line and not the first, and each of the other three has its own row. Clark prints no *Pyrethrum tatsienense*. The Tibetan name *a.byag* is therefore reached from this row by column position and by the meaning of the sentence, as set out below, and not by a printed row.

**Identity doubt.** Clark's entry for *a.byag* (line 1023) prints four identifications in this order: *Chrysanthemum tatsiense*, *Inula britannica*, *Tanacetum* sp. and *Pyrethrum barbigera*. The glossary's *Pyrethrum tatsienense* differs from Clark's first name in its genus and in the spelling of the epithet (*tatsiense* against *tatsienense*). The lines read do not show whether the two spellings name one species, and the Codex does not decide. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" and (line 68) that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect". The Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note (line 686) says that its botanical terms rest on the information of its own Materia Medica Department, and it names no earlier flora. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "A" block).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. Under the headings Phonetic, Transliteration and Scientific Name (lines 5051 to 5055) the phonetic names are eight (lines 5059 to 5063): "Abikha", "Adre", "Agaru", "Ajak", "Arura", "Asho genda", "Atong" and "Awa". The transliterations (lines 5066 to 5071) are "a bi Sha", "a'bras", "a ga ru", "abyag", "a ru ra", "a sho gan+d+ha", "akrong" and "aba". The scientific names are eight (lines 5073 to 5077): "Fritillaria delavayi", "Mangifera indica", "Aquilaria agallocha", "Pyrethrum tatsienense" (all four on line 5073), "Terminalia chebula" (5074), "Mirabilis himalaica" (5075), "Tanacetum mutellinum" (5076) and "Lloydia serotina" (5077). Counted in order, "Ajak" is the fourth phonetic name, "abyag" the fourth transliteration and *Pyrethrum tatsienense* the fourth scientific name. The English column (lines 5149 to 5151) has seven names for the eight rows: "Snake's head lilies", "Mango", "Eagle or aloe wood", "Ragwort", "Chebulic myrobalan", "Ashwagandha" and "Lioydia" (as printed). The fourth English name is "Ragwort". The count is checked by the neighbours: the first, third and fifth rows agree with Clark's *a.bi.sha* (*Fritillaria delavayi*, line 1016), *a.ga.ru* ("Eaglewood or Aloewood: Aquilaria agallocha", line 877) and *a.ru.ra* ("Chebulic Myrobalan: Terminalia chebula", line 936). The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so the pairing is by position only. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3433).* Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225; the chapter's colophon is at line 3583), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428): "Yugu shing joins cuts and cures hot disorder associated with poisoning. Tsed, Guthup and Dhumtak are antidotes to poison. Ajak heals fractured skulls and dries up chhuser. Tsher ngon heals fractured bone and protects spongy bone." "Chhuser" is a Tibetan term that the editors keep untranslated (note at line 686). The index lists "Ajak 207" (line 7258). The sentence gives no part used, no preparation and no taste.

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (lines 1023 to 1025).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). After a clause that belongs to the preceding entries ("... Pleurospermum, Scutellaria and Strychnos cure poisoning."), the entry reads: "a.byag: Chrysanthemum tatsiense: Inula britannica: Tanacetum sp.: Pyrethrum barbigera: [is also known as gzer.hjoms and has five names. It has slightly bitter taste and cures infectious tissue degeneration. The stalk is flexible with a yellow flower like a golden umbrella and a dark green coarse leaf]. It heals skull fractures, dries lymph accumulations [and is beneficial in painful infectious fever]." A page number ("103") stands on line 1024, inside the entry. Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The second name, the taste, the first action ("infectious tissue degeneration"), the plant description and the last clause are therefore the commentary layer. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the four Latin names, which are Clark's identifications, and "It heals skull fractures, dries lymph accumulations". The two editions agree in kind on the skull fracture. The Codex reads the glossary's "Ajak" and Clark's *a.byag* as one drug from the spelling and from that agreement, and neither text says so. Clark's name index lists *a.byag* (line 1775) and the Latin name *Chrysanthemum tatsiense* (line 1570); it lists *Pyrethrum barbigera* (line 1632) and no *Pyrethrum tatsienense*. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Pyrethrum tatsienense*, *Pyrethrum barbigera*, *Chrysanthemum tatsiense*, *a.byag* and *Inula britannica* found no entry on this plant. I_1283 (*Fritillaria delavayi*), I_1320 (*Mirabilis himalaica*) and I_1120 (*Lloydia serotina*) print other binomials of the same glossary "A" block, and I_1283 and I_1320 name *Pyrethrum tatsienense* in describing it. I_1330 (*Peucedanum praeruptorum*) quotes the sentence at line 3433 for the neighbouring drug *Yugu shing*. I_743 (*Inula dysenterica*) is an entry on a different species. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Chrysanthemum*, *Pyrethrum*, *Inula* and *Tanacetum*, the genera Clark and the glossary name, all belong to the daisy family (Asteraceae). The lines read give no composition for the Tibetan drug and the four names may not belong to one species, so nothing is stated here about what it contains.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "heals fractured skulls and dries up chhuser" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3433) and "heals skull fractures, dries lymph accumulations" (Clark, line 1025), with a bitter taste and a use in infectious tissue degeneration in Clark's commentary layer. These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only through column position and spelling, and they are not Codex protocols. A skull fracture is an emergency and the Codex gives no advice for it or for any wound.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary describes the stalk, flower and leaf and does not say which part is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 1023) prints "slightly bitter taste". No power and no post-digestive taste is printed. The Men-Tsee-Khang lines read (3433, 5059 to 5077) print no taste or power, and the name is not in the taste groups of chapter 19 (lines 3204 to 3222) as far as the search for "Ajak" showed.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it heals skull fractures and dries lymph accumulations (Clark) or chhuser (Men-Tsee-Khang). The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names (*a.byag*, *Ajak*, *Pyrethrum*, *tatsie*) found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, colophon at line 3950), and the herb entries of Clark's chapter 20 that mention essence extraction (lines 971, 1043, 1078, 1103, 1121 and 1144) do not include this one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and its link to this binomial rests on column position in one edition.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a skull-fracture and lymph drug in two short sentences, with no preparation and no dose, and the Latin name is one of several the editions offer. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. A head injury needs emergency medical care, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Several daisy-family plants are known to cause contact allergy in some people, but that is a general fact and is not shown for this species. The identity is open, no preparation or dose is given, and no safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
