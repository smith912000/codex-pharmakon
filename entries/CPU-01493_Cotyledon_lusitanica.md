# Cotyledon lusitanica — Kotuledon, Umbilicus veneris (Dioscorides 4-92)

## *Cotyledon lusitanica (the translators' first suggestion; "Kidneywort, Navelwort, Venus's Navelwort"), with Umbilicus erectus and Cotyledon umbilicus also on the line* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The line reads "Cotyledon lusitanica, Umbilicus erectus — Kidneywort, Navelwort, Venus's Navelwort Cotyledon umbilicus — Cotyledon, Navelwort, Pennywort", so it holds two groups of names, and the row takes the first name on the first group. A plate in the chapter is captioned "Cotyledon umbilicus after FAGUET — 1874" (line 8863), which shows the species of the second group. The translators' index lists *Cotyledon lusitanica* at page 643.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-92. KOTULEDON* at line 8858, SUGGESTED line at 8860, text at line 8861.

The whole chapter reads: "Umbilicus veneris has a leaf like an acetabulum [vinegar cruet], round, hidden, and hollow, with a short little stalk in the middle in which is the seed. The root is round like the olive. The juice of this and the leaves (rubbed on with wine or squirted in), loosens the obstructions of the skin in the genitals. Applied, it helps inflammation, erysipela [streptococcal skin infection], chilblains, tumours [possibly goitre], and a burning stomach. The leaves (eaten with the root) break stones [urinary, kidney], and induce urine. It is given with mead [honey wine] for dropsy, and they also use it for love medicines. It is also called scytalium, cymbalium, hortus veneris, terrae umbilicus, stichis, or stergthron, and the Romans call it umbilicus veneris." The bracketed glosses are the translators'.

So Dioscorides describes the plant by one image: a round, hollow leaf like a vinegar cruet with a short stalk in the middle that bears the seed, over a round root like an olive. The juice and leaves are used on the skin of the genitals; applied, the plant is used for inflammation, erysipelas, chilblains, tumours and a burning stomach; the leaves eaten with the root are said to break stones and induce urine; given with mead it is used for dropsy. The words "they also use it for love medicines" report what others do; the chapter does not say that Dioscorides endorses it. Six other names are printed (*scytalium*, *cymbalium*, *hortus veneris*, *terrae umbilicus*, *stichis*, *stergthron*) together with the Roman name *umbilicus veneris*. The chapter gives no habitat and no season.

**Overlap.** The next chapter, 4-93 *KOTULEDON ETERON* (line 8868), describes "another kind of cotyledon" and has its own row, CPU-01494 *Cotyledon orbiculata*. The spine's existing entry I_1002 (*Androsace lactea*, from Dioscorides 3-150) shares only the English name "Navelwort" (in its identity line). Neither is merged here.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records the juice and leaves rubbed on or "squirted in" at the genitals, applications for inflammation, erysipelas, chilblains, tumours and a burning stomach, the leaves and root eaten against stones and for urine, and a mead preparation for dropsy. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol: the identification is the translators', and stones, dropsy, tumours and infections need medical care.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-92 gives these preparations: the juice and leaves "rubbed on with wine or squirted in"; the plant "applied"; the leaves "eaten with the root"; and the plant "given with mead [honey wine]". No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant by the shape of the leaf and the root, and what is claimed for it. The phrase "they also use it for love medicines" is a record of what others do, set down without endorsement; the cultivation spec's rule for this author is that recording is not endorsing. It assigns the plant no status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The uses are skin, urinary and gynaecological remedies with no stated amounts, and the love-medicine remark is reported practice. It is history and not a practice.

### Contraindications

Fluid in the abdomen, kidney or urinary stones, suspected goitre and genital skin conditions need medical care, and a home preparation must not replace it. A liquid "squirted in" to the genitals is not a safe thing to try, and no safety information for pregnancy was read. The identification is the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
