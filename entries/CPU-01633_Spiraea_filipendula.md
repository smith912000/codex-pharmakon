# Spiraea filipendula — Oinanthe (Dioscorides 3-135)

## *Spiraea filipendula [Linnaeus], "Dropwort", on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-135 ("Oenanthe, Filipendula [Fuchs], Filipendula vulgaris [Bauhin], Spiraea filipendula [Linnaeus], Filipendula hexapetala [in Sprague] — Dropwort [Mabberley]"), which gives as "other usage" Oenanthe phellandrium, Phellandrium aquaticum — Water Hemlock, Horsebane, Fine-leaved Water Dropwort* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 7725) prints five names with their namers, and the row takes the one attributed to Linnaeus, *Spiraea filipendula*. The same line then prints an "other usage": *Oenanthe phellandrium* and *Phellandrium aquaticum*, "Water Hemlock, Horsebane, Fine-leaved Water Dropwort". That is a plant of a different genus, a water-side umbellifer, and the translators leave both open. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-135. OINANTHE*, heading at line 7724, SUGGESTED line at 7725, the single word "POISONOUS" on its own line at 7726 (the edition's flag, not a sentence of the text), and the text at line 7727. It stands in Book Three, after *3-134. KIRKAIA* (line 7717) and before *3-136. KONUZA* (line 7728), both different plants. The Codex's own name extraction lists it (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 541); the translators' general index prints "Spiraea filipendula 517" (line 17723).

The whole chapter reads: "Oenanthe has leaves like staphulinus, with white flowers and a thick stalk twenty centimetres long. The seed is like atriplex [1-120, 2-145], and it has a great root with many round heads. It grows on rocks. The seed, stalks and leaves are given to drink (with honeyed wine) to discharge the afterbirth. The root is good (with wine) for slow painful urination and jaundice. It is also called leucanthon, or kerascomion."

The chapter gives a description (leaves like *staphulinus*, which the text does not number; white flowers; a thick stalk twenty centimetres long; seed like *atriplex*, with the translators' cross-references 1-120 and 2-145; "a great root with many round heads"), a place ("on rocks"), two uses and two other names. The seed, stalks and leaves, "given to drink (with honeyed wine)", are to "discharge the afterbirth"; the root, "with wine", is "good for slow painful urination and jaundice". The other names are *leucanthon* and *kerascomion*. The chapter gives no quality. The word "POISONOUS" on the line above the text is a flag of the edition; the text itself prints no warning, and the drinks it describes are of the seed, stalks, leaves and root.

**A name shared with another chapter.** A different chapter, *5-5. OINANTHE* (heading at line 9794), is the fruit of the wild vine and carries the translators' remark "Oenanthe is Water Dropwort or Water Hemlock — poisonous". It is a different chapter on a different plant by Dioscorides' own division, and is already treated in I_975. It is mentioned here only because the translators' remark there matches the "other usage" line of 3-135. That is a labelled cross-reference; the Codex draws no identification from it.

**The plate.** A plate captioned "Oenanthe, Filipendula from FUCHS — 1545" is printed at line 7647, between the page numbers 508 and 509 (lines 7644 to 7648), some twenty lines after the heading of 3-120 (line 7624), not beside 3-135, which falls on page 517 by the translators' index. The text does not say why, and the Codex does not resolve it. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A chapter-level search of the entries folder for 3-135 found one file (III_283), where the string is the end of a line range ("lines 133-135") and not this chapter. No entry on 3-135 as its own plant was found.

### Active Components

The chapter prints none. It is a general fact that *Filipendula* (dropwort, meadowsweet) belongs to the rose family (Rosaceae), while *Oenanthe* (water dropwort) belongs to the carrot family (Apiaceae), and that some species of *Oenanthe* are highly poisonous. The two suggested identities are therefore in different families, one a rose-family herb and the other a potentially deadly umbellifer. The composition of the plant Dioscorides describes was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides reports a drink of seed, stalks and leaves in honeyed wine to discharge the afterbirth, and a drink of the root in wine for slow painful urination and jaundice. These are the text's reports. The edition flags the chapter "POISONOUS", the identification is open between a rose-family herb and a water-side umbellifer, and the Codex gives no protocol. A drink that is meant to expel the afterbirth is also an obstetric use that is not for self-treatment.

### Preparation & Dosing

3-135 gives these preparations: the seed, stalks and leaves "given to drink (with honeyed wine)"; the root "with wine". It gives no quantity, frequency or duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no taste and no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality. The word "POISONOUS" printed above the text is a flag of the edition and not a quality.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use; *leucanthon* and *kerascomion* are names only. What it offers is of the kind the cultivation spec calls a discipline of attention: the description by comparison (two named plants), the figure for the stalk, the great root with its many round heads, the place (rocks), and the separation of the parts used, seed, stalks and leaves in one drink and the root in another. The plant is given no status beyond that of a remedy for the afterbirth, for urination and for jaundice. The edition's "POISONOUS" flag is itself a rule about handling the material. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. What the chapter and its flag teach is that two things that look alike on the rock and by the water may not be the same plant, and that an obstetric drink is not a personal practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The edition flags this chapter "POISONOUS", and the translators' alternative identity, a water dropwort, is a genus with deadly members. Drinks that expel the afterbirth are obstetric treatments, and nothing here is for use in pregnancy or at childbirth. The identity of the plant is open between two very different plants, so no wild plant should be gathered or taken on the strength of this entry. Jaundice and difficult urination need medical assessment. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
