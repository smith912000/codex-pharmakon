# Vulture droppings — "Vulture's droppings" (Clark 1995), "Vulture droppings" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011); no Tibetan name is printed in the lines read
## *The droppings of the vulture, a bird the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary pairs with Aegypius monachus Brisson; Clark prints no binomial for it* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The Explanatory Tantra (*bshad rgyud*), chapter 20, "Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", has a section headed "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin". In Clark's 1995 English (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` lines 1147-1148) it opens: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body. Their indications and powers are explained as follows". Under the sub-heading "Droppings" (line 1199) the first sentence reads: "Vulture's droppings: increase digestive heat, disperse tumors and suppurate swellings." The same line goes on to other droppings (pig, human, horse, rabbit, dog, wolf, pigeon and others), which are separate materials and are not folded in here. The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`) lists the same thirteen (line 3503, with "feces" for droppings) and says at line 3568: "Vulture droppings generate heat, dissolve tumors and suppurates inflammed swellings." The two translations agree on three actions, with one difference of wording: Clark says "digestive heat" where Men-Tsee-Khang says "heat". Which the Tibetan means I cannot say from either English text. [USER: verify]

The name list `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 375 has the row "Vulture droppings" with the source "rGyud-bzhi, Explanatory Tantra ch.20 (Efficacy of Medicinal Substances)", and the identification-uncertain flag set to "yes". The bird itself is named in two other places in the texts read. In Clark's classification of meats the vulture and red vulture are listed among "birds of prey" (line 672), and the Men-Tsee-Khang text lists "the vulture, crow, eagle, kite, raven, owl and hawk" as "the group of birds that survive by their hunting skills" (line 2914). The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary (lines 6904-6906) sets "Vulture" beside "Aegypius monachus Brisson"; that is the only binomial on disk, and the glossary is a two-column list in which OCR has separated the English and Latin names, so the pairing is by order and not certain. *Other texts on disk, not folded in.* The Sushruta Samhita (Bhishagratna) names vulture dung in several places. The Sutrasthana ch.36 plaster for bursting a swelling includes "the dung of pigeons, vultures and storks (Kanka) pasted together" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch36.txt` line 77), and four Uttaratantra chapters on diseases of children direct the dungs of vultures (with those of owls, and in one place a monkey) to be used for fumigating the child (`SS_Uttara_ch29.txt` line 21; `SS_Uttara_ch31.txt` line 26; `SS_Uttara_ch34.txt` line 21; `SS_Uttara_ch36.txt` line 29). The Ebers papyrus in Bryan's English lists "Excrement" among the animal parts used, but the vulture recipes there are for blood, feather and egg (see the separate Vulture entry, III_205). These traditions are not on this entry's tag line, and the Cultivation Register below covers only the Tibetan one. The Sushruta passages are recorded as positive loci for a possible tag addition (reported to the editor, not applied).

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, bird droppings are a mixture of digestive waste and the white urate (uric acid) fraction, birds passing their nitrogen waste as uric acid. A vulture feeds mainly on carcasses, so its droppings can carry the bacteria and other organisms of what it has eaten. Nothing in this entry links any constituent to the actions the Tantra gives.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan line says what the droppings were held to do: raise heat, break up tumors, and bring a swelling to suppuration. The Codex does not recommend any of this. Droppings from a carrion-eating bird are a hygiene hazard; a swelling that is slow to resolve needs medical assessment, not a poultice of excrement; and vultures are among the most threatened birds in the world and are protected in law in most countries, so collecting their droppings from the wild is neither ethical nor legal in many places. The entry exists to record what the Explanatory Tantra says.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither translation gives a preparation, vehicle or quantity for vulture droppings. The twentieth chapter closes (Clark line 1202) with the "medicinal horses", the vehicles for compounded ingredients: raw cane sugar for cold and wind disorders, crystal sugar for disorders of the blood, bile and fevers, and honey for lymph and phlegm disorders. The text does not say that droppings were taken or applied with any of them. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Wild-bird droppings can carry disease-causing bacteria and fungi, and handling or inhaling dried droppings is a recognised hygiene risk; none of this is a reason to think the Tantra's actions are real. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** No taste, post-digestive taste or potency is printed for the droppings. The nearest statement is the action itself: the droppings "increase digestive heat" (Clark line 1199) or "generate heat" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3568). The qualities the Men-Tsee-Khang text gives for the meat of birds of prey ("rough, light and sharp", line 2931) and the sweet taste it gives to all meats (line 2923) belong to the flesh, and are not carried over to the droppings here.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Vulture droppings are named in the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica of animal products with three therapeutic actions and nothing more (Clark line 1199). The wider frame is real but does not reach this substance. In Clark's account the three humors are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration (line 51), and attachment, hatred and closed-mindedness are "the three causes which in turn give rise to wind, bile and phlegm (disorders)" (line 147), so the tradition treats disease as at root a spiritual condition and medicine as one support among several. The chapter on routine behaviour tells the practitioner to refrain from the ten negative actions, the first of which is "taking life" (line 616). The rejuvenation passage in chapter 23 (lines 1266-1275) names garlic butter, the three myrobalans, calcined metals, leadwort and cow's urine, and does not name vulture droppings. No *bcud len* (essence-extraction) use is attested for this substance in the lines read, and the tradition has no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice, so none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The droppings are a therapeutic entry in a list of thirteen kinds of animal product, and the tradition's own frame, which asks that life not be taken and that medicine serve practice and not replace it, is a reason to leave the material in the book. The Codex does not recommend using it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** droppings of a carrion-eating wild bird should not be put on skin, a swelling or a wound, or taken by mouth.
- **Swellings:** a swelling that persists, spreads or hurts needs a clinician; the Tantra's "suppurate swellings" is a historical statement and not a treatment.
- **Conservation and law:** vultures are threatened and protected in most countries; the Codex does not recommend taking any material from wild vultures or their nests.
- **Ethics:** the Tibetan source itself places "taking life" first among the ten negative actions (Clark line 616); this entry is a record of a text and not an endorsement of the practice.
