# Diamond — "diamonds", named in the gem group of the Sushruta Samhita (Sanskrit name not printed in the passage)
## *A gem, rendered "diamonds" by Bhishagratna, listed with pearls, corals, sapphires, Vaidurya (lapis lazuli) and crystals; the text treats the gems as one group and gives diamond no entry of its own* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** Diamond is named once as a medicine in the Sushruta Samhita, in the Sutrasthana, chapter 46, a chapter that opens as the discourse "on food and drink (Annapana-Vidhi-madhyaya)" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` lines 1-6). The chapter's closing stretch describes the alkalis and then "The Metal group" (line 2121), which runs from gold through silver, copper, bell metal, iron, and zinc and lead, and ends with the gems. The passage that holds diamond is at lines 2141-2148:

"Pearls, corals, diamonds, sapphires, Vaidurya (lapis lazuli), crystals, etc. are beneficial to the sight, and cooling in their potency. They are antitoxic and act as liquefacient or corrosive agents. They are possessed of sacred prophylactic virtues, and bring good luck to men who wear them, and cleanse their wearers from all impurities. Thus the description of the salt group is ended."

(The wording is the same in `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/Bhishagratna_Vol1_djvu.txt` around line 21801; "antitoxic" is read from the garbled OCR "anti- toxic", and "tas*e" and "ar«" in the neighbouring lines are OCR errors.)

So the text names diamond only inside a list of gems, and attributes to the whole list: benefit to the sight; a cooling potency (*virya*); antitoxic action; a liquefacient or corrosive action; "sacred prophylactic virtues"; and the power, for the wearer, to bring good luck and "cleanse ... from all impurities". It does not say which of these belongs to diamond more than to the others. It gives no taste for the gems, no preparation, and no dose. It does not name a Sanskrit word for diamond in the passage, and none is supplied here.

*The metals just before it.* The same stretch gives the other substances of the metal group their own entries (lines 2121-2141), for example gold, "a tonic or restorative elixir", and bell metal, "bitter", a "liquefacient agent". Diamond has no separate sentence of this kind. This is why the entry is thin: the passage is one sentence-group about several stones.

*Other mentions of diamond in the Sushruta files.* In the Chikitsasthana, chapter 29, which opens as the discourse "on the restorative and on the constructive agents (Rasayana)" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/chikitsa/SS_Chikitsa_ch29.txt`, opening lines), the new teeth that follow a long course of treatment (a bathing and milk regimen using a Soma-valka decoction, in the chapter that begins with the Soma plant) are described as "strong, hard and as clear as a diamond or crystal or ruby" (line 191). This is a comparison for the appearance of the teeth. It is not a use of diamond as a drug, even though it sits in a rasayana chapter. Bhishagratna's Introduction has a footnote on the Atharva Samhita, which has, the footnote says, "Lead crystal (including diamond) gold and mineral poisons" mentioned at I.16, I.29, I.55 and IV.10 (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Vol1_frontmatter.txt` line 3016); that is the translator's note on another text, and not a Sushruta statement.

### Active Components

No text read gives a composition for diamond. In general terms, diamond is a crystalline form of pure carbon, the hardest natural material, and chemically very inert; it does not dissolve in the digestive tract. The "crystals" and "sapphires" with which the text groups it are other minerals: rock crystal is quartz (silicon dioxide), and sapphire is a form of corundum (aluminium oxide). The translator's word "diamonds" is the identification offered; the passage does not describe the stone. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records diamond, with other gems, as beneficial to the sight, antitoxic and protective when worn. The Codex does not recommend swallowing diamond or gem powders. Ground gems are sharp, hard particles that can irritate the gut lining, and no part of the text read supports a modern use. Wearing a gem is a traditional practice recorded in the text and carries no medical claim here.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives no preparation for diamond and no dose: it states that the gems "are possessed of sacred prophylactic virtues, and bring good luck to men who wear them", which is wearing, not taking. It gives no method of grinding, burning (*bhasma*) or purifying the stones in the passage read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Diamond is chemically inert carbon. Swallowed whole it passes through the gut without being absorbed; ground into sharp-edged particles it can scratch and irritate mucous membranes. Gem stones are not a drug and are not used as medicines in modern pharmacy. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry, and none for any effect on eyesight or poisoning. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Ayurveda (SS):** The text prints, for the gem group that includes diamonds (Sutra ch.46, lines 2141-2148): *cooling in potency* (*virya*, "cooling in their potency"); *beneficial to the sight*; *antitoxic*; *liquefacient or corrosive*; and "sacred prophylactic virtues". It prints no taste (*rasa*) for the gems, no *vipaka*, and no effect on the *doshas* (humours) in this passage. These are qualities of the group as printed; none is assigned to diamond alone.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status found.* Diamond is named only within the gem group of Sutra ch.46, in the dravyaguna frame of quality and action (cooling, antitoxic, good for the sight). The text's one statement that touches the person is that the gems "cleanse their wearers from all impurities"; that sentence is about the group and about wearing, and it names no *ojas*, no *rasayana* and no *vajikarana* status. In the same stretch gold is called "a tonic or restorative elixir"; the gems are not. The one mention of diamond in a rasayana chapter (Chikitsa ch.29, line 191) is a comparison for teeth, not an assignment of status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The text records gems as things worn for protection and luck and as antitoxic, cooling aids to the sight, and the Codex does not recommend swallowing diamond or gem powder. If the entry teaches anything, it is that Sushruta groups the stones and does not separate their properties. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Ingestion:** do not swallow powdered or crushed gemstones; sharp mineral particles injure the gut, and "bhasma" preparations of gems and metals, where made, can carry toxic metals from the process.
- **Eyes:** do not put gem powder in the eye; the text's "beneficial to the sight" is a classical claim, and grit in the eye damages the cornea.
- **Poisoning:** the text calls the gems "antitoxic"; no gem treats poisoning. Poisoning is an emergency for a clinician or poison centre.
- **Corrosive claim:** the text says the gems act as liquefacient or corrosive agents; the Codex has no basis to confirm this for diamond and recommends none.
- **Cross-tradition note, not a Sushruta statement:** Clark's introduction to the Tibetan *rGyud-bzhi* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 66) says "Precious gems such as diamond, aquamarine, sapphire, ruby and emerald are said to protect against spirits, and to cure all diseases (when compounded in medicine)". That line names diamond in the Tibetan tradition and so is a candidate locus for tagging FMT; it has not been added to the tag line.
- **Identification:** "diamonds" is the translator's word; the text gives no description of the stone.
