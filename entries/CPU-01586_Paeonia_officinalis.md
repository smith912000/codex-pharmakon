# Paeonia officinalis — Paionia arren, paionia theleia, the male and female peony (Dioscorides 3-157)

## *Paeonia officinalis var foemina [Linnaeus], the last of the three names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-157 (which also prints Paeonia foemina [Fuchs] and Paeonia communis [Bauhin] — "Female Peony, Garden Peony"), under a heading that names both a male and a female peony* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The chapter heading, *PAIONIA ARREN, PAIONIA THELEIA*, names two peonies, a male (*arren*) and a female (*theleia*), but the SUGGESTED line (line 7872) prints only the female group. The chapter's text speaks of both and distinguishes them in the leaves and the root. The translators also print a label "POISONOUS" on its own line (line 7873) between the SUGGESTED line and the text; it is theirs and not Dioscorides'. The row has not been verified against POWO. **Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-157. PAIONIA ARREN, PAIONIA THELEIA*, heading at line 7871, text at lines 7874, 7878 and 7879, in Book Three among the roots of the *akanthoda* (with a page break at the translators' page 529). It reads, in the first part: "Peonia (or glycyside) some call pentoboron, and they call the root paeonia. The stalk grows as high as two feet with many branches. The male has leaves similar to the carya [1-178], but the female is jagged in the leaves (similar to smyrnium [3-78, 3-79]). It sends out certain pods on the top of the stalk similar to almonds, which opened are found to contain five or six little red grains similar to those of the pomegranate — black in the middle, inclining to purple. The root of the male is about the thickness of a finger and twenty centimetres in height, astringent to taste, white. On the root of the female there are seven or eight tubers similar to acorns, like asphodelus [2-199]."

The uses follow: "The root is given to women who are not cleansed after childbirth. The amount of an almond (of a decoction taken as a drink) induces the menstrual flow. A decoction (taken as a drink with wine) helps pains in the intestines, jaundice, inflamed kidneys, and pain in the bladder. A decoction (boiled in wine and taken as a drink) stops discharges of the bowels. A decoction of ten or twelve red grains of the seed (taken as a drink in black hard wine) stops bloody excessive discharges (in women). Eaten, they help those who feel gnawing at the stomach, and taken as a drink and eaten by children they take away the beginnings of stones [kidney, urinary]. A decoction of as much as fifteen grains of the black [part] (taken as a drink in honey water or wine) is good both for suffocation that comes from nightmares, and for suffocation of the womb and disorders of the mother [pregnancy]. Peony grows on the tops of the highest mountains."

The last paragraph is of another kind: "The herb peony is plucked up in the heat of the dog days [summer] before sunrise; it is hanged about one and is good against poisons, bewitching, fears, and devils and their assaults, and against fevers that come with shivering whether by night, or day, or paroxysm. And it is said that (sometimes) growing on a hill where there were devils, it drove them away." The names follow: "The male peonie some call orobelium, orobax, haemagogum, paeseden, menogenion, menion, paeonium, panthiceratos, idaei dactyli, aglaophotis, theodonium, or selenion, the Magi call it selenogonon, some, phthisi and the Romans, casta."

The chapter is thus both a plant description (two forms, with leaf, pod, seed and root) and a long list of internal uses for the root and the seed, and then an amuletic use of the plucked herb. The amuletic sentences are stated by Dioscorides without distancing ("it is hanged about one and is good against poisons, bewitching, fears, and devils"), and the closing story of the hill is introduced by "it is said". The Codex records them as the text's statements and does not endorse them. The text gives no deity.

**The chapters around it.** 3-156 *BUPHTHALMON* (line 7864) is the chapter before it, which is also gathered and used against "fears, devils, enchantments, and poisons"; it is treated in I_1130 (*Matricaria inodora*), and the Codex does not carry its statements over to 3-157. 3-158 *LITHOSPERMON* (line 7884) is a separate chapter.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter id 3-157 and for the names *paionia*, *paeonia* and *peony* found one existing entry that covers the same chapter: I_696 (*Wild Peony, Paeonia mascula*, tagged EP, DMM, CM), whose DMM paragraph and Cultivation Register paragraph say Dioscorides distinguishes *paiōnía árrēn* (male, "our *P. mascula*") from *paiōnía thḗleia* (female, "*P. officinalis*") and describe the uses of the root. That is the same chapter 3-157, although I_696 cites no chapter number. The two entries are therefore a chapter-level duplicate and are not merged here; the decision is Smith's. I_026 (*Bai Shao, Paeonia lactiflora*) is a Chinese tradition entry and is a different plant. I_1130 (*Matricaria inodora*) cites 3-157 only as the chapter after its own. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The composition of the peony root and seed Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, the peonies (the genus *Paeonia*, Paeoniaceae) contain monoterpene glycosides such as paeoniflorin and also phenolic compounds. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a root and seed drunk for the menses, post-natal cleansing, intestinal pain, jaundice, kidney and bladder complaints, bowel discharges, the stone in children, and nightmare suffocation of the womb, and an herb worn on the body against poisons, fears and devils. The Codex gives no protocol for any of these. The translators label the plant "POISONOUS". The identification is the translators', among peonies of two forms.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives several preparations and quantities, with locus (lines 7874 to 7879): a decoction of the root, "the amount of an almond", drunk for the menses; a decoction drunk with wine; a decoction boiled in wine; "ten or twelve red grains of the seed" in a decoction in black hard wine, for excessive discharges in women; the seed "eaten"; and "as much as fifteen grains of the black [part]" in honey water or wine for suffocation. The herb is plucked "in the heat of the dog days [summer] before sunrise" and hung about the person. These quantities are as printed (translators' English); the "almond" is a size, and no other volume is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved for the other preparations. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that a herb worn on the body is not an antidote to poison and does not prevent fever, and that the uses for post-natal bleeding, jaundice, kidney pain and suffocation need medical assessment. The identification is a translators' suggestion, so the chapter does not show that the plant it describes is *Paeonia officinalis*.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one quality, that the male root is "astringent to taste, white". It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality for 3-157. The label "POISONOUS" is the translators'.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition, and it is a full one: the two forms are told apart by leaf (like *carya*, or jagged like *smyrnium*), the pods and the red and black grains are described, the root of each sex is described, the habitat ("the tops of the highest mountains") is given, and the herb is gathered at a fixed time ("the dog days before sunrise"). He also records an amuletic use, the herb "hanged about one", against poisons, bewitching, fears, devils and fevers, and a story of the plant driving devils from a hill, which he tells with "it is said". Recording is not endorsing; the cultivation spec notes that he sometimes records amuletic uses, and the text gives no further doctrine, deity or subtle body. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a medicinal root and seed and an amulet of report. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The useful lesson is Dioscorides' method of telling two forms of one plant by leaf, pod and root, and of fixing the hour and the season of gathering. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The translators print "POISONOUS" for the plant. Using the root or seed for the menses, post-natal bleeding, jaundice, kidney pain or suffocation of the womb needs medical care, and the chapter's use for "disorders of the mother" (which the translators gloss as pregnancy) is not something to attempt. The chapter's menstrual and post-partum uses are emmenagogue in kind, which are a risk in pregnancy. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
