# Guayusa / Guayusa

## *Ilex guayusa* Loes. | Family: Aquifoliaceae

**Tradition Tags:** SA
**Energetic Classification:** No indigenous humoral classification is documented for guayusa in the sources retrieved; Amazonian Kichwa practice does not operate a hot/cold humoral schema of the Galenic or Ayurvedic type. Any "warming/stimulant" label applied here is an **inferred** Western-herbalist gloss based on its xanthine content, not an attributed traditional category. Marked as such throughout.
**Training Phase:** Foundation — Signature Study

### Origin & Tradition

Guayusa is an evergreen holly of the upper Amazon — Aquifoliaceae, the same family that gives us yerba maté (*Ilex paraguariensis*) and yaupon (*Ilex vomitoria*), and one of the very few plant families outside the tea/coffee/cacao complex to have independently produced a caffeinated beverage culture. It is a small-to-medium tree cultivated in the Ecuadorian and Peruvian Amazon, particularly in Napo and Pastaza provinces, where it grows in the dooryard gardens (*chakras*) of Kichwa households rather than in wild stands. The peer-reviewed review literature is explicit on this point: the plant "has been consumed for thousands of years by the high Amazonian peoples of Ecuador and currently forms part of the gardens of ancestral peoples and mestizo settlers" (Noriega et al., 2025). Guayusa is, in other words, a *domesticate* — a cultivator's plant from the beginning, propagated by cutting and maintained in agroforestry association rather than harvested from forest.

**SA — South American indigenous ethnobotany.** The tradition tag here denotes the ethnobotanical practice of Amazonian indigenous peoples, principally the Kichwa (Napo Runa) of Ecuador, with related use among Shuar, Achuar and Zápara communities.

The ritual framing deserves to be stated accurately rather than romanticised. The characteristic practice is a **pre-dawn communal drinking**: the household rises in the small hours, a large pot of guayusa leaf is set to boil, and the family sits around the fire drinking gourd after gourd of the infusion while the night thins out. It is in this window — between sleep and the working day — that the dream-telling happens. Dreams from the night just past are recounted aloud and interpreted by elders, and the day's hunting, planting or travel decisions are shaped by what the dreams are taken to have indicated. The beverage is not incidental to this; it is the thing that holds a household awake, together, and articulate at three in the morning. It is properly understood as a **social and divinatory technology** — an infusion that structures a daily period of collective interpretation — rather than as a "dream herb" in the sense of a substance that generates or alters dreams. **No retrieved evidence supports any oneirogenic (dream-altering) pharmacology whatsoever.** Guayusa does not make you dream. It makes you awake at the hour when the dream is still retrievable, in a room full of people asking about it. That is a real and elegant mechanism, and it is a cultural one.

An emetic dimension is also reported in some contexts. The genus name of guayusa's North American relative — *Ilex vomitoria*, the yaupon of the southeastern US "black drink" ceremonies — preserves the same association, though in that case the emesis is now generally attributed to volume and ceremonial context rather than to a specific emetic constituent. For guayusa the review literature records purgative/cleansing use among traditional applications, but **the retrieved sources do not isolate an emetic principle**, and the most parsimonious reading is the same as for yaupon: very large volumes of hot bitter infusion taken rapidly will induce vomiting in most people, regardless of phytochemistry. Graded **traditional, mechanism unestablished**; flagged **[USER: verify]** if a stronger ethnographic citation is wanted.

Guayusa reached commercial export markets in the 2010s as a canned/bottled "clean energy" beverage and as a bulk leaf and extract ingredient, with much of the supply chain routed through Ecuadorian producer associations. This commercial layer is the source of most of the claims this entry has to deflate.

### Active Components

The chemistry is, at bottom, **a xanthine plant with a chlorogenic-acid load** — the same architecture as maté and, to a degree, coffee.

- **Caffeine** — the dominant active. Quantified in raw leaf at **2.27 ± 0.05%** by Cadena-Carrera et al. (2022), which is in the range of, or above, black tea leaf and comparable to some coffee beans by dry weight. This is the principal reason guayusa does anything.
- **Theobromine** — the second xanthine, confirmed and quantified alongside caffeine by HPLC in the same study. Theobromine is longer-acting, weaker at adenosine receptors and less anxiogenic than caffeine; it is the pharmacological basis for the *plausible* (not demonstrated) claim of a softer subjective profile.
- **5-Caffeoylquinic acid (chlorogenic acid)** — the third main quantified constituent (Cadena-Carrera et al., 2022), and the principal phenolic. Aqueous extracts carry the highest phenolic content (156.56 ± 1.32 mg GAE/g) and best antioxidant activity of the extraction methods tested.
- **Broader phenolic fraction** — catechol, syringol, 4-propenylsyringol and related compounds; total polyphenol content optimised at ~3.46 g gallic acid equivalents/100 g dry weight (Arteaga-Crespo et al., 2019).
- **Terpenes and volatiles** — a 205-metabolite untargeted profile has been published (Pastuña-Fasso et al., 2026), showing that leaf chemistry shifts measurably with **plant age (4–6 vs 6–8 vs 8–10 years)** and with **sun versus shade**. This is directly actionable for a cultivator: phenolics (catechol, syringol, 4-propenylsyringol) were significantly more abundant under light exposure, lipids (phytol, long-chain alcohols) under shade.

Note what is *absent*: no retrieved source reports any alkaloid, terpene or other constituent that would explain a distinct psychoactive character beyond the xanthines. There is no "guayusa molecule."

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Filed as a **Foundation-phase stimulant and vigil substance**, cross-referenced to the caffeine cluster: **Guarana (CPU-00478)** and yerba maté. Within the codex framework its use case is narrow and honest:

1. **Sustained pre-dawn wakefulness for practice.** A hot infusion taken on rising, at a dose that keeps the practitioner alert without agitation, to support an early-morning recall-and-record period. This is a direct structural borrowing from Kichwa practice and should be credited as such.
2. **A gentler entry point into the xanthine family than guarana.** Guarana delivers a high caffeine load in a concentrated, low-volume form; guayusa delivers it in a large-volume hot infusion with a substantial theobromine and chlorogenic fraction. The *ritual form* differs even where the pharmacology overlaps.
3. **Not a dream substance.** Any use in a dream-work curriculum must be framed as *scheduling* — waking the practitioner into the recall window — and never as pharmacological dream enhancement.

One caution belongs here rather than only in the safety section: caffeine taken too late in the day degrades sleep architecture, and degraded sleep degrades dream recall. Guayusa used carelessly will work against the very practice it is being recruited to support.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Form | Preparation | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional infusion | Dried/toasted leaf boiled or steeped in water; drunk hot, in volume, communally | The ethnographic form. No standardised leaf-to-water ratio is given in the retrieved literature — **[USER: verify]** against a primary ethnobotanical source before publishing a ratio |
| Standardised leaf extract | Studied at **600 mg** and **1200 mg** single oral doses in humans (Helwig et al., 2024) | These are *the only human doses with published controlled data retrieved here*. 600 mg was the better-tolerated dose |
| Aqueous vs ethanolic extract | Water as solvent gave the highest phenolic yield and antioxidant activity (Cadena-Carrera et al., 2022) | Supports the traditional water infusion as a rational extraction |

**Dosing caveats.** No dose in this table should be read as a therapeutic recommendation. The 600/1200 mg figures are experimental doses from a single 25-person crossover trial, not a clinical posology. Total daily caffeine — from *all* sources, including coffee, tea, chocolate, guarana and pre-workout formulas — is the quantity that actually matters; guayusa stacks with everything else in that column. **No fabricated dose is given here because none is available.**

### Scientific Correlation

**Claim: Guayusa contains meaningful caffeine and theobromine.** — **ESTABLISHED.** Directly quantified by HPLC (caffeine 2.27 ± 0.05% of raw leaf; theobromine and 5-caffeoylquinic acid also quantified).

**Claim: Acute improvement in mood, perceived energy and psychomotor speed in humans.** — **SUPPORTED, single trial, acute only.** A randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial in 25 adults found 600 mg guayusa leaf extract significantly improved total mood disturbance (−6.9 ± 2.6 au, p = 0.034), fatigue-inertia, perceived energy, motor speed and psychomotor speed versus placebo. This is real human evidence and should be respected as such — while noting n = 25, acute single-dose, healthy young adults, and no replication retrieved.

**Claim: "Smooth energy, no jitters."** — **REFUTED as stated by the best available human evidence.** In the same trial, jitteriness was **both dose- and sex-dependent, increasing with increasing dose in women (p < 0.001)**. Guayusa produced jitteriness. The marketing claim does not survive contact with the one controlled human study of the plant retrieved here. It may still be true that the theobromine fraction softens the subjective curve relative to equal-caffeine coffee, but **no retrieved study performs that head-to-head comparison**, so the claim is unevidenced in the direction it is usually made.

**Claim: Guayusa is cardiovascularly benign.** — **REFUTED as stated.** Both 600 mg and 1200 mg doses raised systolic and diastolic blood pressure by **4–5 mmHg** (p ≤ 0.022). Reassuringly, neither dose significantly affected QTc interval length (p = 0.31). This is an ordinary caffeine pressor effect, but it is a measured effect and must not be waved away.

**Claim: Antidiabetic activity.** — **PRECLINICAL ONLY, and thin.** Two strands exist. (i) A 1989 mouse study found guayusa retarded the development of hyperglycaemia in streptozotocin-diabetic mice and reduced hyperphagia, polydipsia, weight loss and glycated haemoglobin — with **no effect in normal mice**. (ii) A 2025 in-vitro study found α-glucosidase inhibition (best IC50 68.05 µg/mL) but **no α-amylase inhibition and no haemolysis**. Both are a long way from a human outcome. **There is no clinical trial evidence that guayusa improves glycaemic control in humans.** Anyone marketing it as antidiabetic is extrapolating from a 37-year-old rodent study and a plate assay. Graded **preclinical**.

**Claim: Antioxidant activity.** — **ESTABLISHED IN VITRO, clinically unproven.** Multiple assays (DPPH, ABTS, FRAP, Folin-Ciocalteu) confirm strong radical-scavenging capacity. In-vitro antioxidant capacity is not a health outcome and should never be presented as one.

**Claim: Acute toxicity.** — **LOW.** A functional drink containing guayusa showed no mortality, organ-weight change or histopathology at a single 2000 mg/kg oral dose in mice, classifying as low toxicity under GHS. Note this was a *multi-ingredient formulation*, not guayusa alone.

**Claim: Dental remineralisation.** — **PRELIMINARY / IN VITRO.** Guayusa extract induced phosphate and orthophosphate mineral apposition on amelogenesis-imperfecta tooth slices, and showed low cytotoxicity to human dental pulp stem cells. Interesting; nowhere near clinical use. Graded **preclinical**.

**NULL results worth recording:** no effect on glucose homeostasis in *normal* mice; no α-amylase inhibition; no haemolysis; no significant QTc prolongation; **no evidence of any kind for oneirogenic, dream-enhancing, or nootropic-beyond-caffeine effects.**

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Guayusa sits **outside the humoral systems** the codex normally maps against. There is no Galenic hot/cold/wet/dry assignment, no Ayurvedic dosha attribution and no TCM temperature classification with a documented traditional source for this species. Amazonian Kichwa medicine categorises plants by relational and practical criteria — what the plant is *for*, who may prepare it, when it is taken — rather than by a four-humour or five-element grid.

What can be said honestly: guayusa's traditional classification is **temporal and social**. It is the plant of the hour before dawn, of the household gathered, of the dream told and read. Its "energetics," in the only sense the tradition supports, are those of *assembly and attention*. Any Western herbal source assigning it "warm, dry, stimulant" is applying an external schema by analogy with its xanthine content; that gloss is **inferred**, and this entry declines to launder it as traditional.

### Contraindications

- **Caffeine, in ordinary quantity.** Every caffeine contraindication applies fully. Guayusa is not a caffeine-free or caffeine-light plant: 2.27% of raw leaf by weight.
- **Cardiovascular.** Measured pressor effect of 4–5 mmHg systolic and diastolic at both 600 mg and 1200 mg extract doses in healthy young adults (Helwig et al., 2024). Avoid or use only under medical supervision in hypertension, arrhythmia, or established cardiac disease. QTc was not significantly affected in that trial, which is reassuring but not a clearance.
- **Anxiety and panic disorder.** Jitteriness was demonstrated, dose-dependently, and was significantly greater in women at higher dose. Contraindicated in anxiety-sensitive individuals.
- **Insomnia and sleep-dependent practice.** Late-day use will degrade sleep quality and, by extension, dream recall — self-defeating for the use case this entry describes.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** Standard caffeine limits in pregnancy (commonly ~200 mg/day; follow current national guidance) apply to the *total* caffeine load including guayusa. No pregnancy-specific safety data for guayusa was retrieved. Avoid.
- **Dose stacking.** The single most likely real-world harm. Guayusa in a "natural energy" drink plus coffee plus a guarana-containing pre-workout is a large undeclared caffeine total. Count it.
- **Children and adolescents.** No paediatric data retrieved. Avoid.
- **Drug interactions.** Caffeine is a CYP1A2 substrate; interactions with fluvoxamine, ciprofloxacin, oral contraceptives and smoking status alter caffeine clearance substantially. Caution with other stimulants, theophylline, and MAOIs.
- **Genus-level note, carefully attributed.** The *Ilex* congener yerba maté (*Ilex paraguariensis*) is associated with increased risk of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma in large South American case-control data, with risk increasing linearly with cumulative consumption and, importantly, **increasing with beverage temperature** (EOR/LPDY 0.004 warm, 0.007 hot, 0.016 very hot; p < 0.01 for difference). **This is congener data and it is largely a thermal-injury and possibly PAH signal, not a demonstrated property of any *Ilex* species per se, and emphatically not a guayusa-specific finding — no guayusa carcinogenicity data was retrieved.** The transferable, defensible lesson is behavioural: **do not drink any infusion scalding hot, habitually, for decades.** That caution applies to guayusa because guayusa is traditionally drunk hot and in volume, not because guayusa has been shown to cause cancer. It has not been studied for this.
- **Emetic effect.** Very large volumes of hot bitter infusion may induce vomiting. Relevant to anyone attempting to replicate traditional volumes.
