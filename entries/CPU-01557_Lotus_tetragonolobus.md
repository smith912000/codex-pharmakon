# Lotus tetragonolobus — Lotos emeros (Dioscorides 4-111, "Lotus sativa"); "Garden Winged Pea" on the translators' line

## *Lotus tetragonolobus, the first binomial on the translators' SUGGESTED line for chapter 4-111 (glossed "Garden Winged Pea"; a second binomial, Tetragonolobus palestinus, "Four-winged Garden Pea", follows)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk names this plant under this name. The entry rests on one chapter of one text, and that chapter is two sentences long. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The heading *4-111. LOTOS EMEROS* is at line 9021, the SUGGESTED line at line 9023 and the chapter text at line 9024. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 691. The chapter stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), after 4-110 *KAPNOS* (fumitory, line 9016) and before 4-112 *LOTOS AGRIOS* (line 9038).

The whole chapter reads: "Lotus sativa grows in gardens. Juiced and mixed with honey it dissolves argema [small white ulcer on the cornea], nubeculae [speck or small cloud in the eye], white spots on the cornea, and things that darken the pupils. It is also called tripodion, or trifolium."

So the chapter says where the plant grows (in gardens), what is done (the plant is juiced and mixed with honey) and what it is for (four conditions of the eye, the translators' brackets glossing the first two). It gives no description of the plant, no part used, no quantity and no season. The other names are *tripodion* and *trifolium*. The translators print the Latin phrase "Lotus sativa" in the text, which is the "sown" or "garden" lotus, matching the Greek *emeros*, "cultivated". The translators' own index (line 16373) lists *Lotus tetragonolobus* at page 660, which is the page printed at the end of this chapter, and lists *tripodion* at 660 (line 11554).

**Why the identity is only a suggestion.** The word *lotos* names several unlike things in this text, and the Codex does not treat them as one. 4-112 *LOTOS AGRIOS* (line 9038) is glossed *Lotus corniculatus*, "Bird's Foot Trefoil", with three-leaved clover-like leaves. 4-114 *LOTOS AIGUPTIOS* (line 9055) is glossed *Nymphaea lotus*, a water plant. 1-171 *LOTOS* (line 4776) is a tree, glossed *Diospyros lotus* or *Zizyphus lotus*. The name "Lotus sativa [Fuchs]" is also printed on the SUGGESTED line of a different chapter, 3-123 *TRIPHULLON* (line 7640), glossed there as a trefoil. So the Renaissance name is shared between two chapters, and the binomial of this entry comes from the 4-111 line alone. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** The chapter is not covered by any existing entry (chapter-level search for 4-111, *Lotos emeros*, *Lotus sativa* and *Lotus tetragonolobus* across the three entry folders). The neighbouring chapters 4-112 and 4-114 also have no entry, and nothing here is merged with any of them.

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. As a general fact, *Lotus tetragonolobus* as a modern binomial names a legume, a plant of the pea family. Nothing is stated here about its composition. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a honeyed plant juice for white spots and ulcers of the eye. This is Dioscorides' claim and not a Codex protocol. The Codex gives no protocol. Eye disease needs medical assessment, and nothing should be put into the eye on the strength of this entry.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation: the plant is "juiced and mixed with honey" and, by the sense of the sentence, applied to the eye. The text does not say how, in what amount or how often. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Chapter 4-111 prints no warning. The eye is a delicate organ, and an unidentified garden plant juice mixed with honey is not sterile, so nothing should be applied to the eye on the strength of this entry. Eye ulcers and spots on the cornea need medical care. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
