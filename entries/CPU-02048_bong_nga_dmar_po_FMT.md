# bong.nga.dmar.po [FMT] — "Red Aconite" (Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch. 20); bong nga dmar po, Aconitum sp. (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)

## *A red-rooted, crimson-flowered aconite, Aconitum sp.; the genus is given by both Tibetan-medicine texts on disk and the species is not. The identification is the translators', at low confidence at species level and high at genus level. Clark prints "Aconitum sp." and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 prints "Aconitum sp." for the same name; the white kind of the same family of names is glossed Aconitum heterophyllum, but the red kind is not tied to any species. The aconites are among the most poisonous plants known, and this entry is a historical record only* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** The genus *Aconitum* is among the most poisonous plant genera. Clark's own entry for the white kind of this family of names says "The black type is highly toxic" (line 965). This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should take any aconite on the strength of it.

**Named by the *rGyud-bzhi* tradition, as printed in two modern works.** This is a single-tradition entry. The principal text is Clark 1995, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; the second text on disk, the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 *Root and Explanatory Tantra* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`), is read as a second witness. The name index (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`) holds the row "bong.nga.dmar.po, Clark 1995 (line 968), Red Aconite". The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder; this entry replaces it. Clark's text was not examined in Tibetan, and the Tibetan script is not supplied here.

**Tibetan medicine (FMT).** The entry stands in Clark's rendering of chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (chapter heading at line 792). That chapter's "Specific Section" divides individual natural substances into eight: "(1) precious [metal] medicines (2) stone medicines (3) earth medicines (4) tree medicines (5) resins (6) plants (7) herbs (8) animal products" (line 802). The red aconite entry (line 968) falls between the printed heading "(5) Resins" (line 879) and "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997), so it belongs to section (6), plants; the e-text does not print that section heading, and the placement is ours. Clark's entry reads in full: "bong.nga.dmar.po: Red Aconite: Aconitum sp.: [has a crimson flower and a red root. It has bitter taste and is an antidote to poison]." (line 968). In Clark's convention (stated in his introduction, line 82), square brackets mark interpolations "taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary", so the description, the taste and the "antidote to poison" are commentarial and not the root text's own wording. The root-text line itself is only the name; the entry gives no named condition cured outside the brackets. [USER: verify against the Tibetan]

*The neighbouring aconite entries (not folded).* The white kind, "bong.nga.dkar.po" (line 965), is glossed "White Aconite: Aconitum naviculare Stapf.: Aconitum sinense: Aconitum heterophyllum: [has ten names and is of four types: white, red, yellow and black. The black type is highly toxic, whilst the white one has bitter taste and neutralizes poison ...]. It cures infectious fever with poisoning and bile fever." The yellow kind, "bong.nga.ser.po" (line 969), is "Yellow Aconite: Aconitum autumnale: [has a yellowish flower. It has bitter taste, cool power and is recommended in poison fevers and infectious bile fever. (Roots of) red and yellow aconite] cure meat poisoning and poisoning from black aconite (Aconitum ferrox Wall)." So Clark's comment names red aconite (with yellow) as a remedy for meat poisoning and for poisoning from the black kind. The black kind, which Clark's bracket calls highly toxic, has its own name in the index of Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 ("bong nga nag po", *Aconitum napellus*).

**Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 as second witness.** The glossary of that book (lines 5114-5120) gives the four names together: "Bongnga karpo, bong nga dkar po, *Aconitum heterophyllum*; Bongnga marpo, bong nga dmar po, *Aconitum* sp.; Bongnga nagpo, bong nga nag po, *Aconitum napellus*; Bongnga serpo, bong nga ser po, *Aconitum lycoctonum*." The red kind is thus again identified to genus only. The glossary gives no use for it; the passage of the Explanatory Tantra for this plant in that book was not found in this reading. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, aconites contain highly toxic diterpenoid alkaloids, of which aconitine is the best known, and the roots are poisonous in very small amounts; the toxicity depends on the species and on how the root is treated. Which species is meant by "red aconite" is not settled here, so no assurance can be given that it is a lesser or a greater poison than the others. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Highly toxic genus.**

- **What the text says it is for.** As a commentarial gloss: an antidote to poison, and with yellow aconite a remedy for meat poisoning and for poisoning from black aconite.
- **What the text does not say.** The part used beyond "red root", any preparation, any dose, how the poison of an aconite is first removed in the Tibetan pharmacy, or which of the aconites is the antidote in practice.

The Codex gives no protocol and does not recommend any aconite. That a poisonous genus is recorded as an antidote to poison is a fact about the tradition's reasoning and is not a guide to any use.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Aconites (monkshood, wolfsbane) are perennial herbs of the buttercup family with tuberous roots that contain very toxic alkaloids; poisoning affects the heart and nerves and can be fatal. Traditional systems in Asia process aconite roots before use to reduce this toxicity, but a processed root is still dangerous. The notion that one aconite is an antidote to the poison of another has no modern support in the sources read, and none is claimed. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan medicine (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* The red aconite sits in the materia medica chapter of the Explanatory Tantra as a plant medicine whose commentarial gloss makes it an antidote to poison. The tradition's frame is that medicine is the third of four treatment branches: it supports practice and does not reach the root of illness, which in the Tibetan account is at root a spiritual condition rooted in the three mental poisons of attachment, aversion and delusion (these "poisons" are mental and are a different matter from the toxin of the plant). No *bcud len* (essence-extraction) status is attached to this plant in the lines read, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, so none is asserted. The cultivation status is unattested.

**Practitioner's note.** A highly poisonous genus, recorded by the commentaries as an antidote to poison, and nothing more. A cultivator takes nothing from it and does nothing with it; the only lesson is the old one that a drug of this kind is for a trained physician who knows how it is prepared, and not for anyone else.

### Contraindications

- **TOXIC GENUS.** Aconites can kill in very small amounts. Do not ingest, handle roots bare-handed, or gather.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children, the weak.** No statement in the sources retrieved. Given the toxicity, avoid. [USER: verify]
- **Poisoning, fever.** These are medical conditions; use proper care.
- **Identity.** The species is not settled; do not rely on colour of flower or root to tell the kinds apart.
- **Legal status.** Aconites are controlled or restricted in some countries; no source retrieved states the position. [USER: verify]
