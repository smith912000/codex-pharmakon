# Rhypos gymnasion — RUPOS GYMNASION, "grime from the gymnasium walls" (Dioscorides *De Materia Medica* 1-36)

## *Filth scraped from the walls of the gymnasium, or from statues there: a deposit of oil, sweat, dust and skin scrapings, not a mineral in the ordinary sense; the identification is by description only. The translator gives no "SUGGESTED" line for the chapter, the name-index calls the class "other", and the spine row is classed mineral by a forced rule, so the class is a filing decision and not a finding; confidence in the description is high, in the class low* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

### Origin & Tradition

The chapter was read in full (lines 3826 to 3827).

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The entire chapter is two sentences. The translator's subtitle is "Grime from the gymnasium walls". The text: "The filth on the walls of the gymnasium (or that which is scraped off from statues) warms and dissolves tubercles [growths] that ripen only with difficulty, and it is helpful for abrasions, the removal of scaliness, and old ulcers." The bracket "growths" is the translator's gloss of the Greek term for tubercles. The text names no preparation, no vehicle, no dose and no way of applying it, other than the implied direct application of a scraping.

**The neighbouring chapters, read for context.** The grime chapters form a run of three in Book I, among the oils and unguents (the page opens with an oil chapter, line 3818).

- 1-34 RUPOS, "Grime from the baths" (line 3821): "The scrapings which are taken up in public baths are able to heat, soften, and disperse fluids, and are good for splits in the perineum, and for rubbing on joints."
- 1-35 RUPOS PALAISTRA, "Grime from the wrestling school" (line 3824): "The dirt or filth from the wrestling school helps the joints, applied as a warm compress."
- 1-36 RUPOS GYMNASION (this row): wall and statue filth, "warms and dissolves tubercles", helps abrasions, scaliness and old ulcers.

The three differ by the place the filth was taken from (public bath, wrestling school, gymnasium walls and statues) and by the complaints named. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Warming; dissolving slow-ripening swellings ("tubercles"); abrasions; removing scaliness; old ulcers.
- **What the text does not say.** It gives no dose, no preparation, no length of use, and no warning.
- **Why it is not a practice.** Applying scrapings of public-building filth to broken skin or ulcers risks infection (including tetanus from soil-contaminated material). The Codex records the passage as a piece of the history of materia medica and recommends nothing from it.

The Codex does not translate "tubercles", "scaliness" or "old ulcers" into modern diagnoses and gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives no preparation. The only action implied is that the filth is taken from the wall, or "scraped off from statues", and then used (line 3827). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify before any numerical use.] **Do not use anything from this entry.**

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Dirt and body residues from communal places are not sterile, and applying them to ulcers or abrasions is a recognised route to wound infection. Modern wound care uses cleaning and sterile dressings. Dioscorides's "warms" and "dissolves" are the author's qualities and are not translated into modern pharmacology. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is attested for this substance. What the chapter does show is the attention to provenance that runs through his book: the same kind of filth is sorted by where it was taken, baths, wrestling school or gymnasium wall and statue, and each place has its own named use. That is a record of sorting materials by source and effect, with no claim about the person's vital constitution.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. The tradition records it as a topical remedy, and its place in a book of cultivation is that of a curiosity: the gymnasium, the place where bodies were trained, also gave a substance to the physician's shelf. Nothing in the record supports a cultivation use.

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

The chapter's claimed sites are the skin and the swellings under it. It is said to warm and dissolve tubercles, the slow-ripening swellings or growths, which maps to the subcutaneous nodules and glandular swellings of the skin. It is also helpful for abrasions, which are injuries to the superficial skin layer, the epidermis, and for the removal of scaliness, the scaly skin surface. Old ulcers, the chronic skin wounds, complete the list. The warming action is printed in the chapter as a quality of its own, and the neighbouring bath grime is said to heat, soften and disperse fluids. No internal organ, humour or channel is named. The chapter prints no preparation, vehicle or dose, and the entry does not place any part of the body beyond the skin.

### Contraindications

- **Infection.** Material scraped from walls, statues or public places is unclean. Do not apply it to wounds, abrasions or ulcers.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children, the weak.** No statement in the sources retrieved; avoid. [USER: verify]
- **Heritage.** Scraping walls or statues in historic buildings would be vandalism and, in many countries, an offence.
- **Identity and class.** The substance is described, not identified, and the mineral class is a filing decision. [USER: verify]
