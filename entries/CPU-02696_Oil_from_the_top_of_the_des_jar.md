# Oil from the top of the des-jar — "Oil-from-the-Top-of-the-des-jar" (Ebers, Bryan 1930)

## *An oil taken from the top of a vessel called a "des-jar" in Bryan's English; what the oil was, what the des-jar was, and what Bryan means by placing it among the mineral remedies are all unclear; Bryan's own list separates it from "Oil-of-the-Earth (Petroleum)"; identification unknown, confidence low* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry.** The substance is attested only in the Papyrus Ebers as C. P. Bryan translated it in 1930 (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`). The English text on disk is a translation with summaries; the Egyptian words are not printed, so nothing of the original name, and nothing of what *des* denotes, can be read from it. [USER: verify against a glossary or the hieratic text.]

**Where it stands.** It is in the list Bryan prints in Chapter V, the chapter on mineral remedies, in this line: "Oil-of-the-Earth (Petroleum) Oil-from-the-Top-of-the-des-jar Pieces-of-dried Oil" (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`, line 1194). The list's other oils are "Oil Castor Oil Olive Oil White Oil Oil-of-Two-Days" (line 1193 onward). In the paragraph before the list Bryan says that few of the substances in the chapter "are prescribed in their crude or native state", that "the source of origin of the drugs is strictly laid down" and that "various preparations made from the parent drug are prescribed" (line 1137). The row's check line says the oil is not naphtha, and Bryan marks only "Oil-of-the-Earth" with "(Petroleum)" in his list; the des-jar oil carries no gloss at all.

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP), the one use found.** In the chapter on indigestion and the abdomen, after a remedy "to drive out inflammation in the abdomen" that combines a piece of the stem of the flax plant with fresh milk and is to be applied "to the abdomen of the sick person", the text gives a second remedy: "ANOTHER / Oil-from-the-Top-of-the-des-jar / Put on the Patient's abdomen" (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`, line 1943). Bryan introduces the two remedies with the remark "The inflamed abdomen was not called upon to endure such indignities as these. The remedies were many, but two will suffice" (the lines just before). So the oil appears as a single-ingredient application to the abdomen for abdominal inflammation. Whether the papyrus gives a recitation with it is not shown; Bryan summarises, so absence in the lines read is not proof that none is given. No deity is attributed.

The sources read name no constituent and do not identify the oil. In general terms, an oil skimmed from the top of a vessel would be the lighter, immiscible fraction that rises above water or a watery liquid, such as a fat or plant oil. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says.** The oil is an application to the abdomen, placed on the patient as the second of two remedies for an inflamed abdomen (line 1943).
- **What the text does not say.** What the oil is, how it was obtained beyond the phrase "from the top of the des-jar", how much, how often, or what the remedy was expected to do.
- **What the Codex does.** It records the name and its one use and offers no protocol. Abdominal inflammation is a medical condition that needs proper care, and nothing in the record supports a modern use.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's vital constitution. The oil appears once, as a single-ingredient application for an inflamed abdomen. The Egyptian person is plural (*ka*, *ba*, *akh*, *ib*) and spell and drug are components of one intervention (*heka*), but no source read links this oil to any of them, and Bryan's translation does not print any recitation with it. The identity of the substance is a modern hypothesis at best, and here not even that. No deity is attributed.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to practise. The substance cannot be identified, the record is a single line, and the complaint it addresses needs proper care. The honest cultivator's act is to leave it as a record, and to treat the provenance phrase ("from the top of the des-jar") as a reminder that Egyptian drug names often encode where and how a thing was gathered.

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

Little to map: the entry states one site, the abdomen. The oil is put on the patient's abdomen as the second of two remedies for an inflamed abdomen, in a chapter on indigestion and the abdomen, where the first remedy (flax stem with fresh milk) is applied to the same place. The application is external. The entry gives no temperament, humour or other quality for the oil, and the Ebers material is organised by complaint rather than by constitution. The Egyptian terms for the person (ka, ba, akh, ib) and the heka of spell and drug are general context for the tradition, and no source here links this oil to any of them. The oil's placement among minerals comes from Bryan's list, and the text states no mineral property for it. No quantity or duration is given.
