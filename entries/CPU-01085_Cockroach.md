# Cockroach — SILPHE (Dioscorides 2-38, "blatta") / bse.sbur (Clark 1995, "Tibetan cockroach"; Men-Tsee-Khang "wingless cockroach")
## *Cockroach, the insect itself; Osbaldeston prints "Cockroach — Blatta orientalis", Clark prints* Blatta orientalis *L. with two other possible identities* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM, FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-38 *SILPHE* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 5092-5095, Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). The whole chapter is four lines: the heading "Cockroach — Blatta orientalis", a capitalised editorial caution, "THESE INSECTS ARE CARRIERS OF MANY DISEASES.", and the text: "The inner parts of the kind of blatta which is found in bakehouses and mill houses, pounded with oil or boiled and dropped into the ears lessens their pain." So Dioscorides gives one use (earache), a choice of two preparations (pounded in oil, or boiled), a route (dropped into the ear) and a provenance rule: the kind of *blatta* that lives in bakehouses and mill houses. The caution is the translator's own capitalised note, which the edition uses for warnings (the neighbouring chapter on the liver of a mad dog carries "RABIES IS HIGHLY COMMUNICABLE"); it is not Dioscorides's wording as far as the translation shows. The chapter sits among the small creatures of Book Two (it follows bed bugs, 2-36, and the woodlouse, 2-37, and precedes the sea lung, 2-39) and is indexed on page 197 of the edition.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Two renderings of the Explanatory Tantra ch.20 (the efficacy of medicinal substances). Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 1199) gives, at the end of the animal medicines after the droppings: "bse.sbur: Tibetan cockroach: Blatta orientalis L.: Periplaneta americana: Geotrupes laevistriatus: (a large, black, malodorous, nocturnal insect): [is beneficial in septic fevers and for lte.mkhad, a children's disease]." The same passage continues: "bying.bying.thu.lus: Dung beetle and Scarab beetle: both types found under dung: [has five names and relieves urinary retention. These two types of insect] and Tibetan cockroach are antispasmodic." (Clark's square brackets mark interpolations from the *Blue Aquamarine* and *Spreading Tree* commentaries, Clark's own note at line 82, so the septic-fevers and children's-disease uses come from the commentaries, and the bare root-text action is "antispasmodic".) Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 3573) puts it more briefly: "Dark beetles and wingless cockroaches relieve abdominal cramps." Its animal glossary prints "Jingjing Thulu — Wingless Cockroach" (line 7164). The translator's identification in Clark is a list of three: a cockroach (*Blatta orientalis*), the American cockroach (*Periplaneta americana*) and a dung beetle (*Geotrupes laevistriatus*), so the Tibetan insect is not securely a cockroach. The thirteen kinds of animal medicine in Men-Tsee-Khang (line 3503) end with "the entire body of an animal"; the insects here are the whole-body items, which is an inference from their position, not a statement of the text. ### Active Components

The sources name none. In general: insects are mostly protein and chitin with fat; cockroaches are known carriers of bacteria on their body surfaces and gut, and their body fragments and droppings are common household allergens (tropomyosin among them). Nothing is known from the sources of any pharmacologically active content. No quantities are given here because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Two traditions use the insect for small, specific complaints: the Greek text for earache, the Tibetan texts for spasm or cramp. The Codex has no protocol for either and offers none. Putting crushed or boiled cockroach into the ear is a route to infection of a painful ear and should not be done; the translator's own caution about disease-carrying belongs on the record; there is no dose; and the Tibetan identification is itself unsettled between a cockroach and a dung beetle.

### Preparation & Dosing

Dioscorides gives two preparations for the ear: the inner parts pounded with oil, or boiled, and dropped in (2-38). Clark and Men-Tsee-Khang give an action only, with no method in the lines read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Cockroaches carry bacteria including *Salmonella* and *Escherichia coli* on their bodies and in their gut and droppings, and their cast skins and faeces are a recognised cause of asthma and allergy, especially in children living in infested homes. Earache has many causes, and a perforated eardrum, which cannot be excluded at home, makes dropping any foreign material into the ear dangerous. Nothing in the sources read supports a pain-relieving action of the insect itself; oil alone can soothe, which may be the active part of the Greek recipe, but that is not a claim any text makes. No clinical studies of the traditional uses were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** None is printed for the cockroach. The chapter gives a use and two preparations only.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The animal-medicine lines print an action ("antispasmodic", Clark; "relieve abdominal cramps", Men-Tsee-Khang) and, in Clark's bracketed commentary, uses against septic fevers and a children's disease. No taste, potency or humour is assigned to the insect in the lines read.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied. The cockroach is a plain therapeutic entry for earache. The only attention the text asks of the user is a provenance rule: the kind found in bakehouses and mill houses. That is a discipline of selection, not a claim about the person. The translator's caution about disease carriage is the edition's, and it is the useful part for a cultivator.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The cockroach is one of the small-creature medicines at the end of the Explanatory Tantra's animal list, given for spasm (root text) and for septic fevers and a children's disease (commentary). No essence-extraction (*bcud len*) use is attested, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition; none is asserted. The spine's locus for this tag is the Clark text line itself; `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` has no cockroach row.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do. In both traditions the cockroach is a drug for a small complaint, and the translator in one of them warns against it. A cultivator can take the bakehouse rule as a reminder that old materia medica was selective about where its animal materials came from, and can leave the insect alone. ### Contraindications

- **Infection.** Cockroaches carry pathogens; never put crushed or boiled insect into an ear, wound or mouth. Ear pain with discharge, fever or hearing loss needs a clinician.
- **Allergy and asthma.** Cockroach body parts and droppings are allergens; avoid in people with asthma or insect allergy.
- **Perforated eardrum.** Anything dropped into the ear is hazardous if the drum is damaged, which cannot be judged at home.
- **Identification.** The Tibetan insect may be a dung beetle or another beetle (Clark's list); do not assume a species.
- **Dose unknown.** No dose is given in the sources read.
