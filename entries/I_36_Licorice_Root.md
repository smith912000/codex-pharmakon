### 2. Cross-Traditional Presence

#### SBJ — Shennong Bencao Jing / Bencao Gangmu (China, ~200 BCE–1596 CE)

Gān cǎo (甘草) holds a position unique in all of pharmacy: it is the single most commonly used herb in Chinese medicine — appearing as an adjuvant in the majority of classical formulas. The Shennong Bencao Jing classifies it as a **supreme-grade drug** (上品), and Li Shizhen gives it the honorary title 國老 (*guó lǎo*, Senior Counsellor of the State) — an official who harmonises all the other officials without claiming power himself.

- **Classification:** Sweet (甘), neutral (平); enters all twelve channels (unique property); Lung, Spleen, Heart, Stomach primary
- **Primary uses:** Tonifies Spleen and benefits Qi; moistens the Lung and stops cough; moderates and harmonises other medicinals; detoxifies (reduces toxicity of aconite and other harsh herbs); stops pain via antispasmodic action
- **Source text:** *"Gan cao is sweet in flavor and neutral. It governs wounds from swords and knives, swelling and hemorrhage, and it soothes joints and bones, detoxifies one hundred poisons. Long-term taking can invigorate the body, improve complexion and prolong life."* — Shennong Bencao Jing
- **Formulary role:** Present in over 70% of classical Chinese formulas as a harmonising agent (調和諸藥). Zhang Zhongjing (Han dynasty) uses it in virtually every formula in the Shanghan Lun and Jingui Yaolue — including the emergency Yang-restoration formulas.
- **Honey-roasted (炙甘草, zhì gān cǎo):** Specifically indicated for tonifying Qi and Blood; raw form more for clearing heat and detoxifying.

#### SS — Sushruta Samhita / Charaka Samhita (India, ~600 BCE–200 CE)

*Yaṣṭimadhu* is one of the ten fundamental herbs in the Ayurvedic pharmacopoeia, used in the Tridoshic herbs category and appearing in the classical Dashamoola (ten root formula) and many Rasayana preparations.

- **Classification:** Madhura (sweet), Tikta (bitter secondary); Guru (heavy), Snigdha (unctuous); Shīta virya (cold potency — unusual for a tonic); post-digestive effect: sweet
- **Primary use:** Balances all three doshas; lubricates and soothes mucous membranes (Ayurvedic voice tonic); relieves Pitta inflammation; supports Ojas; used in Vata disorders via its heaviness and sweetness; *Medhya rasayana* (mind-nourishing tonic)
- **Source text:** *"Yaṣṭimadhu is sweet, heavy, unctuous, cold. It is beneficial for the voice, skin, eyes and hair. It is a Medhya (intellect-promoting) drug, a Jivaniya (life-sustaining) drug and a Vayasthapana (anti-aging) drug."* — Charaka Samhita, Sutrasthana 4.18
- **Specific applications:** Combined with ghee and milk for Pitta conditions; with honey for respiratory and voice conditions; in Triphala churna compounds as harmoniser; in Chyawanprash as key ingredient

#### DMM — De Materia Medica / Dioscorides (Greco-Roman, ~70 CE)

Dioscorides provides one of the most detailed classical Western descriptions of licorice (Book III, Chapter 5), noting its origin in Pontus, Cappadocia, and Cilicia, its cultivation, and its taste distinctly different from the root's appearance.

- **Classification:** Warming and moistening; beneficial to the chest and throat; fourth degree of sweetness
- **Primary uses:** Respiratory demulcent (sore throat, dry cough, hoarseness); quenches thirst; beneficial for the liver and kidneys; applied topically for eye disorders; given for gastric complaints
- **Source text:** *"The root of this herb, being chewed or held under the tongue, quenches thirst; it heals ulcers of the mouth; it is good against roughness of the throat, liver and kidney complaints... and the juice is prepared by boiling it."* — De Materia Medica III.5
- **Trade significance:** Among the most widely traded medicinal roots in the ancient Mediterranean; Dioscorides' plant was *G. glabra* (Scythian licorice) from the Black Sea region

#### CM — Canon of Medicine / Avicenna (Arabic-Persian, ~1025 CE)

Ibn Sīnā devotes a substantial section to *ʿirq al-sūs* in the *Qānūn*, noting its action on humoral balance and its remarkable property of entering all organ systems without causing harm.

- **Classification:** Warm in first degree, moist in second degree; suitable for all temperaments due to its balancing nature; the "great harmoniser" parallel to its Chinese designation
- **Primary use:** Chest and respiratory demulcent; anti-inflammatory for the throat and stomach; thirst-quenching; liver protective; anti-ulcerative; harmonising adjuvant for otherwise harsh medicines
- **Source text:** *"Sūs is moderately warm and moist. It removes roughness from the throat, lungs and windpipe. It is good for coughs, thirst, liver disorders... It harmonises other medicines and reduces their harshness."* — Qānūn fi'l-Tibb, Book II
- **Formulary role:** Used in Arabic medicine exactly as in Chinese medicine — as the harmoniser and moderator in compound prescriptions; primary ingredient in Islamic electuary (maʿjūn) preparations

#### EP — Ebers Papyrus (Egypt, ~1550 BCE)

The sweet root (*yrrw* or equivalent) appears in the Ebers Papyrus in at least twelve formulae, making it among the most referenced plants in the entire document.

- **Primary uses:** Respiratory complaints; eye conditions (topical applications); digestive disorders; as a sweetening agent and vehicle for other medicines
- **Administrative context:** Frequently used as a pleasant-tasting vehicle to make bitter medicines more palatable — the same role as *guó lǎo* in Chinese pharmacy
- **Source text reference:** Ebers Papyrus, columns 34–47 (respiratory section); columns 55–63 (digestive section); multiple eye medicine sections

#### AT — Assyrian Tablets (Mesopotamia, ~650 BCE)

*Amurdinnu* appears in Ashurbanipal's Nineveh library tablets in compound preparations for respiratory and digestive conditions, often alongside cedar, juniper, and salt.

- **Primary use:** Healing mouth and throat conditions; respiratory preparations; digestive compound preparations; some texts suggest ritual use alongside incantations for conditions affecting "the throat of the patient" (voice and speech disorders)
- **Identification note:** The identification of *amurdinnu* with licorice is supported by botanical and philological analysis; the compound preparations it appears in are consistent with its known pharmacological profile

#### FMT — Four Medical Tantras / rGyud-bzhi (Tibet, ~12th century CE)

*Shing mnyenpa* (licorice) is one of the five *essential medicines* (*sman rtsa lnga*) in Tibetan medicine — substances so fundamental to the materia medica that they cannot be substituted.

- **Classification:** Sweet, neutral, heavy; reduces mKhris (Pitta/bile) and rLung (Wind); does not increase bad-kan significantly despite its heaviness; unique in being suitable for most constitutional types
- **Primary use:** Respiratory conditions especially dryness and inflammation; blood disorders from excess mKhris; throat and voice; harmonising adjuvant in compound medicines; *Lugs kyi rgyun skyong ba* (constitutional maintenance)
- **Source text reference:** rGyud-bzhi, Explanatory Tantra (Bshad rgyud), Chapter 20 (sweet tastes); Supplementary Tantra (Phyi-ma rgyud), Chapter 25 (respiratory medicines)

#### Convergence Analysis

Licorice root's 7-tradition documentation (every Old World tradition in the survey) makes it the most universally documented medicinal plant in this compendium — a cross-traditional signal of the highest order. Its absence from the New World traditions (BM and SA) reflects its geographic distribution, not its pharmacological value.

**What all seven traditions independently agree on:**

1. **Respiratory demulcent** — every tradition uses licorice for dry, inflamed, or irritated throat, lung, and respiratory tract. The mechanism (glycyrrhizin's anti-inflammatory and saponin-mediated mucous membrane protection) is consistent across all these uses
2. **Harmonising adjuvant** — the most striking convergence: TCM calls it the "Senior Counsellor," Avicenna calls it the "harmoniser," Dioscorides describes its moderating quality, and the Tibetan tradition includes it as one of the five essential medicines precisely because it makes all other medicines safer. This cross-traditional recognition of its formulary role is without parallel
3. **Detoxification and safety** — multiple traditions note that licorice reduces the harshness or toxicity of other medicinals, a pharmacological fact now confirmed via CYP enzyme modulation (glycyrrhizin affects CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein, changing metabolism of coadministered substances)
4. **Gastric/digestive anti-inflammatory** — universal use in ulcers, gastric pain, and digestive inflammation
5. **Anti-inflammatory systemic** — the mineralocorticoid and anti-inflammatory action of glycyrrhizin underlies what traditional texts describe as "clearing heat" or "cooling inflammation"

**Warning signal from convergence:** All traditions that use licorice long-term also warn about electrolyte imbalance with excess use — a remarkable cross-cultural pharmacovigilance signal predating biochemical understanding of pseudoaldosteronism by millennia.

### 3. Scientific Evidence

#### Active Components

| Compound | Class | Known Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Glycyrrhizin (glycyrrhizinic acid) | Triterpenoid saponin | 11β-HSD inhibition → cortisol elevation; anti-inflammatory; antiviral; hepatoprotective |
| Glycyrrhetic acid (metabolite) | Triterpenoid | Active systemic metabolite; mineralocorticoid activity; anti-ulcer |
| Glabridin | Flavonoid (isoflavone) | Antioxidant; skin-depigmenting; estrogenic; neuroprotective |
| Liquiritigenin | Chalcone → flavanone | Phytoestrogenic; anti-inflammatory; neuroprotective via ERβ |
| Isoliquiritigenin | Chalcone | Anti-tumor; anti-inflammatory; cardiac protective |
| Licochalcone A | Chalcone | Antimicrobial (H. pylori); anti-parasitic; anti-cancer |
| Glycyrrhetol | Saponin | Expectorant; mucous membrane demulcent |
| Ononin | Isoflavone | Estrogenic; anti-inflammatory |
| Hispaglabridin B | Flavonoid | Strong antioxidant; anti-melanogenic |

#### Pharmacological Pathways

**HPA axis / cortisol pathway:** Glycyrrhizin and its metabolite glycyrrhetic acid inhibit 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (11β-HSD2), the enzyme that converts cortisol to inactive cortisone in peripheral tissues. This results in: elevated local cortisol activity, anti-inflammatory effects, and at high doses — mineralocorticoid (aldosterone-like) side effects (hypertension, potassium loss, edema). This single pathway explains both the therapeutic anti-inflammatory action AND the risk profile of long-term high-dose use.

**Anti-ulcer pathway:** Deglycyrrhizinated licorice (DGL) promotes mucus secretion in the gastric lining, extends mucosal cell lifespan, and inhibits H. pylori adherence via licochalcone A. This is independent of glycyrrhizin (hence DGL form avoids mineralocorticoid risks while maintaining gastric benefit).

**Antiviral pathway:** Glycyrrhizin inhibits viral replication of SARS-CoV, HCV, HSV, HIV (at high concentrations), and influenza A via multiple mechanisms — disruption of viral envelope integrity, inhibition of viral fusion, stimulation of interferon-γ production. This explains traditional uses for infectious and "toxic" conditions.

**Estrogenic pathway:** Glabridin and liquiritigenin bind estrogen receptor β (ERβ) selectively — with higher affinity than ERα. This produces selective estrogen modulation: tissue-protective estrogenic effects (bone, neural, cardiovascular) without the proliferative risks of ERα activation. Explains traditional use in menopausal conditions.

#### Clinical & Research Evidence

- **Peptic ulcer:** Multiple RCTs demonstrate DGL extract equivalent to cimetidine (H2 blocker) for duodenal and gastric ulcer healing rate; DGL preferable for long-term use (no mineralocorticoid side effects)
- **Addison's disease / adrenal insufficiency:** Glycyrrhizin documented to raise cortisol in hypoadrenal states — clinical use as a natural cortisol-extending agent
- **Hepatitis B and C:** Intravenous glycyrrhizin (Stronger Neo-Minophagen C, used in Japan) reduces liver enzyme elevation and viral load in chronic hepatitis; oral administration less established
- **Menopausal symptoms:** Glabridin and isoliquiritigenin reduce hot flash frequency in small RCTs (ERβ-selective modulation)
- **Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS):** Multiple studies showing licorice (via anti-androgen effects) reduces testosterone in women with PCOS; useful but requires monitoring
- **Cough and respiratory:** Well-documented expectorant and anti-inflammatory effects in upper respiratory conditions; Glycyrrhizin reduces prostaglandin E₂ in bronchial tissue

#### Safety Profile

**The central concern: pseudoaldosteronism.** Long-term high-dose glycyrrhizin consumption produces a syndrome resembling excess aldosterone: sodium retention, potassium loss, hypertension, edema, and in severe cases, hypokalemic paralysis. This is dose- and duration-dependent.

- **Safe threshold:** Most adults can consume <100 mg/day glycyrrhizin indefinitely without mineralocorticoid effects. Standard Chinese formula doses (3–9 g root/day) deliver approximately 50–200 mg glycyrrhizin — at the low-risk threshold when used in formula context
- **High-risk populations:** Hypertension, heart failure, kidney disease, hypokalemia, liver cirrhosis — avoid or use only DGL preparations
- **Drug interactions:** Thiazide diuretics + licorice → additive potassium loss (dangerous). Digoxin + licorice → hypokalemia increases digoxin toxicity (serious). Corticosteroids + licorice → additive cortisol elevation. Antihypertensives → reduced efficacy
- **Pregnancy:** Avoid therapeutic doses — associated with increased cortisol exposure to fetus; observational data suggest association with preterm birth and some neurodevelopmental effects at regular dietary-level consumption

### 4. Phenomenological Record

#### Classical Practitioner Accounts

*"Gan cao is like the senior minister who does not rule but harmonises. With it, every minister performs his role better; without it, even good medicines may quarrel with each other. The practitioner who understands gan cao understands the nature of harmonisation itself."*
— Classical Chinese medical commentary, Song dynasty (attribution uncertain)

*"Yashti-madhu given in warm milk before bed soothes the internal fire without extinguishing it. The patient reports that the chest opens, the throat releases, and a gentleness enters the body that was not there before. It is the herb of reconciliation."*
— Charaka tradition commentary

*"The root of sweetness (ʿirq al-sūs) when taken in decoction produces a quenching of inner roughness — as if something abrasive had been coated with silk. The practitioner reports that the harsh quality of illness is smoothed, and the body becomes more receptive to recovery."*
— Islamic clinical description (12th century)

#### Felt Signature

Licorice's phenomenological signature is among the gentlest and most difficult to separate from the physiological background — it is experienced primarily as **the removal of roughness and the restoration of flow**.

Practitioners consistently describe:
- **Respiratory:** A softening and moistening of the throat and chest passages — as if dry, rough tissue is being lubricated from within. Practitioners with chronic dryness often report a "sigh of relief" quality
- **Systemic:** A subtle but distinct smoothing of internal tension — the anti-inflammatory action is felt as a reduction in an internal friction that one had been living with
- **Energy quality:** Licorice does not produce stimulant energy. Its effect on energy is indirect — by reducing the energy cost of low-grade inflammation and adrenal demand, it frees resources for other functions. Practitioners describe feeling "easier" rather than energised
- **GI:** In gastric inflammation, the demulcent effect is felt as a coating and settling of the stomach — a disappearance of the hollow burning or ache that characterises gastric irritation

#### State Induction Profile

Licorice is not a state-inducing substance. Its action is normalising and harmonising rather than directional. The practitioner's experience is of returning to an easier, less effortful baseline:
- Onset of demulcent effects: 5–20 minutes (mucosal contact effect)
- Onset of systemic anti-inflammatory: 1–3 hours
- Full therapeutic effect: 2–6 weeks of regular use
- The "state" induced is one of **reduced internal friction** — not a different mode of consciousness but the current mode functioning with less resistance

### 5. Traditional Energetic Classification

#### TCM Framework (Jing / Qi / Shen / Yuan)

| Tier | Classification | Organ System | Channel | Dantian |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | ◉ Qi — Circulatory | Spleen, Lung, Heart | All twelve channels | Middle Dantian (harmonising) |
| Secondary | ⊕ Yuan — Constitutional | Kidney (via Spleen support) | Ren Mai | Lower Dantian |

**TCM Taste & Temperature:** Sweet (甘), Neutral (平). Enters all twelve channels. This is the only substance in the classical materia medica documented to enter all channels — a property that underlies its harmonising role.

**TCM Action Summary:**
1. Tonifies Spleen and benefits Qi (補脾益氣) — nourishes the acquired energy source
2. Moistens Lung and stops cough (潤肺止咳) — demulcent anti-inflammatory
3. Clears heat and detoxifies (清熱解毒) — raw form; addresses hot inflammatory conditions
4. Moderates and harmonises (調和諸藥) — the unique formulary function; modifies the actions of other herbs and reduces their harshness

**Specific preparation differentiation:**
- Raw (生甘草, shēng gān cǎo): Clears heat, detoxifies, demulcent
- Honey-roasted (炙甘草, zhì gān cǎo): Tonifies Qi, warms middle, stronger Spleen tonic; used in the emergency cardiac formula (炙甘草湯, Zhì Gāncǎo Tāng)

#### Ayurvedic Framework

- **Rasa (Taste):** Madhura (sweet) primary; Tikta (bitter) secondary
- **Virya (Potency):** Shīta (cold) — notable for a tonic; cooling tonification makes it suitable for Pitta conditions where most tonics are contraindicated
- **Vipaka (Post-digestive):** Madhura (sweet) — sustaining
- **Dosha effect:** Balances all three doshas when used appropriately (tridoshahara); reduces Pitta ↓↓ · Vāta ↓ · mild Kapha ↑ (caution in excess Kapha)
- **Dhatu correspondence:** Primary action on Rasa (plasma/lymph) and Rakta (blood); secondary on Majja (nerve tissue — via neuroprotective glabridin); supports all dhatus through harmonising
- **Prana/Ojas/Tejas:** Significant Ojas builder via its sweet, heavy nature; reduces excess Tejas (inflammation); supports Prāna via respiratory demulcency

#### Tibetan Framework (Sowa Rigpa)

- **Nyon-mong addressed:** Zhe sdang (hatred/anger) arising from mKhris (bile/Pitta) excess; also moha (ignorance) when associated with inflammation
- **rLung/mKhris/Bad-kan:** Reduces mKhris ↓↓; neutral to reducing rLung; slight bad-kan increase at high doses
- **Srog effect:** Protective and sustaining; does not build Srog directly but protects it from depletion through its anti-inflammatory and harmonising action
- **Source text reference:** rGyud-bzhi, Explanatory Tantra, Chapter 20 — *"Among the sweet-tasting medicines, shing mnyenpa is supreme for cooling the fire of bile without extinguishing digestive heat"*

#### Phenomenological Tier Description (◉ Qi / ⊕ Yuan)

The ◉ Qi action of licorice is experienced as **harmonisation rather than activation**. Where ginger activates Qi circulation, licorice smooths its flow — removing the friction and inflammation that impede circulation rather than adding driving force. This is experienced as an easing, a softening, a making-way.

The ⊕ Yuan aspect operates through the Spleen → Yuan Qi relationship in TCM: the Spleen generates acquired Qi from food, which supplements the constitutional Yuan Qi stored in the Kidney. Licorice, by optimising Spleen function and reducing the inflammatory burden on all systems, allows more of what is generated to actually reach the constitutional treasury. It is not a Yuan Qi tonic in the direct sense that ginseng is — it is a Yuan Qi preserver and system-protector.

**Stacking Recommendations:**

*Synergistic stacks:*
- **Licorice + Ginger (dry)**: The fundamental warming-harmonising pair in Chinese medicine. Ginger warms the middle; licorice tonifies and harmonises. Base of dozens of classical formulas. Also anti-ulcer synergy via complementary mechanisms.
- **Licorice + Astragalus**: Qi tonification stack — licorice harmonises, Astragalus builds. Together in *Si Jun Zi Tang* (Four Gentlemen Decoction) base.
- **Licorice + Schisandra**: Kidney-Qi consolidation stack; particularly for adrenal support and stress resilience.
- **Licorice + Curcumin**: Synergistic anti-inflammatory — glycyrrhizin extends curcumin's half-life via CYP inhibition while also contributing its own NF-κB inhibitory activity.
- **DGL + Glutamine + Aloe**: Gastric lining repair stack for leaky gut or ulcerative conditions.

*Monitor/caution:*
- Licorice + Diuretics: Additive potassium loss
- Licorice + Digoxin: Dangerous potassium depletion
- Licorice + Antihypertensives: Reduced efficacy
- Licorice + Corticosteroids: Additive cortisol effects

**Cycling Protocol:**

- Standard formula use (3–9 g/day as adjuvant): Continuous use is typical and safe for most adults
- Higher dose therapeutic use (>9 g/day solo): 4–6 week cycles maximum; 2+ week rest with blood pressure and potassium monitoring
- DGL preparations: Can be used continuously at standard doses without cycling concern (mineralocorticoid compounds removed)
- Blood pressure monitoring: Any therapeutic course >4 weeks warrants regular blood pressure checks

### 7. Contemplative & Practice Integration

#### TCM / Taoist Tradition

In the Taoist formulation of the Four Gentleman Decoction (Si Jun Zi Tang — the foundational Qi tonic), licorice holds the role of *the fourth gentleman* — the one who harmonises the other three (ginseng, atractylodes, poria). This formulary metaphor is the central Taoist insight about licorice: **its value is relational**. It makes everything else in a formula work better, with less conflict, more completely.

In the Taoist practice tradition, this translates to licorice's role as a *preparation of ground* — it is used not for direct benefit but to create the conditions in which other substances can work and in which the practitioner's own cultivation efforts can be received. It is taken as part of tonic formula bases when the practitioner is beginning a new practice period — clearing the ground, smoothing the channels, making the body receptive.

#### Ayurvedic / Yoga Tradition

In the Āyurvedic-yoga integration, *yaṣṭimadhu* holds a specific place in practices oriented toward the voice, the *viśuddha* (throat) chakra, and the prāṇa vaha srotas (respiratory energy channels). Teachers, chanters, and practitioners using mantra extensively take licorice as standard maintenance support for the throat and vocal apparatus.

More broadly, its *medhya rasayana* (mind-clarifying tonic) property places it in protocols for intensive study, scriptural memorisation, and sustained intellectual-contemplative work. The Charaka Samhita specifically recommends yaṣṭimadhu with milk and honey for Brahmacharya practitioners (those maintaining celibacy and dedicating energy to spiritual practice) — addressing both the mental clarity required and the dryness that can arise from intense heat-generating practices.

#### Islamic / Sufi Tradition

In classical Islamic medicine (which informed Sufi healing practices), *ʿirq al-sūs* functions within the framework of *ilāj bi'l-zid* (treatment by opposites) — it is a moderating, tempering medicine. The Sufi healing tradition uses it in preparations for dhikr (remembrance practice) participants who experience heat, throat dryness, or inner turbulence from intense practice.

More broadly, it appears in the *faṣd* (phlebotomy) recovery protocols and in preparations for practitioners undertaking periods of intense activity or fasting — the goal being to sustain the body's internal harmony during conditions that naturally disturb it.

#### Tibetan / Vajrayana Tradition

The Tibetan emphasis on licorice is primarily its role as one of the *five essential medicines* — substances whose universal applicability makes them foundational. Shing mnyenpa is taken regularly by practitioners in cold-climate environments where dryness and blood-heat (from altitude and thin, dry air) are combined challenges.

In the Vajrayana context, licorice appears in preparations for long retreat periods — specifically for practitioners doing practices that generate significant inner heat (tummo) where the risk of imbalance between heating and cooling forces is significant. Licorice is used as a cooling harmoniser to prevent the drying and inflammatory consequences of sustained heat practices.

### 8. Contraindications

| Category | Details |
|---|---|
| **Drug interactions** | Thiazide and loop diuretics: additive hypokalemia (dangerous). Digoxin: hypokalemia amplifies digoxin cardiotoxicity (serious — avoid combination). Antihypertensives (all classes): glycyrrhizin-induced sodium retention reduces drug efficacy. Corticosteroids: additive cortisol effects; monitor for adrenal suppression on withdrawal. Warfarin: variable INR effects via CYP modulation — monitor. Spironolactone: antagonistic (spiro blocks aldosterone; licorice mimics it). |
| **Medical conditions** | Hypertension: glycyrrhizin contraindicated; DGL acceptable. Cardiac failure or edema: avoid glycyrrhizin-containing preparations. Hypokalemia: licorice will worsen. Kidney disease: impaired potassium handling increases risk. Liver cirrhosis: complicated effects on aldosterone metabolism; specialist guidance needed. |
| **Constitutional (TCM/Ayurvedic)** | Damp-heat accumulation: licorice's sweet, tonifying nature may worsen phlegm-damp congestion — use raw form only and with damp-clearing herbs. Severe Kapha excess (obesity, hypothyroid, chronic edema): avoid or use very small doses as adjuvant only. Severe Yin deficiency with fire: its neutral-cool nature is acceptable but warming herbs in the same formula must be monitored. |
| **Pregnancy / Lactation** | Avoid regular therapeutic doses — epidemiological evidence suggests association between licorice consumption and reduced gestational length and possible cognitive effects in offspring (via fetal cortisol exposure from 11β-HSD2 inhibition). Small amounts as adjuvant in formula are likely acceptable but caution is warranted. Lactation: appears safe in small formula doses; avoid as primary therapeutic. |
| **Phenomenological** | Licorice is occasionally "too harmonising" — it can moderate the effect of a strong directional herb that is needed for a specific forceful action. In treatment of acute severe conditions (severe cold invasion, acute pain, acute infection), licorice as the sole herb delays action. In emergency high-dose ginseng or aconite formulas, licorice is reduced or omitted when maximal force is required. |
| **Sourcing / Quality** | Species distinction: *G. uralensis* (Chinese medicine standard); *G. glabra* (Mediterranean standard); *G. inflata* (Xinjiang, used in TCM as inferior substitute). HPLC standard: ≥2% glycyrrhizic acid (Chinese Pharmacopoeia). Major adulteration: *Abrus precatorius* root (toxic — causes fatal poisoning); demand HPLC authentication. Quality licorice is firm, yellowish-white internally, distinctly sweet with slight bitter finish. Avoid powders without species authentication. |
