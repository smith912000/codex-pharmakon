### Origin & Tradition

*Nigella arvensis* is the wild, weedy nigella of arable fields and dry open ground from the Mediterranean across the Aegean into western and central Asia — a slender annual with finely dissected leaves and pale blue flowers, its ripe capsules holding small, black, angular, sharply aromatic seeds. It is the field cousin of the cultivated black-cumin, *Nigella sativa*, and this kinship is the single most important fact about it and the single most abused one: almost everything "known" pharmacologically about nigella seed comes from *N. sativa*, and applying it wholesale to *N. arvensis* is precisely the error this entry exists to prevent. The two are distinct species; *N. arvensis* is itself a taxonomically complex aggregate, and its Aegean populations form a well-studied species complex (see Scientific Correlation). What the wild plant shares with its cultivated relative is a broad family resemblance in seed chemistry and a parallel folk role; what it does not share is a comparable body of evidence.

**Egyptian (EP).** Aromatic black nigella seed belongs to the Egyptian kitchen-and-pharmacy overlap — a pungent condiment and a carminative, warming seed used for the belly and the "wind." Whether Ebers prescriptions naming nigella refer to the cultivated or the wild form cannot be resolved, and the two would have been used interchangeably where both grew. **Greek (DMM).** Dioscorides describes *melanthion* (nigella), the black pungent seed, as a carminative and digestive, a diuretic and emmenagogue, an anthelmintic, and — applied — a resolvent for catarrh and skin complaints, with an explicit warning that the seed in quantity is dangerous. The wild field nigella and the cultivated seed sit under this one description in the Greek pharmacy.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM).** In the Arabic-Persian tradition *shuniz* (nigella, kalonji) is hot and dry, a powerful deobstruent, carminative, emmenagogue and anthelmintic, opening obstructions of the liver and spleen, promoting the menses, expelling wind and worms, and used both internally and as an errhine/resolvent. Again the wild seed inherits the cultivated seed's doctrine.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The doctrine is thermal and kinetic: a hot, dry, pungent seed that *moves* — moves wind out of the gut (carminative), moves the menses (emmenagogue), moves worms and mucus out of the body. "Pungent-aromatic and warming" is the humoral shorthand for a seed whose crushed odour is sharp and whose effect is to disperse and expel. Mapped onto real chemistry, the pungency and warming quality point to a **volatile essential oil** — in the *sativa* type dominated by the quinone **thymoquinone** and monoterpenes such as p-cymene — while the deobstruent/expelling actions point additionally to **saponins** (nigella seeds are saponin-bearing) and fixed oils. The doctrine reads the essential oil correctly as the "moving" principle. The honest qualification is that the *quantitative* essential-oil and thymoquinone profile that underwrites the doctrine has been measured overwhelmingly in *N. sativa*; in *N. arvensis* the same *classes* are present but the *amounts and dominant constituents may differ*, and the tradition's transfer of virtue from cultivated to wild seed is an assumption, not a measurement.

### Active Components

- **Essential (volatile) oil** — the pungent-aromatic fraction; in *Nigella* generally including thymoquinone, p-cymene, thymol/carvacrol-type phenolics and monoterpenes. Present in *N. arvensis* but with a species-specific profile not equivalent to *N. sativa*. [USER: verify *arvensis*-specific oil composition before quoting numbers.]
- **Thymoquinone** — the signature bioactive quinone of the genus; abundant and well-studied in *N. sativa*, of unquantified level in *N. arvensis*. Do not assume *sativa* concentrations.
- **Saponins (triterpenoid/steroidal)** — deobstruent, expectorant and irritant constituents typical of nigella seed; relevant to the emmenagogue and "moving" actions.
- **Fixed (fatty) oil** — the bulk seed oil, rich in unsaturated fatty acids; the nutritive-carrier fraction.
- **Alkaloids and flavonoids** — minor constituents reported across the genus.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. **Carminative / digestive (primary).** The warming pungent seed to disperse gut wind, ease colic and stimulate digestion — the most robust and lowest-risk traditional use.
2. **Emmenagogue (traditional, cautioned).** Used to "open the courses"; the basis of the pregnancy contraindication below.
3. **Anthelmintic / vermifuge.** Expelling intestinal worms — a classical use across the genus.
4. **Resolvent / errhine for catarrh.** Crushed seed as a warming resolvent for cold mucous conditions.
5. **Condiment.** Culinary seasoning, overlapping with the cultivated black-cumin's kitchen role.

### Scientific Correlation

The controlling honesty of this entry: the pharmacology is *sativa*-heavy, *arvensis*-sparse, and the two must not be conflated.

- **Thymoquinone is a real, multi-target molecule — in *N. sativa*.** According to PubMed, Fic et al. (2026, *Nutrients*) review thymoquinone — "the main bioactive constituent" of *Nigella sativa* — as an anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and lipid-modulating agent acting on NF-κB, MAPK and COX-2 pathways [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18091480). This validates the *class* of chemistry behind nigella's warming/resolvent doctrine, **but the study is on *N. sativa*, not *N. arvensis*.** Grade for *arvensis*: **indirect / congeneric only.**
- ***N. arvensis* is a genuine, distinct taxon — established botanically, not pharmacologically.** According to PubMed, Jian et al. (2023, *Molecular Phylogenetics and Evolution*) resolve the *Nigella arvensis* complex as a real, actively diversifying species group in the Aegean [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ympev.2023.107908). This is the strongest *arvensis*-specific literature retrieved — and it is systematics, not therapeutics, which is itself the honest finding: **the wild species is far better characterised as a plant than as a drug.**
- **Carminative/essential-oil rationale.** The carminative and digestive uses are mechanistically plausible for any thymoquinone/monoterpene-bearing aromatic seed, but no controlled trial establishes efficacy for *N. arvensis*. Grade: **plausible, unproven for this species.**
- **Overall grade: data-sparse.** *N. arvensis* has essentially no dedicated clinical pharmacology. Every efficacy statement here is either (a) traditional, or (b) borrowed from *N. sativa* and explicitly flagged as such. No claim of proven therapeutic effect for *N. arvensis* is warranted.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM / Galenic humoral degree:** Hot and dry (commonly hot in the third degree for nigella seed) — a pungent, dispersing, expelling carminative-emmenagogue.
- **Avicenna / Unani mapping:** *shuniz* — hot-dry; kasir-e-riyah (carminative), mudirr-e-hayd (emmenagogue), deobstruent and anthelmintic.
- **Kenetica Qi tier:** ◉ **Qi** — on the warming-drying, moving/dispersing axis; a "wind-mover" and opener, with the emmenagogue caution built in.

### Contraindications

- **⚠️ Pregnancy — avoid.** Emmenagogue reputation across the genus; nigella seed is traditionally contraindicated in pregnancy. Do not use medicinally while pregnant. [USER: verify.]
- **Quantity toxicity.** The classical sources warn that nigella seed in large amount is harmful; medicinal (not culinary) quantities should be conservative and short.
- **Do not import *N. sativa* dosing or efficacy.** Treating *arvensis* as interchangeable with cultivated black-cumin for dose or effect is unsupported.
- **Allergy / sensitisation.** Possible seed-oil sensitivity; patch-test topical oil.
- **Drug interactions (theoretical, thymoquinone-class).** By analogy with *N. sativa*, potential additive effects on blood glucose, blood pressure and coagulation cannot be excluded; caution with relevant medications. [USER: verify.]
