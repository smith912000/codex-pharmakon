# Mountain Lovage — Mutellina, Alpine Lovage, Muttern, Adelgras

## *Ligusticum mutellina* (L.) Crantz (syn. *Mutellina purpurea* (Poll.) Reduron, Charpin & Pimenov) | Family: Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, BM
**Energetic Classification:** Warm and dry (Galenic); aromatic, carminative, mildly stimulant
**Training Phase:** Kindling / Digestive-Warming (Kenetica) — an aromatic that wakes a cold, sluggish gut

---

### Origin & Tradition

Mountain lovage is a small, purple-flushed umbellifer of high alpine pastures — the herb the cattle seek out on the summer meadows, and the herb the herdsmen have long chewed and brewed after a heavy meal. It belongs to *Ligusticum*, the aromatic lovage genus whose members (culinary lovage, Chinese chuanxiong, osha) share a warm, celery-and-angelica pungency and a chemistry built on **phthalides**. As a wild forage and folk digestive it sits closer to the kitchen and the pasture than to the physician's dispensary, and its documentary record is correspondingly thin — a point this entry states plainly rather than papering over.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (AT):** Aromatic umbelliferous seeds and roots — the *Ligusticum/Levisticum/Apium* cluster — appear across the ancient Near-Eastern seed-drug tradition as warming carminatives and digestives. The alpine species itself is a European mountain plant, so any Assyrian link is generic to the aromatic-umbellifer class, not to *L. mutellina* specifically. Stated honestly: this is family-level, not species-level, attestation.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** *De Materia Medica* treats the lovage/*ligustikon* group as warming, wind-dispelling roots and seeds that aid digestion, provoke urine, and warm a cold stomach. Mountain lovage is the alpine folk-heir of this Dioscoridean "ligustikon" reputation rather than a plant Dioscorides named individually.

**rGyud-bzhi / Tibetan (BM):** Himalayan and Tibetan medicine makes heavy use of aromatic high-altitude *Ligusticum* and allied umbellifers as warming, blood-moving, digestive and headache remedies (paralleling the Chinese use of chuanxiong). *L. mutellina* is the European analogue within this same functional class.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Mountain lovage is the encyclopaedia's **pasture-aromatic** — the plant that thrives where the air is thin and cold, and whose warming pungency answers that same cold in the body. Its doctrinal role is small and honest: an aromatic kindling herb, the alpine cousin of grander *Ligusticum* medicines, valued for what the whole genus shares (phthalide-driven antispasmodic, carminative, mildly sedative action) rather than for any unique, documented virtue of its own.

### Active Components

- **Phthalides** — the signature *Ligusticum* constituent class (e.g. ligustilide, butylidenephthalide, senkyunolides in related species); responsible for the genus's antispasmodic, spasmolytic, and mild sedative actions.
- **Essential oil** — terpenes and phthalides giving the warm, celery-angelica aroma; the basis of its carminative use.
- **Coumarins / furanocoumarins** — as is common in Apiaceae (relevant to the photosensitivity caution below).
- Species-specific phytochemical data for *L. mutellina* itself are sparse; most detailed chemistry cited here is from congeneric *Ligusticum* species and is applied by reasonable genus-level inference, not direct measurement.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica, mountain lovage is a minor **Kindling / Digestive-Warming** aromatic: used where digestion is cold, sluggish, and gassy — bloating, cramp, and poor appetite after heavy or cold food. As a warm carminative and mild antispasmodic it relaxes gut smooth muscle and disperses "wind," and, by genus analogy, may lend a mild calming edge. It is a supporting, food-adjacent herb — a bitter-aromatic digestive tea or seasoning — not a headline remedy. Where stronger, better-documented action is needed the practitioner is directed to its studied relatives (chuanxiong, osha) rather than to *mutellina* alone.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, direct clinical or phytochemical research on *Ligusticum mutellina* is essentially absent; the evidence is at the level of the **genus and its phthalides**. A systematic review of *Ligusticum chuanxiong* by Chen et al. identifies phthalides and alkaloids as the main bioactive constituents underlying the genus's anti-spasmodic, blood-vessel-protective, anti-ischaemic, and related pharmacological activities ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fct.2018.02.050)). León et al. isolated dimeric and monomeric phthalides (Z-ligustilide, Z-butylidenephthalide, tokinolide B, senkyunolides) from *Ligusticum porteri* (osha) and demonstrated measurable **sedative and spasmolytic activity** in vivo and in vitro, correlating with the herb's traditional digestive and calmative uses ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2010.534735)). Shao et al. showed the phthalide senkyunolide A has anti-inflammatory and antispasmodic-associated effects via NLRP3 inhibition ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/13880209.2022.2042327)).

**Evidence grade: TRADITIONAL (species-level data-sparse).** There is good pharmacological evidence that *Ligusticum* phthalides are antispasmodic and mildly sedative, which supports the *plausibility* of the traditional digestive/carminative use of mountain lovage — but there is essentially **no direct clinical or controlled evidence for *L. mutellina* itself**. Its use here is graded traditional, supported by genus-level chemistry, and should not be overstated.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** warm and dry (aromatic lovage class); carminative, stomachic, diuretic, wind-dispelling.
- **Tibetan (rGyud-bzhi / BM):** warming, blood-moving, digestive; grouped with aromatic high-altitude umbellifers.
- **TCM (by analogy to chuanxiong):** acrid, warm; moves qi and blood, dispels wind — attribution belongs properly to *L. chuanxiong*, applied to *mutellina* only by genus analogy, not classical listing.
- **Ayurveda:** not a classical dravya; falls loosely with warming aromatic (*ushna*, pungent/*katu*) digestives.

### Contraindications

⚠️ **Cautions (moderate; data-sparse species).**

- **Pregnancy:** aromatic, blood-moving *Ligusticum* species are traditionally treated as emmenagogue/uterine-active; **avoid in pregnancy** as a precaution.
- **Photosensitivity:** as an Apiaceae herb it may contain furanocoumarins; excessive intake or skin contact plus sun exposure could provoke phototoxic reactions. Use culinary/moderate amounts.
- **Apiaceae allergy:** avoid in those allergic to celery, carrot, fennel, or other umbellifers (cross-reactivity).
- **Correct identification is essential.** The Apiaceae family contains deadly look-alikes (hemlock, water hemlock). Only use material of confirmed identity from a reliable source; never wild-harvest an umbellifer on appearance alone.
- Not a substitute for medical care; no established therapeutic dosing.
