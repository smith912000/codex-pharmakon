## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Rosa canina* L. (Dog Rose)
**Related species:** *Rosa rugosa* Thunb. (Wrinkled Rose / Japanese Rose — highest vitamin C content); *Rosa eglanteria* Mill. (Sweetbriar); multiple *Rosa* spp. used for rosehips
**Family:** Rosaceae

**Common Names:**
- English: Dog Rose (plant), Rosehip (fruit), Rose Brier, Wild Rose
- German: *Hagebutte* (rosehip), *Hundsrose* ("dog rose"), *Heckenrose* (hedge rose)
- French: *Églantier* (plant), *Cynorrhodon* (fruit)
- Latin: *Rosa canina* — "dog rose"; *canina* = of dogs; possibly because the roots were used to treat rabid dog bites (ancient claim)
- Arabic: *Ward* (وَرد) — "rose" (the flower); *Warda barriyya* (wild rose)
- Sanskrit: *Shatapatri* — related roses; rose in Ayurveda
- Chinese: *Jin Ying Zi* (金樱子) — *Rosa laevigata* (Cherokee rose) is TCM standard; *R. canina* secondary

**Etymology:**
*Rosa* — ancient Latin name for rose; possibly from Greek *rhodon* (rose) or from a pre-Indo-European root. *canina* = "of/like a dog" — the "dog" epithet in plant names traditionally indicated inferiority, wildness, or coarseness compared to cultivated relatives (cf. *Cynodon*, *Cynoglossum*). The rosehip (*Hagebutte* in German; *cynorrhodon* in French) was regarded as the "common people's rose" — available everywhere, without the prestige of cultivated roses.

**The WWII Vitamin C Emergency:**
During World War II, when citrus fruit imports to Britain were severely disrupted, the UK Ministry of Food organized a national rosehip harvest — primarily schoolchildren and volunteers collecting wild rosehips across the British countryside. The collected hips were processed into **rosehip syrup** (National Rose Hip Syrup) and distributed free through health clinics as vitamin C for children. This represents one of the most direct pharmacological applications of a wild plant as an emergency micronutrient intervention in modern Western medical history. Approximately 450 tonnes of rosehips were harvested annually during peak years.

**Morphology:**
Deciduous shrub; 1–3 m; arching thorny canes; pinnate leaves (5–7 leaflets); pale pink 5-petaled flowers; autumn fruit = hip (accessory fruit, not true fruit) — oval or elongated; bright red-orange; containing bony achenes with irritant hairs (used historically as itching powder).

**Parts Used:**
- Hips (pericarp — fleshy layer around seeds): vitamin C; flavonoids; carotenoids
- Seeds: rosehip seed oil (fatty acids; carotenoids)
- Petals: traditional; rose water; minor medicinal
- CRITICAL: Remove achene hairs before consumption — irritant to mucous membranes

**The Universal Rose:**
*Rosa* species achieve cross-traditional presence because the genus has species native to virtually every temperate region of the Northern Hemisphere — Eurasia, North Africa, North America. Every major Old World culture encountered wild roses in their local flora and made use of them for wounds (astringent tannins), nutrition (vitamin C before vitamin C was chemically identified), and beauty (flower). The rosehip vitamin C content was exploited empirically across traditions without chemical knowledge of the mechanism.

**Primary Convergence Domains:**

1. **Vitamin C (anti-scorbutic / nutritional)** — all traditions (empirical; mechanism identified 1932); **Level A** (nutritional established)
2. **Anti-inflammatory / anti-arthritic** — modern; galactolipid GOPO mechanism; **Level A (OA meta-analysis)**

4. **Antioxidant** — modern; carotenoid + flavonoid; Level B
5. **Wound healing** — DMM, CM, SS; astringent + vitamin C collagen synthesis

**The GOPO Discovery:**
The anti-inflammatory mechanism of rosehip was unexplained until the identification of **GOPO (galactolipid: (2S)-1,2-di-O-[(9Z,12Z,15Z)-octadecatrienoyl]-3-O-β-D-galactopyranosyl-sn-glycerol)** from *Rosa canina* hips in the early 2000s. GOPO demonstrated potent inhibition of human chemotaxis (migration of neutrophils and monocytes toward inflammation sites) in vitro and in vivo. Clinical trials followed, showing significant OA symptom reduction. GOPO is extracted in a specific preparation process — standard hip powder may lose GOPO during processing; specialized preparations (LitoZin®, HYBEN VITAL®) standardize for GOPO content.

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Vitamin C

**1. Ascorbic Acid — 400–3,000 mg/100 g dry hip (varietal/seasonal)**
- *Rosa rugosa* hips: highest (~3,000 mg/100 g = 30× citrus)
- *Rosa canina* hips: 400–800 mg/100 g (still 4–8× citrus)
- **Anti-scorbutic:** Established nutritional requirement (60–90 mg/day RDA)
- **Collagen synthesis:** Required cofactor for prolyl hydroxylase → hydroxyproline → collagen crosslinking
- **Immune function:** Neutrophil accumulation at infection sites; antioxidant
- Heat-labile: destroyed by boiling; cold preparation optimal for vitamin C preservation

### Carotenoids

**2. β-Carotene (pro-vitamin A), Lycopene, Rubixanthin, Zeaxanthin** — 10–60 mg/100 g dry hip
- Antioxidant; anti-inflammatory
- Lycopene in rosehip: one of highest known plant sources

**3. GOPO (Galactolipid)** — trace but pharmacologically active

- **COX-independent mechanism:** Complements NSAID therapy without COX side effects
- Anti-inflammatory mechanism independent of prostaglandins

### Flavonoids / Phenolics

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Osteoarthritis (Rosehip Powder):**
- **Warholm et al. 2003** (*Scandinavian Journal of Rheumatology*): RCT; *R. canina* powder 5 g/day vs. placebo; n=94; significant WOMAC pain and stiffness improvement
- **Rein et al. 2004** (*Phytomedicine*): RCT; 5 g daily rosehip powder; significant OA improvement; reduced NSAID consumption
- **Christensen et al. 2008** (*Osteoarthritis and Cartilage*, meta-analysis): 3 RCTs; pooled analysis; 21.7% reduction in pain scores (NRS) vs. placebo; significant
- **Evidence Grade: Level A** (meta-analysis of multiple RCTs)

**Vitamin C (Nutritional):**
- Well-established nutritional science; anti-scorbutic; RDA-supported
- **Evidence Grade: Level A**

**Rheumatoid Arthritis:**
- Smaller trials; less evidence than for OA
- **Evidence Grade: Level C**

**Topical (Seed Oil):**
- Open-label studies for scar reduction and skin aging; no large RCTs
- **Evidence Grade: Level C**

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The WWII Rosehip Harvest:**
The British national rosehip harvest of 1941–1945 created a fascinating intersection of wartime necessity, folk pharmacology, and organized public health. The Ministry of Food's call for rosehip collection — translated into mass participation by women, children, and volunteers across rural Britain — was based on the recently established knowledge that rosehips contain far more vitamin C than citrus fruit. The program succeeded: rosehip syrup was widely distributed and may have significantly contributed to maintaining child vitamin C status during years of severe citrus restriction. The collective memory of this harvest program persists in British rural culture — "rosehip picking time" retained social meaning for a generation.

**The Itching Powder:**
The achene hairs inside rosehips (irritant to mucous membranes) were traditionally collected by country children and used as "itching powder" — placed down a classmate's neck or in their clothing. The hairs (silicon-tipped, similar to glass fiber) cause mechanical irritation when lodged in skin or mucous membranes. This schoolyard use encodes an accidental discovery: while eating rosehips (the fleshy pericarp), the seeds and their hairs cause GI irritation. Proper preparation removes the seeds and hairs — an important practical processing note that traditional herbalists knew and modern preparations must observe.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**OA (Clinical Trial Dose):**
- Rosehip powder (standardized): 5 g daily in divided doses; 3 months minimum trial
- LitoZin®, HYBEN VITAL® (GOPO-standardized preparations)
- Available as capsules, powder for mixing in food/drink

**Vitamin C Supplement:**
- Rosehip-derived vitamin C: supplement form; doses equivalent to synthetic ascorbic acid

**Tea / Syrup:**
- Fresh or dried rosehips: 2–4 tsp (10–15 g) per 500 mL water; gently simmer 20 minutes; strain carefully through fine cloth (removes seed hairs)
- Traditional rosehip syrup: concentrated decoction + sugar; refrigerate; 1–2 tsp daily
- **Vitamin C is heat-labile** — long boiling destroys most; prefer gentle simmer + short steep

**CRITICAL Processing:**
- **Always remove seeds and their irritant hairs** before oral use
- Strain through fine cloth or coffee filter — achene hairs pass through coarse strainers
- Commercial preparations (standardized powders, capsules) have seeds removed

### Safety Profile

**Excellent general safety.**

**Achene Hairs:**
- Irritant mechanical effect if seeds not removed; GI irritation; urticaria topically
- All commercial preparations are properly processed

**Vitamin C Dose:**
- High doses (>2 g/day supplemental C) → osmotic diarrhea; GI irritation; oxalate kidney stones in susceptible individuals
- Rosehip food doses: safe; therapeutic supplement doses: monitor for GI effects

**Drug Interactions:**
- **Warfarin:** Vitamin C at high doses may reduce warfarin effect (pro-coagulant through improved collagen synthesis); monitor INR
- **Iron absorption:** Vitamin C enhances non-heme iron absorption; beneficial for iron deficiency but monitor in hemochromatosis
- **Statins:** Very minor interaction

**Contraindications:**
- Hemochromatosis: high vitamin C increases iron absorption → avoid high doses
- History of kidney stones (oxalate): high-dose vitamin C increases urinary oxalate
