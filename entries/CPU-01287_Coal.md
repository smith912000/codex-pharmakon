# Coal — rdo.sol (Dhosol; Clark: "rdo.sol: Coal")
## *A black stone medicine of the rGyud-bzhi, rendered "Coal" by Clark, whose commentary says it is "similar to charcoal and is used as such"* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Coal is an item in the materia medica of the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines"), in Clark's English version of the *Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). The name list `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` gives it as "rdo.sol", Clark 1995 (line 844), translator's identification "Coal" (the CSV's class guess there reads "plant"; the text places it among the stone medicines, below), and `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 149 has "Dhosol", class mineral, identification marked uncertain. "Dhosol" is the spelling of the 2011 Men-Tsee-Khang translation, whose index lists "Dhosol 199" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 7331).

*The place of the entry.* Clark's chapter opens (line 794) by saying that "the power of medicines is twofold: (a) the power of taste and (b) the natural power [of the substance]" and that this is taught "according to general and specific sections". The specific section divides the substances into eight groups: "(1) precious [metal] medicines (2) stone medicines (3) earth medicines (4) tree medicines (5) resins (6) plants (7) herbs (8) animal products" (line 802). Coal is listed in group (2), "The Powers of Stone Medicines" (heading at line 816), between *ba.bla* (yellow orpiment) and *ba.nu* (carbonate of chalk) at line 844.

*What the text says (Clark, line 844).* "rdo.sol: Coal: [has a black color similar to charcoal and is used as such. It] dissolves stones [gall stones, kidney stones, etc.] and constricts the mouths of the channels." Clark explains the typography in his introduction (line 82): interpolations "in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". So the root-text statement is that coal dissolves stones and constricts the mouths of the channels; the description ("a black color similar to charcoal and is used as such") and the examples of stones (gall stones, kidney stones) come from the commentaries.

*The 2011 Men-Tsee-Khang version.* In the same chapter's stone section, the 2011 translation says: "Dhosol dissolves calculi and stops bleeding" (line 3356, the passage that opens "To reveal the efficacy of stone medicines"). The two translations agree on the dissolving of calculi (stones). They differ in the second action ("constricts the mouths of the channels" in Clark; "stops bleeding" in Men-Tsee-Khang); the sources retrieved do not say whether these are two renderings of one Tibetan phrase, and no attempt is made here to decide.

*Neighbouring items for comparison.* Clark's list gives no other stone medicine the action "dissolves stones". The next group, earth medicines, gives *ze.tsha* (saltpetre, mirabilite) the actions "dissolves stones [for example in the kidneys, gall bladder, etc.], removes calculi and stone tumors" (line 860). Yellow ochre, *rdo.mkhris*, also "constrict[s] the mouths of the channels" (line 840). The text does not rank these against coal.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, coal is a black or dark-brown sedimentary rock formed from compressed plant matter, composed mostly of carbon with hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and sulfur, and mineral ash. Coal commonly contains small amounts of trace elements such as arsenic and mercury, which vary from seam to seam. Charcoal, which the commentary compares it with, is a different material, made by burning wood without air. The Tibetan text itself does not say which of the two is meant; Clark's gloss "Coal" is the translator's identification and the commentary's "used as such" (as charcoal) leaves the point open. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records a black stone medicine said to dissolve stones in the body and to constrict the mouths of the channels (or stop bleeding in the 2011 rendering). The Codex does not recommend swallowing coal or any coal-derived material. There is no evidence that coal dissolves stones, and coal can carry toxic trace elements. Anyone with stones, bleeding or urinary symptoms should be seen by a clinician.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text read gives no preparation for coal, no instruction on how it is processed, burned or ground, no vehicle, and no dose, in either translation. [USER: verify] No dose is given in the sources retrieved. The only practical note is the commentary's "used as such", that is, used like charcoal.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Activated charcoal, a processed form of carbon, adsorbs some poisons and is used in emergency medicine for certain overdoses; ordinary coal is not activated charcoal and is not used as a medicine. Coal dust is a lung hazard (coal workers' pneumoconiosis). Burning coal releases sulfur oxides and carbon monoxide. Nothing known of coal supports dissolving kidney or gall stones; stones of that kind are managed in clinical medicine by other means. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** The chapter's frame is the power of taste and the natural power of the substance (line 794 and following). For coal the text prints no taste, no post-digestive taste and no named power. The only qualities printed are its colour ("black", in the commentary) and its actions: dissolves stones and constricts the mouths of the channels (Clark), or dissolves calculi and stops bleeding (Men-Tsee-Khang). The text does not say which of the three humours (wind, bile, phlegm) it treats.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* The chapter treats coal as a stone medicine with two stated actions on the body and prints no cultivation framing for it. Clark's introduction (line 66) singles out precious gems and metals as "the most powerful medicinal substances used in Tibetan medicine" and says certain flowers and "types of calcite and mineral exudate" are renowned in "essence-extraction" compounds for meditators (introduction, line 69); coal is not among the items named there, and no passage read ties coal to essence extraction (*bcud len*), to practice, or to the protection against spirits that the same introduction attributes to the gems.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. Coal is a black stone medicine in the list of stone medicines, with two plain actions and no cultivation role. The Codex does not recommend taking it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Toxicity:** coal can contain arsenic, mercury and other trace elements, and coal dust and fumes are harmful; do not swallow, grind or burn it as a medicine.
- **Dust and smoke:** coal dust damages the lungs, and burning coal in closed rooms produces carbon monoxide.
- **Stones and bleeding:** the actions named in the text (dissolving stones, stopping bleeding) describe conditions that need clinical assessment; a stone that blocks the urinary tract or a bleed that does not stop is an emergency.
- **Pregnancy and children:** unsuitable.
- **Identification:** "Coal" is the translator's identification; the commentary compares it to charcoal, and the sources retrieved do not settle the point.
