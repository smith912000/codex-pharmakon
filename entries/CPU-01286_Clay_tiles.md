# Clay tiles (ostraka) — Ostraka (OSTRAKA), "Clay Tiles"
## *Well-burnt clay tiles from furnaces, rubbed on with vinegar for itching, pustules and gout and mixed with wax ointment for glandular swellings; the translator's heading reads only "Clay Tiles"* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Clay tiles are chapter 178 of Book Five of the *De Materia Medica*, in the run of earths under the page heading "METALLIC STONES", headed OSTRAKA (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10754-10757; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` carries it as 5-178, line 10754, translator's identification "Clay Tiles", class mineral; `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 903 has "Ostraka", mineral, identification marked uncertain; the book's page index prints "Clay Tiles 828"). Unlike many chapters of this section, the translator gives no "SUGGESTED" line and no scientific gloss, only the two words "Clay Tiles" (line 10756).

The chapter is two sentences (line 10757): "The tiles in furnaces that are very burnt are crustforming: as a result (rubbed on with vinegar) they heal itching and pustules breaking out, and are good for gout. Used with wax ointments they dissolve scrofulous tumours [glandular swelling] and goitres."

So the text names a single material (tiles from furnaces, "very burnt"), gives a single quality ("crustforming", that is, scab-forming, as the translator prints the word), two ways of use (rubbed on with vinegar; mixed into wax ointments), and its indications: itching, eruptions of pustules, gout, glandular swellings and goitres. It gives no instruction for choosing, preparing or burning the tiles (they are already burnt by the furnace), no place of origin and no dose. The text does not say how the tile is reduced to a form that can be rubbed on or mixed into ointment; whether it was ground to powder is not stated and is an inference only. [USER: verify]

*The chapter after it.* The next chapter, 5-179 GE KAMINON, translator's heading "Burnt Red Earth" (lines 10762-10764), says: "Well-burnt red earth from furnaces has the same qualities as the tiles [above]." It is a separate entry in the text, a second furnace-burnt clay with the tiles as its standard.

*The earths in general.* The first chapter of the run of earths, 5-170 (GE, "Earth", line 10711), sets a general rule: "All earth that falls within the scope of medicinal use is supremely useful for cooling, and for closing the pores." The chapter on Melia earth, 5-180, closes with a rule for the whole class: "Earth of any type should be chosen which is without stones, new, soft, brittle, and easily dissolved when it touches moisture" (line 10765). Burnt clay tiles do not meet that rule on its face (they are hard and burnt); the text does not comment, and no reconciliation is offered here.

*Related wording elsewhere.* The word *ostrakon* and its relatives appear in other chapters with a different object: cadmia of the kind *ostracitis* is "slender and mostly black, with an earthy or baked clay outside" (5-84, line 10222), and an "Ostracean jar (especially an Athenian)" is the vessel in which the cerussa is roasted (5-103, line 10362). Those are other things (a cadmia, a pot). They are named only so the reader does not confuse them with the tile of 5-178; the fossil shell *ostrakites* (5-165) is another substance again and has its own entry.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, fired clay and ceramic are mainly aluminium silicates produced by heating clay, with iron oxides giving the red colour in many clays. Tiles taken from a furnace may also carry soot, ash and fume deposits from whatever was burnt there, including metal residues if the furnace worked metals, and glazed tiles may be lead-glazed; this is a general caution, and the text says nothing of it. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records a plain, hard, burnt clay used as a drying, scab-forming rub for itching and eruptions and as a hardening agent in ointments for swellings. The Codex does not recommend it. Used tiles from furnaces cannot be assumed clean, and no part of the text supports a modern use.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives two uses only: rubbed on with vinegar (itching, pustules, gout) and used with wax ointments (glandular swellings and goitres). No method of grinding, no proportion of tile to vinegar or wax, no frequency and no dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Fired clay is an inert, abrasive material; powdered clays and silicates have a long record as drying, absorbent and protective dusts on skin. Vinegar is a dilute acetic acid solution and is a mild irritant to broken skin. Beeswax is a common base for ointments. A furnace tile may carry heavy-metal residues from the furnace's former use, and inhaled ceramic dust irritates the lungs. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry, and none for any effect on gout or goitre. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** The text prints one quality for the tiles: "crustforming" (line 10757). It prints no hot, cold, dry or moist rating and no degree. The general statement for medicinal earths in 5-170 ("supremely useful for cooling, and for closing the pores", line 10711) belongs to earth in general, and is not printed in this chapter for the tiles.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is asserted here. The chapter is two sentences: a burnt-clay rub for skin, joints and swellings. It gives no provenance to rank, no assay and no adulteration test, so even the discipline of attention that other chapters offer is not on the page. It makes no claim about the person's constitution, vitality or long life.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The tile is a rough hard-fired clay that Dioscorides lists for skin and swelling; the Codex does not recommend using a used tile on the body. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** a tile taken from a furnace or a roof is not sterile; do not apply any dirty material to broken or pustular skin, where it risks infection.
- **Toxicity:** furnace tiles may carry soot, ash and metal residues, and old glazes may contain lead; do not apply or swallow them.
- **Dust:** grinding fired clay makes silica-bearing dust that is harmful to breathe.
- **Vinegar:** do not rub vinegar or acid preparations on open or inflamed skin.
- **Gout and goitre:** no part of the text supports treating either condition; a swollen gland or a goitre should be assessed by a clinician.
- **Identification:** the translator's heading is "Clay Tiles" only; no composition is given.
