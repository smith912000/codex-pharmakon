# Androsace lactea — Androsaces (Dioscorides 3-150)

## *Androsace lactea (the translators' suggestion; also *A. obtusifolia*; "Androsace, Sea Navelwort")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification, and this row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) without being verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The SUGGESTED line also offers *Androsace obtusifolia*, so the chapter is not fixed to one species even in the translators' gloss.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, chapter heading at line 7821.

*3-150. ANDROSACES*, SUGGESTED: "Androsace lactea — Androsace, Sea Navelwort ALSO: Androsace obtusifolia". The full text reads: "Androsaces grows in sea-bordering places in Syria. The herb is thin, with slender, bitter, leafless branches, having on its head a pod containing the seed. Two teaspoonfuls of a decoction (taken as a drink with wine) is able to encourage much urine in dropsy; and a decoction of the herb and the seed (taken as a drink) does the same. It is smeared usefully upon gout. It is also called picras, leuce, or thalassia." The index prints ANDROSACES at p.525. The next chapter (3-151, Asplenon) is a different plant.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read, and no general composition is stated here. *Androsace* is a genus of the primrose family (Primulaceae). [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a bitter herb, in decoction, for dropsy (as a diuretic), and smeared on for gout. These are the text's claims, not a Codex protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

3-150 gives "two teaspoonfuls of a decoction (taken as a drink with wine)" to encourage urine in dropsy, and a decoction of herb and seed drunk (quantity not given) to the same end; the herb is also "smeared" on gout. No dose is given for the second drink or for the external use. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No claim is made about diuretic or gout effects of this plant. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints "bitter" for the branches. No other quality is given for 3-150.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant by its parts, where it grows, what it is called, and what is claimed for it. 3-150 gives the habitat (sea-bordering places in Syria), the look (thin herb, slender bitter leafless branches, a pod on the head), three other names, and the therapeutic claims. It assigns no status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The text is a short therapeutic note, and the answer for cultivation is to learn how it was recognised and do nothing further.

### Contraindications

The identity of the plant is uncertain, so no safety statement can be made for it. Dropsy and gout are conditions for a physician, and a diuretic taken for dropsy can be harmful without supervision. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
