*Chapters naming Kimsuka.* Three passages were found, in the OCR spelling "Kims'uka":

- **Chikitsa ch.1**, `SS_Chikitsa_ch01.txt`, lines 108 to 112, describes a Pittaja ulcer that "assumes a bluish yellow colour, exudes a hot secretion resembling the washings of Kims'uka flowers". The flower is used as a colour comparison.
- **Kalpa ch.6**, `SS_Kalpa_ch06.txt`, lines 62 to 67: "A pasted compound of S'irisha fruit and Kushtha with the washings of the ashes of Kims'uka (flower) should be given to the patient in such a case" (a rat-bite, in a chapter on poison from rats, with vomiting of blood among the signs described).
- **Uttara ch.10**, `SS_Uttara_ch10.txt`, lines 64 to 70, in a passage of eye-salves (Anjana; the flowers at line 68): "Gold-leaf rubbed with breast-milk, or the flowers of Kims'uka rubbed with honey, or of a compound of Rodhra, Drakshd, sugar, Utpala, Vacha and Yashti-madhu rubbed with breast-milk".

*The name Palasha.* The separate name *Palasha* stands in four ch.38 groups of `SS_Sutra_ch38.txt`: the Rodhradi group (line 91, printed "Palasha Kutannata"), the Mushkakadi group (lines 133 to 134), the Amvashthadi group (line 333) and the Nyagrodhadi group (line 349). In Sutra ch.45 (`SS_Sutra_ch45.txt`, lines 998 to 1000) "The Oils of the Madhuka (Maula), Kishmarya, and Palasha seeds are sweet and astringent. They pacify the deranged Kapham and Pittam." These are not attributed to *Kimsuka* here. Group membership in ch.38 is a grouping by action and not a rasayana or vajikarana status. No named rasayana or vajikarana chapter was found to name *Kimsuka*.

### Active Components

*Butea* flowers are generally known to contain flavonoid pigments such as butein and butrin, which give the orange-red colour of the infusion. The composition of the trees named in the texts was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Sushruta records *Kimsuka* flowers in a colour comparison, in a rat-poison remedy and in an eye-salve, and the Tibetan texts record *ma.ru.tse* as a bitter and sweet anthelmintic against phlegm and rheumatism disorders. These are the texts' own claims and not a Codex protocol. The Codex gives no protocol for this tree.

### Preparation & Dosing

Sushruta Kalpa ch.6 (lines 62 to 67) gives the washings of the ashes of the flower, pasted with *S'irisha* fruit and *Kushtha*. Uttara ch.10 (lines 64 to 70) gives the flowers rubbed with honey as an eye-salve. Clark (line 920) states no preparation. No quantity is given in the lines read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* *Kimsuka* appears as a flower in a colour simile (Chikitsa ch.1), in a rat-poison remedy (Kalpa ch.6) and in an eye-salve (Uttara ch.10), and it is listed in the appendix name index. It is not named in a rasayana or vajikarana chapter in the files searched, and nothing read ties it to *ojas*, *rasayana* or *vajikarana*. The Sushruta frame for it is therapeutic. The ch.38 groups that contain *Palasha* are groupings by therapeutic action, and *Palasha* is not shown to be the same name. The identification with *Butea frondosa* rests on an OCR-paired appendix. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra classes *ma.ru.tse* by taste (bitter and sweet) and by use (an anthelmintic for phlegm and rheumatism, ch.20; a member of the anthelmintic class, ch.21). The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice and not reaching the root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. Nothing read assigns the tree a place in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. The glossary scientific name differs from Clark's. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the sources read gives a cultivator a practice with this tree. In Sushruta it is a flower used in remedies and similes, and in the Tibetan texts a bitter-sweet anthelmintic. The abstentive answer is the correct one: use is a matter for a qualified practitioner, and the texts supply no long-term regimen.

### Contraindications

No safety data were read. The flowers are used as a dye and a traditional remedy, and the seeds and gum are different materials about which nothing was read here. None of the traditional uses above is advice, and the eye-salve passage in particular must not be tried at home. Seek a qualified practitioner, and seek ordinary medical care for eye disease, poisoning or worm infection. Do not use in pregnancy.
