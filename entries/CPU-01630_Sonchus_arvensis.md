# Sonchus arvensis — Ierakion mega, the great hieracium (Dioscorides 3-72)

## *Sonchus arvensis [Linnaeus], "Corn Sowthistle", the last name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-72 ("Hieracium maius, Sonchites [Fuchs], Sonchus arvensis [Linnaeus] — Corn Sowthistle"), with a second line giving "other usage" as Hieracium sylvaticum, Hieracium murorum, "Wood Hawkweed, Wall Hawkweed"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 7167) prints three names, the last being Linnaeus, and a second line (7168) prints an "other usage" of two further names (hawkweeds, a different genus); the row takes *Sonchus arvensis*. It was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-72. IERAKION MEGA* ("the great hieracium"), heading at line 7165, SUGGESTED lines at 7167 and 7168, text from line 7169. The text is interrupted by a plate caption ("Hieracium majus from FUCHS — 1545", line 7171), a page number (447, line 7172) and the running head "ROOTS OF AKANTHODA or PRICKLY PLANTS" (line 7174), and resumes at line 7175. It stands in Book Three, after *3-71. KORIANNON* (line 7160) and before *3-73. IERAKION MIKRON* (the little hieracium, line 7176). The Codex's own name extraction lists it (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 478); the translators' general index prints "Sonchus arvensis 447" (line 17623).

The whole chapter reads: "The great hieracium produces a rough stalk — somewhat red, prickly, hollow. It has thinly-jagged leaves at distances, similar in circumference to sonchus [2-159]; and yellowish flowers in somewhat long little heads. It is cooling, indifferent, and gently astringent. As a result it is good applied on a burning stomach, and for inflammation. The juice is sipped to soothe pangs of hunger in the stomach. The herb (with the root) is applied to help one bitten by a scorpion. It is also called sonchiten; the Romans call it lampuca, and the Africans, sithileas."

The chapter gives a description (a rough, somewhat red, prickly, hollow stalk; thinly jagged leaves set at distances, similar in outline to those of *sonchus*, chapter 2-159; yellowish flowers in somewhat long little heads), a quality ("cooling, indifferent, and gently astringent"), and four uses: the herb applied "on a burning stomach" and "for inflammation", the juice "sipped to soothe pangs of hunger in the stomach", and the herb with the root "applied to help one bitten by a scorpion". Its other names are *sonchiten* and, for the Romans, *lampuca* and, for the Africans, *sithileas*. The text says "As a result" after the quality, so the first uses are given as following from the cooling and astringent quality. It gives no habitat.

**The sonchus chapter named in the text.** The chapter compares the leaves to *sonchus* with the reference [2-159]. That chapter is headed *2-159. SONCHOS AKANTHODESTEROS, SONCHOS TRACHOS, SONCHOS TRUPHEROS* (line 5898), which are the sow-thistles. It was not read beyond its heading for this entry; the comparison is Dioscorides', not the Codex's. This is a labelled cross-reference only.

**Overlap.** A chapter-level search of the entries folder for 3-72 and *IERAKION* found one file, I_1060 (*Crepis tectorum*, chapter 3-73, the little hieracium). It quotes the whole of 3-72 in its section on "the uses by reference", because 3-73 says only that it "has the same uses as that previously spoken of [3-72]", and its Overlap note records that 3-72 has its own spine row (this entry, CPU-01630). It is the entry on the other chapter and is not merged here. The spine's sow-thistle entry I_647 (*Sonchus oleraceus*) was not read for this entry and is a different species.

### Active Components

The chapter prints none. It is a general fact that *Sonchus* (sow-thistle) and *Hieracium* (hawkweed) both belong to the daisy family (Asteraceae) and that plants of the group carry a milky latex. Whether the plant of 3-72 does is not stated by the text, which mentions a juice but not its colour. The composition of the plant Dioscorides describes was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides reports a cooling, gently astringent herb applied for a burning stomach and for inflammation, its juice sipped for hunger pangs, and the herb with its root applied for a scorpion bite. These are the text's reports, the identity is the translators' and the "other usage" line offers a different genus, and the Codex gives no protocol. A scorpion bite or a burning stomach needs medical care.

### Preparation & Dosing

3-72 gives these preparations: the herb "applied" to the stomach and to inflammation; the juice "sipped"; the herb "(with the root)" applied for a scorpion bite. It gives no quantity, frequency or duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints three qualities: "It is cooling, indifferent, and gently astringent." It prints no taste and no warming, drying or moistening quality beyond these. The word "indifferent" is printed as it stands in the translation; the text does not explain it.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use; the Roman and African names are names only. What it offers is of the kind the cultivation spec calls a discipline of attention: the stalk (rough, reddish, prickly, hollow), the leaves (thinly jagged, set apart, compared with a named plant), the flowers in elongated heads, a three-part statement of quality, and the part of the plant used (herb, juice, herb with root) for each use. The text also reasons from quality to use with "As a result". The plant is given no status beyond that of a cooling, gently astringent herb with remedial uses. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. What is worth keeping is the method: the chapter derives the use from the quality and says which part is applied. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter states no caution. A scorpion bite is an emergency, and a burning stomach or an inflammation needs medical assessment. The identity of the plant rests on a translators' suggestion with an alternative in another genus, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
