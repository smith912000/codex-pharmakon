# Heracleum sphondylium — Sphondulion (Dioscorides 3-90); "Meadow Parsnip, Cow Parsnip, Hogweed"

## *Heracleum sphondylium [Linnaeus], one of four names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for chapter 3-90 (with Acanthus germanica [Fuchs], Sphondylium vulgare hirsutum [Bauhin] and Sphondylium branca ursina), glossed "Meadow Parsnip, Cow Parsnip, Hogweed"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk names this plant under this name. The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The SUGGESTED line gives four names, one of them an older label (*Acanthus germanica* [Fuchs]) from the Renaissance herbals, and the entry takes the Linnaean one. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed names (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *3-90. SPHONDULION* at line 7337, SUGGESTED line at 7338, a further line in capitals at 7339 ("JUICE CAUSES BLISTERS AND PERMANENT PURPLE PIGMENTATION"), text at line 7340. The chapter stands in Book Three under the running head "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants", after 3-89 and before 3-91 *NARTHEX* (asafoetida), both different chapters. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 496.

The whole chapter reads: "Sphondylium has leaves somewhat similar to platanus [1-107] as well as to those of panax [3-55]. The stalks are a foot high (or rather more) similar to marathrum [3-81]. The seed on the top is double, similar to seselis, but broader, paler, and huskier, with a strong scent. The flowers are white, and the root is white like raphanus [2-137]. It grows in moist, marshy countries. The seed of this (taken in a drink) purges phlegmy stuff through the bowels. Taken in a drink it cures the liver, jaundice, asthma, epilepsy and constriction of the womb. Inhaled, it revives those who fall in a faint. If the head is moistened with it (with oil), it is good for fever of the brain, lethargy, and headaches. Applied with rue [3-52] it restrains herpes [viral skin infection]. The root is given to the jaundiced and liverish. Shaved and inserted it eliminates the hardness of fistulas [ulcers]. The fresh juice from the flower is good for ulcerated and purulent ears. It is also preserved, placed in the sun like other juices. It is also called arangem, phalangium, asterium, nisyris, sphondulis, choradanon, or oenanthe; the Romans call it herba rotularis, the Egyptians, apsapher, and the Magi, osiris." The square brackets are the translators'.

So the chapter gives a description for recognition (leaves like platanus and panax, a stalk a foot high or a little more, a double seed at the top that is broader, paler and huskier than seselis and strongly scented, white flowers, a white root like raphanus, growing in moist marshy country), a list of uses by part (seed, root, flower juice, and the plant inhaled or applied with oil or rue) and a string of other names. The other names are given as the names of the Romans, the Egyptians and the Magi. These are names Dioscorides records. They are not Egyptian or Magian texts, and nothing is attributed to those peoples beyond the name.

**Overlap.** The chapter's first sentence compares the leaf to *panax* [3-55]. Chapter 3-55 *PANAKES HERAKLEION* (heading at line 7021) is a different chapter, and the existing entry I_981 (*Heracleum panaces*) rests on it. Its SUGGESTED line also lists *Heracleum sphondylium* (names.csv line 461), so the same binomial stands on two chapters of the translators' suggestions. The existing entry I_877 (Bear's Breeches, chapter 3-19) mentions 3-90 only because the label "Acanthus germanica [Fuchs]" on this chapter's SUGGESTED line resembles its own subject. Nothing is merged. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. It is a general, well-established fact that *Heracleum sphondylium* belongs to the carrot family (Apiaceae) and that the sap of many large *Heracleum* species contains furanocoumarins, which make skin sensitive to light. Whether the plant of this chapter is that species is the translators' suggestion. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a seed drink to purge phlegm and for liver, jaundice, asthma, epilepsy and a constricted womb, an inhalant for fainting, an oiled head application for fever of the brain, lethargy and headache, and local uses on herpes, fistulas and ulcerated ears. These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for any of these conditions. The translators' own capitalised heading warns of blistering and lasting purple staining of the skin (see Contraindications), and the identity is a suggestion among four names.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations and no quantities: the seed "taken in a drink"; the plant "inhaled"; the head moistened with it "with oil"; the plant "applied with rue"; the root "given" to the jaundiced and liverish; the root "shaved and inserted" into fistulas; the "fresh juice from the flower" for the ears. It also says the juice "is also preserved, placed in the sun like other juices". No dose, frequency or duration is printed. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that contact with the sap of some large *Heracleum* species, followed by sunlight, can cause severe skin burns. The uses Dioscorides lists (liver disease, jaundice, asthma, epilepsy) are conditions that need medical care.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 3-90 prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality and no taste. The only qualities printed are of the seed ("strong scent"; "broader, paler, and huskier" than *seselis*) and of the place it grows ("moist, marshy countries").

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a description for recognition by comparison (the leaves against two other plants, the stalk against a third, the seed against a fourth, the root against a fifth), a habitat (moist marshy country), and a preservation note (juice dried "in the sun like other juices"). It also records that the Magi call the plant *osiris*. That is a recorded name, and the sources read attribute nothing further to it. The text assigns the plant no cultivation status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is the method of recognition by comparison, which asks the reader to know several other plants first. The text offers nothing more, and the abstentive answer is the right one.

### Contraindications

The translators' heading to chapter 3-90 states that the juice causes blisters and permanent purple pigmentation; the chapter text itself prints no warning. On that heading alone the plant should not be handled or applied on the skin on the strength of this entry. The identification is only a suggestion among four names. Epilepsy, jaundice, liver disease, asthma and fever of the brain need medical care. The text gives no dose, and no safety information for pregnancy was read, though the text itself uses the seed for "constriction of the womb". [USER: verify]
