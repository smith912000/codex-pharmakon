## *Smilax china* L. | Family: Smilacaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Hot and dry in the first degree (Galenic reading of the early-modern *radix Chinae*); attenuating, opening, drying — a *desiccans* and *aperiens* rather than a purge
**Training Phase:** Clearance / Depuration (Phase II) — the long, slow, low-intensity phase of the Kenetica sequence, run over weeks rather than days

### Origin & Tradition

*Smilax china* is a spiny, climbing, rhizomatous liana of East Asia — China, Korea, Japan, Taiwan, and into Indochina — whose knotted, reddish, starchy rhizome is the drug. It is the eastern cousin of the New World sarsaparillas (*Smilax aristolochiifolia*, *S. regelii*) and shares with them the two features that made the whole genus famous: a bland, faintly sweet, mucilage-and-starch-rich rootstock that tolerates long decoction, and a load of steroidal saponins that gives that decoction a foam and a faint bitterness.

**Chinese Materia Medica (Shennong lineage) — 菝葜 *bá qiā***
Ba Qia is the settled Chinese name for the rhizome, and its doctrinal placement is unambiguous and stable across the later *bencao* tradition: it is a **wind-damp dispelling** (祛风湿 *qū fēng shī*) drug that additionally **relieves toxicity and disperses swellings** (解毒消肿 *jiě dú xiāo zhǒng*) and **disinhibits damp** in the lower burner. Its classical field of use is therefore threefold: (i) painful obstruction of the joints and sinews — *bì* 痹 syndrome, what a European physician would call arthritic and rheumatic pain; (ii) damp-heat pouring downward — turbid urine, vaginal discharge, and above all pelvic inflammatory conditions, which remain the single most characteristic modern Chinese indication for the herb; and (iii) sores, boils, carbuncles, and swellings treated both internally and by external wash. The modern ethnopharmacological review by Wang and colleagues (2023) states the position plainly: *S. china*, "known as Baqia in China," has been used to treat various inflammatory disorders, "particularly pelvic inflammation" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2023.116992)). The Chinese tradition, note, never made a venereal claim of the kind Europe made. It made a *damp-heat* claim — which is a claim about a class of hot, swollen, discharging, obstructed states, not about a specific pathogen.

**Dioscorides / the Greek stream — *smilax* and the problem of the name**
Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* uses *smilax* (σμῖλαξ) with several referents — *smilax leia* (bindweed), *smilax trachea* (the rough, prickly greenbrier of the Mediterranean, *Smilax aspera*), and *smilax* as the yew. The genus name Linnaeus later fixed comes from this Greek word, and the Mediterranean *S. aspera* is a genuine classical drug: a prickly climber whose root was given in wine as an alexipharmic — a counter-poison and antidote — and whose leaves were used on bites and stings. It is important to be exact here: **the Greeks did not know *Smilax china***. What they knew was a congener with the same growth habit, the same prickles, and a root used for the same purpose — to drive out venom and corruption from the blood. The classical attribution in this entry is therefore a *generic* attribution (Smilax as an alexipharmic root), not a species-level one, and the Kenetica index should be read that way. **Avicenna and the Unani reception — *chob-i chīnī* (چوب چینی), "China wood"**
Here the history becomes genuinely global, and genuinely instructive. *Chob-i chīnī* — "China wood/root" — enters the Perso-Arabic materia medica as an *imported* drug from the East, and in later Unani practice it is classed as a **hot, dry, attenuating, and *muḥallil*** agent: a resolvent that thins thick humours, opens obstructions of the liver and joints, promotes sweat and urine, and is prescribed for *waja' al-mafāṣil* (joint pain), chronic skin corruption, and — in the post-Columbian period — for *ātishak*, the "fire disease," syphilis. The chronology matters and must not be blurred: the *Canon* of Avicenna (d. 1037) sits centuries before the syphilis epidemic and before the China trade in this rhizome; the depurative-for-the-pox indication is a **later Unani accretion, not an Avicennan one**. What is genuinely Avicennan, and what licenses the tag, is the doctrinal *category* — the class of hot, dry, obstruction-opening roots given in long decoction for cold, thick, joint-lodging humours — into which *chob-i chīnī* was slotted the moment it arrived. **Sushruta / Āyurveda — the weakest attribution**
Āyurvedic *Smilax* usage is real but sits mostly with *S. zeylanica*, *S. ovalifolia*, and the imported *chopchini* (which is *chob-i chīnī* under a Sanskritised name, entering Indian practice via Unani, not via the *Suśruta Saṃhitā*). *Chopchini* is used in *āmavāta* (rheumatoid-type joint disease) and *vātarakta* (gout-like disorders), and in the classic Indian formulation for syphilis. But the *Suśruta Saṃhitā* — a text of the early centuries CE — cannot have contained an East-Asian import drug under this name. **Grade this attribution "traditional, late, and indirect."** Do not let the tag imply a Sushrutan citation that does not exist.

**The early-modern global drug — the real story**
In the 1530s and 1540s the Portuguese trade brought *radix Chinae* into Europe, where it landed in the middle of a medical emergency: the syphilis epidemic that had swept the continent from the 1490s. Physicians already had two vegetable "antivenereal" woods — guaiacum (*lignum vitae*, from the Caribbean) and, shortly, sarsaparilla (*Smilax* spp., from Mexico and Honduras). China root joined them as the third of the great "sudorific woods," given as a long, weak, sweat-inducing decoction over a strict multi-week regimen of heat, diet, and confinement. Its most famous European advocate-and-sceptic is Andreas Vesalius, who in 1546 published a letter on the China root (the *Epistola rationem modumque propinandi radicis Chynae decocti* — famous today less for its pharmacology than for the anatomical polemic against Galen he smuggled into it). The important point for a materia medica: **Vesalius' letter is not an endorsement of a cure. It is a cautious, sceptical account of a fashionable remedy** — and the fashion collapsed within a century.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

Three doctrines converge on this root, and only one of them survives contact with the chemistry.

*The purifying/depurative doctrine.* Every tradition that used a *Smilax* used it as a **cleanser of the blood** — Greek *alexipharmakon*, Unani *muṣaffī-yi dam* (blood-purifier), Chinese *jiě dú* (resolve toxicity). This is not a coincidence of translation; it is a convergent reading of the same observable facts. The root is bland, tolerates enormous doses in long decoction without purging, produces sweat and urine, and is given over weeks for chronic, discharging, foul conditions. The doctrine says: *there is a corruption in the humours, and this root, over time, washes it out through the skin and the water.* Map this onto the chemistry and something real appears — but not what the doctrine claims. The saponins and flavonoids of *S. china* are **anti-inflammatory**, acting on innate-immune signalling (TLR-4 → MyD88 → NF-κB, and COX-2). They do not "purify" anything. They damp the *inflammatory response to* the corruption. A depurative doctrine and an immunomodulatory pharmacology produce, in the clinic, the same visible result — the swelling goes down, the sore quiets, the joint eases — while being causally unrelated. This is the single most important lesson the China root teaches: **a doctrine can be predictively useful and etiologically false at the same time.**

*The "sudorific wood" doctrine — and the syphilis failure.* The Galenic-Unani logic was: syphilis is a cold, thick, putrid humour lodged deep; a hot, dry, attenuating root, given in a sweat-forcing regimen, thins and expels it. This is internally coherent, and it was almost certainly **wrong in fact**. Untreated syphilis has a spectacular natural history: a primary chancre that heals spontaneously, a secondary eruption that resolves spontaneously, and then years — often decades — of latency. Any therapy administered during the secondary stage will appear to cure. Add to this that virtually every European syphilis patient was simultaneously receiving **mercury**, which is genuinely (if brutally) treponemicidal, and the confound is total: the sudorific woods were the co-administered vehicle of an active drug, prescribed against a disease that remits on its own. There is no plausible mechanism by which steroidal saponins clear *Treponema pallidum*, and no evidence that they do. **The syphilis reputation of China root should be recorded as a historical failure of inference, not a lost pharmacological secret.** The doctrine was not deflated by better chemistry; it was deflated by the disease's own clock.

*The signature of the thorn.* Smilax is a spiny climber that binds and obstructs a thicket, and it was read, in more than one tradition, as the drug for what binds and obstructs the body — the joint locked by *bì*, the vessel choked by thick humour. This is pure sympathetic reasoning and carries no pharmacological weight. It is recorded here as symbolism, and marked as such.

### Active Components

Per the ethnopharmacological review of Wang et al. (2023), some 134 constituents have been characterised from *S. china*, with **steroidal saponins and flavonoids as the two dominant classes** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2023.116992)).

- **Steroidal saponins** — spirostanol and, characteristically, **furostanol** saponins of the rhizome. Xie et al. (2018) isolated seven new furostanol saponins ("chongrenosides A–G") alongside nine known ones ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.steroids.2018.09.011)). Shao et al. (2006) isolated four new steroidal saponins plus thirteen known compounds ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phytochem.2006.10.026)). These are the foaming, "soapy" principles that made the decoction look and behave like a cleanser.
- **Dihydroflavonol glycosides** — **astilbin**, **neoastilbin**, **isoastilbin**, **neoisoastilbin**, and **engeletin**. These are the chemical signature of the genus (they dominate *S. glabra*, the related Tu Fu Ling, where Zhao et al. (2020) quantified astilbin at 18.10% and engeletin at 2.58% of a standardised flavonoid extract — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules25225295)). In *S. china* root, Feng et al. (2020) prepared a flavonoid-enriched extract at 44.3% flavonoids, "mainly astilbin, engeletin, isoastilbin, cinchonain Ia, quercetin-3-O-α-L-rhamnopyranoside and chlorogenic acid" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2020.112785)).
- **Flavonols and phenolic acids** — quercetin and its glycosides; chlorogenic acid.
- **Proanthocyanidins / condensed tannins and (−)-epicatechin** — the astringent fraction; responsible for the drying, tissue-tightening action on discharges and sores.
- **Starch and mucilage** — bulk of the rhizome; pharmacologically inert but explains the drug's tolerability at heroic doses and its use as a famine/food starch.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

China root is a **Phase II Clearance agent of the slow class** — the archetype, in fact, of the type. It is not an acute intervention. Nothing about its pharmacology suggests rapid effect, and nothing in its traditional use ever claimed one: every tradition prescribes it for weeks.

Its Kenetica role is (1) **low-grade chronic inflammatory load**, particularly joint-and-connective-tissue and pelvic/urogenital, where the target is the sustained innate-immune tone rather than a discrete lesion; (2) **damp-stagnant presentations** — heaviness, discharge, boggy swelling, poor drainage; (3) as a **long-decoction base** in compound formulae, where its mucilage and starch soften the harshness of more aggressive agents while its saponins carry a real if modest anti-inflammatory floor.

It is explicitly **not** an antimicrobial of any clinical consequence, **not** a treatment for any specific infection, and **not** a "blood purifier" in any sense a modern reader should take literally.

### Scientific Correlation

*According to PubMed*, the evidence for *S. china* is real, coherent, and confined almost entirely to preclinical work. Graded honestly:

**Anti-inflammatory action — the one traditional claim that maps (evidence grade: MODERATE preclinical, no human trials).**
Feng et al. (2020, *J Ethnopharmacol*) took a flavonoid-enriched extract of *S. china* root and showed it inhibited xylene-induced mouse ear oedema and cotton-pellet granuloma in rats, and suppressed LPS-induced TNF-α, COX-2, iNOS, IL-1β and IL-6 in RAW264.7 macrophages. Mechanistically the extract suppressed TLR-4 and its downstream MyD88, phospho-IκB-α, NF-κB p65 and p38 MAPK, with a signature "similar to that of TAK-242 (a selective TLR-4 inhibitor)" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2020.112785)). This is a direct pharmacological correlate of both the Chinese *bì*/swelling indication and the Unani joint-pain indication — the same molecular brake, arrived at from two doctrines.

Shao et al. (2006, *Phytochemistry*) found the isolated steroidal saponins inhibited **COX-2** at 10⁻⁵ M, though only one of seventeen compounds inhibited TNF-α production in murine peritoneal macrophages ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phytochem.2006.10.026)) — a useful reminder that the fraction, not every molecule in it, carries the activity. Xie et al. (2018, *Steroids*) reported that four furostanol saponins inhibited LPS-induced TNF-α mRNA by 67–93% at 10 µM ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.steroids.2018.09.011)). Comparable furostanol activity is seen in the congener *S. davidiana* (Zhou et al., 2017, *Steroids* — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.steroids.2017.08.013)).

**The honest counterweight — a NULL result on the marker flavonoids.**
Ruangnoo et al. (2012, *Asian Pac J Allergy Immunol*, PMID 23393906) isolated engeletin, astilbin and quercetin from the congener *S. corbularia* and tested them in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells. Quercetin was potent (NO IC₅₀ 11.2 µg/ml; TNF-α IC₅₀ 1.25 µg/ml). **Engeletin and astilbin — the two signature Smilax dihydroflavonols — had *no* activity on NO or TNF-α production (IC₅₀ > 100 µg/ml)**, showing activity only against PGE₂ release. This matters: the compounds most often paraded as the "actives" of the genus were inert on two of three inflammatory readouts. Any account of *S. china* that presents astilbin/engeletin as the explanation of the herb's activity is over-claiming. The activity of the whole extract is probably carried by the saponin fraction, quercetin and phenolics acting together, and the flavonol glycosides may be markers more than mediators.

**Gout / hyperuricaemia (evidence grade: WEAK–PRELIMINARY).** Total steroidal saponins from a *Smilax* species have been investigated in a gouty-arthritis rat model with inhibition of monosodium-urate-stimulated RAW264.7 inflammation attributed to PI3K/Akt signalling (Yuan et al., 2025, *Nat Prod Res* — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2025.2565701)). Directionally consistent with the *bì*/joint-pain tradition; nowhere near clinical.

**Syphilis (evidence grade: NEGATIVE / REFUTED BY MECHANISM AND HISTORY).** No retrieved study supports any anti-treponemal activity of *S. china* or any *Smilax*. The historical reputation is fully explained by (i) the natural latency of untreated syphilis, (ii) co-administered mercury, and (iii) the placebo-and-regimen effects of a dramatic multi-week protocol. **Record as failure. Do not soften.**

**Overall grade: TRADITIONAL–PRECLINICAL.** No human randomised trial of *S. china* was retrieved. Wang et al. (2023) put it exactly right: pharmacological studies in vitro and in vivo "have demonstrated some of the traditional uses," while "other traditional uses still need to be confirmed" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2023.116992)).

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Chinese (TCM):** Flavour sweet, acrid, slightly bitter; nature neutral to slightly warm; channels entering Liver and Kidney. Actions: dispel wind-damp (祛风湿), relieve toxicity and disperse swelling (解毒消肿), disinhibit urine and free the network vessels.

**Āyurvedic (as *chopchini*, a late import — NOT attested in the *Suśruta Saṃhitā*):** *Rasa* — tikta (bitter), kaṣāya (astringent); *Guṇa* — laghu (light), rūkṣa (dry); *Vīrya* — uṣṇa (heating); *Vipāka* — kaṭu (pungent); *Doṣa* — pacifies kapha and vāta, may aggravate pitta in excess. **Marked as traditional-late; do not cite to Sushruta.**

**Galenic / Unani (as *chob-i chīnī*):** Hot and dry, first degree; *muḥallil* (resolvent), *mufattiḥ* (deobstruent), *muṣaffī-yi dam* (blood-purifier), *mudirr* (diuretic/diaphoretic). **The Galenic degree assignment here is a post-1540 European/Unani reception, not an Avicennan text-attested figure — [USER: verify before printing a degree].**

**Greek (Dioscorides):** *Smilax aspera* — alexipharmic root, given in wine against venoms. Species-level attribution to *S. china* **not attested**.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy and lactation:** avoid. No safety data. The saponin content and the traditional "moving/dispersing" action both argue against use.
- **Yin deficiency with heat / dryness syndromes (TCM):** contraindicated per the drying, attenuating nature of the drug.
- **Saponin-related GI irritation:** high-dose or prolonged decoctions may cause nausea and gastric irritation. Saponins are haemolytic in vitro; this is of little consequence orally but is a hard bar to any parenteral use.
- **Renal impairment:** the diuretic/depurative use is traditional, not tested. Do not use as a "kidney cleanser."
- **Do not use as an anti-infective.** Any suspected sexually transmitted infection, cellulitis, abscess, or pelvic inflammatory disease requires antibiotic treatment. The historical record on this plant is a case study in what happens when a plant is trusted against a pathogen it cannot touch.
- **Drug interaction data: none retrieved.** Grade unknown. Caution with anticoagulants and with any narrow-therapeutic-index drug, on general principle for a tannin- and saponin-rich decoction.
