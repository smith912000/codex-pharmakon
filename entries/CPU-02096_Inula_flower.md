# Inula flower — Xuan Fu Hua 旋复花 (Shennong bencao jing); aliases Jin Fei Cao 金沸草 ("boiling-gold weed") and Sheng Shen 盛椹

## *Inula japonica Thunb., the Japanese inula, a yellow-flowered daisy of damp ground whose flower-heads are the drug xuan fu hua (I. britannica is also used under the name); the identification is ours, from the Chinese name and the standard modern identification, and is of high confidence at genus level and fairly high at species level, since Yang 1998 prints "Flos Inulae" and Guo Pu, quoted by Sun, says the plant "resembles a chrysanthemum"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts on disk, under this Chinese name.** This is a single-text entry. The principal text is Sun Xingyan's 1799 reconstruction, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt`; the other edition on disk, Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`), is read as a second witness and attributed to Yang. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder, "Flos Inulae [SBJ]", taken from Yang's English; this entry replaces it. A search of the other texts on disk found other inulas only: Dioscorides' *Inula helenium* (elecampane; I_095 and I_456) and *I. dysenterica* (I_743), Clark's *Inula racemosa* (the Tibetan *pushkaramula*; I_737) and a Tibetan drug *a.byag* with *Inula britannica* among its binomials (Clark, line 1023). These are other species, from other traditions, and none cites this paragraph. *I. britannica* is a close relative that is also used under the Chinese name, but the Codex does not equate the Tibetan drug with this one.

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** The entry is 旋复花 in the lower classic (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, lower classic, entry 旋复花 *xuan fu hua*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 3175, 味 paragraph; name line 3173). The Shennong paragraph reads: 味咸，温 ("salty, warm"); 主结气 ("governs bound qi"), 胁下满 ("fullness below the ribs"), 惊悸 ("fright and palpitations"), 除水 ("removes water"), 去五脏间寒热 ("clears cold and heat between the five viscera"), 补中下气 ("supplements the centre and brings qi down"); the aliases 一名金沸草 and 一名盛椹; and 生川谷 ("grows in rivers and valleys"). There is no 久服 clause. No Wu Pu layer is printed for this entry.

The Mingyi bielu layer (《名医》曰, line 3177) adds the alias 戴椹 and the habitat 生平泽 ("grows in level marshes"), and a harvest: 五月采花，晒干，二十日成 ("the flowers are gathered in the fifth month and dried in the sun; it is done in twenty days"). This is the only place that names the part used, the flower.

Sun's note (案, line 3179) cites the *Shuowen*: "[glyph lost in this e-text] is 盗庚 (*dao geng*)"; the *Erya*: "[glyph lost], 盗庚"; and Guo Pu: 旋复似菊 ("*xuan fu* resembles a chrysanthemum"). The lost glyph is the plant's older name in each place. Guo Pu's words are the only description of the plant in the sources read. They are the commentator's citations and are not Shennong text.

**Two editions, two grades.** Sun places 旋复花 in the lower classic. Yang's translation places it under the heading "Herbs: Middle Class" (heading at line 3093; the entry at line 3128), and the placeholder row recorded it so. The Codex follows Sun, as the principal text, and records the difference without resolving it. [USER: verify]

**Yang 1998 as second witness.** Yang prints (line 3128): "Xuan Fu Hua (Flos Inulae) is salty and warm. It mainly treats bound qi, rib-side fullness, and fright palpitations, removes water, eliminates cold and heat in the five viscera, supplements the center, and downbears the qi. Its other name is Jin Fei Cao (Boiling Gold Weed). Another name is Sheng Zhan (Profound Clearness). It grows in rivers and valleys." This agrees with Sun's paragraph. The second alias differs: Sun's text has 盛椹, and Yang's "Sheng Zhan" is his rendering of a name that the Codex cannot match to Sun's glyphs. Yang's footnote 190 (line 3145) is modern commentary and not part of the classic: "There is a saying that 'All the hundreds of flowers downbear except for Inula which upbears.' It precipitates the qi and moves water and, therefore, is able to treat phlegm glomus, water swelling due to the large intestine, head wind, and belching."

**Appendix.** The drug-relations table reads 白芷 当归为使；恶旋复花 ("*bai zhi*: *dang gui* is its envoy; it dislikes *xuan fu hua*", line 4391; *bai zhi* is I_1230) and 桑螵蛸 畏旋复花 ("*sang piao xiao* fears *xuan fu hua*", line 4585). The preface (line 4117) says to use pairs that "need" or "serve" one another and not those that "dislike" (恶) or oppose one another; the Codex draws no further conclusion.

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, *Inula* is a genus of the daisy family (Asteraceae), whose flower-heads and leaves characteristically carry sesquiterpene lactones and flavonoids. The flower-heads are small and downy. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify any named constituent before adding it.]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The classic records a salty, warm drug for bound qi, fullness below the ribs, fright and palpitations, for removing water, for cold and heat between the viscera, and for supplementing the centre and bringing qi down. These are the classic's categories and the Codex does not translate them into modern diagnoses. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. It stands in the lower classic in Sun's edition.

### Preparation & Dosing

The Shennong text gives no preparation and no dose. The Mingyi layer gives only the harvest: the flower, gathered in the fifth month and dried in the sun for twenty days (line 3177). The preface (line 4121) says that drugs differ in suitability for pills, powders, decoction, wine or paste, with no rule for this one. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. *Inula* species are daisies, and members of the daisy family can provoke allergic skin and airway reactions in sensitive people. The downy flower-heads of the Chinese drug are, in current practice, wrapped in cloth when decocted because the fine hairs irritate the throat. ### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(b) Framed.* In Sun's edition the entry stands in the lower classic, whose preface (line 2935) reads 下药，一百二十五种，为左使。主治病以应地。多毒，不可久服。欲除寒热邪气，破积聚，愈疾者，本下经: the lower drugs are "the left envoys, mainly treating disease to correspond with earth; mostly toxic, not to be taken long; for those who wish to remove cold and heat evil qi, break accumulations and gatherings, and heal illness". The paragraph has no 久服 clause and no promise of lightening the body or of not ageing; the classic does not call this drug toxic. Yang's edition files it in the middle class, whose preface says the middle drugs "nourish the nature" (line 1677); the Codex follows Sun and does not raise the status on Yang's heading. The register is therefore (b): framed by the lower class, with no cultivation status attested.

**Practitioner's note.** A lower-class, salty, warm drug for bound qi, fullness and water. The tradition's own grading is a short-course warning, no part beyond the flower and no preparation, dose or course is given, and the two editions disagree on its class. A cultivator has no practice to take from this entry.

### Contraindications

- **Allergy.** Members of the daisy family can cause allergic reactions, and the downy flower-heads can irritate the throat. This is a general statement. [USER: verify]
- **Lower-class drug.** The classic's preface warns that lower-class drugs are mostly toxic and not for long taking; this paragraph does not itself say the drug is toxic.
- **Water, palpitations, fullness.** The uses named are historical record only; swelling, palpitations and chest or rib-side fullness need medical care.
- **Pregnancy, nursing, children.** No safety information was read. [USER: verify]
- **Identity.** Other inulas (including elecampane) are different drugs; the species is our identification. [USER: verify]
- **Quantities.** No dose or duration is given.
