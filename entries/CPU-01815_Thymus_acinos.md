# Thymus acinos — Akinos, Okimoeides (Dioscorides 3-50, 4-28)

## *Thymus acinos, printed "Thymus acinos" on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-50 (with Ocimum pilosum and Acinos vulgaris — "Acinos") and "Thymos acinos [Linnaeus]" on the line for 4-28 (with Ocimastrum, Acinos, Ocimum sylvestre [Fuchs], Clinopodium arvense Ocimi facie [Bauhin], Satureja acinos [in Sprague], Ocimum pilosum and Acinos vulgaris)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM); this entry rests on one text, the Osbaldeston and Wood (2000) English translation of *De Materia Medica*.** The row was added because that text prints the binomial under two chapters. The binomial is the translators' SUGGESTED gloss, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. A search of the Sushruta, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Tibetan text folders for *Thymus acinos* and *Acinos* found no hit for this plant (the Chinese and Tibetan hits are *Phytolacca acinosa*, a different plant). The Assyrian overview table lists *hašû* as "Thyme (Thymus)" at genus level (`Sources/AT_Assyrian/AT_Overview.md`, line 114); that is an overview row, not a text, and the Codex does not read it as an attestation for this species. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The row cites two chapters, and each is short.

*3-50. AKINOS* (heading at line 6956, SUGGESTED line at 6958, "see 3-109, 4-28, 4-176" at 6959, text at 6960), in Book Three, between *3-49 MARON* and *3-51 BAKCHARIS*. It reads, in full: "Acinus or aconus is a herb with a small stalk used in making wreaths for the head, similar to basil but rougher. It has a sweet scent, and is also sown in gardens by some. Taken as a drink it stops discharges of the intestines, and the menstrual flow. Applied, it heals both pannus [opaque thickening of cornea with veins] and erysipela [streptococcal skin infection]. It is also called basilicum sylvestre, and the Romans call it ocimastrum."

*4-28. OKIMOEIDES* (heading at line 8253, SUGGESTED line at 8254, "see 3-50, 3-109, 4-28, 4-176" at 8255, text at lines 8256 to 8260), in Book Four, after *4-27 ECHION* and before *4-29 ERINOS*. It reads: "Ocimoides has leaves similar to basil, and rough branches twenty centimetres long, with pods similar to hyoscyamus [4-69] full of black seed similar to melanthium [3-93]. A decoction of the seed (taken as a drink in wine) is able to cure the viper-bitten and the bites of other snakes. It is also given with myrrh [1-77, 1-73, 4-116] and pepper for sciatica. The root that lies underneath is thin and useless. It is also called philetaerium, echion, scorpiuron, sparganon, althaea, amaranthis, probataea, elaphion, antimimon, porphyris, augion, nemesion, hyaenopsolon, thersites, thermutis, or misopathos, while the Romans call it ocimastrum." The translators' cross-reference line at 8255 lists "4-28" among the chapters to see, which is the chapter itself; the line is the translators', not the text's.

The two chapters are not the same plant in the text. 3-50 describes a garden-sown herb "similar to basil but rougher" with a sweet scent, used for wreaths; 4-28 describes a plant with basil-like leaves, rough branches and pods of black seed, whose seed is used for snakebite. Both are tied by the Roman name *ocimastrum* ("wild basil") and by the translators' cross-references, which also point to 3-109 *KLINOPODION* (line 7511) and 4-176 *PUKNOKOMON* (line 9631). Those two chapters have their own SUGGESTED lines (*Clinopodium vulgare*) and are not part of this row and not read for it beyond their SUGGESTED lines.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter ids 3-50 and 4-28, for *akinos*, *okimoeides* and *Thymus acinos* found one entry on both chapters: I_774 (*Alpine Calamint*, *Acinos alpinus*, tag DMM), whose Dioscorides paragraph says Dioscorides' *akinos* (3-50) and *okimoeides* (4-28) are matched by the translators "to *Acinos* only at genus level". That is a chapter-level duplicate of this row under another binomial, and the two entries are not merged here; the decision is Smith's. I_1081 (*Erinus hispanicus*, 4-29) names 4-28 as the chapter before its own and as sharing a Greek name with its plant. I_1206 (*Teucrium marum*, 3-49) is the chapter before 3-50. I_233 (Thyme, *Thymus vulgaris*) is an entry on a different species. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The composition of the plants Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, the genus *Thymus* and its relatives in the mint family (Lamiaceae) commonly carry volatile oils, among them phenols such as thymol. Nothing is stated here about *Thymus acinos* in particular. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Chapter 3-50 records an aromatic garden herb drunk to stop intestinal discharges and the menstrual flow, and applied to the eye (for *pannus*) and the skin (for *erysipela*). Chapter 4-28 records a seed decoction in wine for snakebite and, with myrrh and pepper, for sciatica. The Codex gives no protocol for any of these. The identification is the translators', and the two chapters do not describe one plant.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these preparations, each with its locus. Chapter 3-50 (line 6960): the herb "taken as a drink"; "applied" for *pannus* and *erysipela*. Chapter 4-28 (lines 8256 to 8260): a decoction of the seed "taken as a drink in wine"; the seed given "with myrrh and pepper". The only measure printed in either chapter is the length of the branches (twenty centimetres). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that a drink of leaves or seed cannot be relied on against a snakebite, which needs urgent medical care, and that eye and skin conditions of the kinds named need assessment. The identification is a translators' suggestion, and the two chapters describe different plants, so they do not show that either is *Thymus acinos*.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 3-50 prints that the herb "has a sweet scent" and is "rougher" than basil. Chapter 4-28 prints no taste, smell or temperament. No warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality is printed for either.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapters offer is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition, in a modest form: the herb is told from basil by roughness (3-50), by the pods "similar to hyoscyamus" with black seed (4-28) and by its thin, useless root; and 3-50 records that some sow it in gardens and use it in wreaths for the head. Neither chapter assigns the plant any status beyond that of a garland herb and a medicinal herb, records an amuletic use or names a deity. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapters gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The only horticultural line is that it is sown in gardens and made into head wreaths (3-50). The useful lesson is the method of telling a wild look-alike from basil by roughness and pod. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapters record an intake that "stops ... the menstrual flow" (3-50) and a use for sciatica with myrrh and pepper (4-28); neither is advice. The seed decoction is offered for snakebite, which needs urgent medical care. The identification is a translators' suggestion and the Lamiaceae include look-alikes of differing safety, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
