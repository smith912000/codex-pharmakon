### Origin & Tradition

Anise is a delicate annual umbellifer whose sweet, liquorice-scented seed (botanically a fruit) has been prized as spice and medicine since deep antiquity in Egypt, Greece and Rome. Dioscorides treats it in Book III among the warming aromatic seeds, and Pliny lists a long catalogue of its virtues. The Romans baked anise into the spiced cake (*mustaceae*) eaten after rich feasts to settle the stomach — an ancestor of the digestive and of the wedding cake — and the seed's place as an after-dinner digestive (in seeds, sweets, and anise liqueurs from ouzo to pastis to arak) has continued unbroken to the present.

Anise's dominant and remarkably consistent medicinal identity is as a **carminative** — a warming, sweet aromatic that dispels digestive wind, eases colic and bloating, settles the stomach and stimulates appetite. From this flow its other classic uses: as a gentle **expectorant** loosening cough and chest phlegm (the sweet aromatic seed in cough syrups and pastilles), as a **galactagogue** to promote milk in nursing mothers, as a mild antispasmodic for griping, and — the gripe-water tradition — as a gentle settler of infant colic (a use now approached with more caution). Its sweetness and safety made it one of the most beloved and child-friendly of the aromatic carminatives.

The seed shares its characteristic aroma and much of its action with the unrelated star anise (*Illicium verum*) and with fennel — all owe their sweet liquorice scent to the same principal constituent, **anethole**. Anise is gentle, food-grade and widely used, with the main cautions being its mild oestrogenic activity (via anethole/dianethole) and the rare allergy/phototoxicity of the Apiaceae family.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** No cultivation status is attested, and none should be manufactured — Dioscorides has no subtle-body or life-nourishing system for a substance to occupy. Anise is treated in *De Materia Medica* Book III among the warming aromatic seeds, described by what it *does* (warms, dispels wind, promotes urine and milk, sweetens the breath) rather than by any effect on a person's vital constitution. What the Dioscoridean tradition genuinely offers a cultivator of this seed is a **discipline of attention**: judging the seed by provenance and freshness, by the strength and sweetness of its aroma (its *anethole* character, in modern terms), and detecting adulteration or staleness by assay of smell and taste. That is a real practice of discernment, not a consolation — but it is a discipline of the gatherer, not a cultivation status of the plant.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** Anise is securely attested in the materia medica as a hot-dry warming carminative (*kasir-e-riyah*), galactagogue (*mudirr-e-laban*) and digestive, but **no cultivation status is recorded for it** in the sources retrieved — its identity here is therapeutic, not life-nourishing. It can be *framed* within the tradition's central cultivation idea — the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture, where a gentle warming digestive supports the natural (digestive) faculty rather than depleting it — but this is the general logic of warming carminatives, not a documented claim that anise conserves or strengthens the *ruh* or the innate heat of this substance specifically. No *mufarrih* (cardiac/gladdening) role is asserted here; that class belongs to a separate work and is not attested for anise in the sources at hand. The honest reading is (b): a well-placed therapeutic warming aromatic, with cultivation framing available but no cultivation status attested.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator uses anise as a gentle, food-grade warming digestive — the after-dinner settler of the windy gut — and the honest register stops there. Neither tradition on this entry's tag line assigns it a life-nourishing or subtle-body status; the Greek contribution is a discipline of selecting good seed by nose and provenance, and the Unani contribution is a warming carminative that supports digestion without the depleting force of a stronger drug. Nothing here supports treating anise as a tonic of the vital constitution; its value is quiet, culinary and conservative.

### Contraindications

- **Hormone-sensitive conditions.** The mild oestrogenic activity warrants caution in oestrogen-sensitive conditions; avoid concentrated medicinal use. [USER: verify.]
- **Concentrated essential oil.** Estragole content makes high concentrated-oil intake inadvisable; normal culinary/tea use is fine.
- **Allergy.** Apiaceae (and anethole) allergy/cross-reactivity occurs; some anise allergy noted.
- **Pregnancy.** Culinary amounts are fine; concentrated medicinal/oil use better avoided. [USER: verify.]
- **Infants.** Use gripe-water-type preparations cautiously and dilute; follow current paediatric guidance.
