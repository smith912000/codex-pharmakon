### Botanical Identity
Deciduous tree or large shrub 3–10m; compound leaves (7–11 leaflets); inconspicuous flowers; small round reddish-to-brown nutlets; bark produces a resin (terebinthine) on wounding; native to the Mediterranean basin, Canary Islands, and western Asia; highly drought-tolerant; often forms symbiotic galls with the aphid *Slavum wertheimae* that produce a traditional food item (the galls are edible and eaten in the Middle East). The resin is the primary drug material, though the fruits/nuts and bark have separate applications.

**Pistacia genus**: The terebinth is related to the mastic tree (*P. lentiscus*, I_103) and to the pistachio (*P. vera*) — the familiar culinary nut. The pharmacological properties of *P. terebinthus* differ from mastic while sharing some chemical relatives (the terpenoid resin compounds).

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Dioscorides / Greco-Roman (DMM)
Book I, Chapter 91: "Terebinthine resin — warming; for bladder and chest conditions; diuretic; beneficial for blood spitting and ruptures; topical for skin conditions and mange." Applications: chest/respiratory expectorant; diuretic for kidney/bladder; topical antiseptic and skin cleanser; against intestinal parasites.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine / Ibn Sīnā (CM)
*Baṭm* — warming, drying. Applications: similar to mastic but more heating; respiratory expectorant; diuretic; for nerve pain and weakness; topical for joint disease; tooth strengthener. Ibn Sīnā notes that terebinth resin is stronger-acting than mastic.

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (EP)
Terebinth resin appears in Egyptian compound formulas for skin conditions and respiratory complaints; the Egyptians imported Levantine resins extensively, and terebinth was among the most widely used.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)
*Buṭumtu* (terebinth) is one of the major pharmacological trees of AT medicine — the resin, bark, nuts, and leaves appear in dozens of AT formulas for wounds, skin disease, fever, and ritual fumigation. The terebinth's prominence in AT reflects its geographic availability in Mesopotamia and Levant.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Antiseptic / Antimicrobial (Primary — Pinenes + Terpineol)

**α-Pinene + terpineol + β-pinene**: The turpentine resin is one of the oldest natural antiseptics in the Mediterranean world. Mechanisms:
- Disruption of bacterial cell membranes (terpene intercalation)
- Inhibition of bacterial biofilm formation
- Fungicidal (Candida, dermatophytes) at topically achievable concentrations
- The topical wound antiseptic application across all 4 traditions is mechanistically grounded

**Historical pharmaceutical turpentine**: Oil of turpentine (steam-distilled from *Pinus* species and from *Pistacia* — the names were used interchangeably after large-scale *Pinus* exploitation) was the primary surgical wound dressing from antiquity through the 19th century. The application pre-dates the germ theory by 3,000 years but is pharmacologically valid.

### 5.2 Expectorant / Bronchial (α-Pinene)

**α-Pinene**: The same mechanism as juniper (I_108) — bronchodilation and mucolytic action. *P. terebinthus* resin provides one of the highest natural α-pinene concentrations of any plant source; the expectorant application in DMM and CM is mechanistically identical to the modern use of eucalyptus oil (eucalyptol/1,8-cineole from *Eucalyptus* spp.) for respiratory conditions.

### 5.3 Diuretic (Terpineol + α-Pinene)

Mild renal tubular irritation → increased diuresis → the traditional diuretic and kidney/bladder application. Same mechanism as juniper's terpinen-4-ol.

### 5.4 Anthelmintic

**α-Pinene + monoterpene complex**: Direct toxic to intestinal parasites (Ascaris, tapeworm) at high oral doses — the traditional anthelmintic application. This was the primary pre-modern anthelmintic for Greek and Roman physicians, used at doses high enough to be directly toxic to parasites while tolerable to the host.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Antiseptic**: The pharmacological validity of turpentine as an antiseptic is unquestioned; its topical use declined in the 20th century not because of failure but because synthetic antiseptics (iodine, chlorhexidine) are less toxic to tissues.

**Respiratory**: The expectorant-bronchodilator mechanism is validated by the analogy with eucalyptol (1,8-cineole) inhalation for bronchitis (Cochrane review positive for cineole in acute bronchitis, 2013). Terebinth/turpentine inhalation operates by an identical mechanism.

**Anthelmintic**: Oil of turpentine was used as an anthelmintic in 19th-century medicine; toxic margin is narrow. Modern anthelmintics have replaced it.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The word we spill**: The English word "turpentine" — so common it is used in hardware stores globally without a second thought — is a direct etymological descent from *Pistacia terebinthus*. Medieval Latin *terebinthina* (resin of the terebinth) became Old French *terebentine*, then English *turpentine*. In the 14th–16th centuries, large-scale European forestry operations began distilling turpentine from *Pinus* species, and the word transferred to the cheaper pine-derived product — but the name remained, carrying within it the ghost of the terebinth tree from the Mediterranean whose resin had been the original "turpentine" for 3,000 years.

**The Mamre terebinth**: Genesis 18:1 — "The LORD appeared to Abraham near the great trees of Mamre while he was sitting at the entrance to his tent in the heat of the day." The "great trees" (elon or elah — terebinth) at Mamre were a site of visitation, treaty, and burial throughout the biblical narrative. The terebinth at Mamre was an identifiable, landmark tree — probably a very old specimen of *P. terebinthus* that had been a sacred tree for generations before the biblical narrative was written. This intersection of tree-as-landmark, tree-as-sacred-site, and tree-as-medicine is not coincidental — the terebinth's medicinal reputation made it a cared-for, protected tree, which gave it the longevity to become a landmark.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

| Form | Dose | Application |
|------|------|-------------|
| Resin (raw, small amounts) | 0.5–1g internally | Expectorant; urinary — traditional internal use; not recommended in modern practice without supervision |
| Steam inhalation (1–2ml resin in hot water) | Inhale vapour 10 min | Bronchial; respiratory antiseptic |
| Topical (resin diluted 10–20% in olive oil) | Apply to wounds, skin | Antiseptic; wound care; skin conditions |
| Turpentine liniment (traditional pharmacy form) | Apply to joints, muscles | Counter-irritant for arthritis/rheumatism |
| Galls (edible — traditional Middle Eastern food) | As food | Nutritional/astringent |

**CAUTION on internal use**: Pure oil of turpentine is significantly irritating to mucous membranes and kidneys; internal use requires careful dosing. The traditional preparations used the whole resin at modest doses rather than the distilled oil.

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM (cross-reference)**: Acrid, bitter; warm. Channels: Lung (肺), Kidney (腎). Expectorant, diuretic, antimicrobial; topical warming analgesic (counter-irritant).

**Ayurvedic (cross-reference)**: Pungent; heating. Reduces Kapha ↓↓. *Śvāsahara* (anti-asthmatic), *Mūtralā* (diuretic). Not classical Ayurvedic herb.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Kidney disease**: Terpene renal tubular irritation — avoid in nephritis, CKD
- **Pregnancy**: Avoid internal use — irritant; potential uterotonic effects from monoterpene content
- **Children internal use**: Highly toxic to children — avoid all internal use in paediatric patients (kidney and CNS effects from turpentine poisoning are severe)
- **Topical skin barrier**: On broken skin, can be absorbed systemically at toxic levels — do not apply full-strength turpentine to large wound areas
- **Fire hazard**: The resin and essential oil are flammable — handle with appropriate care
