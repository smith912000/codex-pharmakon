# Colocynth — bitter apple, bitter cucumber, vine of Sodom, handhal

### Origin & Tradition

Colocynth is a trailing desert vine of the gourd family, bearing a smooth, mottled fruit the size of a small orange that ripens yellow and dries to a light, papery shell around an intensely **bitter** white pulp. It grows across the deserts and dry margins of North Africa, the Near East and into India, and is among the most ancient and dreaded of the **drastic purgatives.** Dioscorides treats it in the context of the violent cathartics; the Bible's "wild gourds" gathered into the pot of poisoned pottage (2 Kings, "there is death in the pot") are traditionally identified with colocynth — a memory of its dangerous bitterness. In Arabic and Unani medicine it is **handhal**, a standard but carefully-handled purgative, and across Saharan and Near-Eastern folk practice the bitter pulp is a famous (and feared) remedy for stubborn constipation, "cold" abdominal stagnation, and as an emmenagogue and abortifacient — the last a dangerous traditional use.

The therapeutic logic of colocynth throughout the tradition is the **forcible evacuation** of the bowel: a drastic cathartic for the most obstinate constipation, for "removing phlegm and black bile" in the humoral scheme, and for conditions (dropsy, certain fevers, "torpor") that the old pharmacy treated by violent purgation. Because its action is so harsh — gripping, painful, capable of bloody catharsis and worse — the careful traditions always compounded it (the famous compound purgative **colocynth pill**, *pilula colocynthidis*, combined it with milder aperients and aromatics to temper and direct its action) rather than giving the raw drug alone.

Colocynth's bitter principle is so concentrated that the plant stands as a near-archetype of the cucurbitacin-bearing drastic purgatives, alongside its cousins the squirting cucumber (I_223) and the bryonies (I_244). It belongs to the small class of medicines that were genuinely useful in an age with few effective purgatives, and genuinely dangerous — a balance that placed it always in the specialist's hands.

### Active Components

- **Cucurbitacins (notably cucurbitacin E and related; the bitter glycosides "colocynthin"/elaterin-type principles)** — the intensely bitter, **drastically purgative and cytotoxic** triterpenoid constituents. They irritate the intestinal mucosa, provoking violent peristalsis and fluid secretion (the cathartic action), and are toxic in quantity (mucosal inflammation, bloody diarrhoea, systemic toxicity). Cucurbitacins also have documented **antiproliferative/cytotoxic** activity of pharmacological interest.
- **Colocynthin (cucurbitacin glycoside fraction)** — the traditional name for the bitter purgative principle of the pulp.
- **Fixed seed oil** — the **seeds**, by contrast, are far less bitter (the cucurbitacins concentrate in the pulp) and yield an edible-grade oil used in some desert cultures; the toxic purgative action resides in the **pulp**, not primarily the seed kernel. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Recorded as **historical and specialist material.** Colocynth is not a self-administered remedy; the raw pulp is a violent and dangerous cathartic.

1. **Drastic purgative for obstinate constipation (historical/specialist).** The defining use — forcible evacuation where milder aperients failed; always best compounded and tempered.
2. **Humoral "phlegm/black bile" purge (historical).** Used in the old scheme to evacuate cold, viscid or melancholic humours, and in dropsy and torpid states.
3. **Emmenagogue / abortifacient (traditional — dangerous).** A documented and hazardous folk use; recorded, emphatically not recommended.
4. **Antidiabetic/cytotoxic interest (research).** Cucurbitacins and colocynth extracts are studied for hypoglycaemic and antiproliferative activity — modern pharmacological interest, distinct from traditional purgative use. [USER: verify before asserting efficacy.]
5. **Topical counter-irritant (traditional).** Pulp applied externally for rheumatic pain in some folk traditions.

### Preparation & Dosing

**No therapeutic home dose is given. Colocynth is a drastic, toxic cathartic capable of dangerous and fatal poisoning.**

- **Compound colocynth pill (historical pharmacy):** the raw drug was virtually never given alone; *pilula colocynthidis composita* combined a small amount with aloes, scammony and aromatics to temper and direct it. Specialist/historical.
- **Pulp (raw):** a poison in any but minute, expertly compounded amounts; not a home preparation. [USER: do not reproduce dose figures.]
- **Seed oil:** the de-bittered seed is a separate, food/industrial-oil context in some cultures — not the purgative.

Safety framing: colocynth overdose causes violent bloody diarrhoea, severe cramping, mucosal damage, dehydration, and can be fatal; it is a medical emergency. The abortifacient use is associated with serious maternal harm.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM / Galenic humoral degree:** Hot and Dry in a high degree (commonly third/fourth) — an extreme, "drastic" purgative class; tempered in compounds because of its violence.
- **Unani/Arabic mapping:** *handhal* — hot-dry; a powerful mushil (purgative) for cold/phlegmatic and melancholic conditions; classically corrected (islah) with aromatics and gums to reduce griping.
- **Kenetica Qi/Shen tier:** ◉ **Qi** — at the extreme of the downward-draining/eliminating axis (forcible evacuation), kin to the other cucurbitacin cathartics; a "force-out stagnation" agent of the harshest grade.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian (EP).** The Egyptian tag is carried on this entry's tag line but is not developed in its record — the retained detailed sources name Greco-Roman, Hebrew/Biblical, Unani and Saharan-folk lines, not an Egyptian one. Even where the Egyptian corpus handles drastic evacuants, it is indication-organised: it records the purge sought for an obstructed bowel, not an action on the person's *ka* or *ib*, and no *heka* recitation or deity attribution is attested for this gourd in the retrieved sources. Cultivation status: **(c) absent**.

**Assyrian (AT).** No Assyrian or Mesopotamian record is developed in the entry, and its retained tag line names none. Under the standing blocking warning for Assyrian substance identifications, no cultivation status is claimed here; illness in that tradition is classed by the bodily agency it is set against rather than by a vital-body grade, and this bitter desert gourd is not carried in the entry's sources under any such heading. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides places the wild bitter gourd among the violent cathartics of *De Materia Medica* (Book IV), and his tradition carries no subtle-body system into which a purgative could be given a cultivation status. What it offers a cultivator is instead a discipline of attention — recognising the true drug in the light, papery fruit and its intensely bitter pith, judging the ferocity of its action, and knowing it must be tempered in a compound rather than taken raw. That is a practice of provenance and sensory judgement, not a vital-body doctrine. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** In the Unani corpus colocynth is *handhal*, a hot-and-dry *mushil* (purgative) corrected (*islah*) with aromatics and gums to restrain its griping. The tradition's strongest cultivation idea — the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture across a whole regimen — is a longevity frame of temperance and husbandry, and a drastic evacuant of this order sits outside that frame rather than serving it; no gladdening, heart-strengthening (*mufarrih*) role is claimed for so violent a drug. It is genuinely used, therefore, but carries a therapeutic standing only, not a cultivation status. **(b) framed.**

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with colocynth. It builds nothing and conserves nothing; it is taken — if ever — only as a specialist's emergency evacuant, historically never given raw but compounded and tempered in the colocynth pill, for a defined obstruction and then stopped. Every tradition on this entry files it among the harshest drugs, and the Hebrew memory of "death in the pot" is the plainest warning the corpus gives. Its place is the specialist's shelf, not the cultivator's regimen.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy — absolute.** Drastic purgative and traditional abortifacient; serious harm; contraindicated.
- **Inflammatory bowel disease, GI obstruction, haemorrhoids, anal fissure.** A violent cathartic is contraindicated in any inflamed, fragile or obstructed gut.
- **Children, the elderly, the debilitated, the dehydrated.** Fluid/electrolyte loss is dangerous; contraindicated.
- **Renal/cardiac compromise.** Catharsis-driven fluid and electrolyte shifts are hazardous.
- **No raw or casual use.** Specialist/historical framing throughout; the raw pulp is a poison.
