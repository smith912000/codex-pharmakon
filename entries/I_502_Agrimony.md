# I-502 | Agrimony (*Agrimonia eupatoria* L.)

**Volume I — Plant-Based Substances**
**Family:** Rosaceae (subfamily Rosoideae)
**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (hepatic, digestive, astringent) primary | ◈ Jing (tissue repair, anti-inflammatory) secondary

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary binomial:** *Agrimonia eupatoria* L. (1753, *Species Plantarum*)

**Synonyms & historical names:**
- *Agrimonia officinalis* Lam.
- *Eupatorium* (misapplied in medieval literature; confused with *Eupatorium cannabinum*)

**Common names:**
- English: Common agrimony, church steeples, liverwort, sticklewort, cocklebur
- German: *Gewöhnlicher Odermennig*
- French: *Aigremoine eupatoire*
- Italian: *Agrimonia*
- Spanish: *Agrimonia*, *Hierba de San Guillermo*
- Arabic: *Ghāfith* (Ibn Sīnā's designation)
- Chinese: 龙芽草 (*lóng yá cǎo*, "dragon tooth grass") — *A. pilosa* Ledeb. = TCM species
- Greek: *Agrimonia* (field-medicine plant); the epithet *eupatoria* honors Mithridates VI Eupator (120–63 BCE) — the toxicology king who systematized antidote research

**Botanical authentication:**
- Erect perennial herb 30–100 cm; pinnate leaves with alternating large and small leaflets (characteristic of genus); terminal spike of small 5-petaled yellow flowers; fruit (achene) covered with hooked bristles that cling to fur/clothing (namesake "cocklebur"/"sticklewort"); leaves aromatic when crushed (essential oil); stem hairy; root fusiform, brown, tannin-rich
- *A. eupatoria*: leaflets typically 5–9 large + small alternating pairs; groove on fruit encircles upper portion and the hooked bristles point outward to form a shallow cup = European standard (vs *A. pilosa* = Chinese species with leaflets more uniform; fruit groove encircles fruit fully)
- Authentic *A. eupatoria*: slightly apricot-aromatic when crushed; tannin taste + mild bitterness; hooked burrs cling to wool with remarkable tenacity (utilized in Velcro biomimicry)

**Taxonomy note:** *Agrimonia* contains ~15 species globally. European pharmacopoeia: *A. eupatoria*. Chinese Pharmacopoeia 2020: 仙鹤草 (*xiān hè cǎo*, "immortal crane herb") = *A. pilosa* Ledeb. — phytochemically similar but distinct species. *A. procera* (fragrant agrimony) = European woodland species with different terpene profile. For Codex purposes: *A. eupatoria* primary; TCM entries reference *A. pilosa* equivalency.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

The cross-traditional hepatic, digestive, and wound-healing record for agrimony reflects its dual tannin/flavonoid pharmacology: condensed proanthocyanidins provide astringent anti-diarrheal and wound-contraction activity (consistent across DMM, CM, SBJ); flavonoids (luteolin, apigenin, quercetin glycosides) provide anti-inflammatory and mild hepatoprotective activity (consistent with "liver" indications in DMM and CM).

The TCM parallel species *A. pilosa* adds a dimension absent from European experience: agrimophol, a phloroglucinol derivative present in *A. pilosa* roots, is a potent and selective taenicidal agent that kills *Taenia* tapeworm proglottids. This extends the Rosaceae tannin-based antiparasitic theme established in *Rosa* and *Sanguisorba* entries — phloroglucinol antiparasitic activity is a recurring Codex pattern across Rosaceae subfamily members (cf. male fern *Dryopteris filix-mas*, historically the dominant taenicidal agent in Western medicine).

The epithet *eupatoria* connects agrimony to the same cultural complex as other "Mithridate" herbs — Mithridates VI of Pontus being the archetype of systematic pharmacological experimentation in antiquity, and the associated *Eupatorium* complex (agrimony, hemp agrimony *E. cannabinum*, boneset *E. perfoliatum*) sharing loose historical attribution to his pharmacopoeia.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Tannins (dominant constituents):**
- Condensed proanthocyanidins (catechin/epicatechin oligomers): 4–10% DW; high tannin content explains historical astringent, anti-diarrheal, and wound applications
- Ellagitannins: agrimoniin — a dimeric ellagitannin, 1–3% DW; most studied individual tannin; antitumor activity demonstrated in vitro
- Pedunculagin, casuarictin: additional ellagitannins
- Agrimonolide: lactone tannin-related compound

**Flavonoids:**
- Luteolin-7-glucoside (cynaroside): 0.3–1.0% DW; primary flavone
- Apigenin-7-glucoside (apigetrin): 0.2–0.8% DW
- Quercetin-3-glucoside: 0.1–0.5% DW
- Kaempferol-3-glucoside: present
- Vitexin (apigenin-8-C-glucoside): trace (same GABA-A PAM compound as in passionflower I-500 — appearing recurrently across Codex families)
- Catechin, epicatechin (monomeric flavan-3-ols): present as tannin precursors
- Tiliroside (kaempferol-3-O-(6''-E-p-coumaroyl)-glucoside): anti-inflammatory mechanism

**Triterpenes:**
- Ursolic acid: 0.3–1.2% DW; NF-κB inhibition; same compound recurring across Lamiaceae entries
- Oleanolic acid: 0.2–0.8% DW; hepatoprotective
- Tormentic acid: characteristic Rosaceae triterpene

**Phenolic acids:**
- Chlorogenic acid: 0.2–0.5% DW
- Caffeic acid derivatives: present
- Ellagic acid: hydrolysis product of ellagitannins

**Bitter compounds:**
- Agrimol A, B: phloroglucinol derivatives; bitter taste receptors
- Agrimonolide: bitter lactone

**Essential oil (<0.5% DW):**
- α-ionone, β-ionone: characteristic violet-like aroma
- Geraniol, linalool: minor terpenes

**Polysaccharides:**
- Pectin and heteropolysaccharides: immune-modulating activity in vitro (macrophage activation)

**Vitamins/minerals:**
- Vitamin K: moderate content; relevant to hemostasis (traditional bleeding indication)
- Silicic acid: traditional connective tissue support claim

**PhEur standardization:** *Agrimoniae herba* — ≥2.0% tannins expressed as pyrogallol (hydrolyzable tannin hydrolysis marker).

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Astringent/anti-diarrheal (tannin-mediated):**
Condensed proanthocyanidins and ellagitannins bind to mucosal proteins, forming a protective protein-tannin complex that:
- Reduces mucosal permeability (decreased fluid secretion → anti-diarrheal)
- Precipitates surface proteins → mechanical wound contraction
- Inhibits bacterial adhesion to mucosal surfaces
- Reduces inflammation via reduced NF-κB signaling

These mechanisms are consistent with the DMM, CM, and SBJ anti-diarrheal and dysentery indications.

**Anti-inflammatory:**
- Luteolin and apigenin: COX-2 inhibition, PGE₂ suppression (same mechanism established across Lamiales entries)
- Ursolic acid: NF-κB pathway inhibition, reduced IL-6/TNF-α
- Tiliroside: p38 MAPK pathway inhibition; validated anti-inflammatory in rodent models
- Agrimoniin (ellagitannin): NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition in vitro

**Hepatoprotective:**
- Luteolin, quercetin: oxidative stress protection in hepatocytes; CYP2E1 partial inhibition (reduces toxic metabolite formation)
- Oleanolic acid: liver enzyme normalization in CCl₄ hepatotoxicity animal models
- Tiliroside: hepatoprotective in alcohol-induced liver injury rodent model (Yildirim 2001)
- Mechanisms explain DMM/CM liver application; insufficient clinical trial data for firm hepatoprotective clinical recommendation

**Hemostasis (*A. pilosa* / TCM species):**
- Vitamin K content: modest procoagulant contribution
- Tannin-mediated wound contraction
- Agrimonolide: platelet aggregation promotion (in vitro)
- These mechanisms support TCM use of 仙鹤草 for bleeding disorders (hematemesis, hematuria, excessive menstruation)

**Antiparasitic (agrimophol — *A. pilosa* root specifically):**
- Agrimophol (dimethyl phloroglucinol derivative): selective paralytic toxin for *Taenia* tapeworm musculature; IC50 well below cytotoxic threshold for mammalian cells
- Mechanism analogous to phloroglucinol derivatives in male fern (*Dryopteris*) — the classical taenicidal agent
- *A. eupatoria* root lacks agrimophol; this activity is specific to *A. pilosa* root fraction

**Antimicrobial:**
- Agrimoniin and catechins: membrane disruption of *S. aureus*, *E. coli*, *Streptococcus* spp. at concentrations achievable topically
- Ellagic acid: antifungal vs *Candida albicans*
- Consistent with wound healing use

**Antitumor (in vitro/preclinical — Level D):**
- Agrimoniin: cytotoxic against multiple cancer cell lines (MCF-7 breast, A549 lung); pro-apoptotic via caspase activation
- Not clinically validated; not a treatment recommendation

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Anti-diarrheal/GI — Level B/C:**
- Richter G 1983 (*Z Phytother*): uncontrolled clinical series; agrimony tea effective for acute childhood diarrhea; n=37
- Commission E Monograph (1994): approved for mild, nonspecific, acute diarrhea and mild inflammation of oral and pharyngeal mucosa
- EMA/HMPC (2009): well-established use for mild diarrhea; traditional use for minor oral inflammation
- Mechanism clearly established; clinical data largely pre-RCT era; consistent positive outcome across case series

**Wound healing/topical — Level C:**
- Traditional veterinary and human wound application across European folk medicine; topical agrimony decoction for wounds, abrasions, pharyngeal inflammation, gargle for tonsillitis
- Limited controlled trial data; traditional use monograph level only
- Consistent with tannin protein-precipitating wound-healing mechanism

**Liver/hepatic — Level C:**
- No RCTs for hepatic indications
- Level D evidence: multiple animal and in vitro hepatoprotective studies
- Traditional use well-documented; Level C classification appropriate; not recommended as primary hepatic therapy

**Diabetes/glycemia — Level B (emerging):**
- Swanston-Flatt SK et al. 1989 (*Diabetic Medicine* 6:52-55): *A. eupatoria* aqueous extract → blood glucose reduction in streptozotocin-diabetic mice
- Gray AM, Flatt PR 1998 (*Journal of Nutrition* 128:2319-2323): *A. eupatoria* — improved glucose tolerance, stimulated insulin secretion (Level D/B)
- Possible mechanism: inhibition of intestinal α-glucosidase by tannins; insulin secretagogue effect of flavonoids
- No RCTs; not a clinical recommendation

**Blood pressure — Level D:**
- Rodent antihypertensive studies; mechanism unclear; no clinical data

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Mithridates and the tradition of universal antidotes:**
The epithet *eupatoria* links agrimony etymologically to Mithridates VI Eupator of Pontus (120–63 BCE), arguably antiquity's most pharmacologically obsessed monarch. Mithridates is credited with consuming small daily doses of poisons to build tolerance — an early systematic exploration of dose-response pharmacology — and with assembling a 54-ingredient antidote (the *mithridate*) that circulated in various forms for 1,600 years. Whether agrimony was genuinely in his formulary or simply named for him by Dioscorides to add prestige is uncertain, but the association placed it in the elite tier of classical pharmacopoeia.

**"Liverwort" — the liver-leaf doctrine:**
The vernacular "liverwort" applied to agrimony (distinct from the bryophytes also called liverworts) reflects the doctrine of signatures: the pinnate leaves were said to resemble the lobed liver. Dioscorides' association with hepatic disease, perpetuated by Ibn Sīnā and through European herbalism, may represent either genuine observation of anti-inflammatory hepatic effects or signature-driven attribution — or both simultaneously. The tannin and flavonoid mechanisms do provide a plausible hepatoprotective basis independent of morphological reasoning.

**"Church steeples" — the morphological name:**
The tall, erect flowering spike of agrimony, studded with small yellow flowers, resembles a Gothic church steeple. This English vernacular name captures a purely aesthetic botanical observation that happens to also describe the plant's growth form accurately (30–100 cm, strictly upright, terminal spike).

**The clinging burr and seed dispersal as pharmacological metaphor:**
The hooked bristles of *Agrimonia* fruits are extraordinarily effective epizoochorous seed dispersers — clinging tenaciously to wool, fur, and clothing. Medieval herb-gatherers considered this tenacity a signature of the plant's "holding" quality: stopping bleeding, stopping diarrhea, "holding" the body's fluids. Whether or not this is pharmacologically valid reasoning (astringent tannins do "hold" in the relevant sense), it represents a sophisticated phenomenological observation about the plant's macroscopic properties that correlates with its microscopic mechanisms.

**Use in Bach Flower Remedies:**
Edward Bach (1886–1936) designated agrimony as one of his 38 flower remedies — for "those who hide mental torture behind a cheerful face." This assigns agrimony a psychological rather than physical indication, but one consistent with traditional European nervine uses (tension, restlessness, mental unease associated with physical symptoms). Bach's system has no pharmacological mechanism basis, but the phenomenological tradition it encodes is a genuine reflection of centuries of herbal usage observation.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Preparations and doses (evidence-based):**
- **Tea/infusion:** 3–5 g dried aerial herb per 250 mL water; steep 10–15 minutes; 3× daily between meals; for diarrhea and digestive complaints; primary evidence-base preparation
- **Tincture (1:5, 45% ethanol):** 1–4 mL 3× daily; higher flavonoid and tannin extraction than aqueous; better for hepatic/systemic use
- **Fluid extract (1:1, 25% ethanol):** 1–2 mL 3× daily
- **Standardized extract:** Limited commercial standardization; PhEur ≥2.0% tannins as pyrogallol is quality marker

**Commission E approved indications:**
- Internal: mild, nonspecific, acute diarrhea
- Topical: mild inflammation of oral and pharyngeal mucosa (gargling preparation)

**EMA traditional use monograph (2009):** Same indications as Commission E; 30 days maximum for diarrhea (see GP if not resolved).

**Topical use:**
- Strong decoction (20 g dried herb per 500 mL water, boiled 15 min): wound washing, abrasion care, leg ulcer soaking
- Gargle: 2–3 g herb per 250 mL water; inflamed throat, tonsillitis, mouth ulcers; traditional European pharmacy standard

**Precautions:**
- Tannin content: high-dose long-term use may interfere with iron absorption (bind dietary iron); separate from iron supplements by 2+ hours
- Drug interactions: theoretical tannin-mediated drug binding (similar to black tea interaction with certain antibiotics); separate doses by 2 hours
- *Vitamin K content:* patients on warfarin should exercise caution (consistent daily dose minimizes variability in effect)
- Pregnancy: insufficient data; avoid internal use (uterine effects of tannins not established but precaution standard)
- Duration limit: acute diarrhea — 3–5 days; if unresolved, medical evaluation required

**TCM parallel (*A. pilosa* — 仙鹤草):**
- Standard TCM dose: 10–15 g dried herb decoction; up to 30–60 g for bleeding
- Hemostatic indication: differentiate from *A. eupatoria* (European species lacks equivalent TCM validation)
- Agrimophol taenicidal: *A. pilosa* root extract specific; dose 60–100 g root decoction for tapeworm (traditional; no modern RCT; WHO Traditional Medicine database documents usage)

**Authentication considerations:**
- Verify presence of alternating large/small pinnate leaflets (diagnostic for genus)
- Hooked bristle fruits on spike = distinguishing Rosaceae character
- Tannin taste should be prominent; mild aromatic quality (ionone-type notes in authentic material)
- Confusion risk: *Eupatorium cannabinum* (hemp agrimony) — Asteraceae; different plant entirely despite shared "agrimony" name in some regions; check species name
