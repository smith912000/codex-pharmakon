# Lagoecia cuminoides — Kuminon emeron (Dioscorides 3-69), Cuminum sylvestre

## *Lagoëcia cuminoides, the only binomial the translators print for this chapter (plate caption and SUGGESTED line; "Common Wild Cumin")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the plate caption and SUGGESTED line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The SUGGESTED line (line 7139) reads "Lagoëcia cuminoides — Common Wild Cumin", and the plate caption (line 7137) reads "Lagoecia cuminoides after THIEBAULT - 1881".

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is printed as *3-69. KUMINON EMERON*, heading at line 7135, SUGGESTED line at 7139, text at line 7140. It stands in Book Three, in the section headed "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants". The translators' index gives *Lagoëcia cuminoides* at page 444 (lines 11781 and 16246).

The whole chapter reads: "Cuminum sylvestre grows in Lycia, Galatia in Asia, and Carthage in Spain. These are the most effective. It is a little shrub with a thin stalk twenty centimetres long, on which are four or five little leaves (as it were) sawnaround with incisions (like gingidium [2-167]). It has five or six little round, soft heads on the top, in which is the husky seed, sharper to the taste than the cultivated. It grows in hilly places. The seed is taken in a drink with water for griping and gaseousness. With vinegar it soothes hiccups. It is taken with wine for the poison of venomous creatures and moisture of the stomach. Chewed and applied with honey and grapes it takes away bruises. Applied with the same [things] it cures inflammation from stones [urinary, kidney]. There is also another kind of wild cumin similar to the cultivated. Out of every flower it sends out little horns lifted up in which is the seed (similar to melanthium [3-93]). Taken in a drink this is an excellent remedy for those bitten by snakes. It helps those troubled with slow painful urination and stones [urinary, kidney], and those who urinate drops of blood. Afterwards let them drink boiled apium [3-77] seeds. The Romans call it cuminum agreste, and some call it cuminum silvaticum."

So the chapter covers two wild cumins. The first is a little shrub with a thin stalk about twenty centimetres long, four or five finely cut leaves, five or six round soft heads on top holding a husky seed "sharper to the taste than the cultivated", growing in hilly places and found at its best in Lycia, Galatia in Asia and Carthage in Spain. The second is "another kind", similar to the cultivated cumin, which sends out "little horns" from every flower, with a seed like that of *melanthium*. The SUGGESTED line is printed once for the whole chapter and does not say which of the two kinds the translators' *Lagoëcia* is meant to represent. The chapter names no other plant by foreign or synonym names beyond the Roman *cuminum agreste* and *cuminum silvaticum*.

**Heading and text do not match.** The chapter before, *3-68. KUMINON AGRION* (heading at line 7128), has the SUGGESTED line "Cuminum cyminum, Cuminum odoratum — Cumin" and begins "Cumin is cultivated." So the heading printed over the cultivated-cumin text is *AGRION* and the heading printed over the wild-cumin text is *EMEROS*. Elsewhere in this edition the pair is used in the other sense: chapter 2-165 is headed *THRIDAX EMEROS* and its text opens "Lactuca (the garden lettuce)", while 2-166 is headed *THRIDAX AGRIA* and opens "Wild lettuce". The two cumin headings therefore look transposed. Whether the transposition is in the edition or in the Greek manuscripts used is not stated in the lines read. [USER: verify] This entry takes the text, not the heading, as the evidence, so it speaks of wild cumin.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a wild cumin whose seed is drunk in water for griping and gas, with vinegar for hiccups, and with wine against the poison of venomous creatures and "moisture of the stomach"; it is chewed and applied with honey and grapes to bruises and to inflammation from stones. The second kind is drunk for snake bite, slow painful urination, stones and blood in the urine. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol. Snake and other bites, urinary stones and blood in the urine need emergency or medical care, and the identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

3-69 gives these preparations: the seed "taken in a drink with water"; "with vinegar" for hiccups; "taken with wine"; "chewed and applied with honey and grapes" for bruises, and "applied with the same [things]" for inflammation from stones; and, after the second kind, "let them drink boiled apium [3-77] seeds". No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** 3-69 prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality. It prints only that the wild seed is "sharper to the taste than the cultivated". (The words "hot, astringent, and drying" belong to the cultivated cumin in 3-68, a different chapter.)

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: provenance (Lycia, Galatia in Asia and Carthage in Spain are named as the places where the wild cumin is "the most effective"), habitat (hilly places), plant form (a thin stalk twenty centimetres long, finely cut leaves, round soft heads), the taste of the seed (sharper than the cultivated), and the telling apart of two kinds. It assigns the plant no status beyond that of a household drug. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a short list of digestive, urinary and antidote uses with no stated amounts, so it is history and not a practice. The useful lesson is the care the chapter takes over place of origin and over which of two wild cumins is meant.

### Contraindications

Snake bite, the poison of venomous creatures, urinary stones and blood in the urine are conditions for medical care, and a home preparation must not replace it. No safety information for pregnancy was read. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion for a chapter with two kinds, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
