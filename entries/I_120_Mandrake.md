### Botanical Identity
Stemless perennial herb; large rosette of wrinkled leaves at ground level; bell-shaped purple-greenish flowers; yellow-orange aromatic fruits ("mandrake apples"); large forked brown taproot (often resembling a human figure — the source of the plant's anthropomorphic folklore); native to the Mediterranean basin and Middle East. The root is the primary drug material (highest tropane alkaloid content); the fruit and leaves are also active.

**TOXICITY**: *Mandragora* contains the tropane alkaloids hyoscyamine, scopolamine (hyoscine), and atropine — the same anticholinergic deliriant/anaesthetic compounds found in henbane (*Hyoscyamus*, I_121), deadly nightshade (*Atropa belladonna*), and datura. It is a potent anticholinergic poison and deliriant. Historical use as a surgical anaesthetic operated at the dangerous boundary of anticholinergic toxicity. This is documented as historical pharmacology — NOT a recommendation for use.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Dioscorides / Greco-Roman (DMM)
Book IV, Chapter 75: Dioscorides describes mandrake wine as a surgical anaesthetic — "given to those who are about to be cut or cauterised, that they may not feel the pain." This is one of the earliest explicit descriptions of surgical general anaesthesia in the Western medical literature. Applications: anaesthetic/soporific (primary); for pain; for insomnia; topical for inflammation; as an aphrodisiac/fertility aid. Dioscorides specifies dose carefully due to the danger.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine / Ibn Sīnā (CM)
*Yabrūḥ* — cold and dry (3rd degree). Applications: anaesthetic for surgery; soporific for insomnia; analgesic; for melancholy; topical anti-inflammatory. Ibn Sīnā formalised the mandrake-based "soporific sponge" (*spongia somnifera*) anaesthetic technique that became central to medieval surgery.

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (EP)
Mandrake fruit ("love apples") appears in Egyptian art associated with eroticism and altered consciousness; medical preparations using mandrake for pain and sleep are consistent with the EP therapeutic context.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)
Mandrake in AT magico-medical preparations for pain, sleep, and "possession" (the deliriant effect interpreted as spirit interaction).

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Anticholinergic Anaesthesia / Sedation (Primary — Scopolamine)

**Scopolamine (hyoscine)**: Crosses the blood-brain barrier (more readily than atropine) and blocks central muscarinic acetylcholine receptors:
- CNS depression → drowsiness, sedation, sleep (the soporific effect)
- Anterograde amnesia → the patient does not remember the surgical procedure (a key feature of the anaesthetic application — even if pain was incompletely blocked, the amnesia removed the memory of it)
- At higher doses: delirium, hallucination, unconsciousness

The combination of sedation + amnesia + analgesia (via the general CNS depression) made mandrake the most effective pre-modern surgical anaesthetic. Scopolamine is still used in modern anaesthesia as a premedication (for its amnestic and antisialagogue effects) and for motion sickness and palliative care (the transdermal scopolamine patch).

### 5.2 Antispasmodic / Anti-Inflammatory (Hyoscyamine)

**Hyoscyamine**: Blocks peripheral muscarinic receptors:
- Smooth muscle relaxation → antispasmodic (GI, urinary, biliary)
- Reduces glandular secretions
- The topical anti-inflammatory application relates to the local anaesthetic and anti-secretory effects

### 5.3 The Soporific Sponge (Spongia Somnifera)

The medieval surgical anaesthetic technique — developed from the classical mandrake tradition — involved soaking a sponge in a decoction of mandrake, opium, henbane, hemlock, and other agents, drying it, then re-moistening it and having the patient inhale/suck it before surgery. The combined anticholinergic (mandrake, henbane) + opioid (opium) + GABAergic (hemlock) effect produced surgical anaesthesia. This technique, documented from the 9th–13th centuries (Salerno school), was the standard surgical anaesthesia of medieval Europe and the Islamic world — built on the pharmacological foundation of mandrake described by Dioscorides and systematised by Ibn Sīnā.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Scopolamine (modern pharmaceutical)**: The isolated alkaloid scopolamine is an approved modern drug:
- Transdermal patch for motion sickness and post-operative nausea
- Anaesthetic premedication (amnesia, antisialagogue)
- Palliative care (reduces respiratory secretions, "death rattle")
- This represents direct modern pharmaceutical validation of the active principle in mandrake's anaesthetic/soporific applications.

**Hyoscyamine (modern pharmaceutical)**: Approved antispasmodic for GI conditions (irritable bowel, biliary/renal colic) — validating the antispasmodic application.

**Crude mandrake**: Not used in modern medicine due to unpredictable alkaloid content and toxicity; the isolated alkaloids (scopolamine, hyoscyamine, atropine) are used instead.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The screaming root**: The most famous mandrake folklore — that the root screams when pulled from the ground, and the scream kills or maddens whoever hears it — encodes a genuine pharmacological danger in mythological form. The mandrake root must be harvested and handled carefully; the deliriant alkaloids can be absorbed and the plant is genuinely dangerous. The folklore solution (tie a dog to the root to pull it, so the dog dies instead of the harvester) dramatises the real danger of the plant. The anthropomorphic forked root, the deadly/maddening reputation, and the elaborate harvesting rituals all reflect the genuine potency and danger of the tropane alkaloid content, expressed through the symbolic language of folklore.

**The first surgical anaesthesia**: Dioscorides' description of mandrake wine given "to those about to be cut or cauterised, that they may not feel the pain" is among the earliest explicit accounts of surgical general anaesthesia in the Western tradition (1st century CE). For roughly 1,800 years — until the introduction of ether (1846) and chloroform (1847) — mandrake (and its anticholinergic relatives henbane and datura, combined with opium) was the foundation of surgical pain control. The "soporific sponge" of the medieval surgeons was the direct descendant of Dioscorides' mandrake wine. The history of surgery before 1846 is, pharmacologically, largely the history of the tropane alkaloid plants — and mandrake was the most storied of them.

**The fertility bargain**: Genesis 30:14–16 — Reuben finds mandrakes in the field and brings them to his mother Leah; Rachel (Leah's infertile sister-rival) bargains for them, trading a night with Jacob for the mandrakes, hoping they will cure her infertility. The mandrake's reputation as a fertility aid and aphrodisiac (the "love apples," *dûdāʾîm*) spans the Mediterranean and Near East. The pharmacological basis is uncertain — the anticholinergic alkaloids do not directly promote fertility, but the plant's psychoactive/deliriant effects may have contributed to its erotic-magical reputation, and the relaxant/antispasmodic effects might reduce dyspareunia. The fertility association is more cultural-symbolic (the anthropomorphic root as a homunculus, sympathetic magic) than directly pharmacological.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

**⚠️ Mandrake is a potent anticholinergic deliriant poison. It is NOT for use. This documents historical pharmacology.**

| Form | Historical use | Status |
|------|------|-------------|
| Mandrake wine (classical anaesthetic) | Surgical anaesthesia | HISTORICAL ONLY — dangerous, unpredictable |
| Soporific sponge (medieval) | Surgical anaesthesia | HISTORICAL ONLY |
| **Pharmaceutical scopolamine / hyoscyamine** | Motion sickness, antispasmodic, premedication | The modern safe use is the isolated, dose-controlled alkaloid |
| Homeopathic *Mandragora* (30C+) | Ultra-dilute | Contains no material alkaloid |

**No safe self-administration dose exists for crude mandrake.**

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM (cross-reference)**: Bitter, acrid; cold; toxic. Channels: Liver (肝), Heart (心). Would be among the "settle fright, calm the spirit" toxic substances.

**Ayurvedic (cross-reference)**: Not a classical Ayurvedic plant. Related Solanaceae (*Datura*) are used in processed form (*śodhana*) for specific applications; mandrake itself is not classical Ayurvedic materia medica.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **ABSOLUTE: Do not self-administer crude mandrake.** Anticholinergic toxicity: dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention, tachycardia, hyperthermia, delirium, hallucination, coma, death ("hot as a hare, blind as a bat, dry as a bone, red as a beet, mad as a hatter")
- **Anticholinergic syndrome**: The classic anticholinergic toxidrome; reversed by physostigmine in emergency settings
- **Drug interactions**: Additive with other anticholinergics (antihistamines, tricyclic antidepressants, antipsychotics, other tropane plants)
- **Ornamental/foraging risk**: Mandrake fruit ("apples") have been mistaken for edible fruit, causing poisoning
- This entry is included for completeness of the cataloging mandate and historical/pharmacological documentation — NOT as a therapeutic recommendation
