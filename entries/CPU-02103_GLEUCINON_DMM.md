# GLEUCINON — Dioscorides 1-67 (De Materia Medica, 2000 English text); Latin *gleucinum*, "must-oil ointment"

## *A compound scented ointment made from oil of unripe olives, aromatics and must; the translator's header reads "Gleucinum — Syruped Pulp of Grapes in Oil, Oil of Must", and the identification is the text's own description of a preparation, not a single plant, so it is of medium confidence as to what the thing is and not applicable at species level* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry: named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk.** The locus is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 4064 (chapter 1-67, p.68 of the print), with the name index row at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 68. A search of the same text for the name under other spellings (GLEUKINON, Gleucinum) found only this chapter and the table-of-names line. The chapter stands in the ointments (*myra*) part of Book One, between 1-66 IRINON and 1-68 AMARAKINON. The row is a compound preparation and not a plant, although it is shelved in Volume I.

**Dioscorides (DMM).** The whole chapter, as printed: "1-67. GLEUCINON. SUGGESTED: Gleucinum — Syruped Pulp of Grapes in Oil, Oil of Must. Gleucinum simplex is prepared from oil of unripe olives, schoinos [rushes], calamus [1-17], Celtic nard [1-7], spatha [1-150], aspalathus [1-19], melilot [3-48], costus [1-15] and must. The vinandea [must] is laid in the jar that contains the aromata [fragrant herbs] with the wine and oil. It is stirred up and down twice every day for thirty days and then strained out and stored. It is warming, softening and relaxing; good for chills, sinewy diseases and disorders of the vulva. It is more effective than acopon [medicines to remove fatigue] being softening."

The bracketed cross-references are the translator's, pointing to Dioscorides' own chapters on those ingredients: schoinos, the aromatic rush, at 1-16 (line 3685, "Schoinos" gloss "rushes" here), calamus at 1-17 (KALAMOS EUODES, line 3694), Celtic nard at 1-7 (NARDOS KELTIKE, line 3606), spatha at 1-150 (PHOINIX ELATE, line 4613), aspalathus at 1-19 (line 3716), melilot at 3-48 (line 6941) and costus at 1-15 (KOSTOS, line 3680). The word "simplex" (*gleucinum simplex*) in the text implies that a compound form also existed; the chapter does not describe one. [USER: verify.]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** It is "warming, softening and relaxing; good for chills, sinewy diseases and disorders of the vulva", and "more effective than acopon... being softening".
- **What the text does not say.** The route of use is not stated in this chapter. The section's opening chapter (1-52, line 3962) says ointments are "effective for some diseases, either mixed with other medicines, dropped on, poured on, or smelled". No dose or duration is given.

The Codex does not translate "sinewy diseases" or "disorders of the vulva" into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and recommends no use.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives the one preparation, quoted above: the ingredients are laid with the must in a jar, "stirred up and down twice every day for thirty days", then strained and stored. No quantities of any ingredient are given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Cultivation Register

**Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides offers no subtle-body system, and none is supplied here. The chapter records a preparation and its use against chills and disorders of the vulva and sinews, with no cultivation framing. What it does offer a cultivator is a discipline of attention in making: named ingredients, a stated order of laying them in the jar, a fixed rhythm (stirring twice a day) and a fixed time (thirty days) before straining. The section's opening chapter (1-52) adds an assay: those who test ointments "must determine whether the ointments smell exactly of those herbs from which the mixture is made". That is a record of craft and sensory checking, and not a vital-substrate claim.

**Practitioner's note.** A scented, warming ointment from a craft recipe, with nothing in the record about cultivation. A cultivator reads it as an example of Dioscorides' attention to preparation and does not make or use it for any vital purpose.

### Contraindications

- **Disorders of the vulva.** The text names these as a use; they are medical conditions and call for proper care. No statement of safety in pregnancy is made in the chapter. [USER: verify]
