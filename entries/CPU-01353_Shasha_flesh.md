# Shasha flesh — Shasha (as printed in Bhishagratna's English, Sutrasthana ch.46); S'as'a (hare) (Uttaratantra ch.39)
## *The flesh of the Shasha, a hole-dwelling animal (Vileshaya) in Sushruta's meat chapter. The list row glosses it "hare", and Bhishagratna prints "S'as'a (hare)" in the fever-diet passage of the Uttaratantra; the ch.46 passage itself prints the Sanskrit name without a gloss* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The text is Bhishagratna's 1907 English *Sushruta Samhita*, Sutrasthana ch.46, "the Chapter which treats of food and drink (Annapana-Vidhi-madhyayam)" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt`). The list row is `_sources/sushruta.csv` line 220, "Shasha (hare)", with the source "Sushruta Samhita, Sutrasthana ch.46 (Annapanavidhi); Bhishagratna trans. vol.1". The chapter opens (lines 9-12) with the claim that food "determines the origin of beings and forms the chief source of their bodily strength and complexion, as well as of the albuminous (Ojas) principle in their organisms". That is said of food in general and not of any one meat.

The meat section begins at line 385: "Now I shall describe the properties of the different species of edible meats", and the text says of the kinds listed (aquatic, marsh, village, carnivorous, unbifurcated-hoof, high-ground) that "each succeeding kind is superior to the one immediately preceding it in the order of enumeration" (lines 385-396). The high-ground (Janghala) animals are divided into eight sub-species, one of which is the Vileshaya (lines 403-410). The Shasha sits in that last-but-one group. Line 574-578: "The Vileshaya Group :— Animals such as, the Shvavit, Shalyaka, Godha, Shasha, Vrishadansha, Lopaka, Lomasha-Karna, Kadali, Mriga-Priyaka, Ajagara, Sarpa, Mushika, Nakula and Maha-Vabhru belong to the group of Vileshaya (hole-dwellers)."

The group's general properties follow (lines 580-587): "to increase the consistency of stool and urine. They are heat-making in potency, sweet in digestion as those of the preceding group. They subdue the Vayu and generate the Pittam and Kapham. They are demulcent and beneficial in cough, dyspnoea and cachexia." Then the text singles out the Shasha (lines 587-590): "The flesh of the Shasha is sweet and astringent in taste. It reduces the Pittam and Kapham and neither produces nor subdues the Vayu owing to its moderately cooling potency." So the hare's flesh differs from its group's general statement: the group is "heat-making" and "generate[s] the Pittam and Kapham", while the Shasha is "moderately cooling" and "reduces the Pittam and Kapham". Bhishagratna does not comment on the difference.

The same animal turns up in other chapters, under other spellings, and is named "hare" there:

- *Uttaratantra* ch.39 (the treatment of fever), lines 904-908: "The meat of Lava, Kapinjala, Ena, Prishata, S'arabha, Kala-puchchha, Kuranga, Mriga-matrika (different kinds of deer) or S'as'a (hare) may be prescribed as diet for a fever-patient accustomed to the use of animal food." This is the passage that ties the Sanskrit name to "hare" in the translation itself.
- *Chikitsasthana* ch.39 (distressing symptoms), line 240: a diet of "Shashti grain ... or matured Sali rice, Mudga pulse as well as (the soup of the flesh of) an Ena, Lava, hare, peacock, Tittiri, or deer, and such other light food should be given to a patient after the exhibition of emetics and purgatives." Here the Sanskrit word is not printed.
- *Uttaratantra* ch.40 (diarrhoea), line 667: "The blood-stained flesh of a hare cooked with Samanga, curd and clarified butter should be taken with well boiled rice."
- *Sutrasthana* ch.14, line 399: the blood "of the Ena or common deer, or of a sheep, hare, or buffalo" as the ordinary drink in a passage on checking a flow of blood. This is blood, not flesh, and is a separate substance.

Other mentions are not uses: a hare seen on either side of the road is "an inauspicious sight" (*Sutrasthana* ch.29, line 231); menstrual blood "red like the blood of a hare" (*Sharirasthana* ch.2, line 144); a speck "coloured like a (drop of the) hare's blood" (*Uttaratantra* ch.4, line 32); and a person's traits "seem to resemble those of a goat, jackal, hare, mouse, camel, dog, vulture, crow, and of an ass" (*Sharirasthana* ch.4, line 475).

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, hare flesh, like other lean mammalian muscle, is mainly water and protein, with little fat and some iron. No more specific statement can be made without a source.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Sushruta treats the hare's flesh as a food with stated effects on the *doshas*, and as a light diet for the sick (after emetics and purgatives, in fever, in diarrhoea). The Codex does not prescribe it. Wild hare meat carries hygiene risks (see Contraindications), the hunting of wild animals is regulated in most places, and the sources give no preparation that a reader could safely adapt. The entry's honest use is as a record of how the text classifies and uses the meat.

### Preparation & Dosing

The texts give two preparations of the flesh. A soup of the flesh is part of the light diet after emetics and purgatives (*Chikitsasthana* ch.39, line 240). The blood-stained flesh is cooked with *Samanga*, curd and clarified butter and eaten with well-boiled rice (*Uttaratantra* ch.40, line 667). Neither passage gives a quantity, and the fever passage (*Uttaratantra* ch.39) names the meat without a method. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. The *dosha* terms (*vayu*, *pittam*, *kapham*) and qualities such as "moderately cooling potency" belong to the Ayurvedic frame and have no direct counterpart in modern physiology. Hare is lean game meat, and wild hares can carry infections that pass to people through handling and undercooked meat. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Ayurveda (SS):** From *Sutrasthana* ch.46 (lines 587-590), for the flesh of the Shasha: taste, "sweet and astringent"; potency (*virya*), "moderately cooling"; effect on the humours, it "reduces the Pittam and Kapham and neither produces nor subdues the Vayu". No *vipaka* (taste after digestion) is printed for the Shasha alone. For its group, the Vileshaya (lines 580-587): heat-making in potency, "sweet in digestion", subduing *vayu*, generating *pittam* and *kapham*, demulcent. The two statements differ, as noted under Origin & Tradition.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The hare's flesh is described in a food-and-drug chapter (*Sutrasthana* ch.46), which opens by saying that food is the chief source of strength, complexion and *ojas* in beings (lines 9-12). That is a statement about food in general. I searched the *Chikitsasthana* chapter files for the name; the only hit is the light diet after emetics and purgatives (ch.39, line 240), a post-purgation diet and not a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* regimen. The Vileshaya group's text never uses the word "spermatopoietic", and the Shasha's own sentence says only that it is sweet, astringent, moderately cooling and reduces *pittam* and *kapham*. No passage read assigns the flesh to a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* chapter or says that it builds *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana*. The sattvic and rajasic food lists are *Samkhya*-era material and not Sushruta, and are not used.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The text records hare's flesh as a light, moderately cooling food for the sick, taken after purgation, in fever or in diarrhoea, and as a food with effects on the *doshas*. The Codex does not recommend it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** wild hare meat can carry bacteria and parasites, including those that cause infection through skin contact during skinning; thorough cooking and care in handling are essential, and the Codex does not recommend sourcing it from the wild.
- **Ayurvedic caution:** the text itself ranks the Vileshaya group's flesh as heat-making and as generating *pittam* and *kapham*, with the Shasha an exception only by being "moderately cooling"; it names no one who should avoid the flesh.
- **Legal:** hunting and trade in wild game are regulated, and hares are protected in some jurisdictions or seasons.
- **Ethics:** the Codex does not recommend taking an animal's life for these uses.
