# Eleagnus pungens — phang.ma, Phangma (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Eleagnus pungens, the first of four binomials Clark 1995 prints for phang.ma (then Lycium barbarium, Lycium chinense and Leonurus heterophyllus Sweet); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints "Lycium chinense" against "Phangma"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under this binomial only by Clark.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk found no *Eleagnus pungens*. The only nearby hit is the genus *Elaeagnus*, spelled that way, in Dioscorides, where the translators' SUGGESTED lines for the wild-olive chapters print "Elaeagnus angustifolia — Oleaster, Oil Tree, Zakkoum Oil Plant" (`Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 3835, 4535 and 4554). That is a different species under a different Greek name, and it is not read here as a mention of this plant. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says: "Therefore more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the line prints four, of which the spine row takes the first (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 187). The Codex keeps the spelling "Eleagnus" as printed. The genus is more usually spelled *Elaeagnus*. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1097).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry stands between *mdzo.mo* (*Caragana jubata*, line 1096) and *srad.dkar.wa* (a type of gum tragacanth, line 1098). It reads:

"phang.ma: Eleagnus pungens: Lycium barbarium: Lycium chinense: Leonurus heterophyllus Sweet: [has thin leaves and a pale trunk from which the branches protrude horizontally. It has a sweet-tasting reddish-brown] seed, [which is of two types, black and white, and] cures heart fevers and gynecological disorders."

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the thin leaves, the pale trunk with horizontal branches, the sweet taste and reddish-brown colour, and the two types, black and white. Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the English-and-Latin identifications, which are Clark's and not the root text's, the word "seed", and the clause "cures heart fevers and gynecological disorders". Clark prints no English name for this entry. Clark's index of Latin names lists *Eleagnus pungens* (line 1580) and, from the same entry, the other three binomials.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text on "Phangma" (line 3458).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428). The sentence, which ends a paragraph on the page numbered 210, reads: "The fruit of Phangma cures cardiac hot disorder and gynecological disorders." The sentences before it follow Clark's order (line 3456 names Chhuma tsi, Ji tsher, Dhewa, Tsa tri, Drak cham and Tamik in that order, matching Clark's *chhu.ma.rtsi*, *byi.tsher*, *de.wa*, *rtsa.mkhris*, *brag.lcham*, *rta.rmig* at lines 1079 to 1094), and the drug and its actions agree in kind with Clark's: heart, and gynecological disorders. The index lists "Phangma 210" (line 7467). The text gives no Latin name in the sentence. The Codex reads "Phangma" as the same name as *phang.ma* from the spelling, the agreement of the actions and the run of neighbouring names. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary.* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "P" block (the letter at line 5576), prints this drug as a short run of three lines in turn: "Phangma" (line 5616), "phang ma" (line 5618) and "Lycium chinense" (line 5620). The row before it pairs "Pema gesar" with "Bombax ceiba L." (lines 5612 and 5614) and the row after it begins "Phowari" (line 5622). The glossary therefore names *Lycium chinense* against this drug, and does not name *Eleagnus pungens*. This is one of the three binomials Clark prints after the first. The two Tibetan texts therefore agree on the Tibetan name and on the possibility of *Lycium*, and the binomial the spine row uses, *Eleagnus pungens*, is Clark's first choice only. The Codex does not choose between the four. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Eleagnus*, *Elaeagnus*, *phang.ma* and *Phangma* found no entry on this plant. The spine has a sibling row for *Lycium chinense* (CPU-01748, from the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary line 5620), which at the time of this entry has no written file, and a written entry I_596 (Goji, *Lycium barbarum*, tags SBJ and FMT). I_596's FMT paragraph names no Clark or Men-Tsee-Khang line and gives a Tibetan name "Dre-tsher-ma", which is not the name *phang.ma* in either Tibetan text. *Lycium barbarium*, as Clark prints it, is the second of the four binomials for this drug. None of these is an entry on this Tibetan drug. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Elaeagnus* is a genus of shrubs and small trees of the oleaster family (Elaeagnaceae), often silvery-scaled and evergreen or deciduous, with small fleshy fruits. It is also a general fact that *Lycium* is a genus of the nightshade family (Solanaceae) whose red berries are the goji or wolfberry, that *Leonurus* is a genus of the mint family (Lamiaceae), and that the four names Clark prints therefore point to plants of three unrelated families. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a seed or fruit that "cures heart fevers and gynecological disorders" (Clark, line 1097) and a fruit that "cures cardiac hot disorder and gynecological disorders" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3458). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only by Clark's first choice among four, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. The part used is named as "seed" by Clark (line 1097) and "fruit" by the Men-Tsee-Khang text (line 3458); the commentary layer adds that the seed is of two types, black and white. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Tibetan terms ("heart fevers", "cardiac hot disorder", "gynecological disorders") are not modern diagnoses or measured outcomes, so no modern finding can be set beside them here. The drug's botanical identity is open among three plant families, so a study of any one *Elaeagnus*, *Lycium* or *Leonurus* species would not bear directly on it.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer gives the seed a "sweet-tasting" quality (line 1097). No power or post-digestive taste is printed for it. The name does not appear in the Men-Tsee-Khang taste-group lists of chapter 19 (which begin at line 3204) that were read for this entry, and the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence at line 3458 gives no taste.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: its seed cures heart fevers and gynecological disorders, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same in kind. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the lines cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and the link from this binomial to that framing rests on Clark's first choice among four.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a drug for heart fevers and gynecological disorders in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and its Latin name is one of four that Clark offers. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. The identity is open between plants of different families, and no preparation or dose is given, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Heart disorders and gynecological disorders need a physician, and nothing here supports use in pregnancy. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
