# Aconitum autumnale — bong.nga.ser.po, Bongnga serpo, "Yellow Aconite" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Aconitum autumnale, the one binomial Clark 1995 prints for bong.nga.ser.po ("Yellow Aconite"); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints "Aconitum lycoctonum" for the same phonetic name* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts in this row.** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this binomial. Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one, and the two Tibetan translations print two different binomials for it (see the Men-Tsee-Khang paragraph). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 969).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads: "bong.nga.ser.po: Yellow Aconite: Aconitum autumnale: [has a yellowish flower. It has bitter taste, cool power and is recommended in poison fevers and infectious bile fever. (Roots of) red and yellow aconite] cure meat poisoning and poisoning from black aconite (Aconitum ferrox Wall)."

Clark's preface (line 82) says "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary, whilst those in parentheses ( ) are derived from the oral instructions of Dr. Yeshe Donden and Dr. Pema Dorje." So the yellowish flower, the bitter taste, the cool power and the poison-fever and bile-fever recommendation are the commentary layer, and "(Roots of)" and the identification "(Aconitum ferrox Wall)" are in the parentheses layer. What stands outside both is the predicate: the red and yellow aconites "cure meat poisoning and poisoning from black aconite". The root-text layer thus joins the yellow aconite to the red one (line 968, "bong.nga.dmar.po: Red Aconite: Aconitum sp.: [has a crimson flower and a red root. It has bitter taste and is an antidote to poison]") and gives them one use.

The neighbouring white aconite entry (line 965) carries the only toxicity statement in this run, in the bracketed layer: the aconite "has ten names and is of four types: white, red, yellow and black. The black type is highly toxic, whilst the white one has bitter taste and neutralizes poison." No line read gives a way of processing any aconite to make it safe.

Two more Clark lines name the yellow aconite. In chapter 21, "The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines" (heading at line 1204), line 1212 reads: "The class of medicines which neutralize poisons consists of Musk, Red, Yellow and White Aconites, White and Brown Uncaria, ...". Clark's name index prints "Yellow Aconite" (line 1667) and "Aconitum autumnale" (line 1554).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011.* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. Chapter 20, line 3421: "Bongnga marpo and Bongnga serpo cures meat poisoning and the poisonous effect of Tsendhug." Line 3604 lists "Bongnga serpo" in the group of medicines that cure poisoning, together with Bongnga karpo and Bongnga marpo. The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints, on one line (5120), "Bongnga serpo bong nga ser po Aconitum lycoctonum", so the Tibetan name is paired there with a binomial that is not Clark's. The glossary also prints "Bongnga nagpo bong nga nag po Aconitum napellus" (line 5118), the black one. The index (line 7279) gives "Bongnga serpo 205, 220". Both translations agree on the use (meat poisoning); the binomial differs.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *bong.nga*, *Aconitum autumnale* and the Tibetan names found one entry on the same Clark line: I_903 (*Aconitum lycoctonum*, "Wolf's-bane (yellow)", tags DMM, FMT) quotes line 969 and the Men-Tsee-Khang line 5120. It is the entry for the binomial the glossary prints; this entry is the one for the binomial Clark prints for the same Tibetan name. Whether the two binomials are one plant is not settled by the lines read, and the Codex does not merge them. Related entries on neighbouring aconites: I_407 (*Aconitum heterophyllum*, Ativisha, tags CM, SS, FMT; Clark's white aconite, line 965), I_231 (*Aconitum tanguticum*, whose register discusses the bong.nga drugs) and I_004 (*Aconitum napellus*). None is an entry on Clark's *Aconitum autumnale*.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that the aconites (*Aconitum*) are known to contain toxic diterpenoid alkaloids. Which species the Tibetan "yellow aconite" is, and what its roots contain, was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a bitter, cool-powered yellow aconite, recommended in poison fevers and infectious bile fever (Clark's commentary layer), whose root with the red aconite cures meat poisoning and poisoning from black aconite (Clark line 969; Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3421 and 3604). These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. Aconites are generally known to be deadly poisons, the botanical identity is unsettled, and the Codex gives no protocol for this plant.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give the roots as the part used (Clark line 969, in the parentheses layer: "(Roots of) red and yellow aconite"). No processing, quantity, frequency or duration is given for the yellow aconite in the lines read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Aconite alkaloids are generally known to be potent cardiac and neurological toxins, and poisoning from *Aconitum* plants is a well-documented medical emergency. This is a statement about the genus. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identity of the drug is uncertain between two binomials, so a modern study of one species would not bear directly on it.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark line 969 prints "bitter taste, cool power" inside the square brackets (commentary layer), and nothing on taste or power outside them. The Men-Tsee-Khang text prints no taste or power for Bongnga serpo in the lines read. No other quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica places the yellow aconite among the antidotes to meat poisoning and to poisoning from black aconite (line 969), and the compounding chapter lists it in the class of medicines that neutralize poisons (line 1212). The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this entry places the yellow aconite in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice; the essence-extraction mentions in the herb section are for other plants (for example at lines 971 and 1121). Its framing is therapeutic only, as an antidote.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a poison-neutralising root in the Tibetan texts, from a genus in which one named type is called highly toxic, and nothing more can be said from them. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The only warning in the lines read is Clark's bracketed statement about the aconite group: "The black type is highly toxic" (line 965). No statement says the yellow type is safe. Aconites are generally known to be deadly poisons and must not be gathered or taken on the strength of this entry; the botanical identity is unsettled. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
