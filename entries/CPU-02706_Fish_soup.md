# Fish soup — Zomos neaon ichthuon (as printed in Dioscorides 2-35)

## *Broth made from fresh fish: Dioscorides' "zomos neaon ichthuon" in De Materia Medica 2-35, glossed by the translator "Fish Soup"; the identification is the text's own description, of high confidence as to what it is, and no single species is meant since the text lists several kinds of tender rock fish* | *(Volume III — Animal)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM) among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 8 November 2026 as an unverified placeholder (key "ZOMOS NEARON ICHTHUON [DMM]"); this entry replaces it. It is a separate row from Garum (`III_230_Garum.md`, 2-34), the salted fish liquid that stands in the line just before it.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-35, *Zomos neaon ichthuon*, glossed "SUGGESTED: Fish Soup" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 5079 to 5081). It stands in Book Two, in the part headed "Living Creatures", after 2-34 *Garron* (garum) and before 2-36 *Koreis* (bed bugs). The chapter reads: "Broth made from fresh fish (alone or taken as a drink with wine) is able to soothe the bowels. The best broth for this is made from the fish called phycides?, scorpion fish, julides, perch and other tender rock fish, and those which do not have a poisonous nature, boiled with nothing else but water and oil and anise [3-65] and salt."

The chapter holds one use, a rule for the choice of fish, and a recipe. The use is that the broth is "able to soothe the bowels", taken alone or as a drink with wine. The rule is that the best broth comes from tender rock fish, named as *phycides* (printed with a query mark by the translator), scorpion fish, *julides* and perch, and from fish "which do not have a poisonous nature". The recipe is fresh fish boiled with "nothing else but" water, oil, anise (cross-referred to 3-65) and salt. The translator's query after *phycides* marks a doubtful reading, and the species are not identified further here. [USER: verify the Greek fish names]

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, a fish broth is a water extract of fish flesh, bones and skin with the oil added in the cooking: it holds dissolved protein breakdown products (gelatin and amino acids), minerals, and fat. The anise adds aromatic oils such as anethole, and the salt adds sodium chloride. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a recommended practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** To soothe the bowels, alone or taken in wine.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no quantity of fish or water, no cooking time, no condition for which it is given, and no duration.

The Codex gives no protocol. A fish broth is an ordinary food, and its place here is as a recorded remedy that is also a meal; the Codex does not turn it into a therapy.

### Preparation & Dosing

One recipe, from 2-35 (lines 5079 to 5081): fresh fish, preferably tender rock fish and not poisonous kinds, boiled with nothing else but water, oil, anise and salt; drunk alone or with wine. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Fish broth is a food rich in water, protein breakdown products, minerals and fat. Some fish, notably certain reef and rock fish, can carry toxins, and the text's own rule that the fish must not be of a poisonous nature agrees in kind with that. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** No hot, cold, dry or moist grade and no taste is printed for the broth in 2-35. The only quality given is the action on the bowels, that it is "able to soothe" them.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent; framed only by Dioscorides' discipline of attention.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is attention to the material: the fish must be fresh, of a tender kind, and not poisonous, and the broth is plain, with nothing else added than the four named things. No cultivation status is given to it in the text.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing here beyond a plain fish broth. It is a food, and a cultivator who eats fish may eat it. The Codex draws no vital or spiritual claim from it, and the "soothing" of the bowels is the text's phrase and not a clinical statement.

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

Little to map: the entry states that a broth of fresh fish, drunk alone or with wine, is able to soothe the bowels. That is the only organ and the only action the chapter names. It gives no account of the stomach, the liver or any other organ, and no humour, temperament or quality of heat or cold. The fish is chosen by kind, being tender rock fish and not of a poisonous nature, and the broth is made with nothing but water, oil, anise and salt. The chapter does not say what those ingredients do in the body. The recipe and the rule for choosing fish are the only content, and the chapter gives no dose, no cooking time and no condition for which the broth is given beyond the bowels.

### Contraindications

- **Fish allergy.** Anyone allergic to fish or shellfish must avoid it.
- **Poisonous fish.** The text itself excludes fish "of a poisonous nature"; scorpion fish and some other rock fish need careful identification. [USER: verify]
- **Freshness and hygiene.** Fish spoils quickly, and spoiled fish can cause food poisoning.
- **Salt.** The broth contains added salt, which matters for those with heart, kidney or blood-pressure conditions.
- **Wine.** The text allows it with wine; alcohol is unsuitable in pregnancy and for many people.
- **Bowel complaints.** Persistent bowel disorders are medical conditions and call for proper care.
