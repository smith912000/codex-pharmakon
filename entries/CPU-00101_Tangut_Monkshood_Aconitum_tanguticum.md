### Origin & Tradition

Tangut monkshood is a small alpine aconite of the Tibetan plateau and high Himalaya, one of several *Aconitum* species used — always with great care and after processing — in Tibetan and Himalayan medicine. Where the European wolfsbane (*Aconitum napellus*, this volume) and the Chinese Fuzi (*A. carmichaelii*) are warming "rescue-yang" agents, the Tibetan tradition draws on a range of aconites including "white" types (*bong-dkar*) used differently — notably for **heat, fever, inflammation and infection** ("hot" disorders, including epidemic febrile disease) — reflecting both species differences and a distinct medical framework in the *rGyud-bzhi* (the Four Tantras).

Like all aconites it is fundamentally a **poison**, owing its action to **diterpenoid (norditerpenoid) alkaloids** that act on voltage-gated sodium channels. The Tibetan pharmacy, like the Chinese and Ayurvedic, developed elaborate **processing (detoxification)** methods to render the drug usable — and the entire safe use of the plant rests on that processing. Unprocessed or mis-processed aconite is lethal. This entry stands alongside the other aconites as a paradigm of the poison-into-medicine art, in its specifically Tibetan, "cooling/anti-fever" application.

Botanically, the Flora of China (efloras.org) describes *Aconitum tanguticum* as a perennial with a fusiform or obconical caudex about 2 cm long and a simple or branched stem 8–50 cm tall. Its 7–9 long-petiolate basal leaves have orbicular or orbicular-reniform blades, medially 3-lobed, and the terminal inflorescence carries 3–5 flowers with blue-purple (rarely greenish) sepals, the upper sepal navicular (boat-shaped). It flowers in July–August and has 2n = 16. The Flora gives its habitat as grassy slopes and wet and alpine grasslands at 3200–4800 m, in southern Gansu, eastern Qinghai, Shaanxi, western Sichuan, eastern Xizang (Tibet) and north-western Yunnan.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Tibetan mapping:** *bong-nga/bong-dkar* — a potent processed drug used (in the relevant species/form) for "hot" disorders — fever, inflammation, infection; handled as a poison requiring detoxification.
- **Kenetica Qi/Shen tier:** ◉ **Qi** for the action on febrile/inflammatory "hot" disorders, with a ☿ **Shen** ascription reflecting its life-or-death margin and deep systemic (cardiac/neural) reach — kin to the other aconites.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan medicine (FMT).** Aconitum tanguticum is not named in either translation of the rGyud-bzhi checked (Clark 1995; Men-Tsee-Khang 2011). The Explanatory Tantra's materia medica (ch. 20) treats aconites under the bong.nga drugs, glossed there as other species (Aconitum naviculare, A. sinense, A. heterophyllum, A. autumnale, A. ferrox), and gives no identification with this species. Matching Tibetan aconite names to Linnaean species is modern and disputed, so no attribution to A. tanguticum is made. No bcud-len or rejuvenation status is recorded for it. Grade (c).

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. This is a highly toxic root; where the tradition uses aconites it is as physician-prepared medicine, and there is no self-directed cultivation use.

### Contraindications

- **Raw/unprocessed material — absolute prohibition.** Lethal; small margin.
- **Cardiac disease — absolute.** Arrhythmogenic.
- **Pregnancy/breastfeeding — absolute.** Toxic.
- **Under-processing.** A documented cause of poisoning even with "processed" material.
- **No unsupervised use of any kind.** Specialist framing throughout.
