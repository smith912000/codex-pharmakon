# Rennet — *Pitua* (Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica* 2-85)
## *The coagulating substance taken from the stomach of a young animal; Dioscorides lists the rennets of the hare, horse, lamb, kid, fawn, doe, stag, deer, calf, wild ox and sea calf (seal)* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Rennet is chapter 2-85 of Book Two in the Osbaldeston and Wood English version (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`; "DMM text" below). The chapter is headed "2-85. PITUA", with the editors' line "SUGGESTED: Rennet" (lines 5289-5290), and it falls under the sub-heading "FATS" (line 5281), between lanolin (2-84, line 5282) and goose grease (2-86). The editors say their "SUGGESTED" lines are possibilities offered to the reader (preface, line 43). The chapter is organised by the animal that gave the rennet. Its text is at lines 5291-5295.

*Hare.* "Thirty grains of rennet of hare (taken in a drink of wine) is good for those bitten by venomous creatures, coeliac [intestinal complaints], dysentery, for women troubled with excessive discharges from the womb, for clotting blood together, and for throwing blood up out of the chest." Applied to the vulva with butter "after the cleansing (that is, monthly, and a little before lying together)" it "causes inconception"; taken in a drink it is an abortifacient, and "after the menstrual flow purging it hinders conception" (the editors add "[birth control]"). *Horse.* "Rennet of a horse that some call hippace is particularly good for the abdominal cavity and dysentery." The cheese chapter 2-80 repeats the name: "Some have called the horse's rennet by the name of hippace" (line 5263). *Lamb, kid, fawn, doe, stag, deer, calf and wild ox.* The rennets of "a lamb, kid, fawn, doe, platyceros [stag], dorcas [Dorcatherium aquaticum — ruminant], deer, calf, and wild ox have similar properties and are good (taken as a drink in wine) as an antidote against aconitum [4-77, 4-78], and for clotting milk if used with vinegar." The fawn's, applied the third day after the monthly purgation, "hinders conception". *Sea calf (seal).* The rennet of the "sea calf [seal — Phoca vitulina]" has "a strength very similar to castorium [2-26]", is "thought to be especially good (taken as a drink) for epilepsy, and for constriction of the womb". The chapter ends with a general statement: "In general all rennet gathers together things that are scattered and dissolves things that are gathered together."

The hare chapter, 2-21 (heading at line 5001), has a closely related sentence: "The curds (taken in a drink three days after the menstrual flow) are reported to cause sterility. Likewise it stops excessive discharges of the womb and bowels" (lines 5006-5010). The text there does not call the curds rennet, and this entry does not equate them. Rennet also figures in the plant chapter on Gallion, which "coagulates milk instead of rennet" (line 8895).

### Active Components

Rennet, as the word is used today, is the milk-curdling preparation from the stomach of a suckling mammal. Its main enzyme is chymosin (rennin), which curdles milk casein. The Greek text describes only the effect, clotting milk "if used with vinegar", and does not say what the rennet is made of or how it is gathered, except that the seal's is "taken from the young ones when not yet able to swim together (with the old)" (lines 5291, 5295). The entry makes no claim that chymosin explains the medicinal uses the chapter lists.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Rennet is today a cheesemaking agent, and that use needs no medicine. The medicinal uses recorded here (for dysentery and womb discharges, as an antidote for aconite, for epilepsy) are not practices to follow. Two of the recorded uses are contraceptive and abortifacient, which the chapter states plainly and which are not a safe or reliable method of either. The aconite claim is the most dangerous: aconite is a potent poison, and no rennet drink should be relied on against it. The Codex's application layer has nothing to add.

### Preparation & Dosing

Where the text gives a quantity it is for the hare only: "Thirty grains of rennet of hare (taken in a drink of wine)" (line 5291). Its unit is the translator's "grains", and this entry does not convert it. The other rennets are "taken as a drink in wine" (line 5291, for the antidote use) or "applied", the third day after the monthly purgation (the fawn's), with no quantity. The hare's is applied to the vulva "with butter", before lying together. Clotting milk is done "with vinegar". For all uses other than the hare's drink: No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM).** The chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or astringent quality for rennet. Its one general statement of action is the closing sentence: rennet "gathers together things that are scattered and dissolves things that are gathered together". The chapter compares the seal's rennet to castoreum in strength (line 5291) and says the rennets of the lamb, kid, fawn and the rest have "similar properties", without saying to which of the earlier rennets they are similar. No other tradition is tagged on this entry, so none is applied.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied here. What he offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, detection of adulteration. This chapter shows more of that than most animal chapters. It names the age and condition of the animal for the seal's rennet ("taken from the young ones when not yet able to swim together (with the old)"), and it gives an assay for the authentic seal's rennet: put water on the rennet of another creature, "especially of a lamb", leave it a little, then pour that water on the seal's rennet; "if it is authentic it immediately melts into the water, and if it is not it remains as it was" (line 5291). That is a test by observation, aimed at a substitute. Its status is therapeutic, and the discipline of provenance and assay is the part a practitioner can learn. The text gives no cultivation grade, no endorsement beyond treatment and no nourishing-life use.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports a cultivation use. What a cultivator can take from the chapter is its method, naming the source animal and testing a costly or substitutable material before trusting it. The contraceptive and abortifacient uses are a record of what was done and a reason for caution, not a regimen, and the aconite antidote is a claim never to act on.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy and fertility:** the chapter records abortifacient and contraceptive uses; do not take or apply rennet for either purpose, and avoid it in pregnancy.
- **Poisoning:** never rely on rennet as an antidote for aconite or any other poison; seek emergency care.
- **Hygiene and infection:** animal stomach preparations can carry bacteria if unprocessed; drinks and applications to the vulva are unsafe in any form from an uncontrolled source.
- **Ethics and diet:** rennet is made from the stomachs of slaughtered young animals, which matters to vegetarians and in some religious dietary rules.
- **Legal and conservation status:** the seal's rennet in the text comes from *Phoca vitulina*, a marine mammal protected in many countries. [USER: verify]
