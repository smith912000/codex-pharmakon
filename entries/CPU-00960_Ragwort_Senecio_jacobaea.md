# Ragwort / Tansy Ragwort

## *Jacobaea vulgaris* Gaertn. (syn. *Senecio jacobaea* L.) | Family: Asteraceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Historically rendered "hot and dry" in the humoral stream (inferred from its counter-irritant/wound-cleansing folk use; not a securely attributed classical grading — see note below)
**Training Phase:** Foundation — Signature Study

---

### Origin & Tradition

Ragwort is a biennial-to-perennial herb of the daisy family (Asteraceae), native across Eurasia and now a widespread pasture and waste-ground weed in temperate regions worldwide. It throws a rosette in its first year and, in its second, a ridged stem 30–100 cm tall crowned with dense flat-topped corymbs of bright yellow, ray-and-disc "daisy" flower-heads. The deeply pinnately-lobed leaves give it the older English name "stinking Willie"; the dedication "St James's wort" (Latin *herba Sancti Jacobi*, preserved in the botanical epithets *jacobaea* / *Jacobaea*) reflects its flowering around St James's Day in high summer.

This is a plant whose folk-medical reputation — a wound wash and gargle, an eye lotion, a poultice for inflamed joints and "old sores" — is almost entirely overshadowed by its toxicology. The honest headline of this entry is that **ragwort is the archetype of pyrrolizidine-alkaloid plant poisoning**, and the modern verdict on its internal use is unambiguous: do not.

**Named-tradition attestations.** The two tradition tags carried here (**DMM** = the Dioscorides/Greek herbal stream; **CM** = Avicenna's *Canon of Medicine*, the Unani stream) both require a strong caveat. The classical *senecio* / *erigeron* of Dioscorides is a groundsel-type composite, and the "St James" dedication is a mediaeval hagiographic overlay, not a classical one. Whether either the Greek *senecio* or the Unani materia medica maps to *this* species — *Jacobaea vulgaris* — rather than to the broader groundsel genus (*Senecio* s.l.) or to an unrelated yellow-flowered composite is **[USER: verify]**. Treat the DMM and CM tags as *name-and-genus inheritances* down the herbal tradition, not as secure species-level identifications. The taxonomy itself has moved: the plant was long filed under *Senecio jacobaea* L. and much of the toxicology literature still uses that name, but it is now placed in *Jacobaea* (Gaertner's segregate genus).

The doctrinal layer worth taking seriously is not a claim of efficacy but a claim of *signature*: a brilliant, sun-timed, "hot" yellow flower that is simultaneously a wound-cleanser in folk hands and a slow poison of the liver. In the Kenetica frame this is a Janus-plant — its external, sun-facing gesture and its internal, hepatic gesture point in opposite directions. The cultivator studies it precisely to learn where a signature *lies*.

### Active Components

The defining constituents are **hepatotoxic 1,2-unsaturated pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs)** and their N-oxides (PANOs). In *Jacobaea vulgaris* the characteristic PAs include **jacobine, senecionine, seneciphylline, jacoline, jaconine, jacozine** and related macrocyclic diesters of retronecine-type necine bases. According to PubMed, transfer studies of a defined ragwort extract found the milk PA/PANO pattern dominated by **jacoline (~74%), jaconine (~11%) and jacobine (~7%)**, confirming these as major components of the plant material (Knoop et al., 2024). The genus more broadly is characterised chemically by PAs alongside furoeremophilane sesquiterpenes, flavonoids, phenolic acids and volatile oils (Wu et al., 2025).

The toxicological mechanism is not the alkaloid itself but its **hepatic metabolite**: cytochrome-P450 bioactivation converts the 1,2-unsaturated PA into reactive **pyrrole (dehydro-PA) esters**, which form **pyrrole–protein adducts** and cross-link DNA. These adducts destroy the hepatic sinusoidal endothelial cells, producing the vascular liver injury described below (Yang et al., 2019).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**External / traditional only, and even then discouraged.** The historical uses — as a wound and ulcer wash, a mouth gargle and eye bath ("St James's wort for the eyes"), and a warming poultice over rheumatic joints — are recorded as *counter-irritant and cleansing* applications of the fresh or decocted aerial parts. Kenetica records these as **traditional, ungraded, and superseded**. There is no modern clinical evidence base supporting any of them, and the presence of PAs means that even topical or mucosal exposure is not obviously safe, since PAs can be absorbed across compromised skin and mucous membranes.

**Internal use is contraindicated outright** and is deflated here in the strongest terms. No "tonic," "blood-cleansing," or "cooling" internal indication survives contact with the toxicology. The cultivator's use-case for ragwort is therefore *pedagogical and horticultural*, not medicinal: it is the reference organism for understanding PA hepatotoxicity, honey and milk contamination, and the difference between a plant's outward signature and its inward action.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Route | Traditional preparation | Kenetica status |
|---|---|---|
| Internal (tea, tincture, "blood tonic") | Aerial parts infused/decocted | **CONTRAINDICATED — no safe dose exists** |
| Topical wound/ulcer wash | Decoction of aerial parts | Traditional; discouraged (PA absorption risk) |
| Gargle / eye bath | Dilute decoction | Traditional; discouraged |
| Poultice (joints) | Bruised fresh herb | Traditional; discouraged |

**There is no fabricated dose in this entry because there is no defensible therapeutic dose.** PA hepatotoxicity is cumulative and the injury is frequently irreversible; "small" or "occasional" internal doses are not established as safe. Contamination is a real-world exposure route: PAs from ragwort and related weeds enter the food chain via **honey, milk, and PA-contaminated herbal products and teas**, and dairy-transfer has been demonstrated experimentally (Knoop et al., 2024).

### Scientific Correlation

- **PA content and hepatotoxic potential — GRADED: ESTABLISHED (chemical/mechanistic).** *Jacobaea vulgaris* contains 1,2-unsaturated PAs whose P450-derived pyrrole metabolites form protein adducts and destroy sinusoidal endothelium (Yang et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2025).
- **Livestock veno-occlusive / "seneciosis" liver disease — GRADED: ESTABLISHED (veterinary, in vivo).** Controlled dosing of dairy cows with a defined ragwort PA extract raised the liver-damage enzymes GGT, AST and GLDH at 1.91 mg total PA/kg body weight/day over 28 days (Knoop et al., 2023). This is the archetypal grazing-animal poisoning.
- **Human PA-induced hepatic sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (HSOS/VOD) — GRADED: ESTABLISHED (clinical) for the PA *class*; SPECIES-LEVEL human case attribution to ragwort specifically = traditional/inferred.** PAs as a class are a recognised cause of human HSOS, presenting with painful hepatomegaly, ascites and jaundice, with treatment ranging from anticoagulation and glucocorticoids to TIPS and liver transplantation, and with significant mortality (Valla & Cazals-Hatem, 2016; Yang et al., 2019). Most documented human PA-HSOS series arise from *other* PA sources (herbal teas, *Gynura*, *Heliotropium*, contaminated grain); direct human ragwort ingestion is rare precisely because the plant is unpalatable — so the human hazard is real but the exposure to *this* species is chiefly indirect (honey/milk/contaminant).
- **Food-chain contamination (honey, milk) — GRADED: ESTABLISHED (analytical).** Experimental dairy transfer confirmed (Knoop et al., 2024).
- **Internal medicinal efficacy for any historical indication — GRADED: NULL.** No controlled human efficacy data were retrieved for any traditional internal use of ragwort. Absence of evidence here compounds a strong presence of harm.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Folk and humoral sources treated ragwort as a warming, drying, cleansing "hot" herb (consistent with its counter-irritant poultice use and its high-summer, sun-yellow signature). This grading is **inferred and low-confidence** — it is a reconstruction from use-pattern and signature, not a secured Dioscoridean or Avicennan temperament attribution (see the **[USER: verify]** flag under Origin). In Kenetica the far more load-bearing "classification" is toxicological: a hepatotropic vascular poison whose damage accrues silently.

### Contraindications

- **Absolute contraindication to all internal use.** PA hepatotoxicity is cumulative and often irreversible; no safe internal dose is established.
- **Hepatic sinusoidal obstruction syndrome / veno-occlusive disease** is the characteristic serious injury (hepatomegaly, ascites, jaundice, portal hypertension), potentially fatal.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** PAs cross into milk and are developmentally toxic; avoid entirely. Congener PAs are transferred into human breast milk contexts via contaminated food.
- **Genotoxic / carcinogenic concern:** 1,2-unsaturated PAs are DNA-reactive; regulatory bodies treat them as genotoxic carcinogens.
- **Contamination hazard:** avoid ragwort-contaminated honey, milk, salads (rosette leaves resemble edible greens), and PA-adulterated herbal teas/supplements.
- **Livestock:** highly hazardous in hay and silage, where drying removes the deterrent bitterness but *not* the PAs; a leading cause of fatal grazing-animal liver disease.
- **Topical/mucosal use** (wound wash, gargle, eye bath) is discouraged despite tradition, because PA absorption across broken skin and mucosa is not established as safe.
