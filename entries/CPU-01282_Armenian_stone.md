# Armenian stone (blue copper carbonate) — Armenion (ARMENION), "Armenium"
## *A smooth, azure, brittle stone from the metallic stones, given by Dioscorides the same actions as chrysocolla but weaker; the translator's heading to 5-105 reads "Armenian Stone — blue Copper Carbonate", and the chapter text gives no composition* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Armenian stone is chapter 105 of Book Five of the *De Materia Medica*, in the section "METALLIC STONES", headed ARMENION (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10372-10375; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` carries it as 5-105, line 10372, and `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 831 has "Armenion", class mineral, with the identification marked uncertain; the book's page index prints "ARMENION 798"). The translator's heading (line 10374) reads "Armenian Stone — blue Copper Carbonate". The whole chapter is three sentences (line 10375): "Armenium must be chosen which is smooth, an azure [blue] colour, very even, without stones, and very brittle. It does the same things as chrysocolla [above], only it is not as strong. It is also increases hairs on the eyelids." The last sentence is printed in this form in the English text, with "It is also increases", which appears to be a typographical slip for "It also increases"; it is quoted as printed.

So the text tells the reader how to choose the stone (smooth, azure, very even, free of stones, very brittle), says that its actions are those of chrysocolla at lower strength, and adds one action of its own, that it increases the hairs on the eyelids. It gives no preparation of its own, no place of origin (the name itself points to Armenia, but the text does not say so), and no dose.

*What "the same things as chrysocolla" refers to.* The chapter immediately above, 5-104, CHRUSOKOLLA, marked by the translator "TOXIC" and glossed "SUGGESTED: Chrysocolla. This was a name for Borax or Malachite and means Gold Solder" (lines 10363-10367), is the standard the Armenian stone is measured against. Its text reads in part: "Chrysocolla from Armenia is the best — considerably resembling leeks in colour; that from Macedonia is the second, then the Cyprian. The pure should be chosen, but that which is full of earth and stones must be refused." It then gives the washing (pound it, put it in a mortar, pour on water, rub it with the palm against the mortar, let it settle, strain, repeat "until it is pure and unadulterated", dry it in the sun, store it in jars) and the burning (pound as much as needed, set it in a platter over light coals). Its properties: "Chrysocolla is able to wear off scars, and repress fleshy abnormal growths, and it is cleansing, astringent, warming, gently corrosive and a little biting. It is one of the medicines that cause vomiting and are strong enough to kill." The Armenian stone is said to do "the same things" in lesser degree. This is how its actions and its danger are known from the text, by reference; the Armenian-stone chapter does not itself list the actions, and the translator did not mark the 5-105 heading "TOXIC". The Armenian chrysocolla of 5-104 is a different material from the Armenian stone of 5-105 even though both are named for the country; the text ranks the Armenian chrysocolla first among chrysocollas and says nothing of where the Armenian stone comes from. *The next chapter, for contrast.* The chapter after it, 5-106, KUANOS, glossed "Cyanochroite — blue hydrous Sulphate of Copper and Potassium", is a second blue copper mineral: "burnt like chalcitis [calcium carbonate] and washed like cadmia [5-84]. It has the ability to repress, is a little corrosive, forms scabs, and is ulcerating" (lines 10376-10379). It is a separate material with a separate description.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent, beyond the translator's gloss "blue Copper Carbonate". In general terms, the blue copper carbonate minerals are azurite (blue) and malachite (green), both basic copper carbonates; copper compounds of this kind are soluble in acid and are poisonous when swallowed in quantity, and they irritate the skin and the eye. The identification of the Greek stone with azurite is the translator's opinion and not a statement of the text. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records a blue copper stone that wears the actions of chrysocolla (cleansing, corrosive, an emetic strong enough to kill) in a milder form and that increases the hairs on the eyelids. The Codex does not recommend it. Copper compounds are poisonous when swallowed, caustic on wounds and irritant to the eye, and no part of the text supports a safe use. The entry exists to record what the text says, how the stone was to be chosen, and that Dioscorides knew its ally chrysocolla could kill.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives no preparation of its own for the Armenian stone. It says it does "the same things as chrysocolla", for which the washing and burning procedures are given in 5-104 (line 10367): pounding, washing repeatedly in a mortar with water, settling and straining, drying in the sun, storing in jars; and burning on light coals. Whether the same procedures were to be applied to the Armenian stone is not stated and is an inference only. No dose is given in the sources retrieved for either material. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Copper carbonate minerals, such as azurite and malachite, were ground and used as blue and green pigments from antiquity. Copper salts taken by mouth cause nausea and vomiting and, in larger amounts, damage the liver, kidneys and blood and can be fatal; copper compounds are corrosive to skin and mucous membranes. There is no evidence that any copper mineral grows eyelashes. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** The text prints no quality for the Armenian stone itself except that it is "very brittle" and "azure" and "does the same things as chrysocolla, only it is not as strong". By that reference the chrysocolla properties of 5-104 apply in lesser degree: "cleansing, astringent, warming, gently corrosive and a little biting". These are the qualities of another substance attached by comparison, and are not printed for the Armenian stone.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is asserted here. What the chapter offers a practitioner is a discipline of attention: it fixes the best stone by look, feel and fracture (smooth, azure, very even, without stones, very brittle) and ties it to a stronger relative (chrysocolla) whose own chapter warns that it can kill. That is a practice of assay and it is attested for this substance. It is not a cultivation status, and the chapter makes no claim about the person's constitution, vitality or long life.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The Armenian stone is a copper mineral that Dioscorides treats as a weaker chrysocolla, with no dose and a poisonous relative. What the chapter teaches is how to inspect a mineral before using it, and what the neighbouring chapter teaches is that a mineral that looks like a medicine may be a poison. The Codex does not recommend taking or applying it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Toxicity:** copper compounds are poisonous when swallowed and corrosive to skin, wounds and eyes; do not swallow, burn, inhale the dust of, or apply it, and do not put it near the eyes, including the lids.
- **Related material:** the text says it acts as chrysocolla does, which it says can kill when taken as an emetic; treat the Armenian stone as dangerous.
- **Fumes and dust:** grinding or heating copper minerals can release irritant dust and fumes.
- **Pregnancy and children:** any copper compound is unsuitable.
- **Identification:** the translator's "blue Copper Carbonate" is an opinion; the text identifies the stone only by colour, smoothness and brittleness.
