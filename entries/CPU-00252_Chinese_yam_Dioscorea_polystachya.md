### Origin & Tradition

Chinese Yam occupies a rare position in the materia medica: it is simultaneously a staple food and a Superior-Class tonic. *Dioscorea polystachya* (long known as *D. opposita* or *D. batatas*) is the cultivated source of Shān Yào, the long white rhizome eaten across China and prescribed in equal measure. This dual status is not incidental — it is the whole point. Shān Yào is the herb you reach for precisely when a patient is too depleted to tolerate aggressive tonics. It builds gently, broadly, and without heat.

The herb appears in the *Shennong Bencao Jing* (SBJ) under its old name Shǔ Yù (薯蓣), in the Superior Class, credited with the ability to supplement the Middle, strengthen the Yin, fill out the flesh, and — taken over time — to sharpen the senses, lighten the body, and resist hunger. The later name change to Shān Yào ("mountain medicine") is itself a small historical curiosity: the original Shǔ Yù was altered to avoid the personal names of two Tang and Song emperors (a practice called name-avoidance, 避讳, bìhuì), settling eventually on the present 山药.

What distinguishes Shān Yào from almost every other tonic is its tripartite reach. Classical medicine assigns it to three organ systems at once — the **Spleen** (digestion and Qi transformation), the **Lung** (Qi and the defensive exterior, plus fluid distribution), and the **Kidney** (Essence, Jing, and the root of constitution). Few single herbs are said to nourish all three, and fewer still do so without the cloying, stagnation-prone quality of richer tonics. This makes Shān Yào the connective tissue of countless formulas.

It is one of the six ingredients of **Liù Wèi Dì Huáng Wán** (六味地黄丸), where it supports the Spleen and Kidney while Rehmannia carries the main Yin-nourishing load. It is central to the management of what classical medicine called **Xiāo Kě** (消渴, "wasting and thirsting") — the syndrome corresponding broadly to diabetes — appearing in formulas aimed at the chronic thirst, hunger, and depletion of that pattern, which dovetails with the herb's documented effects on blood-glucose handling.

### Active Components

- **Mucilage (glycoprotein and polysaccharide complex)** — the slippery, viscous fraction that gives the raw rhizome its characteristic texture; the basis of its moistening, demulcent action on the Lung and gut lining. Contributes to the herb's reputation for nourishing without irritating.
- **Dioscorea polysaccharides** — water-soluble polysaccharides with documented immunomodulatory and antioxidant activity in laboratory studies; preliminary evidence for hypoglycemic effect.
- **Diosgenin** — a steroidal sapogenin found in *Dioscorea* species; historically significant as the industrial precursor for semi-synthetic steroid hormone manufacture. Present in the genus, though the content in culinary *D. polystachya* is modest compared with the diosgenin-rich wild yams (*D. villosa*, *D. nipponica*). `[USER: verify diosgenin content figure for D. polystachya specifically before citing a number — do not conflate with D. villosa]`
- **Allantoin** — present in the rhizome; documented wound-healing and mucosal-soothing properties; consistent with the traditional demulcent use.
- **Amylase and other digestive enzymes** — present in the fresh rhizome; part of the empirical basis for its digestive-supporting reputation.
- **Choline and trace amino acids** — minor nutritive constituents.
- **Dioscorin** — a storage glycoprotein of the genus with reported antioxidant and ACE-inhibitory activity in vitro. `[USER: verify dioscorin attribution for this species]`

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Shān Yào is the Kenetica framework's default "building base" — the herb you layer underneath more specialized actives because it strengthens the foundation without adding heat, stimulation, or digestive burden. The clinical reality it addresses is that hard-training athletes frequently cannot absorb what they consume. The Spleen-Lung-Kidney triad maps cleanly onto three failure points: digestion and nutrient extraction (Spleen), respiratory capacity and exterior defense (Lung), and the deep recovery/anabolic reserve TCM calls Jing (Kidney).

During `[BUILD]` phases, Shān Yào's mucilage and digestive-enzyme content support nutrient extraction during high caloric intake, while the polysaccharide fraction's effect on glucose handling smooths the metabolic load of surplus eating. This is the herb's most practical Kenetica use: maximizing absorption per gram consumed rather than simply eating more.

During `[RESET]` phases, the demulcent action soothes a gut lining stressed by high-intensity training, NSAID use, and caloric extremes, and the Lung-moistening quality addresses the dry, depleted respiratory pattern that follows heavy aerobic blocks. The Kidney/Jing-supporting dimension positions Shān Yào as a long-horizon constitutional tonic appropriate for the deep recovery that `[RESET]` is meant to deliver.

Its `[PRIME]` value is subtler — it is non-stimulating and non-disruptive, so it can remain in a stack approaching competition without interfering with sleep, appetite, or fluid balance, quietly underwriting the more aggressive performance actives.

This is firmly a `[GENERAL]` herb. Its food-grade safety profile, gentle action, and broad reach make it suitable for nearly continuous cyclical use, and it is one of the few tonics that rarely causes the stagnation or heat that limits richer materials.

### Preparation & Dosing

- **Standard decoction:** 15–30g dried rhizome (Shān Yào tolerates and often requires larger doses than most tonics); simmer 20–30 minutes; drink warm.
- **Culinary use:** fresh rhizome cooked in soups and congee — a legitimate and traditional delivery method, especially for convalescence and for children/elderly.
- **Stir-fried (chǎo Shān Yào):** preferred for pure Spleen-strengthening and for loose stools; the frying enhances the digestive-tonifying, drying-leaning action.
- **Raw (shēng):** preferred for nourishing Yin and moistening the Lung and Kidney, and in Xiāo Kě (wasting-thirst) patterns.
- **Standardized extract:** available; follow product standardization, typically taken with food. `[USER: confirm a standardized-extract dose range from a current monograph before stating numbers]`
- **Timing:** with meals for digestive applications; flexible otherwise given the non-stimulating profile.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**TCM Organ-Channel Assignment:**
- Sweet, neutral; enters the Spleen (脾), Lung (肺), and Kidney (肾) channels.
- **Primary action:** tonifies the Spleen and Stomach, augments the Lung Qi and Yin, strengthens the Kidney and consolidates Essence (Jing).
- **Specific indications:** chronic diarrhea from Spleen deficiency; chronic cough/wheeze from Lung deficiency; frequent urination, leukorrhea, and seminal loss from Kidney deficiency; and the thirst-and-wasting of Xiāo Kě.

**Earth & Water Element Context:** Shān Yào bridges Earth (Spleen — transformation) and Water (Kidney — Essence and constitution), with a supporting reach into Metal (Lung). This bridging quality is exactly why it appears in formulas that must simultaneously build the daily Qi (post-natal) and the deep reserve (pre-natal Jing) — it is a rare connector across the post-natal/pre-natal divide.

**Cross-tradition parallel:** Ayurveda's closest functional analogue is the *rasayana* (rejuvenative) class — broadly nourishing, deeply tolerated tonics taken over long periods. The Dioscorea genus itself appears in Ayurvedic practice (e.g., *Dioscorea bulbifera*), though with different emphasis. Western herbalism prized the genus chiefly for diosgenin (wild yam) rather than for the gentle pan-tonic role Chinese medicine assigns Shān Yào.

### Contraindications

- **Excess/stagnation patterns:** because Shān Yào is a tonic and mildly astringent, it is contraindicated where there is acute fullness, abdominal distension from food stagnation, or active dampness/phlegm excess — tonifying an excess pattern aggravates it.
- **Constipation from excess:** the astringent quality can worsen excess-type constipation.
- **Diabetes monitoring:** given documented glucose-lowering activity, patients on hypoglycemic medication should monitor blood sugar, as additive effects are plausible. `[USER: verify whether clinical hypoglycemia interaction is documented before stating it as established]`
- **Yam allergy:** rare hypersensitivity to *Dioscorea* species is possible.
- **Drug interactions:** no major documented interactions at food/standard doses; the diosgenin content of culinary Shān Yào is modest, so claims of hormonal activity from eating the yam are not supported — do not market it as a hormone-modulating agent.
