### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** Bryan 1930 lists "Sulphur" in his Chapter V mineral remedies (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1209; `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv`, "Bryan's English identification") and says (line 1137) that few minerals are prescribed in their crude or native state: "Antimony, Calamine, Granite, Ink, Indigo, and Sulphur almost exhaust the list." `_sources/ebers.csv` line 61 marks the identification uncertain ("Sulfur, S ... yes" in the id_uncertain column); Bryan gives no Egyptian word. In the text Sulphur is one ingredient of a pterygium remedy (lines 2847-2851): "ANOTHER TO DRIVE OUT PTERYGIUM. Red-lead, Powdered-wood-from-Arabia, Iron-from-Apollonopolis-parva, Calamine, Egg-of-an-Ostrich, Saltpetre-from-Upper-Egypt, Sulphur, Honey. Make into one and apply to the Eyes." The word "sulphur" occurs three times in the Bryan file (lines 1137, 1209 and 2850), all cited here.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 5-124 THEION (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 10477), translator's heading "Sulphur". "The best sulphur is thought to have never felt fire and is glistening in colour, transparent, and without stones. The best of that which has felt the fire is green and very fat. It is abundant in Melos and Lipara." Uses printed: taken in an egg, or the smoke inhaled, for coughs, those who spit rotten matter and the asthmatic; the smoke inhaled "as an abortifacient"; with *terminthos* rosin it takes off leprosy, lichen and rotten nails; smeared on with vinegar it is good for leprosy and removes vitiligines; with rosin it heals scorpion strikes and, with vinegar, strikes from sea dragons; with saltpetre it soothes itching and is "convenient for gout"; "A spoonful sprinkled on the forehead (or taken with an undercooked egg) helps jaundice, and is good for the nose and catarrh"; sprinkled on it "keeps one from sweating"; the smoke drawn up through a funnel cures hardness of hearing, is inhaled for lethargy and "stops excessive discharges of blood"; with wine and honey it heals bruises on the ears.

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt` lines 1137-1142, p.29), under "Jades and Stones: Middle Class": "Shi Liu Huang (Sulphur) is sour and warm. ### Active Components

Native sulphur is the element S, a yellow solid, mined near hot springs and volcanoes. Burning it gives sulphur dioxide, a pungent gas that irritates the airways and eyes. Sulphur is also a part of the amino acids cysteine and methionine, which the body obtains from protein; elemental sulphur is not the form used for that.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The texts use sulphur on the skin, as a fumigant and in food (an egg), and the Chinese classic itself calls it toxic. Inhaling its smoke, as Dioscorides advises for cough and asthma, is not safe. The Codex offers no protocol, dose or preparation. Modern organic-sulphur supplements are a different matter and are not discussed in these sources.

### Preparation & Dosing

DMM 5-124 gives its uses (above) with no weights; the one measure is "a spoonful sprinkled on the forehead (or taken with an undercooked egg)" for jaundice. The Ebers recipe is a mixture "made into one" and applied to the eyes (lines 2847-2851) with no quantities. Clark has no preparation beyond the remark that white sulphur "often becomes yellow after processing". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Elemental sulphur has long been used on the skin (for instance against scabies and as a keratolytic); this is general pharmacy and no study is cited here. Sulphur dioxide from burning sulphur is a respiratory irritant. No clinical studies were retrieved for the traditional uses. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Sulphur is named in Bryan's list (line 1209) and in one remedy for pterygium (lines 2847-2851), a mixture of mineral and other ingredients applied to the eyes. That is what was done for a complaint, not what sulphur does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); the passage read prints no words to be spoken for this remedy, but Bryan summarises and that is no proof that the papyrus gives none. No deity is attributed. The list marks the identification uncertain. [USER: verify]

**Assyrian (AT).** *(c) Absent, and the attestation rests on a list row.* The only locus is `_sources/assyrian.csv` line 52, whose cited sources I did not read. The blocking warning is in force. Whether sulphur was classed by the agency it opposes cannot be said. [USER: verify]

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. What 5-124 offers is a discipline of attention: sulphur that "has never felt fire" and is "glistening, transparent, and without stones", the places named (Melos, Lipara), and the smoke handled through a funnel. No cultivation status is assigned. He records one use as an abortifacient and states it without comment.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent, and the attestation rests on a spine row.* No sulphur row or text was found. Innate heat and radical moisture cannot be tied to sulphur without a source and are not tied to it here. [USER: verify]

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: middle grade.* Shi Liu Huang stands under "Jades and Stones: Middle Class" (Yang p.29). The preface (lines 348-400) says medium-class medicinals "mainly nurture personality", "may or may not be toxic", to be weighed "before putting them to their appropriate use", for those who "intend to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion". Sulphur is marked toxic in the entry itself, and Yang's note 54 says "one must stop taking it once the disease has been hit." So it is graded as a course drug, not one that nourishes life. The "transform rare matters like gold, silver, copper, and iron" line belongs to the alchemical stratum of the text (Yang's preface note 6 on the Daoist elixir interest). No *jing* action is asserted.

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(c) Absent.* No Sushruta locus for sulphur was found (above). Nothing is said about *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* for it.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* Sulphur is a therapeutic earth medicine of ch.20: "Yellow sulphur dispels spirits, and dries pus, blood, [lymph, etc.]" and black sulphur "cures erysipelas and contagious diseases" (Clark lines 863-866). Medicine is the third of the four treatment branches and supports practice. No *bcud len* use and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice was found for it.

**Practitioner's note.** Sulphur is a skin and fumigant drug in the sources read, graded middle by the Chinese classic with its own warning to stop once the disease is met. Nothing sourced supports taking it for cultivation, and the smoke the Greek text advises inhaling is a respiratory irritant. A cultivator can read the entry for the discipline of sorting and sourcing the material and take nothing from it as a regimen. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Burning sulphur makes sulphur dioxide, which is dangerous to inhale, especially for people with asthma. Do not inhale the smoke or swallow sulphur on the strength of this entry. Sulphur on the skin can irritate. The "abortifacient" use in the Greek text is recorded, not endorsed, and is dangerous. Crude or "processed" mineral sulphur may carry arsenic and other contaminants.
