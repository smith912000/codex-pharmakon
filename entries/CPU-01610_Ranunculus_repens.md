# Ranunculus repens — Chrusanthemon (Dioscorides 4-58); bupthalmum, calchas, chalcitis, chalcanthum, chalcanthemon

## *Ranunculus repens, the last of four Latin names before the English on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-58 ("Ranunculus repens [Linnaeus] — Creeping Buttercup [Mabberley]"; the line begins "Chrysanthemum, Chrysanthemum simplex [Fuchs], Ranunculus pratensis erectus dulcis [Bauhin]"), with two "other usage" glosses, Chrysanthemum segetum (Corn Marigold) and Chrysanthemum coronarium (Garden Chrysanthemum, Crown Marigold, Crown Daisy)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (lines 8511 to 8514) prints three Latin names with attributions, then the creeping buttercup as Mabberley's English name, and then two "other usage" identifications that are different plants. The row takes *Ranunculus repens*. It was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-58. CHRUSANTHEMON*, heading at line 8510, SUGGESTED line at 8511 to 8514, text at line 8515. It stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), after *4-57. ELICHRUSON* and before *4-59. AGERATON* (heading at line 8516). The translators' index gives *Ranunculus repens* and *Ranunculus pratensis erectus dulcis* at page 599, and *bupthalmum* at page 599.

The whole chapter reads: "Chrysanthemon is a tender shrubby herb, bringing out smooth stalks, very jagged leaves all around, and yellowish flowers strongly shining with an eye (which is why it is called this). It grows in towns, and the stalks are eaten as vegetables. The flowers (pounded into small pieces with wax ointment) are said to dissolve steatomata [encysted fatty tumour]. It gives the jaundiced a good colour in good time given to drink after they have spent a long time in the baths. (Chrysanthemon you take out of the earth before the rising of the sun. They are astringent to the body, and are hung around the neck, being good for averting women witches and all enchantments.) It is also called bupthalmum, calchas, chalcitis, chalcanthum, or chalcanthemon, the Romans say acantha, the Thuscans, garuleum, and the Africans, churzeta."

The chapter's description is of a tender, shrubby herb with smooth stalks, "very jagged leaves all around" and yellowish flowers "strongly shining with an eye", and it gives that as the reason for the name. It grows "in towns", and "the stalks are eaten as vegetables". The flowers are pounded with wax ointment, and are "said to dissolve steatomata" (the translators gloss this as an encysted fatty tumour). They are given to the jaundiced to drink after long time in the baths, to give "a good colour in good time". The sentence in parentheses, as printed, says the plant is taken "out of the earth before the rising of the sun", is "astringent to the body", and is "hung around the neck, being good for averting women witches and all enchantments". The chapter prints these further names: *bupthalmum*, *calchas*, *chalcitis*, *chalcanthum* and *chalcanthemon* ("it is also called"), the Roman *acantha*, the Thuscan *garuleum* and the African *churzeta*.

**The parenthesis and the amulet.** The sentence about gathering before sunrise and hanging the plant round the neck is printed in parentheses in this edition. The lines read do not say why the translators or the printed edition enclose it, and the Codex does not claim it is a later addition. What the text records is an amuletic use that Dioscorides' chapter places next to the remedies. Recording is not endorsing. The Codex neither recommends it nor reads it as a cultivation practice (see the Cultivation Register).

**Identity.** The creeping buttercup is a modern identification the translators credit to Mabberley, and two other suggestions appear on the same line. The description (a tender shrubby herb with yellow flowers whose stalks are eaten as vegetables) is general enough that the Codex does not choose among them. [USER: verify]

**Names shared with other chapters.** The name *bupthalmum* is also printed in other chapters. *3-156. BUPHTHALMON* (heading at line 7864; see entry I_1130) is headed with that name, and in *4-33. SIDERITIS* (heading at line 8291) the list of other names includes "Osthenes says buphthalmum" (line 8297). These are different chapters, and the Codex does not read them as one plant.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter number, *Chrusanthemon*, *Ranunculus repens* and *Creeping Buttercup* found no existing entry on 4-58 itself. Three entries mention it. I_888 (Crown Imperial) records 4-58 as a false match on the translators' gloss "Crown Marigold, Crown Daisy" and says the chapter "is about a different plant"; its attachment of 4-58 to that row (EXTRACTION_GAPS line 275) is the source of the confusion, and this entry is the one for 4-58 itself. I_1094 (*Gnaphalium arenarium*, 4-57) names 4-58 as the next chapter. I_1130 (*Matricaria inodora*, 3-156) notes that 4-58 shares several elements and says 4-58 "has no entry of its own". None is merged. Separate entries exist for other buttercups: I_632 (Meadow Buttercup, *Ranunculus acris*) and I_724 (Lesser Celandine, *Ranunculus ficaria*), which are not Dioscorides' chapter 4-58.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. It is a general, well-established fact that buttercups (*Ranunculus*) contain ranunculin, which gives protoanemonin when the fresh plant is crushed, and that fresh buttercup is acrid and can blister skin. The chapter does not mention this, and which plant the chapter describes is uncertain, so the fact is not attributed to it. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records flowers pounded in wax ointment against a fatty tumour, a drink for jaundice after long baths, stalks eaten as a vegetable, and a plant hung round the neck against witches and enchantments. These are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for any of them, and the identification is the translators'. If the plant were a buttercup, the chapter's vegetable and drink uses would be unsafe, as stated under Contraindications.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations: the flowers "pounded into small pieces with wax ointment"; the plant "given to drink" (the chapter does not say in what); the stalks eaten as vegetables; and the plant gathered before sunrise and hung round the neck. No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The one well-established general fact is the one stated under Active Components: fresh buttercups are acrid, which sits uneasily with stalks eaten as a vegetable.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one quality, in the parenthesis: the plant is "astringent to the body". It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. It prints the flowers as "yellowish ... strongly shining with an eye".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter does record an amuletic use, in its parenthesis: the plant is taken from the earth before sunrise and hung round the neck to avert "women witches and all enchantments". That is a record of a practice around the plant and not an endorsement by Dioscorides, and it is not a cultivation status. What the chapter offers as discipline of attention is a plant told by its jagged leaves and the eye in its flower, a place (towns), a time of gathering (before sunrise) and a list of names in several languages. The plant is given no status beyond a list of remedies and an amulet. The identification is the translators'.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant beyond the gathering rule and amulet it records, which the Codex neither recommends nor treats as a cultivation practice. It offers a description, a poultice, a drink and a charm, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. It is a general, well-established fact that fresh buttercup plants are acrid and can irritate or blister skin and mouth and cause poisoning when eaten, so none of the uses above should be tried on a plant identified from this entry. Jaundice and any lump under the skin need medical assessment. An amulet is not protection against anything. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
