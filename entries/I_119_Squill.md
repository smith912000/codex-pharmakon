### Botanical Identity
Large bulbous perennial; very large bulb (up to 15–20cm, 1kg+ — one of the largest bulbs in the plant kingdom); strap-like leaves appearing after flowering; tall spike of white flowers (late summer, leafless at flowering); native to the Mediterranean coast and Middle East, growing in sandy coastal soils. The bulb is the drug material. Two main varieties: **white squill** (medicinal — cardiac glycosides usable therapeutically) and **red squill** (higher scilliroside content — used as a rodenticide because rats cannot vomit and so succumb to the cardiac toxin, while it is emetic-protective in humans).

**Cardiac glycoside source**: Squill was, alongside foxglove (*Digitalis*) and strophanthus, one of the three classical sources of cardiac glycoside drugs. It was official in pharmacopoeias as a cardiotonic and expectorant well into the 20th century. Its cardiac glycoside (proscillaridin) is still used in some countries.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Dioscorides / Greco-Roman (DMM)
Book II, Chapter 171: Detailed account of squill preparation (roasting, vinegar maceration — "squill vinegar" was a standard preparation). Applications: expectorant for chronic cough and chest conditions; diuretic for dropsy (oedema — the cardiac/renal application); for the stomach; as an emetic; topical for skin conditions and calluses. The "Pythagorean" and "Epimenidean" squill preparations were standardised compound forms.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine / Ibn Sīnā (CM)
*ʿUnṣul* — hot and dry. Applications: expectorant for chronic bronchial conditions and asthma; diuretic for dropsy; for the heart (cardiotonic — recognised as strengthening cardiac function); emmenagogue; topical for skin and joint conditions. Ibn Sīnā's recognition of the cardiac application is notable.

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (EP)
Squill bulb appears in EP preparations for dropsy and respiratory conditions; the plant's Egyptian abundance made it readily available.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)
Squill in AT preparations for respiratory and oedema conditions, and as a rodenticide (the red squill rodent-control application is ancient).

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Cardiotonic (Cardiac Glycosides — Proscillaridin + Scillaren)

**Proscillaridin A + scillaren A**: Bufadienolide cardiac glycosides with the digitalis-type mechanism:
- Inhibit Na+/K+-ATPase in cardiac myocytes → increased intracellular sodium → reduced Na+/Ca²⁺ exchange → increased intracellular calcium → increased contractile force (positive inotropy)
- Improves cardiac output in heart failure → reduces venous congestion → resolves the peripheral and pulmonary oedema (dropsy)

The traditional "dropsy" application (DMM, CM, EP, AT) is now understood: dropsy (generalised oedema) was usually caused by congestive heart failure; squill's cardiac glycosides improve cardiac output, resolving the oedema. The ancient physicians observed the oedema resolve without understanding the cardiac mechanism — they classified it as a "diuretic" effect, which is partially correct (improved renal perfusion follows improved cardiac output).

### 5.2 Expectorant (Dual Mechanism)

**Mucilage + calcium oxalate raphides + cardiac glycosides**:
- At low (sub-cardiac) doses: mild gastric mucosal irritation triggers a reflex increase in bronchial secretion (the "nauseant expectorant" mechanism) → thins and mobilises bronchial mucus
- Mucilage content directly soothes irritated bronchial mucosa
- Squill was one of the most important expectorants in pharmacy (Compound Squill preparations, "Gee's Linctus," and similar were standard cough remedies into the 20th century)

### 5.3 Diuretic

The diuretic effect is partly direct (cardiac glycoside improvement of renal perfusion) and partly the cardiotonic resolution of cardiac oedema. In healthy individuals the diuretic effect is modest; in cardiac patients it is significant (secondary to improved cardiac output).

### 5.4 Rodenticidal (Scilliroside — Red Squill)

**Scilliroside**: Red squill's high scilliroside content makes it a selective rodenticide:
- Rats and mice CANNOT vomit (no emetic reflex) → they retain the lethal cardiac glycoside dose → death from cardiac toxicity
- Humans and most other animals VOMIT in response to the emetic action before absorbing a lethal dose → relative safety
- This species-selective toxicity made red squill one of the safest rodenticides for use around humans and livestock; it was widely used before synthetic anticoagulant rodenticides

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Cardiac glycoside (proscillaridin)**: Proscillaridin A was used as an oral cardiac glycoside (similar to digoxin) for heart failure; still available in some countries. Its pharmacology is well-characterised. Largely superseded by digoxin (more predictable pharmacokinetics).

**Expectorant**: Squill was official in numerous pharmacopoeias as an expectorant; Compound Squill Oxymel and similar preparations were standard. Removed from most modern formularies in favour of safer expectorants, but the nauseant-expectorant mechanism is pharmacologically valid.

**Anticancer (proscillaridin)**: Proscillaridin A has attracted recent research interest as an anticancer agent — it inhibits cancer cell proliferation (cardiac glycosides have selective cytotoxicity toward cancer cells via Na+/K+-ATPase and histone modification effects). Active in vitro/preclinical research area.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The Pythagorean squill**: Ancient pharmacy produced standardised squill preparations named after philosophers and sages — "Pythagorean squill" and "Epimenidean squill" were specific compound formulations. The attribution of standardised drug preparations to revered figures (Pythagoras, Epimenides) reflects the ancient practice of lending authority to a pharmaceutical preparation through association with a wise founder — an early form of branding. The squill preparations were among the most carefully standardised drugs of antiquity, reflecting the recognition (correct, given the cardiac glycoside content) that dose precision mattered.

**Squill and the long search for the heart drug**: Squill, foxglove, and strophanthus form the classical trio of cardiac glycoside plants. Of these, squill has the longest documented history (DMM, CM, EP, AT — back to ~1550 BCE in Egypt). The recognition that squill helped "dropsy" (which we now understand as the oedema of heart failure) was an empirical observation made independently across four ancient traditions. The connection between the swollen body (dropsy) and the failing heart was not understood until William Withering's work on foxglove in 1785 — but the squill treatment for dropsy had been correctly applied for 3,300 years before the mechanism was understood. This is one of the longest gaps between effective empirical use and mechanistic understanding in the entire Codex.

**The rat that cannot vomit**: The species-selective toxicity of red squill — lethal to rats (no vomit reflex) but emetically self-protective in humans — represents one of the most elegant examples of differential pharmacology exploited in traditional pest control. The ancient observation that squill killed rats but was relatively safe around humans and livestock (because humans vomit it out) reflects sophisticated empirical pharmacology — the recognition of a differential physiological response (emesis capacity) exploited for selective toxicity, achieved without any understanding of the underlying cardiac glycoside mechanism or the neurology of the vomiting reflex.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

**⚠️ Squill contains cardiac glycosides with a narrow therapeutic margin. Internal use requires medical supervision. This section documents traditional and pharmaceutical use.**

| Form | Dose | Application |
|------|------|-------------|
| Squill vinegar / oxymel (traditional) | Small carefully measured doses | Expectorant; historical cardiac/dropsy use — supervision required |
| Proscillaridin A (pharmaceutical) | 0.25–0.5mg daily (medical use) | Heart failure — in countries where available; specialist cardiac prescription |
| Compound squill expectorant (historical) | Per formula | Chronic bronchitis — largely superseded |
| Red squill (rodenticide) | Not for internal human use | Pest control only |

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM (cross-reference)**: Bitter, acrid; warm; slightly toxic. Channels: Heart (心), Lung (肺), Bladder (膀胱). Strengthens the Heart Yang, transforms phlegm, promotes urination, resolves oedema.

**Ayurvedic (cross-reference)**: Pungent, bitter; heating; toxic without processing. Reduces Kapha ↓↓. *Hṛdaya* (cardiac), *Śvāsahara* (anti-asthmatic), *Mūtralā* (diuretic). Would require *śodhana* (detoxification) processing.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Cardiac glycoside toxicity**: Narrow therapeutic margin; overdose causes nausea, vomiting, arrhythmia, heart block, death — same toxicity profile as digoxin overdose
- **Cardiac medication interactions**: Do not combine with digoxin, other cardiac glycosides, or drugs affecting potassium (diuretics, etc. — hypokalaemia potentiates glycoside toxicity)
- **Pregnancy**: Emmenagogue + cardiac glycoside — avoid
- **Renal impairment**: Reduced glycoside clearance → accumulation → toxicity
- **Not for self-medication**: The cardiac glycoside content makes unsupervised internal use dangerous
- **Calcium oxalate skin irritation**: Handling the fresh bulb causes skin irritation (raphides); wear gloves
