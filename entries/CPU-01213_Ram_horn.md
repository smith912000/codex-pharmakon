# Ram horn — *thug* (Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch.20); *Lug thuk* (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Animals)
## *The horn of the ram (male sheep). Both Tibetan texts print only the English word "Ram"; neither prints a Latin name for it* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is the Tibetan *Four Tantras*. In Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; "Clark" below) chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra is titled "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (line 792). Its materia medica runs through gems, minerals and plants and ends with "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147): "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). The first of these thirteen is horn, and the ram is named in it (line 1158): "thug: Ram: [has nine names]. The horns of Ram and Saiga antelope are ecbolic." The Tibetan name *thug* is printed first, as Clark says he does for every substance (line 83), and the English follows. The words in square brackets are, by Clark's own statement, interpolations from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries (line 82), so "has nine names" is commentary; the sentence about the horn's action stands outside the brackets. Clark does not define "ecbolic" here. Elsewhere in the same list he glosses *gtsod* (Hodgson's antelope) as "oxytocic (facilitates childbirth)" (line 1155), which shows the kind of action the word names in this passage. That is Clark's gloss of another animal and not a statement about the ram.

The second Tibetan text on disk, the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`; "MTK" below), gives the same list of thirteen (line 3503, "horn, bone, flesh, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, fur, urine, feces and the entire body") and the same claim with a different companion: "The horns of serows/gorals and rams are ecbolic" (line 3508). Clark pairs the ram with the saiga antelope; MTK pairs it with serows or gorals. The two renderings disagree on the companion animal and agree on the ram and on the action. MTK's Glossary on Animals gives the Tibetan as *Lug thuk* with the English "Ram" (lines 7119, 7125); the Latin column on that page begins with the deer and has no entry for the ram (line 7130 and above). The glossary's separate "Sheep" row carries "Oris aries Linnaeus" (line 7110, OCR for *Ovis aries*).

### Active Components

Sheep horn is a bony core covered by a sheath of keratin, the fibrous structural protein that also makes up hair, wool, nails and hooves. Neither Tibetan text names a constituent or a mechanism for the horn, and none is supplied here. The "ecbolic" action the texts assert is a traditional claim about effect. This entry does not link it to any component.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The single claim the retrieved sources make for ram horn is that it is ecbolic, an action on the womb, so it is a material to be avoided in pregnancy on the tradition's own terms and not a tonic. Clark's introduction adds that "nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57). The material is also hard to separate from the animal: the horn of a farmed sheep is a by-product, but the Tibetan lists place it beside wild species (takin, wild sheep, saiga, rhino, line 1152 onward), and a modern practitioner has no way to tell from the text which horn is meant. The Codex's application layer therefore has nothing to add. No protocol is offered.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither passage gives a preparation, a part of the horn, a form (powder, ash, decoction) or a dose for ram horn. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] The nearest ram preparation read is in chapter 30, "Direct Therapeutic Techniques" (Clark line 1409): for wind disorders one may "administer the soup of a complete ram's head which has been preserved for three years" (line 1411). That is the head, preserved, as soup. The text does not say the horn is included, and it is not evidence about horn.

### Scientific Correlation

Keratin is a protein that humans digest poorly; as a food or drug it is largely inert. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry, for ecbolic or any other action of ram horn. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT).** Clark prints no taste, potency or post-digestive taste for the ram's horn, and neither does MTK. Clark's preface says that detailed texts such as the *Crystal Orb* record, for thousands of substances, "which of the six tastes, eight natural potencies, three post-digestive 'tastes,' seventeen secondary qualities and individual uses each possesses" (line 57). Those texts were not read, so no quality is assigned to ram horn here. The only attribute the sources give is its action, ecbolic. The entry carries no TCM, Unani or Ayurvedic classification because it is not tagged with those traditions.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Ram horn sits in the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica as one of the horns, in the category "Medicines of Animal Origin" (Clark lines 1147-1158), and is credited with one therapeutic action. The tradition does have a formal division of rejuvenation, "essence-extraction" (Clark line 128, "the division of essence-extraction (rejuvenation, etc.)"; also line 136). In the animal section read (lines 1147-1202), no animal substance is placed in it, and ram horn is not named in the compounding lists of chapter 21 (lines 1204-1225, read in full for the disease-class lists). It carries no status as an essence-extraction material and none is asserted. Clark records that the rejuvenating roots in his survey are plants (line 69: sundew, angelica, *Polygonatum*, salep orchid), and that animal products are now rarely used (line 57). The *nyes pa* and the three poisons from which they derive are the root of disease in this tradition, and medicine is one branch of treatment, not the whole; none of that is attached to ram horn by any text read. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in the Tibetan sources, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports using ram horn for cultivation. The one thing the sources say about it, an action on the womb, is a reason for caution, not for use, and the tradition's own modern editor notes that animal medicines have largely fallen out of use. For a cultivator the honest entry is a record: the Tibetan materia medica lists horn first among its thirteen animal medicines, and the ram's is named in it.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy:** the texts call the horn ecbolic; treat it as a hazard to pregnancy.
- **Hygiene and infection:** raw material from livestock (horn, hair, hide) can carry bacterial spores and parasites, the best known being anthrax spores. Untreated horn is not a clean material.
- **Legal and conservation status:** domestic sheep horn is unrestricted. The Tibetan list also names wild sheep and other horned wild species (Clark line 1152 onward), and trade in wild-sheep parts is regulated in many countries. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** the material is a by-product of an animal; nothing in the sources requires a living animal to be harmed for it.
