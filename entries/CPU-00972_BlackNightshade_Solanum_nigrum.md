### Origin & Tradition

Black nightshade is one of the great paradox-plants of the pharmacopoeia: a cosmopolitan weed of disturbed ground, eaten as a cooked pot-herb across Africa, India, and East Asia, yet belonging to a family (Solanaceae) whose reputation is dominated by poison. The distinction that every tradition had to learn empirically — and that modern chemistry confirms — is between the ripe black berry and cooked young leaf (low in glycoalkaloid) and the unripe green berry and raw mature foliage (high in the toxic glycoalkaloid solanine/solamargine). This single discrimination organizes the entire cross-tradition record: those who respected it used *Solanum nigrum* as a cooling, liver-protective, anti-inflammatory medicine and famine food; those who ignored it were poisoned. The plant appears independently across six literate medical traditions, an unusually high convergence for a "weed."

**EP — Ebers Papyrus / Egyptian tradition (cooling poultice herb):** Nightshade species of the Egyptian flora were employed as cooling topical applications for inflamed skin, swellings, and "hot" eruptions — the characteristic Solanaceae use of a demulcent-cooling leaf poultice on heat conditions. The genus was part of the Nile Valley repertoire of cooling external remedies applied to burns and inflamed swellings. *(General attestation; specific formula details [USER: verify against a primary Ebers translation before citing a specific recipe].)*

**AT — Assyrian / Mesopotamian tradition (cuneiform materia medica):** Solanum-type plants appear among the cooling and swelling-reducing plants of the Mesopotamian medical tablets, applied topically for inflamed swellings and used in the broad Assyrian category of "cooling herbs" that counter feverish heat. As with much of the cuneiform materia medica, plant identifications are partly reconstructed, so this attestation is at the genus/category level rather than the individual recipe.

**DMM — De Materia Medica (Στρύχνον κηπαῖον — "garden strychnon"):** Dioscorides distinguishes the edible "garden nightshade" (*strychnon kēpaion*, our *Solanum nigrum*) from the dangerous sleep- and madness-inducing nightshades (the *strychnon hypnotikon* and *manikon*, belladonna/datura relatives). He describes the garden species as cooling and safe enough to eat, and prescribes the crushed leaf as a cooling poultice for erysipelas, shingles, headache (applied to the temples), inflamed eyes, and burning stomach — the classic Galenic "cold and dry" application against hot inflammatory conditions. His explicit separation of the safe garden nightshade from its toxic cousins is the founding text of the ripe/edible vs. toxic discrimination.

**CM — Canon of Medicine (عنب الثعلب — ʿInab al-Thaʿlab, "fox grape"):** Ibn Sina classifies black nightshade as cold and dry, a cooling repellent (radd) that drives back hot humoral fluxes. Applications: the cooling poultice for erysipelas and "St. Anthony's fire," inflamed swellings, and burning heat of the stomach and liver; the juice for hot headache and inflamed eyes. He warns that the plant in excess or in the wrong (green) form is stupefying and dangerous — preserving the Greek caution about dose and ripeness within the Galenic-Unani framework.

**SS — Sushruta / Charaka Samhita (Kakamachi — काकमाची):** In Ayurveda *kakamachi* (black nightshade) is a well-attested dravya, classified with tikta-katu (bitter-pungent) rasa and a notable reputation as a rasayana-adjacent liver and skin herb. It is described as balancing all three doshas in appropriate preparation, used for shotha (oedema/swelling), skin disorders (kushtha), and especially conditions of the liver and spleen — the pre-modern anticipation of its modern hepatoprotective profile. The cooked leaf is also a recognized wholesome pot-herb (shaka) in the Ayurvedic dietetic literature.

**SBJ — Bencao / Bencao Gangmu (龍葵 — Lóng Kuí, "dragon sunflower/mallow"):** Chinese materia medica lists *lóng kuí* as bitter, slightly sweet, and cold, entering to clear heat and resolve toxicity (qing re jie du), cool the blood, reduce swelling, and promote urination. It is a classic anti-toxic-swelling herb applied both internally (decoction) and externally (crushed fresh herb) for hot sores, abscesses, inflamed swellings, and dysuria — and in later usage as an adjuvant in inflammatory and neoplastic swellings. Li Shizhen's tradition situates it firmly among the cooling, heat-clearing, toxin-resolving plants.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Discriminated Weed:** Black nightshade is the tradition's teaching-plant for the principle that *toxicity is a function of part, ripeness, and dose, not of species*. Every culture that adopted it had to encode the same warning: the same plant is food and poison depending on the black-ripe or green-unripe state of its fruit and the cooked or raw state of its leaf. Symbolically it sits at the boundary of the Solanaceae — the family of the witches' flying-ointment (belladonna, mandrake, henbane, datura) — as the one member domesticated into the cooking pot. It is the "safe sibling" among dangerous kin, and its folk names (fox-grape, dragon-mallow, garden strychnon) preserve the memory of a genus that had to be handled with knowledge. In Kenetica it is filed as a **discrimination substance**: a plant whose correct use is inseparable from knowing exactly which part, at which stage, in which preparation — the embodied lesson that the difference between medicine and poison is craft.

### Active Components

- **Solasodine** — steroidal alkaloid aglycone; the backbone of the steroidal glycosides; studied for hepatoprotective and anti-proliferative activity; also of interest as a synthetic precursor to steroid hormones
- **Solasonine & solamargine** — steroidal glycoalkaloids (glycosides of solasodine); concentrated in fruit; the bioactive-but-double-edged fraction: cytotoxic/anti-proliferative in controlled study, contributory to toxicity in excess
- **Solanine & chaconine** — the toxic glycoalkaloids of unripe green berries and raw mature foliage; cholinesterase-inhibiting and membrane-disrupting; the source of the poisoning hazard (GI, neurological)
- **Solamargine-rich berry glycosides** — the fraction implicated in the hepatoprotective and antioxidant activity of ripe-berry fractions in cell studies
- **Polyphenols & flavonoids** — total phenolics and flavonoids in leaf and berry contributing antioxidant/free-radical-scavenging capacity
- **Steroidal saponins** — additional membrane-active constituents

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**1. Hepatic protection and recovery ([REPAIR] / [RESET])**
The best-supported modern rationale for *Solanum nigrum*'s traditional liver reputation is hepatoprotection: bioactivity-guided fractions of the berry (rich in solasodine-type steroidal glycosides) protect hepatocyte-model (HepG2) cells against chemical injury in vitro. This maps directly onto the Ayurvedic and Chinese use of the plant for liver/spleen swelling and heat, and positions it as a REPAIR-phase hepatic-support botanical — used in supervised, ripe-fruit or standardized-extract form only.

**2. Cooling anti-inflammatory topical ([RESET])**
The unbroken cross-tradition use (Greek, Unani, Chinese, Egyptian) of the crushed fresh leaf as a cooling poultice for hot skin inflammation, erysipelas, and inflamed swellings is the plant's most consistent application. In Kenetica it is a topical heat-clearing agent for localized inflammatory heat — external use only.

**3. Heat-clearing, swelling-resolving internal decoction ([RESET] — supervised)**
The TCM "clear heat, resolve toxicity, reduce swelling, promote urination" profile supports cautious internal use in inflammatory-damp-heat presentations. Given the glycoalkaloid hazard, Kenetica treats internal use as supervised-only, low-dose, ripe-material-only.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:

- **Hepatoprotection / steroidal glycosides:** Chester K, Paliwal S, Khan W, Ahmad S (2017), *Frontiers in Pharmacology* 8:434 — bioactivity-guided fractionation showed the n-butanol fraction of *Solanum nigrum* berries (rich in solasonine, solamargine, solasodine) was the most potent hepatoprotective fraction against D-galactosamine and H₂O₂-induced toxicity in HepG2 cells, with the highest antioxidant capacity and steroidal-glycoside content. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2017.00434)

**Evidence grade:** Preclinical / in vitro (cell-model). The hepatoprotective signal is mechanistically plausible and aligns with multi-tradition liver use, but there is no strong human clinical trial evidence retrieved here — grade as **traditional use with supportive preclinical data**, not clinically proven. The solanine toxicity of unripe fruit is well established toxicologically and is the dominant safety consideration.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** Bitter, slightly sweet, cold; clears heat and resolves toxicity (qing re jie du), cools blood, reduces swelling, promotes urination; heat-toxin and damp-heat swellings
- **Ayurvedic:** *Kakamachi* — tikta-katu rasa; tridosha-balancing in correct preparation; liver/spleen and skin herb, shotha-hara (anti-oedema)

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian (EP).** Nightshade-type cooling poultices belong to the Nile-Valley repertoire of cold external remedies, but the attestation is at genus/category level and the entry flags specific Ebers recipes for verification. The corpus is indication-organised and *heka*-completed in ways not recoverable for this plant; it records a cooling application, not any action on the *ka*, *ba* or *ib*. No cultivation framing — (c) absent.

**Assyrian (AT).** *Solanum*-type cooling, swelling-reducing plants appear in the Mesopotamian tablets only at the category level, with identifications partly reconstructed and the standing blocking warning in force. No vital or spiritual status can be raised here — (c) absent.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides' *strychnon kēpaion* is the founding text of this plant's entire discipline: he explicitly separates the safe, edible "garden nightshade" from the stupefying *hypnotikon* and *manikon* cousins. He has no subtle-body system; what he offers a cultivator is the discipline of attention in its sharpest form — a discrimination of species, part and ripeness on which safety literally depends. (c) absent as to cultivation status, with a real and load-bearing assay discipline in its place.

**Unani — Ibn Sīnā (CM).** *ʿInab al-thaʿlab* is classed cold and dry, a cooling *radd* (repellent) driving back hot fluxes, with the Greek dose/ripeness caution preserved. This is a therapeutic humoral frame; no conservation of innate heat or radical moisture — the tradition's cultivation idea — is attested for it. Framed, status unattested — (b).

**Ayurveda (SS).** *Kākamācī* is a well-attested *dravya* (tikta–kaṭu, a liver/spleen and skin herb, *śotha-hara*). The entry calls it "*rasāyana*-adjacent," but that is not a formal classification: no *rasāyana* or *vājīkaraṇa* cultivation status is attested for it in the sources retrieved, and the "adjacent" wording should not be read upward into one. Framed as therapeutic, no cultivation status — (b).

**Chinese (SBJ).** *Lóng kuí* 龍葵 is bitter–slightly-sweet and cold, a *qing re jie du* 清熱解毒 (clear-heat, resolve-toxicity) drug — but it is documented in the later compilations (Li Shizhen's *Bencao Gangmu*, 1596) rather than the Han *Shennong Bencao Jing*, and no *san pin* 三品 grade is recorded for it. If anything its action is disease-attacking (*zhi bing* 治病), the opposite pole from conservational *yang sheng* 養生; it carries a therapeutic status only. Framed, no grade attested — (b).

**Practitioner's note.** Black nightshade is a discrimination plant, and its cultivation lesson is craft, not vitality: in no tagged tradition is it a longevity or tonic drug. What a cultivator actually does with it is Dioscoridean — know the species, the part, the ripeness, the preparation — and abstain absolutely from the green berry and raw mature leaf. The register's honest verdict is attention plus abstention.

### Contraindications

- **Unripe (green) berries and raw mature foliage — POISON.** High solanine/chaconine content; ingestion causes GI distress, and in quantity, neurological and cardiac toxicity. Never consume green fruit. This is the single most important warning for the plant.
- **Pregnancy and lactation** — avoid internal use; steroidal glycoalkaloids and uncertain safety.
- **Children** — heightened sensitivity to glycoalkaloid poisoning; berries are an accidental-poisoning risk.
- **Solanaceae sensitivity / anticholinergic drug interaction** — glycoalkaloids inhibit cholinesterase; theoretical additive effects.
- **Self-dosing internal use is discouraged** — the therapeutic window between the cooling medicine and the toxic dose is narrow and part-dependent; internal use should be supervised and material-verified.
