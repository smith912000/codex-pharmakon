# Snake flesh — rGyud-bzhi, "Snake meat" (kluhi.sha)
## *The meat of a snake; Clark prints "Snake meat: Elapbe dione (Pallas)" (as printed, the OCR of Elaphe dione), with the Tibetan example name kluhi.sha* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Snake meat is entered in the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, the chapter on the powers of medicines, in Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). It falls in the section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), whose thirteen headings are "horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). Under the heading "Meat" (line 1161), which opens with human flesh, the entry runs across a page break (the page number "114" sits in the middle of the list) and reads (lines 1165-1166): "Snake meat: Elapbe dione (Pallas): [has eleven names, for example kluhi.sha]. It removes growths, dispels urinary retention, is beneficial in eye disorders, [painful fevers in children, lung diseases, leprosy and infected wounds, and it heals bone warts]." The binomial is printed in the file as "Elapbe dione (Pallas)", an evident OCR error for the genus *Elaphe*; it is quoted as printed and not corrected. [USER: verify] The bracketed passages in Clark's chapter look like interpolations from the commentary that he describes in his introduction (line 80, "interpolated liberally from the Blue Aquamarine commentary"), but the lines read do not state the bracket convention outright, so the unbracketed indications (removes growths, dispels urinary retention, helps eye disorders) are the plainer reading of the Tantra and the bracketed ones are Clark's expansion. [USER: verify]

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3529) has the item in its own words: "Snake meat dissolves tumors, clears obstruction and is beneficial for eye problems." The two translations agree on the eye disorders; for "removes growths" Men-Tsee-Khang has "dissolves tumors", and for "dispels urinary retention" it has "clears obstruction", which is similar and possibly the same word translated more generally. The Tibetan wording is not in the files read, so no closer match can be claimed. The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary of animals gives "Drul" for the snake with a binomial that the OCR prints as "Zaocys dhumnades" (line 7138 and following); that is a different genus from Clark's, so the two translations do not identify the snake in the same way. [USER: verify]

The snake is also named in the Tantra's meat classification. Clark's section (h) of the nine, "Burrowing animals", lists "the marmot (Marmota bobak), porcupine, frog, snake, Tibetan badger, lizard (Eremias argus), gecko, and scorpion" (line 672), and Men-Tsee-Khang has the same group (line 2918). Clark then says that meats in general are "sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste" (line 673). Elsewhere in the same animal-origin chapter other parts of the snake are listed separately: "Snake fat: expels bullets, (shrapnel, etc.) [and cures anterior transposition of the calf muscle]" (line 1180) and "Snake skin: cures leucoderma, glang.shu (a skin disease) [and is beneficial against bhuta spirits]" (line 1184). Those are fat and skin and are not applied to the flesh.

### Active Components

Snake meat is muscle: mostly water and protein, with some fat and little carbohydrate. Depending on the species it may also carry parasites and, in venomous snakes, venom in the tissues around the glands. Nothing in the lines read names an active principle, and none specific to this entry is claimed.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is a short list of indications for the meat: removes growths (or tumours), helps urinary retention (or obstruction) and eye disorders, with Clark's bracketed extensions to children's fevers, lung disease, leprosy and infected wounds. The Codex gives no protocol. Tumours, urinary retention and eye disease are all conditions for clinical assessment, and eating wild snake meat carries real risks (see Contraindications). The Tantra gives no preparation, dose or route in these lines.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation, route or dose is given in the sources retrieved: the Clark and Men-Tsee-Khang lines state the indications only, and say nothing of whether the meat is eaten fresh, dried, cooked or in a compound. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Wild snake meat can carry parasites (including pentastomids and the larval stages of tapeworms and roundworms) and bacteria such as *Salmonella*, and it must be cooked thoroughly. Snake meat has no recognised effect on tumours, urinary retention or eye disease. Retention of urine and any growth that is new or changing need examination. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Clark prints for the meats of the animal groups, including the burrowing one, a sweet taste and a sweet post-digestive taste (line 673), and then gives qualities for the meat of dry-land, aquatic and amphibious animals and of birds of prey (cool, light and coarse; oily, heavy and warm; both; coarse, light and sharp). The lines read do not say which of those three habitat classes the snake falls into, and none is applied to it here. For the snake meat itself Clark prints an indication but no taste, potency or humoral action. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* Neither translation read gives snake meat any cultivation framing. It is an entry in a list of animal meats, with a handful of indications. The frame around it is the tradition's own: the project's cultivation spec records that the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) are derived from the three poisons and that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root. That point comes from the spec and not from any line about snake meat. Clark's bracket does tie snake *skin* (not the meat) to *bhuta* spirits (line 1184); no such link is made for the flesh in the lines read. No source read attaches snake meat to *bcud len* (essence extraction), and there is no formal Tibetan category of substances for subtle-body practice. Clark's remark (line 57) that "only very few animal products are used" nowadays is a note on usage, not on status.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a list entry for wild snake meat, with no preparation and no dose, and eating it carries parasite and bacterial risk with no supported benefit. The honest use of the entry is as a record of what the Tantra says.

### Contraindications

- **Parasites and infection:** wild snake meat can carry parasites and *Salmonella*; undercooked meat is a real risk, and the animal's origin is unknown.
- **Venomous snakes:** do not catch or handle wild snakes; bites can be fatal. Venom is also present in the bodies of venomous species.
- **Conditions named in the entry:** growths, urinary retention and eye disease need clinical assessment; do not rely on this line.
- **Legal and conservation status:** many snake species are protected and their capture and trade are controlled in many countries; the identification here rests on the translators' binomials and they disagree. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** killing a wild animal for a substance with no supported benefit is hard to justify, and Clark's omens section (line 431) values saving animals from slaughter; that passage is about merit and omens, not about medicine.
