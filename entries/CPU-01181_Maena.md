# Maena (small sea fish) — Mainis, "moena", as printed in the English edition of Dioscorides
## *A small sea fish "eaten by the poor"; the burnt head and the fermented sauce (garum) are the medicinal parts* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek Tradition (DMM).** The chapter is Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica*, Book 2, chapter 31, headed "2-31. MAINIS" with the editor's identification "SUGGESTED: Maena" and the description "small sea-fish, eaten by the poor" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 5062-5065; row in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 221). The chapter is a single sentence: "The burnt head of the moena (pounded into small pieces and sprinkled on) mends hard-skinned fissures in the perineum; and its garum [Roman sauce made of fermented fish] rolled up and down around the mouth lessens rotten ulcers in there." (line 5065).

The text therefore gives two preparations and two uses.

1. The burnt head, pounded into small pieces and sprinkled on, for "hard-skinned fissures in the perineum".
2. The *garum*, which the editor glosses as a "Roman sauce made of fermented fish", "rolled up and down around the mouth", for "rotten ulcers in there" (that is, in the mouth).

The spelling varies in the printed text: the heading has "MAINIS", the suggestion is "Maena" and the sentence has "moena". The species is not identified beyond "small sea-fish" and the editor's suggested name. [USER: verify]

The chapter before it, "2-30. SMARIS" (line 5059), has the same pattern: "The head of a salted smaris burnt stops abnormal growths of (protuberant) flesh, stops gangrenous ulceration; and removes corns and warty abnormal growths" (line 5061). The translator identifies *smaris* as "Osmerus eparlanus — Smelt" (line 5060). The point of citing it is only that burnt fish heads recur in this section of Dioscorides. The two chapters are separate and their uses are not merged here.

### Active Components

A small sea fish is mostly protein and water with fat, minerals (including calcium in the bones of the head) and, in fish, iodine and omega-3 fatty acids in varying amounts. Burning the head produces ash and charred matter; garum is a fermented, very salty fish product. These are general statements only, and the text names no active compound.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The Greek text records a burnt-head powder for perineal fissures and a fermented fish sauce used as a mouth-ulcer application. The Codex does not recommend either as a treatment, and it gives no protocol. Ash and a very salty fermented sauce on a fissure or an ulcerated mouth are as likely to irritate as to heal. Fish as food is ordinary.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives two preparations, with no quantities: "burnt ... pounded into small pieces and sprinkled on" (the head), and the *garum* "rolled up and down around the mouth" (line 5065). Neither has a dose, frequency or duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] General facts only: fish contains protein and fats; fermented fish sauce is high in salt; burnt animal matter is mainly ash and carbon.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured here. The chapter describes a poor man's fish and two external uses. The one remark that touches on social position, "eaten by the poor", is the editor's description of the fish and says nothing about cultivation. No cultivation status is attested.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. A cultivator has no use for this substance beyond ordinary fish as food, and the text does not claim more.

### Contraindications

Fish allergy is common and can be severe. Raw or poorly handled fish carries bacterial and parasite risks, and fermented fish products are high in sodium and, if badly made, in histamine. Do not apply ash or fish sauce to broken skin or to mouth ulcers; infection risk is high. Persistent perineal fissures or mouth ulcers need a clinician. This entry gives no medical advice.
