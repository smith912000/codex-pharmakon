# Marsilea quadrifolia — Sunishannaka (Sushruta Samhita, Bhishagratna's Appendix No. 3 and the chapters)

## *Marsilea quadrifolia, the Latin that Bhishagratna's Appendix No. 3 ("Names of Drugs, with Latin synonyms", Vol. III) prints (OCR "Marsiba  quadrifolia.", line 33056) against the Sanskrit name Sunishannaka (OCR "Sunnishannaka", line 33036). The pairing of the Sanskrit name with its Latin line is positional, proposed by an agent from two OCR columns and re-checked here by counting; it is not printed on one line. The chapters print the name Sunishannaka many times, as a pot-herb, with no Latin name* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Sushruta text (SS); this entry rests on one text, Bhishagratna's English translation of the Sushruta Samhita, and on a Latin name that comes from the translator's appendix and not from the chapters. The chapters carry real content on the Sanskrit name (a pot-herb in five chapters, with a stated taste and action); what they do not do is name the plant in Latin, so the Latin pairing is positional and agent-proposed and the botanical identity is open.** The chapters are in `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/` and the appendix is in `Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`. The row was added from the printed Latin (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP, confidence "medium") and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. **Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *What the chapters say.* All chapter files (`sutra`, `chikitsa`, `uttara`, `kalpa`, `nidana`, `sharira`) were searched for Sunishannaka and its OCR forms (Sunishannaka, Sunishanuaka, Siinishannaka, Sunishanna) and by fuzzy matching of every word. The name occurs in six chapters, always as a pot-herb or leaf.

*In the diet of an ulcer patient (Sutra ch.19).* `SS_Sutra_ch19.txt` lines 215 to 221, in the chapter on the nursing of a patient with an ulcer: "A diet consisting of boiled rice, the pot-herbs known as the Tanduliyakam, Jivanti, Sunishannaka, Vastuka, immature Mulaka, Vartaku, Patola and Karavella, fried with Saindhava (rock-salt) and clarified-butter, and seasoned with the expressed juice of Dadima and Amalakam, or of Mudga soup treated as above, should be prescribed for the patient." (spellings normalised from the OCR).

*Among wholesome foods (Sutra ch.20).* `SS_Sutra_ch20.txt` lines 64 to 72, in the chapter on the salutary and non-salutary effects of regimen: "the different species of pot-herbs, which may be safely used by a man to give a greater relish to his food, are named as the Chilli, the Vastuka, the Sunishannaka the Jivanti, the Tanduliyaka, and the Mandukaparni, etc. Clarified-butter, the salt known as the Saindhava, and the luscious juice of the pomegranate and the Amalakam, should be generally deemed the most wholesome articles of food." The passage is also quoted in the entry on Amaranthus spinosus (I_1389).

*In the astringent group (Sutra ch.42).* `SS_Sutra_ch42.txt` lines 350 to 361, in the chapter on the specific properties of flavours: the astringent group is made up of the members of the Nyagrodhadi, Amvashtadi, Priyangvadi and Rodhradi groups, Triphala, Shallaki, Jambu, Amra, Vakula and other named fruits and trees, "as well as Kuruvaka, Kovidaraka, Jivanti, Chilli, Palanka and Sunishannaka, etc. and grains and pulse of the Nevara and Mudga species, collectively form the astringent group."

*Its own properties (Sutra ch.46).* `SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` lines 1653 to 1658, in the chapter on food and drink: "The potherbs (leaves of edible plants) such as the Mandukaparni, Saptala, Sunishannaka, Suvarchala, Brahma-suvarchala, Pippali, Guduchi, Gojihva, Kakamachi, Prapunnada, Avalguja, Satina, Vrihati-phala, Kantakarika-phala, Patola, Vartaku, Karavellaka, Katukika, Kevuka, Uruvuka, Parpataka, Kiratatikta, Karkotaka, Arishta, Koshataki, Aetra, Karira, Atarushaka, Arkapushpi, etc., are light and palatable, and prove curative in haemoptysis, Kushtha, Meha, fever, dyspnoea, cough and create a relish for food." The metrical text on the same page, lines 1668 to 1673: "The Sunishannaka species is easily digested and is unattended with any acid reaction. It is astringent in its action and tends to subdue the three deranged bodily humours, and it also arrests the evacuations of the bowels." This is the one place where the text speaks of the herb singly.

*In the eye and the haemorrhage chapters (Uttara ch.17 and 45).* `SS_Uttara_ch17.txt` lines 440 to 444, in the chapter on diseases of the pupil and lens, after a barley meal against Timira: "The use of such edibles viz. Jivanti, Sunishannaka, Tanduliyaka, Vastuka, Chilli, Mulaka as well as meat of birds (such as Lava, etc.) and Jangala animals (e.g. deer, etc.) should be considered as invigorating to eye-sight." `SS_Uttara_ch45.txt` lines 109 to 131, in the chapter on haemorrhage (Rakta-pitta), under "Articles of Fare": the diet includes "leaves of Patola, Sehi, Sunishanna, Yuthika and Sindhuvara (Nirgundi)" cooked with clarified butter.

*Rasayana and vajikarana chapters.* A search of Chikitsa ch.26 (the vajikarana chapter) and ch.27 to 30 (the rasayana chapters) for Sunishannaka and its variants found nothing.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. As a general fact, *Marsilea* is a genus of water ferns (family Marsileaceae) whose leaflets, usually four, float or stand at the water's edge. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. In the Sushruta text the herb is food: a pot-herb in invalid diets (ulcer, haemorrhage), in the list of wholesome foods, and among the eyesight-strengthening edibles. The Codex does not recommend gathering a water plant on the strength of a translator's appendix pairing, and gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapters give these preparations: pot-herbs fried with rock-salt and clarified butter and seasoned with pomegranate or Amalaka juice, or Mudga soup, for an ulcer patient (Sutra 19); leaves cooked with clarified butter (Uttara 45). No quantity is given for Sunishannaka in any of them. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SS:** the herb is easily digested, "unattended with any acid reaction", astringent in its action, subduing "the three deranged bodily humours" and arresting the evacuation of the bowels (Sutra 46, lines 1668 to 1673), and it is counted in the astringent group (Sutra 42). The same chapter says of the potherb group it belongs to that they are "light and palatable" (Sutra 46, lines 1653 to 1658). The text prints no *virya* or *vipaka* for it singly.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Sushruta names Sunishannaka as a pot-herb in invalid and wholesome diets and says it is astringent; it is a food and a regimen item, not a drug assigned to *rasayana* or *vajikarana*. The vajikarana chapter (Chikitsa 26) and the rasayana chapters (Chikitsa 27 to 30) do not name it. The text says nothing of *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* for it. It does say the herb is among the edibles "invigorating to eye-sight" (Uttara 17), which is a claim about sight and is not read here as a vital-substrate claim. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator has one thing in this text: a pot-herb the tradition counts among wholesome foods and invalid diets, fried in ghee and rock-salt. That is a dietary note, not a practice, and the plant behind the name is a translator's pairing. Nothing here supports gathering a wild water plant.

### Contraindications

The Sushruta text retrieved prints no warning for the herb. The identity is open, so no plant should be gathered or eaten on the strength of this entry. A water plant growing in still or polluted water can carry bacteria and parasites; this is a general hygiene point and is not from the text. Haemorrhage and ulcer need medical care.
