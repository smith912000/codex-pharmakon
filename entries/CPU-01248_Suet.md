# Suet (goat, sheep, deer) — Dioscorides, STEAR TRAGEION, KAI PROBATEION, KAI ELAPHEION
## *The rendered kidney fat of goat, sheep and deer; the translator's gloss prints "Mutton Suet etc."* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Goat, sheep and deer suet is chapter 2-88 of the *De Materia Medica*, in Book Two, in the section "Fats", in the Osbaldeston and Wood English version (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 5306-5308; printed page 215). The chapter is headed "2-88. STEAR TRAGEION, KAI PROBATEION, KAI ELAPHEION" and glossed "SUGGESTED: Mutton Suet etc." The chapter itself is a recipe for preparing the fat and states no medicinal use. The uses are in chapter 2-94, "Medicinal uses of Fats" (line 5342), and are quoted below.

The preparation (line 5308) reads: "Goat, sheep and furthermore deer suet is prepared as follows. Take the fat of any of these (as described in the previous discussion) and having washed it (as explained in the preparation of fat of swine) remove the skin, put it into a mortar to temper it and beat it, pouring in a little water until nothing similar to blood comes from it, no fat swims on it, and it becomes clear. Then throw it into a ceramic jar and add to it enough water to cover it, put it over a gentle coal fire and stir it around. When it is all melted, pour it into water and cool it. Having washed the jar, melt the suet a second time and repeat the procedure. Then having melted it a third time without water, strain it out into a mortar moistened with water, and when it is cold bottle it for storage like fat of swine."

The chapter refers back to two earlier chapters. The "previous discussion" is chapter 2-87 ("Lard and Bear Grease", lines 5299-5303), which gives the washing of "the new thick fat that grows around the kidneys" in cold rain water, melting it in a jar over a gentle fire of coals, straining it into water, and repeating, and then storing it "in tightly corked ceramic jars" in "a very cold place". The following chapters give the same for ox and cow suet (2-89, boiled in sea water and with Tyrrhenian wax added, line 5311), and for bulls', panthers', lions' and other fats (2-90, line 5317), and then ways of perfuming fat (2-91, 2-92) and preserving it (2-93).

The uses are given for goat, sheep and deer fat in 2-94 (lines 5344-5347; printed pages 218-219). The general statement is "All fat is warming, softening and purifying; yet that of bulls, cows and calves is somewhat astringent." For deer: "Fat from elephants and deer (rubbed on) drives away snakes." For goat: "That from goats is more astringent, as a result it is given boiled with polenta, rhoe [4-64] and cheese for dysentery; and with barley water as a suppository or enema. The broth of these fats (sipped) is good for those with pulmonary consumption, and is effective given as an antidote to those who have taken a drink of parasitically infected water. Fat from goats has the most dissolving nature and helps the gouty, tempered with the berries of a goat [i.e. with goat's dung] and saffron and applied. Sheep fat is equivalent to this." Chapter 2-93 (line 5341) gives preservation of goose, poultry and calves' fat, with the remark "Fats also remain pure stored in honey"; it does not name goat, sheep or deer.

### Active Components

Suet is rendered ruminant fat: mostly triglycerides rich in saturated fatty acids (which is why it is solid at room temperature), with small amounts of cholesterol and fat-soluble material. Kidney fat is the hard fat around the kidney of the animal. Nothing in the chapters names an active principle.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. In the text, suet is a prepared fat used as a base and as a remedy: boiled with polenta and cheese or given with barley water for dysentery, sipped as broth in consumption, applied with goat's dung and saffron for gout, and rubbed on to drive away snakes. The Codex gives no protocol. Dysentery, consumption and gout need clinical assessment, and the dung-and-fat application and the snake-repelling claim are not supported. As food, rendered animal fat is a dietary choice, not a practice of this book.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparation is given in full in 2-88 (line 5308), as quoted above: wash and skin the fat, beat it in a mortar with a little water until it is clear, melt it in a jar of water over gentle coals, pour it into water to cool, repeat the melting twice, the last time without water, strain it into a mortar moistened with water, and bottle it when cold; the fat in the text is cleaned of blood by repeated washing. The uses in 2-94 are given as combinations without amounts (with polenta, rhoe and cheese; with barley water as suppository or enema; as broth; with goat's dung and saffron). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Animal fat is a concentrated energy source and is high in saturated fat. Melting and washing fat removes some blood and tissue, and clean rendering with straining improves how long the fat keeps, but fat can still go rancid and carry bacteria if poorly stored; the text's cold, covered storage matches that. Rendered fat has no recognised effect on dysentery, consumption or gout, and rectal use is not a treatment for them. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** One set of qualities is printed, for fats in general in 2-94 (line 5344): "All fat is warming, softening and purifying". Goat fat is said to be "more astringent" than cattle fat, and to have "the most dissolving nature"; sheep fat "is equivalent to this". For deer fat no quality is printed beyond the general one. No degree is given. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and the suet chapters attach none to goat, sheep or deer fat. What the chapters offer, as the discipline of attention that the Codex finds in Dioscorides, is a method: take the fat from around the kidneys, strip the skin, wash until nothing like blood comes from it and no fat floats, melt it three times, and keep it in a cold place in a jar. That is a procedure for cleanliness and keeping quality, and it is a real practice of care, but it is not a cultivation claim. The pneuma and the Galenic spirits are later and are not read in these chapters. Dioscorides records, for the lion's fat, that "they say it is an antidote to defend against those who intend treachery", at one remove ("they say"); that is not about suet.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a recipe for a medicinal fat, with a few external and internal uses and no dose. The one thing worth taking from it is the rule of preparation: clean, wash, repeat and store cold. The honest use of the entry is as a record of what Dioscorides says.

### Contraindications

- **Rancid or poorly stored fat:** spoiled fat is unpleasant and can carry bacteria; discard it.
- **Rectal use and internal use:** the text's suppository and enema uses are not endorsed here; dysentery, consumption and gout need clinical care.
- **Dietary:** animal fat is high in saturated fat; people with lipid disorders or heart disease should follow clinical advice.
- **Dung and snakes:** applying goat's dung, or relying on fat to repel snakes, has no supported basis and is unsafe.
- **Ethics:** suet is a by-product of animals killed for meat; the choice of animal food is the practitioner's own and is not a recommendation of this entry.
