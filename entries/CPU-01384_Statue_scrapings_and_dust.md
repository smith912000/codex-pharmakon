# Statue scrapings and dust — "Scrapings-from-a-Statue" / "Dust-of-a-Statue" (Bryan's English rendering of the Ebers Papyrus mineral list)
## *Material scraped or abraded from a statue; no Egyptian word, no stone and no identification is given in the sources retrieved* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian Tradition (EP).** The locus is Bryan's 1930 English *The Papyrus Ebers*, Chapter V "Mineral Remedies", in the alphabetical list of mineral drugs (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1204: "Scrapings-from-a-Statue Dust-of-a-Statue"; name-index rows `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` lines 33 and 34, class mineral, "Bryan's English identification"). The list is a head-word list of drugs the papyrus prescribes and gives no use, prescription number or identification. Bryan's opening of the chapter (lines 1133-1137) says that few mineral drugs "are prescribed in their crude or native state" and that for the rest "the source of origin of the drugs is strictly laid down"; a drug named by the object it comes from, such as a statue, is of that second sort. That is a remark about the form of the name, not about what the material was.

**Where the names recur in Bryan's running text.** The Bryan text on disk was searched for "statue". Three passages prescribe the two items of this entry:

- Line 1381, in Bryan's chapter on organic remedies, as one of his examples of odd compounds: "a mixture of Scrapings-of-a-Statue and Mint-of-the-Mountains, cooked in Oil and Wax, to be taken for four days to 'protect against everything.'"
- Line 2206, in the chapter whose running head is "Minor Medicine" (running heads at lines 2091-2184): after remedies for suppleness and for trembling of the limbs, "the Scribe rounds off the remedies with one by which the limbs 'could be protected against everything.' After mixing the Scrapings-from-a-Statue with Mint-of-the-Mountains and adding sasa-pieces, Oil, and Wax, one cooked the mixture, strained it (a not unnecessary precaution), and swallowed it for four days." Bryan's wording "a not unnecessary precaution" is his comment, not the papyrus's.
- Line 2391, in the "Minor Surgery" chapter (chapter heading at line 2209, running heads to line 2401): after a plaster of honey, cow's brain, mason's clay, linseed-water and date-juice "to drive away the wheals from blows", a second remedy reads "Dust-of-Alabaster Dust-of-a-Statue Granite Fresh Milk. Anoint therewith."

**Related items not used.** "Clay-from-a-Statue" is a separate name in Bryan's clay group (line 1152), used in a poultice for eyes with a flow of matter (lines 2779 and 2856). It is not the same listed item and is not described here. The statue is not identified in any passage read: Bryan does not say of what stone it was made, whether the material is a deliberate abrasion or weathered dust, or whether it was a particular kind of statue. The Ebers material is indication-organised, so the identity of any named substance is a modern hypothesis; for this one even a hypothesis is not offered in the text read. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None can be stated. The material is defined only by the object it comes from, and the stone is not named, so no composition can be given. If the statue was stone, the scrapings would have the composition of that stone; that is a general remark and not a reading of the papyrus.

---

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved show two internal uses taken for four days (mixed with mint, oil, wax and "sasa-pieces", said to protect the limbs "against everything") and one external anointing for wheals from blows. Scraping a statue is destruction of an object that may be an artefact of cultural or legal significance, and swallowing scrapings of unknown stone is hazardous. No modern protocol is supplied and none is implied.

---

### Preparation & Dosing

Only what Bryan reports: scrapings mixed with Mint-of-the-Mountains, "sasa-pieces", oil and wax, cooked, strained and swallowed "for four days" (lines 1381 and 2206); dust mixed with alabaster dust, granite and fresh milk and "anointed" (line 2391). No quantity is given in Bryan's summary for any of these. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] With the stone unidentified, no scientific statement about the material can be made. ### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation status is attested. The Ebers material records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's vital constitution. The one passage that comes closest, the compound "to protect against everything", is a protective remedy for the limbs in the minor-medicine chapter, and Bryan's phrase is a quotation of the papyrus's purpose, not a statement about the *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); Bryan prints no recitation with any of the three statue passages above, and since he summarises, that is no proof the papyrus gives none. No deity is attributed.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. The record offers a name, a protective purpose, a four-day course and a short list of vehicles, with no quantity and no identification of the stone. A cultivator has nothing to do with it, and the one practical lesson is negative: do not damage or remove material from monuments or statues. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Contraindications

- **Unidentified material:** the stone is unknown; unidentified stone dust should not be swallowed or applied to broken skin.
- **Inhalation:** fine stone dust is a respiratory hazard.
- **Heritage and law:** scraping or removing material from statues or monuments may be an offence and damages irreplaceable objects; the legal position in any given country was not retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Wax and oil compounds taken by mouth:** no safety information is given in the text.
