# I-587 | Licorice / Liquorice

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**

**Botanical name:** *Glycyrrhiza glabra* L. (European/Spanish licorice); *G. uralensis* Fisch. (Chinese licorice); *G. inflata* Batalin (Xinjiang licorice)

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary names:**
- English: Licorice, Liquorice; from Medieval Latin *liquiritia* → Old French *licoresse* → English; corruption of Latin *Glycyrrhiza*
- Latin/Greek: *Glycyrrhiza* — from Greek *glukús* (γλυκύς = sweet) + *rhíza* (ῥίζα = root): "sweet root" — the most accurate description of the flavor
- Chinese: *Gān Cǎo* (甘草) — *gān* = sweet; *cǎo* = herb/grass: "sweet herb" — exact parallel to the Greek name
- Japanese: *Kanzō* (甘草) — same characters as Chinese
- Sanskrit: *Yaṣṭimadhu* (यष्टिमधु) — *yaṣṭi* = stick; *madhu* = honey: "honey stick" — referring to sweet root used as chewing stick
- Hindi: *Mulethi*, *Jethi madh*, *Jethimad*
- Arabic: *ʿIrq al-sūs* (عِرق السوس) — "root of the *sūs*"; also *Sūs*, *Assal al-khashab* (wood honey)
- Persian: *Shirin bayān* (شیرین بیان) — "sweet expression/speech"; also *ʿArq al-sūs*
- German: *Süßholz* (sweet wood), *Lakritze*
- French: *Réglisse*, *Bois doux* (sweet wood)
- Turkish: *Meyan kökü*, *Meyan*
- Hebrew: *Shush* (שוש) — source of Arabic *sūs*; one of the oldest recorded plant names
- Tibetan: *Shing-mngar* (sweet wood — direct parallel to Chinese/Greek/Sanskrit)

**The universal "sweet root" convergence:** That Greek, Latin, Chinese/Japanese, Arabic/Persian, Sanskrit, German/French, and Tibetan all independently named this plant "sweet root" or "sweet wood" confirms the transcultural recognizability of the root's flavor. Glycyrrhizin (the sweet compound) is 50–150 times sweeter than sucrose — unmistakeable at first contact.

**Botanical identity:**
Herbaceous perennial, 1–1.5 m; from deep taproot (1–2 m depth) and extensive horizontal stolons. Leaves: pinnate, 9–17 leaflets; alternate; sticky glandular hairs in some species. Flowers: blue-violet to pale purple; butterfly-shaped (papilionaceous — Fabaceae characteristic); June–July. Pods: linear legume, 2–4 cm, hairy (*G. glabra*) or spiny (*G. uralensis*). Distribution: *G. glabra* — Mediterranean, Central Asia, Turkey; *G. uralensis* — China, Mongolia, Siberia; both widely cultivated commercially.

**Commercial production:**
Primary exporting countries: China (largest; *G. uralensis* and *G. glabra*), Uzbekistan, Turkey, Iran, Spain. China produces approximately 80% of world supply.

**Key species comparison:**

| Species | Distribution | Glycyrrhizin % | Primary market |
|---------|-------------|----------------|----------------|
| *G. glabra* | Mediterranean, Turkey | 6–14% | European medicine, candy |
| *G. uralensis* | China, Mongolia | 2–12% | Chinese medicine (primary) |
| *G. inflata* | Xinjiang, China | 1–9% | Chinese medicine |

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Universal functions across traditions:**
The same three applications appear in every tradition that independently encountered licorice:
1. **Respiratory/throat**: cough, sore throat, voice restoration, lung conditions
2. **Digestive**: gastric conditions, harmonizing digestion
3. **Harmonizing other medicines**: the most unique function — licorice used as an adjuvant that reduces harshness of other drugs, prolongs their activity, and "balances" formulas

The harmonizing function is particularly remarkable because it appears in Chinese (*Gān Cǎo* as "pharmacist's herb" or "the harmony drug"), Greek (Dioscorides notes it moderates other medicines), and Ayurvedic (Yashtimadhu as anupana/vehicle) traditions independently. Modern pharmacology explains this: glycyrrhizin inhibits CYP450 enzymes (particularly CYP3A4) → slows metabolism of co-administered drugs → prolongs and potentially enhances their effect. The traditional "harmonizing" observation was pharmacokinetically accurate.

**The most formulaic herb in the world:**
*Gān Cǎo* appears in more Chinese classical formula combinations than any other single herb. Analysis of the classical 113 formulas of Zhang Zhongjing's *Shang Han Lun* (Treatise on Cold Damage, ~220 CE) shows *Gān Cǎo* appearing in approximately 70 of them. Similarly, *Yaṣṭimadhu* appears in hundreds of Ayurvedic compound formulations. This "indispensable adjuvant" status reflects both its harmonizing pharmacokinetic function and its pleasant taste that makes bitter formulas palatable.

**The Mineralocorticoid Discovery:**
The most pharmacologically significant discovery related to licorice was made in 1946 by Dutch physician Revers: eating large amounts of licorice candy caused sodium retention, potassium loss, hypertension, and edema — mimicking hyperaldosteronism. This led to identification of glycyrrhizinic acid's mechanism (11β-HSD2 inhibition → cortisol acts as mineralocorticoid). This discovery subsequently led to pharmaceutical development of carbenoxolone (glycyrrhetinic acid derivative; anti-ulcer drug) in the 1960s.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Primary bioactive compounds:**

**Glycyrrhizin (glycyrrhizinic acid):**
- Triterpenoid saponin; the primary sweet compound
- Structure: 2 molecules glucuronic acid + glycyrrhetic acid (18β-glycyrrhetinic acid aglycon)
- Sweetness: 50–150 times sweeter than sucrose (concentration-dependent; at low concentration sweeter; diminishes at high concentration)
- Content: 6–14% in *G. glabra* root; 2–12% in *G. uralensis*
- Metabolic fate: hydrolyzed by intestinal bacteria → **18β-glycyrrhetinic acid** (the pharmacologically active aglycon)
- The glycyrrhizinic acid itself is poorly absorbed; glycyrrhetinic acid (after hydrolysis) is well absorbed

**18β-Glycyrrhetinic acid (active metabolite):**
- Pentacyclic triterpenoid; the primary pharmacological active form
- Key pharmacological targets (see Section 5)

**Glycyrrhizic acid isomers:** *G. uralensis* contains predominantly 18β form; small amounts of 18α form (different configuration; less sweet; different biological activity).

**Flavonoids (the anti-inflammatory fraction):**
- **Liquiritin**: flavanone glucoside; anti-inflammatory; antidepressant in animal models
- **Liquiritigenin**: aglycone of liquiritin; phytoestrogen (weak ER agonist); neuroprotective
- **Isoliquiritin**: chalcone; anti-inflammatory; antioxidant
- **Glabridin** (*G. glabra* specific): isoflavone; potent antioxidant; anti-melanogenic (tyrosinase inhibition); estrogen-modulating; 0.1–0.3% in *G. glabra* root
- **Licochalcone A** (*G. inflata* specific): unique chalcone; antimalarial activity; anti-inflammatory

**Coumarins:**
- Glycycoumarin, glabrocoumarins: anti-inflammatory; anticoagulant at high concentrations

**Polysaccharides:**
- Glycyrrhiza polysaccharides (GPS-1, GPS-2, GPS-3): immunomodulatory; antiviral

**Glycyrrhizin derivatives:**
- **Diammonium glycyrrhizinate**: injectable form for liver protection; Chinese pharmaceutical
- **Magnesium glycyrrhizinate**: oral supplement form with reduced mineralocorticoid effect

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Primary mechanism: 11β-Hydroxysteroid Dehydrogenase Type 2 (11β-HSD2) inhibition**

**Normal cortisol metabolism:**
Cortisol and aldosterone both activate the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR). In normal physiology, mineralocorticoid target tissues (kidney collecting duct, colon) express 11β-HSD2, which converts cortisol (a glucocorticoid that ALSO binds MR) → cortisone (inactive at MR). This protects MR from cortisol stimulation, allowing aldosterone alone to regulate sodium/potassium balance.

**Glycyrrhetinic acid mechanism:**
18β-Glycyrrhetinic acid inhibits 11β-HSD2 → cortisol no longer inactivated in MR-target tissues → cortisol acts as mineralocorticoid → excessive MR activation → sodium retention, potassium wasting, hypertension, edema.

This is the **apparent mineralocorticoid excess (AME) syndrome** — identical to the rare genetic disorder where 11β-HSD2 is absent, producing severe hypertension and hypokalemia from infancy.

Licorice dose required for significant 11β-HSD2 inhibition:
- Glycyrrhizin 100+ mg/day (approximately 50g black licorice candy daily; or therapeutic doses of extract)
- Doses in most commercial products and herbal teas: usually below significant threshold
- Therapeutic preparations may exceed threshold; patient risk factors amplify effect

**Anti-inflammatory mechanism:**
- Glycyrrhetinic acid → inhibition of phospholipase A₂ (PLA₂) → reduced arachidonic acid release → reduced prostaglandin and leukotriene synthesis
- NF-κB inhibition
- Reduction of reactive oxygen species
- Complement pathway modulation

**Antiviral mechanism:**
- Glycyrrhizin: inhibits viral replication (directly interrupts envelope-dependent penetration into cells)
- Active against: HSV-1/2, HIV (in vitro), SARS-CoV-1 (IC₅₀ ~0.5 mM in Vero cells), SARS-CoV-2 (some evidence)
- Mechanism: glycyrrhizin's amphipathic structure → inserts into viral lipid envelope → prevents fusion with host cell membrane
- IV glycyrrhizin (*Stronger Neo-Minophagen C*; Japanese pharmaceutical): hepatitis B/C clinical use

**Gastroprotective mechanism:**
- Enhances mucus production (via PGE₂ stabilization)
- Promotes bicarbonate secretion (gastric alkalinization)
- Inhibits 15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase → prolongs PGE₂ half-life → mucosal protection
- **Deglycyrrhizinated licorice (DGL)**: glycyrrhizin removed; retains flavonoid/saponin gastroprotective fraction; eliminates mineralocorticoid side effect → standard preparation for GI use

**Carbenoxolone (synthetic glycyrrhetinic acid derivative):**
- Developed 1960s (UK); anti-ulcer drug; strongest evidence pre-PPI era
- Mechanism: identical to glycyrrhetinic acid but optimized absorption
- Largely superseded by proton pump inhibitors (omeprazole 1988)
- Still studied as topical formulation for other conditions

**CYP inhibition (harmonizing pharmacokinetics):**
- Glycyrrhizin → CYP3A4 inhibition (moderate; Ki ~100 μM)
- Net: slows metabolism of co-administered CYP3A4 substrates → prolongs half-life → enhanced/prolonged effect
- Clinical example: licorice in multi-herb formulas prolongs the effect of active components
- Drug interaction risk: with narrowtherapeutic index CYP3A4 substrates (cyclosporine, tacrolimus, certain antivirals)

**Antidepressant/anxiolytic mechanism (liquiritin/liquiritigenin):**
- Monoamine oxidase inhibition (mild)
- GABA-A modulation (isoliquiritigenin)
- BDNF expression increase
- Animal models: consistent antidepressant activity; limited human data

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Level A — Established evidence:**

**Hepatitis B/C (IV glycyrrhizin):**
- **Stronger Neo-Minophagen C** (SNMC; Japan): glycyrrhizin 0.2% + cysteine + glycine IV; approved Japan for hepatitis B/C
- **Crance 2003** (*Antiviral Research*): SNMC reduces serum ALT, improves liver histology in chronic hepatitis C; multiple Japanese RCTs
- **Meta-analysis Soo 2011**: IV glycyrrhizin for chronic hepatitis C → significant ALT reduction; no cure (doesn't eliminate HCV) but reduces liver damage
- Suppression of HCC development in long-term HCV patients on IV glycyrrhizin (Level B cohort data; Arase 1997, n=84; HCC development 7% vs. 34% in historical controls at 15 years)

**Peptic ulcer (carbenoxolone — pre-PPI era; Level A historical):**
- Multiple 1960s–1970s RCTs showing carbenoxolone superior to placebo for gastric ulcer healing; comparable to bismuth preparations
- DGL (deglycyrrhizinated licorice): Kassir 1985 (*Irish Medical Journal*): DGL vs. cimetidine for duodenal ulcer; 12-week ulcer healing equivalent; no mineralocorticoid effects with DGL

**PCOS (Peony-Licorice; see I-584):**
- Shao Yao Gan Cao Tang: Level A RCT evidence; paeoniflorin + glycyrrhizin synergy; LH/androgen reduction

**Adrenal insufficiency (pharmacological mineralocorticoid):**
- Case reports of licorice used intentionally to supplement mineralocorticoid activity in partial adrenal insufficiency; not standard treatment; fludrocortisone preferred

**Level B — Clinical studies:**

**Cough and sore throat:**
- Multiple small RCTs; DGL lozenges and licorice tea; significant improvement in throat pain and cough frequency vs. placebo; mechanism: demulcent + anti-inflammatory
- Postoperative sore throat: gargling licorice solution 15 min before intubation reduces incidence of post-extubation sore throat (Ruetzler 2013, *Anesthesiology*, n=236; significant)

**COVID-19:**
- Multiple in vitro studies showing glycyrrhizin inhibits SARS-CoV-2 replication; limited clinical trials
- Chinese herbal formulas containing *Gān Cǎo* used widely in COVID-19 management in China; glycyrrhizin contribution not isolatable from formula context

**Functional dyspepsia:**
- Licorice root extract (STW5 = Iberogast contains licorice as component): Level A RCT evidence for functional dyspepsia; licorice contribution not separable

**Level C — Traditional evidence:**
- 9/9 traditions; thousands of years of consistent respiratory/digestive/harmonizing use

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**King Tut's Licorice:**
*Glycyrrhiza* root was found among the grave goods of Tutankhamun (c. 1323 BCE) — over 3,300 years old — confirming both its Egyptian medicinal use and its status as a valuable enough commodity to accompany a pharaoh into the afterlife. The same dried root compounds found in the tomb are structurally identical to the commercially sold licorice extract today.

**The Roman Legionnaire's Ration:**
Roman military rations included licorice root, recorded in multiple supply manifests. The combination of functions — thirst quenching (the sweet flavor reduces perceived thirst; mechanism: salivation stimulation), endurance support (adrenal effects provide mild stimulation), anti-diarrheal (astringent saponins), and respiratory protection (useful in dusty campaigns) — made it an ideal military supplement. Julius Caesar's *De Bello Gallico* does not mention it specifically, but Roman military pharmacological records include it.

**The Carbenoxolone Paradigm:**
Carbenoxolone's development in the 1960s from glycyrrhetinic acid represents one of the first systematic natural product → pharmaceutical derivations using 20th-century chemistry. British pharmacologist Frank Hollander synthesized carbenoxolone specifically to retain the gastroprotective effects of glycyrrhetinic acid while eliminating (partially) the mineralocorticoid effect. The resulting drug was more effective than any available ulcer treatment of its era, dominated the market until H₂-receptor blockers (cimetidine, 1976) and then PPIs (omeprazole, 1988) supplanted it. The carbenoxolone story established the principle that natural product chemistry could be optimized for pharmaceutical use — a principle now foundational to drug discovery.

**The Chinese Formula King:**
*Gān Cǎo*'s position in Chinese medicine is without parallel — described as "the king of one hundred herbs" (*bǎi cǎo zhī wáng*) and "the harmonizer of all drugs." The Tang dynasty pharmacopeia *Xinxiu Bencao* (659 CE; first government-issued pharmacopeia in world history) lists *Gān Cǎo* in the premier position. Chinese medical students learn to add *Gān Cǎo* to almost any formula — its presence is so standard that its absence requires explicit justification.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Standardized preparations:**

**DGL (Deglycyrrhizinated Licorice) — GI applications:**
- Standard preparation for long-term GI use; removes 97% of glycyrrhizin
- Dose: 380–760 mg (chewed; tablets) 20 minutes before meals; up to 3x daily
- Form: chewable tablets (DGL must contact GI mucosa; capsule form less effective for GI)
- Indications: GERD, gastric/duodenal ulcer, functional dyspepsia, H. pylori adjunct
- Duration: 8–16 weeks for ulcer healing; can be continued long-term for GERD
- Safety: mineralocorticoid effect eliminated with proper DGL preparation; check label for glycyrrhizin content

**Whole licorice root / standard extract (short-term):**
- Dose: 1–5g dried root/day in decoction (300–750 mg standardized extract)
- Short-term only: maximum 4–6 weeks continuous use for most adults
- Indication: cough, sore throat, adrenal support, respiratory tract infections
- Monitor: blood pressure, serum potassium (especially patients on diuretics, cardiac drugs)

**Chinese Medicine *Gān Cǎo*:**
- Dose: 3–12g in decoction (up to 30g in special circumstances for toxin-clearing)
- *Zhì Gān Cǎo* (炙甘草 = honey-processed licorice root): sweeter; more tonifying; formula *Zhì Gān Cǎo Tāng* for cardiac palpitations/arrhythmia
- *Shēng Gān Cǎo* (生甘草 = raw licorice root): less sweet; more cooling/detoxifying function

**IV Glycyrrhizin (Stronger Neo-Minophagen C):**
- Hospital setting; chronic hepatitis B/C
- 40–100 mL IV infusion (0.2% glycyrrhizin); requires blood pressure and potassium monitoring
- Not available outside Japan as approved pharmaceutical (available as supplement/investigational elsewhere)

**Mineralocorticoid effects — safety parameters:**
- Significant effects at glycyrrhizin >100 mg/day sustained (typically >50g licorice candy/day; or >1.5g whole extract equivalent/day)
- Symptoms: headache, hypertension, edema, muscle weakness (hypokalemia)
- Laboratory: hypokalemia (<3.5 mEq/L), metabolic alkalosis, elevated cortisol, suppressed aldosterone
- Treatment: discontinue licorice; potassium repletion; if severe: spironolactone (MR antagonist) or fludrocortisone reversal
- Risk groups requiring lower threshold: hypertension, heart failure, renal impairment, elderly, those on diuretics or digoxin

**Critical drug interactions:**
| Drug | Interaction | Mechanism |
|------|-------------|-----------|
| Thiazide/loop diuretics | Additive hypokalemia → cardiac risk | Both lower potassium |
| Digoxin | Hypokalemia potentiates digoxin toxicity → arrhythmia | Same as above |
| Antihypertensives | Reduced efficacy | Sodium retention increases BP |
| Corticosteroids | Additive mineralocorticoid | Shared 11β-HSD2 pathway |
| Warfarin | Reduced anticoagulation (CYP3A4 induction by some *G. uralensis* components) | Variable effect |
| Cyclosporine/tacrolimus | Possible increased levels | CYP3A4 inhibition |

**Contraindications:**
- Hypertension (use DGL form only)
- Heart failure, edema
- Renal insufficiency
- Hypokalemia
- Liver cirrhosis (mineralocorticoid status already abnormal)
- Pregnancy: potential oxytocic activity at high doses; avoid whole extract; DGL limited evidence but probably safer
