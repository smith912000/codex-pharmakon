# Common Knotweed / Bian Xu (萹蓄) / Knotgrass / Bird's Knotgrass / Renouée des oiseaux / Vogelknöterich / Centinodia

## *Polygonum aviculare* L. | Family: Polygonaceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM, SBJ

**Energetic Classification:** Bitter, slightly cold (SBJ/TCM); enters Bladder. Astringent, cool and drying in the Greco-Arabic reading. Draining, binding, downward-directing to the water passages.

**Training Phase:** Intermediate — Urinary & Astringent Practice

---

### ⚠️ IDENTIFICATION FIRST — THREE POLYGONACEAE, THREE DIFFERENT DRUGS

The Polygonaceae in this codex are a nomenclatural minefield, and the taxonomy has been repeatedly reshuffled (much of what was *Polygonum* is now *Persicaria*, *Bistorta*, *Fallopia* or *Reynoutria*). Three are already filed and are not this plant:

| | **THIS ENTRY** | Bistort | Water Pepper |
|---|---|---|---|
| Binomial | *Polygonum aviculare* | *Bistorta officinalis* (*Polygonum bistorta*) | *Persicaria hydropiper* (*Polygonum hydropiper*) |
| Habit | Prostrate, wiry, mat-forming; **swollen jointed nodes with silvery ochreae**; tiny axillary flowers | Erect; dense pink flower spike; **twisted (S-shaped) rhizome** | Erect; slender drooping spike |
| Drug part | Whole aerial herb | **Rhizome** | Aerial herb |
| Taste | Bitter, mild | Strongly astringent | **Acridly, burningly pungent** — the diagnostic |
| Core action | **Urinary — promote urination, clear damp-heat** | Astringent — dysentery, haemorrhage | Pungent/acrid — bleeding, dysmenorrhoea; **rheological, not astringent** |
| TCM name | 萹蓄 Bian Xu | 拳参 Quan Shen | 水蓼 Shui Liao |

**The discriminator that never fails:** chew a leaf. **Water pepper burns** — instantly, fiercely, on the tongue tip. Knotweed does not. If it burns, you have the wrong plant.

The second field check is the **node**. *P. aviculare* is built of swollen, papery-sheathed joints strung along a wiry prostrate stem, like a knotted cord thrown on the ground. That structure names it in every language on the entry title — and, as we will see, it also generated a doctrine that turns out to be largely decorative.

### Origin & Tradition

**Chinese Medicine / Shennong Bencao Jing lineage (SBJ) — 萹蓄 (biǎn xù)**

*Bian Xu* is one of the standing urinary herbs of the Chinese materia medica, present in the *Shennong Bencao Jing* and continuous in use to the present pharmacopoeia. The name is descriptive-obscure — **萹** relating to the plant's flattened, low habit and **蓄** carrying senses of storing/accumulating — and unlike Gou Teng (I_760) or Feverfew (I_759) it makes no therapeutic assertion. The doctrine lives entirely in the functions.

Classical functions, stated in the tradition's own terms:

- **利尿通淋 (lì niào tōng lín)** — *promote urination and free strangury*. **淋 (lín)** is the tradition's category for **painful, difficult, dribbling urination** — and it is a genuinely good clinical category, subdivided into 热淋 (heat lín — burning, urgent, scanty: essentially cystitis/UTI), 石淋 (stone lín — **urinary calculi**), 血淋 (blood lín — haematuria), 膏淋 (chylous), 气淋 (qi lín). *Bian Xu* is indicated across the set, most centrally in **热淋** and **石淋**.
- **清热利湿 (qīng rè lì shī)** — *clear heat and drain damp*. The damp-heat construct: a syndrome of heaviness, turbidity, burning and obstruction located in the lower burner.
- **杀虫止痒 (shā chóng zhǐ yǎng)** — *kill parasites and stop itching*. Secondary; used as a wash for eczema, tinea, vaginal itch, and internally for intestinal worms.

Its canonical pairing is in **八正散 (Bā Zhèng Sǎn, "Eight Corrections Powder")**, the tradition's flagship formula for acute damp-heat strangury, where *Bian Xu* sits alongside *Qu Mai* (*Dianthus superbus*), *Che Qian Zi* (*Plantago* seed), *Hua Shi* (talc), *Mu Tong*, *Zhi Zi*, *Da Huang* and *Gan Cao*. **The formula's target, in plain terms, is acute lower urinary tract infection with stone.** The tradition assembled a polyherbal for exactly that syndrome and has used it for two millennia.

**De Materia Medica (DMM) — πολύγονον / *polygonon*, "many-knees"**

Dioscorides' *polygonon* is the source of the modern genus name, and the Greek is doing something specific: **πολύ** (many) + **γόνυ** (knee/joint) — *the many-kneed plant*. The name is pure morphology, like Gou Teng's, and like Gou Teng the therapy attaches elsewhere.

The Greek indications are **astringent-haemostatic**: *polygonon* juice or decoction is given for **fluxes** — dysentery, diarrhoea, excessive menstrual flow, spitting of blood, discharges of all kinds — and applied to wounds and inflammations. It is a **binding** drug. Dioscorides also records a **diuretic** action, which is the point where the Greek and Chinese readings touch, and touch only partially: the Greek tradition foregrounds the binding and mentions the urine; the Chinese tradition foregrounds the urine and barely uses the binding.

The Latin vernacular preserved the morphology — ***centinodia***, hundred-knots — and the European folk use ran with the astringent reading: knotgrass for bleeding, for the bloody flux, for wound washes, and (in the herbalists' least defensible extension) for **restraining growth**.

**Canon of Medicine (CM) — *baṭbāṭ* / *ʿasā al-rāʿī* [USER: verify]**

The Arabic reception, working the Greek material through the four-degree system, places knotgrass as **cold and dry** — the classic astringent signature — with indications for fluxes, haemorrhage, hot swellings, and for **heat and burning of the urine**. The degree assignment is low: this is a mild drug, unlike the hellebores and poppies of the adjacent entries, and the tradition treats it accordingly, as an everyday herb rather than a restricted one. **[USER: verify the Arabic name and degree against the Gruner translation before this tag is treated as precisely attested.]**

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

Three doctrines here. **Two map onto real chemistry. One is a signature that should be deflated and will be.**

**Doctrine 1: the binding.** The Greco-Arabic reading is that knotgrass **binds** — it stops what is flowing out. Dysentery, haemorrhage, discharge. The tradition's evidence is direct and sensory: the herb tastes astringent, it puckers the mouth, and the mouth-pucker is taken as a legible demonstration of what the drug will do to the gut and the vessel. **This is a signature that is not a signature — it is a bioassay.** The tradition was directly perceiving **tannin–protein precipitation** on its own oral mucosa and correctly extrapolating the same reaction to other mucosal surfaces. Polyphenols cross-link salivary proline-rich proteins; that is the pucker. The same polyphenols cross-link gut mucosal proteins; that is the anti-diarrhoeal effect. **The Greeks were running a real assay with their tongue and reading it correctly.** This is one of the cleanest doctrine-to-chemistry mappings in the volume, and it deserves saying plainly: astringency is not a metaphor. It is a chemical reaction with a name.

**Doctrine 2: the draining.** The Chinese reading — 利尿通淋, free the water passages — maps onto a composite. Part of it is the **flavonoid glycoside load**: avicularin, quercitrin, isoquercitrin, myricitrin, juglanin, rutin, hyperoside. Quercetin-type flavonoids are a well-worn pharmacological source of mild diuresis and of the anti-inflammatory action that would matter far more in an inflamed bladder. Part of it is **silicic acid**, which *Polygonum aviculare* accumulates conspicuously (it is one of the classic silica-bearing herbs alongside horsetail) and which the European tradition explicitly exploited. Part of it is simply **water**: any herb taken as a litre of hot decoction three times daily will increase urine output, and the tradition had no way to separate the vehicle from the drug.

**Here the doctrine and the chemistry part company in an interesting way.** The tradition's damp-heat construct predicts a drug that *drains fluid and cools inflammation simultaneously*. The chemistry delivers **the cooling far more convincingly than the draining** — the anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial evidence is real (below), and the diuretic evidence is, as it turns out, **absent**. The doctrine is half-vindicated and half-unexamined, and the half that carries the name (利尿 — *promote urination*) is the unexamined half. That is worth sitting with: **the tradition's headline claim for this herb is the one with no data behind it.**

**Doctrine 3: the knot — DEFLATE.** The plant is a cord of knots. Doctrine of signatures duly proposed that it **binds** and **restrains** — and the Elizabethan herbalists took this to a memorable and entirely false conclusion, that knotgrass taken by a child would **stunt its growth**, a belief solid enough that Shakespeare could use "hindering knot-grass" as an insult in *A Midsummer Night's Dream* and expect the audience to follow.

**This is wrong, and it is wrong in a specific and instructive way.** Note that the knot-signature and the tannin-bioassay **make the same prediction** — *this plant binds* — from completely different evidential bases. One is a chemical reaction on the tongue. The other is a visual pun. The tradition could not tell them apart, and so the correct astringent doctrine and the absurd growth-stunting doctrine travelled together for centuries under a single justification, each lending the other credibility. **A doctrine that gets the right answer for the wrong reason will happily generate wrong answers with the same confidence.** There is no growth-inhibitory chemistry here. If anything the modern data runs the other way — the flavonoid juglanin and the compound (−)-loliolide from this plant *inhibit* cellular senescence in human fibroblasts (Yang et al. 2014, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11418-014-0817-0), PMID 24519811; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12272-014-0435-0), PMID 24993871), and *P. aviculare* extract *accelerates* keratinocyte migration and wound re-epithelialisation via Wnt/β-catenin activation (Seo et al. 2016, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.5593), PMID 26929003). **The knot plant promotes growth.** The signature is not merely unsupported; the available chemistry points in the opposite direction.

### Active Components

**Flavonol glycosides — the characteristic and best-evidenced fraction.**
- **Avicularin** (quercetin-3-O-α-L-arabinofuranoside) — named for this plant.
- **Quercitrin** (quercetin-3-O-rhamnoside), **isoquercitrin**, **myricitrin** — all four confirmed together by LC/MS in *P. aviculare* extract (Park et al. 2018, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2018.03.042), PMID 29655685).
- **Juglanin** (kaempferol-3-O-α-L-arabinofuranoside) — *P. aviculare* is one of its two commercial sources (Rutkowska et al. 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms251910323), PMID 39408653).
- **Rutin**, **hyperoside**, **flavonol-3-O-(2″-galloyl)-glycosides**, flavonol aglycones — the galloylated glycosides identified by Park et al. (2019, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-54546-8), PMID 31792306).

**Phenolic acids** — **chlorogenic acid** and **caffeic acid**, both confirmed present and both shown to be the active principles behind an unexpected activity (He et al. 2025, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pestbp.2025.106655), PMID 41162045); **gallic acid**.

**Tannins** — hydrolysable and condensed. **The astringent principle, and the doctrinal load-bearer.** Quantitation in this species is not well served by the retrieved literature; total phenolics have been measured at **677.4 ± 62.7 µg/g** with flavonoids at **112.7 ± 13 µg/g** in an ethanolic extract (Hsu 2006, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4067/s0716-97602006000200010), PMID 16874403).

**Silicic acid / silica** — accumulated; the basis of the European "mineral" reading. **Not quantified in any source retrieved here — [USER: verify].**

**Other** — **(−)-loliolide** (PMID 24993871); **oxalates** (family-typical for Polygonaceae; see Contraindications).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**A working herb — the codex's least dramatic entry this batch, and the only one with a use.**

**Primary: damp-heat urinary practice.** *Bian Xu* is deployed where the tradition deploys it — acute burning, urgent, scanty, dribbling urination; adjunctively in stone. **It is a formula herb, not a solo agent.** Its two-millennium track record is inside Ba Zheng San, and the honest reading of that is that the tradition never asked it to work alone. Kenetica follows the tradition here rather than the isolation-chemistry temptation.

**Secondary: astringent practice.** The Greco-Arabic reading — loose stool, mild flux, mucosal laxity. Well-grounded mechanistically (tannins), unglamorous, low-risk.

**Tertiary: topical.** Wound wash, itch wash. The Wnt/β-catenin re-epithelialisation finding (PMID 26929003) is the most mechanistically interesting result attached to this plant and it corroborates the *external* folk use rather than the internal one — which is the inverse of the usual pattern and worth noting.

**Not recommended for:** obesity (the pancreatic lipase finding is in vitro), anti-ageing (fibroblast senescence assays are not a clinical claim), fatigue (rodent model), or cancer (the juglanin literature is not about this plant as a drug). **Every one of those is a real published finding and none of them is a use.**

### Scientific Correlation

**Headline grade: MODERATE for anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial; NULL for the tradition's headline claim.**

**1. The diuretic doctrine — NULL. This is the entry's most important finding.**
利尿 — *promote urination* — is the first two characters of this herb's classical function statement, its reason for existing in the materia medica, and the whole basis of its placement in Ba Zheng San. **This literature search retrieved zero studies of *Polygonum aviculare* diuretic activity.** Not weak studies. **None.** Searches for diuretic activity, urolithiasis, cystitis, and bladder effects in this species returned empty. **Grade: NULL — no evidence retrieved, in either direction.**

The intellectually honest reading is not "the tradition is wrong" — it is **"nobody has checked."** *P. aviculare* is a globally distributed weed and a pharmacopoeial herb in the world's largest herbal-medicine market, and its single most important traditional claim appears to be pharmacologically unexamined. That is a striking gap and this entry's chief contribution is to name it rather than paper over it with flavonoid hand-waving. **A quercetin glycoside being present is not a diuretic finding.**

**2. Anti-inflammatory activity — MODERATE, mechanistically specified, and the best support the damp-heat doctrine gets.**
Park et al. (2018) tested *P. aviculare* extract in restraint-stressed mice, finding reduced lethargy-like behaviour, reduced corticosterone/serotonin/catecholamines, and **decreased brain CD68, Iba-1, TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β**; LC/MS resolved four active flavonoids (myricitrin, isoquercitrin, **avicularin**, quercitrin), each of which independently inhibited TNF-α production in neuronal cell culture ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2018.03.042), PMID 29655685). **Grade: MODERATE.** Species-specific, in vivo, mechanism identified down to named compounds. **Grade for relevance to the traditional indication: LOW** — this is a neuroinflammation/fatigue model, not a urinary one. It establishes that the plant's flavonoids are genuinely anti-inflammatory. **It does not establish that they are anti-inflammatory in a bladder**, and I will not pretend the inference is free.

**3. Antimicrobial activity — LOW-MODERATE, and thinner than the doctrine needs.**
Millar et al. (2021) list *Polygonum aviculare* among Chinese herbal medicines with demonstrated antimicrobial properties against clinically significant Gram-negative and Gram-positive pathogens ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/lam.13534), PMID 34219247). Sarametidis-style broad-extract work aside, **this is a review listing, not a primary MIC dataset I retrieved for this species.** **Grade: LOW.** If 热淋 is a urinary tract infection — and it very largely is — then the doctrine predicts activity against *E. coli* at achievable urinary concentrations, and **that specific test does not appear in what I retrieved.** The gap between "antimicrobial in a review list" and "clears a UTI" is the whole distance the doctrine needs to travel.

**4. Antioxidant activity — GOOD in vitro, and near-meaningless clinically.**
Hsu (2006) reports IC₅₀ of 50 µg/mL (DPPH), **0.8 µg/mL** (superoxide scavenging), 15 µg/mL (lipid peroxidation), plus DNA protection against hydroxyl-radical strand scission ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.4067/s0716-97602006000200010), PMID 16874403). **Grade: GOOD for the assay, LOW for meaning.** Antioxidant assays measure the presence of polyphenols. Everything with polyphenols scores. **This tells you the plant has flavonoids, which we knew from the LC/MS, and nothing about what it does in a person.** Deflate accordingly.

**5. Wound healing / Wnt/β-catenin — MODERATE, and the one result that corroborates a traditional use.**
Seo et al. (2016) screened 350 plant extracts for Wnt/β-catenin activation and found *P. aviculare* a hit; the extract accelerated HaCaT keratinocyte migration without cytotoxicity and **re-epithelialised wounds in mice**, with quercitrin hydrate, caffeic acid and rutin each active ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.5593), PMID 26929003). **Grade: MODERATE.** Species-specific, screened rather than cherry-picked, in vivo, mechanism named. **This corroborates the topical wound-wash tradition, and it flatly contradicts the knot-signature's growth-restraining claim.**

**6. Pancreatic lipase inhibition — LOW, and a good example of a finding that is not a use.**
*P. aviculare* had the highest PL-inhibitory activity of 34 Korean medicinal plants screened (**63.97 ± 0.05%**), traced to flavonol-3-O-glycosides and their galloyl esters, with kinetics and fluorescence quenching indicating a tertiary structural change in the enzyme (Park et al. 2019, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-54546-8), PMID 31792306). **Grade: LOW for any human claim.** In vitro, porcine enzyme, no clinical follow-through, no traditional anti-obesity use anywhere in the record. **Do not turn this into a supplement.**

**7. Anti-senescence / juglanin — MODERATE mechanistically, NULL as a use, and cited here mainly to close it off.**
Juglanin and (−)-loliolide from *P. aviculare* reduce SA-β-gal activity, p21 and ROS in adriamycin-induced and replicatively senescent human dermal fibroblasts (PMID 24519811; PMID 24993871). Juglanin has a substantial independent literature — anti-inflammatory, pro-apoptotic in tumour cells, protective across cardiovascular, CNS, metabolic and hepatic models via SIRT1/AMPK/Nrf2/NF-κB (Rutkowska et al. 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms251910323), PMID 39408653). **Grade: MODERATE for juglanin as a molecule, NULL for *P. aviculare* as a drug.** A plant being a commercial source of an interesting compound is a fact about **supply chains**, not therapeutics. The juglanin literature is not evidence for knotweed any more than the paclitaxel literature is evidence for eating yew needles (see I_764).

**8. Off-target curiosity, honestly labelled.**
*P. aviculare* extract screened as a juvenile hormone antagonist; chlorogenic and caffeic acid compete with methoprene at the Met PAS-B domain, inducing precocious metamorphosis in *Ostrinia furnacalis* (He et al. 2025, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pestbp.2025.106655), PMID 41162045). Extract improved mouse sperm motility/morphology after EMF exposure at 50 mg/kg (Milan et al. 2011, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3923/pjbs.2011.720.724), PMID 22308654). **Grade: N/A and VERY LOW respectively.** Neither is a human indication. Included because they exist and should not be discovered later and mistaken for suppressed evidence.

**Honest summary.** The astringent doctrine is chemically vindicated. The anti-inflammatory half of the damp-heat doctrine has real, species-specific, in-vivo support in the wrong organ. **The diuretic doctrine — the herb's actual name and job — is untested.** The knot signature is false and the chemistry contradicts it. Net: **a real, mild, low-risk herb whose central claim rests on two thousand years of use and zero published pharmacology.**

### Contraindications

**General:** a mild herb with real but bounded cautions. None of the following are theoretical hedges — each attaches to a specific constituent class.

- **Pregnancy — AVOID.** The classical caution is explicit: *Bian Xu* is a downward-draining, urination-promoting herb, and the tradition withholds draining/purging agents in pregnancy on principle. No safety data exists. **[USER: verify against a current pharmacopoeial contraindication list.]** Breastfeeding: avoid, no data.
- **Oxalates.** Polygonaceae are an oxalate-bearing family. **Avoid in a history of calcium-oxalate urolithiasis** — which is the sharp irony of this entry, since **石淋 (stone lín) is a classical indication.** The tradition prescribed this herb for stones; the family's oxalate load is a plausible mechanism for *causing* the commonest kind of stone. **This tension is unresolved in the literature and this entry does not resolve it.** No oxalate quantitation for *P. aviculare* was retrieved. **[USER: verify.]** Until it is: do not use for stone prophylaxis, and avoid in oxalate stone-formers, in hyperoxaluria, and in renal impairment.
- **Tannins.** Reduce **non-haem iron absorption** — separate from iron supplements and iron-rich meals by ≥2 h. Avoid in iron-deficiency anaemia. May cause gastric irritation on an empty stomach. May reduce absorption of alkaloidal drugs taken concurrently.
- **Renal impairment.** Avoid — oxalate and unquantified clearance.
- **Diuretic co-medication.** Theoretical additive effect and electrolyte risk. Note this caution is theoretical *precisely because the diuretic action is unproven* — which cuts both ways and is not reassurance.
- **Duration.** Short, acute courses only. No long-term/tonic use is supported by any tradition here, and the tannin + oxalate profile argues against inventing one.
- **Solo use.** Not traditional. The evidence base — such as it is — is for the plant inside a formula.
- **Identification.** If it burns the tongue: *Persicaria hydropiper*, not this. If the drug is a twisted rhizome: *Bistorta*, not this. If it is a bamboo-like thicket eight feet high: **Japanese knotweed (*Reynoutria japonica*)** — an entirely different plant with an entirely different drug (resveratrol/emodin) and an invasive-species legal status in several jurisdictions.
