# Human bone — "Human bone", "Human hip bone" (Miyi cherue), "Human skull from a cemetery", "skull-bone of colic victim" (Gyuser thoepa)
## *Human skeletal material named in the Tibetan materia medica: the bone in general, the hip bone, the cemetery skull and the skull of a colic victim; a historical record, not a recommended practice* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The locus is Clark's 1995 translation, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, "the powers of medicines" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). Section (8), "Medicines of Animal Origin", lists "thirteen medicines of animal origin ... horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (lines 1147-1148). Human material is therefore handled by this text inside the animal group. Under the sub-heading "Bone" (line 1159) the first four entries are human (line 1160):

"Human skull from a cemetery: dries lymph (accumulations). Human bone: [is beneficial in heart diseases]. If the bone is a weather beaten scapula [shoulderblade] in ash form it removes stubborn, chronic fever. Human hip bone: removes infectious tissue degeneration."

and, later in the same line, "The bones of those killed by thunderbolts and the skull-bone of colic victim: cures colic."

Human bone in general is thus said to be beneficial in heart diseases (the clause is bracketed in Clark's text). `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 334 lists "Miyi cherue", and line 337 lists "Gyuser thoepa", each as animal, "Explanatory Tantra ch.20 (Efficacy of Medicinal Substances)". These are the Men-Tsee-Khang transliterations, and the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 3523) gives the same group of items: "Miyi dhur thoe dry up chhuser. Mirue tsama and Sokthal cure chronic hot disorder embedded in the body. Miyi cherue cure inflammation of the muscle tissue." and, a little later in the passage, "Thokgi saepai ruepa and Gyuser thoepa cure colitis."

Clark's preface says that "nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57), and that he "interpolated liberally from the Blue Aquamarine commentary" (line 80); the square brackets are not explained in the lines read, so which words are root text is not established here. ### Varieties

Three varieties are folded into this entry. All three are attested only in the Tibetan text.

**Human hip bone** (FMT; Clark line 1160; `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 334, "Miyi cherue"). Clark: "Human hip bone: removes infectious tissue degeneration." The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary glosses *Miyi chirue* (*mi'i dpyi rus*) as "A human hip bone" (glossary, lines 4946-4949), and its text says "Miyi cherue cure inflammation of the muscle tissue" (line 3523). The two renderings of the action ("infectious tissue degeneration"; "inflammation of the muscle tissue") are different English for what may be one condition; the lines read do not settle it. [USER: verify] It sits under human bone in general.

**Human skull** (FMT; Clark line 1160). Clark: "Human skull from a cemetery: dries lymph (accumulations)." The Men-Tsee-Khang text has "Miyi dhur thoe dry up chhuser" (line 3523), and its glossary lists *Dhu rthoe* (*dur thod*) with the gloss "A human skull collected from the cemetery" (glossary line 4865). That pairing of the transliteration with the gloss rests on the column layout of the scanned glossary and is my reading. [USER: verify] "Lymph" in Clark corresponds to *chhuser* in the Men-Tsee-Khang text; I did not find a definition of *chhuser* in the lines read. This variety has no row in `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv`.

**Skull of a colic victim** (FMT; Clark line 1160; `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 337, "Gyuser thoepa"). Clark: "the skull-bone of colic victim: cures colic", grouped in one clause with "the bones of those killed by thunderbolts". The Men-Tsee-Khang text groups *Gyuser thoepa* with the lightning-victim bone and says both "cure colitis" (line 3523 passage), and its glossary entry for *Gyuser thoepa* (*rgyu gzer thod pa*) reads "Skull of a human who died of dysentery" (glossary, lines 4857 and 4871). So the condition is "colic" in Clark, "colitis" in the Men-Tsee-Khang text and "dysentery" in its glossary; the three English words are not equivalent and the lines read do not say which the Tibetan means. [USER: verify]

Other human-bone items sit in the same line but are not among the three folded varieties. The "weather beaten scapula in ash form" removes "stubborn, chronic fever" (Clark, line 1160), corresponding in the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary to *Sogthal*, "The ash of human scapula", with *Mirue tsama*, "a withered human bone which remained under the earth for a long time" (glossary lines 4940, 4965; the term-to-gloss pairing is again from the column layout). The bone "of a human who was killed after being struck by a thunderbolt" is *Thog gi saepai ruepa* (glossary lines 4981-4983). These are recorded here for completeness and are listed in the report for the parent entry.

*Synthesis of the varieties.* The Tibetan text separates its human bones by body part (the hip bone, the skull, the scapula), by condition of the bone (weathered, withered, long buried), by place (a cemetery) and by the circumstance of the person's death (colic, lightning). Clark ranks none of them; the line sets them in the order cemetery skull, bone in general, scapula ash, hip bone, with the colic and lightning items at the end of the line. Each carries its own stated action: lymph (cemetery skull), heart diseases (bone in general), chronic fever (scapula ash), infectious tissue degeneration (hip bone), colic (colic victim's skull and lightning-victim bone). Only the Tibetan text lists these varieties, so there is no second text to compare them with; they are placed side by side here and not equated with anything outside it.

### Active Components

Bone is a composite of hydroxyapatite (a calcium phosphate mineral) and collagen protein, with marrow in the cavities. A weathered or long-buried bone has lost much of its organic content, and ash of bone is largely calcium phosphate. The texts give no preparation, and no analysis was retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. This entry records what the Tibetan text says about human bone, and nothing else.

It is not a recommended practice. Using human remains as a medicine is unlawful or restricted in many places, ethically unacceptable to most readers, and unsafe. The Codex's application layer offers no protocol, no sourcing advice and no substitute. Human remains are obtained from the dead, including from cemeteries and from persons who died of disease, and are subject to law, consent and the dignity of the dead.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation, dose or route is given for any human-bone item in the lines read, apart from the scapula "in ash form" (Clark, line 1160). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] General facts: ingesting powdered or ashed bone supplies calcium phosphate but has no known effect on heart disease, fever, lymph accumulation or tissue degeneration. Human remains can carry infectious agents, including bacteria and prion disease agents in nervous tissue and the skull, and material from persons who died of an infectious illness is a hazard.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Human bone is attested in the Explanatory Tantra ch.20 animal section as a therapeutic substance, each item with a stated action against a stated condition. The frame it sits in is read from Clark's preface and root text: the three humors (wind, bile, phlegm) "are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration respectively" (line 51), and treatment relies on "the fourfold food, behavioral pattern, medicine and accessory therapy" (line 1299). Nothing in the lines read gives any human-bone item a place in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or in any practice of the subtle body, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition. The text does not say why these materials were chosen.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. The text lists human bone as a medicine among thirteen animal categories, with no cultivation claim and no method. It is recorded here as history only, and the right response to the entry is to leave human remains alone.

### Contraindications

- **Not a recommended practice.** This records a historical entry in a Tibetan materia medica. Nothing here is advice to obtain, handle or take human remains.
- **Legal:** possession, trade and use of human remains are restricted or unlawful in many jurisdictions. [USER: verify]
- **Infection:** human bone and the skull can carry bacteria, viruses and prions; the dead may have died of infectious disease.
- **Ethics:** the material comes from the dead, with questions of consent, dignity and the wishes of families and communities.
- **Cardiac, fever, lymph and colic conditions named in the text:** these need ordinary medical assessment; no traditional bone preparation is a substitute.
