# I-586 | Pheasant's Eye / Adonis

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**

**Botanical name:** *Adonis vernalis* L. (Spring Pheasant's Eye); *Adonis aestivalis* L. (Summer Pheasant's Eye); *Adonis microcarpa* DC.  
**Family:** Ranunculaceae  
**Energetic Tier:** ◈ Jing (cardiac glycoside; Ranunculaceae family — distinct from Scrophulariaceae foxglove)  
**Convergence Score:** 5/9 traditions  
**Entry type:** Medicinal Herb — Cardenolide Cardiac Glycoside (Ranunculaceae source)

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary names:**
- English: Spring Pheasant's Eye, Yellow Pheasant's Eye, Ox-Eye, False Hellebore (outdated misnomer — not *Helleborus*)
- Latin: *Adonis vernalis* — *Adonis* from Greek mythology; *vernalis* = of spring
- Greek: *Adonís* (Ἄδωνις) — named for the god Adonis, beloved of Aphrodite, whose blood supposedly gave rise to the red anemone (or red-flowered *Adonis*) when he was slain by a boar
- German: *Frühlings-Adonisröschen* (spring Adonis rosette), *Teufelsauge* (devil's eye)
- French: *Adonide printanière*, *Œil-de-faisan* (pheasant's eye)
- Russian: *Горицвет весенний* (Goritsvet vesenniy = "flaming spring flower") — extensively used in Russian/Soviet cardiology
- Arabic: *Ayn al-tays* (goat's eye)
- Chinese: *Cèjīn* — Chinese species (*A. davidii*) used in TCM; not the primary Western species

**Mythological name:**
Adonis in Greek mythology was a mortal youth of exceptional beauty, beloved by both Aphrodite and Persephone. He was killed by a wild boar (sent by Ares, jealous of Aphrodite's love). His blood stained the ground, giving rise to red flowers. The red-flowered *Adonis aestivalis* (Summer Pheasant's Eye) and the red *Adonis annua* visually embody this myth. The yellow-flowered *A. vernalis* (the medicinally important species) may have been included in the genus for botanical similarity rather than the Adonis color connection.

**Botanical identity (*A. vernalis*):**
Perennial herb, 10–40 cm; from stout rhizome with black outer coat. Basal leaves appear after flowering; stem leaves deeply pinnate into narrow segments. Flowers: bright yellow, large (4–8 cm), 10–20 petals; March–May (very early); solitary terminal. Distinctive: sepals hairless, appressed to petals. Distribution: dry steppe and calcareous grasslands; Eastern/Central Europe (Hungary, Romania, Ukraine, Russia); declining in many areas due to agricultural pressure; protected species in several countries.

**Species comparison:**

| Species | Flowers | Season | Primary use |
|---------|---------|--------|-------------|
| *A. vernalis* | Yellow, 10–20 petals | Spring | Primary cardiac; most studied |
| *A. aestivalis* | Red with black center | Summer | Less used medicinally |
| *A. annua* | Red | Annual | Ornamental; low cardiac glycoside |
| *A. davidii* | Yellow | China | TCM use; different glycoside profile |

**Note on Eastern European and Russian tradition:**
*Adonis vernalis* has its primary documented medical history in Eastern European and particularly Russian medicine, which is not captured in the 9-tradition framework. The Russian imperial and Soviet pharmacopoeias both listed *Adonis vernalis* preparations, and Soviet cardiologists of the early-mid 20th century used it as a digitalis alternative. This tradition adds substantial practical pharmacological validation outside the 9-tradition framework.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Position as Ranunculaceae cardiac glycoside:**
*Adonis vernalis* fills a pharmacological niche in the Codex narrative: it demonstrates that cardiac glycosides (digoxin-like compounds with Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase inhibitory activity) evolved convergently in multiple plant families:
- Scrophulariaceae: *Digitalis* (foxglove; cardenolide — 5-membered lactone)
- Ranunculaceae: *Adonis vernalis* (cardenolide — same 5-membered lactone!)
- Ranunculaceae: *Helleborus* (bufadienolide — 6-membered lactone)
- Liliaceae: *Convallaria majalis* (lily of the valley; cardenolide)
- Apocynaceae: *Strophanthus* (ouabain; cardenolide)
- Plantaginaceae: *Digitalis* (reclassified from Scrophulariaceae)

This convergent evolution of cardiac glycosides across 6+ plant families is pharmacologically remarkable — the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase binding site is apparently a strong selection target for plant secondary metabolites intended for herbivore deterrence.

**Russian/Soviet pharmacological tradition:**
Soviet cardiologist N.A. Bellokon and colleagues in the 1920s–1950s developed standardized *Adonis vernalis* preparations that were used as alternatives to digitalis, particularly for patients who could not tolerate fox-glove-derived glycosides. The Soviet pharmacopoeias listed both *Adonizid* (liquid extract) and *Adonis tablets* as first-line cardiac preparations. This tradition developed independently from Western European and Asian medicine and represents a distinct pharmacological validation of the plant.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Primary bioactive compounds:**

**Cardenolides (cardiac glycosides):**
- **Cymarin** (*k*-strophanthin-β; primary cardiac glycoside of *A. vernalis*): cardenolide glycoside; strophanthidin aglycone + cymarose sugar; potent Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase inhibitor
- **Adonitoxin** (*A. vernalis* glycoside): cardenolide; slower onset than cymarin; longer duration
- **Adoniside**: Minor cardenolide
- **Strophanthidin**: Aglycon; not itself a major component; pharmacological comparator
- Total cardenolide content: 0.2–0.6% in fresh herb; varies with growth stage, season, plant part

**Cymarin vs. digoxin:**
| Property | Cymarin | Digoxin |
|----------|---------|---------|
| Aglycone | Strophanthidin | Digoxigenin |
| Sugar | Cymarose (1 sugar) | Digitoxose ×3 |
| Oral absorption | Poor (cymarose attachment reduces gut absorption) | 60–80% |
| Duration | Shorter (rapid renal excretion) | 36–40h half-life |
| CNS penetration | Low | Some |
| Therapeutic index | Similarly narrow to digoxin | Narrow |

**Flavonoids:**
- Orientin, vitexin, luteolin, rutin: anti-inflammatory; cardioprotective

**Saponins:**
- Steroidal saponins: detergent-type; contribute to GI side effects

**Cardiostimulatory steroids:**
- Digitoxigenin, strophanthidin (aglycones) in minor concentrations

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase inhibition (identical to digoxin mechanism):**
Cymarin and adonitoxin inhibit Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (the sodium pump) at the same α-subunit ouabain binding site:
1. Pump inhibition → intracellular Na⁺ accumulation
2. Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger (NCX) reversal → intracellular Ca²⁺ accumulation
3. Ca²⁺ accumulation → increased contractility (positive inotropy)
4. Vagal sensitization → bradycardia; reduced AV conduction

**Clinical electrophysiology parallels digoxin:**
- Increased PR interval (AV conduction slowing — therapeutic in atrial fibrillation)
- Shortened QT interval (increased Ca²⁺-driven repolarization)
- ST depression and T-wave changes (strain pattern)
- At toxic concentrations: ventricular ectopy → VT/VF (Ca²⁺ overload → delayed afterdepolarizations)

**Difference from digitalis in practical pharmacology:**
- Cymarin: shorter half-life (incomplete enteral absorption; faster renal clearance) → less accumulation → possibly lower chronic toxicity risk
- Adonitoxin: longer half-life → contributes to variable duration of effect
- Standardization of *Adonis* preparations historically challenging due to variable glycoside content → less predictable than pharmaceutical digoxin

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Level B — Soviet/Eastern European clinical series:**
- Multiple Soviet-era clinical publications (1930s–1970s; *Farmakologiya i Toksikologiya*, Soviet pharmacology journals): *Adonis vernalis* preparations for cardiac insufficiency, atrial fibrillation; comparable to digitalis but shorter duration requiring more frequent dosing; some tolerance advantage in digitalis-intolerant patients
- These publications do not meet modern RCT standards but represent substantial observational clinical experience

**Level C — European botanical tradition:**
- German Commission E: *Adonis vernalis* herba listed; cardiac insufficiency, nervous heart complaints; but Commission E notes "risk/benefit ratio does not justify use" given availability of standardized cardiac drugs; not currently recommended for self-medication
- French pharmacopeia: historically listed; largely superseded by standardized cardiac glycosides

**Level D — Mechanistic:**
- In vitro cardiac glycoside activity: well-characterized; cymarin IC₅₀ for Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase inhibition comparable to digoxin range
- Animal cardiac insufficiency models: positive inotropy demonstrated

**Current clinical status:**
*Adonis vernalis* preparations are essentially obsolete in modern cardiology due to:
1. Availability of pharmaceutical digoxin with known pharmacokinetics
2. Modern heart failure therapy (ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, spironolactone) reducing role of cardiac glycosides
3. Standardization difficulties with botanical preparation
4. Narrow therapeutic index requiring reliable dosing

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The Mythology That Named a Drug:**
Few drugs carry as direct a mythological name as *Adonis vernalis*. The myth of Adonis — beautiful mortal, torn between divine lovers, killed at the peak of beauty — became one of antiquity's most resonant mortality-and-resurrection stories (Adonis festivals, *Adonia*, were celebrated throughout the Mediterranean world; the women of Cyprus and Lebanon mourned annually for the slain god). That this name came to attach to a plant with cardiac activity — a plant that literally touches the heartbeat — has a poetic resonance not lost on later medical writers. The 19th-century German pharmacologist who formally gave the genus this name apparently appreciated the connection.

**The Adonisit Drug in Soviet Medicine:**
Soviet pharmacy developed a proprietary formulation called *Adonizid* — a standardized liquid extract of *Adonis vernalis* — that appeared in Soviet pharmacopoeia editions from the 1920s through 1980s. Soviet cardiologists at the Vishnevsky Institute and affiliated cardiac centers had substantial clinical experience with this preparation. When Soviet medicine was dismantled in the 1990s, many of these preparations disappeared from official use; the clinical knowledge was partially lost. This represents a pharmacological tradition with reasonable empirical basis that was never translated into the controlled trial format required by modern regulatory standards.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Historical preparations (documented; not currently recommended):**

**Adonis vernalis preparations (Soviet pharmacopoeia):**
- *Adonizid* (liquid extract): 20–40 drops BID/TID; standardized by bioassay (not chemical standardization)
- *Adonis tablets*: 0.05g tablet standardized extracts; 1 tablet TID
- Indicated for: chronic cardiac insufficiency, functional cardiac neurosis
- Monitoring: same as digoxin; watch for toxicity signs (nausea, bradycardia, arrhythmia, visual disturbances)

**Why not used today:**
- Pharmaceutical digoxin provides reliable, standardized dosing with defined pharmacokinetics
- Modern heart failure treatments (ACEi, ARB, beta-blockers, sacubitril/valsartan, SGLT2 inhibitors) have largely superseded cardiac glycosides
- No modern regulatory approval pathways completed for *Adonis* preparations

**Emergency management — *Adonis vernalis* poisoning:**
- Digoxin-like toxidrome: GI symptoms → bradyarrhythmia → VT/VF
- Cross-reacts with digoxin immunoassay: falsely "elevated digoxin level" on assay
- Treatment parallels digoxin toxicity:
  - Digoxin-specific Fab fragments (Digibind/DigiFab): may partially neutralize cymarin/adonitoxin via cross-reactivity; case reports support utility
  - Atropine for bradycardia
  - Avoid calcium (potentiates glycoside-induced arrhythmia)
  - Potassium correction if hypokalemic (hypokalemia potentiates glycoside toxicity)
  - Activated charcoal if within 1 hour
