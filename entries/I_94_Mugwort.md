### Botanical Identity
Perennial 60–150cm; strongly aromatic (camphor, eucalyptol); deeply lobed leaves, dark green above, white-silver undersurface (the silver underside is visually distinctive); small yellowish-brown flower heads in branching panicles; native to temperate Europe, Asia, North America; cosmopolitan weed. The **dried leaf** is the primary drug material for both internal use and moxibustion. For moxibustion (*jiǔ*), the dried leaf is processed into *moxa* — compressed cones or sticks of aged dried leaf that burn slowly and emit a characteristic penetrating heat and distinctive smoky aroma.

**Artemisia genus**: *Artemisia vulgaris* is one of ~500 species in the *Artemisia* genus, which includes: wormwood (*A. absinthium*, absinthe), tarragon (*A. dracunculus*, culinary), sagebrush (*A. tridentata*, North American Indigenous), sweet wormwood (*A. annua*, artemisinin anti-malarial source — the Nobel Prize 2015 discovery by Tu Youyou), and southernwood (*A. abrotanum*). The genus is pharmacologically one of the most significant in all plant medicine — the *Artemisia* compounds artemisinin (malaria), absinthin (wormwood), and the mugwort terpenoids represent a constellation of anti-parasitic, anti-infective, and gynaecological pharmacology.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ)

*Ài yè*:
> *"Bitter, acrid; warm; slightly toxic. Enters Liver, Spleen, Kidney. Warms the channels and stops bleeding; dispels cold and relieves pain; adjusts menstruation; calms the fetus. Used for cold-type uterine bleeding, dysmenorrhoea, irregular menstruation, threatened miscarriage, abdominal pain from cold."*

*Ài yè* is the moxibustion herb of Chinese medicine — the material burnt in the *jiǔ* technique to heat acupuncture points. The internal use and the moxibustion use are pharmacologically different applications of the same plant.

### 2.2 De Materia Medica (DMM)

Dioscorides (*Book III, Chapter 113*):
> *"Artemisia: warming. Taken in decoction it draws down the menses, expels the afterbirth, kills intestinal worms. Applied with oil it relieves hardness and swelling of the womb. Good for cramps and convulsions. The dried herb placed under a traveller's shoes prevents fatigue" (the first recorded use as a travel herb).*

### 2.3 Canon of Medicine (CM)

Ibn Sīnā on *baʿīthrān*:
> *"Warm and dry in the third degree. One of the strongest emmenagogues. Expels dead fetus, expels retained placenta. Relieves uterine cold. For intestinal worms. The smoke relieves nervous convulsions."*

### 2.4 Four Medical Tantras (FMT)

Tibetan moxibustion (*ser chen*/*cha ma ti ra*): burning mugwort at acupuncture/acupressure points for cold disorders (*grang ba*), digestive weakness, and gynaecological conditions. The technique is directly inherited from Chinese medicine but integrated into Tibetan medical theory.

### 2.5 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)

Aromatic bitter herb for "purification" rituals, fever, and uterine conditions in Babylonian texts.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Emmenagogue / Uterine Stimulant (Primary Traditional)

**Thujone + camphor + 1,8-cineole**: Multiple mechanisms for uterine smooth muscle stimulation:
- Thujone: GABA-A inverse agonist → increases neuronal excitability → stimulates uterine contractions
- Camphor: direct smooth muscle stimulant
- 1,8-cineole: calcium channel modulation in smooth muscle

The combined volatile oil profile stimulates uterine contractions — explaining the consistent cross-traditional emmenagogue application and the absolute pregnancy contraindication.

### 5.2 Moxibustion (Thermal Mechanism)

**Moxa heat delivery**: The moxibustion application of mugwort is pharmacologically distinct from the internal use:
- *Jiǔ* (moxibustion): dried compressed *ài yè* burnt at or near the skin surface → delivers sustained, penetrating infrared-rich heat to acupuncture points
- The burnt material releases volatile compounds (borneol, 1,8-cineole) that penetrate the skin during heating → local anti-inflammatory + microcirculatory effects
- Thermal stimulation of thermal sensory receptors (TRPV1, TRPM8) → reflexive modulation of meridian points
- Breech baby moxibustion (acupoint BL67): the most widely studied moxibustion application — stimulating *zhiyin* point at the little toe causes uterine contractions and fetal movement → evidence for turning breech babies (Cardini 1998, JAMA RCT)

### 5.3 Antiparasitic (Thujone + Sesquiterpene Lactones)

**Thujone + artemisinin-related sesquiterpenes**: *A. vulgaris* contains sesquiterpene lactones related to (but not identical to) artemisinin (*A. annua*). The antiparasitic tradition (intestinal worms — DMM, CM, AT, SBJ) is mechanistically consistent with the thujone GABA-A antagonism (neurological disruption of parasite motor control) and sesquiterpene effects.

### 5.4 Anti-Inflammatory / Gastroprotective

**Eupatilin + artemisetin**: The flavonoids specifically protect gastric mucosa:
- Eupatilin reduces gastric acid secretion; inhibits *H. pylori* adhesion; promotes mucus secretion → gastroprotective
- Multiple Phase II trials of eupatilin for gastric ulcer in South Korea (Stillen® — a standardised eupatilin product from *A. asiatica*); gastroprotective application well-evidenced in Asian pharmaceutical development

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Moxibustion for breech presentation**: Cardini (1998, *JAMA*): Moxibustion at BL67 acupoint for 30 minutes daily from 33 weeks gestation — 75.4% cephalic presentation at 35 weeks vs 47.7% in control group. This is a rigorous RCT published in a top-tier journal. Multiple confirmatory trials. Cochrane review (2012): moxibustion significantly reduces use of oxytocin and caesarean section in Chinese settings; evidence less consistent in Western settings. The breech presentation application is the best-evidenced moxibustion indication.

**Eupatilin / gastroprotective (Stillen®)**: Multiple South Korean Phase III RCTs: eupatilin (stillen) 60mg twice daily significantly superior to placebo for acute gastritis; comparable to misoprostol for gastric protection in NSAID users. The *A. asiatica* flavonoid fraction enters pharmaceutical use in South Korea through this evidence base.

**Seasonal allergic rhinitis (immunomodulatory)**: German studies on *A. vulgaris* allergen for desensitisation — mugwort is a major allergenic pollen; immunotherapy studies inform both allergy treatment and the plant's immune-modulating properties.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The goddess herb**: *Artemisia* named for Artemis — the Greek goddess of the moon, hunting, wild animals, and childbirth — is the most explicitly divine naming of any herb in this Codex. The emmenagogue application (regulating women's menstrual cycle) and the uterine stimulant application (assisting labour and expelling retained placenta) are direct pharmacological correlates of Artemis's divine domain (childbirth). The moon cycle association (menstrual rhythm) and the Artemis attribution were not metaphorical in the ancient world — they were functional descriptions of a plant that worked in the domain of the goddess. The pharmacological mechanism (thujone uterine stimulation) validates the divine attribution.

**Moxa and the burning art**: Moxibustion has been practised in China for over 3,000 years — the *Huangdi Neijing* devotes substantial sections to *jiǔ* (moxa) technique. The practice of burning a specific aromatic plant over or near the skin at specific body points to treat internal conditions is one of the most sophisticated traditional medicine techniques in any tradition. The combination of thermal stimulation, volatile compound skin penetration, and reflexive point-activation is a multi-modal pharmacological delivery system without modern pharmaceutical parallel. The *ài yè* moxa represents a technology of plant-delivered thermal medicine that modern pharmaceutical science has not improved upon for its specific indications (breech turning, cold-type digestive disorders, certain pain conditions).

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

| Form | Dose | Application |
|------|------|-------------|
| Tea (dried leaf) | 2–4g per cup (short steep — 5 min) | Digestive; carminative; mild emmenagogue; NOT long steep (thujone) |
| Tincture (1:5, 45% ethanol) | 1–3ml 2× daily | Anti-inflammatory; digestive; antiparasitic |
| Moxa stick (indirect) | 15–30 min per session at acupoint | Moxibustion; breech baby; cold conditions |
| Moxa cone (direct — therapist only) | Under trained practitioner | Specific acupoint treatment |
| Combined with cramp bark (I_88) | — | Dysmenorrhoea antispasmodic + emmenagogue |

**Thujone safety**: Short steep tea (5 min) extracts volatile aromatics and flavonoids with minimal thujone extraction. Long steeping, decoction, or concentrated tincture increases thujone content — the dose-response of thujone becomes relevant at higher concentrations. Short-steep tea at 2–4g is safe; concentrated extracts require dose control.

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Bitter, acrid; warm. Channels: Liver (肝), Spleen (脾), Kidney (腎). Warms the channels and stops bleeding; dispels Cold; regulates menstruation; calms the fetus. One of the most important warming *wēn jīng* herbs.

**Ayurvedic (cross-reference)**: Bitter, pungent; warming. Reduces Kapha ↓↓, reduces Vata ↓ (warm). *Artavajanana* (emmenagogue), *Kṛmighna* (antiparasitic), *Pachana* (digestive stimulant).

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Pregnancy**: Absolute contraindication for internal use — thujone + camphor uterine stimulant → miscarriage risk. Moxibustion for breech turning (BL67 at 33+ weeks) is a specific, supervised protocol — NOT general therapeutic use
- **Epilepsy / seizure history**: Thujone is a GABA-A inverse agonist — lowers seizure threshold; avoid in epilepsy or seizure history
- **Liver disease**: Thujone metabolism is hepatic; impaired clearance increases toxicity risk
- **Asteraceae allergy**: Major allergen; patch test; avoid if ragweed or related allergy severe
- **Long-term/high-dose use**: Chronic thujone accumulation → neurotoxicity; use intermittently, not continuously
- **Drug interactions**: Thujone may interact with CNS medications; monitor
