# I-644 | Calendula

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted binomial:** *Calendula officinalis* L. (1753)
**Family:** Asteraceae (Compositae); Tribe: Calenduleae
**Authority:** Carl Linnaeus, *Species Plantarum* (1753)

**Synonyms:**
- *Calendula officinalis* var. *officinalis*
- *Calendula stellata* Cav. (may be different species)
- *Caltha officinalis* (L.) Moench (historical)

**Important species note:**
*C. officinalis* (pot marigold) is the ONLY medicinal calendula species. The garden ornamental *Tagetes* species (African marigold, French marigold) are DIFFERENT plants — different genus, family-related but distinctly different chemistry; do not substitute. The name "marigold" is shared by *Calendula* and *Tagetes* in English folk usage; this creates frequent confusion in herbal products.

**Etymology:**
- *Calendula*: from Latin *calendae* (the Kalends — first day of the Roman month) — the plant flowers at the beginning of every month (*calend*); it was said to bloom on the first of every month; or more practically, it flowers almost continuously throughout the growing season, making it visible "on every calendar"
- *officinalis*: "of the apothecary/pharmacy" — official medicinal plant; sold in apothecary shops
- "Marigold": from Mary's gold — dedicated to the Virgin Mary in medieval Christian Europe; the golden flowers were associated with Our Lady's festivals

**Botanical description:**
- Annual (in temperate climates) or short-lived perennial (Mediterranean); 30–70 cm; erect; branched; sticky-hairy (glandular trichomes — characteristic)
- Stems: round (unlike square Lamiaceae); glandular-hairy; viscid (sticky); pale green to slightly reddish
- Leaves: simple; alternate; oblanceolate; 5–15 cm; slightly fleshy; entire to minutely toothed; glandular-hairy; aromatic (resinous-floral)
- Flower heads: large (3–7 cm diameter); on long terminal peduncles
  - **Ray florets**: 15–30+; strap-shaped; 2–3.5 cm long; bright orange to yellow (intensity varies by cultivar); fertile or sterile
  - **Disc florets**: numerous; orange; tubular; fertile
- Receptacle: flat; solid (unlike chamomile's hollow cone — diagnostic distinction)
- Achenes (fruit): curved, boat-shaped; of three distinct types (a diagnostic feature of *Calendula*)
- Overall: one of the most cheerful-looking of all medicinal herbs; the large orange flower heads have been cultivated and selected for deeper orange coloring and double/triple layers of ray florets in ornamental forms

**Distribution:**
- Native: uncertain — possibly southern Europe (Mediterranean basin); possibly introduced from western Asia; cultivated so long that wild origins are obscured
- Now grown worldwide as ornamental and medicinal herb; thrives in cool, temperate conditions; tolerates frost; flowers best in cool weather (spring and autumn in most climates)
- Major cultivation: Egypt, India, Germany, Netherlands, USA (herb farms), Eastern Europe
- Naturalized: in many temperate regions; found as garden escape throughout Britain, northern Europe, North America

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**European topical medicine — exceptional depth:**
While calendula lacks cross-traditional validation, within the European tradition it achieves something remarkable: consistent use as a topical wound-healing and anti-inflammatory herb documented from the 12th century CE to the present, combined with modern Level A/B RCT evidence confirming the traditional application.

**Primary defining pharmacological profile:**
1. **Wound healing** (primary): triterpenoids (oleanolic acid, ursolic acid) + flavonoids + polysaccharides → accelerated epithelialization + anti-inflammatory + antimicrobial
2. **Anti-inflammatory** (primary): flavonoids + triterpenoids → COX-2 inhibition + 5-LOX inhibition
3. **Antimicrobial** (secondary): sesquiterpene lactones (minor) + flavonoids → broad-spectrum antimicrobial
4. **Lymphatic** (traditional): the traditional use for "swollen lymph nodes" and internal lymphatic congestion is less clearly supported pharmacologically but immunomodulatory effects of polysaccharides may contribute

**Energetic Tier:**
- Primary: ◉ **Qi** — wound healing/anti-inflammatory/antimicrobial
- Secondary: ◈ **Jing** (lymphatic/immune modulation — traditional attribution)

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### Wound Healing (Primary Application)

**Multi-mechanistic — the most comprehensively studied topical healing herb:**

1. **Triterpenoid collagen synthesis enhancement:**
   - Ursolic acid + oleanolic acid: upregulate TGF-β1 → fibroblast proliferation and myofibroblast differentiation → wound contraction
   - Faradiol esters: reduce inflammatory phase duration → allow healing phases to proceed; anti-edema at wound margins
   - α-Amyrin: promotes keratinocyte migration → reepithelialization

2. **Flavonoid anti-inflammatory:**
   - Isorhamnetin + quercetin: COX-2 inhibition; NF-κB suppression; reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) at wound site
   - 5-LOX inhibition: reduces leukotriene-driven inflammatory amplification at wound

3. **Antimicrobial prevention of infection:**
   - α-Cadinol + T-muurolol (EO sesquiterpenes): active against common wound pathogens (*S. aureus*, *E. coli*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*)
   - Quercetin: antifungal; inhibits fungal overgrowth at wound margins

4. **Polysaccharide matrix formation:**
   - Mucilage physically protects wound surface from desiccation
   - Promotes moist wound healing (superior to dry healing for epithelialization rate)

5. **Collagen quality improvement:**
   - Triterpenoids increase Type I collagen production AND improve collagen fibril organization → reduced scarring; higher tensile strength in healed tissue

### Anti-inflammatory

- NF-κB inhibition: oleanolic acid, ursolic acid, faradiol → blocks nuclear translocation → reduced inflammatory gene transcription
- COX-2 selective inhibition: flavonoids (isorhamnetin IC₅₀ ~5 µM COX-2)
- 5-LOX inhibition: flavonoids
- Complement activation inhibition: polysaccharides bind complement components → reduce excessive complement-mediated damage

### Antimicrobial

- *Staphylococcus aureus* (MRSA): sesquiterpene + flavonoid combination active; MIC 0.5–2 mg/mL
- *Candida albicans*: active (same mechanism as other Asteraceae sesquiterpenes)
- *Trichomonas vaginalis*: in vitro active (relevant to traditional gynecological use)
- HPV: some in vitro data for antiviral activity of flavonoid fraction

### Lymphatic and Immune (Traditional)

- Polysaccharide PS-I, PS-II fractions: macrophage activation (phagocytic enhancement); NK cell stimulation; IgM production increase
- These immunostimulatory effects may underlie the traditional "lymphatic" reputation
- NOT well characterized pharmacokinetically — these polysaccharides are large; oral bioavailability uncertain; topical application to lymph nodes (lymphatic compress) represents the historical use

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Evidence summary:** The **Pommier 2004 Level A RCT** (254 patients; breast cancer radiation dermatitis; calendula superior to the comparator trolamine for one of the most challenging dermatological conditions) is one of the most clinically compelling pieces of evidence for any herbal topical preparation in this Codex. Combined with EMA HMPC + Commission E + ESCOP triple regulatory endorsement and multiple Level B RCTs, calendula has the strongest topical evidence base in the Asteraceae cluster. WEAKNESS: no Level A internal medicine RCTs exist.

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Medieval Christian Symbolism

**Marian dedication:**
Calendula was specifically dedicated to the Virgin Mary in medieval Christian Europe — "Our Lady's Gold" or "Marigold" (Mary's gold). The flowers were used to decorate statues and shrines of the Virgin, were strewn at festivals on Lady Day (March 25) and Assumption (August 15), and were grown in monastery gardens for both medicinal and devotional purposes.

This religious association created a virtuous cycle: plants associated with the Virgin Mary were considered especially powerful healers; their use in chapel decorations meant they were constantly available to sick visitors; their bright color made them memorable and recognizable.

**The "herb of the sun":**
The heliotropic behavior of calendula flowers (ray florets that follow the sun across the sky; flowers that open in morning sunshine and close at sunset/cloudy weather) earned it solar associations in multiple traditions. This precise phenological observation — flowers that track the sun like a clock — was noted by multiple ancient authors and contributed to calendula's association with clarity, solar power, and reliable healing ("as dependable as the sun").

### Medieval European Herbal Tradition

**Macer Floridus (*De Viribus Herbarum*, ca. 1070–1100 CE):** One of the earliest clear European references to *Calendula* as a distinct medicinal plant; used for eye conditions, fever, and "malignant conditions" (possibly infection/sepsis).

**Hildegard von Bingen (*Physica*, ca. 1150 CE):** *Ringula* (Hildegard's name for calendula): "Where there is a wound of whatever kind, cook Ringula in fat and make a salve with it and apply to the wound; the wound will heal." The simplicity and directness of Hildegard's instruction — an uncomplicated wound-healing salve — anticipates the modern clinically validated application with remarkable accuracy.

**John Gerard (*Herball*, 1597):** "The flowers of Calendula dried and made up into a powder, and drunken in broth, doth comfort the heart and spirits, and is of especial use against the plague and all pestilent fevers... The juice of Calendula strained and given in wine is a singular remedy against all such poisons as are of a cold quality; it stays the flux of the belly called the laske..."

### 19th Century — Civil War and Modern Use

**American Civil War (1861–1865):** Calendula was extensively used by military surgeons on both Union and Confederate sides as wound dressings when conventional medical supplies were unavailable. The Confederacy in particular relied on calendula (fresh flower poultice, tincture-soaked dressings) for treating infected wounds — consistent with the antimicrobial + wound-healing pharmacology. This large-scale emergency medical use generated informal observational evidence that contributed to calendula's 20th-century revival.

**World War I:** Calendula again featured in military medicine; "Calendula gauze" (calendula infusion-soaked bandages) was distributed by both British and German Red Cross, based on the 19th-century European folk medical tradition for wound dressings.

### Commercial Calendula Products (Modern)

The Weleda company (founded 1921, Anthroposophic medicine tradition) standardized calendula cream as their flagship product in the 1940s — *Calendula Baby Cream* became one of the first commercially successful herbal baby skincare products worldwide and remains a market leader. This commercial success drove the largest-scale quality research into calendula chemistry.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Preparation Forms

| Preparation | Method | Dose | Target |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Infused oil** | Fresh or dried flowers in olive/almond oil; 4–6 weeks cold infusion or 2–4 hours warm infusion (40–50°C) | Apply directly or as cream base | PRIMARY topical preparation; wound healing; eczema; skin inflammation |
| **Calendula cream/ointment** | Standardized commercial preparation OR homemade from calendula oil + beeswax | Apply 2–4×/day | Wounds; burns; eczema; radiation dermatitis |
| Tincture (1:5, 70% ethanol) | Standard | Topical: dilute 1:10 for skin wash; 2–4 mL 3×/day oral | Wound wash; oral: lymphatic; anti-inflammatory |
| Herbal tea (infusion) | 2–3 g dried flower heads / 250 mL; steep 10 min covered | 3 cups/day | Internal: anti-inflammatory; lymphatic; GI inflammation |
| **Mouthwash/gargle** | Strong infusion (5 g / 250 mL) cooled | Gargle 1 min; 3–5×/day | Aphthous ulcers; gingivitis; oral wounds; equivalent to chlorhexidine (Khairnar 2013) |
| Compress | Infusion-soaked cotton; warm | Applied to skin lesion | Eczema; burns; lymph node congestion (traditional) |
| Sitz bath | Decoction (20 g flowers / 2L water) added to bath | Sitz or full bath | Postpartum perineal healing; hemorrhoids; vulvovaginitis (anti-inflammatory) |
| Eye wash | Cooled sterile infusion | Sterile cotton; applied to closed eye | Conjunctivitis (ensure particle-free; or use commercial preparation only) |

### Dosing by Indication

| Indication | Preparation | Dose | Duration | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wound healing / abrasions | Cream or infused oil | Apply 3–4×/day | Until healed | Level B/C |
| Radiation dermatitis (cancer) | Standardized cream (bisabolol-type formulation) | Apply 2–3×/day throughout radiation course | Duration of treatment | Level A (Pommier 2004) |
| Eczema / dermatitis | Cream or infused oil | Apply 2–4×/day | 4–6 weeks; reassess | Level B/C |
| Burns (first degree / sunburn) | Cream or cool infused oil | Apply immediately and every 2–3 hours | Until resolution | Level B |
| Gingivitis / oral hygiene | Mouthwash (strong infusion) | After meals + before bed | Ongoing | Level B (Khairnar 2013) |
| Aphthous ulcers | Mouthwash + direct application | 4–6×/day; hold in mouth | Until resolution | Level C |
| Episiotomy / perineal wound | Sitz bath + cream | Daily sitz bath; cream 2–3×/day | 7–14 days postpartum | Level B (Duran 2005) |
| Internal anti-inflammatory | Tea | 3 cups/day | 4–8 weeks | Level C |

### Contraindications

| Contraindication | Category | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Asteraceae allergy** | Important (see I-642 note) | Cross-reactive sesquiterpene lactones; obtain allergy history; patch test in high-risk patients; calendula allergy contact dermatitis documented |
| Pregnancy (internal high dose) | Caution | Emmenagogue claims in older literature; uterine stimulation possible at high internal dose; topical during pregnancy generally regarded as safe (widely used in maternity products) |
| Use on deep/infected wounds without medical assessment | Caution | Wound-healing herbs can accelerate surface closure over deeper infection — promote surface healing over incompletely debrided deep tissue; ensure deep wounds evaluated medically before herbal wound treatment |
| *Tagetes* confusion | Practical | Commercial products labeled "marigold" may contain *Tagetes* species (different chemistry, potentially irritant to skin); ensure *Calendula officinalis* species on label |

### Drug Interactions

| Drug | Interaction | Clinical action |
|---|---|---|
| Sedatives (BZDs, antihistamines) | Minor additive | Quercetin + isorhamnetin mild CNS effects | Low clinical significance at topical/tea doses |
| Immunosuppressants | Caution (internal use) | Polysaccharide immunostimulation | Discuss with transplant physician; topical use generally safe |
| Anticoagulants | Low (topical) | Isorhamnetin mild antiplatelet | No significant interaction at topical doses; monitor with oral tincture at high dose |

### The Radiation Dermatitis Application — Clinical Priority

The Pommier 2004 trial (Level A) is worth detailing for clinical practice:
- **Study**: 254 women receiving post-surgical breast irradiation; randomized to calendula cream vs. trolamine cream (standard care)
- **Outcome**: Grade 2+ acute radiation dermatitis: 41% in calendula vs. 63% in trolamine arm (p<0.001); pain score significantly lower; need for treatment interruption significantly lower; 96% of patients preferred calendula for comfort
- **Implication**: Calendula cream should be offered as standard care for radiation dermatitis in breast cancer patients — a significant unmet need where conventional options (hydrocortisone, trolamine) are imperfect
- **Practical note**: The cream used was a standardized *Calendula officinalis* flower extract cream (not just EO); the triterpenoid-flavonoid fraction (not EO alone) appears to be the active component for this indication

### Clinical Integration Notes

1. **Topical wound healing is the primary, evidence-based indication** — the Level A radiation dermatitis RCT, Level B episiotomy RCT, and extensive traditional documentation create a compelling case for calendula as first-choice topical wound and inflammation treatment

2. **The mouthwash application (equivalent to chlorhexidine, Khairnar 2013)** is clinically significant — chlorhexidine mouthwash has well-known side effects (tooth staining, taste disturbance, mucosal irritation); calendula offers comparable gingivitis/plaque control without these adverse effects

3. **Internal use is traditional but less evidence-supported** than topical; the lymphatic, GI anti-inflammatory, and immune uses have mechanistic plausibility but no Level A/B RCTs; appropriate as supportive traditional medicine for those indications

4. **Commercial quality**: standardization is important — ensure products specifying *Calendula officinalis* flower extract with triterpenoid content; avoid products containing *Tagetes* (different genus); infused oils should specify oil base (olive oil preferred for skin healing; sunflower oil also acceptable)
