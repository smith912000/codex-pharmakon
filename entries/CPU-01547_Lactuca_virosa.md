# Lactuca virosa — Thridax agria (Dioscorides 2-166)

## *Lactuca virosa, the last of the names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 2-166 (which also prints Lactuca sylvestris [Fuchs], Lactuca scariola [Linnaeus] and Thridax [Latin]); "Wild Lettuce"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 5973) lists four names with no choice among them, and *Lactuca virosa* is the last. It is the name on the plate caption (line 5971, "Lactuca virosa [seed head] after FAGUET — 1880"). The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] Other entries on the spine already treat lettuce under other tags (I_147 and I_679); the loci of those other traditions were not read for this entry, so nothing from them is used here.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *2-166. THRIDAX AGRIA*, heading at line 5969, in Book Two among the vegetables (*Lachana*). It follows *2-165. THRIDAX EMEROS* (line 5964), whose SUGGESTED line gives *Lactuca sativa* and "Common Lettuce". The translators' index gives *Lactuca virosa* at page 292 (lines 11781 and 16246) and *Lactuca sylvestris* at 292 (line 16246) and at 349 (line 11781).

The whole chapter reads: "Wild lettuce is similar to the cultivated only largerstalked, paler in the leaves, thinner and sharper, and bitter to the taste. It is somewhat similar to poppy in properties, as a result some mix the milky juice of it with meconium [4-65]. Twenty grains of the juice (taken in a drink with posca [hot drinks]) purges away watery matter through the bowels. It also wears off albugo [eye disease], and mistiness and dimness of the eyes, and it is good against their burning heat rubbed on with woman's milk. In general it is sleep-inducing and eases pain. It draws out the menstrual flow, and is given as a drink for those touched by a scorpion or harvest spider. The seed of this (as well as the cultivated, taken in a drink) turns away lustful dreams and venereal diseases. The juice is also good for the same purposes yet weaker. The milky juice of it is first exposed to the sun (like other extracted juices) and stored in new ceramic jars. The Magi call it sanguis Titani, Zoroastrians, pherumbras, and the Romans, lactuca sylvestris."

Dioscorides therefore describes the plant by comparison with the garden lettuce: bigger in the stalk, paler in the leaf, thinner, sharper and bitter. He compares its action to the poppy and says that some mix its milky juice with *meconium*. The uses printed are a purge of watery matter, an eye remedy for albugo, mistiness, dimness and "burning heat" (rubbed on with woman's milk), sleep and relief of pain, bringing on the menstrual flow, a drink after a scorpion or harvest-spider touch, and, for the seed, turning away "lustful dreams and venereal diseases". The juice is collected by exposing the milky exudate to the sun and storing it in new ceramic jars. The chapter prints three foreign names: *sanguis Titani* (the Magi), *pherumbras* (Zoroastrians) and the Roman *lactuca sylvestris*. It prints them as names only and gives no use or belief attached to them.

**Neighbouring places in the same text.** Chapter 2-165, on the cultivated lettuce, says that when the plants "shoot up into a stalk they have properties similar to the juice and milk of the wild lettuce" (line 5968). Chapter 4-65 (*MEKON AGRIOS, MEKON EMEROS*, heading at line 8577), on the poppy, says of opium: "They counterfeit it by mixing glaucium [3-100], gum, or juice of the wild lettuce", and gives the test "That of the wild lettuce is faint in its smell and rougher." (line 8586) It also says that the lozenge made by beating the stems and leaves and pressing out the juice is called meconium and "is weaker than opium". Neither passage says which species of wild lettuce is meant, so they are cited here as Dioscorides' own cross-references and not as evidence for the translators' identification.

### Active Components

The composition of the plant Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, plants of the genus *Lactuca* (Asteraceae) carry a white milky latex, and the latex of some species is bitter. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a bitter latex-bearing wild lettuce taken as a drink to purge, to bring sleep, to ease pain and to turn away "lustful dreams", and rubbed on for the eyes. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol. A sleep-inducing, pain-easing latex that, in the same author's words, some mixed with *meconium* (which he calls weaker than opium) is not something to take on the strength of this chapter. The identification is the translators', and the text gives no way to match a modern plant to a dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

2-166 gives these preparations: "Twenty grains of the juice (taken in a drink with posca [hot drinks])" as a bowel purge; the juice "rubbed on with woman's milk" for the eyes; the seed "taken in a drink"; and the method of collection, in which the milky juice "is first exposed to the sun (like other extracted juices) and stored in new ceramic jars." The one quantity, twenty grains, is the translators' rendering, and the weight of the "grain" in the Greek is not stated in the lines read. The chapter gives no dose for the sleep or pain uses, for the eye use or for the seed. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints the plant as "bitter to the taste", "thinner and sharper" than the garden lettuce, and "somewhat similar to poppy in properties". It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality for 2-166. (The words "a little cooling" belong to the cultivated lettuce, 2-165, a different chapter.)

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant (larger-stalked, paler-leaved, thinner and sharper than the garden lettuce, and bitter), how the juice is gathered (exposed to the sun, stored in new ceramic jars), and, through chapter 4-65, how its juice is told apart when it is passed off in opium (faint in smell and rougher). He records Magian and Zoroastrian names without comment, and recording is not endorsing. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a drug for the bowels, the eyes, sleep and pain. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a sleep-inducing, pain-easing and purging juice, with a single stated quantity, compared by Dioscorides to the poppy. The useful lesson is Dioscorides' method of recognising the plant, collecting the juice and detecting it as an adulterant, and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Dioscorides gives the juice as a purge, a sleep-inducer, a pain-easer and a drink that "draws out the menstrual flow", so none of these should be taken lightly or self-treated, and no safety information for pregnancy was read beyond that last statement. A sleep-inducing latex can impair alertness and judgment. Eye disease (albugo, dimness, burning) needs medical assessment, and a home preparation must not replace it. The legal status of lettuce latex and of any preparations made from it varies by place and was not researched. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion with four names, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
