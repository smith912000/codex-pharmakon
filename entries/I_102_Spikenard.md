## Section 1 — Nomenclature

### Primary Scientific Name
*Nardostachys jatamansi* (D.Don) DC. — Spikenard / Nard (Caprifoliaceae / formerly Valerianaceae)

### Botanical Identity
Perennial herb 10–60cm; distinctive matted fibrous rhizome crown (the "spike" of the name) that forms dense interlocking fibrous rootlets; pinkish-purple flowers in dense spikes; grows in alpine meadows of the Himalayas and Tibet at 3,000–5,000m altitude. The rhizome is the drug material — one of the most valued aromatics in antiquity, commanding prices comparable to gold by weight in the Roman imperial period.

**John 12:3**: "Then Mary took about a pint of pure nard, an expensive perfume; she poured it on Jesus's feet and wiped his feet with her hair." Judas objected that the perfume was worth 300 denarii (approximately a year's wages for a common labourer). The pharmacological identification is secure — this was *Nardostachys jatamansi*, the only plant whose price and aromatic properties match the biblical description.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Dioscorides / Greco-Roman (DMM)
Book I, Chapter 7: "Indian nard" — warming, drying in the 2nd degree. Applications: diuretic; emmenagogue; liver tonic; carminative; for oedema, jaundice, and nausea; as an aromatic in compound preparations. Dioscorides distinguishes several grades and origins. Roman physicians used it extensively; Galen prescribed it as a hepatic tonic. The Roman perfume *nardinum* was derived from spikenard root.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine / Ibn Sīnā (CM)
*Sunbul al-ṭīb* — warming in the 2nd degree, drying in the 2nd degree. Applications: hepatotonic (liver strengthener); diuretic; for palpitations (antiarrhythmic application); carminative; for kidney and bladder disorders. Ibn Sīnā specifically notes its action on the heart — one of the earliest recorded antiarrhythmic applications in the pre-modern corpus.

### 2.3 Sushruta Samhita (SS)
*Jāṭāmāṃsī* — one of the important nervine (brain and nervous system) herbs in Sushruta and Charaka's systems. Applications: for epilepsy (antiepileptic); hysteria; sleep disorders (sedative); hair growth (topical); skin complexion; fever. The nervine classification in Ayurveda is significant — spikenard is classified alongside Brahmi (Bacopa) and Shankhpushpi as a brain tonic.

### 2.4 Shennong / Bencao Gangmu (SBJ)
甘松 *Gānsōng* — carminative; for depression and stagnation of Qi in the Spleen-Stomach; abdominal pain and bloating; toothache (topical oil). The Chinese tradition does not emphasise the nervine applications as strongly but does use it in compound formulas for Qi stagnation.

### 2.5 Ebers Papyrus (EP)
Appears in compound preparations for headache and as an aromatic ingredient in medicinal fumigation and kyphi (sacred incense) formulations.

### 2.6 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)
*Lardu* (proposed identification with nard) appears in Assyrian plant medicine texts for fumigation and in compound preparations for headache and nervous disorders.

### 2.7 Four Medical Tantras (FMT)
*Spang spos* — one of the primary Tibetan medicinal aromatics; used for *rLung* disorders (anxiety, insomnia, nervous agitation in Tibetan disease classification); in compound formulas for heart disorders and mental diseases. Central to Tibetan compound formulas for anxiety and wind conditions.

### 2.8 Badianus Manuscript (BM)
Spikenard appears in several BM compound formulas, likely obtained through Spanish colonial trade rather than indigenous Mesoamerican identification — by 1552, European aromatics were traded into Mexico. Its presence reflects the early colonial pharmaceutical fusion.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 CNS Sedation / Anxiolytic (Primary — Jatamansone)

**Jatamansone**: Crosses the blood-brain barrier; mechanisms include:
- GABA-A receptor potentiation (benzodiazepine-like binding site interaction)
- Serotonin system modulation (5-HT₁A partial agonism — anxiolytic without full sedation)
- MAO-A inhibition (weak) → increased serotonin availability → antidepressant effect
- Reduction of corticosterone release → anti-stress effect on HPA axis

Animal studies: jatamansone at 200mg/kg shows anxiolytic effects comparable to diazepam 0.5mg/kg in elevated plus maze; sedative-hypnotic effects at higher doses. The nervine/sedative application in SS and FMT for anxiety, insomnia, and epilepsy is pharmacologically grounded.

### 5.2 Antiepileptic

**Jatamansone + valeranone**: Reduces seizure frequency in pentylenetetrazole-induced seizure models in animals. Mechanism: GABA enhancement (same as 5.1) + possible sodium channel modulation. The Ayurvedic antiepileptic application (*apasmāra* = epilepsy) and Tibetan application for severe nervous conditions have mechanistic support.

### 5.3 Cardioprotective / Antiarrhythmic

**Nardostachone + jatamansic acid**:
- Calcium channel antagonism in cardiac muscle → reduces heart rate
- Antioxidant protection of cardiomyocytes → reduced ischaemic injury
- In vitro: reduces arrhythmia in aconitine-induced cardiac arrhythmia model
- Ibn Sīnā's specific mention of its use for palpitations maps directly to the calcium-channel antiarrhythmic mechanism

### 5.4 Hepatoprotective

**Ursolic acid + β-sitosterol + jatamansic acid**:
- NF-κB inhibition in hepatocytes → reduced inflammatory hepatocyte damage
- Antioxidant → reduced lipid peroxidation in liver tissue
- Phase II enzyme induction → enhanced hepatic detoxification

The hepatotonic application in DMM and CM (liver strengthener, for jaundice) is pharmacologically coherent.

### 5.5 Neuroprotective / NGF-promoting

**Nardosinone**: Induces neurite outgrowth in PC12 cells (neural cell line) and promotes NGF secretion from astrocytes. This is an emerging mechanism relevant to neuroprotection and potentially to Alzheimer's/dementia prevention — not yet clinically translated but actively researched.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Anxiety/insomnia**: Small clinical studies in Ayurvedic medicine showing reduction in anxiety scores; animal data is strong. No large RCTs.

**Epilepsy**: Case series in Ayurvedic practice; animal models strongly positive. No RCTs in human epilepsy.

**Cardiac arrhythmia**: Animal data (rate reduction, anti-arrhythmic in models); no clinical trials.

**Hair loss (topical)**: Jatamansi oil is widely used in Ayurvedic practice for alopecia; one small RCT (n=30) showed improvement in hair density vs mineral oil control. Mechanistic basis: antifungal (Malassezia) + anti-inflammatory of scalp.

**Liver protection**: Animal studies (CCl₄-induced hepatotoxicity model — protective); no clinical trials for liver disease.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The anointing oil of antiquity**: Spikenard was the most expensive aromatic substance in the ancient world for approximately 1,000 years — from the Egyptian New Kingdom (EP) through the Roman Imperial period (DMM) to medieval Islamic medicine (CM). Its value was not purely aromatic but pharmacological: the sedative, anxiolytic, and cardiovascular properties made it a genuine therapeutic substance. The biblical account of Mary Magdalene anointing Christ's feet with a pound of pure nard worth 300 denarii is not merely symbolic — it describes a specific pharmacological preparation (topical nervine-cardiac-aromatic oil) applied in a context of extreme emotional distress, during the week before the crucifixion. The pharmacological properties of spikenard (anxiolytic, sedative, cardioprotective) and the context of application (grief, anticipatory fear) are congruent.

**8/9 tradition convergence**: Spikenard achieves 8/9 tradition convergence — the highest in the Codex alongside a very small number of substances (including saffron, myrrh, and frankincense). This convergence is not independent discovery but trade-route transmission — the plant was physically transported along the Silk Road and Indian Ocean trade networks, and its pharmacological properties were documented by each civilisation that received it. The convergence score is therefore both a pharmacological signal (consistent reported effects) and a historical record of ancient trade routes. No other 8/9 convergence substance in Volume I is found this high in altitude (3,000–5,000m) at its origin — the Himalayan source of spikenard shaped an entire hemisphere's aromatic and medical traditions.

**Celtic nard vs Indian nard**: Dioscorides mentions "Celtic nard" (*Valeriana celtica*, harvested from the Alps) as a European substitute for Indian nard. The two plants are pharmacologically related — both Valerianaceae/Caprifoliaceae, both containing sedative sesquiterpenes, both aromatic. The Celtic substitute was used when the Indian trade route was disrupted, and vice versa. This is one of the earliest documented cases of botanical drug substitution in the pharmacological literature — and the substitution was pharmacologically informed (same chemical family, similar biological action).

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Acrid, sweet; slightly warm. Channels: Heart (心), Liver (肝), Spleen (脾). Calms the Shen, moves Qi stagnation in Liver-Spleen axis, aromatic.

**Ayurvedic**: Bitter, sweet, astringent; heating. Reduces Vata ↓↓, Pitta ↓, increases Kapha mildly in excess. *Medhya* (brain tonic), *Nidrajanana* (sleep-inducing), *Hṛdaya* (cardiac tonic). Tridoshic balancing at standard doses. Combined with Brahmi (Bacopa, I_32) as the classic Ayurvedic nervine pair.

**Tibetan (FMT)**: Cold, sweet, astringent. Primarily pacifies *rLung* (wind/anxiety/nervous agitation). One of the primary *rLung* herbs alongside ashwagandha and valerian.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Pregnancy**: Emmenagogue properties — avoid in pregnancy (uterine stimulation possible)
- **Deep sedation interactions**: Additive effect with benzodiazepines, barbiturates, opioids, anaesthetic agents — reduce dose if co-administering; do not combine with other strong sedatives without supervision
- **MAO interaction**: Weak MAO-A inhibition — caution with MAOI antidepressants (theoretical interaction)
- **Driving / heavy machinery**: Sedative effect at higher doses — avoid if operating machinery
- **CITES/conservation**: Wild Himalayan populations under pressure; prefer cultivated sources; verify supplier
- **Not for children under 12**: Sedative potency in paediatric dose unpredictable
