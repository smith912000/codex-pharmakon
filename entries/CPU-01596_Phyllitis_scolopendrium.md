# Phyllitis scolopendrium — Emionitis, hemionitis, the mulewort (Dioscorides 3-152)

## *Phyllitis scolopendrium [Linnaeus], the last of several names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-152 (which also prints Hemionitis, Scolopendria, Lingua cervina [Fuchs], Lingua cervina officinarum [Bauhin] and Asplenium scolopendrium — "Hart's-tongue Fern", and, marked "other usage", Hemionitis [Bedevian] — "Mulewort, Hemionitis"); a fern with a horned leaf* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter, and no other tradition on disk is tagged for it. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line also gives the same Linnaean binomial that the translators give for a different chapter (3-121, see Overlap), and it offers a second reading under "other usage" (*Hemionitis*, "Mulewort") that the row does not take. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-152. EMIONITIS*, heading at line 7833, the SUGGESTED line at lines 7834 to 7835 and the text at line 7836, in Book Three among the roots (the running head near it reads "ROOTS of AKANTHODA or PRICKLY PLANTS"). The whole chapter reads:

"Hemionitis puts out a horned leaf similar to dracunculus [2-196b] (like the third-day moon). The many slender roots are underneath, but it bears no stalk, seed, or flower. It grows in rocky places. The herb is astringent to the taste and is taken as a drink with vinegar to reduce the spleen. It is also called splenium."

The description is by form: a single "horned" leaf, compared with *dracunculus* (2-196b) and, in a parenthesis, with "the third-day moon" (a crescent); "many slender roots" below; and a list of what it lacks: "no stalk, seed, or flower". The habitat is rocky places. The one quality is that the herb is "astringent to the taste". The one use is that it "is taken as a drink with vinegar to reduce the spleen". The one other name printed is *splenium*; the chapter explains neither that name nor the translators' English "Mulewort". The text gives no deity and no amuletic use.

**The chapters around it.** 3-153 *ANTHULLIS* (line 7837, kidney vetch in the translators' suggestion) follows and is a separate chapter.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter id 3-152 and for the names *hemionitis*, *emionitis*, *splenium* and *scolopendri* found one existing entry sharing the plant and not the chapter: I_1020 (*Asplenium scolopendrium*, Hart's-tongue fern) covers chapter 3-121 *PHULLITIS* (heading at line 7628), whose translators' line gives the same binomial *Phyllitis scolopendrium*; its Overlap paragraph names this spine row (CPU-01596) and the chapter 3-152 as a separate chapter. The two are therefore a **species-level overlap, not a chapter-level duplicate**: two Dioscorides chapters receive the same suggestion from the translators, and the Codex does not merge them. I_1019 (*Asplenium adiantium-nigrum*) prints "splenium" among its names and mentions 3-152 as a nearby fern chapter; I_892 (*Common Honeysuckle*) prints "splenium" in its own list of names. Neither is a duplicate. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The composition of the fern Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about a particular species. As a general fact, ferns reproduce by spores and not by seed or flower, which agrees with the chapter's "no stalk, seed, or flower"; no constituent is named in the chapter. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records one use: the astringent herb drunk with vinegar to reduce the spleen. The Codex gives no protocol for it. An enlarged spleen is a sign of disease that needs medical assessment, and the identification is a translators' suggestion shared with another chapter.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation, with locus (line 7836): the herb "taken as a drink with vinegar". No dose, quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that an enlarged spleen is a medical finding with many possible causes and that no herb drunk in vinegar treats it. The identification is a translators' suggestion, so the chapter does not show that the plant it describes is *Phyllitis scolopendrium*.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one quality: "The herb is astringent to the taste". It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality for 3-152.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers recognition detail only: the horned leaf, the slender roots, the absence of stalk, seed and flower, the rocky habitat and one taste. It names no season of gathering, no assay for quality and no adulteration. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a medicinal herb, and the text records no amuletic use. No cultivation framing is attested.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this fern. The one useful habit it shows is naming a plant by what it lacks as well as by what it has. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

An enlarged spleen needs medical care, not a fern drunk in vinegar. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion that is shared with a second chapter, and the "other usage" reading names a different plant, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
