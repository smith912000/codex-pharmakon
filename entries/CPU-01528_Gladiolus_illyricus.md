# Gladiolus illyricus — Xiphion (Dioscorides 4-20; also phasganon, machaeronion, anactorion, arion; Roman gladiolus, genitalis)

## *Gladiolus illyricus (the translators' first suggestion: "Gladiolus illyricus — Gladiole, Sword Lily"; the same line adds "Gladiolus communis, Gladiolus byzantinus — Sword Lily")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk names this plant. The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The translators offer three sword-lily species on one line, and the entry takes the first as its title. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-20. XIPHION* at line 8183, SUGGESTED line at 8184, text at line 8185 and, after a page break (page number 563 and a running head), line 8189. The chapter stands in Book Four, "Other Herbs & Roots", after 4-19 *EPIMEDION* (read in I_1077) and before 4-21 *SPARGANION*, both different plants. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 600.

*Description.* The chapter says that xiphion "is called phasganon because the shape of the leaf is similar to that of iris, yet smaller, narrower, pointed like a little sword, and fibrous". It "sends forth a stalk a foot long, on which are purple flowers distant from one another by steps, round seed, and two roots — one of them resting on the other — similar to little scallions. That which lies underneath is slender but that above, fuller. It grows (especially) in fields."

*Uses, by root.* The two roots are given different powers. "The root that is on top (applied with wine and frankincense) is able to draw out prickles and splinters, and (with meal of lolium [2-116, 4-140] and honey water) to dissolve the pannus [opaque thickening of cornea with veins]. It is mixed with similar plasters, and used for a pessary it draws out the menstrual flow." Then: "They say that a decoction of the upper root (taken as a drink with wine) encourages sexual intercourse [aphrodisiac]; but that the lowest make them without lust [anaphrodisiac], and that the upper root is effective given to children that are broken [? foreskin or hymen] in a liquid medicine with water." The bracketed glosses are the translators'. The "they say" marks the aphrodisiac and anaphrodisiac claims as reported, not as Dioscorides' own testimony.

*Names.* "It is also called machaeronion, anactorion, or arion; the Romans call it gladiolus, and some, genitalis."

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records external uses (drawing out splinters, a plaster for the cornea) and a pessary to bring on the menstrual flow, together with reported claims about arousal and its opposite. The Codex gives no protocol for any of these. The identity is a translators' suggestion, and an eye application and a pessary are not things to improvise from a translated herbal.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations: the upper root applied with wine and frankincense; the upper root with "meal of lolium and honey water"; the root mixed into plasters; the root as a pessary; a decoction of the upper root drunk with wine; the upper root given to children in "a liquid medicine with water". No dose, quantity or duration is printed for any of them. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 4-20 prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality and no taste. The only qualities printed describe the form of the plant (leaf "fibrous", one root "slender", the other "fuller").

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is close description for recognition (a sword-shaped leaf, flowers set apart "by steps", two roots one on the other) and a rule that the two roots are not interchangeable: the upper and the lower are named for different effects. That is a lesson in using the right part of a plant, not a cultivation status. The aphrodisiac and anaphrodisiac claims are reported by the text ("they say"), which is recording, not endorsing. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is the habit of telling the parts of a plant apart and not treating them as one drug. The text offers nothing more, and the abstentive answer is the right one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 4-20 prints no warning. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. A pessary that "draws out the menstrual flow" is an abortifacient-class claim and must not be used in pregnancy or as a home remedy. Nothing should be applied to the eye on the strength of this entry. The children's use is recorded without a dose and is not advice. [USER: verify]
