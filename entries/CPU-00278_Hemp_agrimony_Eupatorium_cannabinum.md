# Hemp Agrimony — Holy Rope / Eupatoire / Wasserdost / Sweet-smelling Trefoil

## *Eupatorium cannabinum* L. — the riverbank immune-fire that must be handled with care

### Origin & Tradition

*Eupatorium cannabinum* is a tall, downy perennial of damp ground — riverbanks, ditches, wet woodland margins — across Europe, North Africa, and temperate Asia. Its three- to five-lobed leaves resemble those of true hemp (*Cannabis sativa*), which gives the species epithet *cannabinum* and the English name "hemp agrimony," though the two plants are botanically unrelated. The dense flat-topped clusters of dull-pink florets are a magnet for late-summer butterflies, and the whole plant carries a faintly bitter, aromatic scent.

The genus name *Eupatorium* preserves a classical attribution: it honours Mithridates VI Eupator, king of Pontus (1st century BCE), the ruler famed in antiquity for his study of poisons and antidotes. The plant was understood in the Greco-Roman world as a liver and wound herb, and the name carried that prestige forward into the European herbal tradition.

**Greco-Roman / Dioscorides Tradition (DMM):**
The classical *eupatorion* of Dioscorides and Pliny is most reliably identified with *Agrimonia* (true agrimony) rather than with this plant, and the medieval and early-modern transfer of the name onto *Eupatorium cannabinum* is a documented case of nomenclatural drift. [USER: verify which species Dioscorides' "eupatorion" denotes — classical sources are debated; do not assert *E. cannabinum* as the Dioscoridean plant without checking the manuscript identification.] What is securely attested is the later European tradition: hemp agrimony was used as a warming diaphoretic to "break" fevers by promoting sweat, as a bitter to stimulate the liver and bowels, and externally on slow-healing wounds and ulcers.

**Western Botanical / Folk Medicine (BM):**
By the early-modern herbals the plant had a settled reputation. It appears in the European materia medica as a remedy for influenza-type febrile illness, taken hot to induce diaphoresis, and as a spring "blood-cleansing" bitter. Folk use extended to jaundice and sluggish digestion, consistent with a cholagogue-bitter action. In the 19th and 20th centuries the plant attracted renewed interest because related *Eupatorium* species (notably *E. perfoliatum*, boneset, of North America) were prominent febrifuges — and standardised European preparations of *E. cannabinum* were investigated for immunostimulant activity. That immunostimulant reputation, however, is inseparable from a serious safety problem (see Contraindications): the plant contains hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids, and modern herbal practice in regulated markets restricts or prohibits internal use for this reason.

### Active Components

- **Pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs)** — including echinatine, lycopsamine, and related 1,2-unsaturated PAs. These are the defining safety liability of the plant: 1,2-unsaturated PAs are metabolised in the liver to reactive pyrrole esters that alkylate cellular proteins and DNA, producing hepatic sinusoidal obstruction (veno-occlusive disease) on cumulative or high-dose exposure, and are genotoxic/carcinogenic in animal models. [USER: verify the specific PA profile and concentrations for *E. cannabinum* against a phytochemical source before citing exact alkaloid names.]

- **Sesquiterpene lactones** — including eupatoriopicrin, a cytotoxic germacranolide-type lactone documented in the genus and associated with the plant's bitter taste and part of its investigated anti-proliferative and immunomodulatory activity.

- **Polysaccharides** — high-molecular-weight immunostimulant polysaccharide fractions have been studied from *Eupatorium* species and are the basis of the "immunostimulant" framing of standardised preparations.

- **Flavonoids** — including quercetin and kaempferol glycosides (e.g. eupatorin-type flavones reported in the genus); antioxidant and mild anti-inflammatory contribution.

- **Essential oil** — a small volatile fraction of mono- and sesquiterpenes giving the aromatic-bitter character and contributing to the diaphoretic action.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Primary domain: acute febrile-immune support and Damp-stagnation clearance — strictly a `[SPECIALIST ONLY]` agent because of pyrrolizidine-alkaloid hepatotoxicity.**

The traditional functional signature of hemp agrimony is twofold: a **warming diaphoretic** that drives a fever through its sweating phase, and a **bitter cholagogue** that moves a congested liver and bowel. In a Kenetica framing these map onto the acute immune-activation window — the early phase of a self-limiting upper-respiratory or influenza-type illness where promoting controlled diaphoresis and supporting hepatic clearance is the classical aim.

The investigated immunostimulant polysaccharide and sesquiterpene-lactone fractions give a mechanistic plausibility to the febrifuge reputation. However, this entry deliberately does **not** position hemp agrimony as a maintenance or recovery herb. The pyrrolizidine-alkaloid content makes any repeated or prolonged internal use unacceptable on a risk basis: the relevant toxicity is cumulative and hepatic, and there is no training context in which the marginal immune benefit justifies cumulative PA exposure. Where the immunostimulant action is genuinely desired, PA-free alternatives (e.g. *Echinacea* preparations, *Sambucus nigra*) achieve the same functional goal without the hepatotoxic burden.

Topical use on wounds and ulcers — the other historical application — avoids the systemic first-pass hepatic activation of PAs to a degree, but PAs are absorbed across broken skin, so even topical use on open lesions is not risk-free.

The honest Kenetica position: a herb of historical and pharmacological interest, retained in the codex for completeness, but flagged at the highest caution tier and not recommended for internal protocol use.

### Preparation & Dosing

**Safety-first framing:** Because of pyrrolizidine-alkaloid hepatotoxicity, no internal dose of *E. cannabinum* can be presented as routinely safe. The figures below record the *traditional* preparation pattern for documentary completeness only; they are not a recommendation for use.

| Form | Traditional pattern (documentary only) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hot infusion of dried herb | Traditionally taken hot as a diaphoretic | PA exposure — not recommended internally |
| Tincture | Historically used as a bitter/cholagogue | Ethanol extracts PAs efficiently — higher risk |
| Topical wash / poultice | Fresh or dried herb on wounds | PAs absorbed across broken skin — avoid on open lesions |

**Preparation principles (documentary):**
- Diaphoretic intent requires the infusion taken hot, in a warm environment, to carry the fever through its sweating phase — the classical "drive the fever out" method.
- Decoction vs. infusion makes little difference to PA content; PAs are not destroyed by domestic heat or by drying.

**The operative dosing guidance for Kenetica is: do not use internally.** Where the diaphoretic-immune action is wanted, substitute a PA-free diaphoretic. Retain hemp agrimony as a reference entry, not a working preparation.

### Scientific Correlation

**Pyrrolizidine-alkaloid hepatotoxicity (the governing safety fact):**
The mechanism of 1,2-unsaturated pyrrolizidine-alkaloid toxicity — hepatic metabolic activation to reactive pyrroles, protein/DNA alkylation, and hepatic sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (veno-occlusive disease) — is well established in the toxicological literature. The European Food Safety Authority Panel on Contaminants in the Food Chain has published risk assessments concluding that 1,2-unsaturated PAs are genotoxic and carcinogenic and that dietary/herbal exposure should be minimised (EFSA CONTAM Panel, *EFSA Journal*, 2011; updated 2017). ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Primary Tier:** ◉ Qi — a surface-releasing diaphoretic that moves Wei Qi outward to resolve early-stage febrile illness and disperses Damp-stagnation from the riverbank-damp constitution the plant itself favours.

**Western/Galenic Classification:** Warming and drying in quality; a bitter diaphoretic and cholagogue — the warming-sudorific profile shared with other classical febrifuges. Carries the Galenic "liver herb" attribution through the *Eupatorium* name.

**Functional actions (traditional Western):**
- Diaphoretic — promotes sweat to resolve fever
- Cholagogue / hepatic bitter — stimulates bile flow and digestion
- Vulnerary (topical) — traditional wound and ulcer application
- Mild laxative / depurative — the "blood-cleansing" spring-bitter role

**Note on energetic tier:** This entry is deliberately *not* assigned a Jing/constitutional tier. A hepatotoxic herb cannot be framed as a deep-restorative (Jing) agent — its action is acute and surface, and its cumulative cost is borne by the very organ (the liver) that a Jing-tonic framing would claim to nourish.

### Contraindications

**⛔ Pyrrolizidine-alkaloid hepatotoxicity (overriding contraindication):** *Eupatorium cannabinum* contains 1,2-unsaturated pyrrolizidine alkaloids that are hepatotoxic, genotoxic, and carcinogenic. Internal use carries a risk of hepatic veno-occlusive disease on cumulative or high-dose exposure. Internal use is **contraindicated** as a routine practice and is restricted or prohibited for sale in several regulated markets. This contraindication overrides all traditional indications.

**Pregnancy and lactation:** Absolutely contraindicated. PAs cross the placenta and are excreted in breast milk; transplacental and lactational PA hepatotoxicity in the fetus/infant is documented for PA-containing plants as a class.

**Pre-existing liver disease:** Absolutely contraindicated. Any compromise of hepatic function magnifies PA toxicity.

**Concomitant hepatotoxic drugs or alcohol:** Avoid — additive hepatic burden.

**Open wounds (topical use):** PAs are absorbed across broken skin; topical application to ulcers or open lesions is not free of systemic risk despite the historical wound-herb reputation.

**Asteraceae (Compositae) family allergy:** As a composite, the sesquiterpene-lactone fraction can provoke contact dermatitis and cross-reactivity in individuals sensitised to other Asteraceae (chamomile, feverfew, ragweed).
