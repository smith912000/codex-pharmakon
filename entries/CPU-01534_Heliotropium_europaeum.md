### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk names this plant. The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-193. ELIOTROPION MEGA* at line 9742, SUGGESTED line at 9743, text at line 9744. The chapter stands in Book Four, "Other Herbs & Roots", after 4-192 *KUNOKRAMBE* and before 4-194 *ELIOTROPION MIKRON* (the little heliotropium, the subject of the sister entry I_1097). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 771.

*Name and description.* "The great heliotropium is named from the leaves turning around towards the setting of the sun. It has leaves like basil but rougher, darker, and bigger; and (as it were) three or four or five shoots from the root, and on these many wings. On the tops are white flowers inclining to a purple, winding around like the tail of a scorpion. The root is thin and of no use."

*Uses.* "Boiled with water and taken as a drink as much as a handful of this expels phlegm and bile through the bowels. A decoction (taken as a drink with wine and also smeared on) is good for those touched by scorpions. It is hanged around one to cause barrenness. And they say that a decoction of four grains of the seed (taken as a drink with wine one hour before the time of the fit) cures quartains; and three, tertians [recurrent paroxysmic fevers]. The seed is smeared on to dry up protruding warts, hanging warts, warty abnormal growths, and pustules appearing at night. The leaves are usefully smeared on for gout, dislocations, and children that have siriasis [sunstroke]; pounded into small pieces and applied, they induce the movement of the menstrual flow, and are abortifacient."

*Names.* "From the form of the flowers some have called it scorpiuron, heliotropos, dialion, heliopun, scorpioctonon, sesamon sylvestre, or scorpij cauda."

The chapter gives a name explained by the plant's behaviour, a description, a root declared useless, a series of uses and preparations and seven further names. The bracketed glosses are the translators'. The "they say" in front of the fever remedy marks that claim as reported. The amuletic use ("hanged around one to cause barrenness") is recorded in the same list as the drinks and smears, without comment from the author.

### Active Components

No analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. The genus *Heliotropium* is generally known to contain pyrrolizidine alkaloids, which are toxic to the liver, but whether the plant of this chapter is *Heliotropium europaeum* is only the translators' suggestion. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a purgative drink, a remedy for scorpion stings, an amulet against conception, fever cures by dose and hour, wart and pustule smears, and leaf applications for gout, dislocations, sunstroke in children, the menstrual flow and abortion. The Codex gives no protocol for any of these. The identity is a translators' suggestion, and a plant of a genus generally known to contain liver-toxic alkaloids, said by the text to purge and to be abortifacient, is hazardous.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations: the plant "as much as a handful", boiled with water and drunk; a decoction drunk with wine and smeared on; the plant hung around the person; a decoction of the seed "four grains" in wine "one hour before the time of the fit" for quartains and "three" for tertians; the seed smeared on; the leaves smeared on or pounded and applied. The quantities the text prints are "as much as a handful", "four grains" and, for tertians, "three" (the unit is left unstated in that clause), with the timing "one hour before the time of the fit". They are the text's own, quoted, and are not recommendations. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a name taken from observed behaviour (the leaves turning toward the setting sun), a close description for recognition (leaves like basil but rougher and darker, several shoots, white flowers inclining to purple, winding "like the tail of a scorpion") and a clear judgement on the root ("of no use"). The author also records that the plant is hung around a person "to cause barrenness"; that is a recorded use, and the text neither endorses it nor distances itself from it. It is not a cultivation status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable points are the habit of naming a plant by what it does, of saying which part is useless, and of keeping a reported cure ("they say") apart from a stated one. The text offers nothing more, and the abstentive answer is the right one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 4-193 prints no general warning, but it records that the leaves "induce the movement of the menstrual flow, and are abortifacient", and that a handful boiled and drunk "expels phlegm and bile through the bowels". The plant must not be used in pregnancy. Plants of the genus *Heliotropium* are generally known to contain liver-toxic alkaloids. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Scorpion stings, fevers and sunstroke in children need medical care. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
