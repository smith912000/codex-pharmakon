# Jade — Yu Quan (玉泉) "Nephritum" (Yang 1998); other name Yu Zha (Jade Sweet Wine)
## *Jade in the Divine Farmer's Classic; Yang 1998 prints "Nephritum" (nephrite) for Yu Quan, "jade spring" in the literal sense, and his own footnote suspects the name is a misprint for Yu Xue, "Jade Dust", so the substance named is the translator's reading and is not settled* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** The entry stands in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`) on PDF page 21, the first entry of Book Two, under the heading "Jades and Stones: Superior Class" (line 836). The names row is `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv` row 2, line 837, "Yu Quan", translator identification "Nephritum"; the project list `_sources/shennong.csv` has it as 玉泉. The full text (lines 837-843, 866-868): "Yu Quan (Nephritum) is sweet and balanced. It mainly treats hundreds of diseases of the five viscera. It limbers the sinews and strengthens the bones, quiets the ethereal and corporeal souls, promotes the growth of the muscles and flesh, and boosts the qi. Protracted taking may cultivate endurance to cold and summerheat and make one free from hunger and thirst to become a non-aging immortal. If one takes five catties [i.e., 500g] of it when dying, one's complexion will remain unchanged for three years after death. Its other name is Yu Zha (Jade Sweet Wine)." No place of production is printed for this entry; most neighbouring entries close with "It is produced in mountains and valleys". Yang's footnote 32 (lines 844-847) says: "Yu Quan literally means jade spring. There is reason to suspect that this is a typographical error and that this should read Yu Xue (Jade Dust). It is said that, in olden times, there was the practice of grinding Jade into powder and then taking it to keep fit and prevent disease." Footnote 33 (lines 859-863) adds: "It is said that if one has reached a certain level in the Dao through self-cultivation or by taking certain medicinals, one may live for long periods of time without eating and drinking without suffering any deleterious effect." The spine's note is "high", meaning high confidence in the list row, and not in the identity of the stone: what the Chinese name designates is Yang's reading and his own footnote marks it as uncertain.

### Active Components

Jade is the name for two different silicate minerals. Nephrite is a calcium, magnesium and iron silicate of the amphibole group (the tremolite-actinolite series), and jadeite is a sodium aluminium silicate of the pyroxene group. Both are very hard, tough and chemically inert, and are essentially insoluble in water and gastric acid. Which of these, if either, is meant by Yu Quan is not given by the sources read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The classic records a stone taken for the five viscera, the sinews and bones, the muscles and the qi, and for the Daoist aims of enduring cold and heat and doing without food. These are the aims of the elixir tradition that Yang's introduction describes (lines 312-328), and the Codex does not endorse them. The Codex offers no protocol, dose or preparation, and a ground silicate stone is not a recommended thing to swallow.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only quantity printed is the "five catties [i.e., 500g]" taken "when dying" (lines 842, 866), which is a funerary statement and not a dose. Yang's footnote 32 (line 846) mentions the practice of "grinding Jade into powder and then taking it", as something "said" of olden times, with no source. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Scientific Correlation

Jade is insoluble and chemically inert, so little of it would be absorbed from the gut, and powdered stone can scratch or lodge in the gut. Jade has been prized as an ornamental and ritual stone in China for millennia, but no medical benefit of swallowing it is established. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- SBJ: "sweet and balanced" (Yang line 837). Class: Superior (heading line 836). Yang's footnote 7 (lines 356-358) gives the sense of the class: "nontoxic medicinals that are able to nurture life and therefore bestow longevity". The footnote on flavour and qi (lines 849-857) explains that "level or balanced" means neither cold nor hot, neither warm nor cool.

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: an upper-grade drug (上品).* Yu Quan stands under "Jades and Stones: Superior Class" (Yang line 836). Yang's preface says the superior class medicinals "mainly nourish life and correspond to heaven. They are nontoxic and taking them in large amounts and for a long time will not harm people. If one intends to make one's body light, boost the qi, prevent aging, and prolong life, one should base [one's efforts] on the superior class" (lines 352-358). The monograph itself names the cultivation aims in the tradition's own words: the stone "quiets the ethereal and corporeal souls", "boosts the qi", and "protracted taking may cultivate endurance to cold and summerheat and make one free from hunger and thirst to become a non-aging immortal" (lines 837-842). The text prints no *jing* or *shen* claim for it, and none is made here. Yang's introduction (lines 312-328) places the work in the Daoist elixir tradition and warns that "many Chinese, including emperors and even famous doctors ... made themselves ill and even cut off their lives prematurely by taking such external elixirs compounded out of potentially toxic materials". Yang's note 5-6 on the preface (lines 373-377, 398-400) glosses the aim as "immortality" and "extraordinary powers". The grade is a statement of the Classic as translated by Yang in 1998, from a work he places in the late Han (line 891); later materia medica works are not used here. The identity of the stone is the translator's reading and is doubted in his own footnote 32.

**Practitioner's note.** An upper-grade drug in the Chinese scheme, which means it was held to be safe for long use by the Classic's own standard. That standard is Han-period Daoist, the substance named may be a misprint, and a ground stone has no established benefit. Nothing here supports taking jade; the cultivator's honest use of the entry is as a record of how the elixir tradition understood "nourishing life". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Contraindications

Powdered stone can injure the gut, and ground "jade" sold as a remedy may be a different mineral, a dyed stone or a contaminated material. The Classic's "non-aging immortal" and "complexion unchanged after death" claims are not supported and should not lead anyone to forgo medical care. Purchase of mineral medicines is regulated in many places. Jade artefacts may be subject to antiquities or export controls.
