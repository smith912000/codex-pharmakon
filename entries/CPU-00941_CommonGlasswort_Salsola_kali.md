# Common Glasswort — Prickly Saltwort · Russian Thistle · Ushnan (اشنان) · Kali · Ἅλς-plant of the shore
## *Salsola kali* L. (= *Kali turgidum* (Dumort.) Guterm.) | Family: Amaranthaceae (Chenopodioideae; formerly Chenopodiaceae)

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM, EP
**Energetic Classification:** Hot in the second degree, Dry in the second-to-third degree (Galenic/Unani); saline-abstergent (*jālī*, cleansing), aperient, resolvent
**Training Phase:** Phase II — Purification & Clearing (external and eliminative work; not a deep-tissue tonic)

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Salsola kali* is a spiny annual halophyte of coasts, salt flats and disturbed steppe from the Atlantic to Central Asia. Its cultural career is unusual: it is one of the very few plants whose medical identity and whose industrial identity are the *same fact*. Burn the plant and it yields a hard grey ash rich in sodium carbonate — the *soda ash*, *barilla*, *natron*-substitute, or Arabic *qalī* from which the word **alkali** descends. That ash made glass and it made soap. Every tradition below is, in effect, commenting on the same chemistry from a different doctrinal angle: this is a plant that *dissolves what is stuck*.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (AT).** The cuneiform medical corpus of Nineveh and the older Babylonian recipe tablets prescribe plant-ash lyes and "salt-plant" washes for cleansing sores, treating scalp and skin eruptions, and as caustic-detergent components of poultices, alongside their well-attested use of alkaline salts in washing. Attribution to a modern binomial is imprecise — Akkadian plant names remain partly unresolved — so *S. kali* is best read as a member of the Mesopotamian "salt-plant / ash-plant" class rather than a securely identified single taxon. **Dioscorides (DMM).** *De materia medica* treats the maritime saltworts and the related *halimon*/*kali*-type shore plants as saline, drying and abstergent: applied to skin lesions and used internally, in small quantity, as a mover of the bowels and of urine. The Greek doctrine here is straightforward — sharp saline drugs *bite* and *scour* (ῥυπτικόν, cleansing), and they open obstructions.

**Avicenna / Unani (SBJ).** In the Arabic-Persian tradition the plant sits under **ushnān** (اشنان) — the "washing-herb," the classical soap-plant of the bathhouse and the launderer, and simultaneously a drug. The *Qanun*'s pharmacological grammar classes such drugs as *jālī* (detergent/abstergent) and *muḥallil* (resolvent, dispersing gathered matter). Ushnān appears in dentifrices and depilatory/cleansing pastes, in washes for chronic skin disease, and internally as a *mus·hil* (purgative) and diuretic. Its heat and dryness are said to cut *balgham* (phlegm) — thick, cold, viscid matter — and its salinity to prevent putrefaction.

**Egyptian (EP).** The Ebers Papyrus corpus is saturated with natron and salt-based washes, enemas, mouth-rinses and wound cleansers; the Egyptians also worked glass and made cleansing pastes from alkali plus fat. Whether the Ebers scribes distinguished plant-ash alkali from mineral natron is not something the text lets us settle, and no verse can be quoted here as naming *S. kali*. Read the Egyptian tag as *doctrinal continuity* — the same alkali-as-purifier logic — not as species identification.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.**
The Galenic-Unani reading of ushnān as *hot, dry, abstergent, phlegm-cutting* is not metaphor draped over an inert weed; it is an accurate qualitative description of what alkali chemistry does to organic matter.

- **"Cleansing" (jālī) = saponification.** The plant's ash is dominated by **sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃)** with potassium carbonate, chloride and sulfate. Sodium carbonate plus fat is soap. The doctrine that ushnān "removes greasy, sticky, filthy matter" is a literal statement of alkaline hydrolysis of triglycerides into fatty-acid salts — the emulsification of *exactly* the greasy residue the doctrine names.
- **"Cutting phlegm" = mucolysis by pH.** Humoral *balgham* is cold, thick, viscid, adhesive. Alkaline solutions raise pH, deprotonate mucin glycoproteins and disrupt their hydrogen-bonded gel network — thinning viscous secretions. The tradition assigned the plant the exact action that carbonate chemistry performs on mucus.
- **"Hot and dry" = caustic and hygroscopic.** Concentrated plant-ash lye is genuinely caustic (it saponifies skin lipids and denatures protein — hence the burning, "hot" sensation) and its salts are strongly hygroscopic (hence "drying"). A humoral degree here is an empirical potency scale.
- **The glass signature.** The same soda that scours the body fuses sand into a transparent solid. Symbolically the plant is read in the Kenetica corpus as an agent of *clarification*: it takes an opaque, particulate, obstructed medium and renders it clear. That is a doctrinal claim, and it is offered as doctrine — not as pharmacology.

### Active Components

- **Ash fraction (the historically load-bearing part):** sodium carbonate, potassium carbonate, sodium chloride and sulfate; the "barilla" soda of glass and soap manufacture.
- **Whole-plant/aerial parts:** high **sodium** and mineral salt content (obligate halophyte accumulation); **oxalates** (a general chenopod/amaranth-family feature); flavonoids and phenolic acids typical of Amaranthaceae; small quantities of alkaloids reported in the *Salsola* genus (notably in other species, e.g. *S. richteri*-group ephedrine-type alkaloids — do **not** transfer those claims to *S. kali*).
- **Pollen allergens:** **Sal k 1** (a pectin methylesterase) and **Sal k 4** (profilin), among Sal k 1–6.

*Honest note:* the pharmacology of *S. kali* proper is thin. The best-characterised modern chemistry of this plant is its **allergen** chemistry, not its therapeutic chemistry.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

An **external clearing agent**, not an internal tonic. In Kenetica *S. kali* is the type-specimen of the *abstergent* class: preparations of the ash (traditionally, as lye or as ash-soap) belong to washing, scouring and surface-clearing work — dental/oral cleansing pastes, washes for greasy or crusted skin, hard-water and residue removal in the preparation of other medicines. Internal use is a **historical** indication (purgative, diuretic) that the Codex records but does not endorse. It carries the symbolic role of *the clarifier*: the substance that renders a clouded medium legible.

### Scientific Correlation

Modern literature on this plant is dominated by aerobiology and allergy, and this is where the honest evidence sits.

- Peterkova et al. (2024), *Frontiers in Immunology*, characterised the recombinant *Salsola kali* allergens Sal k 1–6 with sera from clinically characterised saltwort-allergic patients: **Sal k 1** and the cytoskeletal **profilin** were the dominant IgE-binding molecules (recognised by 64% and 49% of patients respectively), and profilin-monosensitised patients showed respiratory allergy to saltwort. This establishes *S. kali* as a *bona fide* major pollen allergen source in southern Europe, Turkey, Central Asia and Iran. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fimmu.2024.1379833)
- Shokouhi Shoormasti et al. (2022), *Clinical and Experimental Allergy*, using a 244-allergen nano-bead array in allergic adults, reported *Salsola kali* extract sensitisation in **47.2%** of patients in the earlier phase of that work, with **Sal k 1** among the leading component-level sensitisers. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/cea.14113)
- No controlled human study retrieved for the traditional diuretic, purgative or dermatological indications of *S. kali*.

**Evidence grade — honest:** **Traditional / Historical (industrial chemistry: firmly established; therapeutic pharmacology: unvalidated).** The alkali chemistry is not in doubt and it directly explains the cleansing doctrine. The *clinical* claims — diuresis, purgation, skin cure — have **no** modern controlled evidence retrieved. The single best-evidenced modern fact about this plant is that it is a **major allergen**, which is a caution, not an indication. Grade: *traditional*, with a **safety-relevant modern literature**.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani (attested):** *Ḥārr* (hot) 2°, *yābis* (dry) 2°–3°. Actions: *jālī* (abstergent/detergent), *muḥallil* (resolvent), *mudirr al-bawl* (diuretic), *mus·hil* (purgative). Temperamentally indicated against *balgham* (phlegm) — cold, moist, viscid excess.
- **Greek (Dioscoridean):** saline, drying, biting (δριμύ), abstergent; opener of obstruction.
- **TCM:** not attested — *S. kali* is not a *Bencao* drug; no Chinese energetic assignment is offered here.
- **Ayurveda:** not attested for this species. (Chenopod/saline drugs exist in the *kshara* — plant-ash alkali — class of Ayurvedic surgery, and the *kshara* doctrine of "caustic that cuts and cleanses" is the same alkali logic; but *S. kali* itself is not the classical *kshara* source, so no rasa/guna/virya/vipaka is assigned.)

### Contraindications

- **⚠️ POLLEN ALLERGEN — MAJOR.** *Salsola kali* pollen is a leading cause of seasonal rhinitis, conjunctivitis and asthma across the Mediterranean, Turkey, Central Asia and Iran (Sal k 1; profilin/Sal k 4). Handling, drying or burning the flowering plant may provoke severe respiratory symptoms in sensitised individuals. **Profilin cross-reactivity** means patients sensitised to grass profilin (Phl p 12) may react without prior saltwort exposure.
- **⚠️ HIGH SODIUM.** As an obligate halophyte the plant accumulates sodium salts. Contraindicated in hypertension, heart failure, renal impairment, ascites, and any sodium-restricted diet.
- **⚠️ OXALATE.** Family-typical oxalate content: avoid in calcium-oxalate nephrolithiasis, hyperoxaluria, gout, and chronic kidney disease.
- **⚠️ ASH / LYE IS CAUSTIC.** The alkali ash and its lye saponify skin lipids and can cause chemical burns and permanent ocular injury. Never ingest ash or lye. Never apply undiluted. Eye exposure is a medical emergency.
- **Purgative/diuretic use:** no validated dose. Risk of electrolyte disturbance, dehydration, and interaction with diuretics, lithium, and digoxin. Not for use in pregnancy, lactation, or children.
- **Do not extrapolate** alkaloid pharmacology (ephedrine-type) from other *Salsola* species to *S. kali*.
