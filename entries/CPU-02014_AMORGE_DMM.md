# AMORGE — Amorge, Amurca (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 1-134)

## *Amurca, the sediment (lees) of pressed olive oil, boiled down in a brass jar. The translator's gloss reads "Amurca — Sediment of Olive Oil"; the olive tree (Olea europaea) is the source by general knowledge, and the chapter itself speaks of "unripe olive oil" in its last lines; confidence medium to high. It is a by-product, not a plant part, and is filed here because the spine lists it in the plant volume* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk. This is a single-text entry.** The name was searched in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`: it occurs in chapter 1-134 (line 4514) and in the two indexes (lines 11982 and 14647, "Amurca 134"). The other texts on disk were not searched; no other name is known for it in the sources read.

**Dioscorides (DMM).** Chapter 1-134, AMORGE, in Book One, after the chapters on lycium (1-132, line 4494) and acacia (1-133, line 4504) (lines 4514 to 4520). The full chapter reads, in the translation on disk: "Amurca is the sediment of oil which has been pressed out and boiled in a jar made of Cyprian brass until it is the consistency of honey. It is therapeutic for the bowels, and rubbed on with wine, vinegar, or honeyed wine is as effective as lycium [1-132] for toothaches and wounds. It is mixed with medicines that are good for the eyes and for closing pores. Growing old it becomes better. Taken as an infusion it is good for the perineum, the genitals, and ulcerated vulvas. It extracts spoiled teeth, boiled to the consistency of honey with unripe olive oil and smeared around them. It heals scabs on beasts [veterinary] rubbed on with a decoction of lupins [2-132] and chamaeleon [3-10, 3-11]. Used without boiling and new in a warm pack it assists those troubled with gout in their feet and joints. Put onto a fleece and applied on those who have dropsy it represses the swelling."

The chapter gives no alias, no habitat and no gathering season. The comparison with lycium (1-132) is the text's only yardstick for the strength of the drug.

### Active Components

The chapter names no constituent. In general terms, the sediment left when olive oil is pressed is the watery and solid residue of the fruit, with some remaining oil. The olive fruit is known to carry phenolic compounds and the residue is not a pure substance. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Looseness of the bowels (taken), toothache and wounds (rubbed on), a constituent of eye medicines and of pore-closing medicines, ulcerated vulvas (infusion), rotten teeth (boiled with unripe oil), gout of the feet and joints (new, in a warm pack), and the swelling of dropsy (on a fleece).
- **What the text does not say.** No dose and no quantity of any kind, and no amount of wine, vinegar or honeyed wine.

The Codex does not translate the text's terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend this material.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these preparations, all in line 4516 ff.: (1) the sediment of pressed oil boiled in a Cyprian brass jar to the consistency of honey; (2) that boiled sediment rubbed on with wine, vinegar or honeyed wine; (3) the boiled sediment mixed into eye and pore-closing medicines; (4) an infusion; (5) a boiling with unripe olive oil to the consistency of honey, smeared around a tooth; (6) the unboiled, new sediment in a warm pack; (7) the sediment on a fleece, for dropsy. It also says the drug "growing old ... becomes better". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any dose from this entry.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Olive pressings leave a watery residue that is a by-product of oil making and is today treated as waste with a high content of organic matter and phenols. Boiling in a copper alloy vessel would put copper into an acid or salty liquid, as copper-alloy vessels do in general. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** Dioscorides prints no hot, cold, dry or moist quality for amurca in this chapter. The qualities he gives are functional: "therapeutic for the bowels", "good for ... closing pores", "represses the swelling".

### Cultivation Register

**Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and the chapter claims none. What it offers a practitioner is a discipline of attention to preparation and keeping, which is real but is not a cultivation practice: the boiling is to a stated consistency (honey), the vessel is named (Cyprian brass), the drug is better when old, and it is compared by strength with lycium.

**Practitioner's note.** A kitchen by-product turned into a bowel, tooth and joint remedy in the ancient pharmacy. Nothing here supports any cultivation use, and a cultivator has no reason to prepare it.

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene.** The sediment is a perishable food residue, and the text prescribes it unboiled and new for one use. Do not apply old or spoiled residue to a wound, a tooth socket or the genitals.
- **Vessel.** The recipe boils it in a brass jar; copper alloy can release copper into acidic or salty liquids. Do not copy the vessel.
- **Wounds, teeth, vulvas, dropsy, gout.** These are medical conditions; use proper care.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** No statement in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
