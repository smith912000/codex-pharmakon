# European Beech — *Fagou* (Greek), *Fagus / Buche* (Latin/Germanic), the Mast-Tree

## *Fagus sylvatica* L. | Family: Fagaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Cool and dry (second degree) in the Galenic-Unani frame; astringent, drying, mildly binding — the bark markedly more so than the nut.
**Training Phase:** Grounding / Consolidation (astringent tissue-toning and antiseptic wound phase)

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Dioscoridean Materia Medica (DMM):** The beech (*phēgos* in the older Greek usage, later transferred; Latin *fagus*) is treated in the classical stream chiefly as a food and timber tree whose bark and leaves carry a binding, astringent virtue. Classical and post-classical Mediterranean practice used decoctions of the astringent bark and leaf as a wash for slack, weeping tissue — the same logic applied across the oak-kin (Fagaceae), whose tannins were the reference astringents of antiquity. The mast (beechnuts) was recognised primarily as pannage — food for swine and, in scarcity, for people.

**Avicennan / Unani (AT):** In the humoral synthesis inherited through Avicenna's *Qanun*, drying and binding barks of this class were slotted as cool-dry correctives for fluxes — diarrhoea, excessive discharge, lax gums. Beech sits comfortably in that family of "restringent" woods whose action is attributed to their earthy, astringent substance drawing tissue together.

**Northern & Assyrian-adjacent folk stream (SS):** Across temperate Europe the beech is the archetypal "book tree" (Old English *bōc*, cognate with *book*), its smooth bark historically incised for marks and runes. Beechwood tar — a dark condensate (creosote) obtained by dry distillation of the wood — entered folk and early-industrial pharmacy as a topical antiseptic and expectorant, part of the same beechwood/pine-tar creosote family later standardised in nineteenth-century medicine. This is a genuine, distinct preparation from the nut and the bark decoction and must not be conflated with them.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The beech's doctrine is *binding and inscription* — the tree of the written mark whose smooth grey bark holds what is cut into it. Pharmacologically this maps onto its tannins: condensed proanthocyanidins that literally bind and cross-link proteins, tightening tissue and precipitating microbial surface proteins. The symbol (the surface that holds the mark) and the chemistry (astringent protein-binding) are the same gesture at two scales.

### Active Components

- **Condensed tannins (proanthocyanidins):** beech leaf carries procyanidin/prodelphinidin polymers up to high degrees of polymerisation — the astringent principle.
- **Flavonoids and other polyphenols:** flavonol glycosides and phenolic acids concentrated in the leaf, with measurable antiradical/reducing activity.
- **Beechwood creosote (from wood tar):** a phenolic condensate rich in guaiacol and creosol — the antiseptic/expectorant fraction of the distilled wood, chemically unrelated to the leaf polyphenols.
- **Beechnut constituents:** edible lipid-rich kernels containing a bitter/irritant principle historically called *fagin*, alongside oxalate and tannins responsible for raw-nut toxicity in quantity.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In the Kenetica frame the beech is a **consolidation-phase astringent and topical antiseptic**. The bark/leaf decoction is used externally as a tightening, drying wash for slack or weeping tissue and as a gargle for lax, bleeding gums — a tissue-toning move, not a systemic one. Beechwood creosote belongs to a separate, sharply bounded antiseptic/expectorant use and is treated here as a caution-class preparation, not a self-administered remedy. The roasted nut is a grounding food rather than a medicine.

### Scientific Correlation

Beech phytochemistry is reasonably characterised, though clinical data are sparse. According to PubMed, an alcohol extract of *Fagus sylvatica* leaf was profiled by UHPLC-HRMS/MS and separated into a fatty-acid fraction and a polyphenol/flavonoid fraction, with antiradical and reducing activity correlating positively with the polyphenolic fraction ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27072217), Formato et al., *Molecules*, 2022). The tannin chemistry underlying the tree's astringency was resolved by MALDI-TOF and ¹³C NMR, confirming condensed tannins of mixed procyanidin/prodelphinidin composition in beech leaf ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0031-9422(02)00660-x), Behrens et al., *Phytochemistry*, 2003).

**Evidence grade: traditional, with phytochemical support.** The astringent/antioxidant chemistry is documented; direct clinical trials of beech bark or leaf as a therapeutic are effectively absent. Antiseptic claims for beechwood creosote rest on its phenolic (guaiacol/creosol) chemistry and historical use rather than modern controlled trials. Claims should stay at the level of "traditional astringent, chemistry-consistent."

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** cool and dry, astringent (*qābiḍ*) — binds fluxes, tones lax tissue.
- **TCM (by analogy, not a classical Chinese herb):** would read as astringent/consolidating, "securing and binding" leakage; not a materia medica entry in the Chinese canon.
- **Ayurveda (by analogy):** *kashaya* (astringent) rasa, cooling *virya*, drying to *kapha*-type damp discharge; not a classical dravya.

### Contraindications

- **Raw beechnut toxicity:** raw beechnuts contain the bitter principle *fagin*, oxalate and tannins; eating them raw in quantity causes nausea, vomiting and gastrointestinal upset. Roast before eating and keep portions small. Keep away from children and pets, who may consume fallen mast.
- **Beechwood tar / creosote caution:** wood-tar creosote is a phenolic irritant and is not a casual home remedy. Undiluted or ingested creosote can cause mucosal burns, gastrointestinal and renal irritation, and is unsuitable in pregnancy, lactation, or for children. Treat as a caution-class preparation only.
- **Astringent overuse:** prolonged internal use of high-tannin decoctions can impair iron and protein absorption and irritate the gut; use external/short-course only.
- **Allergy:** avoid in known Fagaceae sensitivity; beech pollen is a recognised aeroallergen.
