# Mirabilite — Xiao Shi (消石) "Mirabilitum" (Yang 1998); other name Mang Xiao (Aristate Lime)
## *The salt Yang 1998 prints as "Mirabilitum" (Glauber's salt, hydrated sodium sulphate) for Xiao Shi in the Divine Farmer's Classic; Yang's own footnote says Mirabilitum is a cathartic and suspects that it and the preceding entry, Po Xiao, have been "mistakenly juxtaposed"* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** Xiao Shi is printed in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`) on PDF page 24, under "Jades and Stones: Superior Class" (headings at lines 907 and 985; names row `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv` row 10, line 968, translator identification "Mirabilitum"; list `_sources/shennong.csv` 消石). The text (lines 968-973): "Xiao Shi (Mirabilitum) is bitter and cold. It mainly treats accumulated heat in the five viscera and stomach distention and block. It flushes away accumulated abiding drink and food, weeds out the old to bring forth the new, and eliminates evil qi. It may be sublimated into a paste. Protracted taking may make the body light. It is also named Mang Xiao (Aristate Lime). It is produced in mountains and valleys." Yang's footnote 41 (lines 977-981): "Mirabilitum is a very good cathartic which is often prescribed to precipitate or purge food and drink accumulation from the stomach and intestines. However, in this text, the translator suspects that Mirabilitum and Slaked Lime have been mistakenly juxtaposed in terms of their indications." "Slaked Lime" is Yang's rendering of the entry just before, Po Xiao (lines 962-967), which has its own entry (CPU-00212, "Crude niter"). The Latin name and the printed indications therefore do not sit together in the translator's own view. The spine's note is "high" for the row, and its label "Mirabilite" and formula Na2SO4.10H2O come from the translator's Latin and not from any chemistry in the text. An older Codex entry, `Volume_II_Mineral_Alchemical/entries/II_100_Saltpeter_Xiao_Shi.md` (CPU-00848), already treats Xiao Shi under the heading of saltpetre across several traditions; the two readings of the same Chinese name (mirabilite by the translator's Latin, saltpetre by that entry) are not reconciled here. ### Active Components

Mirabilite (Glauber's salt) is hydrated sodium sulphate, Na2SO4.10H2O, a colourless crystalline salt that is very soluble in water and loses its water of crystallisation in dry air, leaving a white powder (thenardite). It is a saline osmotic laxative. Saltpetre (potassium nitrate) is a different salt, an oxidiser and the chief component of gunpowder. Which one Xiao Shi was is the open point in the sources read, and no composition is given in them.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Classic records Xiao Shi for "accumulated heat in the five viscera", stomach distention and block, and for flushing out retained food and drink, and says that long taking "may make the body light". A salt purge taken for years is not safe, and the Codex does not endorse the long-taking clause. The Codex offers no protocol, dose or preparation.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation printed is "It may be sublimated into a paste" (line 971). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Sodium sulphate is an osmotic laxative: it draws water into the gut and loosens the stool, and it has been used before bowel procedures. Taken in excess, or for long periods, it causes diarrhoea with loss of fluid and electrolytes. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: an upper-grade drug (上品), with a doubtful identification.* Xiao Shi stands under "Jades and Stones: Superior Class" (Yang lines 907, 985). Yang's preface says the superior class medicinals "mainly nourish life and correspond to heaven. They are nontoxic and taking them in large amounts and for a long time will not harm people. If one intends to make one's body light, boost the qi, prevent aging, and prolong life, one should base [one's efforts] on the superior class" (lines 352-358). The monograph's own line is "Protracted taking may make the body light" (lines 971-972). The remaining indications are for a purge. The text prints no *jing*, *qi* or *shen* claim for it, and none is made here. The status is a statement of the Classic as translated by Yang in 1998, and the monograph's content (a salt that "flushes away" and "weeds out the old") is that of a cathartic, which Yang's footnote 41 states. The Classic's grade and its indications sit uneasily together, and the cultivation status is not widened beyond what the heading assigns. Yang's introduction (lines 312-328) warns that elixirs of this kind made some adepts ill.

**Practitioner's note.** An upper-grade drug in the Chinese scheme which the translator himself describes as "a very good cathartic". A cultivator should read the grade as the Classic's, and the pharmacology as a purge. Nothing here supports taking it for long periods. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Salt purges are not for continued use: they cause dehydration and electrolyte loss, and are unsafe in kidney, heart or bowel disease, in pregnancy and in children without medical advice. If the substance were saltpetre it would be harmful by mouth in more than small amounts. Powdered mineral products are unstandardised, and the identity of what is sold under "Xiao Shi" cannot be assumed. The Classic's "light body" is not a claim of fact.
