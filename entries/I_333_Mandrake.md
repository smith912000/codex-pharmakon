# I-333: Mandragora officinarum (Mandrake)

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary name:** Mandrake  
**Botanical name:** *Mandragora officinarum* L. (syn. *M. autumnalis* Bertol.; *M. vernalis* Bertol.)  
**Family:** Solanaceae  
**Synonyms:** European mandrake, true mandrake, Satan's apple, love apple, gallows man (German Galgenmännchen), alraun  
**Traditional names:** Mandragoras (Greek Μανδραγόρας); Yabrūḥ al-ṣanam (Arabic يبروح الصنم — "idol root"); Mandragora (Latin); Yabrooh (Persian/Hebrew); Dudaim (Hebrew דּוּדָאִים — the biblical love plant of Genesis 30:14); Luo tuo peng (Chinese 骆驼篷 — disputed)

**Botanical description:** Stemless perennial; large taproot often bifurcated (forked), stout, fleshy, 30–60 cm — the anthropomorphic root form (resembling human figure) central to its cultural mythology; leaves large, ovate, undulate, appearing in autumn rosette; flowers pale violet-blue to white, appearing before leaves (*M. autumnalis* = autumn mandrake; *M. vernalis* = spring mandrake); fruit berry orange-yellow, tomato-like, 2–3 cm diameter; entire plant intensely fetid, narcotic odor.

**Species clarification:**
- *M. officinarum* (syn. *M. vernalis*): spring-flowering; Mediterranean basin north and west
- *M. autumnalis*: autumn-flowering; Mediterranean south and east; possibly separate species
- *M. caulescens* Royle: Himalayan mandrake (very different alkaloid profile, less documented)
- *Podophyllum peltatum* ("American mandrake") — ENTIRELY DIFFERENT plant; NOT related despite name

## 3. Convergence Analysis

**Pattern:** *Mandragora officinarum* occupies a unique position in pharmacognosy: its mythological and cultural footprint vastly exceeds its pharmacological potency. Alkaloid content is lower than both belladonna and datura (typically 0.2–0.4% total, vs. 0.3–0.6% datura seeds), yet mandrake accumulated the most elaborate mythology in Western pharmacological history — a function of its anthropomorphic root and its position at the crossroads of Mediterranean trade routes and textual transmission.

Dioscorides describes mandrake wine (oenomel of mandrake) administered before surgical incisions and cauterization — the first documented surgical anesthetic protocol in Western medicine. The prescribed preparation (bark macerated in wine) and dose ("one kyathos/small cup") shows awareness of both mechanism and dose-response. The instruction that patients who receive too much "will be lost permanently" indicates understanding of the lethal dose.

The biblical reference (Genesis 30:14–16) — Rachel bargaining with Leah for mandrake (dudaim) fruits as a fertility plant — represents the deepest integration of any plant into Abrahamic scriptural tradition. The aphrodisiac/fertility attribution is pharmacologically questionable but the plant's scarcity and powerful smell may have established fertility associations.

The Islamic tradition (Ibn Sīnā) treats mandrake (yabrūḥ) as a legitimate surgical anesthetic, with explicit warnings about overdose: "let the dose not exceed what gives sleep, or death may follow." This represents more pharmacological sophistication than the contemporary European tradition's magical elaboration.

### Other constituents
- Scopoletin (coumarin): sedative, anti-inflammatory
- Polysaccharides: mannose-rich; mucilaginous quality of fresh root
- Steroid glycosides: phytochemically present
- Caffeic acid derivatives: antioxidant
- The orange berry: lycopene-related carotenoids; contains alkaloids at lower concentration than root

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**Mechanistic class identical to I-330–332:** competitive muscarinic receptor blockade (M1–M5), producing the same anticholinergic pharmacology as belladonna, henbane, and datura.

**Distinguishing features:**
- **Lower total alkaloid content** than datura → somewhat wider margin before frank toxicity
- **Scopoletin component:** CNS depressant (GABA-A activity at high concentrations); contributes to sedation via a non-anticholinergic mechanism
- **Mandragorine (cuscohygrine):** nicotinic and muscarinic activity; unique to mandrake genus; may contribute to the distinctive phenomenology reported from mandrake vs. belladonna

**Historical "soporific sponge" role:**
Mandrake, combined with henbane, belladonna, and opium in the medieval *spongia somnifera*, contributed the anticholinergic component to surgical anesthetic preparations. The opium provided analgesia; the anticholinergics provided amnesia and sedation; the combination approximated (imperfectly) balanced anesthesia.

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**No modern clinical trials** — *Mandragora* is not used as a pharmaceutical source, not because its alkaloids are less effective than belladonna alkaloids (they are identical in class) but because better-characterized, more reliably quantifiable sources (belladonna, pharmaceutical synthesis) have superseded it.

**Evidence Level D:** Historical and ethnopharmacological record only.

**Pharmacologically valid (but pharmaceutical alternatives available):**
- The sedative, amnesic, and analgesic properties documented by Dioscorides and Ibn Sīnā are pharmacologically real — they reflect genuine muscarinic antagonism
- The fertility/aphrodisiac attributions are pharmacologically unsupported — most likely cultural/sympathetic magic based on root morphology and smell

## 7. Phenomenological Record

### The Mandrake Root — Morphological Mythology
The bifurcated taproot's anthropomorphic appearance (resembling human body with arm/leg branches) generated the most elaborate botanical mythology in Western tradition. Theophrastus (4th c. BCE) describes the root-cutter's ritual for harvesting: draw three circles around the plant with a sword, cut facing west, dance erotic dances while cutting the second portion. By Josephus (1st c. CE), the elaboration includes: the plant glows at night, kills those who touch it, can only be harvested at dusk after weakening it with urine, and uproots itself when the wind blows — each element reflecting increasing magical accretion.

### The Screaming Root Legend
The most famous mandrake legend: the root screams when pulled from the earth, and the scream kills anyone who hears it. Harvesting method: tie a dog to the root, stuff one's ears, withdraw to distance, blow a horn to startle the dog, which runs, pulling the root, killing the dog, leaving the harvester safe. This legend appears in Josephus (*Jewish War* VII), Machiavelli (*La Mandragola*, 1518), and *Harry Potter and the Chamber of Secrets* (2002) — an unbroken 2,000-year textual tradition. Pharmacological basis: zero. Cultural function: creating scarcity (the elaborate ritual limited who could obtain it) and thus commercial value.

### Genesis 30:14 — Dudaim and the Matriarch Bargain
Among the oldest pharmacobotanical references in world literature: Reuben (son of Leah) finds dudaim (mandrake) plants in the fields and brings them to his mother. Rachel bargains with Leah: "Please give me some of your son's mandrakes." Leah agrees in exchange for a night with Jacob. Rachel eventually conceives. The biblical narrative attributes fertility to mandrake — either the aphrodisiac attribution was already established, or the coincidence of conception after mandrake use created it. The passage is 1000+ BCE in origin.

### Shakespeare's Mandrake
Multiple Shakespearean references reflect Elizabethan mandrake lore at its peak:
- *Othello* III.iii: "Not poppy, nor mandragora, / Nor all the drowsy syrups of the world / Shall ever medicine thee to that sweet sleep" — the first pharmacological formulary in English literature
- *Romeo and Juliet* IV.iii: Juliet fears the root's shriek will drive her mad
- *Henry VI Part 2* III.ii: "Would curses kill, as doth the mandrake's groan"
- *The Merchant of Venice* V.i: "Like shrieks of mandrakes torn out of the earth"

The mandrake appears more times in Shakespeare than any other medicinal plant except opium — reflecting its cultural saturation in early modern European imagination.

### Machiavelli's *La Mandragola* (1518)
The most celebrated Italian Renaissance comedy features mandrake as the plot device: a character convinces a Florentine husband that his wife must drink a mandrake potion to become fertile, but the first man to have intercourse with her after the potion will die from the plant's power. The husband is thus manipulated into providing a "sacrificial" lover — the schemer himself. The play is simultaneously a pharmacological comedy and a satire of Florentine bourgeois credulity.

### Witches' Flying Ointment (see also I-330, I-331)
Mandrake root, alongside belladonna, henbane, and datura, featured in the most widely reported "flying ointment" formulae — applied to skin or mucous membranes, producing hallucination of flight. The alkaloid combination (hyoscyamine + scopolamine from multiple sources) produces a synergistic anticholinergic delirium; the flight experience is consistent with vestibular disruption and proprioceptive alterations from M1/M3 blockade.

## 8. Practical Application

### CRITICAL SAFETY DESIGNATION: TOXIC — NOT FOR UNCONTROLLED USE

**No over-the-counter herbal product should contain *Mandragora* root.** All modern therapeutic applications use pharmaceutical-grade belladonna alkaloids (from better-characterized sources). Mandrake has no advantage over belladonna as a pharmaceutical source and no unique clinical application.

**Toxicity profile (relative to Solanaceae triad):**
| Plant | Total alkaloids | Relative toxicity | Therapeutic use |
|-------|----------------|------------------|-----------------|
| Belladonna | 0.3–0.6% | High | Pharmaceutical atropine/hyoscyamine |
| Henbane | 0.04–0.28% | High (higher scopolamine) | Pharmaceutical scopolamine |
| Datura | 0.2–0.6% | Very high (highest variability) | Pharmaceutical bronchodilator legacy |
| Mandrake | 0.2–0.4% | Moderate-high | No modern pharmaceutical use |

**Anticholinergic toxidrome management:** physostigmine IV (as per I-330, I-331, I-332)

**Drug interactions:** identical class to belladonna — additive anticholinergic burden with TCAs, antihistamines, antipsychotics

### Contemporary Context
- No regulatory approval in any major jurisdiction for *Mandragora* preparations
- Wild harvesting is prohibited in most Mediterranean countries (protected species)
- Commercial "mandrake" root sold as witchcraft supply — not pharmaceutical grade; not tested; not recommended medicinally
- Academic ethnobotany and pharmacognosy continue studying mandrake alkaloid profiles for historical reconstruction

### Energetic Classification
**Primary:** ☿ Shen (neuromodulatory) — the amnesic, oneirogenic, and consciousness-altering properties in historical surgical anesthesia  
**Secondary:** ⊕ Yuan (constitutional) — the root's morphological resonance with human form placed it in deep constitutional symbolism across traditions (fertility, life-force, generation)

The Yuan secondary classification reflects not pharmacology but cultural encoding: mandrake was universally treated as a plant mediating life/death thresholds — surgical anesthesia (death-like sleep), fertility (generation of new life), and the lethal dose creating actual death. This threshold function maps to Yuan tier more than any modern pharmacological category.
