# Phaseolus subloleatus — Shukla-mudga (Sushruta Samhita, Bhishagratna's Appendix No. 3); "subloleatus" is an OCR misprint

## *Phaseolus sublobatus Roxb., a wild bean of the mung group, the Latin that Bhishagratna's Appendix No. 3 ("Names of Drugs, with Latin synonyms", Vol. III) prints as OCR "Phaseolus Subloleatus." (line 32996), the "ole" being a misreading of "ob" ("sublobatus"). The Sanskrit name it stands against is, by counting down the OCR columns, "Shukla-mudga" (OCR "l^ukla-Mudga", line 32935), "white mudga". In current botany the name is treated as Vigna sublobata (Roxb.) Verdc., as the row's check note says; confidence medium for the Latin as the translator's, medium for the pairing, low for the modern species* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Sushruta text (SS); this entry rests on one text, Bhishagratna's English translation of the Sushruta Samhita, and on a Latin name that comes from the translator's appendix and not from the chapters.** The chapters are in `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/` and the appendix is in `Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`. The row was opened on 13 October from the extraction (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/extract/names.csv`), where the Latin line is kept as "not paired to a Sanskrit name (OCR column split)". The correction to *Vigna sublobata* (Roxb.) Verdc. is the 30 October checker's and was not verified against a plant list. [USER: verify]

**The pairing, by counting.** On the page that carries this Latin line, the Sanskrit names run "Suka-simbi" (line 32929), "Suka-nasa" (32931), "Sukla-dugdha" (32933), "**Sukla-mudga**" (32935), "Sunthi" (32937), "Surana" (32939) and "Sveta-chandana" (32941). The Latin entries in step with them are "Mucuna pruricns" (the OCR of *Mucuna pruriens*), "Orxylum Indicum" (*Oroxylum indicum*), "See S'ringitaka", "**Phaseolus Subloleatus.**" (line 32996), "Gingiber officinal." (ginger), "Amorphophallus Compomulatus." (*A. campanulatus*) and "Santalum Album." (sandalwood), on lines 32990–33001. The fourth Sanskrit name, Sukla-mudga, therefore stands against the *Phaseolus* line, and the neighbours fit: Sunthi with ginger, Surana with *Amorphophallus*, Sveta-chandana with sandalwood, Suka-simbi with *Mucuna* and Suka-nasa with *Oroxylum*. The meaning of the name ("white mudga", a pulse) also agrees with a bean. The pairing is sound by count and neighbours, but positional. [USER: verify against the page image]

**Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *The name in the chapters.* A search of the chapter folders (Sutra, Chikitsa, Uttara, Kalpa, Nidana, Sharira) for "Shukla-mudga", "Sukla-mudga" and their spellings found no hit. The name is therefore known only from the appendix. The nearest chapter material concerns the pulse Mudga, and is given here for context, without asserting that it describes this plant.

*The pulses (Sutra 46, `SS_Sutra_ch46.txt`, lines 194–213).* "The several varieties of pulse (Vaidala) commonly used as food are known as the Mudga, Vana-Mudga, Kalaya, Makushtha, Masura, Mangalya, Chanaka, Satina, Triputa, Harenu, and Adhaki, etc." The metrical text says: "They are generally cool and pungent of digestion, and have a sweet and astringent taste. They generate the Vayu, arrest the flow of urine and the evacuation of stool, and subdue the Pittam and Kapham. The species known as the Mudga does not excessively generate Vayu in the system but tends to purify and invigorate the organ of vision. The green species is the best of all the varieties of Mudga pulse. The properties of Vanya (Mudga) are similar to those of the common variety." Of all the pulses, "the Mudga and the Masura" are excepted from the tendency to produce abdominal distension (lines 228–230).

*Grains in the aggravating diet and the anti-fat list (Sutra 21, line 268; Sutra 15, line 620).* "Mudga" appears in both passages quoted in the entry for Shyamaka (`I_1597_Panicum_frumentaceuin.md`): a diet that aggravates Vayu, and a list of anti-fat compositions.

*In the rasayana chapter (Chikitsa 27, `SS_Chikitsa_ch27.txt`, lines 63, 95 and 222).* Among the elixirs and rejuvenators of the chapter (headed "recipes and modes of using elixirs and rejuvenators", lines 3–8), the meal taken after the medicine has been digested is rice and clarified butter with "the soup of Amalaka and Mudga pulse unseasoned with salt" (line 63), with "the soup of Mudga pulse" (line 95) or with "unsalted Mudga, and Amalaka soup or with milk alone" (line 222). The Mudga here is the ordinary pulse of the chapter, a diet taken alongside the medicine and not the elixir itself.

**What this does and does not establish.** The chapter lists a "Vana-Mudga" (forest or wild mudga) beside the Mudga, and says its properties are "similar to those of the common variety". Whether the appendix's "Sukla-mudga" is that wild kind, a white-seeded cultivated kind, or something else is not stated in the text read. The Codex's reading, that a wild bean of the mung group could stand behind a wild-mudga name, is an inference. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, beans of the mung group (Fabaceae) are seeds rich in protein and starch. Nothing more is stated here about the composition of the Sushruta drug.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The appendix gives a Sanskrit name and a Latin name, and the chapters give no use for that name. The nearest context, the Mudga pulse of Sutra 46 and Chikitsa 27, is a food. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and recommends nothing.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation is given for the name Sukla-mudga in the sources retrieved. For the common Mudga, the Chikitsa 27 lines above give a soup taken after the medicine; its quantities are not printed there. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Pulses are the dry seeds of bean-family plants and are eaten as food, though raw or undercooked beans of some species contain compounds that irritate the gut. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Sushruta terms (Vayu, Pittam, Kapham) are not modern diagnoses, and the identity rests on a positional pairing, so a study of *Vigna* would not bear directly on the Sushruta drug.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SS:** none is printed for the name "Sukla-mudga". For the Mudga pulse as a class, Sutra 46 prints cool, pungent of digestion, sweet and astringent; it "subdues the Pittam and Kapham" and the Mudga proper "does not excessively generate Vayu"; Vanya (Mudga) is "similar to the common variety". These belong to the pulse in the chapter, and are not carried over as this plant's properties.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *(c) Absent.* The name "Sukla-mudga" is not in the chapters read, so no cultivation status can be attested for it. The common Mudga pulse appears in the rasayana chapter (Chikitsa 27) as the meal taken after an elixir, which frames the pulse as a diet and not as a rejuvenative; the Codex does not carry that to this plant. **Practitioner's note.** Nothing here gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a Latin name in a translator's glossary, against a Sanskrit name the chapters do not use, and the nearest chapter material is a pulse eaten as food.

### Contraindications

The Sushruta passages read print no warning for this name. The Mudga pulse as a class is said to arrest the flow of urine and stool. The identity rests on a translator's appendix and a positional pairing, and a wild bean is not a food to gather on this evidence; raw or undercooked beans can upset the gut.
