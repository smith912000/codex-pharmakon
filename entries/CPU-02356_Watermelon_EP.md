# Watermelon — "Watermelon" (Bryan 1930, *The Papyrus Ebers*); the Egyptian word is not given in the list

## *Citrullus lanatus (Thunb.) Matsum. & Nakai, the watermelon, offered as the identification of Bryan's English "Watermelon". The Egyptian word is not printed in Bryan's lists, and no binomial is given by him; the identification is ours, from the English name, and is of low to medium confidence* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** EP

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Ebers Papyrus among the texts on disk, under Bryan's English name.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder taken from Bryan's plant list; this entry replaces it. The first-pass guess was *Citrullus lanatus*. The reading below keeps that guess but lowers its confidence, because the sources give only Bryan's rendering.

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The English text on disk is C. P. Bryan's 1930 *The Papyrus Ebers*, a translation with summaries and not a transliteration (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`). Bryan introduces his plant list with the remark that "of the recognizable Plants, and of their preparations, there is a not inconsiderable showing as the following list will reveal" (line 1233) and says that many substances are "completely hidden from us under the guise of meaningless words". "Watermelon" is a head word of that list (Ch. VI, "Plant Remedies", line 1342; row 87 of `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv`, "Bryan's English identification"). The extract's own README states that identifications are "Bryan's 1930 English renderings only; no Egyptian word and no binomial is given in these lists".

Where Bryan prints the plant in a prescription, it is always one ingredient of a compound, and the English lines give it no property of its own:

1. **Indigestion plaster.** "Watermelon, Sweet Beer, Wine. Make into one and apply as a plaster" (line 1915 to 1918), in the chapter on the digestive system, under the heading "Indigestion, etc." The quantities are printed as bare numerals in a damaged OCR column and are not read here.
2. **Hardenings.** Bryan's summary says: "A plaster that combines Cat's dung with Watermelon might be counted on to remove 'hardenings' anywhere and in anybody" (line 1923). A separate list for driving away "the hardening in the abdomen" prints "Bread-of-the-Zizyphus-Lotus", "Watermelon" and further items (lines 3544 to 3556).
3. **Swollen glands of the arm.** In the heart and circulatory section: "There are Two Vessels to his Arm. When he suffers in his Arm and his fingers tremble, then thou sayest to him: 'They are swollen glands.' Prepare against them: Fish-glue in Beer, and t'as-plant or Flesh in Watermelon, and poultice his fingers. Thereby he will become well" (line 3414 to 3417).
4. **Dry parts of the met.** A poultice "of Watermelon, Fat-of-the-Egyptian-Goat, and Bullock's Flesh, seasoned with Garlic and Onions, and flavoured with Honey", and a thirty-seven-ingredient poultice that numbers "Hog's dung, Watermelon, and Goose-oil" among its ingredients (line 3170; the list continues to about line 3190 and prints "Watermelon" among the items).
5. **Growth of the uxedu.** A remedy "which frees the pustules and drives out the matter" lists "teun-plant, Xehui-berries, Wasp's Blood, Sea-salt, Watermelon, hemit-grains" and more (lines 3915 to 3925).
6. **General summary.** Bryan also mentions "a poultice consisting of the Zizyphus Lotus, Watermelon, Cat's dung, Sweet Beer, and Wine" among preparations that "confound all criticism" (lines 1375 to 1377).

All six are external plasters. No Egyptian name, no part of the plant (fruit, seed or rind) and no gloss on the fruit is printed in the lines read. [USER: verify that the part used is not stated in a transliterated edition.]

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, the watermelon (*Citrullus lanatus*) is a gourd-family (Cucurbitaceae) fruit whose flesh is largely water with sugars and the pigment lycopene in red varieties; the seeds are rich in oil and protein. This is a statement about the species and does not show that the Ebers plant is that species. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text records.** The material appears as an ingredient in plasters for indigestion (the digestive section), for "hardenings", for swollen glands of the arm, and for a growth with pustules. No use of the plant by itself, and no internal use, is recorded in the lines read.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no part used, no preparation beyond mixing it with other ingredients and applying it.

The Codex does not translate the Egyptian disease names into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend poultices made with the compounds named.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation given is mixing the ingredients "into one" and applying them as a plaster or poultice (line 1917 to 1918; line 3170). The papyrus gives quantities for some compounds as numerals, but the OCR of Bryan's columns is damaged and the numerals cannot be tied to the unit or to the plant. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

The Ebers material is indication-organised, and the lines read give the plant no quality of temperature, taste or potency. No energetic classification is printed, and none is imported here.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's vital constitution. "Watermelon" appears only as a plaster ingredient in prescriptions for local complaints. No passage read says anything of the *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib* in connection with it, and the recitations that accompany Egyptian prescriptions (*heka*) are not recorded for it in the lines read. The identity itself is a modern hypothesis resting on Bryan's English.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here makes watermelon a cultivation substance. The fruit is an ordinary food, and the papyrus's plaster is a historical record. A cultivator has no practice to draw from it.

### Contraindications

- **Identity.** Bryan's "Watermelon" is an English rendering; the species is our guess. Do not treat any plaster in this entry as a recipe.
- **Open skin.** Several of the compounds include dung, fat and raw flesh; applying such mixtures to broken skin or swellings is an infection risk.
- **Swollen glands, hardenings, growths.** These are medical conditions; use proper care.
- **Allergy.** The fruit is eaten widely; cucurbit allergy exists but is uncommon. [USER: verify]
