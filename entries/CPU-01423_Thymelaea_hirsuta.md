# Thymelaea hirsuta — Thumelaia (Dioscorides 4-173); Oinos thumelaites (5-78)

## *Thymelaea hirsuta* (the Dioscorides translators' suggested identification, with *Daphne gnidium*; gnidium, spurge flax) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Identity note.** This plant is named only by Dioscorides (DMM). The binomial is the suggestion printed on the SUGGESTED lines of the translation by Osbaldeston and Wood (2000), not Dioscorides' own: "Thymelaea hirsuta, Daphne gnidium — Gnidium, Spurge Flax, Flax-leaved Daphne". 4-173 adds the note "[Loudon] not the cnidium species of today". Two binomials are offered, so the identity is not single. [USER: verify] Source: `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).**

*4-173 THUMELAIA* (heading at line 9612). "Thymelaea is similar in nature to sown flax. It sends out many thin fair stems, almost two feet high. The leaves are like chamelaea [above] but narrower and fatter, somewhat viscous and glutinous if chewed. The flowers are white, and in the midst is the fruit — as small as that of myrtus [1-155], round, at first green, but afterwards red. The covering of it is hard and black, but within it is white." It "grows in rough hilly places". Uses: "A decoction of the inner part (taken as a drink using as much as twenty of the grains) purges bile, phlegm and water downwards. It burns a rough throat; as a result it is given with meal, polenta, in the kernel of a grape, or covered with boiled honey to swallow it down." To rub those who have difficulty sweating, it is pounded with saltpetre and vinegar. The leaves, "which are properly called cneoron", are gathered at harvest time, dried in the shade in jars, beaten and stripped of their strings; "As much as an acetabulum [vinegar cruet] applied with diluted wine purges by drawing away watery matter. It makes the purging more moderate if it is mixed with boiled lens [lentils] and bruised vegetables." They are made into little balls with unripe-grape juice for storage. "The herb is worthless for the stomach, and given as a pessary it is an abortifacient." Dioscorides warns that some are deceived into thinking "cnidium grain" is the fruit of *chamelaea*. Other names: *chamelaea*, *pyrosachne*, *cestron*, *cneoron*; the cnidium grain is called *aetolium* (Euboeans), *apolinum* (Syrians), *linum*.

*5-78 OINOS THUMELAITES* (heading at line 10186). "Throw thirty teaspoons of the branches of thymelaea (together with its fruit) into two gallons of must. Boil it, seething it gently, until only one and a third gallons are left, then strain it and bottle it. It purges watery matter and reduces the spleen."

### Active Components

Members of the daphne and spurge-flax group (family Thymelaeaceae) are generally known to contain irritant diterpene esters and coumarins that blister skin and mucosa and act as strong purgatives. The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records a drastic purgative and an abortifacient pessary (4-173) and a boiled-down wine (5-78), as his claims and not a Codex protocol. The plant is irritant and the Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

*4-173:* "twenty of the grains" in a decoction of the inner part, swallowed in meal, polenta, a grape kernel or boiled honey; leaves taken in "an acetabulum" with diluted wine, moderated with lentils. *5-78:* thirty teaspoons of branches with fruit in two gallons of must, boiled to one and a third gallons. These quantities are what the text prints. [USER: verify] They are historical, not recommendations.

### Scientific Correlation

Plants of this family are generally known as skin irritants and as toxic when swallowed. This is a general statement; no study of this species was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** 4-173 prints the herb as "worthless for the stomach", burning "a rough throat", and purging "bile, phlegm and water downwards". No warming or cooling quality or degree is printed in either chapter.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. The chapter is a discipline of attention: the habit like flax, leaf and fruit colour, rough hill habitat, gathering time for the leaves, drying in shade, removing the strings, and a warning against confusing the fruit with cnidium grain. No status is assigned and no use for the person's vital state is recorded. The binomial is a translators' gloss. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the source read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. Dioscorides himself notes that it burns the throat and is worthless for the stomach. Abstain.

### Contraindications

An irritant, drastic purgative. The recorded uses include an abortifacient pessary. Do not use in pregnancy. The traditional uses and quantities are not advice; no home preparation is safe.
