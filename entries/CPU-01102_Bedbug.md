# Bedbug — KOREIS (Dioscorides 2-36) / Cimices, Bed Bugs
## *The bed bug, which the editors identify as* Cimex lectularius *(with "Cimex rotandatus", as printed); the chapter itself gives only the Greek name* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-36 *KOREIS* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 5082-5084, Osbaldeston and Wood 2000; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 226, "KOREIS, 2-36 (line 5082), Cimices — Cimex lectularius, Cimex rotandatus — Bed Bugs"). The identification in the heading is the editors'; the Greek name is all the ancient text supplies. The whole chapter: "Seven cimices taken and put in meat with beans and swallowed down before a fit help those with quartains [fever with paroxysm every three to four days]. Swallowed down without beans they help one bitten by an asp. Smelled, they revive those fallen into a faint from constriction of the vulva. Taken as a drink with wine or vinegar they release horseleeches. Pounded into small pieces and put into the urethra they cure painful urination." There are five uses:

- *Quartan fever.* Seven bugs "put in meat with beans and swallowed down before a fit" (the bracket "fever with paroxysm every three to four days" is the editors').
- *The bite of an asp.* The same, swallowed "without beans".
- *Fainting from "constriction of the vulva".* Smelled.
- *Horseleeches.* "Taken as a drink with wine or vinegar they release horseleeches". The English says only "release"; the text does not say where the leeches are lodged, and the Greek was not checked.
- *Painful urination.* "Pounded into small pieces and put into the urethra".

The chapter is the thirty-sixth of Book 2, in the section of the living creatures (the headings around it are sea gudgeon, tunny, garum, fish soup before it and the woodlouse after it, 2-32 to 2-37). The running page head of this part of Book 2 reads "LIVING CREATURES" (the contents, line 30, lists Book Two beginning with "Living Creatures"). No dose beyond the number "seven" is given, and no season, source or test of genuineness. The chapter gives no preparation beyond what is quoted. The chapter does not give a reason for any of the uses. A search of the Dioscorides text for "cimices", "bed bug" and "koreis" finds only this chapter (the plant *Koris*, 3-174, is another word).

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, bed bugs (*Cimex lectularius*) are small blood-feeding insects; their bodies are chitin and protein, and they release an unpleasant-smelling secretion from glands, which is a mixture of aldehydes. The text does not say that the smell is the reason for the "smelled" use.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources record five uses of bed bugs as a drug, all swallowed, smelled or placed in the urethra. There is no basis for any of them in modern medicine, and two (insertion into the urethra and ingestion for a venomous bite) are dangerous. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only quantity and preparation the chapter gives: "Seven cimices taken and put in meat with beans and swallowed down before a fit" for quartan fever; seven swallowed without beans for an asp's bite; "smelled" for fainting; "taken as a drink with wine or vinegar" for leeches; "pounded into small pieces and put into the urethra" for painful urination. Whether seven applies to every use is not stated. No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Bed bugs are common household pests that bite and feed on human blood at night; they are not established as reliable carriers of human disease but cause skin reactions and allergic responses and are difficult to eradicate. Quartan fever corresponds to a periodic malarial fever, which needs antimalarial treatment; a venomous snake bite needs emergency care. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. The discipline of attention that his plant and mineral chapters offer (provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, adulteration detection) is not present in this chapter, which gives five uses and a count. He sometimes records unusual uses while distancing himself from them; this chapter does not distance itself, and recording is not endorsing. The chapter is therapeutic, and no cultivation status is assigned. The identification as *Cimex lectularius* is the editors' suggestion.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. A cultivator takes no practice from this entry; the only lesson is historical, that a Greek physician listed vermin among his drugs. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Infection and allergy:** swallowing or handling crushed insects risks bacterial contamination and allergic reaction, and the insect is a household pest with an unsanitary habitat.
- **Urethra:** inserting any foreign material or crushed insect into the urethra risks injury, infection and obstruction; the text's use is not safe.
- **Venomous bites:** a snake bite needs emergency medical care, not a swallowed insect.
- **Fever:** a periodic fever needs diagnosis (malaria in particular) and treatment.
- **Faint:** a faint needs assessment of its cause.
- **Ethics and hygiene:** none of the uses survives modern standards of safety.
