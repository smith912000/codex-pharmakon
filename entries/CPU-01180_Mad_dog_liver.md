# Mad-dog liver — Hepar kunos lussontos, "Liver of Mad Dog", as printed in the English edition of Dioscorides
## *The liver of a rabid dog, eaten roasted by a person the dog has bitten* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek Tradition (DMM).** The chapter is Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica*, Book 2, chapter 49, headed "2-49. HEPAR KUNOS LUSSONTOS" with the title "Liver of Mad Dog" and the editor's note "RABIES IS HIGHLY COMMUNICABLE" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 5130-5133; row in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 239). The whole chapter reads: "The liver of a mad dog (eaten roasted by those which have been bitten by him) is thought to keep them safe from the fear of water. As a precaution they also use the tooth of that dog which bit, place it into a bag and tie it to the arm." (line 5133).

Several things follow from the lines themselves.

1. "Is thought to keep them safe" is hedged language. The text reports a belief and does not itself vouch for the effect.
2. The "fear of water" is, in modern terms, the sign called hydrophobia, which is a feature of rabies; that reading is mine and the text does not use either word. The editor's note "RABIES IS HIGHLY COMMUNICABLE" is a modern gloss added to the chapter, not Dioscorides' words. The word "rabies" is not in the quoted chapter text.
3. The second sentence records an amuletic practice: the tooth of the biting dog is placed in a bag and tied to the arm. The text says "they also use", which reports what people do; it does not say that Dioscorides adopts it. This is recording, not endorsing.
4. The liver is that of "a mad dog" and the tooth is that of "that dog which bit"; the text does not say whether the liver, too, must come from the biting dog.

The rest of the section on dog bites shows other remedies, none of which involves the dog. For example, the flesh of salted *smaris* "is good for one touched by a scorpion, and for one bitten by a dog" (line 5061), and the sea gudgeon decoction "helps those bitten by dogs or snakes" (chapter 2-32, "Kobios", line 5068). These are cited only to show that this chapter's remedy sat among others.

### Active Components

Liver is a metabolic organ: protein, fat, glycogen, iron and vitamin stores (vitamin A in particular in many mammals). The text names no active compound. The relevant general fact is not a component but a hazard: the liver and other tissues of a rabid animal can carry rabies virus, which is concentrated in the nervous system and salivary glands. Nothing in the text suggests that roasting the liver of the dog is protective.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. It is not a recommended practice and must not be used as a response to a dog bite. Rabies is almost invariably fatal once symptoms appear, and it is preventable only by prompt medical treatment after exposure: wound washing and a post-exposure vaccination course (with immune globulin where indicated). Eating the liver of the dog, or wearing its tooth, has no protective effect and delays real care. Anyone bitten by a dog or other mammal in a region where rabies occurs should wash the wound thoroughly and seek medical care immediately. The Codex provides no protocol for this material.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation is "eaten roasted" (line 5133). No quantity, timing relative to the bite, or duration is given, and the amulet needs no preparation beyond placing the tooth in a bag and tying it to the arm. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] General, well-established facts: rabies is a viral disease transmitted by the bite or saliva of an infected mammal; it affects the nervous system, and one of its signs in humans is a spasm of the throat on attempts to drink, which is the "fear of water". Post-exposure prophylaxis with vaccine is the established treatment. Eating infected tissue or carrying an amulet has no recognised protective effect.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

### Cultivation Register

**Greek (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured here. The chapter records a protective remedy and an amuletic practice with a hedged "is thought". This is a case of recording an amuletic use, not of endorsing it. No cultivation status is attested.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. A cultivator who is bitten by a dog seeks medical care at once. This text offers a historical remedy, not a safeguard.

### Contraindications

Not a recommended practice. Handling or eating tissue from a rabid animal is itself an exposure risk. Rabies after symptoms begin is almost always fatal; the only effective time to act is straight after the bite. Dog bites also carry bacterial infection and tetanus risk, so they need washing and medical review. Wild or stray animal bites need urgent assessment. This entry gives no medical advice.
