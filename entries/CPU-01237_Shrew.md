# Shrew — MUOGALE (Dioscorides 2-73), "Shrew Mouse" / mygale
## *Myogale species — Shrew Mouse, in the edition's own gloss; the Greek name is printed MUOGALE (mygale) and the edition adds no description of the animal* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides treats the shrew in Book Two of *De Materia Medica*, the book that begins with animal products, in the Osbaldeston and Wood 2000 English edition (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). The chapter is **2-73. MUOGALE** (heading at line 5230), glossed "Myogale species — Shrew Mouse" (line 5231). The whole of it is one sentence (line 5232): "The shrewmouse (cut apart and applied) is a remedy for its own bites." It comes between the earthworms (2-72) and the house mouse (2-74). The name index of the edition lists "Shrew Mouse 207" (line 17570), and the project's name list (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row for MUOGALE, locus "2-73 (line 5230)") records the same gloss.

The edition's gloss "Myogale species" is the editor's modern suggestion, and the text itself says nothing about what the animal looks like, where it lives or how it is gathered. Whether the animal Dioscorides meant is what a modern zoologist calls a shrew cannot be settled from the lines read. [USER: verify]

The shrew is also named in other chapters, as the biter that the plant is used against, and not as a drug. In the garlic chapter (2-182) the plant is "a poultice with fig leaves and cumin for those bitten by the mygale [shrew mouse]" (lines 6117 and 6129). In the chapter on wormwood (3-26, *Apsinthion*) wormwood is "an antidote given with wine for (the poison) of ixia [3-103] and hemlock, the bites of the shrewmouse, and bites of the sea dragon [2-15]" (line 6753). In 4-56 (*Chrusogonon*) the pounded root "with vinegar and applied, helps the bites of the shrewmouse" (line 8494). In the subject index the entry "bites of the shrew mouse" appears (line 12840). The picture the text gives is that the shrew's bite was regarded as a hazard with several remedies, of which the shrew itself, cut apart and applied, is one.

### Active Components

Shrews are small insectivorous mammals, and what Dioscorides prescribes is the tissue of the whole animal, which is muscle, skin, fat and organs like those of any small mammal. A few shrew species (the northern short-tailed shrew and the Eurasian water shrew among them) have toxic saliva, which they use on invertebrate prey, but the bites of most shrews are of no consequence to people. No active principle is claimed by the text. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The one sourced use is that the animal that bites is applied to the bite it caused. The Codex draws no protocol from this. A bite from any wild mammal should be cleaned and assessed for infection, tetanus and, where relevant, rabies, and a cut-up animal laid on the wound is the opposite of that care.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparation is stated in the chapter: the animal is "cut apart and applied" (line 5232). No other step, quantity, number of applications or duration is given, and the chapter does not say whether the animal should be fresh or dried, or whether the whole body or a part is meant. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. There is no evidence that applying the body of an animal to a wound it caused helps the wound heal, and animal tissue laid on a bite can introduce bacteria. Bite wounds of any mammal are prone to infection and are treated by washing with soap and running water, medical assessment, and tetanus and sometimes rabies prevention. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** None is given. Chapter 2-73 prints no quality (warming, cooling, astringent or the like) for the shrew. The general statements Dioscorides makes about fats and other animal substances elsewhere in Book Two are not applied to this animal. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied here. What he offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, the detection of adulteration. Chapter 2-73 gives none of those for the shrew; it gives one sentence, with no place, season or test. The only frame is the practical one of the bite and its remedies in the chapters above, and that is a matter of treating injury, not of cultivation. No Egyptian, Assyrian or other framing is imported.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a single remedy for a bite from the same animal, with no preparation beyond cutting the animal apart. What the entry usefully records is the form of the reasoning: the harm and its remedy are placed in the same creature. Treat the bite as a wound, and leave the shrew alone.

### Contraindications

- **Bite wounds:** wash, seek care, and ask about tetanus and rabies; do not apply animal tissue to a wound.
- **Infection:** handling or cutting up wild small mammals can spread bacteria and parasites, and dead animals can carry fleas and other vectors.
- **Legal and conservation status:** shrews are wild animals and some are protected in places; killing wild animals for a substance with no supported benefit is hard to justify. [USER: verify]
- **Identity:** the identification as a shrew rests on the edition's gloss alone, so nothing in this entry should be read as a statement about a particular species.
