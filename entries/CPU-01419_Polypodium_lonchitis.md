# Polypodium lonchitis — Lonchitis, Lonchitis etera (Dioscorides 3-161, 3-162)

## *Polypodium lonchitis* (the Dioscorides translators' suggested identification; holly fern, shield fern) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Identity note.** This plant is named only by Dioscorides (DMM). The binomial is the suggestion printed on the SUGGESTED lines of the translation by Osbaldeston and Wood (2000), not Dioscorides' own identification. For both chapters the line reads "Lonchitis, Polypodium lonchitis, Serapias, Aspidium lonchitis — Shield Fern, Holly Fern". The description in 3-161 (leek-like leaves, hat-shaped flowers with a tongue, a spearhead-shaped seed case, a root like *daucus*) is not that of a fern as the word is used now; 3-162 is compared to *scolopendrium*. The match to a fern is the translators'. [USER: verify] Source: `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).**

*3-161 LONCHITIS* (heading at line 7898; "see 3-162"). "Lonchitis has many leaves very similar to leek, yet broader and somewhat red, broken about towards the root (and as it were) lying on the ground." A few leaves are around the stalk, "on which are flowers — similar to little hats in shape (similar to comical persons opening their mouths wide) — and they are black, but some white thing arises from them... (as it were) a little tongue. The seed is shut in three-cornered cases shaped like a spearhead, from which it was thought worthy of its surname. The root is similar to daucus. It grows in rough, dry places." Use: "A decoction of the root of this (taken as a drink) with wine is diuretic." Other names: cestron, medusa; the Romans call it *venerea* or *lanceola*.

*3-162 LONCHITIS ETERA* (heading at line 7906; "see 3-161"). "Lonchitis altera (also called the rough lonchitis) sends out leaves similar to scolopendrium [3-121], but sharper, bigger, and more jagged. A decoction (taken as a drink with vinegar) is able to cure wounds and remove inflammation, and it reduces the spleen." The Romans call it *longina* or *calabrina*.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records a diuretic root drink (3-161) and a vinegar decoction for wounds, inflammation and the spleen (3-162), as his own claims. The Codex gives no protocol for the plant.

### Preparation & Dosing

*3-161:* a decoction of the root drunk with wine, as a diuretic. *3-162:* a decoction drunk with vinegar. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. The chapters offer a discipline of attention: how to recognise the plant (leaves like leek, odd hat-shaped flowers, spearhead seed case, root like *daucus*, rough dry ground) and which of two kinds is meant (the second compared to *scolopendrium*, the rougher). No status is assigned and nothing is recorded for the person beyond a diuretic and a wound or spleen decoction. The binomial is a translators' gloss. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the source read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The honest answer is that this is a short, two-chapter record of an unsettled identity, and there is nothing to do with it.

### Contraindications

The identity of the plant is not settled, so no safe use can be stated. The traditional uses are not advice. Do not use in pregnancy as a general precaution; seek qualified care for any complaint named.
