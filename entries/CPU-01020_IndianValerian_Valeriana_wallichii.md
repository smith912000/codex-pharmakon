### Origin & Tradition

*Valeriana wallichii* — now botanically folded into *V. jatamansi* — is the Himalayan valerian, the *Tagara* of the Ayurvedic pharmacopoeia and the *spang-spos* of Tibetan medicine. It is a rhizomatous herb of the temperate Himalaya from Afghanistan to south-west China, and its warm, musky, camphoraceous rootstock has been a pan-Asian calmative for at least two millennia. It is a genuine congener of European valerian (*V. officinalis*, treated separately) and shares the valerian chemical signature, but the classical traditions treated it as its own drug — warmer, more aromatic, and directed as much at the *mind* as at sleep. A recurring hazard in the literature, worth flagging at the outset, is nomenclatural: *Tagara* has historically been adulterated with or confused for *V. officinalis*, *Nardostachys jatamansi* (true jatamansi/spikenard), and rhizomes of related Valerianaceae — so "Tagara" in a given text is not always this species.

**SS (Suśruta / Ayurvedic tradition, *Suśruta Saṃhitā* and later nighaṇṭus, classical era):** *Tagara* is a canonical Ayurvedic dravya classed as *medhya* (intellect-supporting/nervine) and *nidrājanana* (sleep-promoting). It is directed at *unmāda* (disordered mind/insanity), *apasmāra* (seizure/epileptiform disorders), *anidrā* (insomnia), and *vāta*-driven pain and agitation. Its heating, pungent-bitter profile is read as pacifying *vāta* (the nervous, mobile principle) and *kapha*, and it appears in classical compound formulations for the nervous system and as an aromatic in medicated oils. The doctrinal category matters: *Tagara* is not merely a hypnotic but a *medhya* calmative — a plant meant to steady and clarify the mind, not simply to sedate it.

**FMT (rGyud-bzhi / Tibetan tradition, the *Four Tantras*, codified c. 12th c. CE):** Tibetan medicine uses the aromatic Himalayan valerians (*spang-spos* group) as warming, "wind"-calming (*rlung*) aromatics — *rlung* being the Tibetan analogue of *vāta*, the nervous/breath principle whose disorder produces insomnia, restlessness, palpitation, and racing thought. Valerian-group rhizomes enter compound formulas aimed at settling disturbed *rlung*, and the plant's musky aroma is itself part of the therapy in a tradition that treats scent as an active carrier. Precise species assignment within the Tibetan *spang-spos*/aromatic group is not always resolvable from the tantric texts alone [USER: verify specific rGyud-bzhi passage/species attribution before citing a named formula].

**CM (Avicenna / Unani tradition, *al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb*, c. 1025 CE and later Unani nighaṇṭus):** In Unani medicine the drug travels as *mushkbālā* / *asārūn*-adjacent aromatic nervine and is classed as Hot & Dry — a warming *muqawwī-yi dimāgh* (brain-strengthener) and *musakkin* (calmative) used for palpitation, nervous agitation, and headache of "cold-wind" type. The Unani heating classification is the mirror image of European valerian's cooler reputation and reflects the more intensely aromatic Himalayan material.

**DMM (Dioscorides / Graeco-Roman tradition, *De Materia Medica*, c. 50–70 CE):** Dioscorides describes *phou* (Valerian, *Valeriana* spp., including the eastern "wild nard" traded along the same routes as spikenard) as a warming aromatic root, diuretic and emmenagogue, and useful for chest and nervous complaints. The Graeco-Roman *phou*/wild-nard material overlaps the Himalayan valerian trade goods, and the sedative-aromatic reputation is continuous with the eastern use, though Dioscorides' species cannot be pinned to *V. wallichii* specifically — the attestation is at the level of the traded valerian/nard aromatic root, not a modern binomial.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The unifying doctrine across all four traditions is *warm aromatic settling of the nervous "wind"* — *vāta* in Ayurveda, *rlung* in Tibet, the cold-wind agitation of Unani. This is a doctrine about the **volatile, mobile, aromatic** quality of the drug, and it maps with striking directness onto the chemistry. The pharmacologically interesting fraction of *V. wallichii* is precisely the **volatile** one: sesquiterpenes such as **valeranone (jatamansone)** and **patchouli alcohol (patchoulol)**, plus the non-volatile but reactive **valepotriates** (valtrate, isovaltrate, acevaltrate) — iridoid esters concentrated in the fresh rhizome. The traditional insistence that the *aroma itself* is medicine is not mysticism: the sedative-relevant sesquiterpenes are the same molecules that carry the musky scent, so the olfactory signature and the CNS-relevant chemistry are literally the same compounds. The *medhya* (mind-steadying) framing rather than mere hypnosis also rhymes with the modern picture: valerian-group activity is generally attributed to modulation of GABAergic tone and to preclinical anticonvulsant/anxiolytic effects of the sesquiterpene fraction — a "settling" rather than a knockout. Where the doctrine over-reaches, and the entry must deflate it, is the classical claim on *apasmāra*/seizure: the anticonvulsant signal for this genus is **preclinical only**, and no human antiepileptic efficacy is established. The heating (*uṣṇa vīrya*) doctrine, finally, is a real chemical contrast with European valerian — the Himalayan drug is richer in aromatic sesquiterpenes and valepotriates — but "heating" is a phenomenological reading of an aromatic pungency, not a thermogenic pharmacology.

### Active Components

- **Valepotriates (valtrate, isovaltrate, acevaltrate; monoene types didrovaltrate, IVHD-valtrate)** — Iridoid triester epoxides concentrated in the fresh rhizome; the chemical hallmark of the drug and the leading candidates for its sedative/spasmolytic action in preclinical work. **Chemically unstable**: they decompose on drying, storage, heat, and in acid to baldrinal and homobaldrinal (and further products), so aged or badly processed material loses valepotriate content — a fact of direct practical importance for dosing and safety.
- **Valeranone (jatamansone)** — A characteristic sesquiterpene ketone of the Himalayan valerians associated with sedative/hypotensive activity in the older pharmacological literature; a principal aroma and CNS-relevant constituent.
- **Patchouli alcohol (patchoulol)** and related sesquiterpenes — Volatile-oil components contributing to the musky aroma and to preclinical CNS/anti-inflammatory activity.
- **Volatile oil (bornyl acetate, and species-variable sesquiterpenes)** — The aromatic fraction; carrier of the "scent-as-medicine" doctrine.
- **Trace lignans and flavonoids** — Minor constituents reported across the genus; not the principal actives here.
- **Note on *V. officinalis* markers:** valerenic acid, the diagnostic marker of European valerian, is characteristic of *V. officinalis* and is **not** the defining constituent of *V. wallichii* — a chemotaxonomic reason not to treat the two drugs as interchangeable.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. `[RESET]` **Wired-and-tired evening down-regulation.** For the sympathetically over-aroused, mentally racing state after high-intensity or high-stress training blocks, Tagara is used as a warming nervine to settle "nervous wind" and ease sleep onset — the *nidrājanana* + *vāta*-pacifying indication. Framed honestly: a mild-to-moderate calmative, additive with other sedatives, not a hypnotic drug.
2. `[CYCLE-OFF]` **Restlessness, palpitation, and agitated recovery.** During deload/recovery phases marked by irritability, palpitation, and shallow sleep, the *medhya* framing is apt — a steadying rather than stupefying calmative, used as a short ritual rather than a nightly crutch.
3. `[RESET]` **Aromatic adjunct in relaxation practice.** Consistent with the Tibetan/Unani scent doctrine, the aroma itself is used to cue parasympathetic wind-down; low pharmacological risk in this mode.

### Scientific Correlation

The honest grade for *V. wallichii* as a sedative/anxiolytic is **LOW-to-MODERATE — plausible mechanism and preclinical support for the valerian group, but sparse species-specific human trial data; the anticonvulsant/"apasmāra" claim is preclinical only.**

According to PubMed, the most decisive species-relevant chemistry paper is Bos R, Hendriks H, Scheffer JJ, Woerdenbag HJ (1998), "Cytotoxic potential of valerian constituents and valerian tinctures," *Phytomedicine* 5(3):219–225 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0944-7113(98)80032-9)). Studying *V. officinalis*, ***V. wallichii* (= *V. jatamansi*)**, and *V. edulis*, it establishes two facts central to this entry. First, the **diene-type valepotriates (valtrate, isovaltrate, acevaltrate) are markedly cytotoxic in vitro** (IC50 ≈ 1–6 µM against human cancer cell lines), with monoene types 2–3× less toxic — a genuine, if in-vitro, safety signal for the fresh-rhizome actives. Second, and practically decisive, **valepotriates decompose on storage into baldrinal/homobaldrinal**, and this decomposition was directly reflected in reduced cytotoxicity of stored tinctures. This is the molecular basis for two doctrinal footnotes: (a) the drug's chemistry is real but **unstable**, so traditional variability in effect is expected, and (b) the in-vitro cytotoxicity is a reason for measured, short-term use rather than reckless high dosing — while noting that the decomposition that lowers cytotoxicity also lowers the presumed sedative principle, so aged material is *weaker*, not obviously *safer* in a useful direction.

The sedative/anxiolytic and preclinical anticonvulsant reputation of the valerian group broadly is consistent with GABAergic modulation, and valeranone/patchouli alcohol and the valepotriates are the plausible actives — but a targeted PubMed search this run did **not** surface a robust, species-specific *V. wallichii* human RCT for sleep, anxiety, or seizures. The defensible statement is therefore: the traditional *nidrājanana*/*vāta*-calming use is mechanistically plausible and supported by genus-level preclinical work, the classical *medhya* (mind-steadying) framing rhymes with a "settling not knockout" pharmacology, and the classical *apasmāra*/anti-seizure claim is **not** validated in humans and should be presented as unproven. Human sedation and anticonvulsant efficacy for this species specifically are **not established** [USER: verify against any newer clinical trials before making an efficacy claim].

### Contraindications

- **Do not treat as interchangeable with European valerian (*V. officinalis*).** Different chemotype (Himalayan material is valepotriate/sesquiterpene-rich, not valerenic-acid-defined), different classical energetics (heating vs cooling). Dose and expectation should not be transferred between the two.
- **Sedative additivity.** The calming effect is real and **additive** with alcohol, benzodiazepines, "Z-drugs," opioids, antihistamines, and other CNS depressants — do not stack on the "herbal = inert" assumption; caution before driving/operating machinery.
- **Valepotriate instability and in-vitro cytotoxicity.** Diene valepotriates are cytotoxic in vitro and chemically unstable; favour short-term use, reputable well-dried material, and avoid high-dose fresh-rhizome preparations. Long-term high-dose safety is **not established** [USER: verify].
- **Pregnancy and lactation.** Insufficient safety data for medicinal doses; classical heating/emmenagogue framing is a further caution — avoid medicinal doses [USER: verify].
- **Adulteration/misidentification.** "Tagara" is frequently adulterated with *Nardostachys jatamansi*, *V. officinalis*, or other Valerianaceae; source-verify before use.
- **Perioperative.** As with valerian generally, discontinue in advance of anaesthesia given the sedative additivity [USER: verify appropriate washout].
