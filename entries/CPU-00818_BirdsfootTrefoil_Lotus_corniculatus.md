# Bird's-foot Trefoil — Bird's-foot, Eggs-and-Bacon, Cat's Claws, Lotus

## *Lotus corniculatus* L. | Family: Fabaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Cool and moist, gently drying at the surface — a mild, "settling" herb in the Galenic-Unani frame; sedative and vulnerary in the folk reckoning.
**Training Phase:** Settling / Calming Phase — a gentle nervine-and-wound herb for the surface and the nerves, handled with respect for its cyanogenic foliage.

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian (AT).** The Mesopotamian herbal tradition drew on the small yellow-flowered legumes of pasture and waste ground as calming and wound-dressing plants — a class of mild "quieting" herbs applied to inflamed sores and taken to soothe agitation. *Lotus corniculatus* belongs to that broad Old World meadow-legume flora; the attribution is general and traditional rather than fixed to a single securely read tablet.

**Dioscorides & the Greek Materia Medica (DMM).** The Greek herbal tradition records *lotos* among the meadow legumes used as poultice and wash for wounds and for cooling inflamed tissue, and mild infusions taken to settle the nerves and encourage sleep. The name *lotos* was applied to several unrelated plants in antiquity, so the specific identity here is the pasture bird's-foot trefoil of the legume flora rather than the lotus of the Nile.

**Avicenna & the Unani synthesis (EP as received through the Canon).** The *Qanun*'s treatment of the mild meadow legumes classes such herbs as cooling, gently astringent and calming — vulnerary washes for sores, and soothing infusions. Bird's-foot trefoil sits within this class of gentle, cooling, settling plants.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The plant's folk names tell its double nature. "Eggs-and-bacon" and "granny's toenails" mark the cheerful yellow-and-orange flowers of the meadow; "bird's-foot" and "cat's claws" mark the clawed seed pods — and, read another way, the hidden hazard, for those same green parts hold cyanide-yielding glycosides. The symbolism of the calming meadow flower is genuine to its traditional sedative use, but the claw in the name is a fair warning: the raw foliage is not innocent.

### Active Components

- **Cyanogenic glycosides:** **linamarin** and **lotaustralin**, concentrated in the foliage — on tissue damage these release hydrogen cyanide (the plant's anti-herbivore defence).
- **Condensed tannins (proanthocyanidins):** abundant; astringent, and (in ruminant nutrition) protein-binding and anthelmintic.
- **Flavonoids:** including glycosides of kaempferol and quercetin.
- **Other:** amino acids and minor coumarin-type constituents typical of forage legumes.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica, bird's-foot trefoil is a **Settling / Calming Phase** herb of two faces. Traditionally it served as (1) a mild nervine — a gentle sedative infusion of the dried, aged flowering tops for restlessness and disturbed sleep — and (2) a vulnerary — a tannin-rich wash or poultice for minor wounds and inflamed skin. The condensed tannins give a plausible astringent/wound rationale; the sedative reputation is folk-attested rather than clinically proven. Kenetica uses only the **dried, properly aged flowering top**, never the fresh green foliage, precisely because drying and wilting dissipate the cyanogenic risk.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the defining modern chemistry of *Lotus corniculatus* is its cyanogenic glycoside content. Chen and colleagues resequenced 272 global accessions and mapped the genetic loci governing cyanogenic-glycoside (linamarin/lotaustralin) accumulation, confirming these compounds as a heritable, variable feature of the species ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12915-023-01670-7)). Salgado and co-workers documented that the plant accumulates cyanogenic glycosides — explicitly naming **lotaustralin and linamarin** — as a defence, with content varying along elevation gradients ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.160226)). On the therapeutic side, Cornara, Xiao and Burlando's review of temperate forage legumes places *L. corniculatus* among Fabaceae whose flavonoids, condensed tannins, coumarins and cyanogenic glycosides underlie anti-inflammatory, anthelmintic and related activities of pharmacological interest ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2015.1038378)).

**Evidence grade: traditional, with well-characterised chemistry.** The cyanogenic and tannin chemistry is thoroughly documented (the plant is a major forage and defence-compound model organism). The traditional sedative and vulnerary uses, however, have not been tested in human clinical trials — the calming reputation rests on folk tradition, not controlled data. The safety hazard (cyanogenesis of the raw foliage) is well established.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** cool and moist, gently astringent and calming; a settling, vulnerary meadow herb.
- **TCM (by analogy, not classical attribution):** a sweet-bland, cool, calming-and-astringent herb that "quiets the spirit" (an nao) and "restrains" a weeping surface — an interpretive mapping, as *L. corniculatus* is not a classical *ben cao* drug.
- **Ayurvedic (interpretive):** rasa — madhura (sweet), kashaya (astringent); virya — cooling (shita); vipaka — pungent (katu); calming to Vata and Pitta. Interpretive, not a classical dravya.

### Contraindications

⚠️ **CYANOGENIC GLYCOSIDES — CYANIDE RISK FROM RAW FOLIAGE.** The green parts of *Lotus corniculatus* contain **linamarin and lotaustralin**, which release hydrogen cyanide when the tissue is crushed, chewed, wilted-but-not-dried, or otherwise damaged. Raw ingestion of the foliage — or of poorly prepared plant material — risks cyanide toxicity (headache, dizziness, rapid breathing, nausea, collapse in severe cases). **Never eat the fresh plant.** Livestock cyanide poisoning on high-cyanogenic stands is documented. Use dried, properly aged flowering tops only, and discard any material with a bitter-almond smell.

- **Pregnancy and lactation:** insufficient safety data and cyanogenic risk — avoid internal use.
- **Children:** do not give internally; keep raw plant out of reach.
- **Do not combine** with any preparation involving fresh, wilted, or improperly dried plant material.
- Discontinue and seek medical help for any symptoms suggestive of cyanide exposure (bitter-almond breath, headache, breathlessness, confusion).
