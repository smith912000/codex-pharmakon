# Porcupine — Ebers Papyrus, "Porcupine" (Bryan's English identification; no Egyptian word given)
## *The porcupine (quills); Bryan 1930 prints only the English word "Porcupine" in his list of animals, with no Egyptian name and no binomial* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The locus is `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` row "Porcupine" (line 99 of that file), which cites "Bryan 1930, Ch. VII Organic Remedies (animals) (line 1392)" and notes "Bryan's English identification; no Egyptian word or binomial given in this list". In the text file (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`) the word stands in Bryan's list of "those animals whose identity is not in doubt, and who were called upon to sacrifice themselves in the cause of the Healing Art" (lines 1388-1398), printed as "Lion, Hippopotamus, Crocodile Ox Cow Calf Mouse Porcupine, Serpent, Tortoise ..." (line 1392). Bryan introduces that list by saying that a complete account of the animal materia medica "would not be possible" because "the identity of a considerable number of the animals mentioned is still beyond us" (line 1388). The identification of the porcupine rests on Bryan's 1930 judgement; the lines read give no Egyptian word for it.

Bryan comments on the animal in his own words: "The Porcupine was an animal which gave them another chance to display that uncanny sense of the fitness of things which we meet with here and there for they found use only for his quills, and then for the sole purpose of stimulating a bald scalp; which, unless porcupines have changed unduly from that day, must have proved very stimulating indeed" (line 1426). That is Bryan's summary and his wit, not a quotation of the papyrus, and "only" is his claim about the whole papyrus, which the lines read cannot confirm.

The remedy itself is described in Bryan's discussion of hair and the scalp: "Alopecia (that is the unsightly bald patches which appear suddenly on the heads of people old and young, male and female) may be noted here although it is treated elsewhere in the Papyrus. It could be attacked in three ways. Quills-of-the-Porcupine could be warmed in Oil and used against it; or Red-lead beaten up in Froth-of-Bitter-Beer. Both were external remedies, one presumes" (line 4059). The third method Bryan gives is a "Broken-up-Figure" warmed in oil with writing fluid in water, and he adds a further alternative of flax plant in oil with wasp's dung. The porcupine remedy is therefore the quills, warmed in oil, for bald patches, and "external" is Bryan's presumption, not a statement of the papyrus.

### Active Components

Porcupine quills are modified hairs made of keratin, the protein of hair and nails, and are barbed in some species. Oil is a vehicle. No active principle is claimed by the sources, and no composition specific to this entry was read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is that Bryan lists the porcupine among the identifiable Ebers animals and reports one remedy: the quills warmed in oil against alopecia (line 4059). The Codex offers no protocol. Hair loss has many causes and needs clinical assessment, and handling quills or hair-like animal material is a splinter and infection risk (see Contraindications). Bryan's remark on the remedy's stimulating effect is a joke, not evidence. [USER: verify]

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparation in the text read is: the quills of the porcupine, "warmed in Oil", "used against" alopecia (line 4059). No quantity, oil type, duration or number of applications is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Hair is made of keratin, and applying keratin or oil to the scalp does not make hair grow; alopecia has medical causes (autoimmune, hormonal, nutritional, infectious, scarring and others) that differ in how they are treated. Porcupine quills can cause painful puncture wounds that carry bacteria. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, here bald patches, not what a substance does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*, and the identity of a named substance is often a modern hypothesis; here the only identification is Bryan's English word. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*), so dropping the recitation would lose half a prescription. In the remedy read (line 4059) Bryan prints no recitation with the quills, which, because the passage is a translator's summary and the papyrus itself was not read, is no evidence either way. No deity is attributed.

### Contraindications

- **Injury and infection:** porcupine quills cause puncture wounds that can become infected and are hard to remove; warmed oil adds a burn risk.
- **Not a treatment for hair loss:** alopecia needs clinical assessment; do not rely on this entry.
- **Legal and conservation status:** porcupines are protected, regulated or endangered in some countries, and taking quills from a live or dead wild animal may be unlawful; quills shed naturally are the lawful form where it is allowed. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** killing an animal for a remedy with no supported benefit is hard to justify.
