# Blood (various animals) — PERI HAIMATON (Dioscorides 2-97), "Blood — CAUTION"
## *The blood of a long list of animals, taken as a drink, eaten fried, or rubbed on; Dioscorides' own chapter on blood, with no single animal and no binomial* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

This entry is the single Dioscorides chapter on blood. Three entries already hold blood from particular animals in the Egyptian and Tibetan texts: donkey blood in `III_18_Donkey_blood.md`, pig blood in `III_23_Pig_blood.md` and deer blood in `III_32_Deer_blood.md`. The deer entry already quotes the Dioscorides line on deer's and goat's blood; it is given here in its place in the chapter, not as a second account of deer blood.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-97 *PERI HAIMATON* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, line 5363, heading and text at lines 5363-5365; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` row "PERI HAIMATON, 2-97 (line 5363)"; Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). The edition prints the heading "Blood — CAUTION"; "CAUTION" is the edition's label, not a statement in the chapter. The chapter lists the blood of many animals and what each is said to do. It states no method of preparation beyond "fried in a pan" and says nothing of dose. In the order the chapter gives them:

- *Goose, duck, drake or kid.* "Usefully mixed with antidotes."
- *Wood dove, turtle, pigeon and partridge.* "Rubbed on new sores on eyes and on eyes that are bloodshot and have dull sight." In particular the pigeon's "stops bleeding from the meninges [membranes of the spinal cord and brain]."
- *He-goat, she-goat, deer or hare.* "Fried in a pan and eaten", stops dysentery and "discharges of the abdominal cavity"; "taken in a drink with wine it is an antidote against poison."
- *Hare.* Rubbed on while warm, "cures sunburn and freckles".
- *Dog.* "Taken as a drink", it is "good for those bitten by a mad dog, or who have taken poison in a drink."
- *Earth tortoise.* "Taken as a drink", "said to be good for epileptics."
- *Sea turtle.* Taken in a drink with wine, hare's rennet and cumin, "good against the bites of venomous creatures, and an antidote for drinking anything hateful or loathsome."
- *Bull.* "Applied with polenta disperses and softens hardness."
- *Stallion horses.* "Mixed with antiseptic medicines."
- *Chamaeleon, and green frogs.* Believed "to make the eyelids hairless".
- *A woman's menstrual blood.* "Rubbed on her (or if she walks over it) is thought to keep her from conception; yet rubbed on it alleviates the pains of gout and erysipela [streptococcal skin infection]."

The wording is Dioscorides' hedged "is said", "is believed", "is thought to". The chapters on either side are 2-96 *Chole pasa* (gall of various animals, line 5352) and 2-98 *Apopatos* (dung, line 5366). The blood of other animals is mentioned in passing elsewhere in the book, and the index lists entries such as "blood of a cat", "blood of an ass" and "blood of a weasel"; I did not open those passages. ### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, blood is a suspension of red cells (haemoglobin, an iron-carrying protein), white cells and platelets in plasma, which carries albumin and other proteins, glucose, lipids, electrolytes and hormones. It clots when shed. Its composition varies by species.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Greek chapter records blood as a drink, a fried food and an applied remedy, with claims across eyes, bleeding, dysentery, bites, poisoning, epilepsy, hardness, sunburn, hair removal and conception. The sources give no outcomes. Raw or fresh blood from an animal is a vehicle for infection, and the Codex gives no protocol for any of it. Menstrual blood as a contraceptive or anti-gout rub has no support and must not be used as a contraceptive.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation in the chapter is that blood of he-goat, she-goat, deer or hare is "fried in a pan and eaten" (2-97, line 5365). Wine is the vehicle for goat, deer and hare blood and for sea turtle blood (with hare's rennet and cumin); polenta is the vehicle for bull's blood. Dog's, earth tortoise's and sea turtle's blood are "taken as a drink"; hare's blood and the menstrual blood are "rubbed on"; for the chamaeleon's and frogs' blood the chapter names no mode. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Blood is a route for bloodborne infection, and raw animal blood can carry bacteria (for example *Salmonella*, *Brucella*), viruses and parasites. Eating blood is common in some cuisines when cooked, and blood is an iron-rich food. Rabies is transmitted by bite and saliva; nothing in blood taken as a drink treats it. ### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** fresh blood from any animal can carry bloodborne pathogens and bacteria; do not drink it raw, apply it to eyes or broken skin, or handle it without precautions.
- **Rabies:** a bite from a possibly rabid animal needs immediate medical treatment, which blood does not replace.
- **Contraception:** menstrual blood is not a contraceptive.
- **Epilepsy, poisoning, venomous bites:** all need emergency medical care.
- **Ethics and law:** several of the animals named (tortoises, sea turtles) are protected in many places, and trade in their parts is restricted.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** avoid raw animal blood.
