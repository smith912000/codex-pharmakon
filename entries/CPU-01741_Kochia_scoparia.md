# Kochia scoparia — Di Fu Zi 地膚子, Fructus Kochiae Scopariae (Shennong Bencao Jing)

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Shennong text (SBJ); this entry rests on one text.** No other text read for this entry names this species. The entry is in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`). The Latin name is the translator's pharmaceutical Latin and was matched to the binomial by stem in the extraction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv`, line 77: "pharmaceutical Latin 'Fructus Kochiae Scopariae' matched by stem to on-disk binomial 'Kochia scoparia'"). The Chinese characters are those of the Codex's own list of Shennong names (`_sources/shennong.csv`, line 55: 地膚子), not of Yang's English text. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk, and of the two Tibetan texts, for the genus name *Kochia* found only Yang's entry, its footnote and the extraction row; a search for *Bassia* found nothing. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Identity note.** As the Codex understands it, some current botanical treatments place this species in the genus *Bassia* and not *Kochia*; the Codex has not checked this against POWO, and the entry keeps Yang's printed Latin and the spine's binomial. [USER: verify] The Latin prints "Fructus", the fruit, as the part.

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *Di Fu Zi, PDF page 52.* The page marker `[[p52]]` is at line 2002, and the heading "Herbs: Superior Class" is printed at line 2003, at the top of that page. The first entry under it is Bai Hao (line 2004 onward); Di Fu Zi is the second, at line 2011, and it ends at line 2015 with the next entry, Shi Long Chu, beginning at line 2016. The whole entry is on this page. The text reads:

"Di Fu Zi (Fructus Kochiae Scopariae)119 is bitter and cold. It mainly treats bladder heat, disinhibits urination, supplements the center, and boosts the essence qi. Protracted taking may sharpen the ears and eyes, make the body light, and slow aging. Its other name is Di Kui (Earth Big Flower). It grows in plains and swamps."

(Lines 2011 to 2015.) The superscript 119 is Yang's footnote number. The footnote (lines 2032 to 2037) is the translator's commentary and not the classic's text. It begins "This short passage summarizes all the actions of Kochia", then lists later clinical uses (including urinary complaints, impotence, "troubles of the testicles" and lumbar pain) and says that "Externally, it can be used in the form of a washing solution" for skin disease. It is a later layer; it is not used here as the Shennong text's claim.

The preface (Book One) says the superior class "mainly nourish life and correspond to heaven", that they are "nontoxic and taking them in large amounts and for a long time will not harm people", and that "if one intends to make one's body light, boost the qi, prevent aging, and prolong life, one should base [one's efforts] on the superior class" (lines 352 to 355 and 383 to 384, across the page break at line 381).

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Kochia*, *Bassia*, "Di Fu Zi", "Fructus Kochiae" and "Di Kui" found no entry on this plant. I_1235 (*Artemisia capillaris*) names the neighbouring entry Bai Hao (line 2004) in a list and is not an entry on this plant. ### Active Components

The entry itself prints no constituent. It is a general fact that *Kochia scoparia* is a plant of the amaranth family (Amaranthaceae, in which the older goosefoot family, Chenopodiaceae, is now included). The sources read do not say what the Shennong drug contained, and no general statement about its constituents is made here.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Shennong entry records a bitter, cold drug that "mainly treats bladder heat, disinhibits urination, supplements the center, and boosts the essence qi", and whose protracted taking "may sharpen the ears and eyes, make the body light, and slow aging". These are the text's claims in its own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and does not translate the entry's terms into modern diagnoses.

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: the superior class (上品), under the page heading, not directly below it.* The class is assigned by the printed heading "Herbs: Superior Class" (line 2003, the top of `[[p52]]`). Di Fu Zi is the second entry on that page, after Bai Hao, so the grade rests on the page heading plus the entry's place after Bai Hao, and not on a heading printed immediately above the entry. No other class heading is printed between the heading and the entry, and the next "Herbs: Superior Class" heading is at line 2075 on a later page. In the preface the superior class is the class of the substances taken for a long time to nourish life, and the only class the preface ties to making the body light, boosting the qi, preventing aging and prolonging life (lines 352 to 355 and 383 to 384).

The entry's own words fit that frame: "Protracted taking may sharpen the ears and eyes, make the body light, and slow aging." "Make the body light" is the Shennong effect category that the Codex does not translate into a pharmacological one, and it is not weight loss. The entry also says the drug "boosts the essence qi"; the Codex records the phrase as Yang prints it, and has not checked it against the Chinese text, so it does not read any further meaning into "essence". [USER: verify] The entry names no *jing*, *shen* or *neidan* action, and none is added here. It is also a drug with urinary indications in the same paragraph, and the translator's footnote is a later clinical layer and not the Shennong text's grade.

**Practitioner's note.** A superior-class entry in the Chinese scheme, and the only class the preface ties to long-term, life-nourishing use. The entry itself is brief and gives no dose, no preparation and no duration, so nothing here supports taking the fruit as an ongoing practice on the strength of the grade. A cultivator can read the entry for what the classic says (a superior-class drug whose long use "may" sharpen the ears and eyes and slow aging) and leave it there. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The entry prints no warning and no toxicity statement; the preface says the superior class is nontoxic and will not harm people even in large amounts and for a long time, a general statement about the class and not a safety finding for this fruit. No dose, preparation or safety information is given for it, and the identity is the translator's pharmaceutical Latin. Do not gather or take a plant on the strength of this entry.
