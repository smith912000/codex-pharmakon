# Echinops sphaerocephalus — Chamaileon melas (Dioscorides 3-11)

## *Echinops sphaerocephalus (the translators' suggestion, from Linnaeus on the SUGGESTED line; "Globe Thistle", glossed "other usage", with the chapter's own plant called "Black Chamoeleon")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed under the chapter heading, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED block, which runs over three lines, reads "Chamaeleon niger, Spina incognita [Fuchs], Carduus sphaerocephalus [Bauhin], Echinops sphaerocephalus [Linnaeus] — Globe Thistle [other usage] Cardopatium corymbosum, Carthamus corymbosum, Brotera corymbosa — Black Chamoeleon". The words "other usage" in the translators' block apply to the second group of names, and the translators do not give one plant. The row takes the one Linnaean binomial on the first line. It has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *3-11. CHAMAILEON MELAS* at line 6630, SUGGESTED lines at 6631 to 6633, text at line 6634. The chapter stands in Book Three, "Roots". The translators' name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 417. The next chapter, 3-12 *KROKODEILION* (line 6635), is a different plant.

The whole chapter reads: "Black chamaeleon has leaves similar to scolymus hispanicus, yet they are smaller, thinner and distinguished with red. It sends out a stalk the thickness of a finger, twenty centimetres high, somewhat red, with a tuft and prickly flowers — small, similar to hyacinth, variously-coloured. The root is thick, black, compact and sometimes eaten-into. When cut it is a pale yellow, and is biting when chewed. It grows in dry rocky grounds and places bordering the sea. The root (pounded into small pieces) is mixed with a little cobblers' ink, cedar oil and swines' grease, and used to remove parasitic skin diseases. It cleans away lichen [papular skin disease], boiled with vinegar and rubbed on (with the addition of sulphur and bitumen). It is used as a mouthwash, and a decoction of it soothes toothache. Wrapped in same amount of pepper and wax it helps pained teeth. Teeth are preserved if it is boiled with vinegar and poured on them. Conveyed warm through a quill [straw] it breaks a sore tooth. It cleans away vitiligines [form of leprosy] and sunburn, is mixed with ripening medicines, and applied, heals spreading wild ulcers, destroying them. It is called chamaeleon because of the various colours of the leaves. For these vary, differing according to the place, often green, pale, azure-coloured [blue], or red. It is also called pancarpon, ulophonum, ixia, cynomachon, ocimoides, cnidium coccum, or cynoxylon; the Latins call it carduus nutans niger, some, vernilago, and the Egyptians, sobel."

The description gives leaves like *scolymus hispanicus* but smaller, thinner and marked with red, a finger-thick reddish stalk twenty centimetres high ending in a tuft, small prickly flowers of several colours, and a thick black compact root that is sometimes eaten into, pale yellow when cut and "biting when chewed". It grows in dry rocky ground and near the sea. The uses are external or of the mouth: the pounded root mixed with cobblers' ink, cedar oil and swines' grease against parasitic skin diseases; boiled with vinegar, with sulphur and bitumen added, against lichen; a mouthwash and decoction for toothache; teeth wrapped or washed with it; and an application against vitiligines, sunburn and spreading ulcers. The name is explained by the changing colour of the leaves with the place. Seven Greek names, two Latin names and one Egyptian name, *sobel*, are printed.

The translators' mention of *Echinops* is the only link between this chapter and the globe thistles. The existing entries I_750 (*Echinops ritro*) and I_567 (*Echinops grijisii*) describe the genus in terms of urine, swellings and milk, and do not cite this chapter. They are not merged here. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a thistle root applied to the skin and used in the mouth for parasitic skin disease, lichen, vitiligines, sunburn, ulcers and toothache. The Codex gives no protocol. Several of the mixtures contain sulphur, bitumen or a cobblers' ink that would not now be applied to skin or teeth, and the identification is uncertain.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these preparations: the root pounded and mixed with "a little cobblers' ink, cedar oil and swines' grease"; boiled with vinegar and rubbed on, "with the addition of sulphur and bitumen"; a mouthwash and a decoction; the root "wrapped in same amount of pepper and wax"; boiled with vinegar and poured on the teeth; and conveyed "warm through a quill". No quantity is given beyond "a little" and "same amount". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality. The one taste it prints is of the root: "biting when chewed". The entry does not assign a further quality.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition: a leaf compared with a known plant, a stalk measured against a finger and in centimetres, the root's colour outside and when cut and its taste when chewed, the ground it grows in, and, in the explanation of the name, the observation that the leaf colour changes with the place. The text does not draw a rule from that observation. It assigns the plant no status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. What the text teaches is how to look at a plant that changes its colour with the ground, and its remedies are for the skin and teeth. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning of its own, but several mixtures it names contain sulphur, bitumen or ink and are not safe to apply to the mouth. Toothache, ulcers and skin disease need proper care and should not be treated with an unidentified wild thistle. The identification is only the translators' suggestion. No safety information for pregnancy was read. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
