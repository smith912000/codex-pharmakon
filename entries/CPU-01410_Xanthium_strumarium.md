### Origin & Tradition

**Identity note.** This row was added 2026-10-09 (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP). The spellings *Xanthium sibiricum* and *Xanthium strumarium* are merged here as printed; the row has not been checked against a modern botanical authority. [USER: verify] Two cautions. First, the Shennong name is a pharmaceutical Latin name given by the translator Yang, and the extraction matched it to the binomial by stem. Second, in Clark's Tibetan entry the first-listed plant is *Fagonia cretica*, not *Xanthium*, and the bracketed description there may be of *Fagonia* (see below).

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** The text is `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt` (Yang 1998). The entry stands on PDF page 78 (marker `[[p78]]`, entry at line 2909), in the section headed "Herbs: Middle Class" (heading at line 2873, marker `[[p77]]`). It reads:

"Xi Er Shi (Fructus Xanthii Sibirici)174 is sweet and warm. It mainly treats wind and cold headache, wind damp generalized impediment, hypertonicity and pain of the limbs, and malign flesh and dead muscles. Protracted taking may boost the qi, sharpen the ears and eyes, strengthen the will, and make the body light. Its other name is Hu Xi (Nomad's Earring). Another name is Di Kui (Earth Big Flower). It grows in rivers and valleys."

Yang's note 174 (line 2939) reads: "The current name of this medicinal is Cang Er." The same text's general preface (lines 383-389) describes the middle class: "There are 120 medium class medicinals which are used as ministers. They mainly nurture personality and correspond to humanity. They may or may not be toxic, and [therefore,] one should weigh and ponder before putting them to their appropriate use. If one intends to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion, one should base [one's efforts] on the middle class."

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch.20* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, line 1080): "byi.tsher: Fagonia cretica Linn: Xanthium sibiricum Patr.: Xanthium strumarium: [has three names and grows in sandy areas. It has a small, dark, thick leaf, short, thick, hard, dark yellowishgreen stalk and burrs which stick to one's clothes. Fagonia has bitter taste, coarse power,] cures infectious fever, poisoning, kidney fever [and relieves urinary retention]." The bracket is Clark's mark for commentarial text. The bracket says "Fagonia has bitter taste, coarse power", so the taste and power are stated for *Fagonia* by name; burrs that stick to clothes fit *Xanthium* by general knowledge. Which plant the description and uses refer to is not settled by the line. The extraction row is `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, row 176.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011*, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, lines 5335-5352). Under J the glossary prints the phonetic names Jagoepoe, Jakang, Jeruk, Ji tsher, Jhapo Tsi tsi, Jhew laphuk, Jitanga; the transliterations bya rgod spos, bya rkang, byi rug, *byi tsher*, bya pho rtsi rtsi, byi'u la phug, byi tang ga; the scientific names *Delphinium brunonianum*, *Delphinium himalayi*, *Mentha longifolia*, *Xanthium strumarium* (line 5345), *Pisum sativum*, *Torularia humilis*, *Embelia ribes*; and the common names "Larkspur", "Larkspur", "Horsemint", "Cocklebur". Read in column order, *byi tsher* is the fourth name and sits against *Xanthium strumarium* and "Cocklebur", as does Clark's byi.tsher. The pairing is by position and is corroborated by Clark's name. (The extraction note on names.csv row 291 says the Tibetan name is not paired, and for this row Clark pairs it.)

### Active Components

*Xanthium strumarium* is generally known to contain sesquiterpene lactones (xanthatin and related compounds) and the toxic glycoside carboxyatractyloside, the latter concentrated in seedlings and seeds. The constituents of the materials named in the two texts were not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The Shennong text records a sweet, warm fruit used for headache, limb pain and "malign flesh", with long-term effects stated in the text's own terms. The Tibetan text records a plant against infectious fever, poisoning and kidney fever. These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. The plant has toxic parts, and the Codex gives no protocol for it.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither entry read gives a preparation. The Shennong line says "Protracted taking", with no quantity or form, and Clark line 1080 gives no preparation. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

The cocklebur plant is a recognised toxic plant for livestock and people when raw seedlings or seeds are eaten. This is a general statement and no study was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SBJ:** "sweet and warm" (line 2909 onward); middle class.
- **FMT:** Clark line 1080 prints "bitter taste, coarse power" in a sentence about *Fagonia*. No taste or potency is attributed to *Xanthium* separately.

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese — Shennong (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: middle class (中品 in the tradition's script, *zhong pin*; "Herbs: Middle Class" at Yang line 2873).* Yang's preface (lines 383-389) says the middle class "mainly nurture personality", corresponds to humanity, may or may not be toxic and calls for weighing before use, and is the class to base one's efforts on to "control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion". It is neither the upper class of long-term life-nourishing drugs nor the lower class of short-course drugs. The entry's own words, "Protracted taking may boost the qi, sharpen the ears and eyes, strengthen the will, and make the body light", are the text's. The phrase "make the body light" (*qing shen*) has no pharmacological equivalent and is not translated into one here. No claim about *jing* is made by the text for this plant. The grade belongs to this text and is not carried into the Tibetan paragraph.

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra lists byi.tsher among the plants of ch.20 by its action on infectious fever, poisoning and kidney fever. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice and not reaching the root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. Nothing read assigns this plant a place in *bcud len*. The identification with *Xanthium* rests on the glossary and on the third of Clark's three binomials. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** In the Shennong frame this is a middle-class drug, to be weighed against the complaint and taken for a purpose, not a long-term tonic, and the text's remark that protracted taking has benefits is the text's, not a recommendation. The plant has toxic parts and the Codex offers no preparation. The Tibetan record gives a cultivator no practice.

### Contraindications

Raw cocklebur seedlings and seeds are toxic (general knowledge, not read in the sources). The Shennong preface itself says that middle-class medicinals "may or may not be toxic" and call for care. Do not use in pregnancy. Do not self-prescribe for poisoning or fever. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
