### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 9458) prints two names with their namers, the second being Linnaeus. It was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. **Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-158. SPARTION*, heading at line 9457, SUGGESTED line at 9458, text at line 9459. The text is interrupted for several lines by page numbers (712, 713 and 714), running heads and two plate captions, "Vitis alba after FUCHS — 1545" and "Vitis nigra after FUCHS — 1545" (lines 9460 to 9470; the plates show vines, not this plant, and the text does not say why they fall here), and resumes at line 9471. The next chapter is *4-159. SILUBON* (line 9472), a thistle by the translators' suggestion. The chapter stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), after *4-157. THAPSIA* (line 9447). The Codex's own name extraction lists it (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 736); the translators' general index prints "Spartium junceum 712" (line 17643).

The whole chapter reads: "Spartium is a shrub with long stems without leaves, strong, hard to break, with which they bind vines. It bears pods like phaseoli [2-130], in which are little seeds like lentils, and the flower is yellowish like leucoion [3-138]. A decoction of fifty grains of the seed of this with its flowers (taken as a drink with honey and water) purges upward with violence like hellebore, yet without danger. The seed induces purging downwards. The stems (steeped in water then pounded and juiced) help sciatica and abscessed throat, as much as a winecupful taken as a drink while fasting. Some steep it in brine or seawater as suppositories for sciatica, for it expels bloody and strimentous stuff. It is also called lobon, or lygon."

The chapter gives a description (a shrub with long, leafless, strong stems, "hard to break", used to bind vines; pods like those of *phaseoli*, chapter 2-130, holding little seeds like lentils; a yellowish flower like *leucoion*, chapter 3-138) and a set of uses: a decoction of "fifty grains of the seed" with its flowers, in honey and water, "purges upward with violence like hellebore, yet without danger"; the seed alone "induces purging downwards"; the stems, steeped, pounded and juiced, taken "as much as a winecupful" fasting, help sciatica and "abscessed throat"; and some steep it in brine or seawater as suppositories for sciatica, "for it expels bloody and strimentous stuff". Other names are *lobon* and *lygon*. The use of the stems to bind vines is a use of the plant in the vineyard and not a medicine. The text calls the purge safe ("yet without danger") in the same sentence in which it compares it to hellebore and says it works "with violence"; the Codex records both phrases as printed and does not decide between them. ### Active Components

The chapter prints none. It is a general fact that plants of the broom group in the pea family (Fabaceae) are known to contain quinolizidine alkaloids, among them sparteine, and that these are active on the heart, on the gut and on the uterus and are toxic in excess. Whether this applies to the plant of 4-158 rests on the translators' suggestion; the composition of the plant Dioscorides describes was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides reports a seed and flower decoction that purges upward "with violence like hellebore, yet without danger", a seed that purges downwards, a stem juice for sciatica and abscessed throat, and suppositories of the stems steeped in brine for sciatica. These are the text's reports. The identification is the translators', and a plant that the text itself compares to hellebore for violence is not one to try; the Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-158 gives these preparations and amounts. "A decoction of fifty grains of the seed of this with its flowers (taken as a drink with honey and water)" (line 9459); the stems "steeped in water then pounded and juiced", "as much as a winecupful taken as a drink while fasting" (lines 9459 and 9471); and the stems steeped "in brine or seawater as suppositories" (line 9471). These are the text's traditional quantities and not modern ones; the weight of a *grain* and the volume of a winecupful are not given in the text read. No frequency or duration is given. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no taste and no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality. It prints an action in the sentence "purges upward with violence like hellebore, yet without danger", which is an action and not a quality.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use; *lobon* and *lygon* are names only. What it offers is of the kind the cultivation spec calls a discipline of attention: the habit of the shrub (long, leafless, hard to break), its everyday use to tie vines, the pods, seeds and flower compared with three named plants, the parts used (seed with flowers, seed alone, stems), the form (decoction, juice, suppository), a measure for the first two and the timing "while fasting". The plant is given no status beyond that of a purge and a remedy for sciatica. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. What the chapter shows is a purge that its own author compares to hellebore, and the discipline that applies is restraint. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter describes a purge "upward with violence", that is, an emetic, and broom-group plants are generally known to contain active alkaloids; vomiting and strong purging can cause dehydration and harm, and the text's amounts (fifty grains, a winecupful) cannot be converted with confidence. The identity of the plant rests on a translators' suggestion, so no wild plant should be gathered or taken on the strength of this entry. Sciatica and abscessed throat need medical assessment. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
