# Trapa bispinosa — S'ringataka (Sushruta); Malabathron and Malabathrinon (Dioscorides, translator's suggestion)

## *Trapa bispinosa Roxb., the singhara or water chestnut, an aquatic plant with horned fruits; for Sushruta the identification is the translator's Appendix equation with S'ringataka (positional, medium confidence); for Dioscorides it is only one of several names the translator suggests for malabathron (1-11) and is not a statement of the text, so low confidence* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Two separate attestations under one Latin name, and they do not rest on the same footing.** In Sushruta the Latin name is the translator's equation for a Sanskrit drug that the text uses. In Dioscorides the Latin name occurs only in the translator's "SUGGESTED" line above chapters on a different substance, malabathron.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The name stands in the heading of two chapters of the English *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). At 1-11, MALABATHRON (line 3647), the translator's line reads "SUGGESTED: Trapa bicornis — Ling Nut Trapa bispinosa — Singhara Nut / Trapa quadrispinosa — Water Chestnut sp Limnantheum indicum, Nymphoides indica — Water Snowflake" (lines 3648-3649). At 1-76, MALABATHRINON (line 4118), the same list appears with *Callitriche* species added (lines 4119-4121), and the chapter text is only "see 1-11" and "Malabathrinum or foliatum that is thickened like nardinum [1-75] has more myrrh, thus it is warming and equal to crocinum [1-64] or amaracinum [1-68]" (line 4122-4123; this is the oil, not the plant).

What Dioscorides says at 1-11 (line 3650 onward): some imagine malabathrum to be the leaf of the Indian nardus [1-6]; "this is not so for it is a particular herb that grows in the Indian marshes with the leaves swimming on the water like the palustris lens [4-88] in the marshes, with no root." They pierce the gathered leaves with a linen thread, dry them and preserve them. When summer dries the water the earth is burnt with the shoots, "and unless this happens it will spring up no more." The best is "new and a pale white inclining to blackness, hard to break, sound, biting the nose with its smell", with a long-lasting sweet smell; it tastes like nardus [1-6] without any salt taste; weak, mouldy or crumbling material is worthless. "It has the same properties as nardus but does everything more forcibly. Malabathrum is more diuretic and better for the stomach." Pounded small and boiled in wine it helps inflammation of the eyes when rubbed on; it is put under the tongue for sweetness of the breath, and among cloths it keeps off moths and scents them. The text does not name a water chestnut or any fruit. The translator's Trapa and water-snowflake suggestions are modern guesses for a floating-leaved marsh herb. Whether the Codex's other treatment of the Indian nard (CPU-00001) should carry this chapter is a question for the spine and is not decided here. [USER: verify]

**Sushruta (SS).** The Latin name is in "Appendix No. 3. Names of Drugs (with Latin synonyms)" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`, line 32986): ".  Trapa  Bispinosa." In the S section the Sanskrit list reads *Sravani*, *Sriga'la-kantaka*, *Sriga'la-koli*, *Srikhanda*, *S'ringa'taka* (line 32916), *S'ringavera* (32917), and the Latin list reads "Sphaeranthus Indicus" (32982), "Argemone Mexicana", "Zizyplus Oenoplia", "Yellow variety of Sandal-wood" (32985), "Trapa Bispinosa" (32986), "Gingiber officinal." (32987). The pairing with S'ringavera and ginger is clear, so Trapa is paired with S'ringataka. The OCR separates the columns, so this is a positional pairing. [USER: verify against the printed page.]

The text itself uses S'ringataka in six places: as a food for the nursing mother, with *Kas'eruka* (Sharira ch. 10, `sharira/SS_Sharira_ch10.txt` line 484); in a *kalka* with S'aluka and Kas'eru, mixed with boiled milk, for the pregnant woman (same chapter, line 776); in a list of substitutes for astringent preparations in the chapter on Prameha (`chikitsa/SS_Chikitsa_ch11.txt` line 195); in an oil cooked with *Vidari*, *Yashti-madhu* and *Kas'eruka* given as an errhine (`chikitsa/SS_Chikitsa_ch22.txt` line 274); in a compound for a Vasti (`chikitsa/SS_Chikitsa_ch38.txt` line 389); and, in the Gulma chapter, "aquatic fruits such as S'ringa'taka" taken with sugar to relieve Pittaja Sula (`uttara/SS_Uttara_ch42.txt` line 605). The last passage is what supports reading it as a fruit.

### Active Components

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.** For Sushruta, S'ringataka is an aquatic fruit used in diets and compounds and named among the foods taken against Pittaja Sula; for Dioscorides, malabathron is a scented leaf. The two should not be merged into one use. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

Dioscorides: leaves strung and dried; pounded and boiled in wine for the eyes (1-11). Sushruta: the preparations above, as part of compounds. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. *Trapa* species are floating aquatic plants whose fruits are eaten as water chestnuts. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** For malabathron: "the same properties as nardus but does everything more forcibly", "more diuretic and better for the stomach" (1-11). The oil is "warming" (1-76).
- **SS.** No rasa, virya or vipaka is printed for S'ringataka in the lines read.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What the chapter does offer is a discipline of attention: where it grows, how it is gathered and dried, and a sensory test (colour, hardness, smell, mould). That is a craft of recognising good material, and it concerns malabathron, not a water chestnut.

**Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed.* S'ringataka sits in the diet and compounds for the nursing and pregnant woman and in a diet taken against Pittaja Sula. No *rasayana* or *vajikarana* chapter was read naming it, so it carries a therapeutic status and no cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do. If water chestnut is eaten as food, that is food; the texts do not turn it into a practice.

### Contraindications

- **Identity.** The Greek attestation is a guess about another substance. Do not treat malabathron as a water chestnut.
- **Raw aquatic plants.** Raw water plants and fruits can carry parasites; cook them. [USER: verify]
- **Pregnancy, lactation.** Sushruta recommends it for them in the passages above, but the Codex gives no advice. [USER: verify]
