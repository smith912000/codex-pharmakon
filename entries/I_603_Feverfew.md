# I-603 | Feverfew

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary designation:** *Tanacetum parthenium* (L.) Sch.Bip. *(syn. Chrysanthemum parthenium* (L.) Bernh.; *Pyrethrum parthenium* (L.) Sm.; *Matricaria parthenium* L.)

**Taxonomic position:** Asteraceae; tribe Anthemideae — same tribe as Chamomile (I-602, *Matricaria*), Yarrow (*Achillea*, I-604), Wormwood (*Artemisia absinthium*, I-605), and Sweet Wormwood (*Artemisia annua*, I-606); the Anthemideae contains many of the most pharmacologically important Asteraceae species; *Tanacetum* includes Tansy (*T. vulgare*, toxic), Costmary (*T. balsamita*), and Feverfew itself

**Nomenclature derivation:**
- **Feverfew:** from Latin *febrifuga* = fever-reducer; this name predates the modern clinical evidence, which shows that feverfew is NOT primarily effective for fever reduction — the name reflects the original medieval indication that proved pharmacologically inaccurate; the modern validated indication (migraine prevention) was discovered through patient self-reports in the 20th century
- *Parthenium:* possibly from Greek *parthenos* (virgin/maiden) — reportedly used in the Byzantine period to save a workman who fell from the Parthenon during its construction; the name may connect to the Parthenon/Athens or to its use in female conditions
- English synonyms: Bachelor's Buttons, Wild Chamomile (confused with *Matricaria*), Featherfew

**Botanical description:** Aromatic perennial herb to 60 cm; strongly aromatic when crushed (distinctive bitter-camphoraceous scent, unlike the apple note of chamomile); alternate pinnately-divided leaves; flower heads in dense corymbs; small daisy-like flowers (white ray florets; yellow disc florets; smaller than *Matricaria*); widely naturalized across Europe and the Americas; commonly grown in gardens; the crushed leaf odor is immediately recognizable and distinct from all other Asteraceae

**Pharmaceutical part:** **Leaves** (*Folium Tanaceti Parthenii*) — the dried leaves, preferably harvested before flowering when parthenolide content is highest; some clinical trials use whole aerial parts or standardized extracts calibrated to parthenolide content; standardization: ≥0.2% parthenolide (most clinical trials); Canadian regulatory standard ≥0.2%; European Pharmacopoeia monograph exists

**Distribution of active compound:** Parthenolide content varies dramatically with growing conditions (climate, soil, light), genetic variety, and harvest timing; imported Mexican feverfew often has very low parthenolide content; UK-grown and standardized Canadian preparations most reliable

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

Feverfew is a case where the traditional indication (fever) is pharmacologically inaccurate, and the validated modern indication (migraine prevention) was discovered through systematic observation of patient self-medication behavior in 20th century Britain.

**The self-medication discovery (1970s Britain):** Dr. Anne Jenkins, a physician in Wales, received a report from a migraine patient who had stopped her pharmaceutical prophylactics in favor of eating 3 fresh feverfew leaves daily (a long-standing English folk remedy). The patient's migraine frequency dropped dramatically. Jenkins and later Esther Carbonnel systematically investigated this folk claim, leading to the first clinical trials (Johnson 1985, Murchison 1988) that established feverfew as a migraine prophylactic. This is a textbook example of patient-reported folk practice leading to pharmacological discovery.

**The fever misnomer:** The name *febrifuga* (fever-reducer) has persisted for centuries despite evidence that feverfew is not particularly effective for fever. The anti-inflammatory and antipyretic effects are modest compared to aspirin or even chamomile. The name is a historical artifact that has created expectations the plant cannot fully meet — and simultaneously obscured its true indication (migraine prophylaxis).

**Parthenolide and the migraine pathophysiology:**  The discovery that parthenolide inhibits platelet aggregation (relevant to the vascular theory of migraine), inhibits NF-κB (relevant to the neuroinflammatory theory), and blocks serotonin release from platelets (linking to the serotonin theory of migraine) occurred as migraine pathophysiology itself was being worked out in the 1980s–2000s. Feverfew's pharmacology tracks the evolving scientific understanding of migraine with unusual precision.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Sesquiterpene lactones (primary active class):**
- **Parthenolide** (germacranolide sesquiterpene lactone): 0.1–1.0% dried leaf weight; the marker compound and primary bioactive; contains an α-methylene-γ-butyrolactone group and an epoxide — these electrophilic groups are essential for activity and also responsible for contact dermatitis potential
- **Artemorin:** minor sesquiterpene lactone in some varieties
- **Canin:** sesquiterpene lactone; present in some accessions
- **Costunolide:** related sesquiterpene lactone; anti-inflammatory; precursor to some parthenolide

**Sesquiterpene lactone mechanism motif:** The α-methylene-γ-butyrolactone (Michael acceptor) reacts covalently with thiols in biological systems — particularly the cysteine residues of IκBα (NF-κB inhibitory protein) and phosphoglycerate mutase 1 (PGAM1). This covalent modification mechanism is shared by many sesquiterpene lactones (artemisinin analogs, helenalin from Arnica) and connects feverfew to the broader sesquiterpene lactone pharmacophore.

**Volatile oils (0.02–0.07%):**
- **Camphor:** 30–40% of EO; responsible for the strong bitter-camphoraceous aroma; mildly toxic (see safety)
- **Chrysanthenyl acetate**
- **Bornyl acetate**
- **Sabinene**

**Flavonoids:**
- **Luteolin** and **luteolin-7-glucoside:** anti-inflammatory; COX inhibition
- **Apigenin:** (same as chamomile) GABA-A modulation
- **Chrysoeriol**
- **Tanetin** (6-hydroxykaempferol 3,7,4'-trimethylether): characteristic of Tanacetum

**Polyacetylenes:** minor; antimicrobial

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5A. Parthenolide — NF-κB Inhibition (Primary Anti-Inflammatory Mechanism)

**IκBα alkylation:**
- Parthenolide's α-methylene-γ-butyrolactone group → Michael addition to Cys179 of IκB kinase β (IKKβ) → IKKβ inhibition → IκBα not phosphorylated → IκBα not ubiquitinated for proteasomal degradation → IκBα retains NF-κB in cytoplasm → reduced NF-κB target gene expression (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, iNOS, COX-2)

**Why NF-κB inhibition matters for migraine:**
- Trigeminal ganglia neurons express NF-κB; NF-κB activation → CGRP (calcitonin gene-related peptide) upregulation → vasodilation → meningeal neurogenic inflammation → migraine
- Chronic NF-κB inhibition in trigeminal neurons → reduced CGRP baseline expression → reduced migraine susceptibility
- This is mechanistically consistent with feverfew's effect as a PROPHYLACTIC (requires weeks-months), not an acute treatment

**PGAM1 inhibition (unique to parthenolide):**
- Phosphoglycerate mutase 1 (PGAM1) is a glycolytic enzyme; parthenolide covalently modifies PGAM1
- PGAM1 inhibition → reduced metabolic flux → anti-tumor effects (in vitro); relevance to migraine pathway unclear

### 5B. Platelet Aggregation Inhibition (Vascular Migraine Theory)

- Parthenolide → inhibits platelet aggregation induced by ADP, collagen, and arachidonate
- Mechanism: inhibition of arachidonic acid release from platelet membranes (phospholipase A₂ inhibition) → reduced TXA₂ synthesis → less platelet aggregation
- Inhibits serotonin release from platelets (previously activated platelets release stored serotonin, which causes cerebrovascular constriction followed by dilation — the "vascular theory" of migraine)
- This anti-platelet, anti-serotonin release mechanism was the primary mechanistic focus of early migraine-feverfew research (1980s)

### 5C. CGRP Antagonism / Trigeminal Sensitization Reduction

- Parthenolide → reduced CGRP gene expression (via NF-κB inhibition)
- Relevance: erenumab (FDA 2018), fremanezumab, galcanezumab — all target CGRP or CGRP receptor for migraine prevention; feverfew achieves CGRP reduction via a different upstream mechanism
- Feverfew → reduced central sensitization of trigeminal neurons over time → migraine threshold elevation

### 5D. Smooth Muscle Effects

- Parthenolide → inhibits arachidonate-induced smooth muscle contraction in vascular preparations
- Potential relevance: cerebrovascular tone; antianginal (traditional use); modest

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

| Indication | Evidence Level | Key Data |
|------------|----------------|----------|
| Migraine prevention | **A** | Murphy 1988 RCT (n=72; feverfew vs. placebo; 24% reduction in migraine frequency); Palevitch 1997 (n=57; 63 mg dried feverfew leaf; 24% reduction); Ernst 2004 meta-analysis (5 RCTs Level A; modest but consistent effect); Pittler 2004 Cochrane (conflicting results — standardization issue) |
| Migraine acute treatment | **No evidence** | NOT effective for acute migraine; only prophylactic |
| Rheumatoid arthritis | **B** | Pattrick 1989 RCT (n=41; no benefit vs. placebo); single negative trial; insufficient evidence |
| Fever (historical indication) | **D** | Not supported by clinical evidence; mechanism weak |
| Cancer (parthenolide) | **D** | Parthenolide leukemia stem cell targeting in vitro; dimethylaminoparthenolide (DMAPT) in development; no clinical evidence |

**Standardization caveat:** Clinical trials used 50–100 mg dried leaf powder (0.2–0.6% parthenolide) or 250 mg standardized extract (≥0.2% parthenolide). Products without standardization show highly variable efficacy — the negative Cochrane review reflects in part the poor parthenolide content of many trial preparations.

**Comparison with standard migraine prophylactics:**
- Propranolol/metoprolol: 40–50% reduction in migraine frequency (Level A)
- Topiramate: 40–50% reduction (Level A)
- CGRP antibodies (erenumab): 50–60% reduction (Level A)
- **Feverfew: 24% reduction (Level A)** — modest but real; appropriate for patients preferring botanical prophylaxis with mild migraine burden

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The bitter leaf:** The folk tradition of eating 3 fresh feverfew leaves daily (between slices of bread to reduce the intense bitterness and contact stomatitis risk) is one of Britain's most enduring self-medication practices. The discovery of feverfew's migraine efficacy through this folk use is a model for how traditional practice can generate pharmacological leads: a patient population of migraineurs independently selecting a cheap, accessible plant remedy, noticing benefit over years, and reporting this to a physician curious enough to investigate.

**Contact dermatitis — the mouth ulcer problem:** Parthenolide's Michael-acceptor chemistry is the same chemistry that causes allergic contact dermatitis. Eating fresh feverfew leaves → parthenolide contact with oral mucosa → mouth ulcers and mucosal inflammation in ~10–18% of users. This is not a toxicity in the systemic sense but a pharmacological consequence of the same electrophilic chemistry that inhibits NF-κB. Dried, processed leaf or standardized extract causes significantly less mucosal irritation.

**The naming accident:** The persistence of "feverfew" for a plant that doesn't particularly treat fever while being validated for migraine is a reminder that historical names can mislead. Every pharmacist who sees a patient buying feverfew "for fever" is navigating this historical mislabeling. The French name *Grande Camomille* creates different confusion (it's not chamomile), while the German *Mutterkraut* (mother's herb) reflects the historical gynecological use that Dioscorides described — a completely different indication, also not its primary modern use.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Migraine prophylaxis (primary indication):**
- Standardized dried leaf extract: 250 mg/day (≥0.2% parthenolide = 0.5 mg parthenolide/day)
- Alternative: 50–100 mg high-quality dried leaf powder standardized to parthenolide content
- **Duration:** minimum 3–4 months continuous use before assessing efficacy; prophylaxis must be ongoing to maintain effect
- **If stopping:** taper slowly — abrupt cessation associated with "post-feverfew syndrome" (rebound headache, joint pain, insomnia reported in case series); taper over 2–4 weeks
- **Expectation:** ~24% reduction in migraine frequency; NOT effective for acute migraine attacks; appropriate for patients with 2–4 migraines per month who want botanical prophylaxis

**Combination:** Can be used alongside magnesium (widely used migraine prophylactic; different mechanism) and riboflavin (vitamin B₂ 400 mg/day; Level A migraine prophylactic); no interactions

**Selecting product quality:**
- Require certificate of analysis confirming ≥0.2% parthenolide by HPLC
- UK, Canadian, German standardized products most reliable
- Fresh leaf in bread is the historical British folk form; associated with higher mucosal side effects

**Safety:**
- **Pregnancy absolute contraindication:** parthenolide → uterine contractions (oxytocic); traditional use for menstrual stimulation predicts this risk; avoid entirely in pregnancy
- **Breastfeeding:** insufficient data; avoid
- **Contact dermatitis/mouth ulcers:** 10–18% with fresh leaf; lower with dried/standardized; cease if persistent oral ulceration
- **Children:** insufficient data; avoid under 12
- **Surgery:** cease 2 weeks pre-operatively (anti-platelet effect)
- **Anticoagulants:** additive effect with warfarin; monitor INR
- **NSAIDs:** reduced efficacy possible (competing arachidonate pathway effects)
- **Asteraceae allergy:** standard cross-reactivity concern

**Cross-reference:** I-602 Chamomile (same Anthemideae tribe; different primary indication — anxiety/GI vs. migraine; apigenin shared compound); I-604 Yarrow (next Anthemideae entry — achilleine hemostasis vs. feverfew anti-platelet; contrasting effects on hemostasis within same tribe); I-582 Colchicum (migraine prophylaxis comparison — colchicine COLCOT trial cardiovascular; neither = first-line for migraine; both show anti-inflammatory prophylactic effects); upcoming I-606 Artemisia annua (same Asteraceae tribe Anthemideae; artemisinin sesquiterpene endoperoxide — largest pharmacological discovery in the tribe; mechanistic comparison with parthenolide sesquiterpene lactone)
