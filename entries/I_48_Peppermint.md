### Botanical Identity
*M. × piperita* is a sterile hybrid; propagated exclusively vegetatively; dark green leaves with serrated margins, purple-tinged stems; characteristic intense aroma from very high menthol content. First described as a distinct species in England (17th century) though hybridisation would have occurred earlier. Peppermint leaf EO contains 40–55% menthol — far higher than any other Mentha species, creating the distinctive intense cooling sensation.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 De Materia Medica (DMM)

Dioscorides (*Mentha* spp., Book III):
> *"Mints: warming and drying. Benefit the stomach and intestines, promote digestion, relieve colic and flatulence. Applied externally they cool inflammation and reduce swellings. The smell benefits the head and relieves headache."*

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine (CM)

Ibn Sīnā:
> *"Na'na': hot and dry in the third degree. Strengthens the stomach, promotes digestion, relieves nausea and hiccup, stops vomiting. The oil applied relieves headache from cold. Relieves toothache when applied. The seed promotes urine."*

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (EP)

Mint preparations (likely *M. longifolia* or *M. aquatica* — precursors to peppermint) identified in Egyptian pharmacological contexts for digestive conditions and febrile states. Mint found at multiple Pharaonic period sites.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)

*Hammu* (mint-type) in Babylonian medical texts for digestive and febrile conditions — consistent with therapeutic profiles across all traditions.

### 2.5 Sushruta Samhita (SS)

*Pudīna* referenced (in texts with later Arabic/Persian influence) for digestive, anti-emetic, and respiratory applications consistent with menthol mechanism.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 TRPM8 / Cooling

**Menthol**: Activates TRPM8 (transient receptor potential melastatin-8) — the "cold-sensing" ion channel — producing a cooling sensation without temperature change. This is the most physiologically straightforward sensory pharmacology in the Codex: menthol fools the cold receptor.

### 5.2 Analgesic / Antispasmodic

**Menthol**: 
- Kappa-opioid partial agonism → analgesic
- TRPV1 desensitisation (initially activates, then desensitises the pain receptor) → counter-irritant analgesia
- Calcium channel antagonism → smooth muscle relaxant → antispasmodic

For irritable bowel syndrome (IBS): menthol relaxes intestinal smooth muscle spasm → reduces IBS-C and IBS-D pain.

### 5.3 Anti-emetic

Menthol + menthone → relaxation of lower esophageal sphincter → reduces nausea reflex. P6 acupoint stimulation (peppermint application to the wrist) enhances anti-nausea effect. Clinical evidence in post-operative nausea and chemotherapy-induced nausea.

### 5.4 Headache Relief (Topical)

**Menthol applied to forehead**: TRPM8 activation → cooling sensation → reduces migraine-associated heat hypersensitivity. Simultaneously, TRPV1 desensitisation → reduces pain signalling from trigeminovascular system. Multiple clinical studies confirm peppermint oil (applied topically) significantly relieves tension headache — comparable to acetaminophen in some RCTs.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**IBS**: Cochrane review: Peppermint oil capsules (enteric-coated, 0.2–0.4ml 3× daily) significantly reduce global IBS symptoms and abdominal pain vs placebo. Meta-analysis (Khanna et al., 2014): 9 RCTs — significant improvement across IBS subtypes.

**Tension headache**: Gobel et al. (1996, *Cephalalgia*): Topical peppermint oil (10% in ethanol) applied to forehead — equivalent to acetaminophen 1000mg for tension headache relief; combined application superior to either alone.

**Post-operative nausea**: Multiple RCTs confirm peppermint inhalation significantly reduces PONV (post-operative nausea and vomiting).

**IBS-associated pain**: Colpermin® (enteric-coated peppermint oil) — widely prescribed in the UK as first-line IBS treatment.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The myth of the nymph**: The story of Minthe — beloved of Hades, transformed by jealous Persephone into the mint plant, condemned to spread prostrate across the earth — gives mint a distinctly chthonic (underworld) origin in Greek cosmology. The plant that emerged from Persephone's punishment of a love rival became the dominant digestive medicine of the classical Mediterranean world. This mythological assignment to the underworld may reflect mint's association with digestive processes — the hidden processes of the body's interior, ruled by the lower realm.

**The cooling-warming paradox**: Peppermint simultaneously feels cold (TRPM8) and hot (TRPV1 initial activation) when applied to the skin, creating a paradoxical dual sensation. Ayurvedic and TCM traditions generally classify it as "cooling" (the dominant subjective experience), while Islamic medicine classified it as "hot and dry." Both observations are pharmacologically correct — the herb hits both the warm and cold receptor systems, and the classification depended on which subjective experience the tradition prioritised.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

| Form | Dose | Application |
|------|------|-------------|
| Peppermint tea | 1.5–3g dried leaf per cup | Digestive; nausea; mild respiratory |
| Enteric-coated oil capsule (IBS) | 0.2–0.4ml 3× daily | IBS — must be enteric-coated to bypass stomach |
| Essential oil topical (headache) | 10% in ethanol/carrier on forehead | Tension headache |
| Menthol inhaler | — | Post-operative nausea; congestion |
| Tincture | 2–3ml before meals | Digestive; anti-emetic |

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Acrid, cool. Channels: Lung (肺), Liver (肝). Disperses Wind-Heat, clears the head and eyes, smooths Liver Qi. Pattern: Early Wind-Heat invasion or Liver Qi stagnation.

**Ayurvedic**: Pungent; cooling. Reduces Pitta ↓↓, reduces Kapha ↓, may increase Vata in large amounts. *Dīpana, Pittahara, Śūlaghna* (anti-colic).

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **GERD / hiatus hernia**: Esophageal sphincter relaxation may worsen reflux — enteric-coated preparations bypass this
- **Infants under 2**: Menthol causes reflex apnea in infants — never apply to face/chest of young children
- **Gallstones**: Choleretic activity — caution with active biliary conditions
- **G6PD deficiency**: Large amounts of menthol may cause haemolytic crisis
