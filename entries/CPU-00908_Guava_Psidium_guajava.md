# Guava — Guayaba, Amrood, Fan Shi Liu (番石榴)

## *Psidium guajava* L. | Family: Myrtaceae

**Tradition Tags:** TCM (Chinese medicine), SS (Sushruta), BM (Badianus)
**Energetic Classification:** Cool and dry, astringent (Galenic); binding and drying to the gut
**Training Phase:** Stabilisation — the astringent, gut-anchoring plants that firm and settle the digestive centre.

---

### Origin & Tradition

**A New World plant, honestly stated.** Guava is native to **tropical Mesoamerica and northern South America**, not to the Old World. It therefore cannot appear in the genuinely ancient strata of the Chinese, Ayurvedic, or Assyrian pharmacopoeias, which predate the Columbian exchange. What is true — and important — is that after the 16th-century Spanish and Portuguese trade routes carried it across the Pacific and Indian Oceans, guava was **absorbed into these living traditions** with remarkable speed and now sits firmly inside their modern folk practice. The tradition tags below reflect that *adopted* status, not an ancient one.

**Badianus / Mesoamerican tradition (BM).** Guava belongs to the plant world of the very cultures that produced the Badianus manuscript (the 1552 Aztec herbal of Martín de la Cruz and Juan Badiano). Across Mesoamerican folk medicine the leaf (*hoja de guayaba*) is a first-line remedy for **diarrhoea, dysentery, and stomach cramps**, taken as a leaf decoction — the single most consistent guava indication anywhere in the world.

**Sushruta / Ayurvedic stream (SS).** In post-Columbian Indian practice guava (*amrood*, *peru*) leaf and unripe fruit are classic *grahi* (binding, antidiarrhoeal) agents, used for loose stools, mouth ulcers, and sore gums, and later folded into folk management of **diabetes** (*madhumeha*) as a hypoglycaemic leaf tea.

**Shennong / Chinese folk stream (SBJ).** Known as *fan shi liu* — literally "foreign pomegranate" — the leaf is used in southern Chinese and broader East Asian folk medicine for diarrhoea, enteritis, and, notably, as an adjunct in **diabetes** management, the "foreign" prefix itself recording its imported origin.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** Every tradition that adopted guava reached for the *same* action: **astringency**. The leaf's mouth-puckering tannins are read across cultures as a "binding" or "drying" virtue — the plant that firms what is loose, whether loose bowels, bleeding gums, or slack tissue. This is a rare case where a New World plant slotted into Old World energetic doctrine precisely because its pharmacology (high tannin, high flavonoid) matched an existing category so cleanly.

### Active Components

- **Quercetin and quercetin glycosides** — principal flavonoids; antispasmodic and antidiarrhoeal, relaxing intestinal smooth muscle and reducing secretion.
- **Tannins (ellagitannins, gallotannins)** — the astringent fraction; bind gut mucosa, precipitate proteins, reduce fluid loss.
- **Triterpenoid acids** — guavacoumaric, ursolic, jacoumaric, guajavanoic, guavenoic, and asiatic acids.
- **Polyphenols and carotenoids** — antioxidant load; the fruit is exceptionally high in vitamin C.
- **Essential oil, reducing sugars, alkaloids** — supporting phytochemicals.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Guava leaf is a **Stabilisation-phase astringent** — the reach-for herb when the digestive centre is loose, leaking, or inflamed. Its primary Kenetica role is **anti-diarrhoeal gut-firming**: quercetin's antispasmodic action calms cramping while tannins reduce fluid loss. Its secondary role is as a **glycaemic-support** leaf tea, drawn from its wide folk use in diabetes, though this is adjunctive and unproven as a treatment. The vitamin-C-rich fruit sits in the nourishing, restorative register.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, guava has some of the better clinical support among traditional antidiarrhoeals.

- **Antidiarrhoeal / antiviral (clinical).** Wei, Li & Chen randomised 62 patients with infantile rotaviral enteritis to *Psidium guajava* versus Gegen Qinlian decoction. The guava group ceased diarrhoea significantly faster (25.1 ± 9.5 h vs 38.7 ± 15.2 h, P < 0.01), had an 87.1% 3-day recovery rate (vs 58.1%, P < 0.05), reduced stool sodium and glucose, and higher rotavirus antigen clearance (*Zhongguo Zhong Xi Yi Jie He Za Zhi* 2000; PMID 11938857 — no DOI registered).
- **Phytochemistry, antidiabetic & broad pharmacology (review).** Chechani et al. systematically reviewed guava's ethnomedicine, phytochemistry and pharmacology, documenting **antidiabetic, antispasmodic, antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective** activity, the triterpenoid acid profile, and successful clinical trials in **infantile rotaviral enteritis and infectious gastroenteritis** (*Comb Chem High Throughput Screen* 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2174/1386207326666230426093315)).

**Evidence grade:** Antidiarrhoeal use — **good** (real randomised clinical data plus strong mechanistic support). Antidiabetic/glycaemic use — **preliminary**: consistent traditional use and preclinical hypoglycaemic signals, but human evidence is limited and it must not replace prescribed diabetes therapy.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM (folk):** Astringent, cool. Binds the intestines, stops diarrhoea, clears damp-heat of the lower burner.
- **Ayurveda (post-Columbian):** *Kashaya* (astringent) rasa, *shita* (cooling) virya, *katu* vipaka; *grahi* (binding), kapha- and pitta-influencing; the leaf pacifies loose *apana*.
- **Galenic / Unani:** Cold and dry, astringent and repercussive — matched to hot, loose, "flowing" states of the gut.

### Contraindications

- **Constipation:** the astringent action can worsen it; avoid in sluggish bowels.
- **Diabetes medication:** the hypoglycaemic effect may add to sulfonylureas, insulin or metformin — monitor blood glucose and never self-substitute the leaf for prescribed treatment.
- **Acute/severe diarrhoea, dehydration, bloody or febrile dysentery:** these need medical assessment and rehydration first; guava is at most an adjunct.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** insufficient safety data on concentrated leaf extracts — use culinary amounts only, avoid therapeutic doses without guidance.
- Tannins may reduce absorption of iron and some drugs if taken concurrently — separate dosing.
