# Bacchar / Baccharis — Bakcharis (Dioscorides 3-51; plate captioned "Baccharis aphylla")

## *Baccharis aphylla, the binomial printed only in the plate caption of Dioscorides 3-51; the translators' own SUGGESTED line gives "Baccharis, Conyza dioscoroidis, Baccharis dioscorides — Bacchar, Ploughman's Spikenard"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). **Named only by Dioscorides in this row.**

**Identity note.** The row's binomial does not come from the translators' SUGGESTED line. It comes from the plate caption above the chapter (line 6963): "Baccharis aphylla from ENGLER-PRANTL — 1897". The SUGGESTED line (lines 6966 to 6967) reads "Baccharis, Conyza dioscoroidis, Baccharis dioscorides — Bacchar [Bedevian], Ploughman's Spikenard", and the next line adds: "Baccharis now applied to an American genus of Compositae." The translators thus say that the modern genus *Baccharis* is American, which is a different thing from the plant Dioscorides described. The identification of the chapter with *Baccharis aphylla* is the weakest of the batch. [USER: verify]

*3-51 BAKCHARIS* (heading at line 6961; text at lines 6969 and 6982). The description: "Baccharis is a herb with many stalks and a sweet scent. It is used to make wreaths for the head. The leaves are sharp, in size between the violet and verbascum [4-104]; the stalk angular, a foot in height, somewhat sharp, with suckers. The flowers are a purple colour, whitish and sweet smelling, and the roots are similar to those of black veratrum [4-151], and similar in smell to cinnamon. It loves rough, dry places."

Uses of the root: "Boiled in water the root helps convulsions, hernia, falls from on high, hard breathing, obstinate coughs, and painful urination. It expels the menstrual flow, and is usefully given with wine to those bitten by snakes. One of the tender roots (applied as a pessary) is an abortifacient, and a decoction of it is good for bathing women in childbirth. It is good in scented powders, having a very fragrant smell." Uses of the leaves: "The leaves are astringent, and are applied to help headaches, inflammation of the eyes, ulcers of the eyes as they begin, breasts inflamed from childbearing, and erysipela [streptococcal skin infection]. The smell is sleepinducing."

The chapter prints no alternative names.

### Active Components

Plants of the daisy family (Asteraceae) are generally known to contain essential oils and a range of flavonoids and terpenoids. The constituents of the plant Dioscorides meant were not read. The American genus *Baccharis* is not shown by the text to be the same plant. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records an aromatic wreath herb whose root is a drink for convulsions, hernia, breathing trouble, cough and painful urination, an emmenagogue, a snakebite remedy and an abortifacient pessary, and whose leaves are astringent applications for the head, eyes and inflamed breasts. These are the text's own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol, and the abortifacient use is recorded as history only.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these preparations. The root "boiled in water" (line 6982). The root given "with wine" for snakebite. The tender root "applied as a pessary". A decoction of the root for bathing women in childbirth. The root in scented powders. The leaves "applied". No quantity or duration is printed for any of them. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identity of the plant is uncertain, so no modern study of any *Baccharis* species would bear on Dioscorides' remedy.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the leaves are called "astringent" (line 6982). No warming, cooling or drying quality is printed for the root. The smell is "sleepinducing" as printed, which is a statement of effect and not a classification.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and the chapter assigns the plant no status. What it offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention, and this chapter is rich in it: a many-stalked herb, angular stalk a foot high with suckers, purple-whitish sweet flowers, roots that smell of cinnamon and look like black veratrum, and a preference for rough, dry places. It also records a use in head-wreaths and scented powders. The identification with any one modern species is the translators' suggestion, and the binomial in this row is from a plate caption. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice beyond recognising an aromatic wreath herb. The abstentive answer is the honest one. The text itself records a root pessary as an abortifacient, which is a reason for caution and not a practice.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning beyond recording the root as an abortifacient and an emmenagogue, so it must be avoided in pregnancy on the text's own showing. The root is compared to black veratrum, a poisonous plant, and the identity of the plant is uncertain, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. The conditions named (convulsions, snakebite, breathing trouble, eye ulcers) need medical care.
