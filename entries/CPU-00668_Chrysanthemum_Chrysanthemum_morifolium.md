# Chrysanthemum — Ju Hua (菊花), Florists' Daisy, Hangbaiju

## *Chrysanthemum morifolium* Ramat. (syn. *Dendranthema morifolium*) | Family: Asteraceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, SBJ, FMT
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and Dry (first–second degree) — a cooling, drying, ascending-then-clearing simple in the Galenic reckoning
**Training Phase:** Tempering (cooling and clarifying)

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Shennong tradition (SBJ):** Cultivated in China for well over two millennia, *ju hua* is among the most esteemed flowers of the classical materia medica, placed in the superior class of tonic, long-life herbs. The dried flower-heads are infused as a clear tea to "disperse wind-heat," calm the Liver, and brighten the eyes. Distinct cultivars are recognised — the white *hangbaiju* favoured for the eyes and the yellow *huangju* favoured for dispersing heat — a differentiation that modern phytochemistry partly vindicates through differing flavonoid profiles between petal colours.

**Avicenna (CM):** The chrysanthemum and its relatives among the cooling composite flowers appear in the Unani tradition (*gul-e-dāūdī* in later Perso-Arabic usage) as refrigerant, resolvent flowers used to cool inflamed heat, soothe the eyes, and relieve headache arising from hot vapours. The Galenic reading as a cold, drying flower aligns with its use against the "hot" complaints of the head and eyes.

**rGyud-bzhi / Tibetan (FMT):** The Four Tantras tradition employs cooling composite flowers of this type to counter disorders of "heat" (*tsha ba*) and disturbances associated with the Liver and the eyes, consistent with the broader Asian medical consensus that this flower drains upward, ascending heat.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The chrysanthemum is the flower of autumn, of the receding *yang* and the clarifying of the year — a symbol of longevity, sober clarity, and the settling of excess. Its doctrinal role, "to clear the eyes and calm the Liver," maps neatly onto its chemistry: the flavone **luteolin** and the chlorogenic (caffeoylquinic) acids that dominate the flower are among the better-studied plant antioxidants and anti-inflammatories, and the eye and the liver are both tissues sensitive to oxidative and inflammatory stress. The "cooling" of wind-heat is, in a modern gloss, the damping of an inflammatory, oxidative flare — the symbolism and the pharmacology point the same way.

### Active Components

The flower-heads are rich in **flavonoids and phenolic acids**. Characterised constituents include the flavones **luteolin**, **apigenin**, **acacetin**, **diosmetin**, and their glycosides **luteolin-7-O-glucoside**, **linarin** (acacetin-7-rutinoside), and **apigenin-7-glucoside**; and the caffeoylquinic (chlorogenic) acids **3-caffeoylquinic acid**, **3,5-dicaffeoylquinic acid**, and **4,5-dicaffeoylquinic acid**. Coloured cultivars additionally carry **anthocyanins**. A fragrant essential oil (chrysanthenone, camphor, and related terpenoids) contributes the characteristic aroma. Flavonoid and chlorogenic-acid content — and thus antioxidant capacity — varies markedly with cultivar, petal colour, and infusion time and temperature.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica, *ju hua* is a **Tempering-phase cooling clarifier** — the gentle flower-tea used to settle an overheated, over-stimulated system, ease eye-strain and hot, tension headache, and provide a mild antioxidant, anti-inflammatory infusion for daily use. It is the archetypal "clearing without depleting" agent: cooling enough to drain wind-heat, mild enough for regular consumption. It pairs naturally with other cooling, eye-directed simples and is used when the practitioner needs to draw down excess heat and restore clarity rather than to purge or tonify.

### Scientific Correlation

Based on articles retrieved from PubMed:

- Han et al. (2019), *Molecules* — HPLC-DAD-ESIMS characterisation of *Chrysanthemum morifolium* Ramat. flower teas showed that chlorogenic acid, luteolin, apigenin and their glycosides, plus (in dark cultivars) anthocyanins, drive strong DPPH and ABTS radical-scavenging activity, and that extracted content and antioxidant activity depend on infusion time and temperature. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules24020329).
- Feng et al. (2022), *Journal of Food Biochemistry* — in a *Drosophila melanogaster* model, chrysanthemum extract (selenium-enriched vs traditional) rich in luteolin, apigenin, linarin and caffeoylquinic acids reduced malondialdehyde, upregulated Cu/Zn-SOD, Mn-SOD and catalase gene expression, and extended lifespan, indicating antioxidant defence induction. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfbc.14503).

**Evidence grade:** The **antioxidant and phenolic chemistry is well characterised** (analytical and in-vitro evidence is solid). Physiological benefits (anti-inflammatory, anti-ageing, eye-protective) are **preclinical** — model-organism and cell-based — and the traditional "clear the eyes / calm the Liver" indications, while consistent with the chemistry, are not established by human clinical trials. Treat as a safe, well-studied functional beverage with promising but not clinically proven therapeutic claims.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM (SBJ lineage):** *Ju hua* — sweet, bitter, slightly cold; enters Lung and Liver channels; disperses wind-heat, clears Liver-heat and brightens the eyes, pacifies Liver yang, clears toxic heat. White cultivar emphasised for the eyes, yellow for dispersing heat.
- **Ayurvedic (inferred, not a classical dravya):** would read as *tikta–madhura rasa* (bitter–sweet), *shita virya* (cooling), *katu vipaka* — a *pitta*-pacifying profile. Inference from constituents, not an attested classical listing.
- **Galenic / Unani (CM):** Cold and dry; refrigerant, resolvent, used against hot disorders of the head and eyes.
- **rGyud-bzhi (FMT):** a cooling flower directed against "heat" disorders and Liver/eye disturbances.

### Contraindications

Chrysanthemum flower tea is generally very well tolerated as a food-grade beverage. Cautions:

- **Asteraceae allergy:** Cross-reactivity is possible in those sensitive to ragweed, daisies, marigold, or chamomile; contact dermatitis and, rarely, respiratory allergy have been reported to chrysanthemum pollen and plant material.
- **Cooling nature:** In TCM terms, its cold quality may aggravate a "cold, deficient" digestion (loose stool, poor appetite) if taken in large amounts; use moderately in those who run cold.
- **Pregnancy/lactation:** Occasional culinary-strength tea is traditionally consumed, but concentrated therapeutic decoctions are best avoided without professional guidance — [USER: verify] for medicinal dosing.
- No significant hepatotoxicity or serious interaction signal is associated with the flower tea at beverage strength.
