**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 5-125, *Kisseris*, "Pumice Stone" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10483-10485; the names row is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 898 and the list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 851). The whole chapter: "Pumice stone should be chosen which is very light, very hollow, easily cut, without stones, and furthermore both brittle and white. It is burnt as follows. Take as much of it as you wish, hide it in burning coals, and when it is red-hot, take it out, quench it with fragrant wine, then burn it and quench it again. The third time you take it off, leave it alone to cool, and put it in jars to use. It is able to tighten and cleanse the gums, purge away things that darken the eyesight with warming, fill up ulcers and form new skins on them, and furthermore, pounded to powder, it is able to repress abnormal growths and clean the teeth. It forms scabs over any sores on the body, and is a suitable medicine to remove hair. Theophrastus says that if one puts pumice stone into a jar of boiling wine that the boiling immediately stops." Pumice appears elsewhere in the same book in three places that were read. (1) In 5-91 *Ios Xustos* (verdigris, line 10276), pumice is named among the adulterants: "Some mix it with pumice stone, some with marble, and others with calcanthum [limestone]", with an assay: wet the thumb of the left hand and rub some of the rust with the other thumb, "because the rust in it spreads out while the particles of pumice stone and marble remain undissolved." (2) In 5-165 *Ostrakites Lithos* (fossilised oyster shells, line 10691), "Women use it instead of pumice stone for taking off hair." (3) In 5-180 *Ge Melia* (line 10765), the earth "rubbed with the fingers ... makes a creaking noise like pumice stone that is being grated." The last two show pumice used as a point of comparison, for a creaking sound and for taking off hair.

### Active Components

Pumice is a pale, glassy volcanic rock full of gas bubbles, so that it is light enough to float. It is made of volcanic glass, mostly silicate, with small crystals; it is hard and abrasive and is not soluble in water. The texts name no active component and give no quantities.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records pumice as a tooth and gum powder, an eye medicine, a dressing for ulcers and a depilatory; the Egyptian attestation is a list row with no text read. The Codex has no protocol for any of this and offers none. Powdered volcanic glass is abrasive to the eye, the teeth and the skin; used on tissue it is rubbing glass on it. If the intent is to smooth hard skin on the feet, a pumice stone is still an ordinary tool for that, and it is a mechanical one.

### Preparation & Dosing

Greek (5-125): choose stone "very light, very hollow, easily cut, without stones ... brittle and white"; burn it by hiding it in coals until red-hot, quench in "fragrant wine", burn and quench a second time, cool after the third, and keep in jars. Pounded to powder for the gums, teeth and growths. The assay in 5-91 (rubbing between the thumbs) is a test for adulteration of verdigris, and not a preparation of pumice. Egyptian: none read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Pumice is used industrially as an abrasive and in building materials, and as a mechanical exfoliant for rough skin. Fine silicate dust from powdered rock irritates the eyes and lungs, and long exposure to some silica dusts is a recognised occupational hazard. Nothing in the sources read ties pumice to a healing effect on gums, ulcers or eyesight. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** None; no Egyptian text was read for pumice.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** One quality is printed, in the verb phrase "purge away things that darken the eyesight with warming" (5-125). That is the only quality the chapter prints. In the same chapter pumice "tightens" the gums and "forms scabs"; these are effects, not qualities.

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent, and the attestation rests on a list row.* The only locus is `_sources/ebers.csv` line 145, and no Egyptian prescription for pumice was read. **Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: how to choose the stone (light, hollow, easy to cut, without grit, white and brittle), how to burn and quench it in three stages with wine, and, in 5-91, how to detect pumice used to cheat a buyer of verdigris with a thumb test. The chapter is therapeutic and assigns pumice no cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do. Pumice earns its place in the Codex as a worked example of Dioscorides's craft of selection, burning and adulteration testing, and as a stone whose Egyptian attestation is only a list row. The honest cultivation use is mechanical: a pumice stone for the skin of the feet is an ordinary tool, and nothing in the sources supports swallowing or powdering it.

### Contraindications

- **Eyes.** Powdered stone in the eye is a mechanical injury; never use it as an eye medicine.
- **Dust.** Do not breathe powdered pumice; fine silicate dust is irritating to lungs.
- **Teeth and gums.** Abrasive powders wear enamel and damage gum tissue; the Greek tooth use is not advised.
- **Depilatory use.** Abrading the skin to remove hair can cause irritation, cuts and infection.
- **Ingestion.** Not advised; no use by mouth is recorded in the chapter read.
- **Dose unknown.** No dose is given for any use in the sources read.
