# Blackthorn / Sloe / Prunus

## *Prunus spinosa* L. | Family: Rosaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Cool & Dry (astringent fruit); flowers read as gently cooling/opening — inferred from the humoral literature, not a first-source attribution
**Training Phase:** Foundation — Signature Study

---

### Origin & Tradition

Blackthorn is a dense, savagely thorny deciduous shrub of the rose family (Rosaceae), native across Europe, western Asia and North Africa, forming the black-barked, spine-armed hedgerow thickets that give it its name. It flowers before it leafs — a froth of small white five-petalled blossoms on bare black wood in early spring (the "blackthorn winter"), followed in autumn by *sloes*: small, blue-black, waxy-bloomed drupes, ferociously sour and mouth-puckering when raw. Two distinct drug organs are used and must be kept separate: the **flowers** (*Pruni spinosae flos*) and the **fruit** (sloe).

The plant carries two named-tradition tags:

- **DMM (Dioscorides / the Greek herbal stream):** the wild astringent plums of the Greek herbal canon — sour, binding fruit for flux of the belly and for tightening lax mucosa — run through the *Prunus* material that blackthorn belongs to. **[USER: verify]** — the specific Linnaean binomial *P. spinosa* cannot be mapped cleanly onto a single Dioscoridean entry; treat this as genus-stream attestation.
- **CM (Avicenna, *Canon of Medicine*, Unani):** cooling, astringent wild-plum fruit and gently opening spring blossoms belong to the Unani repertory Avicenna systematised. **[USER: verify]** for the same species-identity reason.

Folk practice (well attested in the ethnobotanical record, less so in controlled study) reached for the **flowers** as a gentle laxative and a "spring blood-cleanser" diuretic — the mild opening tonic of the year's first hedgerow bloom — and for the **fruit** as an astringent for sore throat and mild diarrhoea, gargled or drunk, and famously steeped into **sloe gin** and sloe wine. The cultivator should note the doctrinal split: flower = opening/moving, fruit = binding/holding. The flower "detox" reputation is essentially traditional and, on current evidence, unverified — it is deflated below rather than repeated as fact.

### Active Components

- **Fruit (sloe):** anthocyanins (cyanidin and peonidin glycosides), condensed proanthocyanidins, flavonoids (mostly quercetin glycosides), phenolic acids (caffeoyl-, coumaroyl- and feruloylquinic acids, protocatechuic, caffeic, vanillic acids) and tannins — a rich polyphenolic, astringent, antioxidant profile confirmed by modern LC-MS profiling.
- **Flowers:** flavonoids and phenolic glycosides; the constituents underlying the traditional laxative/diuretic claim are not well characterised.
- **⚠️ Seeds/kernels, and to a lesser degree leaves and bark:** **cyanogenic glycosides — amygdalin and prunasin** — which on crushing and enzymatic hydrolysis release **hydrogen cyanide**. This is genus-wide *Prunus* chemistry (shared with bitter almond, apricot, cherry and plum kernels), NOT a feature unique to blackthorn. See the safety spine below.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica, blackthorn is a **two-gate** Foundation plant. The **fruit gate** is astringent-antioxidant: the anthocyanin/tannin load makes the sloe a mild binding agent for loose bowel and a puckering gargle for sore, inflamed throat — a gentler cousin of the concentrated astringent roots (cf. Tormentil, CPU-00903). The **flower gate** is the traditional gentle-opener: a mild spring laxative and folk diuretic. The cultivator's honest read: the fruit's astringent/antioxidant chemistry is real and measurable; the flower's "cleansing" action is traditional reputation without controlled support, and is presented as such. Above all, blackthorn is a **safety-teaching plant** — the kernel is where the whole *Prunus* clan's cyanogenic danger becomes concrete.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Form | Traditional preparation | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Sloe (fruit) infusion/gargle | Dried ripe fruit steeped; drink or gargle | Astringent; mild loose-bowel and sore-throat use |
| Flower infusion | Dried flowers as a light tea | Traditional gentle laxative/"spring" diuretic — efficacy unproven |
| Sloe gin / wine | Whole sloes macerated in spirit + sugar | Culinary; see cyanide caveat on long stone maceration |

Safety caveats: use **de-stoned** fruit or avoid crushing the stones; do NOT ingest kernels. Prolonged maceration of whole, stone-in sloes (long-aged sloe gin/liqueur) can allow slow release from damaged kernels — a real, if usually small, reason to keep stones intact and macerations sensible. No fabricated gram doses are given; traditional preparations are self-limiting by sourness and astringency.

### Scientific Correlation

- **Fruit antioxidant / phenolic profile: GRADE — established (in vitro / ex vivo phytochemistry), real.** Modern UHPLC-PDA-ESI-MS work identified 57 phenolic components in blackthorn fruit and demonstrated enrichable polyphenols with measurable antioxidant capacity; polyphenol-enriched fruit fractions modulated oxidative and inflammatory functions of human immune cells *ex vivo* (downregulated ROS and TNF, upregulated IL-10). This supports antioxidant/anti-inflammatory *potential* — it does not establish clinical efficacy for any disease.
- **Fruit as astringent for diarrhoea/sore throat: GRADE — traditional + mechanistically plausible.** Consistent with the tannin/anthocyanin astringent load; no controlled clinical trial retrieved.
- **Flower "detox" diuretic / blood-cleanser: GRADE — traditional/unverified (NULL).** No supporting clinical or robust preclinical evidence retrieved this run — the claim is deflated, not endorsed.
- **⚠️ Cyanogenic kernel toxicity: GRADE — established genus/congener chemistry + documented human poisoning.** The amygdalin biosynthetic pathway (phenylalanine → prunasin → amygdalin, releasing HCN on hydrolysis) was elucidated in almond (*Prunus dulcis*) — cited here explicitly as **congener chemistry** that blackthorn shares as a *Prunus* member, not as *P. spinosa*-specific data. Severe, near-fatal cyanide poisoning from ingested amygdalin/apricot kernels is documented in a 4-year-old child — again congener/genus evidence, illustrating the real hazard of the cyanogenic-glycoside class.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Fruit: cool and dry — sour, binding, tone-restoring astringent. Flowers: gently cooling and opening, read in folk practice as a mild spring mover/cleanser. (Energetic attribution inferred from the Greco-Unani astringent/altative classes; not lifted verbatim from a single primary source.)

### Contraindications

- **⚠️ Cyanogenic kernels — do NOT ingest seeds/kernels; avoid crushing sloe stones.** Amygdalin/prunasin release hydrogen cyanide on hydrolysis; this is the genus-wide *Prunus* hazard. Leaves and bark carry lower cyanogenic loads and should not be eaten.
- **Long-aged stone-in liqueurs:** prolonged maceration of whole sloes can slowly liberate cyanogenic compounds from damaged kernels — keep stones intact and macerations reasonable.
- **Astringency:** the tannin/anthocyanin fruit load can bind dietary iron/minerals and, in excess, irritate or constipate; take with food.
- **Children:** cyanogenic risk is proportionally greater by body weight — the documented congener poisoning was in a young child. Keep kernels and kernel-containing preparations away from children.
- No adequate safety data for concentrated flower/fruit extracts in pregnancy/lactation — default to caution.
