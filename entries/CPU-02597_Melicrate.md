# Melicrate — Melikraton (μελίκρατον, Dioscorides)

## *Honey water, a drink of honey dissolved in water; the identification is the chapter's own: the translator glosses "Melicrate — Water Mead, Honey Water", and the text says it is made from honey and rainwater, so the confidence in the plain English identification is high, and the class is a made preparation filed under the animal product honey* | *(Volume III — Animal)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk under this name.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 8 November 2026 as an unverified placeholder; this entry replaces it. Sushruta's honeys are in other entries (for example CPU-02383) and are not folded in here; Dioscorides' mead (5-16, CPU-02636) is a separate row.

**Dioscorides (DMM).** The chapter is 5-17, "MELIKRATON", glossed "Melicrate — Water Mead, Honey Water" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 9855 to 9856). Full text: "Melicrate has the same strength as mead [honey wine]. We use it (boiled) for those in whom we wish to soften the bowels or cause vomiting, as well as for those that have taken a drink of poison [vomitory], giving it with oil. We use it (boiled) for those who have feeble pulses, the weak, coughers, pneumonia, and those who faint with sweating. That which is provided and put in jars for storage that they properly call hydromel [honey water] is of a middle age and similar to the second-pressed wine and adynamon [for the lack of vital powers] as it also restores the strength. As a result it is more suitable than second-pressed wine for those who have inflammation in some part. That which is older is not allowed for those who have inflammation and are constipated. It is suitable for those with a sore stomach, no appetite, or those who faint with sweating. It is made from two parts of old rainwater mixed with one of honey and placed in the sun. Others use fountain water, boil it down to one third and put it in jars. Honey water is also made from the honeycombs washed out with water and put in jars. When it is necessary to make it like this some also boil it. It is unsuitable for the sick because it has too much waxy stuff."

The chapter follows 5-16 (oinomeli, mulsum, honeyed wine) at line 9852 in the group on wines, and precedes 5-18 (udor, water). The opening sentence ties it to mead: "the same strength".

### Active Components

The source names no constituent. In general terms, honey is mostly the sugars fructose and glucose with water and small amounts of other substances; a solution of honey in water is a dilute sugar solution, and when made from washed honeycomb it also carries beeswax and small amounts of pollen and propolis. If left, a wild-yeast fermentation can begin and produce alcohol, which is why the old accounts sit this beside wine. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Boiled, to soften the bowels or cause vomiting, and as a vomitory with oil after poison; for feeble pulses, the weak, coughers, pneumonia, and fainting with sweating; the middle-aged hydromel restores strength; for a sore stomach and loss of appetite.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose and no number of cups, and no timing within a day.

The Codex does not translate these conditions into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend inducing vomiting or using the drink as a treatment for poisoning.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives the preparation: "two parts of old rainwater mixed with one of honey and placed in the sun"; or fountain water boiled down to one third and put in jars; or honeycombs washed out with water and put in jars, which some boil (lines 9855 to 9856). The age matters: the middle-aged is called hydromel and is of use; the older is not allowed to those with inflammation and constipation; the comb-made kind is "unsuitable for the sick because it has too much waxy stuff". ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Honey in water is a mild sugar solution and a traditional soothing drink for cough; it should not be given to infants under one year because of the risk of infant botulism. Honey-water mixes can ferment if kept warm and unsealed. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** The chapter prints no warm or cold, moist or dry quality and no taste. It prints a strength, "the same strength as mead", and an effect on the bowels (softening). No other quality is imported.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The one phrase near the register is that the stored hydromel "restores the strength" and is "adynamon", glossed "for the lack of vital powers"; this is a therapeutic statement about the weak, not a cultivation status, and the Galenic spirits are later and are not read into it. What the chapter shows is a discipline of attention: the water is specified (old rainwater, or fountain water boiled), the age of the drink is specified, the method is specified, and the unsuitable cases are specified.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing for a cultivator to add. A honey-and-water drink is a plain food, and the chapter's own advice (note the age and the cases for which it is unsuitable) is the lesson.

### Contraindications

- **Infants under one year.** No honey, in any form.
- **Inflammation with constipation.** The text says the older kind is not allowed.
- **Diabetes and blood sugar.** A sugar solution; use care. [USER: verify]
- **Emetic use.** Do not use any drink to induce vomiting after a poisoning; call poison control or emergency services.
- **Hygiene.** Home-fermented honey drinks can spoil.
