# I_137 — Valeriana officinalis | Valerian / All-Heal / Setwall / Garden Heliotrope / Phu

**Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry:** I_137
**Convergence Score:** 5/9 traditions
**Energetic Tier:** ☿ Shen (sedative/neuromodulatory) | ◉ Qi (antispasmodic/cardiac)
**Safety Class:** Good; drowsiness dose-dependent; paradoxical stimulant effect in some individuals; rare hepatotoxicity (case reports); avoid with other CNS depressants; generally safe long-term

## Section 1 — Nomenclature

| Register | Name |
|----------|------|
| Scientific | *Valeriana officinalis* L. (Valerianaceae) [collective species; multiple chemotypes] |
| DMM (Greek) | Phu (Book I.10) — Dioscorides' own description is brief; calls it nardus agria (wild nard) suggesting olfactory similarity to spikenard (I_102); also Phū in Arabic transliteration |
| CM (Arabic/Persian) | Sumbul al-Ṭīb (سنبل الطيب — "fragrant spike"); also Āshūra; extensively documented by Ibn Sīnā for epilepsy, palpitations, headache, and urinary complaints |
| AT (Akkadian) | Possibly *hammu* (one identification proposed for Assyrian sedative herb in BAM tablets) |
| SS | Tagara (तगर) — classical Ayurvedic sedative-nervine herb; *Valeriana wallichii* (Tagara) is the primary Indian species; used in *Tagara Kashaya* preparations |
| SBJ | Xiè cǎo (纈草) — present in later Chinese medical texts; not in classical Shennong Bencao Jing but in Tang-era and later Bencao compilations; used for calming and sleep |
| European folk | Valerian (possibly from Latin *valere* = to be strong/healthy; or from Valeria, a Roman province where it grew); All-Heal; Setwall (Medieval English — from the herbal); Garden Heliotrope (for the perfume of the flowers) |
| German folk | Baldrian (from Nordic "balder" — connection to Norse deity Baldr?); Katzenkraut ("cat herb" — cats are powerfully attracted to valerian root) |

**Botanical identity:** Perennial herb to 1.5m; pinnate leaves; white-pink flower clusters (pleasantly fragrant flowers — the FLOWERS smell nice; the ROOT is the famous unpleasant smell); rhizomatous root system that develops characteristic sour, musty odor upon drying (from isovaleric acid production and valerenic acid decomposition). Multiple chemotypes exist with different valerenic acid content.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Score: 5/9** (DMM · CM · SS confirmed; SBJ · AT tentative)

Valerian demonstrates the neurological convergence that predicts high cross-cultural adoption: any plant that reliably produces observable CNS sedation will be identified independently across traditions. The cat-attractant property (isovaleric acid + valepotriates attract cats as powerfully as catnip) would have made the plant's psychoactive properties visually obvious — every tradition that kept domestic cats near their herbs would have observed cats rolling in the valerian patch.

**The epilepsy application** across DMM (indirectly), CM (explicitly), and SS (explicitly) is significant: epilepsy was one of the most feared conditions of the ancient world, and plants that reduced seizure frequency were among the most valued in any pharmacopoeia. The convergent identification of valerian for epilepsy across Islamic and Ayurvedic medicine (which operated largely independently) suggests genuine anticonvulsant efficacy detectable within the observational medicine framework.

**The sedative-cardiac duality** (DMM: palpitations; CM: palpitations; European folk: nervousness + heart): same as motherwort (I_135) — the anxiety-cardiac overlap generates dual application. Plants that reduce anxiety automatically reduce anxious tachycardia; this convergence is pharmacological predictability in action.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Valerenic acid and derivatives (signature compounds, unique to *V. officinalis*):**
- **Valerenic acid** (0.3–0.8% in root) — principal pharmacologically active compound; GABA-A receptor modulator; 5-HT₅A receptor agonist
- **Acetoxyvalerenic acid, hydroxyvalerenic acid** — metabolites/oxidation products; also active
- **Valerenal** — aldehyde; sedative; contributes to root aroma

**Valepotriates (bicyclic monoterpene epoxide esters — unstable; decompose in storage):**
- **Valtrate, isovaltrate, acevaltrate** — present in fresh root; sedative; antispasmodic; cytotoxic (in vitro — unstable and do not survive to systemic circulation in practical preparations); decompose to baldrinals (also potentially sedative)
- **Note:** Valepotriates are largely absent in dried root products and tinctures; their role in the therapeutic effect of commercial preparations is limited; valerenic acid is the stable active compound in dried herb

**Isovaleric acid:**
- Free fatty acid; characteristic malodor of dried valerian; also present in sweat (hence cat attraction — similar to cat pheromone signal)
- Mild CNS depressant; contributes to sedation

**Flavonoids:**
- Linarin, hesperidin, 6-methylapigenin — GABA-A modulators; antispasmodic
- **6-Methylapigenin** (a flavone unique to *Valeriana*): potent GABA-A positive modulator; the most specific new active compound identified in 21st-century valerian research

**Sesquiterpenes:**
- β-Caryophyllene, valeranone, valeranal — antispasmodic; CNS activity

**Glutamine:**
- High free glutamine content; substrate for GABA synthesis; possibly relevant to sedative action

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**GABA-A receptor modulation (primary — validated mechanism):**
- **Valerenic acid:** allosteric positive modulator of GABA-A receptors (benzodiazepine-like mechanism; binds β₂/β₃ subunit at a site DISTINCT from the benzodiazepine binding site); increases Cl⁻ channel opening frequency → neuronal hyperpolarization → CNS sedation
- **6-Methylapigenin:** positive GABA-A modulator; different binding site from valerenic acid (potentially synergistic)
- **Linarin:** GABA-A modulator
- **Net effect:** multiple GABA-A modulatory compounds acting at different binding sites = synergistic sedation without full benzodiazepine-class dependence risk

**5-HT₅A receptor agonism:**
- Valerenic acid: full agonist at 5-HT₅A (a serotonin receptor with unclear physiological role but expressed in sleep-regulatory brain regions; emerging evidence for role in circadian rhythm modulation)
- This provides a serotonergic mechanism complementary to the GABAergic mechanism → dual-pathway sedation

**Adenosine pathway:**
- Valerenic acid may inhibit synaptic adenosine breakdown → increased endogenous adenosine → sleep-promoting (adenosine is the endogenous sleep-drive molecule — the one blocked by caffeine)

**Antispasmodic (smooth muscle):**
- Valepotriates (fresh herb) and isovaleric acid: direct smooth muscle relaxation
- Explains traditional digestive and cardiovascular antispasmodic applications

**Anticonvulsant (partial):**
- GABA-A modulation: reduces neuronal excitability → anticonvulsant effect
- CM and SS use for epilepsy is pharmacologically consistent (though valerian is not a primary anticonvulsant in modern medicine — too weak for primary management)

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Regulatory:**
- Commission E (Germany): APPROVED for restlessness and nervousness; sleep disorders
- EMA: THMP listing for temporary relief of mild symptoms of mental stress and to aid sleep
- ESCOP: Anxiety; nervousness; excitability; sleep disturbances

**Clinical trials — EXTENSIVE (most researched sedative herb in Europe):**
- Leathwood et al. (1982) *Pharmacology, Biochemistry and Behavior*: Early RCT; valerian significantly improved sleep quality vs. placebo; N=128
- Donath et al. (2000) *Pharmacology, Biochemistry and Behavior*: RCT; valerian 600mg vs. placebo; improved sleep quality (polysomnography confirmed); reduced sleep latency; improved slow-wave sleep
- Bent et al. (2006) *American Journal of Medicine* (meta-analysis): Reviewed 16 RCTs; concluded valerian may improve sleep quality without side effects; methodological limitations noted
- **Valerian + hops (Humulus lupulus) combination:** Multiple RCTs showing synergistic sedative effect; the combination is more effective than either alone (Commission E-approved combination)
- **Anxiety:** Andreatini et al. (2002): Valerian vs. diazepam for GAD — valerian comparable to 5mg diazepam on HAMA psychic factor subscale (no difference in anxiolytic effect); small study (N=36) but significant comparison

**Overall evidence:** Probable efficacy for mild insomnia and anxiety; evidence quality mixed (many small trials); mechanism well-established; clinical application reasonable for primary care and self-medication contexts.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The cat problem — natural blind experiment:**
Every herb garden with valerian is a pharmacological demonstration: domestic cats are powerfully attracted to valerian root (isovaleric acid + valepotriates mimic feline pheromone signals). Cats roll in freshly dug valerian roots, rub their faces on them, enter states of olfactory intoxication resembling catnip response. This creates a naturally observable marker of pharmacological potency: the same volatile compounds that produce the dramatic cat response contribute to the human sedative effect. Ancient herbalists who kept cats would have had constant visual demonstration of valerian's psychoactive properties.

**Pliny the Elder, *NH* XII.47:** "The nardus agria [wild spikenard = valerian] is called Phu by some — it is highly esteemed for its smell." The confusion with spikenard (I_102) is persistent in ancient sources; the two plants share a musky, heavy aromatic quality despite being botanically unrelated. The error was understandable: both produce strongly scented rhizomes with sedative properties.

**Ibn Sīnā, *Qānūn* II:** "Sumbul is hot in the first degree and dry in the second. It is strengthening to the brain and the heart. Good for falling sickness (epilepsy), palpitation, and flatulence." This is among the earliest explicit documentary evidence of valerian's use for epilepsy and cardiac palpitations — two applications that survive into modern clinical use under Commission E approval.

**Medieval "Pied Piper" hypothesis:**
One historical hypothesis about the Pied Piper legend (1284 CE, Hamelin, Germany) suggests that the "piper" scattered valerian root dust along with his music to attract the rats (valerian powerfully attracts rats as well as cats). The hypothesis is unverifiable but pharmacologically plausible: valerian's rat-attractant properties (same isovaleric acid mechanism as cat attraction) could function as a practical rodent control tool. The folkloric encoding of a chemical lure as a magical piper's music is characteristically medieval.

**Paradoxical stimulant effect (documented in ~5% of users):**
Valerian produces sedation in the majority of users but energizing/stimulant effects in approximately 5% — particularly in children and occasionally in adults. The mechanism is unclear (possibly related to chemotype variation affecting specific GABA-A subunit affinities; possibly related to individual differences in GABAergic tone). Herbalists traditionally recognized "cold" vs. "hot" valerian constitutions; this reflects genuine interindividual pharmacological variation.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Primary modern applications:**

1. **Insomnia (primary Commission E indication):** 400–900mg standardized root extract (0.8% valerenic acid) 30–60 min before bed; OR 2–4g dried root as decoction (simmer 10 min); effects accumulate over 2–4 weeks of regular use — not for immediate single-dose effect (unlike benzodiazepines); optimal for chronic insomnia and sleep quality improvement

2. **Anxiety / Nervous restlessness:** 160–300mg standardized extract 3× daily; OR 1–3mL tincture (1:5 in 60% ethanol) 3× daily; most effective for general anxiety and nervous tension; less effective for acute panic (too slow onset for acute situations)

3. **Combined formula (synergistic — preferred over single herb):**
   - **Sleep formula:** Valerian 300mg + Hops (*Humulus lupulus*) 120mg + Passionflower (*Passiflora*) 100mg; Commission E approved combination; more effective than valerian alone in trials
   - **Anxiety formula:** Valerian 200mg + Lemon balm (*Melissa officinalis*) 80mg; Commission E approved; synergistic GABA-A modulation

**Preparation:**
- Standardized extract: 0.8% valerenic acid standardization (the industry standard)
- Dried root decoction: 2–4g root per cup, simmer 10 min (cold infusion extracts valepotriates better but they are less stable)
- Tincture (1:5 in 60% ethanol): 2–4mL; alcohol extraction maximizes valerenic acid
- **Smell warning:** Valerian preparations smell intensely unpleasant (isovaleric acid); this is normal and does not indicate degradation

**Contraindications:**
- **Concurrent CNS depressants:** additive sedation; avoid combining with benzodiazepines, opioids, alcohol (or use with physician supervision)
- **Driving/operating machinery:** sedation; avoid morning doses if drowsiness persists
- **Pregnancy:** insufficient safety data; avoid at medicinal doses; German Commission E notes insufficient evidence
- **Surgery:** stop 2 weeks pre-operatively (potential interaction with anesthetic agents)
- **Rare hepatotoxicity:** <10 published case reports of liver injury associated with valerian products (usually containing multiple herbs); monitor liver function with prolonged use >3 months

**Dose-drowsiness relationship:** Start low (100mg extract) and titrate up. Paradoxical stimulant effect (~5% of users) requires dose reduction or discontinuation.
