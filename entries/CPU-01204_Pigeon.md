# Pigeon — Ebers Papyrus, "Pigeon" (Bryan's English identification; no Egyptian word given)
## *The pigeon (bird); Bryan 1930 prints only the English word "Pigeon" in his list of animals, with no Egyptian name and no binomial* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The locus is `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` row "Pigeon" (line 107 of that file), which cites "Bryan 1930, Ch. VII Organic Remedies (animals) (line 1397)" and notes "Bryan's English identification; no Egyptian word or binomial given in this list". In the text file (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`, lines 1388-1398) Bryan introduces the animals with the sentence that a full account of the animal materia medica "would not be possible since ... the identity of a considerable number of the animals mentioned is still beyond us", and then gives "the list of those animals whose identity is not in doubt, and who were called upon to sacrifice themselves in the cause of the Healing Art, is a formidable one". The list runs, in the printed order, "Lion, Hippopotamus, Crocodile Ox Cow Calf Mouse Porcupine, Serpent, Tortoise Lizard Mole Vulture, Bird-of-Passage, Swallow, Pigeon, Goose Duck Stag Gazelle Ram Goat" and continues with the ass, hog, dog and others (lines 1390-1404). Pigeon is therefore attested in the secondary account of the papyrus as an animal that supplied remedies; it is Bryan's English identification and nothing more is given in the list.

Bryan then prints a second, separate list of the animal parts the physicians used ("Hair Feathers Horns Hoof Hide Eyes ... Blood Bile Bones ... Flesh Shell Wings Excrement Urine Eggs Milk Toes Tail", lines 1408-1415). That list is not tied to any one animal in the lines read, so it does not say which part of the pigeon was used.

The one passage found in the Bryan text that names a pigeon-like bird as an ingredient is a remedy for the "uases" abscess (line 2357): "ANOTHER REMEDY AGAINST THE UASES ABSCESS Blood-of-a-Dove Blood-of-a-Goose Blood-of-a-Swallow Blood-of-a-Vulture. Anoint therewith." Bryan calls the abscess a "stubborn eruption" whose nature the scribe did not describe (line 2355), and remarks that this remedy is "Just as picturesque, and assuredly as useless" as the one before it; that is Bryan's 1930 judgement, not the papyrus's. The text prints "Dove", not "Pigeon", and the lines read do not say whether Bryan's "Dove" and his list's "Pigeon" are the same bird in the papyrus. [USER: verify]

A further line, "TO PREVENT THE PIGEON HAWK STEALING" (line 4248, under "Domestic Hints"), concerns a hawk and a spoken formula over an acanthus rod; it is not a pigeon remedy and is not used here. No dose, preparation time or route is given for the dove blood beyond "Anoint therewith".

### Active Components

Blood is a fluid tissue of water, proteins (among them albumin, globulins and haemoglobin), cells, salts and lipids. Bird blood is similar in kind, with nucleated red cells. No active principle is claimed by the sources, and no composition specific to this entry was read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The only sourced content is that the pigeon stands in Bryan's list of identifiable animals in the Ebers materia medica, and that a "Dove" blood ointment is one of two remedies for an abscess of unidentified type. The Codex offers no protocol. Applying animal blood to a skin eruption is an infection risk, and Bryan himself regards the remedy as useless (see Contraindications). [USER: verify]

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources retrieved give one preparation, the blood of a dove (with the blood of a goose, a swallow and a vulture) "anoint[ed]" on the uases abscess (line 2357). No quantity, vehicle or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Wild and feral pigeons (*Columba livia* and relatives) can carry *Chlamydia psittaci* (the cause of psittacosis) and other pathogens, and fresh blood from any animal can carry bacteria and viruses; applying it to broken skin is unsafe. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The Ebers material, as Bryan presents it, is organised by complaint and carries no taste, temperature or humoral qualities for the pigeon or dove; none is given in the sources retrieved. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*, and the identity of a named substance is often a modern hypothesis; here the only identification is Bryan's English word. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*), so dropping the recitation would lose half a prescription. In the one remedy read (line 2357) no recitation is printed alongside the dove blood, which, because the passage is a translator's summary and the papyrus itself was not read, is no evidence either way. The nearby spell, over an acanthus rod to keep a pigeon hawk from stealing (line 4248), invokes "Horus" in the printed text; that line belongs to a hawk, not to the pigeon, and no deity is attributed to the pigeon.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. There is no recipe to follow beyond one abscess ointment that Bryan himself calls useless, the bird's identification is only an English word, and the Egyptian framework (*ka*, *ba*, *akh*, *ib*, *heka*) gives no basis here for any practice. The entry records that the pigeon appears in Bryan's list of Ebers animals, and nothing more.

### Contraindications

- **Infection:** blood and droppings of pigeons and doves can carry pathogens, and animal blood applied to broken skin is a route for infection.
- **Not a treatment for abscess:** an abscess needs clinical assessment and often drainage; do not rely on this entry.
- **Legal and conservation status:** wild pigeons and doves are protected in many places, and trapping or killing them can be unlawful. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** killing a bird for a remedy with no supported benefit is hard to justify.
