# Phaseolus aconitifolius — Makushtha; Makushthaka (Sushruta Samhita, Bhishagratna's Appendix No. 3 and the chapters)

**Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *What the chapters say.* All chapter files (`sutra`, `chikitsa`, `uttara`, `kalpa`, `nidana`, `sharira`) were searched for Makushtha with the spellings Makushtha, Makushthaka and Makushtaka, for variant transliterations (Makustha, Makusht), and for OCR-garbled letters. The hits are four, all in lists of pulses.

*Sutra ch.20 (regimen, Hitahitiya).* `SS_Sutra_ch20.txt` lines 56 to 60: "The varieties of pulse which form the articles of human food are known as the Mudga, the Vana-Mudga, the Makushtha, the Kalaya, the Masura, the Mangalya, the Chanaka, the Harenu, the the Adhaki and the Satina." The list is a list of names. The doubled "the" is in the OCR.

*Sutra ch.46 (food and drink), the list.* `SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` lines 194 to 197: "The several varieties of pulse (Vaidala) commonly used as food are known as the Mudga, Vana-Mudga, Kalaya, Makushtha, Masura, Mangalya, Chanaka, Satina, Triputa, Harenu, and Adhaki, etc."

*Sutra ch.46, the properties.* Lines 199 to 203, for the pulses as a group: "They are generally cool and pungent of digestion, and have a sweet and astringent taste. They generate the Vayu, arrest the flow of urine and the evacuation of stool, and subdue the Pittam and Kapham." Lines 216 to 218, for the species singly: "The species Makushthaka is vermigenous, while the species Kalaya generates Vayu copiously." (page "475" is printed at line 208). "Vermigenous" is the translator's word; it means that the text counts it among foods that breed worms, and the passage says no more of it. Lines 228 to 231 add for the whole class: "All the varieties of pulse except the Mudga and the Masura tend to produce a distension of the abdomen caused by gas or air in the organism (Tympanites or Adhmana)."

*Uttara ch.39 (fever, Jwara-Pratishedha).* `SS_Uttara_ch39.txt` lines 899 to 902, in the diet of a patient emaciated by a long attack of Satataka or Vishama fever: "The soup of such pulses as Mudga, Masura, Chanaka (gram), Kulattha and Makushtaka, etc. may be given with benefit as diet to the hungry patient suffering from fever."

*Rasayana and vajikarana chapters.* A search of Chikitsa ch.26 (the vajikarana chapter) and ch.27 to 30 (the rasayana chapters) for Makushtha, Makushthaka, Makushtaka and Vana-Mudga found nothing.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. As a general fact, *Phaseolus* and the closely related *Vigna* are genera of the legume family (Fabaceae) whose seeds are eaten as pulses; the seed of a pulse is a source of starch and protein. This is a statement about the family and not a finding of the Sushruta text, and nothing is stated here about the composition of this drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Under the names the chapters print, the plant (if it is this one) is a pulse eaten as food: named in two lists of pulses (Sutra 20 and 46), counted in Sutra 46 as "vermigenous", and named in a soup of pulses given to a hungry fever patient (Uttara 39). The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Sushruta names Makushtha four times, always as a pulse: in two lists of pulses eaten as food, in the properties of the class, and in a soup for a fever patient. Those are dietary and therapeutic contexts. The vajikarana chapter (Chikitsa 26) and the rasayana chapters (Chikitsa 27 to 30) do not name it, and the text says nothing of *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* for it. The one property the text gives it singly, "vermigenous", is a caution and not a status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. The Sushruta text as retrieved gives a cultivator no practice with this pulse: it is food, and the one quality it assigns the plant by itself is a caution. Nothing should be taken on the strength of a Latin name that the chapters do not give.

### Contraindications

The Sushruta text retrieved prints, for the pulses as a group, that they "tend to produce a distension of the abdomen" (Sutra 46, lines 228 to 231), and for Makushthaka that it is "vermigenous". The identity is open, so no plant should be gathered or eaten on the strength of this entry. Raw or undercooked pulses can cause digestive upset, which is a general fact about the food class and not a finding of the text. Fever that is prolonged or severe needs medical care.
