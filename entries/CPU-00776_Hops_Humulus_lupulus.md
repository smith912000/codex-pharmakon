# Hops — Lupulus · Humle · Houblon · Hop Strobile

## *Humulus lupulus* L. | Family: Cannabaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM, TCM
**Tags (previous line, retained for reference):** AT, CM, DMM, SBJ
**Energetic Classification:** Cool & Dry — bitter, sedative, relaxant
**Training Phase:** Settling Night (nervous quieting & sleep onset)

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (AT):** Among the oldest strands of Old-World herbalism, the bitter climbing vine and its resinous cones enter the cuneiform and Near-Eastern pharmacological tradition as a bitter digestive and calmative, and — inseparably — as a preservative and flavouring in fermented grain drinks. The pairing of hops with brewing and with rest is ancient.

**Dioscoridean / Graeco-Roman (DMM):** *Lupulus salictarius* is described in the classical stream as a wild climbing bitter used to cleanse the blood and loosen the belly — the bitter-aperient reading of a plant whose resins later prove genuinely sedative.

**Unani / Avicennan (CM):** In the Graeco-Arabic tradition hops is a cool-dry bitter: a blood-cleanser, a mild aperient, a calmative for an overheated, restless disposition, and a corrective for a bilious stomach. Its cooling-drying temperament underlies its use to quiet agitation and settle a "hot" nervous state.

**Chinese (TCM):** *Pi jiu hua* enters later Chinese materia medica as a bitter, cool herb that strengthens the stomach, aids digestion, calms the spirit and settles restlessness and insomnia — the digestive-plus-sedative pairing seen across every tradition.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Hops is the herb of the drowsy harvest — pickers were long observed to grow sleepy in the fields, and hop-stuffed "sleep pillows" are a durable folk remedy. The doctrine (a bitter that cools heat and brings sleep) tracks the pharmacology precisely: the α-acid degradation product **2-methyl-3-butene-2-ol** accumulates in stored hops and is a demonstrated sedative, while the bitter resins raise inhibitory **GABA** signalling in the CNS. The plant that makes the harvester drowsy and the beer bitter is doing, chemically, exactly what the tradition says.

### Active Components

- **Bitter acids (soft resins):** **α-acids (humulone)** and **β-acids (lupulone)** — the bitter, antimicrobial, brewing-defining resins.
- **2-methyl-3-butene-2-ol:** sedative alcohol formed by degradation of the α-acids during storage; the leading candidate for the hypnotic effect.
- **8-Prenylnaringenin (8-PN):** one of the most potent known **phytoestrogens** — the basis of both the traditional "menstrual" folklore and the modern endocrine-caution flags.
- **Xanthohumol:** prenylated chalcone with antioxidant/anti-inflammatory and investigational metabolic activity.
- **Essential oil:** humulene, myrcene, caryophyllene — aroma volatiles.
- **Flavonoids and tannins:** astringent, antioxidant secondary constituents.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Hops sits in the **Settling Night** phase as a bitter nervous relaxant for sleep onset and restless, over-stimulated agitation — especially the "wired but tired" state with a tense, bilious gut. It is classically synergistic with valerian (the two are combined in most clinical sleep formulas) and with lemon balm and passionflower. Its second, older role is as a bitter digestive tonic that settles a nervous stomach. Because it also delivers a potent phytoestrogen, Kenetica flags it as a **short-course, targeted** sedative rather than an open-ended daily herb, and treats its hormonal activity as a real (not incidental) property.

### Scientific Correlation

Human evidence supports a **mild sedative / sleep-quality** effect, usually for hops combined with other herbs; monotherapy data are thinner. On the Kenetica scale this grades **emerging clinical, mechanistically plausible.** According to PubMed:

- Franco and colleagues (*PLoS One*, 2012) gave work-stressed female nurses non-alcoholic (hopped) beer with supper for 14 days and measured sleep by actigraphy: **sleep latency fell significantly** (12.0 vs 20.5 min, P≤0.05), total nocturnal activity dropped, and STAI anxiety scores decreased — attributed to the hop component, whose bitter-resin degradation product raises GABA [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0037290).
- Scholey and colleagues (*Nutrients*, 2017) ran a **double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial** (n=171) of a multi-component supplement including *Humulus lupulus* for mild insomnia; sleep quality (PSQI) improved in both arms with **no significant between-group difference**, against a marked placebo response over the short 2-week course [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu9020154) — a useful negative/null result that tempers the efficacy claim.

The mechanistic story (2-methyl-3-butene-2-ol as sedative; α-acids up-regulating GABAergic tone) is well characterised, and the actigraphy signal is real, but robust monotherapy RCT evidence for insomnia is limited. Best-supported: a **mild, adjunctive sleep aid**, strongest in valerian–hops combination.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Unani / Graeco-Arabic (CM):** cold and dry; bitter blood-cleanser, mild aperient, calmative for hot/restless temperaments.
- **TCM (SBJ):** *pi jiu hua* — bitter, cool; strengthens stomach and aids digestion, calms the spirit (*an shen*), settles insomnia and restlessness.
- **Western/Galenic (EP, SBJ):** cool-dry bitter; nervine relaxant, bitter tonic, mild anodyne and soporific.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** Under the standing blocking warning for Mesopotamian substance identifications, no cultivation status is claimed — and here even the therapeutic attestation should be held with caution: hops as a brewing and medicinal herb is a medieval European development (hopped ale post-dates the cuneiform corpus by millennia), and its presence in the Assyrian materia medica is not securely established. The Mesopotamian frame classifies a substance partly by the causal agency it opposes, but no such classification is sourced for this plant. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The classical name usually cited — *lupus / lupulus salictarius* — is Pliny's, describing an edible wild shoot, not a Dioscoridean materia medica entry, and hops as a medicine is largely post-classical. Dioscorides in any case carries no subtle-body system that could assign a cultivation status. What the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention — provenance, the season for gathering the cones, the sensory assay of a bitter that changes on storage. That is a real practice, not a vital-body doctrine. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status, and the classical therapeutic attestation itself is thin.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** In the Graeco-Arabic stream hops is read as a cool-dry bitter: a blood-cleanser, mild aperient and calmative for a hot, restless disposition. The strongest cultivation idea in the Unani corpus is the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture; a cooling-drying sedative bitter is a corrective for a "hot" state, not a substance assigned a status for conserving that vital endowment, and no *mufarrih* or *ruh*-role is claimed for it (hops quiets rather than gladdens). Its standing is therapeutic. **(b) framed:** used in the tradition, no cultivation status sourced.

**Chinese medicine — post-classical (TCM).** Hops enters Chinese materia medica very late, as *pi jiu hua* 啤酒花 ("beer flower") — the name itself marks a modern loan, beer being a recent import — and it is not in the classical Shennong *Bencao* stratum at all. No *san pin* 三品 grade is recorded for it; the later Chinese use is calming and digestive (*an shen* 安神, settling restlessness and insomnia), a therapeutic role within *yang sheng* 養生 rather than a cultivation status. **(b) framed**, and expressly not a classical *Bencao* attestation.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator uses hops for one narrow, bounded job: a short-course evening sedative for the "wired but tired" state and a nervous, bilious stomach, most reliably paired with valerian. No tradition assigns it a cultivation status, and its potent phytoestrogen (8-prenylnaringenin) is itself a reason to keep the arc short rather than take it daily. Reach for it at night, for a defined stretch, then stop — there is nothing here to build a long practice around.

### Contraindications

- **Sedation / CNS-depressant additive effect:** hops potentiates alcohol, benzodiazepines, "Z-drugs," barbiturates, opioids, antihistamines and other sedatives — do not combine; caution before driving or operating machinery.
- **Phytoestrogen caution:** 8-prenylnaringenin is a potent phytoestrogen. **Avoid in hormone-sensitive conditions** (oestrogen-receptor-positive breast, uterine or ovarian cancer, endometriosis, uterine fibroids) and with hormonal therapies; theoretical interaction with hormonal contraception and HRT.
- **Depression caution:** hops has a traditional and pharmacological CNS-depressant/sedative profile — use cautiously in, or avoid with, clinical depression, as it may deepen low mood or sedation.
- **Pregnancy & lactation:** avoid medicinal doses given the phytoestrogenic and sedative activity.
- **Allergy:** contact dermatitis and respiratory sensitivity are reported in hop handlers ("hop pickers").
