# Typha — Xiang Pu 香蒲 (Shennong bencao jing); alias Ju 睢

## *Typha orientalis C.Presl, the oriental cattail, whose leaves and shoots are the "herb" (xiang pu) as distinct from its pollen (pu huang); the identification is ours, from the Chinese name and the standard modern identification, and is of medium confidence at species level, since the classic describes no plant and the Yang 1998 translation names the drug only as "Herba Typhae Japonicae"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts on disk, under this Chinese name.** This is a single-text entry. The principal text is Sun Xingyan's 1799 reconstruction, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt`; the other edition on disk, Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`), is read as a second witness and attributed to Yang. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder, "Herba Typhae Japonicae [SBJ]", taken from Yang's English; this entry replaces it. A search of the other texts on disk found cattail only under other names and other species: Dioscorides' *Typha* chapter and Avicenna's cattail row, both treated in I_663 (*Typha angustifolia*, tags SBJ, AT, DMM, EP, CM), which is mainly about the pollen (*pu huang*, CPU-01017). This entry is the leaf-and-shoot drug of the Chinese classic and is not merged with it.

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** The entry is 香蒲 in the upper classic (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, upper classic, entry 香蒲 *xiang pu*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 783, 味 paragraph). The Shennong paragraph reads: 味甘，平 ("sweet, balanced"); 主五脏心下邪气 ("governs evil qi in the five viscera and below the heart"), 口中烂臭 ("rotting foul smell in the mouth"); 坚齿 ("firms the teeth"), 明目 ("brightens the eyes"), 聪耳 ("sharpens the hearing"); 久服，轻身、耐老 ("taken long: lightens the body, bears old age"), with a note that the *Yulan* (Taiping yulan) reads 能老 for 耐老; the alias 一名睢 ("also called Ju"; the *Yulan* has 睢蒲); and 生池泽 ("grows in pools and marshes").

The Wu Pu layer (《吴普》曰, third century, line 785) gives 睢 with the aliases 睢石 and 香蒲, and the flavour "Shennong, Lei Gong: sweet" (神农、雷公：甘); it says it grows in "the southern sea, in pools and marshes" (生南海，池泽中, citing the *Yulan*). The Mingyi bielu layer (《名医》曰, line 787) adds one alias, 醮, and the habitat 生南海, "the southern sea." Neither layer adds indications.

Sun's own note (案, line 789) is philological. It cites the *Shuowen*: 菩，草也 ("*pu* is a herb"); the *Yupian*: 菩，香草也 ("*pu* is a fragrant herb", also read *pu*); and the *Bencao tujing*: "*xiang pu* is the seedling of *pu huang* (蒲黄苗也); in early spring it puts out tender leaves, before it is out of the water they are red-white and downy, and the *Zhouli* made them into a pickle (菹)." The note is Sun's citation of later authorities and is not Shennong text. It is the only place the sources read describe how the plant looks or what part was used; it names the young leaf-shoot, which fits the "herb" reading of the drug.

**Yang 1998 as second witness.** Yang prints (line 2120): "Xiang Pu (Herba Typhae Japonicae) is sweet and balanced. It mainly treats evil qi in the five viscera and below the heart as well as putrefying mouth with foul smell. It fortifies the teeth, brightens the eyes, and sharpens the hearing. Protracted taking may make the body light and slow aging. Its other name is Ju (Osprey). It grows in pools and swamps." This agrees with Sun's text. Yang's translation of 耐老 as "slow aging" is his rendering. Yang prints the pollen, Pu Huang (line 2115), as the previous monograph, with a footnote on the pollen (footnote 125, line 2134); that is a separate drug. In both editions this herb stands in the upper class.

**Appendix.** A search of the drug-relations table (from line 4200) found no entry for 香蒲.

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, *Typha* species are tall marsh monocots whose rhizomes are starchy and whose young shoots are eaten in several cuisines; the pollen is a separate material rich in flavonoids. A leaf-and-shoot drug would be mainly fibre and carbohydrate. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify any named constituent before adding it.]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The classic records a sweet, balanced drug for evil qi in the five viscera and below the heart, for a rotting smell in the mouth, for firming the teeth, brightening the eyes and sharpening the hearing, and, when taken long, for lightening the body and bearing old age. These are the classic's categories and the Codex does not translate them into modern diagnoses. It gives no protocol and no dose. The text names no preparation, so the Codex cannot say which part or form was meant beyond Sun's note on the young shoot.

### Preparation & Dosing

The Shennong text gives no preparation and no dose. The only practical statements are in Sun's note, quoting the *Bencao tujing*: the tender leaves of early spring, red-white and downy, were pickled in the *Zhouli* (line 789). The preface (line 4121) says that drugs differ in suitability for pills, powders, decoction, wine or paste, with no rule for this one. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. *Typha* (Typhaceae) grows in wetlands across the northern hemisphere; its shoots, rhizome starch and pollen have been used as food, and its leaves for weaving. Whether the herb relieves any of the conditions named in the classic has not been tested in sources retrieved for this entry. The classic's claims (lightening the body, bearing old age) have no modern pharmacological equivalent and are not translated into one. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested.* The paragraph for 香蒲 carries a 久服 clause promising the terms the classic uses for life-nourishing status: 久服，轻身、耐老, "taken long: lightens the body, bears old age". The entry stands in the upper classic, whose preface (line 43) reads 上药一百二十种，为君，主养命以应天，无毒。多服、久服不伤人。欲轻身益气，不老延年者，本上经: the upper drugs "nourish life, correspond to heaven, are non-toxic; many doses or long taking do not harm people; for those who wish to lighten the body and benefit the qi, not age and extend the years, base it on the upper classic". This records the classic's wording and not a claim that the plant does these things. (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, upper classic, entry 香蒲 *xiang pu*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 783)

**Practitioner's note.** The classic gives this marsh plant an upper-grade, long-use promise, and the only practical hint is a pickled spring shoot in Sun's note. That is a record of wording, not a practice. No part, preparation, dose or course is given, and nothing here supports taking cattail as a regimen.

### Contraindications

- **Identity.** *Xiang pu* (the herb) and *pu huang* (the pollen) are different drugs and are not interchangeable here; the species is our identification, of medium confidence. [USER: verify]
- **Source of the plant.** Marsh plants take up what is in their water; do not gather from polluted water. This is a general precaution and is not in the sources.
- **Allergy.** Cattail pollen can provoke allergy in sensitive people. This is a general statement.
- **Pregnancy, nursing, children.** No safety information was read for this herb. [USER: verify]
- **Quantities.** No dose or duration is given; "taken long" is not a basis for a regimen.
