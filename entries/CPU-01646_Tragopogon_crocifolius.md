# Tragopogon crocifolius — Tragopogon or tetrapogon, "come" (Dioscorides 2-173); "Wild Salsify"

## *Tragopogon crocifolius, the name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 2-173, glossed "Wild Salsify"; a second line gives Tragopogon porrifolius and Tragopogon pratensis ("Salsify, Vegetable Oyster, Oyster Plant, Goat's Beard")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk lists the plant under this name (the name indexes in `Sources/*/extract/names.csv` were searched). The entry rests on one very short chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain, and the translators print two further species on a second line. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *2-173. TRAGOPOGON* at line 6032, first SUGGESTED line ("Tragopogon crocifolius — Wild Salsify") at line 6033, the second ("Tragopogon porrifolius, Tragopogon pratensis — Salsify, Vegetable Oyster, Oyster Plant, Goat's Beard") at line 6037, text at line 6038. Between them, at line 6035, stands a plate caption, "Orobanche ramosa after FAGUET — 1888", which belongs to the chapter before (*2-172. OROBANKE*, line 6025), not to this one. The chapter stands in Book Two, after 2-172 and before *2-174. ORNITHOGALON* (line 6044), and the page number 299 follows it (line 6040). By page, it falls within the contents list's section "Lachana: Vegetables" (from page 243 to the "Herbs with a Sharp Quality" section at page 304; line 31). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 363, and the translators' index prints "Tragopogon crocifolius 299" (line 17858).

The whole chapter reads: "Tragopogon or tetrapogon (also called come) has a short stalk and leaves similar to saffron. The root is long and sweet. It has a big cup on the stalk and black fruit in the top, from which it took its name. It is an edible herb."

That is all the chapter says. It describes the plant by four points: a short stalk, leaves "similar to saffron", a root that is "long and sweet", and a "big cup" on the stalk with "black fruit in the top", which the text gives as the origin of its name. It prints two further names, *tetrapogon* and *come*. It records no medical use at all. Its only statement about use is the last one, "It is an edible herb", which is in keeping with its place among the vegetables.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter number, the Greek heading, *Tragopogon* and *Salsify* found no entry on 2-173. I_871 (*Truffle*, line 13) and I_1148 (*Ornithogalum umbellatum*, line 16) mention it only as a neighbouring chapter. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. It is a general fact that *Tragopogon* belongs to the daisy family (Asteraceae) and that its species have a milky sap. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the translators' wild salsify. The chapter calls the root "sweet", which is a taste and not a statement of composition. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records no remedy and no medical claim. It records only that the herb is edible, and the Codex draws no protocol from that. A plant cannot be eaten on the strength of a one-line chapter and an identification that is only a suggestion.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives no preparation. "It is an edible herb" does not say which part is eaten, how it is cooked or whether it is eaten raw. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that some plants called salsify are grown for a root eaten as a vegetable. This is a statement about the garden plant and is not a verification of the chapter.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 2-173 prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality. The only taste printed is that the root is "sweet", together with the physical points above.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a short description for recognition (a short stalk, saffron-like leaves, a long sweet root, a big cup and black fruit) and a sentence on why the plant has the name it has. It is the most reduced chapter in the batch, and it is a food chapter. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The identification is the translators', and they print three species. **Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The text offers only a plain statement that it is food. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 2-173 prints no warning. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, and the translators offer three species, so no plant should be gathered or eaten on the strength of this entry. Many wild plants with a milky sap or a similar leaf are not edible, and the chapter does not help to tell them apart. The text gives no dose and no safety information for pregnancy or children.
