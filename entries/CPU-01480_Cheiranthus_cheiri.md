# Cheiranthus cheiri — Leukoion, Leukoion thalassion (Dioscorides 3-138)

## *Cheiranthus cheiri (one of the translators' suggestions; "Wallflower")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Identity doubt.** The SUGGESTED line (lines 7744-7747) does not name one plant. It gives three groups: "Leucoion, Viola alba ... Hesperis hortensis [Bauhin], Hesperis matronalis var hortensis subvar albiflora [Linnaeus] — Dame's Violet, Dame's Rocket, Damask Violet"; then "Viola-lutea [Fuchs], Leucoion-Dioscorides luteum [Brunfels], Leucoium luteum vulgare [Bauhin], Cheiranthus cheiri [Linnaeus] — Wallflower"; then "[other usage] Leucoion [Theophrastus], Viola alba [Fuchs], Leucoium bulbosum vulgare [Bauhin], Leucojum vernum [Linnaeus] — Spring Snowflake". The row takes the wallflower. The text says the yellowish flower is the one fittest for medicine, which is the group in which the translators place the wallflower, but the chapter itself covers several colours. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *3-138. LEUKOION, LEUKOION THALASSION* at line 7743, text at line 7748.

The chapter reads: "Leukoion is commonly known but there are different coloured flowers, for it is found white, yellowish and azure [blue], or else it is purple. The fittest of these for medicinal use is the yellowish, the dried flowers of which (boiled) are good for bathing inflammation around the womb and expelling the menstrual flow. Used in wax ointments they cure cracks in the perineum, and with honey they cure apthas [small ulcers]. Two teaspoonfuls of a decoction of the seed (taken as a drink with wine or applied as a pessary with honey) draw out the menstrual flow and afterbirth, and are an abortifacient. The roots (smeared on with vinegar) repress the spleen and help gout. It is also called basilion; the Romans call it opula alba, some call it viola alba, augustia, viola matronalis, passarina, or polyphura."

Dioscorides names the plant as commonly known and describes it only by flower colour (white, yellowish, blue, purple). The uses concern the dried yellowish flowers (a decoction for bathing, an ointment in wax, a honey preparation for small ulcers), the seed (a decoction, drunk or applied as a pessary, to draw out menses and afterbirth) and the roots (smeared on with vinegar for the spleen and gout). The heading prints a second name, *thalassion*, but the text read describes no separate sea form. The translators' index lists *Cheiranthus cheiri* at page 519.

**Overlap.** No existing entry covers this chapter. I_1007 *Antirrhinum asarina* and I_896 Toadflax quote 3-138 only as a comparison in another chapter (flowers "similar to leucoion [3-138]"). I_598 Stock (*Matthiola incana*) and I_798 Wallflower-Hound's-tongue (*Cynoglossum cheirifolium*) mention wallflowers in general prose without citing this chapter.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. The wallflower is a member of the cabbage family (Brassicaceae). Some plants of the wallflower group are generally reported to contain heart-active glycosides, which was not checked here. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records dried flowers for bathing and ointments, a seed decoction to draw out the menses and afterbirth that he calls an abortifacient, and a root preparation for the spleen and gout. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol: the identification is one of several, the seed preparation is an abortifacient by the text's own word, and the plant may be unsafe.

### Preparation & Dosing

3-138 gives the dried flowers boiled for bathing, the flowers in wax ointments, the flowers with honey, and "two teaspoonfuls of a decoction of the seed (taken as a drink with wine or applied as a pessary with honey)". The roots are "smeared on with vinegar". Apart from the two teaspoonfuls of seed decoction, no quantity is given. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes: it separates the flower colours and says which is fittest for medicine (the yellowish), and it names the part used for each preparation (flower, seed, root). That is a sensory and provenance discipline, not a status. The identification is one of the translators' alternatives. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The seed preparation is described as an abortifacient and should be treated only as history.

### Contraindications

Dioscorides calls the seed decoction an abortifacient, so no part of the plant should be taken or used as a pessary in pregnancy. Seeds and other parts of some wallflower relatives may affect the heart, which was not checked. Do not self-treat gout, spleen complaints or gynaecological inflammation from this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
