# Lesser Centaury — Common Centaury, Feverwort, Kentaurion mikron, Herba centaurii minoris

## *Centaurium erythraea Rafn (syn. C. minus, C. umbellatum)* | Family: Gentianaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM
**Energetic Classification:** Hot in the first degree, Dry in the second (Galenic) — a cooling-to-neutral, drying, tonic bitter
**Training Phase:** Foundation

---

### Origin & Tradition

Lesser Centaury is the true "digestive centaury" of European herbal medicine — a small, pink-flowered annual of the gentian family, botanically unrelated to Greater Centaury (*Centaurea centaurium*, Asteraceae) despite sharing the Chiron myth and the bitter name. Where the greater plant was a wound-root, the lesser plant is above all a **bitter tonic for the stomach**, and it is this species that survives in modern pharmacopoeias as *Centaurii herba*.

**Assyrian tablets (AT):** The Mesopotamian herbal tradition recorded a large family of bitter stomachic and febrifuge simples given for poor appetite, sluggish digestion, and fever. Lesser Centaury belongs to this class of digestive bitters as a member of the recognised group rather than as a securely transliterated single entry — the identification is at the level of plant-class.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** In *De Materia Medica*, Dioscorides describes *kentaurion to mikron* — the small centaury — as intensely bitter, and prescribes it for the stomach and liver, as a purge of bilious and "thick" humours, for fevers, and as a wound-cleanser. He notes its bitterness as its defining and active quality, which aligns precisely with the modern understanding of its secoiridoid content.

**Avicenna (CM):** The *Qanun* carries the lesser centaury as the "small qantariyun," a hot, dry, deobstruent bitter used to open obstructions of the liver and spleen, stimulate appetite and digestion, expel worms, and reduce fevers. The Galenic grading (hot in the first degree, dry in the second) frames it as a warming digestive cleanser, and this classification passed into the later European herbals largely unchanged.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Lesser Centaury is the archetypal *bitter*, and in Kenetica the bitter principle governs the **Foundation** phase: the reflex arc by which a bitter taste on the tongue primes the whole digestive apparatus — saliva, gastric acid, bile flow — is the somatic model for "preparing the ground." The plant's doctrine is that nothing higher can be built until digestion (literal and metaphorical) is kindled. Here the symbolism ties tightly and honestly to pharmacology: the secoiridoid bitters *do* act on bitter (T2R) taste receptors and reflexively stimulate digestive secretion, which is the mechanistic basis of the traditional "appetising" bitter tonic.

### Active Components

The characteristic actives are **secoiridoid glycosides**, chiefly **gentiopicroside** (also called gentiopicrin) and **swertiamarin**, together with **sweroside** and **centapicrin** — among the most intensely bitter compounds known. Alongside these sit **xanthones** (e.g. methylated tri- and tetra-oxygenated xanthones), **flavonoids**, **phenolic acids**, and traces of **triterpenes**. Gentiopicroside is the marker compound for quality control of *Centaurii herba*. This secoiridoid chemistry is what separates Lesser Centaury sharply from the sesquiterpene-lactone chemistry of Greater Centaury.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In the **Foundation** phase, Lesser Centaury is the primary bitter tonic — taken shortly before food to kindle appetite and digestive secretion, to relieve a sense of fullness, sluggishness, or "cold, damp" stagnation in the gut, and to establish the digestive baseline on which later phases depend. It is used as a small-dose, taste-active bitter: the bitterness is not an unpleasant side effect but the working mechanism, so it should be tasted, not masked. Kenetica positions it as a genuine functional simple with a plausible mechanism and long traditional use, while grading its clinical evidence honestly as preliminary.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, Guedes et al. (2019), *Molecules*, prepared decoctions of *Centaurium erythraea* leaves, confirmed by LC-MS/MS the presence of the secoiridoids gentiopicroside and sweroside plus several xanthones, and demonstrated significant antioxidant activity (DPPH reduction, inhibition of lipoperoxidation, NO-radical scavenging) along with measurable acetylcholinesterase and HMG-CoA reductase inhibition — offering a molecular rationale for the plant's traditional gastrointestinal and cholesterol-lowering ethnomedical uses ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules24203795)). Earlier, Kumarasamy et al. (2003), *Fitoterapia*, isolated **gentiopicroside** from the aerial parts of *C. erythraea* and documented antibacterial and free-radical-scavenging activity, with general toxicity assessed by brine-shrimp lethality ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0367-326X(02)00319-2)).

**Evidence grade: Preclinical, with strong traditional and mechanistic support.** The digestive-bitter action is mechanistically plausible (bitter-receptor–mediated secretion) and the plant holds established European herbal-monograph status, but controlled human trials specific to *C. erythraea* are lacking. Antioxidant, antimicrobial, and enzyme-inhibition findings are in vitro.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani (Avicenna):** Hot in the first degree, dry in the second; deobstruent, stomachic, febrifuge, anthelmintic.
- **TCM analogue:** No canonical entry; by action, bitter and cooling, draining damp-heat and rousing the Stomach/Spleen (bitter descending).
- **Ayurvedic analogue:** Not a classical dravya; by properties, *tikta rasa* (bitter), predominantly *sheeta*-to-neutral *virya* with a drying, digestive-kindling (deepana-pachana) effect; reduces Kapha and Pitta-associated sluggishness, may aggravate Vata in excess.

### Contraindications

Contraindicated in active peptic ulcer, gastritis, or hyperacidity, since bitter-stimulated acid secretion may aggravate them. Avoid in pregnancy (traditional emmenagogue/uterine caution). Gentianaceae or general bitter-tonic hypersensitivity is a contraindication. Do not exceed traditional small bitter-tonic doses; excessive intake can cause nausea.
