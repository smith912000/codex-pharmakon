# Artemisia sieversiana — mkhan.pa, Khenpa (rGyud-bzhi); "mugwort"

## *Artemisia sieversiana, printed "Artemesia sieversiana", the first Latin name (after the English "Mugwort") of the four names Clark 1995 prints for mkhan.pa, which go on with Artemesia frigida and Artemesia vulgaris; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary sets the name Khenpa, by column position, against Artemisia vulgaris* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi (Four Tantras), read in two modern translations: Clark 1995 for the binomials and the commentary, and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 for the root-text sentence. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one, and it carries three Artemisia binomials in Clark, so the botanical identity is open.** Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The row was added from the printed names (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP, confidence "medium") and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. **Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (row 174) the row reads "mkhan.pa" with the locus "Clark 1995 (line 1078)", the identification "Mugwort; Artemesia sieversiana; Artemesia frigida; Artemesia vulgaris" and the note "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The first item of the line is the English word "Mugwort"; the key is the first Latin name after it, *Artemisia sieversiana*. The same binomial appears a second time in Clark, as the second name for tshar.bong (line 1040, "Artemesia sieversiana Willd"), which is a different Tibetan entry and has its own row (I_1427, keyed on *Artemisia annua*).

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1078).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), in section "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry reads: "mkhan.pa: Mugwort: Artemesia sieversiana: Artemesia frigida: Artemesia vulgaris: [has three names and is of three types. It is grey in appearance, has a fragrant yellow flower, small lobed leaf which grows close to the ground and is also known as a.krong (dkar.mo). It reduces swellings and is beneficial for wounds, abscesses and (disorders of) the large and small intestines. The white type has a thin petiole and pale blue leaf, whilst the red or brown type yields seeds,] is hemostatic and reduces swellings of the limbs. [The white subspecies is beneficial for kidney disorders and is recommended in essence-extraction compounds.]" Clark's preface (line 82) says that "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". So nearly all of the entry is the commentary layer: the three names, the three types, the grey look, the flower and leaf, the swellings, wounds, abscesses and intestines, and the two bracketed claims about the white subspecies. The only words outside the brackets are the clause "is hemostatic and reduces swellings of the limbs", which is the root-layer claim.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3448).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), at the end of the run on herbal medicines: "Khenpa stops bleeding and reduces swelling of the limbs." The index (line 7407) gives "Khenpa 209". The sentence is the same as Clark's root-layer clause (hemostatic, swellings of the limbs); the text does not say that Khenpa is mkhan.pa, and the Codex reads them together from the sound of the name and the agreement. *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (the "K" block).* The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. The block holds three names, "Khenpa", "Khyungdher" and "Khurmang" (line 5382), the transliterations "mkhan pa", "khyung sder" and "khur mang" (5384 to 5386), and the scientific names "Artemisia vulgaris", "Uncaria Hook" and "Taraxacum officinale" (5388 to 5390), which fit in order. So the glossary sets Khenpa against *Artemisia vulgaris*, which is the last of Clark's three Artemisia names and not the first. The pairing is by position and is not printed on one row. *Other Clark lines that name mkhan.pa.* The entry for a.krong (line 1036) says its types include "mkhan.pa.a.krong". The entry for tshar.bong (line 1040) says that drug "is said to be a type of mkhan.pa" (commentary layer). The entry for zangs.rtsi.wa (line 1107) says its black type "has a strong smell similar to mkhan.pa (Artemesia)". None assigns mkhan.pa a use of its own.

**Identity doubt.** Clark prints *sieversiana*, *frigida* and *vulgaris*, and the glossary prints *vulgaris*. The spine key follows Clark's first Latin name. The Codex does not choose, and the row stands for the Tibetan drug and not for one species.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Artemisia sieversiana*, *Artemesia sieversiana*, mkhan.pa, Khenpa, *Artemisia frigida* and Mongolian wormwood found no entry on this Tibetan drug. **I_1427** (*Artemisia annua*, tshar.bong) is the entry on the drug that Clark's commentary says is a type of mkhan.pa. **I_437** (Mugwort, *Artemisia vulgaris*, tags include FMT) carries a Tibetan paragraph that names "mugwort-type *mkhan pa* plants" as the material of Tibetan moxa for "cold" disorders (its lines 26, 65 and 72); neither Clark's mkhan.pa entry nor the Men-Tsee-Khang line says that (they give a hemostatic and swelling-reducing drug, and in the commentary, wounds, abscesses, the intestines and the kidneys), and the Codex does not carry the moxa statement over. **I_164** (Mugwort, tags include FMT) describes Tibetan artemisia incense and fumigation in its lines 23 and 127 to 128, with no locus in the texts read here. **I_329** (Chinese Mugwort, *Artemisia argyi*) and **I_249** (Wormwood, *Artemisia absinthium*) are entries on other species. ### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. As a general fact, *Artemisia* is a genus of aromatic plants of the daisy family (Asteraceae), and the grey look, the fragrance and the finely lobed leaves of Clark's commentary are typical of the genus. That is a statement about the genus and not a finding of the Tibetan texts, and the Tibetan drug's identity among three species is not settled. Nothing is stated here about its composition. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug, mkhan.pa or Khenpa, that "stops bleeding and reduces swelling of the limbs" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3448; Clark line 1078, outside the brackets) and, in the commentary layer, is beneficial for wounds, abscesses, the intestines and (for the white subspecies) the kidneys. These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation the sources read name is the bracketed one: the white subspecies "is recommended in essence-extraction compounds" (Clark line 1078). No compound is named, no part is named, and no quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(a) Attested, in the commentary layer, for one subspecies.* Clark's bracketed sentence at line 1078, "The white subspecies is beneficial for kidney disorders and is recommended in essence-extraction compounds", places the white subspecies of mkhan.pa among the ingredients of *bcud len* (essence extraction), which the cultivation spec locates inside the Four Tantras. Clark's preface (line 82) says the square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, so this is a later layer than the root text, whose own clause (hemostatic, swellings of the limbs) says nothing of it. Clark's preface (lines 69 to 71) says that essence-extraction compounds, "apart from their invigorating qualities enable meditators and yogis to live without intake of gross food and thus engage in spiritual practices with enhanced mental clarity, sharpness and pliancy", and names other substances as examples (sundew, angelica, *Polygonatum*, salep, certain flowers, calcite and mineral exudate), not mkhan.pa, so that passage is the setting and not a second attestation. The claim is for "the white subspecies" of a drug with three binomials, and the text does not say which binomial it is. The cultivation spec adds that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. Neither rejuvenation chapter (Clark chapter 23, lines 1264 to 1276; Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, contents line 235) names mkhan.pa or Khenpa. If the commentary layer is held not to count as the tradition's own status, or the white subspecies is held not to be *Artemisia sieversiana*, this paragraph falls to (b). [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** The only cultivation role the sources give is as one ingredient of essence-extraction compounds for the white subspecies, with no compound named and no dose. Those compounds are made by specialists, and nothing here supports a cultivator taking an unidentified mugwort on that basis. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. The identity is uncertain between three species of *Artemisia*, and members of the genus are aromatic plants that can cause allergic reactions in people sensitive to the daisy family; no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Bleeding and swellings of the limbs need medical care. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
