### Origin & Tradition

White bryony is a climbing perennial of the gourd family with palmate leaves, tendrils, small greenish flowers, black berries (in *B. alba*; red in the related *B. dioica*), and — most strikingly — an enormous, fleshy, turnip-like taproot that can grow to great size. That massive root made it the "**English mandrake**" or "**devil's turnip**": unscrupulous root-sellers carved bryony roots into the manlike forms of true mandrake (*Mandragora*) to sell as the costly, magically-reputed drug — one of the classic medieval pharmaceutical frauds. Despite the substitution, bryony is botanically and pharmacologically unrelated to mandrake (a Solanaceae); it is a **cucurbit**, kin to colocynth and the squirting cucumber, and shares their bitter **cucurbitacin** chemistry.

Dioscorides treats white bryony among the strong, dangerous plants of Book IV, and the tradition used the great root as a **drastic purgative, emetic and emmenagogue**, and externally as a powerful **resolvent and counter-irritant** for hard swellings, bruises and rheumatic pain. The internal action is violent — a hydragogue catharsis driven by mucosal irritation — and the plant is genuinely toxic: the **berries and root cause serious poisoning**, with severe gastroenteritis. As with the other cucurbit drastics, careful practice tempered and compounded it, and casual internal use was always recognised as dangerous.

White bryony's most active modern afterlife is in **homeopathy**, where *Bryonia* (prepared from the root) is one of the great polychrest "keynote" remedies — classically indicated, in the homeopathic symptom-picture, for complaints **worse from any motion and better from firm pressure and stillness** (dry, painful coughs, pleuritic and rheumatic pains, headaches aggravated by movement). This homeopathic profile is a distinct framework from the material-dose pharmacology and is recorded here as part of the substance's cross-traditional life, not as a claim about the crude drug's action.

- **Cucurbitacins (bitter triterpenoid glycosides — the "bryonin"/cucurbitacin fraction)** — the intensely bitter, **drastically purgative, irritant and cytotoxic** principles, shared with colocynth and the squirting cucumber; they irritate the gut mucosa, provoking violent catharsis, and are toxic in quantity. - **Bryonin / bryonidin** — traditional names for the bitter glycosidic active fraction of the root.
- **Bryoresin and other irritant constituents** — contributing to the mucosal/skin irritation.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy — absolute.** Drastic purgative and traditional emmenagogue; abortifacient risk; contraindicated.
- **Inflammatory/obstructed bowel, haemorrhoids, fissure.** A violent irritant cathartic is contraindicated in any fragile, inflamed or obstructed gut.
- **Children, elderly, debilitated, dehydrated.** Dangerous fluid/electrolyte loss; the berries are a poisoning hazard.
- **Renal/cardiac compromise.** Catharsis-driven fluid shifts hazardous.
- **No crude internal or casual use.** Specialist/historical framing; root identity (vs true mandrake) matters.
