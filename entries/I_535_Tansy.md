# I-535 | Tansy

**Binomial:** *Tanacetum vulgare* L.
**Family:** Asteraceae (Anthemideae)
**Common Names:** Common tansy; bitter buttons; cow bitter; golden buttons; *Rainfarn* (German); *Tanaisie vulgaire* (French); *Pиж*/*Пижма обыкновенная* (Russian); *Shī Yú Ài* (Chinese — limited)
**Part Used:** Aerial parts, flowers; historically aerial herb (*Tanaceti vulgaris herba*) — **CAUTION: NOT CURRENTLY STANDARDIZED AS PHARMACEUTICAL PREPARATION IN EU/US; HIGH TOXICITY RISK**
**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (anthelmintic; emmenagogue; abortifacient — **EXTREME TOXICITY RISK**) — NOTE: This entry is primarily historical/pharmacological documentation; modern therapeutic use not recommended

---

## I. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

*Tanacetum vulgare* L. was described by Linnaeus in *Species Plantarum* (1753). The genus name *Tanacetum* derives from medieval Latin *tanacita*, which may be a corruption of Greek *athanasia* (immortality) — tansy was associated with immortality in medieval European tradition, where it was used as a burial herb (embalming the dead, preserving corpses from decay), and immortality because of its persistence as a perennial. The species epithet *vulgare* (common) reflects its widespread distribution in European meadows and roadsides.

**Disambiguation from Feverfew (*Tanacetum parthenium* — I-525):** Both species are in *Tanacetum*, both Asteraceae Anthemideae, both strongly aromatic. Tansy (*T. vulgare*) lacks the white ray florets of feverfew and has exclusively tubular disk florets forming characteristic bright yellow button-like capitula (hence "golden buttons"). Tansy's EO is dominated by thujone (toxic) whereas feverfew's EO contains camphor/camphene with much less thujone. These are pharmacologically distinct — tansy is primarily documented for its TOXICITY and requires a dedicated entry because it is encountered in historical literature and patient self-medication contexts.

**Botanical features:** Perennial strongly aromatic herb 60–120 cm; erect, often reddish stems; leaves pinnately divided with toothed segments, dark green, gland-dotted, intensely bitter-camphor aromatic; inflorescence: dense corymb of 10–70 capitula; each capitulum 6–9 mm, flat, all DISK FLORETS (no ray florets — the distinguishing feature from feverfew); bright yellow to orange-yellow. The "button" appearance of the flat-topped all-disk capitula is instantly recognizable. Widespread European and Asian meadow/roadside weed; naturalized in North America.

## III. Convergence Analysis

**The toxicity factor in convergence:** Tansy's lower convergence score compared to wormwood partially reflects a genuine pharmacological difference: tansy's thujone content is dramatically HIGHER than wormwood (25–60% of EO for some *T. vulgare* populations vs. 30–70% for high-thujone wormwood chemotypes, but tansy's β-thujone predominance is additionally significant). The high toxicity may have limited its geographic spread as a medicinal plant — communities discovering tansy's potent emmenagogue/abortifacient effects would simultaneously observe its convulsant toxicity at higher doses, potentially discouraging its adoption as a medicinal herb beyond anthelmintic use in low doses.

**The immortality paradox:** The Greek *athanasia* (immortality) naming and the medieval European use in embalming/preservation is the most interesting pharmacological-cultural connection in this entry. Tansy's EO (dominated by thujone) is an effective antimicrobial and insect deterrent — properties that explain both its use in corpse preservation (prevents bacterial putrefaction and insect infestation of the corpse) and in food preservation (medieval use as a strewing herb and meat preservative). The "immortality" encoding is a pre-scientific observation of genuine antimicrobial/insecticidal properties that delayed the biological processes of decay. The plant does not produce immortality in the human sense — it delays microbial decomposition in the biological sense.

## IV. Phytochemical Composition

### A. Essential Oil (0.1–0.6% DW) — HIGH THUJONE, PRIMARY TOXICITY SOURCE

**β-Thujone and α-thujone (total thujone: 25–70% of EO in most populations; some cultivated varieties: 80–90%):** The highest concentrations of thujone in any Codex entry in the *Artemisia*/*Tanacetum* cluster. β-Thujone is the predominant stereoisomer in *T. vulgare* (unlike wormwood where α-thujone may dominate) — significance: β-thujone was historically believed to be less toxic than α-thujone, but modern pharmacological assessment shows β-thujone is also a potent GABA-A competitive antagonist.

**Camphor (5–20% EO):** Uterotonic; CNS stimulant at toxic doses; convulsant threshold lowering additive to thujone. The thujone + camphor combination in tansy EO makes it more potent as an abortifacient/emmenagogue than wormwood at equivalent doses.

**1,8-Cineole, borneol, isoartemisia ketone:** Minor EO components; antimicrobial; expectorant contribution but pharmacologically secondary to thujone.

**Artabsin ketone (*Tanacetum*-specific sesquiterpene):** Bitter; anthelmintic; pharmacologically active but contribution is subsumed by the thujone toxicity risk.

### B. Sesquiterpene Lactones

**Parthenolide (yes — same as feverfew I-525):** *T. vulgare* contains parthenolide (0.02–0.1% DW), the NF-κB inhibitor of feverfew; however concentrations are significantly lower than in *T. parthenium*. This tansy-feverfew shared compound reflects phylogenetic proximity within *Tanacetum* and represents a secondary pharmacological layer beneath tansy's dominant thujone/camphor toxicological profile.

**Tanacetin (germacranolide sesquiterpene lactone; specific to *T. vulgare*):** 0.1–0.3% DW; anti-inflammatory; anthelmintic; bitter taste contributor.

**Achillin (guaianolide):** Minor sesquiterpene lactone; anti-inflammatory.

### C. Flavonoids

**Axillarin, quercetin-3-glucoside, luteolin-7-glucoside:** Anti-inflammatory flavonoid fraction.

**Chrysosplenol C:** Polymethoxylated flavone; anti-inflammatory.

**Isorhamnetin derivatives:** Antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; minor.

### D. Phenolic Acids

Chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid; antioxidant; modest content.

### E. Absence of Commission E / PhEur Standardized Preparation

There is NO current PhEur monograph for *Tanaceti vulgaris herba* as a pharmaceutical drug. Commission E has NOT approved tansy for any indication. The traditional European and Unani medical record for tansy was not transferred into modern pharmaceutical standardization precisely because safer alternatives exist for all tansy's traditional indications AND because tansy EO toxicity makes standardized safe dosing difficult.

## V. Mechanisms of Action

### A. Thujone GABA-A Antagonism (Primary — Toxic Mechanism at Medicinal Doses)

Same mechanism as documented in mugwort (I-531) and wormwood (I-533), but at dramatically higher concentration in tansy EO:

**Thujone pharmacokinetics at tansy doses:** A single "cup of tansy tea" (1–3 g dried herb) delivers approximately 0.5–3 mg thujone (depending on EO extraction efficiency). The lethal dose of thujone in humans is estimated at approximately 10–30 mg/kg body weight (animal data extrapolated); severe toxic effects begin at much lower doses in sensitive individuals. IMPORTANTLY: **the margin between anthelmintic/emmenagogue doses and toxic doses for tansy is narrow** — the same dose range that produces therapeutic anthelmintic effects is dangerously close to the convulsant/toxic dose range.

**Historical tansy toxicity deaths:** Documented cases of tansy poisoning deaths in historical medical literature:
- 1860s-1900s: Multiple case series from European medical journals documenting deaths from tansy oil self-administered as abortifacient
- These cases establish tansy EO as one of the most dangerous herbal abortifacients in the European pharmacopoeia
- Deaths were from convulsions, respiratory failure, and hepatic failure from thujone + camphor toxicity

### B. Anthelmintic (Low-Dose; Clinically Eclipsed by Toxicity)

At sub-toxic doses, thujone GABA-A antagonism in helminths + tanacetin/artabsin ketone membrane disruption → anthelmintic activity. The anthelmintic effect exists pharmacologically but occurs in the same dose range where human CNS toxicity begins — no safe therapeutic window is established.

### C. Emmenagogue / Abortifacient (Primary Traditional Use — Dangerous)

Thujone uterotonic (GABA-A antagonism → uterine smooth muscle hyperexcitability) + camphor direct uterine smooth muscle stimulation → menstrual induction. As abortifacient: these mechanisms are effective for uterine stimulation but the doses required approach or exceed the toxic threshold for convulsions and systemic toxicity. This is why tansy was a significant 19th–early 20th century cause of abortion-related death in Europe and North America.

## VI. Clinical Evidence

### A. Historical Toxicology Documentation

The clinical evidence for tansy primarily consists of:

**Poisoning case series:** Multiple 19th–20th century publications documenting tansy EO poisoning deaths and near-deaths from self-administered abortifacient use. These cases establish the toxicological profile but not therapeutic evidence.

**No modern clinical trials:** No controlled clinical trials exist for tansy in any therapeutic application; the safety profile precludes ethical trial conduct.

**Antimicrobial in vitro (Level D):** Tansy EO effective against *S. aureus*, *C. albicans*, several Gram-negative bacteria — but at concentrations achievable only topically (not clinically useful systemically due to thujone toxicity).

### B. Insecticidal / Repellent (Level B)

The best modern evidence for tansy's pharmacological activity is its insecticidal and insect-repellent properties:

- **Mosquito repellent:** Tansy EO (applied topically to fabric or skin at 1–5% concentration in carrier) repels *Aedes aegypti* and *Culex* mosquitoes comparably to low concentrations of DEET in multiple studies
- **Pyrethrin-like properties:** Thujone and camphor are structurally related to naturally occurring insecticides and share insect neural toxicity mechanisms
- **Traditional strewing herb:** The historical use of tansy as a strewing herb in medieval floors and as a fly/moth deterrent (placed in linen drawers) is pharmacologically validated by the insecticidal/repellent EO

The insect-repellent/insecticidal application is the ONLY pharmacological use of tansy that can be recommended with a favorable safety profile — topical EO on fabric does not result in significant systemic thujone absorption and the target (insect) is distinctly more sensitive than the host (human) to thujone neural toxicity.

## VII. Phenomenological Record

**Tansy cakes (Easter food tradition):** A documented medieval European tradition involved eating "tansy cakes" (tansy-flavored pancakes or omelets) at Easter to "purge" the body after the Lenten fast and to kill intestinal worms acquired during winter. The Easter timing encoded the anthelmintic purpose: the spring helminthic reinfection cycle from contaminated soil + water coincided with Easter → Easter = purging season → tansy = the purging herb. Tansy cakes were eaten in small amounts (the bitter taste limited consumption), delivering sub-toxic anthelmintic doses. The tradition died out in the 18th–19th century as tansy toxicity became better recognized and safer anthelmintics became available (santonin from *Artemisia cina*, then pharmaceutical vermifuges).

**Medieval embalming and the immortality legend:** The use of tansy in corpse preparation (wrapping bodies in tansy, packing tansy into burial shrouds) is documented in multiple medieval European sources. The antimicrobial EO (thujone, camphor) delays bacterial decomposition; the insecticidal properties repel flies and prevent maggot infestation; the bitter taste deters rodents and insects from the corpse. The practical efficacy of these effects for short-term corpse preservation justified the "athanasia" (immortality) naming — the plant genuinely slowed the death process (decomposition) even if it could not prevent death itself.

**Alexander the Great's embalming:** Classical sources claim Alexander the Great's body was embalmed with *athanasia* (tansy) and other aromatic herbs in Babylon (323 BCE), explaining the 30-day delay before decomposition visible enough to trigger official mourning rites. If historical, this represents the earliest documented strategic pharmaceutical use of tansy's antimicrobial/insecticidal properties in state-level politics (the delay in Alexander's visible decomposition may have been strategically important to prevent premature destabilization of his empire).

**19th-century criminal cases:** Several documented murder-by-abortion cases in 19th-century Britain involved tansy oil or "oil of tansy" as the abortifacient agent. These cases were medically investigated and established tansy's toxicological profile in human clinical literature: tansy oil poisoning produces convulsions, coma, and death from respiratory failure. The criminal cases created the primary human pharmacological record for tansy toxicity.

## VIII. Practical Application

### Regulatory Status

**Commission E:** NO APPROVAL — insufficient evidence and/or risk. Commission E specifically noted that tansy could not be approved for any internal use due to high thujone content and toxicity risk.

**EMA:** Not assessed as a traditional herbal medicinal product; high thujone content precludes approval.

**Traditional use status:** Historical/folkloric; documented in traditional European and Unani medicine; NOT translatable to modern pharmaceutical use.

### Why This Entry Exists in the Codex

Tansy appears in the Codex for three reasons:

1. **Completeness:** A complete systematic catalog of plant-based pharmacopoeia cannot exclude documented traditional medicines because they are dangerous — the toxic profile IS part of the pharmacological record.

2. **Clinical relevance of exposure:** Patients may present to practitioners having self-administered tansy (or tansy tea) for anthelmintic, emmenagogue, or abortifacient purposes; practitioners must know the toxicological profile.

3. **Pharmacological contrast with nearby entries:** The *Tanacetum* and *Artemisia* sequences (I-525 feverfew, I-531 mugwort, I-532 sweet wormwood, I-533 wormwood, I-534 tarragon, I-535 tansy) represent a pharmacological continuum from safe (tarragon, feverfew) through therapeutic-risk (mugwort, wormwood) to clearly toxic (tansy). The continuum demonstrates how chemical variation within a genus produces dramatically different safety profiles.

### Emergency Management (Tansy Poisoning)

**Presentation:** Convulsions, vomiting, abdominal pain, hypotension, renal failure, hepatotoxicity within 1–6 hours of ingestion.

**Management:**
- Immediate emergency department assessment
- Gastric decontamination if ingestion <1 hour (activated charcoal 50 g in adults)
- Seizure management (benzodiazepines — IV lorazepam or diazepam; the GABA-A antagonism of thujone makes GABA-A agonists [BZDs] the pharmacologically correct treatment)
- Hepatic function monitoring × 72 hours
- Supportive care

**Dose alert for counseling:** Even a "therapeutic" dose of tansy tea (1–3 g dried herb) can deliver 0.5–3+ mg thujone; the margin for toxicity in sensitive individuals (children, small adults, pregnant women) is minimal. No "safe" dose of tansy can be recommended for internal use.
