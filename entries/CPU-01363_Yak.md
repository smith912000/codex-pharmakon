# Yak — yak / she-yak / wild yak (rgod.gyag)
## *Yak, the domesticated bovid of the Tibetan plateau (glossed Bos domesticus mutus in the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary); meat, milk and dairy products of the yak and she-yak, and parts of the wild yak* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan Tradition (FMT).** The yak enters the Tibetan medical corpus in three ways, and all three are dietetic or materia-medica entries, not rites. The Explanatory Tantra classifies animal flesh in nine sections; in Clark's 1995 translation the sections include "Larger hill and game animals", which lists "wild yak (takin)", and "Domestic animals", which lists "the dzo, yak, camel, horse, donkey, bull, skom.po (the calf of a bull and a dzo), goat, sheep, dog, pig, chicken, cat, etc." (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 672). The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text gives the same two groupings: "The goral, spotted deer, Himalayan tahr, boar, buffalo, black rhinoceros, wild ass, wild yak and wild Zho are the group of larger herbivorous wild animals" (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 2907) and "The zho, yak, camel, horse, donkey, cow, kompo, goat, sheep, dog, pig, hen and cat are the group of domesticated animals" (line 2917). Clark's note 1 explains the hybrid: "A dzo is a yak crossed with a cow" (line 1538).

The yak is therefore two animals in these texts: the domestic yak and she-yak, whose flesh and dairy products are described by their qualities, and the wild yak (*rgod.gyag*), whose meat, horn and blood are used. The sections below take each in turn.

**Flesh.** Clark: "Yak meat is greasy and warm, eliminates cold disorders and increases blood and bile" (line 677). Men-Tsee-Khang: "Yak meat is oily and warm in quality; it helps against disorders associated with cold nature and increases blood and tripa" (line 2931). The two translations agree in substance; the Men-Tsee-Khang version names *tripa* where Clark has "bile".

**Wild yak flesh.** Clark: "Wild yak meat eliminates cold disorders of the stomach and liver and boosts the digestive heat" (line 679). Men-Tsee-Khang: "The meat of wild yak relieves cold disorders of the liver and stomach, and generates digestive heat" (line 2931). In the diet for phlegm (*baekan*) disorders, wild yak meat is listed beside mutton, the meat of carnivorous animals, fish and honey (Clark line 176; Men-Tsee-Khang line 1107); in the treatment passage it appears with mutton, fish, lynx and vulture (Clark line 1415; Men-Tsee-Khang line 4433). In the taste summary, "the meat of the wild yak, fish, mutton and honey are good for baekan disorders" even though the sweet taste in general increases *baekan* (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3249; Clark line 783).

**Milk and dairy.** For the diet in phlegm disorders Clark lists "curd and whey from she-yak's milk" (line 176). In the milk section: "She-yak's milk is harmful in phlegm and bile disorders" (line 708), and "The dairy products of sheep and of the she-yak have warm and highly nutritious power, whilst those of the cow and goat are cool and light. Dairy products obtained from the dzo have balanced (warm and cool) powers" (line 711). The two statements about phlegm sit in different passages and, read together, are not obviously consistent: the she-yak's curd and whey are recommended for phlegm disorders at line 176, while the milk itself is called harmful in phlegm and bile disorders at line 708. The text retrieved does not reconcile them. [USER: verify]

**Bile diet.** Among the foods recommended for bile disorders Clark lists "fresh meat of she-yak-calf or of ox-dzo cross" (line 175).

**Horn and blood of the wild yak.** In Clark's materia medica: "rgod.gyag: Takin: Wild yak: Budorcas taxicolor: [has ten names and is also known as hbrong]. Its horn increases [body] heat, disperses tumors, [and dries (accumulations of) pus in the abdomen]" (line 1158), and "The blood of wild yak and of Hodgson's antelope (gtsod): halts diarrhoea" (line 1173). Men-Tsee-Khang: "The blood of wild yaks and antelopes stop diarrhea" (line 3539).

**Surgical instruments.** A *she-yak tongue* gives its name to a form of instrument: Clark, "the fifteencentimeter-long hbri.lche (she-yak tongue) [spoon] is used to extract all trace (of tumors)" (line 1254); Men-Tsee-Khang, "A twelve finger breadths long surgical instrument shaped like a female yak's tongue withdraws tumors and fluids" (line 3831). This is a shape name; no yak material is involved.

**Identity.** The sources are not consistent about which animal "wild yak" is. Clark glosses *rgod.gyag* as "Takin: Wild yak: Budorcas taxicolor" (line 1158) and lists "wild yak (takin)" among the game animals (line 672). The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary on animals prints "Yak" with "Bos domesticus mutus" and "Wild yak" with "Bos grunniens" (lines 7082-7086, in a glossary whose columns are scrambled in the text file, so the pairing is by adjacency). A takin is a different animal from a wild bovine of the yak type, and the two translations do not agree. This entry reports both glosses and decides nothing. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Stated generally. Yak meat, like other red meat, is a source of protein, iron, zinc and B vitamins; yak milk, like other ruminant milk, contains fat, protein, lactose and calcium. Its fat is mainly saturated, with the usual ruminant fatty-acid pattern. The fat and protein proportions of yak milk differ from those of cow's milk, but no figures were read in the sources retrieved and none are given. Horn is mostly keratin, a structural protein. Blood contains haemoglobin, plasma proteins and electrolytes.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources describe yak flesh, dairy products, horn and blood as dietary and therapeutic items within the Tibetan scheme of *loong*, *tripa* and *baekan*. In that scheme a warm, oily food suits a cold disorder and does not suit a hot one, and the texts themselves treat she-yak milk with caution. No modern protocol is supplied here, and none should be inferred. Horn and blood of the wild yak are not an acceptable practice today: the wild animal is protected in many places and blood and horn carry hygiene and legal problems (see Contraindications). Ordinary yak meat and dairy are food in the regions where the animal is kept; this entry adds no dietary advice to that fact.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources retrieved give the following preparations and no more: meat as food (fresh meat of the she-yak-calf for bile disorders, Clark line 175); curd and whey of she-yak's milk as a phlegm-disorder diet (Clark line 176); horn and blood of the wild yak as materia medica (Clark lines 1158, 1173). No method of preparing horn or blood is given at these lines. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT).** Qualities printed in the texts read: yak meat is "greasy and warm" (Clark line 677) or "oily and warm" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 2931); wild yak meat has the specific action of relieving cold disorders of the liver and stomach and generating digestive heat (Men-Tsee-Khang line 2931; Clark line 679), and the passage separately calls the meat of herbivorous wild animals generally "cool and light in quality" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 2931), so the text gives no single quality for it; the dairy products of the she-yak have "warm and highly nutritious power" (Clark line 711); horn of the wild yak "increases [body] heat" (Clark line 1158). Meats in general are "sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste" (Clark line 673). No taste or potency is printed separately for yak blood beyond the action of halting diarrhoea. The Tibetan scheme has no TCM or Ayurvedic classification here, and none is supplied.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The Tibetan frame is that the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) arise from the three poisons, and that medicine, including diet, is a branch of treatment that supports practice and does not reach the root. Within that frame the yak appears as a food and drug chosen by its qualities against a disorder: warm and oily meat against cold disorders, wild yak meat for *baekan*, she-yak calf meat for bile, horn for cold and tumors, blood for diarrhoea (loci above). The rejuvenation passages in Clark (about line 1274) were read in outline, and the only animal named for rejuvenation in the meat list is the peacock (Clark line 678; Men-Tsee-Khang line 2931: "slows down ageing"). Yak is not named for rejuvenation. No *bcud len* (essence extraction) use of yak was found in the lines searched. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing here for a cultivator to take. The yak is a staple food of the plateau and the Tibetan texts treat it as a warm, oily food to be matched to a cold disorder and avoided in a hot one, with the she-yak's milk specifically flagged as harmful in phlegm and bile disorders at one locus. The wild yak's horn and blood are no practice to revive. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** raw or poorly handled meat, unpasteurised milk and blood can carry bacterial and parasitic infection (for example brucellosis and other zoonoses are general risks of unpasteurised ruminant dairy and raw animal products); blood and unprocessed horn are not suitable for internal use.
- **Conservation and law:** the wild yak is a protected species in many jurisdictions and trade in its parts may be illegal. The exact status is not stated in the sources and should be checked before any use. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** the animal is kept for milk, meat and work; wild-animal parts should not be sought.
- **Tradition's own cautions:** she-yak's milk is "harmful in phlegm and bile disorders" (Clark line 708); warm, oily meat is for cold disorders.
- **Dietary:** lactose intolerance, dairy allergy, and the usual cautions about saturated fat and red meat apply in general.
