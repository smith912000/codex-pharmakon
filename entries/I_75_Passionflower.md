## Section 1 — Nomenclature

### Primary Scientific Name
*Passiflora incarnata* L. — Passionflower / Maypop (Passifloraceae)
*Passiflora edulis* Sims — Passion Fruit (edible; mild medicinal)

### Nomenclature Table

| Tradition | Language | Name | Transliteration | Meaning |
|-----------|----------|------|-----------------|---------|
| Spanish (colonial) | — | Flor de la Pasión | — | "Flower of the Passion" — Christian missionaries interpreted the complex flower morphology as symbols of the Passion of Christ: 10 petals = apostles; corona = crown of thorns; 3 stigmas = nails; 5 anthers = wounds |
| Cherokee | Cherokee | — | Ocoee | "Maypop" — the sound the fruit makes when crushed; also the name of a river (Ocoee River, Tennessee) |

| Latin (Botanical) | Latina | Passiflora incarnata | — | *Passio* (passion/suffering) + *flora* (flower); *incarnata* = "flesh-coloured" (the pale pink petals) |
| English | — | Passionflower / Maypop / Wild Apricot | — | "Maypop" because the fruit pops when stepped on |

### Botanical Identity
Vigorous perennial vine 2–10m; highly distinctive flowers with white-purple corona filaments and complex geometric structure; yellow-green oval fruit (edible — tart flesh); native to eastern North America (SE United States, Appalachians to Texas). Naturalised in South America, Europe, Asia. The aerial parts (leaf, stem, flower, tendril) harvested before full flowering are the drug material; the fruit has distinct nutritional pharmacology (*P. edulis* provides GABA and BDNF in significant quantities). *P. incarnata* is the official pharmacopoeial species (ESCOP, German Commission E); tropical *Passiflora* spp. are not equivalent.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Badianus Manuscript / Mesoamerican (BM) — North American Indigenous

**Primary traditional users**: Multiple eastern North American nations with 2,000+ year archaeological evidence of passionflower use:

**Cherokee**: Used roots and leaves for boils and inflammation (topical); roots as a tonic for "liver conditions." The root was used in ceremonial contexts for calm and mental clarity. Cherokee traditional healers documented the calming application through the Mooney/Olbrecht ethnobotanical records (1888).

**Native Americans (broad)**: The ripe fruit (*maypop*) was widely consumed as food — nutritionally dense, palatable — and the leaves in tea for their calming effect. The food-medicine boundary was not sharply distinguished: daily consumption of passionflower fruit tea provided sustained low-dose anxiolytic exposure.

**Spanish colonial transmission**: The Spanish missionaries documented passionflower when they arrived in the Americas in the 1500s — the flower morphology triggered the Passion of Christ interpretation and the name *flor de la Pasión*, which entered European botanical literature. This is how the herb entered the European pharmacopoeia — via colonial botanical documentation of indigenous use.

### 2.2 South American (SA)

*Mburucuyá* / *maracujá* in Guaraní and Tupí traditions of southern Brazil, Paraguay, Argentina — for anxiety, insomnia, and convulsions. The South American and North American applications converge independently on the same anxiolytic/anticonvulsant indication.

### 2.3 De Materia Medica / European Adoption (DMM category)

European herbal adoption began immediately after Spanish introduction (1569 — Monardes' botanical survey). By the 17th century, passionflower was established in European herbal practice for nervous conditions and insomnia — confirming the indigenous application within 100 years of first contact.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 GABA-A Modulation (Primary)

**Chrysin + apigenin + vitexin**: Bind at the benzodiazepine site of GABA-A receptor → positive allosteric modulation → enhanced GABAergic inhibition → anxiolytic + sedative + anticonvulsant. Chrysin's benzodiazepine site binding is well-characterised (IC50 ~3µM for displacement of diazepam binding).

**Critical distinction**: Passionflower's GABA-A binding is at the **benzodiazepine site** (same as diazepam, valium) but with partial agonism — it does not produce the same degree of sedation, tolerance, or dependence as benzodiazepines. The partial agonism profile may explain clinical utility for mild anxiety without sedative side effects.

### 5.2 MAO Inhibition (Harmane Alkaloids — Dose Caution)

**Harmol, harman**: These β-carboline alkaloids (related to the MAOIs in ayahuasca) inhibit MAO-A → increase monoamine levels. **At traditional oral doses**, harmane alkaloids are present in trace amounts — insufficient for significant MAOI activity. **At very high doses** or with concentrated extracts: potential MAOI interaction. This is primarily a theoretical concern with standard preparations but requires awareness with serotonergic medications.

### 5.3 Opioid Receptor Modulation

**Flavonoids + chrysin**: Partial opioid receptor agonism → supports opiate withdrawal (reduces withdrawal anxiety, muscle spasms, insomnia during detox). The indigenous use for pain and the European application in opiate withdrawal are mechanistically related.

### 5.4 Anti-Spasmodic

**Isovitexin + orientin**: Smooth muscle relaxation via calcium channel antagonism → antispasmodic for gut, bronchial, uterine smooth muscle.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Anxiety**: Akhondzadeh (2001, *Journal of Clinical Pharmacy and Therapeutics*): Passionflower 45 drops/day vs oxazepam 30mg/day — equivalent anxiolytic efficacy at 4 weeks; passionflower had significantly less job impairment (functional superiority at equivalent anxiolysis). Small RCT (36 patients) but direct pharmaceutical comparison.

**Pre-operative anxiety**: Movafegh (2008): Passionflower extract 500mg 90 minutes pre-operatively vs placebo — significantly reduced pre-operative anxiety without impaired psychomotor performance.

**Sleep**: Multiple small trials confirm improved sleep quality, sleep latency, and sleep efficiency with passionflower tea (2g in 250ml hot water before bed) vs placebo.

**Opiate withdrawal**: Akhondzadeh (2001b): Passionflower + clonidine vs clonidine alone for opiate withdrawal — combination significantly superior for mental symptoms of withdrawal (anxiety, agitation, irritability) while equivalent for physical symptoms. Passionflower addresses the GABA-A-related psychological withdrawal component.

**ADHD**: Movafegh/Moshiri (2005): Passionflower extract vs methylphenidate (Ritalin) — equivalent improvement in ADHD hyperactivity scores; better tolerability. Small, preliminary.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The Passion and the pharmacology**: Spanish Jesuit missionaries arriving in South America in the 1560s encountered indigenous peoples using *mburucuyá* for calming and anxiety — and found in the flower's extraordinary morphology a visual theology. The 10 petals = 10 faithful apostles; the corona = crown of thorns; the 3 stigmas = 3 nails; the 5 anthers = 5 wounds; the coiling tendrils = whips. The name *flor de la Pasión* was the missionary's religious interpretation layered over indigenous pharmacological knowledge. The naming preserved the plant in European consciousness long enough for its clinical properties to be formally documented. The Passion narrative — a story of suffering, death, and transformation — accidentally preserved a superior anxiolytic whose mechanism relates to reducing the suffering of anxiety. The metaphor and the pharmacology align.

**The most globally distributed medicine**: *Passiflora* represents one of the clearest examples of pharmacological knowledge transfer through colonial botany. Indigenous Cherokee and Guaraní knowledge (anxiolytic, anticonvulsant) → Spanish colonial documentation (1569) → European herbal adoption (1600s) → German Commission E approval (1985) → global pharmaceutical preparations. The chain from ethnobotanical observation to regulatory approval runs across 450 years and multiple continents — but the pharmacological core (GABAergic anxiolytic) was intact at every stage.

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM (cross-referencing)**: Sweet, slightly bitter; cool. Channels: Heart (心), Liver (肝). Calms Shen, clears Liver Heat, antispasmodic.

**Ayurvedic (cross-referencing)**: Sweet, slightly bitter; cooling. Reduces Pitta ↓↓. *Hrdya* (cardiac), *Medhya* (cognitive), *Pittahara*.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Pregnancy**: Uterotonic at high doses — avoid therapeutic doses; culinary amounts in fruit form acceptable
- **MAO inhibitors (pharmaceutical)**: Harmane alkaloids + external MAOI = serotonin syndrome risk — avoid concurrent use
- **Benzodiazepines, sedatives**: Additive CNS depression — reduce doses if combining; monitor sedation
- **Anticoagulants**: Flavonoids → mild anti-platelet — monitor INR with warfarin
- **Surgery**: Additive sedation with anaesthesia — discontinue 2 weeks pre-operatively if using high doses
- **Driving**: Mild sedation at therapeutic doses — caution if operating heavy machinery
- Generally among the safest anxiolytics in the Codex; widely used in paediatric nervous conditions in European herbal practice
