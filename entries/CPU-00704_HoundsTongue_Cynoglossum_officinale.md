# Hound's Tongue — Gypsywort, Dog's Tongue, *Cynoglosson* (Greek), *Lisan al-Kalb* (Arabic, "dog's tongue")

## *Cynoglossum officinale* L. | Family: Boraginaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT (Assyrian), CM (Avicenna), DMM (Dioscorides)
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry in the second degree (Galenic); mucilaginous, astringent, sedative-narcotic
**Training Phase:** Purification — *restricted; external and symbolic use only*

---

> ⚠️ **HAZARD — INTERNAL USE UNSAFE.** *Cynoglossum officinale* is rich in hepatotoxic, genotoxic and carcinogenic pyrrolizidine alkaloids (heliosupine, echinatine, and related esters). It must **not** be taken internally. This entry documents the plant for scholarly and historical completeness only. See Contraindications.

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Dioscorides (DMM):** *De Materia Medica* records *kynoglosson* — "dog's tongue," named for the soft, tongue-shaped, downy leaf. Dioscorides describes topical use of the bruised leaf and root as an emollient dressing for burns, ulcers and inflammations, and notes a reputation for soothing coughs and fluxes. The soft, saliva-like mucilage of the crushed leaf gave the plant both its name and its demulcent character.

**Avicenna (CM):** In the *Qanun*, *lisan al-kalb* appears among the cooling, mucilaginous simples used externally to check hot swellings and to dress ulcers, and internally (in the pre-modern manner) as an astringent for fluxes and a mild sedative. Avicenna's cold-and-dry classification reflects the plant's binding, quieting surface action. These internal uses are historically genuine but are now understood to have carried grave, unrecognised hepatotoxic risk.

**Assyrian (AT):** The cuneiform medical tradition of the Assyrian tablets includes a wide repertory of downy, mucilaginous wound-herbs applied as poultices and dressings. Hound's-tongue-type Boraginaceae belong to this general class of topical emollients for ulcers and skin lesions; the attribution is at the level of plant-class rather than a single securely identified line.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Hound's tongue is the physician's paradox made flesh — a plant whose soft, comforting tongue conceals a poison in the sap. Its old apotropaic reputation (leaves laid in the shoe to quiet barking dogs, or to ward the bite) mirrors the pharmacological truth the ancients could not see: a remedy that soothes the surface while silently harming the deep organ. In Kenetica it is retained in the Purification curriculum precisely as a lesson in discernment — that the mildness of a demulcent is no guarantee of its safety, and that the liver bears the hidden cost of what the skin finds gentle.

### Active Components

- **Pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) — heliosupine, echinatine, and related 1,2-unsaturated esters** — the toxicologically dominant constituents; metabolised in the liver to reactive pyrroles that alkylate DNA and cellular proteins. *These are the reason internal use is prohibited.*
- **Mucilage (polysaccharide)** — accounts for the soft, demulcent, emollient quality of the crushed leaf and the traditional topical use.
- **Tannins** — astringent principles underlying the binding action on wounds and fluxes.
- **Allantoin (characteristic of Boraginaceae)** — associated with tissue-soothing and epithelial support in related family members.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Hound's tongue is a **restricted, teaching-only** entry. It is *not* dispensed internally under any circumstances. Its documented traditional actions — mucilaginous demulcent, astringent, mild topical anodyne for burns and ulcers — are recorded here for lineage completeness and to anchor the Purification-phase lesson on hepatic hidden-load and the limits of "gentle" surface remedies. Where a practitioner needs a genuinely safe demulcent wound-herb, the curriculum redirects to PA-free alternatives (see cross-references). No Kenetica protocol calls for ingestion of this plant.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, direct clinical pharmacology of *Cynoglossum officinale* itself is sparse; the safety picture is drawn from the well-characterised toxicology of pyrrolizidine alkaloids across the Boraginaceae, to which hound's tongue belongs:

- Brown et al. (2015), *Journal of Applied Toxicology* — in a controlled animal study, a reduced crude Boraginaceae (comfrey) alkaloid extract was *more* hepatotoxic than its purified individual pyrrolizidine alkaloids, indicating a greater-than-additive toxic effect and warning that safety estimates based on single purified compounds may underestimate whole-plant risk. DOI: [10.1002/jat.3205](https://doi.org/10.1002/jat.3205)
- Kimel et al. (2023), *Molecules* — LC-MS/MS profiling of Boraginaceae (comfrey) material found wide variation in pyrrolizidine-alkaloid content, with some commercially available root samples exceeding the daily amount considered safe. DOI: [10.3390/molecules28166171](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules28166171)
- Liu et al. (2009), *Talanta* — analytical work confirming the presence and quantification of hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids (lycopsamine, echimidine, lasiocarpine and N-oxides) in Boraginaceae medicinal material, underscoring how routinely these toxins occur in the family. DOI: [10.1016/j.talanta.2009.08.020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.talanta.2009.08.020)

**Evidence grade: Traditional (for efficacy) / Strong (for hazard).** No credible modern clinical evidence supports internal therapeutic use. The hepatotoxic, genotoxic and carcinogenic potential of the pyrrolizidine alkaloids characteristic of this genus and family is, by contrast, well established in toxicological literature. The risk–benefit balance forbids internal use.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM (assimilated):** cool and astringent; binds fluxes and settles hot sores — *documented historically, not recommended*.
- **Ayurveda (peripheral):** cooling, binding, mucilaginous surface remedy; no established classical *rasa/virya* profile — assimilation is marginal.
- **Galenic/Unani (Avicenna):** cold and dry in the second degree; astringent, emollient, mildly narcotic.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** In the cuneiform medical corpus the *asû* and *āšipu* worked a large repertory of downy, mucilaginous wound-herbs as poultices and dressings, and illness was classified in part by causal agency — the hand of a god, a ghost, a broken oath — so that a material was reckoned partly by what agency it was set against. Hound's-tongue-type Boraginaceae belong to this general topical-emollient class, but the identification here is at the level of plant-class, not a single securely read line, and the standing blocking warning is in force for every Assyrian substance identification, deity attribution and tablet reference. No cultivation status can be responsibly asserted. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* records *kynoglosson* as a topical emollient — the bruised leaf and root for burns, ulcers and inflammations, with a reputation for soothing coughs and fluxes. Dioscorides carries no subtle-body or vital-constitution system into which such a status could be entered; what the tradition offers a cultivator is instead his discipline of attention — the tongue-shaped downy leaf as the identifying mark, the saliva-like mucilage of the crushed leaf as sensory assay, the guard against confusing it with kin. That is a real practice of recognition and provenance, not a cultivation doctrine, and it is sharpened rather than softened here by the plant's hidden hepatotoxicity — the demulcent surface that assay cannot reveal to be poison. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** In the *Qānūn*, *lisān al-kalb* is placed among the cooling, mucilaginous simples, cold and dry in the second degree, used externally against hot swellings and ulcers and (in the pre-modern manner) internally as an astringent for fluxes and a mild sedative. The tradition's central cultivation idea — the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture as a finite vital endowment — is a longevity frame of the whole regimen, not a status the corpus assigns to this binding, quieting surface remedy, and no *mufarrih* or cardiac-*rūḥ* role is recorded for it. Its standing is therapeutic, not cultivational. **(b) framed** — and the pre-modern internal use is now known to have carried grave, unrecognised hepatotoxic risk.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing here for a cultivator to take. Hound's tongue is a teaching-only, internally-prohibited entry: its pyrrolizidine alkaloids are hepatotoxic, genotoxic and carcinogenic, and no tradition on the tag line assigns it a cultivation status — the Assyrian attribution is class-level and blocked, Dioscorides offers only the discipline of correct identification, and the Unani reputation is therapeutic. What the cultivator does with this plant is learn from it: that a soft, comforting, demulcent surface is no proof of safety, and that the liver pays the hidden cost. For an actual demulcent wound-herb the curriculum redirects to the PA-free substitutes.

### Contraindications

**ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION — DO NOT INGEST.**

- **Hepatotoxicity:** *Cynoglossum officinale* contains 1,2-unsaturated pyrrolizidine alkaloids that are bioactivated in the liver to reactive pyrroles. These cause hepatic sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (veno-occlusive disease), acute and chronic liver injury, and can be fatal. There is **no safe oral dose.**
- **Genotoxicity and carcinogenicity:** Pyrrolizidine alkaloids of this class are DNA-reactive genotoxins and are regarded as carcinogenic; damage is cumulative and can follow low, repeated exposure.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** Strictly contraindicated. PAs cross the placenta and are excreted in milk, and are associated with fetal and neonatal liver injury.
- **Children, and hepatic or biliary disease:** Strictly contraindicated; heightened vulnerability to PA hepatotoxicity.
- **Topical use:** Not endorsed. PAs can penetrate broken or inflamed skin, and the safety of dermal exposure is unestablished. Prefer PA-free demulcents.
- **Regulatory note:** Herbal products containing 1,2-unsaturated pyrrolizidine alkaloids are restricted or prohibited for internal use in many jurisdictions; hound's tongue falls within this class.
