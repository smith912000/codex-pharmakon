# Solanum lyratum — Bai Mo 白莫 (Superior Class) and Shu Yang Quan 蜀羊泉 (Middle Class) (Shennong Bencao Jing)

## *Solanum lyratum, as the pharmaceutical Latin "Herba Solani Lyrati" which Yang 1998 prints for two separate entries, Bai Mo and Shu Yang Quan; for Bai Mo Yang's note 116 says there is "no consensus concerning the identity of this medicinal"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Shennong text (SBJ).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. Both entries are in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`). The Latin name is the translator's pharmaceutical Latin and was matched to the binomial by stem in the extraction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv`, rows 75 and 142). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain, and for Bai Mo the translator himself says it is not. The row was added from the printed names (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Chinese characters are those of the Codex's own list of Shennong names (`_sources/shennong.csv`, lines 53 and 182), not of Yang's English text. [USER: verify]

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *Bai Mo, PDF page 51.* The page marker `[[p51]]` is at line 1969 and the entry begins at line 1984. No class heading is printed on this page. The governing heading is "Herbs: Superior Class" at line 1929 on the page marked `[[p50]]` (line 1928), and the next heading, "Herbs: Superior Class" again, is at line 2003 on `[[p52]]` (line 2002). The entry therefore stands under the superior-class heading by position (fifty-five lines and one page break below it), not directly beneath it. The text reads:

"Bai Mo (Herba Solani Lyrati)116 is sweet and cold. It mainly treats cold and heat, the eight categories of jaundice,117 and wasting thirst. It supplements the center and boosts the qi. Protracted taking may make the body light and prolong life. Its other name is Gu Cai (Grain Vegetable)." (lines 1984 to 1988)

Yang's note 116 (lines 1994 and 1995) reads: "There is no consensus concerning the identity of this medicinal. Another possibility is that it is Herba Vincetoxici Atrati." Yang's note 117 (lines 1996 onward) is on the classification of jaundice: it says that "throughout the history of Chinese medicine, jaundice has been classified in different, confusing ways" and that it is "generally" divided into five types, and it then lists categories by name. The entry says "eight categories", the note refers to "five" and "nine", and the Codex does not reconcile them. Both notes are the translator's, not the classic's.

*Shu Yang Quan, PDF page 82.* The page marker `[[p82]]` is at line 3061 and the entry begins at line 3068. No class heading is printed on this page either. The governing heading is "Herbs: Middle Class" at line 3020 on `[[p81]]` (line 3019), and the next heading, "Herbs: Middle Class", is at line 3093 on `[[p83]]` (line 3092). The entry stands under the middle-class heading by position (forty-eight lines and one page break below it), not directly beneath it. The text reads:

"Shu Yang Quan (Herba Solani Lyrati) is bitter and slightly cold. It mainly treats baldness, malign sores, heat qi, scabs, and lichen worms. It cures tooth decay. It grows in rivers and valleys." (lines 3068 to 3070)

The entry has no note number in Yang's text, so the translator offers no comment on its identity.

*The pattern of the headings.* Reading the page markers around both entries, the class heading is printed at the top of alternate pages (p50 and p52 for the superior class; p81 and p83 for the middle class), and the pages between (p51, p82) carry the running head "The Divine Farmer's Materia Medica Classic" in its place. That is the reading of the Codex from the line positions and has not been checked against the printed book. [USER: verify]

So the Shennong text has two separate entries under two names, each with its own taste, indications and (for the second) habitat. They carry the same Latin name in Yang's text, which does not tell us that the classic meant one plant. The Codex keeps them in one entry because the translator gives both the same Latin species name, and it does not decide whether they are one plant, two, or neither of them *Solanum lyratum*.

*The class headings.* Yang's table of contents prints "Herbs: Superior Class" (line 711) and "Herbs: Middle Class" (line 716). The preface (lines 352 and 353) says of the superior class: "There are 120 superior class medicinals which are used as sovereigns. They mainly nourish life and correspond to heaven. They are nontoxic and taking them in large amounts and for a long time will not harm people. If one intends to make one's body light, boost the qi, prevent aging, and prolong life, one should base [one's efforts] on the superior class" (lines 352 to 355 and 382 to 384). Of the middle class it says (lines 385 to 390): "There are 120 medium class medicinals which are used as ministers. They mainly nurture personality and correspond to humanity. They may or may not be toxic, and [therefore,] one should weigh and ponder before putting them to their appropriate use. If one intends to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion, one should base [one's efforts] on the middle class." The counts printed in the preface and in Yang's commentary (lines 365 and 366) differ, and this entry does not rely on either.

**Overlap.** The existing entry I_902 (*American Black Nightshade*, *Solanum americanum*) treats the same two Shennong entries in its Chinese paragraph, because they print "Herba Solani Lyrati", and states that they are not *S. americanum*. It cites them with the same line numbers (1984 and 3068) and the same two headings (lines 1929 and 3020). It is an entry on a different species with the Shennong rows as its nearest matches; the present entry carries them as their own. The overlap is reported and not merged. No other entry on these two Shennong rows was found.

### Active Components

The composition of the plants the Shennong entries mean was not read. It is a general fact that plants of the genus *Solanum* in the nightshade family commonly contain steroidal glycoalkaloids, which are toxic in quantity. Whether this holds for the plant of Bai Mo or Shu Yang Quan is not stated by the text. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Shennong text lists indications in its own terms (cold and heat, the "eight categories" of jaundice and wasting thirst for Bai Mo; baldness, malign sores, heat qi, scabs, lichen worms and tooth decay for Shu Yang Quan), which are not modern diagnoses and need medical care. The identity of Bai Mo is, on the translator's own word, without consensus. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The two entries give no preparation, quantity, frequency or duration. The text gives only taste, indications, other name (for Bai Mo) and habitat (for Shu Yang Quan). Bai Mo's "Protracted taking" is a duration in general terms and not a dose. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SBJ:** *Bai Mo* is "sweet and cold" (line 1984). *Shu Yang Quan* is "bitter and slightly cold" (line 3068). The text prints no toxicity statement for either entry. The preface's general statements about class (superior "nontoxic", middle "may or may not be toxic") are given above and are not entry-specific facts.

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *(a) Attested, by position, for each entry separately.* The Shennong text assigns a class to these two entries by the printed headings above them, and this is a grade of the printed entries, whatever plant they mean.

*Bai Mo* stands under "Herbs: Superior Class" (heading at line 1929, entry at line 1984, by position and not directly beneath). The preface (lines 352 to 355, 382 to 384) gives the purpose of that class: mainly to "nourish life", nontoxic, and to be relied on if one intends to "make one's body light, boost the qi, prevent aging, and prolong life". The entry itself says: "It supplements the center and boosts the qi. Protracted taking may make the body light and prolong life." The Codex records the effect category *make the body light* in the text's own words and does not translate it into anything pharmacological; Yang's own note on that phrase (lines 371 to 375, in a stretch where the OCR order is scrambled) ties such phrases to Daoist elixir writing and is the translator's commentary, not the classic's. Bai Mo's identity is, in Yang's note 116, without consensus, so the status is the status of the entry as printed.

*Shu Yang Quan* stands under "Herbs: Middle Class" (heading at line 3020, entry at line 3068, by position and not directly beneath). The preface (lines 385 to 390) says of that class that it mainly "nurtures personality", may or may not be toxic, and is for controlling disease, supplementing vacuity and replenishing exhaustion. The entry itself states no long-term taking, no effect on the body's lightness and no prolonging of life; it names baldness, sores, scabs, lichen worms and tooth decay. The same Latin name therefore stands in two classes in two different entries. Neither entry asserts a *jing*, *shen* or *neidan* action, and none is added here.

**Practitioner's note.** A superior-grade entry with an open identity and a middle-grade entry for skin and tooth complaints. Taking Bai Mo's "protracted taking" as a practice would rest on a plant nobody can name with confidence, and the text itself says middle-class drugs call for weighing before use. The grade is the classic's; the plant is not known. The abstentive answer is the honest one: do not take a nightshade on the strength of this entry.

### Contraindications

Plants of the nightshade genus include well-known poisons, and the green berries and leaves of several are toxic. The Shennong text prints no warning for these two entries, and its silence is not a statement of safety; the preface itself says the middle class "may or may not be toxic". Yang's note says the identity of Bai Mo is not agreed, so no plant should be gathered or taken on the strength of this entry. Jaundice, wasting thirst, sores and tooth decay need medical assessment. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
