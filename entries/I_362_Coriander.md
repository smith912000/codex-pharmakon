# I-362 — Coriandrum sativum · Coriander / Cilantro

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Coriandrum sativum* L.  
**Family:** Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)  
**Common names:** Coriander (EN — seeds and whole plant); cilantro (EN — specifically fresh leaves, American usage from Spanish); Chinese parsley (EN, archaic); koriander (DE); coriandre (FR); cilantro / coriandro (ES); coriandolo (IT); koriandron [κορίαννον] (GR, classical); kuzbarah [كزبرة] (AR); dhaniya [धनिया] (HI/SA, seeds); hara dhaniya (HI — fresh leaves); yuan sui [芫荽] (TCM); xiang sui [香菜] (TCM, more common modern name); yentsai; dhane (NEP); uan hom [ผักชี] (TH)

**Critical botanical note:** *Coriandrum sativum* is a single species producing two completely distinct culinary products:
- **Coriander** = the dried mature seeds (round; citrus-cedar-spice aroma; essential oil linalool-dominant)
- **Cilantro** = the fresh leaves and stems (pungent; distinctive aliphatic aldehyde character; intensely aromatic)
These two forms share a plant but have dramatically different phytochemical profiles and pharmacological emphasis. The majority of clinical evidence applies to seeds (coriander); the famous "soap gene" phenomenon applies to leaves (cilantro).

**Parts used:** Seeds/fruits (medicinal primary — most pharmacological evidence); fresh aerial parts / leaves (culinary, cilantro); root (Thai cuisine; minor medicinal); essential oil (distilled from seeds or leaves — different compositions)  
**Habitat & distribution:** Annual; native to Southern Europe, North Africa, and Western Asia; among the oldest cultivated plants; continuous cultivation documented from Neolithic sites through all major ancient civilizations; now globally cultivated; grows in a wide range of well-drained soils; temperate to subtropical

## 3. Convergence Analysis

Coriander achieves 7/9 convergence — tied with fennel as among the highest in the Apiaceae family — and holds a singular historical distinction: it is **one of the most frequently mentioned herbs in the oldest comprehensive pharmacopoeias preserved from antiquity**. In the Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE), coriander (*ktyt*) appears in more than 30 prescriptions. In Assyrian cuneiform texts from Nineveh and Nippur (c. 700–500 BCE), ur-nu-hu appears repeatedly in compound digestive preparations. In the Shennong Bencao (attributed c. 200 BCE–200 CE), coriander's role in "bringing out" measles rash represents a specific therapeutic application (diaphoretic pustulant effect) absent from Western traditions — cross-cultural differentiation within confirmed convergence.

Coriander's cross-traditional reputation as a **digestive remedy, carminative, and cooling anti-inflammatory** is unmatched in consistency. The "cooling" classification appears in three independent humoral frameworks (Arabic: cold/dry 2nd degree; Ayurvedic: pitta-reducing; TCM: clears damp-heat) — all agreeing that coriander is appropriate for hot/inflammatory digestive conditions (as opposed to fennel's warming profile), creating a fascinating complementary pharmacological pair within the same family.

The **cilantro "soap" phenomenon** — where 4–14% of the global population perceives fresh cilantro leaves as tasting like soap, dish detergent, or bugs — has been resolved by genetic analysis: variants in the olfactory receptor genes *OR6A2* (and related *OR4C11*, *OR4C12*) determine whether the plant's aliphatic aldehydes (primarily (E)-2-alkenal compounds) register as pleasant/herbaceous or soapy/unpleasant. Population frequencies of the soap-perception allele differ by ethnicity: highest prevalence among European-ancestry populations (~14%), lowest among South Asian and East Asian populations (~3–7%), consistent with evolutionary selection patterns reflecting historical coriander exposure in ancestral diets. This represents one of the most pharmacogenomically characterized food preference variations in human biology.

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**Anxiolytic / Sedative (linalool):**
Linalool (60–80% of seed essential oil) → allosteric potentiation of GABA-A receptors → enhanced chloride ion conductance → neuronal hyperpolarization → anxiolytic and sedative effects. Confirmed in multiple mouse behavioral assays (elevated plus maze, forced swim test, light-dark box) at inhaled and oral doses. The linalool-GABA-A mechanism also underlies lavender's (*Lavandula angustifolia*) well-documented anxiolytic effect — coriander seeds share the same primary mechanism through a different aromatic starting point. Human anxiolytic data: pilot level only.

**Antimicrobial (linalool, aldehydes):**
Linalool disrupts bacterial membrane integrity (increased membrane fluidity → loss of ion gradients → cell death) — active against *E. coli*, *S. aureus*, *Salmonella*, *Listeria*, *Candida albicans*. Cilantro leaf aldehydes (dodecenal, decenal) show particularly strong *Salmonella* and foodborne pathogen inhibition in vitro (Kubo 2004: dodecenal twice as potent as ampicillin against *Salmonella* in vitro). The antimicrobial activity of cilantro has been proposed as explaining the global culinary use of coriander alongside meat and fermented preparations in hot climates — a food preservation as folk pharmacology hypothesis.

**Hypoglycemic:**
Coriander seed extract → α-glucosidase inhibition → reduced rate of glucose absorption from diet; insulin secretagogue activity (possible β-cell stimulation); hepatic glycogen synthesis enhancement. Multiple animal studies (STZ-diabetic rat models) consistently demonstrate blood glucose reduction. Human pilot trials: 3 small RCTs show significant glucose reduction. Mechanism consistent; human evidence scale insufficient for Level A.

**Heavy metal chelation:**
Cilantro leaf extract has received attention as a heavy metal (lead, mercury, cadmium) chelator. In vitro: organic acids and flavonoids in cilantro form complexes with metal ions. Animal studies: cilantro extract reduces lead deposition in bone in Pb-exposed mice (Aga 2001). Human clinical evidence: single case series (patients with amalgam dental restorations who consumed high-cilantro-extract doses showed increased urinary Pb/Hg excretion — not controlled). The heavy metal chelation application is biologically plausible but remains Level D evidence due to absence of controlled human data; "cilantro chelation" as practiced in alternative medicine is not validated.

**Anti-inflammatory:**
Linalool → NF-κB pathway suppression (in vitro); COX-2 inhibition (quercetin, kaempferol). Animal inflammatory models (carrageenan, CFA): consistent anti-inflammatory effect. Human evidence: indirect (dietary pattern association; no direct anti-inflammatory trial with coriander).

**Pitta-cooling / Humoral (TCM/Ayurveda):**
The consistent "cooling" classification across Ayurvedic (pitta-reducing) and TCM (clears damp-heat) frameworks may reflect the linalool-mediated anxiolytic/anti-inflammatory effects — reducing "inflammatory fire" in multiple organ systems. The bitter-aromatic balance of coriander seeds (compared to heating herbs like black pepper or long pepper) supports carminative action without the gastric irritation of pungent spices.

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**Commission E approved (Germany):**
Coriander fruit (seeds) recognized in combination products for dyspeptic complaints; not issued a standalone Commission E monograph (classified under carminative spice combinations). ESCOP does not have a separate monograph; Traditional Herbal Registration in the UK includes coriander seed in carminative combinations.

**Level B:**
- **Carminative/digestive:** Consistent clinical series and combination product RCTs support carminative use; coriander alone not isolated in most trials (typically combined with other Apiaceae carminatives); clinical effectiveness consistent with mechanistic data
- **Hypoglycemic:** Pooled evidence from 3 small RCTs (combined n <150): significant fasting glucose reduction; insufficient power for meta-analysis; direction consistent

**Level C:**
- **Anxiolytic/sedative:** Animal data strong; human pilot data (inhaled linalool, oral linalool from coriander) suggest mild anxiolytic effect; no large RCT
- **Antimicrobial / food preservation:** Laboratory data supports; no clinical trial for infection treatment

**Level D:**
- Heavy metal chelation: animal evidence; 1 uncontrolled human case series; insufficient for clinical use recommendation
- Anti-tumor: linalool and quercetin in vitro; no clinical cancer application

## 7. Phenomenological Record

**The Ebers Papyrus and ancient Egyptian pharmacopoeia:**
In the Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE, possibly 3500 years old), coriander appears in more than 30 out of ~880 prescriptions — a frequency exceeded in the entire papyrus only by beer, honey, and natron (soda ash). The range of applications is extraordinary: compound preparations for eye diseases, wound healing, digestive disorders, "expelling pain," promoting digestion after feasts, and as a pleasant flavoring for medicines. This frequency and diversity establishes coriander as the central aromatic medicine of ancient Egyptian pharmacy, positioning it not as a specialty drug but as the fundamental aromatic vehicle for compound preparations across all organ systems — the original "excipient herb."

**Assyrian cuneiform and temple pharmacy:**
Cuneiform tablets from the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh (c. 668–627 BCE) contain detailed botanical descriptions and prescriptions including coriander. The same tablets describe gardens planted at the Assyrian palace including coriander alongside cardamom, sesame, and multiple Apiaceae — establishing that Mesopotamian royal gardens were deliberate pharmacological collections. Coriander's presence in temple pharmacy (administered in sacred medical rituals) alongside its culinary role shows the continuous collapse of the medical-sacred-culinary boundary in ancient Near Eastern culture, where therapeutic herbs were also divine offerings and daily foods.

**The Biblical/Talmudic manna and coriander:**
Numbers 11:7 — "The manna was like coriander seed and its appearance like that of bdellium." Exodus 16:31 — "its taste was like wafers made with honey." The comparison of heavenly manna to coriander seed (in size and appearance) indicates that coriander was the most familiar small round seed in the ancient Israelite diet, serving as the natural comparison point for the size of a miraculous substance. The Talmud (BT Yoma 75a) extensively debates whether manna resembled coriander in color (white) or taste — a rabbinical argument spanning centuries that presupposes daily familiarity with coriander across Jewish communities.

**The cilantro soap gene and global culinary geography:**
The OR6A2/OR4C11/OR4C12 variant distribution — highest in European-ancestry populations, lowest in South Asian and East Asian populations — maps inversely onto the global history of coriander cultivation and consumption. Populations with millennia of exposure to cilantro have lower frequencies of the soap-perception allele, consistent with either directional selection against the allele in high-coriander-diet populations, or founder effects in populations where cilantro was absent until recent centuries. This represents one of the cleanest examples of dietary-genetic co-evolution in human nutritional history — the plant shaped the genome of the people who ate it most.

**Thai culinary integration (root):**
Thai cuisine is unique among global food traditions in its prominent culinary use of coriander root (ratchet trai rakhang), rather than just seeds and leaves. Coriander root is pounded with garlic and white pepper into the foundational Thai flavor trinity (khrueang thet) used in marinades, soups, and stir-fries. The root contains different essential oil composition from seeds and leaves — higher in decanal and long-chain aldehydes — and represents a pharmacological vehicle entirely absent from the Western or Indian herbal tradition of coriander use, demonstrating that a single species can yield at least three distinct pharmacological preparations (seed, leaf, root) with different phytochemical profiles.

## 8. Practical Application

**Therapeutic dose ranges:**

| Form | Dose | Indication |
|------|------|-----------|
| Coriander seed infusion (tea) | 1 tsp freshly crushed seeds/cup, 2–3×/day | Digestive; carminative; anti-emetic; anxiolytic (mild) |
| Ground coriander (culinary) | 5–15 g/day in cooking | Carminative; hypoglycemic adjunct; anti-inflammatory |
| Coriander seed extract (standardized) | 500–1000 mg/day | Hypoglycemic; digestive |
| Fresh cilantro (leaves) | 30–100 g/day culinary | Nutritive; antimicrobial dietary dose; heavy metal chelation (unproven) |
| Coriander essential oil (aromatherapy — inhaled) | 1–2 drops diffused, 15–30 min | Anxiolytic; stress reduction |
| Coriander tincture (1:5) | 2–3 mL TID | Digestive; carminative |

**Preparation principles:**
Seeds: freshly crush just before preparation — linalool is volatile; pre-ground loses potency rapidly. Toasting seeds (dry pan, 1–2 minutes) enhances flavor compounds and improves bioavailability of some constituents (heat disrupts cell walls releasing bound essential oil). For medicinal anxiolytic use, seed infusion preserves more linalool than long boiling. For culinary hypoglycemic benefit, daily cooking use as seasoning is preferable to episodic high-dose supplementation.

**Contraindications:**
- **Apiaceae allergy:** Cross-reactivity with fennel, dill, anise, celery, carrot; anaphylaxis risk in sensitized individuals; test cautiously in known Apiaceae allergy
- **Pregnancy (medicinal doses of seed oil/concentrated extracts):** Uterotonic potential at very high doses; culinary use as food spice is safe
- **Hypotension:** Linalool anxiolytic at high doses may lower blood pressure; avoid high supplemental doses in patients prone to hypotension

**Drug interactions:**
- Antidiabetic medications (metformin, insulin, sulfonylureas): additive hypoglycemic effect; monitor blood glucose; adjust dosing if necessary
- Benzodiazepines / CNS depressants: linalool GABA-A modulation may enhance sedative effects; caution with concurrent use
- Antihypertensive medications: possible additive blood pressure lowering at high supplemental doses; monitor BP

**Safety profile:**
Culinary use (seeds, fresh leaves): GRAS; extremely safe; consumed daily across the majority of the world's population. Seed preparations at therapeutic doses: very safe; GI cramping possible. Seed essential oil: generally safe in aromatherapy at standard dilutions; avoid concentrated oral essential oil ingestion. No documented serious adverse events from culinary or standard therapeutic use in the published literature.

**Energetic tier:** ◉ Qi (digestive/carminative/anti-inflammatory functional regulation); minor ☿ Shen (anxiolytic/cooling via linalool-GABA pathway)  
**Optimal form:** Freshly crushed seed infusion for carminative-anxiolytic use; daily culinary seasoning for hypoglycemic and anti-inflammatory dietary pharmacology; fresh cilantro at high culinary volumes for antimicrobial and nutritive benefit
