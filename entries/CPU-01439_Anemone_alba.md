*2-207. ANEMONE.* "Anemone has two types — one wild, the other cultivated. Of the cultivated some bear flowers in a Phoenician [red] colour, others of a pale, milky or purple colour. The leaves are similar to coriander but less ragged, next to the ground. The little stalks are downy, thin, on which are flowers like poppies with the heads in the middle black or azure [blue]. The root is the size of that of the olive or bigger. The wild is altogether bigger than the cultivated, broader and harder in the leaves, and it has a longer head. The flower is a Phoenician [red] colour; there are many small little roots; and there is one kind that has black leaves and is sharper. They are both sharp; as a result the juice of the root poured into the nostrils helps in purging the head. The chewed root extracts mucus. Boiled in passum [raisin wine] and applied it cures inflammation of the eyes, and mends scars in the eyes and moisture in the eyes; and it cleans the filthiness of ulcers. The leaves and stalks boiled together with barley water (and eaten) draw out milk [breastfeeding]. In a pessary it encourages the menstrual flow. Rubbed on it takes away leprosy." The index prints ANEMONE at p.344.

### Active Components

Members of the buttercup family (Ranunculaceae) generally contain ranunculin, which on crushing yields the irritant protoanemonin. The composition of the plants in the chapter was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records the sharp root juice snuffed to purge the head, the chewed root for mucus, the root boiled in raisin wine for eye inflammation and scars, leaves and stalks for milk, a pessary for menses, and a rub for leprosy. The text itself calls the plant "sharp" and the translators mark it POISONOUS. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

2-207 gives the root juice poured into the nostrils; the chewed root; the root boiled in passum and applied; leaves and stalks boiled with barley water and eaten; a pessary; and a rub. No quantity is given for any. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

The irritant character of fresh buttercup-family plants is well established in general. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints "sharp" ("They are both sharp") for both wild and cultivated kinds. No other quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant by its parts, where it grows, what it is called, and what is claimed for it. 2-207 distinguishes a wild and a cultivated kind, by flower colour, leaf, stalk and root size, and says that one kind with black leaves is sharper. It assigns no status. The identification, and the species named in this row's title, are not fixed by the text. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The useful lesson of the chapter is the attention to wild and cultivated forms. Do not use the fresh plant.

### Contraindications

The fresh plant is an irritant that can blister skin and mucous membranes, and the chapter's snuff, pessary and eye uses are hazardous. The translators mark the chapter POISONOUS. Do not use in pregnancy. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
