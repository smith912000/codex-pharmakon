# I-183 · Horsetail
**Equisetum arvense** L. | Equisetaceae

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Equisetum arvense* L. (1753)  
**Common synonyms:** Field horsetail, common horsetail, scouring rush, shavegrass, pewterwort, bottle brush plant, puzzlegrass, snake grass

**Etymology:**
- *Equisetum* = Latin *equus* (horse) + *seta* (bristle/tail) = horse tail — descriptive of jointed, bristle-like aerial stems
- *arvense* = Latin *arvum* (ploughed field) = of the field — habitat descriptor
- **Scouring rush:** Historic use to scour/polish pots, pewter, and wooden surfaces using high silica content — one of the earliest industrial applications of any plant
- **Shavegrass:** Same scouring application; European cabinetmakers used dried stems as fine abrasive
- **Pewterwort:** Used specifically to polish pewter (tin-lead alloy); named in English pewter-working guilds

**Ancient lineage — living fossil designation:**

*Equisetum* is among the most phylogenetically ancient vascular plant genera on Earth:
- Carboniferous period (359–299 MYA): tree-like *Calamites* species reached 30 meters; dominated coal forests
- Permian-Triassic extinction: *Calamites* extinct; smaller *Equisetum* lineage survived
- Modern *Equisetum* essentially unchanged for ~100 million years (Cretaceous onward)
- 15 extant species = survivors of a genus once dominant globally
- No direct relatives among modern vascular plants: Equisetopsida is the sole surviving class of its phylum
- Pre-dates flowering plants by ~200 million years; pre-dates seed plants by ~50 million years

**Morphological distinctives:**
- Jointed, hollow, ribbed stems with whorled branches at nodes; distinctly segmented
- Aerial sterile shoot (green, photosynthetic) vs. fertile shoot (brown, spore-bearing, spring-emerging before green stems)
- True leaves reduced to scale-like sheaths at nodes; photosynthesis by green stem tissue
- Rhizomatous; nearly impossible to eradicate once established; depth to 1.5m below surface
- Silica deposited in epidermal cells (phytoliths) = responsible for abrasive quality

**Part used:** Aerial sterile shoot (the green, jointed stems and branches); harvested summer

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Dual convergence cluster: diuretic vs. silica/mineral applications**

The Greco-Roman and Islamic traditions independently converged on horsetail as a urinary/renal herb — its diuretic effect and potential for supporting kidney stone passage. The Chinese tradition identified a different primary application (ophthalmic, hemostatic), potentially reflecting different preparation methods or different *Equisetum* species (*E. hiemale* = "mu zei" is more commonly used in TCM than *E. arvense*).

**The silica paradox:**

Horsetail's most well-known modern claim is silicon supplementation for hair, skin, and nail health — yet the pharmacological evidence base for this is significantly weaker than for the diuretic function. The traditional use of horsetail for urinary conditions is well-documented across traditions; the modern "silicon supplement for beauty" positioning represents a divergence from traditional applications toward a different theoretical mechanism with limited RCT support.

**Living fossil and pharmacological conservation:**

The extreme phylogenetic age of *Equisetum* raises an interesting question about phytochemical evolution: the plant's chemical profile (silica, flavonoids, alkaloids) has been stable for ~100 million years. This makes horsetail one of the most ancient chemical fingerprints in the Codex — compounds whose evolutionary selection pressure predates the angiosperms.

**Alkaloid complexity note:**

*Equisetum arvense* contains nicotine (trace) and equisetonine, but the primary concern is **thiaminase** — the enzyme that destroys vitamin B1 (thiamine). This is clinically irrelevant at culinary/medicinal doses but becomes significant in livestock consuming large quantities (see Section 8: thiamine deficiency in horses feeding on horsetail pastures).

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Primary compounds:**

**1. Silicic Acid / Silicon (orthosilicic acid)**
- 5–8% total silica content (highest in plant kingdom for non-aquatic plants)
- Water-soluble silicic acid [Si(OH)₄]: bioavailable form for collagen synthesis support
- Insoluble silica phytoliths: the abrasive component; largely NOT bioavailable
- Distinction critical: only water-soluble silicic acid has pharmacological relevance
- The soluble fraction provides ~2–3 mg silicon per gram dry plant (highly variable by source)

**2. Flavonoids (0.2–0.9%)**
- Kaempferol-3,7-dirhamnoside (equisetrin): primary flavonoid
- Quercetin, apigenin, luteolin glycosides
- Isoquercitrin, quercetin-3-glucoside
- Anti-inflammatory (COX inhibition), antioxidant, mild diuretic contribution

**3. Phenolic Acids**
- Caffeic acid, ferulic acid, gallic acid
- Hydroxycinnamic acid derivatives
- Anti-inflammatory; antioxidant

**4. Alkaloids**
- Nicotine: trace amounts (0.00004%); pharmacologically irrelevant at standard doses
- Palustrine and palustrinine (Equisetum-specific alkaloids): potential neurotoxicity in large doses; absent or negligible in standardized preparations
- Equisetonine: not clearly characterized

**5. Thiaminase (enzyme — SAFETY CONCERN)**
- Heat-labile enzyme; destroyed by drying/cooking
- Cleaves thiamine (vitamin B1) into inactive fragments
- Relevant in: fresh plant consumption, poorly dried preparations, livestock grazing
- Standard dried, commercial preparations: thiaminase largely denatured
- Still: avoid prolonged use of large doses of fresh plant preparations

**6. Phytosterols and Miscellaneous**
- β-sitosterol; stigmasterol: minor anti-inflammatory
- Saponins (minor): contribute to diuretic effect
- Calcium (high): 4–5% dry weight; one of the richest plant calcium sources

**Mineral profile:**
- Silica: 5–8%
- Calcium: 4–5%
- Potassium: significant (contributes to diuretic effect)
- Manganese, selenium: present in moderate amounts

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**1. Diuretic Mechanism**
- Increased urine volume (aquaretic rather than true diuretic — increases water clearance without proportional electrolyte loss)
- Proposed mechanisms: flavonoid/potassium-mediated osmotic effect; renal tubular actions
- Weaker than pharmaceutical diuretics but well-documented in controlled studies
- Does NOT significantly deplete potassium (unlike loop diuretics) — important in elderly patients

**2. Silicon/Collagen Support (Proposed)**
- Orthosilicic acid [Si(OH)₄] required for prolyl-4-hydroxylase activity → hydroxylation of proline in collagen synthesis
- Silicon stimulates fibroblast collagen synthesis in vitro
- May stabilize glycosaminoglycans in connective tissue
- **Clinical evidence is weak** — mechanism plausible but human RCT data limited
- Serum silicon increases measurably after *E. arvense* extract consumption (Barel 2005)

**3. Antioxidant and Anti-inflammatory**
- Flavonoid pool (kaempferol, quercetin derivatives): free radical scavenging
- COX-1/COX-2 inhibition (phenolic acids)
- Reduction of oxidative stress markers in controlled studies

**4. Hemostatic (SBJ/TCM indication)**
- Caffeic acid + phenolic acids: platelet aggregation support
- Potential mild vitamin K-independent coagulation support
- Evidence: mostly traditional; limited modern mechanistic data

**5. Antimicrobial**
- In vitro activity against *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Mycobacterium* species
- Mechanism: phenolic acid-mediated membrane disruption
- No significant clinical evidence for systemic antimicrobial use

**6. Antispasmodic (Traditional)**
- Traditional use for urinary tract spasms and renal colic
- Proposed mechanism: flavonoid smooth muscle relaxation
- Evidence: observational/traditional; limited mechanistic data

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Diuresis:**
- Carneiro DM et al (2014) *Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine*: RCT N=36; *E. arvense* extract vs. hydrochlorothiazide vs. placebo; **horsetail equivalent to HCTZ for diuretic effect** in 24h urine volume (P<0.001 vs. placebo; P=ns vs. HCTZ); electrolyte impact less than HCTZ
- This is a significant finding: natural aquaretic comparable to pharmaceutical thiazide without proportional electrolyte depletion

**Bone Density (Silicon pathway):**
- Barel AO et al (2005) *Archives of Dermatological Research* 297:147–53: Orthosilicic acid supplementation (ch-OSA form, choline-stabilized); significant improvement in nail brittleness; some hair tensile strength improvement; N=50
- NOT specific to horsetail; used purified orthosilicic acid as supplement
- Murcia-Lesmes (2020): dietary silicon intake associated with better bone density in women; observational

**Urinary Tract Conditions:**
- Limited modern RCT data specifically; Commission E positive opinion based on traditional evidence + in vitro/animal data
- Observational studies support mild diuretic and urinary tract soothing effects

**Wound Healing:**
- Gomez-Estrada H et al (2011) *J Ethnopharmacol*: topical *E. arvense* preparation; accelerated wound closure in animal models; clinical translation not established in RCT

**Regulation:**
- **Commission E (Germany):** Approved for edema and diuresis as supportive treatment
- **EMA HMPC:** Traditional use indication for "relief of minor urinary complaints" — traditional herbal medicinal product classification
- **Commission E:** Also lists positive use for kidney and bladder conditions (WHO monograph consistent)

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The Carboniferous memory:**

Standing in a damp field margin and observing the delicate, jointed green stems of *Equisetum arvense* is to witness a survivor of the Carboniferous forest — the coal forests that powered the Industrial Revolution were built from the compressed remains of *Equisetum*'s ancestors. The coal that ran the steam engines was once a Calamite trunk. The plant fueled the industrial age in compressed form; in living form it persists in every wet meadow.

**Pewter polishing and the craft tradition:**

For centuries before sandpaper, European craftsmen used dried horsetail stems to polish pewterware, wooden furniture, and even musical instrument bodies. The high silica content created a natural fine-grade abrasive. This application was so common that "scouring rush" was a household term; horsetail bundles were sold in markets specifically for this purpose. Cabinetmakers' references to "shavegrass" appear in guild records from 14th-century Germany.

**Horsetail on Roman trade routes:**

Dioscorides describes *Equisetum* as both diuretic and wound-healing; Roman medical practice incorporated it widely. The similarity of the Chinese (*mu zei*, 木賊 = "wood robber/thief," reflecting its invasive agricultural nature) and European names reflect independent descriptive traditions converging on the plant's physical characteristics rather than therapeutic properties.

**The thiamine problem in traditional horse husbandry:**

Horses grazing on horsetail-rich pastures historically developed a syndrome called "equisetism" — progressive weakness, ataxia, and muscular incoordination from thiamine depletion. Medieval horse husbandry records document this as a pasture management problem: horsetail-heavy fields were known as risky grazing land. The pharmacological paradox: the plant named for horses caused a specific disease in horses when consumed in large quantities.

**Fern ally and evolutionary oddity:**

Botanically, *Equisetum* is a "pteridophyte" — a spore-reproducing vascular plant like ferns, distinct from seed plants. It is the only surviving genus of its entire class. Botanists sometimes call it "the loneliest plant" — the last representative of a once-dominant lineage. Every individual horsetail plant growing in a ditch is the last branch of a 350-million-year evolutionary tree that once covered continents.

**The invasive paradox:**

*Equisetum arvense* is considered one of the most difficult plants to eradicate — deep rhizomes, resilient to herbicide, tolerant of soil compaction and pollution. Farmers regard it as a serious weed; it appears on invasive species lists across North America. The same properties that make it pharmacologically interesting (deep mineral extraction from soil via rhizomes, silica accumulation) make it agriculturally problematic. Its persistence is the persistence of the Carboniferous.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Primary evidence-based indications:**
- Minor urinary complaints with associated edema (Commission E / EMA approved)
- Mild diuresis (equivalent to low-dose HCTZ in one RCT)
- Connective tissue support (silicon content — evidence weak but theoretically plausible)

**Evidence-based dosing:**
- **Standard dose:** 300 mg dry extract (4:1 concentration) 2–3× daily, OR 2–4g dried aerial parts as tea 3× daily
- **Tea preparation:** 2–4g dried herb in 150mL boiling water; steep 10 min; strain carefully (remove silica particles)
- **Duration:** Up to 6 weeks continuous; avoid exceeding without medical supervision
- **For silicon supplementation:** Standardized orthosilicic acid preparations preferred over crude herb (defined bioavailable silicon content)

⚠️ **SAFETY CONCERNS:**

**Thiaminase content:**
- Fresh plant: contains active thiaminase → B1 depletion possible with chronic use
- Properly dried commercial preparations: thiaminase denatured by heat
- Practical precaution: use only dried/processed preparations; avoid fresh plant preparations for extended use
- High-dose, long-term use: consider B-complex supplementation

**Nicotine content:**
- Trace; pharmacologically irrelevant at standard doses
- Patients with nicotine sensitivity: theoretical concern (clinically negligible)

**Drug interactions:**
- **Diuretics:** Additive; monitor electrolytes if combined with pharmaceutical diuretics
- **Digoxin:** Potassium depletion (if extended diuretic use) → increased digoxin toxicity; monitor
- **Lithium:** Diuretic effect may alter lithium clearance; monitor levels
- **Antidiabetic agents:** Minor glucose effects; monitor blood glucose
- **Vitamin B1 (thiamine):** Risk of deficiency with very large doses or fresh plant use

⚠️ **CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- Pregnancy and lactation: avoid (insufficient safety data; thiaminase concern)
- Cardiac or renal edema: horsetail NOT appropriate (use pharmaceutical diuretics under medical supervision)
- Thiamine deficiency states or malnutrition: avoid (thiaminase risk)
- Nicotine allergy (theoretical; trace nicotine present)

**Quality and standardization:**
- Look for: 10% silicic acid equivalent OR standardized silicon content
- Avoid: fresh plant preparations, non-standardized powdered herb (variable silica/alkaloid content)
- Commission E: "dried aerial parts, not the spore-bearing shoot" — use summer-harvested sterile shoot
- EU: herbal product classification; THMP (Traditional Herbal Medicinal Product) in UK

**Key patient communication:**
- "This is the plant the Romans used for kidney support; it's been shown to be as effective as a mild diuretic pill for minor swelling"
- "The scouring rush your grandmother may have known was this same plant, used to polish pots"
- "Use only dry tea or standardized extracts — not fresh-squeezed juice or fresh plant preparations"

**Energetic tier:** ◉ Qi (circulatory/functional) — primarily acts on fluid distribution, kidney filtration rate, urinary flow; functional/transport layer
