# Rhus verniciflua — Shri khenda, shri khan da (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary by column position); printed "Rhus vemiciflua"

## *Rhus verniciflua, as the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints it (OCR "Rhus vemiciflua", line 5808) as the fourth scientific name of its second "S" run, which column order sets against the name Shri khenda (shri khan da) with no English name ("-"); Clark 1995 prints no Rhus in his entry for the same Tibetan name, shri.khanda, and gives it Euphorbia sp., Fritillaria verticillata and Stewartia sp.* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi (Four Tantras), read in two modern translations, Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011. The binomial comes from the glossary's column order alone and conflicts with the Latin names Clark prints for the same Tibetan drug, so the botanical identity is open.** Both texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk found *Rhus* only as other species (Dioscorides *rous*, *Rhus coriaria*, 1-147, line 4594; Sushruta *Karkata-shringi* (printed "Earkata-S'ringi"), *Rhus succedanea*, Appendix 3, line 31930) and no *Rhus verniciflua*. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP, confidence "medium") and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. **Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 359) the row is keyed by the scientific name "Rhus vemiciflua", with the locus "Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (line 5808)" and the note "Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". The name stands alone on its line, so the key is the first and only name there. The OCR reads "r" followed by "n" as "m", so the form is read as *verniciflua*, and the spine merges the printed "vemiciflua" into it. The Tibetan name comes from the column order below and not from the CSV.

**The glossary, by column position.** `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the second "S" run. The phonetic names (lines 5774 to 5785) begin "Shingngar", "Shing tsha", "Shomang" (all on line 5774), then "Shri khenda" (line 5775), "Shudag" and "Shugpa tserchen" (5776), "Shumoza" (5777), "So chha" (5778), "Sok kapa" and "Solgongpa" (5779), "Solo", "Solo karpo", "Soma raza" (5780 to 5782) and "Subka" (5783). The transliterations (lines 5787 to 5796) begin "shing mngar shing tsha" and "sho mang shri khan da" (line 5788). The scientific names (from line 5798) read in turn "Glycyrrhiza glabr" (so printed), "Cinnamomum zeylannicum" (5802), "Rumex nepalensis" (5804), "Rhus vemiciflua" (5808), "Acorus calamus L." (5812), "Juniperus communis" (5816), "Trigonella foenum" (5820), "Sesbania sp." (5824), "Capsella bursa-pastoris" (5828), "Sophora hookeriana" (5830), "Rhodiola crenulata" (5834), "Pegaeophyton sacapiflorum" (5838), "Abelmochus manihot" (5840) and "Anemone tatrasepala" (5844). The English names begin "Licorice" (5800), "Cinnamon" (5802), "Field sorrel" (5806), "-" (5810), "Sweet Flag" (5814). Counted in order, "Shri khenda" is the fourth phonetic name, "shri khan da" the fourth transliteration, *Rhus vemiciflua* the fourth scientific name and "-" the fourth English name, so the drug has no English name in the glossary. The count is checked at the third place, "Shomang" against *Rumex nepalensis* (as read in I_1351); at the fifth, "Shudag" against *Acorus calamus*, as Clark's *shu.dag* (line 961) is "Sweet Flag (root): Acorus calamus"; at the sixth, "Shugpa tserchen" against *Juniperus communis* (I_1352); at the eighth, "So chha" against *Sesbania sp.*, as Clark's *so.cha* (line 986) is "Sesbania grandiflora"; and at the fourteenth, "Subka" against *Anemone*, as Clark's *srub.ka* (line 1118) is "Anemone rivularis". The pairing is by position and is not printed in a row. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents line 225), page 206.* Line 3427: "So chha brings up all disorders and is an excellent emetic. Dhenrog and Shri khenda strongly purges all disorders. Dhong ga cures liver disorders and is a mild purgative." *Chapter 21, "The Group and the Method of Compounding Medicine" (contents line 229), page 223.* Line 3661: "Arura, Dhenda, Dhong ga, Shri khenda, Dhurji, Thamu, Chum tsa, Ngonbu, Tronbu, Tsi takmo, Rechagpa and Chhu tsa are the group of medicines that are purgatives." The index lists "Shri khenda 206, 223" (line 7493). The text gives no Latin name, description or taste.

*Clark 1995, chapter 20 (heading at line 792), the run between "(5) Resins" (line 879) and "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997); no "(6)" heading is printed in the text on disk.* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, line 989: "shri.khanda: Euphorbia sp.: Fritillaria verticillata: Stewartia sp.: [has eight names. 'shri.khanda' means dpal.gyi.dum.bu (lit. 'a fragment of the Lord'). It is a large, blue tree with spreading branches not unlike an old man. It has astringent and slightly bitter tastes, and the branches yield a white, curdlike sap which is melted to make the concentrate. The inferior type grows in Nepal and elsewhere, and is a rounded, leafless plant containing similar sap]. Castor oil seed and shri.khanda are drastic purgatives. [The latter also heals abscesses]." Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the three Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the sentence "Castor oil seed and shri.khanda are drastic purgatives". Inside are the eight names, the meaning of the name, the description of the tree and its sap, the tastes and the healing of abscesses. Clark's name index lists *shri.khanda* (line 1763). In chapter 21, line 1225, the purgation class lists "Chebulic myrobalan, Castor Oil seed, Cassia pods, Euphorbia sp., Physic nut ..." (heading at line 1205); "Euphorbia sp." is its fourth item.

*The two translations read as one drug.* Clark's entries run in the order *seng.ldeng* (985), *so.cha* (986), *dan.rog* (987), *dan.khra* (988), *shri.khanda* (989), *dong.ga* (990), and the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence at line 3427 names "Sengdeng ... So chha ... Dhenrog and Shri khenda ... Dhong ga" in the same order, with the same verdict (a drastic purgative). The purgative lists agree too: the fourth item of Clark's list (line 1225) is "Euphorbia sp." and the fourth of the Men-Tsee-Khang list (line 3661) is "Shri khenda", with "Chebulic myrobalan", "Castor Oil seed" and "Cassia pods" before the one and "Arura", "Dhenda" and "Dhong ga" before the other. Neither text states that the two are one drug; the Codex reads them so from the spelling, the order and the action. [USER: verify]

**Identity: the glossary against Clark.** Clark's three names (*Euphorbia* sp., *Fritillaria verticillata*, *Stewartia* sp.) and the glossary's *Rhus verniciflua* do not overlap. Clark's commentary describes a tree whose branches yield a white, curdlike sap, which the Codex does not try to match to a plant. The only *Rhus* Clark prints is *Rhus semilata*, under another Tibetan name, *da.trig* (line 994). The row stands for the Tibetan drug and not for one species, and the Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Rhus verniciflua*, *vemiciflua*, *Rhus semilata*, *Toxicodendron*, *shri.khanda* and "Shri khenda" found no entry on this binomial. **I_1284** (*Fritillaria verticillata*, tag FMT) is an entry on the same Tibetan drug, keyed to Clark's second Latin name, written from Clark line 989 and the Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3427 and 3661; it reports that the glossary's scientific name could not be paired with certainty, and the position count above is the pairing this entry makes. I_1274, I_1275 and I_1276 (the Tibetan spurges) and I_1175, I_1259, I_1304, I_1306, I_1333 and I_1343 quote the same purgative lines for their own drugs. **I_332** (Sumach, *Rhus coriaria*, tags Avicenna and Dioscorides) and **I_1417** (*Rhus succedanea*, tag SS) are entries on other *Rhus* from other texts and are not this drug. There is no entry on Clark's other names for the drug, *Euphorbia* sp. and *Stewartia* sp. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. As a general fact, *Rhus verniciflua* is the lacquer tree of East Asia (also placed in *Toxicodendron*, family Anacardiaceae), whose sap is used to make lacquer and is a well-known cause of severe allergic contact dermatitis. That is a statement about the species named by the glossary and not a finding of the Tibetan texts, which do not say whether the Tibetan drug is that species. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record one action: the drug "strongly purges all disorders" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3427) and is among "the group of medicines that are purgatives" (line 3661); Clark calls it, with castor oil seed, a "drastic" purgative (line 989). That is the tradition's own regimen and not a Codex protocol. The identity is open and the glossary's species is a skin hazard, so the Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

Clark's commentary (inside the brackets, line 989) says that the sap is "melted to make the concentrate"; no method, quantity or compound is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The drug is in the rGyud-bzhi as a drastic purgative (Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3427 and 3661; Clark lines 989 and 1225). Its place is therapeutic. The rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23: Clark lines 1264 to 1276; Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3904 to 3945) calls for internal cleansing before the course; the compound it names for that is the three myrobalan fruits with other drugs, and "Shri khenda" is not named in it. No line read assigns the drug a status in rejuvenation or in *bcud len* (essence extraction). The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this drug on the strength of the texts. It is a drastic purgative of the physician's course, and the glossary's species, if it is the one meant, is one whose sap can burn the skin. Nothing here supports gathering or taking any lacquer-tree or sap-bearing plant on the strength of this entry.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning beyond the word "drastic". As a general fact the sap of *Rhus verniciflua* can cause severe skin reactions on contact. The identity is open, so no plant should be gathered, handled or taken on the strength of this entry. No conservation information and no safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
