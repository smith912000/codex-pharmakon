# Thracian stone — Thrakias Lithos (as printed in the chapter heading, "THRAKIAS LITHOS"); "Thracius" in the chapter text, Dioscorides 5-147
## *A stone found in Scythia near the river Pontus that "can perform the same cures as gagates" (jet or agate, 5-146) and is said to be set on fire with water and quenched with oil, as asphalt is; the translator gives no identification ("UNKNOWN")* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-147, heading "THRAKIAS LITHOS" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 10600; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 920, "DMM, THRAKIAS LITHOS, 5-147 (line 10600), UNKNOWN, mineral"; list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 873, "Thrakias Lithos, , mineral, DMM V, id_uncertain yes"; the book index gives "THRAKIAS LITHOS 819", line 12688). The whole chapter was read. It is two lines (the translator's "UNKNOWN" is line 10601; the text is line 10602):

"Thracius is found in Scythia near the river Pontus. It can perform the same cures as gagates [above] and it is said to be set on fire with water and quenched with oil, which is also done with asphalt [bitumen — asphalt — mineral pitch]."

The chapter therefore gives a place (Scythia near the river Pontus), a statement that it does the same cures as gagates, and one behaviour (it is "set on fire with water and quenched with oil"), which it says is also done with asphalt. It gives no description of colour, hardness, smell or weight, no rule for choosing the best, no disease of its own, no preparation and no dose. The Greek name is an adjective ("Thracian stone"); the chapter does not say why it is so called, and the place it names is Scythia, not Thrace.

**The place.** The translator's gazetteer (line 3527) has a headword "SCYTHIA, near the river PONTUS" which reads: "In Dioscorides' time Scythia was the country south of the Danube delta in modern Romania now called the Dobruja. Its inhabitants were the Scythae or Scythians. After 395CE the northern province of the diocese of Thrace in Greece was called Scythia. Pontus was on the southern shore of the Black Sea." The same entry thus gives Scythia as a country near the Danube delta and Pontus as the south shore of the Black Sea; the two do not lie in one place, and the text's "the river Pontus" is not explained by the gazetteer. Which river is meant is not stated. [USER: verify]

**The chapter it points to: gagates, 5-146.** The comparison is to *GAGATES LITHOS* (line 10593), where the translator suggests "Gagate — Jet or Agate". It reads (lines 10595-10599, across a page break): "Gagates is preferred which is kindled quickly and smells like bitumen. It is commonly black, unattractive, crusty and very light. It softens and dissolves. If the smoke is inhaled it induces epilepsy and calms women who are hysterical. It is mixed with medicines for gout and those to remove fatigue, and smoke from it drives away snakes. It is found in Cilicia at a certain fall of the river flowing into the sea near the city called Plagiopolis. ... (They say that it discerns holy suffering, for if smelt when carried down to the earth they fall suddenly. Perfumed, it heals hidden disorders of women if they take in the vapour.)" Those are the "cures" the Thracian stone is said to share: softening and dissolving, the effect of its smoke on epilepsy and hysterical women, use in gout medicines and fatigue removers, driving away snakes, and the closing parenthesis. The parenthesis (printed in brackets in this translation) is a report ("they say") that Dioscorides sets apart; recording it is not endorsing it. The existing Codex entry for the comparison material is `Volume_II_Mineral_Alchemical/entries/II_59_Jet.md`.

**The asphalt comparison.** The chapter says the setting alight with water and quenching with oil is "also done with asphalt", with the translator's gloss "bitumen — asphalt — mineral pitch". The asphalt chapters are 1-99 *Asphaltos* (line 4278: "Judean bitumen is better than others. The best shines and is purple and heavy with a strong scent ... It is also found (moist) swimming on wells in the country of the Agrigentines (Sicily) which they use for lamps instead of oil") and 1-100 *Pissasphaltos* (line 4281). Neither of those chapters was found to state the water-and-oil behaviour; the sentence in 5-147 is the only place it was found. [USER: verify]

**Identity.** The translator gives no identification ("UNKNOWN") and the Codex list marks the identification uncertain. The clues are few: a stone of Scythia that behaves like bitumen (kindled by water, quenched by oil) and has the cures of gagates. That fits a black, bituminous stone like jet, but the chapter says no more than "same cures as gagates" and the behaviour. Whether Dioscorides meant a form of jet, a bituminous stone, or another material is not settled by the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None can be stated from the chapter. The material is unidentified. By the chapter's own comparison it behaves like asphalt, a bituminous (hydrocarbon) material, and is said to share the cures of gagates, which the translator glosses as jet or agate. In general terms, jet is a dense black form of fossil wood (a coal-like, carbon-rich material) and bitumen is a mixture of heavy hydrocarbons. These are statements about those materials in general, not a reading of the chapter, and no composition is claimed for the Thracian stone.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides gives this stone no use of its own: it is credited with the cures of gagates, which include inhaled smoke said to induce epilepsy and to calm hysteria, and he sets out a reported test for "holy suffering" in brackets. Inhaling smoke from a burning bituminous stone is hazardous, the material is unidentified, and the chapter supplies no instructions. The Codex supplies no modern protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives no preparation. The only procedure is its fire behaviour: it "is said to be set on fire with water and quenched with oil", as asphalt is (line 10602); this is a report ("it is said"), not a recipe. For the cures it shares with gagates, 5-146 mentions inhaling the smoke, mixing the stone into medicines for gout and fatigue, and taking in the vapour of the perfumed stone (lines 10595-10599), without amounts. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

The gagates chapter prints "It softens and dissolves" (5-146, line 10599), which is an action, not an energetic quality; no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality is printed there either. ### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. What the herbal offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, sensory assay, adulteration detection), and this chapter offers only a thin version of it: a provenance (Scythia, by a river), and a fire assay shared with asphalt, which the text itself hedges ("it is said"). It offers no sign of the best kind, no season and no adulterant. The chapter assigns the stone no cultivation status; its cures are those of another stone.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to take from it for cultivation. The chapter is a pointer to gagates and a fire test, and the material it names is unidentified. It is not a recommended practice. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Smoke:** the cures it shares with gagates include inhaling the smoke of the burning stone; do not burn bituminous or unidentified stone and inhale the smoke.
- **Epilepsy:** the gagates chapter says the smoke "induces epilepsy"; it is a hazard for anyone with a seizure disorder and for everyone else.
- **Unidentified material:** the identification is uncertain; nothing should be collected or used on the strength of the name.
- **Reported fire test:** do not try the chapter's fire behaviour; setting hydrocarbon material alight and quenching it is a fire hazard.
