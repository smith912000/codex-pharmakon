# Asios lithos (Asian stone) — ASIOS LITHOS, "Asian stone"
## *A spongy, light, brittle stone like pumice in colour, with yellowish partitions at the bottom, and a yellowish salt fluid ("the flower") that gathers on its outside; the translator marks the identity UNKNOWN, and the Codex adds no identification of its own; the stone is not located, though the text's own comparison is to pumice* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **CAUSTIC CAUTION.** Dioscorides calls the stone and its flower "astringent" and says they "corrode gently"; he also says coffins made of it "dissolve flesh". This entry is a historical record only. Nobody should apply this material to the body.

**Single-text entry.** Only Dioscorides names this stone among the texts on disk. The row began on 8 November 2026 as an unverified placeholder (identity unknown, pumice-like); this entry replaces it. A search of the Dioscorides text for *Asios*, *Asian stone* and *Asius* found the chapter heading (line 10572), the chapter body and the name-index entries (Greek index ASIOS LITHOS 816; English index Asian stone 816). The neighbouring chapters are 5-141 (Phrugios lithos, the Phrygian stone, also marked UNKNOWN by the translator) and 5-143 (Purites lithos).

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Chapter 5-142, ASIOS LITHOS, `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10572-10578, translator's heading "UNKNOWN". The chapter says:

- **Choice of stone.** "Asian stone should be taken which is like pumice stone in colour — spongy, light and brittle, with yellowish partitions at the bottom."
- **The flower.** "The flower [salt] of it is the yellowish salt fluid which gathers on the outside of the stones, which is thin in consistency and sometimes white in colour, sometimes like pumice stone, inclining to a yellow. Applied to the tongue it bites a little."
- **Action.** "Both of them are astringent, corrode gently, and dissolve small swellings used with terminthos [1-91] rosin or liquid pitch, and the flower [salt] is considered the strongest."
- **Further uses.** Dried, it "heals old hard-to-close ulcers, represses abnormal fleshy growths, and with honey it cleans those like mushrooms, and wild ulcers"; it fills hollow ulcer sores and cleans them with honey; mixed with wax ointment it stops "feeding" ulcers; for gout it is used in plasters with bean meal; for the spleen with vinegar and unslaked lime (quicklime); the flower, licked in with honey, is "good for tuberculosis of the lungs".
- **Baths and coffins.** "Stone baths are made from this into which the gouty put in their feet and are helped, and coffins are made of it that dissolve flesh."
- **Weight.** "It relieves very fleshy and thick bodies [weight loss] smeared on instead of saltpetre [potassium nitrate] in a bath." The bracketed gloss is the translator's.
- **Washing.** "If you want to wash it do it in the same way as cadmia [5-84]."

The chapter does not say where the stone is found (the name "Asian" suggests a region; the text states no source place), and gives no quantities.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Caustic.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Cleaning and closing old ulcers, checking proud flesh, dissolving small swellings, gout (as plaster and as a foot bath), the spleen, and, for the flower, the lungs.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no duration, no safe internal use beyond the single line on licking the flower with honey.

The Codex does not translate the Greek conditions into modern diagnoses and gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations: the stone or flower dried and used with terebinth resin (terminthos, 1-91) or liquid pitch; with honey; mixed with wax ointment; in plasters with bean meal; with vinegar and quicklime; as a bath for the feet; washed as cadmia (5-84) is washed. No quantity, time or number of applications is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any figure from this entry.** [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Caustic or corrosive mineral salts injure skin and mucous membranes, and quicklime is strongly caustic; the chapter's own words ("corrode", "dissolve flesh") agree in kind. Ulcers, gout and lung disease are medical conditions managed today by clinicians. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** The chapter prints "astringent" and "corrode gently" for both the stone and its flower, and calls the flower the stronger. No warming or cooling quality is printed. None is imported here.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and this chapter assigns the stone no cultivation status. What the chapter does show of the book's discipline of attention is the careful description of the stone (colour, weight, brittleness, the partitions at the base) and of the flower (its consistency, its bite on the tongue). This is attention to provenance and sensory test, nothing more.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. It is a caustic, astringent stone recorded for ulcers, gout and swellings, whose identity is unknown. The Codex does not recommend it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Caustic.** The text says it corrodes; do not apply it to skin, ulcers or mucous membranes, and do not put it in the mouth (the chapter's own test, applying it to the tongue, is not to be repeated).
- **Quicklime preparations.** The spleen preparation uses quicklime, which is strongly caustic.
- **Identity.** Unknown; the translator says so. Do not treat any modern stone as this material. [USER: verify]
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** No statement in the sources retrieved.
