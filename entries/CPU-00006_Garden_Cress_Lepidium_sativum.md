### Origin & Tradition

Garden cress is one of the oldest cultivated pungent pot-herbs of the Old World, grown for its peppery leaves and, especially, its mucilaginous, fiery seed. Dioscorides records it (under the Greek *kardamon* — a name whose later confusion with true cardamom is a recurring headache for translators) as a heating, pungent plant good for clearing the chest, expelling worms, and stirring sluggish digestion. The plant's most famous practical virtue — that it germinates within days and can be eaten as a microgreen — made it a reliable fresh green across the Mediterranean and Near East long before salad culture was formalised.

In the Galenic system the seed was prized for being **hot and dry in a high degree**: a warming, drying, dispersing agent set against cold, damp and phlegmatic conditions. Its acrid bite — shared with mustard, rocket and the other Brassicaceae — was read directly as a signature of its heating power. The classical and medieval pharmacy used the bruised seed as a rubefacient poultice for cold, painful joints and for drawing out stubborn skin conditions, and internally as an expectorant and emmenagogue.

In **Ayurveda** the seed (**Chandrashura**, Hindi *halim* / *aliv*) holds a distinct and enduring place as a postpartum and convalescent tonic: the seeds are soaked in milk or water (they swell into a gel) and taken to support recovery, lactation and strength after childbirth, and as a galactagogue. They are also a traditional iron- and folate-rich food for anaemia. This nutritive-restorative framing sits alongside the same pungent-heating digestive and anti-helminthic uses found in the West.

### Active Components

- **Glucosinolates (chiefly glucotropaeolin / benzyl glucosinolate)** — the sulphur-containing precursors that, on crushing, are hydrolysed by the enzyme myrosinase to **benzyl isothiocyanate**, the source of the seed's pungency and biological bite. Isothiocyanates are antimicrobial, counter-irritant (rubefacient on skin) and account for the classic "heating" effect.
- **Benzyl isothiocyanate** — the principal pungent hydrolysis product; antimicrobial and irritant; the molecular basis of the rubefacient poultice action.
- **Seed mucilage (arabinoxylan / polysaccharide gel)** — the husk swells into a demulcent gel in water; a bulk-forming, soothing, mildly laxative property exploited in the milk/water soak.
- **Iron, folate and other minerals** — the seed is a recognised traditional source of dietary iron, underpinning the Ayurvedic anaemia/postpartum use. [USER: verify specific nutrient figures before quoting numbers.]
- **Fixed oil and sterols** — seed oil rich in unsaturated fatty acids; contributes to nutritive value.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. **Warming digestive stimulant.** The pungent isothiocyanate fraction stimulates appetite and digestion and disperses cold-damp stagnation in the gut — the classic Galenic and Ayurvedic use for sluggish, cold digestion.
2. **Expectorant for cold/phlegm patterns.** Traditionally used to clear the chest of cold, thick phlegm — the heating, drying action thinning and moving congested fluids.
3. **Postpartum and convalescent restorative (Ayurveda).** The milk-soaked swollen seed taken to rebuild strength, support lactation, and address anaemia after childbirth — a nutritive rather than merely pharmacological role.
4. **Rubefacient poultice (external).** Bruised seed applied to cold, painful joints and chronic skin lesions as a counter-irritant drawing blood to the surface.
5. **Anti-helminthic (traditional).** Recorded from Dioscorides onward as a vermifuge. [USER: verify efficacy claims before any practical statement.]

### Preparation & Dosing

- **Fresh microgreens/leaf:** eaten raw as a peppery salad green; germinates in days on a damp cloth — a reliable fresh vitamin green.
- **Soaked seed (Ayurvedic halim):** a spoonful of seed soaked in water or warmed milk until it forms a gel, sweetened, taken as a postpartum/strength tonic. [USER: verify traditional quantity and frequency from an Ayurvedic source before stating a dose.]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM / Galenic humoral degree:** Hot and Dry in a high degree (commonly given as the third) — a strongly warming, drying, dispersing seed set against cold-damp and phlegmatic states.
- **Ayurvedic mapping:** Chandrashura — *ushna* (hot), pungent/bitter, *kapha-vata* reducing; nutritive and galactagogue; a recognised postpartum restorative.
- **Unani:** garm-khushk (hot-dry); deobstruent and resolvent.
- **Kenetica Qi/Shen tier:** ◉ **Qi** — squarely on the warming/drying/dispersing axis with a nutritive-restorative secondary character (the soaked-seed tonic), a useful bridge between the pungent dispersing herbs and the building tonics.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy — caution/avoid medicinal doses.** Traditional emmenagogue and uterine-stimulant reputation; culinary amounts of leaf are ordinary food, but concentrated seed preparations are traditionally avoided in pregnancy. [USER: verify.]
- **Hot/dry constitutions and yin-deficiency heat.** The strong heating-drying action can aggravate heat and dryness patterns.
- **Hypothyroid/goitrogenic caution.** As a glucosinolate-bearing Brassicaceae, very large habitual seed intake carries the theoretical goitrogenic caution of the family. [USER: verify relevance for this species.]
- **Iron overload.** The deliberately iron-rich seed is inappropriate as a supplement in haemochromatosis or iron-overload states.
- **Mucosal/GI irritation.** Large pungent-seed doses can irritate; reduce if heartburn or gut irritation occurs.
