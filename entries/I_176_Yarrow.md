## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Achillea millefolium* L. (1753)
**Species epithet etymology:**
- *Achillea*: After Achilles (Greek hero of the Trojan War — see Section 7)
- *millefolium*: Mille (thousand) + folium (leaf) = "thousand-leafed" — the characteristic feathery, deeply divided pinnate leaves

**~115 species in *Achillea* genus;** *A. millefolium* is the primary medicinal species; complex with multiple cytotypes and chemotypes across its circumpolar distribution.

**Traditional names:**

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 De Materia Medica (Dioscorides, c. 50–70 CE)
DMM Book IV.36: *Stratiōtēs* (Soldiers' plant) / *Achilleios* — Dioscorides is explicit: "*They say that Achilles used this for stopping wounds. It stays haemorrhages when the fresh herb is applied.*" This is the most direct citation in any classical medical text of a mythological pharmacological attribution where the myth encodes a clinical fact: yarrow applied to wounds stops bleeding. Dioscorides also documents: digestive, anti-inflammatory; "*decoction for those who spit blood*" (hemoptysis — internal hemostasis application).

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine (Ibn Sīnā, 1025 CE)
*Qayṣūm* — warming; tonic; wound healing; digestive; anti-inflammatory. Ibn Sīnā documents the hemostatic application alongside digestive and respiratory properties. The Canon's multi-indication entry for yarrow is consistent with its complex phytochemistry — multiple compound classes acting through different mechanisms.

### 2.3 Shennong Bencao Jing / Bencao Gangmu (SBJ)
*Shī cǎo* (蓍草) — "oracle grass": in ancient China, dried yarrow stalks were the ORIGINAL material used for I Ching (Book of Changes) divination before the transition to coins. Fifty yarrow stalks were cast to generate hexagram patterns. The pharmacological properties of yarrow (medicinal) and its oracular use were parallel but separate traditions in Chinese culture. *Bencao Gangmu* documents: controls bleeding; anti-inflammatory; for abdominal pain and menstrual irregularity.

### 2.4 Sushruta Samhita / Charaka Samhita (SS)
*Gandana* / *Biranjasipha* — wound herb in Ayurvedic classification; hemostatic (*raktasthapana* = blood-stabilizing); anti-inflammatory; used in *visarpa* (erysipelas-type infections) treatment.

### 2.5 Ebers Papyrus (c. 1552 BCE)
*Achillea*-type aromatic wound herbs in Egyptian formulae; hemostatic wound preparations consistent with yarrow application. Geographic range of *A. millefolium* includes Eastern Mediterranean.

### 2.6 Assyrian Medical Tablets (c. 700–600 BCE)
Hemostatic aromatic herb in wound preparations; tentative yarrow identification in cuneiform pharmacopoeial herb lists.

### 2.7 Four Medical Tantras (FMT)
*Spos ngan* / *Khyung shog* (eagle wing herb) — hemostatic; wound healing; anti-inflammatory; documented in Tibetan *rGyud-bzhi* for traumatic injury and wound management.

### 2.8 Badianus Manuscript (BM) — TENTATIVE
Several related *Achillea* species exist in Mexico; Nahua wound-healing plants may include *Achillea* relatives. Tentative.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

### 4.1 Achilleine (Primary Hemostatic)
**Achilleine** (β-(3-carboxy-4-isoxazolyl)-L-alanine / achillein): a rare amino acid alkaloid unique to *Achillea* species. The PRIMARY hemostatic compound: reduces whole blood clotting time and promotes platelet aggregation in animal models and clinical studies. Achilleine acts via **enhancement of factor Xa-mediated thrombin generation** and direct platelet activation.

This is the Codex's most precisely characterized traditional topical hemostatic mechanism — a named compound that explains a folk application older than writing.

### 4.2 Azulene / Chamazulene (Anti-inflammatory — Steam Distillation Artifact)
**Chamazulene** (0.02–0.35% of essential oil): blue-colored sesquiterpene; formed from guaianolide sesquiterpene lactone precursors (achillin, achillifolide) during steam distillation — NOT present in fresh plant (same situation as chamomile's chamazulene, I_155). Anti-inflammatory: COX-2 inhibition + 5-LOX inhibition + NF-κB inhibition. The deep blue yarrow oil (when high chamazulene) is distinguished by color from low-azulene varieties (which are yellow-greenish).

**High-chamazulene yarrow EO** is specifically sought for anti-inflammatory topical and aromatherapy applications; **low-azulene varieties** retain hemostatic (achilleine) properties but lose the anti-inflammatory EO activity.

### 4.3 Essential Oil (Diverse Chemotypes)
*A. millefolium* has multiple chemotypes:
- **Azulene chemotype** (Mediterranean): 15–40% chamazulene; anti-inflammatory; blue oil
- **Camphor chemotype**: camphor-dominant; analgesic; antimicrobial
- **1,8-Cineole chemotype**: AChE inhibition; decongestant
- **Sabinene hydrate chemotype**: antispasmodic; calming
- **Trans-thujone chemotype**: ⚠️ pro-convulsant risk (trace amounts; variable)

**Borneol, bornyl acetate, β-pinene, α-pinene, camphene, terpinen-4-ol**: antimicrobial, analgesic, antispasmodic components varying by chemotype.

### 4.4 Sesquiterpene Lactones
**Achillin, achillifolide, leucodin, achillicin**: bitter sesquiterpene lactones; anti-inflammatory; anti-*H. pylori*; immunomodulatory; precursors to chamazulene on distillation; **Asteraceae allergy cross-reactive** (sesquiterpene lactone sensitization — the primary mechanism of Compositae/Asteraceae allergic contact dermatitis).

### 4.5 Flavonoids
**Apigenin** (GABA-A benzodiazepine site — anxiolytic/antispasmodic — same as chamomile), **luteolin**, **quercetin**, **rutin**, **casticin**: anti-inflammatory; antioxidant; antispasmodic.

### 4.6 Alkaloids (Betaine Class)
**Betonicine** (L-stachydrine/L-3-hydroxy-proline betaine) and **stachydrine**: betaine alkaloids; mild cardiac/circulatory; smooth muscle effects.

### 4.7 Tannins (Astringent)
**Tannin complex** (gallic acid + ellagitannins): astringent; local hemostasis (protein coagulation at wound surface); wound-sealing complementary to achilleine's systemic effect.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Hemostasis (Achilleine — Primary)
**Achilleine** promotes:
1. Enhanced platelet aggregation (ADP-pathway sensitization)
2. Acceleration of intrinsic coagulation cascade (factor X/Xa-mediated thrombin generation)
3. Reduction of whole blood clotting time (~30–50% reduction in animal studies vs. control)

**Tannins** provide complementary LOCAL hemostasis: protein coagulation at the wound surface creates a physical clot scaffold.

Combined: topical application of fresh yarrow to a cut creates (a) protein coagulation at surface (tannins), (b) platelet activation at wound (achilleine), and (c) accelerated coagulation cascade (achilleine) — three simultaneous hemostatic mechanisms operative within 60 seconds of application.

### 5.2 Anti-inflammatory (Multiple Pathways)
**Chamazulene (EO):** COX-2 inhibition; 5-LOX inhibition; NF-κB pathway.
**Sesquiterpene lactones:** NF-κB; direct anti-inflammatory in macrophage models.
**Apigenin/luteolin:** COX-2; NF-κB; additional flavonoid contribution.
**Total:** Four independent anti-inflammatory mechanisms from three different compound classes — unusual breadth for a single plant.

### 5.3 Antispasmodic (Apigenin + EO)
Apigenin: GABA-A benzodiazepine site (same as chamomile, I_155) → smooth muscle relaxation. Borneol, bornyl acetate in EO: calcium channel modulation → antispasmodic. Clinical: reduces menstrual cramp severity (antispasmodic + anti-inflammatory together).

### 5.4 Diaphoretic (Volatile Compounds)
Mild diaphoretic action attributed to the volatile essential oil and flavonoid vasodilatory effects → increased peripheral blood flow → sweating → temperature reduction. Same mechanism as elderflower (I_173) diaphoresis. Used in traditional European fever management.

### 5.5 Antimicrobial (EO + Sesquiterpene Lactones + Tannins)
Diverse antimicrobial activity: EO components (borneol, camphor, 1,8-cineole) → membrane disruption. Sesquiterpene lactones → enzyme inhibition. Tannins → protein coagulation on bacterial surface. *H. pylori* inhibition documented for sesquiterpene lactone fraction.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### 6.1 Hemostasis (Traditional Evidence + Mechanism)
No formal RCTs for isolated topical hemostatic use (ethical and practical challenges in design). The evidence base is:
- Pharmacological characterization of achilleine's clot-promoting activity
- 3,000+ years of consistent cross-traditional empirical use
- Commission E approval for hemostatic applications

**Commission E (Germany) Approved Indications:**
1. Internal — febrile colds; loss of appetite; liver/gallbladder disorders
2. External — wounds and skin inflammation; Sitz baths for pelvic conditions

### 6.2 Menstrual Disorders / Dysmenorrhea
**Jenabi & Ebrahimzadeh 2010 (S Afr J Bot):** N=94; yarrow vs. placebo for dysmenorrhea: significant reduction in pain score. Mechanism: antispasmodic (apigenin + EO) + anti-inflammatory (chamazulene + sesquiterpene lactones). This is the best-designed single-herb dysmenorrhea trial for yarrow.

### 6.3 Wound Healing
Multiple small clinical studies confirm accelerated wound healing with topical yarrow preparations. No placebo-controlled RCTs at large scale. Commission E approval for wounds based on traditional use + mechanistic plausibility.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Achilles and the Wound of Telephos (Trojan War mythology):** The Greek hero Achilles was taught the medicinal use of yarrow by Chiron the Centaur (divine healer and educator of heroes). At Troy, when Telephus was wounded by Achilles' spear — and an oracle stated only the one who wounded him could cure him — Achilles scraped rust from his own spear onto the wound. The "rust" is commonly interpreted as either iron oxide OR, in plant-oriented interpretations, as dried yarrow (*Achilleios poion*) adhering to the spear shaft from previous field use. This mythological interpretation embeds the pharmacological reality (yarrow heals the wound) in the narrative paradox (the weapon that wounds also heals) — a medical truth (the herb grows where soldiers fall; soldiers use it on wounds) encoded in heroic narrative.

**Neanderthal Yarrow Pollen — The Oldest Possible Medicine:** Shanidar Cave (Kurdistan, Iraq): in 1960, Ralph Solecki excavated burial sites of Neanderthals (c. 60,000 BCE). Pollen samples from Shanidar IV burial included: *Achillea*, Ephedra, Senecio, Muscari, and other medicinal plants in concentrated quantities unusual for random soil pollen. This was interpreted as intentional grave flowers — and as possible medicinal plant use — by Neanderthals, 60,000 years before writing. If confirmed, yarrow pollen at Shanidar represents the oldest archaeological evidence of intentional medicinal plant use in any human or human-ancestral species. The interpretation remains contested (rodent burrowing may have concentrated pollen); but the Shanidar data has been cited in every major history of medicine since 1975 as the potential origin point of pharmacobotany. If valid: the first medicine in the Codex is also entry I_176.

**I Ching and the Yarrow Oracle:** The original method of I Ching divination used 50 dried yarrow stalks (*milfoil*), manipulated through a specific counting process to generate the six-line hexagrams of the Book of Changes. The yarrow stalk method is documented from the Zhou dynasty (c. 1046–256 BCE) and is older than the coin-casting method now universally used. *Achillea*'s straight, rigid, uniformly-sized stalks made it ideal for counting divination. The same plant that stopped Achilles' soldiers' wounds was simultaneously generating the fundamental text of Chinese cosmology — one plant serving as both pharmacological and oracular instrument across opposite ends of Eurasia.

**"Soldier's Wound Herb" — Every War Before Germ Theory:** From Troy (c. 1200 BCE) through the American Civil War (1861–1865), yarrow was the most consistently used battlefield wound herb in Western military medical kits. The pattern: troops carried yarrow or knew where to find it; field medics applied it to wounds to stop bleeding; it worked. The American Civil War medical records note yarrow as one of the most commonly employed hemostatics — alongside sumac and goldenseal — by both Union and Confederate medical corps. This military continuity from mythological Greece through the US Civil War is the longest documented continuous clinical indication in military medicine for any plant substance.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Topical Hemostatic Use
- **Fresh plant:** Crush fresh yarrow leaves → apply directly to wound → hold pressure 5 minutes. Most effective — achilleine + tannins + EO all present at highest concentration.
- **Dried herb compress:** Rehydrate dried yarrow in warm water → apply as compress to wound.
- **Powdered herb:** Apply dry powder directly to bleeding wound; absorbs blood + activates tannin-mediated coagulation.
- **For nosebleed:** Traditional: insert loosely packed yarrow leaf into nostril. Tannins + achilleine + physical pressure = triple hemostatic mechanism.

### 8.2 Internal Medicinal Use
| Application | Form | Dose |
|-------------|------|------|
| Digestive/carminative | Dried herb tea | 2 tsp/250mL × 3/day |
| Dysmenorrhea | Tea or liquid extract | 1:1 liquid extract 2–4 mL × 3/day (premenstrual) |
| Fever (diaphoretic) | Hot infusion | 2 tsp/cup × 2–3/day |
| Liver/biliary (traditional) | Tincture 1:5 | 2–4 mL × 3/day |

### 8.3 Safety and Contraindications

**PREGNANCY — ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION:**
Achilleine and some sesquiterpene lactones have uterotonic activity; yarrow is documented as emmenagogue across multiple traditions. Historically used to initiate menstruation/abortion. **Never use yarrow internally at medicinal doses during pregnancy.** Culinary use (very small amounts as herb) considered safe.

**Asteraceae allergy — Sesquiterpene Lactone Sensitization:**
Yarrow sesquiterpene lactones (achillin, achillifolide) are primary sensitizers in Compositae/Asteraceae allergic contact dermatitis — a specific immune-mediated reaction distinct from simple irritation. Gardeners, herbalists, and florists who handle yarrow regularly develop delayed-type hypersensitivity. Avoid topical yarrow if known Asteraceae (ragweed, chamomile, chrysanthemum) allergy. Cross-reactive with all Asteraceae sesquiterpene lactones.

**Anticoagulants:**
Paradoxically, yarrow has BOTH hemostatic (achilleine → promotes coagulation) AND mild anticoagulant (flavonoid platelet modulation) properties. Net clinical effect = hemostatic at standard doses. However, in patients on warfarin or aspirin, the flavonoid component might increase anticoagulant effect → monitor INR; avoid high-dose internal yarrow with anticoagulant medications.

**Photosensitization:**
EO furanocoumarins (trace) may contribute to mild photosensitivity; avoid sun exposure immediately after heavy topical application.

### 8.4 Tradition-Practice Concordance
The hemostatic application is the Codex's single most mythology-embedded pharmacological truth: the plant named after Achilles stops bleeding because of achilleine — a compound discovered in the 20th century to explain what Greeks encoded in myth 3,000 years ago. The I Ching oracle function and the battlefield wound herb function coexisted for millennia without contradiction — in China the plant was metaphysical, in Greece it was surgical, in Siberia it was ritual, in Egypt it was medical. All traditions were correct about different aspects of the same plant, and none identified the mechanism that explains all of them.
