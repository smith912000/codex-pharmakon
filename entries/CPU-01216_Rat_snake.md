# Rat snake (Zaocys) — *Zaocys dhumnades* (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary, Tibetan *Drul*, "Snake"); "Snake meat: *Elaphe dione*" (Clark 1995, *kluhi.sha*)
## *Snake flesh, fat and skin as listed in the Tibetan materia medica. The English "rat snake" is not printed in either Tibetan text; the glossary pairs the Latin name* Zaocys dhumnades *with the plain English "Snake"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is the Tibetan *Four Tantras*, and the identification is the weak point. The spine's locus is the scientific-name column of the Glossary on Animals in the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`; "MTK" below; `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` row 424). Under the heading "Reptiles" the glossary prints three columns that the OCR has split, Tibetan "Drul / Mikpa Tsangpa" (lines 7135-7136), English "Snake Lizard Mountain-Lizard" (line 7138) and Latin "Zaocys dhumnades / Eremias argus peters / Agama himaiayana scora smith" (lines 7140-7142). Read in order, the snake is *Drul*, "Snake", *Zaocys dhumnades*. That pairing is my reading of the column order; the extract itself notes "Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". The glossary never says "rat snake".

The medicinal passages are about "snake" in general. In the animal medicines of chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra, Clark's 1995 translation (`Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; "Clark" below) lists, under "Meat" (heading at line 1161): "Snake meat: Elapbe dione (Pallas): [has eleven names, for example kluhi.sha]. It removes growths, dispels urinary retention, is beneficial in eye disorders, [painful fevers in children, lung diseases, leprosy and infected wounds, and it heals bone warts]" (line 1165; "Elapbe" is the OCR's spelling of *Elaphe*). Under "Fat": "Snake fat: expels bullets, (shrapnel, etc.) [and cures anterior transposition of the calf muscle.]" (line 1180). Under "Skin": "Snake skin: cures leucoderma, glang.shu (a skin disease) [and is beneficial against bhutaspirits]" (line 1184). The square brackets mark interpolations from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries (line 82). The first sentence of each entry is the root-text claim as Clark renders it, and the bracketed parts are commentary.

MTK gives the same three, more briefly: "Snake meat dissolves tumors, clears obstruction and is beneficial for eye problems" (line 3529); "Snake fat expels foreign bodies from the body" (line 3546); "Snake skin cures vitiligo and langshu" (line 3554). The two translators therefore name two different species for what the Tibetan texts call a snake. Clark gives *Elaphe dione* for the meat. MTK's glossary gives *Zaocys dhumnades* for the animal. The sources do not settle whether the Tibetan physicians meant one species, several, or "whichever snake was to hand", and this entry does not choose between them. The diet chapters also place the snake in the "burrowing animals" group (Clark line 672; MTK line 2918), with marmot, porcupine, frog, badger, lizard and scorpion.

### Active Components

Snake flesh is mainly muscle protein with some fat; the skin is keratinised scale and underlying dermis. The Tibetan texts name no constituent and no mechanism, and this entry supplies none. They do not say whether venomous or non-venomous snakes are meant.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources credit snake flesh with several actions (removing growths, relieving urinary retention, the eyes), the fat with expelling foreign bodies and the skin with skin disease. They give no method of use. Clark records that "nowadays only very few animal products are used" (line 57). The identification is unsettled, wild snake flesh is a documented infection route (see Contraindications), and no modern practitioner can tell from the text which animal is meant. The Codex's application layer has nothing to add, and no protocol is offered.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither text gives a preparation (cooked, dried, burnt, rendered), a form or a dose for the snake products. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] The compounding chapter (Clark ch.21, from line 1204) lists medicines by disease; snake products are not named in the lists read.

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The only general facts offered are compositional (above). Raw or undercooked wild snake is a known source of bacterial and parasitic infection; that is a hazard, not a correlation.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT).** Clark and MTK print no taste, potency or post-digestive taste for snake meat, fat or skin as a medicine. The dietary chapters give rules for meats by habitat: all meats in the listed groups are sweet in taste and post-digestive taste, with dry-land flesh "cool, light and coarse" and the flesh of animals living in wet places "oily, heavy and warm" (Clark lines 672-673; MTK line 2923). Where the burrowing group falls in the dry-land, wet-place or both split is not unambiguous in either translation (Clark says nine sections and then "the above eight sections"; MTK says eight groups, and the groups it lists run to nine), so no quality is assigned to the snake. The attributes the sources give are the actions above.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* The spine attests this material through a glossary column that pairs a Latin name with the word "Snake", and the medicinal passages concern snake products in general, with two different species named. A tradition-specific framing for a *rat snake* cannot be sourced, so none is offered. For the snake products the Tibetan materia medica gives only indications (growths, urinary retention, the eyes, foreign bodies, skin disease). The tradition has a formal division of rejuvenation, "essence-extraction" (Clark line 128; also line 136), and no animal substance in the section read (lines 1147-1202) is assigned to it. Snake skin is the one product with a bracketed spirit-related use (against *bhuta* spirits, line 1184), which is a protective claim in commentary and not a cultivation one. In this tradition medicine is one branch of treatment and the root of disease lies in the three poisons; no passage read ties snake products to that frame. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in the Tibetan sources, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports a cultivation use. The animal is not securely identified in the Tibetan sources, the claims are therapeutic and the practical risks are infection and, for collectors, snakebite. The honest entry is a record.

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** snakes carry *Salmonella*, and raw or undercooked snake flesh is a recognised route for parasitic infection, including the tapeworm larvae that cause sparganosis. Handling a wild snake risks a bite, from a venomous or a non-venomous species.
- **Identity:** the Tibetan texts do not fix the species; never substitute one snake for another by assumption. [USER: verify]
- **Legal and conservation status:** protection of wild snakes varies by country and species. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** the products come from a wild animal that must be killed; the sources do not say how it was obtained.
