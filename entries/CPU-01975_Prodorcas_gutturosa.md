# Prodorcas gutturosa — the second Latin name Clark prints for *dgo.wa*, a gazelle whose horn is listed in the Explanatory Tantra (Clark 1995, ch.20)

## *Prodorcas gutturosa is an old genus-and-species combination for the Mongolian gazelle (today Procapra gutturosa); the horn is the drug, and the identification is Clark's, who prints it as a second name beside Procapra pictaudata (the Tibetan gazelle) for the single Tibetan word dgo.wa; the two animals are different species, so the identity of the animal is open; confidence low* | *(Volume III — Animal)*

**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named by the Tibetan text in Clark's translation only.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder, "Prodorcas gutturosa", taken from the extraction of the Tibetan names. The first pass called it a duplicate of the Tibetan gazelle horn entry (III_199, CPU-01256); the audit disagreed, because *Procapra gutturosa* is the Mongolian gazelle and the other name, *Procapra picticaudata*, is the Tibetan gazelle. The two share one line of the source, so everything the text says is the same for both. This entry records that line from the side of the second Latin name and does not repeat III_199's wider discussion. [USER: verify whether the two rows should be merged.]

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The entry is in Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; "Clark" below), line 1156, in chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra, under "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin". Line 1148 says: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body." Horn is the first of the thirteen. The line reads: "*dgo.wa*: *Procapra pictaudata* Hodgson v. Hook: *Prodorcas gutturosa*: [has nine names. It dries pus (accumulations) in the abdomen]. The horn of Hodgson's antelope and Procapra halt diarrhoea."

Clark's introduction (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets are taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. So "has nine names" and "dries pus in the abdomen" belong to the commentary layer, and the unbracketed sentence is the one that attaches an action to the horn: it halts diarrhoea, together with the horn of Hodgson's antelope (*gtsod*, the entry above it, line 1155). The Latin spellings are as Clark prints them; "pictaudata" is his.

The extract (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, row for "Clark 1995 (line 1156)") lists both Latin names for *dgo.wa* and classes the row "plant", which is wrong for an animal horn. Clark's dietary chapter (line 672) names "*Procapra pictaudata*" among the "game animals", which shows only that the animal is hunted for food. The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3508) says "The horns of antelopes and gazelles cure diarrhea" without the Tibetan word or a Latin name. No other tradition's text on disk was found to name this animal. On identity: which of the two gazelles the Tibetan word means is not decided by the text, and this entry does not decide it.

### Active Components

Horn of the bovid type is mainly keratin, a structural protein, over a bony core that carries calcium phosphate. These are general facts about horn. The Tibetan texts name no constituent and no mechanism for the actions claimed, and this entry supplies none.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** The horn halts diarrhoea, with the horn of Hodgson's antelope, and (in the commentary layer) dries pus in the abdomen (line 1156).
- **What the text does not say.** A preparation, a quantity or a duration.

The Codex does not recommend using it. A wild gazelle is a hunted animal, unprocessed horn carries a hygiene risk, and diarrhoea has safer remedies, the first of which is replacing lost fluid. No protocol is offered.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither Clark nor Men-Tsee-Khang gives a preparation (powdered, burnt, in a compound), a quantity or a dose for this horn in the passages read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] Clark remarks (line 57) that "nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts". **Do not use any dose from this entry.**

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Diarrhoea can cause dehydration and loss of salts, and the main treatment is oral rehydration; persistent or bloody diarrhoea, or diarrhoea in infants, the elderly or the ill, needs medical assessment. Keratin is a largely indigestible protein, and nothing in the sources links it to an anti-diarrhoeal action. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi.** No taste, potency or post-digestive taste is printed for this horn in the passages read (Clark line 1156; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3508). The only qualities are the action "halts diarrhoea" and the bracketed "dries pus (accumulations) in the abdomen". Other horns in the same passage carry stated qualities (takin horn "increases [body] heat", line 1158), but these are not extended to this one.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The frame is the thirteen-part animal section of the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica (Clark line 1148), and in it the horn is a drug for a stated action, halting diarrhoea (line 1156). Nothing in the passages read places it in any practice of essence extraction (*bcud len*), and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, so none is asserted here.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports cultivation use. The horn is one line in a list of horn medicines, with a single action, in a section that Clark says is little used today. A cultivator has nothing to take from this entry; the honest practice is abstention.

### Contraindications

- **Diarrhoea:** needs fluid replacement and, if severe, bloody or prolonged, or in infants and the frail, medical care; do not delay it for an animal remedy.
- **Hygiene:** unprocessed animal material from the wild carries a risk of infection.
- **Law and conservation:** wild gazelles of the steppe and plateau are hunted and in places protected, and trade in animal parts may be restricted; the status of a particular species was not checked in the sources and should be confirmed before any trade or use. [USER: verify]
- **Identification:** the animal is taken from Clark's gloss, with two Latin names for two different species; this entry does not say that any product on sale is the *dgo.wa* of the text.
