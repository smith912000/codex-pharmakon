# Telephium imperati — Telephion (Dioscorides 2-217); "Orpine, Livelong, Midsummer Men" on the translators' line

## *Telephium imperati, the name of the plate caption printed under the heading of 2-217; the SUGGESTED line gives Telephium album, Telephium purpurascens and Sedum telephium [Linnaeus] ("Orpine, Livelong, Midsummer Men")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk lists the plant under this name (the name indexes in `Sources/*/extract/names.csv` were searched). The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is not Dioscorides' identification, and it is not on the translators' SUGGESTED line. *Telephium imperati* is the caption of a plate ("Telephium imperati after FAGUET — 1888", line 6495) printed between the heading and the SUGGESTED line, and the name index (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 406) took it from there. The SUGGESTED line (6497) names *Sedum telephium* instead, and the two names are not the same plant. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *2-217. TELEPHION* at line 6493, plate caption at 6495, SUGGESTED line at 6497, text at line 6498. The SUGGESTED line reads: "Telephium album, Telephium purpurascens [Fuchs] Acetabulum alterum album, Acetabulum alterum purpureum [Brunfels], Sedum telephium [Linnaeus], Sedum vulgare, Sedum purpureum, Telefio [Italian] — Orpine, Livelong, Midsummer Men". The chapter stands in Book Two, after *2-216. ISATIS AGRIA* (line 6485), and no further chapter heading stands between it and the preface of Book Three (line 6512); it is the last chapter of Book Two as the text is laid out. By page number it falls in the contents list's section "Herbs with a Sharp Quality" (which begins at page 304, line 31), under a running header with that name at line 6491. The chapter itself prints no "sharp" quality. The name index lists it at names.csv line 406, and the translators' indexes print "Telephium imperati 360" (line 11898) and "Sedum telephium 360" (line 17505).

The whole chapter reads: "Telephium is similar to andrachne [2-150] both in the leaves and stalk, with two wings sticking from every knot of the leaves; six or seven small branches from the root, full of azure-coloured [blue] leaves — thick, tough and fleshy; the flowers a yellowish or whitish colour. It grows in the springtime in vineyards and clay or shale places. The leaves applied for six hours cure white leprosy, but after this you must use barley meal. Rubbed on with vinegar in the sun they take away vitiligines [type of leprosy], but after they have dried they must be wiped off. Some call it sempervivum sylvestre, and some call it portulaca sylvestris, the Romans call it illecebra, the Egyptians, anoth, and the Africans, atirtopuris."

The chapter describes the plant by comparison with *andrachne* (2-150, a chapter not read for this entry): the same leaves and stalk, two "wings" at each knot of the leaves, six or seven small branches from the root, thick, tough, fleshy, azure-coloured [blue] leaves, yellowish or whitish flowers. The habitat and season are given: springtime, vineyards and clay or shale places. Two uses are given, both on the skin: the leaves "applied for six hours cure white leprosy", to be followed by barley meal, and, rubbed on with vinegar in the sun, they "take away vitiligines", to be wiped off after they have dried. The terms "white leprosy" and "vitiligines" are as the translators print them, with the bracket "[type of leprosy]", and the Codex does not match them to a modern diagnosis. The chapter prints other names: *sempervivum sylvestre*, *portulaca sylvestris*, the Roman *illecebra*, the Egyptian *anoth* and the African *atirtopuris*. The last three are names recorded by Dioscorides and not texts of those peoples.

**Plate caption elsewhere.** A second caption, "Sedum telephium after FAGUET — 1888", stands at line 6153, among the chapters of cress and its neighbours (page 314, after 2-185), and not at this chapter. Nothing is drawn from it.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter number, the Greek heading, *Telephium*, *Sedum telephium* and *Orpine* found no entry on 2-217. The other sedums on the spine (I_1186 *Sedum acre* and I_1187 *Sedum rupestre*) belong to their own chapters and were not read for this entry. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. The two candidates belong to different families: it is a general fact that *Sedum telephium* (orpine) is a succulent in the stonecrop family (Crassulaceae), and that *Telephium imperati* is a different plant, in the pink family (Caryophyllaceae). Which of them, if either, is Dioscorides' plant is not known, and a composition cannot be given. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records leaves applied for six hours for "white leprosy" and leaves rubbed on with vinegar in the sun for "vitiligines". These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. The conditions are not matched here to a modern diagnosis, and skin patches that change colour or texture are a matter for a medical examination. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives two modes of use, with directions: the leaves "applied for six hours", to be followed by barley meal; and the leaves "rubbed on with vinegar in the sun", to be wiped off after they have dried. The six hours is the only quantity printed. The chapter does not say how often or for how long the treatment goes on. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that pale or discoloured skin patches have many causes, that a plant rubbed on skin which is then exposed to sunlight can in some cases cause a reaction, and that this is a reason for medical advice before any such treatment.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 2-217 prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality and no taste. The only qualities printed are physical: leaves "thick, tough and fleshy" and "azure-coloured". The running header "Herbs with a Sharp Quality" is the book's section title (line 31) and is not a quality stated in the chapter.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a description for recognition (by comparison with another plant, by the wings at the knots, the number of branches, the colour of leaf and flower), a season and a habitat, and a method with its own checks: a stated time of application, the next step (barley meal), and the instruction to wipe the leaves off once dry. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The identification is the translators', and the plate caption names a different plant from the SUGGESTED line. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is that the text gives an order of steps and a stopping rule, not only a remedy. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 2-217 prints no warning, but it does direct that the leaves be followed by barley meal and wiped off. The identification is uncertain, and the plate and the SUGGESTED line name different plants, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Skin changes need medical assessment, and a plant that is applied and then exposed to sunlight, as the text directs, is a risk that this entry has no source to measure. The text gives no dose and no safety information for pregnancy or children. [USER: verify]
