# Coriander — *Coriandrum sativum* L.

**Family:** Apiaceae (Umbelliferae) | **Class:** plant | **Volume:** I · Plant | **Codex id:** CPU-00080  
**Attested in:** Dioscorides · Avicenna · Ebers Papyrus · Assyrian tablets · rGyud-bzhi

---

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

*Coriandrum sativum* L. is an annual herbaceous plant in the Apiaceae family, native to the eastern Mediterranean and southwestern Asia, widely naturalised across temperate and subtropical regions. The binomial was formalised by Linnaeus in *Species Plantarum* (1753). The genus name derives from the Greek *koríandron* (κορίανδρον), recorded by Dioscorides, itself plausibly from *koris* (bedbug) in reference to the unripe fruit's fetid odour — a scent that dissipates entirely upon drying.

A critical nomenclatural distinction governs culinary and pharmacological usage: **coriander** conventionally designates the dried ripe seed (botanically a schizocarp), characterised by a warm, citrusy, slightly floral aroma dominated by linalool; **cilantro** (from Spanish) designates the fresh leaf and stem, whose volatile profile is markedly different — principally composed of (2E)-decenal and related C9–C10 aldehydes [1]. This entry covers both morphological parts but foregrounds the seed, which is the form attested in all five medical traditions under review.

## 2. Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2a. Dioscorides (*De Materia Medica*, 1st century CE)

Pedanius Dioscorides (c. 40–90 CE), writing in Greek as a military physician, recorded coriander under the name *koriannon* in *De Materia Medica*. His five-volume pharmacopoeia documents approximately 600 plants across more than 1,000 therapeutic applications and became the foundational reference text in European and Islamic pharmacy for over fifteen centuries [2]. Dioscorides noted the plant's initially repellent odour and described its topical application in poultices and its internal use for digestive complaints. His text was subsequently translated into Latin, Arabic, Syriac, and several European vernaculars, with Arabic translations preserved at the British Library and annotated 9th-century Greek manuscripts held at the Bibliothèque nationale de France [2].

### 2b. Avicenna (*Al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb*, c. 1025 CE)

Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna, 980–1037 CE) systematised coriander within the Galenic humoral framework in his *Canon of Medicine* (*al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb*). According to this tradition, coriander (Arabic: *kuzbara*) is classified as **cold** from the later stages of the first degree to the second degree, and **dry** in the second degree [3]. Within this framework, cold-dry substances are indicated for conditions characterised by heat and moisture — qualifying coriander for digestive fevers, excessive bile, and inflammatory gastric states. Avicenna's Canon circulated as the dominant medical textbook in Islamic and European academic medicine through the 17th century.

### 2c. Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE)

The Ebers Papyrus — an Egyptian medical compendium dating to approximately 1550 BCE and among the most extensive surviving records of ancient pharmaceutical knowledge, listing more than 700 remedies — attests coriander as one of its most-used botanicals [4]. Coriander appears in preparations prescribed for urinary complaints, including what the text frames as cystitis-type conditions, and was incorporated into healing ointments [4]. The frequency of coriander's appearance across Ebers prescriptions places it among the core Egyptian materia medica, consistent with archaeobotanical evidence: coriander seeds have been recovered from New Kingdom tomb contexts, including among the botanical remains documented from Tutankhamun's burial assemblage (c. 1323 BCE) [5].

### 2d. Assyrian Cuneiform Tablets (Neo-Assyrian period, c. 7th century BCE and earlier)

Coriander is attested among the plant medicines documented in cuneiform medical texts. The library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh, assembled in the 7th century BCE and containing approximately 5,000 medical prescriptions, is the primary documentary corpus [6]. Broader Mesopotamian cuneiform pharmacopoeias — compiled across the Neo-Assyrian and Babylonian periods and recovered from temple and private libraries — enumerate more than 200 plant species, coriander among them [6]. One cuneiform note cautions that patients with ocular complaints should avoid it — an early record of contraindication logic. The specific Sumerian/Akkadian identification of coriander in these tablets remains subject to ongoing etymological analysis [6].

### 2e. *rGyud-bzhi* (Tibetan Four Medical Tantras, compiled 11th–12th century CE)

The *rGyud-bzhi* (*Gyüshi*), the foundational canonical text of Sowa Rigpa (Tibetan medicine), was compiled in written form largely during the 12th century, attributed to Yutog Yönten Gönpo (1112–1203). Its 156 chapters across four tantra volumes draw substantially from Āyurvedic sources — particularly Vāgbhata's *Aṣṭāṅgahṛdayasaṃhitā* — alongside indigenous Tibetan and Chinese medical knowledge [7]. Within Sowa Rigpa, coriander (*hu su* in Tibetan transliteration) is used for **wind** (*rlung*) disorders, stomach phlegm (*bad kan*), fever, and excessive thirst [8]. Coriander figures as a secondary spice ingredient in compound formulas targeting digestive disturbance, consistent with its pan-traditional role as a carminative. The three humours of Sowa Rigpa — *rlung* (wind), *mkhris pa* (bile), and *bad kan* (phlegm) — each frame illness as energetic imbalance, and coriander's warming-digestive and mild fever-clearing properties position it across bile-phlegm intersections.

## 3. Convergence Analysis

Five independent medical traditions spanning approximately 3,500 years and three continents converge on coriander's primary therapeutic territories: **digestive function** (carminative, anti-spasmodic, appetite-regulating) and **inflammation management** (fever, urinary heat). Secondary convergence appears in metabolic and nervine applications. The Galenic and Unani humoral classifications (Dioscorides, Avicenna) and the Sowa Rigpa energetic classifications differ in explanatory framework but arrive at overlapping indications. This cross-traditional agreement provides historical plausibility for investigating specific phytochemical mechanisms now tractable through modern pharmacology.

---

## 4. Phytochemistry & Composition

The essential oil of *C. sativum* seed is dominated by **linalool** (a monoterpenoid alcohol), which constitutes 55–79% of seed essential oil across geographical populations — Argentinian and European samples record 72.3% and 77.7% respectively, as measured by GC-MS [1, 9]. Secondary seed-oil constituents include camphor, γ-terpinene, geranyl acetate, and β-pinene [9].

Beyond the essential oil fraction, coriander seeds contain:
- **Flavonoids:** quercetin, rutin, apigenin, isoquercitrin
- **Phytosterols:** β-sitosterol, stigmasterol
- **Fatty acids (fixed oil):** petroselinic acid (dominant), linoleic acid, oleic acid
- **Phenolic acids:** caffeic and chlorogenic acid derivatives
- **Polyphenols:** broad antioxidant profile [10, 11]

Leaf volatile composition diverges markedly: (2E)-decenal accounts for 16–47% of cilantro essential oil, with linalool present at only 12–30% [1].

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**Digestive/carminative:** Smooth-muscle relaxant activity attributed to linalool and flavonoid constituents; anticholinergic and calcium-channel antagonist effects have been proposed as mechanisms in preclinical models [10].

**Hypolipidaemic and antidiabetic:** Phytosterols (β-sitosterol, stigmasterol) inhibit intestinal cholesterol absorption; polyphenols modulate insulin signalling and glucose transporter expression in animal models [11].

**Antihypertensive:** Flavonoids achieve ACE inhibition through zinc chelation at the enzyme active site, supporting nitric oxide production and vasodilation [10].

**Anxiolytic/neurological:** Linalool acts on GABA-A receptors via benzodiazepine-responsive sites in rodent models, producing sedative and anxiolytic effects comparable to diazepam at 100–200 mg/kg doses in animal studies [12]. Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant pathways in CNS tissue have also been implicated.

**Antioxidant:** Rutin and quercetin scavenge reactive oxygen species; malondialdehyde reduction has been demonstrated in human supplementation trials [13].

## 6. Clinical Evidence

Human evidence is emerging but requires cautious interpretation.

**Metabolic outcomes (2025 RCT):** A randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (n = 40, type 2 diabetes patients, ages 30–60) administering 1,000 mg/day coriander seed powder for 6 weeks demonstrated statistically significant reductions in total cholesterol (−38.65 mg/dL), triglycerides (−22.10 mg/dL), LDL-cholesterol (−15.95 mg/dL), and fasting blood glucose (−25.85 mg/dL, approximately 16%), alongside improvements in insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) and oxidative stress markers. HDL-cholesterol was unchanged. *Limitation:* small sample (n = 20 per arm), single-centre, short duration [13].

**Neuropsychological outcomes (2024 RCT):** A randomised controlled trial (n = 86 university students, ages 18–25) administering 500 mg *C. sativum* seeds daily found statistically significant improvements in prospective and retrospective memory (PRMQ; p = 0.006), anxiety (Hospital Anxiety Scale; p = 0.04), depression (Hospital Depression Scale; p = 0.002), and sleep quality (PSQI; p = 0.03) versus placebo. Authors characterised results as "initial insights" and called for further research. *Limitation:* healthy young student population, outcomes are self-reported, duration not fully specified in abstract [14].

**Anxiolytic animal evidence:** Extensive rodent studies confirm anxiolytic and sedative effects of coriander extracts and isolated linalool; linalool inhalation studies in mice confirm GABAergic mediation [12]. Direct translation to human clinical populations is not yet established.

**Overall grade:** Promising preliminary human evidence in two specific populations; larger, longer-duration trials across diverse populations are needed before clinical recommendations can be standardised.

---

## 7. Phenomenological Record

Traditional users across cultures consistently report that coriander seed, consumed as a decoction or spice, produces a subjective sense of digestive ease, reduced abdominal tension, and mild calming. No specific first-person phenomenological record survives from antiquity attributing altered states or marked psychoactive effects to coriander; its action is described across traditions as gentle, normalising, and supportive rather than forceful or dramatic.

## 8. Practical Application

**Forms in traditional and modern use:**
- Whole dried seed, ground seed (cooking and infusion)
- Aqueous decoction (seeds simmered 10–15 min)
- Standardised seed powder (capsules, 500–1,000 mg/day in clinical trials)
- Essential oil (topical or aromatic use; not for internal use without professional guidance)
- Fresh leaf (cilantro; distinct chemical profile from seed)

**Dosing (based on clinical trial evidence):** 500–1,000 mg dried seed powder daily, used for 6 weeks in published trials [13, 14]. Traditional decoction doses are not quantified in classical sources.

**Cautions:**
- Apiaceae family cross-reactivity: individuals with allergy to carrot, celery, mugwort, or related plants may react to coriander [10].
- Coriander essential oil is phototoxic in high topical concentrations; avoid UV exposure after topical application.
- Pregnancy: traditionally used as an emmenagogue in some cultures; high-dose supplementation during pregnancy is not established as safe.
- Drug interactions: potential additive effect with hypoglycaemic or hypolipidaemic medications; clinical significance not quantified [13].

## 9. Spiritual & Esoteric Layer

**Ancient Egypt:** Coriander seed's presence in funerary contexts — including botanical remains from New Kingdom royal tombs — suggests an apotropaic or provisioning function in the journey to the afterlife, consistent with Egyptian funerary practice of including food plants and aromatics [5]. Within Egyptian medical-magical tradition, medicinal preparations frequently combined empirical botanical action with invocation; coriander ointments served both material and ritual functions.

**Galenic humoral tradition (Dioscorides, Avicenna):** Cold-dry in quality, coriander corrects excess heat and moisture. Its application in feverish states, bile disorders, and inflammatory conditions follows directly from humoral logic: cooling substances counter hot conditions. Avicenna's cold-second-degree classification places it among moderate correctives — effective without being harshly depleting [3].

**Sowa Rigpa:** Within Tibetan medical cosmology, illness arises from disturbances of *rlung*, *mkhris pa*, and *bad kan*, ultimately rooted in the three mental poisons (ignorance, attachment, aversion). Coriander's therapeutic position — addressing *rlung* (wind, associated with the subtle body and mental agitation) and stomach phlegm — places it at the interface of physical digestion and mental quietude. Its mild, non-forceful character suits auxiliary rather than primary prescription roles in compound formulas [7, 8].

---

## 10. Functional–Anatomical Correlate

**Gastrointestinal:** The primary functional territory is the enteric system — carminative, antispasmodic, and secretion-modulating actions. Cross-traditional consensus is strongest here and is supported by preclinical smooth-muscle mechanism data.

**Metabolic-hepatic:** Hypolipidaemic and hypoglycaemic activity, with preliminary human evidence, points to hepatic and pancreatic involvement — cholesterol metabolism, insulin signalling, and glucose regulation.

**Cardiovascular:** ACE-inhibitory and antiarrhythmic mechanisms identified preclinically; human cardiovascular trial evidence is not yet available.

**Nervous system:** The GABAergic-anxiolytic axis of linalool is well-documented in animal models, with human trial data (memory, anxiety, sleep) now providing preliminary corroboration. Whether the anxiolytic effect in human doses arises from systemic linalool absorption or from indirect GI-vagal pathways remains unresolved.
