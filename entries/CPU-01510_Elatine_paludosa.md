# Elatine paludosa — Elatine (Dioscorides 4-40)

## *Elatine paludosa, as printed in the plate caption "Elatine paludosa after FAGUET — 1888" beside the chapter; the translators' SUGGESTED lines give other plants, Antirrhinum elatine ("Cancerwort, Pointed-leaved Toadflax") and Elatine hydropiper ("Water Pepper, Waterwort, Pipewort")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The identity here is doubtful twice over. The binomial *Elatine paludosa* is not on the translators' SUGGESTED lines. It comes from a plate caption printed in the chapter (line 8345), an illustration credited to Faguet, 1888. The SUGGESTED lines (8347 and 8348) read "Antirrhinum elatine, Linaria elatine, Cymbalaria elatine — Elatine, Cancerwort, Pointed-leaved Toadflax" and "Elatine hydropiper — Water Pepper, Waterwort, Pipewort", which are a toadflax and a waterwort, plants of different families. Neither is *E. paludosa*. The Codex does not state the identity as certain, and the row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The existing entry I_896 (Toadflax) already cites this chapter and reads it as *Linaria elatine*. This entry is kept as the spine row stands, and the two readings are not reconciled here.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-40. ELATINE* at line 8343, text at line 8349. The chapter stands in Book Four, "Other Herbs & Roots". The translators' name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 620, with the caption as the printed name.

The whole chapter reads: "Elatine has leaves similar to helxine [above] but smaller, rounder, and hairy. The five or six branches are thin, twenty centimetres long from the root, full of leaves that are sharp to the taste. It grows among corn and in tilled places. The leaves (applied with polenta) are able to help inflamed rheumatic eyes. Boiled and sipped it stops dysentery."

The "helxine" it points back to is the previous chapter, 4-39 (*ELXINE KUSSAMPELOS*, line 8338, glossed by the translators as *Convolvulus arvensis*, "Common Bindweed"). That chapter gives the plant leaves "similar to cissus but smaller, with long little branches clasping around wherever it occurs", growing "in hedges, vineyards and corn", with leaf juice "taken as a drink" that "has a laxative effect on the bowels". So *elatine* is described by comparison with a plant that is itself described by comparison with *cissus*. In the chapter itself, *elatine* has smaller, rounder, hairy leaves than helxine, five or six thin branches twenty centimetres long from the root, leaves sharp to the taste, and it grows among corn and in tilled places. Two uses follow. The leaves applied with polenta help inflamed rheumatic eyes. The leaves boiled and sipped stop dysentery. The chapter prints no other names.

The translation then places two plate captions after the chapter (*Parnassia palustris* and *Quinquefolium*, lines 8354 and 8358), which name other plants and are not used here.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. The two plants the translators suggest belong to different families: toadflaxes (*Linaria*, *Antirrhinum*) to the plantain family (Plantaginaceae), and the waterworts (*Elatine*) to their own small family of mostly aquatic plants. The text's own habitat, corn and tilled ground, does not describe a water plant. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the plant. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records an eye poultice and a drink for dysentery. The Codex gives no protocol for inflamed eyes or dysentery, both of which need medical care, and the identity is open between a field toadflax, a waterwort and an uncertain third plant.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives two preparations: the leaves "applied with polenta" for the eyes, and the leaves "boiled and sipped" for dysentery. No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter gives the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition: a plant told apart from a neighbour (helxine) by leaf size, shape and hair, by the count and length of the branches, by taste, and by habitat (among corn, in tilled ground). It assigns the plant no status. The identification is the translators' at best, and here the translators and the plate caption do not agree. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a comparison note with two small remedies, and the identity is too unsettled to act on. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Inflamed eyes and dysentery need medical care and should not be left to an unidentified plant. Because the plant is uncertain between genera, nothing should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
