# Turnip — Napus · Gongylis · Shalgam · Wu-jing · Shalajam

## *Brassica rapa* L. | Family: Brassicaceae (Cruciferae)

**Energetic Classification:** Galenic — warm and moist in the first degree (root), the leaf cooler and drier; Unani *har-ratb* (root); TCM cool-to-neutral, sweet-pungent-bitter, lung/spleen/stomach channels
**Training Phase:** Foundation · Purification · Sustainment

### Origin & Tradition

*Brassica rapa* is the turnip's species, but like its sibling *B. oleracea* it is a whole family compressed into one Latin binomial: the swollen white root of the European turnip, the leafy turnip-greens, bok choy and napa cabbage, the oilseed turnip-rape, and — in later infraspecific taxonomy — much of the mustard-cabbage complex of East Asia. It is one of the oldest cultivated root-and-leaf crops of Eurasia, and its double identity as **root-vegetable and pot-herb** is precisely what makes it a cross-tradition medicine: every culture that grew it used both the root below and the greens above, and prescribed them differently.

**Greco-Roman (Dioscorides & the agronomists):** The turnip — *gongylis* or *bounias* to the Greeks, *napus/rapa* to the Romans — is discussed by Dioscorides in *De Materia Medica*, who notes the cooked root as nourishing but wind-producing, the boiled root softening, and topical applications of turnip for chilblains and gout-like swellings. Roman agronomists (Columella, Pliny) treated the turnip as a staple field crop and famine-insurance, and Pliny records its medicinal and warming reputation. The doctrinal reading is that the boiled root nourishes and warms while remaining flatulent, and that its seed and juice carry a sharper, more medicinal edge.

**Egyptian (Ebers horizon):** Root vegetables and oil-yielding brassica seeds circulated in the Nile agricultural economy, where pressed oils and cooling-to-warming greens served both diet and topical medicine. Turnip-type roots and rape-seed oil belong to this general stratum of Egyptian food-medicine; specific pharaonic recipes should be attributed to the broad category of cultivated roots and seed-oils rather than to invented particulars.

**Unani (Avicenna & the Arabic physicians):** In the *Qanun* tradition the turnip (*shalgham*) is a well-known aliment-drug: the cooked root warm and moist, nourishing, aphrodisiac in reputation, and productive of wind; the seed used as a sharper remedy. Avicenna and the later Arabic physicians prescribed turnip for the chest and for cold conditions, and used turnip preparations topically. The humoral logic frames the root as building and warming, to be tempered because of its flatulent, thickening quality.

**Chinese (materia medica tradition):** The turnip and its many *B. rapa* relatives — *wu-jing* (turnip) and the leaf-mustard/cabbage group — are prominent in the Chinese dietary-therapeutic literature. The turnip is read as sweet-pungent-bitter and cool-to-neutral, benefiting the lung and the middle burner, resolving food stagnation, promoting digestion and generating fluids. As with all brassicas, the Chinese frame is emphatically *food-as-medicine*.

**Ayurvedic (dietetic tradition):** In the Ayurvedic and later Indo-Persian Indian dietary corpus the turnip (*shalajam/shalgam*) is nourishing and grounding, the root sweet and building (tending to increase *kapha*), the greens lighter and more cleansing. It is recommended cooked with warming, carminative spices to offset the wind-producing heaviness of the root — the same corrective logic applied independently in the Greek and Unani traditions.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — Root and Leaf, the Two Directions:** The turnip is the Codex figure of the **two-directional plant**: it thrusts a swollen storage-root downward into the earth (the building, warming, nourishing pole) while lifting sharp mustard-flavoured greens upward into the light (the cleansing, pungent, moving pole). In one organism the practitioner reads both movements of the training arc — *storing* and *scouring*, foundation and purification. The turnip's ancient role as famine-crop and field-insurance gives it a second symbolic charge: it is the plant of **provision against scarcity**, the humble root that keeps a household alive through winter. In the Codex it stands for the reserve — the stored resilience drawn on when richer resources fail.

### Active Components

- **Glucosinolates** — aliphatic types predominate, especially **gluconapin** (the characteristic *B. rapa* glucosinolate) and **glucobrassicanapin**, alongside indolic glucosinolates.
- **Isothiocyanates & breakdown products** — released by **myrosinase** on tissue disruption; the pungent, medicinal fraction.
- **Polyphenols / flavonoids** — predominantly **quercetin**, **kaempferol** and **isorhamnetin** glycosides, plus hydroxycinnamic (caffeic/ferulic) derivatives, concentrated in the greens.
- **Organic acids** and dietary fibre in the root.
- **Vitamin C, folate, potassium, calcium**; the greens are a notable source of provitamin-A carotenoids and vitamin K.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Turnip is a **Foundation/Sustainment** provision-food with a **Purification** upper register. The root supplies grounding nourishment and fibre — the "storing" pole — while the greens deliver the brassica glucosinolate–polyphenol package associated with antioxidant defence and gentle detoxification support. In Codex practice the two parts are used for two ends: the cooked root as building ballast in the Foundation phase, the greens (and the sharper seed) as a lighter cleansing green in Purification. Traditional topical use of the mashed root for chilblains and cold swellings is retained as a folk-tier practice.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the phytochemical basis of turnip's traditional value is well characterised:

- Dejanovic et al. (2021), *PLoS One*, in a PRISMA systematic review of turnip greens (*Brassica rapa* ssp. *rapa*), catalogued **129 phytochemicals** — 19 glucosinolates, 33 glucosinolate-breakdown products, 10 organic acids and 59 polyphenols — with **gluconapin** the predominant glucosinolate and quercetin/kaempferol/isorhamnetin the major flavonoids, and linked these constituents to antioxidant activity, enzyme regulation and cell-cycle/apoptotic modulation while calling for bioavailability and dosing studies. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0247032)

The broader *Brassica* isothiocyanate–Nrf2 mechanism reviewed for the cabbage entry applies to *B. rapa* by shared chemistry.

**Evidence grade:** Moderate for antioxidant/phytochemical value (strong compositional characterisation, thinner direct clinical outcome data specific to turnip). High confidence for nutritional value. Traditional aphrodisiac and topical claims remain low-grade.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** Sweet-pungent-bitter, cool-to-neutral; lung, spleen, stomach; resolves food stagnation, aids digestion, generates fluids.
- **Ayurveda:** Root sweet, heavy, building, *kapha*-increasing; greens lighter and cleansing; cook with warming spice.
- **Galenic/Unani:** Cooked root warm and moist (1st degree), nourishing but flatulent; seed sharper; greens cooler.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** Under the blocking warning in force for every cuneiform substance identification, deity attribution and tablet reference, no cultivation status can be assigned to the turnip. Mesopotamian medicine classified illness partly by causal agency, framing a plant by the agency it opposed rather than by any effect on a vital constitution; no such framing for the turnip is securely attested. (c) absent.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** The turnip (*shalgham*) is attested in the *Qanun* stream as a warm-and-moist aliment-drug — nourishing, aphrodisiac in reputation, wind-producing, the seed a sharper remedy for the chest and cold conditions. That is a therapeutic classification, not a cultivation one. Unani's cultivation idea proper is the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture; no source places the turnip in that economy or has it strengthen the *ruh*, so it carries a therapeutic status only. (b) framed.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides discusses *gongylis / napus* — the boiled root nourishing but flatulent, softening, applied topically for chilblains — and the agronomists (Columella, Pliny) treated it as staple and famine-insurance. This is a temperament-and-indication reading; the Greek system holds no subtle-body scheme from which a cultivation status could come. What it offers a cultivator is the discipline of attention: distinguishing root from greens from the sharper seed, judging season and quality, honest assay. (c) for cultivation status, with that discipline as the practice.

**Egyptian (EP).** The pharaonic corpus is indication-organised and framed by *heka*. Turnip-type roots and rape-seed oil sit only within the general category of cultivated roots and seed-oils; no substance-specific cultivation framing is attested, which for an indication-organised corpus is the honest reading. (c) absent.

**Chinese (TCM).** The turnip (*wu-jing*) is prominent in the dietary-therapeutic literature as *food-that-is-medicine* within the conservational register of *yang sheng* 養生 — it resolves food stagnation, aids digestion and generates fluids. These are therapeutic actions; no *san pin* 三品 grade is recorded for it in the sources retrieved, so it carries a dietary/therapeutic status, not an attested cultivation grade. (b) framed.

**Ayurveda (SS).** In the dietary (*dravyaguna*) corpus the root is sweet, heavy and *kapha*-building while the greens are lighter and cleansing, cooked with warming spice to offset the root's wind. These are food qualities, not a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* assignment, and none is attested for the turnip; the sattvic/rajasic/tamasic lists are post-Sushrutan and are not imported. (b) framed.

**Practitioner's note.** Two parts, two uses — cooked root as building ballast, greens and seed as a lighter scour — but both are food registers, not cultivation grades. The turnip's nearest cultivation-adjacent charge is provision: the stored winter root as reserve against scarcity. Nothing here is taken long-term to nourish a vital constitution; the honest practice is dietary, and in the Greek key, careful selection.

### Contraindications

- **Digestive:** The cooked root is notably wind-producing; caution in bloating-prone or cold-stomach constitutions — cook thoroughly with carminatives.
- **Thyroid:** As a glucosinolate-bearing brassica, large habitual raw intakes are mildly goitrogenic; caution with iodine deficiency / hypothyroidism.
- **Vitamin K:** Turnip greens are high in vitamin K — keep intake steady on anticoagulants.
- Otherwise very safe as food.
