# Canadian Fleabane

## *Erigeron canadensis* L. (= *Conyza canadensis* (L.) Cronquist) | Family: Asteraceae

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ, BM
**Energetic Classification:** Dry, cooling-astringent, dispersive at the surface; binding in the interior
**Training Phase:** Consolidation — a minor astringent-and-repellent herb, not an initiatory agent

---

### Origin & Tradition

Canadian fleabane is a slender, unlovely annual of disturbed ground: a single ribbed stem to two metres, crowded with narrow leaves and topped by a panicle of tiny, barely-rayed heads that look less like flowers than like a spray of unopened buds. It is one of the most successful plants on Earth, and almost nobody plants it.

**The biogeographic fact that governs this entry.** *Erigeron canadensis* is a **North American native**. It reached Europe only in the post-Columbian era — the standard account places its European naturalisation in the seventeenth century, and it spread from there across Eurasia. This single fact disciplines every attestation below. Any Old-World tradition using this plant is using a **post-Columbian import**, not an inherited materia medica. Its presence in modern Chinese, Mediterranean, and Levantine herbal practice is real and current — it is now among the most widespread invasive Asteraceae in China's arable land, formally listed as a common alien agricultural weed there (Huang, Lin & Chen 2025, *Heliyon*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2025.e41772)) — but that presence is at most four centuries old, and any claim of a classical Chinese, Ayurvedic, or Greco-Arabic pedigree for *this species* should be treated as a **misattribution**, most often a transfer from a genuinely Old-World *Erigeron* or *Conyza* congener. `[USER: verify]` against any secondary source that asserts otherwise.

**North America (Indigenous and settler-era usage) — "fleabane," "horseweed," "butterweed."** The plant's home range is where its attested use sits. The English names carry the doctrine plainly: *fleabane* — the herb was burned or strewn in bedding and floors to drive fleas out; *horseweed* and *butterweed* record it as a rank weed of trodden pasture and stockyard. Alongside the insecticidal use runs a second, better-documented thread: it is a **haemostatic and astringent**, taken as an infusion for dysentery and diarrhoea, and — the use that gave it whatever place it had in nineteenth-century Eclectic and domestic practice — for **uterine and other passive haemorrhage**. The classical indication is therefore *flux*: anything running out of the body that should not be. `[USER: verify]` the specific attribution of any named nation's use before printing it — the ethnobotanical record for this species is broad but frequently repeated third-hand without primary sourcing, and this book does not launder secondhand attributions into tradition.

**Post-Columbian Eurasia — a naturalised weed with a working pharmacy.** In its adopted range the plant has been picked up wherever it grows abundantly, which is everywhere. Contemporary phytochemical work from Jordan on wild-growing *C. canadensis* documents a plant in active regional use with a substantial phenolic and essential-oil profile (Barhoumi et al. 2024, *Molecules*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules29102403)). This is best read not as an ancient tradition but as **convergent folk empiricism** — a bitter astringent aromatic arrives, people use it as a bitter astringent aromatic.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.**

The fleabane doctrine is unusually honest, because it is a claim about a *volatile* — and volatiles are exactly what a burned herb delivers. "Burn this and the fleas leave" is a testable proposition about smoke chemistry, not a sympathy or a signature. And it partly survives: the *Erigeron* essential oils are genuinely insect-active. A 2025 review of the genus records **insecticidal, larvicidal, and repellent** activity across *Erigeron* oils, tied specifically to the polyacetylenic **matricaria and lachnophyllum esters** and to **limonene** (Judžentienė 2025, *Molecules*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules30142989)). Note the honest caveat: that review is **genus-wide**, and its repellency data are drawn across several *Erigeron* species — it is congener evidence, named as such, not a species-specific demonstration for *E. canadensis*.

The haemostatic doctrine maps differently and less cleanly. "Stops flux" in a plant loaded with **tannins and flavonoids** is a plausible statement about *local* astringency — a tannin puckers a mucosa it touches, and a plant that tightens the gut wall it contacts will slow a dysenteric flux. But the doctrine overreaches when it crosses into the uterus. There is no mechanism by which a gut-lumen astringent reaches and constricts the endometrium; that step is **doctrinal extrapolation from a local effect to a systemic one**, and it should be deflated. The traditional uterine indication is the weakest limb of this plant's reputation and this book will not carry it as a working use.

Then there is the modern layer, which is where the plant's real symbolic weight now sits. Horseweed was the **first weed identified with glyphosate resistance**, and is now a textbook case of it — its chromosome-scale genome was sequenced partly for that reason, and is annotated for ABC transporters, cytochrome P450s and glycosyltransferases implicated in non-target-site resistance (Laforest et al. 2020, *Pest Manag Sci*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ps.5753)). Extreme resistance is associated with a **Pro-106-Ser substitution in EPSPS**, documented in US biotypes (Beres et al. 2020, *Sci Rep*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-64458-7)) and independently in Mexican populations, where reduced translocation compounds it (Palma-Bautista et al. 2023, *J Agric Food Chem*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jafc.2c07833)). The old doctrine said this plant expels what troubles it. The new one is literal: it has evolved a defence against the most-used herbicide on the planet, twice, on two continents. That is the fleabane doctrine reflected back at us — the plant that drives things off is now the thing that cannot be driven off.

### Active Components

- **Essential oil (aerial parts)** — the diagnostic constituent is **matricaria ester**, a polyacetylenic ester. In the Jordanian material it was the single main component, at 15.4% of the inflorescence oil, **60.7% of the leaf oil**, and 31.6% of the stem oil; leaf and stem oils were dominated by polyacetylene derivatives, the inflorescence oil by oxygenated monoterpenes (Barhoumi et al. 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules29102403)).
- **Limonene** and related monoterpenes — reported across the genus as a prevalent *Erigeron* oil constituent, and tied in review to anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial and cytotoxic activity (Judžentienė 2025, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules30142989)). Genus-level attribution.
- **Lachnophyllum ester** — the sister polyacetylene; genus-level, strongly associated with the larvicidal/repellent activity (Judžentienė 2025).
- **Phenolic acids** — **rosmarinic acid** dominates the methanolic extract at 1441.1 mg/kg plant, with **caffeic acid phenethyl ester** at 231.8 mg/kg (Barhoumi et al. 2024).
- **Flavonoids** — total flavonoid content 467.0 ± 10.5 mg QE/g extract; total phenolics 95.59 ± 0.40 mg GAE/g extract (Barhoumi et al. 2024).
- **Tannins** — the basis of the astringent action. Not quantified in the material retrieved this run; treat as traditionally attested rather than measured. `[USER: verify]`

Chemotype variability across the plant's enormous range is substantial, and the oil differs sharply **by plant part** — leaf oil and flower oil are not interchangeable.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

A **surface-dispersive, interior-binding** herb of narrow scope. Two defensible uses:

1. **Repellent and strewing agent (SBJ).** Fresh or dried aerial parts as a strewing herb, smudge, or oil-bearing infused preparation for arthropod deterrence in a working space. This is the plant's honest strength and the one its name records.
2. **Gut astringent (BM).** A short-course infusion for loose, non-infective flux where a tightening, drying action on the gut wall is wanted. Adjunct only; it does not treat a cause.

It is **not** an initiatory, visionary, or nervine agent. It has no place in a training phase concerned with opening. Its whole gesture is closure.

**As a cultivator's plant:** do not cultivate it. This is one of the rare entries where the responsible instruction is *don't*. Outside its native North American range it is a listed invasive and a hard-to-control agricultural weed; deliberately establishing it is ecologically indefensible. A single plant sheds enormous quantities of wind-borne seed. Harvest wild from ground where it is already dominant — you will not lack for supply — and take aerial parts **before seed set** so your harvest is not a dispersal event. Do not compost seeding material.

### Scientific Correlation

Graded honestly, including the nulls.

- **Insecticidal / repellent doctrine — Grade C (plausible, congener-supported, species-unproven).** Genus-level review supports insecticidal, larvicidal and repellent activity in *Erigeron* oils, mechanistically tied to matricaria/lachnophyllum esters and limonene (Judžentienė 2025, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules30142989)) — and *E. canadensis* leaf oil is 60.7% matricaria ester (Barhoumi et al. 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules29102403)). The chemistry lines up. What is missing is a species-specific efficacy assay against fleas. Nobody, in what was retrieved this run, has tested fleabane on fleas.
- **Antimicrobial — Grade C.** The methanolic extract showed significant antibacterial activity with an MIC of 3.125 µg/mL against one organism (Barhoumi et al. 2024). Single study, in vitro, no clinical extension.
- **Antioxidant — Grade C, and unimpressive.** Moderate DPPH scavenging (IC₅₀ 23.75 ± 0.86 µg/mL) and **low iron-chelating activity** (IC₅₀ 5396.07 ± 15.05 µg/mL) (Barhoumi et al. 2024). Reported here because the null belongs in the record.
- **Haemostatic / anti-dysenteric — Grade D (traditional only).** **No clinical trial was retrieved this run** for *E. canadensis* in dysentery, diarrhoea, or uterine bleeding. The tannin/flavonoid content makes a local gut astringency mechanistically reasonable; the uterine claim has no retrieved support and no plausible mechanism, and is deflated accordingly.
- **Glyphosate resistance — Grade A (not a medicinal claim).** Extremely well documented. EPSPS Pro-106-Ser in US biotypes (Beres et al. 2020, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-64458-7)); Pro106Ser plus reduced translocation in Mexican citrus populations (Palma-Bautista et al. 2023, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jafc.2c07833)); chromosome-scale genome annotated for resistance loci (Laforest et al. 2020, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ps.5753)). This is the single best-evidenced fact about the plant, and it is agronomic.

*Overall:* a chemically interesting, clinically unstudied plant. The literature on how to **kill** it vastly exceeds the literature on how to **use** it. That asymmetry is the honest summary.

*All citations above were retrieved from PubMed.*

### Traditional Energetic Classification

| Axis | Reading |
|---|---|
| Temperature | Cool |
| Moisture | Dry |
| Taste | Bitter, astringent, faintly acrid-aromatic |
| Direction | Outward at the surface (volatile, repellent); inward-binding in the gut (tannin) |
| Tissue affinity | Gut mucosa; skin |
| Doctrine of gesture | Expulsion outward, closure inward |

The two directions are not a contradiction — they are two different chemistries in one plant. The oil disperses; the tannin binds.

### Contraindications

- **Asteraceae sensitivity.** Cross-reactive contact dermatitis and allergy are a real risk across this family. Anyone reactive to ragweed, chamomile, or feverfew should avoid it.
- **Pregnancy and lactation — avoid.** No safety data. The traditional uterine indication makes this an active exclusion, not a precaution.
- **Essential oil internally — do not.** The oil is polyacetylene-dominant; polyacetylenes are not benign, and no internal safety margin has been established.
- **Do not use for undiagnosed bleeding.** Uterine or rectal haemorrhage requires diagnosis. Using an astringent herb to mask it is the dangerous move here, and it is the plant's most-repeated traditional indication. That is the specific hazard of this entry.
- **Infective diarrhoea.** Astringing a gut that is clearing an infection is counterproductive. Rule out infection first.
- **Smoke.** Burning any plant material produces respiratory irritants. Not for asthmatics or in unventilated space.
- **Herbicide residue.** Because horseweed is a glyphosate-resistant weed of arable land, wild-harvested material from farmland has a **high prior probability of herbicide exposure** — it survived the spray. Harvest only from unsprayed ground.
