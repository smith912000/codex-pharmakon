# Yarrow

## *Achillea millefolium* L. | Family: Asteraceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, BM

**Energetic Classification:** Cool-dry with a bitter-pungent edge; astringent to the surface, relaxant-diaphoretic to the interior. A plant that closes what is open and opens what is closed.

**Training Phase:** Foundation — Field Medicine / Boundary Work

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Achillea millefolium* is one of the most widely distributed medicinal plants on the planet — a circumboreal aggregate species (properly a species complex, *A. millefolium* sensu lato) growing across Europe, temperate Asia, and North America, and naturalised almost everywhere else. Its ubiquity is the reason it appears, independently, as a wound herb in traditions that had no contact with one another. When a plant that stops bleeding grows on every trampled verge in the northern hemisphere, every culture that walks those verges finds it.

**Greek / Classical Mediterranean — *Achilleios* (Ἀχίλλειος), "the herb of Achilles"**

The binomial carries the doctrine. The genus is named for Achilles, who in classical tradition was said to have been taught the plant's use by the centaur Chiron and to have staunched the wounds of his soldiers with it at Troy. Whether or not any Bronze Age Greek ever did this, the name fixed the plant's identity for two thousand years of European practice: yarrow is the soldier's herb.

Classical indication: applied as a bruised fresh poultice to bleeding wounds, ulcers, and fistulae; internally as an astringent for fluxes. Doctrine: a plant that *binds*. In humoral terms, cold and dry — it contracts loose, hot, flowing tissue back into its proper boundary.

**Latin & Medieval European — *herba militaris*, "soldier's woundwort", "nosebleed", "staunchweed", "carpenter's herb"**

The Latin apothecary tradition inherited the Achillean doctrine and hardened it into a battlefield staple; the vernacular names are diagnostic in themselves. *Herba militaris* and *staunchweed* name the haemostatic use. *Carpenter's herb* names the same use in peacetime — the herb you reach for when the chisel slips. *Nosebleed* names an ambiguity worth flagging: yarrow was used both to *stop* nosebleeds and, in some folk practice, deliberately to *provoke* one (a leaf rolled and inserted into the nostril) as a therapeutic bleed for headache. The plant was understood as governing blood at the margin in both directions.

Classical indication: fresh poultice for wounds; hot infusion of the flowering tops as a diaphoretic for fever, colds, and "breaking" an illness at its onset; bitter tonic for weak digestion; emmenagogue to bring on suppressed menses.

Doctrine: yarrow is the herb of the *threshold*. Hot infusion opens the periphery and drives a sweat; cold infusion or fresh poultice closes it. Same plant, opposite direction, decided by the preparation — a genuinely unusual doctrinal claim, and one that maps onto real pharmacology (see below).

**Aztec / Nahua — *tlalquequetzal*** [USER: verify — the identification of the Nahuatl *tlalquequetzal* with *Achillea millefolium* rests on colonial-era herbal identifications and is not beyond dispute; confirm against a Nahuatl-source specialist before printing as settled]

Yarrow is native to North America as well as Eurasia, and Nahua practice used it independently of any European transmission. The name *tlalquequetzal* is generally read as "earth-quetzal" or "ground plume" — a description of the finely dissected, feather-like leaf that also produced the European *millefolium* ("thousand-leaf") and *milfoil*. Two unconnected traditions named the plant for the same visual fact.

Classical indication: reported in colonial herbal sources for wounds and skin complaints, and for complaints of the head. The convergence with the European wound-doctrine is the point of interest here, not any claim of shared lineage.

Doctrine: as with the European tradition, the plant is read as a surface medicine — one that acts where the body meets the world.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

The Achilles myth is not decoration; it encodes a real observation and then over-reaches on it.

The real observation: crushed yarrow applied to a bleeding wound reduces bleeding and does not obviously make the wound worse. That is a defensible field claim, and it is why the doctrine survived. The plant is rich in condensed tannins, which precipitate surface proteins and produce local astringency and vasoconstriction; it contains the alkaloid amide **achilleine**, historically credited with the haemostatic action; and its blue essential oil contains **chamazulene**, an artefact formed from the sesquiterpene lactone matricin during steam distillation, alongside other anti-inflammatory sesquiterpene lactones. A poultice that constricts the vessel bed, precipitates a protein seal, and damps the inflammatory response is doing something coherent.

The over-reach: the doctrine of the "threshold" — closing outward, opening inward — is the tradition's attempt to reconcile two observed behaviours (poultice stops bleeding; hot tea makes you sweat) into a single essence. There is no single essence. The astringency is tannin-mediated and topical. The diaphoresis is a volatile-oil-and-heat effect that a hot infusion of many aromatic plants would produce. The elegance is in the doctrine, not the plant. Take the two actions as two actions.

The Achilles-and-Chiron transmission story should be read as a name, not as evidence. The plant did not become haemostatic because Achilles used it; it acquired his name because it was already the herb that soldiers carried.

### Active Components

- **Sesquiterpene lactones** — matricin (the precursor to chamazulene), achillicin, and related guaianolides. These carry much of the anti-inflammatory activity and *also* much of the allergenic liability. They are the plant's dose-limiting chemistry in both directions.
- **Chamazulene** — the deep blue-violet compound that colours a properly distilled yarrow oil. Not present in the living plant; it forms from matricin under the heat of steam distillation. A yarrow oil that is not blue was either distilled from a chemotype low in matricin or handled badly.
- **Achilleine** — an alkaloid amide, the historical candidate for the haemostatic action. Note honestly that the modern evidence base for achilleine as a haemostatic in humans is thin; the attribution is largely inherited.
- **Condensed tannins and flavonoids** — apigenin, luteolin, rutin and glycosides thereof; these underwrite the astringent and antioxidant behaviour. LC-MS profiling of a Kashmir-grown *A. millefolium* identified diosmin, pelargonin, and epicatechin among the constituents of the most active fraction (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1155/cjid/9925080)).
- **Monoterpenes** — 1,8-cineole, camphor, borneol, α- and β-pinene, and in some chemotypes appreciable **thujone**. Chemotype variation in this species is severe: thujone content ranges from negligible to substantial depending on population and ploidy level. This is the single most important cultivator-facing fact about yarrow.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Yarrow is a **boundary substance**. It belongs in the Foundation phase not because it is powerful but because it is the clearest available teacher of a specific discipline: *acting at the interface without entering the system.*

Three use cases:

1. **Field haemostasis and wound cover.** Fresh aerial parts, bruised, over a clean minor wound. The intent is mechanical and local — reduce bleeding, damp inflammation, cover. This is the use with the deepest tradition and the most plausible mechanism.
2. **Threshold-of-illness diaphoresis.** A hot infusion of the flowering tops at the very first sign of a cold or feverish onset, taken warm and followed by rest. Traditional and widely reported; the clinical evidence is effectively absent. Use it as a comfort measure with an honest label, not as a treatment.
3. **Bitter digestive tonic.** Small doses of the bitter infusion before food. Ordinary, low-risk, low-drama.

Not an entheogen, not a nervine of any consequence, and not a substance that rewards escalation. Its practice-value is in restraint.

### Cultivation Notes

Yarrow is close to unkillable and this is a trap. It grows from seed surface-sown in spring, spreads aggressively by rhizome, tolerates poor dry soil, and will colonise a bed if allowed. Grow it in a confined position or accept the takeover.

The cultivator's real problem is **chemotype, not survival**. Wild-collected and seed-grown yarrow varies enormously in essential oil composition — particularly thujone. Ornamental *Achillea* cultivars sold for their flower colour have been bred for the border, not the apothecary, and should not be assumed medicinal. If the oil matters, propagate vegetatively from a known plant whose distillate runs blue, and keep the clone. Harvest flowering tops in full bloom on a dry morning; dry fast in shade — the volatiles and the colour both go with heat and light.

### Scientific Correlation

Graded honestly. Yarrow is a plant with a large traditional reputation and a small, mostly preclinical evidence base.

**Moderate (preclinical, direct, species-specific) — wound closure.** An in vitro screen of botanical extracts found that *Achillea millefolium* extract improved the rate of wound closure in a keratinocyte scratch assay, in both human (HaCaT) and canine (CPEK) cell lines — while notably *not* showing the broad antimicrobial activity that eucalyptus, black walnut, chaparral, sage, and ginger did in the same screen (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-025-04777-2)). This is a clean and useful result precisely because it separates the two claims: yarrow moved the closure endpoint and did not move the antimicrobial endpoint. The tradition's wound doctrine is about *closure*, and that is the endpoint it hit.

**Weak-to-moderate (in vivo, but confounded) — topical healing.** A rat model of incision, excision, and thermal burn tested ointments containing plant extracts alongside bee products (honey, propolis, apilarnil) and polymers (collagen, chitosan), reporting substantial contraction rates and restored histological structure by day 21 (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ph18010065)). The design cannot attribute the effect to any single component. Supportive, not probative.

**Weak (genus-level, not species) — anti-inflammatory in wound repair.** An equine-model study of herbal extracts including yarrow found reduced IL-8 and increased macrophage phagocytic activity in vitro, with the authors attributing effects to synergy across the mixture (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.33393/dti.2025.3499)). Again a mixture, again unattributable. A separate diabetic-rat wound study used *Achillea sintenisii*, a different species, in combination with maggot secretions — the combination outperformed the commercial control, but nothing there transfers to *A. millefolium* (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/mve.70075)).

**Weak (antioxidant, in vitro) — free-radical scavenging.** Ethyl acetate fraction, DPPH IC₅₀ of 199.12 µg/mL, with antibacterial and antifungal zones of inhibition reported alongside molecular docking (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1155/cjid/9925080)). An IC₅₀ near 200 µg/mL is unremarkable — this is a crude extract behaving like a crude polyphenol-containing extract. Note the tension with the null antimicrobial result above; different extraction solvents and assays, opposite conclusions.

**Genus-level clinical signals, reported with caution.** A comprehensive review of the genus *Achillea* notes clinical studies indicating therapeutic effect on multiple sclerosis, chemotherapy-induced oral mucositis, and dysmenorrhea — and, importantly, a **null result in atopic dermatitis** (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.22038/AJP.2024.23711)). Record the null with the same emphasis as the positives. The review is genus-wide and the underlying trials are small; nothing here is settled.

**Effectively absent.** There is no adequate human trial evidence retrieved this run for the two claims the tradition cares about most: haemostasis in humans, and diaphoretic abortion of a cold. The achilleine haemostasis story in particular is inherited attribution rather than demonstrated fact. Say so.

**Net grade: the wound-closure doctrine is plausible and has direct preclinical support; the haemostatic mechanism is unproven; the fever doctrine is untested; the antimicrobial claim is contradicted by at least one direct screen.**

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Cool and dry. Bitter, pungent, astringent. Assigned in Western humoral practice to Venus and, in some sources, to Mars — a split that mirrors the plant's double action: Venusian in its soft feathered leaf and its use for the menses, Martial in its blood and its battlefield. Directionally: centripetal in poultice (draws in, binds), centrifugal in hot infusion (drives out, opens). The tradition's insistence that these are one action is doctrine, not chemistry.

### Contraindications

- **Asteraceae sensitisation.** Yarrow is a well-recognised cause of allergic contact dermatitis, driven by its sesquiterpene lactones. Anyone reactive to chamomile, feverfew, ragweed, arnica, or chrysanthemum should treat yarrow as suspect. Patch-test before repeated topical use. This is the most common real-world adverse event with this plant.
- **Pregnancy — avoid.** Two independent reasons. Yarrow is a traditional emmenagogue, and the folk indication for bringing on suppressed menses is exactly the property that makes it unsuitable in pregnancy. Separately, thujone-bearing chemotypes carry the general thujone liability. Avoid in pregnancy and while breastfeeding.
- **Thujone / chemotype uncertainty.** Because thujone content varies by population, an unanalysed yarrow oil or a high-dose long-term internal preparation carries an unquantifiable exposure. Do not take yarrow essential oil internally. Do not use high-dose internal preparations chronically.
- **Photosensitivity.** Reported with yarrow contact in some individuals. [USER: verify — attested in the older literature; confirm before printing as established]
- **Anticoagulant interaction — theoretical.** The haemostatic reputation invites concern in either direction with warfarin and similar agents. No retrieved evidence this run establishes a clinical interaction; flag as unknown rather than as safe.
- **Wound depth.** The traditional field use is for *minor* wounds. Yarrow is not a substitute for pressure, closure, or a clinician on anything that bleeds seriously. The Achilles story is about soldiers who mostly died of their wounds.

**Cross-references:**
- *Matricaria chamomilla* — the other great chamazulene plant; the same sesquiterpene chemistry, the opposite temperament (relaxant rather than binding). Compare their allergen profiles directly.
- *Artemisia absinthium* — thujone as the shared hazard; useful contrast for how differently a tradition treats thujone when it is the point versus when it is a contaminant.
