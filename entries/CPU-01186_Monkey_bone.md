# Monkey bone — spre.hu (as printed in Clark 1995, rGyud-bzhi Explanatory Tantra ch.20)
## *The bone of a monkey; Clark prints the Tibetan name spre.hu and gives no binomial in the line read* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** In the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`) lists the thirteen animal-origin medicines (about lines 1148-1149): horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body. Under the heading Bone (line 1160), after human skull, human bone, dragon bone, tiger bone, cowrie shell, pig bone, sheep bone and porcupine bone, comes: "spre.hu: Monkey bone: [has seven names. It is ecbolic [and abortifacient]." The brackets are as printed in the OCR text on disk and are unbalanced: the bracket opens before "has seven names" and the word "ecbolic" falls inside it, so the printed line does not let me say which words Clark takes from the root verse and which from the commentary (he uses square brackets for his own insertions). The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3523) gives the same item in a run of bone entries: "Pig bone cures baekan mukpo. Sheep bone cures loong disorders. The spherical head of the femur cures obstruction of urine. Porcupine bone stops bleeding. Monkey bone is an ecbolic." So both translations carry one printed action for monkey bone, that it is ecbolic, meaning that it promotes expulsion from the womb. Clark's line adds, inside brackets, that the item has seven names and is abortifacient. Neither text, in the lines read, names a species of monkey, a part of the skeleton, a preparation or a dose.

The monkey also appears elsewhere in the Men-Tsee-Khang text as a type and an omen: the *tripa* constitution is likened to "tigers, monkeys and evil spirits" (line 1803), and a monkey crossing the physician's path is an inauspicious sign (line 1840). Those lines say nothing about the bone as a drug.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, bone is mainly calcium phosphate (hydroxyapatite) in a collagen matrix, with marrow fat and a small amount of other minerals. Nothing in bone is known to act on the uterus. Primate bone also carries a risk of zoonotic infection.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text records monkey bone as a drug that expels or aborts. The Codex repeats that as a record. It does not recommend using monkey bone, or any substance, to end a pregnancy. A pregnancy that is wanted or unwanted, or any condition in which expulsion from the womb is in question, is a matter for a qualified clinician. Monkeys and other non-human primates are protected animals in many countries, and trade in their parts is restricted by national law and by international agreements on endangered species; the Codex does not recommend taking or buying any part. No modern protocol is invented here.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Bone is a mineralised connective tissue and is not a known abortifacient or uterine stimulant; ended pregnancies by non-clinical means can cause haemorrhage, infection and death. Primates can carry viruses that transfer to humans (for example herpes B virus in macaques, and others), so contact with primate tissue is a recognised infection risk. ### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The substance is attested in the Explanatory Tantra ch.20 animal-origin list, among the bones, as a drug that expels from the womb; that is a therapeutic status only. The tradition's frame for illness is that the three humours are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, line 51), and medicine is one branch of treatment within that frame; no line read assigns monkey bone a place in any practice of essence extraction (*bcud len*) or any other cultivation scheme. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. Monkey bone is a drug for expelling from the womb in a list of animal bones; the Codex records it and does not recommend it. The restraint is the practice: nothing is gained by pursuing it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy:** the item is recorded as ecbolic and abortifacient; it must never be used in pregnancy, and the Codex does not recommend any use of it to end one.
- **Infection:** primate tissue and bone can carry zoonotic pathogens; handling and ingestion are hazardous.
- **Legal and conservation:** non-human primates are protected in many jurisdictions and their parts are restricted in trade; possession may be a criminal offence.
- **Ethics:** the Codex does not recommend harming any animal for these uses.
