# Jasmine — Common Jasmine, Poet's Jasmine, Su Xin Hua (素馨花), Chameli, Yasmin

## *Jasminum officinale* L. | Family: Oleaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM, SS, SBJ

**Energetic Classification:** Warm and dry, moderately (Galenic-Unani); warm, aromatic, qi-moving (TCM)

**Training Phase:** Harmonising / Aromatic — nervine and mood-lifting support

---

### Origin & Tradition

Common jasmine is a climbing shrub of the olive family, native to the Caucasus, Persia, and the northern Indian subcontinent, and carried westward and eastward along the perfume and trade routes. Its very name is Persian — *yāsamīn* — and it is prized across almost every classical tradition primarily as an **aromatic**: the flower and its absolute/oil, not the bulk herb, carry the medicine.

**Assyrian / Near Eastern (SBJ):** Jasmine belongs to the ancient Persian–Mesopotamian world of aromatic flowers and perfumed oils. Fragrant flower-oils of this type were used in the Near East for anointing, for perfuming, and for their soothing, mood-lifting effect — the earliest register of jasmine as a substance that acts through scent.

**Dioscoridean / European Pharmacy (DMM):** The Greeks and Romans knew jasmine perfume — *oleum sambacinum / iasme* — as a Persian import. Dioscorides notes the jasmine (Persian "white oil") scented oil, remarking that the Persians used it at banquets for its fragrance while cautioning that so warming a perfume did not suit everyone — an early observation of its warming, heady, potentially over-stimulating character.

**Avicennan / Unani (AT):** Jasmine (*yāsamīn*) is a defined simple in the Unani tradition, classified as warm and dry. Its oil (roghan-e-yasmin) is used as a warming nervine and tonic — massaged for cold nervous conditions, applied to the head for certain headaches of cold origin, and valued as an exhilarant that lifts the spirits.

**Shennong / Chinese Materia Medica (CM):** In TCM the jasmine flower — **Su Xin Hua** (素馨花, referring chiefly to *J. officinale/grandiflorum*) — is a warm, aromatic, qi-regulating flower that **moves stagnant Liver qi, harmonises the Stomach, and relieves constrained emotions and pain** from qi stagnation. It is a gentle, fragrant "coursing" herb rather than a strong drug.

**Sushruta / Ayurvedic (SS):** As **Chameli / Jati** (*Jasminum* spp.), jasmine appears in the Ayurvedic materia medica. The flowers and leaves are used for oral ulcers and sore mouth, for skin and eye conditions, and the fragrant oil as a calming, cooling-yet-uplifting aromatic; jasmine is a classic component of Ayurvedic and Indian perfumery and devotional practice.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Jasmine is the Codex's emblem of **medicine carried on breath and scent** — the substance that acts before it is swallowed. Symbolically it is nocturnal, opening and releasing its fragrance at night, and is tied across traditions to love, sleep, and the lifting of the spirit. The doctrine holds that its warmth "unbinds constraint" — the same unbinding the TCM tradition names as moving stagnant Liver qi. The modern pharmacology gives this an honest twist: the oil is measurably **stimulating/activating**, not merely sedative, matching the "exhilarant" and qi-moving readings better than a purely calming one.

### Active Components

The medicine is concentrated in the **flower's volatile oil / absolute**. Key aroma-active constituents of jasmine absolute include **benzyl acetate**, **linalool**, **benzyl benzoate**, **(Z)-jasmone**, **methyl jasmonate**, and the nitrogenous **indole**, together with **cis-jasmone** and phytol derivatives. The plant also contains **secoiridoid glycosides** characteristic of the Oleaceae (jasminin and related secoiridoids) in leaf and flower, plus flavonoids. The jasmonate/jasmone class and indole give jasmine its heady, animalic, night-blooming signature.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica jasmine is a **Harmonising / aromatic nervine**. Its documented action is on **mood and autonomic tone via olfaction and topical absorption**: an uplifting, alerting, mood-elevating effect rather than a straightforward sedative. The Kenetica use-cases are: low mood and flat affect (an uplifting aromatic), emotional constraint and qi-stagnation-type tension (the Su Xin Hua indication), and as an exhilarant/aphrodisiac aromatic in the classical registers. It is used as inhaled absolute, massage oil, and flower infusion/tea.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, controlled human data support jasmine oil as an **activating/stimulating** aromatic rather than a sedative. Hongratanaworakit (2010, *Natural Product Communications*) conducted a placebo-controlled clinical trial of aromatherapy massage with jasmine oil in 40 healthy volunteers: compared with placebo, jasmine oil significantly increased breathing rate, blood oxygen saturation, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure (indicating increased autonomic arousal), and subjects rated themselves as more alert, more vigorous, and less relaxed — the author concludes jasmine oil is stimulating and proposes its use for uplifting mood and relieving depression (PubMed PMID 20184043; no DOI registered in PubMed for this record). This study used jasmine oil from *Jasminum sambac*, a close congener of *J. officinale*, so the finding is indicative for the genus rather than species-exact — stated here honestly. The broader Oleaceae secoiridoid and jasmonate chemistry is well characterised, but rigorous species-specific clinical trials on *J. officinale* itself remain limited.

**Evidence grade: LOW–MODERATE / TRADITIONAL.** One supportive controlled human aromatherapy trial (on a congeneric species), strong and consistent cross-tradition ethnobotany, and well-defined aroma chemistry; species-specific clinical evidence for *J. officinale* is sparse.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy:** Jasmine (flower and oil) is traditionally regarded as an emmenagogue/uterine aromatic and is used in some cultures around labour; **avoid therapeutic/high-dose use in pregnancy** unless supervised [USER: verify].
- **Essential-oil safety:** The absolute is for inhalation/dilute topical use, **not internal ingestion**; always dilute and patch-test — benzyl benzoate/benzyl acetate can sensitise.
- **Overstimulation:** Because the oil is **arousing rather than sedating** (raises blood pressure and breathing rate), use cautiously where stimulation is unwanted — before sleep for sensitive individuals, or in uncontrolled hypertension.
- **Allergy:** Discontinue on any contact dermatitis or respiratory irritation.
- **Headache:** The heady scent can trigger headache or nausea in the scent-sensitive — Dioscorides' ancient caution still holds.
