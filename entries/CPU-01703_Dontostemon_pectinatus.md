# Dontostemon pectinatus — bye.hu.la.phug, Jhew laphuk (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Dontostemon pectinatus, the second of three binomials Clark 1995 prints for bye.hu.la.phug (after Malcolmia africana and before Draba alata), which the extraction row took as its key; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary sets a fourth binomial, Torularia humilis, against the sound-alike name "Jhew laphuk"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under this binomial only by Clark.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. Both Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the line prints three. The extraction row (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 197) lists them in the order "Malcolmia africana (L.) R. Br.; Dontostemon pectinatus; Draba alata" and its note says that "key uses the first"; the spine row, however, carries the second, *Dontostemon pectinatus*. The first name, with its printed authority, has no spine row. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain, and the binomial of this entry is one of three alternatives the text prints. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1111).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads:

"bye.hu.la.phug: Malcolmia africana (L.) R. Br.: Dontostemon pectinatus: Draba alata: [has two names and grows in marshes. It has a narrow, thick green leaf, white flower and grows about fifteen centimeters high; one type tastes like radish. Both superior and inferior types] cure meat poisoning [and indigestion]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the two names, the marsh habitat, the leaf, the white flower, the height, the radish-like taste of one type, the mention of superior and inferior types, and the closing "and indigestion". Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the three Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the clause "cure meat poisoning". The entry prints no English name, no part used and no preparation. Clark's index of Latin names lists "Dontostemon pectinatus", "Draba alata" and "Draba nemorosa" (line 1578) and "Malcolmia africana" (line 1611); the index therefore carries one *Draba* species that the entry itself does not print.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, the likely same name as "Jhew laphuk".* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The text prints the name in one running-text place and in the index, and gives no Latin name there. In chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), line 3493 begins: "Jhew laphuk cures meat poisoning." The index (line 7403) lists "Jhew laphuk 211". The glossary's "J" block (the letter at line 5335) prints "Jhew laphuk" as the sixth of seven phonetic names (line 5340), "byi'u la phug" as the sixth transliteration (line 5342), and *Torularia humilis* as the sixth scientific name (line 5347), against the English "Alpencress" (line 5354); the pairing is by column position only. The neighbouring positions agree with Clark: "Ji tsher" stands fourth against *Xanthium strumarium* and "Cocklebur", as Clark's *byi.tsher* does, and "Jagoepoe" stands first against *Delphinium brunonianum*, which Clark prints for *bya.rgod.spos* (line 1011). The Codex reads "Jhew laphuk" as Clark's *bye.hu.la.phug* from the sound, though the transliterations differ by a vowel ("bye" in Clark, "byi'u" in the glossary), and from the agreement in action ("cure meat poisoning" in Clark, "cures meat poisoning" in Men-Tsee-Khang); the text says neither. The glossary prints neither *Dontostemon pectinatus* nor *Malcolmia africana* nor *Draba alata*. The two Tibetan texts agree on the name and the action, as read, and differ on the species, and the Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Dontostemon*, *Malcolmia*, *Torularia*, *bye.hu.la.phug* and "Jhew laphuk" found no entry on this plant. I_974 (*Xanthium strumarium*) and I_1250 (*Celosia cristata*) quote the same "J" block of the glossary in their prose and are not entries on this drug. ### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Dontostemon*, *Malcolmia*, *Draba* and *Torularia* are genera of the mustard family (Brassicaceae), and that plants of that family are generally known to contain sulphur-bearing glucosinolates, which give mustard and radish their pungency. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained, because its Latin name is not settled. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that cures meat poisoning, with indigestion added in Clark's commentary (line 1111) and "Jhew laphuk cures meat poisoning" in Men-Tsee-Khang (line 3493). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only by Clark's list of alternatives, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary describes the leaf, flower and height and does not say which part is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Tibetan terms ("meat poisoning", "indigestion") are not modern diagnoses or measured outcomes, so no modern finding can be set beside them here. The identity of the drug is uncertain between four binomials in two texts, so a study of one plant would not bear directly on it. It is a general, well-established fact that food poisoning can be dangerous and needs medical assessment.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** No taste class, power or post-digestive taste is printed for this drug in the lines read. Clark's commentary says that "one type tastes like radish" (inside the brackets, line 1111), which is a description of a taste and not a taste class. The name does not appear in the Men-Tsee-Khang taste-group lists of chapter 19 that were read for this entry.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes this drug by what it does: it cures meat poisoning, with indigestion in the commentary. The Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same ("cures meat poisoning"). The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this drug places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the passages cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and the link from this binomial to that framing rests on one of Clark's alternative names.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a meat-poisoning drug in the Tibetan texts, its Latin name is one of several, and there is no preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. The identity is uncertain among several plants, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Poisoning and persistent indigestion need medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
