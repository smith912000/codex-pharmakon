# Land hare — Lagoos chersaios (Dioscorides 2-21)
## *The hare of the land, as against the sea hare of the preceding chapter; the translation glosses it "Lepus timidus — Hare" and the chapter treats its brain, head, "curds" and blood as medicines* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 2-21, *Lagoos chersaios* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 5001; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 211; the book index sends the reader to page 189, "Hare"). The whole chapter was read; it is the second of two "hare" chapters, following 2-20 *Lagoos thalassios*, the sea hare (line 4997), which is a different animal and is described there as "similar to the little cuttlefish" and used to remove hair. "Chersaios" ("of the land") is what distinguishes the two in the headings. The translator's suggested name is printed on line 5005 as "Lepus timidus — Hare"; that is a gloss supplied by the translation, and the chapter itself says only "land hare". I did not check the gloss against the Greek, and the species Dioscorides meant is not established by the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

The chapter reads (lines 5006-5010; a page break and the number 189 intervene in the OCR):

"The brain of a land hare (eaten roasted) is good for the trembling that comes from fear, as well as rubbed on or eaten for teething in children. The head burnt and rubbed on with bears' grease or vinegar cures baldness. The curds (taken in a drink three days after the menstrual flow) are reported to cause sterility. Likewise it stops excessive discharges of the womb and bowels. It helps those with falling sickness, and taken as a drink with vinegar it is good against poisons [antidote], especially for curdling of the milk [while breastfeeding], and for the bites of vipers. The blood rubbed on while warm cures sunspots, vitiligines [form of leprosy], and freckles."

Four parts of the animal are used: the brain, the head, the "curds" and the blood. The translation does not say what the "curds" are; "likewise it stops..." leaves it unclear whether "it" means the curds or the animal. The use that Dioscorides marks as hearsay, "reported to cause sterility", is the curds taken after the menstrual flow.

**A separate chapter that is relevant.** The rennet chapter 2-85 *Pitua* (line 5289) gives "Thirty grains of rennet of hare (taken in a drink of wine)" for venomous bites, coeliac complaints, dysentery, excessive womb discharges, clotting blood and throwing blood up out of the chest, and says that applied to the vulva with butter after the monthly cleansing it "causes inconception" (line 5291). That chapter is about rennet, and the text does not say that the "curds" of 2-21 and the "rennet" of 2-85 are the same material. It is cited here only because it contains the one dose in the sources for any hare product. It cannot be read as the dose for the products of 2-21. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None can be stated from the text. In general terms, hare brain is lipid-rich nervous tissue; hare blood contains haemoglobin and plasma proteins; "curds" or rennet in a young mammal's stomach contains the milk-clotting enzyme chymosin. These are general statements about the tissues, not readings of the chapter.

---

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The chapter records four hare parts for trembling from fear, teething, baldness, womb and bowel discharge, epilepsy, poisons, viper bite and skin patches, and one reported effect on fertility. Several of those uses concern conditions that require medical care (epilepsy, poisoning, snakebite), and the fertility claim is explicitly reported hearsay. No modern protocol is supplied.

---

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations the chapter gives: the brain eaten roasted, or rubbed on; the head burnt and rubbed on with bears' grease or vinegar; the curds taken in a drink, "three days after the menstrual flow", or taken as a drink with vinegar; the blood rubbed on while warm. No quantity is given for any of them in 2-21. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] As general facts, wild hares can carry tularemia, a bacterial infection transmitted by handling infected animals, and other zoonoses; and no hare product is an established treatment for epilepsy, baldness, snakebite or infertility.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. The chapter is a list of therapeutic uses for four parts of the animal. What the herbal offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, adulteration detection), and this chapter does not give it: it does not say how the animal is to be taken, from where, or how to test the product. No cultivation status is attested, and the note that the sterility effect is "reported" shows the author recording a claim at a distance, not endorsing it.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. The chapter is a record of what was reported of the hare's brain, head, "curds" and blood; there is no instruction a cultivator can follow and no dose for any of these parts. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Zoonotic infection:** handling wild hares, their blood and brain, carries the risk of tularemia and other infections; raw or lightly cooked brain carries additional risk of infectious agents.
- **Fertility and pregnancy:** the sterility claim is reported hearsay in the text; nothing here should be relied on as contraception, and an effect on pregnancy cannot be excluded.
- **Serious conditions:** epilepsy, poisoning and snakebite need emergency or medical care.
- **Wildlife law:** hares are subject to hunting seasons and licensing in most countries; the law in any given place was not retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** use of the animal involves killing it.
