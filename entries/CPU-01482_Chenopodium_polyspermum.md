# Chenopodium polyspermum — Bliton (Dioscorides 2-143)

## *Chenopodium polyspermum (one of the translators' suggestions; "Blite, Wild Amaranth")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Identity doubt.** The SUGGESTED line (lines 5735-5737) reads "Blitum [Fuchs], Bliti genus, Chenopodium polyspermum [Linnaeus], Amaranthus blitum, Albersia blitum — Blite, Wild Amaranth", then "[other usage] Blitum virgatum — Strawberry Spinach". The row takes the goosefoot; the same line equally offers *Amaranthus blitum*, a different genus. The chapter has no description from which to decide. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *2-143. BLITON* at line 5733, text at line 5738.

The whole chapter reads: "Blitum is also eaten as a vegetable, having no use medicinally. The Egyptians call it eclotoripam, others, riplam, the Latins, blitum, and the Dacians, bles."

It is a one-sentence chapter. Dioscorides says the plant is eaten as a vegetable and expressly that it has no medicinal use. He gives no description, habitat, preparation or dose, and four names in four tongues (Egyptian *eclotoripam*, "others" *riplam*, Latin *blitum*, Dacian *bles*). The chapter belongs to Book Two's vegetables. It follows 2-142 Lampsane (common nipplewort) and is followed by a plate and 2-144 Malache agria, malache kepaia (heading at line 5752).

**Overlap.** No entry is built on this chapter. I_436 Amaranth (*Amaranthus hybridus*) cites *bliton* (blite, *Amaranthus blitum*) in its Dioscorides paragraph, and I_450 Lamb's Quarters (*Chenopodium album*, which is 2-145) groups the blites with oraches. Both entries describe the greens as "cooling", but 2-143 as read prints no such quality and says only "eaten as a vegetable, having no use medicinally". This is flagged as a possible data fix for those entries, not changed here.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides names were not read. Many leafy goosefoot and amaranth greens are generally known to contain oxalates, which is a general statement about the group and not about this species. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The record here is that Dioscorides knew the plant as a vegetable and denied it a medicinal use. There is nothing to apply. The Codex gives no protocol, and the identification is uncertain between two genera.

### Preparation & Dosing

2-143 gives eating it as a vegetable and nothing about preparation. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter is a single sentence and its only distinct content is a negative: the plant is a vegetable "having no use medicinally". That is itself a discipline of attention, recording what a substance is not for, but it assigns the plant no cultivation status. The identification is the translators' and split between two genera. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is, in Dioscorides' own words, food and not medicine.

### Contraindications

The chapter says nothing of harm. As a general matter, a wild green should be identified with certainty before eating, and oxalate-rich greens are unsuitable in large amounts for people prone to kidney stones. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
