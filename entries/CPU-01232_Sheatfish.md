# Sheatfish (catfish) — *Ailouros* (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 2-29), the *silurus*
## *"Silurus glanis, Siluridae — Sheath Fish", the translator's suggested identification; the text itself calls the fish "the silurus"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica*, in the 2000 English edition (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`; "text" below). The chapter is 2-29, headed *AILOUROS*, at line 5051 (the spine's locus, `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`: "2-29 (line 5051)", translator's identification "Silurus glanis, Siluridae — Sheath Fish", class animal). The edition prints the identification under "SUGGESTED" (line 5053), so it is the translator's proposal. The English "sheatfish" is the older name of the European catfish *Silurus glanis*, and the spine's "(catfish)" follows it. The Greek heading *ailouros* is a different word from the *silurus* the body of the chapter uses; the chapter does not explain the difference, and the index lists "Sheath Fish 194" (lines 17540, 17633). [USER: verify]

The whole chapter, line 5054, reads: "The silurus eaten whilst it is fresh is nourishing and good for the stomach, but salted it yields no nourishment; yet it cleans the arteries and makes the voice clear. The flesh of that which is salted draws out splinters. Brine from it is good in baths for dysentery at the first sign, drawing the discharges to the outside. Taken as a suppository it cures sciatica." The chapter separates the fish by its state. Fresh, it is "nourishing and good for the stomach". Salted, "it yields no nourishment" but "cleans the arteries and makes the voice clear", and the salted flesh "draws out splinters". The brine from it is used in baths "for dysentery at the first sign", and "taken as a suppository" it is said to cure sciatica; the text does not say what the suppository is made of, and the nearest antecedent is the brine.

The chapters around it keep salted fish together. Chapter 2-30 (*Smaris*, the smelt, line 5059) says "the flesh of this as well as all salted meat is good for one touched by a scorpion, and for one bitten by a dog", and burns the salted head for growths of flesh. Chapter 2-33 (*Omotarichos*, salted tunny, line 5069) is a salted fish by definition, and chapter 2-34 (*Garron*, "Garum — Liquid from Salt Fish", line 5076) is the liquid that comes out of salted fish, again used for dog bites and for dysentery and sciatica as a suppository (line 5078). The sheatfish brine and garum share the dysentery and sciatica uses.

An English "sheat-fish" also appears in Bhishagratna's Sushruta in an alphabetical glossary of animal names at the end of Volume 3 (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`, line 30582: "A huge sheat-fish."). The OCR separates the glossary's Sanskrit names from their English definitions, so the Sanskrit name that carries this definition is not recoverable from the lines read, and no medicinal use of the fish is attached. It is a glossary row, not an attestation of the substance in the Sushruta, and it is not treated as a second tradition here. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Freshwater catfish are an oily and protein-rich fish; salting draws water out of the flesh and leaves a high concentration of sodium chloride. Brine is a strong salt solution. These are general facts. The chapter names no constituent and this entry names none.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The recorded uses are a nourishing food when fresh, a splinter-drawing poultice of salted flesh, a bath of the brine for early dysentery and a suppository for sciatica. Dysentery is an infection that needs rehydration and medical care, and a brine suppository is not a treatment for sciatica. The Codex's application layer has nothing to add, and no protocol is offered.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives the preparations as the fresh fish eaten; the salted flesh applied; and the brine used in baths or as a suppository (line 5054). No quantity, strength of brine, bath volume or number of applications is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The only general facts offered are compositional (above). A strong salt solution is a hypertonic irritant to the mucous membranes, and nothing here supports its use in the bowel.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides.** No quality in the Galenic sense (warming, astringent, and so on) is printed for the silurus in chapter 2-29. The text distinguishes only nourishing (fresh) from non-nourishing (salted), and that is a statement of use, not a classification of quality; none is supplied here.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. Chapter 2-29 distinguishes the fresh fish from the salted and gives uses of each. That is a small piece of the attention to the state of a material that the work values, but it records no provenance, season or assay, and it assigns no status to the person taking the remedy.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing in the source for a cultivator to use. The nourishing food in the chapter is the fresh fish, which is simply a meal, and everything else is a salted-fish remedy for a complaint. The one thing the chapter models is the habit of saying which form of a material is meant.

### Contraindications

Salted fish and brine carry a high sodium load and are unsuitable for anyone on a salt-restricted diet. A brine suppository would irritate the bowel and is not advised. Dysentery and sciatica need medical assessment. Freshwater fish carry parasites if eaten raw or undercooked, and the European wels catfish, the species the translator suggests, is a large predatory fish. These are general cautions and not claims from the text.
