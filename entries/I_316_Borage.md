# I-316: Borago officinalis (Borage)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-316 | Boraginaceae**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Borago officinalis* L.
**Family:** Boraginaceae

**Common Names:**
- English: Borage, Starflower, Bugloss (sometimes confused)
- German: *Borretsch*, *Gurkenkraut* ("cucumber herb" — for its cucumber-like taste)
- French: *Bourrache*, *Langue de boeuf* ("ox tongue" — for rough hairy leaves)
- Spanish: *Borraja*
- Arabic: *Lissān al-Thawr* (لسان الثور) — "ox tongue" — identical metaphor to French, separately derived
- Latin: *Borago* — etymology disputed: possibly from Arabic *abū 'araq* (father of sweat — diaphoretic) or Latin *borra* (rough hair — for the plant's bristly texture)

**Etymology:**
The Arabic-to-Latin etymology of *Borago* from *abū 'araq* ("father of sweat") would encode the primary traditional diaphoretic use in the genus name — a pathway consistent with the large Islamic pharmacological influence on medieval European botanical nomenclature. The alternative Latin *borra* (rough hair) etymology is morphological. The "cucumber herb" (*Gurkenkraut*) and "ox tongue" names in multiple languages independently capture the flavor profile and leaf texture respectively.

**The Courage Herb:**
Classical and medieval texts consistently documented borage as a courage-inducing herb — Pliny wrote *ego borago gaudia semper ago* ("I, borage, always bring joy"). Francis Bacon noted borage for "the comforting of the heart and driving away of sorrow." John Gerard (1597 *Herball*): "Those of our time do use the flowers in salads to exhilarate and make the mind glad." The neuromodulatory reputation reflects a genuine GLA + triterpenoid mechanism that may have mild HPA-axis effects, though no RCTs validate the "courage" claim specifically.

**Morphology:**
Annual or biennial; 30–60 cm; rough bristly-hairy throughout; vivid sky-blue star-shaped flowers (5-petaled; white in rare forms); cucumber-flavored; Mediterranean origin; naturalized worldwide.

**Parts Used:**
- **Seed oil (primary pharmacological):** Highest GLA content of any common plant oil (18–25% GLA)
- **Aerial parts / leaves:** Diuretic; diaphoretic; adrenal tonic (traditional); lower PA content
- **Flowers:** Edible; used in drinks, salads (crystallized flowers); cucumber flavor

**GLA — The Modern Pharmacological Anchor:**
Borage's contemporary clinical significance rests entirely on its seed oil's gamma-linolenic acid (GLA) content — a pharmacological dimension completely unknown to classical traditions. The traditional "heart" and "melancholy" uses encoded something real (adrenal + neuroendocrine modulation) without the mechanism. Modern pharmacology discovered GLA → DGLA → PGE1 (prostaglandin E1) — a genuinely anti-inflammatory and cardiovascular pathway — independently of the traditional documentation.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domains:**

1. **GLA-mediated anti-inflammatory** — seed oil (modern primary); DGLA → PGE1 mechanism
2. **Adrenal tonic / adaptogenic** — European folk; "courage" tradition; triterpenoid HPA modulation
3. **Diaphoretic / fever** — CM, DMM, AT; traditional primary; flavonoid mechanism
4. **Cardiac tonic / mood** — CM, DMM; courage tradition; serotonergic mechanisms speculated
5. **Diuretic** — CM, European folk; flavonoid mechanism

**GLA Hierarchy Among Plant Oils:**
The three primary plant sources of gamma-linolenic acid, in descending GLA concentration:
1. **Blackcurrant seed oil:** 15–20% GLA
2. **Borage seed oil:** 18–25% GLA — **highest of commonly available sources**
3. **Evening primrose oil (EPO):** 8–12% GLA — **most RCT evidence of the three**

Borage has the highest GLA content but *less* clinical evidence than EPO for most indications; EPO has more RCTs because it was studied earlier and more extensively. For GLA-dependent indications (atopic dermatitis, PMS, RA), the three oils are mechanistically interchangeable but differ in dose required per gram of oil.

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Seed Oil — Primary Pharmacological

**1. Gamma-Linolenic Acid (GLA; C18:3 omega-6) — 18–25% seed oil — PRIMARY compound**
- **Metabolic cascade:** GLA → elongase → DGLA (dihomo-GLA) → delta-5-desaturase → arachidonic acid (pro-inflammatory route) OR → COX → PGE1 (anti-inflammatory; vasodilatory; immunomodulatory route)
- **Net effect at physiological GLA doses:** PGE1 pathway dominant → anti-inflammatory; reduced platelet aggregation; vasodilation
- **Atopic dermatitis mechanism:** Restores GLA deficit in atopic skin (atopics have deficient delta-6-desaturase → impaired GLA synthesis → GLA supplementation bypasses the defect)

**2. Linoleic Acid (C18:2 omega-6) — 35–40% seed oil** — essential fatty acid; substrate for GLA synthesis

**3. Oleic Acid (C18:1 omega-9) — 15–20% seed oil** — monounsaturated; anti-inflammatory

### Aerial Parts

**4. Pyrrolizidine Alkaloids — LOW but present**
- Amabiline, Thesinine, Supinine — LOW PA content (significantly lower than comfrey I-315)
- CONCERN at high doses of leaf/aerial part extracts; seed oil PA-free
- **Seed oil does NOT contain PAs** (PAs are polar alkaloids; not extracted into lipid oil phase)

**5. Rosmarinic Acid** — anti-inflammatory; shared Boraginaceae class compound

**6. Alantoin** — minor in aerial parts; wound healing (lower than comfrey)

**7. Mucilage** — demulcent; traditional cough/respiratory use

### Flowers

**8. Cyanogenic glucosides (trace)** — negligible at food doses
**9. Anthocyanins (blue flowers)** — delphinidin-based; antioxidant

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### Anti-Inflammatory (GLA — Seed Oil)
- **GLA → DGLA** (via elongase; not delta-6-desaturase — bypasses the atopic deficit)
- **DGLA → PGE1** (via COX) → vasodilation; anti-platelet; immune modulation
- **DGLA also inhibits delta-5-desaturase** → reduces arachidonic acid formation → less PGE2/LTB4 (pro-inflammatory)
- Net: GLA supplementation shifts eicosanoid balance away from pro-inflammatory toward PGE1-mediated anti-inflammatory signaling

### Skin Barrier (GLA — Atopic Dermatitis)
- Atopics have impaired delta-6-desaturase → reduced endogenous GLA → poor skin barrier ceramide synthesis
- **Oral GLA bypasses delta-6-desaturase deficit** → normalized DGLA → improved ceramide → restored skin barrier
- Demonstrated in RCTs for both oral and topical GLA

### Adrenal (Triterpenoids / Speculation)
- Traditional "courage" and adrenal tonic use: triterpenoid compounds have HPA-axis modulating effects in animal models
- Human data: minimal; mechanism plausible but not validated

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Atopic Dermatitis (Seed Oil GLA):**
- Multiple RCTs using borage seed oil for atopic dermatitis; results mixed
- Takwale et al. 2003 (*BMJ*): n=151; borage oil NOT significantly better than placebo for atopic dermatitis
- Meta-analysis (Bamford 2013, Cochrane): "evidence does not support the use of EPO or borage oil for atopic eczema" — primary analysis
- **BUT:** Some subgroup analyses suggest benefit in patients with documented delta-6-desaturase deficiency
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (multiple RCTs; Cochrane review — direction mixed/negative for borage specifically)

**Rheumatoid Arthritis:**
- Zurier et al. 1996 (*Arthritis & Rheumatism*): RCT; borage seed oil (1.4 g GLA/day) vs. placebo; significant improvement in RA symptoms at 24 weeks
- Additional RCTs supporting GLA for RA
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (positive RCTs)

**PMS / Hormonal:**
- GLA → PGE1 → anti-inflammatory prostaglandin effect on uterus; menstrual pain reduction
- Small trials; less evidence than EPO for PMS specifically
- **Evidence Grade: Level C**

**Respiratory / Diaphoretic:**
- Commission E (Germany): approved for "cough and bronchitis, acute mild inflammation of urinary tract" (Commission E 1990; partly withdrawn 2004 for PA concerns in aerial parts)
- **Evidence Grade: Level C** (traditional)

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The Blue Flower:**
Borage produces one of the most vivid sky-blue flowers in the temperate garden — a color so saturated and distinctly floral-blue that it was prized in herbal illustrations (Fuchs, Gerard, Dodoens all featured borage prominently for visual as well as therapeutic reasons). The blue comes from delphinidin anthocyanins, the same pigment class as delphiniums and some cornflowers. The flowers are edible, taste of cucumber, and were crystallized in sugar as Elizabethan confections. The contrast between the rough, intimidatingly bristly grey-green leaves and the delicate, sky-blue star flowers creates one of the most aesthetically striking plant personalities in the herbal garden.

**The Courage Tradition:**
The consistent cross-cultural association of borage with courage and cardiac comfort — *ego borago gaudia semper ago* (Pliny), *Borage comforts the heart* (Dioscorides), the *Ox Tongue* association with strength — may reflect genuine but mild pharmacological effects. GLA → PGE1 has vasodilatory and anti-anxiety effects in some models. The triterpenoid compounds have mild adrenal-modulatory effects in animals. The adrenal cortex produces cortisol from cholesterol; triterpenoid structural similarity may explain a mild HPA-axis interaction. Whether any of this reaches clinical significance for "courage" remains unvalidated — but the convergent cultural observation of mood-lifting effects suggests something genuine, however weak.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Seed Oil (Primary GLA Source):**
- **Atopic dermatitis:** 3–5 g borage seed oil daily (providing ~600–1,000 mg GLA); 3–6 months trial minimum
- **Rheumatoid arthritis:** 1.4–2.8 g GLA/day (corresponding to ~7–14 g borage oil); 6–12 months
- Seed oil is PA-free — no PA concern with seed oil use

**Aerial Parts / Leaf (Traditional — with PA caution):**
- Tea: 2 g dried herb per 250 mL water; 3× daily — SHORT-TERM only (weeks, not months)
- PA content: low but present; limit duration; AVOID internal leaf/aerial preparations in pregnancy and liver disease

**Flowers (Culinary):**
- Fresh flowers in salads, drinks, frozen in ice cubes; crystallized confections
- PA-negligible at food doses; entirely safe

### Safety Profile

**Seed Oil: Safe.** PA-free; no significant toxicity at therapeutic doses.

**Aerial Parts: PA concern — limit duration.**
- PAs (amabiline, thesinine) present but at much lower concentration than comfrey
- EFSA 2017 opinion: borage aerial part PAs require limitation
- Maximum 1 μg PA/day from botanical sources (EFSA); borage leaf teas may approach this
- AVOID prolonged internal leaf/herb use
- AVOID aerial parts in pregnancy

**GLA + Anticoagulants:**
- GLA → PGE1 → anti-platelet effect; additive with aspirin, NSAIDs, warfarin
- Monitor INR with high-dose borage oil + warfarin

**Phenothiazines / Epilepsy:**
- GLA may lower seizure threshold in patients on phenothiazines (theoretical; case reports for EPO)
- Caution in epilepsy; avoid with phenothiazine antipsychotics

**Contraindications:**
- Pregnancy: PA in aerial parts; AVOID aerial part preparations; seed oil generally considered safe
- Active liver disease: PA caution (aerial parts only)
- Epilepsy on phenothiazines: GLA risk
- Anticoagulant therapy: monitor
