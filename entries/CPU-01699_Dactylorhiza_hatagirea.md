# Dactylorhiza hatagirea — Wangpo lagpa, dbang po lag pa (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Dactylorhiza hatagirea, as the binomial printed on the same line as "Wangpo lagpa dbang po lag pa" in the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary; Clark 1995 prints the Tibetan name as dwang.po.lag.pa, under the English "a type of Salep Orchid", with a different binomial, Orchis incarnata Linn.* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under this binomial only by the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The extraction note reads: "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 389). In the lines read for this entry the glossary does print the name beside the binomial, on one line (below), so the note's "not paired" does not describe this row. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (line 6026).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "W" block (the letter at line 6024). Line 6026 reads: "Wangpo lagpa dbang po lag pa Dactylorhiza hatagirea". The phonetic name, the transliteration and the binomial are on one line. The English-name run in this part of the glossary prints "Dactylorhiza" (line 6048), then "Coriander", "Guinea pepper", "Figwort" and "Turmeric" for the names that follow in the block, so the glossary's English column gives the genus only. The next row, "Wosu / 'u su / Coriandrum sativum", begins at line 6028.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text on "Wangpo lagpa".* The name is printed once in the running text and once in the index. In chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), line 3495 reads: "Wangpo lagpa develops physical strength and increases seminal fluid." The same line goes on to other drugs: "Ri sho and Chang tsher withdraws tripa and baekan disorders respectively." The index (line 7561) lists "Wangpo lagpa 211". A search of the whole Men-Tsee-Khang file for "Wangpo" found no other line about the drug (the other hits are personal names and a book title in the history chapter, lines 506, 587 and 588, and a disease-term list at line 6447, "Wangpoi nae", "Disorders of senseorgans"). The name is not in the taste lists of chapter 19 and not in the compounding groups of chapter 21.

*Clark 1995, the same Tibetan name under another binomial.* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997), line 1130:

"dwang.po.lag.pa: a type of Salep Orchid: Orchis incarnata Linn.: [has twelve names, for example brgya.bying, etc., and grows in meadows and near springs. Its root resembles a man's hand (i.e., with five-fingered tubers), and the flowers are yellowish-blue. It has a green leaf and petiole and an inflorescence resembling the layered structure of a stupa. The six-fingered type is known as tsogs.bdag (Ganesh), whilst the five-fingered type is known as sbang.lag (Indrahanta), the fourfingered type as dri.zahi.lag.pa (Gandhamadna) and the single tuber as thehu.rang. The common type has a yellow flower, and all types having three or more tubers] are tonic, aphrodisiac [and collect poison]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the twelve names, the habitat, the root, the flowers, the types and their names, and the closing "and collect poison". Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the English name, the binomial, which is Clark's identification and not the root text's, and the words "are tonic, aphrodisiac". The line prints no taste, no part used and no preparation, and the drug is named *Orchis incarnata*, which is a different binomial from the glossary's. The Codex reads Clark's *dwang.po.lag.pa* and the glossary's "dbang po lag pa" as one drug from the near-identical spelling, from the English "Salep Orchid" against the glossary's English "Dactylorhiza", and from the agreement in kind between "tonic, aphrodisiac" and "develops physical strength and increases seminal fluid"; neither text says so. The two Tibetan texts agree on the name and the kind of action and differ on the species, and the Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Dactylorhiza*, "hatagirea", "Wangpo lagpa" and *Orchis incarnata* found no entry on this plant. I_073 (*Codonopsis pilosula*, line 105) says, with no locus, that *dbang lag* (*wang lag*) "in Sowa Rigpa standardly denotes the salep orchid (*Dactylorhiza hatagirea*)", and uses that to doubt a Codonopsis identification; the claim is that entry's, was not read as a source here, and rests here on the glossary line above instead. I_973 (*Zanthoxylum armatum*, line 16) quotes the "W" to "Z" English run of the glossary, in which "Dactylorhiza" is printed, and is not an entry on this plant. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Dactylorhiza* is a genus of terrestrial orchids (Orchidaceae) that grow from paired or lobed underground tubers, and that dried orchid tubers, the material called salep, are generally known to be rich in a mucilaginous polysaccharide. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Men-Tsee-Khang text records that "Wangpo lagpa develops physical strength and increases seminal fluid" (line 3495). Clark's line for the same Tibetan name, under another binomial, records that the drug is "tonic, aphrodisiac" (line 1130). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial by a spelling match, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary describes the tubers, and the root is the part it describes, but no line says which part is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Both Tibetan texts class the drug by what it does: it develops physical strength and increases seminal fluid (Men-Tsee-Khang), and it is a tonic and aphrodisiac (Clark, for the same name under another binomial). That is a strengthening action inside the materia medica, and the cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the passages cited above were read in full and none mentions one, and the name is not printed in the Men-Tsee-Khang text outside the lines cited. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a strength-and-seminal-fluid drug in the Men-Tsee-Khang text and a "tonic, aphrodisiac" in Clark, with no preparation and no dose, and the two texts print different binomials for the name. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. The identity is uncertain between two binomials, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. It is a general fact that all wild orchids are covered by CITES Appendix II, which regulates trade, and gathering wild orchid tubers can harm populations; the legal and conservation status of this species was not checked in the sources.
