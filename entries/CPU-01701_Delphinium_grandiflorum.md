# Delphinium grandiflorum — bya.rkang, Jhakang, "Larkspur" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Delphinium grandiflorum, the first of two binomials Clark 1995 prints for bya.rkang under the English "Larkspur" (with Delphinium viscosum); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary sets a different binomial, Delphinium himalayi, against the same Tibetan name* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under this binomial only by Clark.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. Both Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the line prints two, of which the row takes the first (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 207). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (lines 1125 to 1128).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). A page marker ("111", line 1126) interrupts the entry. It reads:

"bya.rkang (lit. 'bird's foot,' referring to the shape of the leaf): Larkspur: Delphinium grandiflorum: Delphinium viscosum: [has nine names, for example, ti.mu.sa, chhe.war.lo.btsan, rma.lo.rkang.gchig and sngon.mo.dar.ya.kan. It has a tall thin stalk, a slightly blue leaf, a reddish-blue flower shaped like the head of a hoopoe bird, and long, thin, green petioles. Larkspur has slightly bitter taste, halts dysentery and] is beneficial against external parasites, lice, etc. Plantago, Polygonum alopecuroides and larkspur halt diarrhea."

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the nine names, the stalk, leaf, flower and petioles, the "slightly bitter taste" and "halts dysentery". Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name with its gloss, the English name, the two Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the clauses "is beneficial against external parasites, lice, etc." and "Plantago, Polygonum alopecuroides and larkspur halt diarrhea". The last sentence names the two entries just before it in the run (line 1123, *tha.ram*, *Plantago depressa*; line 1124, *na.ram*, *Polygonum alopecuroides*).

*Clark 1995, chapter 21.* Under "21. The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines" (heading at line 1205), line 1222 reads: "The class of anti-diarrhoeals consists of both wild and cultivated types of Bael fruit, Schizandra sphaerandra, Acorn, powdered root (of, for example, old pine tree), Plantain (Plantago depressa), Plantago lanceolata, Bastard Teak, the blood of Hodgson's antelope and Larkspur (Delphinium grandiflora Linn.)". The binomial is printed here as "grandiflora". Clark's index of Latin names lists "Delphinium grandiflorum" and "Delphinium viscosum" (line 1578).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, the same Tibetan name as "Jhakang".* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The text prints the name in two places and in the index, and gives no Latin name in either place. In chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), line 3493 ends: "Tharam, Naram and Jhakang stops diarrhea." In chapter 21 (contents, line 229: "The Group and the Method of Compounding Medicine", from page 219), lines 3644 to 3646 read: "Kapae, Bilwa, Dhatig, Moen chhara, Mak, Tharam, Naram, Gya kyeg, antelope's blood and Jhakang are the group of medicines that cure diarrhea." The index (line 7403) lists "Jhakang 211,222". The Codex reads "Jhakang" as Clark's *bya.rkang* from the order, which is the same as Clark's (*tha.ram*, *na.ram*, then larkspur; and "antelope's blood" before "Jhakang" as in Clark's "the blood of Hodgson's antelope and Larkspur"), and from the action (diarrhoea); neither text says so. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary.* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "J" block (the letter at line 5335), prints "Jagoepoe Jakang" (line 5337) among seven phonetic names, the transliteration "bya rgod spos bya rkang byi rug ..." (line 5342), and a scientific run beginning "Delphinium brunonianum Delphinium himalayi Munz. Mentha longifolia" (line 5344) with the English "Larkspur Larkspur" (line 5350). Counting in order, "Jakang" is the second phonetic name and "bya rkang" the second transliteration, which is spelled as Clark spells *bya.rkang*, and the second scientific name is *Delphinium himalayi*. The glossary therefore sets *Delphinium himalayi*, and not *Delphinium grandiflorum*, against this Tibetan name, by column position only. It spells the name "Jakang", where the running text has "Jhakang". The two Tibetan texts agree on the name and the action and differ on the species, and the Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Delphinium* is a genus of the buttercup family (Ranunculaceae) and that larkspurs are generally known to contain toxic diterpenoid alkaloids. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained, because its Latin name is not settled. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a slightly bitter drug that halts dysentery and diarrhoea and is "beneficial against external parasites, lice, etc." (Clark, lines 1125 to 1128), and that "stops diarrhea" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3493). These are the texts' claims and not Codex protocols, and the genus is toxic. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary describes the stalk, leaf and flower, and no line says which part is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Tibetan terms (dysentery, diarrhoea, external parasites) are named conditions and not measured outcomes in these texts, so no modern finding can be set beside them here. The identity of the drug is uncertain between *Delphinium grandiflorum*, *Delphinium viscosum* and, in the glossary, *Delphinium himalayi*, so a study of one species would not bear directly on it. It is a general, well-established fact that larkspurs are poisonous to grazing animals and to people who eat them.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** "slightly bitter taste" (Clark, line 1125 to 1128, inside the brackets). No power (hot or cool) and no post-digestive taste is printed for this drug in the lines read, and the name does not appear in the Men-Tsee-Khang taste-group lists of chapter 19.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes this drug by taste (slightly bitter) and by what it does: it halts dysentery and diarrhoea and is beneficial against external parasites. It stands in the class of anti-diarrhoeals in chapter 21 (Clark line 1222), and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same ("stops diarrhea"; "the group of medicines that cure diarrhea"). The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this drug places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the passages cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is an anti-diarrhoeal and anti-parasite drug in the Tibetan texts, the genus is toxic, its Latin name is not settled, and there is no preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. *Delphinium* is generally known to be poisonous, the identity is uncertain, and no preparation or dose is given, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Dysentery and persistent diarrhoea need medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
