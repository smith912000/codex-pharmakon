### Origin & Tradition

Pitch — the dark, viscous, resinous distillate of pine wood — occupies an unusual position in the ancient pharmacopeia: it is less a plant than a *process*, a substance produced by subjecting the resin-laden heartwood and bark of pines to controlled heat in the near-absence of oxygen. The black pine, *Pinus nigra*, and its close relatives across the Mediterranean basin (notably the Calabrian pine, *Pinus brutia*, and the Aleppo pine, *Pinus halepensis*) were the principal source trees for this craft in the classical world. Dioscorides treats the pines and their products at length in Book I of *De Materia Medica*, the book devoted to aromatics, oils, resins, and the trees that yield them. He distinguishes carefully between the fresh liquid resin (*rhetine*), the solid resin, the pitch obtained by burning (*pissa*), and the still more refined product, *pissa hygra* — the "wet pitch" or wood-tar that drips from heated billets — as well as the soot (*lignys*) collected from the burning, each with its own medical applications.

The production of pitch was one of the great industrial crafts of antiquity. Resinous pine logs were stacked in earthen kilns or pits, covered, and slowly smouldered; the heat drove the volatile and semi-volatile fractions of the wood and resin out as a dark liquid that was collected and concentrated. The product was indispensable far beyond medicine: pitch caulked the hulls and waterproofed the rigging of the Mediterranean fleets, sealed wine amphorae and the great *dolia* of the storehouses, and the resinous lining of wine vessels gave the Greek world its enduring taste for retsina. That a single craft served both shipwright and physician is characteristic of the ancient material world, in which the boundary between technical and therapeutic knowledge was porous.

In the medical sphere, pitch and the related resins were valued above all as agents of *closure* and *drawing*. Dioscorides and the later Galenic tradition assign pitch a warming, drying, and softening (maturating) action: it was spread on wounds and ulcers to draw them together, applied to indolent sores to stimulate them, compounded into plasters and salves for skin eruptions, and used in fumigations and pessaries. The smoke of burning pitch and resin was directed into the chest and lungs in respiratory complaints, and the soot was made into eye-salves. The astringent, antiseptic character that empirical practice attributed to these preparations is consonant with the chemistry of pine tar, which is rich in phenolic constituents.

This lineage runs unbroken into the modern era. The Scandinavian wood-tar industry — "Stockholm tar" — was for centuries a strategic export for naval stores, and pine tar persisted in official pharmacopeias well into the twentieth century as *Pix liquida*, the active of tar soaps and the classic tar ointments for chronic, scaling skin disease. The black pine itself, hardy and drought-tolerant, became one of the most widely planted reforestation conifers of southern Europe, so that the tree, the craft, and the medicine have travelled together from Dioscorides' Aegean to the present.

### Active Components

- **Abietic acid and related diterpene resin acids** (abietane-type diterpenoids): the dominant acids of pine rosin/colophony; contribute the tacky, adhesive, film-forming character of resin and pitch; mildly irritant and sensitizing; the chemical basis of the "drawing" and sealing action of resin plasters.
- **Pimaric acid and isopimaric acid** (pimarane diterpene resin acids): structural companions of abietic acid in the resin acid fraction; contribute to the antimicrobial character attributed to conifer resins.
- **Phenolic constituents of wood tar** (guaiacol, cresols, and related pyrolysis phenols): generated by the dry distillation of the lignified wood; the principal contributors to pine tar's antiseptic, antipruritic, and keratolytic activity on skin; responsible for the characteristic smoky odour.
- **Monoterpenes — α-pinene, β-pinene, Δ3-carene** (bicyclic and related monoterpenes of turpentine): the volatile fraction; rubefacient and counter-irritant when applied to skin; expectorant and mucolytic when inhaled, loosening bronchial secretions; the basis of turpentine's traditional chest-rub use.
- **Longifolene and sesquiterpene fraction** (sesquiterpene hydrocarbons): minor aromatic constituents of the heavier resin.
- **Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)** (pyrolysis products): formed during high-temperature charring; a genuine toxicological concern of crude wood tars and a reason modern dermatological use is controlled and refined (see Contraindications).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. **Topical wound closure and chronic ulcer support** — The classical and through-line indication: pitch and resin preparations applied to slow-healing wounds, fissures, and indolent ulcers as a sealing, drawing, mildly antiseptic dressing. The resin acids form an occlusive, adhesive film while the phenolic and terpene fractions discourage microbial overgrowth. In the Kenetica frame this is a surface-level (exterior, Qi-circulation) intervention — consolidating a breach and re-establishing the integrity of the boundary rather than acting on deep pathology.

2. **Chronic scaling skin disease — keratolytic and antipruritic** — Refined pine tar (*Pix liquida*) is the historical archetype of the tar dermatologicals used for psoriasis, chronic eczema, and seborrhoeic conditions; the phenolic fraction reduces epidermal proliferation and relieves itch. This is the best-attested modern survival of the tradition and is the basis of tar soaps and ointments.

3. **Respiratory — inhaled resin smoke and turpentine vapour** — The monoterpene-rich volatile fraction, whether from burning resin (as Dioscorides directed for the chest) or from later turpentine-based chest rubs and inhalations, acts as an expectorant and mild counter-irritant, loosening secretions in productive coughs. A surface-and-airway Qi application.

4. **Counter-irritant rubefacient for musculoskeletal complaints** — Turpentine and resin liniments redden and warm the skin, drawing blood to the surface and producing the warming, distracting relief sought in rheumatic and muscular aches — the Galenic "warming and drying" action rendered literal.

5. **Veterinary and protective sealant (traditional/anthropological)** — Pine tar's long use on hooves and on damaged tree bark, and as a protective coating, belongs to the same logic of sealing and antisepsis; included to document the continuity between the technical and medical lives of the substance.

**Pine tar ointment / *Pix liquida* (topical, refined):** The pharmaceutical-grade product is incorporated into ointment or soap bases at low percentages for dermatological use. Use the refined pharmacopeial-grade tar, not crude kiln pitch, for any skin application. **Turpentine liniment (counter-irritant):** The volatile oil diluted in a carrier oil and rubbed onto intact skin over aching joints or the chest. **For external use on intact skin only — never ingest turpentine.** [USER: verify a safe dilution range before specifying.]

**Inhalation/fumigation (respiratory):** Resin smoke or turpentine vapour inhaled traditionally for productive cough. Brief, dilute exposure only; smoke itself is an irritant.

**Caution on crude product:** Crude, unrefined wood tar carries a higher load of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and is more sensitizing and potentially more hazardous than the refined dermatological grade. Prefer refined product; restrict skin contact time.

### Contraindications

- **Contact sensitization and irritant dermatitis:** Resin acids (abietic acid and colophony) are well-recognized contact allergens; pine tar and turpentine can both sensitize and irritate. Patch-test sensitivity is common; discontinue on rash, burning, or spreading erythema.
- **Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) content of crude tar:** Crude wood tars contain PAHs; prolonged, extensive, or occluded skin exposure to crude (unrefined) tar is a toxicological concern. Use refined pharmacopeial-grade tar, limit area and duration, and avoid on broken or extensively damaged skin beyond conventional dermatological practice.
- **Photosensitivity:** Tar preparations can sensitize skin to ultraviolet light; advise sun protection of treated areas.
- **Never ingest turpentine or pitch:** The volatile turpentine fraction is toxic if swallowed (CNS, renal, and respiratory toxicity, aspiration risk). All preparations here are external/inhalational only.
- **Respiratory irritation:** Resin smoke and concentrated turpentine vapour are airway irritants; inappropriate for asthma or reactive airway disease and should be brief and dilute in anyone.
- **Pregnancy/lactation:** Insufficient data for any non-trivial therapeutic use; restrict to incidental external use. [USER: verify.]
