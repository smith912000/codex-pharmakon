# Star jasmine stem — 络石 *luo shi* (also 石鲮 *shi ling*)

## *Trachelospermum jasminoides* (Lindl.) Lem., star jasmine, the evergreen climbing vine of the dogbane family; the identification is the modern standard one for the name 络石 and is of high confidence, though Sun Xingyan's notes give only a loose description of the plant* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts on disk.** A search of the Dioscorides, Avicenna, Sushruta, Ebers, Assyrian, Tibetan and Prophetic-medicine texts in `Sources/` for *Trachelospermum* found nothing. The entry rests on one tradition, read in two layers of the same text: Sun Xingyan's Chinese reconstruction (1799) and Yang Shou-zhong's 1998 English translation. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder from the Yang translation under the pharmaceutical name *Folium Trachelospermi Jasminoidis*; this entry replaces it.

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ), the Shennong text proper.** The entry sits in the upper classic (上经). Sun's 味 paragraph reads: "味苦，温。主风热、死肌、痈伤，口干、舌焦，痈肿不消，喉舌肿，水浆不干。" ("Bitter, warm. It governs wind-heat, dead flesh, welling-abscess wounds, dry mouth, scorched tongue, welling abscesses and swellings that do not disperse, swollen throat and tongue, and water and fluids that do not dry [?]"). The last clause is terse; Yang renders it "inability to take in [even] water", and the Codex gives both readings without choosing. The paragraph continues: "久服，轻身，明目、润泽、好颜色，不老、延年。一名石鲮。生川谷。" ("Taken long: it lightens the body, brightens the eyes, moistens and glosses, gives a good complexion, [one] does not age, [it] extends the years. Another name: Stone Mound [石鲮]. It grows in rivers and valleys.") (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, upper classic, entry 络石 *luoshi*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` lines 713-715).

**Second witness, Yang 1998 (attributed to Yang).** Yang's translation (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`, lines 2023-2027) agrees: "bitter and warm", for "wind heat, dead muscles, welling abscesses, wounds, dry mouth, parched tongue, refractory welling abscesses and swellings, swollen throat and tongue", and "Protracted taking may make the body light, brighten the eyes, render the facial…". The page breaks off at the foot of the column, so the end of the clause is not in the Yang file as read, but it matches Sun.

**Wu Pu layer (3rd century, quoted by Sun).** Line 717 gives Wu Pu's aliases (落石, 鳞石, 明石, 县石, 云华, 云珠, 云英, 云丹) and records that the authorities differed on the flavour. "神农：苦，小温；雷公：苦，无毒；扁鹊、桐君：甘，无毒；李氏：小寒" ("Shennong: bitter, slightly warm; Lei Gong: bitter, non-toxic; Bian Que and Tong Jun: sweet, non-toxic; Mr Li: slightly cold"). Line 719 adds the terse "云药中君。采无时" ("a ruler among the cloud medicines [?]; gathered at no fixed time"). The Codex does not interpret 云药中君 beyond the words. [USER: verify]

**Mingyi bielu layer (later additions, quoted by Sun).** Line 721 adds further aliases (石蹉, 略石, 明石, 领石, 县石) and the habitat: Mount Tai, the shady side of stone hills, high-mountain rock faces, or "among people" (人间), gathered in the first month.

**Sun's own note (案).** Sun first suggests that the *Xishan jing* line "the Shangshen mountain has much 硌石" may refer to this plant, then records that Guo Pu's reading of 硌 as "great boulders" is wrong. He then quotes the Tang bencao note: the common name is 耐冬, southerners call it 石血, and "because it wraps stone and wood and grows, therefore it is called 络石" (以其包络石木而生). The *Bielu*, he says, calls it 石龙藤, and the kind growing on stone is the better (line 723, same file). That is the only description of the plant in the file: a plant that wraps around rock and trees, which suits a climbing vine. It gives no leaf, flower or fruit.

**Identity.** The binomial is the modern one. The textual evidence agrees with a climbing evergreen that grips stone and trunks, but it cannot separate *T. jasminoides* from other climbing plants taken for 络石 in later practice. Confidence: high for the modern identification, moderate for what the early text meant. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. As a general fact, *Trachelospermum* belongs to the Apocynaceae, a family rich in alkaloids, cardiac glycosides and latex in many members; reports on this species describe lignans and flavonoids in the stems and leaves. These are general statements, not drawn from the Codex's sources. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Shennong text lists a bitter, warm drug for hot swellings of the throat and tongue, abscesses, and dry mouth, with a promise of long-term benefit (below). These are the text's claims in its own terms. The Codex gives no protocol and does not translate "wind-heat" or "dead flesh" into modern diagnoses. Abscesses and throat swelling need medical assessment.

### Preparation & Dosing

The Shennong text proper gives no preparation. The Wu Pu layer says "gathered at no fixed time" (采无时, line 719). The Mingyi layer says the first month (正月, line 721). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

*Trachelospermum jasminoides* stem (the drug *luo shi teng* in modern Chinese pharmacy) is used in current Chinese practice, which is a general fact and not a finding. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**SBJ.** The Shennong text prints "味苦，温" (bitter, warm). Wu Pu records the disagreement in his time (bitter slightly warm, bitter non-toxic, sweet non-toxic, slightly cold). The Shennong text proper prints no toxicity word; Wu Pu's Lei Gong and Bian Que/Tong Jun readings say 无毒 (non-toxic), which is a later layer.

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested.* The entry stands in the upper classic (上经), whose preface (line 43 of the Sun file) says that upper drugs "nourish life to correspond with heaven, are non-toxic, and many or long doses do not harm people", and that whoever wishes "to lighten the body and benefit the qi, not age and extend the years" takes the upper classic. The entry's own 久服 clause reads 久服，轻身，明目、润泽、好颜色，不老、延年 ("taken long: lighten the body, brighten the eyes, moisten and gloss, good complexion, not age, extend the years"), a long-use promise attached to the drug itself, so the grade is (a). *Qing shen* 轻身 is "lighten the body" and has no pharmacological equivalent; it is not weight loss. The statement is the Shennong text's; the Wu Pu and Mingyi layers add nothing to the claim, and a cultivation procedure or dose is not given. (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, upper classic, entry 络石 *luoshi*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 715)

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator has the text's own rank to go on and nothing more: an upper-grade drug whose entry promises a long-use lightening of the body. The text gives no dose, no preparation and no regimen, and the plant has to be identified correctly before any such claim could bear on it. Without a dose and a verified plant, the honest practice is to read the entry and leave the plant alone.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning; the upper grade is described as non-toxic in the preface, but that is a class statement and not a safety assurance for the plant. Members of the dogbane family can be poisonous, and several plants are sold under similar names, so identification matters. Wounds, abscesses and throat swelling need medical assessment. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
