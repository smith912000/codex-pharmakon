### Origin & Tradition

Yellow Rattle is a slender annual of the broomrape family (Orobanchaceae), native across temperate Europe and into montane North America, where it grows in hay-meadows, chalk grassland, dune slacks and roadside swards. The English name records its signature: as the seed capsules ripen, the loose seeds rattle inside the inflated, papery calyx — a dry sound that meadow-workers once read as the sign that the hay was ready to cut.

Botanically, *Rhinanthus minor* is a **facultative root hemiparasite**. It photosynthesises with its own green leaves but supplements water, minerals and some organic carbon by tapping the roots of neighbouring plants through specialised organs called **haustoria**. According to PubMed, the haustoria and even the "prehaustoria" (haustorium-like structures the plant forms in the absence of a host) lay down lignin-rich interfacial deposits where they meet host tissue, and *R. minor* preferentially and most productively parasitises grasses and legumes over broad-leaved forbs (Pielach et al., 2026, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcaf149); Matthies, 2021, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.7967)). This host-tapping is the reason the plant is famous in ecology as the **"meadow maker"**: by suppressing vigorous grasses it opens the sward for wildflowers, and it is deliberately sown in grassland-restoration projects for exactly this reason (Marin et al., 2018, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/plb.12707)).

For the cultivator's guide, this is the whole point of the entry. Because a hemiparasite draws part of its substance from whatever it is attached to, its tissue chemistry is **variable and partly host-derived** — the plant is, in a literal sense, a composite of itself and its neighbours. That single fact governs everything downstream: dosing, safety, and the honesty of any medicinal claim.

**The tradition tags must be treated with suspicion.** The tags carried into this entry are **DMM** (the Dioscoridean/Greek herbal stream, i.e. *De Materia Medica*) and **CM** (Avicenna's *Canon of Medicine*, the Unani stream). Both are flagged here:

- **DMM — [USER: verify].** *Rhinanthus minor* is an Atlantic-and-montane-fringe plant. The classical Mediterranean herbal record does not clearly name it. Where old herbals use names like *Rhinanthus*, *Alectorolophus*, *Crista galli* or "cockscomb", the referent is uncertain and may be a different genus entirely. This looks like a **name/genus inheritance**, not a documented classical use.
- **CM — [USER: verify].** The same caution applies to any attribution to Avicenna. No specific, substantiable Unani indication for *R. minor* has been located.

Rather than pretend a lineage exists, this entry treats Yellow Rattle the way the Foundation phase treats **Red Bartsia** (*Odontites vernus*, CPU-00852) — as an **ecology-and-signature study**, a plant whose teaching value is its parasitic form and its role in the living meadow, not a pharmacy of proven cures. The two are close kin: PubMed places *R. minor* and *O. vernus* side by side as the model root hemiparasites of European grassland (Pielach et al., 2026, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcaf149)).

### Active Components

There is no reliable, *species-specific* phytochemical profile for *Rhinanthus minor* in the retrieved literature. What can be said honestly is at the **family / congener level**:

- **Iridoid glycosides** — the Orobanchaceae and their allies characteristically produce iridoids of the **aucubin / catalpol** type. These should be read as a **family-level expectation**, not a measured *R. minor* assay. (Labelled congener/family: the aucubin pharmacology below is drawn from *other* species that are known aucubin producers — *Eucommia*, *Aucuba*, *Plantago* — never from Yellow Rattle itself.)
- **Host-derived compounds** — because the plant taps neighbouring roots, its extract may carry substances originating in the host, including any toxins the host produces. This is not a hypothetical flourish; it is the direct consequence of the hemiparasitic biology documented above.
- **Structural phenolics** — lignin and related phenolics are laid down at the haustorial interface (Pielach et al., 2026, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcaf149)), but these are tissue-architecture compounds, not evidence of a medicinal principle.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica, Yellow Rattle is a **Foundation-phase signature teacher, not a remedy**. Its use case is instructional and horticultural:

- **The composite-body lesson.** The hemiparasite embodies a core Kenetica principle — that a substance is inseparable from its field of relations. A plant that is chemically part-neighbour is the clearest botanical illustration of "the medicine is entangled with its ground."
- **The meadow-maker lesson.** Its power to *suppress the dominant and release the diverse* is a signature of regulated abundance — the cultivator's emblem of pruning the strong to let the many flourish.
- **No internal therapeutic role is asserted.** Any claimed medicinal action rests on genus/name inheritance and is deflated here.

| Preparation | Kenetica use | Notes |

| Internal preparation (tea/tincture) | **Not recommended** | No safe dose can be stated; chemistry is variable and partly host-derived. |

No internal dose is given because none can be given honestly. There is no dosing literature, and the plant's chemistry cannot be assumed constant from one meadow to the next.

### Scientific Correlation

Claims graded by evidence:

- **Root hemiparasite that taps host roots via haustoria** — **Established (ecological).** Directly documented.
- **Suppresses vigorous grasses / increases meadow diversity ("meadow maker")** — **Established (ecological).**
- **Contains aucubin-type iridoids** — **Preclinical / congener-inferred (family level).** Expected for the family; not measured in *R. minor* in the retrieved record. Any medicinal reading of these compounds is congener data.
- **Specific medicinal action in humans (any indication)** — **NULL.** No clinical, and no *R. minor*-specific pharmacological, evidence was found. The classical DMM/CM attributions are unsubstantiated for this species.

According to PubMed:

- Matthies D. "Closely related parasitic plants have similar host requirements and related effects on hosts." *Ecology and Evolution*, 2021. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.7967)
- Marin M et al. "Germination characteristics of *Rhinanthus minor* influence field emergence, competitiveness and potential use in restoration projects." *Plant Biology (Stuttgart)*, 2018. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/plb.12707)
- Wanke SC, Matthies D. "Induced defence by a root hemiparasite increases host plant resistance against future infection." *Plant Biology (Stuttgart)*, 2026. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/plb.70187)

Congener / family chemistry (aucubin — NOT measured in *R. minor*; cited to show what the family-level iridoid would do in *other* plants):
- Kartini K et al. "Characteristics, Isolation Methods, and Biological Properties of Aucubin." *Molecules*, 2023. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules28104154)
- Zeng X et al. "A review of the pharmacology and toxicology of aucubin." *Fitoterapia*, 2019. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fitote.2019.104443)
- Bridi R et al. "Iridoids as a Potential Hepatoprotective Class: A Review." *Mini Reviews in Medicinal Chemistry*, 2023. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2174/1389557522666220816130158)

Note on aucubin: reviews report broad in-vitro/in-vivo activity (anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, hepatoprotective) but stress that **human clinical evidence is lacking** and that aucubin is unstable, deglycosylating to aucubigenin. Aucubin/iridoids are also noted to be **irritant and, in quantity, laxative and mildly toxic** — the reason this class is not casually ingested.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

No temperament can be sourced to a named classical authority for this species (see **[USER: verify]** flags above). If a placeholder is wanted for teaching, the bitterness and dryness of the rattling seed-head suggest a **Cold & Dry** reading by analogy to other bitter meadow herbs — but this is an *inference for signature study*, not an attested DMM/CM classification, and should be labelled as such wherever it is used.

### Contraindications

- **Do not use internally.** There is no human safety data for *Rhinanthus minor*.
- **Variable, host-derived chemistry.** As a hemiparasite, the plant may carry compounds — including toxins — from whatever host it was attached to. Two collections from different meadows are not chemically equivalent.
- **Iridoid/aucubin-class risk.** Family-characteristic iridoids are irritant and, in quantity, laxative and can be mildly toxic; aucubin is chemically unstable.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children:** avoid entirely — no data.
- **Livestock/foraging note:** treat as a study specimen, not forage or food.
