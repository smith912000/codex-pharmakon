# Rosemary — Iklīl al-jabal (إكليل الجبل, "crown of the mountain") / Libanotis (λιβανωτίς, "the frankincense-herb") / Rosmarinus, "dew of the sea" / Mí Dié Xiāng (迷迭香) / Rosemary-of-remembrance

## *Salvia rosmarinus* Spenn. (syn. *Rosmarinus officinalis* L.) | Family: Lamiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM, SBJ, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Hot and dry in the second degree (Galenic/Unani); attenuant, cephalic, resolvent, emmenagogue
**Training Phase:** Phase III — Consolidation & Recall (the memory-fixing agent of the Codex; also a Phase I circulatory opener)

---

### Origin & Tradition

Rosemary is one of the very few plants in the Codex attested across five traditions, and — unusually — the traditions do not merely overlap, they *agree*. From Nineveh to Chang'an, wherever this plant or its close analogues appear, the indication clusters around the **head**: the mind, the memory, the senses, the crown. That convergence is the whole argument of this entry.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (AT).** The Neo-Assyrian medical corpus is built around aromatic, resinous, "sweet-smelling" plants used in fumigations, oils and salves — the *riqqū*, the aromatics, deployed both in medicine and in the purification rites that separate the clean from the unclean. Rosemary belongs to that Levantine aromatic economy: a resinous, camphoraceous, mountain shrub of the kind burned and infused to clear the head and drive off what afflicts it. The precise Akkadian term is not securely fixable to *Rosmarinus*, and the Codex will not invent one; the attestation is to the **aromatic-fumigant class** and its head-clearing, apotropaic function. **Category: aromatic fumigant / purifying unguent.**

**Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* treats rosemary as **λιβανωτίς** — literally "the frankincense-plant," named for its resinous odour. The classical indications are warming and opening: it is given for **jaundice** (an obstruction/deobstruent indication), used in oils and ointments, and understood as a warming, thinning agent. Greek practice also gives us the plant's most famous cultural fact: **students wore rosemary garlands while studying and sitting examinations.** This is not a later romantic invention; the association of rosemary with mental acuity and memory is ancient and continuous, running through Greek scholastic custom into the European "rosemary for remembrance" of the funeral and wedding rites, where the herb is carried by mourners to signal that the dead are *not forgotten*. **Category: cephalic, deobstruent, warming aromatic.**

**Avicenna / Unani (CM).** In the Arabic-Persian tradition rosemary is **إكليل الجبل — *iklīl al-jabal***, "the crown of the mountain," and its energetics are stated flatly: **hot and dry in the second degree.** Its functional categories in the Unani scheme are *muqawwī al-dimāgh* (**strengthener of the brain**), *mufattiḥ* (deobstruent), *mulaṭṭif* (attenuant — it thins thick, cold, viscid humours), *muḥallil* (resolvent), and *mudirr al-ṭamth* (emmenagogue). This is the tradition that states the memory doctrine most explicitly as *pharmacology*: rosemary is classed with the **cephalic tonics**, the drugs that warm and dry a cold, damp, sluggish brain — and a cold, damp brain, in Galenic-Unani physiology, is precisely a **forgetful** brain. Nisyān (forgetfulness) is a disease of cold moisture at the back of the head, and its remedy is a hot, dry, aromatic cephalic. **Category: cephalic tonic (*muqawwī al-dimāgh*).**

**Chinese / Shennong lineage (TCM).** Rosemary enters the Chinese materia medica as **迷迭香 *mí dié xiāng*** — a name recorded in the early Chinese record as an imported western aromatic, reaching Han China through the same routes that carried frankincense and myrrh. Its Chinese classification follows the aromatic pattern: **pungent (辛), warm (温)** — an aromatic that **awakens the spirit and opens the orifices** (醒神開竅, *xǐng shén kāi qiào*), dispels wind-damp, and moves qi. Chinese doctrine does not say "memory" in the Greek phrasing; it says **"the orifices of the heart are blocked and the spirit is clouded,"** and it prescribes pungent-warm aromatics to open them. This is the same clinical territory reached by a different road. **Category: pungent-warm aromatic; awakens the spirit, opens the orifices.**

**Sushruta / Ayurvedic reception (SS).** Rosemary is not indigenous to India and is not a classical *Charaka/Sushruta* dravya. It enters the Indian system through the **Unani transmission** and is treated in that reception as a **katu-tikta (pungent-bitter), ushna-virya (hot-potency)** aromatic — *kapha-vata* reducing, *medhya* in effect (intellect-supporting), acting on *prana vayu*, the vital air seated in the head. The Codex flags this honestly as **reception, not origin**: the Ayurvedic classification is real and in use, but it is a later assimilation of a Mediterranean plant, not an ancient Indian indication. **Category: *medhya* (nootropic) aromatic, by later assignment.**

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.**

Five traditions, one doctrine: **rosemary works on the head, and it works by warming, opening, and clearing.** The symbolic register — the student's garland, the mourner's sprig, "there's rosemary, that's for remembrance," the *crown* of the mountain sitting on the crown of the skull — is not decoration laid over a neutral herb. It is a **compressed pharmacological claim**, and the modern chemistry vindicates the claim with unusual precision.

Take the doctrines one at a time and map them:

**(1) "Cephalic tonic / strengthener of the brain" (Avicenna) → cholinesterase inhibition.** Unani says a cold, moist brain forgets, and a hot, dry aromatic restores it. Modern pharmacology says memory consolidation depends on cholinergic transmission, and that rosemary's constituents — notably the monoterpene **1,8-cineole** and the diterpene **carnosic acid** — inhibit **acetylcholinesterase**, the enzyme that degrades acetylcholine. The one working nootropic drug class in Alzheimer's disease (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) is *the same mechanism*. The reviewed preclinical and clinical literature on rosemary explicitly identifies **inhibition of cholinesterase** as a principal neuroprotective mechanism of the plant ([Oresanya & Orhan, *Curr Drug Targets* 25:330–352, 2024 — DOI: 10.2174/0113894501255093240117092328](https://doi.org/10.2174/0113894501255093240117092328)). *Muqawwī al-dimāgh* is, in molecular terms, an AChE inhibitor. That is the single tightest doctrine-to-chemistry mapping in this volume.

**(2) "The frankincense-herb" — the doctrine of the *smell* (Dioscorides, Assyrian fumigation) → the volatile fraction is systemically active by inhalation.** Both traditions treat rosemary as something you *burn, breathe, and wear*, not merely swallow. The classical logic — that the odour itself reaches the brain — sounds like superstition and is not. 1,8-cineole is a small, lipophilic, volatile molecule that is absorbed across the nasal and pulmonary epithelium, crosses the blood–brain barrier, and appears in plasma after aroma exposure. The garland *was* a delivery route. And the effect is measurable: aroma exposure alone significantly enhanced quality of memory and secondary memory in healthy adults ([Moss, Cook, Wesnes & Duckett, *Int J Neurosci* 113:15–38, 2003 — DOI: 10.1080/00207450390161903](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207450390161903)).

**(3) "Opens the orifices, awakens the spirit" (CM) / "deobstruent, resolvent" (Unani) → cerebrovascular and antioxidant action.** The Chinese and Unani doctrines both describe an *obstruction* cleared and *flow* restored. Rosemary ingestion produces a measurable **cerebrovascular** change — significantly higher deoxygenated haemoglobin during cognitive load, interpreted as facilitated oxygen extraction at times of demand ([Moss, Smith, Milner & McCready, *J Psychopharmacol* 32:1319–1329, 2018 — DOI: 10.1177/0269881118798339](https://doi.org/10.1177/0269881118798339)). Meanwhile **carnosic acid** and **rosmarinic acid** are potent antioxidants and anti-inflammatories acting on oxidative and inflammatory proteins in the CNS (Oresanya & Orhan, 2024, DOI above). "Clouded spirit, blocked orifices" and "cold viscid humour obstructing the brain" are both, read forward, descriptions of a poorly perfused, oxidatively stressed brain.

**(4) The dark half of the doctrine: hot and dry *in the second degree*.** The Galenic degree system is a dose-toxicity scale. A second-degree hot-dry drug is one that *overheats* if pushed. This is not a quaint archaism — it is the most clinically important thing the tradition preserved about rosemary, and the modern data confirm it exactly. In elderly subjects, dried rosemary leaf produced a **biphasic dose-dependent effect on speed of memory: the lowest dose (750 mg) significantly improved it (P = .01), while the highest dose (6,000 mg) significantly impaired it (P < .01)** ([Pengelly, Snow, Mills, Scholey, Wesnes & Butler, *J Med Food* 15:10–17, 2011 — DOI: 10.1089/jmf.2011.0005](https://doi.org/10.1089/jmf.2011.0005)). The tradition's warning that a second-degree drug turns on you at high dose is, here, an empirically demonstrated inverted-U. The garland was a small dose. That was the point.

### Active Components

- **1,8-cineole (eucalyptol)** — principal cognitively-active monoterpene of the volatile oil; AChE-inhibitory, BBB-penetrant, absorbed via inhalation.
- **Camphor** — major volatile constituent; a **neurotoxicant and convulsant at high dose**. The chief safety liability of the essential oil.
- **α-pinene, borneol, bornyl acetate, verbenone, limonene** — supporting volatile fraction.
- **Carnosic acid** and **carnosol** — phenolic diterpenes; antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective; contributors to cholinesterase inhibition and to the plant's action on oxidative/inflammatory CNS targets.
- **Rosmarinic acid** — caffeic acid ester; antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, the principal water-soluble active (and therefore the active of a *tea* rather than an oil).
- **Ursolic acid, oleanolic acid** — triterpenes; anti-inflammatory.
- **Flavonoids** (apigenin, luteolin, diosmin classes).

Note the split that governs all dosing below: **rosmarinic acid is in the water; camphor and cineole are in the oil.** The tea and the essential oil are not the same drug.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Rosemary is the Codex's **Phase III consolidation and recall agent** — the substance taken at the point where material must be *fixed* rather than acquired. Its use is deliberately modelled on the ancient practice rather than the modern megadose: **low, aromatic, and repeated**, at the moment of encoding and again at the moment of recall.

Secondary roles: a **Phase I** warming circulatory opener for the cold, foggy, low-perfusion morning state; and an external resolvent (infused oil, liniment) for cold, stiff, wind-damp musculature — the indication on which the Chinese, Greek and Unani traditions again agree.

Its Kenetica signature is *precision at low dose*. It is the standing lesson in the curriculum that **more is not more**: the plant that sharpens memory at 750 mg dulls it at 6,000 mg, and the practitioner who cannot hold that distinction should not be dosing anything.

### Scientific Correlation

*(All studies below retrieved from PubMed; DOIs linked.)*

1. **Aroma alone improves memory.** Moss, Cook, Wesnes & Duckett (*Int J Neurosci* 113:15–38, 2003; [DOI: 10.1080/00207450390161903](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207450390161903)) exposed 144 healthy adults to rosemary aroma, lavender aroma, or no odour while completing the Cognitive Drug Research battery, with participants deceived as to the study aim to exclude expectancy. Rosemary **significantly enhanced overall quality of memory and secondary memory** versus control — but also **impaired speed of memory**, and left participants more alert than controls. Note the honest complication: the benefit is to *accuracy*, and it comes with a speed cost. This is a real effect with a real trade-off, not a clean win. It directly corroborates the Greek garland practice and the Assyrian fumigation logic — the volatile fraction reaches the brain.

2. **Ingestion produces both cognitive and cerebrovascular effects.** Moss, Smith, Milner & McCready (*J Psychopharmacol* 32:1319–1329, 2018; [DOI: 10.1177/0269881118798339](https://doi.org/10.1177/0269881118798339)), a randomised trial in 80 healthy adults, found small but statistically significant beneficial cognitive effects of 250 mL rosemary water versus mineral water, and — the novel finding — **significantly higher deoxygenated haemoglobin during cognitive task performance** on near-infrared spectroscopy, suggesting facilitated oxygen extraction under load. The authors interpret via **putative cholinergic and metabolic mechanisms.** This is the "opens the orifices / clears the obstruction" doctrine, instrumented.

3. **The dose-response is biphasic — the tradition's "second degree" made quantitative.** Pengelly, Snow, Mills, Scholey, Wesnes & Butler (*J Med Food* 15:10–17, 2011; [DOI: 10.1089/jmf.2011.0005](https://doi.org/10.1089/jmf.2011.0005)), a randomised, placebo-controlled, double-blind crossover in 28 adults (mean age 75), tested four doses of dried rosemary leaf powder. **750 mg significantly improved speed of memory (P = .01); 6,000 mg significantly impaired it (P < .01)**, with further deleterious effects on other measures at high dose. The dose nearest ordinary culinary intake was the effective one.

4. **A combination trial, with a negative primary outcome — reported honestly.** Perry et al. (*Phytomedicine* 39:42–48, 2017; [DOI: 10.1016/j.phymed.2017.08.015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2017.08.015)) ran a randomised double-blind placebo-controlled pilot of a combined sage/rosemary/melissa extract in 44 healthy adults. **Overall there were no significant differences from placebo** on immediate or delayed word recall. Only a *subgroup* analysis (under-63s) showed significant improvement in delayed recall (p < .0123, d = 0.92). A subgroup finding in a pilot of 44 is a hypothesis, not a result — and rosemary was one of three herbs. This trial does not support a rosemary memory claim; it is included because omitting it would be selective reporting.

5. **Mechanism review.** Oresanya & Orhan (*Curr Drug Targets* 25(5):330–352, 2024; [DOI: 10.2174/0113894501255093240117092328](https://doi.org/10.2174/0113894501255093240117092328)) survey the preclinical and clinical neuroprotection literature, identifying **cholinesterase inhibition**, dopaminergic and oxytocinergic modulation, and action on oxidative and inflammatory proteins, with efficacy in scopolamine-, LPS-, AlCl₃- and H₂O₂-induced amnesia models and against amyloid-beta neurotoxicity. Their own verdict is appropriately restrained: **"a few clinical studies available supported the neuroprotective effects"** and more, better-controlled trials are needed.

**Evidence grade: MODERATE for acute cognitive/memory effects in healthy adults** — multiple randomised controlled trials, consistent direction, but small samples, small effect sizes, mixed outcomes (speed vs. accuracy trade-offs), at least one null primary outcome, and a demonstrated **inverted-U dose-response**. **Grade: PRELIMINARY / PRECLINICAL for neuroprotection in dementia** — the mechanism (AChE inhibition, antioxidant, anti-amyloid) is well characterised in animals and *in vitro*; the human disease-modifying evidence does not exist. Rosemary is not a treatment for Alzheimer's disease and must never be presented as one.

The synthesis that survives scrutiny is narrower than the folklore but stronger than scepticism expects: **the ancients pointed at the right organ, by the right route, at roughly the right dose, and the mechanism they intuited as "warming the cold brain" is, in molecular terms, cholinergic potentiation.**

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani (EP):** Hot and dry, **2nd degree**. *Muqawwī al-dimāgh* (brain tonic), *mufattiḥ* (deobstruent), *mulaṭṭif* (attenuant), *muḥallil* (resolvent), *mudirr al-ṭamth* (emmenagogue). Corrective: taken with cooling, moist vehicles (rose, violet) in hot temperaments. Indicated in *nisyān* (forgetfulness) understood as cold-moist affection of the brain.
- **Greek (DMM):** λιβανωτίς — warming, thinning; deobstruent; given in jaundice; cephalic by custom and garland-practice.
- **TCM (CM):** 迷迭香 *mí dié xiāng*. Flavour **pungent (辛)**; nature **warm (温)**. Actions: **awakens the spirit and opens the orifices (醒神開竅)**, moves qi, dispels wind-damp. Channels: Heart, Lung, Spleen (per the aromatic class). Imported western aromatic in the Chinese record, not an indigenous *Bencao* staple.
- **Ayurvedic reception (SS):** **Rasa:** katu (pungent), tikta (bitter). **Guna:** laghu (light), ruksha (dry), tikshna (sharp). **Virya:** ushna (hot). **Vipaka:** katu. **Doshic action:** kapha–vata śāmaka; pitta-vardhaka (aggravating) in excess. **Karma:** *medhya* (intellect-promoting), *dīpana*, *vedanāsthāpana*. Flagged as **later Unani-mediated reception, not classical *Sushruta Samhita* origin.**

### Contraindications

**⚠ This is the section that matters. Rosemary the culinary herb is benign; rosemary essential oil is not.**

- **EPILEPSY AND SEIZURE DISORDERS — ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION for the essential oil.** Rosemary EO contains **camphor**, a known convulsant. Ingestion, and in susceptible individuals even heavy inhalation or extensive topical application, can **provoke seizures**. Rosemary essential oil must not be used internally or in high topical concentration by anyone with epilepsy, a seizure history, or an unexplained convulsive episode. This risk is *specific to the volatile oil*, not to culinary use of the leaf.
- **HIGH-DOSE ESSENTIAL OIL TOXICITY.** Ingested rosemary/camphor-containing oil is toxic: reported effects of camphor overdose include vomiting, agitation, CNS depression and seizures. **Do not take rosemary essential oil internally.** The Codex gives no internal EO dose deliberately.
- **PREGNANCY — AVOID medicinal doses.** Rosemary is a classical **emmenagogue** (*mudirr al-ṭamth*) — an established traditional uterine stimulant — and this reputation is consistent across the Greek and Unani records. Medicinal doses, extracts, and especially the essential oil are contraindicated in pregnancy. Culinary quantities in food are conventionally regarded as acceptable. Avoid the essential oil in **lactation** as well.
- **CHILDREN.** Do not apply camphor-containing essential oils to or near the face of infants and young children (risk of laryngospasm/apnoea, a class effect of camphor and cineole-rich oils). Do not give rosemary EO internally to children.
- **DOSE INVERSION — the trained practitioner's caution.** High oral doses of dried leaf **impair** cognition (6,000 mg significantly worsened speed of memory; Pengelly et al., 2011, DOI above). Exceeding the low dose does not "work harder" — it works backwards.
- **Anticoagulants / antiplatelets.** Theoretical interaction risk; monitor and consult a practitioner.
- **Hypertension, hot/pitta constitutions.** A second-degree hot, dry, sharp drug. Aggravating in heat conditions; use with cooling correctives or not at all.
- **Iron absorption / GI.** Astringent phenolics may reduce non-haem iron absorption if taken with meals; separate from iron supplements.
- **Allergy.** Lamiaceae sensitivity; contact dermatitis reported with the oil. Patch-test topicals.
