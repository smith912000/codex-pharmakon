## *Solanum americanum* (spine botanical; no text read gives this binomial. Yang 1998 prints "Herba Solani Lyrati" for two entries, which names a different species of the genus) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Identity warning.** This row was promoted from MASTER_INDEX (two traditions: Badianus, Shennong; idx 2566). Neither tag has a locus for *Solanum americanum*. The Shennong tag rests on a stem match to the genus *Solanum*, and the Badianus tag has no list row for the plant. The spine name also overlaps a finished entry, I_473 Black Nightshade (*Solanum nigrum*, CPU-00972), because *S. americanum* and *S. nigrum* are closely allied and are often confused. That confusion is general botanical knowledge and was not checked in a text read here. [USER: verify]

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** The text is Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`; herbs begin at line 1383, under repeated running headings "Herbs: Superior Class", "Herbs: Middle Class" and "Herbs: Inferior Class"). Two entries print the pharmaceutical Latin "Herba Solani Lyrati", which names *Solanum lyratum*. They are not *S. americanum*, and the extraction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv`, rows 75 and 142) matched them to the genus only by stem.

*Bai Mo* (line 1984). The nearest preceding printed heading is "Herbs: Superior Class" at line 1929 (the next heading is at line 2003). The entry reads: "Bai Mo (Herba Solani Lyrati) is sweet and cold. It mainly treats cold and heat, the eight categories of jaundice, and wasting thirst. It supplements the center and boosts the qi. Protracted taking may make the body light and prolong life. Its other name is Gu Cai (Grain Vegetable)." Yang's footnote 116 (lines 1994 to 1995) says: "There is no consensus concerning the identity of this medicinal. Another possibility is that it is Herba Vincetoxici Atrati." So the identification of Bai Mo with *Solanum* is itself disputed in the translation.

*Shu Yang Quan* (line 3068). The nearest preceding printed heading is "Herbs: Middle Class" at line 3020 (the next heading is at line 3093). The entry reads: "Shu Yang Quan (Herba Solani Lyrati) is bitter and slightly cold. It mainly treats baldness, malign sores, heat qi, scabs, and lichen worms. It cures tooth decay. It grows in rivers and valleys."

The same Latin name therefore stands under two different class headings in two different entries. No entry for black nightshade as a vegetable (the *Long Kui* of later Chinese books) was found in the text: a search for "nightshade", "Long Kui" and "Solan" found only the entries above and *Bai Mo*'s footnote.

**Nahua — Badianus manuscript (BM).** No locus was found. `_sources/badianus.csv` has no row for *Solanum americanum*, and no nightshade of that name appears in `Sources/BM_Badianus/BM_Overview.md` or `BM_Original_Latin_Nahuatl.md`. The nearest Solanaceae items are *Xahtomatl*, identified there as *Saracha jaltomata* with the identification marked uncertain (`_sources/badianus.csv`, row 196), and a *Tomatl* line in the overview glossed as tomato or ground cherry (`BM_Overview.md`, line 113). Neither is *S. americanum*. The Badianus manuscript is itself a naturalistic document. The *tonalli* and related model is reconstructed from later ethnohistory (López Austin) and is not contained in it.

### Active Components

*Solanum* species of the black nightshade group are generally known to contain steroidal glycoalkaloids, which are highest in unripe green berries. The composition of *S. americanum* and of *S. lyratum* was not read. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese — Shennong (SBJ).** *(c) Absent for the species in the spine.* No Yang entry names *Solanum americanum*. The two entries naming *S. lyratum* stand under the printed headings "Herbs: Superior Class" (*Bai Mo*, heading at line 1929) and "Herbs: Middle Class" (*Shu Yang Quan*, heading at line 3020). The Preface (lines 352 to 355 and 383 to 384) says of the superior class: "They mainly nourish life and correspond to heaven. They are nontoxic and taking them in large amounts and for a long time will not harm people", and the entry itself says "Protracted taking may make the body light and prolong life". The Preface (lines 385 to 388) says of the middle class: "They mainly nurture personality and correspond to humanity. They may or may not be toxic" (the file's OCR reads "mayor may not"). Those gradings belong to the *S. lyratum* entries and are not transferred to *S. americanum*. The identification is at genus level at best, one of the two entries carries a footnote disputing the identification, and the two entries disagree on the grade, so this entry does not assign a *san pin* grade to the spine plant.

**Nahua — Badianus (BM).** *(c) Absent.* The tag rests on a MASTER_INDEX row and not on any list row or text read. No attestation and no cultivation framing was found. The Badianus manuscript does not contain the *tonalli*, *teyolia* and *ihiyotl* model, which is reconstructed from later ethnohistory, so none is applied here.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the sources read gives a cultivator a practice with *Solanum americanum*. The Shennong entries concern *S. lyratum*, one is an upper-grade drug and one a middle-grade drug, and neither identification is firm. The Nahua tag is unsupported. The abstentive answer is the correct one: do not use a black nightshade on the strength of this entry.

### Contraindications

Black nightshades are members of a family with poisonous members, and unripe berries and leaves of some species can be toxic. Species are hard to separate, and the plants eaten as greens in some regions are not the same as those that cause poisoning elsewhere. None of the traditional uses above is advice. Seek a qualified practitioner, and seek urgent medical care after eating any unidentified nightshade.
