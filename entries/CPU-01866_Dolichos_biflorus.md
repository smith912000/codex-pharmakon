# Dolichos biflorus — Gyasen, rgya rsan (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary by column position); "Chinese broad bean", "Chinese peas"

## *Dolichos biflorus, as the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints it (OCR "Dolichos bijlorus", line 5317) as the seventh of eight scientific names in its "G" run, which column order sets against the name Gyasen (rgya rsan) and the English "Chinese broad bean"; Clark 1995 prints no Latin name for the drug, and its one passage calls it "Chinese peas"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi (Four Tantras), read in two modern translations: the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text and glossary, and Clark 1995. The binomial comes from the glossary's column order alone, and the one place the drug is named in the text is a rule of avoidance in the rejuvenation chapter, so the botanical identity is open and the entry is short.** Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP, confidence "medium") and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. **Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (row 286) the row is keyed by the scientific name "Dolichos bijlorus", with the locus "Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (line 5317)" and the note "Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". The key is not a first or later name from a list of alternatives. It is the first of the two names on line 5317 ("Dolichos bijlorus Thysanolaena maxima"), which stand against two different Tibetan names. The OCR reads "f" as "j" in the Latin, so the form is read as *biflorus*. The Tibetan name comes from the column order below, not from the CSV.

**The glossary, by column position.** `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "G" run. The phonetic names are eight (lines 5303 to 5306): "Gugul", "Gundrum", "Gurkum", "Guthup", "Gyakhur", "Gyapoe", "Gyasen" and "Gyashuk". The transliterations (lines 5308 to 5311) are "gugul", "rgun 'brum", "gur kum", "rgu thub", "rgya khur", "rgya spos", "rgya rsan" and "rgya shug". The scientific names (lines 5313 to 5317) are, in order, *Commiphora mukul*, *Vitis vinifera*, *Carthamus tinctorius*, then on one line "Peucedanum praeruptorum Picris hieracioides Melilotus officinalis L." (5316), and "Dolichos bijlorus Thysanolaena maxima" (5317). The English names (lines 5319 to 5323) are "Guggul", "Grape", "Saffron", "Hog's funnel", "Hawkweed", "Melilot", "Chinese broad bean" and "Tiger grass". Counted in order, Gyasen is the seventh name, "rgya rsan" the seventh transliteration, *Dolichos biflorus* the seventh scientific name and "Chinese broad bean" the seventh English name. The count is checked at the sixth place: Gyapoe (rgya spos) stands against *Melilotus officinalis* and "Melilot", as Clark's rgya.spos (line 1070, *Melilotus suaveolens*) allows. The same run is counted in I_1330 (*Peucedanum praeruptorum*) and I_1318 (*Melilotus suaveolens*). The glossary does not print the pairing on one row. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23, "Maintaining Health through Rejuvenation", contents line 235).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` lines 3904 to 3945, pages 235 to 238 of the translation. The chapter says that rejuvenation prolongs the lifespan and has four factors: the benefit, the place, the physical state and the procedures (line 3906). It has a preliminary procedure, with oil therapy, a bath and internal cleansing, "Without the internal cleansing, the rejuvenation therapy will be of no benefit, it would be like attempting to dye a dirty cloth" (line 3918), and an actual procedure. In the actual procedure, line 3925 reads: "Finely processed Drak shhun combined with the ash of gold, silver, copper and iron should be compounded with compatible medicines in an iron pot, and then taken orally by gradually increased doses. This cures all disorders and promotes longevity. Moen nae and Gyasen should completely be avoided during the course of rejuvenation therapy." The index (line 7382) gives "Gyasen 238". That is the only place the name is printed in the running text.

*Clark 1995, chapter 23 (line 1270).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, "23. Normal health" (heading at line 1264, end at line 1276). After the second rejuvenation compound (well-purified mineral exudate added to calcinated gold, silver, copper and iron) the text reads: "However, one should abstain from taking Mon Chenopodium (mon.sne, the dark red 'New Year' flower) and Chinese peas (slightly flat white or brown pea)." This is outside the square brackets, which Clark's preface (line 82) reserves for interpolations from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, so Clark prints it as root-layer text. Clark gives no Tibetan name and no Latin name for "Chinese peas". That "Moen nae" and "Gyasen" in the Men-Tsee-Khang line are Clark's Mon Chenopodium and Chinese peas is a reading from the position and the sense of the two rules, which agree in what they forbid and in where they stand. The text does not state it. [USER: verify]

**Identity: three descriptions of one drug.** The glossary prints the Latin *Dolichos biflorus* and the English "Chinese broad bean". Clark prints "Chinese peas (slightly flat white or brown pea)". The Codex does not say that these are one plant: it is a general fact that *Dolichos biflorus* is a pulse (the horse gram), and the texts read do not say that the avoided pulse is that one. The row stands for the Tibetan drug and not for one species.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Dolichos biflorus*, Gyasen, rgya rsan, "Chinese broad bean", "Chinese peas" and horse gram found no entry on this Tibetan drug. **I_1330** (*Peucedanum praeruptorum*, Guthup, tag FMT) and **I_1318** (*Melilotus suaveolens*, rgya.spos, tag FMT) print the same glossary run and are not entries on Gyasen. **I_457** (Hyacinth Bean, *Lablab purpureus*, tags EP, CM, TCM, SS, WA) is the entry on the plant sometimes called *Dolichos* and is not the same species as the glossary's binomial. **I_358** (Mahasaha, *Teramnus labialis*, tag SS) calls its plant a "Horse Gram relative" and is not an entry on *Dolichos biflorus*. The Sushruta appendix prints a *Dolichos* line against the Sanskrit name Kulattha ("Dolichss  Biflorus  ;  D.  uniflorus.", line 32102; `Sources/SS_Sushruta/extract/names.csv`, row 525), and the chapters name Kulattha; the Codex has not read those chapters for this entry, and the spine row for this plant carries only the FMT code. ### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. As a general fact, *Dolichos biflorus* is a legume (Fabaceae) whose seeds are eaten as a pulse, and raw legume seeds contain protein-binding lectins that cooking reduces. That is a statement about the species named by the glossary and not a finding of the Tibetan texts, which do not say whether the avoided pulse is that species. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record no action of Gyasen. They record only that it "should completely be avoided during the course of rejuvenation therapy" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3925) and that one "should abstain from taking" Chinese peas (Clark line 1270). That is a rule of the tradition's own regimen, not a Codex protocol. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Both translations name the drug in the rejuvenation chapter, and only as something to avoid during the course: "Moen nae and Gyasen should completely be avoided during the course of rejuvenation therapy" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3925) and "one should abstain from taking Mon Chenopodium ... and Chinese peas" (Clark line 1270). That is the nearest the sources come to a cultivation statement about the drug, and it assigns it a place in a regimen, as a forbidden article, and not a status as a rejuvenative. It is graded (b) and not (a) because it is a prohibition and because the identity of Gyasen with *Dolichos biflorus* rests on a column count and on a gloss that does not name the plant. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read places Gyasen in *bcud len* (essence extraction). [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** The only thing the Tibetan texts tell a cultivator about this drug is to leave it alone during a rejuvenation course, along with the Mon Chenopodium; the plant is neither a rejuvenative nor given any action. Whoever follows that rule is following the tradition's regimen, which the text makes conditional on prior cleansing and a physician's course; nothing here supports starting such a course on the strength of this entry.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning beyond the rule of avoidance above. The identity is uncertain, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Raw legume seeds should be cooked, and people with legume allergies should avoid them. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
