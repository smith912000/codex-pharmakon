# Aromatic wine — Oinos ek Smurnes, Peperees, Iridos (Dioscorides 5-65)

## *A medicated wine of myrrh, white pepper, iris and dill steeped in wine; the identification is the chapter's own recipe, so confidence in "wine flavoured with those four named drugs" is high, and the botanical identities of the ingredients are the translator's glosses, which we do not repeat except where printed; the translator prints the heading "Aromatic Wine"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 8 November 2026 as an unverified placeholder; this entry replaces it. It is a different chapter from the aromatic wine "oinos aromatites" (5-64, CPU-02697), which sits just before it.

**Dioscorides (DMM).** The chapter is 5-65, "OINOS EK SMURNES, PEPEREOS, IRIDOS", glossed "Aromatic Wine" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 10124 to 10125). The Greek heading means wine from myrrh, pepper and iris. The chapter: "Aromatic wine for dripping fluids, coughs, indigestion, inflation, and mucus of the stomach is made as follows. Take two teaspoonfuls of myrrh [1-77, 1-73, 4-116], one teaspoonful of white pepper, six teaspoonfuls of iris, and three teaspoonfuls of dill [3-67] (thoroughly pounded), bind these in a linen cloth and put them into six pints of wine. After the third day strain it, put it in jars in a stone pot, and give a winecupful of it to drink after waking, giving it unmixed."

The cross-references print the chapters in which the ingredients are described: myrrh at 1-77 (with 1-73 and 4-116), and dill at 3-67 (anethon, "eaten as a vegetable"). The chapter sits in the book on wines, in the run of "Other Wines" made by steeping drugs in must or wine (the preceding chapter 5-64 is the wine of "aromatites", and the following, 5-66, is elecampane wine, "nectarites").

### Active Components

The source names no constituent. In general terms, wine contains ethanol, water, organic acids and phenolic compounds; myrrh is an aromatic gum-resin, pepper carries the pungent alkaloid piperine, iris root carries aromatic oils and starch, and dill carries essential oil rich in carvone. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** "Dripping fluids" (catarrhal discharges), coughs, indigestion, inflation (flatulence), and mucus of the stomach.
- **What the text does not say.** No course of days, and no limit on repetition.

The Codex does not translate these terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend taking medicinal wine.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives both the preparation and the manner of drinking (lines 10124 to 10125): two teaspoonfuls of myrrh, one of white pepper, six of iris and three of dill, "thoroughly pounded", bound in a linen cloth and put into six pints of wine; strained after the third day; kept in jars in a stone pot; "a winecupful of it to drink after waking, giving it unmixed". The winecupful is the only dose in the chapter, printed as the translator renders it, and its modern volume is not stated in the source; no conversion is made here. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Spiced wines have a long history as digestive drinks; the carminative effect of volatile oils such as that of dill is a common general statement in pharmacy references. Alcohol is a recognised health risk, and a medicinal wine is not a harmless food. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter assigns no cultivation status to the wine. What it shows is a discipline of attention: ingredients in measured proportions, a fixed steeping time (three days), a vessel of stone, and a fixed time for the dose (after waking) and manner (unmixed). That is a practice of exactness, not of cultivation.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing for a cultivator beyond the lesson of the recipe, which is to measure, to time the steeping, and to take it in a stated way. Alcohol on waking is not a practice the Codex recommends.

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

The chapter names the bodily areas it treats: the chest and airways (coughs), the digestive tract (indigestion, inflation and mucus of the stomach) and the body's discharges, which the entry reads as dripping fluids or catarrhal discharges. Dioscorides gives these as plain indications, with no humour, dosha or qi channel, and prints no warm, cool, moist or dry quality or taste for the wine. Myrrh, white pepper, iris and dill are not assigned any organ of their own in this text. The only bodily instruction concerns the time and manner of taking the wine, after waking and unmixed, and the volume is not converted to a modern measure. Effects are therefore mapped only to the stated complaints. Spiced wine is a digestive drink in general practice, but that link comes from outside the text and not from Dioscorides. Alcohol is a risk in pregnancy, liver disease and with sedating medicines, and a persistent cough or indigestion needs a clinician.

### Contraindications

- **Alcohol.** Avoid in pregnancy, breastfeeding, children, liver disease, and with sedating or interacting medicines, and when driving.
- **Myrrh.** Generally avoided in pregnancy in modern references. [USER: verify]
- **Chest and stomach complaints.** Persistent cough or indigestion needs proper care.
