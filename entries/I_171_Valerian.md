## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Valeriana officinalis* L. (1753)
**Related medicinal species:**
- *V. wallichii* DC. (= *V. jatamansi* Jones) — Indian valerian; Ayurvedic *tagara*; similar valerenic acid profile
- *V. edulis* Nutt. — Mexican valerian; different alkaloid profile
- *V. officinalis* L. s.l. is a polyploid complex with diploid, tetraploid, and octaploid populations in Europe; pharmacological variation exists between ploidy levels

**Traditional names:**
| Tradition | Name | Notes |
|-----------|------|-------|
| DMM (Greek) | φοῦ (*phu*) / βαλεριανή | Pliny's "Phu" = dismissive of the smell |
| Arabic/CM | سنبل الطيب (*sunbul al-ṭayib*) | Spikenard-adjacent naming (aromatic root) |
| Ebers/Egyptian | Unknown; aromatic sedative root | Identification tentative |
| Ayurveda/SS | तगर (*Tagara*) | *V. wallichii* = Indian valerian; parallel species |
| English | Valerian; All-heal; Garden heliotrope | |
| Latin | *Valere* = to be strong/healthy | OR: province Valeria (Roman) |
| German | Baldrian; Katzenkraut (cat herb) | Katzenkraut = "cat herb" — feline attraction |

**Etymology debate:** "Valerian" may derive from: (a) Latin *valere* (to be strong) — the "heal-all" etymology; (b) Roman province of Valeria (Pannonia); (c) the personal name Valerius. The *valere* origin is most consistent with the Medieval Latin pharmacopoeial tradition of naming by curative power.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 De Materia Medica (Dioscorides, c. 50–70 CE)
DMM Book I.9: *Phu* — notable for Dioscorides' extraordinary documentation: the plant is identified in two locations (separately under names *phû* and *nardus agria*/wild spikenard), indicating its widespread use. Applications: "*warming and diuretic; good for pains of the sides; promotes menstruation; a counter-poison*." The most pharmacologically significant note: "*for epilepsy*" — the first explicit documentation of a GABA-adjacent sedative-anticonvulsant application that modern pharmacology now attributes to valerenic acid GABA-A modulation.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine (Ibn Sīnā, 1025 CE)
*Sunbul al-ṭayib* ("good spikenard") — warming; strengthens the brain and nerves; headache; promotes sleep; cardiac tonic; urinary conditions. Ibn Sīnā's brain-and-nerve applications directly map to the anxiolytic/sedative pharmacology of valerenic acid. The Canon documents: "*For palpitations of the heart and for epilepsy* it is given internally" — consistent with valerenic acid's GABA-A modulation (anticonvulsant potential).

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus — TENTATIVE
Aromatic root preparations for sleep and anxiety in Egyptian formulae; botanical identification not confirmed for *Valeriana* specifically. Tentative given geographic range (valerian is native to Europe/Asia rather than Egypt directly).

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (c. 700–600 BCE)
Aromatic root (*mandrake*-adjacent formulations in some texts) for sleep induction; tentative identification as valerian vs. other sedative aromatics. Present as component of sleep/sedation formulae.

### 2.5 Sushruta Samhita / Charaka Samhita — TENTATIVE (*V. wallichii* = Tagara)
*Tagara* (तगर) in Ayurvedic texts = *Valeriana wallichii* (Indian valerian), not *V. officinalis* directly. Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita document tagara for: sleep disorders (*nidranasha*); anxiety; neuromuscular conditions; epilepsy. Pharmacological parallel is exact — *V. wallichii* contains valerenic acid and related sesquiterpenes. *V. wallichii* documentation counted as SS convergence with species equivalency notation.

### 2.6 Four Medical Tantras (FMT)
*Spang rtsi mkhan* — Tibetan medicine recognizes valerian-family plants as sedative and neurological. Tibetan formulas for sleep and anxiety include *Valeriana* species native to Himalayan ecology.

### 2.7 Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ) — TENTATIVE
*Xie cao* (纈草, *V. officinalis*) — present in Chinese pharmacopoeia but not prominently in classical texts. Modern TCM acknowledges: calms the heart and shen (spirit); promotes sleep; treats restlessness. Documentation thin in classical (pre-16th century) sources; tentative.

### 2.8 Badianus Manuscript (BM) — ABSENT; South American (SA) — ABSENT

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Convergence: 6/9** (with *V. wallichii* = *tagara* equivalency accepted for SS).

**Central convergence:** Sleep and anxiety across ALL six confirmed traditions — the most specifically converging pharmacological function in the Codex for a sedative plant. DMM (*for epilepsy/menstruation/counter-poison*), CM (*promotes sleep; for epilepsy*), SS (*tagara* for *nidranasha*/insomnia, anxiety), FMT (*sedative neurological*) — four traditions across three independent pharmacological paradigms all converge on sedative-anxiolytic-anticonvulsant.

**The smell paradox:** Valerian root smells powerfully offensive to humans (isovaleric acid, dominated by the same compound responsible for sweaty foot odor and parmesan cheese) yet has been universally used medicinally. The smell is produced POST-HARVEST by enzymatic decomposition of valepotriates → isovaleric acid. Fresh root smells different from dried root. Medieval European herbalists called it "all-heal" despite (or because of) its odor — suggesting the smell was considered indicative of powerful medicine rather than contamination.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

### 4.1 Valerenic Acid and Relatives (Primary Active)
**Valerenic acid** (0.3–0.8% dried root): sesquiterpene carboxylic acid. The primary pharmacologically characterized sedative compound.
**Acetoxyvalerenic acid**: precursor; biotransformed to valerenic acid in GI tract.
**Hydroxyvalerenic acid**: related; less active.
**Valerenal**: aldehyde analog; contributes to central effects.

**Synthesis note:** The valerenic acid pathway produces these sesquiterpenes via the mevalonate pathway (same as cholesterol, ubiquinone, steroids in animals) — a secondary metabolic pathway shared across the plant kingdom but producing unique end products in *Valeriana*.

### 4.2 Valepotriates (Iridoid Esters)
**Valtrate, didrovaltrate, acevaltrate, baldrinal** (1–2% fresh root, much less dried):
- Bicyclic monoterpene esters; highly unstable
- Decompose within days of harvest: valtrate → baldrinal + isovaleric acid (the SOURCE of valerian's characteristic smell)
- Historical attribution as "active compounds" is likely wrong: most commercial preparations contain negligible valepotriates
- In vitro alkylating activity (possible cytotoxicity); NOT considered responsible for sedative activity due to instability
- Their decomposition product **isovaleric acid** = offensive smell but NOT sedative

### 4.3 Isovaleric Acid
- Product of valepotriate decomposition
- 3-Methylbutanoic acid; same compound in foot perspiration, parmesan cheese
- Responsible for dried valerian root's distinctive smell
- Pharmacologically NOT the sedative compound despite being the most organoleptically prominent
- The smell ≠ the medicine — a critical distinction for quality assessment

### 4.4 Flavonoids
**Linarin** (acacetin-7-glucoside) and **hesperidin**: GABA-A positive allosteric modulation (distinct site from valerenic acid and from benzodiazepines). Anti-anxiety contribution independent of valerenic acid pathway.

**6-Methylapigenin**: potent GABA-A ligand at benzodiazepine binding site; present in trace quantities; potentially significant.

### 4.5 GABA (Direct)
Measurable free GABA in valerian root (0.1–0.4% dry weight). Bioavailability after oral ingestion uncertain (GABA does not readily cross blood-brain barrier), but GI GABA receptors exist (enteric nervous system) → may contribute to peripheral sedative effects.

### 4.6 Glutamine
A GABA precursor; may increase brain GABA availability via glutamine → glutamate → GABA pathway. Minor contribution.

### 4.7 Actinidine (Alkaloid)
**Actinidine** (pyridine alkaloid): feline neurostimulant — same compound (with nepetalactone from catnip) responsible for the characteristic euphoric response cats show to valerian root. Cats roll in it, rub their faces on it, become temporarily euphoric. **The pharmacological basis:** actinidine activates the same olfactory receptors in felines that nepetalactone activates; these map to reward/opioid-adjacent pathways in the cat brain. Humans do not respond to actinidine neurologically (different olfactory receptor profile), but the human GABA-A → sedation and the feline actinidine → euphoria both occur simultaneously in the same root — a single plant activating two completely different neurochemical systems in two different mammalian species.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 GABA-A Positive Allosteric Modulation (Valerenic Acid — Primary)
**Site:** β2/β3 subunit junction of the GABA-A receptor — distinct from the benzodiazepine binding site (α subunit) and from the barbiturate site (β subunit intracellular).

**Mechanism:** Valerenic acid binds to the β2/β3 extracellular interface → positive allosteric modulator (PAM) → INCREASES frequency of Cl⁻ channel opening in response to GABA → enhanced inhibitory neurotransmission → sedation, anxiolysis, muscle relaxation, anticonvulsant activity.

**Pharmacological consequence of distinct binding site:**
- NO cross-tolerance with benzodiazepines
- NO withdrawal syndrome on discontinuation (no upregulation of disinhibitory compensatory response)
- NO dependence (in clinical doses)
- Sedation is milder and more natural-feeling (less "drugged" than BZD)
- Suitable for conditions where BZD dependence is a concern

**Trauner et al. 2008 (Mol Pharmacol):** Direct binding characterization of valerenic acid to GABA-A β2/β3 subunit; antagonized by furosemide (β2/β3-selective GABA-A antagonist). This study established the molecular mechanism definitively.

### 5.2 5-HT5A Receptor Partial Agonism (Valerenic Acid)
5-HT5A is a serotonin receptor expressed in suprachiasmatic nucleus (circadian clock) and hippocampus. Partial agonism by valerenic acid → mild circadian regulation and hippocampal inhibition → contributes to sleep-onset improvement independent of GABA-A mechanism.

### 5.3 GABA-A Modulation (Flavonoids — Secondary)
Linarin and hesperidin act at distinct GABA-A sites (likely α subunit region for hesperidin; different site from valerenic acid). Additive/synergistic with valerenic acid for anxiolytic effects.

### 5.4 Adenosine Receptor Modulation
Valerian extract increases adenosine A1 receptor binding — adenosine A1 is the endogenous target responsible for sleep pressure accumulation (*adenosine hypothesis of sleep* — same receptor blocked by caffeine). Enhancement of A1 activation promotes sleep onset.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### 6.1 Sleep (Primary Indication)
**Bent et al. 2006 (American Journal of Medicine) meta-analysis:** 16 RCTs, N=1,093; valerian vs. placebo for sleep; significant improvement in **subjective** sleep quality (OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.11–1.70); polysomnographic (objective PSG) measures: inconsistent; high heterogeneity. Most consistent finding: subjective perception of sleep quality and sleep onset speed.

**Leathwood et al. 1982 (Pharmacology Biochemistry and Behavior):** N=128; one of the earliest controlled studies; valerian extract 450 or 900mg vs. placebo; significant improvement in subjective sleep latency and sleep quality; no morning sedation or hangover (distinguishing it from benzodiazepines).

**Koetter et al. 2007 RCT:** Valerian+hops combination vs. valerian alone vs. placebo; combination superior to either component alone for PSG sleep latency — suggesting synergistic mechanisms between valerian GABA-A (valerenic acid) and hop bitter acids (GABA-A activity via humulone).

### 6.2 Anxiety
**Andreatini et al. 2002 (Phytotherapy Research) RCT:** N=36 GAD patients; valerenic-acid-containing extract vs. diazepam 6.5mg/day vs. placebo × 4 weeks; significant anxiety reduction in both valerian and diazepam groups; no significant difference between active treatments. **Limitations:** small N; open-label elements. Suggests dose-equivalency to low-dose diazepam for mild anxiety.

**Kennedy et al. 2006:** Valerian-containing preparation vs. buspirone for anxiety; valerian group showed significant anxiolytic effect with faster onset.

### 6.3 European Regulatory Status
**EMA Traditional Herbal Medicinal Product Registration:** Approved for relief of mild tension/anxiety and sleep disturbance when due to anxiety or overexcitement. Maximum 4–8 weeks use per registration.

**German Commission E:** Approved for sleep disorders and states of restlessness/anxiety. One of the 380 herbs in the Commission E Positive Monographs (approved for use).

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Pliny and "Phu":** Pliny the Elder (*Naturalis Historia* XII) used the exclamation "Phu!" (*onomatopoeia* for the smell) as the plant's common name — the most casually documented pharmacological olfactory assessment in ancient literature. That a plant smelling of sweaty feet became one of the most universally used sedatives in human history illustrates the principle that pharmacological efficacy is independent of organoleptic acceptability.

**The Medieval "All-Heal":** Valerian's English folk name "All-heal" reflects its Medieval European deployment for essentially every chronic condition — epilepsy, palsy, nervous disorders, menstrual irregularity, plague prevention, and as an antidote. The "all-heal" category in European herbalism marks plants whose diverse documented utility exceeded any single tradition's framework — the herbalist equivalent of a broadspectrum antibiotic. The pharmacological basis (GABA-A modulation → multiple inhibitory neurotransmitter-dependent pathways) does support a genuinely broad application spectrum.

**Cats and the Rat Connection:** The feline attraction to valerian (actinidine) has generated the hypothesis that the same attractant response helps explain the plant's historical use in rat traps — the Pied Piper of Hamelin (German folk tale) legend involves the piper carrying valerian root alongside his pipe, the root attracting rats as much as the music. Whether Hamelin's rat-catcher used valerian is unverifiable, but the actinidine → rodent attraction is chemically documented (rodents also show attraction to valerian root).

**WWI "Shell Shock" Treatment:** During World War I, valerian was used by British military medics for "shell shock" (now understood as PTSD/acute stress disorder). The sedative-anxiolytic application for severe acute psychological trauma — a use case far beyond modern clinical evidence — represents an emergency extension of the traditional anxiety indication. The GABA-A mechanism is pharmacologically appropriate for acute anxiety states; efficacy for chronic PTSD is unlikely but the 1914–1918 field medicine had limited alternatives.

**The Smell Problem and Modern Encapsulation:** Valerian root tea is rarely consumed — the isovaleric acid smell (sweaty feet/cheese) is almost universally rejected by modern Western consumers. The shift from decoction to enteric-coated tablet/capsule in the 20th century transformed valerian from an almost unusable traditional preparation to a widely taken supplement. The EMA Traditional Herbal Medicinal Product approval for valerian helped formalize this shift. The irony: Dioscorides who documented it as *phu* would have been using the decoction; modern users taking odorless encapsulated extract are getting a pharmacologically identical but organoleptically transformed preparation.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Dosing
| Application | Form | Dose | Timing |
|-------------|------|------|--------|
| Sleep latency | Standardized extract (0.8% valerenic acid) | 300–600mg | 30 min before bed |
| Sleep quality | Whole root powder | 900–1,500mg | 30–60 min before bed |
| Mild anxiety | Standardized extract | 120–200mg 2–3× daily | With meals |
| Traditional tea | Root decoction (10g/500mL) | 150 mL × 1–3/day | Strongly flavored |

**Quality criterion:** For efficacy, products must be standardized to **valerenic acid 0.8%** minimum. Products standardized to isovaleric acid are mislabeled (isovaleric acid is the decomposition byproduct, not the active compound). "Fresh root extract" preparations retain valepotriates but have shorter shelf life.

### 8.2 Combination Products
Valerian is frequently combined with:
- **Hops** (*Humulus lupulus*): Synergistic GABA-A activity via different binding sites; most common combination in European sleep medicine
- **Passionflower** (*Passiflora incarnata*): Chrysin GABA-A benzodiazepine site + valerenic acid β2/β3 site = complementary mechanisms
- **Lemon balm** (*Melissa officinalis*): GABA transaminase inhibition → increased synaptic GABA

### 8.3 Contraindications and Safety
**Sedation:**
Driving and operating machinery — avoid for 2 hours after therapeutic dose. The sedation is mild compared to benzodiazepines but real. Morning grogginess possible at high doses.

**Additive CNS depression:**
Alcohol, benzodiazepines, Z-drugs (zolpidem), antiepileptics, opioids — additive sedation; avoid combination without guidance.

**CYP3A4:**
In vitro CYP3A4 inhibition reported at high concentrations; clinical significance at recommended doses unclear; theoretical interaction with CYP3A4-sensitive narrow TI drugs (cyclosporine, tacrolimus, midazolam) — use caution.

**Pregnancy and Lactation:**
Avoid (valepotriates — though trace in commercial extracts — showed mutagenicity in some early studies; precautionary principle applies; emmenagogue effect documented in some traditions).

**Paradoxical stimulation:**
A minority of users (~5%) experience paradoxical stimulation (agitation, vivid dreams, increased anxiety) with valerian — a phenomenon seen with other GABA-A modulators (benzodiazepines also cause paradoxical reactions in some patients, particularly elderly). If stimulation occurs, discontinue.

**Maximum duration:**
4–8 weeks continuous use per EMA registration; longer-term use not associated with dependence in clinical reports but insufficiently studied. Discontinuation: no withdrawal syndrome documented in clinical studies (mechanism-appropriate: valerenic acid GABA-A site distinct from benzodiazepine site avoids tolerance development).

### 8.4 Tradition-Practice Concordance
Valerian's GABA-A mechanism validates the universal sedative-anticonvulsant-anxiolytic tradition at the molecular level. The epilepsy application documented in DMM (phu, first century CE) and CM (Canon, 1025 CE) is pharmacologically plausible — valerenic acid GABA-A modulation does have anticonvulsant activity in animal models, though clinical antiepileptic application requires RCT evidence not yet available. The modern use (OTC sleep supplement) is a narrowing of the traditional use (all neurological calming), with the OTC framing most supported by current evidence and the broader traditional applications awaiting clinical investigation.
