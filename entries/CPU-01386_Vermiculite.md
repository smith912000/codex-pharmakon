# Vermiculite — gser.zil (Clark 1995); Serzil (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *A soft, gold-ore-coloured stone that breaks into sparkling needle-like pieces; Clark glosses it "Vermiculite: Litharge: Mineral Oxide of Lead", two identifications that do not agree* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The locus is the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20 (the efficacy of medicinal substances), in Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 831; name-index row `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` line 12; list row `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 139, "Serzil"). Clark's chapter divides the individual substances into eight sections: precious metals, stones, earths, trees, resins, plants, herbs and animal products (line 802). *gser.zil* is in the second, "(2) The Powers of Stone Medicines" (line 816). The entry reads:

"gser.zil: Vermiculite: Litharge: Mineral Oxide of Lead: [resembles gold ore and has various shapes. It is a soft stone which breaks into long, sparkling needle-like pieces]." (line 831)

It is the middle one of three *zil* stones listed together: *stang.zil*, "Massicotite", a black stone which breaks into sparkling needle-like pieces and is "so dark in color that it is said to resemble the hair of the Lord Buddha" (line 830); *gser.zil* (above); and *dngul.zil*, "Mineral Sulphate of Calcium: Carbonate of Lime", greenish, softer than silver ore, breaking into needle-like pieces (line 832). The line that closes the group gives the action: "All three types of zil] remove discoloration of the bone." (line 832). The square-bracketed descriptions are, by Clark's own note (line 82), interpolations from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries; they are not the root text, so the description of the stone is commentarial.

**Second translation.** The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`) gives the action in its summary of the stone medicines at line 3356: "Tangzil, Serzil and Ngulzil give color to bones." Its index lists "Serzil 199" (line 7486), and a glossary entry for "Tangzil" appears at line 5873. The two translations word the action differently, "remove discoloration of the bone" and "give color to bones", and I cannot say which is closer to the Tibetan. They agree that the three *zil* are bone medicines.

**Identity.** Clark's gloss lists "Vermiculite" and "Litharge: Mineral Oxide of Lead" side by side. Vermiculite is a micaceous mineral and litharge is lead oxide; they are not the same material, and the gloss does not say which is intended. The description (soft, needle-like fracture, gold-ore appearance) is the commentary's, and the project's heading "Vermiculite" follows the first word of Clark's gloss. No Tibetan spelling beyond the transliteration *gser.zil* (Wylie style, as Clark prints it) was available, and the translations do not print the script. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Stated generally, and not from the text, because the identity is unresolved. If the material is vermiculite, it is a hydrated magnesium-aluminium silicate mineral of the mica family that exfoliates on heating. If it is litharge, it is lead(II) oxide, a toxic lead compound. The text gives neither a composition nor a test, so neither can be asserted for this drug.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts read credit the *zil* group with an action on the bone and say nothing of use to a cultivator beyond that. Because one of Clark's two glosses is a lead oxide, ingesting the material cannot be recommended, and no modern protocol is supplied.

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] As general facts: vermiculite is used industrially as an insulating and horticultural material and is not a drug; lead oxide is a cumulative toxin affecting the nervous system, blood and kidneys. Neither is drawn from the Tantras.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** None is given for this stone. Clark's chapter-20 entries for some substances print a taste, post-digestive taste or potency (for example, copper and iron in the precious-metal section, lines 806-808); the *zil* entries print the appearance and the bone action only. The general section of the chapter explains the eight potencies and six tastes (lines 796-799) but assigns none to *gser.zil*.

---

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The tradition's pathology is at root a spiritual condition, and medicine is the third of four treatment branches: it supports practice and does not reach the root. *gser.zil* is a therapeutic stone medicine, one of three *zil* credited with restoring the colour of the bone. The Men-Tsee-Khang text does credit gold, in the precious-metal summary, with promoting "longevity" and being "rejuvenating" (line 3330); it makes no such statement for the *zil* stones, and no claim is transferred from gold. No *bcud len* (essence extraction) use of this stone was found, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, so none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. The stone is a bone medicine in a physician's materia medica, its identity is unresolved between a mica mineral and a lead oxide, and no preparation or dose is given. A cultivator has nothing to do with it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Possible lead compound:** if the material is litharge, it is toxic and must not be ingested; Tibetan mineral preparations in general require qualified preparation, which these texts do not describe for this stone.
- **Unidentified mineral:** the identification is unresolved; unidentified mineral matter should not be swallowed.
- **Dust:** fine mineral dust is a respiratory irritant.
- **Pregnancy and children:** lead compounds are especially harmful.
