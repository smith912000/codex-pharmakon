# Sea hare — *Lagoos thalassios* (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 2-20)
## *"Aplysia depilans — Sea Hare", the translator's identification; the text says it "is similar to the little cuttlefish"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica*, in the 2000 English edition (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`; "text" below). The chapter is 2-20, headed *LAGOOS THALASSIOS* ("sea hare"), at line 4997 (the spine's locus, `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`: "2-20 (line 4997)", translator's identification "Aplysia depilans — Sea Hare", class animal).

The whole chapter, line 5000, reads: "The sea hare is similar to the little cuttlefish. Pounded into small pieces and applied (either by itself or with sea nettles) it makes any place without hair [depilatory]." The bracketed word is the translator's. The chapter gives one use, a depilatory, and one preparation, pounding, with or without sea nettles. The "sea nettles" are not further identified in the chapter.

Three other places in the text speak of the sea hare, each time as a poison, with a cross-reference to this chapter. Chapter 1-105 (*Kedros, Kedros mikra*, "cedria" in the translation), line 4329, says the cedria "helps for poison of the sea hare [2-20] taken in a drink with passum [raisin wine]". Chapter 2-12 (*Karkinoi*, crabs, line 4964) says river crabs, "boiled and eaten with their broth", are good "for those in consumption [wasting disease], and those who have swallowed a sea hare [2-20]" (line 4966). Chapter 2-78 (*Gala gunaikos*, woman's milk, line 5253) says that milk "is also an antidote in a drink for someone that has taken sea hare [2-20]". The index to the edition carries the entries "poison of sea hare" and "poison of the sea hare" (line 13870). So in Dioscorides the sea hare is both a depilatory material in its own chapter and a creature whose ingestion is a poisoning, for which three other chapters give remedies. This entry takes the three antidote lines as read; the poisoning itself is not described in any chapter read, and its symptoms are not given. [USER: verify]

The chapter that follows, 2-21 (*Lagoos chersaios*, "the land hare", line 5001), concerns the brain, head and curds of the land hare, an ordinary hare. The edition prints a plate caption "Aplysia depilans — Sea Hare from Davis — 1907" at the top of that chapter (line 5003), a picture caption from the edition's illustrations, not a statement of the text.

No other tradition on the tag line, and no text outside Dioscorides on disk, was found naming the sea hare. A whole-word search of the Sushruta, Ebers, Tibetan and Assyrian texts on disk for "sea hare" returned nothing.

### Active Components

Sea hares (*Aplysia*) are marine sea slugs that feed on algae and take up compounds from their diet; they can release an ink-like secretion when disturbed. These are general facts about the genus. The chapter names no constituent and this entry names none.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The one use is a depilatory applied to the skin. The material is also cast in the text as a poison when swallowed. The Codex's application layer has nothing to add, and no protocol is offered.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives one preparation: the animal "pounded into small pieces and applied (either by itself or with sea nettles)" to the place to be cleared of hair (line 5000). No quantity, number of animals or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The general fact offered is that sea hares are marine molluscs that accumulate compounds from the algae they eat. No depilatory action is claimed here for the animal, and the poison reported by Dioscorides is not matched to a known toxin.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides.** No quality (warming, astringent, or the like) is printed for the sea hare in chapter 2-20, and none is supplied here.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. Chapter 2-20 is two sentences, a likeness and a depilatory use. Elsewhere the work treats the sea hare as a poison and gives antidotes, which is a warning, not a cultivation status. The chapter records no provenance, season or assay such as makes the discipline-of-attention frame useful elsewhere in the work.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing in the source for a cultivator to use. The material's two roles in Dioscorides are a hair remover and a poison, and a hair remover that is also a recorded poison is a reason for abstention. What the work does model is the cross-reference habit: the sea hare is cited by chapter number wherever a remedy for its poison is given, which is a way of keeping a hazard in view.

### Contraindications

Dioscorides himself treats ingestion as poisoning (1-105, 2-12 and 2-78), so the animal must never be swallowed or put into a drink. Applied to the skin it is an unregulated marine animal product with an unknown skin effect, and the text says nothing of safe contact. Sea hares are marine animals in various jurisdictions whose collection is regulated. Poisoning needs medical care, not a household antidote. These are general cautions and not claims from the text.
