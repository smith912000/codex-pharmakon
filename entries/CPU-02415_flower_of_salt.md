# Flower of salt — Alos Anthos (ALOS ANTHOS), "Flowers of salt" (Dioscorides)

## *A saffron-coloured, rank-smelling, fatty-tasting salt efflorescence said to flow down from the Nile and collect on lakes, used on ulcers, ears, eyes and scars and in plasters; the translator's gloss reads "Mineral Salts", which names a class and not a substance, so the material is a salt efflorescence of unfixed composition, identified from the text's account of origin and colour, of medium confidence* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** This is a single-text entry. The chapter is 5-129 ALOS ANTHOS in Book Five of the *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10503-10506; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` carries it as 5-129, line 10503, translator's identification "Mineral Salts", class mineral; `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 855 has "Alos Anthos", mineral). The Greek is "flower of salt". The salt entry (Salt, sodium chloride, CPU-00298, `Volume_I_Plant_Based/entries/I_345_Salt_Sodium_chloride.md`) mentions this chapter in a list of related chapters and summarises its advice on buying; the chapter's uses are not set out there, and this entry does. A search of Sushruta, Shennong, rGyud-bzhi and Ebers on disk found no separate attestation under this name, and none is claimed.

The chapter (line 10506) reads: "Flowers of salt [precipitated solids] flow down from the river Nile and collect on certain lakes. That like saffron in colour must be taken. It has a somewhat bad scent like garum [Roman sauce made from fermented fish], sometimes smelling worse; more biting to the taste and somewhat fat. That coloured with red ochre (or has clots) is considered worthless. The pure salts are only dissolved by oil, the adulterated partly by water."

So the text gives where it comes from (down the Nile and gathered on lakes); the kind to take (saffron-coloured); its smell (like garum, sometimes worse); its taste (more biting than salt, "somewhat fat"); the kind to reject (coloured with red ochre, or in clots); and a test of purity (the pure kind dissolves only in oil, the adulterated partly in water). The bracket "[precipitated solids]" is the translator's gloss.

The uses follow: "It is good for malignancies, spreading erosive ulcers, gangrenous ulcers on the genitals, purulent ears, and dullness of sight, and it takes off scars and cataracts. It is mixed with plasters and ointments to colour them, as well as with rosaceum [1-53]. Taken with wine and water it promotes sweating, disturbs the intestines, and is bad for the stomach. It is also mixed with remedies for the removal of fatigue, and with sebaceous treatments for reducing the intensity of symptoms of the scalp, and in general it is as sharply burning as other salts."

So the uses are: spreading and gangrenous ulcers (including on the genitals), pus in the ears, dull sight, scars and cataracts, as a colouring in plasters and in rose ointment, a drink with wine and water to produce sweating (the text adds that it "disturbs the intestines" and is "bad for the stomach"), a component in remedies for fatigue and in scalp treatments, and the general note that it is "as sharply burning as other salts".

*The salts around it.* The preceding chapter, 5-126 ALES (line 10486), treats ordinary salt at length and says that salts "all have similar properties — useful as an astringent, and to clean and dissolve, as well as repress, reduce the intensity of symptoms, and form scabs — only differing slightly". 5-127 ALOS ACHNE, "Sea Froth" (line 10493), 5-128 ALME, brine (line 10499), and 5-130 NITRON (line 10507) follow in the run. The next after that, 5-131 NITROU APHROS, is treated in the saltpetre entry. Flower of salt is one of these related materials and the text keeps it in a chapter of its own.

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, an efflorescence that forms on lake shores and takes a saffron colour, with an odd smell and a fatty feel, could contain sodium chloride mixed with sodium carbonate or other soluble salts, organic matter and mineral colour from the lake. The mix cannot be fixed from the text. The translator's phrase "precipitated solids" fits a crust left by evaporation. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a recommended practice.** The text records a pungent salt crust used on ulcers, ears and eyes and as a colourant, with a drink that sweats the patient and, in the text's own words, harms the stomach. The Codex does not translate "malignancies" or "cataracts" into modern diagnoses and gives no protocol. Putting an impure salt crust in the ear or eye, or on an ulcer, risks injury and infection.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these uses (line 10506): mixed into plasters and ointments, and into rose ointment (*rosaceum*, 1-53); "taken with wine and water" as a drink; mixed into remedies for fatigue and into scalp treatments. The purity test (dissolving in oil) is a choosing aid, not a preparation. No dose, no proportion, no frequency is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Salt in strong solution irritates and dehydrates tissue and is hostile to many microbes, which fits in kind the text's use on ulcers; saline in the eye or ear is irritant. Salt taken by mouth in quantity causes vomiting and, with a salt load, serious sodium excess, and sodium carbonate crusts are alkaline and caustic. Lake and Nile-side salt crusts can carry other soluble salts and contaminants. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** The chapter prints "more biting to the taste and somewhat fat", and "as sharply burning as other salts" (line 10506). It prints no heat or moisture grade and no degree. The general statement of 5-126 for the salts, that they are "useful as an astringent, and to clean and dissolve", belongs to salts as a class and is not printed in this chapter.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: smell, colour and taste as tests, the rule to refuse the kind coloured with red ochre or in clots, and the oil-and-water test of purity. The chapter assigns no cultivation status, and the material has only a therapeutic one.

**Practitioner's note.** A pungent natural salt crust of the ancient shop, graded by nose, colour and a solubility test. Nothing here supports ingestion or any cultivation use. What the Codex takes from it is the habit of testing a material for adulteration with simple means.

### Contraindications

- **Ears, eyes, ulcers.** Do not put an impure salt crust in the ear or the eye or on an ulcer.
- **Ingestion.** The text itself says the drink "disturbs the intestines" and is "bad for the stomach". Strong salt solutions cause vomiting and sodium overload; people with heart, kidney or blood-pressure disease are at particular risk.
- **Contamination.** A natural crust may carry other salts, organic matter and pollutants.
- **Identity.** A class gloss only. [USER: verify]
