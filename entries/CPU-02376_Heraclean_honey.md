# Heraclean (Pontic) Honey — Meli Pontikon (Dioscorides 2-103)

## *Honey of the honeybee (Apis mellifera) made in Heraclea Pontica on the Black Sea coast; Dioscorides says only that it is made "from the property of certain flowers" and names no plant, and the identification of the honey type is of medium confidence, the flowers being unnamed in the text* | *(Volume III — Animal)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** Dioscorides records that this honey makes those who eat it "beside themselves with sweating" and that they must vomit. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should seek or eat honey from this region on its account.

**Named by Dioscorides only, among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The text is the English *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`), Book 2, chapter 2-103, headed MELI PONTIKON, "SUGGESTED: Heraclean Honey" (the translator's gloss), at line 5397; the name index `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` has the row "MELI PONTIKON, 2-103 (line 5397), Heraclean Honey, animal". The chapter is the third in a run of honeys: 2-101 is Honey (Attic honey the best), 2-102 the Sardinian honey, and 2-104 begins the next substance (sugar).

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The whole chapter (line 5399) reads: "At some times (of the year) honey is made in Heraclea of Pontus which, from the property of certain flowers, makes eaters of it beside themselves with sweating, but they are helped by eating rue [3-52, 3-53, 4-98] and salt meat and drinking mead [honey wine], taking these as often as they vomit. It is sharp and the smell causes sneezing. Rubbed on with costum [1-15] it heals sunburn, and with salt it takes away bruises." Heraclea of Pontus is glossed by the translator in the geographical list (line 3497) as Heraclea Pontica, a town on the Black Sea coast of northwest Turkey, "destroyed by the Romans in the Mithridatic wars"; modern Eregli stands on the site. Dioscorides does not name the flowers and does not give a dose. He gives the antidote (rue, salt meat, mead) and two external uses.

### Active Components

The text names no constituent. In general terms the Black Sea region is known for "mad honey", made when bees forage on certain rhododendrons, whose nectar carries grayanotoxins (substances that act on sodium channels in nerve and muscle). That is a well-established general fact, and the Codex does not claim that Dioscorides's flowers were rhododendrons; he does not say. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Toxic.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Externally: rubbed on with costum for sunburn, and with salt to take away bruises. The text first describes it as a poison: sweating, vomiting, and an antidote.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no way to tell the poisonous honey from the harmless, and no internal use.

The Codex does not recommend the honey, the external uses, or any attempt to obtain it.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives two external preparations: the honey rubbed on with costum [1-15] for sunburn, and with salt for bruises. The antidote for those who have eaten it is rue [3-52, 3-53, 4-98], salt meat and mead, "as often as they vomit". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any dose from this entry.** [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Honey intoxication from grayanotoxins is documented in the modern medical literature as causing sweating, dizziness, slow heart rate and low blood pressure; the Codex states this as a general fact and cites no study. The ancient description (sweating and vomiting) is of the same kind. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** Dioscorides prints "sharp" and that its smell "causes sneezing" (line 5399). No warming or cooling quality is printed for this honey. For ordinary honey (2-101) he prints that it is "cleansing, opens pores, and draws out fluids" (line 5388); that is not carried over.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers is a discipline of attention of the kind he practises throughout the work: provenance (Heraclea of Pontus), season ("at some times"), the sensory test ("sharp", the smell that causes sneezing) and the remedy for the failure of that attention. The honey carries no cultivation status in the source.

**Practitioner's note.** The lesson the text carries is about provenance and season. A honey from the wrong place and time is a poison, and the same text knows the antidote. The Codex advises no one to look for it.

### Contraindications

- **TOXIC.** Honey intoxication can be dangerous, especially in older people and in those with heart conditions or who take heart medicines. Do not eat honey of unknown origin from the Black Sea region.
- **Infants.** Honey of any kind is not given to infants under twelve months; this is a general statement, not from the source.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** No statement in the sources retrieved; avoid. [USER: verify]
- **Identity.** The honey is defined by place and effect; the plant source is not named in the text.
