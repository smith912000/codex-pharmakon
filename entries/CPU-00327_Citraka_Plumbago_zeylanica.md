### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda (Suśruta & Caraka):** Citraka is one of the most emphatically named agents in the classical materia medica — its very synonyms *Agni* and *Vahni* ("fire") announce its function. The Sanskrit *citraka* ("the spotted / the striking one") is glossed by later nighaṇṭus as referring to both the plant's habit and its striking heat on the tongue. Suśruta ranks it among the foremost **dīpana-pācana** (digestive-fire kindling and āma-digesting) dravyas and places it at the head of the **Piṭpalyādi / Citrakādi gaṇa** — the cluster of pungent, agni-stimulating drugs deployed against grahaṇī (sprue/malabsorption), agnimāndya (weak digestive fire), śotha (oedema), arśas (haemorrhoids) and kṛmi (worms/parasites). Caraka lists Citraka among the **dīpanīya** (appetiser) and **lekhanīya** (scraping/anti-obesity) drug groups. The classical galenical **Citrakādi vaṭi** and **Citraka haritakī** are direct descendants of these formulations. The root bark is the officinal part; because of its acridity it is traditionally śodhita (purified) — steeped in lime-water or buttermilk (takra) — before internal use, a caution the texts themselves impose.

**Siddha:** As *Chittiramoolam*, the root is a classic deepana and kirumi-nāśini (anthelmintic) agent, and features in preparations for skin disease and low digestive fire, echoing the Ayurvedic reading almost exactly.

**Folk & regional:** Across the Indian subcontinent, tropical Africa and Southeast Asia the root — hence the English "doctorbush" and "leadwort" — has been used topically as a rubefacient/vesicant counter-irritant on rheumatic joints, and (dangerously) as an abortifacient and arrow-paste ingredient; both uses trade directly on the acrid principle plumbagin. A red-flowered relative, *P. indica* (rakta-citraka), is treated as a substitute in some lineages.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Kindled Hearth:** In Ayurvedic cosmology *agni* is not merely gastric — it is the transformative principle by which the outer world is made into self. Citraka is the plant most nakedly identified with that fire: to name it *Agni* is to say that the drug *is* the hearth-flame externalised into root and leaf. Where digestion falters, self-building falters; āma (undigested residue, the root toxin of Ayurvedic pathology) is the anti-signal, the un-metabolised. Citraka is thus a **purifying flame** — it does not add substance, it *burns through* obstruction so that assimilation can resume. In the Kenetica frame this makes it a re-ignition agent, not a fuel: the spark that restarts a cold engine rather than the food that feeds it.

### Active Components

Principal marker: **plumbagin** (5-hydroxy-2-methyl-1,4-naphthoquinone), a yellow naphthoquinone concentrated in the root. Accompanying constituents include related naphthoquinones (chitranone, droserone, zeylanone, elliptinone, isozeylinone), plumbagic acid, sterols and triterpenoids. Plumbagin carries essentially all of the plant's characteristic pharmacology — and its toxicity.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Citraka sits in `[PRIME]` as a **metabolic re-ignition** agent: the classical dīpana-pācana action maps onto restoring digestive throughput and clearing metabolic sluggishness (āma) before a demanding block of work or training. It is a short-course corrective, not a daily tonic — used to break through a cold, congested, Kapha-heavy state and restore appetite, assimilation and warmth. Because it is sharply Pitta-aggravating and frankly toxic in excess, Kenetica treats it as a low-dose, time-boxed intervention paired with cooling anupānas, never a standing supplement.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the pharmacology of Citraka is dominated by **plumbagin**, and the evidence base is **preclinical** (cell and animal models) — there is no robust human clinical trial data for the whole drug at classical doses, so all clinical claims must remain traditional/preclinical in grade.

- Liu et al. (2024), *Journal of Cellular and Molecular Medicine*, showed plumbagin ameliorated LPS-induced acute lung injury in mice, reducing TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6, and acting via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and Keap1–Nrf2/HO-1 pathways — i.e. combined anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcmm.18386)
- Periasamy et al. (2019), *Letters in Applied Microbiology*, found plumbagin isolated from *P. zeylanica* had consistent in-vitro activity against 100 methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* (MRSA) isolates (MIC 4–8 µg/mL), with 99% kill by 8 h and synergy with ciprofloxacin — supporting the traditional anti-infective/anti-kṛmi use. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/lam.13160)

**Evidence grade:** preclinical / traditional. Antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory signals are consistent with the classical dīpana-pācana and kṛmighna uses but are not human-validated at Ayurvedic dosing.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Rasa (taste):** Kaṭu (pungent)
- **Guṇa (qualities):** Laghu (light), Rūkṣa (dry), Tīkṣṇa (sharp/penetrating)
- **Vīrya (potency):** Uṣṇa (heating)
- **Vipāka (post-digestive):** Kaṭu (pungent)
- **Prabhāva (specific action):** Dīpana-pācana par excellence — the archetypal agni-kindler; also lekhana (scraping)
- **Doṣa action:** Kapha–Vāta śāmaka; Pitta-vardhaka (aggravates Pitta)
- **Karma (actions):** Dīpana (appetiser), pācana (digestive of āma), lekhana (scraping/anti-obesity), kṛmighna (anthelmintic), śothahara (anti-oedema), used in grahaṇī, arśas, agnimāndya

### Contraindications

Pitta-predominant constitutions and Pitta disorders; pregnancy (traditional abortifacient — **absolutely contraindicated**); lactation; bleeding disorders and active peptic ulcer/gastritis. Overdose causes burning, vomiting, diarrhoea and skin/mucosal irritation; the raw root is a vesicant poison. Not for self-medication; use only śodhita material under qualified supervision.
