### Origin & Tradition

The tuberous pea — earthnut pea, earth chestnut, *gesse tubéreuse*, *Erdnuss-Platterbse* — is a slender climbing vetchling of European and western-Asian fields whose roots swell into small, sweet, chestnut-flavoured tubers. For most of its history it was food before it was medicine: a foraged and locally cultivated famine-and-field tuber, roasted or boiled, valued as a nourishing, grounding, starchy root — sweet to the tongue, filling to the belly. It is also, botanically, a *Lathyrus* — a member of the grass-pea / chickling-vetch genus — and that single fact is the reason this entry carries a warning that most nutritive-food entries do not. The genus is famous for two opposite things: sustaining populations through famine, and, when its **seeds** are eaten as a near-sole staple for months, causing **neurolathyrism**, an irreversible spastic paralysis of the legs. The tuberous pea's tubers are the benign face of the genus; its seed-chemistry inherits the family hazard.

**AT — Assyrian (pulse/legume-food class, [native name USER: verify]):** Mesopotamian agriculture and dietetics relied heavily on pulses (lentils, chickling vetch, peas), and Assyrian medical-dietary practice treated such legumes as nourishing, "thickening," and strengthening foods. A tuber-bearing vetchling fits the nutritive-pulse category, but *L. tuberosus* cannot be specifically identified in the tablets. `[USER: verify — genus/food-class attribution only; the chickling vetch L. sativus is the better-attested Near-Eastern Lathyrus.]`

**DMM — De Materia Medica (the *láthyros* / pulse group):** Greek dietetic writers (Dioscorides, and Theophrastus before him) discuss the cultivated pulses — *láthyros*, * eróbinthos* (chickpea), *phakós* (lentil) — as foods with dietary "qualities": nourishing but "thick" and windy, some binding, some thought heavy or hard to concoct. *Lathyrus tuberosus* belongs to this pulse-and-vetchling dietetic family rather than to the acute-drug materia medica; the classical texts describe the genus as *food with humoral properties*, not as a dosed remedy. (Note: the Greek *lathyris* of the drug-lists is **Euphorbia lathyris**, caper spurge — a wholly different, purgative plant — and must not be conflated with the *Lathyrus* pulses.) `[USER: verify the exact referent.]`

**CM — Canon of Medicine (جُلُبَّان — *Julubbān*, the chickling-vetch/vetchling group):** Unani dietetics classes the chickling vetches (*julubbān*) among the nourishing but "thick, windy, and slow-to-digest" pulses — foods that build flesh and strength but burden a weak digestion and generate crude humour if over-eaten. Ibn Sina's frame values such pulses as fattening/strengthening nutritive foods to be balanced with carminative and warming correctives (cumin, etc.). The tuberous pea's edible root falls into this nourishing-pulse dietetic slot.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.**
The doctrine here is a *nutritive-earth* one: a sweet, starchy, grounding root that "builds flesh and strength" — sweet taste (甘/*ḥulw*) read across traditions as tonifying, nourishing, and stabilising. For the **tuber**, the chemistry is straightforwardly supportive: it is a starch-and-protein storage organ, and its sweetness is genuine carbohydrate — the "grounding nourishment" metaphor maps onto ordinary tuber macronutrition, with no exotic pharmacology required. The doctrine holds for the root.

Where the symbolic layer must be **corrected rather than confirmed** is the genus's shadow side, which the ancients could not chemically see. The *Lathyrus* **seeds** (like those of the grass pea *L. sativus*) accumulate the non-protein amino acid **β-N-oxalyl-L-α,β-diaminopropionic acid (β-ODAP, also β-ODAP/BOAA)**, a glutamatergic (AMPA/kainate-type) excitotoxin, together with related neuroactive amino acids such as β-cyanoalanine. Heavy, prolonged, near-monotonous consumption of the *seeds* — historically during droughts and famines when little else was available — produces **neurolathyrism**: a permanent, upper-motor-neuron spastic paraparesis. So the "wholesome nourishing pulse" doctrine is *conditionally* true: nourishing as a varied food, neurotoxic as a famine monodiet of the seed. The tuber does not carry the same seed-borne β-ODAP burden and is the safer organ — but the genus warning belongs on the page.

### Active Components

**Tuber (the food organ):**
- **Starch** — the bulk; the sweet, chestnut-like storage carbohydrate that is the traditional nourishment
- **Protein and free amino acids** — legume storage protein; modest
- **Sugars** — accounting for the noted sweetness
- **Fibre, minerals** — root-vegetable background nutrition

**Seed (the hazard organ — genus-level neurotoxic chemistry):**
- **β-ODAP (β-N-oxalyl-L-α,β-diaminopropionic acid)** — the *Lathyrus* excitotoxic non-protein amino acid; AMPA/kainate glutamate-receptor overactivation → motor-neuron excitotoxicity; the cause of neurolathyrism. Best characterised in *L. sativus*; a genus trait.
- **β-cyanoalanine and related neuroactive amino acids** — additional neurotoxic non-protein amino acids reported across *Lathyrus*.
- Ordinary legume seed protein/starch alongside the toxins.

> Quantitative β-ODAP data are abundant for *L. sativus* (grass pea) and mapped across many *Lathyrus* species; species-specific β-ODAP figures for *L. tuberosus* are sparse in this retrieval. Treat the seed as potentially β-ODAP-bearing by genus default until species-specific assay says otherwise. `[USER: verify L. tuberosus seed β-ODAP content.]`

**1. Nutritive grounding tuber ([PRIME])**
The **cooked tuber** as a sweet, starchy, grounding whole-food — a foraged/cultivated root-vegetable nourishment in the earthy, restorative register, not a dosed medicine. This is the sole use Kenetica endorses for the plant, and it applies to the **root only**.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:

- **β-ODAP as the *Lathyrus* neurotoxin and cause of neurolathyrism (grounds the seed hazard):** Zhang Y et al., *Plant Cell Environ* 2025 — characterises β-ODAP biosynthesis (the LsBAHD3–LsAAE3 module) in *Lathyrus sativus*, and states plainly that β-ODAP is "the causative agent of neurolathyrism when *L. sativus* [is] over-consumed under drought-triggered famines." [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/pce.70167). **Grade: strong for the toxin/mechanism, genus-anchored (L. sativus).**
- **β-ODAP variability across the genus and the stigma of neurolathyrism (supports species-caution + the "some species/organs safer" logic):** Abdallah F et al., *Plants (Basel)* 2024 — screened 183 accessions across 13 *Lathyrus* species for β-ODAP, framing grass-pea's neurolathyrism risk on "prolonged consumption of its grains as a staple diet" and identifying low-β-ODAP wild relatives; establishes that β-ODAP content varies widely by species/accession. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants13223202). **Grade: strong for genus variability; directly motivates species-specific assay before eating seeds.**

**Honest overall grade — DATA-SPARSE for *L. tuberosus* specifically; the meaningful evidence is genus-level and is a hazard, not an efficacy, signal.** What is well established: (a) the tuber is an ordinary nutritive starchy root — the traditional "grounding nourishment" doctrine needs no special pharmacology and is fine; (b) the *genus Lathyrus* seeds carry β-ODAP, a real, well-documented excitotoxin causing irreversible neurolathyrism on heavy prolonged seed-monodiet. What is **not** established in this retrieval: any therapeutic action of *L. tuberosus*, and the precise β-ODAP content of *L. tuberosus* seed. The net editorial stance: eat the cooked **tuber** as food; **do not** treat the seed as a staple; make **no** medicinal claims.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** Mesopotamian dietetics treated the pulses as nourishing, "thickening," strengthening foods, and a tuber-bearing vetchling fits that nutritive-pulse slot — a substance placed by its dietary quality and the strength it was thought to build, not by any working on a vital constitution. But *Lathyrus tuberosus* cannot be identified in the tablets; the better-attested Near-Eastern *Lathyrus* is the chickling vetch *L. sativus*, and the attribution here is genus / food-class only. Under the standing blocking warning in force for every Assyrian substance identification and tablet reference, no cultivation status is claimed. Cultivation status: **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The Greek dietetic writers place the cultivated pulses — the *láthyros* group alongside chickpea and lentil — as foods carrying humoral qualities: nourishing but thick, windy, some binding, some heavy to concoct. This is dietetics, not the acute-drug materia medica, and Dioscorides carries no subtle-body doctrine into which a cultivation status could be entered; the plant is described as food with qualities, not as a substance worked on the person's vitality. (The *lathyris* of his drug-lists is *Euphorbia lathyris*, caper spurge — a different, purgative plant, not this pulse, and the two must not be conflated.) What the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention — the correct identification of the pulse against dangerous look-alikes, the judgement of a food heavy on a weak digestion. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** Unani dietetics classes the chickling vetches (*julubbān*) among the nourishing but "thick, windy, and slow-to-digest" pulses — foods that build flesh and strength yet burden a weak digestion and breed crude humour if over-eaten, to be balanced with warming carminative correctives such as cumin. This is a genuine humoral-dietetic placement of the food, and the tuberous root falls into that nourishing-pulse slot. But it is a dietetic quality, not a cultivation status: the tradition's strongest cultivation idea, the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture across the temperate regimen, is not assigned to this pulse, and no rejuvenative or *mufarrih* standing is attested for the species. **(b) framed:** attested as a humorally-classed nutritive food, with no cultivation status recorded.

**Practitioner's note.** What a cultivator does with the tuberous pea is eat the cooked **tuber** as ordinary grounding, sweet-starchy nourishment — a foraged root-vegetable in the earthy restorative register, not a dosed medicine and not a longevity substance. No tradition frames it as rejuvenative; two assign it no cultivation status at all, and the third treats it as a strengthening but burdensome food to be corrected, not accumulated. The one non-negotiable discipline is genus safety: never let the **seeds** of any *Lathyrus* become a large, sustained fraction of the diet — that famine-monodiet pattern is what causes irreversible neurolathyrism. Eat the root as food; leave the seed alone.

### Contraindications

- **⚠️⚠️ Neurolathyrism / seed hazard (the defining warning):** as a *Lathyrus*, the plant belongs to the genus that causes neurolathyrism — an **irreversible spastic paralysis of the legs** — when its **seeds** are eaten as a large, sustained, near-sole part of the diet (the classic famine-monodiet pattern). **Do not adopt the seeds as a staple food.** The **tubers** are the safer organ and the only part Kenetica endorses as food, but never let any *Lathyrus* seed dominate the diet. Assay species-specific β-ODAP before considering seed use. `[USER: verify]`
- **Populations at higher risk:** adolescents and young adult males, and the undernourished, are historically most susceptible to neurolathyrism; keep any *Lathyrus* intake varied and modest.
- **No medicinal-dose use:** there is no evidence base for therapeutic dosing of *L. tuberosus*; use is culinary only.
- **Foraging safety:** the Fabaceae contain many toxic look-alikes (toxic vetches, laburnum, etc.); positively identify *L. tuberosus*, cook the tubers thoroughly, and avoid roadside/contaminated ground.
- **Pregnancy/children:** absent species-specific safety data, keep to ordinary culinary tuber amounts only; avoid seeds entirely. `[USER: verify]`
