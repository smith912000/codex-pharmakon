# Cyclamen europaeum — Kuklaminos, Cyclaminus (Dioscorides 2-194)

## *Cyclamen europaeum, printed "Cyclamen europaem [Linnaeus]", one of the binomials on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 2-194 (which also prints Cyclamen cyclaminus, Cyclamen littorale and Cyclamen officinale — "Cyclamen, Sow Bread, Bleeding Nun", and Cyclamen graecum — "Greek Cyclamen")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM); this entry rests on one text, the Osbaldeston and Wood (2000) English translation of *De Materia Medica*, and on one chapter of it.** The binomial is the translators' SUGGESTED gloss, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. A search of the Sushruta, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Tibetan text folders for *Cyclamen* found no hit in the primary texts, and the row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The row cites chapter 2-194, *KUKLAMINOS* (heading at line 6222, SUGGESTED line 6223, text at lines 6224 to 6229, printed pages 322 to 323), in Book Two among the "Herbs with a sharp quality" (the section runs from printed page 304; the page header "HERBS WITH A SHARP QUALITY" stands on page 323). The whole chapter was read.

*The plant.* The text says: "Cyclaminus has leaves like cissus, a purple colour, varied, with whitish spots beneath and above; a stalk of four fingers high, bare and naked, on which are flowers similar to roses, tending to a purple colour; the root black, very similar to rape [coleseed] root and somewhat broader." It "grows in shady places, especially under trees". The woodcut caption is "Cyclaminus rotundus from FUCHS — 1545" (line 6232).

*Uses.* The text records, in this order: "Taken as a drink with honey water it drives phlegm and water [fluids] downwards. It induces the menstrual flow either taken in a drink or placed below. They say that if a woman great with child walks over the root that she aborts, and that tied around her it hastens the birth. It is taken as a drink with wine against deadly poisons, especially against the sea hare. Applied, it is a remedy against the bites of snakes, and mixed with wine it causes drunkenness." Then the jaundice use: "Three teaspoonfuls (taken in a drink with passum [raisin wine] or honey diluted in water) drives away yellow jaundice, but whoever drinks it must lie down on his bed in a warm house not open to the wind, and be covered with many cloths so that he may sweat (more easily). The sweat that comes out is found to be a bile colour." Then: "Juice from cyclaminus is put into the nostrils with honey to purge the head. It is inserted on wool into the perineum to bring down excrement from the bowels. Rubbed on the navel and the lower part of the bowels and the hips it softens the bowels, and produces abortions. The juice rubbed on with honey is good for bathing the eyes and moisture of the eyes. It is also mixed with medicines that cause abortion. The juice rubbed on with vinegar restores a fallen perineum." Then the root's external uses: "The root with vinegar (either alone or with honey) cleans the skin, stops pustules from breaking out and cures wounds. Applied, it softens the spleen (and reduces it). It takes away sunburn, and [repairs] the loss of hair. A decoction applied with hot cloths is suitable for dislocations, gout, little ulcers on the head and chilblains. Boiled in old oil and the oil smeared on, it brings wounds to a scar."

*Preparations.* "The pounded root is juiced and squeezed out, the juice then boiled to the consistency of honey." "The root is made hollow, filled with oil, and set over warm ashes (and sometimes a little Tyrrhenian [Etruscan] wax is added so that it is gluey). This is an excellent ointment for those troubled with chilblains." "The root is kept in storage, cut in pieces like squill. It is said that it is pounded and made into lozenges and taken with catapotia [pills]."

*Names.* "It is also called cissanthemon, cissophylon, chelonion, ichthyotheron, chuline, zoroastris, or trimphalites; Osthenes calls it aspho, the Magi, miaspho, the Egyptians, theske, the Romans, rapum terrae, umbilicus terrae, or orbicularis, and it is also called arcar." The attributions to Osthenes, the Magi and the Egyptians are the text's own reports of other peoples' names and are not read as other traditions' sources.

*The next chapter.* **The two cyclamen binomials of this batch do not share a chapter.** Chapter 2-195, *KUKLAMINOS ETERA* ("the other cyclamen"), is a separate chapter at line 6240 with its own SUGGESTED line (*Cyclamen psuedo-graecum*); it is written as a separate entry (I_1398). The binomial of this entry belongs to 2-194 only.

**Identity doubt.** The translators give four *Cyclamen* binomials for 2-194, so the spine's *C. europaeum* is one of several offered. The description (leaves like cissus with whitish spots, a bare four-finger stalk, rose-like purplish flowers, a black root like rape but broader) does not by itself decide the species. **Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Cyclamen*, *cyclaminus*, *kuklaminos* and the chapter number 2-194 found no entry on this chapter. ### Active Components

The text prints no constituent. As a general fact, cyclamen tubers contain saponins, which are irritant when eaten and toxic to people and animals in quantity. That is a general statement about the genus and not a finding about the plant in the text. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a root taken or applied to drive down phlegm and water, to bring on the menses, to treat jaundice by sweating, to purge the head, to loosen the bowels, as an antidote to poisons and against snakebite, and as an external remedy for skin, spleen, gout and chilblains. It also records abortifacient and labour-quickening uses. The Codex gives no protocol for any of these. By the text's own account the root drives fluids downward and produces abortions.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these preparations, each with its locus (line 6224 to 6229): root taken in honey water; in wine; "Three teaspoonfuls" in passum or honey diluted in water for jaundice, followed by lying warm and covered to sweat; juice into the nostrils with honey; juice on wool into the perineum; juice rubbed on the navel, bowels and hips; juice with honey or vinegar; juice boiled to the thickness of honey; root with vinegar (alone or with honey); a decoction applied with hot cloths; root boiled in old oil; an ointment made in a hollowed root with oil and Etruscan wax; and lozenges with pills. The only measure is the three teaspoonfuls for jaundice. No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that saponin-rich roots irritate the gut and can cause vomiting and diarrhoea, which would be consistent in kind with the "downward" and bowel-loosening actions the text records. The identification is the translators' suggestion, so the chapter does not show that the plant it describes is the species named.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality. The translators' section heading groups it among the "Herbs with a sharp quality" (printed page 304 onward), which is a grouping in the book and not a quality the chapter states in words.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: the plant is fixed by its leaves (like cissus, purple, whitish-spotted), a bare four-finger stalk, rose-like purple flowers and a black round root broader than rape; the place is shady ground under trees; and storage is given (the root cut in pieces like squill). The text records a folk report, "They say that if a woman great with child walks over the root that she aborts, and that tied around her it hastens the birth", with the distancing "They say" and a second, "It is said", for the lozenges; recording is not endorsing. The chapter assigns the plant no status beyond that of a medicinal root. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The useful lesson is Dioscorides' method of fixing a plant by leaf, stalk, flower, root and place, and of stating a measure with the instructions for the patient who takes it (lie warm, covered, until the sweat comes). The abstentive answer is the honest one: the text describes a root that drives fluids downward and produces abortions.

### Contraindications

The chapter itself records that the root induces menstruation and abortion, that mixed with wine it causes drunkenness, and that jaundice treatment requires the patient to sweat under covers. Cyclamen tubers are toxic if eaten (a general fact), and the sources read say nothing of its conservation. The identification here is a translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. No safety information for pregnancy beyond the text's own abortion reports was read. [USER: verify]
