# Phillyrea latifolia — Phillurea, mock privet (Dioscorides 1-123, second chapter of that number)

## *Phillyrea latifolia, the single binomial on the translators' SUGGESTED line for this chapter ("Mock Privet, Jasmine Box"); a tree with olive-like leaves and black fruit in bunches* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter, and no other tradition on disk is tagged for it. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**A chapter number printed twice.** The translators print the number 1-123 for two chapters: *1-123. KUNOSBATON* (heading at line 4443, the dog rose in the translators' suggestion) and then, after *1-124. KUPROS* (line 4450), *1-123. PHILLUREA* (line 4453). The next number printed after this chapter is 1-126 (line 4459); 1-125 does not appear as a chapter heading (a search of the heading lines gives 1-123 twice, 1-124 once and 1-126 once). The name index (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 127) carries the note "chapter number printed twice in the translation". The Codex therefore cites the chapter as "1-123 (the second so numbered, line 4453)", and the spine row's "1-123 (line 4453)" is the line, not the number, that identifies it. Whether the true number is 1-125 is a guess that the sources on disk do not settle. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *PHILLUREA*, heading at line 4453, the SUGGESTED line at line 4454 and the text at line 4455, in Book One among the trees (the running head near it reads "GUMS from TREES", on the translators' page 122). The whole chapter reads:

"Phillyrea is a tree like cyprus [1-124] in size. Its leaves are like those of the olive tree but broader and darker. It has fruit like the lentisk [1-90] — black, somewhat sweet, lying in bunches like grapes. It grows in rough places. The leaves are astringent like those of the wild olive [1-37], effective for things that need astringency especially ulcers in the mouth, either chewed or the sores washed with a decoction of it. Taken as a drink it draws out urine and the menstrual flow."

The description is by three comparisons and a habitat: the tree is the size of *cyprus* (1-124, the henna shrub in the translators' suggestion, whose chapter stands just before it in the print), its leaves are like the olive's "but broader and darker", its fruit is like the lentisk's (1-90), black and "somewhat sweet", in bunches like grapes, and it grows in rough places. The one quality is astringency, stated by comparison with the wild olive (1-37). The uses are two: for ulcers in the mouth, the leaves chewed or the sores washed with a decoction, and, drunk, to "draw out urine and the menstrual flow". The chapter gives no other names, no deity and no amuletic use.

**The chapters around it.** 1-124 *KUPROS* (line 4450) stands before it in the print and 1-126 *KUSTOS ARREN, KUSTOS THELUS* (line 4459, the rockrose) after it. The second 1-123, *KUNOSBATON* (line 4443), is a different plant. None is carried over to this entry.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter number 1-123 and for the names *phillyrea*, *phillurea* and "mock privet" found no existing entry on the same chapter. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The composition of the tree Dioscorides describes was not read. As a general fact, *Phillyrea* belongs to the olive family (Oleaceae), and its leaves, like those of its relative the olive, are rich in phenolic compounds; the astringency Dioscorides records is a taste commonly given by such compounds. No constituent is named in the chapter. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records an astringent leaf chewed or decocted for mouth ulcers, and a drink that draws out urine and the menstrual flow. The Codex gives no protocol for either. The menstrual use is of the emmenagogue kind and carries a risk in pregnancy.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives two preparations, with locus (line 4455): the leaves "chewed", or the sores "washed with a decoction of it"; and the plant "taken as a drink" (the part is not stated) for urine and the menstrual flow. No dose, quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that astringent plant preparations can briefly tighten and soothe the lining of the mouth, and that mouth ulcers which persist need assessment. The identification is a translators' suggestion, so the chapter does not show that the plant it describes is *Phillyrea latifolia*.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one quality: the leaves "are astringent like those of the wild olive", and the fruit is "somewhat sweet". It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality for this chapter.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this tree. The abstentive answer is the honest one: a tree known by its comparisons, and an astringent leaf for the mouth.

### Contraindications

A drink that "draws out the menstrual flow" is not to be taken in pregnancy. Persistent mouth ulcers need medical assessment. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, and the chapter's number is itself uncertain, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
