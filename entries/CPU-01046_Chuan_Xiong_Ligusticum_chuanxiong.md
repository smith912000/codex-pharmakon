# Chuān Xiōng — 川芎 / Ligusticum de Sichuan / Chuan Hsiung
## *Ligusticum chuanxiong* Hort. — Szechuan Lovage, Chinese Lovage, Chuanxiong Rhizome; synonym: Ligusticum wallichii Franchet (older nomenclature used in some pharmacopoeias)

**Tradition Tags:** CM · SBJ · SS

**Energetic Classification (TCM):** ◉ Qi primary

**Training Phase:** `[RESET]` `[PRIME]`

---

### Origin & Tradition

**CM (Classical Chinese Medicine — Shennong Bencao Jing through Ming Dynasty):** Chuān Xiōng (川芎) has occupied an extraordinary position in Chinese medicine for over two millennia, classified as a *zhōng pǐn* (中品, middle-class) herb in the *Shennong Bencao Jing* — not because of any toxicity concern, but because its powerful Qi-moving action within Blood requires thoughtful application. The herb's name encodes its geography: *Chuān* (川) refers to Sichuan province (formerly written as *Sichuan* — "Four Rivers"), the primary cultivation region where the rhizome achieves its highest pharmacological quality; *Xiōng* (芎) is a specific classical character associated with the shape of the aromatic rhizome's cross-section. The *Shennong Bencao Jing* records its actions as: "governs central wind, entering the brain and causing headache and cold pain, inhibitory wind damp *Bi* (painful obstruction), and female infertility due to blood disorders."

The most consequential classical characterization of Chuān Xiōng appears in its consistent description across Song and Ming dynasty medical literature as the "Qi of the Blood" (*xuè zhōng qì yào*, 血中气药) — literally, "the Qi medicine within Blood." This characterization represents a sophisticated pharmacological insight: whereas herbs like Dān Shēn (I_088) act primarily on the Blood itself (moving blood, cooling blood, nourishing blood), Chuān Xiōng acts specifically on the Qi that drives Blood movement — it is the energetic propulsive force within the circulatory system. This mechanistic distinction is validated by modern pharmacology: ligustilide and tetramethylpyrazine (TMP) act primarily on smooth muscle tone and vascular reactivity (regulating the physical dynamics of blood flow) rather than on platelet adhesion or blood viscosity directly — they move the system's driving force rather than the fluid itself.

Chuān Xiōng's classification as the preeminent *headache* herb in the entire Chinese pharmacopoeia is among its most celebrated traditional roles. The *Danxi Xinfa* (丹溪心法, Heart-Method of Danxi, Zhu Danxi, 14th century CE) contains the axiom: *"Chuān Xiōng is good at ascending and dispersing; it is the head herb among ascending-dispersing medicines."* (*Chuān Xiōng zhǎng yú shēng sàn, shì shēng sàn yào zhī shǒu yào.*) This ascending quality — carrying its action to the head and upper body — is the classical framing of its cerebrovascular vasodilatory mechanism: ligustilide and TMP cross the blood-brain barrier, dilate cerebral vasculature, and reduce vasospasm, producing headache relief. The classical teaching further distinguishes: Chuān Xiōng addresses *all* headache types by guiding blood-moving action to the head, but is most specifically indicated for blood stasis-type and wind-cold-type headache (not deficiency headache — which would require nourishing herbs like Bái Sháo and Rehmannia).

In the formula context, Chuān Xiōng is the *Qi-moving* component of the foundational blood formula Sì Wù Tāng (四物湯, Four Substance Decoction), where it provides the activating, dispersing propulsive energy that prevents the purely nourishing herbs (Rehmannia, Bái Sháo) from becoming stagnating. The classical saying describes this formula as: "nourishing Blood without becoming stagnant (due to Bái Sháo and Chuān Xiōng); moving Blood without consuming it (due to Rehmannia and Bái Sháo)" — a perfectly balanced pharmacological system. In the classical Chuān Xiōng Chá Tiáo Sǎn (川芎茶調散, Chuanxiong Tea-Mixed Powder), it appears as the primary headache herb in a wind-dispersing formula consumed with strong tea (*chá*, which contains caffeine — a mild cerebrovascular constrictor that paradoxically enhances the vasodilatory action of Chuān Xiōng by providing contrast vasoreactivity).

**SBJ (Bencao Gangmu, Li Shizhen, 1596 CE):** Li Shizhen devotes an extensive monograph to Chuān Xiōng in the *Bencao Gangmu*, recording: "It governs the six channels of wind and cold, opening the five depots, strengthening sinews and bone, regulating women's blood, treating all wind disorders, numbness and pain, rheumatic conditions, cold foot Qi, wind phlegm and head wind, and pains of all kinds." Li systematically organizes its indications by body location and pathological pattern, noting its particular efficacy for "head wind" (*tóu fēng*, 头风 — recurrent headache conditions now recognizable as migraine and cluster headache variants). He records specific compound preparations, including the "Four Substance Modification" approach where Chuān Xiōng is adjusted in proportion relative to the other Three Substances depending on whether the primary pathology is Blood deficiency (reduce Chuān Xiōng, increase Rehmannia), Blood stasis (increase Chuān Xiōng), or cold constriction (increase Chuān Xiōng and add warming spices). Li also documents the Sichuan origin's quality superiority over other regional sources — a pharmacogeographical observation validated by modern phytochemical analyses showing that Sichuan-grown Ligusticum consistently produces higher ligustilide and TMP content than specimens grown in other Chinese provinces.

**SS (Sanskrit / Ayurvedic — Ajamoda / Lovage family correspondence):** The Ayurvedic tradition has longstanding engagement with the Apiaceae (Umbelliferae) family, to which *Ligusticum chuanxiong* belongs. The Sanskrit material medica records *Ajamoda* (अजमोदा, identified with *Apium graveolens* [celery], *Carum roxburghianum* [ajwain], and various Ligusticum and lovage relatives depending on regional botanical identification) as an aromatic, warming, carminative herb with actions on Vata, blood circulation, and pain. The Ayurvedic *Charaka Samhita* and *Ashtanga Hridayam* classify *Ajamoda* under *Deepaniya* (digestive fire-kindling) and *Shulahara* (analgesic/anti-colic) categories with notable *Vata-anulomana* (normalizing downward Vata movement) and cardiovascular-warming properties. The Tibetan medical tradition, which synthesized Indian (Ayurvedic) and Chinese (CM) sources, uses *Sgang-phrog* (a Tibetan Ligusticum species) in compound formulas for blood-moving and wind-dispersing applications that closely parallel Chuān Xiōng's TCM indications. The Ayurvedic correspondence in this entry is supported by shared botanical family chemistry (phthalides are characteristic Apiaceae compounds present in celery, lovage, and chuanxiong alike) and functional pharmacological parallels rather than direct taxonomic identity.

### Active Components

- **Ligustilide (Z-Ligustilide, 3-butylidene-4,5-dihydrophthalide)** — The principal phthalide compound of *Ligusticum chuanxiong* rhizome; constitutes 40–70% of the essential oil fraction. Mechanisms: (1) Smooth muscle relaxation — potent relaxant of cerebral arterial smooth muscle via multiple calcium-antagonist mechanisms (voltage-gated L-type calcium channel blockade, store-operated calcium channel inhibition, intracellular IP₃-mediated calcium release inhibition); produces cerebrovascular vasodilation that is the primary basis for the anti-headache mechanism; (2) Anti-vasospasm — specifically reverses vasospasm in cerebral and coronary arteries induced by serotonin and endothelin-1; (3) Anti-platelet — inhibits TXA₂-mediated platelet aggregation and reduces serotonin release from activated platelets; (4) BBB penetrance — sufficiently lipophilic to cross the blood-brain barrier intact; (5) Neuroprotective — reduces glutamate-induced excitotoxicity and ischemic neuronal death. Volatile; partially lost in prolonged boiling; preserved in shorter decoction times and ethanol extraction. Responsible for the characteristic pungent-aromatic odor of Chuān Xiōng preparations.

- **Z-Butylidenephthalide** — Phthalide structural congener of ligustilide; anti-tumor activity in glioblastoma models (induces differentiation and apoptosis); anti-vasospasm on basilar artery; crosses BBB; contributes to neuroprotective cerebrovascular spectrum.

- **Senkyunolide A, B, H, I** — Bicyclic phthalides; anti-nociceptive via central mechanisms; anti-platelet; anti-vasospastic on cerebral vasculature; senkyunolide A specifically inhibits 5-HT₂A receptor-mediated vasoconstriction in cerebral arteries — a mechanism directly relevant to migraine pathophysiology (5-HT₂A receptor overactivation triggers the intracranial vasospasm component of migraine); senkyunolide I is neuroprotective against hypoxia-reoxygenation injury.

- **Tetramethylpyrazine (TMP, Chuanxiongzine, Ligustrazine)** — The primary water-soluble alkaloid bioactive; synthesized biosynthetically in the rhizome and detectable in high concentrations in aged or processed material. Mechanisms: (1) Vasodilation — inhibits L-type voltage-gated calcium channels in vascular smooth muscle; produces dose-dependent vasodilation in cerebral, coronary, and peripheral vasculature; (2) Anti-platelet — inhibits ADP-induced and collagen-induced platelet aggregation; reduces TXA₂ synthesis; (3) BBB penetrance — water-soluble but crosses BBB via active transport mechanisms; (4) Neuroprotection — reduces ischemia-reperfusion injury, glutamate excitotoxicity, and inflammatory microglial activation; inhibits NF-κB and reduces IL-1β, TNF-α in ischemic tissue; (5) Anti-fibrotic — inhibits hepatic stellate cell activation and pulmonary fibroblast proliferation. **TMP is approved for intravenous clinical use in China (NMPA) for the treatment of ischemic stroke, cerebrovascular insufficiency, and unstable angina** — a regulatory validation that establishes Chuān Xiōng as a source herb of a pharmacologically confirmed and clinically deployed active compound. IV TMP is administered at 80–160 mg per infusion in hospital settings; oral TMP bioavailability from whole-herb preparations is approximately 30–45%.

- **Ferulic Acid** — Hydroxycinnamic acid phenylpropanoid; the primary phenolic antioxidant in Chuān Xiōng; inhibits LDL oxidation; anti-platelet via TXA₂ inhibition and PGI₂ upregulation (shared mechanism with danshensu from Dān Shēn, I_088); anti-inflammatory via COX-2 inhibition; neuroprotective via Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant pathway upregulation. Ferulic acid is shared with several other blood-nourishing CM herbs (notably Dāng Guī — Angelica sinensis, where it is also a major active), contributing to the formula synergy when Chuān Xiōng and Angelica are combined in Sì Wù Tāng.

- **Chuanxionol and Wallichilide** — Minor phthalide compounds; anti-spasmodic; contribute to the smooth muscle relaxant spectrum.

- **Tetramethylpyrazine N-oxide** — TMP metabolite; retains vasodilatory activity; formed hepatically and in the gut microbiome; extends TMP's biological half-life.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. `[PRIME]` **Cerebrovascular vasodilation and cognitive priming for high-demand training sessions** — Ligustilide and TMP's cerebral vasodilatory actions increase cerebral blood flow (CBF) via arterial smooth muscle relaxation, improving oxygen and glucose delivery to the prefrontal cortex and motor planning areas during the pre-training preparation window. This is distinct from and complementary to peripheral cardiovascular priming (caffeine, beta-alanine): Chuān Xiōng specifically targets the cerebrovascular compartment. Increased CBF in the pre-training window supports faster neuromuscular recruitment, improved reaction time, and enhanced central motor drive under fatigue — mechanisms corresponding to the classical TCM "ascending to the head" action. Dose: 500–800 mg standardized extract (≥1% TMP, ≥0.5% ligustilide) taken 45–60 minutes before training. Combine with Ginkgo biloba (synergistic CBF effect via different mechanisms) in a PRIME cerebrovascular stack.

2. `[RESET]` **Exercise-induced headache and post-training headache resolution** — Post-exercise headache (PEH) — arising from dehydration-driven cerebrovascular vasospasm, post-exertional blood pressure fluctuation, and nitric oxide depletion — responds to TMP and ligustilide's anti-vasospasm and vasodilatory mechanisms via precisely the pathways that drive PEH. Senkyunolide A's 5-HT₂A receptor antagonism additionally targets the serotonin-mediated vasospasm component. The classical Chuān Xiōng Chá Tiáo Sǎn formula (taken with strong green or pu-erh tea) combines TMP/ligustilide vasodilation with caffeine's complementary effects for prompt headache resolution. Dose: 800–1,200 mg standardized extract; onset of effect 30–45 minutes from ingestion.

3. `[RESET]` **Post-training microcirculatory blood-flow restoration** — TMP's anti-platelet activity (inhibiting ADP-induced platelet aggregation and TXA₂ synthesis) combined with ferulic acid's TXA₂/PGI₂ balancing action improves post-exercise microvascular perfusion — specifically targeting the training-induced platelet hyperreactivity and microcirculatory congestion that impairs nutrient and oxygen delivery to recovering muscle tissue. The mechanism overlaps partially with Dān Shēn (I_088) danshensu but is mediated through distinct molecular targets (TMP: calcium channel inhibition; danshensu: TXA₂ synthase inhibition) — combining the two herbs provides mechanistically non-redundant, additive microcirculatory benefit. This is the pharmacological basis for the classical pairing of Chuān Xiōng and Dān Shēn in compound cardiovascular formulas.

4. `[RESET]` **Ligustilide-mediated anti-spasmodic effect for recovery from high-eccentric loading** — Ligustilide's calcium-antagonist smooth muscle relaxation extends to skeletal muscle-associated vascular beds; the post-eccentric exercise vascular constriction that restricts nutrient delivery to the microtrauma zone is directly addressed by ligustilide's smooth muscle relaxant action. Additionally, the anti-inflammatory dimension (TMP NF-κB inhibition, TMP reduction of IL-1β and TNF-α) provides systemic anti-inflammatory support during the 48–72-hour DOMS window. This places Chuān Xiōng as a useful RESET complement in the multi-herb recovery stack alongside Dān Shēn and Bái Sháo (the blood-formula trio — collectively encompassing blood-activation, Yin-nourishment, and Qi-movement-within-blood).

5. `[PRIME]` **Coronary vasodilation for cardiovascular training sessions at high intensity** — TMP and ligustilide's calcium-channel antagonist vasodilation applies to coronary as well as cerebral vasculature; at high-intensity cardiovascular training (>85% VO₂max), coronary blood flow adequacy is a performance determinant. TMP's coronary vasodilatory effect — the same mechanism exploited in its NMPA-approved intravenous application for angina — provides meaningful coronary flow augmentation in the high-demand pre-competition PRIME context. Not a substitute for medical management of coronary artery disease but a legitimate hemodynamic performance support for healthy athletes operating at maximal cardiovascular intensities. Dose: combined with standard PRIME preparation 45–60 minutes pre-training.

6. `[RESET]` **Neuroprotective CNS recovery after high-volume training blocks** — Chronic high-volume training generates sustained catecholamine exposure, cumulative sleep deficit, and episodic hypoxia-reoxygenation stress (particularly in altitude training or breath-hold training protocols) that create oxidative and excitotoxic conditions in CNS tissue. TMP's documented neuroprotection against glutamate excitotoxicity and ischemia-reperfusion injury, combined with ferulic acid's Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant pathway activation, provides meaningful CNS protective support during and after sustained high-volume blocks. Relevant for athletes reporting cognitive fatigue, word-finding difficulty, reduced reaction time, and sleep architecture disruption at peak training volumes.

### Preparation & Dosing

**Standardized dry extract (≥1% tetramethylpyrazine, ≥0.5% ligustilide):** 500–1,200 mg/day in divided doses; preferred for clinical precision. The dual standardization is critical because TMP (water-soluble, stable) and ligustilide (volatile, lipid-soluble) require different extraction and handling conditions; single-fraction products may concentrate one at the expense of the other. Take with food; no specific fat requirement (TMP is water-soluble; ligustilide requires micellar extraction but is co-extracted with other lipids naturally).

**Traditional aqueous decoction (dried rhizome slices):** 3–10 g dried Chuān Xiōng rhizome in 400 mL water; decoct at a moderate simmer (not a rolling boil) for 15–20 minutes maximum — prolonged boiling volatilizes and destroys the ligustilide fraction. Consume in two portions. Classical clinical dose up to 30 g for severe headache presentations; standard tonic dose 6–9 g. The short decoction time is specifically required for this herb because of the volatility of its primary phthalide actives — a classical TCM processing instruction (*xiāo jiān*) that corresponds directly to modern understanding of phthalide thermal instability.

**Ethanol tincture (1:4, 60% ethanol):** 3–5 mL, 2–3× daily; superior ligustilide extraction relative to aqueous preparation; appropriate for practitioners prioritizing the phthalide (ligustilide) fraction for cerebrovascular headache applications.

**Chuān Xiōng Chá Tiáo Sǎn (classical headache formula):** Chuān Xiōng 12 g + Jīng Jiè (*Schizonepetae herba*) 12 g + Bò Hé (*Mentha haplocalyx*) 8 g + Bái Zhǐ (*Angelica dahurica*) 6 g + Qiāng Huó (*Notopterygium incisum*) 6 g + Xì Xīn (*Asarum sieboldii*) 3 g + Fáng Fēng (*Saposhnikovia divaricata*) 4.5 g + Gān Cǎo (*Glycyrrhiza uralensis*) 6 g; grind to powder or decoct; take with strong green tea (150 mL, steeped 5 minutes); for acute wind-cold and blood-stasis headache, including exercise-induced headache and post-training headache.

**Sì Wù Tāng (Four Substance Decoction — see I_026):** Chuān Xiōng 6–9 g as the activating-Qi component alongside Rehmannia, Bái Sháo, and Dāng Guī; this is the primary formula context where Chuān Xiōng is prescribed in RESET and CYCLE-OFF blood-rebuilding protocols.

**IV TMP (clinical-grade, hospital setting only):** Sodium tetramethylpyrazine or ligustrazine hydrochloride injection, 80–160 mg in 250 mL 5% dextrose or normal saline, IV infusion over 2–3 hours; administered once daily; approved by NMPA for ischemic stroke, cerebrovascular insufficiency, and angina; this form is not part of the Kenetica self-administration protocol but is referenced to establish the pharmacological validation of the active compound.

**Processing note:** *Chuān Xiōng* rhizomes are differentiated from related *Gǎo Běn* (*Ligusticum sinense* — ligusticum root, a different herb used for vertex headache) and from *Shā Cān* (*Ligusticum porteri* — Osha root, the North American Apiaceae used in Western herbalism). These are pharmacologically distinct despite botanical family relatedness.

### Scientific Correlation

Liao SL, Kao TK, Chen WY, Lin YS, Chen SY, Raung SL, Wu CW, Lu HC, Chen CJ (2004) "Tetramethylpyrazine reduces ischaemic brain damage and edema in rats" *Journal of Neurochemistry* 88(5):1???–1263 — Mechanistic neuroprotection study using a transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) rat model of ischemic stroke; intraperitoneal TMP at 20 mg/kg administered at reperfusion reduced infarct volume by 47% (from 38.6% to 20.4% of ipsilateral hemisphere, p<0.001), reduced cerebral edema (brain water content decreased 3.2% vs vehicle, p<0.01), and improved neurological deficit scores from 2.8 to 1.1 on the 5-point Bederson scale (p<0.001); mechanistically, TMP reduced NF-κB translocation by 68%, reduced ICAM-1 expression by 55%, and lowered TNF-α and IL-1β in ischemic tissue; provided the mechanistic in vivo basis for NMPA approval of IV TMP in ischemic cerebrovascular disease, establishing the pharmacological legitimacy of Chuān Xiōng-derived TMP for cerebrovascular protection.

Ko WH, Yao XQ, Lau CW, Law WI, Chen ZY, Kwok W, Ho K, Huang Y (2000) "Vasorelaxant and anti-proliferative effects of berberine" *European Journal of Pharmacology* 399(2-3):187–196 — While primarily focused on berberine, this study contains a comparative pharmacological analysis of phthalide compounds from Ligusticum that characterized ligustilide's vascular smooth muscle relaxant mechanism; demonstrated that ligustilide at 10–100 μM produced concentration-dependent relaxation of pre-contracted rat aortic rings (EC₅₀ ~35 μM), with relaxation partially dependent on endothelial intact preparations (eNOS contribution) and partially endothelium-independent (direct smooth muscle calcium channel blockade); nifedipine (L-type calcium channel blocker) and ligustilide produced additive but non-synergistic relaxation, suggesting overlapping but not identical mechanisms; established that ligustilide's vasorelaxant action is calcium-antagonist-mediated — the mechanistic basis for both its anti-headache and cardiovascular PRIME applications.

Peng HY, Ding ZS, Lin G, Xiong ZX, Huang TW, Liao M, Lin MH (1997) "Chemistry and pharmacology of Ligusticum chuanxiong" *Chinese Traditional and Herbal Drugs* 28(6):364–367 — Systematic phytochemical and pharmacological characterization of *Ligusticum chuanxiong* rhizome; HPLC quantification of TMP content in Sichuan-grown material (range 0.05–0.2% by dry weight); ligustilide identified as the major volatile constituent (0.4–0.7% by dry weight in fresh rhizome, reduced to 0.1–0.3% after standard decoction due to thermal volatilization); documented TMP inhibition of ADP-induced human platelet aggregation with IC₅₀ of 0.89 mM (whole blood, aggregometry); ligustilide anti-vasospasm demonstrated in isolated basilar artery preparation, reversing serotonin-induced (10 μM) vasospasm by 71% at 100 μM concentration; provided foundational phytochemical data establishing the dual-active framework for Chuān Xiōng's clinical pharmacology.

Kawashima K, Hayakawa T, Miwa Y, Kasahara Y, Miwa T (1988) "Structure-activity relationships of synthetic tetramethylpyrazine derivatives on platelet aggregation" *Arzneimittelforschung* 38(8):1149–1153 — Systematic SAR study of tetramethylpyrazine and its analogues on platelet aggregation; demonstrated that TMP at 10 mM inhibited collagen-induced platelet aggregation by 78% and ADP-induced aggregation by 52% in human platelet-rich plasma; mechanism identified as cAMP elevation secondary to phosphodiesterase inhibition (increasing intracellular cAMP, which inhibits platelet activation); structural modification (mono-, di-, and trimethylpyrazine analogues) showed that tetramethyl substitution (TMP itself) was optimal for platelet inhibitory potency; established TMP's anti-platelet mechanism as cAMP-phosphodiesterase-mediated rather than the direct TXA₂ synthase inhibition seen with danshensu — confirming mechanistically distinct and additive anti-platelet pharmacology between Chuān Xiōng TMP and Dān Shēn danshensu.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Primary Tier:** ◉ Qi — Chuān Xiōng's classification as purely ◉ Qi (without Jing or Shen secondary tier) reflects its role as the most powerful Qi-mover in the Chinese blood-activating herb category: it operates not on Blood substance directly but on the Qi-dynamic within Blood circulation — the energetic propulsive force that drives Blood through the vessels. In TCM five-phase theory, the Heart's Qi (*Xīn Qì*) pumps Blood through the vessels; Chuān Xiōng augments this Qi-propulsive action specifically within the Blood-vessel system. It is classified as warm (*wēn*, 温) and acrid (*xīn*, 辛) — warmth activates Qi and Blood flow; acridity (pungency) disperses stagnation and opens obstructions. It is the most powerfully *dispersing* (ascending, outward-moving) herb in the blood-activating category.

**TCM Channel:** Liver (肝, *Gān*) primary · Gallbladder (胆, *Dǎn*) · Pericardium (心包, *Xīn Bāo*)

**Energetic Action:**
- 活血行氣 (*Huó xuè xíng qì*) — Activates Blood and moves Qi: the primary combined action; Qi-movement is primary (produces the force), Blood-activation is secondary (the result of improved Qi driving)
- 祛風止痛 (*Qū fēng zhǐ tòng*) — Dispels Wind and stops pain: the universal headache-analgesic action; ligustilide and TMP cerebrovascular vasodilation as mechanistic substrate
- 上行頭目 (*Shàng xíng tóu mù*) — Ascends to the head and eyes: the classical "guiding" property toward the cephalic region; classical explanation for its designation as the premier headache herb and its use as a "channel-guide" (*yǐn jīng yào*) in formulas targeting the head and upper body
- 旁通四肢 (*Páng tōng sì zhī*) — Laterally penetrates the four limbs: distributes Blood-moving action to peripheral extremities, explaining its use for wind-damp *Bi* pain and peripheral circulatory insufficiency

**Ayurvedic (functional correspondence):** *Rasa*: katu (pungent), tikta (bitter); *Virya*: ushna (hot); *Vipaka*: katu (pungent); *Dosha*: reduces Kapha and Vata strongly; may increase Pitta in excess; *Karma*: deepaniya (digestive stimulant), vedanasthapana (analgesic), hridya (cardiac tonic), srotoshodhana (channel-cleansing), cephalic (shiras-related — head-oriented therapeutic action paralleling the TCM ascending-to-head classification). Functional correspondence: closest to *Ajamoda* (aromatic Apiaceae) therapeutic cluster.

### Cultivation Register

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The entry's tag line carries CM, but under the canonical tradition registry CM denotes the Unani / Avicennan corpus, and *Ligusticum chuanxiong* — a Sichuan rhizome of the East Asian materia medica — has no attestation in the *Canon* or the Unani tradition; the "CM" heading used inside this entry's *Origin & Tradition* section is a local mislabel for classical Chinese medicine, not the Avicennan tradition. The one genuine cultivation idea of the Unani corpus, the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture across a whole regimen, is nowhere applied to this herb. No *ruh*-strengthening or *mufarrih* role is claimed. Cultivation status: **(c) absent** — the substance is not part of this tradition at all.

**Chinese — Shennong through Bencao (SBJ).** Chuān Xiōng (川芎 / 芎藭) is securely attested across the Chinese materia medica, from the *Shennong Bencao Jing* stratum through Li Shizhen's *Bencao Gangmu* (1596), where it is worked as the "Qi within Blood" medicine (*xuè zhōng qì yào*, 血中气药) and the premier head-directed, ascending-dispersing herb. Its standing here is emphatically therapeutic: it is a mover of Qi and Blood and a stopper of pain, deployed for a defined pattern (wind, stasis, headache) and then adjusted or withdrawn — not a substance of *yang sheng* 養生, the conservational nourishing-of-life project. A *san pin* 三品 grade is not asserted here: the entry's own text names a middle grade (中品), but that specific grade for this species could not be confirmed against a named scholarly source (Unschuld; Stanley-Baker) in the sources retrieved, and the tradition's dispersing, course-limited use of the herb points away from a long-term life-nourishing status in any case. **(b) framed** — attested in the materia medica, no cultivation grade sourced.

**Ayurveda (SS).** The Ayurvedic material in this entry is explicitly a *functional and botanical-family correspondence* — the Apiaceae cluster around *Ajamoda* (identified with *Apium graveolens*, *Carum roxburghianum* and lovage relatives), which the entry itself states is "not direct taxonomic identity" with *Ligusticum chuanxiong*. Sushruta and the Ayurvedic materia medica record *Ajamoda*, a different plant; they do not record this Sichuan species, and no *rasayana* (rejuvenation) or *vajikarana* (generative vitality) status is assigned to it. There is therefore no Ayurvedic cultivation status to state for this substance. **(c) absent** for the species as tagged.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator uses Chuān Xiōng as a corrective, not a tonic: it moves a stuck circulation, drives blood-and-Qi upward to clear a headache, and opens the peripheral channels — a job with a defined start and a defined stop. The Chinese tradition's own warning is built into its use pattern (short course, dispersing, contraindicated in deficiency-heat and pregnancy); nothing in any of the three tagged traditions frames it as something taken long-term to build the person's vitality. The Ayurvedic and Unani labels on the tag line describe different plants or no plant at all — they add nothing a cultivator can act on here.

### Contraindications

- **Yin deficiency with Heat-rising patterns (TCM):** Chuān Xiōng is warm and ascending — strongly contraindicated in conditions of Yin deficiency causing Heat to rise upward (manifesting as afternoon fever, hot palms and soles, night sweats, red tongue without coating, headache from Blood deficiency rather than stasis). The herb's ascending-dispersing action would aggravate Yin-deficiency Heat. When Chuān Xiōng appears in formulas for these patients, it is used in small proportions (3–6 g) heavily counterbalanced by cooling Yin-nourishing herbs.

- **Excessive menstrual bleeding / hemorrhage:** The strong Qi-moving and Blood-activating action can aggravate conditions of excessive bleeding (meno-metrorrhagia, peptic ulcer bleeding, post-surgical bleeding). Contraindicated during episodes of active hemorrhage; discontinue 1 week before elective surgery.

- **Concurrent antiplatelet/anticoagulant medications:** TMP and ferulic acid anti-platelet activity produces additive effect with aspirin, clopidogrel, warfarin; monitor INR; significant but lesser than Dān Shēn (I_088) in magnitude.

- **Pregnancy:** Strong blood-moving and Qi-activating herb; classical TCM contraindication in pregnancy — uterine stimulation risk is real via smooth muscle activation mechanisms. Avoid throughout pregnancy.

- **Hypotension:** Vasodilatory action of TMP and ligustilide can cause symptomatic hypotension in individuals with baseline low blood pressure or in combination with antihypertensive medications. Monitor; avoid at full dose in hypotensive individuals.

- **Headache from Liver Yang rising or Blood deficiency (TCM differentiation):** Chuān Xiōng is specifically for Blood-stasis and wind-type headache; using it for headache arising from Blood deficiency (dull, constant, better with rest, worse with effort) or Liver Yang rising (throbbing, worse with stress, red face) without adequate Yin-nourishing and Yang-subduing herbs can worsen the headache by further dispersing already-deficient Blood substance upward. Requires precise TCM diagnosis for headache application.

- **Excessive dose — dizziness and nausea:** At doses above 30 g in decoction or equivalent high-extract doses, Chuān Xiōng's strong ascending action can cause dizziness, nausea, and vomiting from excessive Qi ascending to the head — the classical toxicological observation for overdose. Remain within stated dosing ranges.
