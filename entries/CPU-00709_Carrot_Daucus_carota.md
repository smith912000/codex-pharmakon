# Carrot / Wild Carrot (Queen Anne's Lace) — Daukos (Greek, Dioscoridean), Gazar / Jazar (Arabic, Avicennan), Garjara (Sanskrit, Sushrutan)

## *Daucus carota* L. | Family: Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM, TCM, SS, EF
**Tags (previous line, retained for reference):** AT (Assyrian) · DMM (Dioscorides) · CM (Avicenna) · SBJ (Shennong) · SS (Sushruta)

**Energetic Classification:** Warm and Dry (Galenic, first/second degree) — the cultivated root is more balanced/moist; the **wild seed** is the hot, penetrating, emmenagogue fraction

**Training Phase:** Foundation (nourishing root) → Tempering (wild seed as a warming, mobilising agent handled with caution)

### Origin & Tradition

*Daucus carota* spans a continuum from the wild carrot (Queen Anne's lace), with its thin white acrid root and pungent seed, to the fleshy orange cultivated carrot bred up over the last millennium. The two ends have distinct traditional roles: the **root** as nourishing food and gentle remedy, the **wild seed** as a hot, mobilising, reproductive-acting drug. Both are genuinely attested across the classical corpora. Historical claims here are kept general and to what the sources actually record.

**Assyrian (AT):** Mesopotamian cuneiform herbals record umbelliferous garden and field plants of the carrot/parsley kind among cultivated and gathered medicinal species; the aromatic "hot" seeds of this family were used within the warming, carminative and menstrual-regulating group. Specific tablet recipes are not reconstructable with confidence and are not invented here.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** *De Materia Medica* treats *daukos* (the wild carrot/its seed) explicitly as a warming, diuretic and **emmenagogue** drug — the seed taken to promote menstruation and urine, to relieve gripes, and reputedly to act on conception. This is the earliest clear European attestation of the wild-carrot-seed reproductive tradition that folk practice carried forward.

**Avicenna (CM):** The *Canon* classes carrot (*jazar*) as warm; the seed is described among the hot, diuretic, emmenagogue and aphrodisiac-leaning drugs, used to open obstructions, promote urine and menstruation, and stimulate. Avicenna's tradition, like Dioscorides', treats the seed as the pharmacologically active, warming fraction distinct from the milder root.

**Shennong / Chinese tradition (SBJ):** Carrot entered the Chinese materia medica and later herbals as a warm, sweet, spleen- and lung-supporting food (*hu luo bo*), used to strengthen digestion, support vision, and move stagnation. (The seed of the related *Daucus* is used regionally; the Chinese "she chuang zi" is a different Apiaceae seed and is not conflated here.)

**Sushruta (SS):** Ayurvedic tradition knows carrot (*garjara*) as a warming, *deepana* (digestive-kindling) and *grahi/vata*-affecting root, used for digestion, vision, and as a mild reproductive tonic, with the pungent seed regarded as heating and menstruation-promoting.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The carrot embodies a doctrinal split that mirrors its real pharmacology — the **root** as *stored, grounded nourishment* (sweet, moist, tap-rooted, carotenoid-gold) and the **seed** as *mobilising heat* (pungent, oily, penetrating). The gold of the cultivated root is not merely symbolic of light and vision: it is literally β-carotene, the provitamin-A pigment behind the plant's genuine association with sight. The wild seed's reputation as a "opener" — of menses, of urine, of obstruction — tracks its content of pungent volatile oils and terpenoids that irritate and stimulate smooth muscle and mucosa. In Kenetica the carrot is read as a two-faced teacher: the root shows *how nourishment is stored and released*, the seed shows *how heat mobilises what is stagnant* — and, crucially, why a mobilising agent that "opens the womb" must be handled with respect for its power.

### Active Components

- **Carotenoids** — α- and β-carotene (provitamin A), lutein, and related pigments; the basis of the vision, antioxidant and skin associations. Orange cultivars are among the richest dietary sources.
- **Falcarinol-type C₁₇ polyacetylenes** — falcarinol, falcarindiol, falcarindiol-3-acetate. These bioactive oxylipins, concentrated in the root and characteristic of the Apiaceae, carry documented anticancer, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial and neuroactive activity in laboratory models.
- **Volatile oils of the seed** — including sesquiterpenes and terpenoids (e.g. carotol, daucol, β-bisabolene, geraniol) responsible for the wild seed's pungency and its traditional emmenagogue/reproductive action.
- **Flavonoids, phenolic acids, coumarins**, dietary fibre (pectin), and vitamin C.
- **Furanocoumarin traces** (family-typical) — relevant to photosensitivity considerations with concentrated preparations.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Two distinct tools live in one species. The **root** is a **Foundation nutritive** — a grounding, carotenoid- and fibre-rich food supporting vision, skin, antioxidant status and steady digestion, safe at food levels and used to build baseline resilience. The **wild-carrot seed** is a **Tempering-phase mobiliser** — a warming, drying agent traditionally used to promote menstruation, urine and the clearing of stagnation. Because the seed's classical and folk reputation includes anti-implantation/contraceptive use, it is explicitly a *caution-gated* tool in the system: employed, if at all, only by non-pregnant practitioners who understand its reproductive action, and never as a casual supplement.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:

- **Dawid et al. (2015), *Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry*** — a review of bioactive C₁₇-polyacetylenes (falcarinol-type) in carrots, summarising evidence for anticancer, antifungal, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory and serotonergic effects and proposing carrots as a breeding target for these compounds. This grounds the root's real pharmacology at review level. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jafc.5b04357
- **Zaini et al. (2012), *Anti-Cancer Agents in Medicinal Chemistry*** — showed that carrot-derived polyacetylenes (falcarinol, falcarindiol, falcarindiol-3-acetate) induced apoptosis and cell-cycle arrest in human lymphoid leukaemia cell lines, whereas the carotenoids β-carotene and lutein did not — identifying polyacetylenes, not carotene, as the cytotoxic principle in vitro. DOI: https://doi.org/10.2174/187152012800617704
- **Purup et al. (2009), *Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry*** — demonstrated differential, dose-dependent (hormetic) effects of falcarinol and related polyacetylenes on normal versus cancerous intestinal cells, with synergy between falcarinol and falcarindiol, supporting these as candidate anticancer principles of carrots. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1021/jf901503a

**Evidence grade: Root — well-nourished, plausible preventive (carotenoids: strong nutritional evidence; polyacetylenes: promising preclinical/in-vitro only, no confirmatory human cancer-treatment data). Wild seed — traditional.** A direct, controlled human study of wild-carrot-seed as a contraceptive was **not** located in this PubMed search; the anti-fertility/anti-implantation reputation rests on historical/folk sources and some animal work in the older literature, and must be presented as *traditional and unproven in humans*, not as an established contraceptive. Do not represent wild-carrot seed as a reliable birth-control method.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM (by analogy):** root — warm/neutral, sweet; tonifies spleen and stomach, benefits the eyes, moves stagnation and supports the lungs.
- **Ayurveda (Sushruta lineage):** *rasa* — *madhura* (sweet), *tikta* (bitter), *kashaya* (astringent); *guna* — *laghu* (light), *ruksha* (dry, esp. seed); *virya* — *ushna* (heating); *vipaka* — *katu* (pungent). Root kindles *agni* and supports vision; seed is heating and *artava*-promoting (menstrual).
- **Galenic / Unani (Avicennan lineage):** Warm and Dry (root first degree, seed second degree); the seed is diuretic, emmenagogue, deobstruent and stimulant.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** Cuneiform herbals record umbelliferous plants of the carrot/parsley kind among cultivated and gathered species, the aromatic "hot" seeds grouped with the warming, carminative and menstrual-regulating drugs. Under the standing blocking warning for Assyrian substance identifications and tablet references, and with Mesopotamian illness classified by causal agency rather than by a substance's action on a vital endowment, no cultivation status is claimed; the attestation is therapeutic. Cultivation status: **(c) absent**.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides treats *daukos* — the wild carrot and its seed — in *De Materia Medica* as a warming, diuretic and emmenagogue drug, the seed taken to promote menstruation and urine and reputed to act on conception. He records that reproductive reputation without building any subtle-body doctrine around it; recording is not endorsing, and there is no vital-body system here into which a cultivation status could be set. What the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention: telling the hot, pungent wild seed from the milder cultivated root, and judging the drug by assay. Cultivation status: **(c) absent**.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** Carrot (*jazar*) is classed warm, and the seed sits among the hot, diuretic, emmenagogue and stimulant drugs used to open obstructions and promote urine and menstruation — a deobstruent, mobilising therapeutic action, distinct from the milder root. The Unani cultivation idea proper — conservation of the innate heat and radical moisture — is not assigned to it, and no *mufarrih* role is claimed; a warming opener of this kind is worked for its therapeutic effect, not held as a status that conserves a vital endowment. Cultivation status: **(b) framed** — used by the tradition, no cultivation status recorded.

**Chinese medicine — post-classical (TCM).** Carrot is *hu luo bo* 胡蘿蔔 — the name ("foreign radish") marks it as a Silk-Road introduction, and it is not in the *Shennong Bencao Jing*: it enters Chinese materia medica only in the Yuan and Ming, discussed by Li Shizhen in the *Bencao Gangmu* (1596). No *san pin* 三品 grade — upper 上品, middle 中品 or lower 下品 — attaches to it: that classical hierarchy belongs to the Shennong stratum it never entered. Its recorded role is that of a warm, sweet spleen- and stomach-supporting food that brightens the eyes and moves stagnation — a dietetic-therapeutic standing within the later materia medica, not a *yang sheng* 養生 nourishing-life cultivation grade. Cultivation status: **(b) framed** — attested in the post-classical materia medica but with no *san pin* grade or *yang sheng* standing recorded.

**Ayurveda (SS).** In the *dravyaguna* carrot (*garjara*) is a warming (*ushna virya*), *deepana* (digestion-kindling) root that supports vision and is *madhura*–*tikta*–*kashaya* in *rasa*, with the pungent seed treated as heating and *artava*-promoting (menstrual). The "reproductive tonic" language sometimes attached to it is not a sourced *vajikarana* assignment, and no *rasayana* (rejuvenation) status is recorded for it; nothing sourced supports an *ojas* claim. Its standing is dietetic-therapeutic, not a formal rejuvenative cultivation status. Cultivation status: **(b) framed**.

**European folk / phytotherapy (EF).** Later Western herbalism — the medieval-to-modern folk and phytotherapeutic stream, distinct from the Dioscoridean record (DMM) it inherited — carried the wild carrot (Queen Anne's lace) forward chiefly as a seed remedy for women: the seed used as an emmenagogue to promote menstruation and, in living folk practice, as an anti-implantation contraceptive taken after intercourse, alongside a milder folk use of the seed infusion as a warming carminative and diuretic. These are recorded therapeutic and folk uses, given in the folk sources as tradition rather than proven method. European folk phytotherapy carries no formal subtle-anatomy or vital-substrate doctrine — there is no European "vital body" here into which a cultivation status could be set — so what the tradition offers is a restorative and reproductive-acting folk practice, worked for its effect and its safety caution, not a longevity grade. Cultivation status: **(c) absent** — no cultivation framing is attested; the standing is folk-therapeutic.

**Practitioner's note.** The carrot gives a cultivator two different things and no cultivation grade for either. The root is Foundation nourishment — eaten or juiced as a grounding, carotenoid- and fibre-rich food to build baseline resilience, vision and steady digestion; used at food level it is simply good nourishment, not a longevity tonic. The wild seed is a caution-gated drug — warming, drying, emmenagogue and reproductively active — to be handled only by non-pregnant practitioners who understand that action, never taken casually and never trusted as contraception (the anti-fertility reputation is traditional and unproven in humans). None of the six tagged traditions assigns carrot a formal cultivation status: the honest answer is eat the root as food and respect the seed as a drug.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy — wild-carrot seed is contraindicated.** Its traditional emmenagogue and anti-implantation reputation means the seed and its concentrated preparations must **not** be used in pregnancy or when pregnancy is possible/desired. This is the single most important caution in the entry.
- **Do not rely on it as contraception:** the anti-fertility action is traditional and not validated in controlled human studies; using it as birth control risks unintended pregnancy and harm.
- **Photosensitivity:** as an Apiaceae bearing furanocoumarins, concentrated seed/leaf preparations and skin contact with the plant sap can cause phototoxic dermatitis in sunlight.
- **Apiaceae allergy / mistaken identity:** wild carrot closely resembles the deadly *Conium maculatum* (poison hemlock) and *Cicuta* (water hemlock); wild harvesting is dangerous without expert identification. Cross-reactive allergy to carrot, celery, and mugwort pollen occurs.
- **Carotenaemia:** very high root/juice intake harmlessly tints the skin orange; not toxic but a marker of excess.
