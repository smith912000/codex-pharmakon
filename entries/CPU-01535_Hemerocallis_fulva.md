# Hemerocallis fulva — Emerokallis (Dioscorides 3-137; also hemerocatallacton, lilium sylvestre, crinanthemon, porphyranthes, bulbus sanguineus, anticantharon)

## *Hemerocallis fulva (the translators' suggestion on the SUGGESTED line: "Hemerocallis fulva — Lemon Lily, Yellow Day Lily")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named by Dioscorides (DMM) as a chapter; one other text mentions the binomial in passing.** The spine row and the tag line rest on the Dioscorides chapter alone. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *3-137. EMEROKALLIS* at line 7737, SUGGESTED line at 7738, text at line 7739 (a page number, 518, follows). The chapter stands in Book Three, "Roots", after 3-136 *KONUZA* and before 3-138 *LEUKOION*, both different plants. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 543.

The whole chapter reads: "Hemerocallis has leaves and a stalk similar to a lily, green like leeks, the flowers in threes or fours at every flowering, similar in their shape to a lily when they begin to open, with a colour very similar to ochre. The root is pounded finely like the great bulbus [2-200, 2-201] and taken as a drink or applied with honey in wool as a pessary for drawing out water and blood. The leaves (pounded into small pieces and applied) lessen inflammation of the breasts that comes with childbirth, and inflammation of the eyes. The root and leaves are effective applied on burns. It is also called hemerocatallacton, lilium sylvestre, crinanthemon, porphyranthes, bulbus sanguineus, or anticantharon; the Egyptians call it iocroi, the Romans, bulbus, some, lilium agreste, some, lilium marinum, and the Africans, abiblabon."

So the chapter gives a description (leaves and stalk like a lily, green like leeks; flowers in threes or fours, ochre-coloured), three groups of uses (the root as a drink or a pessary; the leaves on inflamed breasts and eyes; root and leaves on burns) and a long list of names in several languages. The cross-reference to *bulbus* [2-200, 2-201] is the translators' and was not followed.

*A mention in another text, not a tag.* In the introduction to the Shennong text (Yang 1998, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`, line 360, after the page marker `[[p12]]` at line 349) the commentary describes middle-class medicinals as able to "cultivate personality or modify temperament, as are, for example, He Huan (Cortex Albizziae Julibrissinis) and Xuan Cao (Radix Hemerocallis Fulvae)", and says that "the former resolves anger, while the latter is able to help relieve worry". That is a passage in a modern commentator's introduction naming a plant by a pharmaceutical Latin binomial and a Chinese name, and it is not one of the Shennong entries in the names list (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv` has no row for it). It has not been checked against the Shennong materia medica text for Xuan Cao, and the identity of the Chinese drug is the commentator's. It is recorded here and not used: the tag line stays DMM only, and the Cultivation Register below does not speak for it. A possible SBJ tag addition is reported to the project, not applied. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a root taken as a drink or as a pessary "for drawing out water and blood", leaf applications for inflamed breasts and eyes, and an application of root and leaves on burns. The Codex gives no protocol for any of these. The identity is a translators' suggestion, and a pessary or an eye application is not something to improvise from a translated herbal.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations: the root "pounded finely" and drunk; the root with honey "in wool as a pessary"; the leaves "pounded into small pieces and applied"; root and leaves applied on burns. No dose, quantity or duration is printed for any of them. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 3-137 prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality and no taste. The qualities it prints describe the plant's appearance (green leaves, ochre-coloured flowers).

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a description for recognition (leaves and stalk like a lily but green like leeks, flowers in threes or fours at each flowering, the colour compared with ochre) and a long list of names from Greek, Egyptian, Roman and African usage. It is not a cultivation status. The Shennong mention above belongs to another tradition and is not attached to this paragraph. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is the habit of describing a flower by its count and its colour at each flowering. The text offers nothing more, and the abstentive answer is the right one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 3-137 prints no warning. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. A pessary used "for drawing out water and blood" must not be used in pregnancy. Breast and eye inflammation, and burns, call for medical care. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
