# Feverfew / Featherfew / Parthenium / Bachelor's Buttons / Midsummer Daisy / Wild Chamomile

## *Tanacetum parthenium* (L.) Sch.Bip. | Family: Asteraceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Bitter, acrid, cooling-dispersing; downward-moving in the pelvis, dispersing in the head.

**Training Phase:** Intermediate — Neurological Prophylaxis

---

### Origin & Tradition

The name is a fossil of a mistake.

**Feverfew** descends through Middle English from Latin ***febrifugia*** — *febris* (fever) + *fugare* (to put to flight). The word is a therapeutic claim compressed into a noun: this is the plant that drives out fever. The claim was carried, unexamined, for the better part of two thousand years, into the herbals, into the vernacular, and into the plant's binomial vicinity. And it appears to be **wrong** — or at best so weakly supported that no modern evidence base has formed around it.

What *is* right is something the name never mentions. *Tanacetum parthenium* is, on the modern evidence, a **migraine prophylactic** — and among the better-studied Western botanicals for that indication. The tradition preserved the correct plant under an incorrect banner, and modern pharmacology found the drug by ignoring what the drug was called. There are few cleaner illustrations in this codex of why the etymological layer must be read as *evidence about the tradition* and never as *evidence about the substance*.

**Dioscoridean / Western Materia Medica (DMM)**

The classical thread here must be handled carefully, and the codex's honesty rule bites immediately. The Greek ***parthenion*** (παρθένιον, from *parthenos*, "maiden" — surviving in the specific epithet *parthenium*) appears in the classical materia medica, and *T. parthenium* has traditionally been identified with it. **This identification is contested.** Classical plant names map onto modern binomials unreliably; *parthenion* has been variously assigned, and the "maiden" root points toward the gynaecological rather than the febrile or cephalic use. The persistent folk-etymological story — that the plant was named for having saved a workman who fell from the Parthenon — is a charming late gloss and should not be repeated as history. [USER: verify the Dioscorides *parthenion* identification against a specialist source before this entry is published; do not treat the classical attribution as settled.]

What can be stated with more confidence is the **later Western herbal** record, in which feverfew is used for three things: **fevers** (per the name), **headaches**, and as an **emmenagogue** — an agent to bring on delayed menstruation. The gynaecological use is consistent across the European herbal tradition and is, pharmacologically, the most dangerous thing in the plant's file — because an effective emmenagogue is a potential abortifacient, and the historical record indicates it was used as one.

The headache use is the thread that matters. Long before any trial, the Western herbal tradition applied feverfew to persistent, recurring, one-sided head pain — and the folk method was **prophylactic**, not abortive: a leaf or two eaten *daily*, typically in a sandwich to mask the extreme bitterness, taken continuously rather than at the moment of attack. This is the crucial detail. The tradition independently arrived at the correct *pharmacological strategy* — chronic daily dosing to prevent attacks, rather than acute dosing to treat one — which is exactly how modern migraine prophylaxis is conceived and exactly unlike how one would use a febrifuge. The folk practice was better than the folk theory.

**Chinese Medicine (CM)**

*T. parthenium* is not a classical Chinese drug and has no standing in the Chinese pharmacopoeial tradition. Its presence under this tag reflects the genus **Tanacetum** and the wider Asteraceae aromatic-bitter complex, and modern Chinese pharmacological interest in **parthenolide** — which has become a heavily investigated compound in Chinese cancer and inflammation research, largely disconnected from any traditional use. This is worth marking rather than padding: the tag records a *modern research* relationship, not an ethnobotanical one. Do not construct a Chinese classical lineage for this plant. **Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

Two doctrines sit on this plant. One collapses under examination. The other never announced itself.

**The febrifuge doctrine — deflate it.** "Feverfew" promises antipyresis. No credible modern evidence base supports *T. parthenium* as an antipyretic; the searches conducted for this entry surfaced no antipyretic trial literature at all, and the plant's clinical identity in the modern record is entirely cephalic. The likely explanation is mundane and instructive: feverfew is a bitter, aromatic Asteraceae, morphologically and organoleptically resembling chamomile and tansy, and the pre-modern category "bitter aromatic composite" was itself a febrifuge indication. The plant was probably named **by category membership rather than by observed effect** — classified into the fever drugs because of what it *looked and tasted like*, not because of what it *did*. This is a general failure mode of doctrinal nomenclature and feverfew is its cleanest specimen. The name is not evidence. The name is a taxonomic guess that hardened into a claim.

**The migraine reality — where the chemistry lands.** The active principle is **parthenolide**, a germacranolide sesquiterpene lactone bearing an **α-methylene-γ-butyrolactone** moiety. That functional group is a Michael acceptor: it forms covalent adducts with nucleophilic cysteine thiols on target proteins. This single structural fact explains most of what feverfew does — the good and the bad.

Against the migraine indication, two mechanisms are proposed, and the doctrinal mapping is honest only if their difference in evidential status is stated:

*Serotonin-release inhibition.* Feverfew extracts have long been reported to inhibit serotonin release from platelets — the historical mechanistic rationale, arising when migraine was conceived as a vascular/platelet-serotonin disorder. **This mechanism should now be held loosely.** It rests on an older model of migraine pathophysiology that has been substantially superseded by neurovascular and CGRP-centred accounts, and no primary serotonin-release study was retrieved in this run to cite. It is reported here as a historical mechanistic proposal, **not as an established mechanism.** [USER: verify with primary sources if this mechanism is to be asserted rather than described.]

*NF-κB inhibition and neuroinflammation.* This is on firmer ground and is directly evidenced. Parthenolide is one of the best-characterised natural NF-κB inhibitors, acting through exactly the thiol-adduct chemistry described above. Magni et al. (2012) showed in LPS-stimulated BV-2 **microglia** that parthenolide dose-dependently reduced IL-6 secretion (29% at 200 nM, 45% at 1 µM, 98% at 5 µM, all p<0.001) and TNF-α (54% at 5 µM), with Western blotting confirming strongly reduced NF-κB nuclear translocation ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.3732), PMID 22359368). The cell type is the point: microglia are the resident immune cells of the CNS, and microglial activation is a live component of contemporary migraine models. A daily-dosed drug that suppresses microglial pro-inflammatory signalling is a *plausible prophylactic* in a way that a serotonin-release inhibitor taken once a day is not.

Note the fit with the folk method. Covalent inhibitors with anti-inflammatory transcriptional effects are exactly the class you would expect to require **chronic accumulation** and to be **useless acutely**. The herbal tradition's leaf-a-day-in-a-sandwich is not a quaint delivery detail; it is the correct pharmacokinetic strategy for this mechanism, arrived at empirically, centuries before the mechanism was nameable.

And the same reactive chemistry writes the toxicology. An α-methylene-γ-butyrolactone that haptenises cysteine residues will also haptenise proteins in skin and mucosa. **Asteraceae contact dermatitis and feverfew's oral ulceration are not unrelated side effects — they are the therapeutic mechanism, applied to the wrong tissue.** The doctrine-to-chemistry map here is unusually tight in both directions.

### Active Components

- **Parthenolide** — germacranolide sesquiterpene lactone; the principal active and the standardisation marker. α-methylene-γ-butyrolactone Michael acceptor; covalent NF-κB inhibitor. **Chemically unstable** — this instability is the single most important fact in the entire feverfew clinical literature (see Scientific Correlation).
- **Other sesquiterpene lactones** — canin, artecanin, 3-β-hydroxyparthenolide, santamarine and related germacranolides/guaianolides. Share the reactive lactone motif; contribute to both activity and contact allergenicity.
- **Flavonoid glycosides** — tanetin and related methylated flavonols.
- **Essential oil** — camphor, chrysanthenyl acetate and related monoterpenes. Variable by chemotype; contributes aroma, not the migraine activity.
- **Melatonin** — reported in feverfew preparations; occasionally invoked as a contributory mechanism. Amounts are almost certainly pharmacologically trivial relative to the doses used for sleep. [USER: verify quantities before this is cited as a mechanism — treat as unlikely.]

**Critical note on content variability:** parthenolide content varies enormously between chemotypes, growing conditions, plant parts, harvest timing, drying method and storage duration. Commercial preparations have historically been found to contain anywhere from near-zero to substantial parthenolide. This is not a quality-control footnote — it is the explanation for the clinical literature's contradictions.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Feverfew occupies the **Intermediate — Neurological Prophylaxis** phase and is indicated for **one thing**: reduction of migraine attack frequency in practitioners with established, recurrent, diagnosed migraine. It is not an acute treatment. It will not abort an attack in progress. Taken at the onset of a headache it does nothing, and the practitioner who tries it that way will correctly conclude it is inert.

The three non-negotiable conditions of use:

1. **Chronic daily dosing.** Weeks to months before evaluation. The MIG-99 trial's primary endpoint was measured at treatment months 2–3.
2. **A stable, characterised extract.** This is where the drug lives or dies. See below.
3. **Realistic magnitude.** The best trial data show roughly **0.6 fewer attacks per month than placebo**. That is a real effect and a modest one. A practitioner told to expect transformation will discontinue; a practitioner told to expect one fewer attack in a bad month may be well served.

Feverfew's honest place in the Kenetica framework is as a **low-risk, low-magnitude, long-horizon adjunct** for practitioners with frequent attacks who are not candidates for, or are inadequately served by, conventional prophylaxis — and who can be relied upon to taper rather than stop abruptly. It is not a replacement for pharmaceutical prophylaxis in severe or disabling migraine, and it must not be positioned as one.

**The withdrawal problem is a use-case constraint, not a warning label.** Feverfew must be tapered. Enrol only practitioners who will comply with that.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Preparation | Typical amount | Method | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stable CO₂ extract (MIG-99)** | 6.25 mg three times daily | Oral, continuous | **The only regimen with a positive adequately-powered RCT.** Dose established by prior dose-finding study (Diener 2005) |
| Dried leaf, standardised | 50–150 mg/day, standardised to parthenolide | Oral, continuous | Widely used; parthenolide content the critical variable. [USER: verify the standardisation percentage claimed by any specific product] |
| Fresh leaf (traditional folk method) | 1–4 small leaves daily, in food | Chewed/eaten, masked in bread | **Highest oral-ulceration risk** — direct mucosal contact with reactive lactones. Historically effective, mechanistically sensible, but the worst delivery route available |
| Dried alcoholic extract | 0.5 mg parthenolide/day | Capsule | **Failed** in De Weerdt 1996 — cited here as a negative control, not a recommendation |
| Combination products (w/ riboflavin, magnesium) | feverfew 100 mg/day component | Oral | **Failed** to beat an active-comparator "placebo" in Maizels 2004 |

**Evaluation window:** minimum 8–12 weeks of continuous dosing before judging efficacy. Attack-frequency diary from a ≥4-week baseline is required — the placebo response in migraine trials is very large (see Maizels below) and uncontrolled self-assessment is worthless here.

**Discontinuation:** taper. Do not stop abruptly. [USER: verify a specific taper schedule against a clinical source — no validated taper protocol was retrieved this run.]

### Scientific Correlation

*All citations below retrieved from PubMed. This is a case where the honest grade is more interesting than a favourable one would be.*

**1. The Cochrane verdict — the anchor, and it is equivocal. GRADE: LOW-QUALITY EVIDENCE, per the reviewers' own words.**
Wider, Pittler & Ernst, *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews* 2015 (update of the 2004 review) ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD002286.pub3), PMID 25892430). Six double-blind placebo-controlled trials, 561 patients. **Pooled analysis was not possible** — no common outcome measures, heterogeneity across participants, interventions and designs. All studies at unclear or high risk of bias regarding sample size. The reviewers' conclusion, stated exactly: the newer larger trial "adds some positive evidence to the mixed and inconclusive findings of the previous review. However, this constitutes **low quality evidence**, which needs to be confirmed in larger rigorous trials with stable feverfew extracts and clearly defined migraine populations before firm conclusions can be drawn."

The prior trial landscape as Cochrane characterises it: **three positive trials, all small** (n = 17–60); **two rigorous trials finding no significant difference** (n = 50, 147). Small-and-positive versus large-and-null is the signature of publication and small-study bias, and it must be named as such. Cochrane's one unambiguous positive finding is on safety: only mild, transient adverse events, "feverfew is not associated with any major safety concerns."

**2. MIG-99 — the trial that rescued the indication. GRADE: MODERATE (single adequately-powered positive RCT).**
Diener et al., *Cephalalgia* 2005 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2982.2005.00950.x), PMID 16232154). Randomised, double-blind, multicentre, placebo-controlled, parallel-group; n=170 ITT (MIG-99 n=89, placebo n=81); 6.25 mg CO₂-extract t.i.d.; 4-week baseline, 16-week treatment; IHS diagnostic criteria. **Result: migraine frequency fell from 4.76 baseline by 1.9 attacks/month on MIG-99 vs 1.3 on placebo (p=0.0456). Responder-rate odds ratio 3.4 in favour of MIG-99 (p=0.0049).** Adverse events possibly related: 8.4% MIG-99 vs 10.2% placebo (p=0.654).

**Read this honestly.** The between-group difference is **0.6 attacks per month** and the primary endpoint p-value is **0.0456** — it cleared significance by a hair. The responder-rate analysis is more convincing than the frequency analysis. It is one trial. Cochrane, which included it, still graded the totality low-quality. This is genuine positive evidence at modest magnitude, not a vindication.

**3. The famous null — and why it is the most important paper here. GRADE: INFORMATIVE NULL.**
De Weerdt, Bootsma & Hendriks, *Phytomedicine* 1996 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0944-7113(96)80057-2), PMID 23195074). Randomised double-blind placebo-controlled crossover, 50 patients feverfew-naïve, 44 completing a 9-month study. Capsules of a **dried alcoholic extract on microcrystalline cellulose, containing 0.5 mg parthenolide** daily. **Result: both groups suffered the same number of migraine attacks. No prophylactic effect demonstrated.**

The authors themselves flag the discrepancy with two other positive studies and offer an explanation — that the other studies enrolled patients who *already reported positive experiences with feverfew*, i.e. an enriched, unblindable population. That is a devastating critique of the positive literature and it comes from inside it.

But the preparation is the deeper story. A **dried alcoholic extract delivering 0.5 mg parthenolide** against MIG-99's stable CO₂ extract at 18.75 mg/day of a characterised product. Parthenolide's reactive α-methylene lactone is precisely the sort of electrophile that degrades on drying, on storage, and in the presence of nucleophiles — its instability is its chemistry. The most parsimonious reading of the whole contradictory literature is not that feverfew sometimes works and sometimes doesn't. **It is that trials using unstable or poorly-characterised preparations were testing degraded material, and returned the correct answer for the material they tested.** Cochrane's own recommendation — future trials must use *stable* extracts — endorses this reading.

This is the entry's second lesson, and it generalises well beyond feverfew: **a null result on a botanical is a result about a preparation, never about a plant.**

**4. The combination trial — a null that is really a lesson about placebo. GRADE: NULL, with a methodological sting.**
Maizels, Blumenfeld & Burchette, *Headache* 2004 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1526-4610.2004.04170.x), PMID 15447697). Randomised double-blind placebo-controlled; riboflavin 400 mg + magnesium 300 mg + feverfew 100 mg daily vs "placebo" containing riboflavin 25 mg; 49 completers. **No difference on the primary outcome** (≥50% migraine reduction: 42% active vs 44% placebo, p=0.87) or any secondary outcome. **Both groups improved significantly from baseline, by a margin exceeding the placebo response reported in previous migraine trials** — leading the authors to conclude riboflavin 25 mg may itself have been an active comparator. Their own summary: "conflicting scientific evidence."

Cited for two reasons. It contributes no support to feverfew. And it demonstrates why uncontrolled feverfew testimonials are worthless: **44% of people on a near-placebo halved their migraines.**

**5. Mechanism — NF-κB and microglia. GRADE: MODERATE (in vitro), mechanistically coherent.**
Magni et al., *Phytotherapy Research* 2012 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.3732), PMID 22359368). Detailed above. Parthenolide dose-dependently suppressed IL-6 and TNF-α secretion and NF-κB nuclear translocation in LPS-stimulated BV-2 microglia. The authors position this explicitly as "an additional explanation for the efficacy of orally administered *T. parthenium* as an antimigraine agent." **Honest caveat:** BV-2 is an immortalised murine microglial line; effective concentrations run 200 nM–5 µM; whether oral parthenolide achieves those concentrations at CNS microglia in a dosed human is **not established by this paper and was not established by anything retrieved this run.** The mechanism is plausible and unproven — which is the standard state of botanical mechanism claims and should be stated as such rather than glossed.

**6. Serotonin-release inhibition. GRADE: UNSUPPORTED IN THIS RETRIEVAL — recorded as a historical hypothesis only.**
No primary study demonstrating platelet serotonin-release inhibition by feverfew or parthenolide was retrieved in this run. The mechanism is widely repeated in secondary sources and rests on an older vascular/platelet model of migraine that current pathophysiology has largely moved past. **It is included in this entry as an artefact of the field's history, not as a mechanism the codex asserts.** [USER: verify against primary literature if it is to be retained as a claim.]

**7. The febrifuge doctrine. GRADE: NULL — no supporting evidence retrieved.**
Searches for feverfew's namesake indication returned **no antipyretic clinical literature**. The plant's entire modern evidence base — six RCTs, one Cochrane review, the mechanistic work — concerns **headache**. The name is, on present evidence, simply false. The codex records this as the entry's headline finding: *a two-millennium doctrine, encoded in the drug's name in two languages, with a null evidence base — attached to a plant whose real activity the doctrine never mentioned.*

**8. Safety literature. GRADE: ADEQUATE for the specific hazards.**
Goodyear-Smith, *J Prim Health Care* 2010 ([PMID 21125077](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21125077/)) — a feverfew monograph indexed under oral ulcer, hypersensitivity, gastrointestinal diseases, drug interactions with aspirin and warfarin. No DOI available for this record; PMID given. Cochrane 2015 independently reports mouth ulcers and gastrointestinal complaints as the most common adverse events across six trials.

**Overall honest grade: better-evidenced than most Western botanicals, and that is a statement about the field's low bar rather than about feverfew.** One adequately-powered positive RCT at 0.6 attacks/month; a Cochrane review calling the totality low-quality; informative nulls attributable to unstable preparations; a coherent but unconfirmed CNS mechanism; and a completely null namesake indication. Feverfew is a modest, real, cheap, low-risk prophylactic with a genuine but unimpressive effect size — provided the preparation is stable. Everything hangs on that proviso.

### Contraindications

- **⚠️ PREGNANCY — ABSOLUTE.** Feverfew is a traditional **emmenagogue** with historical use as an **abortifacient**. This is the single most serious item in the plant's file. The traditional indication is itself the contraindication: an agent used to bring on delayed menses is an agent that acts on the gravid uterus. Do not use in pregnancy, in those attempting conception, or where pregnancy is possible and unexcluded. Lactation: no adequate data; avoid. This warning does not depend on the emmenagogue action being *proven* — it depends on it being *claimed and plausibly mechanistic*, which is a sufficient standard for a reproductive contraindication.

- **⚠️ "POST-FEVERFEW SYNDROME" — WITHDRAWAL ON ABRUPT DISCONTINUATION.** Abrupt cessation after chronic use has been described as producing a rebound syndrome — returning or worsened headache, anxiety, insomnia, muscle and joint stiffness. This has a direct practical consequence: **a practitioner who stops feverfew suddenly may experience worse headaches than before starting, and may mistake withdrawal for the underlying condition.** Taper. Every practitioner started on feverfew must be told this at initiation, not at discontinuation. [USER: verify the syndrome's evidential status and a specific taper schedule — characterised in the secondary literature; no primary characterisation study retrieved this run.]

- **⚠️ ORAL ULCERATION AND MUCOSAL INFLAMMATION.** The most common adverse event in the trial literature — reported across the six trials in Cochrane 2015 and indexed as a feverfew hazard in the safety monograph literature. Mouth ulcers, tongue/lip swelling, loss of taste, mucosal soreness. Highest risk with **fresh leaf chewing**, i.e. the traditional method. Mechanistically expected: reactive α-methylene-γ-butyrolactones haptenising mucosal proteins. Encapsulated extracts substantially reduce but do not eliminate this.

- **⚠️ ASTERACEAE CONTACT DERMATITIS AND CROSS-SENSITISATION.** Sesquiterpene lactones are a classic and well-documented contact allergen class. Contraindicated in known sensitivity to Asteraceae/Compositae — ragweed, chrysanthemum, chamomile, tansy, arnica, marigold, echinacea, daisy. Cross-reactivity within the family is the rule. Handlers of fresh plant material can develop occupational sensitisation. Once sensitised, the reaction is lifelong.

- **Anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents.** Feverfew is indexed with **warfarin and aspirin** interaction concerns in the safety literature (Goodyear-Smith 2010, PMID 21125077), consistent with the historically claimed platelet effects. Whether or not the serotonin-release mechanism holds, the interaction caution is retained on the precautionary principle. Avoid or monitor closely with warfarin, DOACs, aspirin, clopidogrel. **Discontinue before elective surgery** — [USER: verify interval; 2 weeks is commonly advised but was not sourced this run].

- **Children.** No adequate data. Not recommended.

- **Preparation is a safety variable, not only an efficacy variable.** An uncharacterised feverfew product may contain no parthenolide (inert, useless) or a great deal (active, and correspondingly more allergenic and ulcerogenic). The practitioner cannot know which without standardisation. Prefer characterised stable extracts on both grounds.

- **This is prophylaxis, and the failure mode is silent.** A practitioner who believes feverfew is treating their migraine while their attack frequency and disability rise is being harmed by the delay, not by the plant. Feverfew is not indicated in status migrainosus, in migraine with concerning neurological features, in new or changed headache, or in headache with red-flag features — all of which require medical assessment, not a botanical. The absence of major toxicity does not make an ineffective treatment safe.
