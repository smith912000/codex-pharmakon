# Agati / Sesbania grandiflora — Agasti, Agastya (Sushruta); so.cha, "Soapnut Tree" (rGyud-bzhi, Clark 1995)

## *Sesbania grandiflora* (printed as "Sesbana grandiflora" in Bhishagratna's Appendix 3 and as "Sesbania grandiflora" first among three binomials in Clark 1995) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS, FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Identity note.** This row was added 2026-10-09 (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP). Two texts on disk each print the binomial, but the Tibetan entry is doubtful. Clark 1995 gives three binomials for the one Tibetan name so.cha (*Sesbania grandiflora*, *Randia dumetorum*, *Aesculus chinensis*), and the extraction key simply took the first. The description Clark prints (a large hard trunk, a "nut" with a kernel like the castor seed, a hot-tasting emetic) is not one this entry can reconcile with *Sesbania grandiflora*. Whether the Sushruta plant and the Tibetan plant are the same species is not established by either text. [USER: verify] The row has not been checked against a modern botanical authority.

**Sanskrit — Sushruta (SS).** The translation is Bhishagratna; the Latin names come from his Appendix 3 (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`), printed in two OCR columns that I paired by position. The Sanskrit list runs Abhaya, Abhraka, Adhaki, Aditya-vaktra, Ardraka, Agaru-dhuma, *Agasti* (line 31136), *Agastya* (line 31137), Agni. The Latin column runs *Terminalia chebula*, "Talc", "See Tuvara", *Cleome viscosa* / *Gynandropsis pentaphylla*, *Zingiber*, "Soot", "Sesbana grandiflora." (line 31196), "Agati." (line 31197), "See Chitraka". The pairs before it are internally consistent (Abhaya with chebulic myrobalan, Abhraka with talc, Ardraka with ginger, Agaru-dhuma with soot), so the pairing of *Agasti* with *Sesbana grandiflora* and of *Agastya* with the synonym "Agati" holds. Agastya is also the name of a sage and of a star in the Sushruta text (`uttara/SS_Uttara_ch64.txt`, lines 98 and 118; `uttara/SS_Uttara_ch52.txt`, line 347, the *Agastya-leha* "first concocted by the holy sage, Agastya"). Those are not this plant and are not cited as such.

The plant's flower is described in Sutrasthana ch.46 (`sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt`), in the "Flower Group" of the chapter on food and drink. At line 1777: "The flowers of the Vrisha and Agastya have a bitter taste, are pungent in digestion and alleviate a wasting cough (Phthisis)." At line 1790: "The Agastya flower is neither too cooling nor inordinately heat-making in its potency and proves specially beneficial in cases of night-blindness (Nyctalopia)." This is the only plant use found for the name. No preparation, dose or recipe was found in the chapters searched for *Agasti* or *Agastya*.

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch.20* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, line 986): "so.cha: Soapnut Tree: Sesbania grandiflora: Randia dumetorum: Aesculus chinensis: [has nine names. It has hot taste, slightly oily power and is an emetic in all types of phlegm disorders. The trunk is large, white and hard with thin glossy leaves and pale yellow flowers. The nut resembles an old man's testicle and contains a kernel similar to the castor oil seed, the castor bean]. It is the supreme all-round emetic for all diseases." The square brackets are Clark's mark for the commentarial text. The next lines are dan.rog (line 987; *Croton tiglium*, "an emetic and purgative of phlegm and tri-humoral disorders") and dan.khra (*Ricinus communis*), so so.cha sits among the emetics and purgatives. The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`) prints "so cha" among the Tibetan names (line 5791) and "Sesbania sp. / Inkberry, sesban" among the scientific and common names (lines 5824-5826), but the OCR splits the columns and the two are not tied to each other on the page. That pairing is therefore not asserted.

### Active Components

*Sesbania grandiflora* is a flowering legume tree, and legumes generally carry proteins, flavonoids and saponins. The constituents of the plants named in the two texts were not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Sushruta records a flower described as bitter, pungent in digestion and temperate in potency, with a night-blindness use. The Tibetan text records an emetic, and its description is of a nut, not a flower. The two uses are different and the Codex does not combine them. The Codex gives no protocol for either.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither text read gives a preparation for this plant. Sushruta ch.46 describes the flower by its properties and Clark line 986 describes so.cha by its powers and use as an emetic. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] A general point: night-blindness is classically a sign of vitamin A deficiency, and this is the only modern fact offered here; no claim is made that the flower treats it.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SS:** ch.46, line 1777: flower "bitter in taste", "pungent in digestion"; line 1790: "neither too cooling nor inordinately heat-making in its potency". No dosha action is printed for the Agastya flower in these lines.
- **FMT:** Clark line 986: "hot taste, slightly oily power".

### Cultivation Register

**Sanskrit — Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The flower is claimed in the food-and-drink chapter by taste, vipaka and potency, with cough and night-blindness named as its use. It is not named in a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* chapter among the passages read, and no *ojas* action is attested for it.

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra classes so.cha as an emetic by taste and power. In the cultivation spec's frame, medicine is the third of four treatment branches in this tradition, supporting practice and not reaching the root, and no category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. Nothing read assigns this plant a place in *bcud len*. The identification with *Sesbania grandiflora* is the first of three binomials in the glossary-style line. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the sources read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. In Sushruta it is a flower of the food group; in the Tibetan text it is a strong emetic whose identity is unsettled. The abstentive answer is correct: no use is recommended.

### Contraindications

The Tibetan so.cha is described as the "supreme" emetic, so the identification matters: *Randia* and *Aesculus* (the other two binomials given) are different plants, and forced vomiting is itself hazardous. Do not self-prescribe emetics. Do not take any part of an unidentified plant. Seek medical advice for night-blindness, which has treatable general causes. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
