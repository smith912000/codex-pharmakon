### Origin & Tradition

Thyme is the premier antimicrobial culinary herb of the Western pharmacological tradition — its essential oil contains thymol and carvacrol, two of the most potent naturally occurring antimicrobial phenols ever characterized. Confirmed across five medical traditions with independent recognition of identical properties — respiratory, antimicrobial, carminative, and aromatic-medicinal — thyme represents the closest thing to a universal Western herb that the Codex contains.

**AT — Assyrian (Hašū — 𒄩𒍮):** R. Campbell Thompson (1924) identifies *hašū* as thyme through linguistic comparison — the Akkadian term traces through Arabic *hasha* into the broader Semitic aromatic herb vocabulary. Assyrian medical tablets use thyme-type plants for respiratory conditions, as aromatic antimicrobial fumigants, and in digestive compound preparations. The Nineveh corpus documents it in formulas for cough, lung conditions, and aromatic purification.

**EP — Egyptian (Tiam — referenced in aromatic preparations):** The Ebers Papyrus includes thyme-type aromatic herbs in respiratory preparations and compound antimicrobial formulas. Egyptian medicine prized aromatic phenolic herbs for both their pharmaceutical activity and their ritual fumigation properties — thyme smoke has genuine airborne antimicrobial activity that the Egyptians empirically recognized.

**DMM — De Materia Medica (Θύμος — Thymos):** Dioscorides covers thyme in Book III. He classifies it as warming in the second degree with drying properties. Primary indications: respiratory (cough, asthma — *orthopnoia*), digestive (flatulence, intestinal pain), antiparasitic (expels intestinal worms), diuretic, and emmenagogue. His description of thyme wine for cough is the earliest documented thymol-containing therapeutic preparation.

**CM — Canon of Medicine (زعتر — Za'tar / Sater):** Ibn Sina documents thyme extensively, noting its warm and dry temperament (second degree). He prescribes it for: respiratory congestion and cough (primary indication); headache via inhalation; digestive complaints; and as an antiparasitic. His distinction between cultivated thyme (*Thymus vulgaris*) and wild thyme (*T. serpyllum* — Sa'tar Barrī) reflects genuine botanical pharmacognosy.

**SS — Ayurveda (Ajwain relative / Hasha — हष):** Thyme enters Ayurveda primarily through the Unani tradition; native Indian Thymus species (*T. linearis*) are used in Himalayan folk medicine. The Ayurvedic thyme-adjacent category is the *Satureja* and *Origanum* group — all high-thymol/carvacrol aromatics used for Kapha-Vata respiratory and digestive conditions. Classification: Katu rasa, Ushna virya, Kapha-Vata shamaka.

### Active Components

- **Thymol (2-isopropyl-5-methylphenol)** — 20–55% of essential oil; the dominant bioactive; phenolic antiseptic; potent antimicrobial against broad spectrum of bacteria and fungi; the active component in Listerine (dental antiseptic, which uses thymol as the primary antimicrobial)
- **Carvacrol (5-isopropyl-2-methylphenol)** — 1–20% of essential oil; isomer of thymol; comparable antimicrobial activity; additional anti-inflammatory effects (NF-κB and COX-2 inhibition)
- **p-Cymene** — terpene precursor to both thymol and carvacrol during biosynthesis; mild antimicrobial; synergistic with phenolic components
- **γ-Terpinene, α-Terpinene** — monoterpene precursors; antioxidant; antimicrobial
- **Linalool** — minor component; contributes mild sedative/calming note
- **Rosmarinic acid** — phenolic acid; anti-inflammatory (COX/LOX); antiviral; antioxidant; also the primary active in rosemary
- **Flavonoids** — luteolin, apigenin, thymonin, cirsilineol; anti-inflammatory, antioxidant
- **Tannins** — astringent; wound-healing

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Primary application: Respiratory and upper airway antimicrobial protection**

High-intensity training creates periods of immunosuppression (the "open window" hypothesis — post-exercise window of increased infection susceptibility lasting 3–72 hours). Thyme's thymol-carvacrol combination provides evidence-based protection against the bacterial and viral respiratory pathogens that preferentially strike during this window.

- **Respiratory antimicrobial** — thymol directly inhibits *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, respiratory viruses; both topical (gargle) and inhalation delivery confirmed active
- **Bronchodilatory + antispasmodic** — flavonoids (particularly cirsilineol) inhibit phosphodiesterase → relaxes bronchial smooth muscle; reduces exercise-induced bronchospasm
- **Mucus regulation** — expectorant action: increases mucociliary clearance; thins viscous mucus; combines with antispasmodic for productive management of respiratory secretions
- **Carminative** — thymol antispasmodic on GI smooth muscle; resolves digestive Qi stagnation post-training

### Preparation & Dosing

**Infusion (standard therapeutic):**
2–4g dried thyme in 250mL hot water; steep 10 minutes covered (preserves volatile oils); drink 3 cups/day for respiratory conditions. This preparation delivers thymol, carvacrol, and rosmarinic acid simultaneously.

**Thyme honey preparation (Mel Thymi):**
Raw honey infused with fresh thyme; antimicrobial synergy between honey and thymol; traditional preparation across all Mediterranean traditions. Superior for throat and upper respiratory infections.

**Thyme gargle:**
Strong thyme infusion (6g/250mL) cooled to body temperature; gargle 30 seconds, spit; repeat 3 times daily for pharyngitis, tonsillitis, oral antimicrobial effect.

**Essential oil (aromatherapy only — not internal):**
1–2 drops thyme essential oil (thymol chemotype) in diffuser for airborne antimicrobial effect. Do NOT ingest thyme essential oil — thymol is toxic in concentrated form.

**Standardized extract:**
*Bronchipret* (thyme + ivy combination, well-studied in RCTs): 5.4mL liquid twice daily for acute bronchitis.

**Cycle:** Safe for continuous culinary use. Therapeutic concentrate cycles: 2–4 weeks maximum.

### Scientific Correlation

- **Antimicrobial (thymol/carvacrol):** Among the most studied plant antimicrobials. Thymol disrupts bacterial cell membranes (increases permeability, inhibits ATPase); active against methicillin-resistant *S. aureus* (MRSA) at clinically relevant concentrations (Nostro A et al., 2004)
- **Bronchitis RCT:** Büechi S et al. (2005), *Arzneimittelforschung*: thyme-ivy combination syrup non-inferior to ambroxol for acute bronchitis; Kemmerich B et al. (2006): thyme-ivy liquid superior to placebo for bronchitis (n=361, double-blind); German Commission E approved thyme for bronchitis
- **Antiviral:** Labieniec-Watala M et al. (2015): thymol inhibits herpes simplex virus; rosmarinic acid inhibits multiple respiratory viruses
- **Anti-inflammatory (rosmarinic acid):** Well-characterized COX-2 and 5-LOX inhibition; anti-inflammatory equivalent to ibuprofen in some in vitro models at high concentrations

### Contraindications

- **Thyroid conditions** — thymol may mildly interfere with thyroid function at high doses; monitor in hyperthyroidism
- **Pregnancy (therapeutic doses)** — emmenagogue at high concentrated doses; culinary use safe; avoid essential oil and high-dose extract internally
- **Essential oil toxicity** — pure thyme essential oil is a skin irritant and toxic if ingested; never ingest undiluted essential oil; dilute to ≤2% for topical use
- **Lamiaceae allergy** — cross-reactivity with oregano, basil, sage, lavender possible in sensitive individuals
