# I-240 | Passiflora incarnata — Passionflower / Maypop

## Section 1 | Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Passiflora incarnata* L.  
**Family:** Passifloraceae  
**Common names:** Passionflower (English), Maypop (English — North American; from the popping sound of the fruit when stepped on), Purple Passionflower (distinguishing from other *Passiflora* spp.), Passion Vine, Apricot Vine, Wild Apricot, Corona de Cristo (Spanish — "Crown of Christ"), Flor de la Pasión (Spanish), Fleur de la Passion (French), Fleischfarbene Passionsblume (German), Passiflore (French), Granadilla (Spanish — sometimes applied to *P. quadrangularis*), Carkifelek (Turkish), Tzi-Tzi-Ka (Algonquin — indigenous North American name)

**Passiflora genus complexity:** Over 500 *Passiflora* species globally; most are South/Central American; only *P. incarnata* is native to North America (southeastern United States). Key medicinal species:
- *Passiflora incarnata* L. — this entry; primary anxiolytic/sedative; North America + SE US; chrysin-containing
- *Passiflora edulis* Sims — Passion Fruit / Granadilla; consumed as food; South America; different phytochemical profile; some overlap
- *Passiflora caerulea* L. — Blue Passionflower; European ornamental; South American origin; different alkaloid profile

**The "Passion" naming:** Named by Spanish Jesuit missionaries in South America (16th–17th century) — they interpreted the flower's morphology as symbolic of Christ's Passion: the 10 petals/sepals = 10 apostles, the corona = crown of thorns, the 3 stigmas = 3 nails, the 5 anthers = 5 wounds. This Christian symbolic interpretation of a New World plant's morphology embedded the plant into Catholic missionary medicine and ultimately drove its introduction to European pharmacopoeia.

**Botanical description:** Tendril-climbing perennial vine 8m+; deeply 3-lobed leaves; extraordinary ornate flowers 6–8cm with fringed corona of filaments (purple-white); oval egg-shaped fruits ("maypops") edible when ripe. Native range: southeastern US (Virginia to Florida, west to Texas and Oklahoma); introduced to Europe and other continents.

**Authenticated part(s):** Aerial parts — primarily leaves and stems; flowers; harvested at flowering stage. Fruit (Maypop) edible but not primary medicinal part. European Pharmacopoeia monograph: standardized to flavonoid content (minimum 1.5% total flavonoids expressed as vitexin).

**Convergence score: 3/9 (BM ✓, SA ✓, European tradition ✓ — entered Old World post-1600s)**

**Important context:** Passionflower's convergence score is necessarily limited by its New World origin — the Old World traditions had no access to it prior to 1565 (first documented Spanish record). However, after introduction to Europe, Passionflower entered German, French, and British pharmacopoeias within 100 years — a remarkably rapid pharmacological adoption driven by indigenous American documentation of its effects. The rapid European uptake represents a cross-cultural pharmacological confirmation: indigenous American sedative/anxiolytic knowledge validated by European practitioners who independently replicated the observed effects.

**The Western hemisphere convergence pattern:** Passionflower + Valerian (I-239) exhibit a striking hemispheric duality for GABA-ergic sedatives: Old World traditions developed Valerian; New World traditions developed Passionflower; both arrived at GABA-A modulation via completely different phytochemical pathways (valerenic acid vs. chrysin/flavonoids). The convergence is pharmacological, not cultural.

## Section 3 | Convergence Analysis

**Primary convergence axis — sedation/anxiolysis:** BM/Aztec, multiple SA traditions, and rapidly adopted European tradition all converge on sedation, anxiety, and sleep-related indications as primary applications. Unlike most Codex entries where convergence reflects independent discovery of the same plant's properties, Passionflower's convergence follows a post-contact diffusion pattern: indigenous American → Spanish missionaries → European pharmacopoeia → global spread. The validation at each step of diffusion (each new culture independently confirming the reported effect) constitutes a form of sequential cross-cultural verification.

**Epilepsy documentation (BM + European):** The BM/Aztec documentation of *Passiflora* for epilepsy aligns with European documentation from the 19th century (American Eclectic practitioners used *P. incarnata* for epilepsy and hysteria). Modern pharmacology confirms GABA-A modulation and anti-epileptic potential. This BM → European → modern research chain for the epilepsy indication is a complete ethnopharmacological validation arc.

**GABA-ergic botanical cluster:** This entry, Valerian (I-239), Lavender (I-241), and Lemon Balm (I-242) form a pharmacological cluster of GABA-A/GABA-T modulating plants from different continents, each with different phytochemical actives but convergent mechanism. The Codex documents this mechanism cluster as a cross-civilizational finding — the human need for anxiolytic/sedative plants is universal, and the GABA-A system appears to be the primary pharmacological target discoverable through empirical plant use across all phytochemical frameworks.

## Section 4 | Phytochemical Composition

**Flavonoids (primary bioactives — 1.5–2.5% in aerial parts):**
- **Chrysin (5,7-dihydroxyflavone):** Primary anxiolytic; GABA-A benzodiazepine site partial agonist; MOA parallel to benzodiazepines but without full agonism → less dependence/tolerance potential; 0.5–1.5% in aerial parts; also aromatase inhibitor (enzyme inhibition: testosterone → estrogen pathway)
- **Vitexin (apigenin-8-C-glucoside):** Primary quality marker; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; GABA-A modulating; Europharmacopoeia standardization marker (minimum 1.5% total flavonoids as vitexin)
- **Isovitexin (orientin analog):** Similar to vitexin; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory
- **Orientin, isoorientin:** Luteolin C-glycosides; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; mild anxiolytic
- **Vicenin:** Apigenin bis-C-glycoside; antioxidant
- **Lucenin-2, schaftoside, isoschaftoside:** Minor flavonoids

**Alkaloids:**
- **Harmane, harmine, harmaline:** β-carboline alkaloids (MAO-A inhibitors); present in trace amounts in *P. incarnata*; at pharmacological doses would require caution with serotonergic drugs; at trace levels in standard preparations: minimal clinical significance but warrant awareness
- **Passiflorine:** pyridine alkaloid; sedative property at isolated doses

**Polyketides:**
- **Maltol:** aromatic compound; GABA-A activity; partially contributes to overall sedative profile (shared with valerian)

**Phenolic acids:**
- Chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid, p-coumaric acid: antioxidant; anti-inflammatory

**Amino acids:**
- GABA (free): low levels; possibly contributes to direct peripheral GABA effect
- γ-Aminobutyric acid precursors

## Section 5 | Mechanisms of Action

**1. GABA-A Modulation (Primary — via Chrysin)**
- **Chrysin:** Binds GABA-A receptor at the benzodiazepine (BZD) binding site (between α and γ subunits)
- Partial agonist behavior: enhances GABA-mediated Cl⁻ channel opening but with lower efficacy than full BZD agonists (diazepam)
- Consequence: anxiolytic and sleep-promoting effects WITHOUT full benzodiazepine receptor occupancy → less tolerance development, less withdrawal potential, no respiratory depression
- Subunit selectivity: chrysin shows preference for α₁/γ₂ and α₂/γ₂ subunit combinations → sedative + anxiolytic effects
- Animal pharmacology: chrysin at 1–2 mg/kg produces diazepam-equivalent anxiolysis (elevated plus maze); at 10 mg/kg: sedation

**2. MAO Inhibition (Harmane alkaloids — trace amounts)**
- Harmane/harmine: reversible MAO-A inhibitors at pharmacological concentrations
- At trace amounts in standard preparations: contribution to overall effect uncertain
- Potential to modulate serotonin/dopamine at therapeutic doses
- CAUTION consideration: see drug interactions

**3. Adenosine A1 Receptor Effects**
- Some *Passiflora* compounds modulate adenosine receptors (shared with valerian)
- Contributes to sleep-promotion orthogonal to GABA mechanism

**4. Anti-Spasmodic (smooth muscle)**
- Chrysin + vitexin: relax intestinal and uterine smooth muscle
- Relevant to traditional "anti-hysteria" application (uterine spasm interpretation)
- Comparable to valerian valerenic acid smooth muscle effect

**5. Antioxidant / Anti-inflammatory**
- Flavonoids: broad antioxidant spectrum; NF-κB inhibition at pharmacological concentrations
- Anti-inflammatory relevant to "fever" uses documented in some traditions
- Neuroprotective: reduces oxidative stress in hippocampus, cortex

## Section 6 | Clinical Evidence

**Generalized Anxiety Disorder / Anxiety (Level A)**
*Akhondzadeh et al. (2001) — RCT (landmark):* N=36 patients with GAD; *Passiflora incarnata* extract (45 drops/day) vs. oxazepam (30mg/day); 4 weeks. Primary endpoints: Hamilton Anxiety (HAM-A) rating, impairment. Results: both groups showed significant equivalent improvement in HAM-A (p<0.01); Passionflower showed significantly LESS impairment of job performance vs. oxazepam (p<0.05). Conclusion: Passionflower equivalent to oxazepam for GAD with superior occupational functioning profile — one of the most significant herbal-vs.-pharmaceutical equivalence RCTs in the anxiety space.

*Ngan & Conduit (2011) — RCT:* N=41 adults; low-dose passionflower tea (2g dried herb) vs. placebo; single-night crossover. Subjective sleep quality significantly better (PSQI component scores); no next-day impairment. Demonstrates acute single-dose effect at low doses.

**Preoperative Anxiety (Level A)**
*Movafegh et al. (2008) — RCT:* N=60 surgical patients; passionflower extract vs. placebo (preoperative); HAM-A, sedation score measured preoperatively. Passionflower significantly reduced preoperative anxiety without affecting psychomotor performance or recovery from anesthesia. Clinically important finding: anxiolytic without anesthetic interaction risk at standard doses.

*Dantas et al. (2017) — RCT:* N=40 dental procedure patients; passionflower vs. placebo; Visual Analog Scale anxiety scores significantly reduced in passionflower group. Dental anxiety is one of the most prevalent specific anxiety presentations globally.

**ADHD / Pediatric (Level B)**
*Akhondzadeh et al. (2005) — RCT:* N=34 children with ADHD; passionflower extract vs. methylphenidate; 8 weeks. SNAP-IV ADHD rating: methylphenidate slightly superior for hyperactivity; passionflower non-significantly different for inattention. Passionflower produced significantly fewer adverse effects (anorexia, anxiety, social withdrawal — typical MPH side effects) vs. methylphenidate. Relevant clinical option for ADHD patients with comorbid anxiety or MPH side-effect intolerance.

**Menopause / Hot Flashes (Level B)**
*Fahami et al. (2010) — RCT:* N=59 menopausal women; passionflower capsule vs. placebo × 6 weeks. Menopausal symptoms score (hot flash severity, sleep) significantly improved.

**Opioid Withdrawal (Level C — emerging)**
*Akhondzadeh et al. (2001b) — RCT:* N=65 opioid-dependent patients; passionflower + clonidine vs. clonidine alone (for opioid detoxification). Primary outcome: withdrawal symptom scores. Both groups showed similar improvements in physical symptoms; passionflower group showed significantly better outcomes for psychiatric withdrawal symptoms (anxiety, irritability, agitation). Passionflower adjunct to clonidine for opioid detoxification — interesting GABA mechanism application.

## Section 7 | Phenomenological Record

**The Jesuit pharmacological accident:** The Spanish Jesuit missionaries who named Passionflower were engaged in religious symbolism, not pharmacology. Their naming of the flower's morphological features as Christ's Passion symbols became the vehicle through which indigenous American anxiety/sedative knowledge entered European medicine. The pharmacological information traveled inside religious interpretation — an example of cultural translation both obscuring and transmitting pharmacological knowledge simultaneously.

**Chrysin and the "herbal benzodiazepine" concept:** The discovery that chrysin binds the BZD receptor site generated enormous popular interest and marketing claims. The marketing interpretation ("natural benzodiazepine") is pharmacologically inaccurate (partial agonist, not full agonist), but the underlying mechanism is real. Chrysin's partial agonism provides the same molecular basis for anxiolytic effects that full BZD agonists exploit, but without the full downstream consequences (tolerance, dependence, respiratory depression). This makes *Passiflora* one of the few herbal medicines where the mechanism precisely parallels a pharmaceutical drug class while the clinical consequence profile is genuinely safer.

**The Akhondzadeh GAD trial and its implications:** The finding that passionflower equaled oxazepam for anxiety while producing significantly less work impairment is one of the most important clinical data points in botanical medicine. Oxazepam is the gold-standard treatment for GAD in many European countries; demonstrating equivalence for efficacy with superior functional profile in a properly designed RCT is a pharmacological achievement that most pharmaceuticals fail to match against each other. The trial deserves more citation in mainstream psychiatric literature than it currently receives.

**Indigenous American pharmacological precision:** The BM/Aztec documentation of *Passiflora* for anxiety and epilepsy — two of the most specific neurological indications in pre-Columbian American medicine — was pharmacologically accurate. The GABA-A mechanism that Aztec physicians empirically identified through their tradition of careful plant observation was eventually confirmed by molecular pharmacology 500 years later. The precision of pre-Columbian neurological pharmacology, embedded in the BM, is not adequately recognized in the standard history of neuroscience.

## Section 8 | Practical Application

**Standardized preparations:**
- **Standardized dry extract (1.5% vitexin):** 200–400mg; 1–3×/day for anxiety; 400mg bedtime for sleep
- **Dried aerial parts (tea):** 1–2g in 250mL boiling water × 10 min; 1–3 cups/day; traditional preparation; Ngan 2011 dose = 2g effective
- **Fluid extract (1:1, 45% ethanol):** 1–4 mL 3×/day; preserves full alkaloid + flavonoid profile
- **Tincture (1:5, 40% ethanol):** 1–2 mL 3×/day; most common commercial form
- **Acute anxiety dose (Akhondzadeh protocol):** 45 drops fluid extract in water = approximately 3–4 mL; 3×/day

**Clinical protocols:**
- *GAD (moderate):* Standardized extract 300mg 3×/day × 4 weeks; Akhondzadeh protocol (fluid extract equivalent); HAM-A monitoring; non-inferior to oxazepam with better work function
- *Insomnia:* 400mg extract or 2–3g herbal tea 30–60 min before bedtime; may combine with Valerian (I-239) or Lemon Balm (I-242) for enhanced GABA-A + GABA-T synergy
- *Preoperative anxiety:* 500mg 90 min pre-procedure (Movafegh protocol); no need to hold anesthesia; inform anesthesiologist
- *ADHD anxiety component:* 200mg 3×/day × 8 weeks; adjunct; particularly useful in MPH-intolerant patients with comorbid anxiety; not substitute for ADHD-specific treatment
- *Opioid withdrawal support:* Adjunct to clonidine protocol; 400mg 3×/day during active withdrawal; addresses psychiatric component (anxiety/agitation)

**Drug interactions:**
- **CNS depressants (alcohol, benzodiazepines, sedatives, opioids):** Additive sedation; caution; significant interaction potential; avoid driving/heavy machinery with combination
- **Warfarin/anticoagulants:** Anti-platelet activity; INR monitoring recommended
- **MAO inhibitors (phenelzine, tranylcypromine, selegiline):** Trace harmane alkaloids are MAO-A inhibitors → potential additive MAO inhibition → serotonin syndrome risk if combined with serotonergic MAO inhibitors; AVOID
- **SSRI/SNRI antidepressants:** Theoretical serotonergic interaction via trace harmane alkaloids; clinical significance minimal at standard doses but monitor for serotonin symptoms
- **Anticonvulsant medications:** GABA-A modulation may interact with AED pharmacology; notify neurologist; generally considered synergistic at standard doses but monitor

**Contraindications:**
- **Pregnancy:** Uterotonic smooth muscle relaxant activity; AVOID pharmacological doses; traditional use as emmenagogue in some traditions confirms concern
- **Children <6 years:** Insufficient safety data for young children
- **Active hepatic disease:** Rare hepatotoxicity case reports with very high doses; monitor LFTs with chronic use

**Safety:** Excellent. One of the safest anxiolytics documented in RCT literature — equivalent to oxazepam efficacy, significantly fewer side effects. Most common adverse effects: mild drowsiness, dizziness. No significant dependence or withdrawal documented at standard doses. No respiratory depression. No hepatotoxicity at standard doses. GRAS status in USA as food ingredient.

**Energetic tier:** ☿ Shen (anxiolytic, sedative, nervous system calming — primary neuromodulatory) primary; ◉ Qi (anti-spasmodic, calms Qi agitation) secondary.
