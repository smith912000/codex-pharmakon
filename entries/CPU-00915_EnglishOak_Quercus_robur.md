# English Oak — Pedunculate Oak / Robur / Chêne / Eiche / Quercus

## *Quercus robur* L. | Family: Fagaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM, SBJ
**Energetic Classification:** Cold & dry (2nd–3rd degree), strongly astringent-binding (Galenic reading)
**Training Phase:** Consolidation / Binding (the flux-and-haemorrhage gate)

> **Codex note:** This entry is the **bark and acorn of the European tree oak** — the astringent tissue of the living tree. It is **distinct from the Aleppo Oak / oak-gall (*Quercus infectoria* nutgall)** already filed in the Codex, which is the insect-induced gall used as an even more concentrated tannin source. Same chemical family (tannins), different drug organ.

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (cuneiform tablets).** The Assyrian herbal repertory includes strongly "binding" and "drying" tree-bark and gall drugs used to staunch fluxes, close wounds and treat looseness of the bowel. Oak and oak-gall products belong to this astringent class, but as with all cuneiform plant names, exact species mapping is insecure. **Hedge the Assyrian attribution: [USER: verify]** — do not invent an Akkadian binomial.

**Dioscorides (*De Materia Medica*).** Dioscorides describes *drys* (δρῦς, the oak): the bark, the inner acorn-cup and the acorn are **binding (styptic) and drying**, used for fluxes of the belly (diarrhoea, dysentery), spitting of blood and other haemorrhages, and applied externally to lax, weeping or bleeding tissue and to strengthen soft gums. This is the founding astringent monograph — oak as the archetype of the drug that *binds and dries*.

**Avicenna (*Canon of Medicine*).** The Arabic-Persian tradition classes oak (*ballūṭ*, the acorn/oak) as cold and dry and powerfully astringent (*qābiḍ*): it arrests diarrhoea and dysentery, checks haemorrhage and excessive discharges, tightens lax tissue, and is used as a gargle for slack, bleeding gums and throat. The doctrinal category is unambiguous — a **retentive, binding cold-dry drug**.

**Shennong / TCM (oak-bark & acorn use).** Chinese materia medica employs oak bark and acorn (*Quercus* spp., e.g. the bark/acorn drug traditions around *xiang shi* / oak acorn) as **astringent, bitter and slightly warm-to-neutral**, used to **bind the intestines and stop diarrhoea/dysentery (*se chang zhi xie*)** and to check discharge — the same "restrain and consolidate" astringent logic, arrived at independently. *(Species attribution within the Chinese oak drugs varies; the astringent antidiarrheal use is genuine. **European folk & official stratum.** *Quercus* bark (*Cortex Quercus*) is an official drug: decoctions and baths for acute non-specific diarrhoea, for weeping/inflamed skin conditions (eczema, chilblains, sweaty feet), for haemorrhoids, and as an astringent gargle for mouth and throat inflammation. This use is formally recognised — the German Commission E and **ESCOP** monograph oak bark for **non-specific acute diarrhoea** and for topical/local inflammations of skin and mucosa.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** Oak's doctrine is the purest example in the entire Codex of a symbolic reading that *is* the molecular fact. Every tradition says the same thing — **"binds, staunches, dries, holds in"** — and that single verb describes, with almost no loss, what tannins physically do. Oak bark and acorn are loaded with **hydrolysable tannins (ellagitannins, gallotannins)** and **condensed tannins (proanthocyanidins)**. A tannin is, by chemical definition, a polyphenol large enough to **cross-link and precipitate proteins**. Poured onto lax, weeping, inflamed or bleeding mucosa, tannins bind the surface proteins of the tissue and of any exudate into a tightened, coagulated, less-permeable layer — clinically, tissue that stops weeping and oozing. This is not analogy: it is **the identical reaction used to tan leather**, in which oak (and its galls) turn soft raw hide into tough, water-resistant, rot-proof material by cross-linking its collagen. The tanner and the physician exploit one chemical event. The astringent "puckering" a person feels from oak or strong tea is that same protein-precipitation happening on the salivary proteins of the tongue — the *sensation* of astringency is the doctrine reporting itself in real time. And the symbolism completes the loop: the oak is the folk-emblem of **strength, endurance, hardness and steadfastness** ("mighty oak," the durable timber that resists rot) — precisely the qualities tannins confer on the tissue (and the hide) they treat. The tree that stands for *not yielding* contains the molecule that makes flesh *not yield, not weep, not flow*.

### Active Components

- **Hydrolysable tannins:** ellagitannins and gallotannins (bark and acorn); on ageing/oxidation these transform (e.g. in oak-barrel maturation) — tannin chemistry is dynamic, not static.
- **Condensed tannins (proanthocyanidins / catechin oligomers).**
- **Roburins** and related ellagitannin-derived compounds (characteristic of *Q. robur* wood; the marker class in the Robuvit® extract).
- **Flavonoids:** quercetin/kaempferol glycosides.
- **Phenolic acids:** gallic and ellagic acid.
- **Bitter and non-tannin phenolics; acorn also carries starch and lipids.**

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Oak is the **Consolidation/Binding** botanical: a short-course astringent decoction for acute non-specific diarrhoea (a few days only), and a topical/local astringent — sitz baths and washes for haemorrhoids, weeping eczema and inflamed skin, and gargles/rinses for sore, slack or bleeding gums, mouth and throat. It is used where the signal is *flux, laxity, weeping, minor bleeding* — anything flowing that should be held. In Kenetica sequencing it is a corrective/consolidating agent, deployed briefly to re-tighten tissue, and always time-limited because the same protein-binding that helps mucosa also impairs nutrient absorption if prolonged.

### Scientific Correlation

*According to PubMed:*

**The astringent chemistry — mechanism confirmed.** The defining property of tannins is protein precipitation. A review of tannin dynamic chemistry states plainly that "tannins are a group of polyphenols that possess the ability to precipitate proteins," produce astringency by binding salivary peptides, and can deactivate digestive enzymes — and it uses oak (*Quercus*) ellagitannins and oak-barrel ageing as worked examples (Tanaka 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1248/yakushi.23-00170)). The astringency mechanism itself — tannins cross-linking and precipitating salivary proteins — has been directly studied with nephelometry and gel electrophoresis (Brandão 2019, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jafc.9b06184)). The traditional "binds and dries" doctrine and the leather-tanning parallel are, at the molecular level, correct.

**The antidiarrheal indication — tested (in animals).** A UHPLC-MS-guided pharmacology study of *Quercus robur* (and *Q. coccinea*) leaf extract, rich in hydrolysable tannins and flavonoid glycosides, showed **dose-dependent antidiarrheal activity** in the castor-oil-induced diarrhoea rat model: significant prolongation of diarrhoea onset, reduced stool frequency, decreased intestinal fluid volume and inhibited GI transit, with the highest dose approaching the loperamide standard (Mady 2023, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2023.1120146)). This is direct experimental support for the Dioscoridean/Avicennan/TCM "binds the intestines, stops the flux" indication — though in an animal model, not a human trial.

**Human data (oak-wood extract).** A pilot study of the *Quercus robur* wood extract Robuvit® (ellagitannin/roburin-rich) in healthy adults reported improved subjective energy and reduced fatigue with no adverse effects (Országhová 2015, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.5368)). Note honestly: this is a **wood** extract studied for an **anti-fatigue** endpoint — it does *not* test the classical bark astringent/antidiarrheal indication, and should not be cited as if it did.

**Evidence grade.** *Mechanism: strong.* The protein-precipitation basis of astringency is established chemistry and directly explains every traditional astringent use. *Clinical indication: moderate-by-tradition, thin-by-trial.* Oak bark for non-specific acute diarrhoea and for local skin/mucosal inflammation is **officially monographed (ESCOP / Commission E)** on the strength of long use and pharmacology; the best experimental antidiarrheal data are animal-model (Mady 2023); rigorous human RCTs of oak *bark* for diarrhoea are lacking. Do not overstate: the doctrine is chemically vindicated and officially recognised, but a large modern human efficacy trial of the bark is not in hand.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani (Avicenna, attested):** Cold and dry (2nd–3rd degree); powerfully astringent/retentive (*qābiḍ*); arrests diarrhoea, dysentery, haemorrhage and lax discharges.
- **Greek humoral (Dioscorides, attested):** Binding (styptic) and drying; for belly-fluxes, haemorrhage and lax/bleeding tissue.
- **TCM (attested at the astringent-drug level; species [USER: verify]):** Bitter, astringent, roughly neutral; binds the intestines to stop diarrhoea/dysentery, checks discharge.
- **Ayurvedic (rasa/guna/virya/vipaka):** *Not attested* for *Quercus robur* as a classical Ayurvedic dravya — do not fabricate a rasa profile.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** Mesopotamian medicine classifies illness partly by causal agency — a divine hand, a ghost, a broken oath — so a drug is characterised in part by what agency it opposes. The standing blocking warning applies in full to oak: the strongly binding and drying tree-bark and gall drugs are attested as a class, but the cuneiform plant names cannot be mapped one-to-one onto *Quercus robur* with confidence, the entry itself declines to invent an Akkadian binomial, and no deity, demon or tablet attribution is claimed. Under that warning no cultivation status is asserted, and the attestation, such as it is, remains a hedged therapeutic one. Cultivation status: **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides gives oak — *drys* (δρῦς) — its founding astringent monograph: bark, acorn-cup and acorn are binding and drying, used for fluxes of the belly, spitting of blood and other haemorrhages, and applied to lax, weeping or bleeding tissue. He carries no subtle-body system with which to assign a cultivation status, and none should be manufactured for him. What the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention: knowing the drug organ (bark, cup, acorn), judging the styptic action by taste and effect, distinguishing the true oak drug from the more concentrated gall. That is a real practice of provenance and sensory assay — the astringent "pucker" is the doctrine reporting itself on the tongue — not a vital-body doctrine. Cultivation status: **(c) absent** as to any vital-constitution status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** Oak (*ballūṭ*) is classed cold and dry and powerfully astringent (*qābiḍ*): it arrests diarrhoea and dysentery, checks haemorrhage and excessive discharge, tightens lax tissue, and serves as a gargle for slack, bleeding gums. The strongest cultivation idea in the Unani corpus is the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture — the finite vital endowment whose depletion is ageing — but that is a frame of the whole regimen, not a status the tradition assigns to a cold-dry retentive drug, and oak is not claimed as a *mufarrih* (cardiac gladdener strengthening the *ruh*). Its standing is corrective and retentive — it holds in what is flowing — not conservational. Cultivation status: **(b) framed:** attested within humoral doctrine, with no cultivation status sourced.

**Chinese — Bencao (SBJ).** Chinese materia medica employs oak bark and acorn as an astringent, bitter, roughly neutral drug that binds the intestines to stop diarrhoea and dysentery (*se chang zhi xie* 澀腸止瀉) and checks discharge. This is a restraining, consolidating function — deployed briefly against an active flux and then stopped, the character of a drug that attacks a disharmony rather than the conservational work of *yang sheng* 養生. The exact Chinese oak drug and species vary and are flagged for verification in the entry; no upper-grade (上品) or other *san pin* 三品 status for this material is recorded in the sources retrieved, and no named scholarship assigns it one that could be cited here. The herb therefore carries a therapeutic status, not a cultivation one. Cultivation status: **(b) framed:** claimed in the materia medica, no *san pin* grade sourced.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a longevity substance, and no tradition on the tag line treats it as one. Oak is a consolidation-and-binding tool — a short-course astringent decoction for acute non-specific diarrhoea, a gargle for slack or bleeding gums, a sitz bath or compress for haemorrhoids and weeping skin. What a cultivator does with it is re-tighten tissue that is fluxing, weeping or minutely bleeding, and then stop: the same protein-precipitation that closes a weeping mucosa also binds dietary iron and impairs absorption if prolonged, so the tradition's own logic makes it time-limited. The oak's folk-emblem of strength and steadfastness names a real molecular fact — tannins make flesh not yield, not weep, not flow — but that is a corrective virtue applied to tissue, not a reserve a cultivator builds and holds in the body.

### Contraindications

- **Raw acorns and high-tannin raw oak material are not to be eaten** — the tannin load causes GI irritation, nausea and, in quantity, can be toxic; acorns must be leached/cooked to be a safe food. Livestock oak-poisoning from acorns/young leaves is well documented.
- **Tannin–iron interaction:** oak tannins bind dietary non-haem iron and other minerals, reducing absorption; avoid prolonged use and separate from iron-containing meals/supplements, especially in anaemia or in children.
- **GI irritation & constipation:** internal astringent use can cause stomach upset and, if overdone, constipation; **use short-term only** for acute diarrhoea (stop within a few days / if no improvement, seek care).
- **Do not use over large areas of broken/damaged skin, in extensive weeping dermatoses, or in full baths in people with major skin loss, febrile/infectious conditions, heart failure or hypertension** (per oak-bark bath cautions in official monographs).
- May reduce absorption of concurrently taken alkaloidal drugs and other proteins/alkaloids (tannin binding); separate dosing.
- **Do not confuse with Aleppo oak-gall (*Q. infectoria*)**, a more concentrated tannin drug filed separately.
