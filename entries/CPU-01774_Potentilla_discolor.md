# Potentilla discolor — stag.sha, Taksha, "tiger flesh" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Potentilla discolor, the first of three binomials Clark 1995 prints for stag.sha (before "Calophaca crassicaulis" and "Oxytropis chiliophylla Royle (white type)"); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints none of the three and sets Oxytropis microphylla against "Tak sha" by column position* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and under this binomial on Clark's edition alone.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Ebers, Assyrian, Sushruta and Shennong text on disk for *Potentilla* found only other species and other drugs: Dioscorides prints *Potentilla* names in translators' lines for the *pentaphyllon* chapter 4-42 (lines 8373 and 8375), and the Shennong text (Yang 1998) has *She Quan* (*Herba Potentillae Kleinianae*, line 3133) and *Lang Ya* (*Radix Potentillae Cryptonis*, line 3546), which are other species. None is *Potentilla discolor* and none is read as this drug. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 136) the row is keyed by the Tibetan name *stag.sha*, with the identification "Potentilla discolor; Calophaca crassicaulis; Oxytropis chiliophylla Royle (white type)", the locus "Clark 1995 (line 1017)" and the note "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The spine row (CPU-01774) took the **first** of the three binomials. The Men-Tsee-Khang binomial for the same name is a different row, *Oxytropis microphylla* (CPU-01761, I_1325); the third Clark binomial, *Oxytropis chiliophylla*, and the second, *Calophaca crassicaulis*, have no spine row as far as the files read show.

**Identity doubt.** Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" and (line 68) that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect". Here three species of three genera are given for one Tibetan name, and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary gives a fourth (an *Oxytropis*, agreeing in genus with Clark's third). The Tibetan name does not fix the species and *Potentilla discolor* is one candidate. The Codex does not choose among them. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1017).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997), which runs to "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147). The entry follows *a.bi.sha* (line 1016) and precedes *spang.rgyan.dkar.po* (line 1018). It reads:

"stag.sha (lit. 'tiger flesh'): Potentilla discolor: Calophaca crassicaulis: Oxytropis chiliophylla Royle (white type): [has five names. It has slightly bitter taste and subdues spirits by its sweet fragrance. The white variety has a green leaf and stalk and a single root from which many stalks branch out. The flower is reddish-blue, slightly coarse and is in the form of three-sided ears (cff. corn). The black or blue type has a yellowish-green leaf, smaller than the white type, as well as a large pod. It has a strong odor]. It heals wounds, cures septic disorders and poisoning, [halts dysentery, reduces swellings and acts as an emetic]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The taste, the sweet fragrance that "subdues spirits", the description and the second set of actions are the commentary layer; "It heals wounds, cures septic disorders and poisoning" is outside the brackets and is the root-text layer as Clark's marks stand. The name is glossed "lit. 'tiger flesh'" by Clark; the drug is a plant. Clark's name index lists *stag.sha* (line 1701) and his Latin index lists *Potentilla discolor* (line 1629).

*Clark 1995, chapter 21 (line 1212).* In Clark's chapter 21 ("The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines", heading at lines 1205 and 1206), the class of poison-neutralizing medicines reads, in part: "... Pleurospermum hookeri, Senecio dianthus, Scutellaria, Oxytropis chiliophylla, Corallodiscus kingianus ...". *Oxytropis chiliophylla* stands in it by its Latin name, and neither *Potentilla discolor* nor *Calophaca crassicaulis* does.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (read through the name, not the binomial).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), line 3432: "Abikha joins fractured skulls and hot disorder associated with poisoning. Taksha heals wounds, is anti-inflammatory and acts as an antidote against poison. Pang gyen karpo cures laryngitis and hot disorder associated with poisoning." Chapter 21 ("The Group and the Method of Compounding Medicine", contents, line 229), lines 3604 and 3605, where the name is split across a line break: "Tsad, Nampar Guthup, Tak- / sha, Havo, Yungwa, ... are the group of medicines that cure poisoning." The index lists "Taksha 207, 220" (line 7506). The glossary's "T" block prints "Tak sha" (line 5860) and "stag sha" (line 5863); the scientific names (from line 5928) put *Oxytropis microphylla* ("Crazyweed", line 5930) second, against "Tak sha", by position, as I_1325 sets out. The Codex reads "Taksha" and Clark's *stag.sha* as one drug from the spelling and from the agreement in kind of the actions: both editions give "heals wounds" and both give a poison action ("cures ... poisoning", "antidote against poison"). Neither text says so, and the Codex does not carry the Men-Tsee-Khang "anti-inflammatory" over to Clark's "cures septic disorders", although the two are alike in kind. The name is not in the taste groups of chapter 19 and not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23).

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Potentilla discolor*, *stag.sha* and *Taksha* found these. I_1325 (*Oxytropis microphylla*, FMT) is the entry for the same Tibetan name through the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary and quotes Clark's line 1017 in full; the two are not merged, because the binomials differ and the spine holds both. I_1324 (*Oxytropis kansuensis*) and I_1315 (*Marrubium incisum*) print neighbouring lines of the "T" block. III_267 (Tiger) and III_200 (Tiger bone) mention *stag.sha* only to say that it is a plant and a homonym of a tiger product; I_1325 also records that. No entry on *Potentilla discolor* was found. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Potentilla* is a genus of the rose family (Rosaceae) and that many species of the genus are generally known to be rich in tannins, which are astringent; this is a statement about the genus and not about this plant. Which compounds the Tibetan drug holds, and which of its candidate plants it is, is not shown by the lines read, so no statement about its composition is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "heals wounds, cures septic disorders and poisoning" (Clark, line 1017) and that "heals wounds, is anti-inflammatory and acts as an antidote against poison" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3432); Clark's commentary adds dysentery, swellings, an emetic action and a fragrance that "subdues spirits". These are the texts' claims in their own terms, attached to this binomial only through the name, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. A wound that is septic or a poisoning needs medical care, and no plant should be used as an antidote or a wound remedy on the strength of this entry.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's line 1017 prints, inside the brackets (the commentary layer), "slightly bitter taste" for the drug, and describes a "sweet fragrance" and a "strong odor"; no power and no post-digestive taste is printed. The Men-Tsee-Khang lines read (3432, 3604, 5860) print none. The "sweet fragrance" is a quality of smell and is not set equal to a sweet taste.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The materia medica classes this drug by what it does: it heals wounds and counters poison, and both editions place it in the poison group. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. The commentary's remark that its fragrance "subdues spirits" is a therapeutic claim about a class of affliction and is not a cultivation status. The drug is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, lines 3900 to 3950; Clark's chapter 23 is headed "Normal health", lines 1262 to 1276), and Clark's materia medica names other drugs as essence-extraction ingredients (for example lines 853, 971 and 1144), none of them this one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name and not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a wound-and-poison drug of uncertain identity, one of four candidate species for a Tibetan name, with no preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. One of the candidate genera, *Oxytropis*, is generally known to include plants that poison livestock, and the plant behind the Tibetan name is uncertain; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Wounds and poisoning need medical care. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
