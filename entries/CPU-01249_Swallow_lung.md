# Swallow lung — rGyud-bzhi, "lungs of khug.rta" (khug.rta), a type of swallow
## *The lung of a swallow; Clark prints "khug.rta: Cuculus melanoleucus: Riparia riparia: Hirundo rustica: (a type of swallow)", Men-Tsee-Khang prints "red-rumped swallows"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The lung of the swallow is entered in the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, the chapter on the powers of medicines, in Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). It falls in the section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), under the heading "Meat" (line 1161), among the organ meats. The entry, in the line numbered 1170, reads: "Lungs of fox (wa) and of khug.rta: Cuculus melanoleucus: Riparia riparia: Hirundo rustica: (a type of swallow): heal punctured lungs." So the Tantra pairs the lung of the fox with the lung of a bird named khug.rta, and Clark glosses the bird with three binomials and the words "a type of swallow". The three binomials are not three birds in the Tantra: they are Clark's identifications of the Tibetan name, and the first, *Cuculus melanoleucus*, is a cuckoo genus, not a swallow, so Clark's own list is mixed. [USER: verify] The action printed is that the lungs "heal punctured lungs".

The same line states the general rule for organ meat: "The lungs, heart, liver, spleen, kidneys and flesh (of various animals) cure diseases of those respective organs." The swallow lung is the specific case for the lung.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3536) reads: "The lungs of foxes and red-rumped swallows stop haemoptysis." Haemoptysis is the coughing of blood. This and Clark's "punctured lungs" are not the same words, and the lines read cannot say whether they translate one Tibetan phrase; both concern the lung and bleeding or injury. The Tibetan wording is not in the files read, so no closer match is claimed. [USER: verify] The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary of birds lists "Khukta" with the English "Red-rumped Swallow" (lines 6847 and 6851) and the scientific name "Hirundo smithii" (line 6885); the glossary is an OCR of columns, and the pairing of the three rests on their order. [USER: verify] Clark's "Hirundo rustica" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "Hirundo smithii" are different species of the same genus, so the two translations do not identify the bird in the same way. The Men-Tsee-Khang index points to "red-rumped swallows" at page 214 (line 7476).

The Men-Tsee-Khang line also states the general rule for offal ("Offal meat like lungs, heart, liver, spleen and kidneys cures disorders of the respective organs", line 3534).

### Active Components

Bird lung is a thin organ tissue of air sacs and blood vessels; it is protein with little fat. A swallow weighs very little and its lung is tiny. Nothing in the lines read names an active principle, and none specific to this entry is claimed.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is a single action: the lung heals punctured lungs (Clark) or stops coughing of blood (Men-Tsee-Khang). The Codex gives no protocol. Coughing blood and chest injury are medical emergencies, and swallows are small protected wild birds in many countries; there is no practice here to recommend.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation, route or dose is given in the sources retrieved: Clark and Men-Tsee-Khang state the action only, and neither says how many lungs, or whether they are eaten fresh, dried or in a compound. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Eating an animal's lung does not repair a human lung. Haemoptysis and chest wounds have many causes (infection, injury, tumour, clotting disorders) and need urgent clinical assessment. Wild-bird organ tissue can carry bacteria and parasites. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** None is printed for the swallow lung. The general statement on meats ("sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste", Clark line 673) concerns the flesh of the animals in the nine groups, and the lines read do not apply it to an organ of a swallow. Clark prints an action and no taste, potency or humoral action for this entry. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* Neither translation gives the swallow lung any cultivation framing. It is one case of the Tantra's rule that an organ cures disease of the same organ, with a single action. The frame around it is the tradition's own: the project's cultivation spec records that the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) are derived from the three poisons and that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root. That point comes from the spec and not from any line about the swallow. No source read attaches the swallow lung to *bcud len* (essence extraction), and there is no formal Tibetan category of substances for subtle-body practice. Clark's remark (line 57) that "only very few animal products are used" nowadays is a note on usage, not on status.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a list entry for an organ of a small wild bird, with no preparation and no dose; blood in the cough and a chest wound need a clinician. The honest use of the entry is as a record of what the Tantra says.

### Contraindications

- **Emergency conditions:** coughing blood and chest injury need urgent medical care; do not rely on this line.
- **Infection:** wild-bird organs can carry bacteria and parasites.
- **Legal and conservation status:** swallows are protected wild birds in many countries, with their nests and capture regulated; the species is not settled between the two translations. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** killing small migratory birds for a substance with no supported benefit is hard to justify.
