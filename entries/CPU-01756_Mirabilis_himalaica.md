# Mirabilis himalaica — Asho genda, a sho gan+d+ha; Bhatu, ba spru (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Mirabilis himalaica, the binomial the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints by column position against both Asho genda (English name "Ashwagandha") and Bhatu (English name "Winter cherry"); Clark 1995 prints Withania somnifera Dunal, Physalis flexuosa and Asparagus lucidus for a.sho.gandha, and no Mirabilis* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and on a binomial that only one of the two editions prints, and that by position, at two places.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk for *himalaica* and for the genus name *Mirabilis* found nothing. (The Codex's I_769 is *Mirabilis jalapa*, a Badianus plant, which is a different species from another tradition.) The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 237) the key *Mirabilis himalaica* is taken from the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary line 5075, with the note "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". It is the only binomial printed at that place. The glossary prints it **twice**: against Asho genda (line 5075, the row's locus) and against Bhatu (line 5106), both set out below. Clark does not print it; Clark's binomials for the corresponding Tibetan drug are *Withania somnifera* Dunal (first), *Physalis flexuosa* Linn. and *Asparagus lucidus*.

**Identity doubt.** This is the most serious identity doubt in the batch, and it lies inside the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary itself. The scientific column prints *Mirabilis himalaica* (a genus of the four-o'clock family as the Codex understands it, not checked against a flora), but the English column prints "Ashwagandha" against Asho genda and "Winter cherry" against Bhatu, and those are the English names Clark gives to *a.sho.gandha* (Clark: "Winter Cherry: Withania somnifera Dunal"). So the glossary's English names agree with Clark's identification and the glossary's scientific name does not. The Codex cannot say which plant the Tibetan texts meant, or whether the glossary's scientific column is misplaced by a row, and the entry stands for the Tibetan drug. The spine already has *Withania somnifera* (CPU-01036, tag rGyud-bzhi among others). [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1145).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry follows *ra.mnye* (line 1144). It reads:

"a.sho.gandha: Winter Cherry: Withania somnifera Dunal: Physalis flexuosa Linn.: Asparagus lucidus: [has seven names, for example, ba.spru.wa. The Sanskrit name (above) means 'horse smell.' It grows on the Indian plains and is of white and red flowering types. The white type has a round, sticky, pale green leaf with thin petiole, whilst the red type has an attractive pale flower. Winter cherry has hot and sweet tastes], cures cold disorders of the lower body, lymph disorders, [wind disorders, and first stage oedema, and dissolves calculi. The dark flowered type is not suitable for use in medicine.]"

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the seven names (one of them *ba.spru.wa*), the gloss "horse smell", the habitat on the Indian plains, the white and red flowering types, the hot and sweet tastes, the further uses (wind disorders, first stage oedema, calculi) and the warning that the dark flowered type is not suitable for medicine. Outside them (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the English name and the three binomials, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the clause "cures cold disorders of the lower body, lymph disorders". The line gives no part used and no preparation. Clark's index lists *Winter Cherry* and *Withania somnifera* (line 1661), and the Tibetan name *ba.spru.wa* (line 1719).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text on "Asho genda" (line 3502).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428). The sentence reads: "Chawa cures disorders of the chhuser and cold disorders associated with the kidney and lower back. Nye shing and Ra nye prolong life and cure chhuser disorders. Asho genda cures cold disorders of the lower body and chhuser disorders. Zema cures difficulty of urination, arthritis and kidney disorders." The index lists "Asho genda 212" (line 7260). Clark's "cold disorders of the lower body" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "cold disorders of the lower body" are the same wording; Clark's "lymph disorders" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "chhuser disorders" are two translators' words, and the Codex does not treat them as one term.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, "the five roots" (lines 1116 and 1144).* In the first part (the Root Tantra, therapeutic methods) the text reads: "Medicinal butter is prepared from Zati, Gokya, the three fruits, the five roots and Menchhen as the main ingredients" (line 1116), and a note (line 1144) says: "The five roots are Ra nye, Nye shing, Chawa, Bhatu and Zema." The sentence at line 3502 above names the same five roots in a slightly different order (Chawa, Nye shing, Ra nye, Asho genda, Zema), with Asho genda where the note has Bhatu. On that evidence the Codex reads Asho genda and Bhatu as two names of one drug in this text; the index lists "Bhatu 33" (line 7272), the page of the note. Clark's list of the five roots differs: "the five roots, including small caltrops, Angelica sp., and black aconite" (line 186), and it does not name winter cherry. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary, first place (the "A" block, lines 5057 to 5077).* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate runs. Eight phonetic names are printed (lines 5059 to 5063): "Abikha", "Adre", "Agaru", "Ajak", "Arura", "Asho genda", "Atong", "Awa". The transliterations (lines 5066 to 5071) include "a sho gan+d+ha" (line 5070). The scientific names (lines 5073 to 5077) are eight: "Fritillaria delavayi", "Mangifera indica", "Aquilaria agallocha", "Pyrethrum tatsienense" (all four on 5073), "Terminalia chebula" (5074), "Mirabilis himalaica" (5075), "Tanacetum mutellinum" (5076) and "Lloydia serotina" (5077). Counted in order, "Asho genda" is the sixth phonetic name and *Mirabilis himalaica* the sixth scientific name, and "Ashwagandha" is the last name on the English line that holds "Mango Eagle or aloe wood Ragwort Chebulic myrobalan Ashwagandha" (line 5150), where "Chebulic myrobalan" falls on Arura (fifth) and "Ashwagandha" follows it. The transliteration "a sho gan+d+ha" is Clark's *a.sho.gandha* in another scheme. The pairing is by position, not printed in one row. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary, second place (the "B" block, lines 5079 to 5126).* The block prints, in consecutive lines, "Bhatu" (5102), the transliteration "ba spru" (5104) and "Mirabilis himalaica" (5106). The row before it is "Bhalu", "Bhampo" and "Bhashaka" with "Rhododendron sp.", "Heracluem millefolium" and "Justicia Adhatoda" (5099 and 5100), and the row after it is "Bilwa", "bil ba" and "Aegle marmelos" (5108 to 5112). The English names around the place are "NeesMalabar nut tree" and then "Winter cherry Bengal quinie" (lines 5154 and 5155), which run in the same order as *Justicia*, *Mirabilis*, *Aegle*. The transliteration "ba spru" is the stem of Clark's *ba.spru.wa*, one of the seven names Clark gives for *a.sho.gandha* (line 1145); the final syllable differs, and the Codex reads them as the same name from the likeness. **Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Mirabilis himalaica*, "Asho genda", "Bhatu" and *a.sho.gandha* found no entry on this drug. I_1283 (*Fritillaria delavayi*) quotes the same "A" block and I_1236 and I_1237 (*Asparagus adscendens*, *Asparagus filicinus*) quote the "five roots" note (line 1144); none treats this drug. The Withania entries I_017 (tags CM, SS, SBJ, FMT), I_248 (SS, CM, FMT) and I_669 (SS, FMT, CM) carry the FMT tag (I_017 and I_669 also carry the English name "Winter Cherry" in their titles), and cover the plant that Clark's line identifies; none of them cites Clark line 1145, Men-Tsee-Khang line 3502 or the name "Asho genda", and the Tibetan claims in I_017 ("rtsa mkhris root tonics", "lus stobs skyed pa", "srog rlung") are not found in the lines read. I_769 (*Mirabilis jalapa*, Badianus, Shennong) is another species. The glossary's binomial and Clark's are two identifications of one Tibetan drug, and the two entries (this one and the Withania entries) are not merged. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Mirabilis* is a genus of the four-o'clock family (Nyctaginaceae) and that *Withania*, *Physalis* and *Asparagus* belong to the nightshade, the nightshade and the asparagus families respectively; the three are unrelated to *Mirabilis*. Which of these the Tibetan texts mean is not settled by the lines read. Clark's commentary calls the plant "of white and red flowering types", with a "round, sticky, pale green leaf" for the white. No statement about this drug's constituents is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "cures cold disorders of the lower body, lymph disorders" (Clark, line 1145) and "cures cold disorders of the lower body and chhuser disorders" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3502), and that belongs to a group, "the five roots", used in medicinal butter (Men-Tsee-Khang, lines 1116 and 1144). These are the texts' claims in their own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and does not translate "cold disorders of the lower body" or "chhuser" into modern diagnoses. Clark's commentary warns that "the dark flowered type is not suitable for use in medicine", which is a reason for caution and not a guide to identification.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give one preparation context: a medicinal butter, made from "Zati, Gokya, the three fruits, the five roots and Menchhen as the main ingredients" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 1116), where Bhatu is one of the five roots (line 1144). No quantity, frequency or duration is given, no part used is stated for the drug, and the sentences give no recipe. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 1145) prints "hot and sweet tastes" for winter cherry. No power and no post-digestive taste is printed. Men-Tsee-Khang prints no taste or power for "Asho genda" or "Bhatu" in the lines read. The actions printed are curing cold disorders of the lower body and lymph disorders (Clark, root-text layer), wind disorders, first stage oedema and dissolving calculi (Clark, commentary), and curing cold disorders of the lower body and chhuser disorders (Men-Tsee-Khang).

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Tibetan texts give this drug by what it does: it cures cold disorders of the lower body and lymph (or chhuser) disorders. It is also, in Men-Tsee-Khang, one of "the five roots" from which medicinal butter is prepared, and that butter is named among the pacifying medicines for wind (*loong*) disorders (lines 1114 and 1116); that is a therapeutic grouping and not a cultivation status. The sentence that names it (line 3502) says of two of the other roots, Nye shing and Ra nye, that they "prolong life", and says no such thing of Asho genda; Clark says the same of *ra.mnye* in essence-extraction compounds (line 1144), and nothing of the kind of *a.sho.gandha*. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes, and not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23 in both editions); no line read places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name, not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a lower-body cold drug and one of the five roots of a wind-pacifying butter in the Tibetan texts, with no recipe and no dose, and the plant behind the Tibetan name is uncertain inside the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary itself. The longevity sentence in the same passage is said of other roots, not of this one. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print one caution, in Clark's commentary: "The dark flowered type is not suitable for use in medicine" (line 1145). Lower-body cold complaints and lymph disorders need medical assessment; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Because the identity is uncertain, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
