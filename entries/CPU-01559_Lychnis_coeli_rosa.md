# Lychnis coeli-rosa — Luchnis stephanomatike (Dioscorides 3-114); "Rose of Heaven" on the translators' line

## *Lychnis coeli-rosa, the first binomial under the genus line "Lychnis — Campion, Lamp Flower, Maltese Cross" in the translators' SUGGESTED block for chapter 3-114 (glossed "Rose of Heaven"; a second binomial, Lychnis coronaria, "Rose Campion, Mullein Pink", follows)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk names this plant under this name. The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The SUGGESTED block has three parts: a genus line, then *Lychnis coeli-rosa* (with the synonym *Agrostemma coeli-rosa*), then *Lychnis coronaria* (with *Agrostemma coronaria*). The entry takes the first binomial, and the second is an equal suggestion that the entry does not follow. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The heading *3-114. LUCHNIS STEPHANOMATIKE* is at line 7558, the SUGGESTED block at lines 7560 to 7562 and the chapter text at line 7563. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 520. The chapter stands in Book Three, under the running head "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants", after 3-113 *LEUKAS* (line 7543) and before 3-115 *LUCHNIS AGRIA* (line 7564), which is the wild lychnis and has its own entry, I_1124.

The whole chapter reads: "Lychnis has a flower similar to a white violet but almost purple, interwoven into little crowns, the seed of which (taken in a drink with wine) helps those bitten by scorpions. It is also called athanates, aquilonium, vallarium, geranopodium, corymbion, taurion, sceptrum, or maloion; the Egyptians call it seneom, the Magi call it the blood of a menstrual woman, and the Romans call it genicularis, or vallaria."

So the chapter describes only the flower (like a white violet but almost purple, and "interwoven into little crowns"), gives one use (the seed, drunk in wine, for scorpion bites) and lists names. It gives no description of the leaf, stem or root, no habitat and no season. The Greek second word of the title is not translated in the text. The other names are *athanates*, *aquilonium*, *vallarium*, *geranopodium*, *corymbion*, *taurion*, *sceptrum* and *maloion*, with *seneom* given as the Egyptian name and *genicularis* and *vallaria* as the Roman. The translators' index lists *Lychnis*, *Lychnis coeli-rosa* and *Lychnis coronaria* at page 499 (line 16398).

**The Magi name.** The text records that "the Magi call it the blood of a menstrual woman". The translators' glossary (line 3509) says that in Dioscorides' time the name *Magi* was used for those claiming occult powers of Babylonian or Oriental origin. Dioscorides records the name and does not say that he endorses it. Recording is not endorsing, and the Codex draws no conclusion from the name.

**Other places in the same text.** The plant is named as a comparison in two other chapters. In 3-9 *KENTAURION MIKRON* (heading at line 6602) the little centaury has flowers "similar to those of lychnis [3-114, 3-115], a faint Phoenician [reddish] purple" (line 6607). In 3-166 *KANNABIS AGRIA* (line 7931, "Cannabis sylvestris") the reddish flowers are "similar to lychnis [3-114, 3-115]". The name also turns up as a synonym of other plants: *lychnis sylvestris* is among the other names of 3-110 *LEONTOPETALON* (line 7529), and 4-133 *ANTIRRINON* (line 9201) says its purple flowers gave it the name *sylvestris lychnis* and that some "have called it lychnis agrestis". Those are different chapters and different plants, and nothing from them is attributed to this one.

**Overlap.** The chapter 3-114 is not the subject of any existing entry. It is cited in I_901 (Small-flowered Catchfly, *Silene gallica*) as "the nearest Dioscorides text" to the genus, and I_901 expressly does not attribute the chapter to *Silene gallica*. I_901 also says that some botanists place *L. coeli-rosa* and *L. viscaria* in *Silene*, a point I_901 marks as not stated in the Dioscorides text; this entry has not checked it. [USER: verify] The same SUGGESTED name *Lychnis* appears in I_994 (Corn Cockle, *Agrostemma githago*) for a different chapter, 2-122. The wild lychnis of 3-115 is I_1124. None is merged.

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. As a general fact, the campion genus belongs to the pink family (Caryophyllaceae), a family in which many members contain saponins. That is a statement about the family and not a finding about *Lychnis coeli-rosa*. Nothing is stated here about its composition. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a wine drink of the seed for scorpion bite. This is Dioscorides' claim and not a Codex protocol. The Codex gives no protocol. A scorpion sting needs medical assessment, and the identity of the plant is only the translators' suggestion between two species.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation: the seed, "taken in a drink with wine". The text does not say how much seed or how much wine. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identity is uncertain between two species, so a modern study of one campion would not bear directly on the Dioscorides drug.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 3-114 prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. The only qualities printed are of colour and form (a flower "similar to a white violet but almost purple", "interwoven into little crowns").

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The name given by the Magi, "the blood of a menstrual woman", is recorded by the text as a name and not as a doctrine, and it supports no cultivation claim. The one thing the chapter offers toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a single field mark, the crowned, near-purple flower, which the text also uses in other chapters as a colour comparison. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a flower description with one remedy and a list of names, so the honest answer is that there is nothing here to take up.

### Contraindications

Chapter 3-114 prints no warning. Scorpion stings need medical care and must not be left to a wine drink of seed. The identification is only the translators' suggestion between two species, and the chapter gives no amount, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
