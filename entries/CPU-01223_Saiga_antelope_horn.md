# Saiga antelope horn — *rgya*, "Saiga Antelope" (Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch.20)
## *The horn of the animal the Tibetan text calls* rgya*; Clark prints "Saiga Antelope" with the Latin names* Saiga tatarica *and* Nemorhaedus cripus*, and the Men-Tsee-Khang translation renders the corresponding sentence with "serows/gorals". "Saiga antelope" is the spine's name and Clark's, and the identity is not settled by the texts* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is the Tibetan *Four Tantras*. In Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; "Clark" below) the animal medicines close chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra with the sentence "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). Horn is the first of the thirteen. Clark's line 1157 reads: "rgya: Saiga Antelope: Saiga tatarica: Nemorhaedus cripus: [has six names. It has slender, black, very finely pleated horns]." The square brackets mark interpolations from the commentaries, so the description of the horn is a commentarial gloss in Clark's rendering and not the root-text claim. The root-text claim about this horn comes in the next line (1158): "The horns of Ram and Saiga antelope are ecbolic." That is the whole of the therapeutic statement. "Ecbolic" is Clark's word and the passage does not define it; the term is used in pharmacology for a drug that hastens labour or expels the foetus. [USER: verify]

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`; "MTK" below) has the parallel sentence at line 3508: "The horns of serows/gorals and rams are ecbolic." The two translations agree on the action and on the ram, and differ on the other animal. Clark's *Saiga antelope* corresponds, by position and by action, to MTK's *serows/gorals*. Clark's own Latin name for it is double: *Saiga tatarica* names the steppe saiga, and *Nemorhaedus cripus* is a name belonging to the serow-goral group. Clark's dietary chapter confuses the matter further: at line 672 he writes "saiga tatarica (Pantholops hodgsoni)" among game animals in group (c) and "saiga antelope (Nemorhaedus cripus)" among the larger hill and game animals in group (d). *Pantholops hodgsoni* is the Tibetan antelope, which Clark elsewhere prints as "Hodgson's Antelope" under the Tibetan name *gtsod* (line 1155). The identity of *rgya* is therefore doubtful: the texts support a horned Himalayan or steppe ruminant of a kind they do not pin down, and the spine's "Saiga antelope horn" follows Clark's English only. ### Active Components

Horn of the bovid type is mainly keratin, a structural protein, built on a bony core that carries calcium phosphate. These are general facts about horn. The Tibetan texts name no constituent and no mechanism for the claimed action, and this entry supplies none.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The claim is a single one: the horn is ecbolic, in the company of ram's horn, in a list of horn medicines. The material is also bound up with conservation. The saiga antelope is a species of conservation concern, and the Tibetan antelope named just above it in the same list is protected. The Codex's application layer has nothing to add, and no protocol is offered.

Neither text gives a preparation (powdered, burnt, in a compound), a quantity or a dose for this horn. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi.** No taste, potency or post-digestive taste is printed for this horn in the passages read (Clark lines 1157-1158; MTK line 3508). The only quality attached to it is the action "ecbolic". Other horns in the same passage carry stated qualities (for example takin horn "increases [body] heat", line 1158), but these are not extended to this one.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The frame is the thirteen-part animal section of the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica (Clark line 1148), and in it the horn is a drug for a stated action, ecbolic (line 1158). Nothing in the passages read places it in any practice of essence extraction (*bcud len*), and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, so none is asserted here. The tradition's wider frame is that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching the root; this entry adds nothing to that.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports cultivation use. The horn appears in the sources as one line in a list of horn medicines, with a single action that the translators do not define, and the animal's identity is not secure. A cultivator has nothing to take from this entry; the honest practice is abstention, which also spares a species of concern.

### Contraindications

The action claimed is ecbolic, which on its own is a reason to avoid it in pregnancy. Horn is an animal product of uncertain source: trade in saiga and in Tibetan antelope products is restricted or prohibited in many countries, and the animal in the text is not securely identified, so any product sold under this name cannot be taken to be what the texts mean. Hygiene (unprocessed animal material) and ethics (a threatened species) are further reasons for abstention.
