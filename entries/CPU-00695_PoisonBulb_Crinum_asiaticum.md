# Poison Bulb / Wen Zhu Lan (文珠兰) / Sudarshan

## *Crinum asiaticum* L. | Family: Amaryllidaceae

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ, SS

**Energetic Classification:** Cooling, dispersing, moving — applied warm to the surface; violently downward-and-outward if taken within. A drawing agent, not a nourishing one.

**Training Phase:** Repair — external adjunct to joint and soft-tissue recovery. Never an ingested tonic at any phase.

---

### Origin & Tradition

The great crinum lily is one of the most widely planted and most widely misunderstood plants in the tropics. It grows from a huge bulb — often the size of a human head — throwing a rosette of strap-shaped leaves a metre long and umbels of white, spidery, night-scented flowers. It is salt-tolerant and lines beaches from India through Southeast Asia to southern China, Japan, the Pacific and, by introduction, much of the warm world. It is planted in temple gardens, hospital forecourts and municipal roundabouts as an ornamental. It is also, unambiguously, poisonous.

The traditional record is remarkably consistent on this point, and consistent in its response: the plant is used **on** the body, not **in** it. Across the whole Asian range, the dominant preparation is the same — a large leaf, softened over a flame or in hot ash, oiled, and bound over a sprain, a bruise, a swollen joint or an inflamed tendon. The doctrine is drawing and dispersing: the warm leaf pulls stagnation out through the skin.

**Ayurveda / Indian tradition — *Sudarshan*, *Sudarsana*, *Nagadamani*, *Vishamungil*.** The Sanskrit *Sudarshan* names the discus of Viṣṇu — a wheel-weapon that cuts and returns. The name is applied to several plants; for *Crinum asiaticum* it fits the leaf's blade-like form and the doctrine of a substance that cuts through obstruction. Classical Indian indication is external: the leaf warmed with oil (commonly castor or sesame) and bandaged over *śotha* (swelling), *sandhi-śūla* (joint pain), sprains, boils and whitlows; the juice has also been used in the ear for earache and the bulb as a deliberate emetic in the hands of a practitioner. The doctrinal frame is *vāta-kapha-hara* applied locally — heat and oil to carry the drug inward through the skin, the drug itself to break the stagnant mass. Note that emetic use is a *cikitsā* (therapeutic) application of a poison by a trained hand — the same act performed by a layperson is a poisoning. [USER: verify specific classical citation before printing — the *Sudarshan* attribution to *C. asiaticum* is well-attested in modern Indian materia medica but the name is shared with *Crinum latifolium* and with the *Sudarshana churna* formulation, which is unrelated.]

**Traditional Chinese Medicine — *Wen Zhu Lan* (文珠兰), also *Luo Qun Dai* (罗裙带).** Recorded in the southern Chinese folk and coastal materia medica rather than the classical northern canon — this is a Lingnan and Hainan plant. Indication: 活血散瘀, 消肿止痛 — invigorate blood, disperse stasis, reduce swelling, stop pain. Applied as a pounded fresh-leaf poultice or the warmed whole leaf over 跌打损伤 (traumatic injury), 痈疖 (boils/abscesses) and joint swelling. The second name, "trailing-skirt", is purely morphological — the leaves fall like a hanging robe. TCM sources that mention internal use flag it as 有毒 (toxic) and confine it to emesis; contemporary Chinese pharmacopoeial practice treats it as external-use.

**Thai traditional medicine — *Phlap phlueng* / *Wan Sawan*.** Thai practice is the best-documented and the closest to a testable protocol: the leaf is **grilled or warmed before use**, then applied to inflammatory symptoms and osteoarthritic joints. The pretreatment is not incidental — it is a specified step, and it is the one point where the tradition makes a chemically falsifiable claim (see Scientific Correlation).

**Southeast Asian and Pacific folk use.** Malay, Indonesian, Filipino and Pacific island practice repeats the pattern: heated leaf as a poultice for sprains, swellings, headache (bound to the forehead) and rheumatic pain; bulb juice as an emetic; the plant recognised everywhere as a poison. Coastal populations also used the bulb as a fish poison in some places [USER: verify].

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.**

The doctrine here is unusually honest, and it maps onto the chemistry almost too neatly.

Every tradition in the range says the same two things at once: *this disperses swelling* and *this will make you vomit and can kill you*. That is not a contradiction the traditions failed to notice — it is the axis the doctrine is built on. Amaryllidaceae isoquinoline alkaloids, lycorine chief among them, are cytotoxic and are potent emetics; lycorine is a central nervous system and gastrointestinal irritant. The plant's route of administration is therefore not a stylistic choice but a dose-limiting one. The skin is a rate-limiter. The traditions independently arrived at the only administration route that lets you use a cytotoxic alkaloid pool on an inflamed joint without emptying the patient's stomach — and they encoded that limit as a doctrine of *drawing outward* rather than as a warning label.

The *Sudarshan* symbolism — a weapon that cuts and returns to the hand — is a fair description of a poison used topically. It goes out, it does its work, it does not stay in the body.

The heating step is where symbol and chemistry meet most tightly. Doctrinally, warmth "opens the channels" and carries the drug in. Chemically, heating a leaf before extraction alters what comes out of it — and this is the rare case where the traditional processing instruction has been put to a test rather than assumed (below).

Where we should **deflate**: the "cooling" attribution some sources give the plant is doctrinal inference from its use on hot, swollen joints, not a property of the plant. There is no coherent cross-tradition energetic consensus here, and the entry should not manufacture one. Likewise the modern gloss that *Crinum asiaticum* is an "immune tonic" or an "anticancer herb" — that is back-formed from in-vitro cytotoxicity data on isolated alkaloids and has no traditional or clinical basis whatever.

### Active Components

**Amaryllidaceae isoquinoline alkaloids** are the defining chemistry and the toxicity. A phytochemical study of *C. asiaticum* var. *japonicum* rhizome and fruits isolated thirteen alkaloids, including **lycorine**, 2-*O*-acetyllycorine, 1,2-*O*-diacetyllycorine, **crinamine**, (−)-crinine, **ungeremine**, 11-hydroxyvittatine, hamayne, (+)-epibuphanisine, yemenine A, epinorgalanthamine and two new compounds (crijaponine A, a crinine-type; crijaponine B, a galanthamine-type) (*J Nat Med*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11418-019-01304-9)).

Structurally these fall into families that matter clinically:
- **Lycorine-type** — the emetic/GI-irritant and cytotoxic core.
- **Crinine-type** (crinine, crinamine, buphanidrine relatives) — cytotoxic.
- **Galanthamine-type** (norgalanthamine, epinorgalanthamine) — **acetylcholinesterase inhibitors**. This is the class that makes ingestion dangerous in a second, independent way.

Regenerated *C. asiaticum* tissue-culture plants confirm galanthamine, lycorine and tazettine as the commercially interesting phytocompounds of the species (*BMC Plant Biol*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-025-07327-7)).

**Non-alkaloid fraction.** Three new flavan-3-ols and two known flavans were isolated from a chloroform extract of *C. asiaticum* (*Planta Med*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1055/a-1585-5877)). A high-molecular-weight polysaccharide (CAL-, ~730 kDa; rhamnose/sorbose/galactose/glucose) has also been characterised (*Saudi J Biol Sci*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sjbs.2019.09.017)).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**External only.** Within a Kenetica repair protocol, *C. asiaticum* has one legitimate slot: a warm-leaf topical over a localised soft-tissue or joint inflammation — a rolled ankle, a bruised quad, a hot arthritic knee — where the goal is local dispersal and analgesia and the herb sits alongside, not instead of, load management.

It is not a systemic anti-inflammatory. It is not a recovery tea. It has no place in any ingested stack, at any phase, at any dose, prepared by anyone reading this book. The emetic dose and the toxic dose are not separated by a comfortable margin, the alkaloid content varies enormously with organ, variety and season, and the plant carries an AChE-inhibitor class on top of the cytotoxic one.

The honest use case is narrow: it is a plant a cultivator in the tropics can grow, cut, warm and bind — a topical that works on the surface, on a defined joint, for a defined injury.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Preparation | Part | Method | Application | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Warmed leaf poultice** (primary) | Fresh mature leaf | Wilt/grill briefly over flame or hot ash until pliable; smear with warm sesame, coconut or castor oil | Bind over the joint or swelling, 1–2 leaves, 20–60 min, 1–2× daily | The pretreatment step is traditionally specified, not optional. **External only.** |
| **Pounded fresh-leaf poultice** (TCM 跌打) | Fresh leaf | Pound to a mash, optionally with a little rice wine | Apply over trauma/contusion under a cloth | Higher irritation risk than the wilted leaf; patch-test |
| **Leaf-infused oil** | Fresh leaf | Warm-infuse chopped leaf in a fixed oil, strain | Massage medium over sore joints | Shelf-stable form of the above; label it poison |
| **Bulb — emetic** | Bulb | — | — | **DO NOT.** Recorded traditionally as a practitioner-administered emetic. Reproduced by a layperson this is a poisoning, not a preparation. Listed here for completeness only. |
| **Any internal preparation** | Any | — | — | **Contraindicated absolutely.** |

**Dosing.** There is no established human dose for this plant in any form, and no clinical trial. Topical application is not standardised. Treat all figures above as traditional practice, not as posology. [USER: verify] any dose figure before it appears in print.

**Cultivator's notes.** Grows readily from offsets and from the massive bulb; salt- and drought-tolerant; full sun to part shade; needs space (a mature clump is 1.5 m across). It is not fussy and it is not rare. A validated **direct somatic embryogenesis** protocol now exists from bulb-scale explants on BAP/NAA media, with genetic-fidelity assessment and GC-MS comparison of regenerants against donor plants — relevant if you want clonally uniform alkaloid content rather than the wide field variation (*BMC Plant Biol*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-025-07327-7)). Watch for *Lasiodiplodia*-type leaf blight in humid climates, documented on spider lilies in Malaysia (*Mycobiology*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/12298093.2019.1682448)). **Do not plant it where children or livestock forage.** Label it. The bulb looks like an onion.

### Scientific Correlation

**Grade: C+ — mechanistically plausible for the topical anti-inflammatory doctrine, in-vitro/animal only, zero clinical evidence, and the toxicity is better established than the benefit.**

**Anti-inflammatory (leaf) — the doctrine's best support, and it is preclinical.** A Thai study explicitly tested the traditional pretreatment step — the one doctrinal claim that is falsifiable — comparing hot-air oven drying, microwave drying and traditional grilling of *C. asiaticum* leaf prior to ethanolic extraction, then assaying anti-inflammatory activity and chemical properties. This is a genuine instance of a processing doctrine being examined rather than assumed (*Evid Based Complement Alternat Med*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1155/2021/8850744)). It is a laboratory assay, not a patient.

**Mechanism — NF-κB.** The isolated flavan-3-ols and flavans inhibited LPS-induced nitric oxide production, and inhibited LPS-induced IL-6 production and NF-κB p65 phosphorylation in RAW264.7 macrophages at ~10 µM (*Planta Med*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1055/a-1585-5877)). This is a coherent molecular route from leaf constituents to reduced local inflammatory signalling — the closest thing the poultice doctrine has to a mechanism. **Grade B for mechanism, F for clinical translation:** RAW264.7 cells are not a knee, and there is no evidence the compounds reach a joint through intact skin at those concentrations.

**Anti-fibrotic (bulb).** *C. asiaticum* bulb extract with betulin attenuated bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis in mice, downregulating IL-6, TNF-α, IL-13, IL-1β and TGF-β1 (*Heliyon*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2023.e16914)). Note this is a **combination** with betulin and a systemic mouse model — it does not support the topical doctrine and it does not license bulb ingestion.

**Cholinesterase inhibition.** Alkaloids from *C. asiaticum* var. *japonicum* were assayed for AChE inhibition, with a new dimeric compound, bis-(−)-8-demethylmaritidine, the most potent, supported by docking and MD simulation (*J Asian Nat Prod Res*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/10286020.2023.2269541)). This is **not** a therapeutic finding for this entry — it is a toxicological one. It means the plant carries a functional AChE-inhibitor load.

**Norgalanthamine — hepatoprotective, and a caution about congener data.** Norgalanthamine (1 and 10 mg/kg oral, 7–14 days) reversed CCl₄-induced rises in ALT, AST and bilirubin in mice (*Drug Chem Toxicol*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/01480545.2021.2015241)). Norgalanthamine is a *C. asiaticum* var. constituent, but this study is about an **isolated compound**, not the plant, and does not make the plant liver-safe.

**Cytotoxicity — real, and irrelevant to use.** Crinamine induced apoptosis in cervical cancer SiHa cells and inhibited spheroid growth, migration and angiogenesis (*Biomolecules*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/biom9090494)). The polysaccharide fraction showed antitumour activity in MTT assay (*Saudi J Biol Sci*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sjbs.2019.09.017)). **These findings support nothing in the traditional record and must not be used to sell the plant as an anticancer herb.** They are, if anything, further evidence that the alkaloid pool is cytotoxic.

**NULLS and absences — stated plainly:**
- **No human clinical trial of *Crinum asiaticum* in any indication, topical or otherwise.** Not one.
- **No pharmacokinetic data** on transdermal absorption of any *C. asiaticum* alkaloid. The entire safety case for topical use rests on the skin being a barrier — which is assumed, not measured.
- **No dose-ranging study.** No established therapeutic index.
- **No controlled comparison** of the warmed leaf against any standard topical anti-inflammatory.
- Much of the recent literature on this species is **not about medicine at all** — it is green-synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles (*Luminescence*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/bio.70158)), silver nanoparticles for forensic blood detection (*Forensic Sci Int*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.forsciint.2024.112257)), plant virology (*Virus Genes*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11262-022-01901-z)), and extraction optimisation (*Saudi J Biol Sci*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sjbs.2019.09.018)). The species is more studied as a nanoparticle reducing agent than as a therapeutic. That is a fair measure of how thin the clinical ground is.

**Toxicology — congener data, named as such.** There is no published human poisoning series for *C. asiaticum* retrieved in this search. The relevant clinical picture comes from **other Amaryllidaceae** and must be read as family-level, not species-specific: an 83-year-old woman who ingested *Lycoris radiata* (which contains lycorine and galanthamine) developed a **delayed-onset cholinergic crisis** — miosis, hypersalivation, airway secretions, vomiting, diarrhoea, myoclonus, hemodynamic and respiratory collapse requiring vasopressors and mechanical ventilation, with markedly low serum butyrylcholinesterase; symptoms began roughly **eight hours** after admission and were prolonged (*Am J Emerg Med*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajem.2025.01.074)). *Crinum zeylanicum*, a congener explicitly recorded in folk use "as a poison", yields crinine, lycorine, ambelline and buphanidrine derivatives with antiproliferative activity (*Phytother Res*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.3468)). **Neither is *C. asiaticum*.** But *C. asiaticum* contains both lycorine and galanthamine-type alkaloids, so the *Lycoris* toxidrome is the mechanistically expected one — and the delayed onset is the detail that kills people, because early reassurance is false reassurance.

### Contraindications

**⚠️ TOXIC PLANT. EXTERNAL USE ONLY. THE BULB IS GENUINELY POISONOUS.**

- **Never ingest any part.** The bulb is a potent emetic and the plant carries cytotoxic lycorine-type alkaloids plus galanthamine-type acetylcholinesterase inhibitors. Amaryllidaceae poisonings are recorded.
- **Onset can be delayed.** In the *Lycoris radiata* case above (congener, same alkaloid classes), the cholinergic crisis emerged ~8 h after presentation and was prolonged. **An asymptomatic patient in the first hours after ingestion is not a safe patient.** Any suspected ingestion → emergency medical care, tell them it is an Amaryllidaceae/lycorine-galanthamine exposure, expect a cholinergic toxidrome, expect it late.
- **Do not confuse the bulb with an onion or a leek.** This is the classic Amaryllidaceae kitchen error and it is how people are poisoned. It grows in gardens, in car parks, on beaches. Children are at risk. Livestock and dogs are at risk.
- **Skin.** Patch-test before any poultice. The plant is irritant. Discontinue on erythema, burning or blistering. **Never apply to broken skin, open wounds, burns or abraded tissue** — you are relying on an intact epidermis as your only dosing control, and a wound removes it.
- **Sap and juice — keep away from eyes.** Wash hands after handling. Wear gloves when cutting the bulb.
- **Pregnancy and lactation: avoid entirely, including topically.** No data. Cytotoxic alkaloids. There is no version of this risk calculus that comes out favourable.
- **Children: no topical use.** Higher surface-area-to-mass ratio, thinner skin, and no data.
- **Anyone on cholinesterase-inhibiting drugs** (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine, pyridostigmine) or with organophosphate exposure: the plant contains AChE inhibitors. Additive risk is theoretical for topical use but the class overlap is real. Avoid.
- **Hepatic or renal impairment:** avoid. The norgalanthamine hepatoprotection data is single-compound and does not extend to the plant.
- **Do not extract, tincture or concentrate this plant.** Concentration removes the only safety margin the tradition built in.
- **Regulatory:** treat as a poisonous ornamental. Not a food. Not a supplement ingredient. [USER: verify] local schedule status before any commercial handling.
