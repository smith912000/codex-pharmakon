# Flint — as listed in Ghalioungui's glossary of the Ebers Papyrus; Bryan's "Knife-stone" is the nearest wording in the text on disk
## *A hard stone of the Egyptian pharmacopoeia, entered as "Flint" in the glossary of medicaments of the 1987 Ghalioungui translation; the Bryan text on disk prints the word "Knife-stone", and the link between the two is not established in the sources retrieved* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The only locus given for this entry is a list row: `_sources/ebers.csv` line 115 reads "Flint, mineral, Ebers Papyrus; Ghalioungui 1987 trans., Glossary I (Medicaments)", with the identification marked uncertain. That translation, with its glossary, is not on disk, so the row itself could not be opened and nothing is known here of the prescription, column or complaint in which Ghalioungui's "flint" occurs. The row is the only source for the statement that the Ebers Papyrus has flint among its medicaments.

The Bryan (1930) text that is on disk (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`) has no word "flint" (searching the file for "flint", "silex" and "chert" finds nothing). Bryan's alphabetical list of mineral remedies (Chapter V, lines 1206-1208) has, instead, a group of stones headed by "Stone-from-the-Shore" and ending in "Knife-stone", "Black Knife-stone" and "Trash-from-the-Knife-Stone" (line 1208). Bryan does not say that Knife-stone is flint. A stone that is called a knife-stone and is used as a mineral drug may be the same stone that Ghalioungui's glossary renders "flint", but the two texts are not shown to agree, and this entry does not claim they do. [USER: verify] Everything in the next paragraphs is Bryan's wording for "Knife-stone", read in the hope that it bears on the entry, and is given as such.

*Where Bryan's Knife-stone is used.* In the passages read, the stone is one ingredient in remedies named for a complaint:

- **For the *uases* abscess.** Bryan's survey of the animal remedies says: "Man's Milk (reinforced it is true by Tortoise-shell, Granite, and Trash-from-the- Knife-Stone) cures the uases-abscess" (lines 1362-1363; the word is broken across two lines in the text).
- **For pains in the bones.** For "Pains in the Bones in Every Limb according to the Truth", the scribe is said to have recommended "a poultice of Black Knife-stone with Fat, Honey, and Saltpetre as adjuvants" (line 2119). Bryan adds that "according to the truth as we know it the use of Black Knife-stone, with or without Fat, Honey, and Saltpetre, in this connection is entirely fallacious, and not to be commended" (lines 2119-2120). That is Bryan's judgment in 1930, not the papyrus.
- **For "fat in the eyes".** The remedy printed as "ANOTHER TO DRIVE OUT FAT-IN-THE-EYES" reads: "Knife-stone. Mix in Fresh Milk and apply thereto very often." Bryan comments that "to-day it is the Knife instead of a Knife-stone that we apply to the eye, while the patient is instructed to drink the Fresh Milk" (lines 2776-2778).
- **For the growth in the eye called pterygium.** "Another poultice is composed of Black Knife-stone and Crocodile-earth, mixed up with Honey and flavoured with Incense" (line 2844).

None of these says where the stone came from, how it was prepared, what quantity was used, or whether a formula was spoken. They are Bryan's English renderings, and the Egyptian word behind "Knife-stone" is not given in the text on disk. The entries for "Stone-from-the-Shore", "Stone-from-the-Meeting-of-the-Waters", "Stone-from-the-Parting-of-the-Waters" and "Stone-from-Memphis" appear in the same list; the text read does not say whether they are the same stone or other stones.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, flint is a hard, fine-grained form of silica (silicon dioxide), a chert that occurs as nodules in chalk and limestone and breaks with a sharp, shell-like edge; for that reason it was used for tools in the Stone Age. Silica is chemically inert and insoluble in the body. Whether the stone behind the Egyptian entry was flint, or only a hard knife-stone, is not stated in the sources read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved say only that a stone called flint (Ghalioungui) or knife-stone (Bryan) is a medicament of the Ebers Papyrus and that Bryan reports it in poultices and in a milk mixture for the eye. The Codex does not recommend any use of a stone on the eye or a wound. Powdered silica is a lung hazard and a hard sharp particle in the eye causes injury, and no part of the sources supports a modern use.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources retrieved give the following preparations for Bryan's Knife-stone: a poultice with fat, honey and saltpetre (line 2119); the stone mixed in fresh milk and applied "very often" (line 2778); and a poultice with crocodile-earth, honey and incense (line 2844). No quantity, weight, grade of the stone, number of applications or duration is given, and no method of reducing the stone to a powder. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Flint is a form of microcrystalline quartz (silica). Inhaled silica dust causes silicosis, and fine silica particles are an eye and skin irritant. Silica is not absorbed when swallowed. There is no evidence that flint or any stone applied to the eye treats an eye disease. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Eyes:** do not put stone, flint or silica dust into the eye; a hard particle abrades the cornea and can blind. Eye complaints, including pterygium, need an eye clinician.
- **Dust:** flint dust is silica; do not grind or breathe it.
- **Wounds and poultices:** a stone poultice is not clean; it risks infection on broken skin.
- **Pregnancy and children:** unsuitable.
- **Identification:** the link between "Flint" (Ghalioungui) and "Knife-stone" (Bryan) is not established in the sources retrieved; Bryan's remedies are his English renderings, and his own judgment that one of them was "entirely fallacious" is his.
- **Cross-tradition note, not an Ebers statement:** Dioscorides names flint in the *De Materia Medica*: boiled milk "becomes astringent to the intestines, especially that which is evaporated by burning flints" (milk section, 2-75, line 5240), and "hot burning flints" placed into the milk of sheep, cows or goats to boil it stop discharges accompanied with ulceration (line 5250, which the page break places after 2-77, so its chapter is uncertain), and quicklime "is also made from flints or pebble stones that are burnt" (5-133, ASBESTOS, line 10528; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). Those lines are candidate loci for tagging DMM; they have not been added to the tag line.
