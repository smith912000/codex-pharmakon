# Anthriscus sylvestris — Skandux (Dioscorides 2-168)

## *Anthriscus sylvestris (the translators' suggestion; "Wild Chervil, Cow Parsley, Cow Weed")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification, and this row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) without being verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The SUGGESTED line also prints "Scandix [Pliny]", so the translators equate the Greek skandux with Pliny's scandix as well as with the binomial.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading at line 5992.

*2-168. SKANDUX.* "Scandix is a wild vegetable — somewhat sharp and bitter, edible whether eaten raw or boiled, good for the intestines and the stomach. A decoction (taken as a drink) is good for the bladder, kidneys, and liver. The Romans call it herba scanaria, and some, acicula." The chapter gives no description of the plant itself, only taste, use and two names. It sits among the vegetables (heading "Lachana: Vegetables") and just after 2-167 Gingidium, a different chervil-like vegetable (line 5989 gives *Anthriscus cerefolium*, "Chervil"). A plate caption at line 6121 reads "Anthriscus sylvestris after THIEBAULT — 1881" (p.309).

*Second chapter, 4-116 MURRIS (line 9073).* The translators' SUGGESTED line for this chapter lists "Scandix [Pliny], Myrrhis, Cicutaria [Fuchs] ... Anthriscus sylvestris [in Sprague], Chaerophyllum sylvestre [Linnaeus] — Wild Chervil, Cow Parsley, Cow Weed", with "[other usage] Myrrhis odorata ... Sweet Cicely" and a pointer "see 2-168". The text of 4-116 describes a plant "similar to hemlock in its stalk and leaves" with a long, tender, round, sweet-smelling root, drunk in wine against harvest-spider bites, to purge menses and afterbirth, sipped for consumption, and said by some to prevent infection in pestilential seasons. The translators do not make the two chapters one plant with certainty (4-116 offers sweet cicely as another usage), so 4-116 is reported here as the translators' cross-reference and is not attributed to this entry. ### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. *Anthriscus* belongs to the carrot family (Apiaceae), a family in which edible herbs and deadly poisonous ones, such as hemlock, can resemble each other. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a bitter-sharp wild vegetable, eaten raw or boiled for the stomach and intestines, and a drink for bladder, kidneys and liver. These are the text's claims. The Codex does not recommend foraging it: members of its family are easily confused with fatal look-alikes.

### Preparation & Dosing

2-168 gives eating the plant raw or boiled, and "a decoction (taken as a drink)". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant by its parts, where it grows, what it is called, and what is claimed for it. 2-168 is a short vegetable note: it says the plant is wild, sharp and bitter, and eaten raw or boiled, and gives two Roman names. It assigns no status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. As a food plant it asks for good identification before any use, which is Dioscorides' own discipline of attention.

### Contraindications

Wild carrot-family plants are easily confused with poison hemlock and other deadly relatives, and handling the sap of wild umbellifers can irritate the skin. Do not forage from this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
