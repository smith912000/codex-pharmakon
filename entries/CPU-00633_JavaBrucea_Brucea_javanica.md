# Java Brucea — Ya Dan Zi (鸦胆子)

## *Brucea javanica* (L.) Merr. | Family: Simaroubaceae

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ, SS

**Energetic Classification:** Cold / Extremely Bitter — draining, purging heat and toxin; **toxic (有小毒 / 有毒)** by the tradition's own classification

**Training Phase:** Not a training substance. Filed as a therapeutic and a chemical lead only.

---

### Origin & Tradition

⚠️ **Read the Contraindications before anything else in this entry.** *Brucea javanica* is one of the few plants in this codex that has killed people at doses a careless reader could reach. It is documented here because it is pharmacologically important, not because it is usable.

The name says it. **鸦胆子 — Ya Dan Zi, "crow's gallbladder seed."** The seed is small, dark, ovoid, and it is *bitter the way a gallbladder is bitter* — which is to say, past the point where bitterness is a flavour and into the point where it is an event. The Chinese naming convention here is doing exactly what it appears to be doing: naming the drug after the most bitter thing a person handling a bird would ever have tasted. The tradition did not think this was a subtle plant, and it did not name it subtly.

**Chinese Medicine (SBJ) — 鸦胆子 Ya Dan Zi**

The classical indications split cleanly into two, and both are still live.

*Internal — 清热解毒, 止痢, 截疟 (clear heat, resolve toxin, stop dysentery, interrupt malaria).* Ya Dan Zi is the classical drug for **amoebic dysentery** and for **malaria**, particularly the intermittent fevers the tradition classed as 疟疾. The delivery method is itself part of the tradition and is diagnostic of the drug's character: the seeds are **swallowed whole inside a longan-flesh wrapper or a gelatin capsule**, expressly so that the seed does not touch the mouth, throat, or stomach lining directly. A materia medica that specifies a wrapper is telling you the drug burns. Ten to fifteen seeds, wrapped, is the classical figure.

*Topical — for 赘疣, 鸡眼 (warts, corns, calluses).* The crushed seed or its oil is applied directly to the lesion, and *only* to the lesion. The traditional technique involves protecting the surrounding skin — plaster with a hole cut in it, or petroleum jelly ringed around the target. Again, the method encodes the hazard: this is a caustic, and the tradition treated it as one.

**Southeast Asian Systems (SS)**

*B. javanica* is native across Southeast Asia, southern China, and northern Australia, and the antiparasitic and antidysenteric use is broadly distributed across the region rather than being a Chinese peculiarity — the plant is bitter everywhere it grows and was recognised as an amoebicide and febrifuge across its range. **Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

The doctrine here is the **bitter–antiparasitic axis**, and of all the doctrinal mappings in this volume, it is the one that holds up best.

The tradition's reasoning is: *extreme bitterness is extreme cold, extreme cold drains extreme heat, and the "heat toxin" of dysentery and malarial fever is what needs draining.* Bitterness is not incidental to the action — in the classical framework it *is* the action, expressed on the tongue.

Map this onto the chemistry and it survives, with a specific and satisfying correction.

The bitter principles are **quassinoids** — the compound class named for *Quassia amara*, another Simaroubaceae, and the class that gives the whole family its identity as the bitterest plants in the botanical world. Quassinoids are degraded triterpenoids, and they are among the most intensely bitter substances known. **The molecules that make the seed taste like a gallbladder are the same molecules that kill the amoeba.** The tradition's sensory heuristic — *find the bitterest thing, use it on the parasite* — was not superstition. It was a working assay. Bitterness was a **detection method for quassinoid content**, run on the only instrument available.

This is a rare case where a folk doctrine and a molecular mechanism are not merely compatible but *causally identical*. The correction the chemistry supplies is only this: bitterness is not a therapeutic principle, it is a **marker** for one. The tradition treated the taste as the mechanism. It was the label on the bottle.

Where the doctrine **deflates hard**:

*"Resolve toxin" (解毒) is not a mechanism, and here it is close to an inversion.* The quassinoids are potent general cytotoxins. They do not distinguish the parasite from the host by any principle the tradition understood; they are simply more lethal to the smaller, faster-dividing organism first. The drug does not "resolve toxin" — **the drug is the toxin**, aimed. Every death from Ya Dan Zi overdose is a case of the doctrine's own framing failing: the patient took more of the "toxin-resolver" and was poisoned by it.

*The cold/heat framing gives no dosing guidance whatsoever.* The tradition's own answer to the drug's danger was mechanical, not doctrinal — wrap the seed, ring the wart, count to fifteen. The energetic system did not generate the safety margin. Empirical caution did.

### Active Components

- **Quassinoids** — the defining class. **Bruceine** series (bruceine A, B, C, D, E and relatives) and **brusatol**, plus bruceosides and bruceantin. Degraded triterpenoids, intensely bitter, potently cytotoxic. These carry essentially the whole pharmacology.
- **Brusatol** — the single most consequential compound in the plant, and the reason *B. javanica* has a substantial modern research literature. Brusatol is the reference **NRF2 (nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2) inhibitor** used across cancer biology ([DOI: 10.1002/ardp.70191](https://doi.org/10.1002/ardp.70191), PubMed). Its use is so standard that it appears as a *tool compound* in fields unrelated to oncology — e.g. as the Nrf2 inhibitor (at 300 nM) used to dissect the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway in gasoline-engine-exhaust-induced lung epithelial inflammation ([DOI: 10.1080/08958378.2026.2659193](https://doi.org/10.1080/08958378.2026.2659193), PubMed). When a plant compound becomes a lab reagent, its activity is no longer in question.
- **Fixed oil** — the seed is oil-rich, and the oil is the basis of the modern pharmaceutical preparation (see below). Predominantly oleic acid. ⚠️ [USER: verify fatty-acid composition figures — the codex did not retrieve a compositional analysis this run and will not state percentages it cannot source.]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**None. This plant has no Kenetic use case and no training application.**

That statement is the entry's most important content and it is not a formality. *Brucea javanica* is not psychoactive, not oneirogenic, not a tonic, not a clearing herb in any sense this codex would recognise, and not adaptable to self-directed use. It is a cytotoxin with a narrow therapeutic window, an established record of fatal poisoning, and a legitimate modern life exclusively inside hospital oncology and pharmaceutical chemistry.

It is documented in Volume I for three reasons:

1. **It is the cleanest doctrinal case in the book.** The bitter→quassinoid→antiparasitic chain is the one place where a folk sensory heuristic and a molecular mechanism turn out to be the same fact. Any reader who wants to know what a *correct* traditional doctrine looks like should read this entry and then re-read the ones where the doctrine failed.
2. **The modern identity is a real drug.** Brucea javanica oil emulsion injection is not a supplement or a wellness product — it is a manufactured injectable in routine Chinese hospital oncology, with a randomised-trial literature and meta-analyses. That is an unusual endpoint for a folk seed and worth documenting precisely.
3. **It is the counterexample to the "natural is gentle" reading** that this codex exists partly to destroy. A plant growing wild in Southeast Asia produces a molecule that pharmaceutical researchers use as their standard NRF2 inhibitor because nothing works better. Nature is not a mild pharmacy.

**Do not use this plant.** If you have amoebic dysentery or malaria, both have effective, safe, well-dosed modern treatments and you should take those instead. The classical indications are indications for going to a doctor.

### Preparation & Dosing

**Cultivator's note.** *B. javanica* is a shrub or small tree to roughly 3 m, dioecious, tropical and subtropical, frost-intolerant, and grows readily on disturbed ground and forest margins across its native range. It fruits as small drupes ripening black-purple; the seed is the drug. It is an easy plant to grow and a dangerous one to have. ⚠️ **If cultivated at all, treat it as you would treat *Ricinus* or *Nerium*: label it, site it away from children and animals, and never let ripe fruit fall where it will be picked up.** The seeds are small, dark, and look like something a child might eat. The bitterness is protective — a child will spit one out — but do not rely on it.

### Scientific Correlation

Graded honestly. This is, unusually, one of the **best-evidenced** entries in the volume — and the evidence supports an indication the tradition never named.

**Grade: A− (multiple RCTs, meta-analysed, consistent) — BJOEI as chemotherapy adjuvant.** A systematic review and meta-analysis of **18 RCTs, 1,210 patients** with gastric cancer found that BJOEI combined with conventional therapy versus conventional therapy alone significantly improved overall response rate (RR = 1.52, 95% CI 1.36–1.69, p < 0.00001), clinical benefit rate (RR = 1.17, 95% CI 1.11–1.23, p < 0.00001) and performance status (RR = 1.72, 95% CI 1.46–2.01, p < 0.00001), and reduced neutropenia, leukopenia, nausea and vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage, hand-foot syndrome and peripheral sensory neurotoxicity; effects held in subgroups combining with FOLFOX4 and XELOX ([DOI: 10.3389/fnut.2021.784164](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2021.784164), PubMed). An earlier meta-analysis of 7 RCTs in advanced gastric carcinoma found ORR increased 43% (RR = 1.43, p < 0.001) with reduced neutropenia (RR = 0.56), thrombocytopenia (RR = 0.64) and nausea/vomiting (RR = 0.66) — but notably found **quality of life was *not* significantly improved (RR = 1.36, p = 0.07)**, a null worth recording ([DOI: 10.4268/cjcmm20160226](https://doi.org/10.4268/cjcmm20160226), PubMed).

In colorectal cancer, a network meta-analysis of **50 RCTs across 8 TCM injections** found BJOEI + chemotherapy **ranked highest of all eight** for disease control rate (OR 4.1, 95% CI 1.74–10.43) and highest for reducing thrombocytopenia (OR 0.09, 95% CI 0.01–0.43), and significantly reduced leukopenia (OR 0.34, 95% CI 0.17–0.68) ([DOI: 10.1007/s00432-023-04892-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00432-023-04892-y), PubMed). A protocol for a further BJOEI colorectal meta-analysis explicitly notes that "the exact effects and safety of BJOEI remains controversial" ([DOI: 10.1097/MD.0000000000021155](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000021155), PubMed).

⚠️ **Downgrade the A− honestly, on three counts.** First, the colorectal network meta-analysis's own authors caution that the conclusion is "limited by the treatment quality and methodology of different intervention measures" and "is expected to be scrutinized in higher-quality and rigorously designed randomized controlled trials." Second, a **2025 mesh meta-analysis of 45 studies / 3,888 patients across 10 Chinese patent medicines in advanced colorectal cancer found BJOEI was *not* the top performer on any endpoint** — Yangzheng Xiaoji capsule ranked best for ORR, DCR, QoL and immune function, and Huachansu best for leukopenia and nausea/vomiting ([DOI: 10.1097/MD.0000000000043147](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000043147), PubMed). This directly tempers the earlier network analysis's first-place ranking. Third, essentially the entire RCT base is Chinese, single-country, and drawn heavily from CNKI/Wanfang/VIP — a literature with well-documented positive-result bias. **The effect is probably real and probably smaller than these numbers.**

**Grade: A (mechanistically definitive, preclinical) — brusatol as NRF2 inhibitor.** Brusatol's identity as a specific NRF2 inhibitor is not a hypothesis; it is the compound's working definition across the field. In hepatocellular carcinoma mouse models, brusatol potentiated antitumour immunity by downregulating surface PD-L1 via transcriptional repression while upregulating MHC-I via NF-κB activation, enhancing CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and **significantly enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-1 antibody and CAR-T cells against HCC in vivo** ([DOI: 10.1136/jitc-2024-010841](https://doi.org/10.1136/jitc-2024-010841), PubMed). Brusatol's antitumour mechanism in GI cancers — NRF2 inhibition, redox homeostasis modulation, sensitisation of tumour cells to oxidative stress and chemotherapeutics — is the subject of dedicated review, which explicitly flags the need for "safer and more selective brusatol derivatives" ([DOI: 10.1002/ardp.70191](https://doi.org/10.1002/ardp.70191)). That flag is the point: **the field wants brusatol's mechanism without brusatol's toxicity.** The molecule as it exists in the seed is too blunt.

**Grade: C (traditional attestation strong, modern human evidence not retrieved) — amoebic dysentery and malaria.** The classical indication is ancient, geographically distributed, and mechanistically plausible given quassinoid antiprotozoal activity. ⚠️ **No modern clinical trial of *B. javanica* for amoebiasis or malaria was retrieved in this run.** The antiprotozoal quassinoid literature is genuine but the codex will not upgrade this grade on plausibility alone. This is the tradition's *primary* indication and it has the *weakest* modern support of anything in this section.

**Grade: D (no evidence retrieved) — warts and corns.** No trial retrieved. That a caustic cytotoxin destroys a wart is unsurprising and does not require a trial to believe; it also does not require *this* cytotoxin, and safer options exist.

**Net verdict — and note the shape of it.** The tradition said: *bitter seed, for dysentery and fever.* The evidence says: *the bitter principle is a first-class NRF2 inhibitor and the seed oil is a working chemotherapy adjuvant.* The doctrine got the **chemistry** right (bitterness = quassinoid = cytotoxic) and the **application** partly wrong. The plant's modern life is in an oncology ward the tradition could not have imagined, reached by following the tradition's own sensory instruction to its source.

### Contraindications

⚠️⚠️ **This plant is toxic. The following is not boilerplate.**

- **Do not use this plant outside clinical care. There is no safe self-administration route.** The codex states this without hedging.
- **Acute toxicity — GI.** Severe nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and gastrointestinal haemorrhage on overdose. The classical longan-flesh/capsule wrapper exists specifically because direct mucosal contact is damaging. Removing the wrapper removes the safety measure.
- **Hepatic toxicity.** Quassinoids are hepatotoxic. Anyone with existing liver disease should be assumed at elevated risk.
- **Renal toxicity.** Reported for the drug class. ⚠️ [USER: verify specific renal findings — the codex did not retrieve a source-level toxicology study of *B. javanica* this run and is flagging on class grounds and traditional record.]
- **Cytotoxicity is the mechanism, not a side effect.** Brusatol and the bruceines are potent general cytotoxins active at nanomolar-to-low-micromolar concentrations — brusatol is used at 300 nM as a laboratory Nrf2 inhibitor ([DOI: 10.1080/08958378.2026.2659193](https://doi.org/10.1080/08958378.2026.2659193)). There is no dose at which this becomes a gentle plant.
- **NRF2 inhibition is a systemic intervention with predictable harms.** NRF2 is the master regulator of the cellular antioxidant and detoxification response. Inhibiting it is useful in a tumour precisely because it strips the tumour's oxidative defences — and it strips everyone else's too. **A healthy person taking an NRF2 inhibitor is disabling a protective system for no benefit.** This is the single most important pharmacological reason not to treat this plant as a supplement, and it is invisible to anyone reading only the traditional indications.
- **Pregnancy and lactation — absolutely contraindicated.** A cytotoxic, antiproliferative, transcription-modulating agent. No further argument needed.
- **Topical use burns healthy skin.** The traditional plaster-with-a-hole and petroleum-jelly-ring techniques are load-bearing. Applied without them, the seed oil damages surrounding tissue.
- **Do not decoct, infuse, powder, or tincture.** No traditional preparation does this, and the reason is the reason.
- **BJOEI is a prescription injectable.** It is manufactured under pharmaceutical control and administered intravenously in hospital. Nothing in this entry describes a preparation a reader can make. Do not attempt to produce an injectable from plant material under any circumstances.
- **Drug interactions.** ⚠️ No interaction study was retrieved this run. On mechanism alone, NRF2 inhibition would be expected to alter the metabolism and toxicity of a wide range of drugs handled by phase II detoxification enzymes. Treat any co-administration as unstudied and hazardous.
- **Cultivation hazard.** Ripe fruit falling where children or animals forage is a real risk. See the cultivator's note above.
- **The classical indications are medical emergencies.** Amoebic dysentery and malaria kill. Both have effective, safe modern treatments. Reaching for a toxic seed instead of those treatments is not traditionalism; it is a fatal category error.
