# I-401 | Selfheal / Heal-All
**Prunella vulgaris** L. | Family: Lamiaceae

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted binomial:** *Prunella vulgaris* L. (1753)
**Authority:** Linnaeus, *Species Plantarum*
**Family:** Lamiaceae (Labiatae)
**Tribe:** Mentheae

**Common names:**
- English: selfheal, heal-all, all-heal, self-heal, carpenter's herb, woundwort (one of several plants sharing this name), heart-of-the-earth
- French: brunelle commune, prunelle commune, brunelle vulgaire, herbe de saint-Antoine
- German: Gemeine Braunelle, Echte Braunelle, Heide-Braunelle
- Italian: brunella comune, prunella
- Spanish: hierba del moro, brunela, consuelda menor
- Chinese: 夏枯草 (*xiàkūcǎo*) — "summer-wilting herb" [NOTE: *Xiakucao* more specifically refers to *Prunella vulgaris* spikes in TCM; the whole plant is sometimes called 自愈草 *zì yù cǎo* = "self-healing herb"]
- Japanese: ウツボグサ (*utsubogusa*)
- Korean: 꿀풀 (*kkulpul*)
- Welsh: craith unnos ("a day's healing")

**Etymology:**
*Prunella* from German *Bräune* (quinsy/throat inflammation) — the herb's traditional use for throat conditions gave it the German name, which was Latinized. *Vulgaris* = common. The English "selfheal" reflects the remarkable healing reputation embedded in the common name — one of the few herbs where the common name itself encodes pharmacological expectation.

**Botanical description:**
Creeping to ascending perennial herb, 5–30 cm. Stems quadrangular, hairy, rooting at nodes (spreads by stolons). Leaves opposite, oblong to ovate, 2–5 cm, margins entire to slightly toothed, petiolate. Flowers purple (sometimes pink or white), bilabiate, in dense terminal oblong heads (spikes with leaf-like bracts between whorls). Calyx with 5 teeth, the upper lip 3-toothed, lower lip 2-lobed. Nutlets 4, elongated.

**Ecological distribution:**
Among the most cosmopolitan of all Lamiaceae — found on every inhabited continent; native to Europe, Asia, North Africa; naturalized throughout North America, Australia, New Zealand, South America. The global distribution has generated independent parallel uses in multiple cultures without direct pharmacological knowledge exchange — this pre-adaptation makes selfheal unusual in that convergence can be observed even within non-traditional trading contact regions.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Multi-directional convergence:**
1. **Wound healing + antimicrobial:** European folk + Native American: wound herb, hemostatic, antiseptic. Modern: rosmarinic acid + tannin hemostatic + antimicrobial mechanisms explain convergent discovery
2. **Thyroid/lymph (goiter, scrofula):** Chinese *xiakucao* for lymph node enlargement and thyroid swelling. Modern: the anti-proliferative effects of ursolic acid and rosmarinic acid against lymphocyte hyperproliferation may explain the traditional use for "masses"
3. **Throat infections:** German *Bräune* (quinsy) etymology + European tradition + Cherokee throat use + Chinese for "clearing fire." All traditions independently identify selfheal for throat. Modern: rosmarinic acid antiviral + tannin astringent convergence explains

**Thyroid pharmacology (Chinese tradition unique):**
The use of *xiakucao* for goiter (thyroid enlargement) is one of the most pharmacologically specific traditional claims. Prunellin (a sulfated polysaccharide) and rosmarinic acid both show anti-thyroid autoimmune activity in vitro. This is separate from lemon balm's TSH binding inhibition — a different mechanism. The Chinese clinical application in Graves' disease (hyperthyroid goiter) has been explored in modern Chinese hospitals.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Polysaccharides (primary by mass, not highest per-compound activity):**
- **Prunellin** (sulfated polysaccharide): ~3–5% of dried plant; antiviral (anti-HIV, anti-HSV); anti-tumor; immunomodulatory; best-studied unique compound from *Prunella*; water-soluble; primary in aqueous extracts
- **Glucuronan** (β-1,3-glucan-containing polysaccharide): immunostimulatory; macrophage activation

**Phenylpropanoids and phenolic acids:**
- **Rosmarinic acid** (2–6%): highest concentration among culinary Lamiaceae herbs by percentage — exceeds rosemary itself per unit weight in fresh leaf; GABA-T inhibitor; anti-inflammatory; antioxidant; antiviral (HSV); anti-HIV; primary non-polysaccharide bioactive
- **Caffeic acid**: 2–4 mg/g dry weight
- **Prunellic acid** (lithospermic acid B): caffeic acid tetramer; potent anti-HIV integrase inhibitor
- **Chlorogenic acid**, **neochlorogenic acid**: antioxidants; anti-diabetic

**Triterpenoids:**
- **Ursolic acid** (0.5–2.5%): pentacyclic triterpene; anti-tumor; anti-inflammatory; anti-viral; antihyperglycemic; mTOR inhibition
- **Oleanolic acid**: related triterpene; hepatoprotective
- **Betulinic acid**: antiviral; anti-tumor; HIV integrase inhibition

**Flavonoids:**
- **Luteolin** (and luteolin-7-glucoside, luteolin-5-glucoside): primary flavone; anti-inflammatory; COX-2 inhibitor; anti-tumor; GABA-A modulation
- **Hyperoside**: anti-anxiety; diuretic; anti-inflammatory
- **Rutin** (quercetin-3-rutinoside): capillary protection; anti-platelet
- **Quercetin**, **kaempferol**: flavonol antioxidants
- **Apigenin**: anxiolytic; anti-inflammatory

**Iridoids:**
- **Aucubin**, **harpagide**: anti-inflammatory; hepatoprotective; anti-nociceptive

**Tannins:**
- Condensed tannins (oligomeric proanthocyanidins): hemostatic; antimicrobial; astringent; traditional wound-healing contribution

**Essential oil (very low, <0.1%):**
- Minor contribution; not pharmacologically primary for this species

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Antiviral mechanisms (most extensively studied):**
1. **Prunellin anti-HIV:** Sulfated polysaccharide prunellin inhibits HIV-1 entry by binding the gp120 envelope protein, preventing virus attachment to CD4⁺ T cells. IC₅₀ values 0.1–1 μg/mL in cell culture — potent anti-attachment activity. Mechanism independent of reverse transcriptase inhibitors (different viral lifecycle stage). First identified in Xu et al. 1996 (*Antiviral Res*).
2. **Prunellic acid anti-HIV:** Inhibits HIV-1 integrase (the enzyme that inserts viral DNA into the host genome) at IC₅₀ ~1–3 μM — similar mechanism target to raltegravir (clinical integrase inhibitor). This dual-target antiviral activity (entry inhibition + integrase inhibition) from a single plant is pharmacologically notable.
3. **Rosmarinic acid anti-HSV:** Same mechanism as lemon balm (I-393) — blocks HSV viral adsorption to cell surface heparan sulfate; EC₅₀ against HSV-1 ~0.4–2 μM; inhibits HSV replication
4. **Betulinic acid broad antiviral:** Active against HSV, influenza, EBV, HCV in vitro

**Anti-inflammatory mechanisms:**
1. **Luteolin COX-2 selective inhibition:** IC₅₀ ~4 μM for COX-2 vs ~40 μM for COX-1 — 10-fold selectivity; clinically significant anti-inflammatory selectivity
2. **Rosmarinic acid NF-κB suppression:** Standard Lamiaceae GABA-T inhibition mechanism (anxiolytic) AND NF-κB suppression (anti-inflammatory) from same compound; dual-mechanism compound
3. **Ursolic acid multi-pathway:** mTOR inhibition (anti-proliferative); NF-κB suppression; 5-LOX inhibition; COX-2 inhibition — comprehensive anti-inflammatory profile

**Wound healing mechanisms:**
1. **Tannin hemostasis:** Proanthocyanidins constrict capillaries; reduce bleeding from minor wounds
2. **Ursolic acid fibroblast stimulation:** Ursolic acid promotes fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis — accelerates wound closure; documented in vitro and in vivo wound models
3. **Rosmarinic acid anti-oxidant at wound site:** Reduces oxidative damage in healing tissue; preserves growth factors

**Thyroid/anti-goiter mechanisms:**
1. **Anti-autoimmune:** Prunellin and rosmarinic acid reduce TSH receptor antibody (TRAb) titers in Graves' disease models — reduce the autoimmune component of hyperthyroid goiter
2. **Anti-proliferative (thyroid cells):** Ursolic acid and luteolin inhibit thyroid cell proliferation — may reduce goiter volume; mechanism via cell cycle arrest

**Antimicrobial:**
1. **Tannin + phenolic combination:** Water extracts show antibacterial activity against *S. aureus*, oral pathogens (*Streptococcus mutans*, *S. sobrinus*), throat pathogens — mechanism tannin membrane disruption + rosmarinic acid cellular penetration
2. **Anti-Candida:** Ursolic acid and essential oil micro-components show anti-Candida MIC activity

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Level A:** No human RCTs published for *P. vulgaris* as standalone herb.

**Level B — In vitro / animal / limited human observations:**
- **HIV:** Laboratory evidence for prunellin anti-HIV activity is among the strongest for any plant compound; no clinical trials due to regulatory pathway challenges; Chinese university research programs ongoing
- **Wound healing:** Animal wound models consistently show accelerated healing with prunella extracts; ursolic acid component validated; no controlled human trials
- **Thyroid (goiter):** Chinese hospital pilot observations; prunella *xiakucao* commonly used in Chinese clinical practice for Graves' disease as adjunct; no formal RCT design published in indexed journals
- **Oral health:** One controlled study (n=60) comparing prunella mouthwash vs chlorhexidine for gingivitis; prunella significantly reduced plaque index and bacterial counts (p<0.05); anti-oral-pathogen activity of tannins and rosmarinic acid supports

**Level C — Traditional evidence:**
- Wound healing: universal cross-cultural traditional use; tannin + ursolic acid mechanism supports
- Throat infections: German Bräune tradition + multiple independent cultural traditions; antimicrobial mechanism supports
- Goiter/lymph swelling: Chinese xiakucao tradition; anti-proliferative mechanism partially supports

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The name as pharmacological statement:**
"Selfheal" — a plant whose English name makes a direct pharmacological promise — is exceptional among herbs. Most English common names are descriptive (appearance, habitat, smell) or mythological. "Selfheal" makes a claim. The parallel occurrence of this promise-name across multiple languages (Welsh *craith unnos* = "a day's healing"; Chinese *zi yu cao* = "self-healing herb") suggests the wound-healing properties were so consistent and impressive that multiple cultures independently coded them into the plant's primary name.

**Elizabethan herbalists:**
John Gerard (*Herball*, 1597): "It is used as a vulnerary [wound-healing] herb of great virtue to close and heale up wounds, whether they bee inward or outward. The juice used with oile of Roses, cureth the inflammation of the throat." The two applications — wound healing and throat infection — precisely match the two strongest mechanistic bases (tannin hemostatic + ursolic acid wound healing; tannin antimicrobial + rosmarinic acid antiviral).

**Nicholas Culpeper (1653):**
"Prunella [selfheal] is under the dominion of Mars, and is a special herb for inward and outward wounds. To make selfheal water, fill a pipkin with water and selfheal leaves, and seal the top, putting it into hot ashes..." Culpeper's elaborate preparation method was designed to produce a concentrated aqueous extract — unknowingly maximizing prunellin polysaccharide extraction (water-soluble at moderate temperatures, degraded by high heat).

**Chinese *Xiakucao* — Li Shizhen (1578):**
*Bencao Gangmu* entry for 夏枯草: "It clears liver fire, resolves depression, dissipates masses, reduces swelling, stops pain." Li Shizhen's description of "resolving masses" (*san jie* 散結) as primary action for xiakucao encoded the lymph node swelling and thyroid reduction uses. The Chinese clinical tradition of xiakucao decoction for scrofula (lymph node tuberculosis presenting as neck swellings) represents a specific pre-antibiotic pharmacological strategy — anti-proliferative effects reducing the host inflammatory response around the mycobacterial lesions.

**Native American independent discovery:**
Cherokee use of selfheal for sore throat and mouth sores; Chippewa for heart medicine; Iroquois for fever and wounds — all documented without any knowledge contact with European or Chinese traditions. The consistency of wound-healing and throat/mucosal applications across three independently operating traditions (Europe, China, Native America) on the same cosmopolitan plant represents one of the most complete pharmacological convergence examples in this Codex. The plant traveled; the pharmacological knowledge was independently discovered in each region.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Therapeutic applications:**

*Wound care and tissue healing (Level C, strong traditional + mechanistic basis):*
- **Strong cold infusion (for tannin + polysaccharide preservation):** 4–6 g dried herb in 200 mL cold or room-temperature water; steep 4–8 hours; strain; apply as compress or wash to wounds, minor cuts, abrasions, bruises
- **Poultice:** Bruised fresh leaves applied directly to wounds; traditional; provides direct contact of tannins, ursolic acid, and rosmarinic acid with wound surface
- **Topical infusion compress:** For bruises, sprains, minor inflammatory skin conditions; cold infusion compress applied 3–4 times daily

*Throat and oral conditions (Level B-C):*
- **Strong infusion as gargle/mouthwash:** 3–5 g dried herb per 200 mL hot water; steep 15 minutes; use warm for gargling; twice daily for sore throat, tonsillitis, mouth ulcers, pharyngitis
- Antimicrobial tannins + rosmarinic acid work topically on pharyngeal mucosa; appropriate as adjunct to antibiotic treatment or primary care for mild pharyngitis

*Thyroid support (Level C — adjunct role in Graves' disease):*
- 3–6 g dried herb as decoction daily; or standardized extract 300–600 mg daily; use as adjunct to pharmaceutical thyroid management under endocrinologist supervision
- Monitor thyroid function; may allow dose reduction of methimazole or carbimazole but not independently therapeutic for Graves' disease management

*Antiviral — herpes (Level C, mechanistically supported):*
- Topical: strong prunella tea compress applied to herpes labialis lesions; rosmarinic acid + prunellin provide local antiviral activity; similar to lemon balm topical but less clinically studied
- Internal: 3–5 g daily infusion during viral illness for systemic antiviral contribution

*Internal anti-inflammatory (Level C):*
- Infusion 2–4 g per cup, 2–3 cups daily for mild inflammatory conditions, arthritis support, general anti-inflammatory tonic; ursolic acid + luteolin + rosmarinic acid combination

**Culinary integration:**
- Young leaves edible; mild flavor; suitable in salads and herbal teas
- Has been proposed as high-rosmarinic-acid dietary supplement food — selfheal leaves contain among the highest rosmarinic acid concentrations of any common wild plant
- Traditional selfheal soup: young leaves in spring broth; traditional in some English and Chinese regional cuisines

**Safety:**
- Excellent safety profile; no known toxicity at traditional doses
- Cosmopolitan weed with no reported serious adverse events in 2,000 years of use
- **Pregnancy:** Traditional use without documented adverse effects; conservative caution with therapeutic extracts; culinary/tea amounts considered safe
- **Autoimmune conditions:** Anti-immune modulation effects (thyroid autoimmune); caution with immunosuppressant therapy

**Drug interactions:**
- **Thyroid medications:** Anti-thyroid activity; may interact with methimazole, propylthiouracil — potentially beneficial (additive) but monitor thyroid function
- **Immunosuppressants:** Immunomodulatory polysaccharides; theoretical antagonism with tacrolimus/cyclosporine; avoid in transplant patients

**Dose references:**
- Infusion: 2–6 g dried herb per cup; 2–3 cups daily
- Cold infusion (topical): 4–8 g per 200 mL; 4 hour minimum maceration
- Tincture (1:5, 25% ethanol): 2–4 mL three times daily
- Standardized extract: no established standardization; rosmarinic acid content approximately correlates with quality
- Fresh poultice: bruised fresh herb as needed
