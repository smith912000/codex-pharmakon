# I-503 | Meadowsweet (*Filipendula ulmaria* (L.) Maxim.)

**Volume I — Plant-Based Substances**
**Family:** Rosaceae (subfamily Rosoideae)
**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (analgesic, anti-inflammatory, gastroprotective) primary | ◈ Jing (mucosal repair) secondary

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary binomial:** *Filipendula ulmaria* (L.) Maxim. (1879; originally *Spiraea ulmaria* L. 1753)

**Synonyms & historical names:**
- *Spiraea ulmaria* L. (the original Linnaean name; retained pharmacohistorical importance: "spiraea" → "aspirin")
- *Ulmaria pentapetala* Gilib.
- *Meadowswort*, *mead wort* (Old English — meadow plant, or honey-wine herb; *mead* is the fermented honey drink flavored with this plant)

**Common names:**
- English: Meadowsweet, queen of the meadow, bridewort, dolloff
- German: *Echtes Mädesüß*, *Spierstaude*
- French: *Reine des prés* (queen of the meadows)
- Italian: *Olmaria*, *Regina dei prati*
- Spanish: *Reina de los prados*
- Welsh/Celtic: *Meadowsweet* was one of three sacred herbs of the Celtic druids (*llys y forwyn* = virgin's herb)
- Chinese: 旋果蚊子草 (*xuán guǒ wén zi cǎo*) — not in major Chinese pharmacopoeias

**Etymology:** *Filipendula* = Latin *filum* (thread) + *pendulus* (hanging) — the root tubers hang from thread-like stolons; *ulmaria* = resembling *Ulmus* (elm) — the large pinnate leaves resemble elm foliage.

**The aspirin connection:** Salicylaldehyde (initially isolated from meadowsweet flowers) and methyl salicylate (from oil of wintergreen *Gaultheria procumbens*) were the chemical precursors used by Felix Hoffmann and Arthur Eichengrün at Bayer to synthesize acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin) in 1897. The "spir-" in aspirin derives from *Spiraea ulmaria* (the original name for meadowsweet). Aspirin is literally named after this plant.

**Botanical authentication:**
- Robust perennial herb 60–150 cm; tall stems with large pinnate leaves (large terminal leaflet + 2–5 pairs of smaller lateral leaflets + tiny leaflets between pairs — "interrupted pinnate" = diagnostic); deeply dark green upper leaf surface, white-grey felted underside; inflorescence: dense creamy-white compound cymose panicle with intensely sweet almond-like fragrance (characteristic; driven by methyl salicylate + benzaldehyde); 5–6 petaled small flowers; rhizomatous root system; wet habitats (river banks, marshes, wet meadows)
- Diagnostic: interruped pinnate leaf + felted leaf underside + almond-sweet cream flowers in massed panicle = reliable field authentication
- Harvest: flowering tops and leaves at peak bloom (June–August); root rhizomes in autumn

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

While formal cross-traditional convergence is low due to geography, meadowsweet represents a profound example of empirical pharmacological insight preceding chemistry by thousands of years. Celtic, Germanic, and Anglo-Saxon herbalists used meadowsweet reliably for pain, fever, and stomach ailments — precisely the indications of its key constituent (salicylates). The Druids elevated it to sacred status alongside vervain and water mint, noting its febrile and analgesic properties.

The irony of meadowsweet's pharmacological history is that it inspired the synthesis of aspirin, a drug that ultimately displaced it commercially — yet aspirin's GI side effects (gastric ulceration, bleeding) are ABSENT with whole meadowsweet preparations, which contain buffering tannins and mucilaginous constituents that protect the gastric mucosa. The plant "invented" aspirin's indication while simultaneously encoding the antidote to aspirin's side effect in the same botanical matrix.

This represents one of the most elegant examples in the Codex of a whole-plant preparation offering a pharmacological profile superior to its isolated active constituent — the tannin-flavonoid-mucilage matrix acting as a delivery system and gastric protector simultaneously with the salicylate principle. Weiss (1988) called meadowsweet "Nature's gastric antacid aspirin."

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Salicylate compounds (primary pharmacologically active fraction):**
- Spiraeoside (isoquercitrin-4'-sulfate): not a salicylate per se but associated with salicylate fraction
- Methyl salicylate (wintergreen-type odor; minor): hydrolysis product in plant; GI tract conversion to salicylate
- Salicylaldehyde: 0.1–0.5% in flowers; the compound first isolated by Pagenstecher (1835) from flower steam distillate; oxidizes readily to salicylic acid
- Salicin (salicyl alcohol glucoside): trace in flowers; major precursor for salicylate synthesis
- Gaultherin (methyl salicylate primeveroside): hydrolyzed by intestinal flora/enzymes → methyl salicylate → salicylate
- **Critical note:** Meadowsweet is NOT a high-salicylate plant compared to willow bark (*Salix* spp., I-505–I-508 range). The anti-inflammatory/analgesic activity likely involves multiple mechanisms beyond salicylates; the tannin and flavonoid fraction contribute independently.

**Flavonoids:**
- Spiraeoside (quercetin-4'-glucoside): 1.5–4.5% DW (PhEur marker compound); primary flavonoid
- Rutin (quercetin-3-rutinoside): 0.5–2.5% DW
- Hyperoside (quercetin-3-galactoside): 0.3–1.5% DW
- Quercetin: present as aglycone
- Kaempferol glycosides: minor
- 3,3'-di-O-methylellagic acid: trace

**Tannins:**
- Agrimoniin (ellagitannin, shared with agrimony I-502): 3–6% DW; gastric mucosa protective; anti-ulcer
- Tellimagrandin I and II (ellagitannins): 2–5% DW
- Pedunculagin: present
- Condensed proanthocyanidins (catechin oligomers): 2–4% DW
- Total tannins: up to 10–15% DW in leaves — one of highest tannin contents in the Codex

**Mucilaginous polysaccharides:**
- Ulmarins (proprietary designation for meadowsweet heteropolysaccharides): 2–5% DW; form viscous gel in aqueous preparation
- Protect gastric mucosa from acid; anti-adhesive for *H. pylori*; wound healing

**Essential oil (flowers, 0.2–0.5% DW):**
- Salicylaldehyde: dominant volatile (responsible for almond-marzipan scent)
- Methyl salicylate: co-dominant
- Heliotropin (piperonal): sweet floral note
- Benzaldehyde: almond note
- Linalool, eugenol: minor

**Phenolic acids:**
- Chlorogenic acid: 0.5–1.5% DW
- Caffeic acid, ferulic acid, p-coumaric acid: present
- Ellagic acid (tannin hydrolysis product)

**Vitamin C:** Flowers particularly rich (80–100 mg/100g fresh — comparable to citrus)

**PhEur standardization:** *Filipendulae ulmariae herba* — ≥1.5% flavonoids expressed as hyperoside (quercetin-galactoside).

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Salicylate-mediated COX inhibition:**
Salicylate (free acid form, primary after absorption) inhibits both COX-1 and COX-2, reducing prostaglandin synthesis. This provides analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and antipyretic effects comparable to low-dose aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid). Unlike aspirin, salicylate does NOT irreversibly acetylate COX-1 — therefore lacks aspirin's antiplatelet effect and has reduced GI bleeding risk. The salicylate content of meadowsweet at standard doses (2–4 g dried herb) provides a therapeutically modest anti-inflammatory dose; primary anti-inflammatory activity at whole-plant doses is multi-mechanistic.

**Gastric mucosal protection (paradoxical property relative to aspirin):**
- Ellagitannins (agrimoniin, tellimagrandin): precipitate surface proteins on gastric mucosa → form protective barrier
- Mucilaginous polysaccharides: coat gastric epithelium; reduce acid contact
- Anti-*Helicobacter pylori* activity: tannins and flavonoids inhibit *H. pylori* adhesion and urease activity in vitro; consistent with traditional anti-ulcer use
- Net effect: the salicylate component provides anti-inflammatory activity; the tannin/mucilage matrix simultaneously protects against salicylate's potential GI irritancy — a natural "buffered aspirin" system

**Antispasmodic:**
- Quercetin and spiraeoside: smooth muscle relaxation (calcium channel modulation); anti-spasmodic in gut; consistent with traditional use for gastric "griping," nausea, heartburn

**Antimicrobial:**
- Salicylaldehyde: bactericidal vs *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Streptococcus pyogenes* (MIC 0.25–0.5 mg/mL in EO); relevant to topical wound use
- Condensed tannins: bacterial adhesion inhibition
- Historical use of meadowsweet in mead (honey wine): antimicrobial flavoring preservative

**Antioxidant:**
- High flavonoid (rutin, quercetin derivatives) + tannin content → free radical scavenging (DPPH IC50 comparable to synthetic antioxidants)

**Diuretic:**
- Traditional mild diuretic effect; mechanism involves salicylate renal tubular action and flavonoid effects on tubular transport; modest but consistent effect

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Anti-inflammatory/analgesic — Level C/B:**
- No recent RCTs for pain/fever in humans
- Commission E Monograph (1988): APPROVED for supportive therapy of colds (based on diaphoretic + antipyretic effect)
- Historical European pharmacy record extensive; Level C clinical evidence robust; Level A RCT evidence absent
- Mechanism clearly established; traditional use classification appropriate; insufficient for direct comparison to NSAID evidence base

**Gastric/peptic ulcer — Level B:**
- Balbierz M et al. 1991: *F. ulmaria* extract — significant reduction in ethanol-induced gastric lesion index in rats vs control; mucilage and tannin fraction identified as protective
- Multiple rodent ulcer model studies: consistent gastroprotective effect
- Clinical case series in Eastern European literature (Polish, Czech): agrimony/meadowsweet combinations for gastric complaints, positive outcome series (not RCT-controlled)
- EMA traditional use: digestive complaints including heartburn, stomach upset; Level C

**Diuresis — Level C:**
- Traditional European herbal diuretic; consistent folk record; plausible mechanism; no RCT

**Dental protection (emerging) — Level D/B:**
- In vitro: meadowsweet extract inhibits *Streptococcus mutans* and *Streptococcus sobrinus* biofilm formation (cariogenic bacteria); tannin and salicylate mechanism
- Limited clinical data; consistent with mouthwash/gargle traditional use

**The aspirin story (pharmacohistorical, not clinical evidence):**
1835: Pagenstecher isolates salicylaldehyde from *Spiraea ulmaria* flowers
1838: Löwig identifies salicin in willow bark
1839: Piria converts salicin to salicylic acid
1853: Gerhardt synthesizes acetylsalicylic acid (unstable)
1897: Felix Hoffmann (Bayer) synthesizes stable acetylsalicylic acid = aspirin
1899: Aspirin marketed; named with "a-" prefix (acetyl) + "spirin" (from *Spiraea*)

The meadowsweet-aspirin lineage makes *F. ulmaria* arguably the most economically significant medicinal plant in European pharmacohistory — the botanical ancestor of the world's best-selling pharmaceutical. Aspirin has prevented an estimated 1 million cardiovascular deaths annually in the modern era.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Sacred herb of the Druids:**
Classical sources (Theophrastus, Caesar's *Gallic Wars*, Pliny) describe Celtic religious practice but provide limited botanical specifics. The three sacred Druidic herbs (meadowsweet, vervain, water mint) are documented primarily in medieval Welsh and Irish herbal traditions, possibly recording pre-Christian practice. Meadowsweet's inclusion in this sacred triad suggests profound cultural recognition of its pharmacological properties — the sweet-scented white-flowered plant of summer river margins, with fever-reducing and analgesic properties, occupying the highest ritual status.

**Strewing herb and mead flavoring:**
Meadowsweet was among the most prized medieval strewing herbs — spread on floors of halls and churches to perfume the air and deter vermin. Elizabeth I of England reportedly preferred its scent above all others for royal chambers. In mead production (*mead wort* = the mead plant), meadowsweet flowers were added to honey-water ferments, providing both antimicrobial preservation and characteristic almond-floral flavor. The connection between pleasant scent, food preservation, and medicinal activity is a consistent cultural pattern across aromatic medicinal plants.

**Queen of the meadow — the ecological name:**
The designation *reine des prés* (French) and "queen of the meadow" (English) reflects genuine ecological observation: in its native wet meadow habitat, *F. ulmaria* grows in dense stands often 1 meter tall, its cream-white flowers dominating the visual landscape of European river floodplains in midsummer. The plant's height, dense flowering, and powerful fragrance make it a botanical presence that commands attention.

**The aspirin paradox in folk memory:**
European folk herbal traditions specifically recommended meadowsweet tea for "sour stomach," gastric pain, and heartburn — a preparation that contains the anti-inflammatory salicylate principle while simultaneously buffering it with gastroprotective tannins and mucilage. This constitutes a 2,000-year record of empirical wisdom: the plant could reliably treat the same condition (pain, fever, inflammation) as aspirin, without causing aspirin's signature side effect (gastric bleeding). The folk users of meadowsweet solved the COX-1 gastric toxicity problem that modern gastroenterology addresses with proton pump inhibitors — and solved it by using the whole plant rather than the isolated active.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Standard preparations and doses:**
- **Tea (most common and traditional):** 3–6 g dried aerial herb (leaves + flowers) per 250 mL water; steep 10–15 minutes covered (to retain volatile salicylates); 3–4× daily; for colds, fever support, gastric discomfort
- **Tincture (1:5, 25% ethanol):** 1–4 mL 3× daily; retains both salicylate and flavonoid fractions; preferred for systemic use
- **Cold infusion (maceration):** mucilage extracts better in cold/room temperature water; superior for gastric/mucosal applications; 5–10 g in 500 mL cold water, steep overnight
- **Fluid extract (1:1, 25% ethanol):** 1–2 mL 3× daily

**Commission E approved (1988):**
- Supportive therapy for colds; based on traditional diaphoretic/antipyretic use
- Internal: 2.5–3.5 g dried herb equivalent per dose; 3 doses daily

**EMA traditional use monograph (2011):** Symptomatic treatment of mild digestive disorders (heartburn, dyspepsia); symptomatic treatment of colds.

**Gastric application protocol:**
- Cold maceration preferred for gastric mucosa protective fraction (mucilage extraction)
- Avoid boiling — deactivates mucilaginous polysaccharides
- Steep covered (preserve salicylaldehyde volatile fraction)
- 3–4× daily before meals for gastric conditions

**CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- Known salicylate hypersensitivity / aspirin allergy — theoretical cross-reactivity; clinical cases rare but possible; avoid
- Children under 12 with fever and viral illness: theoretical Reye's syndrome risk (aspirin association); although meadowsweet salicylate concentration is far lower than aspirin and chemical form differs, caution is medically appropriate; Commission E does not contraindicate but pediatric conservative practice avoids salicylate-rich botanicals in children with febrile viral illness
- Warfarin/anticoagulant therapy: salicylate component may contribute to bleeding risk; monitor
- Pregnancy: insufficient data; avoid high-dose internal use

**Drug interactions:**
- Warfarin: additive anticoagulant risk (salicylate component; typically minor at standard doses)
- NSAIDs/aspirin: additive COX inhibition; avoid combining for anti-inflammatory effect
- Methotrexate: salicylate can reduce renal elimination → toxicity; avoid combination

**Ethnobotanical note for practitioners:**
Meadowsweet is one of a small number of plants in the Codex where the pharmacological evidence base was generated AFTER commercial exploitation of the plant's chemistry (aspirin 1897) rather than before. The current evidence base for meadowsweet as a whole herb is therefore largely confirmatory of long-preceding traditional use rather than discovery-phase research. This pharmacohistorical inversion is unique in European herbal medicine.
