# Tithymalus diffusis — Ippophaiston, "Hippophaeston" (Dioscorides 4-163)

## *Tithymalus diffusis, the first name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-163 ("Tithymalus diffusis, Euphorbia spinosa [Bedevian] — Hippophaiston")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk lists the plant under this name (the name indexes in `Sources/*/extract/names.csv` were searched). The entry rests on one short chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The line prints a second name, *Euphorbia spinosa*, with the attribution [Bedevian], and glosses the plant only with the Greek name ("Hippophaiston"), so it carries no English common name. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-163. IPPOPHAISTON* at line 9504, SUGGESTED line at 9505, text at line 9506. The chapter stands in Book Four, "Other Herbs & Roots", after *4-162. IPPOPHAES* (line 9489) and before *4-164. KROTON E KIKI* (line 9507). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 741, and the translators' indexes print "Tithymalus diffusis 719, 720" (line 17864) and "IPPOPHAISTON 719" (line 12167).

The whole chapter reads: "Hippophaeston grows in the same places in which hippophaes [above] grows, also a kind of fuller's acantha [thistle] but it lies on the ground. It has only small little leaves, with empty little prickly heads. It bears neither stalk nor flower, and a thick soft root. Juice the leaves, root and head of this, dry the juice, and give thirty grains with honey and water to whom you will for purging to expel water and phlegm. This purging is good for asthma, epilepsy, and disorders of the strength. Some also call this hippophaes."

The chapter is a description by reference to the chapter before it. The plant grows "in the same places" as *hippophaes* (4-162, which the text says grows "in sandy maritime places", line 9491), and is "also a kind of fuller's acantha [thistle] but it lies on the ground". It has "only small little leaves, with empty little prickly heads", "neither stalk nor flower", and "a thick soft root". The use is one: the juice of the "leaves, root and head" is made, dried, and given in an amount of thirty grains with honey and water "for purging to expel water and phlegm", and the purging "is good for asthma, epilepsy, and disorders of the strength". The only other name is that "Some also call this hippophaes", which is the name of the chapter before. The Codex reads the two as two chapters and does not merge them.

**Why the genus name is the translators'.** The preceding chapter, 4-165, is headed *TITHUMALOS* and its four SUGGESTED lines (9516 to 9519) include this chapter's own line, "Tithymalus diffusis, Euphorbia spinosa — Hippophaiston Euphorbia — Devil's Milk, Spurge" (line 9517). So the same binomial is printed at the head of the general tithymal chapter and again here. The seven kinds listed at line 9521 do not include *hippophaeston*. Nothing in the text of 4-163 itself uses the word *tithymal*, and it prints no milky juice. The tithymal genus comes from the translators' line only.

**Overlap.** A chapter-level search of the entries folder for 4-163, the Greek heading and *Hippophaeston* found no entry on this chapter. I_1087 (*Euphorbia resinifera*, 4-165) quotes the suggestion line at the head of 4-165 and states that the lines are not tied to any one of the seven kinds. I_580 (*Hippophae rhamnoides*, sea buckthorn) does not cite 4-162 or the Greek name in a search of its text, and 4-162 was read only to place this chapter. Its translators' suggestion is *Hippophae rhamnoides* (line 9490). The sister entry I_1208 (*Tithymalus acutifolius*, 4-166) is a separate chapter in the same group. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. It is a general fact that spurges (*Euphorbia*) belong to the spurge family (Euphorbiaceae), that most of them carry a milky latex, and that the latex of many species is a strong irritant to skin and eyes. The chapter prints no latex, so this is a statement about the genus the translators suggest and not about the chapter. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a dried juice given as a purge for "water and phlegm", and a claim that the purging is good for asthma, epilepsy and "disorders of the strength". These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. Epilepsy and asthma are conditions for medical care, a strong purgative of an unidentified plant can cause serious harm, and the Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation: the juice of "the leaves, root and head", dried, and "thirty grains with honey and water". The grain is the translators' unit and the chapter gives no weight. It does not say how often or for how long, and "to whom you will" is the chapter's own phrase for who is to be given it. The Codex offers no modern dose. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that a strong purgative can cause dehydration and loss of salts, that many spurge latexes are skin and eye irritants, and that purging is not a treatment for asthma or epilepsy.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 4-163 prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality and no taste. The only functional statement is that the dried juice purges "to expel water and phlegm". The text's other words are physical: "a thick soft root" and "empty little prickly heads".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a recognition by reference to a neighbour (the same places as *hippophaes*, a prostrate kind of fuller's thistle), a description of what the plant lacks (no stalk, no flower), and the processing of a juice from three named parts. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a purgative, and a purge is the opposite of a conserving practice. The transferable point is that a plant can be described by where it grows and by what it does not have. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 4-163 prints no warning. For safety on the group the Codex can point only to a neighbouring chapter: 4-165a, on the first of the seven tithymals, directs that one "must not stand against the wind, nor put his hands to his eyes" when juicing it. That is a precaution for a different plant, and nothing here says that it applies to this one, although the present chapter also makes a juice. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Asthma and epilepsy need medical care and must not be left to a purge. The text gives no safety information for pregnancy, children or any other medicine, and gives one dose only as quoted. [USER: verify]
