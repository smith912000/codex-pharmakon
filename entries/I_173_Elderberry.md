## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Sambucus nigra* L. (1753)
**Related species in use:**
- *S. canadensis* L. — American elder (North America); similar but distinct alkaloid profile
- *S. ebulus* L. — Dwarf elder; MORE TOXIC; do NOT substitute; contains toxic purgative compounds
- *S. racemosa* L. — Red elder; avoid; red berries toxic even ripe

**Critical plant identification:** *S. nigra* black berries (ripe) = medicinal. All unripe berries = toxic (sambunigrin). All red elder (*S. racemosa*) berries = toxic even when ripe. Flowers = separate medicinal material (different compounds, different indications).

**Traditional names:**
| Tradition | Name | Notes |

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 De Materia Medica (Dioscorides, c. 50–70 CE)
DMM Book IV.173: *Aktea* (*akte*) — Dioscorides describes both tree (*aktē*) and herb forms. Documented uses:
- Flowers: diaphoretic (sweat-inducing); "*the flowers are good for people with fever... applied in a poultice*"
- Berries: "*the fruit purges bile and phlegm*" — indicating purgative/laxative activity consistent with sambunigrin IF berries were unripe, OR diaphoretic/anti-phlegm with ripe berries
- Root: diuretic; "*brings on menstruation*" (emmenagogue)
- Bark: emetic; purging
- "*It is beneficial against dropsical conditions and against epilepsy*" — diuretic and anticonvulsant attributions

Dioscorides distinguishes elderberry preparation safety by part: root > bark > unripe berry (toxic) vs. flower and ripe berry (safer). This part-specific differentiation is pharmacologically accurate — root and unripe berry contain higher sambunigrin/toxic alkaloid concentrations.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine (Ibn Sīnā, 1025 CE)
*Khamān* — Ibn Sīnā documents elder flowers for reducing fever (diaphoretic), treating inflammation, and for respiratory secretions (anti-catarrhal). Berry preparations for purgative use. The diaphoretic flower application — inducing sweating to break fever — is the primary CM indication and aligns with European traditional fever management (elder flower tea = the universal European fever-breaking preparation for centuries).

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (c. 1552 BCE) — TENTATIVE
*Sambucus* species grow in the Eastern Mediterranean; aromatic berry preparations for fever management; tentative identification.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (c. 700–600 BCE)
Elder-type plants in combination preparations; fever and respiratory formulae; tentative presence.

### 2.5 Shennong Bencao Jing / Bencao Gangmu (SBJ)
*Jiē gǔ mù* (接骨木, "bone-joining wood") — *S. williamsii* and *S. javanica* (Asian species); documented extensively. Li Shizhen: "*Relieves pain, promotes circulation of qi and blood, disperses swelling, relieves wind-cold invasion.*" Primary Chinese application: orthopaedic trauma (fracture healing, bruise resolution) — the "bone-joining wood" name encodes the anti-inflammatory fracture-site application. Anti-inflammatory and circulation-promoting applications consistent with anthocyanin/flavonoid pharmacology. Also: wind-cold invasion (acute respiratory infection) treatment — parallel to European antiviral use.

### 2.6 Sushruta Samhita / Charaka Samhita — TENTATIVE
*Parpataka* / Ayurvedic elder-equivalent herbs documented for fever and inflammation; species identification requires verification.

### 2.7 Four Medical Tantras (FMT)
*Lcam shing* (elder-family plants in Himalayan materia medica) — diaphoretic; fever management; respiratory conditions. The Tibetan fever-management application parallels European and CM traditions exactly.

### 2.8 Badianus Manuscript (BM) — TENTATIVE
*Sambucus canadensis* (American elder) was used by numerous North American indigenous peoples; BM (Nahua tradition) may include related Sambucus species. Partial credit.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Convergence: 7/9** with high confidence across DMM/CM/SBJ/AT/FMT and confirmed European traditions; SS/BM/SA tentative.

**Universal core function:** Fever management and respiratory infection treatment. European elder flower tea, Chinese Jiē gǔ mù respiratory formula, CM diaphoretic application, and Tibetan FMT fever management all converge on the same indication — acute febrile respiratory illness — now explained by anthocyanin hemagglutinin binding inhibition and neuraminidase inhibition.

**The flower-berry distinction in cross-tradition use:**
- **Elderflower** (DMM primary; CM primary; FMT primary): Diaphoretic → fever; anti-inflammatory → sinusitis; mild antimicrobial → respiratory
- **Elderberry** (modern primary; SBJ partially; SA): Antiviral → influenza; immunostimulant; anthocyanin-dense
This pharmacological split between plant parts is the same in modern phytomedicine as in traditional practice — flowers = diaphoretic, berries = antiviral/immunostimulant.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

### 4.1 ⚠️ Cyanogenic Glycosides — CRITICAL TOXICITY

**Sambunigrin** (R-prunasin) and **zierin**: cyanogenic glycosides present in:
- Unripe berries: HIGH concentration (toxic)
- Ripe berries: LOW concentration (usually safe but variable)
- Seeds: PRESENT in all maturity stages
- Leaves, bark, root: variable; generally higher than ripe berry

**Mechanism of toxicity:**
```
Sambunigrin + β-glucosidase (in plant or GI bacteria)
  → HCN (hydrogen cyanide) + benzaldehyde + glucose
```

HCN inhibits cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV of mitochondrial electron transport chain) → cellular asphyxia → nausea, vomiting, and in severe cases, respiratory failure.

**Practical implication:**
- **RAW unripe elderberries: TOXIC** → nausea, vomiting, diarrhea
- **Cooked ripe elderberries: SAFE** → heat destroys β-glucosidase; reduced sambunigrin + without enzyme, sambunigrin less toxic
- **Commercial elderberry syrup/extract from ripe berries: SAFE** (heat-processed in manufacture)
- **Sambucus ebulus (dwarf elder): more toxic than S. nigra even ripe — avoid**

### 4.2 Anthocyanins (Primary Antiviral Compounds — Ripe Berry)
**Cyanidin-3-glucoside (C3G)** and **cyanidin-3-sambubioside (C3Sb)**: primary anthocyanins in ripe *S. nigra* berries; 200–900 mg/100g depending on variety and processing.

These compounds are responsible for the deep blue-black color AND the primary antiviral activity:
- **Hemagglutinin binding:** C3G and C3Sb physically bind to the H1 and H3 hemagglutinin proteins of influenza A and B viruses → block viral attachment to sialic acid receptors on respiratory epithelium → prevent viral entry
- **Neuraminidase inhibition:** C3G inhibits neuraminidase enzyme (viral N protein) → prevents budding of newly formed virions from infected cells → same target as oseltamivir (Tamiflu), different binding site
- **Two independent antiviral mechanisms** operating simultaneously

### 4.3 Sambucus Lectins (Elder Lectins)
**SNA-I** (Sambucus nigra agglutinin I; Sambucin), **SNA-II, SNA-IV, SNLRP**: carbohydrate-binding proteins (lectins) with high affinity for sialic acid residues:
- SNA-I: specific for α-2,6-sialic acid (predominant in human upper respiratory tract — the primary influenza entry receptor)
- Mechanism: competitive binding to sialic acid residues on respiratory epithelium → **directly blocks influenza attachment** independently of anthocyanins
- SNA lectins have been extensively studied for influenza prevention; SNA-I standardized extracts are a potential influenza prophylactic mechanism

### 4.4 Flavonoids
**Quercetin, rutin, kaempferol, isoquercitrin**: anti-inflammatory (NF-κB, COX-1/2); antioxidant; quercetin adds antihistamine (see I_170).
**Chlorogenic acid**: antiviral; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory.

### 4.5 Elderflower-Specific Compounds
**Flavonoids (nicotiflorin, rutin, quercetin-3-glucoside)**: anti-inflammatory; immunomodulatory.
**Triterpenoids (α-amyrin, β-amyrin, ursolic acid)**: anti-inflammatory.
**Essential oil** (trace; 0.03%): *cis*-linalool oxide, hotrienol, nerol — aromatic, possibly diaphoretic.
**Cyanogenic glycosides**: PRESENT in flowers but at lower concentration than unripe berries; large quantities of elderflower preparations → theoretical HCN exposure (historically acknowledged).

### 4.6 Viburnin (Isovaleric Acid Ester)
**Viburnin** in berries: purgative at high doses; contributes to GI effects of large-quantity raw berry consumption.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Antiviral — Hemagglutinin Binding (Anthocyanins + Lectins)
C3G, C3Sb, and SNA lectins bind to influenza hemagglutinin protein:
- Hemagglutinin is the viral surface protein that attaches to sialic acid residues on respiratory epithelial cells — the essential first step in influenza infection
- Blocking hemagglutinin = preventing viral attachment = preventing infection initiation
- This mechanism is prophylactic AND early-infection active (if exposure and treatment overlap)
- **Not dependent on specific viral strains** — hemagglutinin is conserved across influenza A and B subtypes, making this mechanism potentially active against variant strains

**Zakay-Rones 1995 mechanistic study:** Elderberry extract directly binds influenza virions in vitro; prevents virion attachment to human red blood cells (used as proxy for respiratory epithelial cell attachment); mechanism confirmed as hemagglutinin binding.

### 5.2 Antiviral — Neuraminidase Inhibition (Anthocyanins)
Neuraminidase (NA) cleaves sialic acid bonds between budding virions and the infected cell surface, allowing viral release and spread. C3G inhibits NA at sub-micromolar concentrations in vitro.
- Same mechanism as pharmaceutical oseltamivir (Tamiflu) and zanamivir (Relenza)
- Different binding site → potentially active against oseltamivir-resistant strains
- Reduces viral spread within the respiratory tract

### 5.3 Immunostimulation (Anthocyanins + Flavonoids)
Elderberry extract stimulates cytokine production from human monocytes in vitro:
- Increased IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, TNF-α production
- Activation of macrophages and natural killer (NK) cells
- This immune activation contributes to recovery from infection BUT has raised theoretical concern about cytokine storm amplification in severe infections (see safety section)

### 5.4 Diaphoresis (Elderflower — Traditional)
Mechanism not fully characterized but likely involves prostaglandin-mediated hypothalamic temperature-set-point modulation (analogous to aspirin's fever-reducing mechanism) combined with direct peripheral vasodilation → increased sweating → heat dissipation → fever reduction. Flavonoid anti-inflammatory contribution consistent.

### 5.5 Anti-inflammatory (Flavonoids + Anthocyanins)
NF-κB inhibition; COX-1/2 inhibition; reduction of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 in chronic (non-acute) inflammatory conditions. This anti-inflammatory activity is DISTINCT from the acute immune stimulation during active infection — a dose-timing dependency.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### 6.1 Influenza Treatment (Pivotal Trials)
**Zakay-Rones et al. 2004 (J Int Med Res):** N=60; placebo-controlled RCT; Sambucol (standardized elderberry extract) vs. placebo for confirmed influenza (serology + culture):
- **Elderberry group: average recovery 3.1 days**
- **Placebo group: average recovery 7.1 days**
- P<0.001; clinically highly significant (4-day difference)
- Symptom severity scores significantly reduced in elderberry group
- **This remains the most significant elderberry antiviral RCT in the literature.**

**Zakay-Rones et al. 2001 (J Altern Complement Med):** N=60; influenza B outbreak; elderberry syrup vs. placebo; elderberry group recovered on average 4 days earlier.

### 6.2 Upper Respiratory Illness (Meta-analysis)
**Hawkins et al. 2019 (Complement Med Res) systematic review:** 4 RCTs included; elderberry significantly reduced upper respiratory symptom duration (−2.0 days, 95% CI −2.8 to −1.2) and severity. Conclusion: elderberry supplementation substantively reduces upper respiratory symptoms.

### 6.3 Air Travel + Cold Prevention
**Tiralongo et al. 2016 (Nutrients) RCT:** N=312 air travellers; elderberry extract × 10 days before + during travel vs. placebo; 45% fewer cold episodes in elderberry group (P=0.02); duration of cold episodes in elderberry group was shorter (−2 days). Traveller population = immune stress model; elderberry showed prophylactic + treatment benefit.

### 6.4 Elderflower — Fever/Cold (Traditional Evidence)
No formal RCTs for elderflower specifically in fever management (traditional use recognized as adequate by EMA). Commission E monograph exists for elderflower for common cold. The diaphoretic/fever-breaking application is consistent with traditional European use across 2,000 years without formal RCT evidence — supported by mechanism plausibility.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Hyldemoer — The Elder Mother (Danish/Germanic):** In Norse and Danish folk tradition, an elder tree could only be harvested with the permission of the Hyldemoer (Elder Mother), the spirit inhabiting the tree. To cut elder wood without asking permission → illness and death of family members would follow. The practical pharmacological encoding: raw elder leaves, bark, and unripe berries ARE toxic (sambunigrin). The folkloric "spirit permission" = "don't use the parts you haven't learned to prepare correctly." The same protective narrative appears in slightly different form across British (never burn elder wood in the hearth — invites the devil), Slavic, and German traditions. The tree spirit is a pharmacological safety warning.

**Hippocrates' "Medicine Chest":** The attribution of "medicine chest" to elder by Hippocrates is widely cited in herbal literature (e.g., "Hippocrates called elderberry his medicine chest") but the primary Hippocratic source text is not definitively identified — the phrase may be apocryphal or paraphrased from secondary sources. Whether or not the exact quotation is authentic, the function is accurate: virtually every part of elder has been used medicinally across European traditions — flowers (fever), berries (antiviral), bark (purgative/emetic), root (diuretic), leaves (topical anti-inflammatory), and wood (none — toxic to burn).

**Hildegard of Bingen (12th CE):** The abbess Hildegard's *Physica* (c. 1150–1158 CE) documents elder extensively: elderflower for fever; berries for joint inflammation; bark decoction for dropsy (edema). Her documentation is among the most complete medieval European pharmacological records for a single plant. The *Physica* is notable for including dosing considerations and contraindications in its elder entry — pharmacological sophistication unusual for 12th-century herbalism.

**Elderflower Cordial — British Summer in a Bottle:** The British tradition of elderflower cordial (sugar + water + elderflower infusion) is a rare example of a traditional diaphoretic/anti-inflammatory medicine converted into a culturally iconic drink (Belvoir, St-Germain liqueur). The medicinal compounds (flavonoids, ursolic acid) survive the cordial-making process; the tradition encodes a seasonal medicine (elderflower = June harvest = summer fever season) as a summer beverage. The pharmacological rationale is intact: elderflower cordial provides anti-inflammatory flavonoids and mild diaphoretic activity in a form consumed voluntarily without medical framing.

**The Cytokine Storm Question (COVID-19 Era):** During the COVID-19 pandemic, concerns emerged online that elderberry's immunostimulant cytokine-inducing activity might worsen cytokine storm in severe SARS-CoV-2 infection. This theoretical concern was widely circulated and created consumer hesitancy. **Assessment:** The cytokine induction evidence is from in vitro cell culture at concentrations not achievable in human serum from oral consumption; clinical trials of elderberry in viral illness consistently show SHORTENED illness duration, not prolongation or worsening; no clinical case report or trial has documented elderberry-associated cytokine storm. The theoretical concern is pharmacologically plausible at in vitro concentrations but clinically undemonstrated. Current expert consensus: elderberry at standard doses for mild-moderate viral URI is unlikely to worsen cytokine storm; in severe COVID requiring ICU care, caution is appropriate pending evidence. This became the first major phytomedicine controversy of the pandemic era.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Dosing (Evidence-Based)
| Application | Form | Dose | Duration |
|-------------|------|------|----------|
| Influenza treatment | Elderberry extract (Sambucol-standardized) | 15 mL syrup × 4/day | 5 days (Zakay-Rones trial) |
| Cold treatment | Standardized extract | 300–600mg 3× daily | 5–7 days |
| Prophylaxis (travel, exposure) | Elderberry syrup or capsule | 15 mL/day or 300mg/day | Duration of exposure period |
| Fever (diaphoretic) | Elderflower tea | 2 tsp dried flowers/250mL × 2–3/day | Acute illness duration |

### 8.2 Product Quality Criteria
- **Ripe berries only** from *S. nigra* (not *S. ebulus*, not *S. racemosa*)
- **Heat-processed** (syrup, cooked extract) — destroys β-glucosidase; reduces cyanogenic glycoside activity
- **Standardization:** Anthocyanin content (mg C3G equivalent) is the most meaningful quality marker; products without anthocyanin standardization have unpredictable potency
- **Commercial syrups:** Sambucol (original research product) is most well-validated; homemade syrups can be safe if properly made from ripe berries + cooking step

### 8.3 Safety

**NEVER consume RAW unripe elderberries:** Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea within 30–120 minutes. GI toxicity from sambunigrin. Even ripe raw berries in large quantities can cause GI distress. **Always cook or use commercial preparations.**

**Never use *Sambucus ebulus* (dwarf elder):** More toxic than *S. nigra*; often confused in wild-harvest settings.

**Pregnancy:** Limited safety data; sambunigrin theoretical concern; avoid raw/unprocessed; commercial standardized syrups in standard doses not associated with harm but precautionary avoidance advised especially first trimester.

**Autoimmune conditions:** Immunostimulant activity (though mild at clinical doses) — theoretical caution in lupus, MS, rheumatoid arthritis; no clinical evidence of harm but mechanism-based caution appropriate.

**Allergy:** Sambucus allergy (rare) — usually manifest as Rosaceae-adjacent cross-reactivity.

### 8.4 Elderflower vs. Elderberry — Clinical Decision
| Indication | Form |
|------------|------|
| Fever/diaphoresis | Elderflower (DMM/CM primary indication) |
| Influenza/URTIs | Elderberry syrup (anthocyanin-rich) |
| Sinus congestion/hay fever | Elderflower (anti-inflammatory, mild antihistamine via quercetin) |
| Prophylaxis (prevention) | Elderberry (antiviral hemagglutinin binding + immunostimulant) |
| Orthopaedic trauma/bruising | Elderberry topical extract (Chinese SBJ tradition — "bone-joining wood") |

### 8.5 Tradition-Practice Concordance
Elderberry is the Codex's foremost antiviral plant with RCT-level clinical validation. The cross-traditional diaphoretic/fever-breaking application (elderflower) and the antiviral/immunostimulant application (elderberry) are both pharmacologically supported — by flavonoid anti-inflammatory (flowers) and anthocyanin hemagglutinin binding + neuraminidase inhibition (berries). The SBJ "bone-joining wood" application maps to the anti-inflammatory anthocyanin mechanism (reduces tissue inflammation at fracture sites). The cyanogenic glycoside toxicity of raw/unripe berries is encoded in multiple traditions as mandatory cooking instruction — the Elder Mother myth, the Hippocratic part-specific differentiation, and modern product standardization all converge on the same practical message: cook the berries before eating, never eat the other parts raw.
