# I-295: Achillea millefolium (Yarrow)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-295 | Asteraceae (Compositae)**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Achillea millefolium* L.
**Family:** Asteraceae

**Common Names:**
- English: Yarrow, Common Yarrow, Milfoil, Nosebleed Plant, Soldier's Woundwort, Knight's Milfoil, Herba militaris
- German: *Schafgarbe* ("sheep's yarrow" — sheep graze it preferentially), *Tausendblätterkraut* (thousand-leaf herb)
- French: *Achillée millefeuille*
- Arabic: *Uqhuwān al-barrī* (أقحوان البري) — "wild chrysanthemum"
- Chinese: 蓍草 (*Shī cǎo*) / 千叶蓍 (*Qiānyè shī*) — "oracle grass" (used in I Ching divination)
- Hebrew: *Shahar*; Navajo: *Ha'yooko'oh*

**Etymology:**
*Achillea* honors Achilles — the Greek hero who, according to Homeric legend, used yarrow to staunch the wounds of his soldiers during the Trojan War. This mythological attribution is pharmacologically accurate: yarrow does have measurable hemostatic (blood-stopping) activity. *millefolium* = "thousand leaves" (from Latin *mille* = thousand + *folium* = leaf) — describing the feathery, highly divided leaves that appear to consist of thousands of tiny leaflets. The English "yarrow" derives from Old English *gearwe* → Germanic root *garwo* → modern English.

**The I Ching Connection:**
Yarrow stalks (*Shī cǎo*, 蓍草) are the traditional material for I Ching (易經) divination — 50 yarrow stalks are cast and counted in an elaborate ritual to derive hexagrams. This is not merely symbolic: Chinese metaphysical tradition considered yarrow a plant with oracular properties, capable of revealing pattern in chaos. The 49 stalks used in the casting procedure (one set aside) produce the 64 hexagrams. This divination use spans 3,000+ years of Chinese tradition, making yarrow one of the few medicinal plants also central to a major philosophical-divinatory practice.

**Morphology:**
Perennial; 20–90 cm; highly distinctive feathery, finely divided leaves (the mille-folium — hundreds of tiny leaflets from midrib); flat-topped white (or pink/red cultivar) flower clusters (corymb); distinctive sweet, bitter, slightly medicinal aroma; extremely common throughout temperate regions.

**Parts Used:** Aerial parts (flowers + leaves) — dried; fresh juice; EO; root (minor traditional use).

**The Wound-Healing Warrior Herb:**
The Achilles attribution is not accidental mythology — it reflects genuine empirical observation across every civilization that experienced combat: yarrow reliably stops bleeding. The common name "soldier's woundwort" appears independently in multiple cultures and periods; *Herba militaris* (soldier's herb) was its Latin designation in medieval medicine. The hemostatic property is the most universally recognized and most pharmacologically validated.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domains:**

1. **Hemostatic / wound healing** — DMM, CM, SBJ, FMT, EP, AT — universal across all confirming traditions
2. **Anti-inflammatory (topical + internal)** — DMM, CM, FMT; flavonoid mechanism
3. **Diaphoretic / febrifuge** — DMM, CM, FMT; bitter volatile mechanism
4. **Bitter digestive tonic / carminative** — DMM, CM, SBJ; sesquiterpene lactone bitters
5. **Emmenagogue / menstrual regulator** — DMM, CM, European folk
6. **Urinary anti-inflammatory / diuretic** — European folk; German Commission E approved
7. **Anti-spasmodic (GI)** — European; azulene + flavonoid mechanism

**The Chamazulene Bridge:**
Yarrow shares **chamazulene** with chamomile (*Matricaria chamomilla*, I-296) — a blue-colored sesquiterpene produced upon steam distillation that has significant anti-inflammatory activity. This creates a pharmacological link between two very different-looking Asteraceae plants: both yarrow and chamomile produce blue-to-dark-blue EOs with chamazulene + α-bisabolol as primary anti-inflammatory constituents. Yarrow's EO is used for anti-inflammatory preparations in much the same way as chamomile EO.

**Hemostasis Mechanism Complexity:**
Yarrow's hemostatic activity is pharmacologically complex — multiple compounds contribute through different pathways:
- Achillicin → platelet activation
- Flavonoids → vasoconstriction (minor)
- Tannins → protein precipitation on wound surface → physical sealing
- Alkamides → COX modulation
This multi-mechanism hemostasis explains why yarrow has been reliably empirically observed to stop bleeding — it is not a single-compound effect but a convergent hemostatic activity from multiple compound classes simultaneously.

**Energetic Classification:**
- ◉ Qi (functional): Primary — hemostatic Qi; wound healing; GI bitter tonic
- ◈ Jing (structural): Secondary — wound healing structural repair; hemostatic constitutional

**Primary Tier: ◉ Qi**

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Essential Oil (0.2–1.4%)

**1. Camphor (10–20%)** — antimicrobial; stimulant; mild analgesic
**2. β-Pinene (10–15%)** — anti-inflammatory; antimicrobial
**3. Chamazulene (1–35% — variable; formed during steam distillation from matricine precursor)**
- Blue-colored; anti-inflammatory (COX-2 inhibition, cytokine inhibition)
- Higher chamazulene yarrow: better anti-inflammatory effect; blue-oil yarrow varieties preferred
- Shared with chamomile (I-296)

**4. α-Bisabolol (minor)** — anti-inflammatory; wound healing; same as chamomile

### Sesquiterpene Lactones

**5. Achillin** — bitter; anti-inflammatory (NF-κB inhibition); anti-tumor (in vitro)
**6. Achillicin** — hemostatic (platelet activation); bitter

### Flavonoids

**7. Apigenin** — anti-inflammatory; GABA-A PAM (same as chamomile and parsley); anti-spasmodic
**8. Luteolin** — anti-inflammatory; Ca²⁺ channel modulation; anti-spasmodic
**9. Rutin (Quercetin-3-rutinoside)** — capillary-protective; anti-inflammatory; anti-platelet (contradicts hemostatic role — complex dose-dependent balance)

### Alkaloids

**10. Achilleine** — quaternary alkaloid; demonstrated hemostatic activity in animal studies; one of the few alkaloids in Asteraceae
**11. Trigonelline** — alkaloid; minor

### Tannins

**12. Proanthocyanidins** — hemostatic (protein precipitation); anti-inflammatory; antimicrobial

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### Hemostatic Mechanism (Wound Application)
- **Achilleine:** Platelet aggregation activation → platelet plug formation at wound site
- **Tannins:** Protein precipitation on wound surface → physical coagulum → sealing
- **Rutin:** Capillary wall strengthening → reduced bleeding tendency (paradox: also anti-platelet; dose-dependent)
- **Vasoconstrictive flavonoids:** Minor local vasoconstriction
- **Net effect:** Reliable hemostasis when fresh or dried yarrow applied directly to wound; also internal hemostatic for minor bleeding (menorrhagia, epistaxis)

### Anti-Inflammatory
- **Chamazulene + α-bisabolol:** COX-2 inhibition → reduced PGE2 → anti-inflammatory; same mechanism as chamomile
- **Achillin:** NF-κB pathway inhibition → IL-6, TNF-α reduction
- **Apigenin + luteolin:** COX-1/2 inhibition; also direct anti-inflammatory via JAK/STAT

### Diaphoretic (Fever Management)
- Volatile oil → stimulates sweat glands → diaphoresis → evaporative cooling → fever reduction
- This mechanism is one of the oldest fever-management strategies — "sweat the fever out" — which has genuine physiological basis for moderate fever reduction

### Anti-Spasmodic
- Apigenin: GABA-A PAM → smooth muscle relaxation → GI anti-spasmodic; uterine anti-spasmodic

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Wound Healing / Hemostatic:**
- Traditional use universally consistent; Commission E approved for topical wound use
- In vitro hemostatic mechanisms confirmed; achilleine hemostatic activity in animal models
- **Evidence Grade: Level C** (traditional use; Commission E; mechanism-supported); **Level D** (in vitro compound mechanisms)

**Urinary Tract Inflammation:**
- German Commission E: yarrow approved for appetite loss and dyspeptic complaints; traditionally used for urinary tract inflammation
- **Evidence Grade: Level C**

**Menorrhagia / Menstrual Regulation:**
- Traditional hemostatic-emmenagogue duality (same as mugwort): hemostatic at low doses; emmenagogue at higher doses
- No RCTs
- **Evidence Grade: Level C**

**Anti-Inflammatory (Systemic):**
- Chamazulene/bisabolol: in vitro strong; human clinical data for yarrow alone lacking
- **Evidence Grade: Level D** (mechanism); **Level C** (traditional)

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The Most Ubiquitous Wound Herb:**
Yarrow's distinctive feathery leaves and flat white flower clusters are among the most recognizable in the temperate world — and have been recognizable to herbalists, soldiers, farmers, and healers for millennia. The combination of universal availability (yarrow grows everywhere in temperate regions, including disturbed ground, roadsides, fields, and gardens) and reliable hemostatic efficacy made it the universal first-aid herb. Before antiseptics, before sutures, before emergency medicine — fresh yarrow crushed and applied to a bleeding wound was standard practice across virtually every temperate culture.

**The Achilles Story as Clinical Record:**
The Trojan War mythos (if containing historical core material, ~1200 BCE) would place the earliest "clinical record" of yarrow hemostasis in the 12th century BCE — predating the Ebers Papyrus and most written records. Whether Achilles used yarrow as mythology claims or not, the story reflects that by the time the Iliad was composed (8th century BCE), yarrow's hemostatic reputation was already ancient enough to be attributed to a legendary hero. This oral tradition preserved clinical knowledge across millennia.

**I Ching Divination and Pattern:**
The use of yarrow stalks in I Ching divination is not pharmacological but is philosophically significant — it suggests that Chinese tradition perceived yarrow as a plant that reveals hidden order, pattern, and connection in apparent chaos. The medicinal uses (hemostatic, anti-inflammatory, bitter tonic, menstrual regulator) position yarrow as a "balancing" herb — it stops excess (bleeding, inflammation, fever), moves stagnation (bitter digestive, emmenagogue), and was believed to reveal balance in fortune. Whether this is the basis of the divination use or a post-hoc attribution, the philosophy is consistent.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Fresh Plant Poultice (Topical — Primary for Hemostasis):**
- Fresh yarrow leaves/flowers: crush and apply directly to bleeding wound; hold firm pressure
- Traditional "field medicine" for minor cuts, abrasions; effective and immediately available in temperate outdoors
- Replace fresh material every 30 minutes for continued hemostatic effect

**Infusion (Internal — Digestive, Fever, Menstrual):**
- 1–2 teaspoons dried aerial parts per 250 mL boiling water; steep covered 10 minutes; strain
- 1–3 cups/day
- Fever/diaphoretic: drink hot, cover with blankets; promotes sweating

**Tincture:**
- 1:5 in 40–50% ethanol; 2–4 mL 3× daily
- Better anti-inflammatory extraction than tea; standard preparation

**Yarrow EO:**
- Blue-colored (chamazulene-rich) varieties preferred for anti-inflammatory topical use
- Dilute 1–2% in carrier oil; topical anti-inflammatory; wound healing

### Safety Profile

**Generally safe.** Excellent traditional safety record.

**Asteraceae Allergy:**
- Sesquiterpene lactone cross-reactivity (same Asteraceae sensitizer class as elecampane, mugwort)
- Yarrow pollen allergy: common; part of Asteraceae hypersensitivity spectrum
- **Mugwort-Yarrow cross-reactivity** documented — patients allergic to mugwort (MCBS) may react to yarrow

**Drug Interactions:**
- **Anticoagulants (Warfarin):** Paradoxical complexity — rutin anti-platelet + achilleine pro-platelet; net effect at medicinal doses probably pro-hemostatic; monitor with anticoagulants
- **Antihypertensive drugs:** Minor vasoconstrictive potential; unlikely clinically significant

**Contraindications:**
- Pregnancy: emmenagogue + uterotonic at higher doses; AVOID medicinal doses (fresh herb garnish safe)
- Asteraceae allergy
- Not for serious wounds (professional medical care required; yarrow is first-aid only)
