### Botanical Identity
Evergreen shrub/small tree 2–4m; small pinnate leaves; inconspicuous red-black berries; native to Mediterranean basin, Canary Islands, and North Africa. The plant produces a crystalline clear-to-pale-yellow resin (mastic) by spontaneous exudation from cuts in the bark — the resin hardens on contact with air. Commercially produced primarily on Chios island (Greece) where a specific ecotype produces high-quality resin; Chios mastic has Protected Designation of Origin (PDO) status and is harvested by the same methods used for 2,500+ years. The resin contains an unusually high proportion of biologically active terpenoids.

**Pistacia lentiscus var. *chia*** (Chios variety): Produces significantly more resin than mainland populations; the Chios monopoly on quality mastic production has been continuous since ancient Greece.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Dioscorides / Greco-Roman (DMM)
Book I, Chapter 90: "Mastic is produced in Chios more than anywhere else." Applications: for stomach complaints (gastritis, dyspepsia, peptic ulcers); for chest conditions and coughs (expectorant); as a tooth-strengthening chewing gum; wound healing; in compound perfume preparations. Dioscorides is the primary classical source for mastic's medical applications.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine / Ibn Sīnā (CM)
*Maṣṭakā* — warming in the 1st degree, drying. Primary applications: gastric (the central application in Ibn Sīnā's system — "strengthens the stomach, relieves vomiting, and stops diarrhoea"); for liver and spleen; as an expectorant for chest complaints; tooth strengthener; for urinary tract. Ibn Sīnā's gastric application is the most developed in the pre-modern corpus and maps precisely to the modern *H. pylori* eradication research.

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (EP)
Mastic-type resins appear in EP compound formulas for GI conditions and as an aromatic in medicinal preparations. The identification with *Pistacia lentiscus* specifically is partially uncertain for the Egyptian records (other Pistacia species grow in the Levant), but resin preparations consistent with mastic appear.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)
Resin preparations identified as mastic or closely related Pistacia resins appear in AT texts for abdominal complaints and dental applications — consistent with the convergent GI and dental use recorded in DMM and CM.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Anti-*Helicobacter pylori* (Primary — Triterpenoid Acids)

**Isomasticadienolic acid + masticadienonic acid**:
- Direct bactericidal against *H. pylori* at concentrations achievable in the gastric lumen
- MIC values for *H. pylori*: 0.5–4 μg/mL — clinically relevant concentrations
- Mechanism: disruption of bacterial membrane integrity; inhibition of *H. pylori* vacuolating cytotoxin (VacA); urease inhibition (reduces the ammonia-producing enzyme that enables *H. pylori* survival in acidic pH)
- Active against clarithromycin-resistant *H. pylori* strains — potential role in antibiotic-resistant cases

This mechanism is the pharmacological explanation for the 2,000-year convergent empirical observation that mastic treats peptic/gastric conditions. The ancient physicians observed ulcer healing; the mechanism is *H. pylori* eradication.

### 5.2 Gastroprotective (Gastric Mucosal Protection)

**Oleanolic acid + masticadienonic acid + resin polymer**:
- Promotes gastric mucus secretion → protects gastric mucosal barrier
- Reduces PGE2-mediated inflammation in gastric mucosa
- The physical resin coats the mucosal surface → mechanical protective film
- Prostaglandin pathway modulation → cytoprotective prostaglandin synthesis (PGE2 in mucosa is gastroprotective at physiological levels)

### 5.3 Hepatoprotective

**Oleanolic acid + ursolic acid**:
- NF-κB inhibition in hepatocytes → reduced inflammatory hepatocyte damage
- Antioxidant protection of hepatic tissue
- Lipid-lowering properties (oleanolic acid reduces hepatic cholesterol biosynthesis)
- The liver application in Ibn Sīnā's CM has mechanistic support

### 5.4 Antimicrobial (Dental / Oral)

**Isomasticadienolic acid + α-terpineol + linalool**:
- Bactericidal against *Streptococcus mutans* (primary tooth decay pathogen)
- Active against *Porphyromonas gingivalis* (periodontal disease pathogen)
- Biofilm disruption: α-terpineol inhibits S. mutans biofilm formation
- The chewing gum application in DMM and CM for "strengthening teeth" maps to oral antimicrobial action

### 5.5 Expectorant / Anti-inflammatory (Bronchial)

**α-Pinene + β-myrcene + ursolic acid**:
- α-Pinene: bronchodilator (clinical data); anti-inflammatory; mucolytic
- Ursolic acid: COX-2 inhibition → reduced bronchial inflammation
- The chest/cough application in DMM and CM is pharmacologically grounded

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Peptic ulcer / H. pylori**: Huwez et al. (1998, *NEJM* letters, n=38): mastic gum 1g/day for 2 weeks eradicated *H. pylori* in 7/10 patients vs 0/10 placebo. Bebb et al. (2003, *Gut*): failed to replicate eradication in well-controlled RCT (different *H. pylori* strain virulence?). Marone et al. (2016, *J Ethnopharmacol*, n=52): mastic gum 350mg three times daily for 3 months — significant improvement in dyspepsia scores vs placebo.

**Crohn's disease**: Kaliora et al. (2007, *World J Gastroenterol*): mastic powder 2.2g/day for 4 weeks — significant reduction in Harvey-Bradshaw index and inflammatory markers (IL-6, CRP) vs placebo. The anti-inflammatory mechanism in IBD is well-supported.

**Oral health**: Multiple in vitro and small clinical studies confirming anti-biofilm activity against dental pathogens; chewing mastic gum reduces plaque index.

**Lipid lowering**: Triantafyllou et al. (2014, pilot RCT, n=26): mastic gum supplementation reduced total cholesterol and LDL significantly vs placebo over 8 weeks.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The island of tears**: The cultivated area of Chios where mastic trees are grown (*mastichochoria* — mastic villages) is one of the oldest continuously operating pharmaceutical production zones in the world. The trees are scored with iron tools to bleed the resin in July and August; the resin falls to a cleaned ground surface and is harvested as "tears" (*δάκρια*) — pale crystalline drops. The name "tears of Chios" is both poetic and literal — the resin is the tree's wound-response product. Every building of this Codex's gastric/anti-ulcer knowledge ultimately traces to a small island in the Aegean where trees have been scored in the same way for 2,500 years.

**The word we use every time we eat**: "Masticate" — to chew — derives from Greek *mastíchē*, which derived from the verb *mastichan* (to grind the teeth), and the name was given because mastic gum was the original chewing gum. Every use of the English word "masticate" carries embedded within it a 2,500-year-old pharmaceutical reference to *Pistacia lentiscus* and its use as a dental/aromatic chew.

**The 1998 NEJM letter that launched modern mastic research**: The publication of Huwez et al.'s letter in the *New England Journal of Medicine* — "Mastic Gum Kills Helicobacter pylori" — was a landmark in ethnopharmacological validation. A substance used continuously for gastric disorders for 2,500 years (in DMM, CM, and folk tradition) was demonstrated to kill the pathogen now known to cause most peptic ulcers. The gap between Ibn Sīnā's 11th-century prescription for "*maṣṭakā* strengthens the stomach" and the 20th-century discovery that peptic ulcers are caused by *H. pylori* was bridged in one letter. The traditional observation was correct; the mechanism was just unknown for 2,400 years.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

| Form | Dose | Application |
|------|------|-------------|
| Mastic gum (raw resin tears) | 1–2g chewed 20 min before meals | Gastric / H. pylori; oral health; the traditional form |
| Mastic powder capsules | 500mg–1g 3× daily with meals | Peptic ulcer; dyspepsia; Crohn's (the RCT-studied form) |
| Mastic tincture (1:5, 80% ethanol) | 2–3ml 3× daily before meals | Gastric; hepatic; convenient liquid form |
| Mastic essential oil (topical, 2% in carrier) | Apply to chest or abdomen | Expectorant; aromatic; anti-inflammatory topical |
| Combined with DGL (deglycyrrhizinated licorice, I_36) | — | Synergistic gastroprotective formula |

**Efficacy note**: Chewing raw mastic tears (the traditional form) may provide better *H. pylori* eradication due to prolonged contact of the resin with the gastric mucosa than capsules, which may be partly digested before reaching the stomach.

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM (cross-reference)**: Bitter, acrid; slightly warm. Channels: Stomach (胃), Liver (肝), Lung (肺). Harmonises the Stomach, moves Qi stagnation, astringes the Stomach.

**Ayurvedic (cross-reference)**: Bitter, astringent; slightly cooling. Reduces Pitta ↓↓. *Hṛdaya* (strengthening), *Amaghna* (ama-destroying). Not a classical Ayurvedic plant but equivalent in role to Kutaja (*Holarrhena*) for GI conditions.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Pistacia/cashew allergy**: Anacardiaceae family — potential cross-reactivity with cashew, pistachio, mango; avoid if allergic
- **Peptic bleeding (active)**: Ulcer with active GI bleeding requires immediate medical management; mastic is not a replacement for emergency care
- **Pregnancy**: Insufficient safety data; traditional use suggests caution; avoid high doses
- **H. pylori eradication**: Clinical evidence supports mastic as adjunct therapy, not replacement for antibiotic regimens in confirmed *H. pylori* infection with documented ulcer; medical supervision recommended
