### Origin & Tradition

*Chenopodium album* — lamb's quarters, fat-hen, wild spinach, and in South Asia **bathua** — is one of the great cosmopolitan pot-herbs of the human diet, a nutrient-dense weed that follows agriculture across the temperate world. Archaeobotany finds its seeds among the earliest gathered and semi-cultivated plant foods of Eurasia, eaten long before spinach displaced it from the European table. For the Codex it is the archetypal **volunteer ally**: uncultivated, unbidden, springing up in disturbed and cultivated ground, and offering a free green of exceptional nutritional density to anyone who recognises it. Its medicine sits in two registers: the mild, cooling, nourishing pot-herb, and the sharper **anthelmintic** (worm-expelling) fraction concentrated in the seed and aromatic tissue of the *Chenopodium* genus.

**Greco-Roman (Dioscorides horizon):** The chenopod pot-herbs and oraches (*Atriplex*, *Blitum*, and their relatives) appear in the Greek dietary-medical corpus as cooling, moistening, laxative greens, eaten boiled to soften the belly and cool heat. *Chenopodium album* belongs to this cluster of "wild spinach" pot-herbs the classical writers grouped together for their mild, humid, loosening quality. The genus's later fame as a vermifuge (through the American *Chenopodium ambrosioides*, wormseed) extended a medicinal reputation that the European *C. album* shares in attenuated form through its aromatic constituents.

**Egyptian (Ebers horizon):** Leafy pot-herbs and gathered greens were dietary and medicinal staples along the Nile, where cooling green vegetables were used to soften the bowel and temper heat. *C. album* and its chenopod relatives belong to the general category of gathered greens in that food-medicine economy; specific pharaonic prescriptions should be attributed to the broad pot-herb category rather than invented.

**Unani (Avicenna & the Arabic physicians):** In the *Qanun* tradition the chenopod greens (*bathua/chandan* in Indo-Persian Unani usage) are cold and moist, cooling, laxative, and cleansing to the blood. Unani practice used bathua as a gentle aperient and blood-cooling green, and recognised chenopod seed preparations among the vermifuge remedies. The humoral logic frames it as a mild corrector of hot, thick, obstructed states.

**Chinese (materia medica tradition):** *Chenopodium album* (*li* / *bai-li*, "goosefoot") is recorded in the Chinese dietary-medical literature as a cool, sweet-slightly-bitter green that clears heat, resolves dampness, benefits the bowel and is applied topically for itching and skin complaints and taken for parasitic and digestive conditions. It is treated as both food and mild medicine.

**Ayurvedic (Sushruta & the dietetic tradition):** Bathua (*vastuka*) is a named leafy vegetable in the classical Ayurvedic dietary corpus, valued as cooling, digestive-stimulating (*deepana*), mildly laxative and *tridosha*-balancing when properly cooked, and specifically employed as an **anthelmintic** (*krimighna*) — a worm-expelling green — and for digestive and urinary complaints. Its inclusion among named *shaka* (pot-herbs) with therapeutic properties gives it a genuine, substantial place in Indian materia medica, and bathua remains a widely eaten seasonal green across North India today.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Uninvited Nourisher:** *Chenopodium album* is the Codex emblem of **grace in the weed** — abundance that arrives without cultivation, the free food of disturbed ground. Where cabbage and turnip embody the *disciplined* wild (form drawn out by human training), bathua embodies the *undisciplined* gift: it needs no sowing, no tending, and yet delivers dense nourishment to those with the eyes to see it. Symbolically it is the plant of **recognition** — the medicine hidden in plain sight, dismissed as a weed by the untrained and gathered as food-and-medicine by the initiated. Its second charge is *the scourer*: through its anthelmintic seed-oils it clears the hidden parasites of the gut, making it, in the training arc, a Purification ally that empties before it fills.

### Active Components

- **Ascaridole** and related volatile terpenoids — the aromatic, anthelmintic-associated fraction of the *Chenopodium* genus (most concentrated in seed/aerial oils).
- **Saponins** — triterpenoid saponins characteristic of the family.
- **Flavonoids & phenolics** — including kaempferol and quercetin derivatives; strong antioxidant fraction.
- **Bio-active carbohydrates / dietary fibre.**
- **Vitamins & minerals** — high vitamin C, provitamin-A carotenoids, calcium, iron, and folate — a genuinely nutrient-dense leaf.
- **Oxalates** — present at meaningful levels (the family's caution constituent).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Bathua/lamb's quarters is a **Foundation and Purification** green. In the Foundation register it is a nutrient-dense cooling pot-herb — iron, calcium, vitamins C and A — a building leaf for the depleted. In the Purification register it carries the genus's traditional **anthelmintic** and gentle laxative action, used to clear intestinal parasites and loosen the bowel. The Codex reads it as an *empty-then-nourish* substance: it scours the gut of hidden burdens (worms, stagnation, heat) while simultaneously restocking mineral reserves. It is a seasonal, food-tier ally rather than a concentrated drug.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the traditional profile is supported by a recent compositional review:

- Singh et al. (2023), *Molecules*, an update on the nutrition, phytochemistry and health effects of Bathua (*Chenopodium album*) as a non-conventional edible plant, documents its rich content of bioactive carbohydrates, flavonoids, phenolics, minerals and vitamins, and identifies **ascaridole** in the aerial parts as the phytoconstituent underpinning its **anthelmintic** property — while framing its anticancer, antidiabetic, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial and antioxidant activities as areas needing further pharmacological and safety research. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules28134902)

**Evidence grade:** Moderate-to-low. Strong evidence for nutritional density and phytochemical composition; the anthelmintic and other bioactivities rest on traditional use plus preclinical/compositional data, with the review itself calling for rigorous pharmacology and safety studies. Graded honestly as a well-characterised food with traditionally-attested, not yet clinically-confirmed, medicinal actions.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** Sweet-slightly bitter, cool; spleen, large intestine; clears heat, resolves dampness, benefits the bowel, applied for parasites and skin itching.
- **Ayurveda:** Cooling, *deepana* (digestive), mildly laxative, *krimighna* (anthelmintic), *tridosha*-balancing when cooked.
- **Galenic/Unani:** Cold and moist (1st degree); cooling, laxative, blood-cooling.

### Contraindications

- **Oxalates:** Contains meaningful oxalate — caution with a history of calcium-oxalate kidney stones; cooking reduces but does not eliminate it.
- **Anthelmintic/ascaridole fraction:** Concentrated seed/oil preparations are potentially toxic (ascaridole is a hazardous terpene at dose) — the culinary leaf is safe, the concentrated vermifuge preparation is not casual.
- **Pregnancy:** Avoid the concentrated anthelmintic preparations in pregnancy; ordinary culinary use of cooked greens is traditionally accepted `[USER: verify]`.
- Nitrate accumulation possible in leaves from heavily fertilised ground.
