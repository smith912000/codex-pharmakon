### Origin & Tradition

*Stellera chamaejasme* is a perennial of the Thymelaeaceae — the daphne family — that carpets the degraded grasslands and dry slopes of the Tibetan Plateau, Mongolia, northern China and the Central-Asian steppe. Above ground it is deceptively beautiful: a low cushion crowned in early summer with dense heads of pink-tipped white flowers, so that herders know it on sight. Below ground it carries a thick, woody, deeply toxic root, and it is the root that the medical and pastoral traditions both feared and used.

In Chinese medicine the drug **Lang Du** (狼毒, "wolf's poison") is a name applied historically to more than one acrid, toxic root, and *Stellera chamaejasme* is one of the principal botanical sources recognised under that heading, sometimes distinguished as **Rui Xiang Lang Du** (瑞香狼毒) to mark its Thymelaeaceae (daphne) affinity. The "wolf's poison" name signals its classical category: a violently acrid, toxic, dispersing-and-purging agent reserved for stubborn accumulations, parasites and skin lesions, never a gentle everyday herb. It belongs to the small set of frankly poisonous roots used in minute, processed quantities under expert supervision.

Among the Tibetan and Mongolian pastoral peoples the plant has a double identity. As medicine its root was applied externally to skin disease, scabies and parasitic complaints, and used cautiously for internal accumulations. As an agricultural fact it is an **indicator of overgrazed, degraded pasture** — a plant that spreads as the grassland collapses, that livestock avoid because it is toxic, and whose roots have been used as a natural insecticide, fish poison and even as a fibre and papermaking source on the plateau. Its expansion across damaged rangeland made it both a symptom of and, locally, a poison within the pastoral economy.

The modern interest in *Stellera* is pharmacological. Its roots are rich in toxic diterpenoid esters of the daphnane/tigliane type — the same broad chemical family that gives other Thymelaeaceae and the Euphorbiaceae their caustic, irritant and tumour-relevant activity — together with bioactive flavonoids. This has made the plant a subject of contemporary pesticidal and anti-tumour research, even as its toxicity keeps it firmly in the specialist category. In the Codex it stands as a representative of the toxic Thymelaeaceae roots: caustic diterpene chemistry, traditional use confined to expert hands, and a living link between rangeland ecology and pharmacology.

### Contraindications

- **Raw/unprocessed root — absolute prohibition.** Toxic and caustic; never to be ingested in any unsupervised context.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding — absolute.** Toxic, dispersing/purging; traditionally contraindicated for toxic acrid roots of this class.
- **Children, the frail, the depleted — avoid.** A draining, toxic drug; inappropriate for deficiency states.
- **Skin and eye contact.** Diterpenoid esters are potent irritants; protect skin, eyes and mucous membranes when handling the raw root.
- **Livestock.** Toxic to grazing animals; a recognised poisonous plant of degraded pasture.
