# Meadow Buttercup — Tall Buttercup, Common Buttercup, Giant Buttercup, Crowfoot (Latin *ranunculus*, "little frog")

## *Ranunculus acris* L. | Family: Ranunculaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM
**Tags (previous line, retained for reference):** AT, DMM, BM (Avicennian/Unani)
**Energetic Classification:** Hot and dry in the extreme (Galenic) — a caustic, acrid rubefacient of the fourth (burning) degree

### ⚠️ SAFETY BANNER

**This plant is a topical, external-use-only historical remedy. It is a potent skin-blistering vesicant and is toxic if ingested. It is documented in this Codex for completeness of the tradition, NOT as a recommended preparation. See Contraindications before any use.**

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian (AT).** The Near-Eastern tradition of **counterirritation** — deliberately raising a controlled blister or reddening over a painful joint or region to "draw out" the disease — is ancient and cross-cultural. Acrid, blistering plants belong to this class of external rubefacients applied over joint and muscle pain. *Ranunculus* is attributed here by therapeutic category (caustic counterirritants), graded *traditional*.

**Dioscorides / Graeco-Roman (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* explicitly describes the *batrachion* (buttercup / crowfoot) group as **acrid, blistering plants**: applied externally they raise blisters and were used to burn away warts, corns, and hardened growths, and as a caustic counterirritant. Dioscorides already warns of their sharp, ulcerating quality — the toxicity was understood from antiquity, and the plant was firmly an *external* caustic, never an internal medicine.

**Avicennian / Unani (BM).** In the Galenic-Persian synthesis, buttercup falls among the intensely hot, dry, "burning" simples used as escharotics and rubefacients — agents to redden or blister the skin over rheumatic pain, applied briefly and locally, with clear recognition of their corrosive danger.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The buttercup's traditional identity is inseparable from its chemistry. Unlike the doctrine-of-signatures herbs read by appearance, the buttercup earned its place by a directly felt, unmistakable property: **crush the fresh plant against skin and it burns and blisters.** The tradition harnessed exactly this — the acrid principle as a deliberate counterirritant to draw blood to a painful area and provoke a "revulsion" reaction believed to relieve deeper joint pain. The symbolism (drawing out, burning away, revulsion) maps precisely onto the real pharmacology of protoanemonin, a genuine vesicant. Crucially, the tradition also encoded the safety limit: fresh and brief, external only, and dried/aged material rendered inert.

### Active Components

- **Ranunculin** — the stable glycoside present in the intact living plant; itself relatively harmless.
- **Protoanemonin** — the true toxic principle. On crushing or wounding the plant, ranunculin is enzymatically hydrolysed to **protoanemonin**, an unstable, volatile, acrid lactone that is a potent **vesicant / blistering agent** and mucous-membrane irritant. This is the chemistry behind every traditional (and every accidental) buttercup burn.
- **Anemonin** — protoanemonin spontaneously dimerises to anemonin, which is far less irritant; on further degradation the toxicity is largely lost. This is why **dried, aged, or thoroughly processed plant material becomes non-blistering and non-toxic**.
- Minor flavonoids and other phenolics of no established therapeutic role here.

The whole safety logic of the plant rests on this pathway: **ranunculin → (crushing) → protoanemonin (toxic, blistering) → (aging/dimerisation) → anemonin → inert.**

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**None internal.** Kenetica does **not** admit *Ranunculus acris* as an ingestible or systemic remedy under any preparation. Its only historical role is as an **external counterirritant / rubefacient** — a brief, localised topical application of the fresh plant to redden (not ulcerate) skin over rheumatic or joint pain, on the pre-modern theory of revulsion. In a modern practice this use is **superseded and not recommended**: the therapeutic margin between reddening and chemical burn is small and unreliable, and safer counterirritants (e.g. capsaicin, methyl salicylate) exist. It is retained here as a documented historical entry, flagged hazard.

| Preparation | Dose | Notes |

| Dried plant | Rendered largely inert (protoanemonin lost) | Historically the "safe" form — but efficacy also lost |
| Internal use | **CONTRAINDICATED — NEVER** | Toxic: severe oral, GI and mucosal injury |

No safe standardised dose exists. There is **no recommended internal dose. Do not ingest.**

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the chemistry and hazard are well documented; there is **no credible evidence for internal therapeutic use**, and the profile is one of toxicity, graded accordingly.

- Analytical work across four *Ranunculus* species quantified **anemonin** content and confirmed the toxic pathway via **ranunculin and its degradation products protoanemonin and anemonin**, noting these compounds define the genus's toxicity and that milder extract forms are "more safely for human administration." Neag et al., *Pak. J. Pharm. Sci.* 2018 (PubMed PMID 30393208; no DOI registered — cite by PMID).
- A clinical case series reported **three chemical skin burns** from topical folk use of a *Ranunculus* species (*R. arvensis*) applied for knee pain — one severe enough to require surgical flap repair — and identified **protoanemonin** as the causative vesicant, noting it may be more heat-resistant than previously assumed (burns occurred even after boiling). Koçak et al., *Ann. Saudi Med.* 2016 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.5144/0256-4947.2016.89)).
- Phytochemical profiling of *Ranunculus acris* specifically documented pronounced primary and specialised metabolite variation (surface sugars, volatiles, metabolic fingerprints) across individuals and organs — confirming a chemically variable plant, which further undermines any notion of a predictable, safe dose. Gaube et al., *Environ. Microbiol.* 2023 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/1462-2920.16382)).

The literature converges on a single practical message: this is a **toxic, blistering plant** whose only real-world clinical footprint is chemical burns, not cures.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani (as attested):** hot and dry in the fourth (burning) degree — caustic, escharotic, acrid; a counterirritant, never a nourishing or moistening simple.
- **TCM / Ayurveda:** *Ranunculus acris* is a European/temperate meadow plant outside the classical Chinese and Ayurvedic materia medica; no authentic TCM channel-entry or Ayurvedic *rasa/guna/virya/vipaka* attribution exists. Any acrid-hot cross-mapping would be analogical only and is not asserted as traditional.

### Contraindications

⚠️⚠️ **PROTOANEMONIN VESICANT HAZARD — READ IN FULL.**

- **NEVER ingest.** Internal consumption of fresh *Ranunculus acris* causes severe irritation and inflammation of the mouth, throat, and gastrointestinal tract: **oral and mucosal ulceration, intense burning, salivation, vomiting, colicky abdominal pain, and bloody diarrhoea.** Larger ingestions can cause systemic toxicity. There is no internal therapeutic use — none.
- **Skin contact blisters and burns.** Crushing the fresh plant releases **protoanemonin**, which causes contact dermatitis, redness, painful vesicles, and full chemical burns. Documented case reports include burns severe enough to require surgical repair. Reported/implied heat-resistance means boiling does **not** reliably detoxify the fresh plant.
- **External use only, and even then not recommended.** The historical rubefacient use carries a narrow, unpredictable margin between the intended reddening and an actual chemical burn; the plant's chemical variability makes dosing unreliable. Safer modern counterirritants should be used instead.
- **Eyes and mucous membranes:** severe irritant — avoid all contact; handle with gloves.
- **Livestock note:** the plant is a well-known cause of poisoning and oral/GI irritation in grazing animals when fresh; it becomes safe in dried hay as protoanemonin degrades to anemonin — the same detoxification pathway as above.
- **Absolute avoidance:** pregnancy, breastfeeding, children, and any internal route under all circumstances.

**Bottom line: external, historical, hazard-flagged. Never internal. Fresh plant is a chemical vesicant.**
