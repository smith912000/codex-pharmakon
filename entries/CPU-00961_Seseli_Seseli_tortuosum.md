# Tortuous Seseli / Hartwort — *seseli* (Gk. σέσελι), *tordylion* (late Gk. usage, contested), Ar. *sisālīyūs* / *saqmūniyā al-barr* [USER: verify Arabic transliteration], Fr. *séséli de Marseille*, It. *finocchiella*

## *Seseli tortuosum* L. | Family: Apiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM
**Energetic Classification:** Hot and dry in the second degree (Galenic reading of the aromatic umbellifer seed; *ḥārr yābis* in the Arabic reception)
**Training Phase:** Phase II — Digestive Consolidation (carminative / warming class); NOT a Phase III neuro-active agent

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Seseli tortuosum* is a biennial or short-lived perennial umbellifer of dry, stony, coastal and sub-Mediterranean ground, from Iberia and the Maghreb through Sicily, Sardinia, the Balkans and Anatolia. It is a slender, glaucous, much-branched plant — the epithet *tortuosum* records the crooked, zig-zag branching that distinguishes it in the field. It bears the small, ribbed, strongly aromatic mericarps ("seeds") that are the drug of the classical tradition. It grows in exactly the habitat that Mediterranean pastoral medicine drew on: sand-dune, cliff and roadside, gathered rather than cultivated.

A caution belongs at the head of this entry rather than buried at the foot. **This is one of the most poorly studied plants in the Codex.** The classical record is real and reasonably continuous; the modern pharmacological record consists of a handful of essential-oil and extract papers, most of them on Sicilian, Turkish, Algerian or Portuguese accessions, none of them clinical. There is no human trial of *Seseli tortuosum* for any indication. Everything below is graded accordingly, and where the traditional claim has no modern counterpart, it is left standing as tradition rather than dressed up as pharmacology.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (AT).** The cuneiform medical corpus contains a large aromatic-seed pharmacopoeia — umbelliferous seeds used in fumigations, poultices and potions for "wind" in the belly, for retained menses, and for the cold, griping abdomen. Several standard reference lists of Mesopotamian drug names include entries plausibly matched to Apiaceae seed drugs of the *seseli* / *silphion* / *ammi* complex, but **the specific identification of any Akkadian plant name with *Seseli tortuosum* is not secure, and no confident binomial equivalence should be asserted** [USER: verify against a current Assyriological materia medica — e.g. the CAD entries for aromatic seed drugs; do not cite a specific Akkadian name without a source]. What the Assyrian layer legitimately contributes here is *doctrinal*, not nominal: the Mesopotamian category of a hot, pungent seed that "drives out the wind" from the interior and "opens" a blocked womb is the direct ancestor of the Greek carminative-emmenagogue category into which *seseli* is later placed. The tag AT is retained on that structural basis and flagged as an attribution of class, not of species.

**Dioscoridean / Greek (DMM).** *Seseli* is a named drug of the Greek herbal tradition and the source of the modern genus name; Dioscorides treats *seseli* among the warming aromatic seeds, and the classical indication cluster is stable across the Greek and Latin reception: **wind and griping in the belly (carminative), difficult or suppressed menstruation (emmenagogue), difficult breathing and old coughs, and difficulty of urination.** It is also given, characteristically for this class, in obstructed labour — the "opening" indications and the "expelling" indications are one and the same doctrine applied to two organs. The Greek herbals recognise several *seseli* types (Massiliot, Ethiopian, Peloponnesian) and the *seseli* of Marseilles is conventionally referred to *S. tortuosum*; **this identification is a reception-history convention, not a certainty**, and the reader should treat any pre-modern *seseli* citation as a claim about a drug-name, not a claim about a Linnaean species.

**Avicennan / Unani (CM).** The Arabic-Persian reception inherits *seseli* through the Greek translations and files it where the Galenic humoral grid demands: a seed **hot and dry in the second degree**, therefore *muḥallil li-l-riyāḥ* (dissolvent of wind), *mudirr* (a promoter of flow — of urine and of menses), and *muftitiḥ li-l-sudad* (an opener of obstructions). This is the entire Unani theory of the aromatic umbellifer in three terms, and it is applied to *seseli* exactly as it is applied to anise, cumin, ajwain and fennel. Avicenna's tradition, characteristically, does not distinguish the *action* of *seseli* from that of its neighbours; it distinguishes the *degree*. That is the key to reading it correctly.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.**

The doctrine to be mapped is not a claim about a specific disease. It is a claim about a *direction of motion*. The Greek and Arabic categories into which *seseli* falls — carminative, emmenagogue, diuretic, expectorant, ecbolic — are not four indications; they are one indication seen through four organs. Every one of them says: *matter is stuck inside a hollow organ and must be moved outward*. Wind in the gut, blood in the womb, urine in the bladder, phlegm in the chest. The classical mind saw a single power (*dissolving, opening, driving*) and simply pointed it at whichever cavity was blocked.

That doctrine has a real chemical correlate, and the correlate is more honest than the doctrine deserves.

An aromatic umbellifer seed is, chemically, a **monoterpene delivery system with a coumarin backbone**. Analyses of *S. tortuosum* essential oils from Sicilian and Portuguese accessions find them overwhelmingly dominated by **monoterpene hydrocarbons** — α-pinene, β-pinene, *p*-cymene, sylvestrene, 3-carene, β-ocimene, limonene — with sesquiterpenes (germacrene D) in some plant parts, and the genus is a well-known reservoir of **coumarins** (Badalamenti et al., 2022, *Nat Prod Res*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2022.2078819); Vaglica et al., 2024, *Plants*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants13050678); Slimani et al., 2024, *Chem Biodivers*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/cbdv.202400482); all retrieved from PubMed).

Lipophilic monoterpenes of exactly this class are the standard chemical basis of carminative action across the Apiaceae: they partition into smooth-muscle membranes and reduce spasmodic tone, and they are volatile enough to be carried into the airway on the breath — which is why the same seed is simultaneously a "wind-dissolver" for the gut and a "loosener" for the old cough. The classical doctrine did not know about smooth muscle. It knew that one class of pungent, oily seed relaxed a griping belly *and* eased a tight chest, and it built a category around that co-occurrence. The category is chemically real even where the individual claims are unproven: **the "hot and dry" umbellifer seed is a monoterpene-rich, coumarin-bearing antispasmodic, and the humoral grid was, in effect, a crude chemotaxonomy.**

Where the doctrine over-reaches is precisely at the womb. The emmenagogue and ecbolic uses — "brings down the menses," "eases hard labour" — are the *symbolic* extension of the opening doctrine into the organ where opening carried the greatest social stakes. There is **no modern evidence of a uterine action of *S. tortuosum***, and the extension should be read as doctrinal logic, not as pharmacology. This entry declines to launder it.

### Active Components

- **Monoterpene hydrocarbons (essential oil, dominant class, 80–91% in some Sicilian samples):** α-pinene, β-pinene, *p*-cymene, sylvestrene, 3-carene, β-ocimene, limonene, sabinene.
- **Sesquiterpenes:** germacrene D (notable in stem/root oils of *Seseli* spp.).
- **Coumarins:** the genus *Seseli* is chemically characterised by coumarins and furanocoumarins; phytochemical screening of Algerian *S. tortuosum* confirmed coumarins, terpenoids and flavonoids (quercetin) in the extracts (Slimani et al., 2024).
- **Flavonoids/polyphenols:** quercetin and related polyphenols, concentrated in dichloromethane and methanolic extracts.

Exact quantitative composition is **strongly accession-dependent** — the Sicilian, Algerian, Turkish and Portuguese chemotypes differ substantially. There is no standardised commercial extract, no pharmacopoeial monograph, and no marker-compound assay in general use.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Filed as a **Phase II digestive/aromatic agent** with an explicitly *traditional* grade. Its legitimate place in the system is as a member of the warm-carminative class — the class to which fennel, anise, caraway and ajwain belong — where its function is the classical one: to relieve the cold, griping, distended abdomen and to accompany heavy or cold foods.

It is **not** recommended as a first-line member of that class. Its congeners are better characterised, better standardised, safer with respect to photosensitisation, and free of the identification hazard described below. *Seseli tortuosum* is retained in the Codex for scholarly completeness — because it is the plant that gave the classical *seseli* its name — and not because it earns a place in a working protocol. **A practitioner who reaches for it is choosing the obscure over the evidenced, and should know that.**

Its emmenagogue and ecbolic reputations are recorded as history. They are **not** endorsed, and the Contraindications section should be read as forbidding that use outright.

### Scientific Correlation

*Based on articles retrieved from PubMed. The total literature on this species is small; what follows is essentially all of it that bears on the traditional claims.*

**1. Anti-inflammatory / antinociceptive — the closest thing to a doctrinal confirmation.** Küpeli, Tosun & Yeşilada screened ten Turkish *Seseli* species in vivo. The ethyl acetate extract of *S. tortuosum* produced **37.6% inhibition** in the *p*-benzoquinone-induced abdominal constriction (writhing) test and **27.9–31.3% inhibition** in carrageenan-induced paw oedema at 100 mg/kg, against indomethacin's 41.8–44.8%, **without gastric damage** and without mortality in acute toxicity screening (*J Ethnopharmacol*, 2005; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2005.09.021)). This is a rodent study, not a clinical one — but note precisely what the writhing assay measures: **visceral abdominal pain**. The classical indication for *seseli* is the griping belly. This is the single point in the modern record where the doctrine and the data touch, and it is a *modest* touch: a moderate effect, in mice, from a crude extract, at a dose that cannot be translated.

**2. Cytotoxicity of the essential oil (not a traditional claim).** Beeby et al. found *S. tortuosum* essential oil among the most antiproliferative of six Portuguese sand-dune plants against breast and colorectal cancer lines in 2D and 3D spheroid models, with the effect associated with increased p21 expression and apoptosis (*J Ethnopharmacol*, 2020; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2020.112803)). Vaglica et al. found stem and root oils of *S. tortuosum* subsp. *tortuosum* reduced HCT116 colon-cancer cell viability by 42% and 95% respectively at 100 µg/mL, identifying **β-ocimene (IC₅₀ ≈ 64.5 µg/mL)** as the most active single constituent, while α-pinene, β-pinene and *p*-cymene were only modestly active (*Plants*, 2024; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants13050678)); a companion paper on *S. tortuosum* subsp. *maritimum* found stem oil cut HCT116 viability by 82% at 125 µg/mL, flagging germacrene D (*Fitoterapia*, 2023; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fitote.2023.105672)). **This has nothing to do with any traditional indication, does not support any oncological use, and is reported here only so that the Codex record is complete.** Cytotoxicity in a dish at ~100 µg/mL of neat essential oil is a screening result, not a therapy.

**3. Antioxidant and cholinesterase activity — a genuinely NULL-ish result worth stating.** Slimani et al. analysed Algerian *S. tortuosum*: the antioxidant activities across DPPH, ABTS, reducing power, phenanthroline and silver-nanoparticle assays were reported as **"a moderate potential"** — i.e. unremarkable. The dichloromethane extract inhibited **butyrylcholinesterase** (IC₅₀ 9.14 ± 1.74 µg/mL) more strongly than galantamine (34.75 ± 1.99 µg/mL) (*Chem Biodivers*, 2024; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/cbdv.202400482)). Two honesty notes. First, **BChE is not AChE**, and a crude-extract BChE result is not a cognitive claim — the authors' own "anti-Alzheimer" framing rests on an in-silico docking exercise. Second, and more important: **there is no traditional cognitive indication for *seseli* to correlate with.** Unlike Rosemary, where Avicenna's *muqawwī al-dimāgh* gave us a doctrine to test against cholinesterase inhibition, the classical record here points at the belly and the womb, not the brain. A convergence that the tradition never claimed is not a vindication of the tradition. It is a loose end, and it is recorded as one.

**4. What is absent.** No human trial. No carminative or antispasmodic assay on isolated gut smooth muscle for this species. No emmenagogue or uterotonic study. No pharmacokinetics. No toxicology beyond a single acute rodent screen. No standardisation.

**EVIDENCE GRADE: TRADITIONAL.** One supportive rodent visceral-pain/anti-inflammatory study; otherwise in-vitro screening only, none of it addressed to the classical indications. Any claim that modern science has "confirmed" *seseli* is false. The correct statement is that a single rodent study is *consistent with* the classical use for abdominal griping, and that everything else remains untested.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Galenic / Unani (attested).** Hot and dry, second degree. Actions: *muḥallil li-l-riyāḥ* (dissolvent of flatus), *mudirr li-l-bawl wa-l-ṭamth* (promoter of urine and menses), *muftitiḥ* (deobstruent), *muskin* (soothing to griping). The second-degree rating places it as a *moderate* heater — stronger than fennel, weaker than the true hot-third-degree drugs — and dosing in the Unani tradition is scaled to that degree.

**Traditional Chinese Medicine: not attested.** *Seseli tortuosum* is a western-Mediterranean plant with no presence in the Chinese materia medica. (Do not confuse it with the Chinese *Seseli* / *Libanotis* taxa or with *Fang Feng*, *Saposhnikovia divaricata*, which is a distinct drug with a distinct doctrine.) No TCM taste/temperature/channel assignment exists and none is invented here.

**Ayurveda: not attested.** No rasa, guṇa, vīrya, vipāka or prabhāva assignment exists for this species in the Ayurvedic corpus. The Indian aromatic-umbellifer category (*ajamoda*, *shatapushpa*, etc.) belongs to other species and its attributes must not be transferred.

### Contraindications

- **IDENTIFICATION HAZARD — READ FIRST.** The Apiaceae contains **Conium maculatum** (hemlock), **Oenanthe crocata** (hemlock water-dropwort — the most lethal plant in Britain) and **Cicuta virosa**. These are umbellifers with finely divided leaves and white umbels, and they have killed foragers who believed they had a wild carrot, parsley or hartwort. **Wild-collected Apiaceae must never be used without positive expert identification of the specific plant, in flower and fruit.** No traditional indication justifies that risk. This alone is sufficient reason to prefer a cultivated congener.
- **Furanocoumarin photosensitisation.** *Seseli* is a coumarin-rich genus and furanocoumarins are widespread in the family. Handling the fresh plant or applying its oil to skin, followed by UV exposure, can produce **phytophotodermatitis** — burning, blistering, and long-lived hyperpigmentation. Do not apply topically before sun exposure; wear gloves when handling the fresh plant.
- **Pregnancy — absolute contraindication.** The classical emmenagogue and ecbolic reputation is a reason to *avoid* this plant in pregnancy, not to use it. Any drug traditionally said to "bring down the menses" or "hasten a hard labour" must be treated as potentially abortifacient until proven otherwise, and *S. tortuosum* has been proven nothing.
- **Not for use during breastfeeding**, in children, or in anyone attempting conception. No safety data.
- **Photosensitising drug interaction:** additive risk with other photosensitisers (tetracyclines, amiodarone, thiazides, St John's Wort, retinoids).
- **Essential oil not for internal use.** Monoterpene-dominant oils are mucosal irritants and hepatically demanding at scale.
- **Anticoagulation:** coumarin-bearing plants warrant caution alongside warfarin and DOACs. (Note: plant coumarins are not, in themselves, anticoagulant — the anticoagulant is *dicoumarol*, a spoilage product. But the caution is prudent given the total absence of interaction data.)
- **No data at all** on hepatic or renal impairment, on children, or on chronic use. Absence of reported harm in a plant nobody has studied is not evidence of safety.
