## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Origanum vulgare* L. (1753)
**Critical species distinctions — pharmacologically non-interchangeable:**

| Taxon | Common name | Carvacrol % | Use |
|-------|-------------|-------------|-----|
| *O. vulgare* subsp. *hirtum* | Greek/Turkish oregano | 60–85% | Medicinal EO source |
| *O. vulgare* subsp. *vulgare* | Common/Italian oregano | 0–5% | Culinary (mild) |
| *O. compactum* | Compact oregano | 50–80% | North African medicinal |
| *O. heracleoticum* | Greek winter oregano | 40–70% | DMM reference plant |
| *Lippia graveolens* | Mexican oregano | — | NOT *Origanum*; different genus, similar culinary use |
| *O. majorana* | Marjoram | <5% carvacrol | See I_168 |

**The "oregano" sold in supermarkets in the US and Western Europe is primarily *O. vulgare* subsp. *vulgare* — a linalool/sabinene-dominant chemotype with <5% carvacrol, bearing pharmacologically little resemblance to the Greek/Turkish oregano (*subsp. hirtum*) that is the medicinal reference substance across historical texts.**

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 De Materia Medica (Dioscorides, c. 50–70 CE)
DMM Book III.36 (immediately adjacent to thyme, III.36): *Origanon* — Dioscorides distinguishes multiple types including *Herakleotic oregano* (= high-carvacrol type from Herakleia) as most potent. Properties: warming, digestive, carminative; treats coughs, asthma ("used for those who are choked by phlegm"); skin eruptions; venomous bites (antimicrobial application); added to baths for body pain. Dioscorides describes oil extraction: "*The juice of it, with old oil, is poured warm into the ears against deafness and hissings in the ears.*" Otitis media application consistent with modern antimicrobial activity.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine (Ibn Sīnā, 1025 CE)
*Zaʿtar* (combined oregano-thyme entry); warming (3rd degree) and drying; for cough, bronchial secretions, digestive gas, spasms; antimicrobial application to wounds. Ibn Sīnā: "*It assists digestion and kills intestinal worms*" — antiparasitic claim now supported by carvacrol activity against *Giardia*, *Cryptosporidium*, and intestinal parasites.

### 2.3 Shennong Bencao Jing / Bencao Gangmu (SBJ)
*Niú zhì* (牛至) — documented in Chinese materia medica as warming herb for damp-cold conditions; treats gastroenteritis, nausea, diarrhea; skin conditions from damp-heat. Li Shizhen documents antimicrobial-adjacent applications consistent with carvacrol activity.

### 2.4 Sushruta Samhita / Charaka Samhita (SS)
*Satrahva/Maruvaka* — digestive, carminative; treats respiratory conditions with excess mucus; applied topically for skin infections. Ayurvedic classification: warming, pungent, bitter; reduces Kapha; potential Pitta aggravation in excess.

### 2.5 Ebers Papyrus (c. 1552 BCE)
Aromatic mountain herb identified in Egyptian formulae for wound care and GI complaints; botanical identification tentative but consistent with *Origanum* species available via Mediterranean trade routes.

### 2.6 Assyrian Medical Tablets (c. 700–600 BCE)
*Ur-nu* — mountain herb in antimicrobial wound preparations; consistent with carvacrol-bearing Lamiaceae used across Fertile Crescent.

### 2.7 Four Medical Tantras (FMT) — present
Tibetan medicine documents oregano-family herbs for respiratory and digestive conditions; *snying zhim* (aromatic warming digestive) category.

### 2.8 Badianus Manuscript (BM) — ABSENT; 2.9 South American (SA) — ABSENT

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Convergence: 6/9.** The antimicrobial + digestive-carminative dyad is the universal documented function across all six traditions. Three traditions (DMM, CM, SS) document respiratory use. Two traditions (DMM, CM) explicitly document antiparasitic/vermicidal activity. The Italian pizza-herb and the Greek medicinal oregano oil are chemically and pharmacologically different substances from the same species complex — a species-level pharmacological divergence that underlies the gap between culinary and medicinal oregano.

**The Greek/Mediterranean kitchen as antimicrobial preserve:** The use of high-carvacrol oregano in Mediterranean cooking (salads, grilled meats, tzatziki) consistently throughout history reflects intuitive antimicrobial food preservation. Carvacrol inhibits *Salmonella*, *E. coli*, *Listeria*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Campylobacter* — all common food pathogens. Cooking in the Mediterranean with heavy oregano over centuries was, functionally, antimicrobial food safety.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

### 4.1 Essential oil constituents (high-carvacrol chemotype)
**Carvacrol** (5-Isopropyl-2-methylphenol): 60–85% of EO in medicinal chemotypes. Phenolic monoterpene. Isomer of thymol (same molecular formula C₁₀H₁₄O; structural isomers with overlapping but distinct activities). Carvacrol is more potent than thymol against most organisms tested.

**Thymol** (2-isopropyl-5-methylphenol): 2–10% in high-carvacrol types; 30–55% in thymol chemotypes. Pharmacologically complementary to carvacrol.

**p-Cymene**: 8–20%; monoterpene hydrocarbon; precursor in carvacrol biosynthesis; antimicrobial synergist (membrane permeabilizer that enhances carvacrol penetration into bacterial cells).

**γ-Terpinene**: 5–15%; antioxidant synergist.

**Rosmarinic acid**: 0.5–2% (dry leaf weight); polyphenol ester; anti-inflammatory (PLA2 inhibition, COX-1/2 inhibition, NF-κB); antiviral; antioxidant; responsible for much of oregano's anti-inflammatory activity distinct from EO antimicrobial activity.

**Luteolin, apigenin** (flavones): anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, GABA-A modulation.

**Ursolic acid, oleanolic acid** (triterpenic acids): anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, antitumor in vitro.

### 4.2 Chemotype variations
Six chemotypes of *O. vulgare* exist based on dominant EO compound:
1. Carvacrol (60–85%) — Greek/Turkish subsp. *hirtum* — MEDICINAL reference
2. Thymol (30–55%) — Spanish/Turkish varieties
3. Sabinene hydrate (20–40%) — sweet/Italian varieties — CULINARY; minimal medicinal value
4. Linalool (15–30%) — mild chemotype
5. Geraniol (15–25%) — rare; sweet floral
6. Mixed carvacrol-thymol — variable

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Membrane Disruption (Carvacrol — Primary Antimicrobial)
Carvacrol inserts into phospholipid bilayer between acyl chains → increases membrane fluidity and permeability → loss of membrane potential → ion gradient dissipation → ATP depletion → cell death. This mechanism is:
- Bactericidal (not just bacteriostatic)
- Active against biofilms (disintegrates biofilm matrix)
- Effective against drug-resistant organisms (mechanism bypasses most antibiotic resistance mechanisms — MDR efflux pumps do not efficiently export membrane-active compounds)
- Dose-dependent: sub-MIC concentrations synergize with antibiotics

**MIC values (illustrative):**
- *E. coli*: 0.1–0.5 μL/mL
- *S. aureus* (including MRSA): 0.2–1.0 μL/mL
- *Candida albicans*: 0.06–0.25 μL/mL (8× more potent than thymol against Candida)

### 5.2 Anti-inflammatory (Rosmarinic Acid + Carvacrol)
**Rosmarinic acid:** Inhibits PLA2 (upstream of arachidonic acid cascade) → reduces prostaglandin and leukotriene synthesis. Inhibits NF-κB. Scavenges reactive oxygen species directly.
**Carvacrol:** TRPA1 agonism (pain modulation); PPARγ agonism (anti-inflammatory transcription factor, same target as thiazolidinedione diabetes drugs); reduces IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β in macrophage models.

### 5.3 Antifungal (Carvacrol)
Against *Candida* spp. (including azole-resistant strains): disrupts ergosterol biosynthesis in addition to membrane disruption; inhibits mycelium-to-hyphal transition (virulence factor). Comparable to fluconazole in some MIC studies.

### 5.4 Antispasmodic (Direct and Indirect)
Calcium channel antagonism (L-type VGCC blockade); anti-cholinergic at smooth muscle → reduces intestinal spasm and cramping. Explains cross-traditional digestive-carminative indication.

### 5.5 PPAR-γ Agonism (Metabolic)
Carvacrol activates peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) — nuclear receptor regulating adipogenesis, glucose homeostasis. In vitro: increased insulin-stimulated glucose uptake. Animal models: reduced fat accumulation, improved insulin sensitivity. Clinical evidence limited.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### 6.1 SIBO / Gut Dysbiosis
**Chedid et al. 2014 (Global Advances in Health and Medicine):** 104 patients with SIBO confirmed by breath test. Herbal therapy group (oregano oil-containing preparation) vs. triple antibiotic therapy (rifaximin) × 4 weeks. Both groups: significant reduction in hydrogen breath test at 4 weeks. Oregano oil group: 46.7% normalization rate vs. 34.1% rifaximin (non-inferior). Conclusion: herbal antimicrobials including oregano are as effective as rifaximin for SIBO — a clinically actionable finding for a poorly-tolerated condition.

### 6.2 Food-Borne Pathogen Inhibition (Applied Microbiology)
Numerous in vitro and food safety studies confirm carvacrol and oregano EO inhibit *Salmonella enteritidis*, *E. coli* O157:H7, *Campylobacter jejuni*, *Listeria monocytogenes*, *Staphylococcus aureus* — all at concentrations achievable in food matrices. Oregano EO at 0.5–1% in ground beef delays microbial spoilage by 3–5 days at 4°C. Commercial food safety application (marinades, antimicrobial films).

### 6.3 Antifungal (Candida)
**Manohar et al. 2001:** Oregano oil (containing carvacrol) active against *Candida albicans* and non-*albicans* species including azole-resistant strains. BMDM (bone marrow-derived macrophage) studies: enhanced fungicidal activity of macrophages exposed to subMIC carvacrol.

### 6.4 Respiratory (Traditional/Insufficient Formal RCT Data)
No large RCTs for oregano specifically in respiratory conditions. Commission E has no approved monograph for oregano (thyme received approval — see I_153). Traditional use for bronchial secretions/mucolytic not disputed but not RCT-confirmed.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**"Mountain joy" — etymology and geography:** The Greek *origanon* (ὄρος = mountain + γάνος = brightness/joy) captures the ecological truth: oregano's carvacrol content peaks when grown at altitude in poor, dry, calcareous soils with intense UV exposure — the stress conditions that trigger secondary metabolite production. The highest-quality medicinal oregano grows wild in the mountains of Greece, Turkey, and Crete. This is not romanticism; it is chemotypic ecology.

**The Pizza Paradox:** Pizza — globally the most consumed oregano delivery vehicle — uses low-carvacrol Italian oregano (*O. vulgare* subsp. *vulgare*) with negligible medicinal value. Medical-grade oregano oil (high-carvacrol Greek/Turkish subsp. *hirtum*) is rarely used in food. The herb in the pharmacy and the herb on the pizza are from the same species complex but are chemically non-equivalent. This is the most widespread confusion in popular herbalism: people taking "oregano oil" for infections are using high-carvacrol product; people sprinkling "oregano" on food are using a different chemotype with different chemistry.

**Sacred herb of Aphrodite:** Greek mythology assigned oregano to Aphrodite — she created it as a symbol of joy on Mount Ida. It was planted on graves to bring departed souls peace. Greek wedding crowns included oregano alongside myrtle. The mountain origins + spiritual associations encode the ecological requirement (altitude = potency) in mythological language.

**Ancient "ear oil":** Dioscorides' prescription for deafness — warm oregano oil in the ear — is precisely the mechanism now explained by carvacrol antimicrobial activity against *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae*, *Moraxella catarrhalis* (the three most common otitis media pathogens). Traditional ear drop = topical antimicrobial. The prescription in DMM III.36 written c. 60 CE remains pharmacologically rational for bacterial otitis media.

**The Olive Oil Test:** Traditional Greek practice: pour a drop of olive oil into oregano — authentic, unoxidized olive oil dissolves oregano aroma differently from adulterated oil (blended with lower-grade oils). This olfactory quality test, transmitted through Mediterranean food culture, was both sensory assessment and an implicit understanding that carvacrol's interaction with lipid matrices differs with oil quality.

**"Oil of Oregano" supplement industry:** The commercial *Origanum vulgare* oil supplement industry developed primarily in North America and Europe in the 1990s–2000s. Products vary 10–100-fold in carvacrol content (0.1–85% carvacrol reported in commercial products). Consumer expectation (antimicrobial, antifungal) is pharmacologically valid for high-carvacrol products; many commercial preparations do not standardize carvacrol content. Laboratory testing of commercial "oil of oregano" products frequently reveals mislabeled chemotype or dilution with cheaper oils.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Dosing (Evidence-Referenced)
| Application | Form | Carvacrol content | Dose |
|-------------|------|-------------------|------|
| Antimicrobial (SIBO) | Standardized EO capsule | ≥55% carvacrol | 200mg EO × 2–3 times daily (enteric-coated preferred) |
| Antifungal (topical) | EO diluted in carrier | 1–3% dilution | Apply to affected area |
| Digestive carminative | Dried herb tea | — | 1–2 tsp dried herb / cup × 3/day |
| Food antimicrobial | Culinary, any chemotype | Variable | Dietary amounts indefinite |

**Quality criterion:** For medicinal use, product must specify *Origanum vulgare* subsp. *hirtum* or state carvacrol content ≥55%. Products without specification are likely low-carvacrol culinary type.

### 8.2 Safety
- **Topical EO:** Must dilute — undiluted carvacrol is a skin irritant (strong acid-phenol); 1–3% in carrier oil maximum for topical
- **Oral EO:** GI irritation at high doses; enteric-coated capsules preferred for gut effects; maximum 200–400mg EO/day
- **Pregnancy:** Emmenagogue at therapeutic doses; avoid concentrated EO in pregnancy (culinary amounts safe)
- **Thyroid:** Theoretical goitrogen in large quantities (Brassica-family analogy, disputed for Lamiaceae)
- **Anticoagulants:** In vitro antiplatelet activity of rosmarinic acid; monitor INR if taking warfarin + therapeutic oregano doses
- **Pediatrics:** Avoid topical to face/nose (phenol irritation); dilute more for children

### 8.3 Tradition-Practice Concordance
The universal cross-traditional antimicrobial + digestive indication is now explained by carvacrol membrane disruption. However, the gap between "oregano the culinary herb" and "oregano oil the antimicrobial agent" must be explicitly communicated: these require different chemotypes with fundamentally different phytochemistry. The Dioscoridean instruction to use Herakleotic oregano (highest potency) rather than common oregano prefigures the modern species-and-chemotype differentiation by 2,000 years.
