# Equisetum sylvaticum — Ippouris etera (Dioscorides 4-47)

## *Equisetum sylvaticum, as printed in the plate caption "Equisetum sylvaticum after FAGUET — 1891" beside the chapter; the translators' SUGGESTED lines give other plants, Equisetum palustre ("Meadow Horsetail") and, as another usage, Equisetum hyemale ("Common Scouring Bush")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The identity is doubtful. The binomial *Equisetum sylvaticum* is not on the translators' SUGGESTED lines. It comes from a plate caption printed in the chapter (line 8416), an illustration credited to Faguet, 1891. The SUGGESTED lines (8418 and 8419) read "Equisetum longius, Hippuris, Polygonum foemina [Fuchs], Equisetum palustre [Linnaeus] — Meadow Horsetail" and "[other usage] Equisetum hyemale, Equisetum scirpoides — Common Scouring Bush", and none of these is *E. sylvaticum*. The Codex does not state the identity as certain, and the row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-47. IPPOURIS ETERA* at line 8414, text at line 8420. The chapter stands in Book Four, "Other Herbs & Roots". The translators' name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 627, with the caption as the printed name. The heading's second word and the text's first words, "Hippuris alterum", mark it as a second *hippuris*. The chapter before it, 4-46 *IPPOURIS* (line 8405), is the first.

The whole chapter reads: "Hippuris alterum has a stalk that is straight, even higher than a foot (as it were) empty, with shorter, whiter, softer filaments at distances. Mixed with vinegar it heals wounds, having the same strength as that above. It is also called equitium, chedra, or gyon, while the Romans say salix equinalis."

So the plant has a straight stalk, taller than a foot and as it were hollow, with filaments that are shorter, whiter and softer than the first plant's, set at intervals. Mixed with vinegar it heals wounds, and "having the same strength as that above" points to 4-46. Three other names are printed, *equitium*, *chedra* and *gyon*, and the Roman name *salix equinalis*. The chapter gives no habitat and no season.

*The chapter it refers to (4-46, lines 8405 to 8413).* The first *hippuris* is glossed by the translators as *Equisetum arvense* ("False Horsetail, Horsepipe, Bottlebrush, Meadow Horsetail"), with *Hippuris vulgaris* as another usage. Dioscorides says it "grows in moist places and ditches", with "empty little reddish stalks distinguished by joints growing one into another", many thin rushy leaves, black filaments "similar to the tail of a horse", and a "woody and hard" root, and "the herb is astringent". The juice "stops discharges of blood from the nostrils". A decoction "taken as a drink with wine" helps dysentery and induces urine. Pounded and sprinkled on it "closes bleeding wounds". Root and herb help coughs and asthma, and a decoction of the leaves in water is "said also" to join openings of the intestines, "any cutting-apart of the bladder", and hernia. The text names the Egyptians' *pherphram*, the Magi's *cibus Saturni* and several others. The phrase "the same strength as that above" carries these properties to 4-47 by Dioscorides' own words, but the chapter itself prints only the wound use, and the entry does not assign the rest to 4-47 as attested. ### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. It is well established that horsetails (*Equisetum*) take up silicon and deposit it in their tissues, which is why the stalks are rough and were used as a scouring material, which fits the translators' English name "Scouring Bush". Nothing further is stated here about the composition of this plant. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records only that the plant mixed with vinegar heals wounds, and that its strength is that of the first *hippuris*. The Codex gives no protocol for wounds, which need proper care, and the identity is uncertain.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives one preparation: "Mixed with vinegar it heals wounds." No quantity, frequency or duration is given. For 4-46 the text gives a juice, a decoction "taken as a drink with wine" and a powder sprinkled on, again without a quantity. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter gives a short discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition: how to tell this plant from its fellow (a straight, hollow stalk taller than a foot, with shorter, whiter, softer filaments) and the many names it goes by in Greek and Latin. It assigns the plant no status. The identification is the plate's at best, and the translators suggest other horsetails. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a recognition note that points back to another chapter, with a single wound use. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Wounds that bleed or become infected need proper care and should not be dressed with an unidentified wild plant and vinegar. The identification is uncertain (a plate caption against the translators' suggestions), so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
