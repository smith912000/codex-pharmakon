# Castoreum (beaver testicle) — Kastoros Orchis (Dioscorides 2-26) / Castorium, castoreum
## *Castoreum, the secretion of the beaver's two sacs in the groin, which Dioscorides calls the beaver's "stones" [testicles]; Osbaldeston's edition prints "Castoreum — Beaver" with the note "obtained from two sacs in the groin of the Beaver". **Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The English is the 2000 edition of Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). Castoreum is Book 2, chapter 26, headed *KASTOROS ORCHIS* ("beaver's testicle"), under "Living Creatures" (from line 5035; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` locus "2-26 (line 5035)"). The editor's heading reads "Castoreum — Beaver" with the note "obtained from two sacs in the groin of the Beaver".

I read the whole chapter. It opens with the animal: "The beaver is a living creature with a double nature nourished for the most part in the waters with the fishes and crabs." The "stones [testicles]" are the drug. The uses given, in the chapter's order: they are "good against the poisons of snakes"; "they cause sneezing and are generally effective for many purposes"; "Two teaspoonfuls (taken as a drink with pulegium [3-36]) encourage the menstrual flow, put out the afterbirth, and are an abortifacient"; taken as a drink with vinegar "against gaseousness, griping, hiccups, deadly poisons [antidote] and ixia [3-103]"; "moistened with vinegar and rosaceum [1-53] it revives the lethargic or those brought low in any way", and "it does the same when smelled or inhaled as smoke"; and "good taken either as a drink or rubbed on for trembling, convulsions, and all diseases of the nerves". He sums its quality in one sentence: "Generally it is warming."

The chapter then turns to how to choose and how to detect fraud. "Always choose those stones [testicles] which are connected together from one beginning (for it is impossible to find two follicles [small glands] knit together in one membrane); always distinguished by their natural loose skins; and that have waxy stuff within, with a strong smell, poisonous, sharp, biting in taste, easily crumbled." The adulteration he names: "Some adulterate it by pouring ammoniacum or gum tempered with blood and castoreum into the follicle and drying it." He rejects a popular story: "It is not true that this beast when it is pursued bites off his stones [testicles] and throws them away, for it is impossible that he should touch them as they are joined underneath like those of a boar." The last sentence gives the method of collection: "Those who take off the skin must take the liquid in there which looks similar to honey together with the loose skin that contains it, dry it, then bottle and store it."

Castoreum is cited by cross-reference elsewhere in the same text, which I opened:

- *Smyrna* (1-77, line 4140): myrrh "rubbed on with the flesh of a snail" cures broken ears and pus in the ears "with meconium [4-65], castorium [2-26] and glaucium".
- *Naphtha* (1-101, line 4292): "Taken as a drink with wine and castoreum [2-26] it draws out the menstrual flow."
- *Onux* (2-10, line 4955): the burnt operculum of a shellfish gives a smell "somewhat resembling castor [2-26] in scent".
- *Pitua*, rennet (2-85, line 5291): "Rennet of the sea calf [seal — Phoca vitulina] has a strength very similar to castorium [2-26]", and is "especially good (taken as a drink) for epilepsy, and for constriction of the womb".
- *Ammoniakon* (3-98, line 7438): the best ammoniacum is "similar to castor [2-26] in smell, but bitter to the taste". This is a comparison of smell, not a use.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, castoreum is a secretion stored in the paired castor sacs of the beaver, which lie near the anal glands and are not the testes (anatomically the sacs and the testes are different organs; Dioscorides' "stones" is the ancient name for the sacs, and the translator's own gloss, "two sacs in the groin", names the actual material). It is a strongly scented, resinous mixture; its odour comes from volatile compounds. It is used in perfumery. No quantities are given here because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records castoreum as a drug against snake poison, as a sneeze-provoking and reviving agent, as a drink for gaseousness, griping, hiccups and poison, as a remedy for trembling, convulsions and nerve disease, and as an agent that brings on the menses, expels the afterbirth, and is an abortifacient. The Codex does not recommend any of these uses: the material is an animal product of uncertain purity, the first and last uses are dangerous, and no protocol is built on this chapter.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations the text gives: a drink of "two teaspoonfuls" with pulegium for the menstrual flow, the afterbirth and abortion (2-26); a drink with vinegar for gaseousness, griping, hiccups and poisons; a mixture moistened with vinegar and rosaceum, smelled, inhaled as smoke, or taken to revive the lethargic; and a drink or an external rub for nerve disease. Preparation of the drug: the skin and the honey-like liquid inside are dried together, bottled and stored. The only measure printed is the two teaspoonfuls above, and it is the translator's rendering; whether it applies to the other uses is not said. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Castoreum is an animal secretion used historically in perfumery and, in small amounts, as a food flavouring; it is not an established modern drug. Dioscorides' statement that it is a menstrual stimulant and an abortifacient is the reason for caution: a substance said to end a pregnancy should not be handled as a folk remedy. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** 2-26 prints one quality, "Generally it is warming." No other temperature, moisture or taste is printed in the chapter; the quality words "sharp" and "biting in taste" occur only in the test of good castoreum. No further classification is supplied here.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention, and this chapter is a good example of it: provenance and anatomy (the two sacs, joined from one beginning), a sensory assay (waxy within, strong smell, "sharp, biting in taste, easily crumbled"), a named adulteration (ammoniacum or gum tempered with blood and castoreum), the correction of a rumour (the beaver does not bite off its "stones"), and the method of collecting and drying the drug. The chapter is therapeutic, and it records no amuletic use. No cultivation status is assigned. Smelling and inhaling castoreum to "revive the lethargic" is a clinical use in the text; it is not a practice of spirit or breath.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here makes castoreum a cultivation substance, and the chapter's strongest uses (menstrual drive, expulsion of the afterbirth, abortion) are reasons to leave it alone. What a cultivator can take from the chapter is the method of its choice: check the material, be able to tell the real from the adulterated, and do not repeat a story about it that the text itself says is false. No dose beyond the two teaspoonfuls above is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy:** the text says castoreum, taken in a drink, is an abortifacient and puts out the afterbirth. It must not be taken in pregnancy.
- **Toxicity:** the text itself describes the good drug as "poisonous, sharp, biting in taste", though it also gives it as a drink against "deadly poisons". The text says it provokes sneezing, and strong odours and smoke can irritate the airways.
- **Adulteration and purity:** the text warns that it is often adulterated; no modern source of castoreum is vouched for here.
- **Allergy:** animal-derived fragrance materials can cause skin and respiratory reactions.
- **Law and ethics:** beaver populations are protected or managed in different jurisdictions, and the trade in beaver products is regulated in some of them. The Codex does not recommend it.
