### Origin & Tradition

Dioscorides describes *asaron* in *De Materia Medica* Book I among the aromatic roots near the nards, naming it for its small, leafy, ground-creeping habit and its slender, jointed, intensely aromatic rootstock. The plant of European herbalism — *Asarum europaeum*, hazelwort or asarabacca — is a low evergreen of shaded deciduous woodland, with kidney-shaped glossy leaves and a hidden, dull-purple bell-shaped flower at ground level. Its creeping rhizome carries a pungent, peppery, faintly camphoraceous odour that fixed its place among the warming aromatics.

The classical and medieval pharmacy valued asarum chiefly as a powerful evacuant: an emetic when taken internally and, when the dried leaf or root was powdered finely and snuffed, a sternutatory (sneeze-provoking) errhine used to "purge the head." This errhine use persisted into the early-modern European pharmacopoeias, where asarabacca powder was a standard "cephalic snuff" for headache, drowsiness, and chronic catarrh of the head and eyes. The same drastic action made it a traditional diuretic and emmenagogue in the Galenic system, classed as hot and dry and reserved for robust constitutions.

Across folk traditions (BM) of central and eastern Europe the root retained a reputation as a violent purge and as a notorious adulterant or substitute in older preparations — and, troublingly, as a folk additive to wine and a means to provoke vomiting after excess. This last use is part of why the plant must now be handled with full knowledge of its toxicology: the very constituent that gives asarum its aromatic pungency, asarone, is also the source of its danger.

Within Kenetica, asarum is filed as a strong dispersing aromatic of the head and chest whose traditional indications (errhine, emetic, diuretic) are real but whose toxicology removes it from any casual or general use. It is preserved here as a documented historical aromatic and a cautionary entry, not as a recommended remedy.

### Active Components

- **α-Asarone and β-asarone (phenylpropene ethers)** — the principal aromatic constituents of the essential oil, structurally related to safrole and to the asarones of *Acorus calamus*. They carry the characteristic odour and the pharmacological activity, but β-asarone in particular is genotoxic and a recognised hepatocarcinogen in animal models — the core safety concern.
- **Essential oil (asarum oil)** — a volatile fraction dominated by asarone isomers; responsible for the emetic, sternutatory, and irritant actions.
- **Bitter and resinous principles** — contribute to the drastic purgative and diuretic reputation.

Note honestly: the asarone/safrole class is why this plant is restricted. β-Asarone is regulated as a food contaminant in many jurisdictions precisely because of its genotoxic and carcinogenic potential.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. **Historical errhine (cephalic snuff).** Finely powdered leaf or root provoked sneezing and nasal discharge; the tradition used this to "unload the head" in chronic headache, heaviness, and catarrh. Documented for completeness; not advised given safer modern errhines.

2. **Drastic emetic / evacuant.** Internally, asarum was a strong emetic. This action is real but dangerous and obsolete — modern emesis is never induced this way. Recorded as historical context only.

3. **Traditional diuretic and emmenagogue.** Classed hot-and-dry, used to promote urine and menses in the Galenic frame. Superseded by safer plants; retained as historical classification.

4. **Aromatic dispersant (read, not recommended).** In the Kenetica energetic map asarum represents the extreme "strongly dispersing aromatic" pole — useful conceptually to anchor where milder nards sit, while being itself off the practical formulary.

### Preparation & Dosing

Because of the genotoxic asarone content, **Kenetica assigns no recommended internal dose.** Historical preparations are recorded for scholarship, not prescription:

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM / Galenic:** Hot and dry in the third degree; sharp, bitter, aromatic. A drastic evacuant of the head (errhine), stomach (emetic), and kidneys (diuretic).
- **CM (by analogy):** Compares to the strongly dispersing, orifice-opening aromatics; an extreme ascending/dispersing action. (*Asarum* species are used in Chinese medicine — *xi xin*, *Asarum heterotropoides/sieboldii* — but those are different species with their own strict dose limits; do not conflate with *A. europaeum*.)
- **Kenetica tier:** ◉ Extreme dispersing aromatic — restricted. Marks the toxic outer boundary of the nard family.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides places *asaron* in Book I among the aromatic roots near the nards, describing its creeping habit and jointed, intensely aromatic rootstock, and the classical pharmacy worked it as a drastic evacuant — an emetic taken in, a sternutatory errhine snuffed to "purge the head," a diuretic and emmenagogue in the hot-and-dry Galenic frame. None of this is a subtle-body doctrine; Dioscorides has no such system and one must not be manufactured for him. What the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention — placing this small ground-nard correctly among the aromatic roots, judging the rhizome by its peppery, camphoraceous pungency, guarding against the adulteration for which asarum itself was later notorious. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** Asarabacca enters the Unani frame as a hot-and-dry dispersing aromatic — an evacuant and deobstruent of the head and kidneys — though the profile marks the classification as reached partly by analogy to the strongly orifice-opening aromatics rather than by a sourced *Canon* passage for this species. The corpus's central cultivation idea, the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture, is a frame for the whole regimen and is not extended to this drastic, dispersing evacuant; no *mufarrih* or vital-strengthening standing is claimed, and given the plant's genotoxic asarone content none would be appropriate. Its attested standing is therapeutic and evacuant, not a longevity status. **(b) framed:** claimed by the tradition, no cultivation status sourced.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this plant. It is filed as a documented historical aromatic and a cautionary entry, not a remedy: its active principle, β-asarone, is genotoxic and hepatocarcinogenic in animal studies, and Kenetica assigns it no recommended internal dose. Where its traditional errhine and emetic actions were once "cultivational" in the loose sense of clearing and unloading the head, that role is obsolete and superseded by safer plants. The honest instruction is abstention.

### Contraindications

- **Genotoxic / carcinogenic constituent:** β-asarone is genotoxic and hepatocarcinogenic in animal studies — this alone restricts internal use. **[SPECIALIST ONLY]**
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** absolutely contraindicated (emmenagogue, emetic, genotoxic).
- **Children, frail, hepatic impairment:** contraindicated.
- **Self-administration:** not appropriate as a general remedy under any preparation.
- **Regulatory:** asarone exposure is legally limited in many jurisdictions; respect those limits.
