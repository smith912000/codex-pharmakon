# I-379 | Bai Zhi / Dahurian Angelica (*Angelica dahurica*)

**Volume I — Plant-Based | Family: Apiaceae**
**Convergence Score: 4/9 | Energetic Tier: ◉ Qi**

---

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Binomial:** *Angelica dahurica* (Fisch. ex Hoffm.) Benth. & Hook.f. ex Franch. & Sav., 1878
**Variety:** *Angelica dahurica* var. *formosana* (Boiss.) Shan & Yuan — used in some pharmacopeias alongside the species

**Family:** Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)
**Subfamily:** Apioideae
**Tribe:** Selineae

**Common names:**
- Chinese: 白芷 (*Bái Zhǐ*) — literally "white root/plant" (*bái* = white; *zhǐ* = an archaic term for aromatic plants, specifically used for plants with fragrant white roots); one of the oldest plant names in classical Chinese literature, appearing in the *Chu Ci* (Songs of Chu, ~4th century BCE) as a symbol of virtue and fragrance
- English: Dahurian Angelica (botanically accurate); Chinese Angelica Root (pharmaceutical English — confuses with *A. sinensis*/Dong Quai, a different herb)
- Japanese: 白芷 (*Byakushi*) — Kampo reading
- Korean: 백지 (*Baekji*)
- Vietnamese: Bạch chỉ
- Pharmaceutical name: *Angelicae Dahuricae Radix* (Pharmacopoeia of PRC)

**CRITICAL DISAMBIGUATION — *Angelica dahurica* vs. *Angelica sinensis*:**
Despite sharing the *Angelica* genus, these two Chinese herbs are pharmacologically DISTINCT:
| Feature | *A. dahurica* (Bai Zhi) | *A. sinensis* (Dang Gui) |
|---------|------------------------|------------------------|
| Primary action | Expels wind-cold, opens orifices, dispels dampness | Nourishes blood, moves blood, regulates menstruation |
| Key compounds | Furanocoumarins (imperatorin, isoimperatorin, byakangelicin) | Phthalides (Z-ligustilide), ferulic acid, polysaccharides |
| Primary indications | Sinusitis, headache, toothache, nasal discharge | Dysmenorrhea, anemia, blood stasis conditions |
| Safety | Phototoxic risk (furanocoumarins); neurotoxic at excess | Pregnancy contraindicated; warfarin interaction |
| Energetic tier | ◉ Qi (wind-dispersing, surface) | ◈ Jing/Blood (constitutional nourishing) |

This entry covers Bai Zhi (*A. dahurica*); Dong Quai (*A. sinensis*) is entry I-378.

**Botanical description:** Large perennial herb; stems 1–2.5m (very tall for an herbaceous plant); hollow purple-tinged stems; leaves large, 2–3× ternate-pinnate; flowers white, umbels; root stout, 10–25cm long, multi-rooted from the crown, strongly aromatic — the external surface shows distinctive brown lenticels (small pore-like markings) in the dried root, a key quality marker. Habitat: riverbanks, forest margins; distributed across Northeast China (Dahuria/Manchuria region), Korea, Japan, Eastern Russia.

**Parts used:** Dried root (primary); fumigation (leaves, historical pest control); cosmetic (root powder).

## 3. Convergence Analysis

Bai Zhi is among the oldest continuously documented herbs in Chinese materia medica, with textual attestation stretching from the *Shennong Bencao Jing* (1st–2nd century CE pharmacopoeial documentation) back to the *Chu Ci* poetry anthology (~4th century BCE) where it appears as a symbol of virtuous fragrance — one of the aromatic plants used to characterize a cultivated person. This pre-pharmacological literary presence is unusual: most herbs enter the written record through medical rather than literary texts. Bai Zhi entered Chinese cultural consciousness as a fragrant emblem before entering it as a drug.

The pharmacological convergence between the *Shennong Bencao Jing* applications (fever, headache, nasal discharge) and modern pharmacology is unusually direct for an ancient text: the furanocoumarins responsible for anti-inflammatory and anti-bacterial activity in modern analysis correspond neatly to the classical fever/infection indications; the antispasmodic mechanisms underlying pain relief map onto classical headache/toothache indications. This degree of traditional-to-modern mechanistic alignment is pharmacological validation rather than coincidence.

The furanocoumarin pharmacology of Bai Zhi also reveals the connection between the Chinese *Angelica* tradition and the European furanocoumarin pharmacology encountered in parsley, parsnip, angelica, and lovage — all Apiaceae sharing this compound class. The difference is application: European tradition emphasized furanocoumarins as phototoxic hazards or skin treatment agents (vitiligo PUVA); Chinese tradition encountered them primarily as anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and analgesic compounds for internal/topical use at non-phototoxic doses.

## 4. Phytochemical Composition

**Furanocoumarins (primary pharmacological fraction — most distinctive compound class):**
- **Imperatorin** (主分 *zhǔ fēn* — primary constituent): 0.2–0.8% DW in root; spasmolytic (smooth muscle Ca²⁺ channel antagonism); anti-inflammatory (NF-κB inhibition); calcium channel blockade in pain pathways; some antibacterial activity; CYP3A4 inhibition (drug interaction significance)
- **Isoimperatorin**: 0.1–0.5% DW; pharmacological profile similar to imperatorin; anti-nociceptive (pain-reducing) in animal models
- **Byakangelicin** (白当归素): spasmolytic; analgesic; anti-inflammatory; named specifically for *A. dahurica*
- **Byakangelicol**: related to byakangelicin; similar activity
- **Oxypeucedanin**: antibacterial; anti-tumor (in vitro, preliminary)
- **Phellopterin** (花椒毒素): phototoxic potential if concentrated/isolated
- **Psoralen, bergapten, xanthotoxin**: standard PUVA-active furanocoumarins (trace at typical medicinal doses; phototoxicity concern at high concentrated extract doses)
- Total furanocoumarin content: 0.5–3.0% DW depending on geographic origin and processing

**Volatile oil:**
- α-Pinene, β-pinene, camphene, sabinene (monoterpenes): contribute to characteristic aromatic quality
- Ligustilide traces (phthalide class — very low; contrasts with *A. sinensis* where phthalides dominate)
- Total volatile oil: 0.2–0.5% DW (lower than *A. sinensis*)

**Phenolic acids:**
- Ferulic acid (lower than in *A. sinensis*); caffeic acid; chlorogenic acid

**Coumarin glycosides:**
- Anomalin, demethylsuberosin: anti-inflammatory; less potent than primary furanocoumarins

**Alkaloids (trace):**
- Minor, not pharmacologically significant

**Phytosterols:**
- β-Sitosterol, stigmasterol (minor amounts)

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**Anti-inflammatory mechanism (primary):**
Imperatorin + isoimperatorin → inhibition of NF-κB nuclear translocation → reduced transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8) → reduced inflammation in nasal mucosa, sinuses, and peripheral tissues. Additional: COX-2 and iNOS inhibition by furanocoumarins → reduced prostaglandin E₂ and nitric oxide in inflammatory tissue.

**Analgesic mechanism:**
1. Ca²⁺ channel blockade by imperatorin/isoimperatorin in peripheral nociceptors → reduced pain signal transmission; mechanism distinct from opioid or COX-based analgesia
2. TRPA1 (transient receptor potential ankyrin 1) channel modulation by volatile compounds → modulation of pain sensation in trigeminal nerve pathways (relevant to sinusitis-associated facial pain and headache)
3. Central mechanism: unclear; some data suggest CNS activity at higher doses

**Antibacterial mechanism:**
Furanocoumarins disrupt bacterial cell membranes; oxypeucedanin and byakangelicin show activity against *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Streptococcus* spp., and various oral pathogens in vitro. Relevant to traditional use for dental infections, sinusitis, and infected skin lesions.

**Nasal/sinus-specific mechanism:**
Volatile monoterpenes (α-pinene, camphene) → stimulate nasal mucosal secretion → increased watery nasal secretion → mechanical clearance of viscous discharge; analogous to eucalyptol/menthol mechanism in other nasal preparations. TCM concept: "opens the nasal orifice" (开鼻窍) = secretolytic/mucociliary stimulation.

**Phototoxic mechanism (safety relevant, not therapeutic at standard dose):**
Psoralen/bergapten/xanthotoxin (trace amounts in standard herbal use) → UV-activated DNA damage if concentrated. Standard therapeutic doses: below threshold for clinically significant phototoxicity. High-dose concentrated extracts: phototoxicity risk increases. Topical application of root preparations directly to sun-exposed skin: potentially phototoxic.

**Cosmetic/skin mechanism:**
Historically used as skin brightening agent in Chinese and Korean cosmetics (Bai Zhi powder in skin preparations). Mechanism: furanocoumarins may inhibit tyrosinase (melanin synthesis enzyme) at sub-phototoxic concentrations → skin lightening; anti-inflammatory reduces hyperpigmentation from inflammatory lesions. This represents pharmacological application of the same furanocoumarin chemistry in the opposite direction from PUVA (where UV is added to activate; cosmetic use avoids UV to prevent activation while maintaining tyrosinase inhibition).

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**Sinusitis/nasal congestion:**
- **Level B** — Chinese RCTs for rhinitis and sinusitis using Bai Zhi in formula (e.g., Cang Er Zi San: Cang Er Zi + Xin Yi Hua + Bai Zhi + Bo He); significant improvement in nasal airflow and discharge versus placebo; Bai Zhi's independent contribution cannot be isolated from formula

**Headache/pain:**
- **Level B/C** — Traditional use validation studies; Chinese hospital series for tension headache and facial pain responsive to Chuanxiong Chatiao San (which includes Bai Zhi); effect on headache improvement significant in several non-blinded trials

**Antibacterial (in vitro):**
- **Level D** — In vitro activity against multiple pathogens; no RCT for clinical infection

**Skin conditions:**
- **Level C/D** — Traditional topical use for skin sores and infections; furanocoumarin antibacterial mechanism provides pharmacological rationale; cosmetic use well-established commercially

**Anti-tumor:**
- **Level D** — Imperatorin cytotoxicity against several cancer cell lines (leukemia, hepatocellular, colorectal); preliminary; no clinical translation

**Overall:** Evidence base primarily traditional use (Level B/C in formula context); pharmacological mechanisms well-characterized; limited high-quality independent RCT data for this herb specifically.

## 7. Phenomenological Record

**The *Chu Ci* and botanical virtue (4th century BCE):**
The earliest textual references to 白芷 (*Bai Zhi*) appear not in medical but in poetic-philosophical literature. The *Chu Ci* (楚辭, Songs of Chu), attributed primarily to Qu Yuan (屈原, c. 340–278 BCE) and associated poets, uses *Bai Zhi* and other fragrant plants as symbols of moral cultivation. In the poem "Li Sao" (离骚, "Encountering Sorrow"), the poet describes adorning himself with fragrant plants — including *Bai Zhi* — as a metaphor for cultivating virtue amid corrupt court politics:

*"I have cultivated the white root and the orchid, / That which is fragrant is also upright."*

This poetic use precedes pharmacological documentation by three to four centuries, making *Bai Zhi* one of the few herbs in this Codex whose cultural-symbolic presence predates its medical record. The implication: the plant's aromatic quality was recognized and valued before its pharmacological applications were systematized.

**Shennong Bencao Jing — upper-grade classification:**
白芷 appears as an upper-grade herb in the *Bencao*, reflecting its classification as safe for long-term use without significant toxicity — appropriate given that furanocoumarins at typical culinary/medicinal doses do not present phototoxicity problems in internal use. The original text: "白芷，味辛，温。主女人漏下赤白，血闭阴肿，寒热，风头侵目泪出，长肌肤，润泽，可作面脂。" — "Bai Zhi, pungent, warm; governs women's red-and-white vaginal discharge, blood obstruction and vaginal swelling, alternating cold and heat, wind-headache penetrating to the eyes with tearing, nourishes muscle and skin, moistens and beautifies, can be made into facial cream."

The cosmetic application (facial cream) documented in the earliest Chinese pharmacopeia confirms the dual medicinal-cosmetic use that persists in Chinese beauty traditions today.

**Classical formula — 苍耳子散 (Cang Er Zi San / Xanthium Powder):**
Bai Zhi's most famous classical formula for nasal/sinus conditions: Cang Er Zi (*Xanthium sibiricum* fruit) + Xin Yi (*Magnolia* flower bud) + Bai Zhi + Bo He (*Mentha* leaf). This elegant four-herb formula addresses sinusitis (chronic and acute) and rhinitis through: Cang Er Zi (insecticidal/antibacterial, opens orifices) + Xin Yi (pungent, aromatic, anti-allergic) + Bai Zhi (anti-inflammatory/antimicrobial, pain-relieving) + Bo He (cooling, anti-inflammatory, decongestant). The formula encodes evidence-based pharmacological coverage of sinusitis pathophysiology in a classical herbal combination.

**Cosmetic tradition — 白芷散 (Bai Zhi San) and Tang Dynasty beauty:**
Chinese palace records from the Tang Dynasty (618–907 CE) document extensive use of 白芷散 (Bai Zhi Powder) as a facial brightener and blemish treatment. The compound, made with Bai Zhi root powder mixed with other herbs and applied as a facial mask, was prescribed in imperial medical texts for "making the face lustrous" (令面色光泽). This represents the earliest documented systematic use of furanocoumarin-bearing plant materials for cosmetic skin treatment — the modern use of kojic acid and other tyrosinase inhibitors in skin-brightening cosmetics represents a pharmacological lineage traceable to Tang Dynasty Bai Zhi preparations.

## 8. Practical Application

**Clinical prescribing principles (TCM):**
- Standard dose: 3–10g dried root in decoction daily; start lower and assess response
- Key indications: frontal headache and facial pain; sinusitis (wind-cold or wind-heat type with yellow-green discharge); rhinorrhea; toothache (especially upper teeth, corresponding to maxillary sinus/trigeminal territory); skin infections and non-healing sores; leucorrhea (white vaginal discharge)
- TCM pattern: wind-cold or wind-heat attacking the upper body and face; associated with nasal obstruction, headache, facial pain

**Contraindications:**
- **Yin deficiency with heat:** Warming/pungent nature can aggravate; avoid in patients with chronic dry throat, night sweats, afternoon fever
- **Blood deficiency/excessive bleeding:** Not a blood-nourishing herb; does not address blood deficiency conditions; bleeding disorders — anti-inflammatory activity may affect coagulation at high doses
- **Prolonged high-dose use:** Furanocoumarin accumulation risk; monitor for skin photosensitivity in patients with extended sun exposure

**Drug interactions:**
- **CYP3A4 inhibition:** Imperatorin inhibits CYP3A4 in vitro → potential interactions with drugs metabolized by CYP3A4 (many medications including some HIV drugs, immunosuppressants, statins, calcium channel blockers); clinical significance at typical herbal doses uncertain but monitor in polypharmacy patients
- **Anticoagulants:** Coumarins (minor anticoagulant potential); at food doses, clinically negligible; at high medicinal doses, INR monitoring in warfarin patients

**External applications:**
- Bai Zhi powder (finely ground root): facial mask for oily skin, acne, hyperpigmentation; mix with honey, rosewater, or aloe vera gel; leave 15–20min; rinse; avoid strong sun exposure after application (furanocoumarin + UV = phototoxicity even at cosmetic doses)
- Nasal steam: Bai Zhi + Xin Yi (magnolia bud) + menthol crystals in hot water; inhale steam for 10 min for sinus congestion — aromatic volatile compounds deliver anti-inflammatory and secretolytic effects directly to nasal mucosa

**Preparation:**
- Decoction: 3–10g in 300ml water, simmer 20 minutes covered; the volatile monoterpenes are partially lost in open simmering; cover the pot
- Granule extract: standardized; convenient; may specify furanocoumarin content
- Tincture (60% ethanol, 1:5): 2–4ml 3× daily; good furanocoumarin extraction

**Energetic classification:**
- Tier: **◉ Qi** — primarily affects Qi-level defensive circulation and surface wind-dispersal; does not tonify blood or constitutional Jing; mobilizes defensive Qi to expel wind-cold/heat invasion; pain relief operates at Qi/channel level (通则不痛 — when there is free flow there is no pain)
- Thermal nature: warm (温); flavor: pungent (辛); organ meridians: Lung (上部 — upper body, face), Stomach, Large Intestine
- Classical characterization: 解表散风，通窍止痛，燥湿止带，消肿排脓 — releases the surface/expels wind; opens orifices and stops pain; dries dampness and stops leucorrhea; disperses swelling and expels pus
