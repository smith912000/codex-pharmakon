# Jet (gagates) — Gagates Lithos (Γαγάτης λίθος), "Gagate" in the translator's gloss (Dioscorides 5-146)
## *A black, light, quickly kindled stone smelling of bitumen; the translator's suggestion is "Gagate — Jet or Agate", two different materials* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek Tradition (DMM).** The main locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-146 *GAGATES LITHOS* in the 2000 English translation (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10593-10599; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 919, "GAGATES LITHOS, 5-146 (line 10593), Gagate — Jet or Agate, mineral"; list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 872, "Gagates Lithos, , mineral, DMM V, id_uncertain yes"; book index "GAGATES LITHOS 818", line 12134). The translator's heading is "SUGGESTED: Gagate — Jet or Agate."

Dioscorides' account, read in full, is:

**Choice.** "Gagates is preferred which is kindled quickly and smells like bitumen. It is commonly black, unattractive, crusty and very light" (lines 10595-10599). The tests are by fire, smell, colour and weight.

**Actions.** "It softens and dissolves. If the smoke is inhaled it induces epilepsy and calms women who are hysterical. It is mixed with medicines for gout and those to remove fatigue, and smoke from it drives away snakes" (line 10599).

**Place.** "It is found in Cilicia at a certain fall of the river flowing into the sea near the city called Plagiopolis. The place and the river at the mouth of which these stones are found are called Gagas" (line 10599). The translator's place-note at line 3484 reads "GAGAS, river mouth — Gagae, a town on the coast of Lycia, east of Myra, and the source of gagate, or jet, stone", which gives Lycia where the chapter text says Cilicia. [USER: verify]

**Parenthetical report.** The chapter ends with a passage set in parentheses in this translation: "(They say that it discerns holy suffering, for if smelt when carried down to the earth they fall suddenly. Perfumed, it heals hidden disorders of women if they take in the vapour.)" (line 10599). It is reported as hearsay ("They say") and Dioscorides does not vouch for it. Why the translation sets it in parentheses was not read. Recording the report is not endorsing it.

**Neighbouring chapter.** 5-147 *Thrakias Lithos* (line 10602), the Thracian stone, "can perform the same cures as gagates [above] and it is said to be set on fire with water and quenched with oil, which is also done with asphalt." The chapter is marked "UNKNOWN" by the translator.

**Identity.** The translator offers "Jet or Agate". These are not one material: jet is a hard coal-like organic stone, agate a banded quartz. The chapter's tests (quick to catch fire, bitumen smell, very light, black) fit a combustible organic stone and not a quartz, which is the reason the entry is named for jet; but that is a reading of the chapter's description, and the translator does not choose. The list row marks the identification uncertain. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Stated generally. Jet is a dense, hard, black form of fossil wood (a type of lignite or coal), so it is mostly carbonaceous organic matter, which fits the chapter's "kindled quickly" and bitumen smell; agate, by contrast, is silica and does not burn. The text gives no composition. Burning any carbonaceous material produces smoke containing carbon monoxide and other combustion products.

---

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records an inhaled smoke said to bring on epilepsy or to calm hysterical women, an ingredient in gout medicines and fatigue medicines, and a smoke said to drive off snakes. Inhaling the smoke of a burning mineral to provoke a seizure or to quiet a condition is a hazard, not a practice, and the sources give no protocol. No modern protocol is supplied.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives the form of use but no quantity: the stone is burnt and its "smoke is inhaled"; it "is mixed with medicines for gout and those to remove fatigue"; the parenthetical report speaks of smelling it and of taking in a perfumed vapour (line 10599). No preparation beyond burning is described. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers is a discipline of attention, and this chapter shows it: the best stone is picked by how quickly it kindles, by its smell and by its weight, and the source place is named. He also records amuletic or diagnostic uses, but as report ("They say"), set apart from his own account, which is the pattern the spec describes: recording is not endorsing. The chapter is therapeutic and no cultivation status is assigned to the stone.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here for a cultivator to take or burn. What carries over is the habit of testing a material by simple means (fire, smell, weight) and of separating what an author states from what "they say". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Smoke inhalation:** the chapter's main use is to inhale the smoke; smoke from burning carbonaceous material is a respiratory hazard and carbon monoxide is poisonous, especially in closed rooms.
- **Epilepsy:** the text says the smoke "induces epilepsy"; nothing here supports deliberately provoking a seizure, and people with seizure disorders should avoid it.
- **Gynaecological use:** the vapour treatments for "hidden disorders of women" are unsupported and not recommended.
- **Identity:** "jet or agate" are different materials; the name cannot guarantee what is meant.
- **Legal and conservation status:** jet is a collected and traded gem material in some places and regulated at some sites; not retrieved here. [USER: verify]
