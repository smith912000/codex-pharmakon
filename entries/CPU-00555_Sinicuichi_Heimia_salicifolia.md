# Sinicuichi

## *Heimia salicifolia* (Kunth) Link | Family: Lythraceae

**Tradition Tags:** BM, SA

**Energetic Classification:** Cool, relaxant, descending. Sedative-hypotensive in tendency; the tradition calls it solar, the pharmacology reads lunar.

**Training Phase:** Intermediate — Discernment / Reputation Testing

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Heimia salicifolia* is a willow-leaved shrub of the loosestrife family, native from the southwestern United States through Mexico and Central America into South America as far as Argentina. It is a riverside and wet-ditch plant — unremarkable to look at, yellow-flowered, common. It occupies a strange position in the literature: famous, and almost unstudied.

This entry exists partly to correct the record. Sinicuichi is one of the most confidently described entheogens in popular ethnobotanical writing and one of the least substantiated. Read it as an exercise in discernment.

**Aztec / Nahua — *tonatiuh yxiuh*, "the sun herb"** [USER: verify — the Nahuatl name and its attachment to *H. salicifolia* circulate widely in the ethnobotanical literature but the primary-source chain is not solid; confirm against a Nahuatl specialist before printing as settled]

The name given in most sources is *tonatiuh yxiuh* — the herb of Tonatiuh, the sun. The reported classical use is a fermented preparation of wilted leaves, taken as an intoxicant with divinatory or mnemonic significance; the plant is repeatedly linked in the secondary literature to a claimed capacity to recall distant or ancestral memory.

Classical indication (as reported): fermented leaf-juice as an inebriant; the plant also credited with febrifuge and diuretic use.

Doctrine: solar. The plant is said to induce a suffusing golden or yellow light in the visual field, and to bring the past into the present — a memory-plant, a plant that shines backwards. This doctrine is the whole reason sinicuichi is famous.

**Handle the "sun herb" doctrine with care.** The linkage of a specific Nahuatl name to a specific binomial across four centuries of colonial transmission, popular ethnobotany, and 20th-century enthusiast literature is exactly the kind of chain where an attractive claim gets laundered into a fact. The doctrine may well be genuine. The evidential chain, as far as this entry can honestly represent it, is not verified.

**Mexican & Central American folk medicine (BM) — *sinicuichi*, *sinicuiche*, *hierba de la vida*, *abre-o-sol*, "sun opener"**

The living folk use is far better attested than the entheogenic one, and far more modest. Aerial parts are used as a decoction across a broad indication set: as a febrifuge, a diuretic, a wound wash, and for gynaecological complaints. The Brazilian *abre-o-sol* ("sun opener") is where the English trade-name "Sun Opener" comes from.

Classical indication: decoction of aerial parts for fever, inflammation, and as a diuretic.

Doctrine: cooling and opening. Note that this is a plain anti-inflammatory/antipyretic doctrine — no visions in it at all.

**South American (Argentine) folk medicine (SA)**

At the southern end of its range *H. salicifolia* appears in Argentine folk pharmacopoeia as an aerial-part remedy, and it survives contact with modern screening: a systematic screen of 132 Argentine folk-medicinal samples against *Salmonella typhi* found *H. salicifolia* aerial parts among the more active extracts (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0378-8741(94)90097-3)). Whatever else is uncertain about this plant, its folk-medicinal use in the south is real, documented, and not about visions.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

The solar doctrine makes a specific, testable, phenomenological claim: *this plant turns the world gold and bends sound.* The classic description — reported and re-reported across the popular literature — is of a suffusing yellow light, a pleasant physical heaviness, and a distinctive auditory distortion in which sounds seem distant, attenuated, and pitch-shifted, as though heard from far away.

**The honest position: this reputation is poorly substantiated.** The auditory-distortion effect is the plant's entire claim to fame, and attempts to reproduce it have largely failed. The effect appears in the literature primarily by citation rather than by demonstration — later sources repeating earlier sources, with the original observations resting on a narrow base. Researchers and self-experimenters working with the plant have not reliably reproduced the golden-light/auditory-shift syndrome. This entry does not have, and does not pretend to have, a controlled human study establishing it. There is no such study.

What we do have from this run is a single forensic case report, and it is instructive. A 26-year-old man drank an unmeasured quantity of sinicuichi tea he had fermented for 24 hours with yeast and sugar, and presented at hospital near midnight with **muscle pain of the extremities and tongue and slightly raised temperature** — not with golden light, not with distorted hearing. Five *Heimia* alkaloids were identified in the plant material by LC-MS/MS and all five confirmed in his serum, so ingestion and absorption are not in doubt (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.forsciint.2008.05.009)). He was treated with dimenhydrinate and released. The one modern documented case of a person absorbing a serious dose of this plant produced an unpleasant somatic syndrome, not the celebrated one.

So what *is* the chemistry doing? The plant's quinolizidine alkaloids — **cryogenine** (identical to **vertine**) and **nesodine** among them — have a defensible pharmacology, and it is a *sedative and anti-inflammatory* pharmacology, not a psychedelic one. Cryogenine and nesodine inhibit prostaglandin synthetase at roughly 2.5× and 2.2× the potency of aspirin in a bovine seminal vesicle preparation, though only a fraction of indomethacin's potency; the authors explicitly proposed this as the explanation for the plant's traditional medicinal use in the Americas (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0378-8741(86)90153-4)). Cryogenine's name is itself a doctrine-in-miniature — it was named for a hypothermic effect, and the mid-century pharmacodynamic work on it examined blood pressure, carotid sinus reflex, and nictitating membrane responses in dogs and rats (PubMed; PMID 14204497, no DOI available for this 1964 record).

**Map the doctrine honestly onto that chemistry:**

- The *folk* doctrine — cooling, febrifuge, anti-inflammatory, diuretic — maps cleanly onto COX inhibition and a hypotensive/hypothermic alkaloid. This is a coherent, well-fitted doctrine.
- The *solar* doctrine — golden light, distant sound, ancestral memory — maps onto nothing in the retrieved pharmacology. Quinolizidine alkaloids with a sedative, hypotensive, prostaglandin-inhibiting profile do not obviously produce visual and auditory distortion. A heavy, cool, sedated, hypotensive state *could* plausibly be narrated as "sounds seem far away" — that is a common feature of near-syncope — but that is a deflation of the doctrine, not a confirmation of it.

The likeliest honest reading: sinicuichi is a real folk anti-inflammatory whose entheogenic reputation was built on a thin observational base, amplified by repetition, and preserved because it is a beautiful story. The book should say so.

### Active Components

- **Quinolizidine alkaloids** — the defining chemistry. **Cryogenine** (= **vertine**), **lyfoline**, **lythrine**, **nesodine**, **heimine**, and a substantial set of minor congeners. Total alkaloid content is low.
- **Biphenylquinolizidine lactones** — the structurally distinctive class. In vitro-grown shoots of *H. salicifolia* yield vertine, lythrine, and lyfoline, along with ester alkaloids and phenylquinolizidinols; notably, **alkaloid synthesis was not detectable in heterotrophic callus or suspension culture** — only in shoots (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/np50037a006)). Alkaloid production in this plant requires a photosynthesising, differentiated shoot. That is a cultivator-relevant fact, and a deep one.
- **Phenylquinolizidines in seedlings** — isomeric 2-hydroxy-4-(3-hydroxy-4-methoxyphenyl)quinolizidines are present within 5–10 days of germination, indicating the biosynthetic pathway runs from the very start of the plant's life (PubMed; PMID 1221246, no DOI available).
- **Synthetic tractability** — related biphenylquinolizidine alkaloids from the family have been made by asymmetric total synthesis (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.orglett.9b02962)), so the class is well characterised chemically even where it is not characterised pharmacologically.

Note the asymmetry: the chemistry of this plant is known in considerable structural detail, and its human pharmacology is nearly unknown. That gap is where the folklore lives.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Sinicuichi's place in the training sequence is **Discernment**. It is included precisely because its reputation exceeds its evidence, and the practitioner needs one clear case of that pattern held in the hand.

Do not approach this plant expecting the golden light. If you approach it expecting the golden light you will probably report the golden light, and you will have learned nothing except how suggestible you are — which is, admittedly, the lesson.

The defensible use cases:

1. **As a cultivator's subject.** It grows readily, it is legal in most jurisdictions, and its alkaloid biosynthesis is unusually well documented. Growing it teaches more than drinking it.
2. **As a mild folk anti-inflammatory / febrifuge**, in the tradition of its actual living use, at conservative doses. Cool, heavy, sedative. Unglamorous.
3. **As a reputation-testing exercise.** Compare the popular literature's description of this plant against what this entry's Scientific Correlation section can actually support. The gap is the teaching.

It is not a reliable entheogen. It should not be scheduled into any practice that depends on a predictable altered state, because it does not deliver one.

### Cultivation Notes

*Heimia salicifolia* is one of the easier entheogen-adjacent plants to grow, which is part of why it circulates.

Propagate from seed (very fine — surface-sow, do not bury, keep moist and bright) or from softwood cuttings. It wants full sun, consistent water, and tolerates a wide pH range; in its native range it is a ditch and streambank plant, so it will forgive wet feet that would kill a Mediterranean herb. Hardy to roughly USDA zone 7–8 with dieback and regrowth; it behaves as a herbaceous perennial where winters bite and as a shrub where they do not. In containers it is unfussy.

Two cultivator's cautions worth printing:

- **The plant is a confirmed alternative host for crapemyrtle bark scale (*Acanthococcus lagerstroemiae*).** A feeding-preference study across 49 plant species and cultivars confirmed CMBS infestation on *Heimia*, alongside apple and several pomegranate cultivars — though at levels much lower than on *Lagerstroemia* (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/insects11070399)). If you have crapemyrtles, know this before you plant.
- **Tissue culture will not make alkaloid.** As above: callus and suspension culture produce no detectable alkaloid; only in-vitro-grown *shoots* do ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/np50037a006)). Whatever the plant is doing chemically, it is tied to the differentiated, green, light-driven shoot. Do not expect a cell culture shortcut, and do not expect etiolated growth to be equivalent to sun-grown.

Harvest aerial parts; the traditional preparation uses wilted rather than fresh leaves, which is a strong hint that the tradition believed something changes on standing. What changes, chemically, is not established.

### Scientific Correlation

Graded honestly. This is a data-sparse plant and the entry says so plainly.

**Moderate (in vitro, direct, mechanistically apt) — prostaglandin synthetase inhibition.** Cryogenine and nesodine inhibited prostaglandin synthetase at 2.48× and 2.24× aspirin's potency respectively, versus indomethacin at 2800× and phenylbutazone at 8.75× (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0378-8741(86)90153-4)). Read this carefully: the alkaloids beat aspirin and are dwarfed by indomethacin. This supports the *folk anti-inflammatory* doctrine and nothing else. It is the strongest correlation in the entry, and it corroborates the boring claim, not the famous one.

**Moderate (in vitro, screening) — antibacterial.** Aerial-part extract among the more active of 132 Argentine folk-medicine samples against *Salmonella typhi* by agar-well diffusion ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0378-8741(94)90097-3)). A screen, not a therapy.

**Established (analytical) — the alkaloids are real and they are absorbed.** Five *Heimia* alkaloids identified in plant material and confirmed in human serum after ingestion ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.forsciint.2008.05.009)). Also well established: the phytochemistry of the biphenylquinolizidines ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/np50037a006); PMID 1221246) and their synthetic accessibility ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.orglett.9b02962)).

**Weak / historical — cardiovascular and hypothermic pharmacodynamics.** Mid-century animal work on cryogenine examined blood pressure, carotid sinus reflex, and nictitating membrane in dogs and rats (PubMed; PMID 14204497 — 1964, no DOI, abstract not available in the retrieved record). Cited here as the origin of the sedative/hypotensive reputation, not as modern evidence. An early monograph on the species exists in Dutch (PMID 13590903, 1958). Neither can be independently assessed from what was retrieved.

**NULL / ABSENT — the auditory-distortion and golden-light syndrome.** No study retrieved this run demonstrates it. No controlled human trial of this plant exists in the retrieved literature. The one modern documented human intoxication produced myalgia of the limbs and tongue plus mild fever, and required an antiemetic — a somatic, unpleasant, non-psychedelic presentation ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.forsciint.2008.05.009)). The famous effect is, on the retrieved evidence, unsubstantiated.

**NULL / ABSENT — the memory doctrine.** Nothing retrieved supports any mnemonic, nootropic, or recall-enhancing effect. Not weakly supported: *unsupported*.

**Net grade: the mundane folk doctrine (cooling, anti-inflammatory, febrifuge) is preclinically plausible via COX inhibition. The celebrated entheogenic doctrine is unsubstantiated and should be treated as folklore until someone does the work.**

### Traditional Energetic Classification

The tradition assigns it to the sun — *tonatiuh yxiuh*, *abre-o-sol*. The pharmacology assigns it the opposite: cool, heavy, descending, hypotensive, hypothermic (the name *cryogenine* is literally built from that observation). Sinicuichi is the book's clearest case of a solar name on a lunar plant.

Taste: bitter, slightly astringent. Direction: downward and inward. Tissue state addressed: heat and irritation. Where the folk tradition and the alkaloid chemistry agree, they agree that this plant *cools*.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding — avoid absolutely.** Quinolizidine alkaloids as a class carry teratogenic concern, and *H. salicifolia* has folk gynaecological use, which is itself a warning flag. There is no safety data. Do not use.
- **Alkaloid toxicity.** These are alkaloids with cardiovascular and hypothermic activity and no established human dose. The single documented modern intoxication produced myalgia of the extremities and tongue, raised temperature, and required hospital attendance ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.forsciint.2008.05.009)). Bradycardia and hypotension are reported in the descriptive literature. This is not a safe plant by virtue of being obscure; it is an unassessed plant.
- **Hypotension / bradycardia / cardiac medication.** The hypotensive tendency is the most consistent thread in the animal literature. Anyone on antihypertensives, rate-control agents, or with baseline low blood pressure should not use it.
- **Fermented preparations are the highest-risk form.** The one documented case involved a home ferment with added yeast and sugar over 24 hours. An uncontrolled ferment of an alkaloid-bearing plant has unknown and variable composition. The traditional preparation is also the most dangerous one.
- **Chronic use — unknown and unassessed.** No chronic toxicity data was retrieved. The descriptive literature contains claims of cumulative or chronic effects [USER: verify] that this entry cannot substantiate in either direction. Absence of evidence here is genuinely absence of evidence.
- **Do not combine with NSAIDs uncritically.** If cryogenine and nesodine really are prostaglandin synthetase inhibitors at ~2.5× aspirin, the stacking concern is not theoretical hand-waving — it follows directly from the one solid mechanism the plant has.
- **Expectation as a hazard.** A person who takes an unmeasured dose of an unstudied alkaloid plant chasing a reputation that may not be real will escalate. That is the actual mechanism by which this plant hurts people.
