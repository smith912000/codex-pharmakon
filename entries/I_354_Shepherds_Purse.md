# I-354: Capsella bursa-pastoris (Shepherd's Purse)

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary name:** Shepherd's purse  
**Botanical name:** *Capsella bursa-pastoris* (L.) Medik.  
**Family:** Brassicaceae (Cruciferae)  
**Synonyms:** Lady's purse, shovelweed, witches' pouches, mother's heart (UK), pickpocket (folk name), shepherd's bag  
**Traditional names:** Bourse-à-pasteur (French — "shepherd's purse"); Hirtentäschel (German — "shepherd's little pocket"); Bolsa del pastor (Spanish); Nazuna (Japanese なずな); Ji cai (Chinese 荠菜 — "shepherd's purse greens"); Bursa pastoris (historical Latin pharmacy); Kasni (Hindi)

**Botanical description:** Annual or winter annual herb 10–50 cm; cosmopolitan weed of disturbed ground worldwide; basal leaves in rosette, lyrate-pinnatifid to entire; stem leaves arrow-shaped (sagittate), clasping; flowers tiny, white, 4-petaled; CHARACTERISTIC FRUIT: triangular to heart-shaped silicle ("shepherd's purse") — the compressed, triangular fruit pod is the diagnostic feature and name origin; seeds small, numerous, oily.

**Significance of cosmopolitan distribution:** One of the most widespread weeds on Earth — found on all inhabited continents; appears in gardens, roadsides, agricultural fields globally. This ubiquity means it has been independently discovered and used by many cultures.

**Part used:** Aerial parts including flowers (harvested before/during flowering); traditional fresh plant tincture or dried herb; seeds (traditional food in China)

## 3. Convergence Analysis

**Pattern:** Shepherd's purse is the first plant in the Codex to achieve its primary medicinal reputation from hemostatic (bleeding-stopping) activity rather than the anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, or nervous system mechanisms that characterize most Volume I entries.

The hemostatic reputation is ancient: European herbalists from Dioscorides onwards recommended shepherd's purse for nosebleeds, menorrhagia (heavy menstrual bleeding), and wound bleeding. The Chinese tradition independently identified ji cai as a hemostatic food-medicine. The convergence on hemostasis is pharmacologically logical: shepherd's purse contains oxalic acid and tannic acids (astringent vascular protein precipitation) and possibly choline-related compounds that affect uterine smooth muscle tone.

The primary modern use remains gynecological: shepherd's purse is widely used in European herbal medicine for menorrhagia and postpartum hemorrhage. The 20th-century herbal tradition (Weiss, Hoffmann) elevated shepherd's purse as a key hemostatic herb; the compound bursine (a choline derivative) was proposed as the uterotonic active constituent, though its isolation and characterization remains incomplete in the modern literature.

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

### Hemostatic/Uterotonic Mechanisms (primary — debated)

**Proposed mechanisms for hemostatic activity:**
1. **Astringent tannin effect:** protein precipitation on damaged blood vessels → vasoconstriction (same mechanism as oak bark, witch hazel)
2. **Fumaric acid:** uterine smooth muscle stimulation; direct oxytocic-like activity proposed but not definitively established via receptor pharmacology
3. **Choline/acetylcholine:** uterine smooth muscle receptor stimulation (M3 muscarinic) → uterine contraction → reduced blood loss; very unstable in plant material
4. **Vitamin K₁ contribution:** supports coagulation cascade at micro-nutritional level

**Critical caveat:** The hemostatic mechanism of shepherd's purse remains pharmacologically incompletely characterized. The in vitro and in vivo animal evidence is consistent with hemostatic effect, but precise receptor-level mechanisms and primary active constituents have not been definitively established in the modern pharmacological literature. This places shepherd's purse in a different epistemic category from entries where the mechanism is fully characterized (e.g., capsaicin/TRPV1, morphine/μ-opioid, atropine/muscarinic).

### Anti-inflammatory (flavonoid fraction)
- Rutin: capillary protective; reduces capillary fragility → reduced bleeding tendency
- Quercetin: NF-κB inhibition; prostaglandin synthesis reduction
- This mechanism provides a secondary rationale for menorrhagia treatment: reduced prostaglandin-driven uterine hyperstimulation

### Diuretic (minor)
- Traditional "cooling" diuretic effect; mechanism uncertain; possibly flavonoid-mediated renal vasodilation

## 6. Clinical Evidence

### Menorrhagia (Heavy Menstrual Bleeding) — Level C

- No high-quality RCTs for shepherd's purse as monotherapy
- Traditional European herbal medicine (ESCOP; German Commission E declined approval due to insufficient evidence, though listed in many European herbal references)
- Multiple observational case reports: reduction in menstrual blood loss with shepherd's purse tincture/tea
- German herbalist Rudolf Weiss (*Herbal Medicine*, 1988): shepherd's purse is the herbalist's "most important hemostatic for uterine and bladder bleeding"
- Evidence grade: Level C — traditional use, plausible mechanism, no RCT confirmation

### Postpartum Hemorrhage — Level D

- Traditional midwifery use across Europe for postpartum hemorrhage
- Extremely high-stakes indication — NO substitute for medical intervention (oxytocin, tranexamic acid, surgical management) in true PPH
- No clinical trials; historical midwifery use only
- **CRITICAL NOTE:** In ANY true postpartum hemorrhage, SEEK EMERGENCY MEDICAL CARE immediately; shepherd's purse is not an appropriate treatment for acute PPH

### Nosebleed (Epistaxis) — Level C

- Traditional topical application (cotton pad soaked in shepherd's purse juice) for epistaxis
- Astringent tannin + possible vasoconstriction: pharmacologically plausible
- No RCTs; consistent traditional use; appropriate as supportive measure (not replacing medical treatment for recurrent/severe epistaxis)

## 7. Phenomenological Record

### Ji Cai — Chinese Spring Festival Food
In China, shepherd's purse (ji cai, 荠菜) is a culturally beloved spring wild vegetable — one of the "seven spring herbs" consumed on the morning of the Qi Cao Festival (January 7th by lunar calendar, one week after New Year) for health in the coming year. Ji cai is used in dumplings (ji cai jiaozi), wontons, and mixed with tofu (ji cai doufu) — the delicate, slightly peppery flavor making it a spring delicacy. The practice of eating seven spring herbs reflects ancient Chinese seasonal pharmacology: spring greens after winter provided vitamin C, minerals, and flavonoids to support health after cold months. This is one of the few examples in the Codex where a medicinal plant is simultaneously a dietary staple at population scale on specific calendar dates.

### Nazuna — Japanese New Year's Seven-Herb Porridge
Similarly in Japan, nazuna (shepherd's purse) is one of the traditional *nanakusa* (seven spring herbs) eaten in *nanakusa-gayu* (seven-herb rice porridge) on January 7th — the same tradition as the Chinese Ji Cao Festival, reflecting historical Chinese-Japanese cultural exchange. The herbs: seri (water parsley), nazuna (shepherd's purse), gogyo (cudweed), hakobera (chickweed), hotokenoza (henbit), suzuna (turnip), suzushiro (daikon). The porridge tradition is documented to 1,000+ BCE in Japan. Modern supermarkets sell packaged nanakusa herb sets every January; millions of Japanese households eat the porridge. The pharmacological logic: seven different Brassicaceae/Apiaceae spring greens provide diverse glucosinolates, vitamin C, and minerals after winter.

### The Medicine Chest Weed
Shepherd's purse is emblematic of a category the Codex designates "medicine chest weeds" — plants of minimal ecological distinction, occurring everywhere, with modest but real pharmacological activity. These plants were historically the first-line treatment for generations of peasants and subsistence farmers who lacked access to specialized pharmacies: the bleeding stopped more quickly with shepherd's purse pressed to a wound, the heavy menstrual flow reduced with shepherd's purse tea. The evidence was empirical, the mechanism later deduced, and the effect real if modest. This pattern — widespread traditional use for a consistent indication, plausible mechanism, absent modern RCT — characterizes a significant fraction of European folk medicine.

## 8. Practical Application

### Menorrhagia (Heavy Menstrual Bleeding) — Mild to Moderate Cases

**Before use:** Rule out underlying pathology requiring medical evaluation:
- Fibroids, endometrial polyps, endometriosis, coagulopathy, cancer → MUST be medically evaluated
- Shepherd's purse appropriate only for otherwise well-investigated, mild-to-moderate idiopathic menorrhagia

**Dosing:**
- Fresh plant tincture (1:2): 2–4 mL 3× daily in menstrual period
- Dried herb tea: 5–10g per cup, 2–3 cups/day
- Begin 2–3 days before expected menstrual start; continue through heavy days

**Combination (traditional):**
- Shepherd's purse + yarrow (*Achillea millefolium*) + lady's mantle (*Alchemilla vulgaris*): classic European hemostatic combination for menorrhagia; synergistic traditional rationale

### Nosebleed First Aid

**Cotton soaked in fresh shepherd's purse juice** (or strong tea): apply to nostril; hold for 5–10 minutes
- Appropriate for simple, minor epistaxis only
- Persistent, recurrent, or severe nosebleeds → ENT evaluation required

### Safety and Contraindications

**Pregnancy — ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION:**
- Shepherd's purse stimulates uterine smooth muscle (fumaric acid, choline-related compounds)
- Historical use as an emmenagogue (to induce menstruation)
- Potential for miscarriage or preterm labor at medicinal doses
- AVOID all medicinal doses in pregnancy; cooking (Chinese culinary tradition) may reduce uterotonic compound activity but formal safety data absent

**Lactation:** Avoid medicinal doses; culinary food preparation amounts uncertain safety; insufficient data

**Thyroid disease:** Minor glucosinolate content → monitor thyroid in hypothyroid patients using regular large doses

**Drug Interactions:**
- **Oxytocin:** additive uterotonic effect → AVOID concurrent use (hospital context)
- **Warfarin:** vitamin K₁ contribution; monitor INR with high/inconsistent intake

### Energetic Classification
**Primary:** ◉ Qi (functional) — hemostatic and uterotonic properties relate to regulating blood circulation and controlling its movement (Chinese: zhi xue = stop bleeding)  
**Secondary:** None distinctive
