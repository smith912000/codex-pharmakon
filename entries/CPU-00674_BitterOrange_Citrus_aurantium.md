# Bitter Orange / Seville Orange / Naranj / Zhi Shi–Zhi Ke

## *Citrus aurantium* L. | Family: Rutaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM, SBJ, SS

**Energetic Classification:** Cool-and-dry to temperate in the classical humoral reckoning (the sour peel and juice "cooling," the aromatic oil "subtly warming and thinning"); in the Kenetica frame an *aromatic mover* — a substance whose virtue is to unstick, circulate, and lift, mirrored chemically by both a volatile carminative fraction and a stimulant amine.

**Training Phase:** Foundational for the aromatic/culinary preparations (peel, flower water, neroli); the standardised synephrine extract is an advanced-caution material, not a beginner practice.

---

### Origin & Tradition

Bitter orange is the sour, thick-skinned citrus behind marmalade, Cointreau, **neroli** oil (from the blossom) and **petitgrain** (from leaf/twig), and — in a completely different register — the immature dried fruit of Chinese medicine, **Zhi Shi** (枳實) and its slightly riper form **Zhi Ke** (枳殼). One plant thus straddles perfumery, the kitchen, classical humoral medicine and a modern weight-loss-supplement identity.

**Chinese (SBJ-adjacent / classical TCM).** The immature fruit *Zhi Shi* and mature peel *Zhi Ke* are core **qi-regulating (理气) / qi-moving** drugs. Doctrine: they "break stagnant qi," "guide qi downward," and disperse epigastric and abdominal fullness, distension, and food stagnation — a digestive/decongestant action. *Zhi Shi* is the stronger, more downward-driving of the pair. (Noted for completeness; the primary tag set for this entry is EP/AT/DMM/CM.)

**Greek (DMM — Dioscorides tradition).** The sour citrus of the eastern Mediterranean was valued for the fragrant, "cordial" peel and the sharp juice: the aromatic rind as a stomach-strengthening carminative and breath-sweetener, the sour juice as a cooling, thirst-quenching, mildly astringent agent. The classical reading splits the fruit into a *cooling sour* part and a *warm aromatic* part — a division modern chemistry vindicates.

**Greco-Arabic (CM — Avicenna, *Canon*).** Under **nāranj / nārinj** the bitter orange is a settled part of the Arabic materia medica: the aromatic peel a warming, thinning carminative and cardiac "gladdener" (a *mufarriḥ*, cordial), the sour juice cooling and appetite-sharpening, the flower-water (neroli/*zahr*) a calming aromatic. Avicennian doctrine treats the peel's volatile virtue as "subtle" (penetrating, dispersing) — precisely the carminative essential-oil action.

**Egyptian (EP) & Mesopotamian (AT).** Citrus arrived in the Mediterranean relatively late, so a securely dated Ebers or Assyrian entry for *Citrus aurantium* specifically is **[USER: verify]** — aromatic-peel and sour-fruit uses are plausible within both aromatic pharmacopoeias but the species-level attribution is not philologically secured here. Both EP and AT tags are carried on tradition-list authority and should be treated as provisional.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** Across every tradition the doctrine is the same verb: **move**. Greek and Arabic sources make the peel a carminative that disperses trapped wind and "gladdens"; Chinese medicine makes the immature fruit a breaker of stagnant qi that drives fullness downward. Both are doctrines of *unsticking a stalled interior* — restoring flow to a congested middle. Kenetica maps this onto two distinct chemistries hiding in one fruit. The **aromatic-carminative** doctrine (disperse wind, gladden, calm) maps cleanly onto the **volatile terpene fraction** — limonene and linalool — whose real smooth-muscle and mild anxiolytic/aromatic effects are the material substrate of "gladdening" and "dispersing." The **qi-breaking / downward-driving / lipolytic** doctrine — the sharper, more forceful action ascribed to the immature *Zhi Shi* and later re-read by the supplement industry as "fat-burning" — maps onto **synephrine**, an adrenergic protoalkaloid that genuinely raises sympathetic tone. The symbolic caution the traditions themselves encode (that *Zhi Shi* is "strong" and "descending," to be used carefully) becomes, in pharmacology, the cardiovascular signal of an adrenergic amine. The mapping honours the doctrine without inflating it: "moving qi" is a real felt effect of a stimulant, but it is *not* evidence of fat metabolism.

### Active Components

- **Synephrine (p-synephrine)** — the principal protoalkaloid, a phenylethanolamine adrenergic agonist structurally related to (but weaker and more peripherally acting than) ephedrine. Concentrated in the immature fruit. The stimulant/"weight-loss" identity.
- **Octopamine** and **tyramine** — minor trace biogenic amines; adrenergic/vasoactive, present in a characteristic ratio to synephrine (~100:1:5 synephrine:octopamine:tyramine in authentic material) used as an authenticity fingerprint.
- **Limonene** — dominant peel monoterpene; carminative, aromatic.
- **Linalool** and **linalyl acetate** — key constituents of neroli/petitgrain; calming, aromatic.
- **Flavonoids** — hesperidin, naringin (the bitter principle), neohesperidin.
- **Furanocoumarins** (e.g. bergapten-type) — the "grapefruit-effect" fraction relevant to CYP3A4 drug interaction and photosensitivity.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Kenetica separates the fruit into its two honest identities. The **aromatic fraction** (peel, neroli hydrosol, petitgrain) is used as a genuine carminative and calming aromatic — settling a congested, "wind-full" digestion and lifting a low mood in the classical cordial sense. This is the safe, foundational use, well supported by the terpene chemistry and low-risk at culinary/aromatic doses. The **synephrine fraction** (standardised extract, immature-fruit *Zhi Shi* decoction) is filed as an *advanced-caution stimulant mover* — it does produce a real adrenergic lift and short-term appetite/energy effect that the qi-moving doctrine describes, but Kenetica explicitly refuses the "fat-burner" claim the supplement market attaches to it, because the weight-loss evidence does not hold up and the cardiovascular signal is real (see below). Use case: understand bitter orange as the archetype where a *true* traditional carminative and a *dubious* modern slimming claim ride on the same plant, and keep them separate.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the evidence cleanly splits the doctrine: the aromatic-carminative reading is plausible and low-risk, while the synephrine "weight-loss" reading is weak on efficacy and positive on cardiovascular signal.

- **Weight-loss efficacy — negative/null.** Koncz et al. (2022, *Nutrients*), a systematic review and meta-analysis of 18 articles (placebo-controlled human trials), found weight loss in the synephrine group **non-significant** after prolonged treatment, with **no effect on body-composition parameters** — while systolic and diastolic blood pressure rose significantly after prolonged use (SBP +6.37 mmHg; DBP +4.33 mmHg). Conclusion: synephrine tends to raise BP and heart rate, and there is **no evidence it facilitates weight loss.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14194019) (PMID 36235672). This is the decisive human evidence and it deflates the slimming claim.
- **Safety — a genuine but debated CV signal.** Stohs (2017, *Phytother Res*), a review of ~30 human studies, argues that at *commonly used doses* p-synephrine and bitter-orange extract do not produce significant cardiovascular effects and do not act as stimulants, cautioning that rodent adrenergic-binding data overstate human effects. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.5879) (PMID 28752649). Kenetica reports this honestly as the *counter-position*: the meta-analysis (Koncz 2022) found a real BP rise, while this review argues the signal is small at label doses — the truth sits in the tension, and the risk concentrates when synephrine is stacked with caffeine and other stimulants.
- **Mechanism plausibility (adrenergic/thermogenic).** Guimarães et al. (2024, *Front Nutr*) found *C. aurantium* and synephrine (a β-3-adrenergic-type agonist) improved brown-adipose-tissue thermogenic markers (UCP-1, PRDM16, PGC-1α, PPARγ) and normalised leptinemia in overfed *adolescent mice*, **without** elevating heart rate or blood pressure in that model. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2023.1278121) (PMID 38274208). This supports a *mechanistic* basis for the "moving/lipolytic" doctrine but is an **animal** model — it cannot be extrapolated to human weight loss, which the human meta-analysis found absent.
- **Product-quality caveat.** Koh et al. (2020, *J Pharm Biomed Anal*) found only ~42% (5/12) of *Citrus aurantium*-labelled pre-workout supplements had trace-amine ratios matching authentic reference material; some contained no detectable amines. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpba.2020.113746) (PMID 33190081). Label claims are unreliable — a reason to distrust both efficacy and dose figures on commercial products.

**Honest grade.** *Aromatic-carminative doctrine (limonene/linalool):* **plausible, low-risk, well-rationalised** by terpene chemistry (mechanistically supported; strong clinical trials specific to bitter-orange carminative endpoints were not retrieved this run — graded *plausible*, not *proven*). *Qi-moving / adrenergic "lift" (synephrine):* **real pharmacology, real CV signal.** *Weight-loss / fat-burner claim:* **not supported — human meta-analysis null; deflated.** **Null findings explicitly retained:** Koncz 2022 (no weight loss, no body-composition change) and Stohs 2017 (argues no significant CV effect at label dose) are both reported, and they disagree — that disagreement is the honest state of the evidence.

**Drug-interaction note (mechanistic, cite not retrieved this run — [USER: verify with a primary source]):** bitter orange's **furanocoumarins** are the same chemical class behind the grapefruit–drug interaction, inhibiting intestinal **CYP3A4** and potentially raising blood levels of CYP3A4-substrate drugs; the same and related coumarins drive **photosensitivity** with topical/EO use. These are well-established for the furanocoumarin class but a *C. aurantium*-specific primary paper was not pulled in this run and should be verified before citing.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Dual by nature: the **sour juice/whole fruit** cool-and-dry (astringent, thirst-quenching, appetite-sharpening); the **aromatic peel and flower** temperate-to-subtly-warming, thinning, dispersing, cordial (a *mufarriḥ* in Avicennian terms). The Chinese reading adds a *downward-moving, qi-breaking* vector to the immature fruit. Kenetica files the composite as an *aromatic mover with a stimulant tail* — gentle circulation from the terpenes, a sharper adrenergic push from synephrine that must be respected as cardiovascular, not metabolic-magic.

### Contraindications

- **⚠️ Cardiovascular — synephrine fraction.** Raises blood pressure and heart rate on prolonged use (Koncz 2022, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14194019)). Contraindicated in hypertension, arrhythmia, coronary disease, and for anyone with cardiovascular risk. **Do not combine with caffeine or other stimulants** — the risk signal concentrates in stimulant stacks (the typical pre-workout context).
- **⚠️ Drug interactions — furanocoumarin / CYP3A4 ("grapefruit-type").** May raise levels of CYP3A4-substrate medications (statins, some calcium-channel blockers, immunosuppressants, many others). Avoid the extract/high-peel intake with such drugs. **[USER: verify]** with a primary source before relying on specifics.
- **MAOI / sympathomimetic interaction.** Tyramine/octopamine content and adrenergic action contraindicate use with MAO inhibitors and other sympathomimetics.
- **⚠️ Photosensitivity.** Furanocoumarins in peel/petitgrain/neroli oils are phototoxic; avoid sun on treated skin after topical essential-oil use.
- **Pregnancy & breastfeeding:** avoid the stimulant extract and *Zhi Shi* (traditionally cautioned; adrenergic and qi-breaking action). Aromatic culinary use of peel is low-risk.
- **Thyroid disease, anxiety, insomnia:** the adrenergic fraction may aggravate. Discontinue before surgery (BP/HR and interaction risk).
- **Product unreliability:** commercial synephrine content and identity are frequently misrepresented (Koh 2020, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpba.2020.113746)) — do not trust label doses.
