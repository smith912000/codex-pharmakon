# Peucedanum praeruptorum — Guthup, rgu thub; "Hog's funnel" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Peucedanum praeruptorum, the first of three binomials the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints on one line (5316) in fourth place of its "G" scientific-name run, with the English name "Hog's funnel" (as printed), which the Codex reads by position against the phonetic name "Guthup" and the transliteration "rgu thub"; Clark 1995 prints a different plant, Scutellaria baicalensis, for rgu.thub* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and on a species name that only one of them prints, set against the Tibetan name by column position and not on one printed line.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Ebers, Assyrian, Badianus, Avicenna and Sushruta material on disk for *Peucedanum* found other species in Dioscorides' translators' lines (for example *Peucedanum officinale* at 3-92, line 7357, and *Peucedanum ostrithium* as printed, line 7238) and none called *praeruptorum*. The Shennong text (Yang 1998) has *Fang Kui* (*Radix Peucedani Japonici*, line 1825, `names.csv` line 65, matched at genus level only), which is another species and is not read as this drug. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 284) the key *Peucedanum praeruptorum* is taken from the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary line 5316 with the note "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". Line 5316 prints **three** binomials on one line ("Peucedanum praeruptorum Picris hieracioides Melilotus officinalis L."); the key is the **first** of the three on the line and the fourth of the block's eight scientific names. Clark does not print *Peucedanum praeruptorum*. Clark's only *Peucedanum* is the genus-level "Peucedanum sp. (white type)" under a different Tibetan name, *tang.kun* (line 1041), which is the subject of I_1254.

**Identity doubt.** This is the entry in the batch where the two editions look furthest apart. The glossary's pairing, "Guthup" with *Peucedanum praeruptorum*, is positional. Clark's *rgu.thub* (line 1021), in a spelling the Codex reads as the same name as "rgu thub", is *Scutellaria baicalensis*, and its action is different in kind: "cures all types of fevers", where Men-Tsee-Khang says that Guthup is an antidote to poison. Clark's chapter 21 poison class also lists *Scutellaria baicalensis* (twice, as printed), and the Men-Tsee-Khang poison group lists "Guthup". These point to Clark's identification for the Tibetan name; the glossary's English name "Hog's funnel" for the slot (apparently "fennel", an OCR or printing error that the Codex keeps as printed) is also the English name the glossary prints for "Tangkun" ("Hog fennel", line 5938), a different Tibetan name. The Codex does not choose. [USER: verify] The Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note (line 686) says that the botanical terms in their text "are based on the information procured from the Materia Medica Department of this institute"; it names no flora or earlier work.

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "G" block, lines 5303 to 5323).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. Eight phonetic names are printed (lines 5303 to 5306): "Gugul", "Gundrum", "Gurkum", "Guthup", "Gyakhur", "Gyapoe", "Gyasen" and "Gyashuk". The transliterations (lines 5308 to 5311) are "gugul", "rgun 'brum", "gur kum", "rgu thub", "rgya khur", "rgya spos", "rgya rsan" and "rgya shug". The scientific names (lines 5313 to 5317) are, in order, *Commiphora mukul*, *Vitis vinifera*, *Carthamus tinctorius*, then on one line "Peucedanum praeruptorum Picris hieracioides Melilotus officinalis L." (5316), and "Dolichos bijlorus Thysanolaena maxima" (5317). The English names (lines 5319 to 5323) are "Guggul", "Grape", "Saffron", "Hog's funnel", "Hawkweed", "Melilot", "Chinese broad bean" and "Tiger grass". Counted in order, "Guthup" is the fourth phonetic name, "rgu thub" the fourth transliteration, *Peucedanum praeruptorum* the fourth scientific name and "Hog's funnel" the fourth English name. The count is checked at the places where Clark's lines allow it: "Gugul" is first against *Commiphora mukul* and "Guggul", "Gundrum" second against *Vitis vinifera* and "Grape", "Gurkum" third against *Carthamus tinctorius* and "Saffron", and "Gyapoe" (*rgya spos*) sixth against *Melilotus officinalis* and "Melilot", as Clark's *rgya.spos* (line 1070, *Melilotus suaveolens*) allows, a count set out in I_1318. The glossary does not print the pairing in one row. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3433).* Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428): "Yugu shing joins cuts and cures hot disorder associated with poisoning. Tsed, Guthup and Dhumtak are antidotes to poison. Ajak heals fractured skulls and dries up chhuser." The sentence gives no Latin name, no part used and no preparation. The name is not in the taste groups of chapter 19 (lines 3204 to 3222) and not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, chapter 21 (lines 3604 and 3605).* In the chapter on the groups and the method of compounding medicine (contents, line 229), the group of medicines that cure poisoning begins, across lines 3604 and 3605: "La tsi, Bongnga karpo, Bongnga serpo, Bongnga marpo, Khungdher karpo and mukpo, Pawo karpo and serpo, Tsad, Nampar Guthup, Tak- / sha, Havo, ..." and ends "... are the group of medicines that cure poisoning". The text prints "Nampar Guthup" with no comma between the two words, and the index lists both "Guthup 207, 220" (line 7381) and "NamparGuthup 220" (line 7449). Whether "Nampar" and "Guthup" are one drug or two is not settled by the lines read; the glossary prints "Nampar" separately (line 5503, against *Senecio dianthus*, "White ragwort"). The Codex does not say which is meant here. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1021).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). It reads: "rgu.thub or glang.chen.chig.thub: Scutellaria baicalensis: [grows on shady, rocky hills and has a large 'elephant-like' root. It has small leaves and flowers and dark red umbrella-like fruit. The common type is the yellow type of dpah.wo (Scutellaria) with leaf and petiole, known as glang.chen, and has a huge taproot. It has bitter and astringent tastes, cool power and] cures all types of fevers." Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The description, the tastes and the power are therefore the commentary layer, and "cures all types of fevers" is outside the brackets, so it is the root-text layer as the brackets mark it. Clark's name index lists *rgu.thub* and *glang.chen.chig.thub* (line 1680). The neighbouring line 964 (*dpab.wo*) lists *Scutellaria baicalensis* as the second of three binomials for a different drug, which "neutralizes poison" and is a *Phytolacca* in the first and third. In Clark's chapter 21 ("The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines", heading at lines 1205 and 1206), the class of poison-neutralizing medicines (line 1212) lists "(yellow) Scutellaria baicalensis" and "Scutellaria". The Codex does not say that either of these is *rgu.thub*.

**Overlap.** I_1318 (*Melilotus suaveolens*, tag FMT) prints this "G" block and names *Peucedanum praeruptorum* as a neighbouring scientific name, and gives "Guthup" no entry. I_1254 (*Cnidium dahuricum*) treats *tang.kun*, whose Clark line prints "Peucedanum sp. (white type)", and quotes the glossary's "Hog fennel" at "Tangkun"; it is a different Tibetan name. I_1157 (*Peucedanum officinale*, Dioscorides 3-92) and I_1024 (*Athamanta cervaria*, Dioscorides 3-62, whose translators' line has "Hog Fennel") are Greek entries and do not cite the Tibetan texts. I_324 (Chinese Skullcap, *Scutellaria baicalensis*, tag SBJ) is an entry on Clark's plant for *rgu.thub* from the Shennong text and carries no FMT tag; the Codex does not read it as covering the Tibetan drug. No entry on *Peucedanum praeruptorum* or on "Guthup" was found. The spine has no row for Clark's *rgu.thub* line. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Peucedanum* is a genus of the carrot family (Apiaceae), and that plants of that family are generally known to produce aromatic oils and coumarins, some of which (furanocoumarins) make skin sensitive to sunlight. Nothing in the lines read says what the Tibetan drug contained, and the plant behind the name is uncertain between *Peucedanum* and *Scutellaria*. No statement about its composition is made. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Men-Tsee-Khang records that "Tsed, Guthup and Dhumtak are antidotes to poison" (line 3433) and lists the name in the group of medicines that cure poisoning (line 3605). Clark records, for *rgu.thub* under another binomial, that it "cures all types of fevers" (line 1021). These are the texts' claims in their own terms, and they differ in kind. They are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. Poisoning and fever need medical care, and no herb should be used for either on the strength of this entry.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used (Clark's commentary on *rgu.thub* mentions the root, leaves, flowers and fruit in describing a plant that may not be the glossary's) and give no quantity, frequency or duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes this drug by what it does: it is an antidote to poison (Men-Tsee-Khang) or cures fevers (Clark, under another binomial). The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names ("Guthup", "rgu thub", "rgu.thub") found it only in the lines cited above and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23 in both editions), and Clark's materia medica names other drugs as essence-extraction ingredients (for example lines 853, 971 and 1144), none of them this one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name and not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a poison-antidote drug in Men-Tsee-Khang, a fever drug in Clark, with no preparation and no dose, and the two editions identify it as different plants. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Poisoning and fever need medical care. The identity is open between two genera, no preparation or dose is given, and because many carrot-family plants contain skin-sensitising furanocoumarins, no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
