# Swertia angustifolia — gser.tig (rGyud-bzhi, Clark 1995; no Men-Tsee-Khang counterpart found)

## *Swertia angustifolia, the first of three Swertia names Clark 1995 prints for the Tibetan name gser.tig on line 1010 (then Swertia wolfgangiara and Swertia vacillans, spelled as printed); the Codex keeps the first because the spine row is keyed to it, and the entry stands for the Tibetan drug* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, and in practice on one edition of it, because Clark 1995 is the only one of the two editions on disk in which the drug has been found.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta text folders on disk for *Swertia* found no hit in the primary texts apart from the Sushruta appendix's "Swertia Cherata" and "Swertia Chirata" (Bhishagratna Vol. III, lines 31477 and 32043), which are a different species and a different drug (the entries I_373 and I_065 are on it). The row was added from a printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 133) the row is keyed by the **Tibetan name** *gser.tig*, with the identification "Swertia angustifolia; Swertia wolfgangiara; Swertia vacillans", the locus "Clark 1995 (line 1010)" and the note "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The spine's binomial is therefore the **first** of three names, and *Swertia wolfgangiara* and *Swertia vacillans* are later names for the same Tibetan drug and have no entry of their own. The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary has one *Swertia* row in `names.csv` (line 376, "Swertia chirayta", glossary line 5968), which is the chirata and not this drug.

**Identity doubt.** Clark offers three *Swertia* species for one Tibetan name and the text does not choose between them. The spelling "wolfgangiara" is printed so and may be a misprint; the Codex does not correct it. Clark's preface says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" (line 83) and that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect" (line 68). Clark's name index lists the Tibetan name *gser.tig* (line 1768). A search of the Men-Tsee-Khang text for the transliteration "Sertig" and for "Ser tig", "Sertik" and "gser tig" found nothing, and its glossary has no phonetic name that reads as this drug (its "S" block holds "Sergyi metok", "Sergyi phuebu", "Ser kue" and "Sewai metok" among the "Ser-" names). Men-Tsee-Khang's chapter 20 run for the herbs (it begins at line 3428, and its first lines are on that one long line) names, in this part, "Kyiche", "Sumchutik", "Tiyangku", "Zangtik and Chaagtik" and then "Solo and Sugdra"; Clark's same stretch runs *kyi.lche* (line 1003), *sum.chu.tig* (1004), *pri.yang.ku* (1005), *zangs.tig* (1008), *lchags.tig* (1009), then *gser.tig* (1010), then *sro.lo* and *sug.hdra* (1011). The Codex reads "Tiyangku" as the counterpart of *pri.yang.ku* by order and similar sound only, and the Men-Tsee-Khang run has no name at the place where Clark has *gser.tig*. Whether that edition omitted the drug or names it otherwise, the Codex does not know. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1010).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry stands on a line of its own, after *lchags.tig* (line 1009) and before the long line (1011) that opens with *sro.lo*. It reads:

"gser.tig: Swertia angustifolia: Swertia wolfgangiara: Swertia vacillans: [has a yellowish-green leaf (like) the beak of the rko.ma bird and bluish-yellow flowers spotted like the ngur.pa bird. It has bitter taste and cures disorders of blood, bile and infectious fevers]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. **Everything after the Latin names is inside the brackets**, so the leaf, the flowers, the bitter taste and the three indications are the commentary layer. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are only the Tibetan name and the three Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's. The root-text layer, as far as the marks show it, prints **no action for this drug at all**. The names of the two birds ("rko.ma" and "ngur.pa") are left untranslated in Clark's text, and the Codex does not gloss them. The neighbouring entries on the lines before and after (*zangs.tig*, "red gentian cures infectious fevers", line 1008; *lchags.tig*, "Both red gentian and Gentianopsis cure bile fevers", line 1009) carry root-text clauses that this entry lacks.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011.* No passage was found that names the drug (see the identity note above). The edition therefore does not confirm or contradict Clark's commentary.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Swertia angustifolia*, *wolfgangiara*, *vacillans*, *gser.tig* and "Sertig" found no entry on this drug. The entry for the line before it is **I_1294** (*Gentianopsis paludosa*, Tibetan *lchags.tig*, Clark line 1009), and the entry for the line after it is **I_1344** (*Rhodiola saera*, *sro.lo*, Clark line 1011); both mention *gser.tig* as a neighbour. **I_373** (Kiratatikta, *Swertia chirata*; tags CM, SS, FMT) and **I_065** (Chirata, *Swertia chirayita*; tag FMT) are entries on a different species, which Clark prints as *tig.ta* (line 974, "Chiretta: Swertia chirata"). Clark's *kyi.lche* (line 1003) prints "Swertia petiolata (white type)" as its second name; it is a different Tibetan drug, with its own entries (I_316 for *Gentiana straminea*, its first name). Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Swertia* belongs to the gentian family (Gentianaceae) and that the genus is known in the pharmacological literature for intensely bitter secoiridoid glycosides and for xanthones; that is a statement about the genus and not about this Tibetan drug. The commentary's phrase "bitter taste" is in keeping with that general fact, and does not show which species or which compounds the Tibetan texts meant. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The only statement Clark prints for the drug is the commentary's: that it "cures disorders of blood, bile and infectious fevers". It is the commentary's claim, attached to this binomial only through Clark's identification, and it is not a Codex protocol. Fevers that are infectious, and disorders of the blood, need medical care. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's line 1010 prints "bitter taste", inside the square brackets, so it belongs to the commentary layer. It prints no power or post-digestive taste. Nothing is printed for the drug in the Men-Tsee-Khang lines read.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica, in Clark's edition, gives this drug only through the commentary: it is bitter and cures disorders of blood, bile and infectious fevers. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's name (*gser.tig*) found it only in line 1010 and the Tibetan-name index. It is not in Men-Tsee-Khang's rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23, colophon at line 3950) or in Clark's chapter 23, "Normal health" (lines 1264 to 1276), and the herb entries of Clark's chapter 20 that mention essence extraction (lines 971, 1043, 1078, 1103, 1121 and 1144) do not include it. Its framing is therapeutic only, and what therapeutic framing there is comes from the commentary.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a bitter fever-and-bile drug in a commentary, with no preparation, no dose, no counterpart in the other edition and an identity left open among three species. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Fevers and disorders of the blood need medical care; the identity is open among three species, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No conservation information and no safety information for pregnancy were read. [USER: verify]
