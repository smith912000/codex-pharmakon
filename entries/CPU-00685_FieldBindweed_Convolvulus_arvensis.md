# Field Bindweed — Field Bindweed, Corn Lily, Lesser Bindweed, Possession Vine

## *Convolvulus arvensis* L. | Family: Convolvulaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry (Galenic reckoning); bitter and drastically purgative — a downward-moving, evacuant humour

**Training Phase:** Purification

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Convolvulus arvensis* is a deep-rooted, twining perennial of the morning-glory family, native to Europe and Asia and now one of the most tenacious agricultural weeds on Earth. Its trumpet flowers and relentless, rope-like roots gave it both its charm and its reputation — the "bindweed" that strangles crops and resists eradication. The medicinal tradition around it is inseparable from its family resemblance: the Convolvulaceae are the great **resin-glycoside purgatives** of the old pharmacy, the family of jalap, scammony, and turpeth. Field bindweed is the humble, milder, weedy cousin of those violent cathartics, and its traditional use follows the same logic — a plant that *moves things downward and out*.

**Assyrian (AT):** The Mesopotamian materia medica made heavy use of drastic purgatives and "cleansing" roots to evacuate the bowel and expel morbid matter, a central therapeutic strategy in a humoral system that read disease as accumulation. Twining, milky-rooted Convolvulaceae of this type belong to the class of evacuant roots gathered for exactly this downward-clearing purpose.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** The *De Materia Medica* documents the convolvulus/scammony group at length, describing the resinous root latex as a powerful purgative to be used with great caution and correction — an agent that draws watery and bilious humours through the bowel. Field bindweed sits at the gentler end of this recognised purgative continuum.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM):** The *Qanun* and the Unani tradition classed scammony-type Convolvulaceae resins as cold-dry, drastically cathartic drugs demanding careful dosing, mixing, and correction (often with mastic or almond oil) to blunt their griping. The doctrine treated them as heavy artillery of purgation — reserved, corrected, and never casually given. Field bindweed inherits this cautionary framing.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The plant's symbolism is *unbinding by force* — a vine that binds the field is turned, in the pharmacy, into an agent that unbinds a bound bowel. The morning-glory family's resin glycosides are genuinely, mechanically purgative: amphipathic glycolipids that irritate and stimulate intestinal secretion and motility. The symbolic "loosening" is a literal pharmacological catharsis, and — as with all the family's members — the line between purge and poison is thin.

### Active Components

- **Resin glycosides (glycolipid "convolvulins"/arvensic acids)** — the defining Convolvulaceae actives; amphipathic ester glycosides responsible for the purgative and cytotoxic effects.
- **Arvensic acids (e.g. K and L)** — pentasaccharide glycosidic acids isolated from the resin fraction, with reported cytotoxic and anti-migration activity.
- **Tropane alkaloids** (trace, e.g. tropine derivatives) — reported from the genus.
- **Flavonoids and phenolic acids** — quercetin, gallic, ferulic, caffeic acids; the antioxidant fraction.
- **Fatty acids and sterols** — minor constituents.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Field bindweed is a **Purification**-phase agent of the most cautionary kind: a traditional **drastic purgative / cathartic** used to force downward evacuation of the bowel. It is not a gentle laxative and not a daily tonic — in the classical framing it is a corrected, occasional evacuant for severe, stubborn stagnation, always blunted and always brief. In modern Kenetica practice it is best treated as a **historical/reference purgative** rather than a recommended intervention: the same resin glycosides that empty the bowel also irritate and, in excess, poison it. Where the tradition reached for it to "unbind" a bound system, contemporary practice reaches instead for gentler damp-drainers and osmotic agents, and files bindweed as a documented but largely superseded cathartic.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the chemistry supports the traditional purgative identity while the therapeutic evidence base is thin. Lu et al. (2019), *Natural Product Research*, isolated the new resin-glycoside acids **arvensic acids K and L** from *C. arvensis*, characterising their pentasaccharide chains and evaluating cytotoxic and anti-migration activity — confirming the plant carries the amphipathic glycolipids that make the Convolvulaceae purgative and cytotoxic. DOI: [10.1080/14786419.2019.1672069](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2019.1672069). Azman et al. (2015), *Antioxidants*, quantified the antioxidant capacity of *C. arvensis* extract (ABTS, ORAC, FRAP) and demonstrated lipid-oxidation protection in food models, documenting the phenolic/flavonoid fraction. DOI: [10.3390/antiox4010170](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox4010170).

**Evidence grade: Traditional / preclinical, data-sparse.** There are no controlled human clinical trials establishing safety or efficacy for any therapeutic use of field bindweed. The purgative reputation is genuine and mechanistically plausible (resin glycosides), and antioxidant chemistry is documented in vitro, but human therapeutic claims are unsupported. (Note: some published wound-healing work on this species has been formally retracted and is not cited here.)

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani (as attested):** Cold and dry; bitter; drastically purgative/cathartic (family of scammony, jalap, turpeth) — requiring correction and caution.
- **TCM (by analogy, not classical attribution):** would read as bitter, cold, and downward-draining/purgative. Not a canonical TCM herb; comparative note only.
- **Ayurvedic (by analogy):** *rasa* bitter (tikta); *virya* cooling (shita); strong downward (*adhobhaga*) evacuant action. Comparative only.

### Contraindications

**⚠️ Toxicity and purgative hazard.** Field bindweed is a drastic cathartic containing irritant resin glycosides; internal use can cause violent griping, cramping, watery diarrhoea, dehydration, and electrolyte disturbance, with risk of toxicity in overdose. It is **contraindicated in pregnancy and breastfeeding** (purgatives can stimulate the uterus and are unsafe), in children, in the elderly or frail, and in anyone with inflammatory bowel disease, intestinal obstruction, or bowel fragility. Do not combine with other laxatives, cardiac glycosides, or potassium-depleting drugs. The plant is also reported to be **toxic to grazing livestock** (notably horses) and contains trace tropane alkaloids.

**⚠️ Noxious-weed status.** *Convolvulus arvensis* is legally designated a noxious/invasive weed in many jurisdictions (including much of the United States, Canada, and Australia); its cultivation, propagation, or spread may be regulated or prohibited. Wild-harvesting risks contamination with agricultural herbicides given its cropland habitat. Treat as a reference/historical purgative, not a practical remedy.
