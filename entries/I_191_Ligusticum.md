# I-191 · Ligusticum (Chuan Xiong)
**Ligusticum chuanxiong** Hort. | Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Ligusticum chuanxiong* Hort. (1979 formal Chinese Pharmacopoeia listing; common usage earlier)  
**Pharmacopoeial synonym:** *Conioselinum chuanxiong* (S.H.Qiu et al.) M.L.Sheh & R.H.Shan (APG reclassification)  
**Common names:** Chuan Xiong, Sichuan lovage, Chinese lovage, rhizome of Sichuan lovage  
**Traditional names:**
- **Chuan Xiong (川芎):** "Sichuan *xiong*" — *chuan* = Sichuan (province); *xiong* originally a plant name from archaic texts; named for its primary origin in Sichuan province
- Japanese: *Senkyu* (川芎); Korean: *Cheonggung* (천궁)
- Former name: *Ligusticum wallichii* (Franch.) — now differentiated; *L. wallichii* is the Himalayan species; *L. chuanxiong* is the cultivated Chinese Sichuan form; pharmacopoeially distinct
- Also related to: *Cnidium officinale* (Japanese pharmacopoeial equivalent)

**Apiaceae blood-moving cluster:**

*Ligusticum chuanxiong* is the third major Apiaceae blood-mover in TCM alongside Dang Gui (I-189):
- Both contain Z-ligustilide and ferulic acid (shared phthalide-ferulic platform)
- Chuan Xiong: HIGHER Z-ligustilide, MORE blood-moving/Qi-activating
- Dang Gui: MORE blood-nourishing (ferulic acid hematopoietic dominant)
- Combined in Si Wu Tang and Chuan Xiong Cha Tiao San as complementary pair

**Botanical morphology:**
- Perennial herb to 60cm; aromatic, hollow stems; pinnately compound leaves; white umbellate flowers (Apiaceae); compact knotty rhizome (NOT a root — a rhizome with pronounced nodes)
- **The "rhizome" distinction:** Chuan Xiong is harvested rhizome (thickened horizontal stem = *rizoma*), NOT root — different from Dang Gui (root). Cross-section shows central pith cavity
- Primarily cultivated (rarely found wild); Dujiangyan area (Sichuan) = primary daodi production region
- Harvested May when aerial shoots yellowing

**Part used:** Dried rhizome (*Rhizoma Chuanxiong*); irregular nodular pieces, strong aromatic smell

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Phthalide convergence across plant families:**

Z-ligustilide (the primary active compound shared between Chuan Xiong and Dang Gui) also occurs in significant concentrations in: *Apium graveolens* (celery), *Levisticum officinale* (lovage), *Petroselinum crispum* (parsley), *Oenanthe javanica* (water parsley), and *Ferula* species. This means that the "blood-moving" phthalide pharmacology was independently available in every major culinary Apiaceae — a pharmacological commons embedded in the food plants that also served as medicine.

**The headache herb:**

Chuan Xiong's most consistent traditional application across all confirming traditions is headache — specifically headache associated with blood stagnation, vascular constriction, and tension. The pharmacological basis: Z-ligustilide is a cerebrovascular vasodilator (via NO pathway and Ca²⁺ channel inhibition), reduces platelet aggregation (ferulic acid → thromboxane inhibition), and inhibits neurogenic inflammation. These mechanisms map directly onto migraine and tension headache pathophysiology.

**South American osha root parallel:**

*Ligusticum porteri* (osha root) used by Southwestern U.S. Native Americans and in Andean mestizo medicine for respiratory infection, altitude sickness, and as a ceremonial protective plant represents independent discovery of *Ligusticum* therapeutics. Osha root contains the same phthalide class of compounds (ligustilide homologs) and has demonstrated antiviral (coronavirus inhibition), bronchodilatory, and anti-inflammatory properties — essentially the same pharmacological platform as *L. chuanxiong* but independently discovered in the Americas. This is rare authentic genus-level pharmacological convergence across continents.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Primary active compounds:**

**1. Phthalides (PRIMARY actives — higher concentration than Dang Gui)**
- **Z-Ligustilide:** 1–6% of volatile oil; 0.3–1.0% total rhizome weight
  - Primary vasodilatory, anti-spasmodic, anti-platelet active
  - Mechanism: Ca²⁺ channel (VGCC) inhibition → smooth muscle relaxation; NO upregulation → vasodilation
  - Volatile; lost in prolonged decoction; maintained in tinctures/ethanol extracts
- **Senkyunolide A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H, I (Chuanxionglactone series):** Chuan Xiong-specific phthalides; additional smooth muscle and anti-platelet actions
- **Dihydroligustilide, n-butylidenephthalide:** Related phthalides; vasodilatory

**2. Ferulic Acid and Derivatives**
- Similar to Dang Gui but lower ratio relative to phthalides → more blood-moving, less nourishing
- Caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid also present

**3. Alkaloids**
- **Tetramethylpyrazine (TMP / Ligustrazine):** LANDMARK compound; 0.01–0.04% dry weight
  - The most extensively studied pharmaceutical from *L. chuanxiong*
  - Developed as pharmaceutical: ligustrazine injection (Chuanxiongqin) — registered Chinese drug for ischemic stroke, coronary artery disease, peripheral vascular disease
  - Mechanisms: Ca²⁺ channel antagonism (similar to verapamil), PDE inhibition → cAMP → vasodilation, platelet aggregation inhibition, RBC deformability improvement
  - Also found in fermented soybean (*douchi*), in smaller amounts
- **Perlolyrine:** β-carboline (also in Codonopsis); weak MAOI activity

**4. Polysaccharides**
- Chuanxiong polysaccharides; immunomodulatory; minor contribution

**5. Phenolic Acids**
- Protocatechuic acid, caffeic acid: antioxidant, anti-inflammatory

**Tetramethylpyrazine (TMP) — the pharmaceutical derivative note:**

TMP is not a phthalide but an aromatic alkaloid; it is the single most important pharmacological discovery from Chuan Xiong, developed into a full pharmaceutical drug class. TMP injection is a registered Chinese pharmaceutical used in hospital cardiology and neurology — representing the cleanest herbal → pharmaceutical translation among TCM herbs.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**1. Cerebrovascular Vasodilation (Z-Ligustilide + TMP)**
- Z-Ligustilide: VGCC (voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel) inhibition → cerebrovascular smooth muscle relaxation → increased cerebral blood flow
- TMP: Ca²⁺ channel antagonism (verapamil-like) + PDE inhibition → cAMP → vasodilation + reduced vascular resistance
- Net: increased cerebral and coronary blood flow; reduced vasospasm
- Primary headache mechanism

**2. Anti-platelet Aggregation (Ferulic Acid + TMP)**
- Ferulic acid: thromboxane A2 synthesis inhibition → reduced platelet activation
- TMP: adenylate cyclase stimulation → cAMP elevation → PKA activation → reduced platelet aggregation
- Together: dual-pathway anti-platelet effect comparable to low-dose aspirin + dipyridamole combination
- **Clinical significance for stroke/TIA prevention**

**3. RBC Deformability Enhancement (TMP)**
- TMP: improves red blood cell membrane fluidity → cells squeeze through small capillaries more efficiently
- Reduces blood viscosity → improved microcirculation
- Traditional "removes blood stasis from small vessels" = improved microcirculatory rheology

**4. Anti-inflammatory (Multiple)**
- Z-ligustilide: NF-κB pathway inhibition; PGE₂ reduction
- TMP: inhibits TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 production; reduces leukocyte adhesion to endothelium
- Senkyunolides: additional anti-inflammatory mechanisms

**5. Neuroprotection (TMP)**
- TMP: reduces ischemia-reperfusion injury in brain; multiple mechanisms:
  - Antioxidant (ROS scavenging + Nrf2 activation)
  - Anti-apoptotic (Bcl-2 upregulation, caspase inhibition)
  - BDNF upregulation
  - Mitochondrial protection
- Animal data: reduces infarct volume in ischemic stroke models

**6. Emmenagogue/Uterotonic (Z-Ligustilide — Dose-Dependent)**
- Same biphasic uterine mechanism as Dang Gui
- At low dose: relaxes uterine smooth muscle (anti-spasmodic)
- At high dose: stimulates uterine contraction (emmenagogue)
- **PREGNANCY CONTRAINDICATION — same as Dang Gui**

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Ischemic stroke (best human evidence — TMP pharmaceutical):**
- Multiple Chinese RCTs and meta-analyses of TMP injection (ligustrazine IV) in ischemic stroke
- Chen J et al (2012) *Acta Pharmacol Sin* 33(2):161–9: Review; 29 RCTs; TMP in acute ischemic stroke; significant neurological improvement, reduced disability; quality variable
- TMP injection: registered Chinese pharmaceutical; hospital-standard treatment in China for ischemic cerebrovascular disease

**Coronary artery disease:**
- TMP (ligustrazine): multiple Chinese trials in stable angina; significant exercise tolerance improvement; anti-ischemic effects

**Migraine/Headache:**
- Limited Western-standard RCT for Chuan Xiong specifically; positive observational evidence
- Chuan Xiong Cha Tiao San (6-herb formula including Chuan Xiong) for headache: used clinically; mechanism-supported

**Traditional formula evidence:**
- Si Wu Tang (containing Chuan Xiong): extensive Chinese clinical use for blood deficiency and gynecological conditions; evidence primarily within Chinese clinical literature

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Tetramethylpyrazine — from herb to stroke drug:**

The development of TMP (tetramethylpyrazine/ligustrazine) represents one of the most complete pharmacological translation arcs in TCM history:
1. *L. chuanxiong* used for "blood stasis/circulation" for 2,000 years
2. 1978: Chinese chemists isolate TMP from Chuan Xiong rhizome at Shanghai Institute of Materia Medica
3. 1980s: TMP injection enters Chinese clinical trials for cerebrovascular disease
4. 1990s: Registration as pharmaceutical drug (Chuanxiongqin injection)
5. Current: Standard-of-care in some Chinese hospitals for ischemic stroke

TMP also occurs in small amounts in fermented soybean products (*douchi*) — an independent traditional food source of the same compound, embedded in the diet of East Asian populations centuries before pharmaceutical isolation.

**"Blood stasis" as viscosity:**

The TCM concept of "blood stasis" (瘀血, *yū xuè*) — blood that pools, thickens, fails to circulate — maps with reasonable pharmacological fidelity onto: elevated blood viscosity, platelet hyperaggregability, reduced RBC deformability, microcirculatory impairment, and endothelial dysfunction. Chuan Xiong's treatment of "blood stasis" = improvement of all these measurable hemodynamic parameters. The traditional conceptual model captured a real physiological cluster.

**The Si Wu Tang — the most prescribed blood formula:**

Si Wu Tang (四物湯) has been the most prescribed Chinese herbal formula for blood conditions for approximately 1,000 years. Its four herbs: Shu Di Huang (blood-nourishing), Dang Gui (blood-nourishing + moving), Bai Shao (anti-spasmodic, liver-soothing), Chuan Xiong (blood-moving Qi). Each herb operates via different mechanisms (erythropoiesis, vasodilation, smooth muscle relaxation, platelet inhibition), yet all contribute to the aggregate "blood health" outcome. Modern pharmacological analysis of Si Wu Tang shows additive-to-synergistic effects across all four herb components — the formula pharmacology is greater than the sum of its parts.

**Osha root — the American cousin:**

*Ligusticum porteri* (osha root) holds significant ceremonial and medicinal status among Southwestern and Rocky Mountain Indigenous cultures. Called "bear root" from the observation that bears emerging from hibernation seek out and eat osha root; "bear medicine" in multiple tribal traditions. Used for respiratory infection, fever, altitude sickness, and as ceremonial protective plant. Modern pharmacology: ligustilide-family compounds with antiviral, bronchodilatory, and anti-inflammatory properties — the same platform as the Chinese *L. chuanxiong*, discovered independently across the Bering land bridge's descendants.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Primary indications:**
- Headache (especially vascular/tension type with blood stasis pattern)
- Ischemic cardiovascular and cerebrovascular conditions (adjunct; TMP pharmaceutical when IV required)
- Dysmenorrhea, irregular menses (in formula with Dang Gui)
- Chest bi syndrome (angina-equivalent in TCM)
- Traumatic injury with bruising and swelling
- Post-stroke rehabilitation (as part of formula)

**Dosing:**
- **Standard dose:** 3–10g dried rhizome as decoction daily (lower end than Dang Gui due to potency)
- **Si Wu Tang:** 9g each of four herbs; classic formula
- **TMP pharmaceutical:** 80–160mg/day IV (hospital use only); not equivalent to oral herb dose
- **Duration:** 4–8 weeks for circulatory conditions; acute headache formula can be shorter

⚠️ **ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION: PREGNANCY** — uterotonic (same mechanism as Dang Gui)

⚠️ **DRUG INTERACTIONS:**
- **Anticoagulants (warfarin, heparin):** ⚠️ Significant — ferulic acid antiplatelet + TMP antiplatelet → additive bleeding risk; monitor INR; discontinue 2 weeks before surgery
- **Antiplatelet drugs:** Additive (aspirin, clopidogrel); bleeding risk
- **Antihypertensives:** TMP vasodilatory effect → additive hypotension; monitor BP
- **MAOIs:** Perlolyrine β-carboline MAOI activity → serotonin syndrome risk with pharmaceutical MAOIs
- **Nifedipine, verapamil (Ca²⁺ channel blockers):** TMP shares mechanism → additive vasodilation/hypotension

⚠️ **CAUTIONS:**
- **Hemorrhagic stroke or bleeding disorders:** Anti-platelet activity contraindicated in hemorrhagic contexts
- **Deficiency (TCM: pale, thin, weak, empty pulse):** Chuan Xiong is strongly blood-moving; without blood-nourishing herbs, may deplete; always combine with Dang Gui and Shu Di Huang
- **Yin deficiency with fire:** May aggravate (aromatic, ascending nature)
- **Headache from Qi/blood deficiency** (pale, dull, relieved by rest): Different from blood stasis headache; may worsen if used alone

**Preparation notes:**
- ADD LATE in decoction (last 5–10 minutes) — preserves Z-ligustilide content
- Strong aromatic smell indicates good quality (Z-ligustilide content)
- Pairs universally in TCM with Dang Gui; rarely used alone

**Standardization:**
- Chinese Pharmacopoeia 2020: minimum 0.1% ligustilide on dry basis
- Quality: strong aromatic smell; brownish irregular nodular pieces; cross-section shows pale yellow pith

**Energetic tier:** ◉ Qi (circulatory/functional) — primary blood Qi activator; operates specifically at the level of circulation, flow, and distribution; not constitutional but vascular-functional
