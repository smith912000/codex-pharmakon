### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *The names in the chapters.* A search of the chapter files, allowing for the OCR's accents, found Nirgundi on eight lines (Sutra 38; Chikitsa 18, where line 216 is an editor's note, and Chikitsa 37; Uttara 11, 21, 31 and 45) and Sindhuvara or Sinduka on seven (Sutra 46; Uttara 31 and 45; Kalpa 5, twice, Kalpa 6 and Kalpa 7). The passages were read.

*A group (Sutra 38, lines 119 to 130).* Nirgundi is named in the Surasadi group: "Surasa, white Surasa, Fainjjhaka, Arjaka, Bhustrina, Sugandhaka, Sumukha, Kalamala, Kashamarda, Kshavaka, Kharpushpa, Vidanga, Katphala, Surasi, Nirgundi, Kulahala, Indurakarnika, Phanji, Prachivala, Kakamachi and Vishamushtika". Of the group: "The group acts as a vermifuge and is an aseptic agent. It subdues the deranged Kapham and proves beneficial in catarrh, non-relish for food, asthma and cough."

*A flower (Sutra 46, lines 1795 to 1806).* In the chapter on food and drink, among the properties of flowers: "The Sindhuvaram is renowned for its virtue of destroying the Pittam."

*An emetic (Chikitsa 18, lines 195 to 199).* In the chapter on glandular swellings, scurvy (*Apachi*) and tumours: "A strong emetic composed of Nirgundi, Jati (flower) and Varihistha (Vala) together with Jimutaka, profusely mixed with honey and Saindhava, should be given warm to the patient. It is a very powerful emetic, and leads to the recovery of even a malignant form of Apachi." The editor notes that "Some read 'Nirgundi' after it" at another place in the chapter (line 216).

*Plugs for the bladder (Chikitsa 37, lines 734 to 742).* In the chapter on the *Anuvasana* and *Uttara* injections: plugs "of the size of a Mudga-pulse, cardamom-seed, or mustard-seed should be made up of Saindhava and the leaves of the Aragvadha pasted with the expressed juice of Nirgundi and cow's urine, and these plugs ... should, according to the age of the patient, be inserted (into the mouth of the bladder) with (the top-end of) a rod or an indicator ... for the out-flow of the injected fluid."

*Eye and ear (Uttara 11, lines 76 to 86; Uttara 21, lines 293 to 298).* In the eye chapter an eye-salve (*Anjana*) is made from ashes of blue barley soaked in milk, mixed with an equal part of the burnt ashes of "Arjaka, Ashphotaka, Kapittha, Vilva, Nirgundi and Jati flowers" and an alkali, in a case the OCR prints as "VaWsa-Grathita". In the chapter on ear diseases, in Puti-karna with fetid discharge, "the use as ear-drops of the compound composed of oil, the expressed juice of Nirgundi and honey mixed together arrests an attack of Puti-karna."

*A diet in haemorrhage (Uttara 45, lines 124 to 131).* In the chapter on haemorrhage (*Rakta-pitta*): "Sindhuvara (Nirgundi) as well as the tender sprouts of Vata and Atimukta (Tinduka) as pot-herbs and cooked with clarified butter are recommended as diets." The start of the sentence falls across a page break in the retrieved text.

*A charm for a child (Uttara 31, lines 22 to 30).* In the chapter on an attack by Revati: "Neck-ornament should be made with (the wood or pith of) Varuna, Arishta (Nimba), Ruchaka, Sinduka (Nirgundi), and Putranjivaka and should be always worn on the neck (as a charm) by the child." The words "as a charm" are the translator's.

*Against poisons (Kalpa 5, 6 and 7).* In the chapter on snake-bite, an *Agada* of leaves of Surasa, Vilva, Kapittha and Dadima, with half a part each of "black Sindhuvara, Amkotha and Gairika" in honey, is "highly efficacious especially in the case of poisoning by a Mandali-bite" (Kalpa 5, lines 460 to 468); the same chapter has Sindhuvara in the *Ekasara Agada* with Somaraji, Choraka, Varuna and others (lines 505 to 507, the name split across two lines). In the chapter on rat-poisoning a lambative of "the two kinds of" a name the OCR garbles, glossed by the translator "(Mudga-parni and Masha-parni)", and Sindhuvara pasted with honey is for the bite of a Kulinga rat (Kalpa 6, lines 102 to 107). The *Mahasugandhi* antivenom of Kalpa 7 takes "flowers and fruits of Champaka, Asoka, Sumanas ... and of Sindhuvara" (lines 124 to 130).

*What the chapters do not say.* A search of the vajikarana chapter (Chikitsa 26) and the rasayana chapters (Chikitsa 27 to 30) found none of the names. No *rasa*, *virya* or *vipaka* is printed for Nirgundi or Sindhuvara in the passages read. The Latin identification is the appendix's. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. As a general fact, *Vitex* is a genus of the mint family (Lamiaceae; older books place it in the verbena family), and the plants of the genus are aromatic. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Sushruta text records Nirgundi as a member of a group against Kapha, as an emetic, as an ingredient in bladder plugs, eye-salves, ear-drops and antivenoms, and Sindhuvara as a flower that quiets Pitta, as a pot-herb diet in haemorrhage and as an ingredient of antivenoms, and treats Sinduka as a child's neck charm. These are the text's statements about Sanskrit names, and the Latin name the appendix attaches to them is the translator's, by position. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these preparations, each with its locus. The emetic of Nirgundi, Jati flower and Vala with Jimutaka, honey and Saindhava, warm (Chikitsa 18, lines 195 to 199). The bladder plug pasted with the juice of Nirgundi and cow's urine (Chikitsa 37, lines 734 to 742). The ash of the burnt drug in an eye-salve (Uttara 11, lines 76 to 86). The expressed juice with oil and honey as ear-drops (Uttara 21, lines 293 to 298). The pot-herb cooked in ghrita (Uttara 45, lines 128 to 131). The leaves and flowers in antivenoms (Kalpa 5, 6 and 7). Only the relative parts of the Kalpa 5 *Agada* (half a part of the black Sindhuvara to two parts of each of the other leaves) are given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SS:** the passages read print for the drug its place in the Surasadi group, whose stated virtue is that it is a vermifuge and aseptic, "subdues the deranged Kapham" and helps in catarrh, loss of appetite, asthma and cough (Sutra 38, lines 127 to 130), and that the Sindhuvara flower is "renowned for its virtue of destroying the Pittam" (Sutra 46, line 1805). No *rasa*, *virya* or *vipaka* is printed for Nirgundi or Sindhuvara in the lines read.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Sushruta text names the drug as a member of the Surasadi group, as an emetic and local application, as a pot-herb diet in haemorrhage and as an antivenom ingredient, and mentions the wood as a child's neck charm. A search of the vajikarana chapter (Chikitsa 26) and the rasayana chapters (Chikitsa 27 to 30) found none of its names, so the text assigns it no rejuvenative or generative status in the lines retrieved. The chapters say nothing of *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* for it. The Latin identification is the appendix's. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. In the Sushruta scheme it is a group member and an ingredient in a physician's emetics, applications and antivenoms. The pot-herb diet in haemorrhage is the only place it is eaten, and it is a diet for a stated disease, not a regimen. Nothing should be gathered or taken on the strength of a Latin name that the chapters do not give.

### Contraindications

The Sushruta passages read print no warning for the drug itself. The one oral use is as an emetic (Chikitsa 18), which is a physician's procedure and unsuitable for the weak, the pregnant and the very young without supervision. The antivenom, eye and bladder uses describe emergencies that need medical care, not a household remedy. The identity is open, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
