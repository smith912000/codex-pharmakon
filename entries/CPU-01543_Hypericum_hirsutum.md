# Hypericum hirsutum — Askuron (Dioscorides 3-172, "Ascyrum"); "Siberian St John's Wort" on the translators' line

## *Hypericum hirsutum [Linnaeus], the last of three Renaissance and Linnaean names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for chapter 3-172 (Ascyrum [Fuchs], Androsaemum hirsutum [Bauhin]), which adds a second line, "Siberian St John's Wort — Hypericum ascyron"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk names this plant under this name. The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The SUGGESTED block is in two parts: the first line gives three names, of which the last is *Hypericum hirsutum* [Linnaeus]; the second line gives a different species, *Hypericum ascyron*, glossed "Siberian St John's Wort". The entry takes the Linnaean binomial of the first line. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *3-172. ASKURON* at line 7960, SUGGESTED lines at 7961 and 7962, text at line 7963. The chapter stands in Book Three under the running head "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants", after 3-171 *UPERIKON* (the hypericum proper, glossed *Hypericum perforatum*) and before 3-173 *ANDROSAIMON*, both different chapters. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 578.

The whole chapter reads: "Ascyrum is also a kind of hypericum, differing in size, bigger in the branches, more full of sprigs, and with the small leaves appearing a purple colour; it bears yellow flowers, and fruit (similar to hypericum) smelling of rosin, and bruised (as it were), staining the fingers with blood, so that it is called acrosemon for this. A decoction of the fruit (taken as a drink with a pint of honey water) is available for sciatica. It expels much bilious excrement. It must be given continuously until they are cured. Smeared on, it is good for burns. It is also called ascyroides, or acrosemon."

So the chapter places itself by comparison with another plant ("also a kind of hypericum"), says how it differs (bigger in the branches, more full of sprigs, small leaves appearing purple), gives the flower (yellow) and the fruit (like hypericum's, smelling of rosin, and staining the fingers as if with blood, which is the stated reason for the name *acrosemon*), and records three uses: the fruit decoction for sciatica, an expulsion of "much bilious excrement", and a topical use for burns. The drink is taken "with a pint of honey water". The chapter also gives a regimen ("It must be given continuously until they are cured"). The other names are *ascyroides* and *acrosemon*.

**Overlap.** The chapter is not covered by any existing entry (chapter-level and name search for 3-172, *Askuron*, *Ascyrum* and *Hypericum hirsutum*). Neighbouring chapters are covered under other binomials: 3-173 *ANDROSAIMON* by I_744 (Tutsan), and 3-171 by the general entries I_224 and I_738. The chapter-3-173 text lists "ascyron" among the other names of androsemum, and the 3-172 SUGGESTED line names *Hypericum ascyron*, so the two chapters share a name; the entries are not merged and the 3-173 chapter is not read as this plant. ### Active Components

The text prints no constituent and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. It is a general fact that the genus *Hypericum* contains many species and that the best-known medicinal one, *H. perforatum*, contains hypericin and hyperforin. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the translators' *Hypericum hirsutum*. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a fruit decoction for sciatica that "expels much bilious excrement", and a topical use for burns. These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for either, and the identity is a suggestion between two different species.
### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one drink: "A decoction of the fruit (taken as a drink with a pint of honey water)". The quantity printed is "a pint" of honey water. The text read does not define the measure, and no amount of fruit is given. The regimen is "continuously until they are cured". For burns it is "smeared on". The chapter gives no frequency. ### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identity is uncertain between two species, so a modern study of one *Hypericum* would not bear directly on the Dioscorides drug. It is a general, well-established fact that sciatica has many causes and that a purge is not a treatment for it.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 3-172 prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality and no taste. The qualities printed are of colour (small leaves "appearing a purple colour"; yellow flowers) and smell (the fruit smells "of rosin"), and that the fruit stains the fingers "with blood" when bruised.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a comparative description against a known plant (size, branching, leaf colour), a sensory assay (the smell of rosin; the staining of the fingers when the fruit is bruised) and a regimen of continuous dosing until cure. The name *acrosemon* is explained by the staining, so the plant's name is itself a field mark. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is the sensory test: bruise the fruit, smell it, and look at the fingers. The text offers nothing more, and the abstentive answer is the right one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 3-172 prints no warning. The only use it records for the drink is a purge ("expels much bilious excrement") given continuously, and the identification is only the translators' suggestion between two species, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Sciatica and burns need medical care. The text gives no dose of the fruit, and no safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
