# Stonebreaker / Bhumyamalaki / Chanca Piedra

## *Phyllanthus niruri* L. | Family: Phyllanthaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, SBJ, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Cool · Dry · Bitter-Sweet (draining, clearing damp-heat)
**Training Phase:** Phase IV — Clearing the Liver & Urinary Tract

---

### Origin & Tradition

Few plants wear their doctrine so openly in their names. *Chanca piedra* — "stone-breaker" — and the English "stonebreaker" both encode a single claim: this small, weedy annual, with its row of tiny fruits hidden beneath the leaf-rib, dissolves or expels the stones of the urinary tract. A second, equally ancient reputation runs alongside the first — that of a premier **liver herb** for jaundice and hepatitis. Three traditions, on three continents, converged on the same two organs.

**Chinese (CM) — Ye Xia Zhu 叶下珠** 
In Chinese medicine the herb is *Ye Xia Zhu* (叶下珠, "pearl beneath the leaf"), named for the seed-capsules strung under the leaf midrib. It is classed among the cool, bitter, damp-heat-clearing herbs and is used to *clear heat and drain damp*, *promote urination*, and *benefit the liver and eyes* — given traditionally for jaundice, hepatitis, urinary strangury and stones, and hot, red eyes. The doctrine is one of draining damp-heat downward and outward through the urine, unburdening the liver.

**South Asian / Ayurvedic (SBJ) — Bhumyamalaki (भूम्यामलकी)** 
In Ayurveda the plant is *Bhumyamalaki* — "the amalaki of the earth," a ground-hugging echo of the great liver-fruit amalaki. It is a classic *yakrit* (liver) and *mutrala* (diuretic) herb, bitter and cooling, used to pacify aggravated *pitta* and *kapha*, and prescribed for *kamala* (jaundice) and liver disorders, as well as for urinary calculi and burning micturition. The doctrine: bitter-cool herbs drain the heat and damp that, in the Ayurvedic frame, underlie both jaundice and stone-forming urinary heat.

**South American / Amazonian (SS) — Chanca Piedra** 
Across Amazonian and broader South American folk and shamanic practice the herb is *chanca piedra* (Quechua-Spanish, "break stone") or *quebra pedra* in Brazil. Its signal use is exactly its name — a tea drunk to pass kidney and gallbladder stones and to relieve the pain of their passage — alongside use for hepatitis, and as a bitter tonic for the liver and urinary tract. Here the doctrine is nominalist and direct: the plant *is* the stone-breaker, named for what it does.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**
The stonebreaker's two doctrines — *break the stone* and *heal the liver* — each attach to real chemistry and to real, but unequal, evidence.

The name-doctrine maps onto the **lignans and the plant's action on urinary crystallisation**. *Phyllanthus niruri* is rich in the lignans **phyllanthin** and **hypophyllanthin**, and the whole-plant infusion has measurable effects on the urine's stone-forming chemistry: it modulates calcium oxalate crystallisation and shifts urinary excretion of the ions (magnesium, potassium, oxalate, uric acid) that govern whether crystals aggregate into stones. So "stone-breaker" is better read as *"crystallisation-modulator and stone-expeller"* than as a literal solvent — the plant does not chemically dissolve calcium oxalate, but it alters the milieu that lets stones grow and helps small stones pass. The doctrine is largely vindicated, if reworded.

The liver-doctrine maps onto **hepatoprotective and antiviral signals** — and here we must deflate carefully. The traditional jaundice/hepatitis use is genuine and cross-cultural, and there are preclinical hepatoprotective and anti-hepatitis-B-virus signals. But the *clinical* anti-HBV evidence is **weak, heterogeneous, and Cochrane-graded as unconvincing** (see Scientific Correlation). The doctrine points at a real target (the liver) with a plausible mechanism, but the confident folk claim of "curing hepatitis B" outruns the trial data badly. We keep the liver-tonic and adjunct framing; we refuse the cure claim.

The visual signature — the "pearls beneath the leaf" — is a naming mnemonic (the fruit resembling both pearls and, to the folk eye, the small stones the plant expels), not a mechanism; graded **Null** as pharmacology, retained as memory-aid.

### Active Components

- **Phyllanthin & hypophyllanthin** — the characteristic lignans; markers of the species and carriers of much of the hepatoprotective/antiviral research interest.
- **Niranthin, nirtetralin and related lignans** — congeners.
- **Flavonoids** (quercetin, rutin, kaempferol glycosides) — antioxidant fraction.
- **Ellagitannins & phenolic acids** (corilagin, geraniin, gallic/ellagic acid) — antioxidant, astringent, and implicated in antiviral activity.
- **Alkaloids (securinine-type)** and **triterpenes** — minor constituents.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Stonebreaker is the Phase IV **urinary-and-hepatic drainer**: chosen for damp-heat presentations centred on the liver and lower urinary tract — recurrent small calculi, gravel and burning micturition, and sluggish, heat-burdened liver states (the herbalist's "toxic, congested liver"). Its role is *preventive and adjunctive*: reducing stone recurrence and easing passage of small stones (< ~10 mm in the trial context), and supporting liver function as a bitter cooling tonic. It is explicitly **not** positioned as a stand-alone antiviral cure for hepatitis B, but as a supportive liver herb. It pairs with the diuretic-lithotriptic cluster and with bitter hepatics.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:

- **The stone doctrine has modest but real clinical support.** A prospective study of 56 patients with stones < 10 mm found that 12 weeks of *P. niruri* infusion was safe, increased urinary magnesium and potassium excretion, reduced urinary oxalate in hyperoxaluric and uric acid in hyperuricosuric patients, and was associated with a fall in stone number (Pucci et al., 2018, *Int. Braz. J. Urol.*) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1590/S1677-5538.IBJU.2017.0521). A systematic review/meta-analysis (two eligible controlled studies) found statistically significant, though modest, reductions in mean stone size and number, concluding "limited clinical evidence supports modest efficacy... pending further study" (Dhawan & Olweny, 2020, *Can. J. Urol.*, PMID 32333735). **Grade: Moderate-to-Weak** — real, small, and under-powered.
- **Honest null.** A Cochrane review of dietary interventions in idiopathic hypercalciuria noted that in a small hypercalciuric subgroup, *P. niruri* produced **no significant effect on calciuria** after three months (Escribano et al., 2014, *Cochrane Database Syst. Rev.*) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD006022.pub4). So the effect is on oxalate/urate and crystallisation milieu, **not** on urinary calcium. **Grade: Null** for the calcium-lowering hypothesis.
- **The anti-hepatitis-B claim is weak and contested — the key deflation.** An early systematic review found *Phyllanthus* had a positive effect on HBsAg clearance versus placebo/no intervention but stressed the evidence was "not strong due to low methodological quality" (Liu et al., 2001, *J. Viral Hepat.*) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2893.2001.00307.x). Subsequent Cochrane reviews are more deflating: there is **"no convincing evidence"** that *Phyllanthus* versus placebo benefits chronic HBV patients (Xia et al., 2011) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD008960.pub2), and **"insufficient evidence to support or refute"** its use versus antiviral drugs, with all trials at high risk of bias (Xia et al., 2013) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD009004.pub2). **Grade: Weak/Insufficient — the traditional hepatitis reputation is NOT confirmed by controlled trials.**

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Cool, drying, bitter with a sweet undertone; draining and clearing. Given to *damp-heat* of the liver and urinary tract — jaundice, hot urinary gravel, burning micturition. Contraindicated by traditional logic in cold, deficient, or dry constitutions, where its cooling bitterness would deepen depletion.

### Contraindications

- ⚠️ **Hypoglycaemia / diabetes.** *Phyllanthus* may lower blood glucose; monitor and use caution with antidiabetic drugs to avoid additive hypoglycaemia. *[USER: verify current interaction data.]*
- ⚠️ **Pregnancy & lactation.** Avoid — traditional emmenagogue/uterine associations and insufficient safety data. *[USER: verify.]*
- **Large or obstructing stones.** Not appropriate as sole therapy for stones too large to pass or causing obstruction/infection — requires urological assessment.
- **Antihypertensive and anticoagulant co-use** — theoretical additive effects; monitor. *[USER: verify.]*
- **Not a treatment for hepatitis B.** Must not replace antiviral therapy or medical monitoring in chronic HBV; controlled evidence does not support efficacy.
- Correct species identification essential — the genus *Phyllanthus* is large and its species differ pharmacologically.
