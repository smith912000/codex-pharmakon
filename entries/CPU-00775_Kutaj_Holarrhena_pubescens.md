# Kutaj / Kurchi / Conessi Bark / Tellicherry Bark

## *Holarrhena pubescens* Wall. ex G.Don (= *H. antidysenterica*) | Family: Apocynaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, SS

**Energetic Classification:** Bitter, astringent, pungent; cooling (*shita virya*); drying — a "sealing" bark that binds and cools an over-loosened, over-heated gut.

**Training Phase:** Cleansing / Consolidation — deployed when the digestive channel is leaking, cramping, and inflamed, to close and re-astringe it. Not a builder; a stopper.

---

### Origin & Tradition

The name is the function. When Wallich and later botanists fixed the epithet *antidysenterica* on this Apocynaceous tree, they were only ratifying what the Indian pharmacopoeias had said for a thousand years: this is *the* dysentery bark. The synonymy is worth stating plainly, because it trips people up — *Holarrhena antidysenterica*, *Holarrhena pubescens*, and the trade names Conessi, Kurchi, and Tellicherry bark all point at one taxon. The currently accepted name is *H. pubescens*; *antidysenterica* survives as a synonym and as a fossilised clinical claim embedded in the Latin.

**Ayurveda (CM).** In the Ayurvedic materia medica the drug is *Kutaja*, and it is the archetypal *grahi* (astringent, gut-binding) and *dipana* (digestive-kindling) agent for *atisara* (diarrhoea) and *pravahika* (dysentery with mucus and tenesmus). Two parts are used: the stem bark (*Kutaja twak*) and, distinctively, the seeds, called *Indrayava* (also transliterated *Indrajau*). Classical formulations — *Kutajarishta*, *Kutajaghana vati* — are still standard OTC antidiarrhoeals in India. The doctrine assigns it *tikta-kashaya rasa* (bitter-astringent taste), *shita virya* (cooling potency), and a specific pull on *pitta* and *kapha* in the lower gut: it is given precisely when the stool is loose, hot, bloody, or mucoid — a *pitta*-type dysentery — rather than for cold, atonic diarrhoea.

**Unani (SS — Greco-Arabic/South Asian stream).** In the Unani tradition of the subcontinent the bark and seed carry over as an antidysenteric and vermifuge, prescribed for *zaheer* (dysentery/tenesmus) and intestinal worms, with the same cooling-astringent logic. Its persistence across both the Sanskritic and the Greco-Arabic systems of South Asia is itself evidence: two independent doctrinal frameworks converged on the same bark for the same complaint.

**Name-collision caution.** The vernacular *Indrajao* / *Indrajau* is shared between *Holarrhena* seeds and the seeds of *Wrightia tinctoria* (a separate Apocynaceous genus already in this codex). "Sweet Indrajao" tends to mean *Wrightia*, "bitter Indrajao" *Holarrhena* — but market substitution runs both ways, and a cultivator or buyer must key on the plant, not the bazaar name. **Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The doctrine here is unusually literal and unusually testable. "Cooling astringent that seals a leaking, inflamed lower gut" is a phenomenological description of what an antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antisecretory bark would *feel* like to a pre-microbial clinician. Dysentery is inflammation plus fluid loss plus an invading organism; the tongue-felt astringency (tannins precipitating mucosal protein) maps onto a real physical tightening of an inflamed surface, and the "cooling" maps onto the subjective relief of reduced inflammation. Where the doctrine overreaches is in treating *grahi* as a universal virtue — the same binding action that helps infectious dysentery is exactly wrong in a constipated or atonic gut, and the tradition's own contraindication logic (don't give to *vata*-cold states) is the corrective. The symbol to deflate is the idea that Kutaj "purifies the blood" (*raktashodhaka*) — a generic honorific attached to most bitters, with no specific mechanism behind it.

### Active Components

The pharmacologically defining class is the **steroidal alkaloids**, concentrated in bark and seed. The lead compound is **conessine**, accompanied by a family of congeners — isoconessimine, conessimine, conimine, conarrhimine, conkuressine, holarrhimine, kurchine and related bases; recent phytochemistry from the seeds has added new members such as isoconkuressine and N-formylconessimine (according to PubMed, Zhou et al. 2017, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1875-5364(17)30080-8)). Alongside the alkaloids the bark carries tannins (the astringent principle), triterpenes, and flavonoids (according to PubMed, Tiwari et al. 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2174/1386207326666230821102502)).

Conessine is the molecule that carries the drug's modern scientific identity. It is a lipophilic, brain-penetrant steroidal alkaloid — which is both the source of its pharmacological interest and the root of its toxicity profile (see Contraindications).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Within Kenetica, Kutaj is a **Cleansing/Consolidation-phase gut-sealing agent**: the tool reached for when the digestive channel is simultaneously infected, inflamed, and leaking. Its use case is acute — sub-acute infectious or inflammatory diarrhoea and dysentery with mucus, heat, and cramping — not chronic tonic support. The felt action is a rapid "tightening and cooling" of the lower gut: reduced urgency, reduced tenesmus, firmer stool.

It pairs conceptually with pungent digestive-kindlers (to prevent the astringency from stalling digestion) and is explicitly *not* stacked into constructive/tonic protocols. The cultivator's note: bark stripped sustainably from mature stems and the seeds (*Indrayava*) are the two working drugs; leaf preparations are weaker and more variable.

### Scientific Correlation

Graded honestly, by claim:

**Antibacterial — MODERATE, mechanistically plausible, in-vitro.** Steroidal alkaloids from the seeds show intrinsic antibacterial activity against *Staphylococcus aureus* including MRSA, and — more interestingly — synergy with penicillin and vancomycin, i.e. the alkaloids act as antibiotic adjuvants that lower the effective MIC of conventional drugs (according to PubMed, Zhou et al. 2017, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1875-5364(17)30080-8)). A separate study found methanol extracts of *H. antidysenterica* inhibit biofilm formation in toxigenic *Vibrio cholerae* (~55% at 0.6 mg/mL) and down-regulate the *aphA/aphB* virulence regulators, without killing the bacteria outright (according to PubMed, Jose et al. 2017, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4315/0362-028X.JFP-17-091)). Both are cell-culture studies; neither is a clinical trial.

**Antidysenteric / antiamoebic — TRADITIONALLY STRONG, MODERN DATA SPARSE (honest null on high-quality clinical evidence).** This is the headline claim and the one where I must be careful. The traditional and pharmacological review literature repeatedly assigns anti-diarrhoeal and antidysenteric activity to the bark (according to PubMed, Tiwari et al. 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2174/1386207326666230821102502)), consistent with the antibacterial and antibiofilm findings above. However, in this run I retrieved **no** modern randomised controlled trial demonstrating clinical cure of amoebic or bacillary dysentery in humans, and no species-specific antiamoebic assay against *Entamoeba histolytica*. The antiamoebic reputation is old, coherent, and pharmacologically not implausible — but as of this retrieval it rests on tradition plus mechanistic antibacterial data, **not** on controlled clinical proof. State it as promising, not proven.

**Conessine as an H3-receptor tool compound — CONFIRMED (but this is congener pharmacology, not a Kutaj clinical use).** Conessine is a well-characterised, brain-penetrant histamine H3-receptor antagonist and is used in neuroscience as a research tool: in vivo it attenuates methamphetamine-induced hyperlocomotion in mice at 20 mg/kg, comparably to the clinical H3 antagonist pitolisant (according to PubMed, Kitanaka et al. 2020, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brainres.2020.146873)). This is real and important — but it is a property of the *isolated molecule at injected doses*, and it does **not** license any CNS claim for Kutaj bark taken as a gut remedy. It is named here as congener/compound data, not as a species-specific therapeutic effect.

**Antimalarial / antiviral — WEAK / EXPLORATORY.** Conessine appears in screening lists of natural compounds with reported activity against human coronaviruses (according to PubMed, Khalifa et al. 2020, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2020.153311)), and older literature explored conessine as an antimalarial lead. These are screen-level and historical signals, not therapeutic endorsements; treat as "molecule of interest," nothing more. A molecular-dynamics study modelling conessine against a *Serratia marcescens* enzyme target (according to PubMed, Dhusia et al. 2018, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.meegid.2018.11.001)) is purely computational.

**Bottom line:** the antibacterial/antibiofilm and adjuvant findings give real mechanistic support to the classical antidysenteric use; the specific "cures amoebic dysentery" claim is under-evidenced by modern standards and should be flagged, not asserted.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Ayurveda:** *Rasa* — *tikta* (bitter), *kashaya* (astringent); *Virya* — *shita* (cooling); *Vipaka* — *katu* (pungent post-digestive); *Guna* — *laghu* (light), *ruksha* (dry). Action: *grahi* (gut-binding), *dipana* (digestive), *kapha-pitta*-reducing, *raktashodhaka* (blood-cleansing — an unsupported honorific, deflated above).
- **Unani:** cooling and drying temperament; antidysenteric, astringent, vermifuge.
- **Kenetica synthesis:** a cold, dry, sealing bark for a hot, wet, leaking lower gut — a stopper, not a builder.

### Contraindications

- ⚠️ **Conessine CNS toxicity at dose.** The defining caution. Conessine is lipophilic and brain-penetrant; historical and pharmacological literature associates the isolated alkaloid with central nervous system toxicity and evidence of neural accumulation with repeated/high dosing. This is a specific reason **not** to treat purified conessine or heavily alkaloid-enriched extracts as a stronger version of the bark — the tannin-buffered traditional decoction is a different, safer object than the isolated base. Avoid escalating dose to chase effect.
- **Atonic/cold diarrhoea, constipation, and *vata* states.** The astringent, binding action is contraindicated where the gut is already slow, dry, or constipated — the tradition's own rule, and a sound one.
- **Pregnancy and lactation.** Insufficient safety data for the alkaloid-bearing preparations; avoid. [USER: verify]
- **Species/identity risk.** Because *Indrajao* is shared with *Wrightia tinctoria*, mis-sourced seed is a real hazard — confirm botanical identity before use.
- **Not a monotherapy for confirmed amoebic/bacillary dysentery.** Given the sparse clinical evidence, do not rely on Kutaj alone where a confirmed invasive infection needs definitive antimicrobial treatment.
