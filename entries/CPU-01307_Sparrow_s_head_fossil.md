# Sparrow's-head fossil — bye.mgo (Clark 1995) / Jego (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *A fossil stone named for the shape of a sparrow's head, one of the stone medicines of the Explanatory Tantra; Clark glosses it "Spiriferoides: Cyrtiospirifer sinensis" and does not say what kind of organism that is* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The loci given for this entry are `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (row "bye.mgo", "Clark 1995 (line 827)", "Sparrow's Head Fossil; Cyrtiospirifer sinensis") and `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 137, where the name is spelled "Jego", class mineral, identification marked uncertain. Line 827 of `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` reads: "bye.mgo: Sparrow's Head Fossil: Spiriferoides: Cyrtiospirifer sinensis: [The superior type is light blue, smooth and small. The ordinary type is pale red, large, coarse and resembles a hawk's head. Both types] promote flesh growth." The entry is in chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (heading at line 792), under "(2) The Powers of Stone Medicines" (line 816). By Clark's own statement (preface, line 82), the square-bracketed parts are interpolations from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries; the statement that both types "promote flesh growth" is outside the brackets, and the description of the two types is inside them.

So the text gives the stone two types: a superior one that is light blue, smooth and small, and an ordinary one that is pale red, large and coarse and "resembles a hawk's head". It gives one action for both: they "promote flesh growth". It gives no place of origin, no way of testing, and no preparation. The names are by shape: the stone is a "sparrow's head" and the coarse type resembles a "hawk's head". As with its neighbours, the "pig's head" fossil (line 826) and the "frog's back" hematite stones (lines 817-818), the animal name describes the shape of the stone, and the text does not say that these are the remains of those animals.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`) has the same material in its list of stone medicines (line 3356): "Phag go restores weak bone and drains out chhuser. Jego gives the same therapeutic effect as Phag go and regenerates damaged tissues. Ser dho and Ngul dho drain out chhuser." Its name index cites "Jego 199" (line 7396). The tie between "Jego" and Clark's *bye.mgo* is not stated in either book. It is supported by the order of the lists: Clark has *phag.mgo*, then *bye.mgo*, then *gser.rdo* (gold ore, line 828) and *dngul.rdo* (silver ore); Men-Tsee-Khang has Phag go, then Jego, then Ser dho and Ngul dho. The reading is an inference from position and sound and is not confirmed by the Tibetan, which is not on disk. [USER: verify] The two renderings of the action also differ: Clark's "promote flesh growth" is the Men-Tsee-Khang's "regenerates damaged tissues", and the Men-Tsee-Khang adds that Jego has "the same therapeutic effect as Phag go", which in Clark's book is "heals bone disorders and drains lymph [accumulations]" (line 826). Clark does not state that equivalence for the sparrow's-head fossil.

A search of Clark's text for "sparrow" returns the entry (line 827), the index entries ("Sparrow's Head fossil", "Spiriferoides"), and unrelated passages on the bird: sparrows among the birds that dig with their beaks for food (line 672) and "flesh of chicken and sparrow increases semen" (line 678), plus a sparrow-feather lancet (line 1249). Those concern the bird and the instrument, not the fossil, and are not used here.

**Identity.** The translator's gloss, "Spiriferoides: Cyrtiospirifer sinensis", is a genus and species name in the form used for fossil shells; the spine's label "brachiopod" does not appear in the sources retrieved. In general palaeontology, spiriferid fossils are shells of brachiopods, marine animals with two shells, and a spiriferid shell with its pointed hinge can look like a bird's head in profile; that is a general fact and not a statement of the source. Whether the Tibetan stone was this fossil is the translator's identification and is marked uncertain in `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv`. [USER: verify]

**Other traditions.** None is tagged for this entry. The Ebers (Bryan), Assyrian (Thompson), Shennong (Yang) and Dioscorides texts on disk were searched for "sparrow": Ebers, Assyrian and Shennong returned nothing, and Dioscorides returned only "Sparrow Grass" (asparagus) and an index entry. The Sushruta chapter files returned the bird (a simile for sexual vigour in Chikitsa ch.26, and a sparrow's egg in ch.38), which is not the fossil. No fossil stone of this name was found in them.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, a fossil shell is the mineralised remains of a shell, usually calcium carbonate (calcite) or silica, sometimes with iron oxides that give red and brown colours; the colours described (light blue, pale red) could come from trace minerals or staining, but the text does not say. The composition of the Tibetan stone is not known from the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved say that the Tibetan Explanatory Tantra lists a fossil stone, in two types, as promoting flesh growth, and that the Men-Tsee-Khang text says it regenerates damaged tissues. The Codex does not recommend taking powdered fossil to grow flesh or to heal a wound. A wound or loss of tissue needs clinical care and cleaning; no mineral powder is a substitute.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources retrieved give no preparation for the sparrow's-head fossil: the lines read do not say how it was crushed, cleaned, compounded or taken, or in what vehicle, or whether it was used on the body or by mouth. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Fossil shells are largely calcium carbonate or silica, and they do not stimulate tissue growth; calcium carbonate is a common antacid and calcium supplement, but a fossil powder from an unknown source is not a standardised material and can carry contaminants such as lead or arsenic. Wound healing depends on cleaning, closure, nutrition and the control of infection. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** the entry prints no taste, no power (hot, cool, heavy, light and the like, in the sense of chapter 20, line 794) and no post-digestive taste for *bye.mgo*. The only qualities are physical: the superior type is light blue, smooth and small; the ordinary type is pale red, large and coarse. The Men-Tsee-Khang text adds none. ### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for the sparrow's-head fossil in the lines read. The entry is a statement of one action, on the flesh, and a description of two types. Clark records elsewhere in chapter 20 which substances are named for essence-extraction (*bcud len*): calcite (line 853), heart-leaved moonseed (line 971) and mineral exudate (line 870); *bye.mgo* is not among them. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted. The resemblance to a sparrow's head is a description of shape and is not given a meaning in the text.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The record is a short entry in a list of stone medicines: a fossil of two types, one light blue and small and one pale red and coarse, said to promote flesh growth. It is not securely identified, and no preparation or dose is sourced. A reader who meets this substance meets it as a curiosity of the Tibetan list, not as a remedy to take. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Wounds and tissue loss:** these need cleaning, assessment for infection and, where required, closure; a stone powder does not substitute and can introduce infection.
- **Unidentified material:** the composition of the "sparrow's-head fossil" is not established in the sources read, and an unrefined mineral may contain heavy metals.
- **Ingestion of stone powder:** abrasive and indigestible; avoid.
- **Pregnancy and children:** unsuitable.
- **Identification:** the link between Clark's *bye.mgo* and the Men-Tsee-Khang "Jego" is an inference from the order of the lists. [USER: verify]
- **Conservation and trade:** fossils are often protected or regulated in the country of origin; collecting and export are not covered here. [USER: verify]
