### Origin & Tradition

**Suśruta Saṃhitā & Ayurvedic pot-herb tradition:** Tarkārī / Ajagandhā is treated as a **pungent, warming, digestion-kindling leafy pot-herb (śāka)** with sharp medicinal reach. In the classical and nighaṇṭu literature its character is **kaṭu-tīkṣṇa-uṣṇa** — used as a **dīpana** (appetiser/digestive), **kṛmighna** (anthelmintic), **kaphaghna** and **śothahara** agent, and as a **rubefacient/counter-irritant** (the mustard-like pungency of the crushed leaf and seed raising a blister or warmth over painful, rheumatic joints, and clearing the head in catarrh). The seed is a classical vermifuge and the leaf-juice a febrifuge and ear/eye instillation in folk practice. It belongs to the pungent Brassicales-adjacent greens whose glucosinolate chemistry gives them a shared "mustard-oil" therapeutic logic.

**Bhāvaprakāśa & regional Ayurveda:** Listed among the śāka (edible greens), valued as a rocana (appetising), agni-stimulating vegetable that "cuts through" Kapha and āma (metabolic residue). Regional names and identifications overlap with the *Cleome viscosa* (yellow hurhur) kin, and care is needed in botanical attribution.

**Pan-African & Southeast-Asian folk medicine:** *C. gynandra* is one of the most important **traditional leafy vegetables of sub-Saharan Africa** ("African cabbage," spider plant), a nutrient-dense famine and staple green, and simultaneously a folk medicine for **inflammation, rheumatism, skin disorders, wounds, ear infections, scurvy, and as an anthelmintic and blood-tonic**. In Southeast Asia it appears in ethnobotanical surveys as a top-rated weed remedy for skin disease. This dual food-and-medicine status parallels Shigru and Kadalī.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Wayside Fire-Green:** Tarkārī's doctrinal signature is **humble pungent vitality**. It is a weed — a wayside, self-seeding, disturbance-loving plant — yet nutrient-dense and sharply medicinal. In the codex's symbolic grammar it belongs with the **"despised greens that carry fire"**: unglamorous pot-herbs whose value is precisely their pungent, agni-lighting, Kapha-cutting sharpness combined with mineral-rich nourishment. The spider-limbed flower (the "cat's whiskers" / spider plant) and its readiness to colonise poor ground make it an emblem of **accessible, resilient vitality** — the medicine of the ordinary field. For a PRIME entry this is the signature of *cheap, sharp, nourishing activation*: the green that wakes the body and feeds it at once.

### Active Components

*C. gynandra* leaf/seed contribute: **glucosinolates and derived isothiocyanates** (the pungent, rubefacient, anthelmintic and anti-inflammatory mustard-oil class); **flavonoids and phenolics (including quercetin-type)**; **carotenoids (β-carotene) and high vitamin C**; **dense minerals — calcium, potassium, magnesium, phosphorus, iron**; and **the cleomiscosin/coumarinolignan and terpenoid constituents characteristic of the genus**, plus fixed oil in seed. The glucosinolate/isothiocyanate + polyphenol axis underwrites the sharp pharmacology; the mineral/vitamin density underwrites its food value.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Filed under **`[PRIME]`**. Tarkārī is Kenetica's *accessible pungent activator-green*: warming, digestion-stimulating, Kapha-cutting, and richly mineralised — an economical activation-window food that raises metabolic readiness while supplying calcium, iron, potassium and vitamin C. Its traditional anti-inflammatory/rubefacient and anthelmintic actions and modern antioxidant/micronutrient data support this role. Used **as a cooked green (food-first)**; crushed leaf/seed externally as a warming counter-irritant.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, *C. gynandra* has a **modest, mostly nutritional and ethnobotanical** evidence base, with pharmacology largely preclinical; the strongest documented data concern its micronutrient/antioxidant profile and its ethnomedical skin/anti-inflammatory use.

- **Micronutrient & antioxidant characterisation (compositional study):** *Gynandropsis gynandra* (= *C. gynandra*) accessions were quantified for minerals (Ca 0.82–2.32, K 1.61–7.29, Mg 0.61–1.48, P 0.27–1.44 mg/g FW), vitamin C, phenolics, flavonoids and antioxidant activity; a serving qualifies as an excellent vitamin-C source — supporting its status as a nutrient-dense leafy vegetable (Jiménez-Aguilar & Grusak, 2015, *Plant Foods for Human Nutrition*). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11130-015-0512-7)
- **Ethnopharmacology of skin disorders (field survey + literature):** In a survey of medicinal weeds of southern Thailand, *C. gynandra* recorded a **100% Fidelity Level** for its use-category, with the reviewed biological basis for such skin-remedy weeds spanning antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, wound-healing and antioxidant activity (Neamsuvan & Bunmee, 2016, *J Ethnopharmacol*). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2016.09.048)

**Evidence grade: preclinical / nutritional & ethnobotanical.** The micronutrient-antioxidant value and traditional skin/anti-inflammatory use are documented; **no human therapeutic trials** validate specific medicinal claims. Attribution: PubMed.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Rasa (taste):** Kaṭu (pungent), Tikta (bitter)
- **Guṇa (quality):** Laghu (light), Rūkṣa (dry), Tīkṣṇa (sharp)
- **Vīrya (potency):** Uṣṇa (hot)
- **Vipāka (post-digestive):** Kaṭu (pungent)
- **Prabhāva (specific action):** Kṛmighna & śothahara — a specific anthelmintic/anti-inflammatory potency; rubefacient counter-irritant action of the crushed herb
- **Doṣa action:** Kapha-Vāta-śāmaka; Pitta-vardhaka (pungent-hot; aggravates Pitta/heat in excess)
- **Karma (actions):** Dīpana-Pācana (digestive-kindling), Rocana (appetising), Kṛmighna (anthelmintic), Śothahara (anti-inflammatory), Kaphaghna (Kapha-reducing), Vraṇaropaṇa (wound-supportive, folk), Jvaraghna (febrifuge, folk)
- **Gaṇa/varga membership:** Śāka-varga pungent pot-herbs; grouped near the Varuṇādi-type pungent-warming greens

### Contraindications

- **Pitta excess / heat & acidity:** pungent-hot nature aggravates Pitta, gastritis and bleeding tendencies — moderate in high-Pitta states.
- **Oxalate/bitter load:** like many leafy greens it should be **cooked/blanched**; excessive raw intake is unpalatable and may burden those prone to oxalate issues — `[USER: verify]` for kidney-stone-prone individuals.
- **Topical irritation:** crushed leaf/seed is a mustard-type vesicant — can blister skin and irritate eyes/mucosa; keep away from the eyes.
- **Pregnancy/lactation & paediatrics:** medicinal (non-food) internal use lacks safety data — avoid concentrated preparations.
- **Botanical confusion:** distinguish *C. gynandra* from *C. viscosa* and other "hurhur" species, which differ in constituents and strength.
