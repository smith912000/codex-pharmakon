# Hart's-tongue fern — Phullitis (Dioscorides 3-121)

## *Asplenium scolopendrium, also written Phyllitis scolopendrium (the Dioscorides translator's suggestion for chapter 3-121)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). The binomials below are the translator's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification. **Named only by Dioscorides in this row.**

*3-121 PHULLITIS* (heading at line 7628). The translator's line reads "Asplenium scolopendrium, Scolopendrium vulgare, Scolopendrium officinarum, Phyllitis scolopendrium, Adiantum scolopendrium — Hart's Tongue Fern, Horse Tongue". The text (line 7630): "Phyllitis sends out six or seven upright leaves similar to rumex [2-141] yet somewhat longer and more flourishing, smooth on the front parts, but on the back parts having (as it were) thin little worms hanging. It grows in shady places and pleasure gardens. It is bitter to the taste and has no stalk, seed, or flower. A decoction of the leaves (taken as a drink with wine) is good for those bitten by snakes. It is helpful for four-footed beasts [veterinary] poured in through the mouth. It is taken as a drink for dysentery and diarrhoea. It is also called phyllis, acaulon, or lapathum sylvestre."

**Overlap.** The same binomial is given by the translator for chapter 3-152 *Emionitis* (heading at line 7833; "Asplenium scolopendrium, Phyllitis scolopendrium ... Hart's-tongue Fern [other usage] Hemionitis — Mulewort"). That chapter has its own spine row, CPU-01596 *Phyllitis scolopendrium*. 3-152 gives a horned leaf, many slender roots, an astringent herb taken in a drink with vinegar to reduce the spleen. Dioscorides' own text has two separate fern chapters under the two Greek names. Other chapters compare their leaves with the *scolopendrium* of 3-121 (lines 7909 and 8445). The black spleenwort chapter 3-151 (I_1019) also uses the names scolopendrium and splenium. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Ferns of the genus *Asplenium* are generally known as spore-bearing plants without flowers or seeds, which agrees with the text. The back of the frond bears the spore-lines the text calls "thin little worms". Their chemistry was not read for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The text records a leaf decoction in wine for snakebite and for dysentery and diarrhoea, and a veterinary use. The Codex does not recommend the plant as a practice. The translator does not mark it poisonous. No protocol is given.

### Preparation & Dosing

Leaves: "A decoction of the leaves (taken as a drink with wine)" for snakebite; "taken as a drink" for dysentery and diarrhoea; "poured in through the mouth" for animals (line 7630). No quantity is printed. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Ferns have no well-established antivenom or antidiarrhoeal action in modern medicine. Snakebite requires emergency medical care. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** 3-121 prints "bitter to the taste". No warming or cooling quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers is the discipline of attention the cultivation spec describes: where the plant grows (shady places and pleasure gardens), how to know it (six or seven upright dock-like leaves, smooth in front, with the spore-lines behind like little worms; no stalk, seed or flower), and its names (phyllis, acaulon, lapathum sylvestre). It records no use for the person's vital constitution and assigns no status. The identification is the translator's. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator takes the chapter's description of a plant seen in its place, front and back of the leaf. The uses are for snakebite and dysentery and give no practice for the self.

### Contraindications

Do not rely on any herb for snakebite; it needs emergency medical care. No traditional use above is advice. Dysentery and diarrhoea with blood, fever or dehydration need medical care. No dose is given.
