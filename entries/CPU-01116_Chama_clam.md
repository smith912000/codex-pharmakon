# Chama clam — Chamai (Dioscorides 2-9) / Chamae
## *Chama, a bivalve mollusc: the translator's heading is "Chamae species — Chama, bivalve molluscs"; no common species is named in the chapter* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The English is the 2000 edition of Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). The entry is Book 2, chapter 9, headed *CHAMAI*, with the translator's identification "Chamae species — Chama, bivalve molluscs", under "Living Creatures" (line 4948; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` locus "2-9 (line 4948)", class "animal"). The chapter is two sentences long and I read all of it: "The broth of chamae and other shellfish boiled in a little water stirs the bowels. It is taken with wine."

So the whole of what the text says is this: a broth, made by boiling the shellfish "in a little water", moves the bowels, and is taken with wine. The chapter names "other shellfish" with the chamae without saying which, and it gives no taste, no season, no place of origin and no way to tell a good one from a bad one. The identification as a bivalve mollusc is the translator's. The English text does not say which bivalve a *chama* is, and the species-level identity is not settled by anything I read. Another Dioscorides chapter should not be mistaken for it: the plants whose names begin *chamai-* (germander, chamomile and others in Books 3 and 4) are unrelated herbs.

The neighbouring shellfish chapters give the setting, and I read them: *Tellinai* (2-8, line 4941) says that tellinae "whilst they are fresh and new are good for the bowels, especially their broth", and that salted, burnt and powdered they are dropped on the eyelids so that plucked hairs do not grow back; *Muakes*, mussels (2-7, line 4938), are best from Pontus and "their flesh is effective applied to one bitten by a dog"; the burnt shells of *Porphura* (2-4) and *Kurekes* (2-5) are used to clean teeth and as caustics. Those chapters belong to their own entries, but they show that a broth of bivalves for the bowels is a recurring item in this part of the book. Of the chama, the text says only the two sentences above.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, bivalve molluscs are filter feeders. The flesh is protein, with minerals including zinc, iodine and iron, and the broth carries soluble salts and amino acids. Their filtering concentrates whatever is in the water, which is why they can carry microbes, algal toxins and heavy metals. No quantities are given here because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records one use: a broth of chamae and other shellfish, boiled in a little water, taken with wine to stir the bowels. The Codex does not recommend it. The identity of the animal is uncertain, the broth is an ordinary preparation of a food, and shellfish carry well-known risks, set out below. Where bivalves are an ordinary food they need no protocol from this Codex.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparation the text gives is the broth: the shellfish are "boiled in a little water" and the broth is "taken with wine" (2-9). No quantity of shellfish, no quantity of water, no quantity of wine, no time of boiling and no amount to take is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Raw and undercooked bivalves are a recognised source of foodborne illness, including norovirus, hepatitis A, *Vibrio* bacteria and algal toxins (such as those responsible for paralytic and diarrhoeic shellfish poisoning). Shellfish are also a common cause of food allergy. A shellfish broth is a source of salts and amino acids. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry, and nothing retrieved shows that it moves the bowels. [USER: verify]

**Greek (DMM):** 2-9 prints one action, that the broth "stirs the bowels". It prints no temperature, moisture, taste or other quality. The neighbouring 2-8 prints "good for the bowels" for the tellinae, which is a different animal and a different statement. No further classification is supplied here.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The chama is a name, a translator's gloss and a broth. For someone who eats bivalves the sensible lesson is the ordinary one: know where they come from and cook them. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Infection and toxins:** raw or lightly cooked bivalves can carry viruses, bacteria and algal toxins; harvest-area warnings (red tide, sewage) apply. Boiling reduces some but not all of these risks, and does not remove algal toxins.
- **Allergy:** shellfish allergy can be severe and is lifelong in many people; a broth carries the allergen.
- **Alcohol:** the text takes the broth with wine; the usual cautions about alcohol apply, and people who avoid it need not follow that part.
- **Bowel conditions:** a preparation said to stir the bowels is unsuitable in diarrhoea, in gastroenteritis, and in any acute bowel illness.
- **Identity:** the animal is not identified with certainty, so no safe-harvest claim can be made for "chama" as the text names it.
- **Conservation:** wild shellfish harvesting is regulated in many places; follow local rules.
