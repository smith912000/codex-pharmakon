### Botanical Identity
Annual or short-lived perennial 30–60cm; distinctive orange-yellow daisy-like flowers; resinous, slightly sticky stems and leaves; flowers throughout the growing season (hence *calendula* = calendar plant). Native to Mediterranean; now cosmopolitan garden plant. The flower heads (harvested at full bloom, dried) are the drug material — the orange colour from carotenoids (lutein, zeaxanthin, lycopene) is a visible quality indicator; paler flowers have lower carotenoid content and less potent anti-inflammatory activity.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 De Materia Medica (DMM)

Dioscorides on yellow Compositae (*chrysanthemon* group) for wound healing and skin conditions. Later Roman tradition (Pliny) specifically describes *calendula* for skin conditions, "dispelling poison" (anti-infective), and wound care.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine (CM)

Ibn Sīnā on *qahwān*:
> *"Warm and moist. Resolves inflammations and swellings, heals wounds. Taken internally it benefits the heart and reduces fever. The flowers in oil, applied to affected skin, reduce inflammations and promote healing. Useful for menstrual irregularity."*

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (EP)

Yellow/orange Compositae in Egyptian preparations for skin conditions and wound care — consistent with calendula's traditional applications.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)

Orange-yellow flower preparations in Babylonian texts for wound care and fever.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Wound Healing (Primary)

**Oleanolic acid saponins + polysaccharides**: Stimulate fibroblast migration into wound bed; increase collagen deposition; promote re-epithelialisation. The specific saponin fraction of calendula is more potent than asiaticoside (gotu kola) for epidermal regeneration in some comparative models.

**Mechanism detail**: Calendula extract activates TGF-β1 signalling → fibroblast differentiation to myofibroblasts → wound contraction + collagen synthesis. Also stimulates keratinocyte migration → faster epithelial closure.

### 5.2 Anti-Inflammatory

**Isorhamnetin + quercetin**: NF-κB inhibition; COX-2 inhibition; 5-LOX inhibition; reduction of TNF-α and IL-6. The triple COX/LOX/NF-κB inhibition makes calendula exceptionally broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory topically.

**Triterpenoids**: Inhibit prostaglandin E2 synthesis — confirms the traditional anti-inflammatory application for skin.

### 5.3 Antimicrobial / Antifungal

**Essential oil (α-cadinol) + resins**: Active against *Staphylococcus aureus*, *E. coli*, *Candida albicans*, *Dermatophytes* (athlete's foot fungi) in vitro. The traditional use for infected wounds and fungal skin conditions is supported.

### 5.4 Lymphagogic / Venous

**Saponins**: Improve lymphatic drainage; reduce oedema. The traditional application for swollen lymph nodes and pelvic congestion (emmenagogue + anti-inflammatory).

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Radiation dermatitis (oncology)**: Pommier (2004, *Journal of Clinical Oncology*): Calendula cream vs trolamine (standard) in breast cancer patients receiving radiotherapy — calendula significantly reduced grade 2+ radiation dermatitis (41% vs 63%). Clinically meaningful reduction in radiation skin damage. This is the most significant single RCT for calendula.

**Post-surgical wound healing**: Multiple RCTs confirm faster wound healing and reduced infection rates with calendula preparations vs controls.

**Venous leg ulcers**: Duran (2005): Calendula ointment significantly improved ulcer healing rates vs standard wound care.

**Neonatal diaper rash**: Panahi (2012): Calendula cream vs petroleum jelly — calendula significantly superior for resolution of diaper dermatitis in infants.

**Oral mucositis (cancer patients)**: Barasch (2011): Calendula mouthwash significantly reduced oral mucositis in head-and-neck cancer patients during radiotherapy.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The calendar flower**: The naming of *calendula* from the Roman *calendae* (the first day of each month) reflects an agricultural-pharmacological observation: this plant flowers continuously, month after month, through the growing season. A wound-healing herb that is always available — always in flower — in the kitchen garden would be the default choice for any acute skin injury in the Mediterranean household. The Latin name encodes both the pharmacological application (always available for wounds) and the botanical observation (continuous flowering) in a single word.

**Mary's gold**: The medieval European name "Mary's gold" (*marigold*) embedded calendula in the Marian tradition of healing plants — flowers placed on altars of the Virgin Mary, used in women's conditions, associated with divine protection. The yellow-orange colour (the colour of gold, of the sun, of divine light in Western iconography) reinforced the sacred-medicinal association. The plant's actual wound-healing pharmacology was preserved within a religious framework that maintained both cultivation and therapeutic knowledge across the medieval period.

### 8.1 Preparation

|------|------|-------------|

| Oil infusion (flowers in olive oil) | Topical | Skin healing; massage; scar prevention |
| Mouthwash (diluted tea/tincture) | Gargle 1–2 min | Oral mucositis; gingivitis |

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Bitter, slightly sweet; cool to neutral. Channels: Liver (肝), Stomach (胃). Clears heat, reduces toxins, activates Blood.

**Ayurvedic**: Bitter, slightly pungent; cooling. Reduces Pitta ↓↓. *Twakya* (skin-beneficial), *Kuṣṭhāghna* (skin disease), *Shoṇitasthāpana* (haemostatic), mild *Artavajanana*.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Asteraceae allergy**: Cross-reactivity with ragweed, chamomile, chrysanthemum — screen allergy history; patch test before topical use
- **Pregnancy**: Emmenagogue effect — avoid therapeutic internal doses; topical at standard concentrations probably safe
- **Sedatives**: Mild sedation at high internal doses — additive
- Generally one of the safest topical herbs in the Codex; extraordinary safety record in neonatal and paediatric use
