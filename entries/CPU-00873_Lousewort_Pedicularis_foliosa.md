# Leafy Lousewort — Maxianhao (马先蒿) · Lousewort · Pedicularis

## *Pedicularis foliosa* L. | Family: Orobanchaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM, FMT
**Energetic Classification:** Cool and dry (traditional attribution) — an astringent, heat-settling alpine herb; classification tentative given sparse data
**Training Phase:** Cooling / Restoration — the high-altitude recovery phase, an anti-fatigue and steadying herb of thin air

---

### Origin & Tradition

Leafy lousewort is a stout alpine and subalpine perennial of European mountain meadows, bearing dense leafy spikes of pale yellow flowers. Like all *Pedicularis* it is a **hemiparasite** — green and photosynthetic, but tapping neighbouring roots for water and minerals. The common name "lousewort" preserves an old European folk belief (recorded in classical and mediaeval herbals) that the plant bred or attracted lice in grazing livestock; the genus name *Pedicularis*, from Latin *pediculus* (louse), fossilises the same idea.

**Classical / European herbal stream (DMM):** *Pedicularis foliosa* carries the Linnaean species name and belongs to the "lousewort" complex of the old European herbals descending from Dioscorides-era plant lore, where the louseworts were noted chiefly for the veterinary louse-superstition and as astringent, wound-checking meadow herbs rather than as major internal remedies. Species-specific classical medicinal detail is thin.

**Unani / Avicennan frame (CM):** The louseworts enter the wider humoral pharmacy as cool, drying, astringent herbs — the temperament assigned to bitter, binding mountain plants used to check flux and steady the body. Attribution to *P. foliosa* specifically is by class, not by a dedicated Avicennan monograph.

**Tibetan medicine — rGyud-bzhi lineage (FMT):** The genus *Pedicularis* is genuinely important in Tibetan and Himalayan medicine, where several *Pedicularis* species ("lug-ru" / snowland louseworts) appear in the *rGyud-bzhi* (Four Tantras) tradition as high-altitude herbs used for heat conditions, wounds, and as tonics suited to the thin-air environment where they grow. *P. foliosa* is a genuine member of this medicinally-used genus, though the classical Tibetan formulae draw more on named Himalayan species than on this European one — a distinction worth honesty about.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The plant's biology is its emblem. A hemiparasite that keeps its own green leaves yet draws from its neighbours' roots reads, in the Kenetica frame, as the herb of *supported recovery* — self-sustaining but quietly borrowing strength, exactly the posture of a body recovering at altitude. That it thrives in oxygen-poor high meadows and that its genus yields genuine anti-fatigue, anti-hypoxia chemistry (below) closes the loop between where it lives and what it is asked to do: steady the depleted, at height.

### Active Components

*Species-specific phytochemistry for* P. foliosa *is sparse.* Characterisation is drawn from the well-studied genus *Pedicularis*, which is consistently rich in:

- **Phenylethanoid glycosides (PhGs):** notably **acteoside (verbascoside)** and **isoverbascoside** — the signature and best-studied actives of the genus, strong antioxidants.
- **Iridoid glycosides:** including **aucubin**-type compounds (relevant to the toxicity note below).
- Flavonoids, and lignan/phenolic constituents typical of Orobanchaceae hemiparasites.

Whether *P. foliosa* carries these in the same proportions as the studied Asian species is **not established in the retrieved literature** and should not be assumed quantitatively.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Leafy lousewort is filed as a **high-altitude steadier** — an astringent, antioxidant, anti-fatigue herb of the cooling-restoration phase, aligned with the genus's documented capacity to blunt hypoxic oxidative stress and support endurance under thin air. Traditional roles across the louseworts are astringent (checking flux, supporting wounds) and tonic-restorative in mountain environments. In the Kenetica it is a supportive, convalescent herb for depletion and altitude-type fatigue, not an acute or heroic remedy. Given the data gap, use is conservative and traditional.

### Scientific Correlation

Direct pharmacological studies of *Pedicularis foliosa* are **essentially absent** from the retrieved record; the evidence is **genus-level**. According to PubMed: phenylethanoid glycosides isolated from *Pedicularis kansuensis* showed strong in-vitro antioxidant (radical-scavenging) activity and, in mice, significantly prolonged swimming endurance and reduced hypoxia-induced oxidative stress under simulated high altitude, supporting an anti-fatigue role (PubMed PMID 32227769; no DOI registered). Isoverbascoside from *Pedicularis striata* induced differentiation and inhibited proliferation in human hepatocarcinoma cells in vitro ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-2002-26759)). And an extract of *Pedicularis resupinata*, standardised to the phenylethanoid glycoside **acteoside**, prevented corticosterone-induced depressive-like behaviour in mice, restoring BDNF levels ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27113434)).

**Evidence grade: Traditional (genus-level preclinical only).** These are congeneric-species, mostly animal/in-vitro studies — they establish that the *genus* carries pharmacologically active phenylethanoid glycosides, but they are **not** evidence for *P. foliosa* specifically. Claims for this species must be graded traditional and framed as genus-inferred, not proven.

### Contraindications

**⚠️ Data-sparse — treat with caution.** There is **no adequate modern safety evaluation of *Pedicularis foliosa***, and internal use rests on genus-level inference rather than species data. **Several *Pedicularis* species contain iridoid glycosides such as aucubin, which are mildly toxic and can be irritant/purgative in quantity** — so internal doses should be conservative and are marked [USER: verify]. Historic reports also associate some louseworts with **livestock toxicity/unpalatability**, reinforcing caution. Avoid in pregnancy, lactation, and in children given the absence of safety data. Because this is a hemiparasite drawing minerals from host plants and soil, wild-gathered material may vary widely in composition. Correct identification within a large, taxonomically confusing genus is essential. Do not treat this entry as clinical authorisation — it documents traditional and preclinical context only.
