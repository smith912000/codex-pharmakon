# Aster himalaicus — Metok lugmig (me tok lug mig), as paired by column position in the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Aster himalaicus, as the binomial printed in the scientific-name column of the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary; the Tibetan name beside it is "Metok lugmig" by column position only, and no English common name can be matched to it. Clark 1995 prints other binomials for me.tog.lug.mig* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts, and under this binomial only by the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. Both Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The extraction note says: "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, row 319). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (Explanatory Tantra, glossary).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate blocks. The block at lines 5471 to 5484 prints four phonetic names (lines 5471 to 5474): "Maru tse", "Men ga", "Metok lugmig" and "Metok serchen". It prints four Tibetan transliterations (lines 5476 to 5479): "ma ru rtse", "sman sga", "me tok lug mig" and "me tok ser can". It prints four scientific names (lines 5481 to 5484): "Butea monosperma", "Hedychium spicatum", "Aster himalaicus" (line 5483) and "Calendula officinalis". The English names that follow (lines 5495 to 5497) are three: "Bastard teak", "Kind of ginger" and "Calendula, marigold". "Aster himalaicus" is the third of the four scientific names, and "Metok lugmig" is the third of the four phonetic names. The glossary does not print them on one row, and the Codex does not state that it pairs them. The pairing is by column position only. The English names, by the same order, fall to the first, second and fourth entries, so none is matched to the third. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, the name "Metok lugmig" in the running text.* The name appears once in the Explanatory Tantra text and once in its index. Chapter 20, in the efficacy of herbal medicines (the section begins at line 3428), line 3448 reads: "Metok lugmig is an antidote against poison and cures epidemic diseases." The index (line 7441) gives "Metok lugmig 209". That the glossary's "Metok lugmig" is the name in this line is a reading from the identical spelling. The text does not say that the plant in the line is *Aster himalaicus*. The same line holds, several sentences earlier, "Metok Serchhen treats wounds and necrotic channels", which the Codex reads as the neighbouring glossary name "Metok serchen" (a spelling difference only). [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, the same Tibetan name with other binomials (line 1077).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads: "me.tog.lug.mig (lit. 'sheep's eye flower'): Aster barbellatus: Aster heolini: Aster flaccidus Bge.: [grows in rocky meadows and has small, rounded, pale blue leaves, tall, brown stalk and blue flowers with yellow pistils. It has slightly bitter taste, neutralizes] poison and cures contagious fevers." Clark's preface (line 82) says "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". So the habitat, the description, the slightly bitter taste and the verb "neutralizes" are the commentary layer. Outside the brackets are Clark's own identifications (editorial, not root text), the gloss in parentheses and the words "poison and cures contagious fevers", which are the root-text layer, with the first verb supplied from the commentary. Clark does not print *Aster himalaicus*. The agreement of the two texts on poison and on fevers or epidemic disease supports the reading that "Metok lugmig" and *me.tog.lug.mig* are one name, but the text does not state it. The spine holds a separate row for Clark's first binomial (CPU-01674, *Aster barbellatus*). [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Men-Tsee-Khang text records, under the name "Metok lugmig", an antidote against poison that cures epidemic diseases. These are the text's claims, under a name that the glossary pairs with this binomial only by position, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for poisoning or epidemic disease, and the botanical identity is uncertain.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Men-Tsee-Khang materia medica classes "Metok lugmig" by what it does: it is an antidote against poison and cures epidemic diseases. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. The chapter on maintaining health through rejuvenation (chapter 23, lines 3906 to 3942) was read for the names of the drugs it uses, and it does not name "Metok lugmig". No line read assigns the plant to *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Its framing is therapeutic only, and the link from this binomial to that framing rests on a column position and a spelling.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is an antidote-and-epidemic drug in the Men-Tsee-Khang text, and the link to *Aster himalaicus* rests on a column position. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. The identity rests on a column position and the species is not certain, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Poisoning is a medical emergency, and a plant should not be taken as an antidote on the strength of a materia medica. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
