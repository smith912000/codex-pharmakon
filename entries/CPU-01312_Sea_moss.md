# Sea moss (Corallina) — Bruon Thalassion / Muscus marinus (as printed in the 2000 English edition of Dioscorides)
## *A hairy, stalkless "sea moss" growing on stones and shells by the sea; the translator's heading to 4-99 suggests "Corallina officinalis — Thread Moss, Wall Moss" among other names, and the chapter text itself gives no binomial* | *(Volume IV — Fungal/Algal, shelved in Volume I)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus given for this entry is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` ("4-99 (line 8919)", Bryon, Bryum, Brion, *Corallina officinalis*, Thread Moss, Wall Moss, Corallina, *Muscus arboreus*, Moss, Lichen Moss), class plant; `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 641 has the entry as "Thread Moss", *Corallina officinalis*, plant, DMM IV, identification marked no. The chapter is *De Materia Medica* 4-99 *Bruon Thalassion* in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`: heading at line 8919, the translator's suggestion at line 8920, the text at line 8921. It sits in Book 4, "Other herbs and roots", after 4-98 *Thaliktron* (line 8904) and before 4-100 *Phukos Thalassion* (a sea plant glossed *Fucus vesiculosus*, bladderwrack; line 8922). The heading means "sea *bruon*", and the translator's note at 1-20 (*Bruon*, hops; line 3732) says that "Splachnum and Bryum have been used to name various mosses in modern times [Loudon]", which explains the name Bryum in the gloss.

The chapter reads in full (line 8921): "Muscus marinus grows on stones and shells by the sea — hairy, slender, without a stalk, very astringent — good for inflammation, and gout in the feet that needs astringency. It is also called ballaris, or irane, and the Romans call it gnomeusilum." The dashes are the translator's. So the text gives the plant the Latin name *muscus marinus* ("sea moss") and three other names, *ballaris*, *irane* and (Roman) *gnomeusilum*; a habitat (stones and shells by the sea); a description (hairy, slender, without a stalk); a quality (very astringent); and two uses: for inflammation, and for gout in the feet that needs astringency. The chapter says nothing of the form in which it was used (poultice, drink, wash), the part used, the way of gathering or drying it, a place of origin beyond "by the sea", or a quantity.

The next chapter, 4-100, treats three sorts of *phukos thalassion* (sea *fucus*), all "cooling in poultices, good for gouty afflictions and inflammation, but they must use them while they are still moist (before they dry)" (line 8924), and says that the Phoenician kind is "good against snakes". That chapter is a different plant, not part of this entry; it is noted only because it shares the gout and inflammation indications and gives the one preparation rule (poultice, used moist) that this chapter lacks. The sources retrieved do not say that 4-99 was to be used in the same way. Another passage near the name is 4-53 *Leichen* (lichen, line 8471), which says "Lichen grows on rocks and is also called bryon. It is a moss sticking to moist rocks", applied to stop blood discharges, lessen inflammation, heal the skin disease called lichen and, with honey, help jaundice (line 8476); it is a land lichen or moss and a separate entry in the text.

**Identity.** The translator's suggestions at line 8920 are a list of names, not a single identification: *Bryon*, *Bryum*, *Brion* [French], *Corallina officinalis* with common names Thread Moss and Wall Moss, *Corallina*, *Muscus arboreus*, Moss and Lichen Moss. The spine's label "Sea moss (Corallina)" follows the *Corallina* suggestion; the `_sources/dioscorides.csv` row marks id_uncertain "no", but the gloss is the translator's, and the chapter's own description (hairy, slender, stalkless, on stones and shells by the sea) does not itself fix a species. In general biology, *Corallina officinalis* is a calcified red alga, a seaweed and not a moss, and "sea moss" is also a trade name for other seaweeds; the text's *muscus* ("moss") is read here as a descriptive word for what grows on stones and shells, not a botanical statement in the modern sense. **Other traditions.** None is tagged for this entry. A search of the other texts on disk for "seaweed", "algae", "kelp", "sargass" and "corallina" found one relevant chapter: the Shennong text (Yang 1998, `Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`, line 3099, under "Herbs: Middle Class", line 3093) has *Hai Zao* (Herba Sargassii), "bitter and cold", which "mainly treats goiters and tumors and nodes in the neck", with "Luo Shou (Fallen Head)" as another name and "grows in pools and swamps" as its habitat. ### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, the red alga *Corallina officinalis*, if that is the plant, is stiff because its tissue is encrusted with calcium carbonate; seaweeds in general contain polysaccharides (such as agar- and carrageenan-type gels in red algae), minerals including iodine in many species, and small amounts of pigments and proteins. Which sea plant the Greek *bruon thalassion* was is not established, so the composition of the ancient material is not known from the sources. "Very astringent", in the chapter, is a sensory report and not a named constituent. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved say that Dioscorides recorded a hairy, stalkless sea moss from stones and shells by the sea as very astringent, good for inflammation and for gout in the feet. The Codex does not recommend gathering unidentified seaweed to treat gout or inflammation. Gout is a metabolic arthritis that needs diagnosis and prescribed treatment, and a wild-gathered seaweed from an unknown shore is of unknown identity and cleanliness. The chapter gives no preparation, so there is no practice to describe.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives no preparation for the sea moss: it does not say whether it was applied fresh, dried, pounded or boiled, or whether it was used on the skin or taken by mouth. The nearby chapter on *phukos thalassion* (4-100, line 8924) says that plant was used as a cooling poultice while still moist; that rule is stated of that plant and is not transferred. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Seaweeds are eaten and used in many food traditions; some concentrate iodine, which in excess can disturb the thyroid, and some concentrate heavy metals such as arsenic or cadmium from polluted water. Calcified red algae are hard, chalky and indigestible. Gout is caused by uric acid crystals in a joint and is treated with medicines; there is no evidence that a seaweed treats it. Astringent substances tighten tissue by precipitating proteins, which is a plain description of the sensation and not an anti-inflammatory action. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for the sea moss. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, season of gathering, a sensory description), and this short chapter gives a habitat (stones and shells by the sea) and a description by sight and touch (hairy, slender, stalkless, very astringent), with three further names. It records no amuletic use. The chapter is therapeutic and assigns no cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The entry records that a Greek physician described a small hairy sea moss from rocks and shells, called it very astringent, and named inflammation and gout in the feet as its uses. The Codex does not recommend gathering seaweed for these. The thing a reader can take is the habit of naming where a thing grows and what it looks like before saying what it is for. ### Contraindications

- **Gout and inflamed joints:** need diagnosis and prescribed treatment; a hot, red, swollen joint can also be infection and needs urgent assessment.
- **Thyroid disease and iodine:** seaweeds can carry large amounts of iodine; avoid in thyroid disease and in pregnancy unless a clinician advises.
- **Contaminated shores:** wild seaweed can carry heavy metals, pollutants and microbes; this entry does not cover how to gather or clean it.
- **Identification:** "sea moss" and "Corallina" are the translator's suggestions among several names; the chapter itself names no species, and other seaweeds sold as "sea moss" differ.
- **Conservation and gathering rules:** collecting seaweed from shores is regulated in some places; this entry does not cover it. [USER: verify]
