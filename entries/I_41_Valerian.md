## Section 1 — Nomenclature

### Primary Scientific Name
*Valeriana officinalis* L. (Caprifoliaceae; formerly Valerianaceae)

### Botanical Identity
Perennial herb 50–150cm; compound pinnate leaves; clusters of small pale pink/white flowers; thick rhizome with many fibrous roots (the drug); native to Europe and Asia; extensively naturalized in North America. The fresh root has a mild pleasant earthy-green odour; the dried root develops the characteristic pungent isovaleric acid smell (from valerenic acid degradation) — distinct and polarising. Three main species pharmacologically: *V. officinalis* (European), *V. wallichii* DC. (Himalayan/Indian), *V. jatamansi* Jones (Indian, sometimes called Indian valerian).

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 De Materia Medica (DMM)

Dioscorides (*Book I, Chapter 8*):
> *"Phu: warming; promotes urine; benefits the chest; taken in drink it counteracts poisons. Applied externally it disperses flatulence and promotes menstruation."*

Galen and Pliny both describe valerian as promoting sleep and relieving epilepsy — establishing the primary neurological application that would persist through the Middle Ages.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine (CM)

Ibn Sīnā:
> *"Sunbul: temperament warm in the second degree, dry. Strengthens the brain, heart, and liver. Relieves epilepsy and convulsions, promotes sleep, opens obstructions of the brain. Benefits the eyes applied as kohl. One of the most useful herbs for neurological disorders."*

### 2.3 Sushruta Samhita (SS)

*Charaka Samhita* on *Tagara* (*V. wallichii*):
> *"Tagara: bitter, pungent; hot. Reduces Kapha and Vata. Promotes sleep, relieves epilepsy and anxiety, benefits the heart, removes bad odours from the body. Used in compound preparations for anxiety, insomnia, and epilepsy."*

### 2.4 Four Medical Tantras (FMT)

*Rgyud bzhi*:
> *"Spang spos (valerian): bitter, acrid; hot. Reduces rLung and phlegm. Calms disturbed rLung, promotes sleep, relieves anxiety and agitation, benefits the heart channel. The foremost rLung-pacifying herb among the mountain aromatics."*

### 2.5 Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ)

Referenced in later Chinese texts via Silk Road transmission for nervous conditions and sleep. The application mirrors the other traditions.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

| Compound | Content | Activity |
|---------|---------|---------|
| **Valerenic acid** | 0.3–0.8% | Primary sedative compound; GABA-A positive modulator; 5-HT5A receptor agonist; anxiolytic |
| **Valerenol** | EO component | Sedative; antispasmodic |
| **Isovaleric acid** | Volatile | Responsible for characteristic smell; mild sedative (low level) |
| **Valepotriates** (didrovaltrate, valtrate) | 1–2% (fresh root) | Antispasmodic; mild sedative; degrade rapidly in dried root |
| **GABA** (free) | Minor | Inhibitory neurotransmitter (direct); crosses BBB poorly |
| **Glutamine** | Amino acid | GABA precursor in CNS |
| **Linarin, hesperidin** | Flavonoids | Sedative; anti-inflammatory |
| **Isovaltrate** | Valtrate isomer | Anticonvulsant |

**Valerenic acid** is the primary pharmacological anchor — it is unique to valerian and has a confirmed GABA-A binding site interaction validated in human brain PET studies.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 GABAergic / Sedative

**Valerenic acid**: Positive allosteric modulator of GABA-A receptor → enhanced GABAergic inhibitory neurotransmission → anxiolytic + sedative. The specific binding site has been mapped (different from benzodiazepine site, similar to barbiturate site in some studies). This provides sedation without the respiratory depression of barbiturates.

**5-HT5A receptor agonism**: Valerenic acid activates 5-HT5A serotonin receptors → anxiolytic + regulation of circadian rhythms → improved sleep architecture.

**Free GABA**: Present in the root — direct GABA supplementation. CNS penetration disputed; may act peripherally or via gut-brain axis.

### 5.2 Anticonvulsant

**Valepotriates** (in fresh root): Antispasmodic via smooth muscle relaxation; mild anticonvulsant. Degrade rapidly to homobaldrinal in dried preparations — activity partially lost on drying (explains why some preparations are more potent fresh).

**Valerenic acid GABA-A modulation**: Same GABAergic mechanism provides anticonvulsant activity.

### 5.3 Anxiolytic (HPA modulation)

Valerenic acid + anxiolytic flavonoids: Reduce cortisol and corticosterone response to stress. Milder effect than benzodiazepines — appropriate for mild-moderate anxiety without dependency risk.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Insomnia**: Leathwood et al. (1982) — landmark early study: valerian extract significantly reduces sleep latency and improves sleep quality. Multiple subsequent RCTs (Donath et al., Bent et al.) — mixed results but meta-analysis suggests significant improvement in subjective sleep quality; effect on objective polysomnography measures weaker.

**Anxiety**: Multiple RCTs show significant anxiolytic effect — notably comparable to diazepam in one study (Andreatini et al., 2002).

**Menopausal sleep disturbance**: Taavoni et al. (2011) — valerian significantly improves hot flash frequency and sleep quality in menopausal women.

**Consistency note**: Clinical trials show high heterogeneity — likely due to variable extract standardisation (valerenic acid content), fresh vs dried root, and individual variation in response.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The smell paradox**: Valerian root has a smell that divides people absolutely — to some, it is warm, earthy, and reminiscent of loamy soil; to most modern Western users, it is unpleasant (described variously as dirty socks, cheese, or barnyard). Yet in antiquity, the Arabic name *Sunbul al-Ṭīb* ("fragrant/pleasant spike") suggests it was considered pleasant. This inversion may reflect cultural association — in contexts where valerian was valued and familiar, the smell associated with relaxation and sleep became pleasant through conditioning. The pharmacological effect (genuine GABA-A modulation producing relaxation) could literally train olfactory preference.

**Cats, rats, and valerenic acid**: The attraction of cats to valerian root (similar to catnip response) is not incidental. Isovaleric acid and some valerenic acid metabolites trigger a response in felids that resembles the nepetalactone response to catnip — a pheromone-like effect. Rats are also attracted to valerian, and it was reportedly used as bait in the Pied Piper legend. The animal behavioural responses validate the CNS-active profile of the volatiles — different species, different receptors, same pharmacological signal.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

| Form | Dose | Application |
|------|------|-------------|
| Dried root tea | 2–3g per 250ml, steep 10 min | Sleep; taken 30–60 min before bed |
| Standardised extract (0.8% valerenic acid) | 400–600mg/day | Clinical sleep and anxiety dose |
| Tincture | 4–6ml 1:5 | Acute anxiety; flexible dosing |
| Capsule | 400–900mg 30–60 min before bed | Standardised sleep protocol |
| Combined with hops, lemon balm | Classical sleep formula | Synergistic GABA-A support |

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Bitter, acrid; warm. Channels: Heart (心), Liver (肝). Calms Shen, moves Liver Qi, antispasmodic. Used for Liver Qi stagnation with Shen disturbance (insomnia with anxiety and tension).

**Ayurvedic**: Bitter, pungent; hot. Reduces Kapha and Vata. *Nidrājanana* (sleep-inducing), *Apasmārahara* (anti-epileptic), *Vātahara*.

**Tibetan**: Bitter, acrid; hot. Primary rLung-pacifying aromatic. Most important sleep/anxiety herb in Tibetan mountain medicine.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Benzodiazepines / alcohol**: GABA-A potentiation — additive sedative/respiratory depression risk at high doses
- **Driving / operating machinery**: Sedative effect — caution
- **Pregnancy**: Insufficient safety data; valepotriates potentially genotoxic in vitro — avoid
- **Liver disease**: Case reports of hepatotoxicity at very high doses; rare but documented
- **Morning hangover**: Residual sedation if taken too close to wake time — take 2 hours before intended sleep time
