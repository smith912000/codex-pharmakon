# Electric eel head — "Electric Eel", "Head-of-the-Electric-Eel" (Bryan's English renderings of the Ebers Papyrus; no Egyptian word is printed)
## *The head of the creature Bryan calls the "Electric Eel"; Bryan prints no Egyptian word and no binomial, so the spine's "Malapterurus electricus (Nile electric catfish)" is the list-maker's gloss and is not read from the text* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian Tradition (EP).** The locus is Bryan's 1930 English *The Papyrus Ebers*. "Electric Eel" stands in Bryan's list of the animals "whose identity is not in doubt" in Chapter VII "Organic Remedies" (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1404, directly after "Eel" and before "Crab"; name-index row `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` line 122, class animal, "Bryan's English identification; no Egyptian word or binomial given in this list"). The spine row reads "Electric eel head", "Malapterurus electricus (Nile electric catfish)", note "Ebers; Wikipedia/Ebers Papyrus; id-uncertain" (`_sources/ebers.csv` line 90). Wikipedia was not used. The parts-of-animals list (lines 1405-1416) includes "Brain" and "Bones" but ties no part to any animal; the head is named only in the prescription below.

**Where the head is prescribed.** The Bryan text on disk was searched for "electric" and "eel". "Electric Eel" occurs in two lines only: the list (line 1404) and one prescription. The plain word "Eel" occurs only in the same list line.

- **Burns, lines 2226-2231**, in Chapter XII "Minor Surgery" (heading lines 2208-2209). After Bryan's remark that the Ancients had remedies for burns "although we might not be able to see any particular merit in the preparations they employed" (line 2226), the series reads: "TO PREVENT BURN WOUNDS / A Frog / Warm in Oil and rub therewith. / ANOTHER The Head-of-the-Electric-Eel Warm in Oil and apply to the part of the body." (lines 2228-2231). The head is thus the second of two alternatives, the first being a frog.

**Related items, printed separately, not counted as the electric eel.** "Eel" is its own head word in the list (line 1404); the sources retrieved give no prescription for it.

**What is not said.** The sources retrieved do not say what oil was used, whether the head was fresh or dried, or how often it was applied. Nothing says that the Egyptian scribe meant the Nile electric catfish, as the spine's gloss supposes; Bryan's English calls it an eel. Bryan's list is of animals "whose identity is not in doubt", but he prints no reasoning for this one. The Ebers material is indication-organised, so the identity of any named substance is a modern hypothesis. The 1987 translation and the Egyptian word could change any reading above. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None can be stated for the material as the papyrus names it. In general terms, a fish head is mostly water, protein, fat and bone. Electric fishes carry a specialised electric organ; no text read connects it to the use. Nothing in the sources retrieved says that any component acts on a burn. This is a general statement, not a reading of Bryan.

---

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The one use the sources retrieved show is a head warmed in oil and applied to a burn. Putting animal material on burnt skin is an infection risk, and burns need medical care. No modern protocol is supplied and none is implied.

---

### Preparation & Dosing

Only what Bryan prints: "The Head-of-the-Electric-Eel Warm in Oil and apply to the part of the body" (line 2231), for burns. No quantity, oil or frequency is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] As general facts, the Nile electric catfish and the South American electric eel are different animals, and the true electric eel is not African; raw fish tissue can carry bacteria; and open burns are easily infected by unsterile material. No fish-head preparation is an established treatment for burns. These points are not drawn from the papyrus.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's vital constitution, and the one use found is an external application for burns. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); no recitation is printed with this prescription in Bryan's summary, which is no proof the papyrus gives none. The person as plural (*ka*, *ba*, *akh*, *ib*) is not engaged by this entry. No deity is attributed.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. The record is one line, a fish head warmed in oil for burns, with no quantity and no cultivation role. A cultivator has nothing to do with it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** raw animal material on a burn risks infection; burns need medical care.
- **Handling:** a living electric fish can deliver a shock; the species meant is not known from the sources retrieved.
- **Conservation and law:** the status of the fish and any restriction on collecting it were not retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** the material is a killed animal.
