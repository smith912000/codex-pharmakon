# Rosa avicularis — se.rgod, Saegoe; "Wild Rose" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Rosa avicularis, the first of three binomials Clark 1995 prints at line 984 for se.rgod, "Wild Rose" (then Rosa sericea and "Rosa laevigate", as printed); the same binomial is also the third name of Clark's Se.wahi.me.tog at line 1033, and the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints no Rosa avicularis* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and under this binomial on Clark's edition alone.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the other texts on disk for *Rosa* found drugs of other species and no *Rosa avicularis*. The Dioscorides text prints *Rosa* in translators' SUGGESTED lines for Greek drugs (for example lines 3965, 4444, 4484 and 9956, which name *Rosa gallica*, *Rosa canina* and *Rosa rugosa*), which are not read as this plant. Yang 1998 prints "Ying Shi (Semen Rosae Multiflorae)" at line 3158, a Shennong drug of another species, which is not read as this one either. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 114) the row is keyed by the Tibetan name *se.rgod*, with the identification "Wild Rose; Rosa avicularis; Rosa sericea; Rosa laevigate" and the locus "Clark 1995 (line 984)". The spine row (CPU-01783) took the **first** binomial, not a later one. The same index has two further wild-rose rows: line 144, keyed *Se.wahi.me.tog* (Clark line 1033, where *Rosa avicularis* is the third binomial printed), and line 148, a second *se.rgod* (Clark line 1042, *Rosa macrophylla*). Clark's Latin index prints it (line 1639).

**Identity doubt.** Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" and (line 68) that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect". Here the Tibetan name has two entries in Clark with different binomials, and a third "Wild Rose" entry under another Tibetan name. The Tibetan name does not fix the species, and the Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 984).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792). The line falls after the heading "(5) Resins" (line 879) and before "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997), in the run of plants that Clark's footnote 12 (line 1543) describes: "Section 6, 'Exposition of the Powers of Plants,' does not appear as a separate section in the ..." (the footnote continues beyond the part read). The preceding entry (line 983) is *skyer.pa*, "Indian Barberry: Berberis aristata: Berberis asiatica". The entry reads: "se.rgod: Wild Rose: Rosa avicularis: Rosa sericea: Rosa laevigate: [has two names. With respect to the female type, the interior of the stem is red with brown bark and red flower. The roots resemble iron hooks and the hips are reddish-green and furry. The stem of the male type is hollow, brown and without thorns]. Indian barberry and wild rose collect toxins and cure lymph disorders. [Wild rose is also recommended for use in disorders caused by spirits.]"

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The two names, the description of the female and male types and the last sentence on spirits are therefore the commentary layer. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the English name, the three Latin names and "Indian barberry and wild rose collect toxins and cure lymph disorders", a clause the entry shares with the preceding drug (*skyer.pa*), so the root layer gives wild rose no action of its own. Clark's name index lists *se.rgod* (line 1765).

*Clark 1995, other lines.* A second entry has the same Tibetan name (line 1042): "se.rgod: Wild Rose (female type): Rosa macrophylla: [is of superior and inferior sub-types and grows on very high hills]. Its rosehip cures liver fevers and fever caused by poisoning." In chapter 21, "The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines" (heading at line 1205), line 1212 lists "Wild Rose" in the class of medicines which neutralize poisons ("... Gentiana algida, Pterocephalus hookeri, Wild Rose and the cambium (middle bark) of Indian Barberry"); it does not say which wild-rose entry is meant.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (read through the name, not the binomial).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225). Line 3427 reads: "Kyerpa and Saegoe neutralized poison and cure chhuser disorders." Line 3438 reads: "Fruit of Saegoe cures hot disorder associated with poisoning and the liver." In chapter 21 (colophon at line 3689), line 3605 lists *Saegoe* among the group of medicines that cure poisoning. The index lists "Saegoe 206,208,220" (line 7481). "Chhuser" is a Tibetan term that the editors keep untranslated (note at line 686). The glossary's first "S" block has fifteen phonetic names (lines 5685 to 5719), fifteen transliterations (lines 5687 to 5725) and fifteen scientific names (lines 5727 to 5765). "Saegoe" is the second phonetic name (line 5689), "se rgod" the second transliteration (line 5692), and the second scientific name is *Rosa macrophylla* (line 5727), with the English "Rose" second at line 5730. The count is checked on "Sedu" (fifth, *Punica granatum*), "Sengdeng" (sixth, *Acacia catechu*) and "Shingkun" (fifteenth, *Ferula asafetida*), the same count as in I_1324. By position, then, the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary sets *Saegoe* against *Rosa macrophylla*, the binomial of Clark's second *se.rgod* entry (line 1042), and prints no *Rosa avicularis*. The glossary does not print the pairing in one row. [USER: verify]

The two editions agree in kind: Clark's pair of drugs, Indian barberry and wild rose, "collect toxins", and the Men-Tsee-Khang *Kyerpa* (Clark's *skyer.pa* is spelled close to it) and *Saegoe* "neutralized poison", where the names are placed together in both texts. The Codex reads *Saegoe* and *se.rgod* as one drug from the spelling and that agreement; neither text says so. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** I_1178 (*Rosa macrophylla*; *se.rgod*, Clark line 1042) is the entry on the second Clark entry with the same Tibetan name and quotes line 984 as well, together with the Men-Tsee-Khang *Saegoe* lines. This entry covers the line 984 reading and its binomial, and the two are not merged. I_1348 (*Rosa roxburghii*, *Se.wahi.me.tog*, line 1033) is the sibling row of the same batch. I_204 (Rose; tags CM, SS, EP, DMM, SBJ, FMT) has an FMT paragraph on rose in Tibetan medicine that names other species and does not name *se.rgod*, as I_1178 records. I_681 (*Rosa canina*) has no FMT tag. I_1254 (*Cnidium dahuricum*) and I_1324 (*Oxytropis kansuensis*) quote Clark's line 1042 for neighbouring drugs. No entry on *Rosa avicularis* exists. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general, well-established fact that *Rosa* is a genus of the rose family (Rosaceae) and that rosehips are generally known to contain vitamin C. The lines read give no composition for the Tibetan drug, and nothing is stated here about what it contains.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a wild rose that, with Indian barberry, "collect[s] toxins and cure[s] lymph disorders" (Clark, line 984), is "also recommended for use in disorders caused by spirits" in the commentary layer, is among the medicines that neutralize poisons (Clark, line 1212; Men-Tsee-Khang, lines 3427 and 3605) and whose fruit "cures hot disorder associated with poisoning and the liver" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3438). These are the texts' claims in their own terms, attached to this binomial through Clark's first of three names, and they are not Codex protocols. Poisoning and liver disease need medical care, and the Codex gives no protocol and no dose for them.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation and no quantity. Clark's commentary describes the stem, bark, flower, roots and hips of the female and male types and does not say which part is taken at line 984. The Men-Tsee-Khang text names "Fruit of Saegoe" (line 3438), which is the only part named for the drug. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's line 984 prints no taste, power or post-digestive taste for *se.rgod*, and neither do the Men-Tsee-Khang lines read (3427, 3438, 3605). Clark's separate entry for *Se.wahi.me.tog* (line 1033) prints "sweet and sour tastes, and oily power" under a different Tibetan name, and these are not carried over to this entry. The male and female types are plant descriptions in the commentary layer.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it collects toxins and cures lymph disorders, and the commentary adds disorders caused by spirits. The last clause names a cause of illness and the tradition's frame for it; it assigns the plant no practice or status. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names (*se.rgod*, *Saegoe*, *Rosa*) found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, colophon at line 3950), and the herb entries of Clark's chapter 20 that mention essence extraction (lines 971, 1043, 1078, 1103, 1121 and 1144) do not include it. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name more securely than to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a toxin and lymph drug in a shared clause, with a note about spirit-caused disorders in the commentary, no preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Poisoning needs emergency medical care and the Codex gives no advice for it. Wild roses have thorns, and the identity is open among several *Rosa* species; no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No conservation information and no safety information for pregnancy were read. [USER: verify]
