# Fragrant thoroughwort — Lan Cao 兰草 (Shennong bencao jing); alias Shui Xiang 水香, "water fragrance"

## *Eupatorium fortunei Turcz., fragrant thoroughwort (modern Chinese pei lan), an aromatic composite herb; the identification is ours, from the Chinese name and the standard modern reading, and is of medium confidence: the Sun Xingyan note itself debates whether lan cao is this plant or the plant called ze lan* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts on disk.** The only text read for this entry is Sun Xingyan's 1799 reconstruction of the Divine Farmer's Classic, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt`. Its other edition on disk, Yang 1998, also lists the drug (names.csv line 84, "Lan Cao", pharmaceutical Latin "Radix Eupatorii Chinensis"); it is the same tradition and is not read here. Dioscorides' *eupatorion* (DMM 4-41, entry I_299, *Eupatorium cannabinum*) is a different plant of the same genus and is not tagged here.

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** The entry is 兰草 in the upper classic (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, upper classic, entry 兰草 *lan cao*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 911). The text proper, the Shennong layer, reads: 味辛，平 ("pungent, even"); 主利水道，杀蛊毒，辟不祥 ("governs freeing the water passages, kills gu poison, wards off the inauspicious"); 久服，益气、轻身、不老、通神明 ("taken long: benefits qi, lightens the body, no ageing, opens through to the spirit-light"); alias 一名水香 ("also called water fragrance"); habitat 生池泽 ("grows in pools and marshes"). No dose, part or preparation is given in this paragraph.

The Mingyi bielu layer (《名医》曰, a later stratum) adds only that it grows at "Da Wu" (生大吴) and is gathered in the fourth and fifth months (四月、五月采) (line 915).

Sun Xingyan's own note (案, line 917) is a run of philological citations, and it is the part that bears on identity. He cites the Shuowen (兰，香草也, "lan is a fragrant herb"), the Guangya, the Yijing ("its scent is like lan", 其臭如兰), Zheng Xuan, the Xia xiaozheng (五月蓄兰, "in the fifth month they store lan"), and Lu Ji on the Mao Shi, who says it is lan, a fragrant herb, "its stalk and leaf like the medicinal herb ze lan" (其茎、叶似药草泽兰). He cites the Fanzi jiran (large lan from Hanzhong and the Sanfu; lan from Hedong and Hongnong, the white kind good). He then gives a Yuan-dynasty commentator on a poem of Li Bai, Yang Qixian, quoting a "Bencao": lan cao and ze lan "are two things with the same name"; lan cao is also called shui xiang and is the plant of Duliang; the Shuijing says the stream at the small hill west of Duliang county in Lingling has lan cao growing all over it, "green leaf, purple stem" (绿叶紫茎), while ze lan is "like bohe (mint), faintly fragrant", commonly planted by families of Jing, Xiang and south of the ranges. Sun closes by rejecting Yan Shigu, who took lan cao to be ze lan: 颜师古以兰草为泽兰，非也. Several characters in these quotations are missing in the transcription.

**Identity.** Everything above is the commentators' and not the Shennong text's. The Shennong paragraph gives no description at all, only the water-fragrance alias and the marsh habitat. The note's two features, a fragrant herb of marshy ground and "green leaf, purple stem", are consistent with a fragrant composite such as *Eupatorium fortunei*, but they are also consistent with other aromatic marsh herbs, and the note's own debate with ze lan (which modern practice commonly reads as a *Lycopus*, a mint-family plant) and the alternative *Acorus* reading mean the Codex cannot settle the plant from the text. The Codex's name is the modern conventional one. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, *Eupatorium* is a genus of the daisy family (Asteraceae) and the species used in Chinese pharmacy as pei lan is a fragrant herb whose scent comes from volatile oils. Some *Eupatorium* species are known to contain pyrrolizidine alkaloids, which are liver-toxic; whether *E. fortunei* does is not stated in any source read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Shennong paragraph records a fragrant marsh herb that frees the water passages, kills gu poison and wards off the inauspicious, and that when taken long is said to benefit qi, lighten the body, prevent ageing and open the spirit-light. "Gu poison" and "the inauspicious" are the text's categories and are not translated here into modern diagnoses. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The Shennong paragraph names no part and no preparation; the Mingyi layer names the harvest months (fourth and fifth, line 915). The classic's general preface says medicines differ in suitability for pills, powders, water decoction, wine steeping or paste (line 4121), and it gives no rule for this plant. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested.* The paragraph for 兰草 reads 味辛，平, and the classic files it in the upper grade (上品). Its preface (line 43) says the upper medicines are "to nourish life, corresponding to heaven, non-toxic; taken much or long they do not harm people; for those who wish to lighten the body and benefit qi, not age and extend the years, base it on the upper classic" (上药……主养命以应天，无毒。多服、久服不伤人。欲轻身益气，不老延年者，本上经). The plant's own 久服 clause is exactly this promise: 久服，益气、轻身、不老、通神明, "taken long: benefits qi, lightens the body, no ageing, opens through to the spirit-light". The register is therefore (a). This is a statement of the classic's grade and wording and not a claim that the plant does any of these things. The earlier-layer caution applies: the Mingyi bielu, Wu Pu and Sun's own note add nothing to the status. (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, upper classic, entry 兰草 *lan cao*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 911)

**Practitioner's note.** The classic grades this as a long-use, life-nourishing herb, and that grade is the whole of its cultivation status. A modern cultivator should not read it as licence to take an uncertainly identified aromatic composite for long periods: the plant behind the name is itself debated in Sun's note, some thoroughworts carry liver-toxic alkaloids, and no dose is given. The honest use of the entry is as a record of how the classic framed the drug.

### Contraindications

- **Identity:** plants sold under lan cao, pei lan or ze lan are not interchangeable; the note itself argues that lan cao and ze lan are two things.
- **Alkaloids:** some *Eupatorium* species contain liver-toxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids; long use is not supported by anything retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Pregnancy and nursing:** no safety information was read. [USER: verify]
- **Marsh habitat:** wild plants from pools and marshes may be contaminated; do not gather and use on the strength of this entry.
