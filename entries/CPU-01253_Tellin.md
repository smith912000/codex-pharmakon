# Tellin (bivalve) — *Tellinai* (ΤΕΛΛΙΝΑΙ), "Tellen" (Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica* 2-8)
## *A small marine bivalve shellfish. The translator's gloss prints "Tellina, family Tellinidae — Tellen, bivalves"; the text itself gives only the Greek plural name and the species is not settled by it* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is Dioscorides. In the English text on disk (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`) the chapter is headed "2-8. TELLINAI" (line 4941), with the translator's gloss "SUGGESTED: Tellina, family Tellinidae — Tellen, bivalves" (line 4942). The name index (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 198) carries the same gloss and locus "2-8 (line 4941)". "Suggested" marks the identification as the modern translator's, not Dioscorides'. The chapter sits in Book Two, which Dioscorides opens (line 4904) by saying it will deal with "living creatures and of honey and of milk and of animal fat", before moving on to cereals and pot herbs. The book is headed "LIVING CREATURES" in this edition.

The whole chapter (lines 4943-4944) says two things. First: "Tellinae whilst they are fresh and new are good for the bowels, especially their broth." Second: "Seasoned with salt, burnt, pounded into small pieces and dropped on with cedria [1-105], they do not allow hairs on the eyelids to spring up again after they are once plucked out." Cedria is the substance of *De Materia Medica* 1-105 (heading at line 4321), which the translator glosses "oil of cedar" and which Dioscorides describes there as able to "decay living bodies", as corrupting "cloths and skin because it heats and dries extraordinarily", and as good for eye medicines (line 4329). The vehicle is therefore a strong one, and the chapter does not say the shellfish alone does the work.

The neighbouring chapters give the frame in which the passage is read. 2-7 *Muakes* (mussels, "Mytilacea species") has the Pontic kind best, burnt, and washed like lead for eye medicines (lines 4939-4940). 2-9 *Chamai* (the translator's "Chama, bivalve molluscs") says "The broth of chamae and other shellfish boiled in a little water stirs the bowels. It is taken with wine" (line 4951). 2-4 to 2-6 give burnt purple-shells and whelks for the teeth, for boils and for burns (lines 4926-4937). The tellin chapter is one in a run of shellfish chapters, each with a short note of an internal use (broth) and an external one (the shell burnt).

The Dioscorides list on disk, `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 189, reads "Tellinai,,plant,DMM II,yes". Its class column says "plant", which is plainly wrong for a chapter of Book Two, "Living creatures"; the spine row correctly files it as an animal. The last field flags the identification as uncertain. No other tradition's text on disk was found to name the tellin: a search of the Ebers, Assyrian and both Tibetan texts for "tellin", "shellfish", "bivalve" and "mussel" returned nothing relevant, and the other source lists have no row for it. Sushruta's food chapter names "a species of small bivalve molluscs" (*Shankhanakha*) with *Shukti* and *Shambuka* in the "Conchiferous (*Koshastha*)" group (Sutrasthana ch.46, lines 758-761), but the text gives no way of matching them with the tellin and I do not.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, a bivalve's shell is mainly calcium carbonate, and the soft parts are a protein-rich tissue with water and some minerals. A shellfish broth is a water extract of the flesh and its juices. Burnt shell leaves mainly calcium oxide and carbonate, an alkaline, caustic powder. None of this explains, or is offered as an explanation of, the actions Dioscorides states.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The record is two lines long: a fresh broth as a mild bowel remedy, and a burnt, salted shell powder in cedar tar to stop eyelashes regrowing after plucking. The Codex does not recommend either. Shellfish broth carries the usual risks of raw or ill-cooked shellfish, and putting a caustic, burnt-shell and tar preparation on the eyelid risks the eye. No protocol is offered.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparations in the sources are those quoted: the broth of the fresh, new shellfish (no method, quantity or timing given), and the salted, burnt and pounded shell dropped on with cedria. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Bivalve shellfish filter-feed and can concentrate bacteria, viruses and algal toxins from the water, which is why they are a known cause of food poisoning if raw or taken from polluted water. Burnt calcium carbonate is caustic and irritating to the eye. Permanent removal of eyelashes needs a medical method; plucked lashes grow back. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** None. The chapter prints no quality (warming, cooling, drying, astringent) for the tellin. The only effects named are a benefit to the bowels from the fresh broth ("good for the bowels") and the lash-preventing action of the burnt shell with cedria. The neighbouring chamae chapter says the broth "stirs the bowels" (line 4951), and the two should not be read as the same claim. No other tradition's classification is available for this material.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. The chapter offers no cultivation status; it is a two-line therapeutic note on a shellfish. What Dioscorides characteristically gives a practitioner is a discipline of attention (provenance, season, sensory assay, adulteration), and this chapter has little of it: it says only that the shellfish be "fresh and new". The best kind of mussel is named by place in the preceding chapter (the Pontic, line 4940), but no such statement is made for the tellin. No amuletic use is recorded here.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing here for a cultivator to take. The tellin is a minor shellfish in a short chapter, recommended for the bowels and for an eyelash trick, both of which the Codex leaves as history. The one piece of practical advice the text carries, to use the shellfish fresh and new, is ordinary shellfish care.

### Contraindications

- **Shellfish allergy:** bivalve shellfish are a common cause of severe allergy; avoid in anyone with a known shellfish allergy.
- **Food poisoning:** filter-feeding bivalves from polluted waters or in a bloom of toxic algae can cause serious illness; they should not be eaten raw or of uncertain origin.
- **Eye and eyelid:** a burnt-shell and tar preparation is a caustic irritant; do not apply it to the eyelid or near the eye.
- **Identification:** the species is a translator's suggestion; no product can be taken to be the tellin of the text.
