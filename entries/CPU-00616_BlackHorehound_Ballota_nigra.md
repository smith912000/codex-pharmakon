# Black Horehound — Fetid Horehound, Stinking Roger, Marrube Noir

## *Ballota nigra* L. | Family: Lamiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT (Assyrian / Mesopotamian herbal lineage), CM (Unani / Avicenna-era humoral practice), DMM (Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica*)
**Energetic Classification:** Warm and Dry (Galenic — resolving, deobstruent), bitter and foetid; nervine-antispasmodic
**Training Phase:** Tempering (settling of nausea, agitation, and nervous unrest)

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian lineage (AT):** The bitter, strong-smelling Lamiaceae of the Near East belong to the old cuneiform tradition of *foetid* remedies — herbs whose repellent odour was itself read as therapeutic power against nervous agitation, gripping of the gut, and the influence of malign spirits. Black horehound sits in this class of aromatic bitters used to settle a disturbed interior.

**Dioscorides / *De Materia Medica* (DMM):** Dioscorides describes *ballote* (black or stinking horehound) as a rank-smelling herb; the bruised leaves, applied with salt, were used on the bites of dogs, and the plant was employed against gripes and to move sluggish conditions. Its very name derives from the Greek *ballo* ("to reject") — a plant so foetid that cattle refuse it, which fixed its reputation as an emphatically "expelling" herb.

**Unani / Avicenna-era practice (CM):** In the humoral frame a warm, dry, bitter aromatic is a *resolvent* and *deobstruent* — used to disperse cold, phlegmatic stagnation, calm hysterical and hypochondriacal agitation, and quiet rising nausea, particularly the nausea accompanying nervous or bilious upset.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The doctrine here is the doctrine of stench-as-signature: a plant too foetid to eat was believed to "reject" disease as cattle reject the plant. Modern chemistry gives the metaphor a real substrate — the diterpenoids and phenylpropanoid glycosides that produce the disagreeable, bitter character are the same fraction that binds central receptors and scavenges reactive oxygen species. The "rejecting" herb genuinely acts on the nervous system it was said to settle.

### Active Components

- **Phenylpropanoid glycosides** — verbascoside (acteoside), forsythoside B, arenarioside, ballotetroside, and caffeoyl-malic acid: the principal nervine/antioxidant fraction.
- **Labdane diterpenoids** — ballonigrin, ballotinone, marrubiin-type constituents (bitter principles).
- **Flavonoids** and **caffeic-acid derivatives** — antioxidant support.
- **Essential oil** — small quantity, responsible for the characteristic foetid odour.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica, black horehound is a **Tempering**-phase herb — used to settle the stomach and steady the nerves during the load-and-adapt work of that phase. Its traditional and pharmacological profile (antiemetic, mild anxiolytic/sedative, antispasmodic) makes it a candidate where nausea is bound up with nervous tension: travel or anticipatory nausea, restless agitation, and the queasy unease that accompanies stress. It is used as a short-course bitter infusion, its intense bitterness deliberately un-masked as part of the tempering discipline. Because the human evidence is thin, it is positioned as a supportive traditional agent, not a primary therapeutic.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the mechanistic evidence for black horehound centres on its phenylpropanoids. Daels-Rakotoarison et al. (2000), *Arzneimittel-Forschung* — a receptor-binding and antioxidant study — found that four of five phenylpropanoid esters isolated from *Ballota nigra* (verbascoside, forsythoside B, arenarioside, ballotetroside, caffeoyl-malic acid) bound benzodiazepine, dopaminergic and morphinic receptors (IC50 0.4–4.7 mg/mL) and scavenged reactive oxygen species with potency comparable to reference antioxidants (mesna, N-acetylcysteine) — a plausible molecular basis for the herb's traditional neurosedative reputation. DOI: [https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0031-1300158](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0031-1300158).

Nusier et al. (2007), *Neuroendocrinology Letters* — an albino-rat study of a 70% ethanol extract (400 mg/kg for 7 days) — reported significant reductions in blood glucose, total cholesterol and creatine kinase, and in a glucose-tolerance test a fall in blood glucose with a rise in serum insulin, indicating hypoglycaemic, insulin-releasing and cholesterol-lowering activity. *(PubMed PMID: 17627272; no DOI registered for this article — cite by PMID.)*

**Evidence grade:** Preclinical and traditional. Receptor-binding and antioxidant data give biological plausibility to the neurosedative/antiemetic use, but there are no robust human clinical trials; efficacy in people rests on long-standing traditional use, not controlled evidence.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Warm and dry (second degree); resolvent, deobstruent, antispasmodic nervine; corrects cold-phlegmatic stagnation and nervous nausea.
- **Dioscoridean / Greek:** Foetid, "rejecting"; expellent and topical for bites.
- *(No canonical TCM or Ayurvedic classification — this is a European/Near-Eastern herb; energetics are given in the Galenic-Unani and Greek frames as attested.)*

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy and lactation:** avoid — insufficient safety data and traditional emmenagogue/uterine cautions for bitter Lamiaceae.
- **Diabetes / hypoglycaemia:** the rat data show a hypoglycaemic and insulin-releasing effect — monitor and use caution alongside antidiabetic drugs.
- **Sedative interaction:** possible additive effect with CNS depressants, benzodiazepines, and alcohol given the demonstrated benzodiazepine-receptor binding.
- **Dopaminergic medication:** theoretical interaction owing to dopaminergic-receptor binding — caution in Parkinson's therapy and with antipsychotics.
- **Children:** not recommended without professional guidance.
- Discontinue if allergic/hypersensitivity reaction occurs (Lamiaceae cross-sensitivity possible).
