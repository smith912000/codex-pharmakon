# Sponge — Dioscorides, SPOGGOI (spoggoi; the hardest called "tragi")
## *Sponges; the translator's gloss prints "Sponges", with no species or binomial* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The sponge is chapter 5-138 of the *De Materia Medica*, in Book Five, in the section "Metallic Stones", in the Osbaldeston and Wood English version (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 10550-10556; printed pages 813-814 in the index). The chapter is headed "5-138. SPOGGOI" and the translator's gloss reads "Sponges". The project's source list files it as a mineral from Book Five (`_sources/dioscorides.csv`, line 864, class "mineral"), which follows its place in the book; the Codex shelves it in Volume III because a sponge is an animal. [USER: verify] The chapter gives no binomial.

The chapter first sorts sponges: "Sponges are sometimes called males when they are thick and full of small holes. The hardest of these they have named tragi. The others are called females and are different." It then says "They are burnt the same way as alcyonium [5-136]." The burning method itself is in 5-136 (line 10545): "If you want to burn any of these put it with salt into an unfired clay pot, and having sealed around the mouth of it with clay, put it into a furnace. When the clay pot has baked enough take it out, put it in jars, and use it." Chapter 5-136 deals with alcyonium, which the translator glosses as "Zoophyte — Bastard Sponge" and which Dioscorides describes as sponge-like (line 10541); it is another entry, and only its burning method is used here.

The uses, in the order the chapter gives them (lines 10552-10556), are all external or applied to the eyes. (1) Sponges "empty and without fat are good for wounds, repress oedema, and with water or posca [hot drinks] they seal new wounds; boiled with honey they rejoin old wounds." (2) "Old sponges are of no use." (3) "Sponges open contracted ulcers and calluses, bound with a thread and put in dry like tents." The sponge here is a dilating plug. (4) "They dry up flowing undermining ulcers and old ulcers, applied dry, new and empty; and they restrain excessive discharges of blood." (5) "Burnt with vinegar they are good for dry sore eyes, anywhere there is need to clean, and are astringent for anything. They are better for eye medicines if they are washed." (6) "Burnt with pitch they are good for excessive discharges of blood."

The chapter ends with a method of whitening: "The softest of them are whitened in the burning heat of summer, moistened with froth of salt standing on the rocks, and placed in the sun. But take care that the hollow side of them faces upward and the cut side is underneath. If it is clear summer weather they are also moistened with froth of salt or seawater and set out under the moon. These are the whitest."

Two related entries are separate in the book and are only named here. Chapter 5-163 (line 10681), "Stones found in Sponges", says: "Stones found in sponges (taken as a drink with wine) break stones in the bladder." And a sponge appears as a tool, not a medicine, in the preparation of fats: lard is put "into a mortar moistened with a sponge" (line 5301, chapter 2-87).

### Active Components

A bath sponge is the skeleton of a marine animal. Its fibrous skeleton is mostly a collagen-like protein called spongin; other sponges have silica or calcium carbonate spicules. Burning removes the organic part and leaves ash and salts (the chapter burns it with salt, vinegar or pitch). Nothing in the chapter names an active principle.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Sponge in the text is a wound dressing and an absorbent plug, with burnt sponge used on the eyes and for bleeding. The Codex recommends none of these; modern wound care uses sterile dressings, and a burnt or reused natural sponge is a contamination risk. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations without quantities: sponges applied dry, new and empty to ulcers; sponges with water or posca on new wounds; sponges boiled with honey for old wounds; sponges bound with a thread as a tent in contracted ulcers; sponges burnt "the same way as alcyonium" (in an unfired clay pot sealed with clay, put into a furnace; line 10545) and then used with vinegar or with pitch; sponges washed before use in eye medicines; sponges whitened in summer sun with salt froth or seawater. No dose, amount or schedule is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. A natural sponge is absorbent and holds bacteria; an unsterilised sponge in an open wound or ulcer is an infection risk, and a "tent" left in a cavity can become a source of infection. Burning a sponge makes ash that is irritating to the eye. Sterile dressings are the modern equivalent of the absorbent function. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** One quality is printed: burnt sponges "are astringent for anything" (line 10556). Nothing else is printed (no warming or cooling, no degree). No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and the chapter attaches none to the sponge. What the chapter does offer, as the discipline of attention that the Codex finds in Dioscorides, is a set of discriminations and processes: the sorting of males, females and the hardest "tragi", the rule that "old sponges are of no use", the instruction that sponges "are better for eye medicines if they are washed", and the exact orientation (hollow side up, cut side down) for whitening in the summer sun. The pneuma and the Galenic spirits are later and are not read in this chapter. The moonlight whitening is a procedure for colour and is not a cultivation claim.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a wound-care entry; the lasting lesson is Dioscorides' attention to which sponge, how old, how washed and how prepared, and not any benefit to the person. The honest use of the entry is as a record of what Dioscorides says.

### Contraindications

- **Infection:** a natural sponge or its ash in a wound, ulcer or eye is an infection risk; do not use them.
- **Eye:** burnt sponge with vinegar or pitch is irritating and unsafe in or near the eye. Eye conditions need examination.
- **Bleeding:** heavy bleeding needs pressure and emergency care; do not rely on this line.
- **Allergy and conservation:** some people react to natural sponge material, and wild sponge beds and their harvest are regulated or depleted in places. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** the sponge is an animal, though a simple one; harvesting it for a substance with no supported benefit is hard to justify.
