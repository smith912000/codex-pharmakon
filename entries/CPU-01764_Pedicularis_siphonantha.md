# Pedicularis siphonantha — Lugru mukpo, lug ru smug po (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Pedicularis siphonantha, the one binomial the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints in eighth place of its "L" scientific-name run, with the English name "Lousewort", which the Codex reads by position against the phonetic name "Lugru mukpo" and the transliteration "lug ru smug po"; Clark 1995 prints Pedicularis olivertana, Pedicularis pectinata and Pedicularis pyramidate for lug.ru (smug.po) and does not print siphonantha* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and on a species name that only one of them prints, set against the Tibetan name by column position and not on one printed line.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Ebers, Assyrian, Badianus, Avicenna and Sushruta material on disk for *Pedicularis* found only Dioscorides lines that print *Pedicularis* inside translators' lists for other drugs (for example a SUGGESTED line for *staphisagria*, line 9431, and index lines, 16928 and 18274, for *Pedicularis palustris*), which are not this drug. The Shennong text (Yang 1998) has *Ma Xian Hao* (*Herba Pedicularis Resupinatae*, line 3063, `names.csv` line 141), a different species that is not read as this drug. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 314) the key *Pedicularis siphonantha* is taken from the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary line 5448 with the note "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". Line 5448 prints **four** binomials on one line ("Phlomis younghusbandii Corydalis meifolia Pedicularis siphonantha Pedicularis longiflora"), and each has its own row: this one is the third of the four on the line, the eighth of the block's nine scientific names, and a later one, not the first. Its sibling *Pedicularis longiflora* (names.csv line 315) is the fourth. Clark does not print *Pedicularis siphonantha*.

**Identity doubt.** The glossary's binomial is set against the name by position only, and Clark's three binomials for the same Tibetan name (*lug.ru*, with *smug.po* "brown" in brackets after it) are other species of the same genus. The two editions agree on the genus and on the name's colour word ("mukpo", "smug.po") and differ on the species. The glossary's English name for the slot, "Lousewort", is a genus name. The Codex does not choose among the species. The Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note (line 686) says that the botanical terms in their text "are based on the information procured from the Materia Medica Department of this institute"; it names no flora or earlier work. **Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "L" block, lines 5433 to 5454).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. The phonetic names are nine (lines 5435 to 5437): "Lalaphue", "Langthangtse", "Lishi", "Looh dhue dorje", "Lugchhung", "Lugmur", "Lugnyal", "Lugru mukpo" and "Lugru serpo". The transliterations (lines 5439 to 5441) include "lug ru smug po" and "lug ru ser po" (line 5441). The scientific names are nine (lines 5445 to 5448): "Cnidium monnieri", "Hyoscyamus niger L.", "Syzygium aromaticum", "Codonopsis thalictrifolia", "Aster strachei", "Phlomis younghusbandii", "Corydalis meifolia", "Pedicularis siphonantha" and "Pedicularis longiflora". The English names (lines 5450 to 5454) are seven: "Celery", "Henbane", "Clove", "Lesser starwort", "Mountain turkeycom", "Lousewort" and "Yellow lousewort". Counted in order, "Lugru mukpo" is the eighth phonetic name and *Pedicularis siphonantha* the eighth scientific name, and the last two English names ("Lousewort", "Yellow lousewort") stand against the last two rows. The count is checked on three names by Clark: "Lalaphue" is first against *Cnidium monnieri* (Clark's *la.la.phud*, line 896), "Lugchhung" is fifth against *Aster strachei* (Clark's *lug.chhung*, line 1105) and "Lugnyal" is seventh against *Corydalis meifolia* (Clark's *lug.ngal*, line 1106). The glossary does not print the pairing in one row. It is positional; the colour words support it ("mukpo" brown, "serpo" yellow, and the English "Yellow lousewort" for the ninth). [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3433).* Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428): "... Muk chung dhenyon has the same efficacy as Tsher ngon. Serkue cures hot disorders of the lungs, liver, channels and poisoning. Lugru mukpo nutrilizes toxin and cures meat poison. Lugru serpo and Chhurug dries up chhuser. Sewai metok cures tripa disorders and suppresses loong." The spelling "nutrilizes" is the printed text's. The index lists "Lugru 207", "Lugru mukpo 207" and "Lugru serpo 207" (line 7428). The name is not in the taste groups of chapter 19 and not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23). The sentence gives no part used and no preparation.

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1030).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). It reads: "lug.ru (smug.po): Pedicularis olivertana Prain: Pedicularis pectinata: Pedicularis pyramidate: [is also known as thal.tre and is of red, yellow and brown types. It has a long, lobed green leaf and a brown flower bearing the design of a sheep's horn. Brown Pedicularis has bitter taste, fluid power and] collects poisons, cures meat poisoning, [brown phlegm, channel fever, dysentery with fever, stimulates hair growth and turns grey hair black. Red Pedicularis differs from the above by the color of the flower.]" Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The names, types, description, taste, power, the second group of actions (brown phlegm, channel fever, dysentery with fever, the hair claims) and the last sentence are therefore the commentary layer; "collects poisons, cures meat poisoning" is outside the brackets and is the root-text layer as the brackets mark it. The next two lines are the yellow type (*lug.ru.ser.po*, line 1031, treated with I_1327 and I_1329) and *chhu.rug*, which closes: "Both types, as well as] Pedicularis (above) dry fluid accumulations" (line 1032). The name *lug.ru.smug.po* also occurs in the commentary on *bya.po.tsi.tsi* (line 1059), which "resembles ma.ma.sgo.lchags and lug.ru.smug.po". Clark's name index lists *lug.ru* (line 1758), and his Latin index *Pedicularis oliveriana*, *pectinata* and *pyramidate* (line 1626) and no *siphonantha*.

The two editions agree in kind on the action: Clark's "collects poisons, cures meat poisoning" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "nutrilizes toxin and cures meat poison". The Codex reads Clark's *lug.ru (smug.po)* and the glossary's "Lugru mukpo" as one drug from the spelling and that agreement; neither text says so. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Pedicularis siphonantha*, *lug.ru* and "Lugru" found no entry on this drug. I_1310 (*Linaria buriatica*), I_1256 (*Codonopsis thalictrifolia*), I_1258 (*Corydalis meifolia*) and I_1022 (*Aster strachei*) print the same "L" block and give "Lugru mukpo" no entry. I_1250 (*Celosia cristata*) quotes Clark's line 1059 with the name *lug.ru.smug.po*. I_617 (*Pedicularis foliosa*, tags CM, DMM, FMT) says, with no locus, that "lug-ru" louseworts are used in the rGyud-bzhi as "tonics" and for "heat conditions" and wounds; the lines read here print poison, meat-poison, phlegm, fever and hair actions and no tonic, and that claim was not found. I_856 (*Pedicularis sylvatica*) is a European species. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Pedicularis* is a large genus of hemiparasitic herbs (they photosynthesise but draw water and nutrients from the roots of neighbouring plants) and that lousewort is the genus's English name. Which compounds this species or the Tibetan drug holds is not shown by the lines read, so no statement about its composition is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "nutrilizes toxin and cures meat poison" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3433) and "collects poisons, cures meat poisoning" (Clark, line 1030). Clark's commentary adds brown phlegm, channel fever, dysentery with fever, and a hair-growth and hair-darkening claim. These are the texts' claims in their own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. Poisoning needs emergency medical care, and no herb should be used as an antidote on the strength of this entry.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary describes the leaf and flower and does not say which part is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 1030) prints "bitter taste" and "fluid power" for the brown *Pedicularis*. No post-digestive taste is printed. Men-Tsee-Khang prints no taste or power for "Lugru mukpo" in the lines read (3433, 5437), and the name is not among the taste groups of chapter 19. Clark's taste and power belong to the lines of other binomials (olivertana, pectinata, pyramidate), and the Codex does not carry them over to *P. siphonantha*.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes this drug by what it does: it collects poisons and cures meat poisoning, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same in kind. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23 in both editions), and Clark's materia medica names other drugs as essence-extraction ingredients (for example lines 853, 971 and 1144), none of them this one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name and not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a poison and meat-poison drug in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and the species is the glossary's by a positional reading. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Poisoning needs medical care; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Because the species is uncertain, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
