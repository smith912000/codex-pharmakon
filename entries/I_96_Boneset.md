## Section 1 — Nomenclature

### Primary Scientific Name
*Eupatorium perfoliatum* L. — Boneset / Common Boneset (Asteraceae)

### Nomenclature Table

| Tradition | Language | Name | Transliteration | Meaning |
|-----------|----------|------|-----------------|---------|
| Greek (Botanical) | Ἑλληνική | — | Eupatorion | Named for Mithridates Eupator (same attribution as agrimony, I_93) — several *Eupatorium* species were used in antidote preparations |
| Latin (Botanical) | Latina | Eupatorium perfoliatum | — | *Perfoliatum* = "through the leaf" — the stem appears to pass through the leaves (the paired leaves are fused at the base, encircling the stem) |
| Algonquian (BM) | — | — | — | Multiple names; used specifically for the severe myalgia ("bone-breaking fever") of dengue/influenza |
| English | — | Boneset / Throughwort / Feverwort / Ague-Weed / Indian Sage | — | "Boneset" — not for broken bones but for the deep bone pain of influenza/dengue; "Ague-Weed" = malaria-like fever herb |

### Botanical Identity
Perennial 60–150cm; distinctive perfoliate leaves (stem grows through the fused leaf base, appearing to pierce the leaf — *perfoliatum*); flat-topped clusters of small white flowers; grows in wet meadows, marshes, and stream banks of eastern North America. The aerial parts (leaf, stem, flower) harvested at full bloom are the drug material. The "boneset" name refers specifically to "break-bone fever" — the colloquial American name for dengue fever and severe influenza, characterised by extreme bone and muscle pain. Boneset tea was the standard American domestic remedy for flu and dengue from the colonial period through the early 20th century.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 North American Indigenous (BM)

Multiple eastern North American nations used boneset extensively:
- **Algonquian**: Tea for "bone-breaking fever" (influenza/dengue); sweating therapy
- **Cherokee**: Hot tea for fever, rheumatism, and colds
- **Iroquois**: For "colds and fevers" and muscle pain
- **Menominee**: For "extreme cold with bone pain"

The indigenous application was for the specific symptom cluster of severe myalgia + fever — exactly the presentation of influenza and dengue. The plant was one of the most widely used indigenous fever medicines in North America.

### 2.2 South American / Broader Pan-American (SA)

Multiple *Eupatorium* species (*E. ayapana*, *E. cannabinum*, *E. glutinosum*) used by South American and Caribbean indigenous peoples for fever, influenza, and rheumatic pain — the same application cluster.

### 2.3 US Eclectic Medicine

Boneset was one of the most widely prescribed herbs in 19th-century American Eclectic and Thomsonian medicine:
- King's *American Dispensatory* (1898): "Boneset is specifically indicated in influenza with deep bone pain, remittent fever with bone soreness, and dengue fever — it is the great remedy for deep bone ache in febrile disease."
- Standard preparation: hot strong tea inducing diaphoresis (sweating) for fever clearance
- Listed in US Pharmacopoeia 1820–1905

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Immunomodulatory (Primary — Polysaccharides)

**Eupatorium polysaccharides**: Activate macrophages (phagocytosis enhancement) and NK cells (natural killer cell cytotoxicity) via TLR-2/TLR-4 → increased interferons and IL-12 → enhanced antiviral innate immunity. This mechanism is directly relevant to the traditional application for influenza and viral fever — the polysaccharides prime the innate immune response to speed viral clearance.

### 5.2 Diaphoretic / Antipyretic

**Volatile oil + polysaccharides**: The hot boneset tea produces diaphoresis (profuse sweating) — the classic fever-breaking mechanism of the indigenous and Eclectic tradition. Mechanistically:
- Volatile EO compounds stimulate thermoregulatory centres → promote evaporative cooling via sweating
- Polysaccharide immune activation → shifts cytokine profile from fever-driving to resolution-phase

The combined immunostimulant + diaphoretic mechanism explains why the traditional preparation was always hot tea — the thermal stimulus and the pharmacological mechanism synergise.

### 5.3 Anti-Inflammatory / Analgesic (Sesquiterpene Lactones)

**Eupatoriopicrin + related sesquiterpene lactones**: NF-κB inhibition (class mechanism) + direct prostaglandin E2 suppression → reduced musculoskeletal inflammation → the bone and muscle pain relief application. The sesquiterpene lactone NF-κB inhibition is the same mechanistic class as cnicin (I_95) and parthenolide (I_84).

### 5.4 Antiviral

**Quercetin + caffeic acid + polysaccharides**: Direct viral inhibition (quercetin: influenza neuraminidase inhibition; caffeic acid: viral polymerase inhibition) combined with innate immune activation. The antiviral profile is broad-spectrum within the influenza/coronavirus/enterovirus category.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Formal RCT data**: Absent for boneset specifically. Evidence base is:
- Strong traditional consensus across multiple North American indigenous nations (convergent pre-colonial use)
- US Pharmacopoeia listing (1820–1905) — official recognition of clinical utility
- Eclectic physician case records — extensive clinical documentation for influenza and dengue
- Mechanistic pharmacology: polysaccharide immunostimulation, sesquiterpene anti-inflammatory, quercetin antiviral — all well-characterised mechanisms

The absence of RCTs reflects the economic dynamics of herbal research in the 20th century (no patent incentive) rather than any doubt about the traditional evidence.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Break-bone fever and the bone-setter**: The name "boneset" is an encoding of a symptom, not an anatomy lesson. "Break-bone fever" (*quebranta huesos* in Spanish — literally "bone-breaker") was the Caribbean and American colonial name for dengue fever, derived from the characteristically severe myalgia that patients describe as feeling like their bones are breaking. Boneset tea was the specific domestic remedy for this symptom complex — not because it healed broken bones but because it treated the fever that made bones feel broken. The naming is a complete symptom-to-medicine record in two words. The indigenous naming in Algonquian languages preserved the same functional description: the herb for the breaking-bone disease.

**The American flu remedy**: From the 1600s through the early 1900s, boneset tea was the standard American household flu remedy — prepared by pouring boiling water over the fresh or dried herb, drinking it as hot as tolerable, and sweating it out under blankets. This is a complete traditional anti-influenza protocol: immune activation (polysaccharides), fever management (diaphoresis), anti-inflammatory (sesquiterpene lactones), antiviral (quercetin/caffeic acid) — all in one hot cup. The 1918 influenza pandemic killed 50–100 million people worldwide; in communities where boneset tea was the standard domestic response, its use was intensified. Whether it provided meaningful protection or symptom reduction is unverifiable from historical records — but the pharmacological rationale for its traditional application is sound.

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM (cross-reference)**: Bitter, acrid; slightly cool. Channels: Lung (肺), Liver (肝). Clears exterior Wind-Heat, releases the exterior through diaphoresis, reduces fever, moves Blood.

**Ayurvedic (cross-reference)**: Bitter, pungent; slightly cooling. Reduces Pitta ↓↓. *Jvaraghna* (febrifuge), *Svedana* (diaphoretic), *Vājikaraṇa* (vitality restoration). Not a classical Ayurvedic plant.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Pyrrolizidine alkaloids (trace)**: *Eupatorium* species may contain trace pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PA) — hepatotoxic and potentially carcinogenic with chronic high-dose exposure. For ACUTE use (1–5 days during flu) at standard doses: risk is minimal. For CHRONIC use: avoid; the PA content is the primary long-term safety concern
- **Liver disease**: PA risk amplified in liver disease — avoid
- **Pregnancy**: PA risk + diaphoretic/immunostimulant concerns — avoid
- **Children**: PA safety in children unclear — use under supervision only
- **Asteraceae allergy**: Cross-reactivity possible
- **Short-course acute use** (as the traditional application specifies) is the appropriate use pattern — this is NOT a long-term tonic herb
