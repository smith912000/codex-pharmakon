# Plantago psyllium — Psullion (Dioscorides 4-70)

## *Plantago psyllium, the last of three names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-70 ("Psyllium [Fuchs]", "Psyllium majus erectum [Bauhin]", "Plantago psyllium [Linnaeus]"), glossed "Psyllium, Flea Seed"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 8642) prints the three names, the last the Linnaean one. The row was added from the line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-70. PSULLION*, heading at line 8641, SUGGESTED line at 8642, description at line 8643 and uses at line 8644. It stands in Book Four, before *4-71. STRUCHNOS KEPAIOS* (heading at line 8645). The translators' index gives *Psyllium* and *Psyllium majus* at page 616.

The description reads: "Psyllium has a rough leaf similar to coronopis [2-158] only longer, stems twenty centimetres long, and the whole herb little like hay. The stem comes from the middle of the stalk, with two or three little pods rolled on the top close together, in which is seed similar to fleas, black and hard. It grows in fields and untilled places." The leaf is compared to the one in chapter 2-158 (the subject of entry I_1162).

The uses, in the order the chapter gives them: "Applied with rosaceum [1-53], vinegar, or water it is cooling; it helps the arthritic, inflammation of the parotid gland, tumours, oedema, dislocations and aches. Smeared on with vinegar it heals the hernias of children and those whose navels protrude. Having pounded an acetabulum [vinegar cruet] of it into small pieces, they must steep it in two fingers of water, and smear it on (when the water has grown thick) for it cools abundantly. Put into boiling water it suppresses heat, and it is also good against erysipela [streptococcal skin infection]. They say that brought into a house (fresh) it does not allow fleas to breed. Pounded with grease it cleans the foulness and malignancies of ulcers, and the juice (with honey) is good for running ears, and ears with worms."

Every use printed in the chapter is external: a smear, a poultice or an ear application. The chapter does not say that the seed is swallowed, and it prints no use of the seed as a bulk-forming or laxative preparation. That use belongs to later and other traditions, and the chapter cannot be read as attesting it. The flea statement is a reported belief ("They say"), and the name *Flea Seed* in the translators' gloss matches the chapter's own comparison of the seed to fleas. The chapter's closing list of names reads: "It is also called cataphysis, cynocephalion, crystallium, cynomuia, psylleris, or sicelioticon; the Sicilians call it conidijs, the Romans, silvacium, some, herba pulicaria, and the Africans, vargugum." They are names only.

**In the translators' introduction.** Psyllium appears in a modern-pharmacopoeia list in the introduction (line 383: "...poppy, psyllium, rhubarb, rosemary..."). That is the translators' sentence about drugs "still in some modern pharmacopoeia", not Dioscorides' text, and it is not read as a statement about 4-70.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter number, *Psullion*, *Plantago psyllium* and *psyllium* found no existing entry on 4-70. I_189 (*Plantago major*) and I_676 (*Plantago lanceolata*) mention psyllium or *Plantago ovata* as a separate seed-husk drug (an Ayurvedic and modern use), and I_767 (carob) and I_748 (manna ash) mention psyllium in passing. None is an entry on this chapter.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. It is a general, well-established fact that the seed coat of psyllium species is rich in mucilage, which swells and becomes gelatinous in water. That fact is stated generally. The chapter's own description of the seed softening in water ("when the water has grown thick") is of the same kind of observation, but the chapter does not name a constituent. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a seed used as a cooling external preparation for arthritic joints, glands, tumours, oedema, dislocations, aches, hernias, erysipelas, ulcers and ear complaints, with a reported effect on fleas. These are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for these conditions, and the Codex does not extend the chapter to any internal or bulk use. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations: the seed applied with *rosaceum* (the translators' cross-reference is to 1-53), vinegar or water; smeared on with vinegar; pounded, "an acetabulum [vinegar cruet] of it", steeped "in two fingers of water" and smeared on when the water has thickened; put into boiling water; pounded with grease; and the juice with honey for the ears. The only quantity is the one acetabulum steeped in two fingers of water, an external preparation. No other quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved for any internal use. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that psyllium husk is used today as a bulk-forming fibre taken by mouth with water. That modern use is not what the chapter describes, which is external.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints "cooling" ("it is cooling") and says the preparation "cools abundantly" and that, put into boiling water, it "suppresses heat". It prints no taste and no other quality.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter does record a household belief about fleas, in the form "They say", which reports what others hold and does not endorse it. Recording is not endorsing. What the chapter offers is the small discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: a leaf compared with a leaf in another chapter, a stem measured (twenty centimetres), pods counted (two or three), a seed compared with a flea in colour and hardness, and a preparation timed by watching the water thicken. The plant is given no status beyond a list of external remedies. The identification is the translators'.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It offers a description and a list of cooling external applications, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one, and it does not license taking psyllium seed by mouth on the chapter's authority.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Skin conditions such as erysipelas, ear complaints with worms, hernias and tumours need medical assessment, and a home remedy must not replace it. It is a general fact that psyllium can cause allergy in some people and must be taken with plenty of fluid when swallowed, and neither point comes from the chapter. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
