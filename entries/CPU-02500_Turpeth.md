# Turpeth — تربد (turbud / tarbad), Indian purgative root

## *The root bark of* Operculina turpethum *(the usual modern identification of Arabic* turbud*); the Canon describes only cut pieces of wood brought from India* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** CM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Avicenna (CM).** Turpeth is named only by Avicenna's *Canon* among the texts on disk. It is read in the Arabic (`Sources/CM_Avicenna/text/IbnSina_Qanun_OpenITI_Shamela0010706.txt`, Book II, entry *turbud*, heading at Arabic text line 18609).

*Al-māhiyya* (what it is): "cut pieces of wood, thick and thin, brought from India" (قطاع خشبة غلاظ ودقاق يؤتى به من الهند). *Al-ikhtiyār* (choice): "the best is the white one that is not worm-eaten, coiled like tubes of fine reed, and smooth, quick to crumble, not thick; it may be eaten away and its strength weakens; the very light and the holed is weak" (أجوده الأبيض الغير المسوس الملتف كأنابيب القصب ... والخفيف جدا والثقوب ضعيف). The sentence on its correction (*iṣlāḥuhu*) breaks off in the Arabic text (وإصلاحه أن ...), so no correction is reported here.

*Al-khawāṣṣ* (actions): "its use brings dryness and desiccation to the body because it expels the thin moistures, and therefore it is used with oil of almonds" (يورث استعماله يبسا وجفافا في البدن لأنه يخرج الرطوبات الرقيقة ولذلك يستعمل مع دهن اللوز).

*Ālāt al-nafḍ* (excretory): "it purges much phlegm and a little of the burnt humours, this when it is taken powdered; but cooked, the reverse." Avicenna then records disagreement: "Māsarjawayh said it purges the thick sticky humours. Some said it purges the raw humour from the hips; the more correct is that it purges the burnt (*muḥraq*) phlegm. If it is strengthened with ginger and what has sharpness, its strength purges the thick and raw; alone it does not purge the thick unless it finds it already detached in the stomach and intestines." Māsarjawayh is cited by name; Avicenna chooses his own reading ("the more correct").

**Identity.** *Turbud* is regularly identified with the root of *Operculina turpethum* (Convolvulaceae), which is traded as "turpeth" or "nishoth". The *Canon* text here gives only the trade form (cut wood from India), so the botanical identification is the modern one and fairly secure on trade-name and origin, but it is ours. No identity doubt arises from the text.

### Active Components

Turpeth root contains resin glycosides, which are the class of compounds responsible for the purgative action of many Convolvulaceae roots (jalap and scammony belong to the same family). This is a general botanical-chemical fact; no figure is given. The text itself attributes the action to the powdered substance and says the decoction acts the reverse.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. In the *Canon* turpeth is a purgative of phlegm (and, less, of burnt humours), made stronger and able to move thicker matter when combined with ginger or another sharp drug. Its drying effect on the body is given as the reason it is mixed with almond oil. It is not offered as a recommended practice: it is a strong purgative whose use belongs to a humoral frame that the Codex records, not endorses.

### Preparation & Dosing

Preparations given: powder; decoction (with a reversed action); powder strengthened with ginger or another sharp drug; combination with almond oil. Dose, as printed: "the draught of it is up to two dirhams, and in decoctions up to four" (والشربة منه إلى درهمين وفي المطبوخات إلى أربعة), line 18609 ff. The text does not convert the dirham to a modern weight and no conversion is offered here. [USER: verify the dirham equivalent before quoting a metric figure.]

### Scientific Correlation

Resin glycosides of the jalap type are irritant purgatives; this is a general, well-established fact about the compound class. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Avicenna (CM).** No temperament degree is printed in the turpeth entry. Quality given: drying and desiccating the body (*yabsan wa-jafāfan*), expelling thin moistures; purging phlegm first, burnt humours less.

### Cultivation Register

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent.* The turpeth entry is a purgative's entry: grades of the drug, which humour it moves, how to strengthen it, and the draught. Its only effect on the body's constitution is the one the text names, drying, and that is given as a side-effect needing almond oil, not as a benefit. No attestation of a cultivation status, and nothing on innate heat, radical moisture or the *ruḥ*.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. A drug whose own entry warns that it dries the body is a drug that works against conservation of the body's moisture; the text's own remedy for that drying is another substance, almond oil.

### Contraindications

Historical record only. Turpeth is a purgative; the entry itself says it dries and desiccates the body, and the dose it gives is a ceiling ("up to"). Resin-glycoside purgatives in general cause cramping and fluid loss and should not be used in pregnancy, which is a general statement, not one in the entry. Do not treat any use here as medical advice.
