# Winter Squash — Pumpkin, Butternut-type Squash, Kaddu / Kaboča (Cucurbita maxima)

## *Cucurbita maxima Duchesne* | Family: Cucurbitaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM (Avicenna), TCM (Chinese medicine), SS (Sushruta), BM (Badianus)

**Energetic Classification:** Flesh cold and moist in the first-to-second degree (cooling, nourishing, demulcent); seed neutral-to-warm and drying.

**Training Phase:** Purification

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Cucurbita maxima* is one of the great domesticated winter squashes — the source of many buttercup, kabocha, Hubbard and "giant pumpkin" cultivars — grown for a dense, sweet, carotenoid-rich flesh and for large flat seeds. A botanical honesty note must lead this entry: *Cucurbita maxima* is a **New World species**, domesticated in South America and unknown to the Old World until after the Columbian exchange (post-1492). Therefore any "Avicenna," "Shennong" or "Sushruta" attribution below is by **transfer within a shared gourd/pumpkin category**, not direct citation of this species. The classical physicians described the Old World bottle gourd and related cucurbits (*Lagenaria*, *Cucumis*, *Citrullus*); *C. maxima* was later slotted into those same energetic and culinary roles once it reached Eurasia, and Indian and Chinese medicine absorbed it readily because it behaved like the gourds already in the pharmacopoeia.

**Avicenna (CM):** The *Qanun* treats the gourds (qarʿ) as cold and moist, cooling to a hot stomach, laxative and demulcent, good in fevers — a profile the later-arriving winter squash inherited wholesale.

**Badianus (BM):** The Badianus Manuscript (Codex de la Cruz-Badiano, 1552) is the one tradition here with a genuine **New World** claim on the genus: Aztec medicine used native *Cucurbita* squashes and their seeds among food-medicines, and this Mesoamerican lineage is the true home range of *C. maxima*'s relatives.

**Shennong / TCM (SBJ):** Chinese medicine classifies pumpkin (nan gua) and its seeds (nan gua zi) as sweet and warm, tonifying to the spleen/stomach and — for the seeds — a classic vermifuge, particularly against tapeworm and roundworm; the seed's anthelmintic reputation is one of the most consistent cross-cultural claims for the genus.

**Sushruta / Ayurveda (SS):** The *Sushruta Samhita* tradition places gourds (kushmanda and allied fruits) as cooling, nourishing, laxative and calming to pitta; pumpkin flesh entered this role, and the seeds serve as a krimighna (anti-worm) agent.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The squash is a **vessel** — a hard rind sealing a soft, sweet, seed-bearing interior that stores for months without spoiling. In Kenetica it is the emblem of *Purification through nourishment*: it cleanses (the seed expels parasites; the flesh is gentle, laxative, hydrating) while it feeds (dense carotenoids and carbohydrate). The symbolism tracks the pharmacology precisely — a plant that empties and fills at once.

### Active Components

**Seed:** the non-protein amino acid **cucurbitin** (3-amino-3-carboxypyrrolidine), the principal anthelmintic principle; plus abundant unsaturated fatty acids, phytosterols (Δ-sterols), squalene, tocopherols (notably γ-tocopherol), zinc and magnesium. **Flesh:** carotenoids in quantity — β-carotene, α-carotene, lutein and zeaxanthin (the source of the deep orange colour and pro-vitamin-A value), along with pectins, soluble fibre and vitamin C. Peel and flesh also carry polysaccharides studied for antioxidant and metabolic activity.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Winter squash is a **Purification-phase staple with a dual action**: the seed as a gentle, food-grade anthelmintic (a traditional first-line worm expellent, classically paired with a purgative to sweep the paralysed worm out), and the flesh as a nourishing, hydrating, mildly laxative carotenoid food that rebuilds during a cleanse rather than depleting. It is the "safe cleanser" of the volume — used where harsher vermifuges are contraindicated, in the young, or as maintenance.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed: Grzybek et al. (2016), *International Journal of Molecular Sciences* — pumpkin (*Cucurbita*) seed extracts showed dose-dependent anthelmintic activity in vitro and in vivo, reducing faecal egg counts and adult worm burden in nematode-infected mice (highest reduction at the 8 g/kg dose), with cucurbitin confirmed among the constituents. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms17091456). Xiao et al. (2010), *Advances in Parasitology* — a 60-year Chinese review documents cucurbitin, the amino acid isolated from pumpkin seeds, as showing in vitro and in vivo activity against *Schistosoma japonicum*, situating the traditional vermifuge in formal antischistosomal research. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-308X(10)73009-8). On the nutritional side, Grajzer et al. (2025), *Foods* — analysis across eleven *Cucurbita* cultivars (including *C. maxima*) quantified the seed lipid fraction's carotenoids (5.7–19.4 mg/kg), squalene (up to ~3092 mg/kg) and γ-tocopherol, and linked these to oxidative stability and nutraceutical value. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/foods14030354).

Evidence grade: **moderate for the seed's anthelmintic action** (consistent traditional use plus in vitro/in vivo pharmacology; note much of the controlled work is on the sister species *C. pepo* and on animal models rather than large human trials) and **moderate–strong for the flesh/seed as a carotenoid and antioxidant food** (well-characterised nutrient chemistry). Human clinical trial data specific to *C. maxima* anthelmintic dosing remain limited.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**TCM:** pumpkin (nan gua) — sweet, warm; enters Spleen and Stomach; tonifies qi, resolves damp; seeds (nan gua zi) — sweet, neutral-warm; expel worms (tapeworm, roundworm, schistosome). **Ayurveda:** rasa madhura (sweet); guna guru–snigdha (heavy, unctuous) for flesh; virya shita (cooling) for flesh, seeds more ushna (warming); vipaka madhura; pacifies pitta and vata, may increase kapha; seeds krimighna (anti-parasitic). **Galenic/Unani:** gourd-flesh cold and moist, cooling, laxative, demulcent, fit for hot fevers.

### Cultivation Register

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The *Qanun* classes the gourds (*qarʿ*) as cold and moist, cooling to a hot stomach, laxative and demulcent — a therapeutic profile the later-arriving winter squash inherited once it reached Eurasia. That inheritance is the honest limit of the claim: *C. maxima* is a post-Columbian New World plant Avicenna never saw, so what stands here is a category-transfer of the gourd role, not a status assigned to this species. The strongest cultivation idea in the corpus — conservation of innate heat and radical moisture as a finite endowment — is a frame of the whole regimen and is not assigned to a cooling laxative food; no *mufarrih* or cardiac-*ruh* role is claimed. **(b) framed:** a therapeutic gourd role by transfer, no cultivation status.

**Chinese medicine (TCM).** Pumpkin (*nan gua* 南瓜) and its seed (*nan gua zi* 南瓜子) are read as sweet and warm, tonifying the Spleen and Stomach and resolving damp, the seed a classic vermifuge. Because the plant is a post-Columbian arrival in China, it belongs to the later *bencao* stratum, not to the Shennong classic, and carries no *san pin* 三品 grade in the sources retrieved — it could not, being unknown to the classical grading. Within *yang sheng* 養生, which is conservational rather than curative, a worm-expelling seed sits on the attacking side of the ledger, not among substances taken long-term to nourish life. Its standing is therapeutic and dietary, not a cultivation grade. **(b) framed**, dated to the post-Columbian *bencao* stratum.

**Ayurveda (SS).** In the *Sushruta* tradition the gourds enter as cooling, nourishing, laxative fruits pacifying pitta, with the seed serving as a *krimighna* (anti-worm) agent — a role *C. maxima* took up by transfer after it reached India. No *rasayana* or *vajikarana* assignment is recorded for this species in the sources retrieved; the famous *Kushmanda rasayana* belongs to the ash gourd (*Benincasa hispida*), a different plant, and must not be read onto this squash. Its standing here is therapeutic and dietetic, not a formal rejuvenative status. **(b) framed.**

**Nahua — Badianus (BM).** The genus is the one tradition here with a genuine New World claim: the Badianus manuscript (Codex de la Cruz-Badiano, 1552) records native *Cucurbita* squashes and their seeds among food-medicines, and this is the true home range of *C. maxima*'s relatives. But the Badianus manuscript is itself naturalistic and does not contain the *tonalli* / *teyolia* / *ihiyotl* model of the person; that scheme is reconstructed from later ethnohistory (López Austin) and is not applied to the squash in the manuscript. The record is of a food-medicine use, with no cultivation status attached. **(b) framed.**

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator uses this plant on both sides of a cleanse at once: the ground seed as a gentle, food-grade worm expellent taken fasting and classically chased with a mild laxative, and the cooked flesh as a hydrating, carotenoid-dense food that rebuilds during the purge rather than depleting. That is its whole role — a safe cleanser and a nourishing vessel, taken for the defined job of a Purification cycle and not as a longevity substance. No tradition here grants it a status beyond food and remedy, and its post-Columbian history means the classical gradings could never have reached it.

### Contraindications

Broadly safe as food. The seed anthelmintic protocol should be **weight-adjusted and, in the classical method, paired cautiously with a purgative** — avoid aggressive purgation in the frail, dehydrated, or in pregnancy without supervision. Cucurbitacin-related bitterness in any *Cucurbita* signals possible "toxic squash syndrome" (cucurbitacin poisoning: cramping, vomiting, diarrhoea) — discard any bitter-tasting squash or seed. Excess carotenoid intake can cause harmless carotenoderma (orange skin). Rare cucurbit allergy exists. Diabetic patients should count the flesh's carbohydrate load.
