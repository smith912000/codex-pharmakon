# Donkey tongue — "Donkey tongue" under "Meat" (Clark's English); "Donkey tongue" (Men-Tsee-Khang's English)
## *The tongue of the donkey as a remedy in the Tibetan Explanatory Tantra. Clark's translation prints "Donkey tongue" with no Tibetan headword and no binomial; the Men-Tsee-Khang English prints "Donkey tongue"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The text on disk is Barry Clark's 1995 *The Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). Chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (line 792), opens by saying that "The power of medicines is twofold: (a) the power of taste and (b) the natural power [of the substance]" (line 794). Its Specific Section (line 801) lists the individual substances in eight kinds, the eighth being animal products. The donkey's tongue is in the run of organs and meats under "Meat" (line 1170):

"Wolf's stomach: [has twelve names] It increases digestive heat and digests undigested food, [especially meat]. Its tongue removes swellings of the tongue. Pig tongue: removes bone warts. Dog tongue: [has ten names and] heals all types of wounds. Donkey tongue: [has fourteen names]. It halts diarrhoea."

So for the donkey's tongue Clark prints that it "has fourteen names" and that it "halts diarrhoea". Clark says in his introduction that interpolations in square brackets are taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries (line 82), so the count of fourteen names is an interpolation from commentary, and the action is the root line. The English prints no Tibetan headword for the donkey's tongue and no binomial, and the names are not given in the lines read.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`) has the same line (line 3536): "Dog tongue heals wounds. Donkey tongue cures diarrhea." The two translations agree: "halts diarrhoea" in Clark, "cures diarrhea" in Men-Tsee-Khang. The tongue is one of a run of tongue and stomach statements in both, for the wolf, pig, dog and donkey.

Clark's introduction says that "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57). The Men-Tsee-Khang preface says that the "use of animal products was negligible" and that animal products "have been completely substituted by equally effective herbal substances", and that the Tantra's potencies for them are given "to preserve the understanding of the therapeutic values of animal products" (line 687).

Other donkey materials in the same chapter and the same translations, which I read:

- *Blood.* "Donkey blood: cures rheumatism and lymph disorders in the joints" (Clark line 1176). The Men-Tsee-Khang version has "Donkey blood cures arthritis and an excess accumulation of ..." (line 3540; the sentence runs on beyond the part I quote).
- *Hoof.* "bong.buhi.rmig.pa: the (right) hoof of (a one-year-old black) Donkey: cures urinary retention [and infectious fevers]" (Clark line 1188). The Men-Tsee-Khang version has "Donkey hooves cure obstruction of urine" (line 3556).
- *Milk.* In the section on milks, "Horse and donkey milk strengthen the lungs, but also cause mental dullness" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3059).
- *Meat in the diet.* For a person with a *loong* disorder the recommended diet "consists of the meat from horse, donkey, marmot, one-year-old meat, shachhen, mustard oil, aged butter ..." (Men-Tsee-Khang line 1100); the same text repeats donkey meat among the heavy, oily, smooth and warm foods for *loong* (line 4426). The same translation lists the donkey among the group of domesticated animals (line 2917). The bone of a "Packhorse/Mule" is a separate Clark line ("cures lymph disorders", line 1160) and is not the donkey.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms the tongue is skeletal muscle covered by a mucous membrane, made mostly of protein with some fat. Nothing more specific can be said without a source.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text records the donkey's tongue as halting diarrhoea. The Codex does not recommend it. Diarrhoea is treated by replacing fluid and salts, and by medical care where it is severe, bloody, prolonged or in a child or an older person; an animal organ is an infection risk and not a remedy. The tradition's own translators say that animal products have largely been replaced by herbal substances.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives no preparation for the donkey's tongue. It does not say whether it is eaten, dried, burnt or powdered, and the lines read give no quantity. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. No animal organ is an established treatment for diarrhoea; the established first treatment is oral rehydration, with medical care for severe cases. Raw or poorly cooked animal tissue can itself cause diarrhoea and other infection through bacteria and parasites. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** None printed for this item. Chapter 20 says that medicines act by taste and by natural power, and lists eight powers (heavy, oily, cool, blunt, light, coarse, hot, sharp; Clark line 796). The donkey-tongue entry (line 1170) prints no taste, power or post-digestive taste, and neither does the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence (line 3536). The Men-Tsee-Khang text prints qualities for a list of foods that includes donkey meat ("heavy, oily, smooth and warm in qualities", line 4426), but those belong to the meat list and not to the tongue, and are not carried over. Clark's introduction says that the later *Crystal Orb* and *Crystal Rosary* give each medicine's tastes, potencies and qualities, but that text is not on disk.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for the donkey's tongue in the lines read. Chapter 20 gives it one therapeutic action, and says nothing about essence extraction (*bcud len*) or any other practice. The Men-Tsee-Khang text places donkey meat in the diet for a *loong* disorder, which is a dietary recommendation for an illness and is about the meat; it is not a cultivation status, and it is not carried over to the tongue. The Men-Tsee-Khang preface records a Buddhist objection to causing harm to any being as the reason animal products have been replaced by herbs (line 687). No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The donkey's tongue is one clause in a list of organs, and its tradition describes animal products as largely replaced. The honest use of the entry is as a record. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Diarrhoea:** replace fluids; seek medical care for blood in the stool, fever, dehydration, or illness in a young child or older person.
- **Infection and hygiene:** raw or poorly prepared animal organ meat is an infection risk.
- **Ethics and law:** the Codex does not recommend harming any animal for these uses, and the Tibetan translators themselves record a Buddhist objection to harming any being. Donkeys are working animals in many places, and the slaughter of donkeys for parts is illegal or restricted in some regions.
