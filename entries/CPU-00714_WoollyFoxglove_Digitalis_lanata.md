# Woolly Foxglove / Grecian Foxglove

## *Digitalis lanata* Ehrh. | Family: Plantaginaceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM [USER: verify — see Origin & Tradition; both attestations are doubtful]
**Energetic Classification:** Cold, Dry — a contracting, slowing, water-moving agent (Northern European folk framing); no reliable classical humoral assignment exists for this species
**Training Phase:** Study Only — Phase Ø. This plant is not used at any phase of Kenetica practice. It is included as a teaching substance and a cultivator's hazard.

---

## ⚠️ CATEGORY: DEADLY — STUDY ONLY

**No dose for the plant is given in this entry, and none should be sought.** *Digitalis lanata* is the industrial source of digoxin, a drug with one of the narrowest therapeutic indices in the modern pharmacopoeia. The gap between a therapeutic serum concentration and a lethal one is roughly a factor of two. There is no domestic preparation — no tea, no tincture, no "gentle" dose — that can be titrated safely, because the cardenolide content of the leaf varies with variety, season, leaf age, and drying method by margins larger than the therapeutic window itself. Every self-administration of foxglove leaf is, functionally, an unmeasured overdose that has not yet declared itself. **This plant belongs in the garden and the factory, never in the kitchen.**

### Origin & Tradition

*Digitalis lanata* is a Balkan and Danubian plant — native to south-eastern Europe, Hungary, Romania, the former Yugoslavia, Greece and Turkey — with woolly, glandular flower-spikes and narrow lance-shaped leaves, quite unlike the soft broad rosettes of its purple cousin. It is the *younger* of the two foxgloves in medical history, and this is the single most important fact about its tradition: **it has almost none.**

**Classical Mediterranean — Dioscorides (DMM tag): NOT SUPPORTED [USER: verify].**
The DMM tag on this entry is inherited from the codex's Plantaginaceae grouping and should be treated as unverified. *De Materia Medica* contains no identification the codex can honestly map to *Digitalis lanata*. The genus was not named until Fuchs coined *Digitalis* in 1542, and the classical Greek and Roman herbal corpus — despite the plant growing in Greece and the Balkans within the ambit of that corpus — records no cardiac or dropsical use for it that survives to us. Attempts by later herbalists to retrofit a Dioscoridean identity onto foxglove are speculative. **The codex declines to assert this attestation.** If a reader has a source that supports it, it should be added; absent that, the tag is a filing convention, not a claim.

**Arabic-Persian — Avicenna (CM tag): NOT SUPPORTED [USER: verify].**
The *Canon of Medicine* is a Mediterranean-and-eastward corpus. Foxglove is a northern and western European plant of acid soils and woodland edges (*D. purpurea*) or Balkan uplands (*D. lanata*); it is essentially absent from the materia medica of the Arabic-Persian tradition. There is no reliable Avicennan indication for *Digitalis*. **The codex declines to assert this attestation.** Where other Kenetica entries carry DMM/CM tags with confidence, this one does not — and saying so plainly is more useful to the reader than a plausible-sounding fabrication.

**Balkan & Central European folk medicine: sparse, and mostly downstream of pharmacy.**
Unlike *D. purpurea*, which has a genuine, documented lay-healer tradition in the British Isles, *D. lanata*'s medical reputation is largely a twentieth-century industrial artefact. It entered the pharmacopoeia not through a folk healer but through a chemist's assay: the plant was found to contain a richer and more tractable cardenolide profile than *D. purpurea*, and Hungarian and German cultivation programmes followed the chemistry, not the tradition. Where Balkan folk uses of *lanata* are reported, they should be read with suspicion that they were back-formed from knowledge of *purpurea* or from the pharmacy.

**Modern pharmacognosy — the real lineage.**
This is where *D. lanata* actually lives. It is the commercial source of **digoxin**, and of the lanatosides (A, B, C) and their semi-synthetic derivative **deslanoside** (deacetyl-lanatoside C). Digoxin remains on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines. According to PubMed, LC-MS/MS profiling of fresh Digitalis leaf extracts identified seventeen cardenolides, with **lanatosides A, C and E as the major cardenolides in *D. lanata***, versus purpurea glycosides A, B and E in *D. purpurea* — a chemical fingerprint that separates the two species cleanly ([DOI: 10.1016/j.chroma.2020.460903](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chroma.2020.460903)).

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.**
The doctrine attached to foxglove — in its *purpurea* form, from which *lanata* inherits its reputation — is the doctrine of **dropsy**: a body waterlogged, swollen at the ankles, breathless when it lies flat, the flesh pitting under a thumb. Folk doctrine read this as *water that will not move*, and foxglove as the agent that moves it. The remedy was classed as a diuretic, and it works: the patient passes urine, the swelling falls, the breath returns.

The doctrine is right about the *observation* and wrong about the *organ*. Foxglove is not a kidney drug. What the doctrine calls "moving the water" is a cardiac event: the cardenolide binds and inhibits the **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase** of the myocyte membrane. Intracellular Na⁺ rises; the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger, which runs on the sodium gradient, slows its export of calcium; intracellular Ca²⁺ rises; the contraction strengthens. A stronger contraction means better renal perfusion, and the kidney — never touched by the drug — does the diuresis itself. The water moves because the pump was mended, not because the sluice was opened.

This is the most instructive doctrine↔chemistry mapping in the whole codex, because it shows the two possible failure modes at once. **The empirical claim survives translation** (the swelling does fall). **The mechanistic claim does not** (it is not a diuretic). A tradition can be reliably right about what happens and entirely wrong about why, and the "why" is not decoration — it is what determines whether the next inference you draw from the doctrine is safe. A healer who believes foxglove is a diuretic will reach for it in the wrong dropsies, and some of those patients will die.

The second doctrinal layer is the vernacular one: the *purpurea* names — dead man's bells, witches' gloves — encode the toxicity in the name itself. *D. lanata* has no such folk-name warning, because it had no folk tradition to generate one. **A plant with no tradition also has no inherited caution.** That is exactly why the deadliest of the two foxgloves is the one that sounds harmless.

### Active Components

- **Lanatoside C** — the flagship glycoside of this species, and the industrial precursor to digoxin. Also studied in its own right: according to PubMed, lanatoside C inhibits proliferation and induces apoptosis in human prostate cancer cell lines via TNF/IL-17 signalling ([DOI: 10.3390/ijms26062558](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms26062558)) — *in vitro* only; see Scientific Correlation for the grading.
- **Lanatosides A and E** — major co-occurring cardenolides in this species ([DOI: 10.1016/j.chroma.2020.460903](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chroma.2020.460903)).
- **Digoxin** — obtained by hydrolysis/deacetylation of lanatoside C. The aglycone is digoxigenin; the sugar chain is three digitoxose units.
- **Deslanoside** (deacetyl-lanatoside C) — a semi-synthetic parenteral derivative.
- **Glucodigifucoside** — identified as an unexpected major cardenolide in *D. lanata* ([DOI: 10.1016/j.chroma.2020.460903](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chroma.2020.460903)).
- **Aglycone series** — diginatigenin, digoxigenin, gitoxigenin, gitaloxigenin, digitoxigenin, distinguishable by their stepwise loss of hydroxyls under MS fragmentation (same source).

All cardenolides share the same target: the α-subunit of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. Potency differences across the series are pharmacokinetic (absorption, half-life, protein binding) more than pharmacodynamic.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**None. This substance has no Kenetica use case at any phase.**

Its value to the practitioner is entirely didactic, and the lesson is worth the page:

1. **The narrow-index lesson.** Most of the Volume I materia has a therapeutic window wide enough to absorb a practitioner's error. Foxglove does not. It is the codex's reference point for what "narrow therapeutic index" actually means in the body: a drug where the difference between the dose that helps and the dose that kills is a factor of two, and where the *plant* cannot deliver either reliably.
2. **The tradition-is-not-safety lesson.** *D. lanata* is more chemically potent than *D. purpurea* and has essentially no folk record. If a practitioner's heuristic is "traditional use implies relative safety," this plant breaks it in both directions at once.
3. **The doctrine-audit lesson.** Dropsy → diuresis → "it's a kidney herb" is a perfectly reasonable inference from perfectly good observation, and it is wrong. Every doctrine in this codex should be interrogated the way this one has to be.

**Cultivator's note.** *D. lanata* is a handsome biennial-to-short-lived-perennial for a dry, sunny, sharply drained bed — it dislikes the wet winters that *purpurea* tolerates. Sow surface-pressed (the seed needs light) in late spring; expect a rosette in year one and the woolly spike in year two. Grow it for the spike, not for the leaf. Wear gloves when deadheading or clearing the rosette in autumn: handling is low-risk, but sap in the eye or a cut is not worth discovering. **Never dry the leaf. Never store dried leaf in a jar near your culinary herbs.** In North America, *D. lanata* is a listed noxious weed in some states — check before planting. If you grow it near a vegetable garden, mark it, and tell anyone who forages your ground what it is.

Graded honestly. The evidence base here is unusually strong *for the isolated drug* and unusually weak *for the plant*.

**The honest summary:** the *molecule* is one of the best-characterised drugs in medicine. The *plant* is a poison with no domestic use. Those two sentences are not in tension — they are the whole point.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Cold and dry in the Northern European folk framing that the codex can actually support: a **contracting, slowing, downward-and-outward-moving** agent. It slows (bradycardia — real: vagal tone and AV nodal conduction), it contracts (inotropy — real), and it moves water down and out (diuresis — real, but secondary).

The energetic reading is, unusually, a decent *phenomenological* description and a terrible *mechanistic* one. Note also the classic toxidrome the folk classification never captured: **xanthopsia** — objects haloed and washed yellow-green, the visual field of a Van Gogh — plus nausea, vomiting, confusion, and arrhythmia. No traditional energetic system predicts yellow vision, because no energetic system had a concept of retinal Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. Where the doctrine has no vocabulary for a symptom, the doctrine cannot warn you about it. **[USER: verify] any energetic tag applied to this species specifically rather than inherited from *D. purpurea*.**

### Contraindications

**⚠️ ABSOLUTE — this plant is not to be ingested by anyone, in any preparation, at any dose, for any reason.**

- **Not for self-use. Not for practitioner use. Not for "microdosing." Not for experimentation.**
- **Toxidrome to recognise:** nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain and diarrhoea first (often mistaken for gastroenteritis); then dizziness, confusion, and **xanthopsia / yellow-green vision with halos**; then bradyarrhythmias or tachyarrhythmias, and **hyperkalaemia**. Cardiac arrest. Onset may be delayed hours; distribution is incomplete for around 6 h.
- **Delayed presentation is the trap.** The GI phase looks survivable. The cardiac phase arrives afterwards.
- **Renal impairment massively increases risk** — digoxin's half-life extends beyond 100 h in renal failure, and so does the antidote's ([DOI: 10.3109/15563650.2014.943907](https://doi.org/10.3109/15563650.2014.943907)).
- **Never combine with any digitalis-class or digitalis-adjacent substance:** oleander, lily-of-the-valley, squill, toad venom (bufadienolides), or prescribed digoxin. Cross-reactivity is real; oleander poisoning is managed with the same antidote ([DOI: 10.3390/toxins17030115](https://doi.org/10.3390/toxins17030115)).
- **Anyone on prescribed digoxin must not go near this plant** — including handling large quantities, and including growing it where children or animals forage.
- **Do not confuse with comfrey.** See the *D. purpurea* entry: a nine-patient poisoning outbreak arose from "comfrey" tea that was foxglove leaf ([DOI: 10.1016/S1726-4901(10)70009-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1726-4901(10)70009-5)). The confusion applies to any non-flowering foxglove rosette.
- **Children, pregnancy, lactation, animals:** absolute. Livestock and pets have been poisoned by garden clippings.
- **If ingestion is suspected:** emergency services immediately. Bring the plant. Say "foxglove — possible cardiac glycoside." The antidote exists; the delay is what kills.
