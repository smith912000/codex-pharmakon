# Red ochre — "Hematite" (Bryan's English rendering of the Ebers Papyrus mineral list); Bryan's note: the Egyptian *d'd'* (*didi*), "red ochre or hematite"
## *A red iron-bearing earth or ore: Bryan prints "Hematite" in his mineral list and, in a note under that list, reports that the word once read as "Mandrake" was shown to be "red clay" and then "red ochre or hematite"; the only prescription in his text names "Hematite-from-Elefantine"* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian Tradition (EP).** The locus is Bryan's 1930 English *The Papyrus Ebers*, Chapter V "Mineral Remedies", the alphabetical list of mineral drugs (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1173: "Hematite Honey Fresh Honey Wild Honey Honeycomb"; name-index row `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` line 15, class mineral, "Bryan's English identification"). The spine row for this entry reads "Red ochre, Fe2O3 (hematite)", and cites Nunn 1996 and a website; neither was opened for this entry, so the spine's label "Red ochre" is read here through Bryan's own note, set out next.

**Bryan's note on the identification (lines 1223-1229).** Directly after the mineral list Bryan writes: "Included in the above list is the substance Hematite and the positive identification of this 'drug' is interesting and illustrative of the difficulties that beset the original translators of the Egyptian Papyri. The Egyptian word for this is d'd' (or didi) and it was originally translated as the Mandrake (or love-apple) on account of its resemblance to the Hebrew word dudaim in the Old Testament." He continues that Professor G. Elliot Smith drew attention to the error in 1919; that "Gauthier's researches ... had led him to the conclusion that didi was nothing else but red clay, while Griffith had gone further and properly identified the substance as red ochre or hematite"; and that Dawson's 1927 summary in the *Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society* "appears to have clinched the matter for good and all, consequently no mention of the Mandrake will be found in these pages, the *alraunen* of the German translations being interpreted as Hematite." That is the only place in the Bryan text on disk where the words "red ochre" appear. Bryan thus gives "Hematite" as his rendering and "red ochre" as an alternative equation reported from earlier scholars; he does not give separate drugs for the two.

**Where the drug is prescribed in Bryan's text.** The Bryan text on disk was searched for "hematite", "ochre", "didi", and "mandrake". The only prescription is at lines 3713-3728, in the chapter headed "Diagnosis" (heading line 3438): "When thou examinest a person who is suffering in his abdomen, his limbs are tender and quite flaccid; thou examinest him and findest no wound on his body except in the genitals ... (uncertain) ... like a little ball, then say thou to him: 'There is something dangerous in thee.' Make him as a remedy against it: Hematite-from-Elefantine, crushed Linseed Onions Cook in Oil and Honey. Let the Patient eat this for four mornings in order to quench his thirst and to drive away the dangerous matter from his stomach." The gap and the "(uncertain)" are in Bryan's text. "Hematite-from-Elefantine" is a drug with its place of origin laid down, as Bryan says of many mineral drugs at lines 1133-1137.

**Related items not used.** "Red-lead" is a separate mineral in Bryan's text (for example lines 1909, 2165, 2995) and is not this drug. "Red Natron" (line 1191) is a form of Natron and also not this drug. Two Codex project files under `Sources/EP_Ebers/` (`EP_Original_Hieroglyphic.md` lines 415, 475 and 841; `EP_Overview.md`) print passages labelled as Ebbell translations that name "red ochre" and "yellow ochre". These do not match Bryan's text on disk, give no verifiable page references, and disagree with each other on other items, so they were not used as authority for this entry. [USER: verify]

**What is not said.** The sources retrieved do not say what hematite or red ochre looked like to the scribe, how it was crushed, or whether the material in the abdomen remedy was ore or earth. The Ebers material is indication-organised, so the identity of any named substance is a modern hypothesis; for this one Bryan reports a scholarly argument and takes a side.

### Active Components

None can be stated from the papyrus. In general terms, hematite is the mineral form of iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3, and red ochre is a natural earth coloured red by it, usually mixed with clay and silica. These are insoluble, dark-red to red-brown solids and are not significantly absorbed when swallowed. This is a general statement about the mineral, not a reading of Bryan.

---

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved show one prescription: crushed hematite from Elefantine, cooked with linseed and onions in oil and honey, eaten for four mornings for an abdominal complaint whose description is partly illegible, "to quench his thirst and to drive away the dangerous matter from his stomach". It is a single passage; the papyrus gives no further use. No modern protocol is supplied and none is implied.

### Preparation & Dosing

Only the one preparation at lines 3722-3728: Hematite-from-Elefantine, crushed, with linseed and onions, cooked in oil and honey, and eaten "for four mornings". No quantity is printed in Bryan's summary. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] As general facts: iron oxides are used as pigments; red ochre pigments have been used by many cultures for body and wall decoration; iron oxide as a mineral is not a source of well-absorbed iron; and natural earths can be contaminated with heavy metals. These points are not drawn from the papyrus.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's vital constitution. The one hematite passage is part of a case in the diagnostic style (lines 3713-3728): the physician is told what to look for and to "say thou to him: 'There is something dangerous in thee.'" before giving the remedy. That is a spoken part of the case, and Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*), but the words are the physician's pronouncement to the patient, not a recitation over the drug, and they are not read as a cultivation framing. The person as plural (*ka*, *ba*, *akh*, *ib*) is not engaged by this entry. No deity is attributed.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. The record is one four-morning course for an abdominal complaint, with no quantity. A cultivator has nothing to do with it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Unspecified earth:** natural red earths can carry heavy metals and other contaminants; the material is not defined beyond Bryan's English word.
- **Abdominal illness:** the papyrus's complaint is only partly legible; abdominal pain, thirst and swelling need medical assessment, not a pigment.
- **Cooked animal and plant fats:** the compound is cooked in oil and honey; spoilage and contamination are hazards of any such preparation.
