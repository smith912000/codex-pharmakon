# Diospyros ebenum — Ebenos (Dioscorides 1-129)

## *Diospyros ebenum (the translators' first suggestion; "Ceylon Ebony"), with Ebenus cretica / Anthyllis cretica, "Cretan Silver Bush", as a second name* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names this wood as a drug. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The line (line 4477) reads "Diospyros ebenum — Ceylon Ebony Ebenus cretica, Anthyllis cretica — Cretan Silver Bush". The translators thus offer two different plants for the one chapter, a tree from Ceylon and a Cretan shrub. The chapter itself speaks of an "Ethiopian" ebenus as best and an "Indian" kind as second, and never mentions Ceylon or Crete. The Codex does not choose between them. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *1-129. EBENOS*, heading at line 4476, SUGGESTED line at 4477, text from line 4478 to line 4482 (across a page break). It stands in Book One among the trees and gums, after 1-128 *LADANON*.

The whole chapter reads: "The Ethiopian ebenus [ebony] is best — black, without veins, as smooth as a horn that has been polished — which shows thick [close or compact] when broken, biting to the taste and gently astringent. Laid on coals and burnt like incense it yields a sweet smell without smoke. That which is new when put on fire is quickly kindled because of its fat, and it turns somewhat yellow on a whetstone. There is another (called Indian) with streaks of white and yellow running between as well as many spots. But the former is better. Some sell the wood of sesame and acanthus (because they are somewhat similar) as ebenus. They are known by this: — they are hollow like a sponge, and are formed into small pieces inclining to a purple colour with nothing biting in the taste nor sweet-smelling when burnt. Ebenus cleans away things which darken the pupils of the eyes, and it is good for old discharges and pustules. If anyone makes a grinding pad of it, and uses it to rub eye salves or suppositories on they will work better. It is made into eye medicines: the scrapings or dust from it steeped in Chian [from Scios in the Aegean sea] wine for a day and a night, then carefully pounded and converted into eye salves or suppositories. Some beat it first, then sift it and proceed in the same way. Some use water instead of wine. It is also burnt in a new ceramic jar until it becomes coals, and is then washed like burnt lead [5-96]. This is good for itching eyes and dry inflammation of the eyes."

So the larger part of the chapter is a connoisseur's test of the wood: the best is Ethiopian, black, veinless, smooth as polished horn, close-grained when broken, biting to the taste and gently astringent; laid on coals it gives a sweet smoke-free smell; new wood kindles quickly "because of its fat" and turns somewhat yellow on a whetstone. An Indian kind, streaked white and yellow and spotted, is lesser. The chapter also records an adulteration: some sell the wood of sesame and acanthus as ebenus, and the fraud is detected because those woods are hollow like a sponge, purplish, and neither biting to the taste nor sweet-smelling when burnt. The medical uses are for the eyes and skin: it "cleans away things which darken the pupils", is good for old discharges and pustules, and works as a grinding pad that makes eye salves and suppositories rub up "better". There are three prepared forms: scrapings or dust steeped in Chian wine for a day and a night, then pounded into eye salves or suppositories; the same beaten and sifted first, or steeped in water instead of wine; and the wood burnt to coals in a new ceramic jar and "washed like burnt lead [5-96]", said to be good for itching eyes and dry inflammation of the eyes. Only the name *ebenos / ebenus* is printed; no other names are given.

### Active Components

The constituents of the wood Dioscorides describes were not read. In general terms, ebony wood is a very dense, dark heartwood, but no composition is asserted for the material of this chapter, and the translators' two candidates are different plants. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a black wood used as a tool and a drug for the eyes, for old discharges and for pustules, with a long practical passage on telling genuine from adulterated wood. The Codex gives no protocol: the eye uses are for inflammation and "things which darken the pupils", for which medical assessment is needed, and ebony is a high-value timber whose trade is often restricted.

### Preparation & Dosing

1-129 gives these preparations: the wood used as a grinding pad for eye salves and suppositories; scrapings or dust "steeped in Chian wine for a day and a night", then "carefully pounded" and made into eye salves or suppositories; the wood "beat" and sifted first; water used "instead of wine"; and wood "burnt in a new ceramic jar until it becomes coals", then "washed like burnt lead [5-96]". The only time given is "a day and a night". No quantity is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints the wood as "biting to the taste and gently astringent". It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality. (It says the new wood kindles quickly "because of its fat", which is a note on the wood and not a quality of the drug.)

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. Of all the chapters in this batch this is the one that best shows the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: provenance (Ethiopian first, Indian second), a sensory assay (colour, grain on breaking, taste, smell on the coals, behaviour on a whetstone), and adulteration detection (sesame and acanthus wood sold as ebony, told apart by a hollow sponge-like body, purplish colour and lack of bite and scent). It assigns the wood no status. The identification is the translators' and is two plants. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this wood in the sense of the spec. What it gives a reader is a sound example of judging a material by looking, tasting and burning it, and of knowing the usual fakes. Its medical uses are eye and skin remedies with no amounts, so they are history and not a practice.

### Contraindications

Eye inflammation, itching and anything darkening the pupils need medical assessment, and eye preparations made at home from scraped wood are not safe. Wood dust is a general irritant. Ebony timbers may be protected or traded under restrictions in many places. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion of two different plants, so no wood should be obtained or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
