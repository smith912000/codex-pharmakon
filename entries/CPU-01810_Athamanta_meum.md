# Athamanta meum — Meon, Tordulion (Dioscorides 1-3, 3-63)

## *Athamanta meum, printed "Athemanta meum [Linnaeus]", one of the binomials on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 1-3 (which also prints Daucus creticus, Tordylon, Seseli creticum [Fuchs], Aethusa meum, Meum athemanticum [in Sprague], Athemanticum meum and Seseli meum — "Bald-money, Meu, Spignel, Bear Root") and repeated, with a cross-reference, on the SUGGESTED line for 3-63* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM); this entry rests on one text, the Osbaldeston and Wood (2000) English translation of *De Materia Medica*.** The row was added because that text prints the binomial under two chapters. The binomial is the translators' SUGGESTED gloss, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. A search of the Sushruta, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Tibetan text folders for *Athamanta* and *Meum* found no hit, and the row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The row cites two chapters, and they are not the same drug in the text.

*1-3. MEON* (heading at line 3564, SUGGESTED lines 3565 to 3566, "see 3-63" at line 3567, text at line 3568), in Book One, Aromatics. This is the chapter the binomial belongs to. It reads: "The meum which is called athamanticum grows abundantly in Macedonia and Spain, and is similar in the stalk and leaves to anethum [3-67], but thicker than anethum, sometimes rising up to two feet, scattered underneath with thin, winding, straight, long roots, smelling sweet and warming the tongue. The roots (boiled with water or pounded smooth [or fine] without boiling and taken in a drink) lessen pains caused by obstructions around the bladder and kidneys. They are good for urinary difficulties, a gas-filled stomach, griping, diseases of the womb and pain in the joints. Pounded into small pieces with honey and taken as syrup they help a rheumatic chest; boiled for a hip bath they draw out the blood of the menstrual flow. Applied as a plaster to the lower part of children's bellies they induce the movement of urine. If more of a decoction than is suitable is taken as a drink it causes a headache." The chapter gives no alternative names.

*3-63. TORDULION* (heading at line 7094, SUGGESTED lines 7095 to 7099, text at line 7100), in Book Three, among the roots. The translators print the same list of binomials as for 1-3, including "Athemanta meum [Linnaeus]", with "see 1-3", and then a second block: "[other usage] Tordyilum suaveolens, Pastinaca dissecta, Pastinaca schekakul — Rough Parsnip Tordylium officinale — Small Hartwort Tordylium maximum — Hartwort; formerly included in genus Seseli". The text itself describes a different plant: "Tordylium grows on the hill Amanus in Cilicia. It is a little herb full of shoots, with a little round double seed similar to little shields, somewhat sharp and aromatic. It is taken in a drink for painful urination, and to expel the menstrual flow. The juice from the stalk and seed (while yet green) taken as a drink for ten days with as much as thirty grains of passum [raisin wine], makes any kidney disease sound. The root is licked in with honey to draw up matter that stops the chest. It is also called tordylum, while others call it creticum." The chapter does not call the plant meum. The translators' cross-reference, and not the text, links 3-63 to the meum of 1-3. The index prints "Meu 5, 439", "Meum 5", "Meum athemanticum 5, 439" and "Tordylon 5, 439" (lines 16607 and 17901), which places both pages under one heading in the translators' index.

The two chapters are not merged here. The Codex records 1-3 as the chapter for the binomial and 3-63 as the translators' cross-referenced chapter that describes a "tordylium", a plant with its own hartwort glosses. The translators' index also prints "Athamanta 464" (line 14843), which is the page of 3-87 *LIBANOTIS*, whose SUGGESTED line reads "Libanotis, Athamanta — Mountain Spignel"; that chapter is not part of this row and is not read for it.

### Active Components

The composition of the roots and seed Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, *Athamanta* is a genus of the carrot family (Apiaceae), a family that commonly carries volatile oils in root and fruit. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Chapter 1-3 records a sweet-smelling, warming root drunk or applied for obstruction around the bladder and kidneys, urinary difficulty, gas, griping, womb disorders, joint pain and a rheumatic chest, and a hip bath that "draws out" the menstrual blood. Chapter 3-63 records a seed drunk for painful urination and to expel the menstrual flow, a green juice for "any kidney disease", and a root licked with honey for the chest. The Codex gives no protocol for any of these. The identification is the translators', among several names, and the text of 3-63 describes a plant the translators gloss as a hartwort.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these preparations, each with its locus. Chapter 1-3 (line 3568): roots boiled with water, or pounded smooth without boiling, and taken as a drink; pounded with honey as a syrup; boiled for a hip bath; applied as a plaster to children's lower belly. No measure is given, only the warning that "more of a decoction than is suitable" causes a headache. Chapter 3-63 (line 7100): the seed taken in a drink; the green juice of stalk and seed "taken as a drink for ten days with as much as thirty grains of passum [raisin wine]"; the root licked in with honey. The thirty grains is a measure of the wine as printed (the translators' English), and the text does not say what is measured in grains. No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that the Apiaceae contain both aromatic edible members and very poisonous ones (hemlock, water-dropwort), and that a plant cannot be safely recognised from an ancient description alone. The identification is a translators' suggestion, and the second chapter describes a different plant from the first, so the chapters do not show that either describes *Athamanta meum*.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 1-3 prints that the roots are "smelling sweet and warming the tongue". Chapter 3-63 prints that the seed is "somewhat sharp and aromatic". No other warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality is printed for either.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What chapter 1-3 offers is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: the plant is told by its likeness to *anethum* and its greater thickness, by the stalk's height ("sometimes rising up to two feet"), by the thin, winding, straight, long roots, by their sweet smell and the warmth they leave on the tongue, and by its places (Macedonia and Spain). He also gives a plain warning of excess, a headache. Chapter 3-63 adds a place (the hill Amanus in Cilicia) and the form of the seed ("a little round double seed similar to little shields"). Neither chapter assigns the plant any status beyond that of a medicinal root or seed, records an amuletic use or names a deity. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapters gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The useful lesson is Dioscorides' method of fixing a plant by stalk, root, smell and place, and of recording the overdose along with the use. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Both chapters record emmenagogue uses (the menstrual flow is "drawn out" or "expelled") and a use for "diseases of the womb"; those are a risk in pregnancy. The chapter itself warns that too much of a decoction causes a headache. The carrot family includes some of the most poisonous plants of the temperate flora, and the identification here is a translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
