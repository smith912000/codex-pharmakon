# Ash salt — thal.tsha (Clark 1995); Thai tsha (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011); "artificial ash salt"

## *An artificial salt that the Men-Tsee-Khang note says is made by burning different kinds of wood; Clark prints it with horn salt as "both artificial salts of no fixed color" and says they cure cold disorders of the vessel organs. Identification: a wood-ash salt, an artificial alkaline salt obtained by burning wood, from the Men-Tsee-Khang note only; its chemistry is not given; confidence medium. The "kshara" in an earlier pre-fill is a Sanskrit term that does not occur in the Tibetan texts on disk and is not used* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry** in the sense that only the Four Tantras tradition, in two English renderings, names the substance. The two texts read are Barry Clark's 1995 *The Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20) and the Men-Tsee-Khang edition of 2011 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`). The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder, "ash salt", from `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 213 (the row is spelled "Thai tsha", the Men-Tsee-Khang phonetic form) and Clark line 932.

**rGyud-bzhi, Clark 1995 (FMT).** Chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (Clark line 792 onward), treats the powers of natural substances under eight heads (precious metals, stones, earths, trees, resins, plants, herbs, animal products). The salts are listed in a run at lines 927 to 935, after the resins (section 5, line 879) and before the myrobalans; the heading of the section they fall in is not legible in the e-text, so the Codex does not name it. The entry is at line 932: "rwa.tsha and thal.tsha: Horn Salt and Ash Salt: [are both artificial salts of no fixed color] cure cold disorders of the vessel organs." The Tibetan name *thal.tsha* is the second of two names given together in one entry, with *rwa.tsha* (horn salt); the two are glossed together and share the single action. In Clark's translation, material in square brackets is taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries (Clark line 82, translator's note), so the description "artificial salts of no fixed color" is commentary, and the action "cure cold disorders of the vessel organs" is the root text's. Clark's English "Ash Salt" is the translator's rendering of *thal.tsha*, and the Tibetan *thal* means ash (the stone-ash entry *rdo.thal*, line 854, is glossed "lit. 'stone ash'"). No colour, taste, origin or method of making is given in Clark's entry beyond "artificial".

**rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (FMT).** Four places. (1) The salty taste group (line 3211) lists "Gyam tsha, Gya tsha, Che nyang tsha, Ra tsha, Kharu tsha, Tsabru tsha, Ze tsha, Wa tsha, Zae tsha, Thai tsha, Shing tsha, Yabak khyara, Bultog and other similar substances" as the group having a salty taste. (2) The efficacy of medicinal substances (line 3400): "Ra tsha and Thai tsha cure cold disorders of the vessel organs." (3) The group of medicines that cure cold disorders associated with baekan (lines 3621 to 3626) includes "Tsabru tsha, Gya tsha, Gyam tsha, Ra tsha and Thai tsha". (4) The note to the salty-taste list (line 3288): "Thai tsha literally means artificial ash salt. It is made by burning different kinds of woods." That note is the only statement in the sources on disk of how the salt is made. A neighbouring note (line 3294) describes tree salt (*shing tsha*) as obtained from the decayed roots or a log of a pine tree, which is a different salt with its own row.

**The vessel organs.** Both texts use the phrase "vessel organs" without a gloss in the passages read. The Codex does not define it from outside the text. [USER: verify]

A search of the Sushruta, Dioscorides, Shennong and Ebers texts on disk for ash salt under this name found nothing, and none is claimed.

### Active Components

The sources name no constituent. In general terms, salts made by burning wood and leaching the ash are alkaline mixtures in which potassium carbonate is a main part, with other salts. Whether the *thal.tsha* of the Tibetan text is such a product cannot be shown from the note, which says only that it is made by burning woods. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the texts say it is for.** Curing cold disorders of the vessel organs (Clark 932; Men-Tsee-Khang 3400), and grouped among the medicines for cold disorders associated with *baekan* (Men-Tsee-Khang 3621 to 3626).
- **What the texts do not say.** No dose, no preparation beyond the note on its making, no frequency.

The Codex gives no protocol. Alkaline wood-ash preparations are caustic, and nothing here recommends taking or making one.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation given is the note on the salt itself: it is made by burning different kinds of woods (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3288). No method, wood or quantity is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any dose from this entry.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Wood ash leached with water gives an alkaline solution (lye) rich in potassium carbonate; such solutions are caustic to skin and gut. Salts in general are not interchangeable, and the Tibetan list distinguishes many. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011).** Thai tsha is listed in the salty-taste group (line 3211). The salty taste is described (line 3231) as one that "relaxes stiffness of the body, clears bowel obstructions and blocked energy channels", with heated-salt compresses that "induce sweating, generates body heat and improves appetite", and an excess that causes baldness, grey hair, wrinkles, loss of strength, thirst, leprosy and blood and *tripa* disorders. The same chapter says the salty taste pacifies *loong* and *baekan*, but that "with the exception of Che nyang tsha and Gyam tsha, all other salty substances aggravate tripa disorders" (line 3251). Ash salt is not one of the two exceptions. The post-digestive taste of the salty taste is sweet (line 3262). These are the taste's qualities and rules, and the entry for Thai tsha itself prints no separate taste or potency.
- **FMT (Clark 1995).** No taste or potency is printed in the entry (line 932).

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan medicine, rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* Ash salt has no cultivation status in the texts read; it is a therapeutic substance for cold disorders. The frame it sits inside is the tantra's own account of disease: Clark's text says that "the sole cause of all disease is said to be ignorance due to lack of understanding of the meaning of selflessness" (line 440), that "from ignorance arise the three poisons of attachment, hatred and closedmindedness whence are produced in turn the humors wind, bile and phlegm" (line 441), and that the humors are "rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration respectively" (line 51). A medicine such as this one acts on a humoral disorder, which the text places downstream of that root cause. No source retrieved assigns this salt a place in essence-extraction (*bcud len*) or any practice, and the Codex asserts none; there is no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice to place it in.

**Practitioner's note.** An artificial salt for cold disorders and nothing more in the record. A cultivator should not take it: no cultivation use is attested, and the text itself places the root of disease in the mind, not in a substance.

### Contraindications

- **Caustic when strong.** Wood-ash salts are alkaline. Do not swallow or apply a preparation of unknown strength.
- **Other salts.** The text says all salty substances except two aggravate *tripa* disorders and that excess salt does harm (Men-Tsee-Khang 3231, 3251). [USER: verify]
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children, heart, kidney and blood-pressure conditions.** No statement in the sources retrieved. Avoid. [USER: verify]
