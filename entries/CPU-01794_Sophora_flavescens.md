# Sophora flavescens — Ku Shen 苦參 (Shennong Bencao Jing)

## *Sophora flavescens, as the pharmaceutical Latin Yang 1998 prints for Ku Shen, "Radix Sophorae Flavescentis", matched to the binomial by stem in the extraction; Yang's note 161 gives the plant as "Sophora Flavescens"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Shennong text (SBJ); this entry rests on one text, the Shennong Bencao Jing in Yang Shou-zhong's 1998 translation, and on one entry in it.** The text is `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`. A search of the Dioscorides, Ebers, Assyrian, Sushruta and Tibetan text folders on disk for *Sophora* found only the Tibetan *Sophora moorcroftiana* (Clark 1995, line 1116, with the emetic class at line 1224) and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary's "Sophora hookeriana" and "Sophora moorcroftiana" (lines 5830 and 5398). Those are other species in another tradition, they have entries of their own (I_1359 and I_1360, the siblings of this batch), and none is read as Ku Shen. The Latin name is the translator's pharmaceutical Latin and was matched to the binomial by stem in the extraction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv`, line 118: "pharmaceutical Latin 'Radix Sophorae Flavescentis' matched by stem to on-disk binomial 'Sophora flavescens'"). The Chinese characters 苦參 are given in the Codex's own list (`_sources/shennong.csv`, line 156: "Sophora Root (苦參 Kǔ Shēn)") and in its reconstructed Chinese file (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/SBJ_Original_Chinese.md`, line 141, a reconstruction from Tao Hongjing's recension, cited here for the characters only); Yang's English text prints no characters. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** The row is keyed by the name as Yang prints it, *Ku Shen*, which is the first name of the entry and the only one the extraction records; the entry's two other names (below) have no row of their own. The locus in the extraction is "Herbs: Middle Class, PDF p.74 (line 2761)".

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *Ku Shen, PDF page 74.* The page marker `[[p74]]` is at line 2757. The entry begins at line 2761, after the end of the entry for Xuan Shen (whose last words, "brightens the eyes. Its other name is Chong Tai (Multi-storey Platform). It grows in rivers and valleys", open the page at lines 2759 to 2760). The entry reads, at lines 2761 to 2777:

"Ku Shen (Radix Sophorae Flavescentis)161 is bitter and cold. It mainly treats bound qi in the heart and abdomen, concretions and conglomerations, accumulations and gatherings, jaundice, and dribbling after voiding. It expels water, eliminates welling abscesses and swellings, supplements the center, brightens the eyes, and stops tearing. Its other name is Shui Huai (Water Scholartree). Another name is Ku Shi (Bitter Aniseed). It grows in mountains and valleys as well as fields."

(The printed line breaks, which the OCR splits one word to a line in the list of indications, are removed.) The entry gives the taste and nature, indications, actions, two other names with Yang's glosses, and the habitat. It states **no toxicity**: unlike the entry for Xuan Shen directly above it ("It is nontoxic"), it neither says "nontoxic" nor "toxic". The Codex does not translate the names further. The entries that follow on the same page are Gou Ji (printed "Gou Ii" in the OCR; "bitter and balanced", note 162) and Bei Xie ("bitter and balanced", note 163).

Yang's note 161 (lines 2786 to 2792) is the translator's modern commentary and not part of the classic. It reads: "Sophora Flavescens drains fire and dries dampness, supplements yin and boosts the essence, nourishes the liver and gallbladder, disinhibits the nine orifices, and quenches thirst. It treats dysentery, jaundice, reddish urine, malign sores, and invisible worm sores (i.e., vaginal ulcers). In the text, this medicinal is said to be able to supplement the center. This is because it is bitter in flavor and, as such, is able to dry dampness. The spleen is averse to dampness. When dampness is removed, the spleen is fortified." Most of the actions listed there, including "supplements yin and boosts the essence", are the commentator's and are **not** in the classic's entry; the note's last four sentences explain the classic's own phrase "supplements the center".

*The class headings.* Yang's table of contents prints "Herbs: Middle Class" (line 716). The section's opening heading is printed at line 2263 (`[[p59]]` at line 2262), the running head recurs on later pages (it is at line 2719 on page 73 and at line 2801 on page 75), and page 74 itself carries the book title in place of the class as its running head. The next section heading, "Herbs: Inferior Class", is at line 3157 (`[[p85]]` at line 3156). No other class heading intervenes between lines 2263 and 3157 (the running heads in this range all read "Herbs: Middle Class"). The preface (lines 385 to 390) says of the middle class: "There are 120 medium class medicinals which are used as ministers. They mainly nurture personality and correspond to humanity. They may or may not be toxic, and [therefore,] one should weigh and ponder before putting them to their appropriate use. If one intends to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion, one should base [one's efforts] on the middle class." Yang's commentary on it (lines 358 to 362) says: "medium grade or middle class medicinals are able to cultivate personality or modify temperament, as are, for example, He Huan (Cortex Albizziae Julibrissinis) and Xuan Cao (Radix Hemerocallis Fulvae). The former resolves anger, while the latter is able to help relieve worry. Since this class of medicinals may be toxic, their prescription requires care." His note on "personality" (lines 404 to 405) reads: "It was believed that some medicinals are effective for treating disease and, at the same time, are good for the cultivation of various virtues in human beings." The count printed in the preface (120) and the count in Yang's commentary (120 middle class, lines 365 to 366) agree.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Sophora flavescens*, *Ku Shen*, *Kushen*, *Shui Huai* and *Radix Sophorae* found no entry on this plant. I_784 (*Brucea javanica*) names a modern "Compound Kushen" injection in a cross-reference and is not an entry on this plant, and I_435 (*Ruta graveolens*) mentions *Sophora* only as a source of rutin. I_1359 (*Sophora hookeriana*) and I_1360 (*Sophora moorcroftiana*) are Tibetan entries of the same genus from the same batch. The Xuan Shen entry above Ku Shen on the same page is I_641 (tags CM, DMM, SBJ). Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Sophora* belongs to the legume family (Fabaceae) and that the roots of *Sophora flavescens* are known in the pharmacological literature to contain quinolizidine alkaloids, among them matrine and oxymatrine, together with flavonoids; this is a general statement and not a finding of the Shennong text. Which compounds the root named by the classic held is not shown by the lines read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record, with a continuing use of the root in Chinese medicine under the name Ku Shen, if Yang's note 161 is read as a description of the modern practice. The Shennong entry lists indications in its own terms (bound qi in the heart and abdomen, concretions and conglomerations, accumulations and gatherings, jaundice, dribbling after voiding) which are not modern diagnoses, and these conditions need medical care. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. The entry's silence on toxicity is not a statement that the root is safe, because the preface says the middle class "may or may not be toxic" and requires weighing, and nothing here supports gathering or using it.

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: the middle class (中品), by position.* Ku Shen stands in the section headed "Herbs: Middle Class" (heading at line 2263; running heads at lines 2719 and 2801 on the pages either side of its own; next section heading at line 3157). It is **not** directly under the heading: the heading is on page 59 and the entry on page 74, so the grade rests on its position within the section and not on standing beneath a heading. In the preface (lines 385 to 390) the middle class "mainly nurture[s] personality", "may or may not be toxic" and calls for weighing, and is the class on which one bases the effort "to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion". Yang's commentary (lines 358 to 362) says that middle class medicinals "cultivate personality or modify temperament" and need care because they may be toxic, and his note on "personality" (lines 404 to 405) says some are good both for treating disease and for "the cultivation of various virtues". The cultivation spec places the middle class between the upper grade (nourishing life, taken long-term) and the lower grade (attacking disease, short course), and the Codex's reconstructed Chinese preface (`SBJ_Original_Chinese.md`, line 17) names the middle class's charge with the character 性 (養性), the same ground. The entry's own wording is closest to the class's "supplement vacuity" in its phrase "supplements the center", and Yang's note 161 explains that phrase by the root's bitterness drying dampness, not by any nourishing action. The entry says nothing of long-term use, "making the body light", nourishing life, personality or temperament, or any change of jing, qi or shen, and none is asserted here. "Boosts the essence" is in Yang's modern note 161 only, not in the classic, and it is not carried into this register. The class position rests on the OCR text of Yang's headings and has not been checked against a page image. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** In the Chinese scheme this is a middle-grade drug: weighed, used for a defined complaint, and with no statement in its entry that it is for long use or that it is safe. The class's own warning ("may or may not be toxic ... weigh and ponder") is the operative one, and the entry gives no dose. The identity rests on a name matched by stem. Nothing here supports long-term use.

### Contraindications

The Shennong entry prints no warning and no statement of toxicity. The middle class is described in the preface as one that "may or may not be toxic" and that calls for weighing before use, and this is a general statement about the class and not about this plant. Concretions, jaundice, welling abscesses and urinary dribbling need medical care. The identity is a name matched by stem, so no root should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
