# Angelica glauca — Chawa (lca ba, "lea ba" in the OCR), as paired by column position in the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Angelica glauca, as the binomial printed in the scientific-name column of the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary; the Tibetan name beside it is "Chawa" by column position only, and no English common name can be matched to it. Clark 1995 does not print this binomial* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts, and under this binomial only by the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. Both Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP). The extraction note says: "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, row 249). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain, and the row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (Explanatory Tantra, glossary).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (heading at line 5049, page number 309 at line 5162) prints its columns in separate blocks, under the column headings "Phonetic", "Transliteration" and "Scientific Name" (lines 5051 to 5055) and "Common Name" (line 5148). The "C" block begins at line 5128. It prints eight phonetic names (lines 5130 to 5133): Chaga, Chaagtik, Champa, Chang tsher, Chawa, Che tsha, Chhuma tsi, Chhurugpa. It prints eight Tibetan transliterations (lines 5135 to 5140), in which the fifth reads "lea ba" (line 5138; the Codex reads this as an OCR form of *lca ba*, which is a reading from the sound and not stated in the text). It prints eight scientific names (lines 5142 to 5146): "Zingiber officinalis", "Gentianopsus paludosa", "Malva botka", "Cryptothladia polyphylla", "Angelica glauca" (line 5144, on one line with "Cryptothladia polyphylla" and "Ranunculus tanguticus"), "Ranunculus tanguticus", "Rheum nobile" and "Helerpestes cymbblar". "Angelica glauca" is the fifth of the eight scientific names, and "Chawa" is the fifth of the eight phonetic names. The pairing is by column position only. The glossary does not print them on one row, and the Codex does not state that it pairs them. [USER: verify]

The common-name block (lines 5149 to 5160) runs across the whole page and holds only six English names for the eight entries of the "C" block ("Ginger", "Iron coloured gentian", "Blue mallow", "Thistel", "Buttercup", "Himalayan rhubarb"). None of them can be matched to the fifth entry by position, so no English name is given here.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, the name "Chawa" in the running text.* The Explanatory Tantra text uses the name in four places and in its index. In the first part (the Root Tantra, "Basic Tantra", therapeutic methods) the passage on soups and medicinal butters (line 1116) says "Medicinal butter is prepared from Zati, Gokya, the three fruits, the five roots and Menchhen as the main ingredients", and a note (line 1144) reads: "The five roots are Ra nye, Nye shing, Chawa, Bhatu and Zema." In chapter 16, on dietetics, line 3046 reads "Cooked Chawa and Ra nye relieves baekan and loong disorders." In chapter 19 (tastes), line 3204 lists "Shingngar, Gundrum, Gurkum, Chugang, Dongga, Ra nye, Nye shing, Chawa, sugar, molasses, honey, meat, butter and other similar substances" as "the group of medicinal substances having a sweet taste". In chapter 20, in the efficacy of herbal medicines (the section begins at line 3428), line 3501 reads "Chawa cures disorders of the chhuser and cold disorders associated with the kidney and lower back." The index (line 7290) gives "Chawa 30, 33, 170, 186, 212". That the glossary's "Chawa" is the name in these lines is a reading from the identical spelling. The text does not say that the plant in these lines is *Angelica glauca*. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, the sounding name lcha.wa (line 1142).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, in the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads: "lcha .wa: Angelica archangelica: Angelica dahurica: Polygonatum odoratum (Mill) Druce var. plurtflorum: [has nine names, for example stong.nag, and grows on shady hills. The superior type has a leaf similar to a turquoise mandala, a white umbel flower and brown stalk. The common type has a brown stalk, white flower and is favored among the 'Five Roots.' Both types have hot, bitter and sweet tastes and] cure lymph disorders, cold disorders of the waist and kidneys, [cold phlegm diseases, stomach disorders, first stage oedema and wind diseases. ba.lang.lcha.wa has appearance and uses similar to the common type above. Applied as a fomentation it is beneficial in swellings and distention.]" Clark's preface (line 82) says "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". So the tastes, the description, the names and the further indications are the commentary layer. Outside the brackets are Clark's own identifications (the three Latin names, not root text) and the root-text clauses "cure lymph disorders, cold disorders of the waist and kidneys". Clark does not print *Angelica glauca*, and that the Men-Tsee-Khang "Chawa" is Clark's *lcha.wa* is a match of sound only. Clark's three Latin names are for the Tibetan drug and do not say which species the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary meant. [USER: verify]

Clark's preface (line 69) also says, of Himalayan materia medica in general, "the roots of Angelica, Polygonatum and Salep orchid have exceptional fortifying and rejuvenating properties". That is Clark's own remark about a genus, in the preface, and not a root-text line or a line on this species.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Angelica glauca*, *Chawa* and *lcha.wa* found no entry on this drug. I_010 (*Angelica sinensis*) and I_011 (*Angelica sinensis*) name *Angelica glauca* in their Sushruta paragraphs as a possible reading of *Choraka*; the Sushruta files on disk print *Choraka* (for example `SS_Sutra_ch38.txt` line 168) and no text on disk prints *Angelica glauca*, so that reading is not made here. Both entries carry an FMT tag; the Codex did not check on which line it rests. The glossary prints *Angelica sinensis* separately (line 5936). I_1230 (*Angelica dahurica*, Shennong) is the entry on one of the two other Angelica binomials that Clark prints for *lcha.wa*; it carries no FMT tag. None of them is an entry on this drug. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Angelica* is a genus of the carrot family (Apiaceae) and that roots of plants of this family are generally known to contain coumarins, including furanocoumarins. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the Tibetan drug, which may not be this species. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Men-Tsee-Khang text records, under the name "Chawa", a drug that cures disorders of *chhuser* and cold disorders of the kidney and lower back, that relieves *baekan* and *loong* disorders when cooked, and that belongs to the sweet-tasting group and to the "five roots". These are the text's claims, under a name that the glossary pairs with this binomial only by position, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give three preparations in which the name stands: a cooked dish (line 3046), the "five roots" in medicinal butter (lines 1116 and 1144), and, in Clark, grain beer made with "angelica root" (line 174) and a stew of "Angelica and Polygonatum cirrifolium" (line 702), both of which name the genus and not this species. No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identity of the Tibetan drug is uncertain, so a modern study of one *Angelica* would not bear directly on it. It is a general, well-established fact that furanocoumarins can make the skin more sensitive to sunlight, but no source read for this entry says that of this plant.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** The Men-Tsee-Khang text places "Chawa" in the sweet-tasting group (line 3204) and prints no power. Clark's *lcha.wa* line (1142) prints "hot, bitter and sweet tastes" inside the square brackets, so in the commentary layer, and only for the Clark drug. No other quality is printed for this binomial.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Men-Tsee-Khang materia medica classes "Chawa" by taste and by what it does: it belongs to the sweet-tasting group, to the "five roots", and to the medicines for *chhuser* and cold kidney and lower-back disorders. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. The chapter on maintaining health through rejuvenation (chapter 23, lines 3906 to 3942) was read for the names of the drugs it uses, and it does not name "Chawa". The only line that links the genus to rejuvenation is Clark's own preface (line 69), which is not a root-text line and names no species. No line read assigns the plant to *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Its framing is therapeutic and dietary only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The Tibetan name behind the binomial is a root of the "five roots" group and a cooked food in the Men-Tsee-Khang text, and the link to *Angelica glauca* rests on a column position. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. The identity rests on a column position and the species is not certain, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Plants of the carrot family that contain furanocoumarins can sensitise skin to sunlight, a general fact about the family and not a finding about this plant. Kidney and lower-back complaints need medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
