# Yellow Horned Poppy — Mekon keratites (Dioscorides 4-66), "Horned Poppy, Sea Poppy" (translator's gloss)

## *Glaucium flavum* (spine botanical; the translator's gloss for Dioscorides 4-66 lists "Glaucium flavum [in Sprague], Glaucium luteum — Horned Poppy, Sea Poppy") | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Identity warning.** Only the Dioscorides tag has a locus. The Assyrian and Avicenna tags have none (details below). A finished entry already exists for the neighbouring Dioscorides chapter 3-100 GLAUKION under *Glaucium corniculatum*, I_879 "Red Horned Poppy" (CPU-00758, tagged EP, AT, DMM, CM). Dioscorides himself separates the two: of 4-66 he says "Some have been deceived, thinking that glaucium [3-100] was made of this, because of the resemblance of the leaves." He treats them as different plants. The identification of 4-66 with *Glaucium flavum* comes from the translator's list and is credited there to Sprague. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 4-66, "MEKON KERATITES" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, text line 8602; the translator's gloss at line 8604 lists "Papaver corniculatum [Fuchs, Brunfels], Chelidonium glaucum [Linnaeus], Glaucium flavum [in Sprague], Glaucium luteum"; an engraving "Glaucium flavum after FAGUET — 1874" follows at line 8607). The description reads: "Papaver cornutum has rough white leaves similar to verbascum [4-104], jagged like a saw all around like those of wild poppy, with the stalk the same, the flower a pale yellow, with a little pod (bending like a horn) similar to that of fenugreek, from which it is named. The seed is small, black, similar to that of poppy. The thick black root grows on the surface of the ground. It grows in rough maritime places." Uses: "The root (boiled in water until half the amount remains and taken as a drink) is able to cure sciatica and liver disorders, and to help those who urinate thick or cobweb-like stuff. An acetabulum [vinegar cruet] of a decoction of the seed (taken as a drink with honey and water) purges the bowels gently, and the leaves and flowers (smeared on with oil) root out the crusts of ulcers. Rubbed on, it takes away argema [small white ulcer on the cornea] and small clouds in the eyes of beasts." He then warns: "In eating or drinking this same horned poppy the same symptoms occur as in the taking of opium [above], and they fall under the same remedies." The seed "is gathered dry in the summer", and "a decoction of the root is taken as a drink and it cures dysentery." Other names printed are paralion, agreste papaver, thalassium, pabulum marinum (Romans) and sisimaca (Africans).

### Active Components

Plants of the genus *Glaucium* are generally known to contain isoquinoline alkaloids, and *G. flavum* is commonly associated with glaucine. The content of the plant in Dioscorides' chapter was not read, and its identity is the translator's. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides' uses (root drink for sciatica and liver complaints, seed decoction as a gentle purge, leaves and flowers on ulcers) are recorded as his, and the Codex does not recommend them. His own warning that eating or drinking it brings on opium-like symptoms is the reason: this is not a plant for casual use.

### Preparation & Dosing

Dioscorides gives: the root boiled in water to half and drunk; "an acetabulum of a decoction of the seed (taken as a drink with honey and water)"; leaves and flowers smeared on with oil (4-66, line 8602). The only measure is the acetabulum, a vinegar-cruet volume, for the seed decoction. No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** *(c) Absent.* No attestation was found and none is asserted. The blocking warning on Assyrian identifications applies, and no tablet number or deity is given.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent* as to cultivation status. Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system. What he offers a cultivator is attention: he records the habitat ("rough maritime places"), the leaf, the horn-like pod, the black root, the season of gathering the seed ("dry in the summer"), and a confusion with glaucium that other people had made. He also records the poisoning warning plainly. Recording is not endorsing.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing to cultivate here in the Codex's sense. The one real account is a Greek therapeutic and descriptive one that itself warns of opium-like effects, and the Assyrian and Unani tags are unsupported.

### Contraindications

Dioscorides warns that eating or drinking this plant produces the same symptoms as opium (4-66). Plants of the genus are generally regarded as toxic, and the plant should not be eaten, brewed or applied to the eye on the strength of this entry. The plant is a coastal wild flower, and wild plants should not be collected for the purpose. Seek ordinary medical care for sciatica, liver disease, dysentery or any eye complaint.
