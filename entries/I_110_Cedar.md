### Botanical Identity
Large evergreen conifer 20–40m; horizontal tiered branches; needle-like leaves in rosettes; large upright cones; resin-saturated reddish-brown aromatic wood; native to the mountains of Lebanon, Turkey (*C. libani*), North Africa (*C. atlantica*), and the western Himalayas (*C. deodara*). The resin, essential oil (steam-distilled from wood), bark, and wood chips are all drug materials. Cedar was the pre-eminent tree of the ancient Mediterranean and Near East — used for ship timber, temple construction, and (critically for the Codex) as a medicine and embalming agent.

**Conservation**: Wild *Cedrus libani* forests are critically reduced — the famous Cedars of God at Bcharreh, Lebanon are a relict grove of ~375 trees, protected since 1876. Sustainable sourcing critical.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Dioscorides / Greco-Roman (DMM)
Book I, Chapter 105: "Cedar oil (*cedrium*) — most effective cedar oil comes from Phoenicia and Africa." Applications: preservative (noted that "insects and worms cannot harm what has been treated with it"); for skin parasites (scabies, mange); for alopecia (topical oil to scalp); in ear preparations; for leprous skin conditions; embalming/preservation.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine / Ibn Sīnā (CM)
*Arz Lubnān* — warming, drying. Applications: skin antiseptic and parasiticide; respiratory (aromatic inhalation); for nerve pain; topical joint and muscle pain.

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (EP)
**Cedar resin is the primary embalming agent of ancient Egypt** — the Ebers Papyrus and the Edwin Smith Surgical Papyrus both reference cedar preparations. Archaeological analysis of Egyptian mummies has confirmed the presence of *Cedrus* terpenoids in the embalming resin. Beyond embalming, EP compound medicines for skin conditions, ear problems, and wound treatment include cedar preparations.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)
The Cedar Forest of the Gilgamesh Epic — the sacred cedar forest guarded by Humbaba — was the source of prized timber and sacred resin. AT medical texts use cedar resin and oil extensively for fumigation (ritual and medical), skin conditions, and internal preparations. The *erēnu* (cedar) is one of the most frequently cited plants in AT medical literature.

### 2.5 Sushruta Samhita / Ayurveda (SS)
*Devadāru* (*Cedrus deodara*) — one of the important warming herbs in Ayurveda. Applications: Kapha and Vata reducing; for rheumatism, urinary disorders, skin diseases, respiratory conditions; as a diuretic; for fever. The Deodar cedar has its own specific Ayurvedic pharmacological profile distinct from the Lebanese variety but with significant overlap in chemical composition.

### 2.6 Four Medical Tantras (FMT)
Cedar wood and oil enter Tibetan medicine through Indian (Deodar) and Silk Road (Lebanese/Atlas) influences; used in compound formulas for cold-type conditions and skin disease.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Antimicrobial / Parasiticide (Primary)

**Cedrol + cedren + α-himachalene + deodarone**:
- Direct toxicity to Sarcoptes scabiei (scabies mite) — insecticidal sesquiterpenes disrupt mite nervous system
- Antibacterial (gram-positive emphasis: Staphylococcus, Streptococcus)
- Antifungal (Trichophyton, Candida — clinically relevant for skin fungal infections)
- Insect repellent (cedrol is the specific compound responsible for cedar's traditional use in chest/wardrobe storage to protect clothing from moths)

The skin parasiticide/antiseptic application across all 6 traditions is mechanistically grounded in the sesquiterpene antimicrobial activity.

### 5.2 Anti-Inflammatory / Analgesic

**β-Himachalene + himachalol + atlantone**:
- COX-2 inhibition
- NF-κB pathway modulation
- Analgesic in animal models (formalin test)
- The rheumatic/joint pain application in CM, AT, SS, and FMT has anti-inflammatory mechanistic support

### 5.3 Sedative / Anxiolytic

**Cedrol + himachalol**:
- GABA-A receptor modulation (similar to valerian's valerenic acid)
- Reduces locomotor activity in animal models (sedative)
- Inhalation of cedrol reduces anxiety-related behaviour in mice
- This may explain the use of cedar incense in ancient temple rituals — the aromatic sedative/anxiolytic effect created appropriate contemplative states

### 5.4 Embalming / Preservative (Historical)

**Cedar resin overall**: The ancient embalming mechanism was:
- Cedar oil's antimicrobial compounds (cedrol, cedren, himachalenes) destroy putrefactive bacteria
- The resin's physical properties create an anaerobic barrier around tissue
- Combined with natron (sodium bicarbonate) dehydration, the cedar resin coating was the chemical basis of Egyptian mummification's extraordinary longevity

The preservative efficacy is pharmacologically understood: the sesquiterpene antimicrobials in the resin are genuinely effective at preventing bacterial decomposition at the concentrations achievable by saturation of tissue.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Antimicrobial**: In vitro validation of cedar essential oil against dermatological pathogens; strong evidence base for topical antimicrobial. No large clinical RCTs for cedar oil specifically.

**Alopecia**: Cedar oil in combination (typically with rosemary, lavender, thyme) in a small RCT (Hay et al., 1998, *Archives of Dermatology*, n=84): significant improvement in alopecia areata in the active treatment group vs placebo. Cedar's specific contribution is unclear in the blend.

**Sedative (cedrol)**: Animal studies confirm CNS sedative effect; human study: inhalation of cedrol reduced salivary chromogranin A (stress marker) and pulse rate in a small human study (Kagawa et al., 2003). Not RCT scale.

**Embalming validity**: Quantitatively confirmed by mass spectrometry of Egyptian mummification samples — *Cedrus* sesquiterpenes (specifically himachalene series) are identifiable in 3,000-year-old mummy wrappings. This is one of the best-documented ancient pharmaceutical applications in the Codex.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The Cedar Forest and the first pharmaceutical war**: The Epic of Gilgamesh (~2100 BCE) — the oldest written literary narrative — includes the Cedar Forest episode: Gilgamesh travels to the Cedar Forest to cut timber, guarded by the monster Humbaba, who himself is associated with cedar's divine/sacred status. The timber of Cedrus libani was so valuable (for shipbuilding, temple construction, and aromatic/medicinal resin) that control of the cedar forests was a geopolitical objective. The forests of Lebanon were systematically stripped by Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Phoenician, and Roman civilisations. The current relict grove at Bcharreh (the "Cedars of God") is what remains of forests that once covered the entire Lebanese mountain range. The Codex's pharmaceutical use of cedar (embalming, antimicrobial, aromatic) was one of the economic drivers of the first recorded environmental degradation caused by human pharmacological demand.

**The smell of eternal life**: Cedar resin (*cedrium*) was described by Dioscorides as "what insects and worms cannot harm" — a preservative against decay. Applied to papyrus scrolls, wooden objects, and to human remains, the antimicrobial sesquiterpenes created a genuine anti-putrefaction barrier. The Egyptians, recognising this property empirically, made cedar resin central to mummification — the process aimed at preserving the body for eternal life. The pharmacological mechanism (sesquiterpene antimicrobials) was the chemical basis of the theological aspiration (eternal life through preservation). The smell of the cedar — the aromatic sesquiterpenes — was literally the smell of immortality in ancient Egyptian culture.

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Bitter, warm. Channels: Lung (肺), Bladder (膀胱). Antimicrobial, astringent, expectorant.

**Ayurvedic**: Bitter, pungent; heating. Reduces Kapha ↓↓, Vata ↓. *Kandughna* (antipruritic), *Tvagdoṣahara* (skin disease remover), *Mūtralā* (diuretic). Devadaru is a classical Ayurvedic drug in the *mahākaṣāya* group (major decoctions group).

**Tibetan (FMT)**: Hot, rough. Pacifies *Badkan* and *rLung*. Used in warming formulas.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Pregnancy (internal use)**: Emmenagogue potential from sesquiterpene content; topical at standard aromatherapy dilutions likely safe; avoid internal use
- **Topical concentration**: Never apply undiluted cedar essential oil to skin — irritant at >5% concentration
- **Kidney disease (internal Ayurvedic preparations)**: Diuretic + irritant effect — caution in CKD
- **Sustainability**: Prefer *C. atlantica* (cultivated commercial source) or certified deodar sources over wild Lebanese cedar; wild populations are critically reduced
