# Teucrium scordium — Skordion (Dioscorides 3-125); "Garlic Germander, Water Germander"

## *Teucrium scordium [Linnaeus], the name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-125 (which also prints "Scordium [Fuchs]"), glossed "Garlic Germander, Water Germander"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk lists the plant under this name (the name indexes in `Sources/*/extract/names.csv` were searched). The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *3-125. SKORDION* at line 7658, SUGGESTED line at 7659, text at line 7660. A page number (511) and the running header "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants" fall inside the chapter (lines 7661 and 7663), and the text resumes at line 7664 ("to a scar."). The chapter stands in Book Three, after *3-124. POLION* (line 7655, the chapter of I_716) and before *3-126. BECHION* (line 7665). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 531, and the translators' indexes print "Teucrium scordium 511" (line 17860) and "Scordium 511" (line 17465). A woodcut caption, "Scordium from FUCHS — 1545", stands on a different page (line 7374, page 473), which is why one index also prints "Scordium 473" (line 11893).

The whole chapter reads: "Scordium grows in marshy, mountainous places. It has leaves similar to chamedrys but bigger and not as cut-in around the circumference. It resembles garlic in its smell somewhat, and is astringent and bitter to the taste. Pale red flowers grow from the little four-cornered stalks. The pounded herb (green or dry) is warming and diuretic given in drink. Boiled with wine it is good for snakebite and poisons. For pangs of hunger in the stomach, dysentery, and frequent painful urination give two teaspoonfuls with honey water. It expels purulent thicknesses out of the chest. It helps old coughs, hernias, and convulsions mixed dry in a linctus [syrup] with nasturtium [2-185], honey and rosin. Used in a stiff ointment it relaxes hypochondrium [nervous gastric disorder] with long-lasting inflammation. Smeared on with sharp vinegar (or applied with water) it is good for gout. Applied, it induces the menstrual flow, and heals wounds. With honey it cleans old ulcers and brings them to a scar. Used dry, it restrains abnormal growths of the flesh. The juice is taken as a drink for the same sores. The most effective is the Pontic and Cretan. It is also called scorbium, pleuritis, dysosmon, calamintha sylvestris, chamedrys, or mithridanios; the Magi call it sanguis podotis, the Egyptians, apho, and the Romans, trisago palustris."

The chapter is organised by route. The description comes first: marshy, mountainous places; leaves like *chamedrys* but "bigger and not as cut-in around the circumference"; a smell that "resembles garlic ... somewhat"; a taste "astringent and bitter"; pale red flowers on "little four-cornered stalks". Taken by mouth, the pounded herb, green or dry, is "warming and diuretic". Boiled with wine it is "good for snakebite and poisons". Two teaspoonfuls with honey water are given for "pangs of hunger in the stomach, dysentery, and frequent painful urination". It "expels purulent thicknesses out of the chest" and, mixed dry in a linctus with nasturtium, honey and rosin, "helps old coughs, hernias, and convulsions". In a stiff ointment it "relaxes hypochondrium" with long-lasting inflammation. Smeared on with sharp vinegar, or applied with water, it is "good for gout". Applied, it "induces the menstrual flow, and heals wounds". With honey it "cleans old ulcers and brings them to a scar", and dry it "restrains abnormal growths of the flesh". The juice is drunk "for the same sores". The provenance printed is "The most effective is the Pontic and Cretan". The names printed include *chamedrys* and *calamintha sylvestris* (names that also belong to other chapters), *pleuritis*, the "Magi" name *sanguis podotis*, the Egyptian *apho* and the Roman *trisago palustris*. The last three are names recorded by Dioscorides and not texts of those peoples.

**Overlap.** A chapter-level search of the entries folder for 3-125, the Greek heading, *Teucrium scordium* and *Water Germander* found no entry on this chapter. Three entries touch it by name. I_712 (*Teucrium scorodonia*, wood sage) summarises *skordion* in one sentence without a chapter number (its line 17), and I_740 (*Teucrium fruticans*) lists *T. scordium* as a species not to be substituted for the other germanders (its line 103). The name *chamedrys*, printed here among the other names, is also the name of a separate chapter of the text and of the entry I_654. The chapter 3-124 *POLION* next to it is the chapter of I_716. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. It is a general fact that *Teucrium* (the germanders) belongs to the mint family (Lamiaceae) and that its species are generally aromatic and bitter. The text's "garlic" smell is a sensory note and not a statement of composition. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a broad list of uses (poisons and snakebite, dysentery and painful urination, chest, cough, hernia and convulsions, gout, wounds and ulcers, and bringing on the menstrual flow). These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. Snakebite and poisoning are emergencies, and nothing here replaces emergency care. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives several preparations. Internal: the pounded herb "(green or dry)" in a drink; the herb "boiled with wine"; "two teaspoonfuls with honey water" for hunger pangs of the stomach, dysentery and frequent painful urination; a dry mixture in a linctus with nasturtium, honey and rosin; and the juice "taken as a drink". External: a stiff ointment; smeared on with sharp vinegar or applied with water; with honey on old ulcers; dry on abnormal growths of the flesh. The only quantity is the two teaspoonfuls, and the chapter prints no frequency or duration. For every other preparation no dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that some germander products (taken by mouth, in the species commonly sold as medicine) have been linked to liver injury, and that snakebite, poisoning and dysentery need medical care. Nothing is stated about the effect of *Teucrium scordium* in particular.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 3-125 prints the qualities "astringent and bitter to the taste" and says the pounded herb "is warming and diuretic given in drink". It prints no moist or dry quality.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is the fullest set in the batch: a habitat (marshy, mountainous places), a comparison for recognition (the leaves against *chamedrys*, bigger and less cut-in), a sensory assay (a smell somewhat like garlic, an astringent and bitter taste), the shape of the stalk (four-cornered), the colour of the flower, a statement of the best provenance (Pontic and Cretan), and the use of the herb green or dry. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is the combination of a smell check, a taste check and a stalk check to recognise a plant, and the note that some supplies are better than others. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 3-125 prints no warning, and it includes an internal use for snakebite and poisons that the Codex does not endorse. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Germanders as a group have been linked to liver harm when taken by mouth. The chapter also brings on the menstrual flow, so pregnancy is an obvious question that the text does not answer. The text gives one quantity and no safety information for children or for any medicine taken at the same time. [USER: verify]
