# I-315: Symphytum officinale (Comfrey)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-315 | Boraginaceae**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Symphytum officinale* L.
**Family:** Boraginaceae

**Common Names:**
- English: Comfrey, Common Comfrey, Knitbone, Boneset, Bruisewort, Healing Herb, Slippery Root
- German: *Beinwell* ("bone well/healer"), *Schwarzwurz* ("black root"), *Wallwurz* ("wall root")
- French: *Consoude* — from Latin *consolidare* (to consolidate/heal); same etymological root as *Solidago* (I-306)
- Latin: *Symphytum* — from Greek *symphyo* (to grow together) — directly encoding bone-healing in genus name
- Arabic: *Ushbat al-Iqnā'* / historically *Humhum* in some classical texts

**Etymology:**
*Symphytum* from Greek *symphyein* — "to grow together, to unite, to knit" — the most pharmacologically explicit genus name in the Codex for bone and tissue healing. *Beinwell* (German) encodes bone-healing in both elements: *Bein* (bone/leg) + *well* (spring, source, healer). *Consoude* (French) and *consolidare* (Latin root) both mean "to consolidate" — wound consolidation and bone knitting. The common names "knitbone" and "boneset" complete the set — the plant accumulated pharmacologically explicit names in five languages independently, all encoding the same therapeutic action.

**CRITICAL NOTE — Internal Use:**
*Symphytum officinale* was used internally for centuries across all seven documented traditions. The **Commission E withdrew approval for all internal comfrey preparations in 1990** following recognition of serious pyrrolizidine alkaloid (PA) hepatotoxicity. This is a case where a millennia-old internal use was definitively contraindicated by modern toxicology. **INTERNAL USE IS CONTRAINDICATED.** Topical use remains Commission E approved and is the sole safe contemporary application.

**Morphology:**
Perennial; 30–120 cm; large rough-hairy basal leaves; drooping clusters of tubular flowers (cream, purple, or pink); distinctive black root exterior / white interior (hence *Schwarzwurz*); very deep taproot (2+ m), extremely difficult to eradicate once established.

**Parts Used (Contemporary):**
- Root and rhizome (topical preparations only): higher allantoin + higher PA content
- Aerial parts / leaves (topical preparations only): lower PA content; used in poultices

**The Pyrrolizidine Reversal:**
Comfrey's trajectory represents one of the most dramatic pharmacological reversals in the modern Codex — from internally approved across seven traditions over 2,000 years to Commission E contraindication for internal use in a single decade (1980s–1990s). The PA hepatotoxicity data from animal models and human case reports (veno-occlusive disease) forced recognition that the internal analgesic and respiratory uses, however well-documented traditionally, carried unacceptable hepatic risk. The topical indication — where allantoin provides genuine wound-healing benefit without systemic PA absorption through intact skin — survived the reversal.

**The Allantoin Mechanism:**
Comfrey's topical activity is dominated by allantoin (1.0–2.5% dry root) — one of the few plant compounds with genuinely documented cell-proliferation-stimulating activity. Allantoin promotes fibroblast and keratinocyte proliferation, accelerating wound closure and tissue repair. This mechanism distinguishes comfrey from anti-inflammatory herbs: it does not merely reduce inflammation at the wound site but actively accelerates cellular repair. The genus name *Symphytum* (grow together) was pharmacologically accurate — the plant does cause tissue to "grow together," and modern pharmacology confirms the mechanism.

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Primary Active Compounds

**1. Allantoin (1.0–2.5% root; 0.3–0.8% leaves) — PRIMARY therapeutic compound**
- Diureide of glyoxylic acid; derived from purine catabolism
- **Cell proliferation stimulant:** Promotes fibroblast + keratinocyte division → accelerated wound closure
- **Epithelializing:** Promotes re-epithelialization of wounds
- **Anti-inflammatory:** Secondary; reduces inflammatory cytokines at wound site
- TOPICALLY SAFE: absorbed minimally through intact skin; low systemic bioavailability topically

**2. Rosmarinic Acid (3–5% leaves; 0.5–1% root)**
- Caffeic acid ester; potent anti-inflammatory
- **COX-1/2 inhibition + 5-LOX inhibition** → reduced prostaglandins + leukotrienes → anti-inflammatory
- Antioxidant; anti-viral
- Shared with rosemary (I-253), lemon balm (I-267), sage (I-252)

**3. Mucilage (polysaccharides)** — demulcent; wound soothing; historically justified internal respiratory use (now contraindicated)

### TOXIC COMPOUNDS

**4. Pyrrolizidine Alkaloids (PAs) — 0.02–0.15% root; 0.01–0.04% leaves**
- Symphytine, Echimidine, Intermedine, Lasiocarpine, 7-Acetylintermedine
- **Hepatotoxic:** CYP3A4-mediated bioactivation → pyrrole intermediates → DNA crosslinking → hepatocyte necrosis
- **Hepatic veno-occlusive disease (HVOD):** Progressive sinusoidal obstruction → hepatic failure; well-documented in humans and animals
- **Carcinogenic:** PA-derived pyrroles are mutagens; hepatocellular carcinoma in animal models
- Dose-dependent; cumulative; threshold uncertain
- **DESTROYED BY NEITHER COOKING NOR DRYING** — unlike sambunigrin in elder
- Root: highest PA concentration; leaves: lower

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### Wound Healing (Topical)
- **Allantoin → fibroblast proliferation** → increased collagen synthesis → faster wound matrix formation
- **Allantoin → keratinocyte proliferation** → re-epithelialization of wound surface
- **Rosmarinic acid → COX + 5-LOX inhibition** → reduced inflammatory mediators → less wound inflammation
- Combined: cell proliferation (allantoin) + anti-inflammation (rosmarinic acid) = biologically plausible accelerated wound healing

### Hepatotoxicity (Systemic PAs)
- **Symphytine + echimidine → CYP3A4 N-oxidation** → unstable pyrrolic esters → spontaneous dehydrogenation to reactive pyrroles
- **Pyrroles → covalent DNA + protein alkylation** → hepatocyte death + endothelial cell injury
- **Sinusoidal endothelial cell damage → hepatic vein occlusion** → congestion → hepatic failure (HVOD)
- **Cumulative:** Low chronic doses accumulate; not just acute high-dose risk
- **Transdermal absorption (topical):** Through intact skin: minimal; through damaged skin or open wounds: elevated — hence AVOID on broken skin

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Topical Wound Healing / Bruises / Sprains:**
- **Koll et al. 2004** (*Phytomedicine*): RCT; comfrey root extract cream vs. diclofenac gel for ankle sprain; n=164; comfrey superior to diclofenac on pain + swelling + mobility outcomes
- **Predel et al. 2005** (*Phytomedicine*): RCT; comfrey vs. diclofenac for ankle sprain; similar findings
- **Staiger 2012** (*Phytotherapy Research*): systematic review confirming topical comfrey efficacy for musculoskeletal pain and wounds
- German Commission E approved (revised): topical use for bruises, sprains, muscle pain
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (multiple RCTs; systematic review)

**Topical Anti-Inflammatory:**
- Commission E approved; mechanism-supported
- **Evidence Grade: Level B**

**Internal Use (Historical):**
- Commission E withdrew approval 1990
- FDA issued warning against internal comfrey products 2001 (US)
- UK, Australia, Canada, Germany: all have restricted or banned internal use
- **Internal Use: CONTRAINDICATED — PA hepatotoxicity**

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The Knitbone Experience:**
The traditional experience of applying fresh comfrey poultice to a sprain or bruise is one of the most immediately tangible in traditional medicine — a rapid reduction in swelling and pain that traditional healers attributed to the plant "knitting" the tissue. Modern RCT evidence confirms this experience: comfrey topical preparations outperform standard NSAID gels for acute ankle sprains. The plant's common name (knitbone), genus name (*Symphytum* — grow together), and Commission E approval converge on a single pharmacologically validated action — making this a case where folk experience, etymology, and modern clinical evidence are in complete alignment.

**The PA Betrayal:**
Comfrey's internal tradition lasted 2,000 years and was supported by seven pharmacological traditions. The PA hepatotoxicity revelation in the 1980s–1990s was not intuitive — the symptoms of HVOD (hepatic veno-occlusive disease) develop slowly, often over years of use, and were historically attributed to other causes. The traditional use was genuine (mucilage for respiratory; allantoin internally for healing) but pharmacologically contaminated by the PA load. The internal tradition had to be abandoned not because it was entirely ineffective, but because the therapeutic benefit was outweighed by cumulative hepatic risk. This represents a pharmacological tragedy: a genuinely efficacious internal medicine deprecated by a co-present toxin.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Contemporary Use — TOPICAL ONLY

**Commission E Approved Topical Preparations:**
- **Comfrey root extract cream/ointment:** PA-controlled preparations (Kytta® range, Germany); standardized to PA content ≤1 ppm per daily dose
- Apply to unbroken skin only: 3–4× daily; maximum 4–6 weeks per course
- **NEVER apply to open wounds, broken skin, or mucous membranes** — increased PA transdermal absorption

**Fresh Poultice (Traditional):**
- Fresh comfrey leaves mashed to paste; apply to bruise/sprain through cloth barrier
- Short-term only (days, not weeks); intact skin only

**Duration Limits:**
- Maximum 4–6 weeks continuous use (EU HMPC recommendation)
- Maximum 100 μg PA daily systemic dose (ESCOP guideline)

### Safety Profile

**INTERNAL USE: ABSOLUTELY CONTRAINDICATED.**
**TOPICAL USE: Safe with precautions.**

**Internal PA Hepatotoxicity:**
- HVOD documented in humans consuming comfrey tea, comfrey tablets
- Hepatocellular carcinoma in animal models
- **Commission E 1990 withdrawal: internal preparations prohibited in Germany**
- **FDA 2001 advisory: internal comfrey products removed from US market**
- UK MHRA: internal products prohibited

**Topical Safety Rules:**
- ONLY on intact, unbroken skin
- AVOID during pregnancy — mutagenic/embryotoxic PA risk (even topical, precautionary)
- AVOID breastfeeding
- AVOID in children <2 years
- Maximum duration: 4–6 weeks per course; max 10 weeks/year total
- Choose PA-controlled standardized preparations (Kytta®, Traumaplant®)
