# I-314: Sambucus nigra (Elderberry / Elderflower)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-314 | Adoxaceae (formerly Caprifoliaceae)**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Sambucus nigra* L.
**Family:** Adoxaceae (reclassified from Caprifoliaceae per APG IV)

**Common Names:**
- English: Elder, Elderberry (fruit), Elderflower (flower)
- German: *Schwarzer Holunder* (black elder), *Holunder*; *Flieder* (dialectal)
- French: *Sureau noir*, *Sambuc*
- Latin: *Sambucus* — possibly from Greek *sambuke* (a musical instrument made from elder wood) or from a Thracian word
- Arabic: *Khazz al-aswad* (خز الأسود) / *Yābisān*
- Welsh: *Ysgawen* — tree of ancient Celtic mythology; "elder mother" (*Hyldemoer*) in Scandinavian tradition

**Etymology:**
*Sambucus* — the ancient name, traced possibly to the *sambuca* (a harp or triangular stringed instrument) whose soundbox was made from elder wood — encoding the plant's non-pharmacological utility in the genus name. *nigra* = "black" — referring to the ripe black-purple berries. The Germanic *Holunder* derives from Proto-Germanic *holantreo* ("hollow tree") — reflecting elder wood's characteristic hollow pith (historically used to make pipes, bellows, and toys).

**The Elder in European Folk Tradition:**
Elder occupies a uniquely liminal position in Northern and Central European folk culture — simultaneously feared and revered. The "Elder Mother" (*Hyldemoer* in Danish; *Frau Holle* cognate in German mythology) was a spirit said to inhabit elder trees. Cutting elder without permission was taboo; elder planted near homes was protective. This reverence had partial pharmacological basis — elder is simultaneously highly useful (flowers: antiviral; berries: immunostimulant) and potentially dangerous (raw berries, bark, leaves: cyanogenic). The folk reverence encoded both utility and toxicity warnings in mythological form.

**Raw Plant Toxicity:**
- **Raw berries:** contain *sambunigrin* (cyanogenic glycoside) → hydrolyzes to HCN + benzaldehyde → GI upset (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea) at sufficient raw doses. Cooking destroys sambunigrin.
- **Bark, leaves, roots:** Higher cyanogenic glycoside content; historically used as purgatives + emetics at dangerous doses. NOT safe.
- **Ripe cooked berries and elderflower preparations are safe** — cooking or drying eliminates sambunigrin.

**Morphology:**
Deciduous shrub or small tree; 3–10 m; pinnate leaves (5–7 leaflets); flat corymbs of small white fragrant flowers (elderflower); dark purple-black berries (elderberry) in drooping clusters; hollow pithy stems; common in European hedgerows and North American habitats.

**Parts Used:**
- Flowers (elderflower): fresh or dried; tea; tincture; syrup — **anti-catarrhal; diuretic; Commission E approved**
- Berries (elderberry): cooked, dried, extract; syrup — **antiviral; immunostimulant; Level A influenza**
- Bark, leaves, roots: **NOT RECOMMENDED** — cyanogenic; historically toxic

**The Hippocratic Elder:**
Hippocrates reportedly called elder "the medicine chest of the country people" — a statement (apocryphal but historically attributed) that accurately encodes elderberry's position as the universal medicine in pre-industrial European village medicine. The flowers were used for fevers and colds; the berries for winter immunity and rheumatism; the bark as purgative and emetic. The elder tree standing in the cottage garden represented a complete seasonal pharmacy: spring flowers for hay fever and colds; summer flowers for elderflower water and cordials; autumn berries for winter immune preparations. This integrated seasonal use is among the most pharmacologically sophisticated examples of traditional plant pharmacy in the European tradition.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domains:**

1. **Anti-viral / anti-influenza (berry)** — European folk; modern RCTs; Level A; hemagglutinin binding mechanism
2. **Anti-catarrhal / upper respiratory (flower)** — DMM, CM, AT, European folk; Commission E approved
3. **Diuretic (flower)** — DMM, CM, EP; flavonoid mechanism
4. **Anti-inflammatory** — DMM, CM; anthocyanin + flavonoid mechanism
5. **Immunomodulatory (berry)** — folk tradition; cytokine induction; modern validation

**The Flower vs. Berry Dichotomy:**
Elder is among the few medicinal plants where flowers and fruits have different primary indications validated by separate clinical evidence streams:
- **Elderflower:** Commission E approved for "upper respiratory catarrh" — diaphoretic, anti-catarrhal, mild diuretic
- **Elderberry:** Multiple RCTs demonstrating shortened duration and reduced severity of influenza — antiviral mechanism via neuraminidase inhibition + hemagglutinin binding

This pharmacological duality means elder provides both prophylaxis (flower immune modulation) and acute treatment (berry antiviral) within a single plant — explaining the "medicine chest" characterization.

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Berry — Anthocyanins

**1. Cyanidin-3-glucoside — PRIMARY berry compound**
- Major anthocyanin (75–88% total anthocyanin content)
- **Anti-influenza:** Binds hemagglutinin on influenza surface → inhibits viral cell attachment
- **Neuraminidase inhibition:** Prevents viral release from infected cells (same target as oseltamivir/Tamiflu)
- **Antioxidant:** High ORAC value; radical scavenging
- **Pro-inflammatory cytokine induction:** IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, IL-12 (immunostimulant effect)

**2. Cyanidin-3-sambubioside** — second major anthocyanin; similar mechanism

**3. Cyanidin-3,5-diglucoside** — minor anthocyanin

### Berry — Polyphenols

**4. Chlorogenic Acid** — antioxidant; anti-viral
**5. Quercetin, Rutin** — anti-inflammatory; anti-viral; flavonols
**6. Sambucin** — phenolic; antibacterial

### Flower — Flavonoids

**7. Rutin (Quercetin-3-rutinoside)** — diuretic; capillary-protective; anti-inflammatory
**8. Isoquercitrin** — anti-inflammatory; diuretic
**9. Astragalin (Kaempferol-3-glucoside)** — anti-inflammatory

### Flower — Triterpenes

**10. α-Amyrin, β-Amyrin, Ursolic Acid** — anti-inflammatory; antimicrobial

### Cyanogenic Glycosides (RAW PLANT ONLY)

**11. Sambunigrin (raw berries/leaves/bark)** — cyanic glycoside → HCN on hydrolysis; **DESTROYED BY COOKING**

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### Anti-Influenza (Berry)
- **Cyanidin-3-glucoside + cyanidin-3-sambubioside → hemagglutinin binding** → blocks viral attachment to respiratory epithelium
- **Flavonoid → influenza neuraminidase inhibition** → prevents viral release from cells → reduced spread
- **Cytokine induction (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-12)** → enhanced innate immune response
- Compared to oseltamivir: elder works via hemagglutinin (entry inhibition) + neuraminidase (exit inhibition); oseltamivir works only via neuraminidase. Mechanistically complementary.

### Anti-Catarrhal / Diuretic (Flower)
- **Rutin + isoquercitrin → K⁺ channel modulation** → mild aquaresis
- **Triterpenes → anti-inflammatory** → reduces nasal/sinus mucosal edema
- **Diaphoretic effect:** Elder flower has traditional fever-reducing use; mechanism: mild peripheral vasodilation → heat dissipation

### Immunomodulatory
- **Cyanidin anthocyanins + polyphenols → TLR-mediated innate immune activation** → enhanced macrophage phagocytosis; NK cell activation

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Influenza (Berry Extract):**
- **Zakay-Rones et al. 1995** (*Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine*): RCT; elderberry extract vs. placebo in influenza B outbreak; n=27; recovery 2.4 days shorter in elderberry group; 90% recovery within 3 days vs. 6 days in placebo
- **Zakay-Rones et al. 2004** (*Journal of International Medical Research*): RCT; n=60; influenza A or B; Sambucol® 4× daily for 5 days; 4-day shorter recovery; 56% reduction in symptom duration
- **Vlachojannis et al. 2010** (Cochrane-related meta-analysis); **Randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial (Tiralongo et al. 2016 Nutrients, air travel URI):** consistent positive direction
- Meta-analysis (Hawkins et al. 2019): confirmed significant reduction in influenza duration and severity
- **Evidence Grade: Level A** (multiple RCTs; consistent direction; meta-analysis)

**Upper Respiratory / Anti-Catarrhal (Flower):**
- German Commission E approved for "colds, influenza-like infections, and fevers"
- ESCOP monograph: anti-catarrhal; diaphoretic
- No large RCTs specifically for elderflower; traditional use supported
- **Evidence Grade: Level C** (Commission E/ESCOP approved)

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The Elder Mother (*Hyldemoer*):**
Scandinavian and Germanic folk belief held that every elder tree was inhabited by an elder spirit (*Hyldemoer* — elder mother in Danish). To cut elder without asking permission was to risk illness or death. The taboo had a partial pharmacological basis: cutting elder releases benzaldehyde and hydrogen cyanide (from sambunigrin in bark and leaves), which have noticeable bitter-almond odors and are genuinely toxic. People who violated elder trees may actually have experienced mild cyanide exposure symptoms — dizziness, headache — which would have reinforced the belief in spiritual retribution. Folk taboo and toxicology converged into mythology.

**Elderflower Cordial:**
The annual British elderflower harvest (late May/June) remains a cultural event — foragers collecting fragrant white flower corymbs before the berries set. Elderflower cordial (flower heads steeped in sugar water with lemon) and elderflower champagne (slightly fermented) are distinctly English seasonal preparations. The flavor — delicately floral, distinctly *Sambucus* — is produced by hotrienol and other linalool-related volatiles plus the flavonoids. The pharmacological anti-catarrhal compounds are present in the cordial; traditional use as spring tonic/hay fever remedy has genuine mechanistic backing.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Elderberry Syrup (Anti-Influenza):**
- Sambucol® standardized extract: 15 mL 4× daily for 5 days (clinical trial dose)
- Homemade: 400 g ripe elderberries + 500 mL water; simmer 20 minutes (destroys sambunigrin); add honey/ginger; 1–3 tbsp daily
- **CRITICAL: Cook berries; do not use raw**

**Elderflower Tea (Anti-Catarrhal):**
- Commission E standard: 3–5 g dried elderflower per 250 mL boiling water; steep 10 minutes; 2–3 cups daily
- For acute cold: begin at symptom onset; continue 5–7 days

**Prevention:**
- Daily elderberry preparation during influenza season; no established optimal dose for prevention (clinical data primarily from treatment)

### Safety Profile

**Safe when properly prepared.**

**RAW BERRY / BARK / LEAF — TOXIC:**
- Sambunigrin cyanogenic glycoside → nausea, vomiting, diarrhea; potentially serious at large doses
- **Never consume raw elderberries in quantity; never consume bark or leaves medicinally**
- Cooking (minimum 15–20 minutes) or drying thoroughly destroys sambunigrin

**Immune Stimulation Concern:**
- Elderberry's cytokine-inducing activity raises theoretical concern in autoimmune conditions
- Theoretical: TNF-α, IL-6 induction may worsen autoimmune flares (rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn's)
- Clinical evidence for this harm is absent; caution is theoretical
- AVOID in patients on immunosuppressive therapy (transplant, active autoimmune disease)

**Drug Interactions:**
- Immunosuppressants (cyclosporine, tacrolimus): theoretical antagonism via immunostimulation
- Diuretics: additive (elderflower diuretic)
- Antiviral drugs: theoretically complementary; no interaction evidence

**Contraindications:**
- Raw or undercooked berries, bark, or leaves
- Active autoimmune disease (theoretical; immunostimulant)
- Immunosuppressive therapy (theoretical)
- Pregnancy at medicinal doses: insufficient data; food doses likely safe
