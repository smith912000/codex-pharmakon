# Geode — Geodes Lithos (as printed in the 2000 English edition of Dioscorides)
## *"Geodes: nodular stones with crystalline cavities", the translator's gloss under the heading 5-169 GEODES LITHOS; no species, locality or colour is given by the text itself* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus for this entry is *De Materia Medica* 5-169 *Geodes Lithos* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, line 10707; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row "5-169 (line 10707)"; `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 895, class mineral, "id_uncertain" marked yes). It sits in Book 5, among the stones, between 5-168 *Akone* (whetstone, line 10703) and 5-170 *Ge* (earth, line 10711). The translator's gloss, printed under the heading, is "Geodes: nodular stones with crystalline cavities" (line 10709). Only one name is printed; the other Greek names that Dioscorides gives for many of his materials are not printed for this one.

The whole chapter is two sentences (line 10710): "Geodes are astringent and drying, and take away things that darken the eyes. Smeared on [ground up] with water they soothe inflammation of the breasts, and that from stones [urinary, kidney]." The words in square brackets are the translator's, not Dioscorides'. The second clause is compressed in the printed text: it can be read as inflammation "from stones", and the translator has resolved "stones" as urinary or kidney stones. This entry reports the sentence as printed and does not choose a reading beyond the translator's. [USER: verify against the Greek]

The chapter gives no source place for the stone, no colour, no size, no test of a genuine piece, and no way of preparing it beyond the translator's bracket "[ground up]". Dioscorides' longer mineral chapters (for example 5-85 on *pompholyx*, line 10228) often describe how to choose and detect a counterfeit; this one does not. The Greek name is the only identity the text offers, and the translator's gloss is a modern identification, so the stone is listed in `_sources/dioscorides.csv` as an uncertain identification.

No other tradition is tagged for this entry. A search of the Dioscorides text on disk for "geode" returns only the chapter heading (line 10707), the chapter text (line 10710), and the index entries ("GEODES LITHOS 825", at the index of Greek names and the English index). The Ebers (Bryan), Assyrian (Thompson) and Shennong (Yang) texts and Clark's 1995 Tibetan translation were also searched for "geode" and returned nothing; the Sushruta chapters and the Men-Tsee-Khang text were not searched for it.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, a geode is a roughly rounded rock with a hollow centre lined with mineral crystals, most commonly quartz (silicon dioxide) or its fine-grained forms such as chalcedony, sometimes with calcite or other minerals. Quartz is insoluble in water and chemically inert in the body. Which mineral the Greek *geodes lithos* was is not stated in the sources read, so the composition of the ancient material is not known from them. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved say that a stone called *geodes* was ground, mixed with water and smeared on for inflammation of the breasts, and was called astringent and drying and good for "things that darken the eyes". The Codex does not recommend grinding stone for use on the breast or in the eye. Mineral dust is an irritant and a hygiene risk on inflamed skin, and no part of the sources supports a modern use.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation in the text is grinding and smearing with water (line 10710, with "[ground up]" a translator's bracket). The chapter gives no quantity, no number of applications, no duration, and no instruction for use in the eye beyond the phrase "take away things that darken the eyes", which does not say how the stone was applied there. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Quartz and chalcedony are silica; inhaled silica dust causes silicosis, and fine mineral particles irritate the eye. Silica is not absorbed when swallowed. There is no evidence that a ground stone treats breast inflammation (mastitis) or urinary stones. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** the only qualities printed are in line 10710: "astringent and drying". No taste, no degree, and no hot or cold rating is given. No other tradition is tagged, so no other classification is offered.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for the geode. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, assay, adulteration), and this two-sentence chapter does not even give that: it names the stone, its qualities and two uses, and stops. It records no amuletic use. The chapter is therapeutic and assigns no cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The entry records that a Greek physician called a hollow crystal-lined stone astringent and drying, and that he used it ground in water for breast inflammation; the Codex does not recommend repeating that. The one thing a reader can take from it is the limit of the record: the chapter is short, the stone's identity is a translator's gloss, and the line about "stones" can be read two ways. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Breast inflammation:** swelling, heat and pain in the breast, with or without fever, can be infection (mastitis) or an abscess and needs a clinician; a stone paste does not treat it.
- **Urinary or kidney stones:** these cause severe pain, obstruction and infection and need medical care; nothing in the sources shows that a ground stone dissolves or passes them.
- **Eyes:** do not put mineral dust in the eye; a hard particle abrades the cornea.
- **Dust and hygiene:** grinding a silica stone produces breathable dust; a paste of stone dust is not sterile on broken or inflamed skin.
- **Identification:** "geodes" as a stone is a translator's gloss for a Greek name, and the sources read do not say which mineral it was.
