# Prayer Bead Plant / Rosary Pea — Gunja (Ayurveda), jequirity, crab's-eye, rati

## *Abrus precatorius* L. | Family: Fabaceae (legume / pea family)

**Tradition Tags:** FMT (rGyud-bzhi / Tibetan) · Ayurveda (Gunja) · folk-tropical
**Energetic Classification (TCM):** ⚠ not a TCM staple — classified here by toxicological tier, not energetics

---

> ⛔ **LETHAL-TOXIN WARNING.** The seed of *Abrus precatorius* contains **abrin**, a type-II ribosome-inactivating protein among the most potent plant toxins known. A single thoroughly chewed seed can be fatal to an adult. This entry documents the plant for completeness and for **hazard-identification**; it is **not** a use recommendation. Nothing here should be read as endorsing internal use of the seed in any form.

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda (Gunja).** *Abrus precatorius* is the classical **Gunja** of Ayurveda — a pantropical climbing legume whose scarlet, black-eyed seeds are famous worldwide as rosary beads and, historically, as the "rati" jeweller's weight standard. Ayurveda recognised Gunja as a *visha* (toxic) drug requiring rigorous **śodhana** (purification/detoxification) before any external or highly-processed use. Classically the **root and leaf** — which do not carry the seed's abrin load in the same way — were the parts favoured, and the seed itself was subjected to elaborate processing and reserved chiefly for **external** applications (topical and scalp/hair preparations) rather than casual internal use. The seed's toxicity was well understood: it belongs to the tradition's controlled, purification-dependent poison drugs, not its everyday tonics.

**Tibetan (rGyud-bzhi / FMT).** The plant appears among the imported tropical materia medica referenced in the Tibetan/Sino-Himalayan formulary; as in Ayurveda, any traditional use is hedged by recognition of the seed as a dangerous substance. Attestation here is documentary — this is not a routine Tibetan therapeutic.

**Folk-tropical.** Across its range the seeds have been used ornamentally (rosaries, jewellery). But the dominant cross-cultural fact about the seed is its **toxicity** — it is a well-known agent of accidental poisoning (chewed beads, drilled beads that pierce the hard seed coat) and of deliberate poisoning of livestock and people.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — the beautiful poison.** The rosary pea is the archetype of the *pharmakon* as both remedy and poison. A seed of striking beauty — glossy scarlet with a black eye, used to count prayers and to weigh gold — carries death in its kernel. Its symbolic lesson in a materia medica is precisely that beauty and lethality coexist, and that the sacred bead and the deadly toxin are the same object. The **hard, intact seed coat** is the whole story pharmacologically: an unbroken swallowed seed often passes harmlessly because abrin cannot be absorbed through it, whereas a **chewed or drilled** seed liberates the toxin — a rare case where the "signature" (the impenetrable shell) is literally the mechanism of safety, and its breach the mechanism of harm.

### Active Components

- **Abrin (a type-II ribosome-inactivating protein / lectin toxin)** — the lethal principle, concentrated in the **seed**. It irreversibly inactivates the 60S ribosomal subunit, halting protein synthesis and killing the cell. It is homologous to, and more potent than, ricin from *Ricinus communis*. Attribution: findings from PubMed.
- **Abrus precatorius agglutinin (APA)** — a related but markedly **less toxic** type-II RIP co-occurring in the seed (its lower toxicity has a documented structural basis; see below).
- **Abrine (an amino-acid alkaloid) and triterpenoid/saponin constituents** — non-protein constituents; the leaves have a sweet (glycyrrhizin-like) taste. [USER: verify.]

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the modern literature on *Abrus precatorius* is dominated by **toxicology and biodefence**, not therapeutics:

- **Detection / clinical toxicology.** Worbs et al. (2021, *Toxins*) developed and validated sandwich ELISAs and a lateral-flow assay to detect and discriminate abrin from the related *Abrus* agglutinin (limit of detection ~22 pg/mL for abrin), and applied them to a **clinical sample from an attempted-suicide case involving the seeds** — underscoring both the toxin's potency and its status as a substance of malevolent-use concern. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/toxins13040284)
- **Relative potency of abrin vs agglutinin.** Cheng et al. (2010, *J Biomed Sci*) determined the crystal structure of *Abrus* agglutinin and provided a biophysical explanation for why the agglutinin is **less toxic** than abrin-a, despite structural similarity in the active cleft. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/1423-0127-17-34)
- **Countermeasure development.** Tiwari et al. (2017, *Toxicon*) noted abrin is roughly **75× more potent than ricin**, that no vaccine is yet available against it, and designed a chimeric abrin/agglutinin protein that protected mice against otherwise-lethal abrin challenge — a research countermeasure, not a therapeutic. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.toxicon.2017.01.008)

Evidence grade: **toxicological, not therapeutic.** There is no clinical evidence supporting a safe medicinal internal use of the seed. The plant's scientific profile is overwhelmingly that of a hazard and biodefence target.

### Contraindications

- **Absolute contraindication to internal self-use of the seed.** Abrin poisoning causes severe gastroenteritis, haemorrhage, multi-organ failure and death; there is no specific antidote — treatment is supportive/decontamination only. A single well-chewed seed can be lethal.
- **Accidental exposure.** Beware chewed or drilled ornamental beads, especially around children; an intact swallowed seed may pass without harm, but a breached seed is dangerous.
- **Handling / dust.** Powdered seed and dust are hazardous by ingestion and mucosal exposure.
- **Pregnancy / children / everyone.** No safe internal dose exists; treat the seed as a poison, not a medicine.
- **This entry is not medical advice.** If poisoning is suspected, seek emergency medical care and contact a poison control centre immediately.
