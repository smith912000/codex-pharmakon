# Mole — "Mole", "Entrails-of-Moles" (Bryan 1930, Ebers Papyrus; no Egyptian word or binomial printed in the list)
## *The animal Bryan's English calls "Mole"; his list gives no Egyptian word and no binomial, so which small burrowing mammal is meant is not stated in anything read* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The only text on disk for this tradition is C. P. Bryan's 1930 English *The Papyrus Ebers* (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`). In his chapter VII, "Organic Remedies", Bryan lists the animals "whose identity is not in doubt, and who were called upon to sacrifice themselves in the cause of the Healing Art" (lines 1383-1392). The list reads "... Tortoise Lizard Mole Vulture ..." (line 1394; row 103 of `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv`, "Bryan 1930, Ch. VII Organic Remedies (animals) (line 1394)", which notes "Bryan's English identification; no Egyptian word or binomial given in this list"). Bryan says in the same place that a complete list of the animals is not possible because the identity of a considerable number is "still beyond us". Whether the Egyptian word is rendered mole correctly, and which animal it was, is therefore a matter of Bryan's judgement and a modern hypothesis.

Two passages in Bryan's chapters give a remedy with the mole in it.

First, among the "minor surgery" remedies, under "WHAT TO DO TO DRAW OUT SPLINTERS IN THE FLESH" (line 2336), a series of recipes includes (line 2338): "ANOTHER Worms' blood, cook and crush in Oil; Mole, kill, cook, and drain in Oil; Ass's dung, mix in Fresh Milk. Apply to the opening." The mole here is "killed, cooked and drained in oil" and the whole is applied to the opening of the splinter wound.

Second, in the eye section (under "DISEASES OF THE EYES", page 103 of Bryan, about lines 2884-2927), a series of recipes concerns the hair at the place where it has been pulled out. One of them reads (lines 2922-2928): "ANOTHER / Fat-of-an-Ox / Olive Oil / Entrails-of-Moles / Crush into one, put on the fire, and put in the place of the Hair." The preceding recipe in the series is a mixture of dung, blood of cow, ass, pig, dog and stag, collyrium and incense, "place[d] on the part whence the Hair has been pulled out so that it may not grow again" (about lines 2884-2895), so the series is a set of preparations for the place of an extracted hair. I did not read the series heading in Bryan, and what Bryan prints may be a selection; the reading of the complaint is mine.

Note on the word: Bryan also prints "REMEDY AGAINST A MOLE" (line 4132; "Berries-of-the-t'as-plant mamer-grain Pound, and let the person take who has the Mole") and says the papyrus has remedies "to remove moles" (line 1038). Those are the skin mark, a cosmetic complaint, and have nothing to do with the animal.

The animal is also a name on a list; I searched Bryan's text for "mole" and found only the lines above. Bryan's book is a summary with selected quotations, not a full translation, so recipes may exist that he did not print. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, a mole's body is ordinary mammalian tissue, with muscle protein, fat, and in the entrails digestive contents and gut bacteria. The oil in which it is cooked is the main fat-soluble carrier in both recipes. Nothing in the animal is known to treat a wound or affect hair growth.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Ebers recipes use the dead animal, or its entrails, cooked in oil or fat and applied to the opening of a splinter wound or to the place of an extracted hair. The Codex does not recommend either. Applying animal tissue and gut contents to a wound invites infection, including tetanus, and the animal's capture and killing serve no tested purpose. Splinters and wounds are treated with cleaning, removal of the foreign body and, where indicated, tetanus prophylaxis. No modern protocol is invented here.

### Preparation & Dosing

The two preparations are the ones quoted: "Mole, kill, cook, and drain in Oil" (line 2338, with worms' blood and ass's dung, applied to the opening) and "Fat-of-an-Ox, Olive Oil, Entrails-of-Moles: crush into one, put on the fire, and put in the place of the Hair" (lines 2922-2928). Neither gives a quantity as Bryan prints it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Soil-dwelling animals and their guts carry bacteria and spores, among them *Clostridium tetani* and *Clostridium perfringens*, which cause tetanus and gas gangrene in deep wounds. Cooking in oil does not make a preparation sterile for a wound. Hair growth is controlled by the follicle and hormones, not by animal fat. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Egyptian (EP):** None. Bryan's text gives no quality (hot, cold, moist, dry) for the mole. The Ebers corpus is organised by complaint and records what was done for a condition, not a classification of the substance.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. The two recipes are for a splinter wound and the place of an extracted hair. The Egyptian practice of treating spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*) is relevant in general, and Bryan prints "words to be spoken" elsewhere in the papyrus, including a prayer before the preparing of medicines (about line 1504); none is reported with the mole recipes, and since Bryan summarises, that is no proof that the papyrus gives none. No deity is attributed.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The mole is an animal named in two cooked-in-oil remedies for a splinter and for the place of a pulled hair, and the Codex does not recommend either. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Wounds:** do not apply animal tissue, entrails or fat to an open wound or the opening of a splinter; this risks wound infection, tetanus and gangrene.
- **Hygiene:** wild animals carry parasites and pathogens; handling and cooking carcasses for medicine is not advised.
- **Conservation and law:** moles are protected or regulated in some places; the Codex does not recommend capturing or killing any animal for these uses.
- **Identification:** the animal's identity in the papyrus is Bryan's English only; do not infer a species.
