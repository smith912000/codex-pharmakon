# Wild sheep (argali) horn — gnyan, "Wild Sheep: Ovis ammon" (Clark 1995); "the argali" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *The horn of the wild sheep, which Clark glosses as Ovis ammon; the Men-Tsee-Khang text calls the animal the argali, and its glossary pairs "Argali" with "Oris [sic] ammon hodgsoni"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The Explanatory Tantra (*bshad rgyud*), chapter 20, "Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", has a section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" listing thirteen kinds of animal product, of which horn is the first (Clark 1995, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` lines 1147-1148, sub-heading "Horn" at line 1150). The wild sheep comes last in the horn run, on a line (1158) that also holds the ram, the saiga antelope and the takin. In Clark's English it reads: "gnyan: Wild Sheep: Ovis ammon: [has thirteen names. Its horn is shaped like that of a ram but is larger and] cures infectious fevers." The bracketed matter is not in the main verse in Clark's rendering (I did not confirm his exact convention), so the plain statement is that the horn "cures infectious fevers", and the bracket adds thirteen names and a description: the horn is shaped like a ram's but larger. The ram's horn, two entries earlier on the same line, is treated differently: "the horns of Ram and Saiga antelope are ecbolic". The same line gives the takin or wild yak horn (*rgod.gyag*) as one that "increases [body] heat, disperses tumors, [and dries (accumulations of) pus in the abdomen]".

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 3508) has the same list in a different order and wording: "The horns of serows/gorals and rams are ecbolic. Wild yak horn generates heat and dissolves tumors. The horn of the argali cures and control epidemic diseases." So Clark has "infectious fevers" where Men-Tsee-Khang has "epidemic diseases"; I cannot tell from either English text whether the Tibetan is one term or two. [USER: verify] Men-Tsee-Khang names the animal "argali" and does not give the Tibetan name *gnyan*. Its glossary (lines 7005-7007) sets "Argali" beside "Oris ammon hodgsoni" (sic), a pairing made by list order in an OCR-split two-column list and therefore not certain. [USER: verify] Clark's identification, *Ovis ammon*, and Men-Tsee-Khang's "argali" are consistent with each other.

The name list `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` has no row for the wild sheep's horn (it has "Rhino horn", line 332, and "Sheep bone", line 339), so the attestation rests on Clark's line and the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence. Clark's preface (line 83) warns that Tibetan materia medica is often "either totally unidentified or only tentatively identified".

The animal is named elsewhere in the same Tantra as food. Clark's classification of meats puts *Ovis ammon* among the "game animals" with deer, musk deer, rabbits and the saiga (line 672), and the Men-Tsee-Khang text lists "the argali" among "the group of herbivorous wild animals" (line 2907). The botanical entry for *Incarvillea* in Clark describes seed-pods "shaped like the curved horns of a wild sheep" (line 1115), which shows the horn's curve as a familiar image. None of these describes a medicinal use of the horn.

*Other texts on disk, not folded in.* I searched the Sushruta chapters, Dioscorides, the Ebers text, Yang's Shennong translation and Thompson's Assyrian text for "argali", "wild sheep" and "mountain sheep" and found nothing. The Tibetan "Burrhel sheep" fur (*gnah.wa*, Clark line 1195) and "Blue sheep fur" (`_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 372) are different materials.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, horn is keratin, a structural protein of the same family as hair and nails, over a bony core; it is not a drug in the pharmacological sense and has no known fever-lowering constituent. Nothing links any constituent to the action the Tantra gives.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts say the horn of the wild sheep cures infectious fevers or epidemic diseases. The Codex does not recommend it. An infectious fever may be a serious infection that needs diagnosis and treatment, and horn is not one. Argali are large wild mountain sheep, protected by law in much of their range and by international trade rules, and trading in their horns is regulated or banned in many countries. The entry exists to record what the Explanatory Tantra says.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither translation gives a preparation, vehicle or quantity for the horn. The "medicinal horses" at the end of the chapter (Clark line 1202), raw cane sugar, crystal sugar and honey, are named as the vehicles for compounded medicines in general; the text does not say that horn was taken with any of them. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Horn is keratin, which the human body does not digest, and powdered horn has no established action against infection. Infectious fevers are treated according to their cause. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** No taste, post-digestive taste or potency is printed for the horn of the wild sheep. Its only printed action is against infectious or epidemic fevers. For comparison the next horn on the line, that of the takin or wild yak, "increases [body] heat" (Clark line 1158); the text says nothing of the kind for the wild sheep, and no quality is carried over.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The horn of the wild sheep is named in the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica of animal products with a single therapeutic action (Clark line 1158). The wider frame is real but does not reach it. In Clark's account the three humors are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration (line 51) and these three are "the three causes" of wind, bile and phlegm disorders (line 147), so disease is at root a spiritual condition and medicine is one support; the chapter on routine behaviour asks the practitioner to refrain from the ten negative actions, the first of which is "taking life" (line 616). The rejuvenation passage in chapter 23 (lines 1266-1275) does not name this horn, and the lines read give it no *bcud len* use. The tradition has no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The wild sheep's horn is a one-line fever remedy in a list of thirteen kinds of animal product, and the tradition's first precept is against taking life. The Codex does not recommend using it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Fever:** a fever that is high, prolonged, accompanied by stiff neck, rash, confusion or breathing difficulty needs urgent medical assessment, not horn.
- **Conservation and law:** argali are protected wild sheep; trade in their horns is regulated or illegal in many countries; do not buy or collect them.
- **Infection:** animal material from wild herbivores can carry pathogens (anthrax spores among them in some regions) when handled or powdered.
- **Ethics:** the Tibetan source itself lists "taking life" first among the ten negative actions (Clark line 616).
