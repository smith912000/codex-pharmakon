# Thymus angustifolius — Thumos (Dioscorides 3-44); Oinos thumites (5-59)

## *Thymus angustifolius* (the Dioscorides translators' suggested identification, with *T. glaber*; wild thyme, mother of thyme) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Identity note.** This plant is named only by Dioscorides (DMM). The binomial is the suggestion printed on the SUGGESTED lines of the translation by Osbaldeston and Wood (2000), not Dioscorides' own: "Thymus angustifolius, Thymus glaber — Wild Thyme, Mother of Thyme". Both chapters carry the same line. Existing entries cover thyme under other binomials: I_233 (*Thymus vulgaris*) and I_711 (*Thymus serpyllum*, on the Dioscorides *herpyllos*, 3-46). This row records the *thumos* chapter (3-44) and the thyme wine (5-59) under the translators' gloss; the overlap is flagged for editorial decision. **Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).**

*3-44 THUMOS* (heading at line 6911). "Everyone knows thyme. It is a little shrub full of branches surrounded with many narrow little leaves, and little heads with flowers resembling purple on the top. It grows chiefly in rocky and barren places." Uses: "Taken as a drink with salt and vinegar it is able to drive out phlegmy matter through the bowels. A decoction with honey helps orthopnoea [form of asthma] and the asthmatic, expels worms and the menstrual flow, is an abortifacient, expels the afterbirth, and is urinary. Mixed with honey and taken as a linctus [syrup] it makes matter come up [vomitory]. Applied with vinegar it dissolves new swellings and clots of blood, and takes away thymos [hormonal glandular enlargement] and hanging warts. Applied with wine and polenta it is good for hip pains. Eaten with meat it is good for poor vision. It is good instead of sauce for use in health." Other names: white thyme, *cephalotus*, *epithumis*, *thyrsium*; the Romans *thymus*, the Egyptians *stephane*, the Dacians *mozula*.

*5-59 OINOS THUMITES* (heading at line 10087). "Bind one hundred ounces of pounded sifted thyme in a linen cloth, put it into nine gallons of must for three months, and then jar it. It is good for digestive difficulties, lack of appetite, dysentery, disorders of the nerves, and hypochondria [nervous gastric disorder]; for winter shiverings, and for poison from venomous creatures [bites] that chill and putrify."

### Active Components

Thyme (genus *Thymus*, Lamiaceae) is generally known to contain an essential oil rich in phenolic monoterpenes such as thymol and carvacrol. The composition of the plant Dioscorides describes was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records thyme as a bowel-clearing drink, an expectorant and urinary drug, a kitchen herb "for use in health", and a wine; these are his claims and not a Codex protocol. The Codex gives no protocol; thyme as a kitchen herb needs no entry-level guidance.

### Preparation & Dosing

*3-44:* drunk with salt and vinegar; decoction with honey; linctus with honey; applied with vinegar or with wine and polenta; eaten with meat. *5-59:* one hundred ounces of thyme, pounded and sifted, bound in linen and left in nine gallons of must for three months. No dose for drinking is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Thymol is a well-known antiseptic compound, and thyme is widely used as a culinary herb. No study of this species was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. 3-44 opens "Everyone knows thyme" and gives a plain description: a branched shrub, narrow leaves, purple-ish flower heads, on rocky and barren ground. It closes with the single sentence that thyme is "good instead of sauce for use in health", which is the nearest the chapter comes to an everyday-health use. That is a food use, not a cultivation status. The binomial is a translators' gloss. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the source read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant beyond eating it with meat, as the chapter says. Use as a kitchen herb is the only use supported here.

### Contraindications

The medicinal uses recorded include abortifacient and afterbirth-expelling uses; do not use medicinally in pregnancy. Concentrated thyme essential oil is an irritant and should not be taken internally. The traditional uses are not advice.
