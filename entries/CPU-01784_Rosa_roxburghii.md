# Rosa roxburghii — Se.wahi.me.tog, Sewai metok; "Wild Rose" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Rosa roxburghii, the first of three binomials Clark 1995 prints at line 1033 for Se.wahi.me.tog, "Wild Rose" (then Rosa sericea Lindl and Rosa avicularis); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text prints Sewai metok, and its glossary prints no Rosa roxburghii* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and under this binomial on Clark's edition alone.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the other texts on disk for *Rosa* found drugs of other species and no *Rosa roxburghii*. The Dioscorides text prints *Rosa* in translators' SUGGESTED lines for Greek drugs (for example lines 3965, 4444, 4484 and 9956), which are not read as this plant, and Yang 1998 prints "Ying Shi (Semen Rosae Multiflorae)" at line 3158, a Shennong drug of another species. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 144) the row is keyed by the Tibetan name *Se.wahi.me.tog*, with the identification "Wild Rose; Rosa roxburghii; Rosa sericea Lindl; Rosa avicularis" and the locus "Clark 1995 (line 1033)". The spine row (CPU-01784) took the **first** binomial. Clark's third binomial, *Rosa avicularis*, is the spine row of I_1347, which rests on Clark's separate entry *se.rgod* (line 984); the second, *Rosa sericea*, has no spine row of its own as far as the files read show. Clark's Latin index prints all of them (line 1639).

**Identity doubt.** Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" and (line 68) that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect". The name *Se.wahi.me.tog* is one of three Tibetan entries glossed "Wild Rose" in Clark (it, *se.rgod* at line 984 and *se.rgod* at line 1042), and the three share binomials in part. The Tibetan name does not fix the species, and the Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1033).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). It follows *chhu.rug (sbal.lag)* (line 1032). It reads: "Se.wahi.me.tog: Wild Rose: Rosa roxburghii: Rosa sericea Lindl: Rosa avicularis: [has five names and is of black and white types. It is a large thorny bush with small, round, slightly coarse leaves, white flower and red hips. It has sweet and sour tastes, and oily power, and] cures bile disorders and alleviates wind imbalances."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The five names, the two types, the description of the bush, and the tastes and power are therefore the commentary layer. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the English name, the three Latin names, which are Clark's identifications, and "cures bile disorders and alleviates wind imbalances". Unlike the *se.rgod* entry at line 984, the root-layer sentence here belongs to this drug alone. Clark's name index lists *se.wahi.me.tog* (line 1765).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (read through the name, not the binomial).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225). Line 3433, within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428): "Sewai metok cures tripa disorders and suppresses loong." The index lists "Sewai metok 207" (line 7486). "Tripa" and "loong" are Tibetan terms that the editors keep untranslated (note at line 686). The glossary prints "Sewai metok" and "se ba'i me tog" together on line 5715, in its first "S" block, which has fifteen phonetic names (lines 5685 to 5719), fifteen transliterations (lines 5687 to 5725) and fifteen scientific names (lines 5727 to 5765). Counted in order, "Sewai metok" is the tenth phonetic name, and the tenth scientific name is *Rosa branonii* (line 5753), with the English "Musk rose" at line 5755. The count is checked on "Saegoe" (second, *Rosa macrophylla*), "Sengdeng" (sixth, *Acacia catechu*) and "Shingkun" (fifteenth, *Ferula asafetida*), the same count as in I_1324. By position, then, the glossary sets *Sewai metok* against another rose, *Rosa branonii*, and prints no *Rosa roxburghii*. The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position only. [USER: verify]

The two editions agree in kind: Clark's drug "cures bile disorders and alleviates wind imbalances", and the Men-Tsee-Khang *Sewai metok* "cures tripa disorders and suppresses loong", where "bile" and "wind" in Clark and *tripa* and *loong* in the Men-Tsee-Khang text are the two translators' words for the editors' kept Tibetan terms. The Codex reads *Sewai metok* and *Se.wahi.me.tog* as one drug from the spelling and that agreement; neither text says so. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Rosa roxburghii*, *Se.wahi.me.tog* and *Sewai metok* found no entry on this plant. I_1178 (*Rosa macrophylla*; *se.rgod*, Clark line 1042) mentions this Clark line (1033) as a third "Wild Rose" entry under a different Tibetan name; it is not an entry on this drug. I_1347 (*Rosa avicularis*, *se.rgod*, Clark line 984) is the sibling row from the same batch. I_204 (Rose; tags CM, SS, EP, DMM, SBJ, FMT) and I_349 (*Rosa damascena*; tags EP, DMM, CM, SBJ, SS, FMT) carry the FMT tag as general rose entries; I did not find *Se.wahi.me.tog*, *Sewai metok* or *roxburghii* in them. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general, well-established fact that *Rosa* is a genus of the rose family (Rosaceae) and that rosehips are generally known to contain vitamin C. The lines read give no composition for the Tibetan drug, and nothing is stated here about what it contains.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a wild rose that "cures bile disorders and alleviates wind imbalances" (Clark, line 1033) and a *Sewai metok* that "cures tripa disorders and suppresses loong" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3433). These are the texts' claims in their own terms, attached to this binomial through Clark's first of three names and through the spelling, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary names white flowers and red hips but does not say which part is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 1033) prints "sweet and sour tastes, and oily power". No post-digestive taste is printed. The Men-Tsee-Khang lines read (3433, 5715 to 5730, 7486) print no taste or power.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it cures bile disorders and alleviates wind imbalances, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same in kind. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names (*Se.wahi.me.tog*, *Sewai metok*, *Rosa*) found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, colophon at line 3950), and the herb entries of Clark's chapter 20 that mention essence extraction (lines 971, 1043, 1078, 1103, 1121 and 1144) do not include it. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a bile and wind drug with a taste and a power in the commentary, no preparation and no dose, and its Latin name is the first of three that Clark offers. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Wild roses are thorny, the identity is open, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No conservation information and no safety information for pregnancy were read. [USER: verify]
