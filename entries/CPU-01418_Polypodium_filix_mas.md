# Polypodium filix-mas — Pteris (Dioscorides 4-186); Druopteris (4-189)

## *Polypodium filix-mas* (the Dioscorides translators' suggested identification; male fern, shield fern) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Identity note.** This fern is named only by Dioscorides (DMM). The binomial is the suggestion printed on the SUGGESTED lines of the translation by Osbaldeston and Wood (2000), not Dioscorides' own identification. The same line for both chapters also offers *Dryopteris filix-mas*, *Aspidium*, *Polystichum* and *Lastrea filix-mas* as synonyms and "Male Polypody, Male Fern, Shield Fern". Two Greek chapters, *pteris* (4-186) and *druopteris* (4-189), each carry the gloss and each says "see" the other. They describe two different things (a ground fern with a shallow black root; a fern on old oaks with rough roots), so the single binomial may merge two plants. **Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).**

*4-186 PTERIS* (heading at line 9698). The leaves are "without stalks, flowers or seed out of one stem, the height of about a foot, cut-in and spread out wide like a wing, with a somewhat strong smell". The root "lies shallow, black, somewhat long, with many shoots, somewhat astringent to the taste. It grows in rocky hilly places." Uses: "Four teaspoonfuls of the root of this is taken with honey and water to draw out broadworms. It works better if one gives it with forty grains of scammony or black hellebore. It is necessary for those who take it to eat garlic first." For the splenetic it is given "to restore them to their former state"; "A decoction of the root (taken as a drink with goose grease and applied as well) is good for those hurt with a fistula." A note on the plant's nature follows: "where there is much seed and much fern enclosing it, there the fern vanishes." Other names: blechnon, polyrrhizon, pterion, pterineon, dasyclonon, anasphoron; the Magi call it *surculum Mercurii*, the Romans *filix fanaria*, *laculla* or *filix*, "and the Egyptians call it the blood of an ass".

*4-189 DRUOPTERIS* (heading at line 9718). It "grows on the mossy parts of old oaks like fern, but there are fewer incisions. It has rough roots enfolded by plaiting — astringent to the taste, inclining to sweetness." Use: "This is applied (pounded into small pieces with its roots) to make hair fall off. You must wipe away the first application after it has moistened the skin, and lay on fresh." Other names: pterion, nymphaea pteris.

Neighbouring chapters (4-187 *theluipteris*, 4-188 *polupodion*) are different ferns in the translation and are not attributed to this row. 4-188, common polypody, is the subject of I_335.

### Active Components

The male fern is generally known to contain phloroglucinol derivatives (filicin) in the rhizome, which have been used as anthelmintics and are toxic in excess. The constituents of the ferns Dioscorides describes were not read in any source. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records an anthelmintic root (4-186) and a depilatory (4-189), as his claims and not a Codex protocol. The plant is toxic in excess, the identification is a translators' gloss, and the Codex gives no protocol for it.

### Preparation & Dosing

*4-186:* "Four teaspoonfuls of the root of this is taken with honey and water to draw out broadworms"; better with "forty grains of scammony or black hellebore", after eating garlic. A decoction of the root is drunk with goose grease and applied for fistula. *4-189:* the pounded fern with its roots is applied to the skin to make hair fall off, the first application wiped away and fresh laid on. The quantities above are the only ones given for the root. [USER: verify] Scammony and black hellebore are strong purgatives and are not recommended here.

### Scientific Correlation

Male fern extract was used in modern medicine as a tapeworm treatment before safer drugs replaced it, and its toxicity (including damage to vision and the nervous system) is well documented. This is a general statement about the species; no study was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** The root is "somewhat astringent to the taste" (4-186); the rough roots are "astringent to the taste, inclining to sweetness" (4-189). No warming, cooling or degree is printed in either chapter.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. The two chapters offer a discipline of attention: where each fern grows (rocky hills, mossy old oaks), leaf and root form, taste of the root, its names, and one preparatory rule (eat garlic first). They assign no status and record no use for the person beyond expelling worms, splenic complaint and fistula. The binomial is a translators' gloss and may merge two plants. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the source read gives a cultivator a practice with these ferns. The answer for the person is to abstain: the preparation involves strong purgatives and the plant is toxic in overdose.

### Contraindications

Male fern preparations are toxic and can cause poisoning; they should not be taken as home remedies. The purgatives paired with it in 4-186 (scammony, black hellebore) add risk. Do not use in pregnancy. The traditional uses above are not advice.
