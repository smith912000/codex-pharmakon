# Goat brain — rGyud-bzhi, "Goat brain" (brain, one of the thirteen animal-derived medicines)
## *Brain of the domestic goat; the translator prints only "Goat brain", with no Tibetan name and no binomial* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Goat brain is listed in the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, the chapter on the efficacy of medicinal substances, in Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). It sits in section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), whose thirteen headings are "horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). Under "Brain" (lines 1181-1182) the whole list reads: "Goat brain: unites severed ligaments and tendons. Sheep brain: cures dizziness and vertigo. Brain of game animals (e.g. deer): halts diarrhoea. Rabbit brain: cures colic. Human brain: reduces swellings and cures lymph disorders." Goat brain heads the list and carries no bracketed commentary in Clark's rendering. Clark marks commentary interpolations from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries in square brackets (line 82); none appears on this line, so the sentence is the root-text wording as Clark gives it.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3548) agrees: "Goat brain heals torn ligaments and tendons. Sheep brain cures dizziness and reeling. The brain of herbivorous wild animals stops diarrhea. Rabbit brain cures dysentery." (The file prints "tom", an OCR error for "torn".) The two translations differ in small ways: Clark has "unites severed ligaments and tendons", Men-Tsee-Khang "heals torn ligaments and tendons"; Clark has rabbit brain for colic, Men-Tsee-Khang for dysentery. The Tibetan wording is not in the files read, so which rendering is closer cannot be said. [USER: verify] Neither translation prints a Tibetan name for goat brain.

Clark also notes in his introduction (line 57) that "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts." The same Clark chapter gives, in the "Hair" and "Blood" sections, other goat-derived items (goat blood, line 1173; pubic hair of billy-goat, line 1195), and under "Meat" goat liver "supremely beneficial in eye diseases" (line 1170). They are separate substances and have their own entries or none; they are not folded here.

### Active Components

Brain is soft nervous tissue. It is high in water and in lipids, including cholesterol and phospholipids (the lipids of cell membranes and myelin), with proteins and smaller amounts of vitamins and minerals. A ruminant's brain can also carry prion disease (transmissible spongiform encephalopathy), and goats and sheep are hosts of scrapie, a prion disease; that general fact is a safety point, not a constituent of the medicine. Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Codex offers no protocol. Injured ligaments and tendons call for assessment and treatment by a clinician, and eating animal brain carries real risk (see Contraindications). ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Ligaments and tendons are dense connective tissue made largely of collagen; they heal slowly because they are poorly supplied with blood, and healing of a complete tear depends on the injury and on surgical or non-surgical management. Eating the brain of an animal does not supply "ligament" to the body; dietary protein is digested into amino acids. Consuming nervous-system tissue from ruminants is a recognised risk factor for prion disease, which is why many countries regulate the handling of such tissue ("specified risk material"). ### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* Neither translation read assigns goat brain any cultivation framing. It is a one-line indication in a list of animal medicines, and the line concerns injured ligaments and tendons. The frame around it is the tradition's own: the opening invocation in Clark (lines 100-102) praises the Master of Medicine who "dispels the three poisons", and the project's cultivation spec records that the *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) are derived from the three poisons and that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root. Those points come from the spec and not from any line about goat brain. No source read attaches goat brain to *bcud len* (essence extraction), and there is no formal Tibetan category of "substances for subtle-body practice". Clark's remark that few animal products are now used (line 57) is a statement about practice in his day.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a single indication for torn ligaments in a list of animal medicines, rarely used according to the translator, and eating brain from a ruminant carries a recognised disease risk with no supported benefit. The honest use of the entry is as a record of what the Tantra says.

### Contraindications

- **Prion disease:** brain and spinal tissue from sheep and goats can carry transmissible spongiform encephalopathies; many countries ban or restrict human consumption of such tissue.
- **Infection:** raw or undercooked offal can carry bacteria and parasites.
- **Not a treatment for injury:** a torn ligament or tendon needs clinical assessment; do not rely on this line.
- **Legal and animal-welfare status:** obtaining brain requires slaughter, which is regulated in most places. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** killing an animal for a substance with no supported benefit is hard to justify, and Clark's omens section (line 431) values saving animals from slaughter; that passage is about merit and omens, not about medicine.
- **Religious or dietary rules:** some faiths and diets restrict offal; this entry takes no position beyond noting it.
