### Origin & Tradition

Birthwort (*Aristolochia*) represents one of the most pharmacologically important examples of the gap between traditional use and modern safety evidence. The name itself — from Greek *aristos* (best) + *locheia* (childbirth) — encodes its primary ancient application: facilitating labor and expelling the afterbirth. Across six traditions, *Aristolochia* was consistently prescribed for exactly this application plus wound healing and snakebite treatment. The pharmacological mechanism underlying both traditional applications is now characterized: aristolochic acids I and II are potent DNA adduct-forming compounds that cause irreversible renal tubular necrosis and urothelial carcinoma. The 1990s Belgian epidemic of "Chinese Herb Nephropathy" (from inadvertent substitution of *A. fangchi* for *Stephania tetrandra* in a slimming preparation) caused end-stage renal failure in over 100 patients and is the defining modern pharmacovigilance event establishing aristolochic acid as a human carcinogen (IARC Group 1).

This entry is retained in the Codex for:
1. Historical and ethnobotanical completeness — *Aristolochia* is documented in all six major traditions
2. Understanding why traditional use does not equal safety — the aristolochic acid case is the canonical example
3. Documentation of the species confusion problem (*A. fangchi* / *Stephania* substitution)
4. The fraction of *Aristolochia* pharmacology that may be deliverable via isolation (aristolactam derivatives, without the nephrotoxic acid fraction — active research area)

**EP — Ebers Papyrus (documented in labor-facilitating preparations):** Birthwort appears in the Ebers Papyrus in preparations to facilitate delivery, expel the placenta, and treat retained lochia — the primary ancient application reflected in its name.

**AT — Assyrian (Zarāwand in Mesopotamian texts):** *Aristolochia* preparations in Assyrian medical tablets for wound healing, snakebite treatment (anti-venom belief — it was used against snake venom), and labor facilitation.

**DMM — De Materia Medica (Ἀριστολοχία — three species: round, long, and clematitis):** Dioscorides gives one of the most extensive ancient *Aristolochia* treatments in Book III. He documents: round birthwort (*A. rotunda*) for uterine applications; long birthwort (*A. longa*) as more potent; *A. clematitis* for wound cleansing. He explicitly describes the uterotonic and post-partum applications and notes its use as an alexipharmic (antidote/protective). He does not mention toxicity at standard doses — indicating the aristolochic acid cumulative nephrotoxicity was not apparent at acute-use traditional doses.

**CM — Canon of Medicine (زراوند — Zarāwand; round and long types distinguished):** Ibn Sina covers birthwort in detail. He classifies it as hot and dry in the third degree. Primary applications: emmenagogue; facilitates childbirth; expels placenta; wound cleansing and antiseptic; anti-venom; the warm bitter astringent profile. He notes that excessive use causes harm — the earliest Arabic medical caution about over-use, likely reflecting cumulative toxicity observation.

**SS — Sushruta Samhita (documented as snake-bite antidote and wound medicine):** *Aristolochia* species appear in Ayurvedic preparations primarily as anti-venom (Sarpadamanī — "snake subduer"), wound-cleansing astringent, and emmenagogue. The Ayurvedic classification: Tikta-Katu rasa, Ushna virya, Vata-Kapha shamaka.

**SBJ — Bencao Gangmu (马兜铃 — Mǎdōulíng — "horse bells"; 关木通 — Guān Mùtōng; 细辛 — Xì Xīn):** Li Shizhen documents *Aristolochia* species for respiratory conditions (Mǎdōulíng for cough and asthma), uterotonic applications, and as diuretics. The Chinese species (*A. fangchi*, *A. manshuriensis* as Guān Mùtōng) were widely used in TCM formulas for these indications before the 1990s nephrotoxicity findings.

### Active Components

- **Aristolochic acid I (AAI) and II (AAII)** — nitrophenanthrene carboxylic acids; the primary toxicological concern; form covalent DNA adducts (aristolactam-DNA adducts) → permanent mutations in *TP53* and *HRAS* genes → urothelial carcinoma; cause direct renal tubular necrosis (irreversible fibrosis); IARC Group 1 (confirmed human carcinogen); there is no safe threshold for cumulative exposure
- **Aristolactams** — the reduced metabolites of aristolochic acids; less nephrotoxic but retain some genotoxic activity; the fraction studied for anti-cancer activity in cell culture (pharmacological interest but does not mitigate the parent acid risk)
- **Magnoflorine, cepharanoline** — isoquinoline alkaloids; anti-inflammatory; antimicrobial; the pharmacologically active fraction that explains the wound-healing and anti-infective traditional applications
- **Terpenoids** — anti-inflammatory; contribute to the traditional wound-healing activity
- **Aristolactam-type alkaloids** — the compounds with documented cytotoxic/anti-tumor activity in isolation studies (preclinical only)

### Preparation & Dosing

**No general dosing recommendation provided.** All internal *Aristolochia* preparations carry cumulative aristolochic acid nephrotoxicity risk without a documented safe dose. Topical wound applications of diluted preparations (external only) are the only application with plausible benefit-risk ratio, and only under specialist supervision with verified aristolochic acid-depleted extract.

### Scientific Correlation

- **Aristolochic acid nephrotoxicity (Belgian epidemic):** Vanherweghem JL et al. (1993) *Lancet* 341:387-391: the landmark paper reporting rapidly progressive renal fibrosis in 9 women who consumed a slimming preparation containing *A. fangchi* (misidentified as *Stephania tetrandra*); end-stage renal disease requiring transplant in multiple patients; the first clinical establishment of aristolochic acid nephropathy
- **Carcinogenicity (IARC):** Arlt VM et al. (2002) *J Am Soc Nephrol* 13:1561-1572: aristolactam-DNA adducts identified in renal tissue from patients with aristolochic acid nephropathy; mutations in *TP53* tumor suppressor gene confirmed; mechanism of urothelial carcinogenesis established
- **Species confusion (substitution):** Lord GM et al. (2001) *N Engl J Med* 345:1611-1612: confirmed that the Belgian slimming preparation contained *Aristolochia* misidentified as *Stephania*; DNA fingerprinting of plant material used; the defining pharmacovigilance paper establishing species substitution as a public health risk
- **DNA adduct formation:** Schmeiser HH et al. (1996) *Carcinogenesis* 17:89-94: aristolochic acid-derived DNA adducts identified in kidneys of patients with Balkan endemic nephropathy (long-term low-level dietary exposure from contaminated flour); confirmed human carcinogen at chronic sub-acute doses

**CONTEMPORARY CLASSIFICATION NOTE:** The TCM herbs *Guān Mùtōng* (关木通 — *A. manshuriensis*) and *Guǎng Fáng Jǐ* (广防己 — *A. fangchi*) have been withdrawn from the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2005 edition) and replaced with non-aristolochic-acid-containing substitutes. *Mǎdōulíng* remains in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia with restricted use and mandatory labeling.

### Contraindications

- **Internal use — confirmed aristolochic acid accumulation risk** — there is no documented safe internal dose; cumulative exposure from multiple short courses may exceed nephrotoxic threshold without acute symptoms until fibrosis is advanced
- **ANY ARISTOLOCHIA SPECIES — renal function monitoring required** — if traditional practitioners prescribe any *Aristolochia* preparation, baseline and follow-up serum creatinine and urinalysis is mandatory
- **Pregnancy — absolute contraindication** — uterotonic at therapeutic doses; may cause miscarriage or premature labor; and fetal aristolochic acid exposure is an additional DNA adduct risk
- **Pre-existing renal impairment** — the nephrotoxicity is additive; any reduced renal function is an absolute contraindication to internal use
- **Herb-drug interactions** — aristolochic acids inhibit organic anion transporters (OAT1/OAT3) used for renal drug excretion; may accumulate co-administered nephrotoxic drugs
