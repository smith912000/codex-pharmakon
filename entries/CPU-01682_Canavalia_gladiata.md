# Canavalia gladiata — mkhal.zho.sha, Khelma shho sha, mkhal ma zho sha (rGyud-bzhi); "Cowhage"

## *Canavalia gladiata, the first of two binomials Clark 1995 prints for mkhal.zho.sha (with Mucuna prurita, as printed), under the English name "Cowhage"; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints "Phaseolus vulgaris" on the same line as the name* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts, and under this binomial only by Clark.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. Both Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the line prints two, of which the row takes the first (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, row 88). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 949).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20. The chapter lists eight sections of "individual natural substances" at line 802, and (6) is "plants". The text file prints the headings "(4) Tree Medicines" (line 871) and "(5) Resins" (line 879), then "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997), and prints none for (6). This line lies between the (5) and (7) headings, in the stretch the list calls plants; the Codex does not state which section the file's own headings place it in. The entry reads:

"mkhal.zho.sha: Cowhage: Canavalia gladiata: Mucuna prurita: [hKhrungs.dpe says, “Cowhage has a slender trunk and smooth leaves. The flower is attractive and indigo-hued whilst the yellow pods contain black kidney-shaped seeds of sweet taste and oily power. These seeds cure kidney disorders and the white type is superior to the black or yellow types]. It cures kidney fever.”"

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the quoted description of the tree, the flower, the pods and the seeds, the sweet taste and oily power, the indication "cure kidney disorders" and the grading of the white type above the black and yellow. Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the English and Latin names, which are Clark's own identifications and not the root text's, and the clause "It cures kidney fever." The quotation marks in the line do not agree with the brackets: the opening mark of the quotation from "hKhrungs.dpe" is inside the bracket, and its closing mark stands after "It cures kidney fever.", outside the bracket. The Codex does not decide which source the last clause belongs to, and records it as outside the brackets and so, on Clark's own marking, as root text. [USER: verify]

The two lines next to it are also *zho.sha* drugs: *snying.zho.sha* (line 948) ends "It cures heart fevers", and *gla.gor.zho.sha* (line 950) ends "cures spleen fever". Neither is the subject of this entry.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3417).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20. The sentence reads: "Nying shho sha, Khelma shho sha and Laagor shho sha cure hot disorders of the heart, kidneys and spleen respectively." Taken in order, the second drug is the kidney drug, which agrees with Clark's "kidney fever". The Codex reads "Khelma shho sha" as the same name as *mkhal.zho.sha* from the spelling and from the agreement of the three-part series with Clark's lines 948 to 950; the Men-Tsee-Khang text gives no Latin name in this sentence. The printed index lists "Khelma shho sha 205" (line 7406). [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (line 5380).* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints, on one line in the "K" block: "Khalma shho sha mkhal ma zho sha Phaseolus vulgaris". The phonetic name, the Tibetan transliteration and the scientific name therefore stand on a single line here (they are not split into separate columns), and the glossary names *Phaseolus vulgaris* for this drug, and not *Canavalia gladiata*. The extraction took that binomial as a separate row ("Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)", `names.csv` row 299), which is not what this line shows. No English name is printed on the line. The two Tibetan texts therefore disagree on the plant: Clark prints *Canavalia gladiata* and *Mucuna prurita*, and the glossary prints *Phaseolus vulgaris*. The Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Canavalia*, cowhage, *mkhal.zho.sha*, *Khelma* and *Khalma* found no entry on this drug. I_140 (Kapikacchu, *Mucuna pruriens*) and I_163 (*Mucuna pruriens*) are entries on *Mucuna pruriens*, the nearest binomial to Clark's second (*Mucuna prurita*, as printed), under the Sanskrit name. I_163 carries an FMT tag, and its prose gives a Tibetan name "Mu pa / hairy bean" with no locus; that is not the name *mkhal.zho.sha*, and I_163 has no row on the spine as far as the index file shows. I_689 (Common Bean, *Phaseolus vulgaris*) is the entry on the glossary's binomial and carries no FMT tag (its spine row lists Avicenna, Badianus and Chinese medicine). IV_11 (Chyawanprash) names *Canavalia gladiata* in its prose as an alternative identification of another plant in a Sushruta context, and it is not an entry on this drug. None is an entry on this Tibetan drug. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Canavalia* is a genus of legumes (Fabaceae), that seeds of *Canavalia* species are generally known to contain lectins (the best known is concanavalin A, from the jack bean, *C. ensiformis*), and that legume seeds of this kind are eaten only after cooking. *Mucuna* is a legume genus whose pods are covered in irritant hairs and whose seeds are generally known to contain levodopa. *Phaseolus vulgaris* is the common bean. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained, which may not be any of the three. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record, under the name *mkhal.zho.sha* or "Khelma shho sha", a drug for the kidney: "It cures kidney fever" (Clark, line 949) and it cures "hot disorders of the ... kidneys" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3417). These are the texts' claims, and the plant behind them is not settled. They are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation for this drug. The commentary layer of Clark's line says that the seeds have "sweet taste and oily power" and that the white type is "superior to the black or yellow types". No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identity of the Tibetan drug is uncertain between a *Canavalia*, a *Mucuna* and a *Phaseolus*, and the commentary layer is separate from the root text, so a modern study of one legume would not bear directly on it.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's line 949 prints "sweet taste and oily power" for the seeds, inside the square brackets and attributed to "hKhrungs.dpe", so in the commentary layer. No post-digestive taste is printed. The Men-Tsee-Khang sentence prints no quality, only that the drug cures a "hot disorder" of the kidneys. Sweet and oily are the qualities of the commentary, and the root-text layer prints none.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes this drug by what it cures, which is kidney fever, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text puts it in a series of three drugs for the heart, kidneys and spleen. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this drug places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: a search of both Tibetan texts for *mkhal.zho.sha*, "Khelma shho sha", "Khalma", cowhage and *Canavalia* returned only the lines above and the index and glossary entries. Clark's essence-extraction mentions in the materia medica (lines 853 and 971, for example) belong to other drugs and do not name this one. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a kidney drug in the Tibetan texts, and the two Tibetan texts print different plants for it. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. The identity is uncertain, and seeds of several legumes of this kind are harmful if eaten raw, so no plant or seed should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Kidney disease and fever need medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
