# Brine — Alme (Dioscorides 5-128), printed in the translation as "Muria"

## *A salty liquid or salt-derived preparation that Dioscorides says acts like ordinary salts; the translator's gloss is "Muriates — Chloride Salts" and the text prints the Latin name "Muria". The chapter does not say how it is made or what it looks like. Identification: brine, a salt-water preparation of chloride salts, from the Latin name "muria" and the translator's gloss only; confidence medium* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry.** Brine is named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk. The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-128, *Alme* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading line 10499, translator's gloss line 10501, text line 10502, book page 810 of this translation; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`; list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 854, "Alme", mineral). The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder, "brine (alme)", and this entry replaces it.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The whole chapter, in the translator's English, reads: "Muria has the same action as ordinary salts in cleansing, and it is a suppository for dysentery with gangrenous ulceration, and those with a history of sciatica. It is good in warm packs where seawater is effective." That is all that the chapter says. The translator's heading gloss is "SUGGESTED: Muriates — Chloride Salts". The chapter gives: a comparison ("the same action as ordinary salts in cleansing"); two uses as a suppository (dysentery with gangrenous ulceration, and sciatica in people with a history of it); and one use in warm packs, "where seawater is effective". It gives no place, no colour, no method of making it, no test of quality and no dose. The Greek heading is *Alme*, and the English of the chapter uses the Latin word *muria*; the translator's index lists "Muria 810" and "Muriates 810" (name index, near the end of the text). Whether *alme* and *muria* name exactly the same thing is not stated in the chapter. [USER: verify]

**The salts around it.** The chapter stands among the salts. The preceding chapters are 5-126, *Ales* ("Salts — Sodium Chloride, Ammonium Salts": "Mineral salt is the most useful... Thick sea salt must be taken, white and even... These salts all have similar properties — useful as an astringent, and to clean and dissolve, as well as repress, reduce the intensity of symptoms, and form scabs"), and 5-127, *Alos Achne* ("Sea Froth": "Spuma salis are fragments of the foaming sea found on the little rocks, and it has the same properties as salt"). The following chapter is 5-129, *Alos Anthos* ("flowers of salt", from the Nile and certain lakes). The words "the same action as ordinary salts" in 5-128 refer back to these. Salt itself is a spine entry in Volume I (I_345), and this entry does not repeat it. Dioscorides also mentions brine in the plant chapters as a preserving liquid, for example for olives and for the leaves of various herbs (chapters 1-139 on pickled olives, 2-205 on lepidium and 3-24 on eryngium; lines 4546, 6360 and 6729). Those are mentions of a pickling liquid in other drugs' chapters and are not this chapter's statement about *alme*.

A search of the other texts on disk found no Sushruta, Shennong or Ebers passage tied to this chapter, and none is claimed.

### Active Components

The chapter names no constituent. In general terms, brine is water with dissolved salts, chiefly sodium chloride, and sea water or salt-lake water also carries magnesium, calcium and other salts. The strength of Dioscorides' *muria* cannot be fixed from the text. [USER: verify with a source on Roman *muria* before adding any detail about how it was made.]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Cleansing like ordinary salts; as a suppository for dysentery with gangrenous ulceration and for sciatica; in warm packs where seawater is effective.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no strength, no method of making it, no frequency.

Strong salt solutions are irritating to the bowel and to raw tissue. The Codex gives no protocol, and a salt-water enema or pack in the manner of the chapter is not recommended.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives the forms of use only: as a suppository, and in warm packs ("warm packs where seawater is effective"). It gives no method of making it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any dose from this entry.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Concentrated salt solutions draw water and irritate mucous membranes, and sodium chloride is the main solute of sea water and of most brines. Saline solutions have long been used as washes in general. The chapter's disease names (dysentery with gangrenous ulceration, sciatica) are not translated into modern diagnoses. ### Contraindications

- **Do not use as a suppository, enema or pack.** Strong salt solutions can injure the bowel and raw tissue, and the chapter's conditions (dysentery, gangrenous ulcers, sciatica) need medical care.
- **Heart, kidney and blood-pressure conditions.** Salt loads matter in these. No statement in the sources retrieved. Avoid. [USER: verify]
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** No statement in the sources retrieved. Avoid. [USER: verify]
