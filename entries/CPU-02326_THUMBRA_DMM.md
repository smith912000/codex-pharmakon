# Thymbra — Thumbra (θύμβρα), Dioscorides 3-45

## *A small wild thyme-like plant of barren places, "similar to thyme, only smaller and more tender"; the Dioscorides text gives no binomial, and the 2000 English edition suggests Sisymbrium (Pliny), Serpyllum, Thymus serpyllum (creeping or wild thyme); the identification is the editor's, of low to medium confidence, and the plant is kept apart from the tragoriganum chapter (3-35) and the Thymbra wine (5-60) that the spine files under Satureia thymbra* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named by Dioscorides (DMM).** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder with the locus "DMM 3-45 (line 6914)"; this entry replaces it. The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. Spine row CPU-00414 (Thyme) is genus-level and CPU-01984 (*Satureia thymbra*) takes the *tragoriganum* chapter (3-35) and the wines (5-55, 5-60); neither folds in this chapter, and the *Satureia thymbra* entry says so.

**Greek, Dioscorides (DMM).** Chapter 3-45 is headed THUMBRA (line 6914). The editor's line reads: "SUGGESTED: Sisymbrium [Pliny] see 2-155, Serpyllum sylvestre [Fuchs], Serpyllum vulgare minus [Bauhin], Thymus serpyllum [Linnaeus] — Creeping Thyme, Wild Thyme, Mother of Thyme". That line is the editor's guess and not Dioscorides'. The full text of the chapter (lines 6916 to 6918) is: "Thymbra is also well known. It grows in barren and rough places — similar to thyme, only smaller and more tender, and bearing a stalk full of flowers of a greenish colour. It can do the same things as thyme (taken the same way) and it is suitable for use in health. There is also a cultivated satureia, of less value in everything than the wild, yet more effective for meat [sauce] because it does not have as much sharpness."

*The chapter it points to (3-44, THUMOS, line 6910 ff.).* Dioscorides says thyme "taken as a drink with salt and vinegar is able to drive out phlegmy matter through the bowels"; "a decoction with honey helps orthopnoea [form of asthma] and the asthmatic, expels worms and the menstrual flow, is an abortifacient, expels the afterbirth, and is urinary"; mixed with honey as a linctus "it makes matter come up [vomitory]"; applied with vinegar "it dissolves new swellings and clots of blood" and "takes away thymos and hanging warts"; applied with wine and polenta it is "good for hip pains"; eaten with meat "it is good for poor vision"; and it "is good instead of sauce for use in health." Chapter 3-45 says Thymbra "can do the same things as thyme (taken the same way)", so these are the uses the text gives to it by reference. They are not separately tested for Thymbra in the text.

*Other occurrences of the word.* The Greek word *thymbra* also occurs in other chapters of Dioscorides: in 4-178 (KOLOKUNTHIS, line 9647) as a name that "Zoroastres" gives to colocynth, which is a different plant; and in 5-60 (OINOS THUMBRITES, line 10094) as a wine made "in the same way" as thymites. Those are not this chapter and are not the subject here.

**What the text does not give.** No habitat beyond "barren and rough places", no gathering season, no dose, no preparation beyond "the same way" as thyme, and no synonyms. Unlike 3-44 it records no alternative names.

### Active Components

Dioscorides names no constituent. In general terms, the thyme-like plants of the mint family (Lamiaceae) carry volatile oils, among them phenols such as thymol and carvacrol in many species. This is stated of the family only; the species of 3-45 is not established. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a recommended practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** By reference to thyme: a purgative of phlegm with salt and vinegar, an aid to asthma and breathlessness, worms, the menstrual flow, the afterbirth, urine, swellings, warts, hip pains and poor sight, and as a culinary herb "for use in health".
- **What the text does not say.** A dose, a duration, or any use that differs from thyme's.

Several uses here concern abortion and the menstrual flow; the Codex gives no protocol and does not recommend them. As a seasoning, thyme-type herbs are ordinary food herbs; that is the only sense in which the chapter's last sentence bears on modern use, and the Codex adds no claim to it.

### Preparation & Dosing

Only what is printed: the plant is "taken the same way" as thyme (see the 3-44 forms above: a drink with salt and vinegar, a decoction with honey, a linctus with honey, applied with vinegar or with wine and polenta, or eaten with meat). No quantity is printed for Thymbra. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Thyme-type herbs are common culinary herbs whose volatile oils have antiseptic properties in the laboratory, and they are generally regarded as safe as seasoning in food amounts. The identification of the Dioscorides plant is the editor's suggestion, so nothing here is tied to a species. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** Chapter 3-45 prints no quality of its own (no "warming" or "astringent"). It refers to thyme, whose own chapter 3-44, in the lines read, also prints none. In the neighbouring *tragoriganum* chapter (3-35, line 6830), by contrast, Dioscorides writes "All are warming, urinary, and good for the intestines"; that is a different chapter and is not transferred here.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek, Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is attested for this plant. What the chapter offers a cultivator is the discipline of attention that runs through the book: a plain statement of the habitat ("barren and rough places"), of the form (smaller and more tender than thyme, with a stalk "full of flowers of a greenish colour") and of a cultivated look-alike (*satureia*) that is weaker but milder in sauces. That is a practice of identification by provenance and sensory comparison; it is not a cultivation status. No text read gives the plant a vital or spiritual role.

**Practitioner's note.** A small wild thyme-like herb that Dioscorides tells the reader to treat as thyme. A cultivator uses it as a herb for food and learns to tell the wild from the cultivated kind; nothing here supports more than that.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy.** The text lists the menstrual flow, the afterbirth and abortion among the uses of thyme, and Thymbra is said to do the same; avoid medicinal amounts in pregnancy. This is general caution and is not a source claim for Thymbra.
- **Identity.** The species is not settled; several plants carry the name, and the chapter's own look-alike *satureia* is a different herb. Do not gather on this entry. [USER: verify]
- **Essential oil.** The concentrated oil of thyme-type herbs is an irritant and is not a food; the chapter concerns the herb only.
- **Allergy.** People allergic to mint-family herbs should avoid it. General caution.
