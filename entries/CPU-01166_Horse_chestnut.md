# Horse chestnut (spavin, ergot of the horse leg) — Leichenes Hippon (LEICHENES HIPPON), "Spavins of Horses"
## *A hardened growth on the legs of the horse, at the knees and hooves; not the tree called horse chestnut* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 2-45, *Leichenes Hippon* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 5117-5119; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 235, "Spavins of Horses", animal). The chapter in this edition reads, in full:

"Spavins of Horses. The lichen [bony excrescence on legs] of horses is (according to description) that hardened substance which grows at their knees and hooves. Pounded into small pieces and taken in a drink with vinegar they are said to cure epilepsy."

Three points about the words. First, the chapter title is printed in this edition only in transliteration, LEICHENES HIPPON, and the English headings are "Spavins of Horses" and, in the text, "the lichen". The word "chestnut" is not printed in the chapter. The entry's name, "Horse chestnut (spavin, ergot of the horse leg)", is the spine's, and I could not confirm from the chapter that "chestnut" and "ergot" are names this text uses for the growth. [USER: verify] Second, the bracketed gloss "bony excrescence on legs" belongs to the edition's translator, and the sentence "according to description" is the edition's hedge that the identity of the substance is given at second hand. Third, "they" in the last sentence is plural: the chapter speaks of the pieces of the substance, not of one object.

The English word "chestnut" occurs elsewhere in the same edition only for plants: the sweet chestnut *Castanea vulgaris* (lines 3914, 4586) and the water chestnut (line 3649). The tree called horse chestnut is a different thing and has no place in this entry.

The chapter belongs to Book Two, "Living Creatures". Its neighbours show the context. Chapter 2-44, "Ass's Hooves" (lines 5114-5116), says that "two spoonfuls of an ass's hoof that has been burnt (taken in a drink daily for many days) are said to cure epilepsy", and 2-42, "Ass's Liver", that the roasted liver "is good for epilepsy but it should be taken while fasting" (line 5111). Chapter 2-45 is thus one of several animal parts the book assigns to epilepsy. Whether Dioscorides takes the use from a source is not stated.

### Active Components

The growth described is hardened, bony or horny tissue at the joints and hooves of the animal. Bone and horn are largely mineral (calcium phosphate) and structural protein (collagen, keratin). No analysis of the material the chapter means was retrieved, and no composition figures are offered. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The one use given is in a drink with vinegar for epilepsy. The Codex's application layer adds nothing, and the entry is not a protocol.

It is not a recommended practice. Epilepsy is a medical condition that needs diagnosis and modern treatment, and taking pounded animal tissue for it is not a treatment. The chapter is useful as a record of what a first-century pharmacopoeia assigned to the condition, in the same way as the ass's hoof and ass's liver chapters beside it.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparation is stated in the chapter: the growth is "pounded into small pieces and taken in a drink with vinegar". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] How often, for how long and in what volume of vinegar are not stated. Compare 2-44, which does give a measure (two spoonfuls of burnt ass's hoof, daily for many days); the 2-45 chapter does not.

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] General facts: epilepsy is a disorder of abnormal electrical activity in the brain, and seizures are controlled by anti-seizure medication and, in some cases, other medical measures. No animal bone or horn preparation has been shown to prevent or stop seizures. Swallowing unprepared animal tissue carries ordinary hygiene risks. The chapter's own phrase, "are said to cure", marks the claim as report and not as Dioscorides' own finding.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Greek / Dioscorides (DMM): no quality (warming, cooling, drying, astringent) is printed for this substance in 2-45, and none is assigned here.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. The chapter assigns the substance a single use against epilepsy, and nothing in it gives a cultivation status. What Dioscorides generally offers is a discipline of attention: provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay and detection of adulteration. The chapter has little of that: the only attention it records is the hedge "according to description", which shows him reporting the identity of the substance as given to him, not as observed. No amuletic or ritual use is recorded here.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. The chapter offers no cultivation claim and a remedy for a condition that needs medical care. The one thing worth carrying away is the habit the hedge models: to say plainly when an identification is at second hand.

### Contraindications

- **Not a recommended practice.** This is a historical record. Nothing here is advice to use horse tissue.
- **Epilepsy:** do not substitute any traditional preparation for prescribed treatment; seizures can be dangerous and are treatable.
- **Hygiene and infection:** pounded tissue from a live or dead horse's leg can carry bacteria and parasites.
- **Identity:** the substance is described "according to description" and its identity in modern terms is uncertain. [USER: verify]
- **Not the tree:** horse chestnut seed (the tree) is a different material and is not what this chapter describes.
