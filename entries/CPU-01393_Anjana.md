# Anjana — Anjana (Sushruta, as Bhishagratna prints it; also Rasanjana, Srotonjana)
## *The first-named drug of the Anjana'di group of Sushruta Sutra 38, for which the chapter gives no identification; in other chapters Bhishagratna glosses "Anjana" as "black Antimony" and also uses the word for collyrium (eye-salve) in general* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The Codex locus is *Sushruta Samhita*, Sutrasthana ch.38, the Anjana'di group (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch38.txt` lines 300-306, Bhishagratna Vol. I line 15642; name-index row `Sources/SS_Sushruta/extract/names.csv` line 218, which reads "group list (ch.38); no identification given here" and files the name under class "plant"). The passage was read in full. Chapter 38 opens (lines 8-10): "These drugs are usually made into thirty-seven different groups (Gana) which are as follows". The group then reads (lines 300-306):

"The Anjana'di Group :-The group known as the Anjanadi Gana consists of Anjana, Rasanjana, Nagapushpa, Priyangu, Nilotpala, Nalada, Nalina, Keshara and Madhuka. Metrical Texts :- An attack of haemoptysis readily yields to the curative virtue of the group under discussion. It is anti-toxic in its character and allays the internal burning sensation of the body."

That is all chapter 38 says about Anjana: it is named first in a group of nine drugs, and the group (not any one member) is credited with three effects: it relieves haemoptysis, it is anti-toxic, and it allays internal burning. The chapter gives no rasa, virya or vipaka for Anjana, no description of the material, and no statement that it is a mineral. The other members of the group are listed by name only. Rasanjana is named again in the Priyangvadi group (line 323; names.csv line 241), where it again has no identification.

**Where the word "anjana" is identified elsewhere in the text.** The Codex's own label ("collyrium mineral") rests on the translator's glosses, which differ from place to place, so they are set out here.
- Uttara ch.11, in an eye-disease recipe: "Hingu, (Asafoetida), Triphala, Yashti-madhu, Saindhava, Prapaundaika, Anjana (black Antimony), Tuttha (Sulphate of copper), and copper pasted together with..." (`uttara/SS_Uttara_ch11.txt` line 41).
- Uttara ch.10, a translator's footnote to a couplet: "Anjana (black-antimony) rubbed over a stone-slab with clarified butter and breast-milk is also recommended as a best remedy in such cases" (`uttara/SS_Uttara_ch10.txt` line 5).
- Uttara ch.50 (hiccough), where the translator writes "Anjana (Souvirdnjana)" (`uttara/SS_Uttara_ch50.txt` line 154); and Uttara ch.45 (haemorrhage, Rakta-pitta), a lambative of "Laja (parched paddy) and Anjana" with honey (line 223), whose footnote (line 248, Vol. III line 12010) reads: "Kdldnjana is a variant, in place of Laja and Anjana. For Kdldnjana Dallana reads Souvirinjana. This should be the proper reading". In these two places, then, the translator and the commentator Dallana name a particular kind, Souvirinjana.
- Uttara ch.60, in a list of exorcising articles: "Anjana (RasAnjana), Srotonjana, Naipdii, Haritdla and other articles" (`uttara/SS_Uttara_ch60.txt` line 296). The bracket is the translator's.
- Uttara ch.19: "Rasdnjana (Antimony)" (line 129); Chikitsa-sthana ch.24, "Collyrium :- Srotonjana, produced in the river Indus, is the best and purest of Collyhums [sic, OCR for Collyriums]" (`chikitsa/SS_Chikitsa_ch24.txt` line 112); Uttara ch.11 footnote (line 139): Dallana explains *Nadeya* as Saindhava "but it generally means Srotanjana (black antimony)".
- Sutra ch.1, a translator's footnote (`sutra/SS_Sutra_ch01.txt` line 479): "Oxide of Iron, sand, yellow sulphurate of arsenic (Orpiment), salt, Gairika (ferruginous earth), Rasdnjana (antimony) should be regarded as appertaining to the class of earthy substances."

**Anjana as a procedure.** In other chapters "anjana" is the generic word for eye-salve or collyrium and for its application, not a single drug: Sutra ch.7 speaks of the probe "used in applying Anjanams medicated collyria to the eyelids" (`sutra/SS_Sutra_ch07.txt` line 178); Chikitsa ch.24 says "The use of collyrium (Anjana) furthers the growth of large and beautiful eye lashes, cleanses the eyes by removing the unhealthy secretions..." (line 396); and Uttara ch.18 (Kriya-kalpa) classifies anjanas by purpose (see Preparation & Dosing). In these places the word may or may not refer to the drug.

**Identity.** The Codex entry is filed as a mineral, but the Sutra 38 entry is a bare name in a drug group, filed as "plant" in the name index, and the translation does not say what Anjana is there. In other places the same translation calls it black antimony, collyrium in general, and (via Dallana) Sauviranjana. Which material the Anjana'di group's first member is, and whether the group's Anjana, Rasanjana and Srotonjana are one substance or several, is not settled by the sources read. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None can be stated from the text. In general terms, black eye-cosmetic and eye-salve minerals in the medical and cosmetic traditions of many regions have included antimony sulphide (the mineral stibnite) and lead sulphide; antimony and lead compounds are both toxic if swallowed or used on broken tissue. This is a general statement about such materials, not a reading of Sushruta, and the sources read do not say that the Sushruta word means either of them.

---

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Sushruta names Anjana as a group member credited, as a group, with relief of haemoptysis, with being anti-toxic, and with allaying internal burning, and the same word is used throughout the ophthalmic chapters for eye-salves. The identity of the material is unresolved, the translator's most frequent gloss for it is a heavy-metal sulphide, and the application is to the eye, a site where contamination or toxic metals cause serious harm. The Codex supplies no modern protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives no preparation for Anjana as a single drug in Sutra 38. Where it describes eye-salves in general (Uttara ch.18, Kriya-kalpa), the following is printed, and is about the procedure, not about this drug:

- **Purposes** (lines 359-398): "Proper Anjana for Lekhana (scraping), Ropana (healing), or Prasadana (invigorating) purposes should be applied after the cleansing (purging, etc.) of the system in cases where the deranged bodily Doshas would manifest themselves in the region of the eye only." A Lekhana anjana uses "the drugs of one or more tastes (Rasa) except the sweet one"; a Ropana anjana "the drugs of bitter and astringent tastes (Rasa) mixed with (a little quantity of) clarified butter"; a Prasadana anjana "the drugs of sweet taste and with (a profuse quantity of) Sneha".
- **Times** (lines 399-402, with a translator's footnote): "in the morning, evening or in the night ... in accordance with the nature of the deranged bodily Dosha or Doshas".
- **Forms** (lines 425-430): "pills, liquid (Rasa-kriya) and powder each succeeding, one being more efficacious than the one preceding it".
- **Sizes and doses** (lines 431-442): the Varti pill is "equal to that of one and a half and twice as much as a Kalaya pulse for ocular affections in general"; the liquid dose "should be equal to that of the Varti"; the powder dose "twice, thrice and four times as much as would be contained at the end of a Salaka (rod)".
- **Vessel and rod** (lines 444-450): made of "gold, silver, horn, copper, Vaidurya (a kind of precious stone), bell-metal and iron respectively" according to the taste of the drugs.

Specific eye-salve recipes that name Anjana or Rasanjana exist (for example Uttara ch.19, where an anjana of Rasanjana, conch-shell, curd and Saindhava is "kept together for a period of half a fortnight" and applied "to the affected organ of the child", line 129), but they belong to the conditions treated and are not given here as a dose for Anjana. For the Anjana'di group itself, no preparation or dose is given. No dose for the drug alone is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

Outside the eye: Sutra ch.25 uses a powder of "Priyangu, Anjanam, Yasthyahva and Rodhra" to dust a sutured ulcer (`sutra/SS_Sutra_ch25.txt` line 162); Chikitsa ch.19 uses a plaster of "Gairika, Anjana, Yashti-madhu, S'drivd, Us'ira, Padmaka, (red) Chandana and Utpala mixed with a Sneha" for a Pittaja Upadamsha (`chikitsa/SS_Chikitsa_ch19.txt` line 233). No amounts are given in the lines read.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** None is given for Anjana. Chapter 38 prints no rasa, virya or vipaka for the drug, and a search of `sutra/SS_Sutra_ch40.txt` to `SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` (which include chapters 45 and 46) for "anjana" and "antimon" found no entry for it. The only qualities printed belong to the group: it is "anti-toxic" and "allays the internal burning sensation of the body". Uttara ch.18's footnote ties the choice of anjana to the taste of its ingredients (acid and saline tastes for deranged Vayu, astringent for Pitta, astringent, bitter and pungent for Kapha), but this is a rule for compounding, not a taste assigned to Anjana. The class "earthy substances" quoted from the Sutra ch.1 footnote is a translator's note about Rasanjana, not a quality of the Sutra 38 Anjana.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Anjana sits in the Anjana'di *gana* of Sutra 38, a therapeutic grouping credited with relief of haemoptysis, anti-toxic action and relief of internal burning. It is not named in any rasayana or vajikarana chapter that was read, so no rasayana, vajikarana, ojas, tejas or prana role is attested for it. The ophthalmic chapters treat anjana as a local eye measure to be applied after the body has been cleansed (Uttara ch.18, line 359 onward). That is a therapeutic frame, not a cultivation one. Whether the Sutra 38 Anjana is the same material as the black antimony glossed elsewhere is not settled.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to take from it for cultivation. The text records a drug of uncertain identity used on the eyes, and the translator's most common reading of it is a toxic metal sulphide. It is not a recommended practice, and the tradition's own rule (apply only after cleansing, by the purpose of the case) is the only guidance the sources supply. No dose for the drug is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Toxic metals:** the translator's usual gloss ("black Antimony", "Antimony") is a heavy-metal material; antimony and lead compounds are poisonous.
- **The eye:** anything placed in the eye must be sterile; non-sterile mineral powders cause infection and injury. The text itself restricts the use of collyrium (Chikitsa ch.24, lines 112-139: "its use is forbidden just after taking one's meal or bath ... and after the fatigue of vomiting, or riding, etc., nor after keeping late hours and also not during an attack of fever").
- **Unidentified material:** the identification is not settled; nothing here should be sourced or used on the strength of the name.
- **Serious conditions:** haemoptysis (coughing of blood) and eye disease need medical assessment.
