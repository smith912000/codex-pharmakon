# Purple Viper's Bugloss

## *Echium plantagineum* L. | Family: Boraginaceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Cool, moist, mucilaginous-emollient by texture; hepatotoxic-cumulative by chemistry — a plant whose *sensory* character and *metabolic* character point in opposite directions.

**Training Phase:** Restricted — Study Only. Not to be ingested at any phase. Included in the Codex as a hazard-literacy entry and as a food-chain contaminant, not as a materia medica item.

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Echium plantagineum* is a Mediterranean and western-European annual/biennial of dry disturbed ground, with rough bristled leaves, a spotted or flecked stem, and a curved violet-purple corolla from which the stamens protrude. It was carried to Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, Argentina and the western United States as an ornamental and a garden escapee, and in the southern hemisphere it became one of the defining invasive pasture weeds of the temperate dryland zone. Its folk record is therefore split in two: an Old-World record of a minor emollient herb hardly distinguished from its relatives, and a New-World record — barely 150 years old — in which the plant acquired two contradictory names and a genuine agricultural doctrine.

**European folk usage (Doctrine of Signatures / Dioscoridean inheritance) — "viper's bugloss," "echium"**
The genus name descends from Greek *ekhis*, a viper. The classical and early-modern European doctrine attached to the genus — most explicitly to *Echium vulgare*, the plant that actually carries the name in the herbals — held that the flecked and speckled stem resembled a snake's skin, that the nutlets resembled a viper's head, and that the protruding style resembled a forked tongue; therefore the plant was held to cure the bite of the serpent it resembled. This is the textbook example of the Doctrine of Signatures used to teach the Doctrine of Signatures. Its transfer to *E. plantagineum* is largely by generic association rather than by a distinct textual tradition. **[USER: verify]** any claim that *E. plantagineum* specifically — as opposed to *E. vulgare* — carries an independent classical snakebite indication; the herbal record here is thin and mostly generic.

Beyond the serpent doctrine, European use of the genus was mundane and topical: the mucilaginous, bristly-leaved aerial parts as a cooling emollient poultice, and the flowers as a minor diaphoretic infusion. The recorded indications are the standard demulcent set — coughs, inflamed skin, "hot" conditions — and are not species-specific.

**Australian settler and pastoral usage — "Paterson's Curse" / "Salvation Jane"**
This is the entry's real material, and it is not a healing tradition; it is a working doctrine about a plant, held simultaneously in two contradictory forms by two groups of people who both had evidence.

*Paterson's Curse* names the plant as a catastrophe. It invades overgrazed and degraded pasture, outcompetes sown species, is unpalatable relative to good feed, and — the operative fact — poisons the stock that eat it over months. The name attaches the blame to the Paterson family, credited with introducing it as a garden ornamental. **[USER: verify]** the specific introduction narrative and family attribution before printing it as history; it is widely repeated in Australian agricultural literature but functions partly as folk etymology.

*Salvation Jane* names the same plant as a rescue. In drought, when sown pasture has failed and there is nothing else standing green, *E. plantagineum* is still standing green — it is deep-rooted, drought-tolerant, and it holds a paddock together when nothing else will. Stock eat it and survive the season. It is also a heavy nectar producer and therefore a significant honey flora. Beekeepers, in particular, have never regarded it as a curse.

Both names are correct. The plant does not change; the timescale of the observation changes. On a short timescale — one drought, one season — the plant is feed and the alternative is starvation. On a long timescale — years of cumulative intake — the plant is a hepatotoxin and the animal dies of a liver that has been quietly destroyed. This is not a case of two traditions disagreeing because one is wrong. It is a case of a toxicological property (cumulative, delayed, irreversible) being invisible at the timescale at which one of the two observations was made.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

Two doctrines are on the table, and they deserve opposite verdicts.

The **serpent signature** should be deflated without hesitation. The spotted stem, the viper's-head nutlet, the forked-tongue style, and the inferred antidotal power against snakebite constitute a chain of reasoning with no chemical anchor whatsoever. There is no snake-venom-antagonising principle here. The irony is sharper than that: the doctrine promises that the plant *neutralises* a venom, when the plant in fact *is* one — a slow one, delivered by mouth rather than by fang, taking months rather than minutes. The signature identified a real association between this plant and death, and then inverted its direction. It is a good teaching case precisely because the signature is so visually compelling and so completely wrong.

The **two-names doctrine**, by contrast, maps beautifully onto real chemistry and deserves to be preserved intact.

The pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) of *Echium* are not acutely toxic in the way that, say, a cardiac glycoside is. They are protoxins. They require hepatic bioactivation — CYP450-mediated oxidation of the necine base — to generate reactive dehydropyrrolizidine (pyrrolic) esters, which alkylate proteins and DNA in the hepatocyte and, critically, in the hepatic sinusoidal endothelium. The consequences are structural and cumulative: antimitotic damage produces **megalocytosis** (hepatocytes that grow but cannot divide, yielding grotesquely enlarged cells), progressive fibrosis and cirrhosis, veno-occlusive changes, and — because the failing liver can no longer clear phylloerythrin, a chlorophyll breakdown product from the rumen — **hepatogenous (secondary) photosensitisation**: sun-exposed unpigmented skin blisters and sloughs. An animal on a green paddock in bright sun, burning on its white patches, is telling you its liver is gone.

Every element of that cascade is *slow* and *silent*. There is no warning signal at the moment of eating. There is no acute illness to teach an animal or a stockman that this plant is dangerous. The dose accumulates across a season and the damage integrates across seasons. The chemistry, in other words, is precisely engineered to produce a two-names doctrine: a plant that saves your stock this drought and kills them the drought after next. "Salvation Jane" is the correct read of the first derivative. "Paterson's Curse" is the correct read of the integral. The tradition split because the molecule has a delay line in it.

The cultivator's corollary is simple and worth stating: this is not a plant to grow. In most Australian states it is a declared weed under statute, and cultivation or sale carries legal consequence. Where it appears, control is by preventing seed set — the seedbank is long-lived and dense stands regenerate for years. Grazing management (avoiding sustained exposure, particularly in sheep and horses; providing alternative feed) is a mitigation, not a solution. If it is on your ground, your task is to remove it, and to keep your bees off it if you sell honey.

### Active Components

**Pyrrolizidine alkaloids (the defining class).** *E. plantagineum* is a 1,2-unsaturated PA producer — the toxic subclass. **Echimidine** is the principal alkaloid. Recent chemistry substantially complicates this picture: Gleńsk et al. (2022) determined by NMR that the single HPLC peak conventionally reported as echimidine (m/z 398 [M+H]) in fact co-elutes with two isomers, **echihumiline** and **hydroxymyoscorpine**, under standard acidic HPLC conditions — the first report of these two in the species — and that before zinc reduction the alkaloids were present ~90% as the **N-oxides**, eluting as a single peak. Their retrospective analysis found the same three-isomer co-occurrence in material collected in New Zealand, Argentina and the USA (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27092869)). The practical implication is that published "echimidine content" figures for this species may misrepresent the true alkaloid composition, which matters for both regulation and toxicology.

The N-oxide point is not a technicality. PA N-oxides are the dominant storage form in the living plant, are more water-soluble, transfer readily into nectar and hence into honey, and are reduced back to the parent tertiary alkaloid *in vivo* — meaning the N-oxide is not a detoxified form but a delivery form. Any analytical method that does not reduce N-oxides before quantification will under-report total PA burden, sometimes severely.

Additional PAs reported from the genus include lycopsamine-type esters; the species profile also carries the usual Boraginaceae accompaniments — **allantoin**, **mucilage/polysaccharide**, **rosmarinic acid** and other caffeic acid derivatives, and **flavonoids**. Moreira et al. (2020) characterised the polyphenol fraction of *E. plantagineum* honeys and found several polyphenols present (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2020.127169)). The seed oil of the genus is a commercial source of stearidonic and gamma-linolenic acid; note that refined *Echium* seed oil is a distinct commercial article from the aerial parts and is processed specifically to remove PAs — **do not read any part of this entry as applying to refined seed oil, and do not read seed-oil safety data back onto the herb.**

**Distinguishing *E. plantagineum* from *E. vulgare* (catalogued separately in this Codex).** *E. vulgare* is typically a stiffer, taller biennial with narrow, many-flowered lateral cymes, numerous unequal exserted stamens (usually 4–5 clearly protruding), and narrow stem leaves. *E. plantagineum* is softer-haired, with broad, prominently veined basal leaves (the *plantagineum* — plantain-like — epithet), a larger and more flaring corolla, and typically only 2 stamens exserted. Both are PA producers; the alkaloid profiles differ, with echimidine prominent in *plantagineum*. Betteridge et al. (2005) validated their honey PA method using *E. plantagineum* extracts and then applied it to honeys attributed to *E. vulgare*, finding 7 of 9 floral honeys positive for PAs and N-oxides characteristic of *E. vulgare* (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf0480952)) — i.e. neither species is the safe one.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

None. There is no Kenetica use case, at any phase, by any route, in any preparation.

This entry exists for three reasons, none of them therapeutic.

**First, as the Codex's reference case for cumulative protoxicity.** Most of the hazard entries in this volume describe substances that hurt you promptly enough to teach you. *Echium plantagineum* is the canonical demonstration that a plant can be harmless-feeling on every single exposure and lethal in aggregate. The practitioner-level lesson is that *absence of acute effect is not evidence of safety*, and that this is the specific failure mode against which traditional empiricism has the least defence. A tradition learns by feedback. Remove the feedback and the tradition learns the wrong thing — and it did, and it named the plant Salvation.

**Second, as a food-chain exposure.** This is the rare case where the human exposure route is not a medicine at all but an ordinary grocery item. Honey from *Echium* nectar carries PAs. Beales et al. (2004), in a survey deliberately biased toward PA-producing floral sources, detected PAs up to approximately 2000 ppb in a honey attributed to *E. plantagineum*; up to ~800 ppb in honeys the collector had not attributed to any PA source; and up to ~250 ppb in retail blended honeys carrying no labelling to suggest a PA floral source. About 30% of the 63 samples had none detected, and some showed co-presence of PAs from multiple sources such as *E. plantagineum* and *Heliotropium europaeum* (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf049102p)). He et al. (2020) analysed 255 Chinese retail honeys from 17 regions and found 58% PA-positive, total content 0.2–281.1 μg/kg, with echimidine (assigned to *E. plantagineum*) among the three predominant PAs alongside monocrotaline and lycopsamine; applying a potency-weighted risk model, they estimated 12% of the PA-contaminated honeys might pose a potential health risk (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envpol.2020.115542)). The detection of PAs in honey from this species dates back at least to Culvenor, Edgar & Smith (1981) (PubMed; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf00107a018)).

The blended-honey finding is the one that should change behaviour. A consumer cannot see the floral source. Neither, often, can the packer.

**Third, as a boundary marker for the Boraginaceae.** Comfrey, borage, heliotrope, hound's-tongue and the echiums share this chemistry to varying degrees. A practitioner who understands why this entry is closed understands why several neighbouring entries carry restrictions.

### Scientific Correlation

**Graded honestly, including the nulls.**

**STRONG — PA hepatotoxicity of the plant's own alkaloids, in vitro.** Gleńsk et al. (2022) tested the isolated alkaloids in rat hepatocyte primary culture and found concentration-dependent inhibition of hepatocyte viability across 3–300 μg/mL, with mean IC50 values from 9.26 to 14.14 μg/mL ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27092869)). This is species-specific data on this species' own alkaloids — not congener extrapolation.

**STRONG — Honey as a real human exposure route.** Four independent studies retrieved here converge: Culvenor et al. 1981 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf00107a018)); Beales et al. 2004, up to ~2000 ppb in *E. plantagineum*-attributed honey and ~250 ppb in unlabelled retail blends ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf049102p)); Betteridge et al. 2005 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf0480952)); He et al. 2020, 58% of 255 Chinese retail honeys PA-positive with echimidine among the top three PAs ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envpol.2020.115542)). The transfer is established.

**NULL — and it matters.** Moreira et al. (2020) searched one monofloral and two multifloral *E. plantagineum* honeys for PAs and **detected none**, while characterising several polyphenols and showing the honey extracts reduced NO in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages by up to 40% at 0.25 mg/mL ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2020.127169)). This is a genuine negative and must not be buried. Read it correctly: it does **not** overturn the positive surveys, because PA content in honey is enormously variable by season, region, floral competition and analytical method — Beales et al. found ~30% of their own deliberately-biased sample set PA-free. What the null establishes is that *Echium* honey is not uniformly contaminated, and therefore that a floral-source label is a poor predictor in either direction. Absence in three samples is not absence in the commodity.

**MODERATE, with a methodological caveat that cuts both ways — the analytical picture is unsettled.** The Gleńsk finding that echimidine co-elutes with echihumiline and hydroxymyoscorpine, and that ~90% of alkaloid is present as N-oxide forming a single pre-reduction peak ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27092869)), means published composition figures for this species — including some feeding into regulation — may be describing a mixture as a single compound. This is an honest "we know less than the decimal places suggest" flag.

**WEAK / EXTRAPOLATED — the classical veterinary syndrome.** Megalocytosis, cirrhosis, veno-occlusive disease and hepatogenous photosensitisation are the well-established PA syndrome and are attributed to this plant throughout Australian veterinary practice. That attribution is not in serious doubt, but **no in vivo feeding study of *E. plantagineum* itself was retrieved in this run** — the syndrome as described here rests on the general PA literature plus field attribution. Labelled as extrapolation, not as species-specific experimental data. **[USER: verify]** before citing any specific morbidity or mortality figure.

**UNSUPPORTED — snakebite.** No evidence retrieved, none expected. Deflated above.

**UNSUPPORTED — the demulcent/diaphoretic folk indications.** No clinical data retrieved for this species. Even if the mucilage and rosmarinic acid do what mucilage and rosmarinic acid do, the risk-benefit is absurd: every one of those effects is available from a dozen non-hepatotoxic plants. Data-sparse, and the sparseness is irrelevant to the verdict.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

The Old-World read — cool, moist, softening, "for hot and dry conditions" — is a straightforward reading of texture: a bristled leaf that yields mucilage, an emollient poultice, a demulcent infusion. It is honest sensory empiricism and it is entirely superficial. It describes the plant's surface and says nothing about its metabolism.

The Kenetica classification must therefore be written in two registers, because the plant genuinely has two:

- **Surface register:** cool, moist, mucilaginous, emollient. Real, verifiable by touch, and dangerously reassuring.
- **Depth register:** cumulative-destructive, hepatotropic, latent. Nothing in the surface register predicts it.

The Australian pastoral doctrine is the only tradition on record that captured the depth register, and it captured it by accident, by naming the plant twice and refusing to reconcile the names. That refusal was epistemically correct. **Filed under a permanent classification of *Latent Toxin*:** substances whose energetic surface and metabolic depth are decoupled, where the surface reading is not merely incomplete but actively misleading, and where the tradition's confidence is inversely related to its safety.

### Contraindications

**ABSOLUTE — all persons, all routes, all doses.** *Echium plantagineum* aerial parts, root, flower and preparations thereof are not to be ingested, infused, tinctured, smoked, or applied topically.

- **Hepatic:** Contraindicated in all hepatic disease, and equally in healthy livers — PA damage is initiated in normal hepatocytes by normal CYP-mediated metabolism. There is no protective baseline.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** Absolute contraindication. PAs cross the placenta and are excreted in milk. Neonatal and infant hepatic veno-occlusive disease from maternal PA exposure is a recognised outcome of the PA class.
- **Infants and children:** Absolute. Lower body mass against a genotoxic, cumulative exposure. The honey route is the concerning one here.
- **Concurrent hepatotoxins:** Alcohol, paracetamol at any regular dose, methotrexate, azole antifungals, and other PA sources (comfrey, coltsfoot, butterbur, borage leaf, heliotrope, hound's-tongue, some traditional Chinese and Ayurvedic articles). **PA exposure is additive across sources.** The relevant question is never "is this dose safe" but "what is this person's total lifetime PA burden."
- **CYP450 status:** Because bioactivation is CYP-mediated, inducers may plausibly increase toxicity. **[USER: verify]** — this is mechanistic inference, not retrieved data.
- **Genotoxicity / carcinogenicity:** 1,2-unsaturated PAs are a genotoxic class; the dehydropyrrolizidine metabolites form DNA adducts. There is no threshold below which genotoxic risk is defined as zero. This is why regulators pursue ppb-level limits in honey rather than a tolerable herbal dose.
- **Occupational / handling:** Bristles cause mechanical dermatitis. Handle with gloves during control work; do not compost dense flowering material into vegetable beds; do not burn large quantities in a confined space. **[USER: verify]** whether any inhalational PA hazard from smoke is documented — none was retrieved this run and it should not be asserted.
- **Legal:** A declared/noxious weed across much of Australia. Cultivation, sale and transport are restricted or prohibited by jurisdiction. Check local statute; this is a plant you may be legally obliged to destroy.
- **Apiculture:** Beekeepers siting hives near *Echium* stands should assume PA transfer into the crop regardless of yield attractiveness, and should not sell such honey as food without analysis that includes N-oxide reduction. Note that the null result of Moreira et al. shows analysis can come back clean — which is a reason to test, not a reason to assume.

**Recognition of poisoning (livestock):** Weight loss and ill-thrift despite adequate feed; jaundice; photosensitisation of unpigmented, sun-exposed skin; neurological signs (hepatic encephalopathy) in late stages; sudden death after apparent health. Onset may follow months of grazing and may present *after* the animal has been moved off the plant. Veterinary attention required; the hepatic lesion is not reversible.
