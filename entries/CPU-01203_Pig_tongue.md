# Pig tongue — rGyud-bzhi, "Pig tongue" (tongue of the pig, listed under "Meat" in the animal medicines)
## *Tongue of the domestic pig; the translator prints only "Pig tongue", with no Tibetan name and no binomial* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Pig tongue is listed in the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, the chapter on the efficacy of medicinal substances, in Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). It falls in section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), whose thirteen headings are "horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). Pig tongue stands under the heading "Meat" (line 1161), in the long line 1170, in a run of tongues and organs: "Wolf's stomach: [has twelve names] It increases digestive heat and digests undigested food, [especially meat]. Its tongue removes swellings of the tongue. Pig tongue: removes bone warts. Dog tongue: [has ten names and] heals all types of wounds. Donkey tongue: [has fourteen names]. It halts diarrhoea." The pig-tongue clause carries no bracketed comment and no name count. Clark says in his introduction (line 80) that he has "interpolated liberally from the Blue Aquamarine commentary"; the bracketed passages in this chapter look like such interpolations, but the lines read do not state the bracket convention outright. The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3536) has the same neighbours but a different indication: "Wolf tongue reduces swelling of the tongue. Pig tongue removes fragmented bones from comminuted fractures. Dog tongue heals wounds. Donkey tongue cures diarrhea." Clark therefore reads "bone warts" where Men-Tsee-Khang reads removal of fragmented bones in comminuted fractures. The Tibetan wording is not in the files read, so which rendering is closer cannot be said. [USER: verify] Elsewhere Clark uses the same words "bone warts" for snake meat, which "heals bone warts" (line 1165), so the phrase is the translator's consistent rendering of one Tibetan complaint; what that complaint is, the lines read do not explain. [USER: verify]

Only the tongue is covered by this entry. Other pig parts are separate items in the same chapter (pig bone, line 1160; pig blood, line 1176; pig fat, line 1180; pig's stool, line 1199) and are not folded in here. Clark's general statement on meats, that "Pork is cool and light, heals wounds and eliminates brown phlegm" (line 677), concerns flesh as food.

### Active Components

The tongue is a muscular organ covered by a mucous membrane. Like other skeletal muscle it is mostly water and protein (including collagen and other connective tissue), with fat, and small amounts of vitamins and minerals; it also holds taste buds and salivary-gland tissue. No composition specific to this entry was read, and no active principle is claimed by the sources. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is one indication in a materia medica list: pig tongue "removes bone warts" (Clark) or "removes fragmented bones from comminuted fractures" (Men-Tsee-Khang). The Codex offers no protocol. Bone injury and bony growths call for clinical assessment, and eating or applying raw animal tissue carries infection risk (see Contraindications). The Tantra gives no preparation, dose or route in these lines. The list pairs the tongue of one animal with one complaint and the tongue of another animal with another; that pairing is the tradition's own organising principle and is recorded as such, with no reason given in the lines read. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Bone is a living mineralised tissue that heals by its own repair process; setting and immobilising a displaced fracture is a medical matter, and nothing eaten or applied from an animal tongue removes a bony fragment. Raw or undercooked pork can carry parasites and bacteria, among them *Trichinella* and the pork tapeworm *Taenia solium*, and organ meats are handled under food-safety rules in most countries. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The Clark line (1170) gives an indication only. No taste, potency, post-digestive taste or humoral action is printed for pig tongue in the sources retrieved. Clark's general statements on meats (line 673, that meats are "sweet tasting" with "a sweet post-digestive taste"; line 677, pork "cool and light") concern flesh eaten as food and are not applied to this listed tongue. ### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* Neither translation read assigns pig tongue any cultivation framing. It is a short indication in a list of animal medicines, and the complaint it addresses is a bone condition. The frame around it is the tradition's own: the project's cultivation spec records that the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) are derived from the three poisons and that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root. That point comes from the spec and not from any line about pig tongue. No source read attaches pig tongue to *bcud len* (essence extraction), and there is no formal Tibetan category of substances for subtle-body practice. Clark's remark (line 57) that "only very few animal products are used" nowadays, although many are documented, is a note on usage, not on status.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a one-line indication in a list of animal parts, the two translations disagree about what the indication is, and it brings no cultivation claim. Its use today is as a record of what the Tantra says.

### Contraindications

- **Infection and parasites:** raw or undercooked pork and offal can carry bacteria and parasites; the entry recommends no raw or home preparation.
- **Not a treatment for bone injury:** a fracture or bony growth needs clinical assessment; do not rely on this line.
- **Allergy and diet:** pork allergy, and religious or dietary rules that exclude pork or offal, are matters this entry notes without taking a position.
- **Legal and animal-welfare status:** obtaining the tongue requires slaughter, which is regulated in most places. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** killing an animal for a substance with no supported benefit is hard to justify, and Clark's omens section (line 431) values saving animals from slaughter; that passage is about merit and omens, not about medicine.
