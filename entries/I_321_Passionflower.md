# I-321: Passiflora incarnata (Passionflower)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-321 | Passifloraceae**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Passiflora incarnata* L.
**Family:** Passifloraceae

**Common Names:**
- English: Passionflower, Purple Passionflower, Maypop, Wild Apricot
- German: *Passionsblume* ("passion flower")
- French: *Fleur de la Passion*, *Grenadille*
- Spanish: *Flor de la Pasión*, *Maracuyá* (colloquially, though *P. edulis* is true maracuya/passion fruit)
- Latin: *Passiflora* = "passion flower" — from Latin *passio* (suffering) + *flos* (flower)
- Cherokee: *Ocoee* — gave name to Ocoee River in Tennessee; *P. incarnata* indigenous to southeastern North America

**Etymology:**
*Passiflora* — named by Spanish Jesuit missionaries in 16th-century South America who interpreted the flower's morphology as symbolic of the Passion of Christ:
- 3 stigmas → 3 nails of the cross
- 5 anthers → 5 wounds of Christ
- Corona filaments → crown of thorns
- 10 petals/sepals → 10 faithful apostles (excluding Judas and Peter)
- Tendrils → whips used to flog Christ
- Round fruit → sponge soaked in vinegar

This botanical-theological interpretation made *Passiflora* one of the most religiously charged plant names in the Codex. *incarnata* = "flesh-colored" — the reddish-purple corona filaments.

**Disambiguation from Passion Fruit:**
*Passiflora incarnata* (passionflower — medicinal, native North America) ≠ *Passiflora edulis* (passion fruit — culinary, native South America). Both are *Passiflora* species but different plants with different primary uses. Medicinally, *P. incarnata* is the Commission E-approved species; *P. edulis* is the commercial passion fruit juice/food species.

**Morphology:**
Perennial vine; climbing via tendrils; 3–9 m; deeply lobed leaves; extraordinary complex flowers — white petals + striking purple corona filaments; edible oval fruit (maypop; yellowish when ripe; edible but bland); native to southeastern US, naturalized in Mediterranean Europe.

**Parts Used:** Aerial parts (leaves + stems + some flower) — harvested during or just after flowering; dried.

**The Cross-Cultural Cognitive Misattribution:**
The missionaries who named *Passiflora* as the "passion flower" based on Christological symbolism created one of the most elaborate taxonomic theological interpretations in botanical history. The same physical flower was interpreted entirely differently by the Cherokee and other southeastern US tribes — as a sedative, food plant, and fever herb — with no religious symbolism whatsoever. Two cultures encountering the same plant simultaneously extrapolated in entirely opposite directions: one toward transcendent theological allegory, one toward empirical pharmacological observation. The pharmacological tradition proved pharmacologically valid; the theological interpretation was projective but culturally generative (drove widespread European knowledge of the plant).

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domains:**

1. **Anxiolytic / sedative** — North American indigenous primary; Commission E; GABA-A mechanism; **Level B**
2. **Sleep (hypnotic)** — Commission E; Level B
3. **Antispasmodic** — South American indigenous; flavonoid mechanism; Level C
4. **Analgesic** — North American indigenous (Cherokee); Level C

**The Chrysin and GABA-A Story:**
Early passionflower research proposed **chrysin** (a flavone) as the primary anxiolytic compound via GABA-A benzodiazepine site binding. This was pharmacologically exciting — a plant flavone with benzodiazepine-like binding. However, chrysin's oral bioavailability is very poor (<1%), making it pharmacologically implausible as the primary mechanism at dietary doses. Later research shifted to **GABA-increasing effects** mediated by passionflower's flavonoid complex acting on GABA-A receptors via multiple partial agonist sites — not the benzodiazepine site specifically, but other modulatory sites. The mechanism remains partially elucidated. Commission E and ESCOP approved passionflower based on clinical evidence despite the incomplete mechanistic picture.

**Energetic Classification:**
- ☿ Shen (neuromodulatory): Primary — anxiolytic; hypnotic

**Primary Tier: ☿ Shen (anxiolytic-hypnotic)**

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Flavonoids (Primary Class)

**1. Vitexin (C-glycosyl flavone) — PRIMARY marker compound**
- European Pharmacopoeia standardization marker for *P. incarnata*
- GABA-A modulation; mild anxiolytic
- Anti-inflammatory; antioxidant

**2. Orientin** — C-glycosyl flavone; similar to vitexin; GABA-A interaction
**3. Isovitexin, Isoorientin** — related glycosyl flavones; anti-anxiety
**4. Chrysin** — flavone; GABA-A benzodiazepine site partial agonist (low bioavailability limits clinical relevance)
**5. Luteolin** — anti-inflammatory; antioxidant; GABA-A modulatory
**6. Quercetin** — anti-inflammatory; anti-anxiety (5-HT2C antagonism; serotonergic)

### Alkaloids

**7. Harman (harmane), Harmaline, Harmine (trace)** — beta-carboline alkaloids (MAO inhibitors at high concentrations)
- MAO-A inhibition → increased serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine; mild antidepressant at relevant concentrations
- At trace levels in plant: MAO inhibition clinically insignificant; at extracts with concentrated alkaloids: possible interactions

**8. Passiflorine** — indole alkaloid; sedative (historical claim; poorly characterized)

### Other

**9. GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid)** — present in plant tissue; direct GABA supply (though CNS penetration minimal via oral route)
**10. Maltol** — flavoring compound; mild sedative in rodent models

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### Anxiolytic / Sedative (Primary)
- **Flavonoid complex (vitexin, orientin + related) → GABA-A receptor positive allosteric modulation (PAM)** → enhanced chloride conductance → reduced neuronal excitability → anxiolysis
- **NOT primarily via benzodiazepine site** (despite chrysin's theoretical BZD-site activity, bioavailability is too low)
- **Quercetin → 5-HT2C partial antagonism** → additional serotonergic anxiolytic contribution
- **Harman alkaloids (trace) → MAO-A mild inhibition** → modest serotonin preservation → antidepressant adjunct

### Antispasmodic
- **Flavonoids → smooth muscle Ca²⁺ channel modulation** → reduced GI + uterine spasm
- Commission E: antispasmodic secondary indication

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Anxiety:**
- **Akhondzadeh et al. 2001** (*Journal of Clinical Pharmacy and Therapeutics*): RCT; *P. incarnata* extract vs. oxazepam for generalized anxiety disorder; n=36; 4 weeks; comparable efficacy on Hamilton Anxiety Scale; passionflower had significantly less occupational impairment adverse effect
- **Movafegh et al. 2008** (*Anesthesia & Analgesia*): RCT; passionflower tablet 500 mg vs. placebo as pre-operative anxiolytic; n=60; significant anxiety reduction without sedation
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (RCTs; comparable to benzodiazepine in one RCT)

**Sleep:**
- Open-label studies and one crossover RCT (Ngan & Conduit 2011, *Phytotherapy Research*): passionflower tea vs. placebo; significant improvement in sleep quality diary entries
- Commission E approved for "nervous unrest and mild sleep disorders"
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (RCT + Commission E)

**ADHD (Children):**
- Akhondzadeh et al. 2005 (*Phytotherapy Research*): passionflower vs. methylphenidate for ADHD; comparable efficacy on parent-rated scales; passionflower significantly less social problems adverse effect
- Small n; single study; needs replication
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (single RCT; preliminary)

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The Theological Flower:**
*Passiflora incarnata*'s flower is among the most structurally complex in temperate North America — the concentric layers of corona filaments (purple-tipped white threads), the central gynophore rising with three stigmas and five pendant anthers, the white petals and sepals beneath, the whole assembly suggesting mechanical precision and exotic provenance. It was genuinely shocking to 16th-century European missionaries encountering it for the first time in the New World — nothing in European flora prepared them for this flower. The theological interpretation was one response to the cognitive dissonance of encountering radical botanical novelty; the pharmacological investigation another. Both responses were generated by the same encounter with an extraordinary flower.

**The Cherokee Analgesic Record:**
Cherokee traditional medical documentation (James Mooney, 19th-century ethnobotanical records; Daniel Moerman's *Native American Ethnobotany* database) records *Passiflora incarnata* (ocoee) for: root infusion for liver complaints; leaves for cuts and bruises; root tea for "boils, headaches"; plant parts for anxiety and sleeplessness. This constitutes a documented traditional record from the plant's native habitat that predates all European pharmacological investigation — the anxiolytic/sedative application that modern RCTs validate was already in Cherokee practice before European contact.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Commission E Standard:**
- Dried aerial parts: 4–8 g daily; tea: 2 g per 150 mL water; steep 10 minutes; 3–4× daily
- Liquid extract (1:1): 0.5–1 mL 3× daily
- Tincture (1:5, 45% ethanol): 2–4 mL 3× daily (up to 8 mL at bedtime for sleep)

**Pre-Operative Anxiolytic (Clinical Trial Dose):**
- 500 mg standardized passionflower extract; 30 minutes before procedure

**Sleep:**
- 300–400 mg standardized extract (vitexin-standardized) at bedtime; or 1–2 cups passionflower tea

### Safety Profile

**Generally safe. One of the better-tolerated herbal anxiolytics.**

**Sedation:**
- Additive with CNS depressants (alcohol, benzodiazepines, z-drugs, opioids); AVOID co-administration
- Impairs driving at higher doses; caution

**MAO Inhibitor Consideration (Alkaloids):**
- Harman alkaloids present; at standard doses: clinical MAO inhibition very unlikely
- High-dose concentrated extracts: theoretical MAO-I interaction; AVOID with SSRIs, MAOIs, tramadol
- At standard herb doses: MAO concern is theoretical only

**Uterotonic:**
- Traditional emmenagogue use documented; AVOID in pregnancy
- Potential uterine stimulation at medicinal doses

**Drug Interactions:**
- CNS depressants: additive sedation (benzodiazepines, alcohol, opioids)
- MAOIs: theoretical interaction at high doses
- Anticoagulants: minor interaction (flavonoid anti-platelet)

**Contraindications:**
- Pregnancy: AVOID (uterotonic/emmenagogue tradition)
- Driving/machinery: sedative
- Combined with CNS depressants: additive
