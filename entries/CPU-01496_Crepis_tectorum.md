### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The line reads "Hieraceum minus [Fuchs], Crepis tectorum [Linnaeus] — Hawksbeard [Mabberley]" and a second line adds "[other usage] Hieracium pilosella — Mouse-ear Hawkweed". The row takes the only Linnaean binomial on the first line. The translators' index lists *Crepis tectorum* at page 448.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *3-73. IERAKION MIKRON* at line 7176, SUGGESTED lines at 7177 to 7178, text at line 7179. The chapter stands in Book Three under the running head "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants" (line 7174).

The whole chapter reads: "The little hieracium also has jagged leaves at distances. It sends out tender little green stalks on which are yellow flowers in a circle. It has the same uses as that previously spoken of [3-72]. Some call this sonchiten, others, entimon agrion, the Romans, intubus agrestis, and the Africans, sithilesade."

So the chapter is a short description and a cross-reference. The plant has jagged leaves set at intervals and tender green stalks bearing yellow flowers in a circle. It gives no use of its own and says only that it has "the same uses as that previously spoken of", which is 3-72. Four other names are printed: *sonchiten*, *entimon agrion*, *intubus agrestis* and *sithilesade*. The chapter gives no habitat, no season, no taste and no preparation.

*The uses by reference (chapter 3-72, lines 7165 to 7175).* The "great hieracium" (*IERAKION MEGA*, SUGGESTED *Sonchus arvensis*, "Corn Sowthistle") "produces a rough stalk, somewhat red, prickly, hollow", with "thinly-jagged leaves at distances, similar in circumference to sonchus [2-159]", and yellowish flowers in somewhat long little heads. The text then says: "It is cooling, indifferent, and gently astringent. As a result it is good applied on a burning stomach, and for inflammation. The juice is sipped to soothe pangs of hunger in the stomach. The herb (with the root) is applied to help one bitten by a scorpion." It adds the names *sonchiten*, *lampuca* (Roman) and *sithileas* (African). Both chapters print the name *sonchiten*, and the African names differ by a few letters (*sithileas* in 3-72, *sithilesade* in 3-73). The cross-reference to sonchus (2-159) was not read for this entry. The chain of references is the reader's, and the entry does not assign these uses to 3-73 as attested beyond the text's own words "the same uses". [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** The chapter it refers to, 3-72, has its own spine row, CPU-01630 *Sonchus arvensis*. The spine's existing sow-thistle entry I_647 (*Sonchus oleraceus*, tags AT, CM, DMM, EP) was not checked for the chapter it rests on, and is not merged here. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Chapter 3-73 itself records only a description and names and refers its uses to 3-72: application to a burning stomach and to inflammation, the juice sipped against hunger pangs, and the herb with root applied to a scorpion sting. The Codex gives no protocol and does not carry the 3-72 uses over as 3-73's own. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

3-73 gives no preparation. For 3-72 the text gives "applied" to a burning stomach and for inflammation, the juice "sipped", and the herb "with the root" applied to a scorpion bite. No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to tell a small plant from its larger fellow (jagged leaves at intervals, tender green stalks, yellow flowers in a circle), and how the names are shared between them in several languages. It assigns the plant no status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a recognition note that points to another remedy, so it is history and not a practice.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning and no use of its own. The remedy it refers to includes an application for a scorpion sting, which needs medical care, and a burning stomach should not be self-treated with an unidentified wild plant.
