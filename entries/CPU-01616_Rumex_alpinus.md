# Rumex alpinus — Hippolapathon (Dioscorides 2-141); Hippolapathum

## *Rumex alpinus, the fourth Latin name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 2-141 ("Rumicis secundum genus, Rhabarabum monarchorum [Fuchs], Hippolapathum latifolium [Bauhin], Rumex alpinus [Linnaeus], Rumex hydrolapathum — Water Dock, Horse Sorrel")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (lines 5726 to 5727) prints two Fuchs names, a Bauhin name, the Linnaean *Rumex alpinus*, a fifth binomial, *Rumex hydrolapathum*, and the English "Water Dock, Horse Sorrel". The row takes *Rumex alpinus*, and the Codex does not choose between the line's two binomials. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *2-141. HIPPOLAPATHON*, heading at line 5724, SUGGESTED line at 5726 to 5727, text at line 5728. It stands in Book Two, in the part headed "Lachana: Vegetables", after *2-140. LAPATHON* (heading at line 5716) and before *2-142. LAMPSANE* (heading at line 5729). The translators' index gives *HIPPOLAPATHON*, *Rumex alpinus* and *Rumex hydrolapathum* at page 264.

The whole chapter reads: "Hippolapathum is a great olus [one that is well known] growing in marshes. It has the same properties as those mentioned above."

That is the whole of it. It is the shortest kind of Dioscorides chapter: a size ("a great olus", with the translators' gloss "one that is well known"), a habitat ("in marshes") and a reference back. It prints no description of leaf, stalk, root, flower or seed, no medicinal use, no preparation and no other names. "Olus" is the translators' retained word; the chapter appears in the vegetable book, and the chapter itself does not say whether the plant was eaten.

**What "above" refers to.** The chapter does not name the plants "mentioned above". The chapter directly before it is *2-140. LAPATHON*, which the Codex reads as the nearest candidate and does not claim is the intended one. Its text (line 5716 onwards) says lapathum grows in marshy places, that "the herbs of all of these (boiled) soothe the intestines", that the herb applied raw as a poultice with rosaceum or saffron dissolves *melicerides*, that the seed of the wild lapathum, oxylapathum and oxalis "(taken in a drink of water or wine)" is effective "for dysentery, abdominal afflictions, a scorpion strike, and a nauseous stomach", and that the roots boiled with vinegar or applied as a poultice cure "leprosy, impetigo and rough nails" after the place is rubbed "in the sun with nitre or vinegar". It adds that a sorrel decoction relieves itchiness and earache and toothache as a rinse, that the roots boiled in wine dissolve "scrofulous tumours, goitres, and swollen parotid glands", that some hang the roots round the neck as an amulet for goitre, and that the roots, boiled with wine and drunk, "help jaundice, break stones in the bladder, draw out the menstrual flow, and help those touched by scorpions". These are the words of 2-140, not of 2-141, and the entry does not transfer any of them to this plant. They are given only so a reader can see what the single sentence of 2-141 may be pointing back to. [USER: verify]

**Other chapters that cite this one.** Two chapters compare their plant to *rumex* and carry the translators' cross-reference to this chapter: *3-121. PHULLITIS* (heading at line 7628; "Phyllitis sends out six or seven upright leaves similar to rumex [2-141] yet somewhat longer", line 7630) and *3-143* (the chapter on *Saturion*; "similar to rumex [2-141] or lily, yet smaller and reddish", line 7779). In both, the translators' bracket points the word *rumex* to 2-141. Those two chapters give no property of the dock.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter number, *Hippolapathon*, *Hippolapathum*, *Rumex alpinus* and *Water Dock* found no existing entry on 2-141 itself. I_1020 (hart's-tongue fern, *Phullitis*, 3-121) and I_1140 (*Orchis bifolia*, *Saturion*, 3-143) quote the cross-reference to 2-141 from the other side. I_1113 (*Lampsana communis*, 2-142) quotes 2-140 for its own comparison. I_720 (Broad-leaved Dock, *Rumex obtusifolius*) has a DMM paragraph on *lapathon* (the docks and sorrels) in Book Two, which is the neighbouring chapter 2-140 in its translators' identification; it is not an entry on 2-141. Other Rumex entries exist in the folder (I_301 Himalayan Dock, I_690 Curly Dock, I_699 Common Sorrel), none of which is on this chapter. None is merged.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. It is a general, well-established fact that docks (*Rumex*) are plants of the knotweed family (Polygonaceae) and that their leaves are generally known to contain oxalic acid and their roots anthraquinones. The chapter mentions neither, and the species is the translators' suggestion. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a large marsh plant and says only that it has "the same properties as those mentioned above". It prints no use of its own. The Codex gives no protocol for it, and the identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives no preparation. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The one general fact is the one stated under Active Components: docks contain oxalates, which is why large amounts of sorrel and dock leaves are not harmless. The chapter itself says nothing on the point.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. It prints only that the plant is "a great olus" growing "in marshes" and has "the same properties as those mentioned above".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use. (The amulet for goitre belongs to the preceding chapter, 2-140.) What the chapter shows of the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition is a habitat and a size, and a classification by reference: a plant placed beside a better-known one by its likeness in properties, which is itself a way of working with plants. The plant is given no status beyond that. The identification is the translators'.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It offers a size, a habitat and a cross-reference, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. It is a general, well-established fact that docks and sorrels contain oxalic acid and that large quantities can be harmful, particularly to people with kidney stones or kidney disease, and the Codex does not recommend eating any wild marsh plant. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion with two different docks on the same line, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
