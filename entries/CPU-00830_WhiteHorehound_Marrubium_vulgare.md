# White Horehound — Common Horehound, Marrubium, Hoarhound, "Seed of Horus" (Ebers tradition)

## *Marrubium vulgare* L. | Family: Lamiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM, EP
**Energetic Classification:** Hot (1st degree) & Dry (2nd degree) — a warming, drying bitter (Galenic/Unani)
**Training Phase:** Clearing Phase — used to open and drain congested passages before deeper tonification.

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ebers Papyrus (EP):** White horehound is among the bitter, aromatic herbs of the Nile pharmacopoeia. Egyptian practice associated the plant with remedies for the chest and breath, and later herbal lineages preserved the folk name linking it to Horus ("seed of Horus") — a symbolic, not botanical, attribution. Its long-standing role was as a loosener of thick phlegm and a settler of the stomach.

**Assyrian Tablets (AT):** The cuneiform medical tablets of Mesopotamia record numerous bitter aromatics of the mint family prepared as decoctions and washes for cough and chest oppression. White horehound sits within this Assyrian current of drying, phlegm-cutting herbs taken in honeyed water — the honey both preserving and masking the plant's pronounced bitterness.

**Dioscorides — De Materia Medica (DMM):** Dioscorides describes *prasion* (horehound) explicitly for the chest: the leaves and seed boiled and drunk, or made into an electuary with honey, to help those who are "orthopnoeic" (short of breath), phthisical, and troubled by cough. He also notes its use to expel afterbirth and bring on menstruation — the emmenagogue reputation that underlies the pregnancy caution below.

**Broader Materia Medica (BM):** Through the Greco-Arabic transmission, Avicenna and the Unani physicians classed horehound (*farāsiyūn*) as hot and dry, a deobstruent and expectorant (*munaddij* and *muqaṭṭi'*) for cold, viscid humours of the lung. Mediaeval and early-modern European herbals (Gerard, Culpeper) inherited this verbatim, and horehound cough syrups and lozenges persisted into modern commerce.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Across every tradition the plant is read the same way — a *bitter that moves stuck cold matter*. The doctrine of "hot and dry cutting cold and wet" maps cleanly onto the observed pharmacology: the labdane diterpene marrubiin and associated phenolics stimulate secretion and act as bitter tonics on the digestive tract. The symbolic image of clearing the chest of "heavy, standing water" is a folk description of mucolytic and expectorant action.

### Active Components

- **Marrubiin** — the characteristic labdane diterpene lactone and principal marker of the genus; quantified at roughly 156 mg/g in a methanol extract of aerial parts by HPTLC densitometry. Regarded as the lead expectorant/bittering constituent.
- **Premarrubiin** — a precursor diterpene converting to marrubiin on processing.
- **Phenylpropanoids** — marrubiic acid, and phenolic acids including chlorogenic and rosmarinic-type compounds.
- **Flavonoids** — apigenin and luteolin glycosides (quercetin-equivalent flavonoid content ~12 mg/g of extract).
- **Volatile oil** — a minor, variable essential-oil fraction.
- **Bitter principle** — the marrubiin/phenolic complex accounts for the intense bitterness that drives its reflex salivary and gastric secretory effects.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Filed as a **Clearing-Phase bitter expectorant**. The intended use case is the congested, "cold-damp" chest — a productive cough with thick, hard-to-raise phlegm, often paired with sluggish digestion and poor appetite. As a simple bitter it is also used pre-prandially to prime gastric secretion in dyspepsia and bloating. It is a *mover*, not a *builder*: deployed briefly to open and drain, then withdrawn in favour of tonifying agents.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, evidence for *Marrubium vulgare* is largely preclinical, with a well-characterised chemistry but a thin clinical base:

- Yousefi et al. (2016), *Advanced Pharmaceutical Bulletin*, quantified marrubiin at 156 mg/g in the methanol extract by HPTLC and reported strong DPPH radical-scavenging (RC50 8.24 µg/mL), attributing the antioxidant effect to marrubiin plus phenolics/flavonoids acting synergistically. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.15171/apb.2016.019)
- Rezgui et al. (2020), *Journal de Mycologie Médicale*, found significant antioxidant activity of methanol/acetone extracts and moderate antidermatophyte activity of marrubiin (~50% inhibition of *Trichophyton mentagrophytes* and *Epidermophyton floccosum* at 100 µg/mL). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mycmed.2020.100927)
- Aćimović et al. (2020), *Molecules*, a phytochemical/pharmacological review, summarises antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, hypolipidemic, antihypertensive and sedative potential — while explicitly concluding that "more firm scientific evidence and well-designed clinical trials are necessary." [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules25122898)

**Evidence grade: Traditional-plus-preclinical.** Chemistry and antioxidant/antimicrobial activity are reproducibly demonstrated in vitro; the expectorant, hypotensive and hypolipidemic claims rest on tradition and animal/mechanistic data, not robust human trials. The antihypertensive signal is what motivates the arrhythmia/hypotension caution below.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Hot in the 1st degree, dry in the 2nd. A deobstruent, resolvent and expectorant for cold, viscid phlegm; a bitter stomachic. (Attested — Dioscorides, Avicenna.)
- **TCM:** No classical Chinese materia medica placement (non-attested tradition; not tagged CM). By analogy to its actions it behaves as a bitter, cool-dispersing, phlegm-transforming herb, but this is interpretive, not canonical.
- **Ayurvedic:** No classical Ayurvedic entry (not tagged SS). Functionally *tikta* (bitter) rasa with a phlegm-reducing (kapha-lowering), light/dry *guna* — interpretive only.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian (EP).** White horehound belongs among the bitter, aromatic chest-and-breath herbs of the Nile pharmacopoeia, and the surviving folk epithet "seed of Horus" is, as this entry's own record concedes, a symbolic attribution rather than a secured Ebers identification. The Egyptian corpus is indication-organised — it records what was given for thick phlegm or a troubled stomach, not what the plant does to a person's *ka*, *ib* or vital constitution — and in *heka*, where spell and drug form a single intervention, no operative recitation is attested for this herb in the retrieved sources; nor is any deity attribution securely sourced beyond the late folk name. There is therefore no cultivation status to state. **(c) absent.**

**Assyrian (AT).** The cuneiform medical tablets prescribe bitter mint-family aromatics as honeyed decoctions and washes for cough and chest oppression, and horehound sits within that drying, phlegm-cutting current — but at the level of the class, and such drugs are classified by the causal agency they oppose rather than by any status they confer on the person. Under the standing blocking warning that governs every Assyrian substance identification and etiological claim, no cultivation status is asserted; the engagement is therapeutic. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Here the attestation is genuinely textual and species-level: Dioscorides names *prasion* explicitly, prescribing the boiled leaves and seed, or an electuary with honey, for the orthopnoeic, the phthisical and the cough-troubled, and noting its power to expel afterbirth and bring on menstruation. Yet *De Materia Medica* carries no subtle-body doctrine into which a cultivation status could be set; what it offers a cultivator is the discipline of attention — recognising true *prasion* by its bitterness, judging the honeyed electuary, gathering at the right season — a practice of assay rather than a vital-body teaching. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The Unani physicians class horehound (*farāsiyūn*) as hot in the first degree and dry in the second, a deobstruent and expectorant — *munaddij* and *muqaṭṭi'* — that ripens and cuts the cold, viscid humours of the lung. The corpus's strongest cultivation idea is the conservation of the *ruh* and of innate heat and radical moisture, the finite endowment whose depletion *is* ageing; but that governs the whole regimen of living, not this warming, drying bitter, and no gladdening cardiac (*mufarrih*) role is claimed for it. Its standing is a humoral-therapeutic placement, hot and dry, with no cultivation status recorded. **(b) framed.**

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator uses horehound as a mover, not a builder: a very bitter clearing-phase expectorant taken briefly — usually honeyed, to carry the bitterness — to open and drain a congested, cold-damp chest and to prime a sluggish gut before meals, then withdrawn once the phlegm is loosening in favour of tonifying agents. None of its traditions assign it a longevity or vital-nourishing status, and the honest instruction is the traditional one: use it to shift stuck cold matter, then stop.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy — avoid.** Dioscorides and the traditional record cite horehound to expel afterbirth and provoke menstruation (emmenagogue/uterine action). Its use in pregnancy is contraindicated on this traditional basis.
- **Lactation:** Insufficient data; avoid therapeutic doses.
- **Cardiac / blood pressure:** Traditional and preclinical reports of hypotensive and possible antiarrhythmic/arrhythmogenic activity mean the herb should be used cautiously by anyone with hypotension, cardiac arrhythmia, or on antihypertensive or antiarrhythmic medication. Monitor for additive blood-pressure lowering.
- **Diabetes:** Hypoglycemic activity is reported; caution with glucose-lowering drugs.
- **Gastritis / peptic ulcer:** As a strong secretory bitter, large doses may aggravate active ulceration.
- **Dose:** Culinary and standard tea doses are well tolerated; exceed pharmacopoeial doses only with professional oversight.
