# Bird of passage — "Bird-of-Passage" (Bryan's English rendering of the Ebers Papyrus animal list)
## *A bird named only as "Bird-of-Passage" in Bryan's list of the animals that supplied the Egyptian materia medica; no Egyptian word, no species and no binomial is given in the sources retrieved* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian Tradition (EP).** The locus is Bryan's 1930 English *The Papyrus Ebers*, Chapter VII "Organic Remedies (animals)" (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1395; name-index row `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` line 105, class animal, "Bryan's English identification; no Egyptian word or binomial given in this list"). Bryan introduces the list at lines 1385-1388 by saying that a complete catalogue of the animal materia medica "would not be possible since (as with many of the plant and mineral substances) the identity of a considerable number of the animals mentioned is still beyond us", and that the list printed is of the animals "whose identity is not in doubt". "Bird-of-Passage" is one entry in it, after "Vulture" and before "Swallow" (lines 1394-1396). The list gives no Egyptian name, no use and no part of the bird. The English phrase is a generic description, not a species, and Bryan adds no gloss to it.

**The one prescription in Bryan's text.** The Bryan text on disk was searched for "passage" and "migrat". The bird occurs once in running text, at line 2197, in the chapter whose running head is "Minor Medicine" (lines 2091-2184), in a group of remedies for the limbs. After a salve "to make supple the hardenings" (line 2191), Bryan prints:

"ANOTHER TO SOFTEN THE HARDENINGS / Fruit-of-the-Dompalm / Beans Edelkraut Fresh Milk sebesten / CRUSH IT IN THE Bird-of-Passage! CRUSH IT IN ITS FEATHERS! / Apply as a Poultice." (lines 2194-2198)

The OCR on disk reads the capitalised words as "Brrp-or-Passace" and "1TS"; I read them as "Bird-of-Passage" and "ITS" from the context and from the list entry at line 1395. The next remedy is the compound for protecting the limbs "against everything" (line 2206), which is a different item and is described in the entry for statue scrapings.

**What the passage seems to say, and what it does not.** The instruction is to crush the vegetable ingredients and milk "in the bird" and "in its feathers", and to apply the result as a poultice. It does not say whether the bird was alive, dead, plucked or whole, whether the crushing took place in the bird's body or on its skin, or whether the instruction is a direction of preparation or a spoken formula. The capitals and exclamation marks are in the style Bryan uses for other passages of his chapter, for example "Put a Dog's Hide on him! Do not press the hand on him!" (line 2178), which follows a compound of copper and verdigris for trembling; Bryan does not say that either is a spell. I cannot decide from the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None can be stated. The bird is unidentified, so no composition or constituent is known. In general terms, bird feathers are made of keratin, a structural protein, and a bird's body is, like any animal tissue, protein, fat and water; neither point is drawn from the papyrus.

---

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved show that a bird of passage was named among the animals the Egyptian physicians drew upon, and that one limb-softening poultice is to be crushed "in the bird" and "in its feathers". Migratory birds are in many places protected by law, may carry avian disease, and the identification is unknown. No modern protocol is supplied.

### Preparation & Dosing

Only the poultice at lines 2194-2198: dompalm fruit, beans, *edelkraut* (Bryan's name, printed untranslated), fresh milk and *sebesten*, crushed in the bird and its feathers, applied as a poultice. No quantity is printed in Bryan's summary. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint (here, "hardenings" of the limbs), not what a substance does to the person's vital constitution. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); the exclamatory wording of the single passage may be a direction or a spoken formula, and the sources retrieved do not settle which, so no *heka* claim is made. The person as plural (*ka*, *ba*, *akh*, *ib*) is not engaged. No deity is attributed.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. A name in a list, and one poultice for hardened limbs whose wording is unclear and whose bird cannot be identified. A cultivator has nothing to do with it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Disease risk:** wild and migratory birds can carry transmissible infections; handling carcasses or feathers carries a hygiene risk.
- **Legal and conservation status:** migratory birds are legally protected in many countries and many species are endangered; taking one without authorisation may be an offence. The status of the species meant here is unknown. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** the passage is read as requiring the use of a bird's body; no modern justification is offered.
- **Unidentified material:** no preparation of the poultice is recommended here.
