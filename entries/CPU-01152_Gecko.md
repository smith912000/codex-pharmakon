# Gecko — rtsangs.pa (Tibetan, as transliterated by Clark 1995), "a type of Gecko: Agama himalayana sacra"; "mountain-dwelling lizards" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *A lizard whose flesh the rGyud-bzhi lists as a medicine; Clark glosses it "a type of Gecko" with the binomial "Agama himalayana sacra", and the Men-Tsee-Khang translation has "mountain-dwelling lizards"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The text is Clark's 1995 translation of *The Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`), Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, "the powers of medicines". Under "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147) it lists "thirteen medicines of animal origin", the third of which is meat (line 1148). The gecko is in the meat section, in a run of short entries (line 1170):

"bya.wang.sha: Bat flesh: [has thirteen names]. It is anti-emetic [and dispels spirits]. mchhil.pa: Sparrow: Passer montanus: [has seven names]. nas.zan bird (lit. 'barley feeder'): Asian sparrow: [has ten names]. rtsangs.pa: a type of Gecko: Agama himalayana sacra: [has eleven names, for example sder.chhags. The flesh of] these three creatures increases semen, [is aphrodisiac, tonic and anodyne, and drains pus.]"

The Tibetan name *rtsangs.pa*, the gloss "a type of Gecko", the binomial "Agama himalayana sacra", and the example name *sder.chhags* are as Clark prints them. Three creatures share one statement: the sparrow (*mchhil.pa*), the "barley feeder" (*nas.zan*), and the *rtsangs.pa*. Their flesh "increases semen"; the further words "is aphrodisiac, tonic and anodyne, and drains pus" are in square brackets. In his preface Clark says that he "interpolated liberally from the Blue Aquamarine commentary" (line 80), so I take the square brackets to mark such additions, but his text does not say so for this entry. [USER: verify] The binomial's genus, *Agama*, is the name of a group of lizards usually called agamas, not geckos; Clark's own gloss is "a type of Gecko", and I print both as he does and do not settle the identity. [USER: verify]

The chapter's account of meats gives context. The nine sections of meat include "(h) Burrowing animals include the marmot (Marmota bobak), porcupine, frog, snake, Tibetan badger, lizard (Eremias argus), gecko, and scorpion" (line 672). So the gecko is a burrower in this classification (the footnote number "3" after "gecko" is in the OCR text, but I did not find the footnote itself). The general statement about meats follows: "All the different types of meats in the above eight sections are sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste" (line 673). The same chapter says elsewhere in general terms that "Flesh of chicken and sparrow increases semen and is beneficial for wounds" (line 678), which is the same property as the gecko's line gives to the group of three.

The second translation on disk is the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 *Root and Explanatory Tantra* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`). Its meat passage reads (line 3536): "The meat of sparrows and mountain-dwelling lizards increase semen." The two translations agree on the property and on the sparrow; I read "mountain-dwelling lizards" as the 2011 text's counterpart to Clark's *rtsangs.pa*, but the text does not give the Tibetan, so this is a reading and not a statement. [USER: verify] That text also lists "marmot, porcupine, frog, snake, badger, lizard, mountain-dwelling lizard and scorpion" as "the group of animals that live in burrows" (line 2918). It has a further entry in the Explanatory Tantra's list, "Lizard cures ein disorders and poisoning" (line 3573), which belongs to a different row, and in Clark to the lizard (*rmigs.pa*, *Eremias argus*), "anthelmintic and neutralizes poisons"; it is not about the gecko, and I do not carry it over.

Both translators' prefaces say that animal products have largely fallen out of use. Clark: "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57). The Men-Tsee-Khang translators: "the use of animal products was negligible" and "animal products in Tibetan medicine have been completely substituted by equally effective herbal substances", but their potencies are recorded to preserve the understanding of their therapeutic values (line 687).

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, lizard flesh is mainly water and protein with little fat, and wild lizards can carry bacteria such as *Salmonella* and parasites. Nothing in the entry links a constituent to the property "increases semen".

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The rGyud-bzhi records the flesh of this lizard, with that of the sparrow and the "barley feeder", as increasing semen, and in Clark's bracketed words as an aphrodisiac, tonic and anodyne that drains pus. The Codex does not recommend it. Nothing in the sources retrieved supports a modern use; wild reptiles carry *Salmonella* and other pathogens; and several lizard species are protected. The entry is a record of what the two translations say.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** The gecko's own line (Clark 1170) prints no taste, potency or post-digestive taste. Two statements frame it as a meat: "All the different types of meats in the above eight sections are sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste" (Clark line 673); and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the "taste and post-digestive taste of meats from the animals in the above eight groups are sweet" (line 2923), adding that the meat of dry-land animals is "cool, light and rough in quality" and that of animals living in wet places "oily, heavy and warm" (line 2923). The text does not say which of these the burrowing animals fall under, so none is assigned to the gecko. Its action as printed is that it increases semen.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The flesh of this lizard is named in the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica, in the meat section, with the property that it increases semen. That is a therapeutic property of a meat; no passage read presents it as a cultivation practice. The project's cultivation spec places Tibetan medicine as the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice and not reaching the root of illness (the three poisons); that is a statement about the tradition's frame, not about this substance. No passage read connects this lizard to *bcud len* (essence extraction) or to any practice, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition. None is asserted. Both translators say animal products have largely been given up in practice, and the 2011 text gives Buddhism's rejection of harm to any being as part of the reason (line 687). The attestation rests on single lines of two translations.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The line is a record of a meat that increases semen, the tradition itself has largely replaced animal products with herbs, and the Codex does not recommend it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** wild reptiles carry *Salmonella* and other pathogens; do not handle or eat them raw or undercooked.
- **Sexual or fertility complaints:** need medical assessment; do not use animal products for them.
- **Conservation and law:** many lizard species are protected in law, and wild collection can be illegal; do not take any animal for these uses.
- **Ethics:** the Tibetan tradition itself, in the 2011 translators' words, denounces causing harm to any being; the Codex does not recommend harming any animal for these uses.
