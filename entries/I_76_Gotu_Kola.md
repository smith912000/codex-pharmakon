### Botanical Identity
Low-growing stoloniferous perennial; kidney-shaped leaves with scalloped edges; pale pink-purple flowers; grows in marshy, watery habitats across tropical and subtropical Asia, Africa, and Americas; prefers disturbed moist ground near water. The "tiger herb" legend: tigers are observed rolling in gotu kola patches after injury — the wound-healing application observed through animal behaviour. The entire plant (leaves + stems) is the drug material; eaten fresh in salads (particularly in Sri Lanka, India, Indonesia — *gotu kola sambol* is a daily Sri Lankan condiment).

**Brahmi nomenclature confusion**: In Ayurveda, *brāhmī* refers to BOTH *Centella asiatica* (in Southern India and Sri Lanka tradition) AND *Bacopa monnieri* (see I_32, Northern India tradition). This creates significant clinical confusion in Ayurvedic commerce — products labelled "brahmi" may contain either species. The distinction matters: Centella emphasises collagen synthesis and wound healing; Bacopa emphasises AChE inhibition and dendritic arborisation.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Sushruta Samhita / Ayurveda (SS)

*Maṇḍūkaparṇī* in classical texts:
> *"Bitter, astringent; cooling. Reduces Pitta and Kapha. The supreme *medhya rasāyana* — promotes intelligence, memory, and longevity. Useful for skin diseases, leprosy, wound healing. A sacred herb — promotes clarity and spiritual intelligence. For madness and epilepsy."*

The *medhya* (brain-enhancing) and wound-healing applications are the Ayurvedic poles of gotu kola's pharmacology — both are independently validated.

### 2.2 Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ)

*Jī xuě cǎo*:
> *"Bitter; cold. Enters Liver, Spleen, Kidney. Clears heat and dampness, detoxifies, resolves swelling. Used for urinary tract infections, traumatic injury (promotes wound healing), liver and hepatitis conditions, skin infections."*

The Chinese application emphasises the wound healing, liver, and anti-infective — different emphasis from the Ayurvedic cognitive application, but from the same plant.

### 2.3 Four Medical Tantras (FMT)

*Rgya spos* in Tibetan medicine for cognitive decline, wound healing, and skin conditions.

### 2.4 Canon of Medicine (CM)

Ibn Sīnā describes *barahmī* (imported from India) for cognitive strengthening and nervous conditions.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Collagen Synthesis / Wound Healing (Primary Pharmacological Identity)

**Asiaticoside + madecassoside**: Directly stimulate fibroblast proliferation and type I collagen synthesis via TGF-β1 pathway → enhanced wound healing, reduced hypertrophic scarring, improved venous ulcer healing.

The wound-healing mechanism makes gotu kola one of the most pharmacologically validated traditional wound herbs:
- Stimulates fibroblasts to produce collagen
- Reduces scar tissue hyperproliferation (anti-fibrotic via Smad3/TGF-β modulation)
- Enhances angiogenesis in wound beds (VEGF upregulation)

### 5.2 Venous Insufficiency / Connective Tissue

**Asiatic acid + madecassic acid**: Strengthen vascular endothelium; reduce venous leakage; improve microcirculation in chronic venous insufficiency. This application (varicose veins, venous leg ulcers) is not present in the ancient traditions but emerges directly from the collagen-synthesis mechanism — the same mechanism that heals skin wounds also strengthens vascular walls.

### 5.3 Anxiolytic / Neuroprotective

**Brahmoside + asiaticoside**: GABA-A modulation → anxiolytic. BDNF upregulation → dendritic growth (complementing Bacopa's mechanism). Reduction of corticosterone (in animal models) → HPA axis modulation. Neuroprotection against Aβ toxicity (Alzheimer's models) — asiaticoside reduces Aβ aggregation and neuronal loss.

### 5.4 Anti-Inflammatory

**Asiatic acid**: NF-κB inhibition; 5-LOX inhibition; COX-2 inhibition. Multiple anti-inflammatory pathways active simultaneously.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Venous insufficiency**: Cesarone (2001, *Angiology*): TTFCA (total triterpene fraction of *Centella asiatica*) 60–120mg/day vs placebo — significantly reduced oedema, ankle swelling, and symptoms of chronic venous insufficiency. Multiple European RCTs confirm (ESCOP monograph supports this indication).

**Wound healing**: Multiple RCTs: gotu kola preparations significantly accelerate wound healing and reduce hypertrophic scar formation post-surgery (standardised triterpenoid content).

**Anxiety**: Bradwejn (2000, *Journal of Clinical Psychopharmacology*): Single dose gotu kola (12g) significantly attenuated the acoustic startle response (a validated anxiety biomarker) vs placebo in healthy adults. Wattanathorn (2008): Gotu kola extract significantly improved cognitive function and reduced anxiety in elderly volunteers.

**Alzheimer's (pilot)**: Tiwari (2008): Gotu kola extract significantly improved cognitive scores vs placebo in mild cognitive impairment patients over 2 months (small pilot).

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The tiger's wound medicine**: The legend that injured tigers roll in gotu kola patches is cross-cultural (documented in Indian, Chinese, and Thai traditions). The ethological claim is unverified — but the observation that an animal with access to diverse ground cover selectively uses a particular plant for wound healing would be a meaningful traditional signal, whether accurate or mythological. Animals self-medicate; the observation of animal self-medication with specific plants is a documented traditional ethnobotanical knowledge-gathering method. The tiger is the strongest and most feared animal of the Asian forest — attributing wound healing to its medicine-plant made the observation memorable and transgenerationally preserved.

**Fresh food as medicine**: Gotu kola (*gotu kola sambol* — fresh leaves with coconut, lime, chili) is a daily Sri Lankan side dish consumed at every meal. Sri Lanka has historically high centenarian rates (documented in colonial-era records) in villages where gotu kola is a dietary staple. The *rasāyana* effect of daily fresh gotu kola consumption — providing sustained low-dose asiaticoside and vitamin C — is the Ayurvedic longevity claim with a plausible dietary epidemiology basis. Daily food versus weekly medicine is a false dichotomy; gotu kola collapses it.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

| Form | Dose | Application |
|------|------|-------------|
| Standardised extract (40% asiaticoside) | 60–120mg/day | Venous insufficiency; cognitive; wound healing |
| TTFCA (total triterpene fraction) | 60–120mg 2× daily | Clinical venous insufficiency (best evidence form) |
| Fresh leaf (food) | 20–50g/day | Nutritive; daily *rasāyana*; cognitive maintenance |
| Tea (dried herb) | 3–5g per cup | Mild cognitive; anxiolytic; wound healing support |
| Topical (2% extract) | Applied 2× daily | Wound healing; scar reduction; venous ulcers |

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Bitter; slightly cold. Channels: Liver (肝), Spleen (脾), Kidney (腎). Clears heat-damp, resolves toxins, promotes healing.

**Ayurvedic**: Bitter, astringent; cooling. Reduces Pitta ↓↓, reduces Kapha ↓. The supreme *Medhya rasāyana* (*brāhmī*) — brain tonic, cognitive enhancer, longevity herb. *Cakṣuṣya* (eye), *Twakya* (skin-beneficial).

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Hepatotoxicity (rare)**: Case reports of liver injury at high doses — monitor LFTs with prolonged use; avoid with active liver disease
- **Pregnancy**: Limited safety data — avoid therapeutic doses (culinary amounts probably safe)
- **Sedatives**: Additive sedation at high doses
- **Anticoagulants**: Mild anti-platelet — monitor with warfarin
- **Cholesterol-lowering medications**: May interact via cholesterol synthesis pathways (triterpenes)
- Fresh leaf and standard tea preparations: extremely safe; clinical extracts at high doses require monitoring
