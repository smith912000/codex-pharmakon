# Ground Ivy — Alehoof, Gill-over-the-ground, Tunhoof, Creeping Charlie, Cat's-foot

## *Glechoma hederacea* L. (syn. *Nepeta glechoma*) | Family: Lamiaceae (Labiatae)

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Warm and dry (first–second degree) — a bitter-aromatic that opens, dries catarrh, and moves stagnant fluids
**Training Phase:** Clearing / Opening — a decongestant of the head and chest, used at the threshold of a working

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian stratum:** The creeping bitter-aromatic mints of the Old World materia medica belong to the deep register of head-and-chest remedies attested on the Mesopotamian medical tablets — plants steeped, snuffed or drunk to "clear the head," relieve the stuffed nose and ringing ear, and dry rheum. *Glechoma* sits squarely within this class of pungent-bitter creeping herbs used against catarrhal heaviness.

**Dioscoridean / Greco-Roman (De Materia Medica):** Dioscorides and the classical herbalists catalogue the small trailing mints among the warming, thinning, incising remedies (*leptynein*, *temnein*) — agents that cut thick humour, provoke the discharge of phlegm, and clean wounds. Ground ivy's later European reputation for catarrh, chronic cough, tinnitus and slow-healing sores is a direct inheritance of this Dioscoridean logic of thinning and incising.

**Avicennan / Unani reception:** In the Unani frame the plant reads as hot-and-dry in the early degrees: a *muhallil* (resolvent) and *mufatteh* (deobstruent) that opens obstructions of the head and chest, dries excess phlegm, and by its aromatic bitterness supports digestion and the passage of urine. Medieval and early-modern European practice — where the herb was famously used to clarify and preserve ale before hops (hence *alehoof*) — carried the same warming, drying, clearing signature into the tavern and the sickroom.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Ground ivy is the *unblocker of the passages* — the herb of the clogged head, the ringing ear, the stubborn catarrh. Its symbolic role as a thinner-and-clearer of thick fluid maps onto a genuine phenolic pharmacology: it is unusually rich in **rosmarinic acid**, a caffeic-acid ester whose antioxidant and anti-inflammatory action, and its documented anti-fibrotic effect in Lamiaceae extracts, gives a modern face to the old idea of a plant that keeps tissue supple, resolves congestion and resists the "hardening" of chronically inflamed passages. The Kenetica reading treats it as a Clearing-phase opener — used to lift catarrhal fog at the threshold of work — while heeding the pulegone caution below.

### Active Components

- **Rosmarinic acid** — the signature phenolic ester; a potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory constituent and the most abundant identified phenolic in the herb.
- **Other phenolic acids** — chlorogenic and protocatechuic acids.
- **Flavonoids** — rutin, isoquercetin and related glycosides.
- **Essential oil** including **pulegone** and other monoterpene ketones (see Contraindications), plus terpenoids contributing the aromatic, decongestant character.
- **Bitter principles (marrubiin-type / sesquiterpene lactones)** and tannins contributing the drying, tonic-bitter action.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica, ground ivy is a **Clearing-phase decongestant** — reached for where the presenting pattern is catarrhal heaviness: blocked sinuses, post-nasal drip, chronic productive cough, the muffled ear and its ringing, and the general "damp fog" of the head that dulls attention before a working. Its warming-drying, thinning action is used to open and lighten the passages; its bitterness supports digestion and gentle diuresis; its rosmarinic-acid-driven antioxidant profile lends a tissue-protective, anti-inflammatory undertone. It is a short-arc opener, not a long tonic — taken to lift congestion and restore clarity at a threshold, then set down.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, ground ivy is a phenolic-rich Lamiaceae whose bioactivity is dominated by rosmarinic acid, though clinical trials in humans are lacking — the evidence is chemical, in-vitro and animal-model.

- An extraction-optimisation and bioactivity study confirmed rosmarinic, chlorogenic and protocatechuic acids plus rutin and isoquercetin in the dried herb, and found that water and ethanol extracts had **high antioxidant activity** (DPPH, FRAP) correlating with phenolic content, while being **non-toxic to normal human cells** in vitro (Grabowska et al., *Plants* 2022 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants11172217)).
- Work on hot-water *Glechoma* extract and rosmarinic acid in renal-cancer and normal renal cells showed the extract was low-cytotoxic to healthy tubular cells while modulating cancer-cell migration, identifying rosmarinic acid as the most abundant active phytochemical (Chou et al., *Chin Med* 2020 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13020-020-00358-2)).
- Rosmarinic acid and rosmarinic-acid-rich Lamiaceae extracts have documented **anti-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory** effects across liver, kidney, heart and lung models, acting via NRF2/PPARγ/AMPK activation and TGF-β/Wnt suppression (Boo, *Antioxidants* 2024 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox13020146)).

**Evidence grade:** *Traditional-plus* — strong phytochemical and preclinical antioxidant/anti-inflammatory support for the plant's constituents; direct clinical evidence for the specific traditional indications (catarrh, tinnitus) is essentially absent. Efficacy claims should be framed as traditional.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Unani/Avicennan:** hot and dry (first–second degree); *mufatteh* (deobstruent), *muhallil* (resolvent), mild diuretic and expectorant.
- **Greco-Roman/Dioscoridean:** warming, thinning, incising — cuts thick phlegm, provokes discharge, cleans wounds.
- **Ayurvedic mapping (by analogy, not classical):** *tikta*/*katu* (bitter/pungent) rasa, *ushna* (warming) virya, *laghu-ruksha* (light-dry) guna; kapha-reducing.

### Contraindications

**⚠️ PULEGONE CONTENT — HEPATOTOXICITY RISK.** Ground ivy's essential oil contains **pulegone**, a monoterpene ketone that is **hepatotoxic and abortifacient** at concentrated exposure (the same toxin responsible for pennyroyal poisoning). Do **not** use the isolated essential oil internally, and avoid prolonged high-dose use of the herb. Standard short-course water infusions of the aerial herb deliver far less pulegone than the neat oil, but the toxin is the reason courses must stay brief and the oil must never be taken.

**⚠️ PREGNANCY — AVOID.** On the basis of pulegone's abortifacient and hepatotoxic reputation, ground ivy is contraindicated in pregnancy and best avoided in lactation.

**⚠️ EPILEPSY / SEIZURE DISORDERS — CAUTION.** Pulegone and related monoterpene ketones are associated with convulsant potential; avoid in people with epilepsy or a seizure history.

**Other cautions:** avoid in known Lamiaceae allergy; use caution in renal disease given the herb's diuretic action; theoretical additive effect with anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy is unquantified — exercise caution. Keep courses short and doses within the traditional range.
