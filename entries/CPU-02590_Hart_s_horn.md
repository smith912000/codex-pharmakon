# Hart's horn — Elaphou keras (ἐλάφου κέρας, "deer's horn"), burnt horn of a hart, Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 2-63

## *The horn of the hart, the adult male red deer, burnt and washed. The chapter header prints "Burnt Horn of a Hart — adult male Red Deer"; no binomial is printed in the text, and Cervus elaphus is our reading of "Red Deer", of medium confidence. The material is a horn (antler) preparation, a different part from the Ebers deer row.* | *(Volume III — Animal)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The locus is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 5184, chapter 2-63 "ELAPHOU KERAS", in the part of Book Two headed "LIVING CREATURES". The existing entries "Deer (stag)" (III_70, CPU-01127, from Ebers) and "Aidoion arrenos elaphou" (deer testes, CPU-02657) are other parts or other texts and are not merged here.

**Dioscorides (DMM).** The chapter reads: "The horn of a hart (burnt, washed and two spoonfuls taken in a drink) is good with gum tragacanth for bloodspitters, dysentery, coeliac [intestinal complaints], jaundice and disorders of the bladder. It is also good for women troubled with excessive [menstrual] discharge, given with some liquid suitable for that suffering. It is burnt in an oven (first pounded and put into an unfired clay pot, sealed around with clay, and fired) until it is white, and then afterwards it is washed in a similar way to cadmia [5-84]. This is good for discharges and ulcers in the eyes; and rubbed on the teeth it cleans them. If burnt raw it drives away snakes with the smell. Boiled with vinegar and used as a mouthwash it soothes pain of the inner teeth."

Four routes of use are named: drunk (with gum tragacanth, or with a liquid suited to the complaint), applied for eye discharges and ulcers, rubbed on the teeth, and boiled in vinegar as a mouthwash. The burnt raw horn is also burnt as a fumigation against snakes. The preparation is given in detail: the horn is first pounded, put in an unfired clay pot sealed with clay, and fired until white, then washed as cadmia is (the cross-reference is 5-84, which is not read for this entry).

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, deer horn (antler) is bone-like tissue made largely of calcium phosphate (hydroxyapatite) with a protein (collagen) matrix. Firing until white burns off the organic matter and leaves a mineral ash rich in calcium phosphate. No analytical source was retrieved. [USER: verify.]

### Preparation & Dosing

The one dose in the text is: "two spoonfuls taken in a drink" of the burnt, washed horn (2-63, line 5185), given "with gum tragacanth" for the first group of complaints. The size of the "spoonful" is not stated. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Burnt bone and antler leave a calcium phosphate ash that is gritty and mildly abrasive, which agrees in kind with its use rubbed on the teeth; calcium phosphate is also a normal constituent of tooth and bone. The classical indications (jaundice, dysentery, bloodspitting) are not translated into modern equivalents. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify.]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and no cultivation status is attested for hart's horn. What the chapter does give is a discipline of attention: the horn is burnt in a sealed pot until it is white, and the end point is observed, not guessed.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports a cultivation use. It is a burnt-bone preparation for specified complaints, and the only practical content is the care in firing it.

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

Dioscorides names the blood and lungs, the bowel, the liver and bladder, and the female reproductive tract. Drunk with gum tragacanth or a suitable liquid, the burnt horn is good for spitting blood, dysentery, coeliac complaints (intestinal disorders), jaundice and bladder disorders, and for women's excessive menstrual discharge. Applied as washed ash, it treats discharges and ulcers of the eyes. Rubbed on the teeth it cleans them, and boiled in vinegar as a mouthwash it soothes inner tooth pain, which maps to the teeth and gums. The smoke of raw horn drives off snakes, a use with no organ. The chapter gives no quality or temperament for the burnt horn. In modern terms, spitting blood maps to the respiratory tract, jaundice to the liver and bile, and coeliac complaints to the intestines. The text gives one quantity, two spoonfuls with no stated size, and no duration. Bloodspitting, jaundice and dysentery need medical care.

### Contraindications

- **Bleeding, jaundice, dysentery.** These are medical conditions; use proper care. The text's own cure for bloodspitting is not a replacement for it.
- **Source of the material.** Deer horn is collected from live or dead animals; legal status and trade rules for deer parts differ by jurisdiction. [USER: verify.]
- **Burnt preparations.** A hot clay firing is a burn and fume hazard.
- **Snake fumigation.** The smoke use is recorded only; it is no protection against snakebite.
