# Vine-prunings ash — tephra klematine (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 5-135); cinis sarmentitius

## *The ash of vine twigs or prunings, a burnt preparation; the chapter heading is TEPHRA KLEMATINE and the 2000 English edition's gloss is "Ash from twigs or prunings", with the Latin cinis sarmentitius ("caustic"). The chapter does not name the vine; the grape vine is the usual reading of *klema* and *sarmentum* but is our inference, of medium confidence* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

> **CAUSTIC CAUTION.** Dioscorides himself calls the ash "caustic". Wood ash and the lye made from it are strongly alkaline and burn skin and eyes. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation.

This is a single-text entry. The row was entered on 8 November 2026 from a residual extraction key, "TEPHRA KLEMATINE [DMM]", at line 10536 of the English text, with no entry written for it; this entry replaces that placeholder.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The chapter is 5-135 in the Book 5 section on metallic stones and earths (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 10536 and following). The heading is "5-135. TEPHRA KLEMATINE", with the translator's line "SUGGESTED: Ash from twigs or prunings". The whole chapter reads: "Cinis sarmentitius is caustic. Smeared on with goose grease or oil it is good for bruised sinews, dislocated joints and knotted nerves. With saltpetre [potassium nitrate] and vinegar it represses fleshy abnormal growths in the scrotum, and smeared on with vinegar it heals the bites of snakes or dogs. It is mixed with scab-forming medicines. Lye is made from it, good for precipitations [antidotes to mercury poisoning], and [the effects of] mushrooms, used with vinegar, salt and honey."

The chapter sits between gypsum (5-134) and alcyonium (5-136), among burnt and mineral materials, which is why a plant ash is filed in the mineral book. The ash is a made product: the vine is pruned, the twigs burnt, the ash used. Other parts of the vine were not read for this entry, so nothing is said here about its leaves, tendrils or sap.

### Active Components

The sources name no constituent. In general terms, wood ash is mostly calcium carbonate with potassium and other mineral salts. Water leached through it gives a strongly alkaline solution of potassium salts (lye), the "lye" of the chapter. No analytical source was retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Caustic.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Outside: bruised sinews, dislocated joints and "knotted nerves" (with goose grease or oil); fleshy growths in the scrotum (with saltpetre and vinegar); bites of snakes or dogs (with vinegar). A component of scab-forming (escharotic) medicines. Lye from the ash is "good for precipitations [antidotes to mercury poisoning], and [the effects of] mushrooms", with vinegar, salt and honey.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no amount of ash to fat, no length of application, and no instruction for making the lye.

The Codex does not translate the terms into modern diagnoses and recommends none of these uses. The lye-as-antidote line in particular must not be read as advice for poisoning.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives vehicles, not amounts: goose grease or oil; saltpetre and vinegar; vinegar alone; vinegar, salt and honey for the lye. Making the ash is implied (burn the twigs) and not described. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Alkaline wood-ash solutions are caustic to skin and mucous membranes, which agrees in kind with the word "caustic", and were historically used for cleaning and soap-making. Vinegar neutralises alkali. Alkali is not an antidote to mushroom or mercury poisoning. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM (2000 English).** The only quality printed is "caustic". Warming, cooling, or astringent are not stated for this chapter, and none is imported.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is asserted. The chapter makes no claim about the person's constitution, vitality or length of life. What it offers a practitioner is the discipline of attention that runs through Book 5: knowing that a common waste, the ash of pruned vines, is a made material with a named strength, a stated set of vehicles, and a danger the author names first.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. It is a caustic ash for external rubs and for a lye, recorded in a book of materials. The Codex does not recommend any of the uses. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Caustic.** The ash and its lye burn skin, eyes and the gut. Do not apply to broken skin or ingest.
- **Poisoning.** Nothing in this entry is a treatment for snake or dog bite, mushroom or mercury poisoning. Seek emergency care.
- **Identity.** The vine is inferred, not named by the text. [USER: verify]
