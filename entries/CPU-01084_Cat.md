# Cat — Cat, Cat's dung, Cat's Oil, Cat's Fat (Bryan's Ebers) / Felis domestica Brisson (Men-Tsee-Khang glossary)
## *The domestic cat and its products; the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints* Felis domestica *Brisson; the Ebers names are Bryan's English renderings* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP, FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The English text on disk is C. P. Bryan's 1930 *The Papyrus Ebers*, a translation with summaries; the Egyptian words are not printed. In his chapter on organic remedies Bryan lists the animals that "were called upon to sacrifice themselves in the cause of the Healing Art", and the cat stands in that list between the dog and the frog (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1404, page 35 of the book); the parts and products the papyrus draws from animals are listed separately (lines 1410-1417). The spine's list row is `_sources/ebers.csv` line 104, "Cat", with source field "Ebers Papyrus; Ghalioungui 1987 trans., Glossary I (Medicaments)"; that glossary is not on disk and was not read. The cat appears in Bryan's pages in six forms:

- *Cat's dung.* In a plaster "to drive out the hardening in the abdomen": bread of the *Zizyphus lotus*, cat's dung, red lead (in one version), watermelon, sweet beer and wine, "make into one and apply as a plaster" (lines 1900-1923, and again in the diagnosis section at lines 3548-3560). In a burns series, a first-day dressing of elderberries, *uah*-corn and cat's dung "in Cake-water" (lines 2250-2251). In the skin chapter, for common scurf complicated by "Hardenings in all the Limbs of a Person", a poultice of "Pieces-of-excrement, Cat's dung, Dog's dung, and Berries-of-the-Xet-plant", of which the Scribe adds "It drives out all the scurf" (line 2643); and two further poultices of cat's dung with dog's dung, one with a piece of lead and one with a hog's tooth (lines 2682-2688).
- *Cat's oil.* One ingredient of a salve "to make supple the hardenings", with hog's fat, worm-oil, mouse oil and others (line 2191), and one of the salves offered "to strengthen the *met*" (line 3153; Bryan's note at lines 3148-3150 is that *met* probably refers to the nerves or the blood-vessels, the interpretation resting on what the Egyptians knew of anatomy).
- *Cat's fat.* In a compound for "the growth of the hair on a head which is becoming bald" with the fats of the lion, hippopotamus, crocodile, serpent and "Egyptian goat" (lines 4020-4028), and alone as a recipe "to keep mice away from clothes": "Cat's Fat. Smear on everything possible" (lines 4177-4179).
- *Hair of a cat.* Crushed with "a portion of cake" and applied to a burn as the dry remedy (line 2241).
- *Womb of a cat.* "Warmed in Oil with the Egg-of-the-gabgu-bird" and rubbed into the cropped head as a hair-dye (lines 1377, 4056).

Bryan's own comments are caustic ("a plaster that combines Cat's dung with Watermelon might be counted on to remove 'hardenings' anywhere and in anybody", line 1923) and are his, not the papyrus's. No recitation is reported with any cat compound in the lines read; since Bryan summarises, that is not proof the papyrus gives none. No deity is attributed.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The cat is a named animal in the Tibetan texts but no medicinal line for it was found. In the Explanatory Tantra's meat chapter the cat is the last of the domestic animals ("the dzo, yak, camel, horse, donkey, bull, skom.po ... goat, sheep, dog, pig, chicken, cat, etc."), Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 672; Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 2917 "the zho, yak, camel, horse, donkey, cow, kompo, goat, sheep, dog, pig, hen and cat are the group of domesticated animals"). The group is one of the classes into which the texts sort animal flesh by habitat and quality; no line singles out cat flesh. The cat also appears twice outside materia medica: in the prognostic dreams, "dreaming that one is riding a cat, monkey, tiger, fox or corpse" is a sign that the patient "has been seized by the Lord of Death" (Clark line 402; Men-Tsee-Khang line 1856), and as a simile for method, where an unascertained disorder is to be treated experimentally "in the way that a cat lies in wait [for a mouse ...]" (Clark line 1358; Men-Tsee-Khang line 4214). The Men-Tsee-Khang animal glossary prints "Felis domestica Brisson" under Cat (line 7039; glossary heading at line 7036). The spine's locus for this tag is that glossary line. No cat part, blood, fat or dung is in the thirteen animal medicines of Clark ch.20 or the Men-Tsee-Khang list at lines 3503-3573.

### Active Components

An animal body, its fat and its faeces are not a defined compound, and the sources name no active component. In general: animal fat is mostly triglycerides; cat faeces carry the gut flora of a carnivore and, in some cases, parasites (below). No quantities are given here because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Egyptian cat compounds are a plaster, poultices and salves made from faeces, oil, fat, hair and womb, for abdominal hardening, burns, scurf, stiffness, "the *met*" and baldness; the Tibetan texts name the animal and give no medicine. The Codex has no protocol for any of it and offers none. The reasons are specific: faeces applied to burns and scurf is a route to infection; the cat is a protected companion animal in many countries and its slaughter or use as a drug is illegal or unacceptable in many places; the sources give no dose; and the Ebers identifications are modern hypotheses (see the register).

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations Bryan reports are the plasters and poultices above (the ingredients "made into one" and applied), the salve (cat's oil, hog's fat, worm-oil, mouse oil gathered "into one" and used to "anoint therewith", line 2192), the hair-dye (womb warmed in oil with an egg, rubbed on the cropped head), and the mouse-repellent (fat smeared on clothes). He gives no quantities for the cat items. The Tibetan texts give no preparation. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Cat faeces can carry *Toxoplasma gondii* oocysts, *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter* and the eggs of roundworms (*Toxocara cati*); *Toxoplasma* is a particular risk in pregnancy. Cat bites and scratches carry *Pasteurella* and *Bartonella henselae* (cat-scratch disease), and cats can transmit rabies in areas where it persists. Fat, oil and hair of the cat have no established therapeutic action. Cat fat as a mouse-repellent is a claim no source read tests. No clinical studies of the traditional uses were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** None is printed. Bryan's translation gives compounds by ingredient and complaint, with no quality, taste or temperature for any ingredient.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** None is printed for the cat. The cat is a member of the "domestic animals" group, one of the classes whose flesh is described by quality in the meat chapter; the group's stated qualities (sweet in taste and post-digestive taste for all the meats, Clark line 673) are not assigned to the cat in any line read.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* Every cat item in Bryan is a remedy for a complaint or a household use: abdominal hardening, burns, scurf, suppleness, hair colour and growth, and keeping mice from clothes. Nothing says what the cat does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); Bryan reports no recitation with the cat compounds, and since he summarises, that is not proof the papyrus gives none. No deity is attributed, and the cat's later religious standing in Egypt is not in the sources read and is not asserted here. Because Ebers identifications are modern hypotheses, "Cat" is Bryan's English rendering, and the spine row (`_sources/ebers.csv` line 104) rests on a list whose glossary was not read. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent, and the attestation rests on a glossary name.* The cat is named in the domestic-animals group, in a dream omen and in a simile, and no cat product is given as a medicine in the lines read. No essence-extraction (*bcud len*) use is attested, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition; none is asserted. The dream omen is a prognostic sign of the Lord of Death, not a cultivation claim. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do. For a cultivator the cat entry records what the Egyptian scribe did for burns, scurf and baldness, and what the Tibetan texts say about the cat's place in the animal groups and dream signs; neither tradition ties it to vitality. The cat is a companion animal, and the fats and faeces of the sources are hazards, not remedies.

### Contraindications

- **Infection.** Cat faeces and bites carry *Toxoplasma*, *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter*, *Toxocara* and *Bartonella*; never apply faeces to burns, wounds or skin disease. Pregnant people should avoid contact with cat faeces.
- **Burns and wounds.** Burns need clean cooling and medical care; the Egyptian burn dressings read are not guidance.
- **Legal and ethical.** The cat is a companion animal, and cruelty to it, including killing it for medicine, is an offence in many jurisdictions. [USER: verify specifics]
- **Dose unknown.** No dose is given in the sources read.
