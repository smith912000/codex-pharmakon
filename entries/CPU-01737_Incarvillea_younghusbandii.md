# Incarvillea younghusbandii — aoog.chos, Ukchhoe (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Incarvillea younghusbandii, the first of four identifications Clark 1995 prints for aoog.chos (the others are Incarvillea compacta, Gossipium herbaceum and Paeonia anomala); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints "Incarvillea younghusban" beside Ukchhoe* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk for the genus name *Incarvillea* found nothing; it occurs only in the two Tibetan texts and the Tibetan extraction. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 198) the Tibetan name *aoog.chos* is recorded at Clark line 1112 with the identification "Incarvillea younghusbandii; Incarvillea compacta Maxim; Gossipium herbaceum; Paeonia anomala" and the note "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The key, *Incarvillea younghusbandii*, is therefore the first of the four names Clark prints, and not a later one. The row is a Tibetan drug name with four candidate plants. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" and that "sometimes the same name refers to different plants growing in unrelated geographical locations".

**Identity doubt.** Two of the four names are *Incarvillea* species. The other two are a cotton (*Gossipium herbaceum*, Clark's spelling) and a peony (*Paeonia anomala*), which are plainly different genera. The Codex cannot say from the sources read which plant the Tibetan texts meant, and has not set Clark's description against a flora. The entry stands for the Tibetan drug. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (lines 1112 to 1115).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry begins at line 1112, a page number (110) is printed at line 1113, and it ends at line 1115. It reads:

"aoog.chos: Incarvillea younghusbandii: Incarvillea compacta Maxim: Gossipium herbaceum: Paeonia anomala: [has a red, yellow or white flower. The red type grows on rocks or cliffs. It has lobed leaves lying close to the ground, flowers similar to bunches of coral that contain vajra-like pistils, and seed-pods shaped like the curved horns of a wild sheep that contain leguminous seeds. Incarvillea has bitter and sweet tastes], cures ear diseases, purges swellings [and is carminative]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the flower colours, the habitat of the red type, the leaf, the flowers, the seed-pods, the tastes and "is carminative". Outside them (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the four Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the actions "cures ear diseases, purges swellings". The word "Incarvillea" in the commentary is Clark's own word for the drug and the commentary names only that genus. The line gives no part used and no preparation.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* In chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), line 3493 reads, in the middle of a longer passage: "Ukchhoe cures problems ofthe ears and eliminates distention of the abdomen." ("ofthe" is the OCR's reading.) The index lists "Ukchhoe 211,314" (line 7551). Clark's "ear diseases" and "swellings" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "problems of the ears" and "distention of the abdomen" are the two translations' wordings. The ears agree; the second action is worded differently, and the Codex does not treat "swellings" and "distention of the abdomen" as one term.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "U" block, lines 6004 to 6022).* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate runs. The phonetic names are "Ukchhoe", "Upal Upal ngonpo" and "Ushir" (lines 6006 to 6008). The transliterations are "ug chos ut+pal ut+pal sngon po" and "au shir" (lines 6010 and 6011). The scientific names are in one line (6013): "Incarvillea younghusban Meconopsis sp. Meconopsis sp. Cymbopogon citrates". The English names are "Light blue poppy" and "Lemon grass" (lines 6021 and 6022). Counting in order, "Ukchhoe" is the first phonetic name and the first scientific name is "Incarvillea younghusban"; "Upal" and "Upal ngonpo" take the two *Meconopsis* and "Ushir" takes *Cymbopogon*, which fits the "poppy" and "grass" English names. The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position. "younghusban" is evidently the epithet *younghusbandii* cut short, a reading and not a printed fact. [USER: verify] The two Tibetan texts agree on the Tibetan name, on the ears as the place of action, and, through Clark's first Latin name and the glossary's binomial, on an *Incarvillea*. They give no other detail in common.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Incarvillea*, *Paeonia anomala*, *aoog.chos* and *Ukchhoe* found no entry on this plant or passage. ### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Incarvillea* is a genus of the trumpet-creeper family (Bignoniaceae), and that cotton and peony belong to other families. Which part was used, and which of the four alternatives the Tibetan texts mean, are not settled by the lines read, so no general statement about constituents is made for the drug.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "cures ear diseases, purges swellings" (Clark, line 1112) and "cures problems of the ears and eliminates distention of the abdomen" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3493). These are the texts' claims in their own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and nothing in this entry should be put into the ear or taken by mouth. Ear complaints need medical assessment.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 1112) prints "Incarvillea has bitter and sweet tastes". No power and no post-digestive taste is printed for it. Men-Tsee-Khang prints no taste or power for the name in the lines read. The actions printed are curing ear diseases and purging swellings (Clark, with "carminative" in the commentary), and curing problems of the ears and eliminating distention of the abdomen (Men-Tsee-Khang).

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it cures ear diseases and purges swellings, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says it cures problems of the ears and eliminates distention of the abdomen. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the chapter 20 lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes, and not in the rejuvenation material; no line read places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name, not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is an ear and swelling drug in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and the plant behind the Tibetan name is uncertain. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Ear disease needs medical assessment, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Because the identity is uncertain, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
