# Branched Broomrape — Hemp Broomrape, Ramose Broomrape, Lie Dang

## *Orobanche ramosa* L. (syn. *Phelipanche ramosa* (L.) Pomel) | Family: Orobanchaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, SBJ, CM
**Energetic Classification:** Warm and dry, mildly astringent — a consolidating tonic that binds and steadies depleted tissue
**Training Phase:** Consolidation / Grounding (the binding, tonifying phase that anchors and firms after leakage or depletion)

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Botanical nature first.** *Orobanche ramosa* is a chlorophyll-less **holoparasitic plant**: having no green tissue, it draws all its water and nutrients from the roots of a host (tobacco, hemp, tomato, brassicas and other crops), emerging as a pale, scaly, branched flowering spike. Agronomically it is a serious **crop-parasite weed** — a devastating pest of tobacco and solanaceous/hemp crops — and much of the modern literature treats it as something to eradicate rather than to harvest. Its medicinal use is therefore modest and regionally scattered, and its pharmacology is genuinely under-studied.

**Shennong / Chinese Materia Medica (SBJ):** The strongest medicinal thread for the genus is East Asian. *Orobanche* species (Chinese *lie dang*) sit within the Chinese tonic tradition as kidney-*yang*–warming, tissue-firming remedies — conceptually adjacent to the famous *rou cong rong* (*Cistanche*, a related Orobanchaceae parasite), and used for the same broad ends: to reinforce the Kidney, firm what leaks (spermatorrhoea, incontinence), steady the low back and knees, and support the aged or depleted. Broomrapes are increasingly described as nourishing "tonic" foods and medicines in this frame.

**Dioscorides / De Materia Medica (DMM):** The Greek herbal names *orobanche* (literally "vetch-strangler," from its parasitism of legumes) and notes it among edible/pot-herb plants — the young shoots eaten like asparagus — with the astringent, drying character typical of the plants Dioscorides grouped as gentle binders. Its Mediterranean use leaned as much toward food as toward formal medicine.

**Assyrian tablets (AT):** Parasitic and "rootless" plants of striking appearance drew note in the Mesopotamian plant-lists; a pale spike erupting from bare soil invited both culinary use and the sympathetic-magic associations that clung to such oddities. Precise identification behind the cuneiform names is not securely recoverable, so this attribution is at the level of plant-class, not confirmed species.

**Avicenna / Canon of Medicine (CM):** In the Greco-Arabic tradition an astringent, drying, mildly warming herb of this type answers to the binders used to check fluxes and firm loose tissue. Broomrape's placement here follows its Dioscoridean astringent-food character carried into the Unani synthesis.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Broomrape's signature is *borrowed vitality* — a plant with no roots or leaves of its own that lives entirely on another's strength. Traditions read this as a plant that "takes and holds," and assigned it consolidating, retaining, tonifying roles: firming the Kidney, binding leakage, propping up the depleted. The pharmacology offers partial support: like its relatives, *Orobanche* accumulates phenylethanoid/phenylpropanoid glycosides (the acteoside/verbascoside class) with real antioxidant and immune-modulating activity — the biochemical basis on which *Cistanche*, its close cousin, is a mainstream tonic. Whether *O. ramosa* carries these at useful levels is not well established.

### Active Components

- **Phenylethanoid / phenylpropanoid glycosides (acteoside/verbascoside class):** the genus's signature actives, carrying antioxidant and immune-modulating activity (well established in relatives such as *Cistanche*; presence in *O. ramosa* inferred from the genus rather than richly documented).
- **Alkaloids:** reported among the genus's bioactive constituents.
- **General phenolics:** contributing antioxidant capacity.

*Note: the specific phytochemical profile of* O. ramosa *itself is sparsely characterised; much of the above is generalised from the genus.*

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Broomrape is filed as a **Consolidation-phase binder/tonic**: a warming, firming agent for states of leakage and depletion — the traditional remit being kidney-*yang* support, checking of involuntary loss (urinary, seminal), and steadying of a worn-down low back and knees. In the Kenetica frame it is a *grounding* remedy, used to hold and rebuild rather than move or disperse. Given the thin evidence base and its weed status, it is treated as a minor, traditionally-graded member of the tonic class — a footnote beside its far better-established cousin *Cistanche* (*rou cong rong*), which is the herb most trainees should reach for when this action is wanted.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, evidence specific to *Orobanche ramosa* as a medicine is very thin; the best synthesis is at the genus level:

- Shi *et al.* (2020, *J Ethnopharmacol*) — "The genus *Orobanche* as food and medicine: an ethnopharmacological review" — surveyed 60 years of literature and found *Orobanche* spp. used as food and medicine mainly in China and North America (as food in Europe), with **phenylpropanoid derivatives and alkaloids** as the main bioactives showing **antioxidant, immune-enhancing, androgenic, antibacterial and antiviral** properties, and increasing use in China as kidney-tonifying remedies for impotence, spermatorrhoea, dermatological problems and infantile diarrhoea. Crucially, the authors state plainly that the pharmacological studies done to date were **not sufficient to rationally explain the traditional uses**, and that more investigation and quality control are needed [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2020.113154).
- Much of the remaining *O. ramosa*-specific PubMed literature concerns it as an **agricultural parasite/weed** rather than a medicine — e.g. Kaya & Doğanlar (2015, *Ecotoxicol Environ Saf*) study it in the context of the herbicide imazapic used to control it, underscoring that the dominant scientific interest in this species is its eradication from crops [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecoenv.2015.11.026).

**Evidence grade: Traditional / data-sparse (low).** The genus carries plausible actives (phenylethanoid glycosides), and its close relative *Cistanche* is a well-supported tonic — but for *O. ramosa* specifically the medicinal evidence is minimal, and a leading review explicitly notes that existing pharmacology does not yet justify the traditional claims. This entry is graded **traditional use, unconfirmed by clinical data**.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM (by analogy within the *lie dang* / Orobanchaceae tonic group):** Sweet, warm; enters Kidney channel; warms and reinforces Kidney *yang*, firms *jing*/essence, checks leakage. (Formal classical status is far better fixed for *Cistanche* than for *O. ramosa*.)
- **Greco-Roman / Unani (Dioscoridean–Avicennan):** Astringent, drying, mildly warming; a gentle binder and astringent pot-herb.
- **Ayurveda:** No well-attested classical *rasa/guṇa/vīrya/vipāka* assignment for this specific European–West-Asian parasite; not formally placed.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** Parasitic and "rootless" plants of striking appearance — a pale scaly spike erupting from bare soil with no leaf or green of its own — drew note in the Mesopotamian plant-lists, inviting both culinary use and the sympathetic associations that clung to such oddities. In the cuneiform scheme illness is classified by causal agency, so a strange bare-earth plant might be read for the agency it counters; but the identification behind the Akkadian names is not securely recoverable, this attribution sits at the level of plant-class rather than confirmed species, and the standing blocking warning over every Assyrian substance identification, deity or demon attribution and tablet reference forbids any cultivation status here. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The Greek herbal names *orobanche* — "vetch-strangler," from its parasitism of legumes — and places it among edible pot-herbs, the young shoots eaten like asparagus, with the astringent, drying character of the gentle binders. Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system into which a cultivation status could be entered; the Galenic three spirits are later than him, and *pneuma* is context, not an assignable doctrine. What the tradition gives a cultivator is his discipline of attention — recognising a chlorophyll-less holoparasite by its host and habit, telling the edible shoot from lookalikes, and judging the season of gathering. Its Mediterranean standing leaned as much toward food as toward formal medicine, and no vital-body grade is assigned. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** In the Greco-Arabic synthesis an astringent, drying, mildly warming herb of this type answers to the binders used to check fluxes and firm loose tissue, and broomrape's placement here follows its Dioscoridean astringent-food character carried into the Unani frame rather than an independent Unani attestation of the species. The strongest cultivation idea in the corpus — conservation of innate heat and radical moisture, ageing being their depletion, so that longevity practice is conservation not acquisition — is a discipline of the whole regimen and is not a status assigned to this minor astringent binder; no *mufarrih* or *ruh*-strengthening role is claimed. Its standing is therapeutic and inherited. **(b) framed:** attested as an astringent binder, no cultivation status sourced.

**Chinese — Bencao (SBJ).** The strongest medicinal thread for the genus is East Asian: *Orobanche* species (*lie dang* 列當) sit within the Chinese tonic tradition as Kidney-*yang*–warming, tissue-firming remedies, conceptually adjacent to the famous *rou cong rong* (*Cistanche*, a related Orobanchaceae parasite) and reached for to reinforce the Kidney, firm what leaks (spermatorrhoea, incontinence), and steady the low back and knees. But the *san pin* 三品 grading that would give a cultivation status — the upper grade of substances taken long-term to nourish life — is fixed for the cousin *Cistanche*, **not** for *Orobanche ramosa* itself, and no upper-grade classing is recorded for this species in the sources retrieved. The documented tonic use of the genus is modern and genus-level (Shi et al., *J. Ethnopharmacol.* 2020), and that same review states plainly that existing pharmacology does not yet justify the traditional uses. Within *yang sheng* 養生 — conservational, not curative — this species therefore carries a therapeutic-by-analogy standing, not an attested cultivation grade of its own. **(b) framed**, with the classical status belonging to *Cistanche*, not to this plant.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator who wants this consolidating, Kidney-firming action reaches for the well-characterised *rou cong rong* (*Cistanche deserticola*), not for branched broomrape — which is a data-sparse crop-weed whose tonic reputation is borrowed, by botanical analogy, from its far better-attested cousin. Nothing sourced here gives *O. ramosa* a longevity grade of its own. If it is used at all it is as a minor grounding binder, and the host matters as much as the plant: material drawn up from a toxic or herbicide-treated host carries the host's contaminants, so it is never harvested from treated fields. The honest instruction is to treat this as a footnote to *Cistanche* and to prefer the graded herb.

### Contraindications

Data are insufficient to define a safety profile; treat as **traditional-use only, not clinically validated**. Correctly identify the plant and its host before any use — broomrape draws its substance from whatever it parasitises (tobacco, solanaceous crops), so material grown on a toxic or pesticide-treated host may carry host-derived contaminants; **never harvest from herbicide-treated fields**, given that this species is a primary target of agricultural herbicides. Avoid in pregnancy and lactation (no safety data). Because genus members show androgenic and immune-modulating activity, exercise caution in hormone-sensitive conditions.
