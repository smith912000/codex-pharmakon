# I-201 | Centella asiatica (Gotu Kola)

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted binomial:** *Centella asiatica* (L.) Urb.  
**Family:** Apiaceae (Umbelliferae) — carrot/parsley family  
**Authority:** (Linnaeus) Urban, 1879

**Synonyms:**
- *Hydrocotyle asiatica* L. (basionym, 1753)
- *Hydrocotyle lurida* Hance
- *Trisanthus cochinchinensis* Lour.

**Common names by tradition:**
- **Sanskrit/Ayurveda:** Mandūkaparṇī (frog-leaf — from leaf shape), Brahmi (contested — see disambiguation)
- **Hindi:** Brahmi, Brahma-manduki, Khulakhudi
- **Sinhala:** Gotu kola (primary common name in English)
- **Tamil:** Vallārai
- **Kannada:** Ondelaga
- **Chinese:** Jī Xuè Cǎo (积雪草) — "accumulated snow herb"
- **Japanese:** Tsubokusa (壺草)
- **Thai:** Bua bok
- **Malay/Indonesian:** Pegagan, Daun kaki kuda (horse's hoof leaf)
- **French:** Hydrocotyle asiatique, Herbe du tigre (herb of the tiger)
- **German:** Wassernabel, Tigergras
- **English:** Gotu kola, Indian pennywort, Asiatic pennywort, tiger herb

**CRITICAL DISAMBIGUATION — THE "BRAHMI" CONFUSION:**  
Two entirely different plants are called "Brahmi" in different regions of India:
- **Northern India:** "Brahmi" = *Centella asiatica* (this entry)
- **Southern India/Classical Ayurveda:** "Brahmi" = *Bacopa monnieri* (I-200)
- These are botanically unrelated (Apiaceae vs. Plantaginaceae), pharmacologically distinct (triterpenoids vs. bacosides), and have different but overlapping therapeutic profiles
- Mechanism of cognitive action: C. asiatica = structural repair/BDNF/collagen; B. monnieri = AChE inhibition/synaptic enhancement
- Any product labeled "Brahmi" requires species verification — substitution is common and clinically significant
- In Ayurvedic texts: Mandukaparni is the classical Sanskrit name for C. asiatica; Brahmi in classical texts more reliably refers to B. monnieri

**Tiger herb etymology:** In Asian folklore, wounded tigers roll in C. asiatica to heal wounds — a piece of ethnopharmacological observation that correctly predicted its wound-healing properties, later confirmed pharmacologically through collagen synthesis mechanisms.

**Botanical description:**  
Slender, creeping perennial herb; stolons long-rooting at nodes; leaves rounded to reniform, 1–4 cm, crenate margins, long petioles; inflorescences umbellate, 3–7 small pink-white flowers; fruit small cremocarp. Aquatic/semi-aquatic preferences — found in wet rice paddies, riverbanks, roadside ditches throughout tropical/subtropical Asia, tropical Americas, South Africa. Pantropical distribution — same species found across these regions. Can grow as leaf vegetable (Sri Lanka, South Asia, Southeast Asia), consumed raw in salads or cooked.

**Part used:** Whole aerial herb (leaves, stems); fresh or dried; occasionally root  
**Harvest:** Leaves before flowering for maximal triterpenoid content  
**Active fraction:** Ethanol or water-ethanol extracts; triterpenoid-enriched extracts

**Convergence Score: 2/9 confirmed, 3/9 tentative (2✓ + 3~)**  
Despite modest formal-text convergence, geographical distribution (pantropical) creates de facto independent discovery wherever plant occurs. Wound-healing and cognitive-tonic uses appear independently across South Asian, Southeast Asian, East Asian, and Caribbean traditions.

**Detailed tradition notes:**

*Sushruta Samhita / Charaka Samhita (Classical Ayurveda):*  
Mandūkaparṇī is one of the four *medhya rasāyana* (intellect-promoting rejuvenatives) in Charaka Samhita alongside Bacopa, Shankhapushpi, and Guduchi. Used as a brain tonic in children's formulas, longevity preparations, and wound-healing pastes. Charaka describes it specifically for memory, intellect, voice, complexion, and longevity — remarkably accurate description of mechanisms now understood as BDNF upregulation, collagen synthesis, and neuroprotection. Applied topically to wounds, burns, skin diseases, and leprous lesions. Internally for nervous disorders, epilepsy, and general debility.

*Bencao Gangmu (Li Shizhen) — Jī Xuè Cǎo:*  
Listed under herbs that "clear heat, eliminate dampness, and detoxify." Used for skin disorders, snake bites (topically and internally), trauma, and urinary infections. Li Shizhen recognized both internal (liver/lung heat clearing) and external (wound healing) applications. Modern Chinese medicine continues to use it for dermatological applications and venous insufficiency.

*Sri Lanka:*  
Gotu kola is consumed as a food vegetable — fresh leaves in coconut sambol, cooked as a side dish. This nutritional integration means daily consumption is normalized, providing consistent low-dose triterpene exposure at population level. Sri Lankan traditional medicine uses it specifically for memory enhancement, longevity, and skin health. Highest traditional knowledge density for this plant.

*Southeast Asian traditions:*  
Pegagan used across Indonesian, Malaysian, Thai, and Vietnamese traditional medicine for wound healing, skin conditions, memory, and febrifuge applications. Prepared as fresh juice, poultice, or decoction. Integrated into food culture in Thailand (bua bok juice).

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Mechanistic convergence across traditions:** Despite different naming conventions and cultural frameworks, three independent convergence points emerge:

**1. Wound healing / skin repair** (present in ALL traditions where plant is known):  
- Ayurveda: topical paste for wounds, burns, leprous ulcers
- Chinese medicine: skin disorders, trauma, snakebite wound healing
- Southeast Asia: wound poultice
- Sri Lanka: skin health (food-level integration)
- European herbal: wound herb
*→ Validated by: asiatic acid + madecassic acid stimulation of fibroblast collagen synthesis (Types I, III, IV), TGF-β1 upregulation, wound contraction acceleration*

**2. Brain tonic / memory / longevity** (convergence across Ayurvedic, Chinese, Southeast Asian traditions):  
- One of four classical Ayurvedic medhya rasayana
- Sri Lankan folk medicine for memory ("food for the brain")
- TCM: some traditions use for senile dementia, mental clarity
*→ Validated by: BDNF upregulation, neurogenesis in dentate gyrus, axonal sprouting, anxiety reduction via GABA-A modulation*

**3. Venous tone / circulatory** (partially traditional, strongly confirmed clinically):  
- Some traditional references to varicose conditions, swollen legs
- Most strongly confirmed pharmacologically — venous wall strengthening through collagen/elastin restructuring
*→ Validated by: 7 RCTs for chronic venous insufficiency; pycnogenol comparison trials; venous hypertension reduction*

**The triterpenoid unification:** All traditional wound-healing and skin-repair applications converge on a single pharmacological mechanism — titrated extract of asiaticoside/asiatic acid activating fibroblasts. This is one of the cleanest traditional-to-mechanism validations in the codex.

**Cultural integration as validation:** The fact that C. asiatica is eaten as a regular food vegetable in Sri Lanka, while simultaneously being classified as a "brain tonic" in those same traditions, implies the cognitive benefits were noticed at food-level doses — a lower threshold for perceptibility than typically required for an "herb" to be designated medicinal.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Primary bioactive triterpenoid saponins (asiaticoside complex):**

| Compound | Type | Primary Activity |
|----------|------|-----------------|
| **Asiaticoside** | Saponin glycoside of asiatic acid | Pro-drug → asiatic acid in vivo; collagen synthesis; wound healing |
| **Madecassoside** | Saponin glycoside of madecassic acid | Pro-drug → madecassic acid; anti-inflammatory; collagen synthesis |
| **Asiatic acid** (aglycone) | Pentacyclic triterpenoid (ursane) | Direct: fibroblast stimulation, TGF-β1; neuroprotection; anti-tumor |
| **Madecassic acid** (aglycone) | Pentacyclic triterpenoid (ursane) | Anti-inflammatory; collagen I/III synthesis; VEGF modulation |
| **Brahminoside** | Minor saponin | Minor activity |
| **Centelloside** | Minor saponin | Minor activity |

*The standard TTFCA (titrated extract of total triterpenoids of C. asiatica) = 40% asiaticoside + 30% asiatic acid + 30% madecassic acid + madecassoside. This standardization is used in the most rigorous clinical trials.*

**Secondary phenolic compounds:**
- **Quercetin** — flavonoid; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory
- **Kaempferol** — flavonoid; antioxidant
- **Rutin** — flavonoid glycoside; vascular capillary integrity; overlaps with venous indication
- **Caffeic acid** — phenolic acid
- **Chlorogenic acid** — antioxidant; glucose metabolism
- **Rosmarinic acid** — anti-inflammatory phenolic
- **Castilliferol, Castillicetin** — minor flavonoids

**Volatile oil (minor, <1%):**
- Caryophyllene, farnesol, germacrene — minor contribution to total effect

**Nutritional components (relevant as food vegetable):**
- Vitamin C, beta-carotene, B vitamins
- Calcium, potassium, iron, zinc
- Chlorophyll (substantial in fresh leaves)
- Amino acids including glutamine, aspartate

**Standardization markers for quality:**
- TTFCA: ≥40% total triterpenoids (sum asiaticoside + asiatic acid + madecassoside + madecassic acid)
- ECa 233 extract (used in some studies): 80% triterpenoids
- Fresh herb: highly variable triterpenoid content depending on growth conditions, age, season
- Commercially unreliable without triterpenoid standardization

**Geographic phytochemical variation:** Studies show C. asiatica from different regions has substantially different triterpenoid profiles. Sri Lankan material tends to be high in asiaticoside; Indian material more variable; Chinese material often higher in madecassoside. This has clinical implications for product selection.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**PRIMARY MECHANISM: Triterpenoid-mediated collagen synthesis and wound repair**

**Fibroblast activation cascade:**
1. Asiaticoside (pro-drug) → enzymatic hydrolysis → asiatic acid (active aglycone)
2. Asiatic acid enters fibroblasts → activates TGF-β1/SMAD2/3 pathway
3. Upregulates procollagen type I alpha-1 and alpha-2 gene expression
4. Increases fibronectin, laminin, and other extracellular matrix proteins
5. Promotes fibroblast proliferation and migration
6. Net: accelerated wound closure, improved tensile strength of healed tissue
7. Parallel effect: madecassic acid stimulates procollagen I and III simultaneously → balanced matrix reconstruction

**Anti-fibrotic paradox:** At wound-healing doses, C. asiatica promotes collagen synthesis for tissue repair. However, at higher doses/concentrations, it INHIBITS excessive collagen formation (anti-fibrotic). This paradox explains its clinical utility in:
- Wound healing (pro-collagen at appropriate doses)
- Keloid/hypertrophic scar prevention (anti-fibrotic at high doses)
- Liver fibrosis experimental models (anti-fibrotic)
Dose and context determine which direction predominates.

**VEGF modulation:** Asiatic acid modulates VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor) — promoting angiogenesis in wound beds while potentially restraining pathological neovascularization.

---

**SECOND MECHANISM: Venous wall restructuring and venous tone**

Chronic venous insufficiency (CVI) involves degradation of the venous wall extracellular matrix (collagen/elastin) under venous hypertension:
- TTFCA directly strengthens venous wall through collagen/elastin synthesis stimulation
- Reduces venous hypertension-induced permeability (capillary leakage)
- Decreases fibrin cuff formation around capillaries (a hallmark of venous ulcer pathology)
- Inhibits matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) that degrade venous wall matrix
- Net: improved venous tone, reduced edema, reduced varicosity progression

This is the same triterpenoid mechanism applied to a different anatomical target (venous wall connective tissue rather than skin wound). The clinical evidence for CVI is the strongest of any indication.

---

**THIRD MECHANISM: Neuroprotection and neurotrophic signaling**

1. **BDNF upregulation:** Asiatic acid increases BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) in hippocampus and cortex → synaptic plasticity, neurogenesis, neuroprotection
2. **Neurogenesis:** Promotes neurogenesis in dentate gyrus (hippocampal neurogenesis zone) — axonal growth, dendritic sprouting
3. **Mitochondrial protection:** Asiatic acid protects neurons against oxidative stress-induced mitochondrial permeability transition (similar mechanism to schisandrin B, I-185)
4. **Glutamate excitotoxicity:** Reduces glutamate-induced neuronal apoptosis
5. **Amyloid protection:** In vitro/animal: asiatic acid reduces β-amyloid aggregation and neurotoxicity
6. **Acetylcholinesterase inhibition:** Mild AChE inhibition documented (weaker than Bacopa) — contributes to but is not primary cognitive mechanism

Note: C. asiatica cognitive mechanism differs fundamentally from Bacopa (I-200):
- Bacopa: primary AChE inhibition → acute cholinergic enhancement
- C. asiatica: primary structural/trophic → BDNF/neurogenesis (slower onset, may be more durable)
- Combination possible but AChE additive effect requires monitoring

---

**FOURTH MECHANISM: Anxiolytic via GABAergic modulation**

- Brahmoside and brahminoside (minor saponins): bind GABA-A/B receptors
- Asiatic acid: reduces startle response amplitude in animal models (acoustic startle response — a validated anxiolytic biomarker)
- Clinical: reduces anxiety symptoms without sedation at standard doses
- Mechanism: positive allosteric modulation of GABA-A (similar principle to Ashwagandha withanolides, Paeonia paeoniflorin, Bacopa apigenin)
- This GABAergic anxiolytic action may contribute to the traditional classification as a nervine tonic

---

**FIFTH MECHANISM: Anti-inflammatory**

- Inhibition of NF-κB pathway (same master inflammatory switch targeted by multiple adaptogens)
- Reduction of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 via madecassic acid
- Inhibition of COX-2 (weaker than NSAIDs, clinically mild)
- Relevant for inflammatory skin conditions, wound inflammation phase management

---

**Topical vs. systemic considerations:**
- Topical: direct local triterpenoid delivery to wound/skin
- Systemic: oral bioavailability of asiaticoside moderate; hydrolysis to asiatic acid necessary; hepatic first-pass
- Standardized extracts (TTFCA) designed to optimize systemic delivery of triterpenoid aglycones

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**STRONGEST EVIDENCE: Chronic Venous Insufficiency (CVI)**

*Pointel et al. (1987) — Angiology:*  
N=94, double-blind RCT; TTFCA 120mg/day vs. placebo; 12 weeks. Significant improvement in heaviness, edema, nocturnal cramps, paresthesia (p<0.001). Venous distensibility improved. One of earliest rigorous trials.

*Cesarone et al. (2001) — Angiology:*  
N=40, TTFCA vs. placebo; 4 weeks. Significant reduction in venous hypertension and ankle edema. Plethysmographic confirmation of venous tone improvement. Supported Pointel findings.

*Systemic review — De Sanctis et al. (2001):*  
Reviewed 9 RCTs for CVI with C. asiatica extracts. Concluded consistent evidence for symptomatic relief of CVI including edema, pain, heaviness, cramps. Recommended 60–120mg TTFCA daily.

*Comparison with pycnogenol:*  
Multiple studies compare TTFCA to pycnogenol (French maritime pine bark extract) for CVI. Both show efficacy; TTFCA often shows faster reduction in permeability and ankle edema. Different but complementary mechanisms.

**Practical implication:** C. asiatica has among the best-evidenced herbal treatments for CVI — comparable evidence base to horse chestnut (I-182) but through a different mechanism. Horse chestnut: aescin venotonic/anti-enzymatic. C. asiatica: collagen/elastin structural strengthening.

---

**GOOD EVIDENCE: Wound Healing / Burn Treatment**

*Widgerow et al. (2000) — Aesthetic Plastic Surgery:*  
Topical Centella extract significantly reduced keloid/hypertrophic scar formation post-surgery. First rigorous surgical scar prevention study.

*Shukla et al. (1999) — International Journal of Experimental Pathology:*  
Animal model: C. asiatica extract accelerated wound closure, increased collagen content, and improved tensile strength vs. controls. Mechanism (TGF-β1/collagen synthesis) confirmed histologically.

*Burns trials:*  
Several controlled studies (particularly from Asian centers) show topical C. asiatica accelerates re-epithelialization in partial-thickness burns. Mechanism: fibroblast stimulation in wound bed. Used as ingredient in commercial burn treatment creams in Asia and France (Madecassol, Centelase).

*Pharmaceutical application:*  
Madecassol (TTFCA cream) is a licensed pharmaceutical wound treatment in France, Belgium, and several Asian countries. ECa 233 extract is a standardized Thai pharmaceutical approved for wound healing. This represents traditional-to-pharmaceutical translation parallel to TMP (I-191), DDB (I-185), TGP (I-190).

---

**EMERGING EVIDENCE: Cognitive Enhancement / Anxiety**

*Wattanathorn et al. (2008) — Journal of Ethnopharmacology:*  
N=28 elderly, single-blind; 500mg vs. 750mg vs. placebo; 2 months. Significant improvement in delayed word recall and working memory. Also reduced anxiety on DASS-21. One of the clearest cognitive trials.

*Tiwari et al. (2008) — Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine:*  
N=33 healthy adults, 12 weeks C. asiatica extract. Significant improvement on Wechsler Memory Scale and sustained attention tasks. Cognitive onset appears faster than Bacopa (weeks rather than months) — consistent with BDNF mechanism (faster than structural synaptic changes).

*Acute anxiolytic — Bhatt et al. (2003):*  
Open study: oral C. asiatica reduced acoustic startle response amplitude (validated anxiolytic biomarker) significantly within 30–60 minutes. Suggests GABAergic mechanism with rapid onset component alongside longer-term BDNF effects.

*Systematic review — Puttarak et al. (2017) — Scientific Reports:*  
Meta-analysis cognitive studies. Concluded: "positive effects on cognition, especially in domains of attention, working memory, and mood." Evidence quality moderate. Dose and standardization heterogeneity limits pooled effect size.

---

**EMERGING EVIDENCE: Radioprotection / Skin**

Several in vitro and animal studies show C. asiatica extracts protect skin cells against UV-B radiation damage via antioxidant mechanisms and DNA repair promotion. Used in cosmeceutical sun damage treatment.

---

**WEAK/INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE:**
- Alzheimer's disease (only animal and in vitro data for β-amyloid effects)
- Diabetes (some preclinical hypoglycemic data, no adequate human RCTs)
- Epilepsy (traditional use; very limited modern evidence)
- HIV/antiviral (in vitro only)

---

**Dosage parameters (clinical evidence basis):**

| Indication | Form | Dose | Duration |
|------------|------|------|----------|
| Chronic venous insufficiency | TTFCA (standardized extract) | 60–120 mg/day in 2–3 divided doses | ≥4–12 weeks |
| Wound healing (topical) | TTFCA cream 0.1–1% | Apply 2–3× daily | Until healed |
| Cognitive/anxiety | Standardized extract (≥10% asiatosides) | 500–750 mg/day | ≥4–8 weeks |
| Whole herb powder | Fresh herb equivalent | 3–6g/day dried herb | Variable |

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The tiger wound narrative:**  
The consistent cross-cultural tiger-wound healing story (observed in Sri Lanka, India, Southeast Asia, and European colonial natural history accounts) represents an extraordinary piece of empirical observation — humans noticing that tigers and other large cats seek out this plant when wounded. This qualitative folk observation predated any understanding of fibroblast biology by centuries and proved pharmacologically correct.

**Taste and immediate sensory experience:**  
Fresh leaves: slightly bitter, grassy, faintly pungent, mild astringency. Eaten as vegetable in Sri Lanka (as "gotu kola sambol"), the taste is distinctly green and vegetable-like. Not strongly aromatic. The lack of strong taste makes it easy to consume as food — unlike many medicinal herbs whose palatability is a limiting factor.

**Reported cognitive effects (patient/subject accounts):**
- Subjective clarity described as "gentle" and "spacious" rather than stimulatory
- Unlike Bacopa (which some users describe as mildly sedating acutely), C. asiatica users more often describe subtle mood brightening alongside cognitive effects
- Anxiety reduction reported as "edge taken off" rather than sedation
- Sleep improvement noted in some accounts — possibly GABA-A modulation; not sedating at daytime doses
- Onset of cognitive effects: 2–4 weeks (faster than Bacopa's 8–12 week minimum) consistent with BDNF mechanism rather than structural synaptic reorganization
- Consistent food-consumer reports in Sri Lanka: lifelong daily consumption associated with mental sharpness and longevity in folk perception ("old people eat gotu kola and have sharp minds")

**Wound healing phenomenology:**  
Clinical and patient accounts of topical application to burns/wounds describe:
- Reduced itching during re-epithelialization
- Improved healing appearance (less hypertrophic scarring)
- Mild cooling/soothing sensation on application
- Reduction in wound edge inflammation

**Skin texture observations:**  
Consistent cosmetic/patient reports of improved skin texture with sustained oral or topical use — firmer, more elastic appearance. This has driven substantial cosmeceutical industry use. The subjective reports align with the objective fibroblast collagen synthesis mechanism.

**Traditional longevity accounts:**  
Multiple Asian traditions record centenarians as habitual C. asiatica consumers. While not evidence, the consistency of these accounts across Sri Lanka, India, Thailand, and Indonesia is striking. The neurogenesis and BDNF mechanisms provide a plausible basis for a genuine contribution to cognitive aging — though not "immortality" in the traditional sense.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (circulatory/connective tissue) primary; ☿ Shen (neuromodulatory) secondary  
*Unique designation: "Structural Shen" — cognitive effects operate through structural tissue repair (BDNF/neurogenesis) rather than direct neurotransmitter modulation; venous connective tissue reconstruction = primary Qi-circulatory action*

**Canonical indications:**
1. Chronic venous insufficiency, varicose veins, leg edema (STRONGEST evidence)
2. Wound healing, burns, surgical scar prevention (topical; strong evidence)
3. Keloid and hypertrophic scar prevention (anti-fibrotic at higher doses)
4. Cognitive support, memory, attentional function (moderate evidence; faster onset than Bacopa)
5. Anxiety without sedation (GABAergic modulation; moderate evidence)
6. Skin aging / cosmetic skin quality (cosmeceutical; collagen synthesis)
7. Radiation skin damage (topical; emerging evidence)

**Preparation protocols:**

*For venous insufficiency (therapeutic):*  
TTFCA standardized extract (40% asiaticoside/asiatic acid/madecassoside/madecassic acid), 60mg 2× daily (120mg/day) with meals. Minimum 8–12 weeks. Monitor for edema reduction, symptom scores.

*For wound healing (topical):*  
TTFCA cream 0.1–0.5%; apply to clean wound 2–3× daily after initial inflammatory phase (days 3–5 post-injury). Can apply to burns during re-epithelialization. For keloid prevention: apply from wound closure through 6 months.

*For cognitive/anxiety support:*  
Standardized extract 40–60% triterpenoids, 500–750mg/day with food. Or: 3–5g dried herb daily. Effect onset 2–6 weeks. Compatible with Bacopa (I-200) but monitor for cumulative AChE and GABA effects.

*As food (preventive/longevity):*  
Fresh herb in salads, juices, cooked vegetable dishes (Sri Lankan tradition). 30–50g fresh leaves provides meaningful triterpene dose. Gotu kola juice (fresh pressed): 30–60ml daily. Easiest sustainable long-term protocol.

**Compatibility and combination logic:**

*Synergistic combinations:*
- **Bacopa + C. asiatica (complementary cognitive):** Bacopa handles AChE/synaptic chemistry; C. asiatica handles structural/BDNF/anxiety. Different mechanisms, genuinely complementary. Monitor cumulative AChE (additive weak inhibition from C. asiatica + strong inhibition from Bacopa).
- **Horse chestnut + C. asiatica (venous CVI):** Complementary mechanisms — aescin (venotonic, anti-enzymatic) + TTFCA (collagen restructuring). Used together in some European CVI protocols.
- **Pycnogenol + C. asiatica:** Both clinically validated for CVI; pycnogenol strong OPC antioxidant capillary protection; TTFCA structural collagen. Rational combination.
- **Ashwagandha + C. asiatica:** Both medhya rasayana in Ayurvedic context; Ashwagandha handles HPA-axis/stress; C. asiatica handles BDNF/structural. Classical Rasayana pairing.
- **Vitamin C + C. asiatica (wound healing):** Vitamin C is co-factor for collagen cross-linking; C. asiatica stimulates collagen synthesis; complementary at step-wise level.

*Cautions and interactions:*
- **Sedatives, anxiolytics, benzodiazepines:** Additive CNS depression possible (GABA-A modulation); use caution with concurrent pharmaceutical anxiolytics/sedatives; not absolute contraindication but requires monitoring
- **AChE inhibitor pharmaceuticals:** Mild additive AChE inhibition; less concern than with Bacopa but monitor in patients on donepezil/rivastigmine
- **Hepatotoxicity history:** Early reports of hepatotoxicity with C. asiatica (notably Humber et al. 1999, O'Sullivan et al. 2011) — case series of cholestatic liver injury with high-dose, prolonged use. Mechanism unclear (possible pyrrolizidine-like effect or impurity). LIVER FUNCTION MONITORING recommended with sustained high-dose oral use. Standard doses for CVI (120mg/day TTFCA) appear safe based on clinical trial safety data.
- **Pregnancy:** LIMITED DATA — avoid high-dose supplemental use during pregnancy (uterine effects possible at high doses); food-level consumption traditional and likely safe; clinical judgment required
- **Diabetes medications:** Some preclinical hypoglycemic activity; monitor blood glucose in diabetic patients
- **Thyroid:** No documented thyroid interaction (unlike Bacopa T4 reduction); appears thyroid-safe

**SAFETY CONCERN — HEPATOTOXICITY:**  
Multiple published case reports of cholestatic hepatitis with C. asiatica use. Most involved high doses or prolonged use. Mechanism unknown but dose-dependent. Key recommendations:
- Standard doses (≤120mg/day TTFCA for ≤8 weeks continuous): risk appears low based on clinical trial safety profiles
- High-dose, prolonged use (>12 weeks): consider LFT monitoring at baseline and 6–8 weeks
- Any symptoms of jaundice, fatigue, right upper quadrant pain: DISCONTINUE immediately
- History of liver disease: use with caution; reduced doses; monitoring
This is a more significant safety concern for C. asiatica than is commonly appreciated in mainstream herbal literature.

**Dosage summary:**

| Use | Dose | Notes |
|-----|------|-------|
| CVI (standardized) | 60–120 mg TTFCA/day | Divided doses; with food |
| Wound healing topical | 0.1–1% TTFCA cream | 2–3× daily |
| Cognitive/anxiety | 500–750 mg standardized extract/day | With food; 4–8 weeks |
| Whole herb (therapeutic) | 3–6g dried herb/day | Infusion or powder |
| Food (preventive) | 30–50g fresh leaves daily | Sri Lankan food tradition |

**Quality and sourcing considerations:**
- Titrated extract (TTFCA) is the most validated form — look for ≥40% total triterpenoids
- Geographic source matters (Sri Lanka and India typically higher quality than mass-produced)
- Fresh herb most bioavailable but rapidly perishable
- Madecassol (France) and ECa 233 (Thailand) are pharmaceutical-grade references
- Verify no adulteration with Bacopa (different species but sold interchangeably)

**The Brahmi disambiguation in clinical practice:**  
When a patient presents using "Brahmi," always determine:
1. Which plant? — ask for Latin name, or have them bring the product
2. What are they treating? — cognitive = often Bacopa; wound/venous = likely C. asiatica
3. Drug interactions? — Bacopa has stronger AChE-inhibitor concerns; C. asiatica has hepatotoxicity and sedative concerns
Never assume one when the other may be in use.
