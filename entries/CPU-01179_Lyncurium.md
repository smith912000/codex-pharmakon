# Lyncurium (lynx urine) — Lungourion, as printed in the English edition of Dioscorides
## *A "stone" said by report to form from the urine of a lynx; Dioscorides records the report and calls it foolish* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek Tradition (DMM).** The chapter is Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica*, Book 2, chapter 100, headed "2-100. LUNGOURION" with the title "Lyncurium — Urine of a Lynx" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, line 5384; row in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 290). The whole chapter reads: "Lyncurium [urine of a lynx] is thought (as soon as it is pissed out) to grow into a stone, as a result it has only a foolish report. Some call this succinum pterygophoron [the wing of accompaniment] because it draws feathers to it. Taken as a drink with water it is good for a stomach and intestines troubled with excessive discharge." (line 5386).

Four points can be drawn from those lines, and only from them.

1. The material is said to form when lynx urine hardens into a stone "as soon as it is pissed out". The wording "is thought" marks this as hearsay.
2. Dioscorides' own comment, as translated, is that the material "has only a foolish report". The translator's wording is that the reputation is foolish; the text does not say whether he thinks the use is foolish too. He still records a use, so the chapter records a thing he doubts. This is the Dioscoridean pattern of recording a claim while keeping his distance from it, which the Codex's register notes already describe for amuletic uses.
3. A second name is given, *succinum pterygophoron*, glossed by the editor as "the wing of accompaniment", because the stone "draws feathers to it". That is the property of attracting light objects that is nowadays associated with electrostatically charged amber. The text does not say "amber" in this chapter, and I draw no identification from it. [USER: verify]
4. One use is given: taken in a drink with water, it is "good for a stomach and intestines troubled with excessive discharge".

The text on disk offers no identification of the stone and no origin, colour or place. The editor's header gives only the translation of the name.

The chapter before it, "2-99. PERI OURON" (on urines, line 5377), lists among others the urines of bulls, boars, goats and asses (lines 5382-5383). Lyncurium is not among them and is placed in its own chapter.

### Active Components

If lyncurium was a real stone, its composition is unknown from the sources. Fresh mammal urine is mostly water with urea and salts, and that is the only general statement that can be made about a lynx's urine. Nothing is asserted about the stone.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, and with the text's own doubt attached. There is no reliable identification of the material, so there is nothing to use, and the Codex gives no protocol. Anyone selling "lyncurium" today is selling something whose identity cannot be checked against this chapter. Lynx are protected in many countries (see Contraindications).

### Preparation & Dosing

"Taken as a drink with water" (line 5386). No quantity, frequency or duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] General fact: urine does not harden into a gem-like stone as the report claims, and attracting feathers is a property of electrostatic charge, not of any medicine. The chapter's own wording ("a foolish report") agrees on the first point.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Greek: no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality is printed for lyncurium. Nothing is given beyond its use against excessive discharge. No other tradition is tagged.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter is a case of recording without endorsing: the report is set down, called foolish, and the one use is listed. For a cultivator, this is a lesson in attention: the text separates what is said of a material from what is known of it. No cultivation status is attested.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. The material cannot be identified from the text, and the text itself distrusts it.

### Contraindications

The identity of any product sold under this name cannot be verified, so its safety is unknown. Lynx are protected or endangered in many places, and collecting their urine or parts can be illegal. "Excessive discharge" from stomach or intestines (persistent diarrhoea) risks dehydration and may signal infection; see a clinician. This entry gives no medical advice.
