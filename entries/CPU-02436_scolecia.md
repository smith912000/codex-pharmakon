# Scolecia (worm verdigris) — Ios Skolex (as transliterated in the heading); "Scolecia — Rust", Dioscorides 5-92

## *A rust of copper or brass, called scolecia: one kind mineral (dug up) and one kind made by beating vinegar, alum, salt and urine in a brass mortar and rolling the paste into little "worms"; identified by us as a copper corrosion product of the verdigris kind, from the tool material (Cyprian brass), the neighbouring chapter on verdigris and the text's comparison to burnt brass, with medium confidence; the translator prints only "Rust"* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** The translator heads this chapter "RISK OF BLOOD POISONING". Copper corrosion products are toxic. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should make, swallow or apply this material.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-92, *Ios Skolex* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10280-10288; the Codex's name-index row in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` gives locus "5-92" and class mineral, with the identification flagged as uncertain; list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 818; the book index sends the reader to page 789). The whole chapter was read. Sushruta, Shennong, Ebers and the other texts on disk were not searched for a counterpart, and none is claimed.

The chapter opens: "There are two types of rust called scolecia; for the one is a mineral and the other is made as follows" (line 10283). The made kind is then described: a quarter pint of white sharp vinegar is poured into a mortar "made from Cyprian brass" and beaten with a pestle "of the same material" until viscous; one teaspoon each of ground alum (the translator's cross-reference is 5-123) and of "transparent mineral salt or sea salt (as white and strong as possible)", or saltpetre if neither is to hand, is added; the mixture is beaten "in the sun in the extreme heat of the hottest days until the mixture becomes rusty in colour but gluey in consistency", then made "into little worms like rhodianda [? stamens of roses]" and put in jars. A second recipe is given: with "one part of vinegar and two parts of old urine" it is made "effectively and with a good colour".

Line 10284 adds a fraud and a trade note. "Some mix that which is useless with aerugo rasilis [5-91], form it into a shape with gum and sell it. This is to be refused as worthless." And: "A type of rust is also a made by the goldsmiths with a Cyprian brass mortar and pestle and a child's urine, with which they solder gold." The text then ranks the kinds: "the dug-up or mined scolecia [rust] is the best, next to that is the scraped or smooth, and then the manufactured. This last is more biting and astringent but that from the goldsmiths is similar to the scraped or smooth." The rusts are said to be "commonly similar to burnt brass. They are stronger in effect."

The uses (line 10284): "All rust is suitable as an astringent, to warm, to wear off and relieve scars in the eyes, to draw tears, stop gangrenous ulcers, keep wounds from inflammation, and form a skin over ulcers with oil and wax. Boiled with honey they purge callos [pus under a hard skin] and foul ulcers. Taken with amoniacum [3-98] the same way as eye salves or suppositories, they remove fistulas and their callos. They are good for abnormal growths, and protuberances of the gums; rubbed on with honey they greatly lessen [swollen] eyelids. After rubbing you must bathe with a sponge and warm water. Taken with terminthos [1-91] rosin, brass or saltpetre [potassium nitrate] they take away leprosy."

**Identity.** The translator gives "Scolecia — Rust" and no mineral name. The Greek *ios* means rust or verdigris and *skolex* a worm (our reading of the Greek heading, not stated in the translation) [USER: verify], which fits the worm-shaped rolls the text describes. The tools are brass, the reagents are vinegar, salt and urine, and the result is "similar to burnt brass". These clues point to a green or rust-coloured copper corrosion product of the verdigris kind; the preceding chapter 5-91 (*Ios Xustos*, translator: "Verdigris — Brass Oxide", line 10270) is made in a similar way, and the text itself separates the two when it warns against mixing useless material with *aerugo rasilis*. The same author's iron rust is a different chapter (5-93, *Ios Siderou*, line 10289) and is not this entry. The identification is ours and of medium confidence. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None is stated in the chapter. In general terms, the corrosion of copper or brass under vinegar (acetic acid), salt and urine gives copper acetates, chlorides and carbonates in various mixtures, which are known as verdigris; they are toxic copper salts. The composition of the text's "dug-up" kind is not given and no composition is asserted for it. No analytical source was retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Toxic.**

- **What the text says it is for.** An astringent, warming rust for eye scars, tears, gangrenous and foul ulcers, fistulas, growths and gum protuberances, swollen eyelids and, taken with the substances named, leprosy.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose and no duration; the made kind's quantities are those of the recipe, not of a dose.

The Codex does not translate the text's conditions into modern diagnoses and gives no protocol. Copper salts are caustic and poisonous, the eye and open sores are the worst places to put an unknown copper preparation, and the text's own translator flags blood poisoning.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives two ways of making the rust (vinegar, alum and salt or saltpetre in a brass mortar beaten in the sun; or vinegar and old urine), the goldsmiths' variant (a child's urine in a brass mortar), and a way of burning it (line 10288): "Break it into very small pieces, put it in a ceramic platter, set it over burning coals and stir it around until it changes and looks a somewhat ashy colour, then cool it, put it in jars and use it. Some burn it in an unfired clay jar, as previously described, but burnt like this it will not change altogether into the same colour." Uses are given with honey, with oil and wax, with ammoniacum as an eye salve or suppository, and with terebinth resin, brass or saltpetre. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any quantity from this entry.** [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM).** The chapter prints qualities for the rusts as a group: "astringent" and "to warm" (line 10284). It also ranks the manufactured kind as "more biting and astringent" than the mined and scraped kinds. No degree is given and no moistening or drying quality is printed. No Chinese, Indian or Tibetan classification is carried over.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. What the herbal offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: provenance, assay and adulteration detection. This chapter shows that discipline plainly: it ranks mined above scraped above manufactured, and it names a fraud (useless material mixed with verdigris, shaped with gum and sold) to be refused. These are the marks of careful attention to material, not a cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to take from it for cultivation beyond the author's insistence on checking what you are sold. The material is a toxic copper compound, and the chapter's own translator warns of blood poisoning. It is not a recommended practice. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **TOXIC.** Copper corrosion products are poisonous if swallowed and corrosive to the eye and to open wounds. Do not make, swallow or apply them.
- **Blood poisoning.** The translator's own warning at the head of the chapter.
- **Eyes, fistulas, ulcers, leprosy, gangrene.** These are medical conditions that need medical care.
- **Identity.** The mineral kind is unidentified in the sources read; the made kind is our identification by analogy to verdigris. [USER: verify]
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** No statement in the sources retrieved. Given the toxicity, avoid. [USER: verify]
