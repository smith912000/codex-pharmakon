# Granite powder — "Granite", "Powdered Granite" (Bryan's English rendering of the Ebers Papyrus mineral list)
## *Granite, listed by Bryan among the few mineral drugs "prescribed in their crude or native state"; powdered granite is named once in the running text; the papyrus's own word and the rock actually meant are not given in the sources retrieved* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian Tradition (EP).** The locus is Bryan's 1930 English *The Papyrus Ebers*, Chapter V "Mineral Remedies", in the alphabetical list of mineral drugs (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1168: "Granite"; name-index row `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` line 13, class mineral, "Bryan's English identification"). The list gives no use, no prescription number and no identification beyond the English word. The spine row reads "Granite powder, Silicate rock dust" and cites Wikipedia; that phrase is the list-maker's gloss, and the Wikipedia page was not used. Bryan's opening of the chapter (lines 1133-1137) says that few mineral drugs "are prescribed in their crude or native state": "Antimony, Calamine, Granite, Ink, Indigo, and Sulphur almost exhaust the list." Granite is therefore one of six minerals Bryan names as prescribed in their native state, as opposed to the many whose source of origin is "strictly laid down" (for example "Iron-from-Apollonopolis-parva").

**Where Granite is prescribed in Bryan's running text.** The Bryan text on disk was searched for "granite". It occurs seven times, which are the list entry, Bryan's remark at line 1137, and five prescriptions.

- Line 1362, in Bryan's chapter on organic remedies, as one of his examples of odd compounds: "Man's Milk (reinforced it is true by Tortoise-shell, Granite, and Trash-from-the-Knife-Stone) cures the uases-abscess".
- Line 2355, in the chapter section headed "Minor Surgery" (heading line 2209): "One that cures the abscess by 'making the blood fall' is composed of Man's Milk, Granite, Tortoise-shell, and Trash-from-the-Knifestone." Bryan adds that the scribe did not describe the *uases* abscess, so the complaint cannot be identified.
- Line 2391, in "Minor Surgery", after a plaster for wheals from blows: "ANOTHER Dust-of-Alabaster Dust-of-a-Statue Granite Fresh Milk. Anoint therewith."
- Line 2804, in "Diseases of the Eyes" (heading line 2721), for leucoma: "Next they dressed the eyes with a poultice of Powdered Granite." Bryan's sentence follows a lotion of cream and milk, one of collyrium, ink and water, and a powder of ebony wood and collyrium; it is followed by a paste of collyrium and fish bile, and then by an incantation and the brain of a tortoise (see the Cultivation Register).
- Line 2995, also in "Diseases of the Eyes", for squint, after a paste of collyrium, red-lead and soda: "a poultice of Onions, Granite, and Resin, was tried".

**What is not said.** The sources retrieved do not say which rock the scribe meant by the word Bryan renders "Granite", where it was obtained, or how it was reduced to powder. In geology granite is a coarse igneous rock; whether the Egyptian word meant that rock, a crystalline stone in general, or a particular quarry stone cannot be settled from these lines. The Ebers material is indication-organised, so the identity of any named substance is a modern hypothesis. The Ghalioungui 1987 glossary row cited in the list (`_sources/ebers.csv` line 121) was not opened. [USER: verify]

**Related items not used.** "Dust-of-a-Statue" and "Scrapings-from-a-Statue" (line 1204) are separate listed items; the dust-of-a-statue prescription at line 2391 names Granite as a separate ingredient. "Black Knife-stone" and "Stone-from-the-Shore" are separate stones.

### Active Components

None can be stated from the papyrus. In general terms, granite is a rock composed mainly of quartz, feldspars and micas, with small amounts of other minerals; powdered granite is therefore an insoluble silicate dust. Fine dust containing quartz is a respiratory hazard if inhaled. This is a general statement about the rock, not a reading of Bryan.

---

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved show powdered granite in poultices for the eye (leucoma, squint) and in applications for an abscess and for a surface injury, always mixed with other drugs, and without any quantity. Putting stone powder in or near the eye is hazardous. No modern protocol is supplied and none is implied.

---

### Preparation & Dosing

Only what Bryan reports. Granite is mixed with human milk, tortoise-shell and knife-stone trash (lines 1362, 2355); with alabaster dust, statue dust and fresh milk and "anointed" (line 2391); applied as "Powdered Granite" in a poultice (line 2804); or poulticed with onions and resin (line 2995). No quantity is printed in Bryan's summary for any of these. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's vital constitution. The nearest speech to a granite passage is the incantation printed at lines 2806-2815 for "the white growth in the eyes" ("There is a Shouting in the Southern Sky in the Darkness [...]"), which Bryan says is "a formula to repeat over the Brain-of-a-Tortoise that is mixed in Honey": it belongs to the tortoise-brain remedy and not to the Powdered Granite poultice that precedes it, and it is not transferred to the stone. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*), but Bryan prints no recitation with any of the five granite prescriptions, and since he summarises, that is no proof the papyrus gives none. The person as plural (*ka*, *ba*, *akh*, *ib*) is not engaged by this entry. No deity is attributed.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. The record is a stone powder added to eye and wound applications, with no quantity and no identification of the rock. A cultivator has nothing to do with it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Eye:** powdered rock is abrasive; it must not be put in the eye.
- **Inhalation:** granite dust contains crystalline silica and is a respiratory hazard.
- **Unspecified stone:** the rock is not identified beyond Bryan's English word, and stone dust of unknown origin can contain heavy metals.
- **Compounds with human milk and animal parts:** the cited mixtures use human milk, tortoise-shell and animal matter; they carry infection risk and are not recommended.
