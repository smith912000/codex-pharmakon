# Sea Bindweed

## *Calystegia soldanella* (L.) R.Br. | Family: Convolvulaceae

**Also known as:** Soldanella, Sea Bells, Seashore Bindweed, Beach Morning Glory, Sea Rocket (erroneously), Scottish Bindweed, 갯메꽃 (*gaenmaekkot*, Korean), ハマヒルガオ (*hamahirugao*, Japanese)

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Cold, Dry, Downward-Moving, Draining, Salt-Tempered (per the classical drastic-purgative reading — see *Traditional Energetic Classification*)

**Training Phase:** Not a Kenetic training substance. Filed as a **doctrinal-lineage and research-thread entry** — retained for the Convolvulaceae purgative complex and for a genuinely substantial modern phytochemical literature, not for practitioner use.

### Origin & Tradition

*Calystegia soldanella* is the bindweed that left the hedgerow and went to the beach. It is a prostrate, mat-forming halophyte of coastal sand dunes and shingle, circumglobal in temperate zones — Europe, East Asia, Australasia, the Americas. Where its congener *C. sepium* (I_785) climbs and strangles, *C. soldanella* creeps flat across bare sand, throwing up thick, fleshy, glossy, **kidney-shaped** leaves (the epithet *soldanella* is from Italian *soldo*, a small coin — the leaves are coin-round) and solitary pink funnel flowers. It runs on long white rhizomes buried deep in the dune, and its seeds are salt-water buoyant and long-lived, which is how a beach plant became a world plant.

Its ecology is well characterised and matters for the cultivator. It is a **clonal integrator**: connected ramets share resources across patchily lit and fertilised ground, achieving greater total biomass in heterogeneous habitat than severed ramets, with a demonstrated division of labour in growth allocation but *not* in clonal propagation (Li et al., 2024, *AoB PLANTS*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/aobpla/plae028)). It is a true halophyte, but its establishment is more sensitive to **nitrogen than to salt** — nitrogen supply significantly delayed germination and reduced cumulative germination percentage in coastal halophyte field trials (Chen et al., 2022, *PeerJ*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.14164)). It carries a distinctive endophytic community, including a gibberellin-producing *Cadophora malorum* strain isolated from its roots (You et al., 2012, *J Basic Microbiol*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/jobm.201200002)) and a bacterium novel enough to be named for the genus — *Jejubacter calystegiae*, a moderately halophilic enterobacterium from surface-sterilised stem tissue of Jeju Island plants (Jiang et al., 2020, *J Microbiol*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12275-020-9294-1)).

**A statement on the evidence, up front.** This entry is **data-sparse on the ethnomedical side and data-rich on the chemical side** — an unusual and instructive combination, and the reverse of what the practitioner might expect. Sea Bindweed has a genuine, named place in the old European pharmacopoeias as *soldanella*, which is more than *C. sepium* ever achieved. But the *content* of that place is thin and formulaic, while the modern phytochemistry — driven almost entirely by Japanese and Korean groups working on halophyte resources — is detailed, sustained and species-specific. The Codex reports both honestly.

**European Domestic Materia Medica (DMM) — "Soldanella", "Brassica marina", "Sea Colewort"**

Here the attestation is real. **Soldanella** was a named drug of the old European pharmacopoeias — not a folk gloss but an officinal entry, gathered from coastal sand and traded regionally. It was classed among the **drastic hydragogue purgatives**, and its indication was almost entirely singular: **dropsy** (oedema/ascites). The doctrinal logic was direct — a plant that draws water violently from the bowel was the instrument for a disease understood as water improperly retained in the body. Secondary indications reported in the same frame include costiveness and "obstruction". [USER: verify] the specific pharmacopoeial citations, degree-of-quality assignment, and any secondary indications against a primary source (Dioscorides via the Renaissance commentators, Matthiolus, Gerard, or a regional coastal folk-botany survey) rather than a modern compilation.

Two points of note the Codex will stand behind. First, the plant's classification among the *drastics* rather than the *laxatives* is doctrinally significant: it was understood as dangerous, and used when the disease was thought more dangerous still. Second, the name **soldanella** is a persistent source of confusion — it is also the genus name of the Alpine snowbells (*Soldanella*, Primulaceae), which are entirely unrelated plants. Historical references to "soldanella" must be checked against habitat: **sand and salt = Convolvulaceae; alpine snowmelt = Primulaceae.** This is not a pedantic distinction; it is a live misidentification risk in the older literature.

**Chinese Medicine (CM) / East Asian record** *(⚠️ code-gloss correction 2026-08-10: in this project **CM denotes Avicenna / Unani**, not "Chinese Medicine" — the label below is legacy drift from an old scheme in which CM meant "Chinese Materia Medica". The Chinese content here is genuine; whether the **CM tag itself** belongs on this entry is under audit and has NOT been ruled.)*

The East Asian attestation is the weaker half of this entry's tag set, and the Codex flags it. *C. soldanella* is present and studied throughout the East Asian coastal record — the modern research literature is overwhelmingly Korean and Japanese, and the plant is a recognised coastal food and folk resource (Korean 갯메꽃, Japanese ハマヒルガオ). Its congener *Calystegia hederacea* has a **documented** medicinal use across India and East Asia, where all parts are used for menoxenia, gonorrhoea and related indications (Ono et al., 2019, *J Nat Med*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11418-019-01366-9)) — but that is **a different species**, and the Codex will not transfer its indications here.

Tellingly, the Korean group that did the most substantial pharmacological work on this plant described it as having had "only a limited use thus far as a medicinal plant" (Eom et al., 2021, *Antioxidants*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox10101639)) — a direct statement, from researchers with every incentive to claim tradition, that the ethnomedical base is thin. **The CM tag is therefore carried as [USER: verify]** and may warrant downgrade to a general East Asian ethnobotanical note. Do not let a *C. hederacea* indication, or a genus-level name, license a species-level claim.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

The signature reading here is one of the cleaner ones in the family, and — as with *C. sepium* — it maps onto real chemistry while being blind to the chemistry that actually turned out to matter.

*The signature of the salt shore.* Sea Bindweed grows where nothing should: in bare, dry, sterile, salt-blasted sand, hoarding water in thick succulent coin-leaves. To the humoral eye this is a plant that has mastered water in a place of drought and salt — and by the logic that a plant's virtue lies in what it has conquered, a plant that *governs water in the body*. Salt draws water; the plant lives in salt; therefore the plant draws water. Add the family's known purgative identity and the conclusion writes itself: **soldanella is the drug for dropsy.** The kidney-shaped leaf sharpens it further — dropsy was a disease of retained water, kidneys were the organ of water, and the leaf named the organ. The doctrine is elegant. It is also, in its therapeutic conclusion, wrong.

*Where the doctrine is right.* The purgative claim has a genuine chemical referent, and in this species it is unusually well documented. *C. soldanella* is a confirmed producer of **resin glycosides (jalapins)** — the macrolactone class that constitutes the actual purgative principle of Scammony, Jalap and Orizaba root. The species has yielded at least eighteen characterised **calysolins** (I–XVIII) plus soldanelline B, isolated from leaves, stems and roots (Ono et al., 2014, *Chem Pharm Bull*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1248/cpb.c13-00610); Ono et al., 2015, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1248/cpb.c15-00307); Ono et al., 2017, *Nat Prod Res*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2017.1280492)). The Convolvulaceae resin glycosides are explicitly and repeatedly described in the modern chemical literature as "the purgative ingredients" characteristic of the family (Ono, 2017, *J Nat Med*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11418-017-1114-5)). So the tradition, reasoning from taste, habit and family resemblance, located a real compound class in a real plant. Credit where due.

*Where the doctrine must be deflated.* Three deflations.

First: **the mechanism is the harm.** Dropsy is not a disease of the bowel, and evacuating the bowel violently does not treat it. Oedema and ascites are consequences of cardiac, hepatic or renal failure. Inducing catastrophic enteric fluid loss in a patient in heart or liver failure does not remove the cause; it removes circulating volume and potassium from someone who can least afford to lose either. The visible "success" — the patient passes water, the swelling appears to subside — was the doctrine confirming itself on an endpoint that measured the drug's action rather than the patient's recovery. This is the central lesson of the drastic-purgative class and the reason the Codex holds these entries at all.

Second: **there is no dose behind the name.** That *soldanella* was officinal means it was named, not that it was standardised. Wild dune-gathered rhizome varies by season, population, salinity and part. A drug class with a narrow margin and an unquantified content is not made safe by having an entry in a pharmacopoeia.

Third, and most striking: **the doctrine is silent on everything the modern work found.** The species-specific pharmacology that has actually emerged is antiviral (herpes simplex), phosphatase-inhibitory (PTP1B), antioxidative and anti-inflammatory (Nrf-2/NF-κB). Not one of these was available to a signature reading. No amount of contemplating the kidney-shaped leaf yields HSV-1 inhibition at 2.3 μM. The tradition asked "what does this plant's form say it governs?" and got dropsy. The chemistry asked "what does this plant make?" and got an antiviral macrolactone library. The two questions do not converge, and the Codex declines to pretend that they do. As with *C. sepium*, the honest verdict is: **the doctrine tracked one real compound class into a therapeutic dead end, and was wholly blind to the live science.**

### Active Components

**Resin glycosides (jalapins) — species-specific, extensively characterised.** This is the best-documented resin glycoside library of any *Calystegia*.
- **Calysolins I–XVIII** and **soldanelline B**, isolated from leaves, stems and roots. Structures determined by spectroscopic and chemical evidence.
- Two structural sub-types, and the distinction is pharmacologically load-bearing: **macrolactone** types (22-membered and 27-membered rings) and **non-macrolactone** types. Sugar moieties partially acylated by **tiglic, 2*S*-methylbutyric, and 2*S*,3*S*-nilic acids**. Calysolins IV and V were the first representatives with calysolic acid C as the component glycosidic acid (Ono et al., 2014, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1248/cpb.c13-00610)).
- **Calysolin XVIII** — 11*S*-jalapinolic acid glycoside; the first known resin glycoside to feature **3-hydroxy-2-methylenebutyric acid** as a component organic acid (Ono et al., 2017, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2017.1280492)).
- Family context: Convolvulaceae resin glycosides divide into **jalapin** (ether-soluble) and **convolvulin** (ether-insoluble) classes; almost all characterised jalapins share intramolecular macrocyclic ester structures (Ono, 2017, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11418-017-1114-5)). **Congener/family-level framing — not a species-specific claim.**

**Alkyl *p*-coumarates.**
- Nine known alkyl *p*-coumarates isolated from the n-hexane fraction — the fraction carrying the strongest PTP1B inhibitory activity (Lee et al., 2014, *Nat Prod Commun*, PMID 25532287).

**Polyphenols — flavonoids and phenolic acids.**
- Characterised by UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS in the ethyl acetate fraction. Principal flavonoids: the quercetin glycosides **isoquercitrin** and **quercitrin**. Principal phenolic acids: the caffeic acid derivatives **chlorogenic acid** and **dicaffeoylquinic acid** (Eom et al., 2021, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox10101639)).

**Calystegines — polyhydroxy nortropane alkaloids.**
- ⚠️ **Not confirmed for this species this run.** Calystegines are named for the genus and are documented across Convolvulaceae, and it is *reasonable* to expect *C. soldanella* to produce them — but no quantified calystegine profile for *C. soldanella* was retrieved. The biosynthetic and inhibitory work sits on *C. sepium* root cultures, potato, and *Ipomoea carnea* (see I_785). **This is inference, not data. Marked [USER: verify] and not asserted.**

**Associated organisms (not plant chemistry — recorded to prevent confusion).**
- Endophytic *Cadophora malorum* Cs-8-1 from roots, a confirmed gibberellin producer (You et al., 2012, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/jobm.201200002)). *Jejubacter calystegiae* gen. nov., sp. nov. from stem tissue (Jiang et al., 2020, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12275-020-9294-1)). Neither is a constituent of the drug; both are reasons to be careful about what a crude extract of a wild dune plant actually contains.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Kenetica has no use case for this plant. It is not a training substance at any phase.**

The reasoning is identical to that given for Hedge Bindweed and bears repeating rather than cross-referencing, because the temptation is the same. Kenetic practice concerns trained modulation of attention, arousal and interoceptive discrimination. A drastic hydragogue purgative contributes nothing. Violent enterically-driven fluid loss is not a "clearing", not a "purification", not a "downward reset" — it is dehydration with a cosmological gloss. The purgative rites of the humoral tradition were coherent *inside* a theory of retained morbid humours; Kenetica does not hold that theory, and will not re-import it by renaming its central operation.

There is an additional and specific temptation here that must be named: this plant is *beautiful*, *coastal*, *edible-adjacent*, and now carries a real halophyte-nutraceutical research literature with attractive words in it — antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, Nrf-2. That combination invites exactly the error the Codex exists to prevent: reading a mouse-macrophage NF-κB result as a licence to make tea from a plant whose principal characterised constituents are purgative macrolactones. **The polyphenol data and the resin glycoside data describe the same plant.** You do not get the first without the second.

**What the entry is for:**

1. **Doctrinal completeness — the instructive contrast with I_785.** Read Sea Bindweed against Hedge Bindweed and the family pattern resolves. Two congeners, same genus, same purgative doctrine: one (*C. sepium*) never earned a drug-name and carries only back-formed indications; the other (*C. soldanella*) earned an officinal name, *soldanella*, and a specific indication, dropsy. The difference is not in the chemistry — both make resin glycosides. It is in **accessibility and habitat**: a mat on an open beach is gatherable in quantity by anyone with a basket, while a rhizome tangled through a hedge is not. The pharmacopoeia recorded what could be harvested. This is a lesson about how materia medica actually forms, and it is not a lesson about efficacy.

2. **A genuine research thread.** The antiviral resin glycoside work is real, sustained, and has a real structure-activity finding attached (see below). That thread runs toward synthetic macrolactone chemistry, not toward the kitchen.

**For the cultivator.** Unlike its congener, this one is worth growing — and the guidance is genuinely different. *C. soldanella* is **not** an aggressive invader of garden soil; it is a specialist of open, sharply drained, low-nutrient, saline sand, and it is in decline across much of its European range through coastal development, dune stabilisation and trampling. Land-use change and tourism are explicitly named as threats to its habitat (Li et al., 2024). Two research-grounded points for the grower:

- **Do not feed it.** Nitrogen is the constraint that matters, not salt. Nitrogen supply significantly delayed germination and reduced cumulative germination percentage, and had an even larger negative effect on seedling growth and individual biomass than on germination (Chen et al., 2022, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.14164)). Fertile compost is the wrong medium. Sharp sand, minimal organic matter, full sun, free drainage.
- **Propagate clonally and give it room to run.** The plant achieves its biomass through **clonal integration** across heterogeneous ground — connected ramets outperform severed ones, and connected ramets in heterogeneous habitat outperform those in uniform conditions (Li et al., 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/aobpla/plae028)). A rooted rhizome section in a long, shallow, sandy run will establish where a severed fragment in a pot will sulk. Do not sever the connections. Do not confine it to uniformity.
- **Collect ethically, or not at all.** Where it is native and declining, take rhizome sections or seed from abundant stands only, or source from cultivation. The dune is not a dispensary.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

### Contraindications

⚠️ **The Codex does not recommend internal use of *Calystegia soldanella* under any circumstance, at any dose, for any indication.**

- **Drastic purgation — the defining hazard.** *C. soldanella* is a confirmed producer of a large library of jalapin-class resin glycosides (calysolins I–XVIII, soldanelline B), the compound class explicitly identified as the purgative principle of the Convolvulaceae drugs (Ono, 2017). The drastic hydragogue class produces violent watery evacuation with cramping, colic and tenesmus, and at excess haemorrhagic enteritis. The historical drugs of this class killed people. There is no basis for assuming this one is gentler — only that it is less standardised, which is worse.
- **The resins are in the leaves and stems, not only the root.** Note this specifically, because it breaks the intuition that governs many purgative plants. The calysolins were isolated from **leaves, stems and roots** (Ono et al., 2014; 2015; 2017). There is no "safe aerial part". Any reported potherb use must be treated as unverified and unsafe until the preparation and quantity are established. [USER: verify]
- **No verified dose exists.** Content unquantified; wild dune material varies by part, season, population and salinity.
- **Dehydration and electrolyte loss.** The mechanism *is* fluid loss. Hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia, hypovolaemia. Compounding risk with diuretics, cardiac glycosides (hypokalaemia potentiates digitalis toxicity), corticosteroids, or any renal or cardiac compromise. **Note the vicious circularity of the historical indication: the dropsy patient is in cardiac, hepatic or renal failure — precisely the population in whom drastic purgation is most lethal. The drug was aimed at the people it could most easily kill.**
- **Pregnancy — absolute contraindication.** Drastic purgatives are reflex uterine stimulants and were historically used as abortifacients. Do not use. Do not use while breastfeeding.
- **Absolute contraindications:** intestinal obstruction or suspected obstruction, undiagnosed abdominal pain, appendicitis, inflammatory bowel disease, GI bleeding, dehydration, children, the elderly, the debilitated, anyone with renal, cardiac or hepatic disease.
- **Cytotoxicity.** Macrolactone-type resin glycosides from this genus show clear cytotoxicity toward human cell lines, and the macrolactone ring is the structural feature carrying that activity (Ono et al., 2019). The DCM fraction induces mitochondrial depolarisation and caspase-mediated apoptosis (Kim et al., 2021). Cytotoxic *in vitro* is not "anticancer"; it is cytotoxic.
- **Misidentification — two distinct risks.**
  1. **The *Soldanella* name collision.** Historical "soldanella" references may indicate the Alpine snowbells (*Soldanella*, Primulaceae) — unrelated plants. Check the habitat: sand and salt = this plant; alpine snowmelt = not this plant.
  2. **Within-genus and within-family confusion.** *Calystegia* species intergrade and hybridise; *C. soldanella*, *C. sepium*, *C. hederacea* and *Convolvulus* spp. are confused in casual identification, and the *C. hederacea* medicinal indications are frequently and wrongly transferred to this species.
- **Wild dune material carries an endophytic load.** Novel halophilic Enterobacteriaceae (*Jejubacter calystegiae*) and gibberellin-producing fungi (*Cadophora malorum*) have been isolated from surface-sterilised tissue of this plant (Jiang et al., 2020; You et al., 2012). Neither is characterised for human pathogenicity, and neither is the point — the point is that a crude extract of a wild dune plant is not a defined substance.
- **Conservation.** Where native and declining, wild harvest is a habitat harm before it is a health harm. Do not strip dune stands.
- **On the "antioxidant/anti-inflammatory halophyte" framing.** Nothing in the Nrf-2/NF-κB macrophage data (Eom et al., 2021) constitutes evidence that consuming this plant is safe or beneficial. That work used a solvent-partitioned polyphenol fraction in cultured mouse cells. It says nothing about a human ingesting whole plant material that also contains eighteen characterised purgative macrolactones. **Do not let the attractive fraction launder the plant.**
