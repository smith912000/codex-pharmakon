# Lampsana communis — Lampsane (Dioscorides 2-142)

## *Lampsana communis (Lapsana communis is the translators' variant spelling on the same line); "Common Nipplewort"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the SUGGESTED line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The line (line 5731) reads "Lampsana communis, Lapsana communis — Common Nipplewort".

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *2-142. LAMPSANE*, heading at line 5729, SUGGESTED line at 5731, text at line 5732. It stands in Book Two among the vegetables (*Lachana*). The translators' index gives *Lampsana communis* and *Lapsana communis* at page 264 (line 16248 and following).

The whole chapter reads: "Lampsana is a wild olus [one that is well known], more nourishing and more agreeable to the stomach than lapathum, whose stalks and leaves are eaten boiled [vegetable]. The Latins call it napium [wood nymph], and the Egyptians, euthmoe."

That is the entire text. Dioscorides gives no description of the plant, no habitat, no season and no medicinal use. He says only that it is a wild vegetable (the translators gloss *olus* as "one that is well known"), that it is more nourishing and kinder to the stomach than *lapathum*, and that its stalks and leaves are eaten boiled. He gives two foreign names: *napium* for the Latins and *euthmoe* for the Egyptians.

**The comparison plant.** The chapter measures *lampsane* against *lapathum*, the subject of *2-140. LAPATHON* (heading at line 5716). That chapter's SUGGESTED line gives *Rumex obtusifolius* and *Rumex acetosa* ("Water Sorrel, Water Dock", "Dock, Sorrel"), and says that the herbs of its kinds, "boiled", "soothe the intestines". It is a medicinal chapter as well as a vegetable chapter, whereas *2-142* gives nothing medicinal. The next chapter, 2-143 *BLITON* (heading at line 5733), states of its plant that it is "also eaten as a vegetable, having no use medicinally"; the form of that sentence makes the absence of a medicinal use explicit there, and 2-142 is silent on the point. That chapter is the subject of the existing entry I_1046 (*Chenopodium polyspermum*), which cites 2-142 only as the preceding chapter and not for its content.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a wild green boiled and eaten, rated more nourishing and more agreeable to the stomach than *lapathum*. He records no medicine. The Codex gives no protocol, and the identification is the translators'.
