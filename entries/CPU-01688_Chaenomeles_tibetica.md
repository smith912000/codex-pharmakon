# Chaenomeles tibetica — bse.yab, Seyab (rGyud-bzhi); "Tibetan quince"

## *Chaenomeles tibetica, the first of two binomials Clark 1995 prints for bse.yab (with Chaenomeles lagenaria, as printed); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text prints the name as "Seyab" and its glossary sets Chaenomeles speciosa beside it* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under this binomial only by Clark.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. Both Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the line prints two, of which the row takes the first (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 62). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 908).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20. The text file prints "(5) Resins" at line 879 and "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" at line 997 and no heading for a section (6) between them; this entry stands between the two, and the file does not show which section heading governs it. The entry reads:

"bse.yab: Chaenomeles tibetica: Chaenomeles lagenaria: [The hKhrungs.dpe text says, “It is a large tree with a large leaf and a white flower. Its pods contain kernels similar to those of the Bengal Kino tree. It has sour and slightly sweet taste and is of nectarlike benefit in ear diseases. The leaf, trunk and flower resemble the apple tree. One type has a fruit similar to an unripe walnut and does not change color in autumn.”] It cures phlegm and fever."

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". The whole description, including the quotation attributed to the *hKhrungs.dpe* text, is the commentary layer. The root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it, is the Tibetan name, the two Latin names (Clark's identifications and not the root text's) and the clause "It cures phlegm and fever". The line gives no part used and no preparation.

*Clark 1995, two other places.* Chapter 19, "Groups of Medicines Having the Same Taste" (heading at line 764), line 766: "The group of medicines having sour taste includes pomegranate, sea buckthorn (Hippophae rhamnoides), Chaenomeles tibetica, emblic myrobalan, Indian juniper, Schizandra sphaerandra, curd, whey and curd ‘culture’, grain beer, etc." Chapter 21, "Compounding of Medicines" (heading at line 1204), line 1215: "The category of medicines which cure phlegm-fever combinations includes Tibetan quince [bSe.yab, Chaenomeles lagenaria (Loise)], Iris germanica, Coriander, Sea Buckthorn, Blue Lily, Pomegranate, Ginger and Emblic Myrobalan." So the same drug is a sour-group member and a phlegm-fever drug, and Clark uses both of his binomials for it.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, the same Tibetan name.* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The text prints the name as "Seyab". It is in the sour group (lines 3205 to 3209); chapter 20 says "Seyab cures baekan and hot disorders" (line 3394); and the compounding groups of chapter 21 (line 3614) put it among the medicines that "cure hot disorder associated with baekan". The index (line 7487) lists "Seyab 186,202,221,267". The Codex reads "Seyab" as Clark's *bse.yab* from the spelling and the sour-group agreement; the Men-Tsee-Khang running text gives no Latin name. The glossary (Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, "S" block) prints "bse yab" among the Tibetan transliterations (line 5721) and, by column position, sets *Chaenomeles speciosa* and the English "Tibetan quince" against it (lines 5757 and 5760); it prints no *Chaenomeles tibetica*. The Tibetan texts therefore agree on the name and the sour taste and differ on the binomial. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Chaenomeles*, *bse.yab*, *Seyab* and "Tibetan quince" found no entry on this plant. I_1178 (*Rosa macrophylla*) mentions "Seyab" as a neighbour in the glossary block and is not an entry on this plant. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Chaenomeles* is a genus of the rose family (Rosaceae) with hard, sour, quince-like fruit that is generally known to be acidic. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained, because its Latin name is not settled. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Clark records a sour tree-fruit drug that "cures phlegm and fever" (line 908), and the commentary he quotes adds that it is "of nectarlike benefit in ear diseases". The Men-Tsee-Khang text records that Seyab "cures baekan and hot disorders" (line 3394). These are the texts' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation for this drug. The commentary describes a pod, kernels and a fruit but does not say which part is taken, and no quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Sour, by the group list (Clark line 766; Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3205 to 3209). The commentary Clark quotes says "sour and slightly sweet taste" (line 908). No power (hot or cool) and no post-digestive taste is printed for this drug in the lines read. The commentary is bracketed, and the group lists are not, so the "slightly sweet" qualification belongs to the commentary layer only.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes this drug by taste and by what it cures: phlegm and fever, and phlegm-fever combinations. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this drug places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the lines cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a sour fruit drug for phlegm and fever, named in three places in Clark and three in the Men-Tsee-Khang text, with a Latin name that is not settled. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. The identity is uncertain, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. A fever that does not settle, or an ear disease, needs medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
