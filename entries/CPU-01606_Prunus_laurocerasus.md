# Prunus laurocerasus — Chamaidaphne (Dioscorides 4-149)

## *Prunus laurocerasus, the first of four names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-149 ("Prunus laurocerasus [Pliny]", with Cerasus laurocerasus, Padus laurocerasus and Laurocerasus officinalis), glossed "Dwarf Laurel, Cherry Laurel"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 9343) prints four binomials, all of them names for the one laurel-cherry, and two English names. The row takes the first. It was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-149. CHAMAIDAPHNE*, heading at line 9342, SUGGESTED line at 9343, text at line 9344. It stands in Book Four, after *4-148. DAPHNOEIDES* and before *4-150. ELLEBOROS* (heading at line 9345). The translators' index gives *Cerasus laurocerasus* and *Laurocerasus officinalis* at page 696, and *Laureola* at page 695.

The whole chapter reads: "Chamaedaphne sends out single-branched rods a foot long — straight, thin and smooth; the leaves of this are similar to the [other] bay but much smoother, thinner and greener. The fruit is round and red, growing near to the leaves. The leaves of this (pounded into small pieces and smeared on) helps headaches and burning of the stomach. They cease griping, taken as a drink with wine. The juice (given to drink with wine) expels the menstrual flow and urine, and applied in a pessary it does the same. Some have called this alexandrina, daphnitis, or hydragogon, the Romans, laureola, some lactago, and the Gauls, ousubim."

Dioscorides gives a description built on the bay ("the [other] bay", with the leaves "much smoother, thinner and greener"), single-branched rods "a foot long" and a round red fruit near the leaves. The leaves, pounded and smeared, help "headaches and burning of the stomach", and taken as a drink with wine "cease griping". The juice, drunk with wine, "expels the menstrual flow and urine", and the chapter says that in a pessary "it does the same". The chapter prints six further names: *alexandrina*, *daphnitis* and *hydragogon* ("some have called"), the Roman *laureola*, *lactago* ("some") and the Gaulish *ousubim*. They are names only.

**Names shared with the preceding chapter.** The names *daphnitis*, *hydragogon*, *laureola*, *lactago* and *ousubim* are also printed at the end of *4-148. DAPHNOEIDES* (the end of its text at line 9341), the chapter treated in entry I_1064 (*Daphne mezereum*). That chapter's plant is described with a flowering shrub of its own, and its leaves "expel phlegm through the bowels" and "encourage vomiting", which are different uses from the ones here. The same names are printed for both chapters, and the Codex does not read the two chapters as one plant. The English "Dwarf Laurel" also stands on the SUGGESTED line of 4-148 (as printed in the I_1064 title line), so the English gloss is shared as well. This overlap is in names and glosses only.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter number, *Chamaidaphne*, *Chamaedaphne*, *laurocerasus* and *Cherry Laurel* found no existing entry on 4-149 itself. I_1064 (*Daphne mezereum*) is the neighbouring chapter with shared names. I_029 (Bay Laurel, *Laurus nobilis*) is the plant to which this chapter compares its leaves ("the [other] bay"), and is a different plant.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. It is a general, well-established fact that the leaves of the cherry laurel contain cyanogenic glycosides, which release hydrogen cyanide when the leaf is crushed or chewed. That fact is stated generally, and the chapter does not mention it. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records leaves smeared for headaches and a burning stomach, a leaf drink for griping, and a juice drunk or used as a pessary to expel the menstrual flow and urine. These are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for any of them. The plant is toxic, as stated under Contraindications, and a pessary or drink intended to expel the menstrual flow is not something the Codex describes or recommends. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations: the leaves "pounded into small pieces and smeared on"; the leaves "taken as a drink with wine"; the juice "given to drink with wine"; and the juice "applied in a pessary". No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved, and the Codex gives none. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The one well-established general fact is the one stated under Active Components: cherry laurel leaves are cyanogenic and are a known source of poisoning.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. It prints the leaves as "much smoother, thinner and greener" than the bay and the fruit as "round and red".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use. What it offers is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: a plant told apart from another by its leaf (smoother, thinner, greener than the bay), its rods measured at a foot, and its fruit colour noted. The long list of shared names that this chapter and the preceding one carry is also a lesson in the hazard of identifying plants by name alone. The plant is given no status beyond a list of remedies. The identification is the translators'.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It offers a description, external and internal remedies and an expelling juice, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one, and the plant should not be eaten or drunk.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. It is a general, well-established fact that cherry laurel leaves and seeds are poisonous because of cyanogenic compounds, so none of the drinks above should be tried. A drink or pessary said to "expel the menstrual flow" would endanger a pregnancy, and the Codex gives no advice on it. Headaches, stomach burning and griping that persist need medical assessment. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
