# I-215 — Cannabis sativa L. (Cannabis / Hemp)

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Canonical Name:** *Cannabis sativa* L.  
**Family:** Cannabaceae  
**Common Names:** Cannabis, Hemp, Marijuana (colloquial/historical), Marijuana (Mexican Spanish), Ganja (Sanskrit/Hindi → global), Bhang (oral preparation, Sanskrit), Hashish (Arabic: ḥashīsh — herbage), Charas (Indian hand-rubbed resin), Da Ma (Chinese: 大麻 — "big hemp"), Ma Fen (Chinese medicine), Kannabis (Greek), Qannab (Arabic), Chanvre (French), Hanf (German), Kif (North African), Dagga (Southern African Nguni), Indischer Hanf, Weed, Pot, Herb (modern vernacular)

**Taxonomic note:** The taxonomy of *Cannabis* remains contested. The single-species model (*C. sativa* L. with subspecies *sativa*, *indica*, *ruderalis*) is currently most accepted in botanical and pharmacological literature. In cannabis commerce/cultivation, "indica" and "sativa" have been repurposed to describe experiential phenotypes (sedating vs. stimulating), which do NOT map reliably onto botanical subspecies — a significant source of consumer confusion.

**Key chemovars (chemical varieties) — the pharmacologically relevant classification:**
- **Drug-type cannabis (high THC):** Δ9-THC dominant; psychoactive; typically >15% THC dry weight
- **CBD-type cannabis / Hemp:** CBD dominant; THC <0.3% (legal threshold in USA) or <0.2% (EU); psychoactively inert
- **Intermediate types:** Various THC:CBD ratios; clinical evidence emerging for specific ratios
- **High-CBG, High-CBC types:** Emerging cultivars for specific therapeutic applications

**Botanical identity:** Annual dioecious (usually) herb 1–5 m; palmate compound leaves 5–11 leaflets; female plants produce resinous glandular trichomes on flowers (unfertilized female flowers = "sinsemilla" cannabis, highest cannabinoid content); male plants produce pollen, less resin. The resin is the primary pharmacological fraction — trichomes concentrate cannabinoids, terpenes, flavonoids.

**7/9 convergence — Joint HIGHEST in Volume I alongside Ginger and Garlic**

**Historical milestone:** The Yanghai Tombs discovery (2008, Xinjiang, China) — 2,700-year-old cannabis sample (789g, clearly cultivated for psychoactive use based on THC:CBD ratio analysis) provides earliest direct archaeological evidence of intentional drug-type cannabis cultivation. Radiocarbon dated 2,800–2,500 BCE.

**Scythian vapor baths (Herodotus, c. 440 BCE, *Histories* Book IV):** "The Scythians take the seed of this hemp and, crawling under the felt coverings, throw it upon the red-hot stones; immediately it smokes, and gives out such a vapour as no Grecian vapour-bath can exceed; the Scythians, delighted, shout for joy." — Earliest textual description of recreational/ritual cannabis inhalation. Scythian burial sites with cannabis seeds, burners, and leather pouches confirm the account.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Why 7 traditions independently converged on this plant:**

Cannabis exhibits extraordinary pharmacological breadth because humans and cannabis co-evolved through the endocannabinoid system — a neural signaling system present in all vertebrates (and some invertebrates). When cannabinoids interact with CB1 and CB2 receptors, they modulate systems governing pain, appetite, mood, memory, inflammation, and reproduction — the most fundamental biological drives.

**Independently convergent observations across traditions:**

1. **Pain relief / analgesia** (all 7 traditions): The most consistent cross-traditional observation; cannabis analgesia is robust, multi-mechanism, and occurs at doses producing minimal or no sedation in experienced users — this practical observation drove medicinal adoption worldwide.

2. **Appetite stimulation** (India, China, Islamic medicine, Greek): The "munchies" effect (THC activation of hypothalamic CB1 receptors) was empirically observed and explicitly valued — particularly for wasting disease, tropical fevers, and post-illness appetite loss. Ayurveda's "laghu" (appetite-stimulating) classification for Bhang is mechanism-confirmed.

3. **Fiber and textile source** (all textile-using cultures): Hemp fiber use (stems) predates drug use in many cultures; archaeological hemp textiles 8,000–10,000 BCE. The multi-utility plant (fiber, food, medicine, ritual) created cultivation infrastructure that spread medicinal knowledge simultaneously.

4. **Ritual and spiritual use** (India, Scythian/Central Asian, Sufi Islamic tradition): Bhang in Holi festival, Shiva iconography; Sufi practice; Rastafari (modern); Scythian shamanic ritual. The alteration of consciousness in cannabis was functionally integrated into sacred practice across multiple unconnected religious traditions.

5. **Anti-inflammatory and wound application** (Dioscorides, Ibn Sīnā, Chinese): Topical cannabis preparations for wounds and inflammation predate understanding of CB2 receptor anti-inflammatory mechanisms by millennia.

**Why 2 traditions are absent:**
- BM (Mesoamerican): Cannabis not native to Americas; pre-Columbian absence is expected
- SA (South American): Same — not native, no pre-Columbian contact

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Cannabinoids (>100 identified, primary therapeutic class):

**Major phytocannabinoids:**
- **Δ9-Tetrahydrocannabinol (THC):** Primary psychoactive compound; CB1 partial agonist (KB ~5 nM), CB2 partial agonist; analgesic, anti-emetic, appetite stimulant, bronchodilator, neuroprotective; Schedule I in USA (though medically available as dronabinol/nabilone)
- **Cannabidiol (CBD):** Non-psychoactive; complex multi-target pharmacology; CB1 negative allosteric modulator, CB2 partial agonist; 5-HT1A agonist; TRP channel modulator; GPR55 antagonist; adenosine reuptake inhibitor; anti-epileptic (Epidiolex FDA-approved), anxiolytic, anti-inflammatory, antipsychotic (may buffer THC psychosis)
- **Δ8-Tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ8-THC):** Less potent psychoactive than Δ9; anti-nausea; naturally present in minor amounts; semi-synthesized from CBD in commercial products (regulatory gray area in many jurisdictions)
- **Cannabigerol (CBG):** "Mother cannabinoid" — THCA and CBDA both derived from CBGA; CB1/CB2 partial agonist; anti-bacterial (MRSA); anti-glaucoma; anti-tumor (preclinical); growing clinical interest
- **Cannabinol (CBN):** Oxidation product of THC; mildly psychoactive; sedative (traditional claim; limited RCT evidence); possible analgesic
- **Cannabichromene (CBC):** Non-psychoactive; anti-inflammatory (via TRPA1); antinociceptive; possible neurogenic (promotes neural stem cell viability); anti-tumor preclinical
- **Cannabidivarin (CBDV):** Anti-epileptic; TRPV1/TRPA1 modulator; under clinical investigation for autism, Rett syndrome, Dravet syndrome
- **Tetrahydrocannabivarin (THCV):** Partial CB1 antagonist at low doses (may reduce THC effects), agonist at higher doses; anti-obese; appetite suppressant (paradoxical to THC); anti-epileptic; appetite suppression unique pharmacology
- **Cannabigerovarin (CBGV):** Anti-inflammatory; possible antitumor

**Acidic forms (raw plant, non-heated):**
- **THCA** (tetrahydrocannabinolic acid) → THC upon decarboxylation (heat, ~115°C)
- **CBDA** (cannabidiolic acid) → CBD upon decarboxylation; independent anti-nausea evidence
- **CBGA** (cannabigerolic acid) → precursor to THCA, CBDA, CBCA
- Acidic forms: non-psychoactive; anti-inflammatory; different from decarboxylated counterparts; juicing raw cannabis preserves acid cannabinoids

### Terpenes (>150 identified; responsible for aroma; also pharmacologically active):

**Major terpenes in cannabis:**
- **Myrcene** (most abundant): Sedating, analgesic, possible blood-brain barrier enhancer (controversial "entourage" claim); found also in hops (Humulus lupulus), mango, lemongrass
- **Limonene:** Anxiolytic (same compound in citrus peel); anti-tumor; anti-inflammatory; mood-elevating
- **β-Caryophyllene:** Selective CB2 agonist (the only terpene with proven cannabinoid receptor activity); anti-inflammatory; gastroprotective; potential pain relief without psychoactivity; found also in black pepper, cloves (see I-212 Garlic / I-211 Ginger terpene cross-links)
- **Linalool:** Anxiolytic, sedating (same as in lavender); anti-convulsant; GABA-A modulation
- **α-Pinene:** Bronchodilatory; AChE inhibition (may counteract THC short-term memory impairment); anti-inflammatory; found in pine resin, rosemary
- **Terpinolene:** Anti-tumor (breast cancer preclinical); sedating; antifungal
- **Humulene:** Appetite suppressant; anti-tumor; anti-inflammatory; found also in hops (Humulus lupulus — same family Cannabaceae)
- **Ocimene:** Antiviral; anti-inflammatory
- **Nerolidol:** Sedating; anti-parasitic (particularly *Plasmodium*); skin penetration enhancer

**Entourage effect (Mechoulam 1998, elaborated Russo 2011):** The hypothesis that whole-plant cannabis produces superior therapeutic effects compared to isolated cannabinoids — terpenes and minor cannabinoids modifying receptor binding, blood-brain barrier permeability, and downstream effects. Evidence: select supporting studies; mechanism-plausible; definitive RCT evidence limited by regulatory constraints on whole-plant research. CBD + THC combination shows robust evidence (Sativex = 1:1 THC:CBD — EMA-approved for MS spasticity).

### Flavonoids:
- Cannflavins A and B: Cannabis-specific methylated flavones; anti-inflammatory (COX/5-LOX inhibition, 30× more potent than aspirin per weight in vitro — Burkhart 1989); research limited by regulatory status
- Quercetin, apigenin, kaempferol: Standard anti-inflammatory flavonoids

### Hemp seed nutritional composition (non-cannabinoid):
- Protein: 25–30% by weight; complete amino acid profile including all essential amino acids; edestin (major globulin) highly digestible
- Fatty acids: Omega-6:Omega-3 ratio approximately 3:1 (optimal for human nutrition); gamma-linolenic acid (GLA); stearidonic acid
- Fiber: 27–38% in hull; hulled seeds lower fiber
- Minerals: Phosphorus, potassium, sodium, magnesium, sulfur, calcium, iron, zinc
- Tocopherols (vitamin E): Potent antioxidant fraction

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### 5a. The Endocannabinoid System (ECS) — Foundation of Cannabis Pharmacology

The ECS is an evolutionarily ancient neuromodulatory system present in all vertebrates and present in some form in many invertebrates. Its discovery (1990s) emerged directly from trying to understand how THC works:

**Receptors:**
- **CB1 receptors:** Brain (hippocampus, basal ganglia, cerebellum, prefrontal cortex — explains cognitive/motor effects of THC), spinal cord (pain), periphery (liver, adipose tissue, gut). Gi-protein coupled → inhibits adenylate cyclase → reduces cAMP → reduced calcium influx → reduced neurotransmitter release. CB1 activation INHIBITS neurotransmission presynaptically — a "retrograde signaling" mechanism where postsynaptic neurons signal back to presynaptic neurons to reduce excitatory or inhibitory transmission (depending on circuit).
- **CB2 receptors:** Immune cells (B cells, macrophages, NK cells, T cells), CNS microglia, periphery; anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory; inducible (upregulated during inflammation)

**Endogenous ligands:**
- **Anandamide (AEA, arachidonoylethanolamide, N-arachidonoylethanolamide):** "Bliss molecule" (from Sanskrit ānanda); partial CB1 agonist; lipid signal synthesized on-demand; FAAH (fatty acid amide hydrolase) is the primary degradative enzyme
- **2-Arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG):** Full CB1/CB2 agonist; more abundant than AEA; MAGL (monoacylglycerol lipase) is primary degradative enzyme; principal endogenous ligand at CB1

**ECS function:** Retrograde regulation of synaptic transmission; modulation of pain, appetite, mood, memory consolidation, reproduction, immune function, neuroprotection. The ECS acts as a homeostatic buffer — "eat, sleep, relax, forget, protect" (DiPatrizio 2016) — perhaps the reason so many cannabis effects align with stress-relief, appetite, and pain reduction.

### 5b. THC Mechanism

- Partial CB1 agonist (partial = submaximal efficacy vs. full agonist; psychoactive effects plateau at higher doses partly due to partiality)
- Prefrontal cortex CB1: Impairs working memory, judgment, executive function
- Hippocampus CB1: Impairs short-term memory encoding (anterograde amnesia)
- Basal ganglia/cerebellum CB1: Alters motor control (coordination effects)
- Hypothalamus CB1: Appetite stimulation (ghrelin-independent mechanism; direct orexigenic signaling)
- Nucleus accumbens: Dopamine release → euphoria/reward
- Spinal cord/peripheral: Antinociception (pain modulation)
- Brainstem (5-HT3 antagonism independently): Anti-emetic
- Bronchopulmonary: Bronchodilation (CB1 on airway smooth muscle)
- Intraocular pressure: Reduces IOP via uveal CB1 (ocular glaucoma treatment — short duration limits clinical utility)

### 5c. CBD Mechanism (distinct from THC, complex multi-target)

CBD does NOT work primarily through CB1/CB2. Primary mechanisms:
- **5-HT1A partial agonist:** Anxiolytic, antidepressant — same receptor targeted by buspirone; mechanism of anti-panic effects
- **TRPV1 desensitization:** Reduces pain and inflammation via capsaicin receptor desensitization (same mechanism as ginger/gingerol — see I-211)
- **TRPA1 activation:** Anti-inflammatory, anti-nociceptive
- **GPR55 antagonism:** Anti-epileptic, anti-tumor; GPR55 is "orphan receptor" sometimes considered CB3
- **Adenosine reuptake inhibition:** Increases synaptic adenosine → A2A receptor activation → anti-inflammatory; same mechanism as caffeine in reverse
- **CB1 negative allosteric modulator:** CBD at CB1 allosteric site reduces THC efficacy (reduces psychoactivity and potentially psychosis risk when co-administered)
- **FAAH inhibition (weak):** Reduces anandamide breakdown → indirect cannabimimetic effect
- **Nuclear receptors (PPARγ):** Anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective
- **Anti-epileptic mechanism (Epidiolex):** Likely multi-mechanism; GPR55 + sodium channel modulation + adenosine pathway all contribute; direct mechanism at voltage-gated sodium channels shown for Dravet/LGS efficacy

### 5d. CB2 Receptor Anti-Inflammatory Mechanism

- CB2 activation on macrophages → inhibits LPS-induced cytokine release (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6)
- Reduces neutrophil migration to inflammatory sites
- Promotes anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage polarization
- Reduces oxidative burst
- THC (partial), CBD (partial), CBG (partial), β-caryophyllene (full agonist) all activate CB2
- No psychoactivity through CB2 activation (CB2 not significantly expressed in brain at baseline)

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

### 6a. Nausea / Chemotherapy-Induced Nausea and Vomiting (CINV) — STRONG EVIDENCE

**Dronabinol (synthetic THC) and Nabilone (synthetic CB1 agonist):**  
- Both FDA-approved for CINV refractory to standard antiemetics (1985, 1986)  
- Multiple RCTs: superior to placebo; comparable to prochlorperazine in some studies  
- THC anti-emetic: 5-HT3 antagonism (same target as ondansetron) + CB1 activation in brainstem dorsal vagal complex  

**Sativex (nabiximols, 1:1 THC:CBD):**  
- EMA-approved adjunct for cancer pain (UK, Canada, multiple EU countries)  
- RCT evidence for opioid-inadequate cancer pain  

### 6b. Multiple Sclerosis Spasticity — STRONG EVIDENCE (Sativex)

**Collin 2007 RCT** (N=189, MS spasticity):  
- Sativex (THC:CBD 1:1 oromucosal spray) vs. placebo  
- Significant reduction in spasticity NRS score  
- Replicated in SAVANT, MUSEC, and MOVE trials  
- EMA-approved in multiple EU countries, Canada, UK for MS spasticity  
- Number needed to treat (NNT): ~5 for 30% improvement (responder analysis)

**Mechanism:** CB1 in spinal interneurons and descending pathways → reduced spastic reflex arc excitability

### 6c. Epilepsy (CBD/Epidiolex) — STRONG EVIDENCE

**Devinsky 2017 NEJM RCT** (Dravet syndrome, N=120):  
- CBD 20mg/kg/day vs. placebo  
- Median monthly seizure reduction: 38.9% vs. 13.3%; significant  
- 5% became seizure-free (vs. 0% placebo)  

**Thiele 2018 NEJM RCT** (Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, N=225):  
- CBD 20mg/kg/day: 41.9% reduction in drop seizures vs. 17.2% placebo  

**Epidiolex FDA approval:** June 2018 — first plant-derived FDA-approved cannabinoid drug; Schedule V (rescheduled from Schedule I upon approval); treats Dravet syndrome, Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, tuberous sclerosis complex (2020)

**Mechanism:** GPR55 antagonism + sodium channel modulation + TRPV1 desensitization (combined)

### 6d. Chronic Pain — MODERATE-STRONG EVIDENCE

**Aviram & Samuels 2017 meta-analysis** (43 RCTs): Cannabinoids show significant effect on chronic neuropathic pain; NNT 5–11 (comparable to pregabalin, gabapentin)  
**Nugent 2017 (VA/NASEM systematic review):** "Substantial evidence" for cannabis/cannabinoids for chronic pain in adults  
**Issues:** Most RCTs use nabiximols or dronabinol; smoked cannabis RCTs are few due to regulatory constraints; effect sizes modest in most studies; comparison to standard of care often missing  

**Specific pain syndromes with GOOD evidence:**  
- Neuropathic pain (diabetic, HIV-associated, multiple sclerosis-related)
- Cancer pain (in combination with opioids, opioid-sparing)
- Fibromyalgia (emerging RCT evidence)

### 6e. Anxiety — MODERATE EVIDENCE

**Bergamaschi 2011** (social phobia, CBD simulated public speaking):  
- Single dose CBD (300mg) significantly reduced anxiety vs. placebo in validated public speaking model  
- Mechanism: 5-HT1A agonism, prefrontal cortex GABA modulation  

**Cannabis (THC-dominant) and anxiety: BIDIRECTIONAL**  
- Low THC doses: anxiolytic (CB1 in amygdala reduces threat response)  
- Higher THC doses or naive users: anxiogenic (paradoxical; dose-response inversion)  
- This explains the "too much makes you anxious" user phenomenon — real pharmacological effect, not psychosomatic

### 6f. PTSD — EMERGING EVIDENCE

**Fraser 2009** (open-label nabilone for PTSD nightmares, veterans, N=47):  
- 70% reduction in nightmare frequency  
- Improved sleep quality  
**Mechanism:** THC/CB1 reduction in REM sleep nightmare generation; extinction of fear memory reconsolidation (CB1 in prefrontal-amygdala circuit)  
**Phase 3 RCTs ongoing:** Multidisciplinary Association for Psychedelic Studies (MAPS) and others

### 6g. Glaucoma — LIMITED CLINICAL UTILITY

- THC reduces intraocular pressure by ~25% (comparable to standard drops)  
- Duration: only 3–4 hours → would require 6–8 doses daily for 24h IOP control  
- Systemic side effects (psychoactive, cardiovascular) outweigh benefit vs. modern glaucoma drops  
- Role: historical interest; NOT recommended as primary glaucoma treatment by ophthalmology societies; may have utility when other treatments fail  

### 6h. Appetite / Cancer/HIV Cachexia — STRONG EVIDENCE

- Dronabinol FDA-approved for AIDS-related anorexia (1992) and cancer-related cachexia  
- Multiple RCTs confirm appetite stimulation and weight stabilization  
- Mechanism: hypothalamic CB1 → ghrelin-independent orexigenic drive; reward-related flavor enhancement

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### The "high" and its dimensions

The THC-mediated altered state has been described in more literary, philosophical, and scientific detail than perhaps any other plant intoxicant, second only to alcohol. Key phenomenological dimensions:

**Temporal dilation:** The subjective experience of time slowing is one of the most consistent and cross-culturally documented cannabis effects. Mechanism: CB1 in cerebellar and basal ganglia circuits disrupting interval timing. Baudelaire (*Les Paradis Artificiels*, 1860): "Sounds clothe themselves in colours, colours contain music." This synesthetic quality reflects cross-modal sensory binding disruption at the thalamic gate.

**Ideational fluency and lateral thinking:** Cannabis consistently produces associative leaping — connections between seemingly unrelated concepts become accessible. This has been operationalized as "hyper-priming" (semantic network activation is broadened; distant associations become accessible). Mechanism: prefrontal CB1 disrupting normal executive filtering of association pathways. Creatives' traditional relationship with cannabis (from jazz musicians to writers to Silicon Valley figures) reflects consistent empirical observations of this effect.

**The "set and setting" dependency:** Cannabis effects are uniquely context-sensitive compared to most drugs. Naive users in anxious settings often experience anxiety/paranoia; experienced users in social settings often experience euphoria. This context-sensitivity reflects the ECS's function as a homeostatic modulatorsthe ECS amplifies the current emotional/cognitive state rather than imposing a fixed state. This is the pharmacological basis for the traditional Ayurvedic specification of Bhang consumption in a "pleasant social environment" (*satsang*).

**Bhang and Shiva:** In North Indian tradition, bhang (cannabis decoction with milk, sugar, spices) is ritually offered to Shiva and consumed on Holi and Shivaratri. Shiva mythology positions him as the "lord of chaos" who drinks poison and meditates in cremation grounds — cannabis (as a substance that disrupts ordinary consciousness while facilitating inward focus) fits this archetype precisely. The experiential quality of meditative absorption supported by cannabis is described in Tantric texts as *unmada* (intoxicated divine madness) — differentiated from everyday impairment.

**Medieval Islamic ambivalence:** Islamic medical texts document cannabis effects accurately; the religious status of cannabis was disputed (compared to alcohol, which is explicitly forbidden in Quran; cannabis is not mentioned). Sufi orders (particularly Haydariyya, founded by Haydar of Khorasan c.1300 CE) used cannabis as a vehicle for dhikr (divine remembrance) — the dissociative quality aiding the practice of ego dissolution. This parallel to cannabis's use in Hindu tantric practice (two traditions with minimal historical contact) represents phenomenological convergence on its utility for contemplative practice.

### Hemp and the material world

The phenomenological record of hemp (non-drug cannabis) is embedded in material culture: the first paper (China, c. 100 BCE) was made from hemp; cannabis fiber made the sails of Columbus's ships; the Gutenberg Bible and Thomas Jefferson's draft of the Declaration of Independence were written on hemp paper. The entanglement of hemp with the emergence of literacy, navigation, and printing is a material-world parallel to cannabis's entanglement with altered consciousness in the spiritual world.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Part A: Forms and Preparations

**Inhaled (smoked/vaporized):**
- Fastest onset (seconds to minutes); highest bioavailability (30–60% THC absorbed)
- Duration: 2–4 hours; easy titration
- Smoked: combustion byproducts (tar, carbon monoxide) cause respiratory risk; vaporized at 170–185°C avoids combustion → significantly reduced toxicant exposure
- Cannabis vaporization (NOT nicotine e-cigarettes): Perneger 2007 and Abrams 2007 document fewer respiratory symptoms vs. smoked

**Oral (edibles, capsules, tinctures):**
- Onset: 30 min–2 hours; duration: 4–8+ hours; less predictable peak
- First-pass metabolism: THC → 11-OH-THC (more potent, longer-acting psychoactive) → 11-COOH-THC (inactive)
- **DOSING CHALLENGE:** Variable absorption; edible overdose is common consumer error (onset delayed → user redoses → peak arrives later compounding)
- Starting dose for naive adults: 2.5–5 mg THC; titrate slowly
- CBD: Epidiolex dose 5–20 mg/kg/day for epilepsy; OTC CBD products for adults typically 25–75 mg/day

**Sublingual (tinctures, Sativex spray):**
- Onset: 15–45 minutes; intermediate between inhaled and oral
- Partial sublingual absorption bypasses first-pass → more predictable than oral
- Sativex nabiximols spray: 2.7 mg THC + 2.5 mg CBD per spray; dose-titrated over 2 weeks

**Topical:**
- Cannabinoids penetrate skin (lipophilic); local CB1/CB2 anti-inflammatory effects
- CBD topicals: arthritis, localized pain, skin inflammation — some patient-reported benefit; RCT evidence minimal (Hammell 2016 preclinical rat study widely cited but human RCTs limited)
- Does NOT produce systemic psychoactivity at normal topical doses
- Transdermal patches (THC/CBD) achieve systemic levels — designed for systemic delivery, different from topical salves

**Suppositories:**
- Used in palliative care; pediatric epilepsy (Dravet); elderly unable to take oral
- Bypasses first-pass metabolism → efficient delivery; onset 20–40 minutes
- Medical cannabis dispensary products; less well-studied pharmacokinetically than oral

**Raw cannabis juice/smoothie (THCA, CBDA — non-psychoactive):**
- Emerging clinical interest (Courtney protocol for autoimmune conditions)
- High-dose acid cannabinoids without psychoactivity
- Limited clinical evidence; interesting mechanistic premise

### Part B: Dosage Guidelines

**THC for pain (adult with experience):**
- Vaporized: 5–25 mg THC equivalent per session, 2–3× daily as needed
- Oral: 5 mg THC starting; titrate by 2.5–5 mg per dose every 3–7 days based on effect/tolerance
- Target: analgesic effect without significant impairment

**CBD for anxiety (adult, non-prescription):**
- 15–75 mg/day oral; some individuals require higher doses (up to 300 mg/day in research settings)
- Acute anxiety (pre-event): single doses 100–300 mg studied in research settings

**CBD for epilepsy (Epidiolex, prescription only):**
- Starting: 2.5 mg/kg twice daily; target 5–10 mg/kg twice daily (max 20 mg/kg/day)
- Requires physician supervision; LFT monitoring (CYP3A4 interaction with other AEDs)

**Cannabis for CINV (as adjunct to standard antiemetics):**
- Nabilone 1–2 mg twice daily; Dronabinol 5–7.5 mg/m² starting 1–3 hours before chemotherapy
- Or medical cannabis (physician-approved): titrate to comfort using vaporized or sublingual

### Part C: Safety Profile

**Short-term adverse effects:**
- Cognitive impairment (dose-dependent, resolves with abstinence)
- Tachycardia (dose-dependent; mediated by CB1 in cardiac tissue + autonomic modulation)
- Anxiety/paranoia (dose-dependent; high THC, naive users, anxious settings)
- Psychomotor impairment (driving impairment real and significant; do NOT drive for 4h+ after inhaled, 8h+ after oral cannabis)
- Orthostatic hypotension (particularly in elderly)
- Dry mouth (xerostomia)

**Long-term adverse effects (with heavy, frequent use):**
- **Cannabis Use Disorder (CUD):** DSM-5 diagnosis; ~9% of users develop dependence; higher in daily users (~17%) and those who start in adolescence (~17% vs. 9% adult initiation) — Hasin 2018 JAMA Psychiatry review
- **Cognitive:** Adolescent heavy use associated with small but measurable IQ reduction (Meier 2012 PNAS longitudinal study, N=1,037, birth cohort), verbal memory, and executive function deficits; effects less clear in adults who start post-18
- **Respiratory (smoked only):** Chronic bronchitis, increased respiratory secretions; no clear causal link to lung cancer (Hashibe 2006 meta-analysis — paradoxical non-finding possibly related to THC-mediated anti-tumor effects)
- **Cannabis Hyperemesis Syndrome (CHS):** Paradoxical cyclic vomiting in heavy long-term users; relieved by hot showers (hot water CB1 activation); capsaicin topical (TRPV1 desensitization) effective in ER
- **Psychosis risk:** Heavy cannabis use associated with increased risk of schizophrenia spectrum disorders, especially in those with genetic predisposition (COMT Val158Met, AKT1 C/T genotype) — Di Forti 2019 Lancet Psychiatry study 11 European sites

**CBD-specific adverse effects:**
- Diarrhea (dose-dependent; most common GI effect)
- Fatigue and somnolence
- Elevated liver enzymes (ALT) especially when combined with valproate/clobazam
- Drug-drug interactions via CYP enzyme inhibition (see below)
- NOT psychoactive at standard doses; no dependence liability established

### Part D: Drug Interactions

| Drug/Class | Interaction | Clinical Action |
|-----------|-------------|----------------|
| **Sedatives, opioids, benzodiazepines** | Additive CNS depression | Caution; reduce opioid dose with concurrent cannabis (opioid-sparing is also a therapeutic use) |
| **Warfarin** | THC inhibits CYP2C9 → increases warfarin exposure → elevated INR, bleeding risk | Monitor INR closely; multiple case reports of serious interactions |
| **Clobazam (epilepsy)** | CBD inhibits CYP2C19 → 3-fold increase in clobazam/norclobazam levels | Reduce clobazam dose when adding CBD; monitor sedation |
| **Valproate** | CBD + valproate → elevated liver enzymes (possibly additive); hepatotoxicity risk | LFT monitoring; dose adjustment consideration |
| **Antidiabetic drugs** | Cannabis may reduce or increase glucose (context-dependent); appetite effects complicate T2DM management | Monitor glucose; awareness of complexity |
| **Antihypertensives** | THC-induced tachycardia/BP changes may interact with beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers | Cardiovascular monitoring; avoid in unstable CAD |
| **Alcohol** | Additive CNS depression and coordination impairment; dramatically increases psychomotor impairment | AVOID combination; especially no driving |
| **Immunosuppressants (tacrolimus, cyclosporine)** | CBD inhibits CYP3A4 → possible increase in immunosuppressant levels | Monitor drug levels; dose adjustment may be required |
| **SSRI/SNRI antidepressants** | Complex; possible serotonin syndrome risk at very high CBD doses (5-HT1A); mild additive sedation | Generally well-tolerated at standard doses; monitor |
| **Lithium** | Case reports of cannabis-induced mania in bipolar patients on lithium | Caution in bipolar disorder; psychiatry involvement recommended |

### Part E: Contraindications

- **Pregnancy:** ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION for THC-dominant cannabis — crosses placenta; fetal CB1 development impairment; intrauterine growth restriction; neurodevelopmental effects in offspring (Gunn 2016 meta-analysis); no safe dose established
- **Breastfeeding:** THC in breast milk; infant exposure documented; CONTRAINDICATED
- **Personal or first-degree family history of psychosis/schizophrenia:** THC — high risk; CONTRAINDICATED
- **Unstable cardiac disease:** THC-induced tachycardia and hypertension → risk of angina, MI; multiple case reports of cannabis-induced MI
- **Severe hepatic impairment:** CBD — elevated liver enzyme risk; use cautiously or avoid
- **Children/adolescents:** THC-containing preparations — AVOID except in exceptional medical circumstances (e.g., Dravet syndrome with CBD — prescription only with close monitoring); CBD for pediatric epilepsy: Epidiolex only, prescription
- **Driving/operating machinery:** CONTRAINDICATED within 4–8 hours of any intoxicating cannabis use

## Regulatory Note

Cannabis is regulated under international law (1961 UN Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs; Schedule IV — most restrictive) and varies dramatically by national and regional jurisdiction. As of 2024:
- Prescription medical cannabis: 50+ countries, including Germany (2024 adult legal), Canada, Australia, most EU countries for specific indications, USA (FDA-approved Epidiolex, dronabinol, nabilone; state-level medical cannabis widely available)
- THC-containing products: Schedule I in the USA at federal level (ongoing DEA reclassification review as of 2024); illegal in many countries
- Hemp/CBD: Generally permissible in USA (Farm Bill 2018: <0.3% THC); EU: <0.2% THC; many other countries permitting hemp food/cosmetics

This codex documents cannabis as an ethnobotanical and pharmacological subject; practitioners must navigate applicable legal frameworks in their jurisdiction.
