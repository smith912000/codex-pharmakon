# I-480 | Rosemary (*Salvia rosmarinus* Schleid.)

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Salvia rosmarinus* Schleid. (since 2017 APG IV reclassification)  
**Former name:** *Rosmarinus officinalis* L. — widely still used in commerce, pharmacopoeias, and literature; the 2017 molecular phylogenetic reclassification merged *Rosmarinus* into *Salvia* based on clade position  
**Family:** Lamiaceae (Mint family)  
**Common names:** Rosemary, sea dew, dew of the sea, compass weed, polar plant  
**Synonyms:** *Rosmarinus officinalis* L. (valid through 2016; still widely used); *Salvia rosmarinus* subsp. *rosmarinus*; cultivars include 'Albus' (white flower), 'Prostatus' (prostrate), Tuscan Blue, Barbecue, and dozens of others

**Etymology:** *Rosmarinus* (former genus) from Latin *ros* ("dew") + *marinus* ("of the sea") — "dew of the sea" or "sea dew." The plant grows on Mediterranean coastal cliffs where morning dew condenses on its needle-like leaves. *officinalis* = "of the apothecary" (same as Melissa, Salvia, Valeriana). The new genus assignment *Salvia rosmarinus* places rosemary within the giant *Salvia* genus (1,000 species) — the same genus as common sage (*Salvia officinalis*), clary sage (*S. sclarea*), and diviner's sage (*S. divinorum*).

**The 2017 reclassification debate:** Many pharmacognosists, herbalists, and regulatory bodies continue to use *Rosmarinus officinalis* because: (1) all pharmacopoeial monographs still reference this name; (2) all clinical research was conducted under this name; (3) the practical identity of the plant is unchanged. The Codex notes the official scientific name change (*Salvia rosmarinus*) while acknowledging that *Rosmarinus officinalis* remains dominant in applied pharmacology literature.

**Botanical description:** Aromatic evergreen shrub to 2 m; stems woody; leaves linear-lanceolate (needle-like), dark green above, white-tomentose below, sessile; flowers small, 2-lipped, blue-violet to white; calyx with 2 lips; intensely aromatic — the scent of the Mediterranean. Native to Mediterranean basin; cultivated globally.

**Culinary-medicinal integration:** Rosemary is among the most complete examples of a plant simultaneously and unambiguously in both the culinary and medicinal domains — not merely historical food use or folk medicine, but active contemporary use in both haute cuisine and clinical phytotherapy. The exact same rosmarinic acid and carnosic acid responsible for culinary antioxidant food preservation (meat preservation historically; oxidative stability) are responsible for the medicinal antioxidant neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects.

**Part used:** Leaves (fresh or dried); essential oil (steam distillation of flowering tops); whole flowering tops  
**Preparation:** Infusion; tincture; essential oil (aromatherapy; topical diluted); culinary use; encapsulated extract

## 3. Convergence Analysis

**Mediterranean medical consensus — the culinary-medicinal herb:** DMM and CM — the two principal traditions with the highest geographic exposure to Mediterranean rosemary in its native range — both document extensive therapeutic use. This reflects not merely academic knowledge but daily culinary familiarity that naturally extended into medicinal application. The ancient Greek-Roman and Islamic traditions both had rosemary growing in their gardens, cooking in their kitchens, and in their apothecaries simultaneously.

**"For the brain and the head" — cognitive application pre-dating neuroscience:** Ibn Sīnā and Dioscorides both describe rosemary for conditions associated with the brain and nervous system — Ibn Sīnā explicitly for memory strengthening, Dioscorides for headache. Shakespeare encoded this in *Hamlet* (Ophelia: "There's rosemary, that's for remembrance") — drawing on a well-established European association. The discovery that 1,8-cineole, camphor, and carnosic acid in rosemary upregulate nerve growth factor (NGF), inhibit acetylcholinesterase (AChE), and increase cerebral blood flow provides mechanistic grounding for what classical tradition encoded as "good for the brain."

**Rosmarinic acid — the anchor compound crossing multiple traditions:** Rosmarinic acid appears in rosemary, lemon balm (I-478), holy basil (I-479), and other Lamiaceae species — reflecting a shared biosynthetic pathway in the Lamiaceae family. Its recurring appearance across multiple sequential Codex entries illustrates how family-level phytochemistry creates overlapping but distinct pharmacological profiles across related medicinal plants.

**Antioxidant food preservation — the practical roots of medicinal use:** Rosemary was used to preserve meat (particularly lamb) in Mediterranean cooking from at least Roman times — the rosmarinic acid and carnosic acid prevented lipid oxidation in meat during storage. This practical antioxidant use in food directly parallels the biological antioxidant activity responsible for neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory medicinal effects. The culinary tradition inadvertently documented a key pharmacological property (potent antioxidant activity) through practical food application.

## 4. Phytochemical Composition

### Diterpene phenols (primary antioxidant/neuroprotective actives)
- **Carnosic acid** (1–2.8% leaf dry weight; catechol diterpene; Nrf2 activator; the most powerful antioxidant in rosemary; lipophilic; converts to carnosol under oxidative stress conditions)
- **Carnosol** (oxidation product of carnosic acid; COX-2 inhibitor; anti-tumor; anti-inflammatory; NF-κB inhibition)
- **Rosmanol, epirosmanol, methyl carnosate** (minor diterpene antioxidants)

### Phenolic Acids
- **Rosmarinic acid** (1–5% leaf; GABA-T inhibitor [as in lemon balm, holy basil]; antiviral; anti-inflammatory; NF-κB inhibitor; potent antioxidant DPPH radical scavenger)

### Essential Oil (1–2.5% leaf — higher than lemon balm)
- **1,8-Cineole (eucalyptol)** (35–55% EO; AChE inhibitor; bronchodilatory; increases cerebral blood flow; cognitive effects via olfaction)
- **Camphor** (10–25% EO; TRPV1 activation; analgesic; counterirritant; caution: neurotoxic at high oral doses)
- **α-Pinene** (10–15% EO; AChE inhibitor; antimicrobial; anti-inflammatory)
- **β-Pinene** (2–9%)
- **Borneol** (acetylcholinesterase inhibition; antimicrobial)
- **Camphene** (antioxidant; antimicrobial)
- **Linalool** (anxiolytic)
- **Verbenone** (dominant in some chemotypes; hepatoprotective; thyroid-modulating)

**Chemotype variation:** Rosemary EO occurs in three main chemotypes:
1. **Camphor-borneol type** (Spain): high camphor content; analgesic; less cognitive application
2. **1,8-Cineole type** (Morocco, Tunisia): high cineole; cognitive and respiratory emphasis
3. **Verbenone type** (Corsica): hepatoprotective; detoxifying; gentler neurological profile

This chemotype variation is clinically significant — select oil based on intended application.

### Flavonoids
- **Ursolic acid** (also in holy basil, plantain; anti-inflammatory; anti-tumor; adaptogenic)
- **Rosmarinic acid** (shared with lemon balm, holy basil — Lamiaceae family acid)
- **Luteolin** (COX/LOX inhibition)
- **Apigenin** (GABA-A modulation; anti-inflammatory)
- **Diosmin** (capillary protective; anti-inflammatory; vascular)

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

### Cognitive Enhancement (1,8-Cineole + Carnosic Acid)
**AChE inhibition:**
- 1,8-Cineole: inhibits acetylcholinesterase → increased synaptic acetylcholine → pro-cognitive; mechanism comparable to donepezil (weaker but safer)
- α-Pinene, borneol: additional AChE inhibitory activity
- Combined volatile fraction: AChE inhibition IC₅₀ ~0.5 mg/mL (vapor phase measurement)

**Cerebral blood flow:**
- 1,8-Cineole olfactory/vascular mechanism → increased cerebral circulation → improved brain oxygenation → cognitive performance enhancement

**NGF induction:**
- Carnosic acid: activates Nrf2 → induces neurotrophic factors including NGF; promotes neuronal survival and differentiation
- Relevant for Alzheimer's and age-related cognitive decline

**Olfactory-cognitive pathway:**
- Inhalation of rosemary EO → olfactory bulb activation → limbic system → cognitive performance
- Distinct from oral pharmacokinetics; measurable plasma 1,8-cineole after inhalation
- Moss et al. 2003: olfactory rosemary environment → improved memory performance — Level B

### Antioxidant / Neuroprotective (Carnosic Acid — Nrf2 activation)
- Carnosic acid: activates Nrf2/ARE pathway → induces HO-1, NQO1, GCL (glutamate-cysteine ligase), thioredoxin reductase → antioxidant enzyme induction cascade
- Particularly relevant in neural tissue (high oxidative stress vulnerability)
- Nrf2 activation also anti-inflammatory (cross-talk with NF-κB suppression)

### Anti-inflammatory (Carnosol + Rosmarinic Acid)
- **Carnosol:** COX-2 inhibitor (IC₅₀ ~1–5 μM; selective COX-2 over COX-1 — more like celecoxib than ibuprofen profile); NF-κB inhibitor; 5-LOX inhibitor
- **Rosmarinic acid:** NF-κB inhibition; COX-2 suppression; 5-LOX inhibition
- Combined: triple-pathway anti-inflammatory (same as chamomile but different compounds)

### Antimicrobial (EO)
- 1,8-Cineole, camphor, α-pinene: membrane disruption of bacterial cell walls
- Broad spectrum: *S. aureus*, *E. coli*, *P. aeruginosa*, *C. albicans*
- Biofilm inhibition at sub-MIC concentrations

### Hair Loss / Alopecia
- Topical rosemary oil (2%): inhibits 5α-reductase (same mechanism as finasteride for androgenic alopecia) — reduces DHT-mediated hair follicle miniaturization
- Pantuso et al. 2022 review: multiple studies show rosemary oil comparable to 2% minoxidil at 6 months for androgenic alopecia

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**Cognitive function:**
- Moss et al. 2003 (J Neuropsychiatry Neuroscience): rosemary aroma (EO diffused) vs control; improved spatial memory performance — Level B
- Pengelly et al. 2012: 750 mg dried rosemary/day; dose-dependent improvement in cognitive speed — Level A (small RCT, n=28 older adults)
- Akhondzadeh et al. 2015: rosemary leaf extract vs placebo (RCT, n=69, 1 month): improved image processing time — Level A

**Alopecia (hair loss):**
- Panahi et al. 2015 (*Skinmed*): rosemary oil vs 2% minoxidil, 6-month RCT (n=100): rosemary significantly increased hair count vs minoxidil (equivalent efficacy; less scalp itching in rosemary group) — Level A
- Kashkoli et al. 2020: similar comparison confirming rosemary equivalence to minoxidil

**Anti-inflammatory:**
- No RCT specifically for rosemary anti-inflammatory endpoint; supported by Level D mechanisms

**Antioxidant (food preservation validated — indirect human evidence):**
- Rosemary extract (E392) is a licensed food antioxidant in EU — prevents oxidative rancidity in fats, oils, meat products; documented efficacy as food antioxidant
- This regulatory validation, while not medicinal, provides rigorous efficacy evidence for the antioxidant activity

**Summary by evidence level:**
- Level A: Cognitive function (Pengelly 2012; Akhondzadeh 2015); alopecia (Panahi 2015)
- Level B: Olfactory cognitive enhancement (Moss 2003); traditional evidence
- Level C: Commission E Germany: dyspeptic complaints, circulatory problems; regulatory food antioxidant approval
- Level D: AChE inhibition; Nrf2 activation; anti-inflammatory; antimicrobial

## 7. Phenomenological Record

**Remembrance and Ophelia's pharmacology:** Shakespeare's *Hamlet* (1600) contains the most quoted botanical medicine passage in English literature: Ophelia's "There's rosemary, that's for remembrance — pray you, love, remember." This was not literary invention — it drew on a well-established European tradition of rosemary as the herb of memory. Rosemary was placed on the bodies of the dead (the dead should be remembered) and used by students at examinations (memory enhancement). The contemporary finding that 1,8-cineole from inhaled rosemary EO increases plasma cineole levels and improves memory performance in humans (Moss 2003) provides molecular grounding for the Shakespearean encoding — one of pharmacobotany's most culturally resonant validations.

**Wedding, funeral, and pharmacy:** Rosemary was simultaneously the herb of weddings (brides carried it for fidelity and luck), funerals (placed on coffins for remembrance), and medicine (the apothecary's standard aromatic antioxidant and digestive herb). This triple social role — joy, grief, and healing — made rosemary perhaps the most emotionally freighted herb in European culture. The chemical continuity across all three uses: the same antioxidant compounds (carnosic acid) that preserved food, the same volatile compounds (1,8-cineole) that enhanced memory, the same aromatic intensity that marked both celebration and mourning.

**Eau de Cologne and the Hungarian Water:** *Aqua Reginea Hungaricae* ("Queen of Hungary's Water") — reputedly the first alcohol-based perfume, appearing around 1370 CE — was primarily a rosemary distillate. The 14th-century Hungarian queen who reportedly used it to restore her health and beauty at age 70 gives the preparation its name. Later, *Eau de Cologne* (1709) by Italian-German perfumer Johann Maria Farina also had rosemary as a principal ingredient. These fragrance traditions parallel the medicinal tradition: the same volatile compounds (1,8-cineole, camphor, α-pinene) responsible for the scent are responsible for the pharmacological activity.

**Minoxidil comparison — phytotherapy achieving pharmaceutical-equivalent efficacy:** The Panahi 2015 RCT comparing rosemary oil (2% topical) to 2% minoxidil for androgenic alopecia is notable as one of the few cases where a botanical preparation achieved equivalent efficacy to a standard pharmaceutical in a properly controlled trial, with a favorable side-effect profile (less scalp itching). This positions rosemary oil as a genuine alternative for patients unable or unwilling to use minoxidil, not merely a folk remedy. The 5α-reductase inhibition mechanism is distinct from minoxidil's vasodilatory mechanism, suggesting potential for combination therapy.

## 8. Practical Application

**Energetic tier:** ◉ Qi (anti-inflammatory; circulatory; digestive) + ☿ Shen (cognitive; memory; neuroprotective)

### Preparation Methods and Dosing

**Infusion (digestive / cognitive):**
- 2–4 g dried rosemary per 250 mL boiling water; steep 10 min
- 2–3 cups daily
- Commission E dose: 4–6 g herb/day for dyspepsia; circulatory complaints

**Tincture (1:5, 45% ethanol):**
- 3–5 mL, 3× daily

**Standardized extract:**
- 200–400 mg, 3× daily; standardized to ≥6% rosmarinic acid + 2% carnosic acid

**Essential oil — aromatherapy (cognitive):**
- 3–5 drops in diffuser; 30 min sessions in workspace for cognitive enhancement
- 1,8-Cineole chemotype (Morocco/Tunisia) preferred for cognitive application
- Inhaled, not ingested (camphor neurotoxicity at oral doses)

**Topical hair loss treatment:**
- Dilute 2% rosemary EO in carrier oil (jojoba, coconut); massage into scalp daily
- Leave-in or wash-out; consistent 6-month trial required for alopecia assessment
- Use camphor-borneol OR 1,8-cineole chemotype (both active for alopecia)

**Culinary (preventive daily exposure):**
- Regular culinary use (2–5 g fresh herb with cooking) provides rosmarinic acid and carnosic acid
- Rosmarinic acid heat-stable; carnosic acid converts to carnosol during cooking (both active)

### Drug Interactions

- **Iron supplements / food iron:** Rosmarinic acid chelates iron → reduce iron absorption; separate administration by 2 hours
- **Anticoagulants:** Rosmarinic acid antiplatelet activity; monitor INR
- **Lithium:** Diuretic activity of rosemary may affect lithium excretion (theoretical)
- **Diabetes medications:** Minor glucose-modulating effects
- **Chemotherapy:** Carnosic acid Nrf2 activation — discuss with oncologist as for holy basil; potential interaction with chemotherapy-induced oxidative cell damage (protective in normal cells, potentially also protective in tumor cells)

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy (high doses / EO internally):** Camphor neurotoxicity; uterine stimulant at high dose; culinary amounts safe; medicinal doses and EO internal use — avoid
- **Epilepsy (camphor-rich preparations):** Camphor is proconvulsant; use 1,8-cineole-dominant chemotype; avoid camphor-rich preparations
- **Verbenone-rich EO:** Additional hepatotoxic concern at high doses; for internal use, verbenone-type should be used only under professional guidance

### Quality Markers

- Rosmarinic acid ≥3% (HPLC, dried herb or extract)
- Carnosic acid ≥2% (HPLC, dried herb)
- EO chemotype declaration required: camphor% + 1,8-cineole% + verbenone% reported
- Fresh dried herb: strong characteristic aroma; grey-green needles; no yellowing
