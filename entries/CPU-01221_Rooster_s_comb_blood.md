# Rooster's comb blood — *bya.(tsha.lu.)yi.ze.khrag* as Clark prints it (Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch.20); "Blood from a rooster's comb" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *Blood taken from the comb of the domestic cock. Neither Tibetan text prints a Latin name for it in the medicinal passage* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is the Tibetan *Four Tantras*. In Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; "Clark" below) the animal medicines close chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). Blood is the fourth, and its section opens with the deer's (heading "Blood", line 1171; deer blood opens it at line 1172). The entry reads (line 1176): "bya.(tsha.lu.)yi.ze.khrag: Rooster's comb blood: [has fifteen names]. It stimulates flesh growth, maintains bone resin [and cures anterior transposition of the calf muscle.]" The Tibetan is printed first, as Clark says he does for every substance (line 83), with the parentheses as Clark prints them. The square brackets mark interpolations from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries (line 82), so the two actions that stand outside them, flesh growth and bone resin, are the root-text claims as Clark renders them, and the calf-muscle condition is commentary. Neighbouring entries in the same line are pig blood ("collects [scattered types of] poisoning and brown phlegm"), donkey blood (rheumatism and lymph disorders in the joints) and womb blood ("constricts the mouths of the channels, regenerates flesh [around wounds, and is beneficial for warts]"). The spine's locus is that line 1176.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`; "MTK" below) gives the same entry in different wording (line 3544): "Blood from a rooster's comb heals muscle tissue and protects spongy bone." Where Clark has "stimulates flesh growth" MTK has "heals muscle tissue", and where Clark has "maintains bone resin" MTK has "protects spongy bone". The two translators render the same two actions, one on the flesh and one on the bone, with different English. The term "bone resin" also appears in Clark's list for tiger bone ("It increases bone resin", line 1160), so it is Clark's regular rendering and not a one-off. The MTK glossary prints a "Hen" (Tibetan *Jha*, *Gallus domesticus*, lines 6894-6898) but no rooster or comb. The dietary chapter says that "Flesh of chicken and sparrow increases semen and is beneficial for wounds" (Clark line 678), which concerns the flesh and is not about the blood.

### Active Components

Blood is plasma (water, proteins, fats and salts) carrying red cells, white cells and platelets, with haemoglobin as the oxygen-carrying pigment in the red cells. A cock's comb is a fleshy outgrowth of the head that is rich in blood vessels. The Tibetan texts name no constituent and no mechanism for the claimed actions, and this entry supplies none.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources say that the blood stimulates the growth of flesh and maintains or protects bone. They do not say whether it is drunk, applied or dried, or how the blood is taken from the comb. Clark adds that "nowadays only very few animal products are used" (line 57). Blood from a living or freshly killed bird is a poor candidate for any modern use, because of the infection risk (see Contraindications), and the Codex's application layer has nothing to add. No protocol is offered.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT).** Clark and MTK print no taste, potency or post-digestive taste for the rooster's comb blood. Clark's preface says that detailed texts such as the *Crystal Orb* record for each substance "which of the six tastes, eight natural potencies, three post-digestive 'tastes,' seventeen secondary qualities and individual uses each possesses" (line 57); those texts were not read. The attributes the sources give are the two actions above. A rule for blood in general is not printed in the passages read.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The rooster's comb blood is attested in the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica as one of the bloods in the animal-medicines list, with actions on flesh and bone (Clark line 1176; MTK line 3544). The tradition has a formal division of rejuvenation, "essence-extraction" (Clark line 128, "the division of essence-extraction (rejuvenation, etc.)"; also line 136), and no animal substance in the section read (lines 1147-1202) is assigned to it; this blood is not named in the compounding lists of chapter 21. The seven bodily constituents in the same text run from essential nutriment to semen, with flesh and bone among them (line 145). The actions here are on the first two named tissues of that list and carry no status beyond treatment. Clark's own statement of what is rejuvenating is about plants (line 69). In this tradition medicine is one branch of treatment and the root of disease lies in the three poisons; no passage read ties this blood to that frame. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in the Tibetan sources, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports a cultivation use. The sources give tissue-level actions for a material that is hard to obtain safely, and the tradition's own modern translator says animal medicines have largely dropped out of use. The honest entry is a record.

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** poultry blood can carry *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter* and avian influenza viruses, and blood-borne pathogens; drinking or applying untreated blood is unsafe.
- **Bleeding the bird:** drawing blood from the comb of a live bird causes it pain and risks infection in the bird; the sources do not say how it was done.
- **Legal and ethical status:** domestic fowl are unrestricted livestock in most places; animal-welfare law may apply to bleeding a live bird. [USER: verify]
