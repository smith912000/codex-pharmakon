### Origin & Tradition

The chapter sits in the section headed "METALLIC STONES" of Book Five, between the lodestone (5-148, line 10603) and *galaktites lithos* (5-150, line 10614).

**Dioscorides (DMM).** The whole chapter reads: "Arabicus stone is like a piece of ivory. Pounded into small pieces and smeared on it dries up haemorrhoids, and burnt it is a dentifrice for the teeth."

That is all the text says. It gives no colour beyond the likeness to ivory, no place, no hardness, no taste and no test for the genuine stone. "Arabicus" (Arabian) may point to a source in Arabia, but the chapter does not say so. [USER: verify.] The Greek name is not equated here with any mineral.

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

Little to map: the text names two sites and no others. Pounded and smeared on, the stone is said to dry up haemorrhoids, which in modern anatomy are the vascular cushions of the anal canal and rectum, so the claimed site is the anorectal tissue. Burnt, it is used as a dentifrice for the teeth, which maps to the teeth and gums of the oral cavity. The chapter gives no quality, humour, taste or temperament, and it names no organ, channel or energy. The two actions are therefore described in ordinary anatomical terms only. The entry records that the translator leaves the material unidentified, and that the chapter gives no dose, vehicle or frequency for either use.

### Contraindications

- **Unknown material.** The stone is unidentified; do not substitute any mineral for it.
- **Abrasion and ingestion.** Powders used on teeth and gums can abrade enamel and irritate tissue, and some minerals are toxic if swallowed.
- **Haemorrhoids.** A medical complaint; use proper care.
