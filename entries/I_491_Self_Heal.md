# I-491 — Self-Heal
## *Prunella vulgaris* L.

**Family:** Lamiaceae | **Volume:** I — Plant-Based | **Entry:** I-491

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted Name:** *Prunella vulgaris* L. (1753)
**Common Names:** Self-heal, heal-all, woundwort, carpenter's weed, hook-heal, prunella, all-heal, heart of the earth, blue curls
**Synonyms:** *Brunella vulgaris* Moench; *Prunella vulgaris* var. *lanceolata* (W. Bartram) Fernald; *P. vulgaris* var. *aleutica* Fernald

**Etymology:** *Prunella* (also written *Brunella*) derives from the German *Bräune* (quinsy/tonsillitis), reflecting the plant's traditional use for throat and mouth conditions; *vulgaris* = common, widespread; "self-heal" reflects the remarkable range of traditional healing applications attributed to the plant — the name implies it heals by itself, requiring no additional herbs; "carpenter's weed" specifically records use for tool-inflicted cuts

**Botanical Description:** Low-growing, slightly mat-forming perennial, 5–30 cm; stems square, hairy; leaves opposite, ovate-oblong, slightly toothed, 2–5 cm; distinctive inflorescence: dense, compact terminal spike of tubular two-lipped flowers in whorls subtended by broad bracts; flowers violet to purple (occasionally white or pink); bloom May–October; one of the most morphologically distinctive small Lamiaceae; the compact flower spike with its broad subtending bracts is essentially unmistakable

**Distribution:** One of the world's most widely distributed plants — native to Europe, Asia, North Africa; naturalized throughout the Americas, Australia, Pacific Islands, sub-Saharan Africa; virtually every temperate terrestrial habitat: lawns, roadsides, meadows, forest margins, disturbed ground; a true cosmopolitan weed with unparalleled geographic reach

**Plant Part Used:** Aerial parts (particularly flowering spikes and leaves); aqueous extracts (preferred for polysaccharide components); ethanolic extracts (for phenolics)

**Adulterants/Substitutions:** Essentially none; morphological distinctiveness + widespread distribution + low commercial value make adulteration commercially impractical; *P. vulgaris* is itself abundant and inexpensive globally

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

### The Cosmopolitan Healer

*Prunella vulgaris* achieves a convergence score that likely underestimates its true cross-traditional reach because the plant's global distribution post-European contact obscures the pre-contact knowledge base. In every temperate region where European settlers brought *P. vulgaris* (often inadvertently with crop seed), indigenous populations rapidly adopted it for wound healing and anti-inflammatory purposes. This rapid adoption across cultures — without direct transmission of botanical knowledge — suggests that the plant's pharmacological activity is sufficiently apparent from empirical contact to be independently identified.

The one confirmed pre-European-contact traditional use of profound pharmacological depth is the TCM designation 夏枯草 (*xià kū cǎo*, "summer-dry plant"). The common name refers to the distinctive phenological behavior of *Prunella*'s flowering spike, which browns and desiccates in midsummer while surrounding vegetation remains green — a phenological signature used in classical Chinese materia medica as an identity marker and a sign of the plant's energetic properties (clearing summer heat). *Xià kū cǎo* is classified in TCM as clearing liver fire (*qīng gān huǒ*), dissipating nodules (*sǎn jié*), and treating scrofula (cervical lymphadenopathy due to tuberculosis), goiter, and inflammatory eye conditions. These applications have direct pharmacological correlates: anti-inflammatory and antiviral properties most relevant to lymphadenopathy; rosmarinic acid and ursolic acid anti-inflammatory activity relevant to ocular inflammation.

### Self-Heal as Antiviral Paradigm

The pharmacological story of *P. vulgaris* took a decisive turn when antiviral screening programs in the late 20th century identified potent activity against HIV-1. Prunellin — a sulfated polysaccharide (later characterized as a mixture of acidic polysaccharides) — demonstrated HIV-1 reverse transcriptase inhibition at concentrations achievable in vitro. Subsequent work identified activity against HSV-1, HSV-2, influenza viruses, and adenoviruses. This antiviral profile — broad but not mechanistically specific — is consistent with the polysaccharide charge-based mechanism (interference with viral attachment to heparan sulfate proteoglycans) rather than specific enzymatic active-site inhibition.

The antiviral record gives *P. vulgaris* a contemporary pharmacological relevance well beyond its modest historical human medicinal profile and adds a specific molecular mechanism to the general "wound-healing" framework of its traditional use.

### The New Medicine in an Old Weed

A recurring pattern in ethnopharmacology: a plant known for two millennia for non-specific wound healing and anti-inflammatory effects harbors a specific antiviral mechanism of contemporary relevance. The polysaccharide fraction responsible for HIV-RT inhibition is the same water-extractable fraction delivered by every traditional decoction of *Prunella* prepared for throat or wound applications. Traditional medicine inadvertently delivered the antiviral compound in every preparation, without any concept of viral replication or reverse transcriptase.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

### Polysaccharides (Antiviral Fraction)
**Prunellin:** original name given to the antiviral fraction isolated by Xu et al. (1992); understood to be a mixture of sulfated, branched polysaccharides with molecular weights 10–100 kDa; the sulfation pattern is critical for HIV-1 RT inhibition and viral attachment blockade; analogous to heparin sulfate in charge but with distinct pharmacological profile and without heparin's anticoagulant risk at typical concentrations

**Acidic polysaccharides (APS):** complementary immunomodulatory fraction; activate macrophages, NK cells, complement cascade; overlap with immunostimulant polysaccharides of *Echinacea* spp. and *Astragalus membranaceus*; aqueous extraction required for full polysaccharide recovery

### Phenolic Fraction (Primary Anti-Inflammatory)
**Rosmarinic acid:** up to 6% dry weight in flowering tops; leading phenolic; GABA-T inhibitor; anti-inflammatory via COX/LOX dual inhibition; antiviral against HSV (independent of prunellin mechanism); the 16th consecutive Lamiaceae entry to feature rosmarinic acid as a primary phenolic — this compound is the defining pharmacological signature of the family across entries I-476 through I-491

**Caffeic acid:** hydroxycinnamic acid antioxidant and anti-inflammatory

**Chlorogenic acid:** antioxidant; glycemic regulation

**Hyperoside (quercetin-3-galactoside):** quercetin glycoside; anti-inflammatory; antiviral

**Rutin (quercetin-3-rutinoside):** capillary-protective; anti-inflammatory

**Luteolin:** flavone; NF-κB inhibition; NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition; anti-inflammatory

**Isoquercitrin:** quercetin-3-glucoside; antioxidant

### Triterpenes
**Ursolic acid:** 2–4% dry weight; anti-inflammatory (PLA₂, COX, LOX triple inhibition); anti-proliferative in vitro (relevant to TCM lymphadenopathy/thyroid applications); adaptogenic; recurring Lamiaceae compound (see I-479 Holy Basil, I-480 Rosemary, I-481 Sage, I-490 Catnip)

**Oleanolic acid:** related pentacyclic triterpene; hepatoprotective; anti-inflammatory

**β-Sitosterol:** phytosterol; anti-inflammatory; cholesterol-competing

### Essential Oil (Minor Contribution)
**1,8-Cineole, camphor, β-caryophyllene:** minor EO fraction; less pharmacologically significant than phenolic/polysaccharide fractions in *P. vulgaris*; unlike high-EO Lamiaceae (thyme, oregano, lavender), betony's activity is primarily phenolic and polysaccharide-driven

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Antiviral Mechanisms

**Prunellin (sulfated polysaccharide) → HIV-1 RT inhibition:**
IC₅₀ ≈ 5 μg/mL against HIV-1 reverse transcriptase (Xu et al. 1992, *Antiviral Research* 14(4-5):281-287); mechanism involves direct binding to RT enzyme through sulfate-mediated electrostatic interactions; the sulfated polysaccharide mimics heparan sulfate to which HIV-1 gp120 normally binds for cell attachment, creating a dual mechanism: RT inhibition + competitive blockade of viral-cell attachment

**Rosmarinic acid → HSV virucidal + attachment inhibition:**
Direct virucidal activity against HSV-1 and HSV-2 by binding viral glycoprotein B, preventing heparan sulfate attachment to host cells; the same mechanism documented for lemon balm (I-478, Koytchev 1999 Level A) where the antiviral effect is clinically validated; *P. vulgaris* water extracts show comparable HSV activity at Level B (Tang 2011; Chiang 2003)

**Aqueous extract → broad antiviral (multiple mechanisms):**
Water extracts demonstrate antiviral activity against influenza A/B and adenovirus in vitro; relevant concentrations for systemic in vivo effect remain to be validated; topical application most evidence-supported

### 5.2 Anti-Inflammatory Mechanisms

**Rosmarinic acid → COX/LOX dual inhibition + NF-κB suppression:**
Standard Lamiaceae anti-inflammatory triple mechanism; inhibits prostaglandin synthesis (COX-1/2) and leukotriene synthesis (5-LOX); reduces NF-κB activation; relevant to lymphadenopathy and inflammatory eye conditions matching TCM *xià kū cǎo* applications

**Ursolic acid → PLA₂ inhibition:**
Upstream arachidonic acid cascade inhibition; broader anti-inflammatory footprint than COX inhibition alone; comparable to indomethacin in some in vitro models at equivalent concentrations

**Luteolin → NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition:**
Recent mechanistic research (2018–2023) confirms luteolin's NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitory activity — adds a fourth anti-inflammatory mechanism relevant to chronic inflammatory conditions and pyrexia

### 5.3 Immunomodulatory Mechanisms

**Acidic polysaccharides → macrophage activation + NK cell stimulation + complement:**
APS fraction activates peritoneal macrophages (increased TNF-α, IL-6); stimulates NK cell cytotoxicity; activates classical complement pathway; immunostimulant effect combined with direct antiviral activity suggests dual mechanism of antiviral defense: direct viral inhibition + immune potentiation against intracellular pathogens

### 5.4 Wound Healing Mechanism

**Rosmarinic acid + caffeic acid → collagen synthesis stimulation:**
Hydroxycinnamic acids promote fibroblast proliferation and collagen type I synthesis; relevant to topical wound-healing applications

**Tannin-mediated astringency:** minor tannin content provides tissue-tightening hemostatic effect at wound margins

**Ursolic acid → anti-scar modulation:** ursolic acid reduces excess collagen deposition (anti-fibrotic at lower concentrations) while promoting wound closure — the same compound contributes to both wound healing and prevention of excessive scarring

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### 6.1 Antiviral (HIV-1)

**Evidence Level: D (in vitro; no clinical trials)**

- Xu HX et al. 1992 (*Antiviral Research* 14(4-5):281-287): Prunellin HIV-1 RT inhibition IC₅₀ 5 μg/mL; plaque reduction assay confirmed; landmark study initiating *P. vulgaris* antiviral research program
- Multiple subsequent in vitro studies confirming activity against HIV-1 and HIV-2
- No progression to clinical trials: HIV antiretroviral drug development advanced rapidly post-1992; plant-derived polysaccharides with HIV-RT activity are mechanistically redundant with established agents; clinical development appropriately deprioritized in favor of specific antiretrovirals with superior therapeutic indices

### 6.2 Antiviral (HSV)

**Evidence Level: B (in vitro + limited in vivo)**

- Tang HF et al. 2011 (*Journal of Ethnopharmacology* 133(2):519-524): *P. vulgaris* water extract inhibition of HSV-1 and HSV-2; EC₅₀ 0.022–0.052 mg/mL; selectivity index >10 (wider margin than some synthetic antivirals in vitro)
- Chiang LC et al. 2003 (*Journal of Medical Virology* 69(2):240-245): *P. vulgaris* aqueous extract antiviral against HSV-1; mechanism: virucidal + absorption inhibition
- No RCTs; Lemon Balm (*M. officinalis* I-478, Koytchev 1999) remains the validated Lamiaceae for HSV topical therapy at Level A; self-heal as accessible alternative or adjunct where lemon balm is unavailable

### 6.3 Anti-inflammatory / Immunomodulatory

**Evidence Level: B-C**

- Multiple pre-clinical studies confirming anti-inflammatory activity of rosmarinic acid, ursolic acid, luteolin fractions; no RCTs for specific inflammatory conditions in humans
- TCM clinical evidence for *xià kū cǎo* in lymphadenopathy and eye conditions represents Level C (traditional evidence backed by Chinese Pharmacopoeia monograph)

### 6.4 Wound Healing (Topical)

**Evidence Level: C (traditional evidence; pre-clinical mechanistic support)**

- Long-standing traditional use across virtually every temperate culture; Level B mechanistic support (rosmarinic acid/caffeic acid fibroblast stimulation in vitro)
- Commission E: NOT monographed (insufficient controlled clinical evidence)

### 6.5 Regulatory Status

- **Commission E:** NOT monographed
- **ESCOP:** NOT monographed
- **EMA:** No herbal monograph
- **Chinese Pharmacopoeia 2020:** 夏枯草 (*xià kū cǎo*) — official monograph; thyroid and lymph node swellings, eye conditions, hypertension; daily dose 9–15 g herb or 1–2 g extract

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

### 7.1 "All-Heal" — The Name as Pharmacological History

The common names of *P. vulgaris* constitute an ethnobotanical archive. "Self-heal," "heal-all," "all-heal," "heart of the earth," "carpenter's weed" — each name reflects a different aspect of the plant's traditional profile from a different cultural and occupational lens. "Carpenter's weed" specifically records use for cuts and wounds from woodworking tools; "heart of the earth" reflects the plant's low-growing, mat-forming habit that places it literally at ground level while emphasizing its perceived deep vitality. The multiplicity of names, each in a different semantic register across different European languages, reflects genuine cross-cultural breadth of independent applications.

### 7.2 The Weed as Universal Pharmacist

*P. vulgaris* may be the most accessible medicinal plant in temperate human environments worldwide. In virtually every temperate lawn, garden edge, roadside, or park, *Prunella* is present and identifiable from May through October. The plant's cosmopolitan distribution means that anywhere a person in the temperate world finds themselves — urban or rural, developed or developing country, spring through autumn — they are within minutes of finding self-heal. This accessibility gave the plant a cultural role as "emergency first aid" — the plant you reach for when nothing else is at hand. The pharmacological validity of this role is supported by its wound-healing and anti-inflammatory constituents.

### 7.3 Scrofula, Lymph Nodes, and Thyroid — The TCM Prescience

The Chinese application of *xià kū cǎo* for scrofula (cervical lymphadenopathy from tuberculosis) and goiter is pharmacologically prescient at two levels. First: scrofula involves enlarged, inflamed lymph nodes; goiter involves thyroid hyperplasia. The anti-proliferative and anti-inflammatory properties of ursolic acid and rosmarinic acid — both present in *Prunella* at significant concentrations — provide a mechanistically coherent basis for using the plant in conditions requiring lymph node and thyroid size reduction. Second: the immunomodulatory polysaccharides (activating macrophage and NK cell activity) would directly support clearance of *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, the pathogen causing scrofula, by enhancing innate immune function. The TCM application targets the pathogen (immunostimulation) and the consequence (lymph node anti-inflammatory reduction) simultaneously.

### 7.4 The Summer-Dry Phenology and Traditional Pharmacological Epistemology

The Chinese name 夏枯草 (*xià kū cǎo*, "summer-dry plant") encodes a phenological observation as a pharmacological identifier. *Prunella*'s spike browns and desiccates in midsummer — a visible transformation that the TCM tradition interpreted as the plant having "fulfilled its fire-dispersing purpose" and returned its heat-clearing properties to the earth, leaving behind the dried medicinal structure. This phenomenological interpretation — reading plant behavior as a sign of pharmacological action — is characteristic of classical Chinese medical epistemology and maps, at the mechanistic level, to the plant's genuine ability to clear inflammatory heat in human tissues. Whether the observational leap from "plant dries in summer heat" to "plant treats summer heat conditions" was systematic reasoning or fortunate coincidence, the pharmacological outcome is the same.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Indications (Evidence-Based, Within Safety Parameters)

**Topical Wound Healing and Minor Skin Conditions:**
Fresh herb poultice or strong tea compress; traditional use; Level B mechanistic support; appropriate for cuts, abrasions, minor burns (not major), oral ulcers (as mouthwash/gargle)

**Oral/Pharyngeal Anti-Inflammatory (Gargle/Mouthwash):**
Strong infusion as gargle for mouth ulcers, aphthous stomatitis, sore throat, pharyngitis; rosmarinic acid anti-inflammatory; mild astringent tannins; traditional + Level B mechanistic evidence

**Antiviral Support (Adjunctive — Topical):**
Aqueous extract or strong tea as topical application during HSV outbreaks; Level B in vitro evidence; *Melissa officinalis* (I-478) remains preferred Lamiaceae for clinical HSV evidence at Level A; self-heal as accessible alternative or adjunct where lemon balm is unavailable

**Immune Support (Acute Viral Illness):**
Traditional use; polysaccharide immunomodulant fraction; Level B; appropriate as supportive therapy during acute viral illness; not a replacement for specific antivirals where indicated

### 8.2 Preparations and Dosing

**Herb Tea:**
- 2–4 g dried aerial parts (preferably flowering tops) in 250 mL boiling water; steep 15 minutes covered
- 3 cups daily; up to 4–6 weeks
- Aqueous extraction preferable over high-alcohol tincture for polysaccharide retention

**Topical Poultice:**
- Fresh herb: crush well, apply to wound, cover with clean cloth; change every 4–6 hours
- Strong tea compress: cool to body temperature; apply with clean cloth 15–20 minutes; repeat as needed

**Tincture (Phenolic Fraction):**
- 1:5 in 25% ethanol (lower alcohol preserves polysaccharides); 2–4 mL three times daily
- High-alcohol tinctures (60%+ ethanol) preferentially extract phenolics and largely lose polysaccharide fraction; choose preparation method based on target indication

**Topical Extract (HSV):**
- Water-based cream/gel formulation preferred; apply at prodrome; continue 5–7 days; lemon balm (I-478) preferred if available with demonstrated clinical efficacy

**TCM Preparation:**
- *Xià kū cǎo* decoction: 9–15 g dried spike in 400 mL water; simmer 15 minutes; divide into 3 doses; used for lymphadenopathy, hypertension, eye inflammation

### 8.3 Contraindications and Safety

**CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- **Organ transplant recipients:** Immunostimulant polysaccharide fraction could theoretically interfere with immunosuppressive regimens; prefer specific pharmaceutical immunosuppressants; avoid or monitor closely
- **Active autoimmune disease:** Immunostimulation theoretically could exacerbate autoimmune activity; theoretical concern with limited clinical evidence; use with monitoring if at all

**SAFETY PROFILE:** Excellent; among the safest medicinal plants in Volume I; no documented serious adverse effects from normal use across any culture; young leaves edible as food plant in parts of Asia; two millennia of use without toxicity signals

**PREGNANCY:** No known contraindications; historically used during pregnancy in multiple traditions; reasonable to use as food or herb tea at normal doses; no RCT safety data but no safety signals either

**DRUG INTERACTIONS:**
- Immunosuppressants: theoretical; as above
- No documented significant pharmacokinetic interactions in literature

### 8.4 Energetics

**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi + ◈ Jing (wound healing → constitutional tissue support)
**Thermal Quality:** Cooling (TCM: bitter, cold) — distinctive among Lamiaceae, which are predominantly warming; this cooling quality aligns with the TCM "clearing fire" applications
**Directional Action:** Clearing and descending (liver heat); astringent (wound-healing)
**TCM Classification:** Bitter, pungent; cold; liver and gallbladder channels; clears liver fire, dissipates nodules, clears heat and detoxifies

### 8.5 Quality and Authentication

**Botanical Authentication:** Morphologically distinctive; the dense compact spike with broad bracts subtending whorled flowers is essentially unmistakable in temperate field settings; no commercially significant adulteration risk; fresh field identification straightforward

**Harvest Timing:** Peak rosmarinic acid and polysaccharide content: full flower; harvest when 50–75% of spike in flower; July–August in Northern Hemisphere temperate zones; dried whole spike (*xià kū cǎo* as used in TCM) harvested when browning

**Preparation Method Note:** Aqueous decoction for maximum polysaccharide (antiviral/immunomodulatory); 25% alcohol tincture for combined phenolic + partial polysaccharide; 60%+ alcohol for phenolics only (anti-inflammatory, GABA-modulatory); match preparation to intended mechanism
