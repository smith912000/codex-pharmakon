# Lychnis viscaria — Luchnis agria (Dioscorides 3-115, "Lychnis sylvestris"); "Viscid Campion" on the translators' line

## *Lychnis viscaria, the only binomial on the translators' SUGGESTED line for chapter 3-115, glossed "Viscid Campion"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk names this plant under this name. The entry rests on one chapter of one text, and that chapter describes the plant only by comparison with the chapter before it. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The heading *3-115. LUCHNIS AGRIA* is at line 7564, the SUGGESTED line at line 7566 ("Lychnis viscaria — Viscid Campion") and the chapter text at line 7567. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 521. The chapter stands in Book Three, after 3-114 *LUCHNIS STEPHANOMATIKE* (line 7558, the garden lychnis, entry I_1123) and before 3-116 *KRINON BASILIKON* (line 7569, a lily). The translators' plate "Lychnis viscaria after FAGUET — 1888" is printed at line 7573, after the lily chapter's heading and SUGGESTED line.

The whole chapter reads: "Lychnis sylvestris is similar to the culivated in all things. Two teaspoons of a decoction of the seed (taken as a drink) expels bilious matter through the intestines and helps those touched by scorpions. They say that when this herb is laid near scorpions they become numb and unable to hurt. It is also called tragonoton, atocion, hieracopodion, or lampas, the Egyptians call it semura, the Magi call it genitals of a menstrual woman, the Romans, intybus agrestis, some, lapathum, or caphaguina, and others, seris." The word "culivated" is printed so in the translation, and the sense is "cultivated".

So the chapter places the plant by comparison ("similar to the cultivated in all things", which sends the reader to 3-114), names it *Lychnis sylvestris* (the wild lychnis), and gives three claims. A seed decoction drunk "expels bilious matter through the intestines". It "helps those touched by scorpions". And, on report, "They say that when this herb is laid near scorpions they become numb and unable to hurt". The last is a report with "they say" and the text does not vouch for it. The text lists many other names: *tragonoton*, *atocion*, *hieracopodion* and *lampas*; the Egyptian name *semura*; the Magi name "genitals of a menstrual woman"; the Roman name *intybus agrestis*; and *lapathum*, *caphaguina* and *seris* given by "some" and "others". The translators' index lists *Lychnis viscaria* at page 499 (lines 11759 and 16398) and *lychnis agrestis* at page 680.

**The Magi name.** As at 3-114, the text records a Magi name and does not say that Dioscorides endorses it. The translators' glossary (line 3509) says that in Dioscorides' time the name *Magi* was used for those claiming occult powers of Babylonian or Oriental origin. The Codex draws no conclusion from the name.

**One name in several chapters.** The names *lychnis sylvestris* and *lychnis agrestis* are not confined to this chapter. 3-110 *LEONTOPETALON* (line 7529) lists *lychnis sylvestris* among its other names, and 4-133 *ANTIRRINON* (line 9201) says its flowers gave it the name *sylvestris lychnis* and that some "have called it lychnis agrestis". Those are different chapters and different plants. Their uses are not attributed to this one. This is one reason the identity can only be a suggestion. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** The chapter is not the subject of any existing entry. 3-115 is cited in I_901 (Small-flowered Catchfly, *Silene gallica*) as part of "the nearest Dioscorides text" to that genus, and I_901 expressly does not attribute the chapter to *Silene gallica*. I_901 quotes the "Two teaspoons" sentence and says it is not a dose for the catchfly. The pair 3-114 and 3-115 are both listed in the cross-references of 3-9 (line 6607). The garden lychnis of 3-114 is I_1123. 3-110 *Leontopetalon* has the entry I_1117 (*Leontice leontopetalum*). None is merged.

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. As a general fact, the campion genus belongs to the pink family (Caryophyllaceae), and many members of that family contain saponins, which irritate the gut. That is a statement about the family and not a finding about *Lychnis viscaria*. Nothing is stated here about its composition. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a seed decoction as a purge of "bilious matter" and a remedy for scorpion touches, and a report that the herb numbs scorpions. These are Dioscorides' claims, and the third is reported by him as hearsay. The Codex gives no protocol. A purge is not a health practice, and a scorpion sting needs medical assessment.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation with a quantity: "Two teaspoons of a decoction of the seed (taken as a drink)", for expelling bilious matter. The translation prints "teaspoons", and the text read does not define the measure or the volume of water. The chapter says nothing of the frequency, and gives no quantity of seed. This is a quantity as Dioscorides gives it, from a translation, and not a modern dose. No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that a purge (an agent that moves the bowels) does not treat a scorpion sting. The identity is only a suggestion, so a modern study of one campion would not bear directly on the Dioscorides drug.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 3-115 prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. The only quality is the comparison "similar to the cultivated in all things".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The report that the herb numbs scorpions when it is laid near them is introduced with "They say", and the Magi name is recorded as a name. Recording is not endorsing, and neither supports a cultivation claim. What the chapter offers toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is its method of identification by comparison with a known plant. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a wild form identified by comparison, with a purge, a scorpion remedy and a report, so the honest answer is that there is nothing here to take up.

### Contraindications

Chapter 3-115 prints no warning, but the only drink it records is a purge that "expels bilious matter through the intestines", and a purge can dehydrate. Scorpion stings need medical care and must not be left to a seed decoction. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, and the chapter's quantity ("Two teaspoons") is of a decoction whose strength it does not state, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
