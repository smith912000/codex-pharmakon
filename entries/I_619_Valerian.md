## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted Name:** *Valeriana officinalis* L. (1753)
**Authority:** Linnaeus, *Species Plantarum*
**Family:** Caprifoliaceae (following APG IV 2016; previously Valerianaceae)
**Common Names:** Valerian, Garden Valerian, Common Valerian, All-Heal, Setwall, Valérien (Fr.), Baldrian (Ger.), Valeriana (It./Sp./Port.), Baldriana (Port.), Хмélь (Khmel, Rus.)

**Related Species — *Valeriana* genus (~250 species):**
- *Valeriana wallichii* DC. — Indian Valerian (*Tagara*; Ayurvedic); closely related; similar chemistry
- *Valeriana sitchensis* Bong. — Sitka Valerian (North American)
- *Valeriana edulis* Nutt. ex Torr. & A.Gray — Edible Valerian; starchy root food
- *Valeriana celtica* L. — Celtic Nard; aromatic European alpine species
- *Nardostachys jatamansi* DC. — Spikenard/Jatamansi (formerly *Valeriana jatamansi*; now separate genus): major Ayurvedic, biblical (*nard* = spikenard); distinctly different chemistry and tradition

**Botanical Description:** Perennial 50–200 cm; rhizome and roots with characteristic unpleasant odor (isovaleric acid from valerenic acid oxidation); leaves opposite, pinnately divided, leaflets 7–10 pairs; stem hollow; flowers in terminal cymose corymbs, small, white to pale pink, bisexual; fruit achene with feathery pappus. The fresh root has minimal odor; characteristic smell develops upon drying as esters (isovalerate esters) hydrolyze.

**Key Chemical Markers:** Iridoid esters (valerenic acid = sesquiterpene; isovaleric acid; valerenol; valeranone); valepotriates (iridoid triesters — baldrinal, valtrate, isovaltrate; UNSTABLE); flavonoids (linarin, 6-methylapigenin, hesperidin); free amino acids (GABA, glutamine); phenylpropanoids (caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid); alkaloids (actinidine, valeranine, alpha-methylpyrryl ketone; trace).

**Pharmacopoeial Status:** European Pharmacopoeia 10th ed. (*Valerianae radix*; minimum 0.17% valerenic acid; minimum 0.5% sesquiterpenic acids); Commission E approved; ESCOP monograph; WHO monograph; USP (dietary supplement monograph); included in pharmacopoeias of Germany, France, UK, Switzerland, and others.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

### 4.1 Sesquiterpenic Acids — Valerenic Acid Group

**Valerenic acid** (C₁₅H₂₂O₂):
- Content: 0.17–0.8% DW dried root; EP minimum 0.17%
- Structure: Bicyclic sesquiterpenic acid; stable in dried root; pharmacopoeial marker
- **PRIMARY BIOACTIVE COMPOUND** — all three CNS mechanisms centered on valerenic acid (see Section 5)

**Valerenol:** Reduction product of valerenic acid; similar activity
**Valeranone:** Ketone; contributes to characteristic aroma
**Acetoxyvalerenic acid:** Ester; similar activity to valerenic acid

### 4.2 Valepotriates (Iridoid Triesters) — Complex History

- **Valtrate, isovaltrate, baldrinal** (didrovaltrate): Bicyclic iridoid triesters with epoxide moiety
- Former belief: valepotriates were primary active compounds (dominant 1960s–1980s)
- **Problem:** Valepotriates are unstable; rapidly hydrolyze at room temperature, in acidic/basic conditions, and in GI tract → **absent from dried root preparations**, tinctures, and dried extracts
- **Current status:** Valepotriates may have alkylating activity (cytotoxic); complete absence from conventional preparations resolves cytotoxicity concern; no clinically relevant contribution to anxiolytic/sedative effect
- **Historical lesson:** A decade of valepotriate research was pharmacologically irrelevant to marketed products because the compounds were absent in the finished products studied

### 4.3 Flavonoids

- **Linarin** (acacetin-7-glucoside): Sedative in mice; 6-methylapigenin synergy with valerenic acid (Fernandez et al. 2004)
- **6-Methylapigenin:** Positive allosteric modulation at GABA-A BZD site (same mechanism as chamomile apigenin I-602, passionflower chrysin I-618); 6-methyl group increases GABA-A affinity vs. apigenin
- **Hesperidin:** Anti-inflammatory; mild anxiolytic

### 4.4 Free GABA and Glutamine

- Valerian root contains measurable free GABA (0.1–0.3 mg/g root)
- As with passionflower GABA, systemic CNS effect from oral GABA is debated (BBB impermeability)
- Glutamine → endogenous GABA synthesis precursor; may support GABAergic function indirectly

### 4.5 Essential Oil (0.2–0.8% DW)

- Bornyl acetate, beta-caryophyllene, valerenal, isovaleric acid (decomposition product)
- Characteristic unpleasant smell from isovaleric acid (rancid/sweaty); paradoxically attracts cats (similar to catnip, *Nepeta cataria* nepetalactone)
- Pharmacological role of EO in overall CNS effects: minimal

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 GABA-A Positive Allosteric Modulation (Valerenic Acid)

**Primary mechanism:** Valerenic acid → GABA-A receptor (allosteric site distinct from BZD site; independent of BZD binding):
- Valerenic acid interacts with **α1/β2/β3 subunit interface** (distinct from benzodiazepine α/γ site)
- Positive allosteric modulation → enhanced Cl⁻ conductance → neuronal hyperpolarization
- Müller et al. 2002 (*Mol Pharmacol*): Valerenic acid and valerenol bind β2/β3 subunit-containing GABA-A receptors; mutation of β2 Asn265 abolishes activity → specific binding site demonstrated

**Binding site novelty:** Valerenic acid's binding at the β2/β3 interface (rather than the α/γ BZD site used by benzodiazepines, chamomile apigenin, passionflower chrysin) represents a pharmacologically distinct GABA-A modulation mechanism. This means:
- No cross-tolerance with benzodiazepines
- Different subunit selectivity profile
- Additive (not redundant) with BZD-site ligands

### 5.2 GABA-Transaminase Inhibition

**GABA-T mechanism:** Valerenic acid inhibits **GABA-transaminase** (GABA-T; the enzyme catabolizing GABA → succinic semialdehyde):
- GABA-T inhibition → reduced GABA catabolism → increased synaptic GABA availability
- Same mechanism as the antiepileptic drug vigabatrin (Sabril®) — which is an IRREVERSIBLE GABA-T inhibitor
- Valerenic acid inhibition is REVERSIBLE and weak (IC₅₀ ~4 µM) vs. vigabatrin (irreversible)
- Consequence: valerian simultaneously enhances GABA receptor sensitivity AND increases GABA availability — dual GABAergic action

**Anti-convulsant rationale:** GABA-T inhibition → increased inhibitory GABA tone → reduced seizure likelihood. Explains cross-traditional use for epilepsy.

### 5.3 Adenosine System

- Valerenic acid: adenosine reuptake inhibitor (in vitro) → increased extracellular adenosine → A1 receptor (sleep-promoting) and A2A receptor modulation
- Synergy with GABA-A effects → enhanced sedative profile

### 5.4 5-HT₅A Serotonin Receptor

- Valerenic acid: partial agonist at 5-HT₅A receptor (in vitro)
- 5-HT₅A: G-protein coupled; expressed in hippocampus, cerebellum; function incompletely understood; may contribute to sleep-promoting effects
- Less well-established than GABA-A mechanism

### 5.5 Serotonin Reuptake Inhibition (Minor)

- Linarin: weak SERT inhibition; minor anxiolytic contribution
- At clinical doses: serotonin interaction insufficient for antidepressant effect; complement to primary GABAergic mechanism

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### 6.1 Sleep / Insomnia — Level B

**Bent et al. 2006** (Systematic review; *Am J Med*): 16 RCTs; significant heterogeneity in preparations, doses, outcomes; overall positive trend for sleep quality improvement; conclusions limited by quality issues.

**Leathwood et al. 1982** (*Pharmacol Biochem Behav*): RCT (n=128); aqueous valerian extract 400mg vs. placebo; significant improvement in sleep quality (p<0.05); especially in habitual poor sleepers and smokers.

**Donath et al. 2000** (*Pharmacol Biochem Behav*): Polysomnographic RCT (n=16 insomniacs); 400mg valerian root extract; reduced subjective sleep latency; no significant change in objective polysomnography markers (REM, SWS percentages). Discrepancy between subjective and objective suggests anxiolytic component.

**Overall:** Level B for subjective sleep quality improvement; evidence for objective sleep parameter change inconsistent. Likely mechanism is anxiolysis/reduced sleep onset anxiety rather than direct hypnotic (unlike benzodiazepines which alter sleep architecture).

### 6.2 Anxiety — Level B

**Andreatini et al. 2002** (*Phytother Res*): RCT (n=36); valepotriate-containing extract vs. diazepam vs. placebo for GAD; psychic anxiety (HAMA-A psychic subscale) improved vs. placebo (not significantly different from diazepam). Level B.

**Kennedy et al. 2006** (*Hum Psychopharmacol*): Valerian alone and valerian/hops combination; significant anxiolytic effect in healthy volunteers using STAI (p=0.035); Level B.

### 6.3 Menstrual Pain (Dysmenorrhea) — Level B

**Mirabi et al. 2011** (*J Reprod Med*): RCT (n=100); valerian 255mg 3×/day for 3 days during menstruation vs. placebo; significant pain reduction (VAS -34% vs. -4%; p<0.001). Level B. Mechanism: antispasmodic on uterine smooth muscle (GABA-A smooth muscle receptors + adenosine mechanism).

### 6.4 Menopausal Hot Flashes — Level B

**Mirabi et al. 2012** (*Evid Based Complement Alternat Med*): RCT (n=68 menopausal women); valerian 255mg 3×/day for 8 weeks; hot flash frequency significantly reduced vs. placebo (p<0.001). Level B.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

### 7.1 Classical Descriptions

**Dioscorides** (*De Materia Medica* I.10, ca. 70 CE): *"Phū — the roots warm and are diuretic and beneficial to the menstrual flow; for epilepsy they are dried and given in wine."* The epilepsy indication — one of the most cross-traditionally consistent for valerian — appears in Dioscorides' first-century record.

**Galen** (129–216 CE): *"Phū strengthens the head; it is useful for palpitations of the heart when taken as a drink."* The cardiac palpitation application suggests recognition of the anxiolytic effect before the concept of anxiety was clinically differentiated.

**Ibn Sīnā** (*Al-Qanun*, Book II): *"Sumbul — it is sedative to the brain and nerves; it is useful in palsy, convulsions, epilepsy, and swimmings of the head; the root is given in wine for nervous affections."* Most comprehensive classical description; specificity for neurological use unambiguous.

**Hildegard von Bingen** (*Physica* ca. 1150–1160): *"Baldrian — is warm and dry; it promotes good sleep and rids a person of harmful fluids."* The sleep promotion indication appears explicitly in Hildegard, making this one of the earliest European sleep medicine prescriptions.

### 7.2 The "All-Heal" Legacy

**Medieval European name "All-Heal"** (Allheal, Setwall): Reflects the extraordinarily broad traditional application of valerian for conditions ranging from epilepsy, hysteria, heart conditions, menstrual disorders, and sleep disorders. The name suggests valerian occupied a position in medieval European folk medicine analogous to Ayurvedic *Rasayana* herbs — a general tonic for the nervous system.

**Catnip parallel:** The unpleasant human-perceived odor of dried valerian root (isovaleric acid) is highly attractive to cats — documented since Dioscorides. Cats respond to valerian with behaviors identical to catnip (*Nepeta cataria* nepetalactone response): rolling, rubbing, euphoria. Both nepetalactone (catnip) and valerian-derived isovaleric acid appear to mimic cat pheromone signals. Medieval cat-trapping used valerian as bait.

**Pied Piper Connection (disputed):** Some historians argue the Pied Piper of Hamelin (1284 CE legend) used valerian (rat attractant property — rats also attracted to valerian) in addition to music to lead the rats/children away. This folk attribution — whether true or not — reflects valerian's reputation as an animal attractant dating to medieval period.

### 7.3 20th-Century WWII Sedative Use

**World War II civilian use:** During German bombing raids on London (the Blitz, 1940–1941), valerian was distributed by the British government to civilian populations to treat "nervous excitability, anxiety, and sleeplessness caused by air raids." This represents one of the most extensive documented public health deployments of an herbal anxiolytic in modern history — and predates the first benzodiazepine (chlordiazepoxide, Librium) by 20 years.

**German military medical use:** Valerian preparations also used in German military hospitals for psychological stress during WWI and WWII — both warring sides independently deploying valerian for the same anxiety indication.

### 7.4 Spikenard and Biblical References

**Nardostachys jatamansi** (formerly *Valeriana jatamansi*) — Spikenard, related genus:
- Biblical *nard* (*nardos*): used to anoint Jesus at Bethany (Mark 14:3; John 12:3); "the whole house was filled with the fragrance of the ointment"
- Extensively used in Ayurveda (hair, brain tonic, sedative), Islamic medicine, Egyptian perfumery
- Dioscorides *De Materia Medica* I.7: *Nardos Indike* — distinguished from Valeriana *Phū* but clearly related
- The anointing scene implies it was the most costly aromatic in 1st-century CE Palestine; price "three hundred denarii" (a year's wage)
This historical crossroads of medicine, religion, and trade establishes the *Valeriana/Nardostachys* genus complex at the center of ancient pharmacopoeia and religious ritual simultaneously.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Therapeutic Indications

**Commission E approved:**
- Restlessness and sleeping disorders based on nervous conditions

**Level B evidence:**
- Insomnia (subjective sleep quality)
- Anxiety / generalized anxiety (adjunct)
- Dysmenorrhea (antispasmodic)
- Menopausal hot flashes (adjunct)

**Traditional (Level C):**
- Epilepsy adjunct (never monotherapy; adjunct to medical management)
- Cardiac palpitations (anxiety-related)

### 8.2 Dosing

| Form | Dose | Indication |
|------|------|------------|
| Dried root infusion | 2–3g in 150mL water, 30–60 min before bed | Insomnia |
| Aqueous-ethanolic extract (standardized 0.17% valerenic acid) | 300–600mg, 1h before bed | Insomnia (Level B dose) |
| Tincture (1:5, 60% ethanol) | 4–6 mL 30 min before bed | Sedative |
| Commission E dose (sleep) | 400–900mg root extract 1h before bed | Insomnia |
| Anxiety | 120–200mg extract 3×/day | Daytime anxiolytic |

**Onset:** Unlike benzodiazepines (minutes), valerian has 2–4 week onset for consistent sleep benefit in some studies; single-dose use may produce less reliable effect.

### 8.3 Safety and Contraindications

**Safety profile:** Among the safest CNS-active herbal preparations; no dependence/withdrawal documented; no next-day sedation at standard doses; no significant adverse effects in RCTs.

**Contraindications:**
- **Pregnancy:** Valerenic acid uterotonic potential at high doses (confirmed antispasmodic on uterine smooth muscle — beneficial for dysmenorrhea but risk in pregnancy); avoid in first trimester; traditional contraindication
- **Severe hepatic impairment:** Metabolic considerations; valepotriates (absent from most commercial preparations) theoretically hepatotoxic; standardized preparations without valepotriates considered safe
- **Children <3:** Insufficient safety data

**Drug interactions:**
- **CNS depressants (benzodiazepines, alcohol, opioids):** Additive; reduce dose of either; monitor
- **Pentobarbital:** Enhanced sedation (animal data); avoid
- **No CYP450 interactions** of clinical significance at standard doses (contrast with St. John's Wort I-617 — significant CYP3A4 inducer)
- **Benzodiazepine withdrawal:** Valerian sometimes used to facilitate benzodiazepine discontinuation — some observational support; not proven; requires medical supervision

### 8.4 Valerian vs. Other Herbal Sedatives — Mechanistic Comparison

| Compound | Binding site | Subunit | Notes |
|---------|-------------|---------|-------|
| Valerenic acid | β2/β3 interface | β-subunit | Distinct from BZD site; anti-convulsant |
| Valerenic acid | GABA-T inhibition | — | Increases GABA availability |
| Chamomile apigenin | α/γ BZD site | α-subunit | Same site as diazepam |
| Passionflower chrysin | α/γ BZD site | α-subunit | Same site as diazepam |
| SJW biapigenin | α/γ BZD site | α-subunit | Same site as diazepam |
| Diazepam (reference) | α/γ BZD site | α1/2/3/5 | Full agonist at BZD site |
| Alcohol | Allosteric (multiple) | β-subunit | TM2 domain; different from valerenic acid |

**Key insight:** Valerian (valerenic acid at β2/β3) and chamomile/passionflower (flavones at α/γ BZD site) act at different GABA-A binding sites — additive, not redundant. This mechanistic complementarity explains why valerian + passionflower or valerian + chamomile combinations are pharmacologically rational.
