# French Rose — rhodon, common rose (Dioscorides 1-130), oil of roses (rhodinon, 1-53), pomanders of roses (rhodides, 1-131)

## *Rosa gallica* (spine botanical; the translator's gloss for Dioscorides 1-53, 1-130 and 1-131 reads "Rosa hortensis et sylvestris [Fuchs], Rosa rubra [Bauhin], Rosa gallica [Linnaeus] — Common Rose, French Rose") | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Identity warning.** In Dioscorides the "rose" is a translator's identification from several Latin names, and *Rosa gallica* is only the Linnaean one of the three. The same three chapters are the basis of a separate, finished rose entry (I_349, *Rosa damascena*, CPU-00291), so the two rows share loci. No Egyptian or Assyrian rose locus was found, and the Avicenna row is for another species. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Three chapters, all in Book I.

*1-130 RHODON* (text line 4483): "Rodon [roses] cool and are astringent, and dried roses are more astringent." The juice is pressed "whilst they are still young, first cutting off that which is called the nail (which is the white that is in the petal)". The leaves are dried in the shade and turned "now and then least otherwise they putrefy or grow mouldy". Dried roses boiled in wine and strained "are good for headaches, as well as the eyes, ears and gums, and pain of the perineum, intestine, rectum and vulva". Bruised and boiled, they serve for "inflammation of the area below the ribs, moistness of the stomach and erysipela". They go into "compositions called antherae" and "wound antidotes", and are burnt "for medicines to make the eyelids look pleasing". The middle of the flower, dried and sprinkled on, "is good for gum discharges". "The heads [hips] (taken in a drink) stop loose intestines and blood-spitting."

*1-53 RHODINON* (line 3964): the oil of roses, called rosaceum in the translator's cross-references, is a long recipe. Dioscorides takes "five pounds eight ounces of juncus odoratus and twenty pounds five ounces of oil" and steeps "a thousand counted dry rose petals" in it, with hands rubbed in honey. The first pressing may be re-used on fresh petals up to seven times. He adds that "some use the roses alone... half a pound of petals to one pint of oil", left in the sun forty days. The oil "is astringent and cooling, good for cleaning and mixing with poultices", loosens the bowels when drunk and cools "a heated stomach", and is rubbed on for "penetrative ulcers, catarrh in the head, and heated eruptions".

*1-131 RHODIDES* (line 4490): pomanders are made from "forty teaspoonfuls of fresh roses (which are beginning to fade)", "ten teaspoonfuls of Indian nard and six teaspoonfuls of myrrh", pounded and rolled into balls "the size of half a teaspoonful". They are worn "around women's necks instead of necklaces, dulling the unsavoury smell of sweat".

### Active Components

Rose petals are known in general for a fragrant volatile oil and for tannins, which fits Dioscorides' "astringent". Hips are the fruits. The proportions in *R. gallica* were not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record plus a plainly benign garden and kitchen plant. The Dioscorides uses (a cooling astringent wash, a scented oil, a body pomander) are recorded as his. The Codex recommends nothing here beyond ordinary, informed use of rose petals and hips as food and fragrance.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian — Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No Egyptian locus for any rose was found. The corpus is organised by complaint, and there is no *heka* recitation or placing against the person's *ka*, *ib* or *akh* to preserve.

**Assyrian (AT).** *(c) Absent.* No attestation was found, and none is asserted. The blocking warning on Assyrian identifications applies; no tablet number or deity is given.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent* as to cultivation status. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. What the three chapters offer a cultivator is a discipline of attention: pick the young flower, cut away the white "nail", dry in the shade and turn the petals, count them, choose the jars, and keep the dregs from the oil. That is provenance, preparation and assay, and not a vital-body doctrine.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent.* The tag rests on a list row for *Rosa damascena*. Nothing read assigns the French rose a place in the Unani frame of innate heat and radical moisture.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator may grow the rose and follow Dioscorides' care in picking and drying, but should not read a longevity or vital-endowment claim into any of it. Dioscorides gives cooling, astringency and fragrance, nothing more. Two of the four tags are unverified, and the Avicenna row belongs to a different rose.

### Contraindications

Rose petals and hips are ordinary foods, and the main cautions are those of any plant material: allergy, thorns and pesticide residues on bought or garden flowers. The traditional eye, ear and ulcer washes are not advice. Do not put homemade infusions or oils in the eye or ear. Seek ordinary medical care for infection.
