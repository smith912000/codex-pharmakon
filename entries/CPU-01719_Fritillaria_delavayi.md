# Fritillaria delavayi — a.bi.sha, a.bi.ka, Abikha, "non-poison" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Fritillaria delavayi Franch., the first of four binomials Clark 1995 prints for a.bi.sha or a.bi.ka (then Fritillaria cirrhosa, Lilium nepalense and Uraria crinita); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints Fritillaria delavayi in the position that column order gives to "Abikha"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk (including the three Bhishagratna volumes) for the genus name *Fritillaria* found no species name. Yang 1998 prints "Bei Mu (Bulbus Fritillariae)" at line 2666, which gives the genus only, so it is not attributed to this species. The Codex's own lists carry Fritillaria roylei as Kakoli (`_sources/sushruta.csv`, line 96) and Fritillaria cirrhosa as Fritillary Bulb (`_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv`, line 74); neither is a text locus for *F. delavayi*. Clark's preface (line 83) says that more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance, and here the line prints four, of which the spine row takes the first. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1016).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). It reads:

"a.bi.sha or a.bi.ka (lit. ‘non-poison’): Fritillaria delavayi Franch.: Fritillaria cirrhosa: Lilium nepalense: Uraria crinita: [has six names. It has a brown hanging flower, brown trunk, green petiole and six-sided pod containing flat seeds. Fritillaria has sweet taste, cool power and] heals skull fractures and fever caused by poisoning."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the brackets (commentary layer) are the six names, the flower, trunk, petiole and pod, and the statement that "Fritillaria has sweet taste, cool power". Outside them (root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the gloss "non-poison", the four Latin names, which are Clark's identifications, and the sentence "heals skull fractures and fever caused by poisoning". The commentary describes a "brown trunk" and a "six-sided pod" and also names the genus *Fritillaria* itself; it does not say which of the four Latin names the description fits. The Codex records both and does not reconcile them. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* Line 3432 reads: "Abikha joins fractured skulls and hot disorder associated with poisoning." The index lists "Abikha 207" (line 7257). The action agrees in kind with Clark's "heals skull fractures and fever caused by poisoning".

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary.* The first block ("A", after the headings Phonetic, Transliteration and Scientific Name at lines 5051 to 5055) lists eight phonetic names: "Abikha Adre" (line 5059), "Agaru Ajak" (5060), "Arura" (5061), "Asho genda Atong" (5062) and "Awa" (5063). The transliterations begin "a bi Sha a’bras" (line 5066). The scientific names begin at line 5073: "Fritillaria delavayi Mangifera indica Aquilaria agallocha Pyrethrum tatsienense", then "Terminalia chebula", "Mirabilis himalaica", "Tanacetum mutellinum" and "Lloydia serotina" (lines 5074 to 5077), eight in all. The first scientific name stands against the first phonetic name, "Abikha", and the order of the others (third, *Aquilaria agallocha*, against "Agaru"; fifth, *Terminalia chebula*, against "Arura") is consistent with that pairing. The pairing is by position and was not printed in one row. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** I_1120 (*Lloydia serotina*) is the entry for the last scientific name of this same glossary block. I_309 (Fritillary Bulb, *Fritillaria cirrhosa*), I_747 (Zhe Bei Mu, *Fritillaria thunbergii*), I_417 (Kakoli, *Fritillaria roylei*) and I_888 (Crown Imperial) are entries on other species. The spine has a sibling row for *Fritillaria verticillata* (CPU-01720), written in the same batch from Clark line 989. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Fritillaria* is a genus of bulbous plants of the lily family (Liliaceae) whose bulbs are known to contain steroidal alkaloids, that *Lilium* is another lily-family genus, and that *Uraria* is a genus of the legume family, so the four names Clark prints point to plants of different families. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained or which part was used.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "heals skull fractures and fever caused by poisoning" (Clark, line 1016) and "joins fractured skulls and hot disorder associated with poisoning" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3432). Its Tibetan name is glossed "non-poison". These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial through Clark's first of four names and the glossary's column position, and they are not Codex protocols. Poisoning and skull fracture are emergencies and the Codex gives no advice for them.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] Clark's commentary describes flower, trunk, petiole and pod and does not say which part is taken.

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Tibetan terms ("skull fractures", "fever caused by poisoning") are not modern diagnoses or measured outcomes. The identity is open between four genera, and the name "non-poison" is a drug name that is not evidence about safety. It is a general fact that some plants of the lily family are toxic.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 1016) prints for the genus "sweet taste, cool power". No post-digestive taste is printed. The name does not appear in the Men-Tsee-Khang taste-group lists read for this entry (they begin at line 3204). The one action printed is the healing of skull fractures and of fever caused by poisoning (Clark), or the joining of fractured skulls and hot disorder associated with poisoning (Men-Tsee-Khang).

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it heals skull fractures and fever caused by poisoning, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same in kind. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the sentences cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and the link from this binomial to that framing rests on Clark's first choice among four.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a drug for skull fracture and poisoning fever in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and its Latin name is one of four that Clark offers. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug; its name, "non-poison", is not a safety statement. Poisoning and skull fracture need emergency medical care, the identity is open between plants of different genera, and no preparation or dose is given, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
