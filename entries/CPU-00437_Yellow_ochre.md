# Yellow ochre — no printed name in Bryan's Ebers Papyrus; the spine row's label, not a reading of the text
## *A yellow iron-bearing earth; the spine row reads "Yellow ochre, Fe2O3·H2O (goethite/limonite)" and cites Nunn 1996 and a Wikipedia page, and Bryan's English text of the Ebers Papyrus on disk contains no word "yellow", "ochre" in this sense, "goethite" or "limonite" among its drugs* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian Tradition (EP).** The spine row names this substance as "Yellow ochre", with the note "Ebers; Nunn 1996; Wikipedia/Ebers Papyrus" (`_sources/ebers.csv` line 52). The row's chemical formula is the list-maker's and is not given by Bryan. The sources on disk were searched for a locus, and none was found.

**What the search covered.** The Bryan 1930 English text (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`) was searched for "yellow", "ochre" and "ocher", and for "goethite", "limonite", "orpiment" and "gold-coloured". The word "yellow" does not occur in the text. "Ochre" occurs once, at line 1229, in Bryan's note on the drug *didi*, where it means red ochre or hematite (see the entry *Red ochre*); that sentence does not mention a yellow kind. Bryan's alphabetical list of mineral drugs (Chapter V, from line 1138; the 40 mineral rows at `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` lines 2-41) contains no yellow earth. The name-index file has no ochre row of any colour. Bryan himself says (line 1233, at the head of Chapter VI) that the foregoing "by no means constitute a full list of the Mineral substances employed in the pharmacopoeia", for there are "many others whose identity is completely hidden from us under the guise of meaningless words". It is therefore possible that the papyrus prescribes a yellow earth under a name Bryan does not render as such, but the sources on disk do not say so. [USER: verify]

**The Codex project files that name it, and why they are not used.** Two Codex project files under `Sources/EP_Ebers/` do name "yellow ochre". `EP_Original_Hieroglyphic.md` line 415 prints, under the label "Ebbell Translation" and the heading "Prescription 351", "To drive out pain in the teeth: fresh dates; malachite; honey; yellow ochre. Make into a paste. Place in the opening of the tooth."; and lines 841-842 list "Yellow ochre", *kAj*, "Hydrated iron oxide (FeOOH)". These files do not give an edition, page or column for the quoted lines, their prescription numbers cannot be checked against the Bryan text on disk (which has no prescription numbers), the same files print a hair-fat remedy that differs from Bryan's (the file at line 371 lists an ibex and a "wild goat", Bryan at lines 4023-4029 lists lion, hippopotamus, crocodile, cat, serpent and Egyptian goat), and they attach modern research to the translations. The Ebbell 1937 translation itself is not on disk. They are therefore treated as unverified secondary notes and not as a source for a yellow ochre in the papyrus. [USER: verify]

**What is not said.** Nothing in the sources read says that any yellow earth was used in the Egyptian medical tradition recorded in Bryan. This entry records that gap and does not fill it. The spine row stays in the list because its stated source (Nunn 1996) was not opened and may carry a locus; the entry is a placeholder until that is read. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None can be stated from the papyrus. In general terms, yellow ochre is a natural earth coloured yellow by hydrated iron oxide-hydroxide (the mineral goethite or related iron oxyhydroxides), mixed with clay and silica; it is insoluble and is used as a pigment. This is a general statement about the mineral, not a reading of Bryan.

### Contraindications

- **Unspecified earth:** natural earths can carry heavy metals and other contaminants; nothing here defines a safe material.
- **Dental or oral use:** an unverified project note prints a paste with yellow ochre and malachite (a copper carbonate) for a tooth; copper compounds are toxic if swallowed, and the Codex does not recommend placing any mineral paste in a tooth.
