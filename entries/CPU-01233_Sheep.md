# Sheep — "Ram" (Bryan 1930's English rendering of the Ebers animal list)
## *The ram, the male sheep; Bryan prints only the English word "Ram", with no Egyptian name and no binomial* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The Ebers material reaches this Codex through Cyril P. Bryan's 1930 English version, *The Papyrus Ebers* (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`). In his Chapter VII, "Organic Remedies", Bryan says that a complete list of the animal materia medica "would not be possible", because the identity of many of the animals is "still beyond us", and he then gives the animals "whose identity is not in doubt" (lines 1382-1390). The list is printed in alphabetical groups, and the line that carries the sheep reads "Goose Duck Stag Gazelle Ram Goat" (line 1398). The word is "Ram" and nothing else: the extract file records it as "Bryan's English identification; no Egyptian word or binomial given in this list" (`Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv`, row for "Ram", locus line 1398). The ewe, the lamb and the wool are not named in the list or anywhere else in the text read.

Bryan also prints a second list, of the parts that the animals "were forced to yield": "Hair Feathers Horns Hoof Hide Eyes ... Fat Tallow Flesh ..." and so on (lines 1408-1415). That list is not sorted by animal, so it does not say which parts of the ram were used. For that, the text has to be searched under the name, and a search of the whole file for "Ram" finds three places: the list (line 1398), a prescription that includes "Ram's Hair" (line 2318), and a poultice that includes "Fat-of-the-Ram" (line 3151).

The first is a burn remedy. Bryan introduces it by saying that some burns were "beyond the ingenuity of mortal man", and that the physicians "were forced to invoke the aid of the gods to reinforce the therapeutic effect of the remedies" (line 2287). He then prints an "Incantation for a Burn" ("O son, Horus! There is Fire in the Land! Water is not there and thou art not there! ... to quench the Fire") and an "Another Incantation for a Burn", the latter followed by the lines "To be spoken over Milk-of-a-Woman-who-has-Borne-a-Son. / Cake / Ram's Hair / Apply to the Burn." (lines 2289-2320). A page break (the running head "72 THE PAPYRUS EBERS") falls between the two incantations, and the ingredient lines follow the second; that the cake and the hair belong to the second incantation rests on Bryan's printed layout and on the OCR of it. [USER: verify]

The second is a remedy for the *met*. Bryan says the word has "exercised the ingenuity of every Egyptologist": here it "undoubtedly refers to the nerves of the body", there to the blood-vessels. "If it were a mere matter of refreshing the met, a poultice of Fat-of-the-Ox, Fat-of-the-Ass, Fat-of-the-Ram, Fruit-of-the-Dompalm, Xehua-grain, Poppy-plant, and Sea-salt, served the purpose" (lines 3149-3151). The ram's fat is one of seven ingredients, and Bryan gives no quantity.

### Varieties

**Ram** (EP; Bryan 1930, Ch. VII, line 1398). The only form of the sheep that the Ebers text names. It is Bryan's English word for the animal, with no Egyptian term. Its two uses found in the text are those above: the hair in the burn prescription and the fat in the poultice for the *met*. The text read gives no further detail on how the hair was prepared (whole, burnt, or ground) or on which animal's fat counted as "ram" fat beyond the English name. [USER: verify]

*Synthesis of the varieties.* There is one variety and one text, so there is nothing to compare. The Ebers list separates the ram from the goat, which stands next to it in the same alphabetical group (line 1398), but Bryan gives no ground for the separation, and no ewe, lamb or wool is named. The entry sits under "Sheep" because the spine row does; the text itself says only "Ram".

### Active Components

Sheep hair and wool are made mostly of keratin, a fibrous protein, together with some lipid (wool grease). Sheep fat is mostly triglycerides rich in saturated fatty acids, and it is solid at room temperature. Nothing in the Ebers lines read names an active principle, and none is claimed here.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Ebers lines read give two uses: ram's hair applied to a burn, as part of a prescription that is also a spoken incantation to Horus, and ram's fat in a poultice for the *met*, a word whose meaning Bryan says is unsettled. The Codex offers no protocol for either. Putting animal hair or fat on a burn is not a treatment and can contaminate the wound; a burn needs proper first aid and assessment (see Scientific Correlation and Contraindications).

### Preparation & Dosing

Two preparations are given in the lines read. The burn remedy is "Cake / Ram's Hair / Apply to the Burn", spoken together with the incantation over "Milk-of-a-Woman-who-has-Borne-a-Son" (lines 2312-2320). The *met* remedy is a poultice of seven named ingredients including "Fat-of-the-Ram" (line 3151). No quantity, preparation of the hair or fat, or duration is given in the sources retrieved for either. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Burns are treated by cooling with clean running water, protecting the wound with a clean non-adherent dressing, and clinical assessment for anything more than a small area; fats and hair are not part of that care. Animal fat has long been used as an ointment base because it holds other ingredients and keeps moisture in, but it carries no wound-healing property of its own that this entry can state. Sheep can carry organisms that infect people, including *Brucella*, *Coxiella burnetii* (Q fever) and the orf virus. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** None is given. Bryan's chapter lists the materials and, in places, the complaint; it prints no taste, temperature, humoral or other quality for the ram or its parts. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The one sourced frame is the burn prescription, in which the drug is not given alone: the ram's hair stands inside a text that also contains a spoken incantation (Bryan, lines 2287-2320). The Ebers corpus is organised by complaint, so what it records is what was done for a burn, not what ram's hair does to the person's vital constitution, and none of the Egyptian terms for the person (*ka*, *ba*, *akh*, *ib*) is attached to the sheep in any line read. The fat for the *met* has no spell attached in the lines read. Whether this is better understood as *heka* than as an ordinary poultice is not something the text settles for the ram, and the identification of any named substance in Ebers is a modern hypothesis. **Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is two ingredients in two prescriptions, one for a burn that a clinician should see and one for a complaint whose name Bryan himself cannot translate with confidence. The one thing worth carrying away is the structural lesson of the burn passage: in this tradition the recitation and the drug are parts of one intervention, and reading the ingredient without the words loses half of it.

### Contraindications

- **Burns and wounds:** do not apply animal hair, fat or other raw animal material to a burn. Cool it with clean running water and seek care for anything large, deep, on the face, hands or genitals, or in a child.
- **Infection:** sheep and their hair, wool and fat can carry brucellosis, Q fever and orf, and unprocessed animal material can introduce wound infection and tetanus.
- **Legal and conservation status:** domestic sheep are livestock and unregulated as a species, but wild sheep (argali and others) are protected in many places, and the text does not say which animal was meant beyond "Ram". [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** taking fat requires slaughter; for a substance with no supported benefit that is hard to justify.
