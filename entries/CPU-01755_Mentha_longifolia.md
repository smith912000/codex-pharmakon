# Mentha longifolia — Jeruk, byi rug; Clark byi.rug (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Mentha longifolia, the binomial the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary sets by column position against Jeruk (English name "Horsemint"); Clark 1995 prints "Elscholtzia sp." for byi.rug, with Elscholtzia calycocarpa and Stachys baicalensis as the black type, and no Mentha* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and on a binomial that only one of the two editions prints, and that by position.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk for *Mentha longifolia* and for *longifolia* in the genus found nothing (the Sushruta appendix has "Pinus Longifolia", a different plant, at `Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt` line 32847). The genus *Mentha* occurs in Dioscorides only in the translators' glosses to the mint chapters (for example `Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 5865, 6836 and 6883, naming *Mentha aquatica*, a pennyroyal and *Mentha sativa*) and in the Sushruta appendix as "Mentha Arvensis" (line 32619); these are other species and other drugs and are not read as mentions of this one. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 290) the key *Mentha longifolia* is taken from the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary line 5344 with the note "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". It is one of three binomials printed on that line ("Delphinium brunonianum Delphinium himalayi Munz. Mentha longifolia"), and the **third**, the one that falls on Jeruk by position. It is not printed by Clark. Clark's own row for the same Tibetan name, *byi.rug* (names.csv line 189, Clark line 1099), is keyed to "Elscholtzia sp." and has no spine row as far as the files read show.

**Identity doubt.** The two editions name two different genera for the drug: the glossary's *Mentha* (by position), and Clark's *Elscholtzia* (Clark's spelling; the genus is usually spelled *Elsholtzia*), with *Stachys baicalensis* named for the black type. All three are of the mint family as the Codex understands it (not checked against a flora), so the two editions agree in kind. The Codex cannot say which plant the Tibetan texts meant, and the entry stands for the Tibetan drug. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "J" block, lines 5335 to 5355).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. Seven phonetic names are printed (lines 5337 to 5340): "Jagoepoe", "Jakang", "Jeruk", "Ji tsher", "Jhapo Tsi tsi", "Jhew laphuk", "Jitanga". The transliterations (line 5342) are seven: "bya rgod spos", "bya rkang", "byi rug", "byi tsher", "bya pho rtsi rtsi", "byi'u la phug", "byi tang ga". The scientific names (lines 5344 to 5348) are seven: "Delphinium brunonianum", "Delphinium himalayi Munz." and "Mentha longifolia" (all on 5344), "Xanthium strumarium" (5345), "Pisum sativum" (5346), "Torularia humilis" (5347) and "Embelia ribes" (5348). The English names (lines 5350 to 5355) are "Larkspur Larkspur", "Horsemint", "Cocklebur", "Garden pea", "Alpencress" and "Marsh nut". Counted in order, "Jeruk" is the third phonetic name and the third transliteration, "byi rug", whose spelling is Clark's *byi.rug*; *Mentha longifolia* is the third scientific name and "Horsemint" the third English name. The count can be checked at the fourth place: "Ji tsher" ("byi tsher") stands against *Xanthium strumarium* and "Cocklebur", as Clark's *byi.tsher* gives (see I_974, which counts the same block and records that agreement). The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text on "Jeruk" (lines 3464 to 3468).* Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428). The sentence is split by the layout: line 3464 ends "Saekar is a diuretic which drains generalized edema and ascites. Jeruk", line 3466 continues "prevents wound infections and cures rectal abscesses in children caused", and line 3468 begins "by ein." ("ein" is the text's word; the Codex does not know what it renders). The next sentence names "Tshe dhum". The index lists "Jeruk 210" (line 7402). The text gives no Latin name, no taste and no part used.

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1099).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry follows *srad.dkar.wa* (line 1098) and is followed by *mtshe.ldum* (line 1100). It reads:

"byi.rug: Elscholtzia sp.: [is said to be of yellow and black types (black type: Elscholtzia calycocarpa; Stachys baicalensis). The superior type has thin, flexible petioles, an elongated, green leaf, an inflorescence similar to a golden stupa and a pungent smell. The types with black, or yellowish-black ears have round leaves. All three types have hot and astringent tastes and] heal wounds and sores infected by parasites, worms, etc."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the types, the leaves, the inflorescence "similar to a golden stupa", the pungent smell and the hot and astringent tastes. Outside them (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the identification, which is Clark's and not the root text's, and the clause "heal wounds and sores infected by parasites, worms, etc.". The line gives no part used and no preparation. Clark's name index lists *byi.rug* (line 1722) and the Latin index lists *Elscholtzia* (line 1580). Clark's "heal wounds and sores infected by parasites, worms" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "prevents wound infections and cures rectal abscesses in children" share wounds and infection but are not the same claim, and the Codex does not equate them. The Codex reads "Jeruk" as *byi.rug* from the spelling of the transliteration and from the shared concern with wounds and infection. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Mentha longifolia*, *Elscholtzia*, *Elsholtzia*, "Jeruk" and *byi.rug* found no entry on this drug. Four entries quote the same glossary block for their own names: I_1264 (*Delphinium brunonianum*), I_1266 (*Delphinium himalayi*), I_974 (*Xanthium strumarium*) and I_1250 (*Celosia cristata*); none gives "Jeruk" an entry. I_330 (Peppermint, *Mentha piperita*) and I_461 (Spearmint, *Mentha spicata*) are mints of other species from other traditions and do not cite the Tibetan texts. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Mentha* is a genus of the mint family (Lamiaceae), that mints characteristically hold volatile oils, and that *Elsholtzia* and *Stachys* belong to the same family. Clark's commentary describes the drug as having "a pungent smell" and "hot and astringent tastes". Which plant the Tibetan texts mean is not settled by the lines read, so no further statement about this drug's constituents is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "heal[s] wounds and sores infected by parasites, worms, etc." (Clark, line 1099) and, under the name Jeruk, "prevents wound infections and cures rectal abscesses in children" (Men-Tsee-Khang, lines 3464 to 3468). These are the texts' claims in their own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and nothing in this entry should be put on a wound or given to a child. Infected wounds and abscesses need medical assessment.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 1099) prints "hot and astringent tastes" for all three types. No power and no post-digestive taste is printed. Men-Tsee-Khang prints no taste or power for "Jeruk" in the lines read. The actions printed are healing wounds and sores infected by parasites and worms (Clark) and preventing wound infections and curing rectal abscesses in children (Men-Tsee-Khang).

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it heals wounds and sores infected by parasites and worms, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says it prevents wound infections and cures rectal abscesses in children. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the chapter 20 lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes, and not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23 in both editions); no line read places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name, not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a wound and infection drug in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and the plant behind the Tibetan name is uncertain between the glossary's horsemint and Clark's *Elscholtzia*. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Infected wounds, abscesses and parasitic infestation need medical assessment, particularly in children; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Because the identity is uncertain, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
