# Pedicularis tenuirostris — lug.ru.ser.po, Lugru serpo (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Pedicularis tenuirostris, the second of three binomials Clark 1995 prints for lug.ru.ser.po (after "Pedicularis longiflora Rudolph var. tubiformis (Klotz) Tsoong" and before "Pedicularis bicomuta", as printed); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text prints no tenuirostris and sets Pedicularis longiflora against the name "Lugru serpo"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and under this binomial on Clark's edition alone.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Ebers, Assyrian, Badianus, Avicenna and Sushruta material on disk for *Pedicularis* found only Dioscorides lines that print *Pedicularis* inside translators' lists for other drugs (for example line 9431, a SUGGESTED line for *staphisagria*), which are not this drug. The Shennong text (Yang 1998) has *Ma Xian Hao* (*Herba Pedicularis Resupinatae*, line 3063, `names.csv` line 141), a different species that is not read as this drug. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** The spine row (CPU-01765) was added from Clark's line 1031 (note: "Clark 1995 (line 1031)"). The `names.csv` row for that line (line 143) keys the **first** of the three binomials, *Pedicularis longiflora*, and has no row of its own for *Pedicularis tenuirostris*. The key of this entry is therefore a **later** name on the line: the second of three. It is Clark's identification and not the root text's. The first binomial has its own entry (I_1327), and the glossary's brown neighbour has another (I_1328).

**Identity doubt.** Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" and (line 68) that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect". Here three species of one genus are given for one Tibetan name, and Men-Tsee-Khang prints a fourth (*Pedicularis longiflora*, without the variety) for "Lugru serpo", so the Tibetan name does not fix the species and the second binomial is one of several candidates. The Codex does not choose among them. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1031).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry follows *lug.ru (smug.po)* (line 1030) and reads:

"lug.ru.ser.po: Pedicularis longiflora Rudolph var. tubiformis (Klotz) Tsoong: Pedicularis tenuirostris: Pedicularis bicomuta: [grows in shady meadows and has a yellow flower similar to that of the brown type. It collects poisons and dries 'reservoirs' of pus.]"

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. **The whole of the description and both actions are inside the brackets**, so, as Clark's marks stand, the root-text layer gives this drug only the name and the identifications, and the actions come from the commentary. The "brown type" it is compared with is *lug.ru (smug.po)*, the line before (line 1030), whose root-text layer reads "collects poisons, cures meat poisoning". The line after, *chhu.rug*, closes with "Both types, as well as] Pedicularis (above) dry fluid accumulations [and Halerpestes sarmentosa cures bone fever]" (line 1032), which is outside the brackets and names "Pedicularis (above)" without saying which type is meant; the Codex does not carry it over to the second binomial. Clark's name index lists *lug.ru.ser.po* (line 1758), and his Latin index (line 1626) lists *Pedicularis tenuirostris*, with *Pedicularis longiflora*, *Pedicularis bicomuta*, *oliveriana*, *pectinata* and *pyramidate*.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (read through the name, not the binomial).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The glossary's "L" block prints "lug ru ser po" among its transliterations (line 5441) and "Lugru serpo" among its phonetic names (line 5437); its scientific-name run (lines 5445 to 5448) ends "Pedicularis siphonantha Pedicularis longiflora" and prints no *tenuirostris*. The block is treated in I_1327, I_1328, I_1310 and I_1258. The running text, chapter 20 (herbal run, opening line 3428), line 3433, reads: "Lugru mukpo nutrilizes toxin and cures meat poison. Lugru serpo and Chhurug dries up chhuser." "Chhuser" is a Tibetan term kept untranslated (the Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note, line 686). The index lists "Lugru serpo 207" (line 7428). Clark's "dries 'reservoirs' of pus" (commentary layer) and Men-Tsee-Khang's "dries up chhuser" are both drying actions; the Codex records that and does not set "reservoirs of pus" and *chhuser* equal. The name is not in the taste groups of chapter 19 and not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23).

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Pedicularis tenuirostris* and *lug.ru.ser.po* found no entry on this plant or drug. I_1327 (*Pedicularis longiflora*) is the sibling entry from the same Clark line and also covers the glossary row; I_1328 (*Pedicularis siphonantha*) covers the brown type's glossary row. The three are not merged. I_617 (*Pedicularis foliosa*, tags CM, DMM, FMT) says, with no locus, that "lug-ru" louseworts are used as "tonics" and for "heat conditions" and wounds; the lines read here print poison, pus and fluid actions and no tonic, and that claim was not found. I_856 (*Pedicularis sylvatica*) is a European species. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Pedicularis* is a large genus of hemiparasitic herbs (they photosynthesise but draw water and nutrients from the roots of neighbouring plants) and that "lousewort" is its English name. Which compounds this species or the Tibetan drug holds is not shown by the lines read, so no statement about its composition is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Clark's commentary layer records a drug that "collects poisons and dries 'reservoirs' of pus" (line 1031), and Men-Tsee-Khang, under the same Tibetan name, one that "dries up chhuser" (line 3433). These are the texts' claims in their own terms, attached to this binomial only through the name, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. A poisoning or a pus-filled swelling needs medical care, and no herb should be used for either on the strength of this entry.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary describes the habitat and the flower and does not say which part is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** No taste, power or post-digestive taste is printed for this drug in Clark's line 1031 or in the Men-Tsee-Khang lines read (3433, 5437). The only quality printed is the colour of the flower (yellow, "similar to that of the brown type"), which is a description of the plant and not a taste or power. The brown type's line (1030) prints "bitter taste, fluid power", and the Codex does not carry those over.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Clark's materia medica gives this drug only by name and identifications in the root-text layer (the brackets enclose the rest of the line), and the commentary classes it by what it does: it collects poisons and dries pus. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23 in both editions), and Clark's materia medica names other drugs as essence-extraction ingredients (for example lines 853, 971 and 1144), none of them this one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name and not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a poison-and-pus drug in Clark's commentary layer, one of three candidate species for a Tibetan name, with no preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Poisoning and pus-forming infections need medical care; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Because the species is one of several candidates, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read.
