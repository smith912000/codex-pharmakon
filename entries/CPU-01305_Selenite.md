# Selenite — Selenites Lithos / Aphroselenon (as printed in the 2000 English edition of Dioscorides)
## *"Selenite — Moonstone — crystalline Lime Sulphate", the translator's gloss under the heading 5-159 SELENITES LITHOS; the text itself gives only a place (Arabia) and a look (white, transparent, light)* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The loci given for this entry are `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` ("5-159 (line 10656)", gloss "Selenite — Moonstone — crystalline Lime Sulphate", class mineral) and `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 885 ("Selenites Lithos", DMM V, identification marked yes). The chapter is *De Materia Medica* 5-159 *Selenites Lithos* in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`: heading at line 10656, the translator's gloss at line 10658, the text at line 10659. It sits among the stones of Book 5, after 5-158 *Memphites Lithos* (the Memphis stone, line 10652) and before 5-160 *Iaspis Lithos* (jasper, line 10664).

The chapter reads in full (line 10659): "Selenites stone some have called aphroselenon because it is found in the nighttime during the increase of the moon. It is found in Arabia and is white, transparent and light. They file it and give the dust in a drink to the epileptic. Women hang it about themselves as an amulet, and it is thought that bound to trees it makes them bear fruit." The words "selenites" and "aphroselenon" are the two names printed; the English edition's index lists *aphroselenon* at page 822 and *Selenites stone* at page 822, which is this chapter, and a separate *Selenites* at page 777, which is the wine *Oinos Selenites* (5-74, line 10171) and not the stone. Another name sometimes met in the index, "Selenite of Lead" (lines 10297 and 10329), belongs to the lead chapters and is a different substance.

So the text gives the stone five things: two names; a reason for the second name (it is found at night during the waxing moon, which is a report, in Dioscorides' own voice, of when it is found, not a claim about the moon's action); a provenance (Arabia); a description (white, transparent, light); and two uses, one medicinal and one amuletic. The medicinal use is internal: the stone is filed, and "the dust" is given "in a drink to the epileptic". The amuletic uses are worn by women, and, "it is thought", tied to trees to make them fruit. The phrase "it is thought" marks the second as a report of belief, not Dioscorides' own assertion; the first, "Women hang it about themselves as an amulet", is stated as a custom. This is the recording-without-endorsing pattern the Codex's specification asks readers to notice: the chapter records a practice and does not say that the stone works. The editor's introduction says in general terms that "a few superstitious practices are recorded in De Materia Medica" (line 394), and that note is the editor's, not the Greek author's.

Compare the neighbouring stone chapters for the same device. 5-160 *Iaspis* says jasper stones "are said to be amulets used as personal ornaments, and to speed delivery [in childbirth] tied to the thigh" (line 10666), and 5-161 *Aetites* is tied to the arm and the thigh in pregnancy and is used to expose a thief (line 10670). The selenite chapter is the only one of these that also gives a medicinal dose form, the filed dust in a drink, and it gives no amount.

The chapter gives no test of a true stone, no price, no season of gathering beyond the night and the waxing moon, no instructions for filing, no vehicle for the drink other than the generic "in a drink", and no number of days. Several other stone chapters give such tests; this one does not.

**Identity.** The three names in the gloss do not describe one mineral in modern terms. In general mineralogy, selenite is the clear, colourless crystalline form of gypsum, hydrated calcium sulphate; "moonstone" is a different mineral, a feldspar. The translator's "crystalline Lime Sulphate" is the gypsum reading. The text's own description (white, transparent, light) fits a clear, soft crystal, but it does not decide between minerals. ### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, selenite is calcium sulphate dihydrate (CaSO₄·2H₂O), a soft, water-poor mineral that scratches with a fingernail, slightly soluble in water and not toxic in itself in small amounts; it is the material of plaster of Paris once heated. The Greek stone's composition is not stated in the sources retrieved, and "moonstone" in the translator's gloss, a feldspar, would be a different material. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved say that Dioscorides recorded filed selenite dust in a drink for epilepsy, and recorded its use as a woman's amulet and as an amulet on trees for fruit. The Codex does not recommend swallowing mineral dust for epilepsy. Epilepsy is a medical condition that needs diagnosis and treatment, and a stone has no known anticonvulsant action. The amuletic uses are customs, not practices to adopt; wearing a piece of selenite as an ornament is harmless, and the text claims nothing for it beyond custom.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation is filing the stone and giving "the dust in a drink" (line 10659). The chapter gives no amount, no vehicle other than "a drink", no frequency, no length of treatment and no instruction for preparing the stone beyond "file". The amulet is hung on the body or "bound to trees"; no preparation is described. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Gypsum is calcium sulphate; it is used in plaster, in some building materials, and as a food additive in small controlled amounts. It has no known anti-epileptic effect. Epilepsy is a disorder of the brain's electrical activity that is treated with prescribed medicines under medical supervision, and an unproven remedy risks seizures going uncontrolled. Filed mineral dust of unknown purity could contain other minerals, including sulphates and silicates that irritate the gut. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for selenite. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, season of gathering, a sensory description), and this chapter shows it briefly: the stone is found in Arabia, is white, transparent and light, and is gathered in the night during the waxing moon. It records amuletic uses (worn by women, bound to trees), and recording is not endorsing: the second is introduced with "it is thought". The chapter is therapeutic and amuletic and assigns no cultivation status. The link between the stone and the moon is carried by its name, and the text does not give the moon any action on the person.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The entry records that a Greek physician reported a white, transparent stone from Arabia, gave its dust in a drink for epilepsy, and noted that women wore it as an amulet. The Codex does not recommend using selenite as a medicine; the one thing a reader can take from it is the habit of describing a stone by place, look and weight before saying what it does. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Epilepsy:** needs medical diagnosis and prescribed treatment; a stone dust does not replace it, and stopping prescribed medicine risks seizures.
- **Ingestion of mineral dust:** abrasive and of unknown purity; avoid, particularly in children and in pregnancy.
- **Filing the stone:** produces fine dust that irritates the lungs and eyes; a hygiene risk, not a method.
- **Identification:** "selenite" in the English edition is the translator's gloss, joined to "moonstone", a different mineral; the sources retrieved do not say what the Greek stone was.
- **Conservation and trade:** collecting from natural caves and gypsum deposits is regulated in some places; this entry does not cover it. [USER: verify]
