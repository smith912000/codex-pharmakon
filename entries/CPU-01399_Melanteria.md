# Melanteria — Melantheria (as printed in the chapter heading; "melanteria" in the text), Dioscorides 5-118
## *A salt-like growth at the mouths of copper (brass) mines, best sulphur-yellow, smooth and clear, that turns black on touching water and is "sharp like misy"; the chapter names no use, and the translator gives no identification ("UNKNOWN")* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-118, heading "MELANTHERIA" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 10438; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 891, translator identification "UNKNOWN", class guess mineral; list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 844, spelt "Melantheria", mineral, flagged as an uncertain identification; the book index sends the reader to page 803). The whole chapter was read. It reads (line 10440):

"Melanteria grows together in the manner of salt at the mouths of mines out of which brass is dug, and some comes from the earthy upper surfaces of these places. Some is also found that is dug out in Cilicia and in certain other places. The best is a sulphurous colour [yellow], smooth, even and clear, and on touching water it quickly turns black. It is sharp like misy [above]."

The chapter thus gives a mode of occurrence ("grows together in the manner of salt"), a place (the mouths of brass mines and the earthy upper surfaces there; also dug in Cilicia and elsewhere), a rule for choosing the best (sulphur-yellow, smooth, even, clear), a sensory test (on touching water it "quickly turns black"), and one quality ("sharp like misy"). It names no disease, no application and no preparation.

**Chapters that mention it.**
- 5-84 (*Kadmeia*, cadmia): in describing a hill over Solis from which a burnt stone called *purites* is dug, Dioscorides says "in the same mound are found (as it were) some veins of chalcitis, misy, sory, melanteria, coeruleus, chrysocolla, calcanthus and diphryges" (line 10223). This places melanteria with the other copper-bearing materials of the book, as a vein-mineral occurring in the same mound.
- 5-117 (*Misu*, misy), the chapter the present one points to ("sharp like misy [above]"): "Cyprian misy must be chosen — looking like gold, hard, and if broken a golden colour, glistening like a star. It has the same strength as chalcitis [5-115], and is burnt the same way without psoricum [5-116] being produced from it, differing both in excess and defect. That from Egypt (compared to others) is the best and most effective, but for eye medicines it is not comparable to that previously mentioned." (line 10437). That chapter says what misy is used for (eye medicines) only by reference; it does not state that melanteria is used for the same purposes.
- 5-119 (*Soru*, sory), the next chapter: "Some are deceived, supposing sory to be melanteria [above], for it is a different thing though not unlike. Sory is more poisonous and causes nausea." (line 10443). So Dioscorides warns that sory is sometimes mistaken for melanteria and that sory is the more poisonous. The same chapter ends with a general rule "for these and almost all others, those which are not burnt are considered to be stronger than the burnt; except for salt, wine sediment, saltpetre [potassium nitrate], chalk, and other similar things which are weak raw, but are more effective burnt." That rule is stated for "these" materials in 5-119; the text does not name melanteria in it.

**Identity.** The translator gives no identification ("UNKNOWN"), the Codex list marks the identification uncertain, and the Greek heading spelling differs from the text spelling. The chapter's clues (a yellow, salt-like efflorescence at copper-mine mouths that blackens in water) do not name a mineral. The relationship to misy, chalcitis and sory (the neighbouring chapters 5-115 to 5-119) is given only as comparison, not as equivalence. Which material Dioscorides meant is not settled by the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None can be stated from the chapter. The material is unidentified, so no composition is asserted. The chapter ties it to copper mines ("out of which brass is dug") and says it turns black on touching water; in general terms, salt-like crusts at mine mouths are mineral efflorescences, often water-soluble salts of the metals in the ground, and some are toxic. This is a general statement about such deposits, not a reading of the chapter, and no composition is claimed for melanteria.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records melanteria as a mine-mouth mineral with a sensory test and a quality ("sharp") but names no use for it in 5-118, and warns in 5-119 that a more poisonous material, sory, is mistaken for it. The material is unidentified, was collected from mine surfaces, and may be a toxic metal salt. The Codex supplies no modern protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation is given in 5-118: no burning, washing, grinding or mixing, and no use to which to put it. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM).** The chapter prints one quality: it is "sharp like misy [above]". No warming, cooling, drying, moistening or astringent quality is printed for melanteria itself. The misy chapter (5-117) says misy "has the same strength as chalcitis [5-115]"; the strength of chalcitis (5-115, line 10426 and following) is stated in its own chapter and is not carried across to melanteria.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. What the herbal offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, adulteration detection), and this chapter is a clear small example of it: it gives the place (mine mouths and their upper earthy surfaces; Cilicia), the colour and surface that mark the best (sulphur-yellow, smooth, even, clear), a simple assay (it blackens quickly on touching water), and, in the next chapter, a named look-alike that is more poisonous (sory) and so a substitution to guard against. It gives no season and no use. The chapter carries no cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to take from it for cultivation beyond the discipline the chapter shows: know where a material forms, test it with something as plain as a drop of water, and know its more dangerous look-alike. The material itself is an unidentified mine-surface mineral, and the text's own warning (sory is "more poisonous and causes nausea") is the caution. It is not a recommended practice. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Possible toxic metal salt:** mine-mouth efflorescences may contain copper or other toxic metals; do not swallow, inhale as dust or apply to the eyes or broken skin.
- **Look-alike:** the text warns that sory, "more poisonous and causes nausea", is mistaken for melanteria.
- **Unidentified material:** the identification is uncertain; nothing here should be collected or used on the strength of the name.
- **Mines:** old mine workings are hazardous (collapse, bad air, contaminated water); the status of any site was not retrieved. [USER: verify]
