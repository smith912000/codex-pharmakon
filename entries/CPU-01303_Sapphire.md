# Sapphire — "sapphires" as printed in Bhishagratna's Sushruta Samhita (Sutrasthana ch.46)
## *The blue gemstone "sapphires" of Bhishagratna's 1907 English; the Sanskrit name behind the English word is not printed at the passage, and the sources retrieved give no mineral description, so the stone's identity rests on the translator's word alone* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The locus given for this entry is `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt`, line 2141 (Sutrasthana, chapter 46, "the Chapter which treats of food and drink", line 4; the same text is in `Bhishagratna_Vol1_djvu.txt`, line 21801). The word "sapphires" occurs once in the English of that chapter, in a list of gems at the close of the group of metals. The chapter's own opening frames food as "the chief source of their bodily strength and complexion, as well as of the albuminous (Ojas) principle" (lines 10-12), and then runs through groups of substances: grains, flesh, fruits, the salt group (line 2025), the alkalis (line 2087), and the metals (line 2121).

The passage reads (lines 2139-2149): "Zinc and lead are vermifugal, as well as liquefacient and corrosive. They have a saline taste. Pearls, corals, diamonds, sapphires, Vaidurya (lapis lazuli), crystals, etc. are beneficial to the sight, and cooling in their potency. They are anti-toxic and act as liquefacient or corrosive agents. They are possessed of sacred prophylactic virtues, and bring good luck to men who wear them, and cleanse their wearers from all impurities. Thus the description of the salt group is ended." So the sapphire is not described alone. It is named in a list of gems with pearls, corals, diamonds, Vaidurya and crystals, and the statements that follow are made of the whole list: good for the sight, cooling in potency, anti-toxic, liquefacient or corrosive, and with "sacred prophylactic virtues" that bring good luck to the wearer and cleanse him from impurities. The sentence closing the passage speaks of "the salt group", although the list stands after the metals; whether that is Bhishagratna's phrasing or an effect of the arrangement of his translation is not stated, and this entry does not resolve it. [USER: verify]

The translator glosses Vaidurya as lapis lazuli, in brackets, but gives no gloss and no Sanskrit name for the "sapphires". A footnote in the Uttarasthana (`SS_Uttara_ch18.txt`, line 707) lists "the seven kinds of gems" as "(i) Padmaraga, (2) Marakata, (3) Nila, (4) Vaidurya, (5) Mukta (pearl) (6) Pravala and (7) Hema (gold)", where it glosses only Mukta (pearl). Nila is the third gem in that list, but the sources retrieved do not say that Nila is the "sapphires" of chapter 46, and nothing in them ties the two passages together. [USER: verify] A third passage names gems in the ordinary regimen: in the Chikitsasthana (`SS_Chikitsa_ch24.txt`, line 379) "the wearing of gems, flowers and clean clothes is beneficial in a variety of ways", in the chapter on rules of hygiene; it names no gem. Chapter 45 (`SS_Sutra_ch45.txt`, line 208) uses "the gems known as the Gomedha" and a pearl or crystal among seven ways of purifying water, and again does not mention sapphire.

A search of the Sushruta chapter files on disk for "sapphire" returns only line 2141 of chapter 46. The text gives no place of origin for the stone, no test of a true stone, no colour, no way of working it and no preparation.

**Other texts.** Dioscorides is not tagged for this entry. Chapter 5-157 of *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, line 10648) is headed *Sapheiros Lithos*, with the translator's gloss "Sapphire — previously Lapis Lazuli — Alumina", and says the stone "is thought to be good (taken in a drink) for those touched by scorpions", for "internal ulcers and abnormal growths in the eyes", and that it "represses adenoids [haemorrhoids?] and pustules, and rejoins herniated membranes". That is a separate tradition's record and a gloss that points to lapis lazuli, which Bhishagratna keeps apart as Vaidurya; it is noted here, not merged. Clark's 1995 Tibetan translation (`Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, line 66) names "diamond, aquamarine, sapphire, ruby and emerald" as precious gems "said to protect against spirits, and to cure all diseases (when compounded in medicine)", with a citation to the *Crystal Rosary*, not to the Four Tantras. Neither is a tag on this entry.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, blue sapphire is corundum, aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃), coloured by traces of iron and titanium; it is hard, chemically inert and insoluble in water, and it is not absorbed from the gut. Whether the "sapphires" of the translation were corundum is not established by the sources, which is a reason for the gloss in the identity line. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved say that Sushruta's chapter on food and drink names sapphires among gems that are good for the sight, cooling, anti-toxic, and protective when worn. The Codex does not recommend swallowing, grinding or applying a gemstone. A cut stone is an ornament; no part of the sources supports a modern use.

### Preparation & Dosing

The passage gives no preparation for the sapphire. It says the gems "act as liquefacient or corrosive agents" (line 2145), which could imply some working of them, but the lines read do not say how, and no use in food or in a recipe is given. The only action tied to wearing is the one in lines 2146-2149. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Corundum is among the hardest minerals and is inert in the body; swallowed or powdered, it has no pharmacological action, and fine hard dust is an abrasive and an irritant to the eye and gut. A gemstone has no antitoxic action, and no gem improves eyesight. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Ayurveda (SS):** the passage prints a group potency, "cooling in their potency", for the gems as a list (lines 2142-2143), and calls them "anti-toxic" and "liquefacient or corrosive". No *rasa* (taste) is printed for the gems; the saline taste in line 2141 is stated of zinc and lead, which come just before the list. No *vipaka* (post-digestive taste) is given. The potency is the list's and is not assigned to the sapphire alone. No other tradition is tagged, so no other classification is offered.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The sapphire sits inside a chapter that links food to strength, complexion and *ojas* (lines 10-12), but that is a statement about food in general, and sapphires are not food. The passage assigns the gems "sacred prophylactic virtues": they bring good luck to the wearer and "cleanse their wearers from all impurities" (lines 2146-2148). That is a protective and purificatory claim made of a list of gems, not a *rasayana* (rejuvenation) or *vajikarana* (generative vitality) placement, and it does not mention *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana*. No *shodhana* of the stone is described. The one regimen passage on gems (Chikitsa ch.24, line 379) says wearing gems is beneficial in many ways and does not name the sapphire. The *sattva*, *rajas* and *tamas* categories are Samkhya and are not in the passage.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator beyond what the text itself says: a gem worn as an ornament and a protection. The Codex does not recommend taking a sapphire internally or applying it. For a reader the useful result is the limit of the record: one sentence in one chapter, a list of gems, a translator's English word, and no way to tie it to a Sanskrit name from the lines on disk. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Do not swallow or grind a gemstone:** it is abrasive and indigestible, and powdered mineral can scratch the gut and the eye.
- **Eye use:** the text calls gems "beneficial to the sight"; do not put gem dust or a stone in or near the eye.
- **Poisoning:** the text calls the gems "anti-toxic"; no stone is an antidote, and poisoning needs emergency care.
- **Identity:** "sapphires" is the translator's word; "Vaidurya (lapis lazuli)" is glossed apart from it, and the Dioscorides translator glosses a different Greek stone as "Sapphire — previously Lapis Lazuli".
- **Trade:** natural gemstones are often traded under certification and origin rules, and treated or synthetic stones are common; this entry does not cover buying a stone. [USER: verify]
