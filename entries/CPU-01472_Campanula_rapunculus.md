# Campanula rapunculus — Gongole agrioe, "wild rapum" (Dioscorides 2-135); rampion

## *Campanula rapunculus* (printed by the Dioscorides translators on the second of two SUGGESTED lines for 2-135, glossed "Rampion [Mabberley]"; the first line gives *Barbarea vulgaris*, "Winter Cress, Yellow Rocket") | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry.** This plant is named only by Dioscorides (DMM). It was added 2026-10-09 from the extraction list (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) at the locus 2-135, line 5666.

**Identity note (read before the rest).** The binomial is the translators' (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) suggestion and not Dioscorides' identification, and it is not their first suggestion. The SUGGESTED lines (5668 and 5669) read: "Rapum sylvestre, Napus-Bunias sylvestris [Fuchs Plate #99], Barbarea vulgaris — Winter Cress, Yellow Rocket" and, on the next line, "Rapunculus esculentis [Bauhin], Campanula rapunculus [Linnaeus] — Rampion [Mabberley]". So the list offers a crucifer (winter cress) and a bellflower (rampion), and the chapter's description, which is a plant with a finger-thick root and seed in husks, does not decide between them. Rampion is a bellflower in the same genus as the plant of CPU-01471 (*Campanula medium*, 4-18), but that is a different Dioscorides chapter. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is in Book Two, under "Lachana: Vegetables", immediately after 2-134 GONGULIS (the turnip, line 5661) and before 2-136 BOUNIAS.

*2-135 GONGOLE AGRIOE* (heading at line 5666; text at lines 5670 and 5683, with two pages of plates between them). The chapter reads: "The wild rapum grows in fields, a shrub of two feet high, bearing many boughs, smooth on the top; [the root] is the thickness of a finger or more. It bears seed in husks similar to cups. When the coverings that contain the seeds are opened there is another husk within (similar to a head) in which are little black seeds. When these are broken they are white within. They are put into sebaceous treatments for clearing the face and other parts of the body, such as those made from the meal of lupins [2-132], wheat, lolium [2-116, 4-140], or ervum [2-129, 2-131]."

The chapter gives a habitat (fields), a description (a two-foot shrub with many smooth boughs, a root of a finger's thickness or more, seed in cup-like husks with a second husk inside, black seeds that are white when broken), and one use: the seeds go "into sebaceous treatments for clearing the face and other parts of the body", alongside the meals of lupin, wheat, *lolium* and *ervum*. It gives no other name and no medicinal use for the body. Nothing is said of eating the root. The plates between the two halves of the text are Fuchs's *Napus Bunias sativus* and *N. Bunias sylvestris* and are not part of the text.

*2-134 GONGULIS* (the cultivated turnip; heading at line 5661), read for context, is the chapter this one is "wild" against: "The boiled root of gongule is nourishing yet very windy", with a decoction "a warm pack for gout and chilblains", and the root pickled in brine. It is a separate chapter and its uses are not attributed to this row.

### Active Components

*Campanula rapunculus* is a biennial bellflower of the Campanulaceae whose root and leaves have been eaten as a salad vegetable in parts of Europe, which is general knowledge and was not checked in this session. Winter cress (*Barbarea*) is a crucifer, and crucifers generally contain glucosinolates. The composition of the plant Dioscorides meant was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records the seed of a wild root plant as one ingredient of the cleansing preparations (*sebaceous treatments*) for the skin of the face and body, in company with grain and legume meals. The Codex gives no protocol for it. The chapter's plant is not securely identified.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation printed (2-135) is that the black seeds are "put into sebaceous treatments for clearing the face and other parts of the body", such as those made from lupin, wheat, *lolium* or *ervum* meal. No recipe, quantity or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. The chapter offers the discipline of attention only: where the plant grows (fields), how to recognise it (the height, the many smooth boughs, the finger-thick root, the double husk and the black seeds that are white within), and the single cosmetic use of the seed. It records no status as a long-term or life-nourishing substance. The identification with rampion is the translators' second suggestion. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice. Whether the plant is a wild turnip relative or a bellflower cannot be settled from the chapter, so there is nothing to grow or to take. The abstentive answer is the correct one.

### Contraindications

No contraindication is printed in the chapter. The use is external. As a general caution, a plant that the translators cannot place between a crucifer and a bellflower must not be gathered or used from this description.
