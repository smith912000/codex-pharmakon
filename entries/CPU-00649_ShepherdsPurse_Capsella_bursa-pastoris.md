# Shepherd's Purse — Mother's Heart / Blindweed / Ji Cai / Bursa Pastoris

## *Capsella bursa-pastoris* (L.) Medik. | Family: Brassicaceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM, SBJ
**Energetic Classification:** Cool and drying — an astringent, contracting haemostatic (Galenic: cold and dry)
**Training Phase:** Tempering (astringent consolidation and staunching)

---

### Origin & Tradition

Shepherd's Purse is a cosmopolitan little mustard-family weed named for its flat, heart- or purse-shaped seed pods, and its medical reputation is remarkably consistent across the traditions that recorded it: it is, above all, a **staunching herb** — a plant to stop bleeding and check excessive flux. Recently introduced to the European Pharmacopoeia, it carries an old and cross-cultural identity as a haemostatic and a supporter of the digestive and gynaecological systems.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** The Greek herbal tradition records the little purse-podded cress among the astringent, drying herbs used to check flux and haemorrhage — the contracting register appropriate to a cool, dry plant that tightens tissue and slows bleeding.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM):** The *Qanun* and the broader Unani materia medica employ astringent, cooling herbs of this class to arrest bleeding, tighten relaxed tissue and moderate excessive discharge; shepherd's purse fits the deobstruent-astringent grouping used for haemorrhagic and fluxive complaints.

**Shennong / Chinese (TCM):** As **Ji Cai**, shepherd's purse is both a food and a medicine in the Chinese tradition — a cooling herb regarded as benefiting the eyes, supporting the liver and spleen, and used in folk practice for bleeding and for damp-heat conditions of the lower body. Its role as an edible spring green sits alongside its remedial use.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The plant's iconography is unusually literal. The seed pod resembles a purse — or, in the folk name *Mother's Heart*, a heart — and its central traditional action concerns blood: staunching haemorrhage, checking heavy menstrual flow, and (traditionally) toning the uterus after childbirth. The symbolic association of the heart-shaped pod with the management of blood is one of the classic doctrine-of-signatures readings, and here it aligns with a genuine ethnomedical consensus on haemostatic use. The pharmacology — flavonoids, phenolic acids, amino acids and other constituents with reported antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and vessel-acting effects — offers a plausible, if not yet clinically settled, mechanism.

### Active Components

- **Flavonoids** (including quercetin and related glycosides) — antioxidant and vessel-modulating.
- **Phenolic acids** — contributing to antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity.
- **Amino acids and peptides** — proposed contributors to the herb's traditional haemostatic/uterotonic reputation.
- **Phytosterols, vitamins (including vitamin K activity in fresh material) and bioelements** — relevant to the coagulation-supporting folk use.
- Reported minor **acetylcholinesterase-inhibitory** and antimicrobial constituents.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica, shepherd's purse is a **Tempering-phase astringent** — a consolidating, staunching herb deployed to check excess: to slow bleeding, tighten lax tissue, and moderate heavy or prolonged flux. Its cool, drying, contracting character makes it a corrective for conditions of over-openness and leakage, the counterpart to the opening aromatics of the Purification phase. It is used traditionally for heavy menstrual bleeding, minor haemorrhage and as a general astringent tonic. Because its most-cited traditional action — uterine toning — carries a pregnancy hazard, its Kenetica use is bounded by clear contraindication (see below).

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed: Łukaszyk, Kwiecień & Szopa (2024), *Nutrients*, reviewed the traditional uses, bioactive compounds and modern pharmacology of *Capsella bursa-pastoris*, noting its recent introduction to the European Pharmacopoeia and identifying flavonoids, phenolic acids, amino acids, phytosterols, vitamins and bioelements as the constituents behind its activity. The review reports that modern studies support its traditional digestive and antihaemorrhagic uses and additionally describe anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antibacterial, antifungal, acetylcholinesterase-inhibitory and anticancer activities, along with supportive action in gynaecological conditions. DOI: https://doi.org/10.3390/nu16244390

**Evidence grade:** **Traditional to preclinical.** The haemostatic and uterotonic reputation rests on long, cross-cultural ethnomedical use and a supporting phytochemical rationale, with modern data drawn largely from in vitro and animal work and review synthesis. Rigorous human clinical trials for the haemostatic/gynaecological indications remain limited, so claims are graded accordingly and specific efficacy figures are not asserted here.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** Ji Cai — sweet, cool (some sources neutral); enters Liver, Spleen, and (in folk use) supports the eyes; cools blood and is used to check bleeding and clear damp-heat.
- **Ayurveda:** Not a classical dravya; by analogy to cooling astringents it reads as *kashaya* (astringent) *rasa*, *ruksha/laghu* (dry, light) *guna*, cooling *virya*, pungent *vipaka* — Pitta- and Kapha-moderating, potentially Vata-increasing.
- **Galenic/Unani:** cold and dry; astringent, styptic, contracting — used to arrest flux and haemorrhage.

### Contraindications

⚠️ **PREGNANCY — UTEROTONIC CAUTION.** Shepherd's purse has a strong traditional reputation as a **uterine stimulant/haemostatic used around childbirth**, and this same action makes it a concern in pregnancy.

- **Avoid during pregnancy** except under qualified professional supervision at term; the traditional uterotonic use means it should not be taken as a general remedy by pregnant women.
- **Use caution while breastfeeding** — insufficient safety data.
- **Anticoagulant / bleeding-disorder interaction** — because the herb's reputation is haemostatic and it may carry vitamin K activity, it could theoretically oppose anticoagulant (e.g. warfarin) therapy; those on such medication should avoid or supervise use. [USER: verify individual context]
- **Cardiac and blood-pressure caution** — traditional and preliminary reports of cardiovascular activity mean those on cardiac or antihypertensive medication should use with care.
- **Brassicaceae/mustard-family allergy** — possible cross-sensitivity.
- Prolonged high-dose use is not established as safe; reserve for short, targeted courses.
