# Chenopodium botrys — Botrus (Dioscorides 3-130)

## *Chenopodium botrys (one of the translators' suggestions; "Purple Goosefoot")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Identity doubt.** The SUGGESTED line (lines 7696-7698) offers two plants: "Botrys [Fuchs], Botrys ambrosioides vulgaris [Bauhin], Chenopodium botrys [Linnaeus] — Purple Goosefoot", and "Chamaedrys vera foemina [Fuchs], Botrys Chamaedryoides [Bauhin], Teucrium botrys — Cut-leaved Germander", with the pointer "see 3-112". Chapter 3-112 (Chamaidrus, heading at line 7537) prints the same *Teucrium botrys* gloss with "see 3-130" going the other way. The row takes the first, a goosefoot; the second is a germander and a member of a different plant family. The text read does not decide between them. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *3-130. BOTRUS* at line 7695, text at line 7699.

The chapter reads: "Botrus is a yellowish herb like a shrub, broad-spread, having many wings, and the seed grows around all the branches. The many leaves are similar to chicory, and all of it has a wonderful sweet scent, and so it is also laid among cloths. It grows especially near running waters and brooks. A decoction (with wine) is used as a drink to ease difficult breathing. The Cappadocians call this ambrosia, and it is also called artemisa."

So Dioscorides describes a yellowish, shrub-like, spreading herb with "wings", seed all round the branches, chicory-like leaves and a strong sweet scent, which is why it is laid among cloths. It grows by running water. The single medicinal use is a wine decoction drunk "to ease difficult breathing". The names given are the Cappadocian *ambrosia* and *artemisa*. The translators' index lists *Chenopodium botrys* at page 515. A plate caption "Botrys from FUCHS — 1545" appears at line 7403, well before the chapter; whether the plate is meant for this chapter was not checked.

**Overlap.** The preceding chapter, 3-129 Ambrosia (heading at line 7688), is already on the spine as I_997 *Ambrosia maritima*. Its alternative names include "botrys", and 3-130 reports that the Cappadocians call this plant *ambrosia*, so the two chapters share names by Dioscorides' own report. I_997 reports 3-130 as a separate chapter. The two should not be merged on this evidence.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. The goosefoot genus, to which the translators assign the first suggested plant, is generally known as a group of aromatic or leafy herbs, but nothing is stated here about this species' composition. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a sweet-scented herb used among cloths and a wine decoction drunk for difficult breathing. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol: the identification is split between a goosefoot and a germander, and no dose is given.

### Preparation & Dosing

3-130 gives "a decoction (with wine) ... as a drink" and laying the plant among cloths. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No study of the plant was opened. "Difficult breathing" is a symptom with many causes, some serious. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes: habitat (near running water and brooks), appearance (yellowish, winged, chicory-like leaves), smell and a household use among cloths. It assigns the plant no status. The identification with *Chenopodium botrys* is one of two alternatives given by the translators. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant beyond the scent among cloths. Difficult breathing is not something to self-treat from a historical entry.

### Contraindications

Difficult breathing can be a sign of a serious condition and needs medical assessment. The plant's identity is uncertain, so no wild plant should be gathered or drunk on this basis. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
