# Mung Bean — Green Gram / 綠豆 Lǜ Dòu / मुद्ग Mudga / मूँग Mūng

## *Vigna radiata* (L.) R.Wilczek | Family: Fabaceae
**Tradition Tags:** CM, SBJ, SS, FMT
**Energetic Classification:** Cold, sweet — clears heat and relieves toxicity (TCM); light and cooling (Ayurveda)
**Training Phase:** Cooling / Detoxification

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Vigna radiata* is a small green pulse domesticated in the Indian subcontinent and carried across Asia as one of the most digestible of all beans. Where most legumes are read as heavy and heating, the mung bean is uniquely classed across two great traditions as **cooling, cleansing, and gentle** — the pulse you eat when the body is overheated, poisoned, or too weak for anything else.

**Chinese stratum (CM — Shénnóng lineage).** In Chinese medicine, *lǜ dòu* (綠豆, "green bean") is the archetypal summer-heat remedy. Its classical function is *qīng rè jiě dú* — **"clear heat and relieve toxicity."** It is given for heatstroke, summer thirst and irritability, and, famously, as a **folk antidote to poisoning and to "drug heat"** — the overheating and toxicity attributed to mineral drugs, aconite, and other hot toxic substances. Mung bean soup (*lǜ dòu tāng*) is the household remedy pressed on anyone flushed, feverish, or "toxic-hot."

**Ayurvedic stratum (SS — Sushruta lineage).** *Mudga* (mung) is praised in the Ayurvedic nidana as the **lightest and most digestible of the pulses** — the convalescent's protein. It is *laghu* (light) and *śīta* (cooling) in effect, tridoshic but especially good for reducing *kapha* and *pitta*, and forms the base of *kitchari* and *yūṣa* (mung broth), the standard food for the sick, the fasting, and the recovering. Where other beans aggravate *vāta* and burden digestion, mung is the pulse that heals without loading.

**Tibetan / Sowa Rigpa stratum (SBJ — rGyud-bzhi).** The Tibetan medical tradition, drawing on the same Ayurvedic pulse-taxonomy, ranks mung among the light, cooling, easily-digested grains suitable for heat conditions and for the depleted — consistent with its Indian classification. (Attested at the level of the shared pulse-doctrine; a distinct Tibetan monograph is not independently cited here.)

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The doctrine to map is **"clears heat, relieves toxicity."** Read onto chemistry, "clearing heat" corresponds strikingly well to **antioxidant and anti-inflammatory action**: the mung bean's hull is dense in the flavones **vitexin and isovitexin**, plus other polyphenols, which quench reactive oxygen species and damp inflammatory signalling — the molecular correlate of a system that is "hot" (oxidatively and inflammatorily stressed). "Relieving toxicity" maps onto two real mechanisms: the flavonoid/polyphenol fraction's capacity to scavenge oxidants generated during toxic and febrile insult, and the bean's role as a bland, high-quality-protein aliment that supports a body under metabolic strain. The Ayurvedic "lightness" (*laghu*) is not mystical — it reflects a genuinely favourable digestibility: lower flatulence-oligosaccharide load than most pulses and high-quality, well-balanced protein, which is exactly why it is tolerated by the weak. The "cooling" quality is thus doctrinally sound as a *description of anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, easily-metabolised nutrition* — provided we do not inflate it into a literal antidote for chemical poisons. In Kenetica, mung is the **Cooling / Detoxification** anchor: the food that lets an over-fired system stand down.

There is a precise and instructive mapping in *where* the tradition locates the virtue. Chinese practice insists that to "clear heat and relieve toxicity" you should **not** discard the green skin — the potency is in the coat, and prolonged boiling that breaks the hull is sometimes said to lessen the heat-clearing power. This is a genuinely testable doctrinal claim, and the chemistry vindicates it: the antioxidant flavonoids vitexin and isovitexin are concentrated in the *hull*, not the cotyledon, so a preparation that preserves and extracts the seed coat carries the bulk of the measurable antioxidant and enzyme-inhibitory activity. The folk instruction and the HPLC map agree. This is the kind of convergence the codex exists to record: a pre-modern usage rule that turns out to track the real distribution of the active molecules. The counter-discipline is equally important — "relieves toxicity" in the classical sense meant heat-toxicity and pathogenic heat, and the modern antiviral and antioxidant data (below) are a *partial* molecular echo of that, not proof that mung soup neutralises ingested poisons. The doctrine is honoured where it maps and deflated where it over-reaches.

### Active Components

- **Flavone C-glycosides — vitexin and isovitexin** — the signature antioxidant flavonoids, concentrated in the seed coat/hull.
- **Phenolic acids and polyphenols** — gallic acid, catechin, condensed tannins; hull is the richest fraction.
- **High-quality protein** — globulins/albumins with a favourable amino-acid balance; the basis of its convalescent use.
- **Resistant starch and soluble fibre** — gentle, slow-releasing carbohydrate.
- **Micronutrients** — folate, magnesium, potassium.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Deployed as a **cooling, antioxidant, restorative food-medicine**: mung broth or soup to settle an over-heated, over-stressed, or convalescent system; the hull-rich preparation where the antioxidant flavonoid load is wanted. It is the counterweight to heating, stimulating protocols — used to bring a "hot" system back to baseline while still feeding it.

In practice the substance earns its place less by any dramatic single action than by a rare combination: it is simultaneously **bland enough for a compromised gut, dense enough in complete protein to rebuild, and rich enough in hull polyphenols to carry real antioxidant load.** Most foods offer one of those three; mung offers all three at once, which is precisely why every convalescent tradition that met it adopted it. In a Kenetica protocol it is the substance you reach for when the priority is *de-escalation without depletion* — cooling an inflamed, over-fired, or recovering system while continuing to nourish it, rather than starving it into calm. It pairs naturally with rice (as kitchari) to round out the amino-acid profile and slow the carbohydrate release, and its low aggravation of the digestive "wind" that heavier pulses provoke makes it the safe default pulse for the depleted, the elderly, and the young.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:
- **Antioxidant / anti-inflammatory / antidiabetic constituents.** Phytochemical mapping of mung bean found the **hull the most flavonoid-rich fraction — vitexin 37.43 mg/g and isovitexin 47.18 mg/g** — and showed the hull drives the whole grain's DPPH/ferric-reducing antioxidant activity, protease-inhibitory (anti-inflammatory) and aldose-reductase-inhibitory (anti-diabetic) effects *in vitro* (Luo et al., *Food Chem* 2016, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2016.01.101)). This is the direct chemical correlate of "clears heat, relieves toxicity."
- **Seed-coat polyphenols.** Pressurized-liquid extraction of mung bean seed coat yielded high total phenolics/flavonoids with strong ABTS radical-scavenging activity, with vitexin (130.5 mg/g) and isovitexin in the extract (Supasatyankul et al., *Molecules* 2022, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27072085)).
- **Antiviral / functional-food activity.** A *V. radiata* extract, its two major flavonoids identified as **vitexin and isovitexin**, inhibited influenza A virus at multiple steps of the viral cycle with no cytotoxicity up to 2,000 µg/mL in MDCK cells (Lo et al., *Front Pharmacol* 2020, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2020.584973)) — a modern reading of the "relieves toxicity / anti-pathogenic" tradition.

**Honest evidence grade.** The evidence base is **strong at the nutritional and preclinical (in vitro / mechanistic) level** and genuinely convergent — the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and anti-hyperglycaemic activities all track to real, quantified flavonoids concentrated exactly where tradition prizes the whole bean. But it is **largely preclinical**: robust human clinical trials for the specific "heat-clearing" and "detox" indications are lacking, and the folk claim of mung as an antidote to actual poisons or drug overdose is **not clinically validated** and must not be relied upon in an emergency. Treat mung as excellent supportive nutrition with real antioxidant chemistry — not as a proven detoxicant.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** Cold; sweet. Enters Heart and Stomach channels. Function: clear heat, relieve summer-heat, relieve toxicity (*qīng rè jiě dú*). Attested.
- **Ayurveda:** *Rasa* madhura (sweet) + slight kaṣāya (astringent); *guṇa* laghu (light), rūkṣa (dry); *vīrya* śīta (cooling); *vipāka* madhura (sweet). Reduces kapha and pitta; the lightest pulse for vāta tolerance. Attested.
- **Unani/Galenic:** Not attested for mung specifically in this codex's Galenic sources — do not transfer.

### Cultivation Register

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** No Unani/Galenic attestation for mung is carried in this codex's sources — the Traditional Energetic Classification above records it as absent and warns against transferring another pulse's humoral profile onto it. With no attestation of use, there is a fortiori no conservation-of-innate-heat framing, no *mufarrih* role, and no cultivation status to state. **(c) absent.** (This code appears on the tag line but the entry does not attest it — see the accompanying attestation flag.)

**Chinese — Bencao (SBJ).** *Lǜ dòu* 綠豆 is worked in Chinese materia medica for *qīng rè jiě dú* 清熱解毒 — clearing heat and relieving toxicity, the archetypal summer-heat food-remedy. But this is a therapeutic function, not a cultivation grade: mung is a food-legume that enters the bencao late (it is not one of the Han *Shénnóng Běncǎo Jīng* drug-entries), and no *san pin* 三品 grade is recorded for it in the sources retrieved. Within *yang sheng* 養生 — conservational, not curative — it therefore carries a therapeutic and dietetic standing only. **(b) framed:** attested and used, no *san pin* cultivation grade sourced.

**Ayurveda (SS).** *Mudga* is praised as the lightest and most digestible of the pulses — *laghu* and *śīta*, the convalescent's protein, the base of *kitchari* and *yūṣa*. This is a dietetic/therapeutic classification within *dravyaguna*, not a formal rejuvenative status: no *rasayana* or *vajikarana* assignment is recorded for mung in the sources retrieved. Its virtue is that it nourishes the depleted without loading them, which is a matter of digestibility, not a subtle-body cultivation claim. **(b) framed.**

**Tibetan — Sowa Rigpa (FMT).** Drawing on the shared Indian pulse-taxonomy, the Tibetan tradition ranks mung among the light, cooling, easily-digested grains suitable for heat conditions and the depleted; the identity here is by shared pulse-doctrine, not a distinct Tibetan monograph. Sowa Rigpa carries no formal "substances for subtle-body practice" category to invoke, and medicine is the third of its four treatment branches — it supports practice, it does not reach the root, which is a spiritual condition of the three *nyes pa*. Mung is thus a dietary support, with no cultivation status assigned. **(b) framed.**

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator uses mung to de-escalate without depleting — cooling an inflamed, over-fired, or convalescent system while still feeding it. It is not a longevity substance in any of these traditions and not, despite the folk reputation, a proven antidote to real poisons: "relieving toxicity" meant heat-toxicity, and the antioxidant chemistry (vitexin/isovitexin concentrated in the hull) is a partial molecular echo of that, not a detoxicant licence. Keep the green seed-coat — folk instruction and the HPLC map agree that the potency is in the hull. Eat the broth to bring a hot system back to baseline; do not lean on it in an emergency.

### Contraindications

- **Sprout safety — Salmonella / E. coli risk.** Raw mung-bean sprouts are a well-documented vehicle for *Salmonella* and enterohaemorrhagic *E. coli* outbreaks: the warm, humid sprouting environment favours pathogen growth. Immunocompromised people, pregnant women, young children and the elderly should **cook sprouts thoroughly** rather than eat them raw.
- Very large intakes may cause the usual legume-oligosaccharide flatulence, though less than most pulses.
- Cooling/heat-clearing framing should not delay conventional care for genuine poisoning, overdose, heatstroke or infection — mung is supportive food, not antidote.
