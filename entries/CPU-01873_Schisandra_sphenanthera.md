# Schisandra sphenanthera — Dhatig, da trig (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary by column position); "Chinese magnolia vine"; printed "Schisandra spenanthera"

## *Schisandra sphenanthera, as the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints it (OCR "Schisandra spenanthera", line 5217, as the second of five scientific names on that line) in its "D" run, which column order sets against the name Dhatig (da trig) and the English "Chinese magnolia vine"; Clark 1995 prints no Schisandra sphenanthera, and gives the same Tibetan name, as da.trig, Schizandra sphaerandra and a Rhus* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi (Four Tantras), read in two modern translations, Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011. The binomial comes from the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary's column order alone, and Clark's entry for the same Tibetan name prints a different Schisandra, so the botanical identity is open.** Both texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk found no *Schisandra sphenanthera*: Yang 1998 prints "Wu Wei (Fructus Schisandrae Chinensis)" at line 2465, a different species named in a different tradition. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP, confidence "medium") and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. **Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 257) the row is keyed by the scientific name "Schisandra spenanthera", with the locus "Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (line 5217)" and the note "Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". The key is not the first name of its line: line 5217 reads "Hippuris vulgaris Schisandra spenanthera Lepidium apetalum Arisaema j acquemontii Croton tinglium", and *Schisandra* is the second of the five. It is the third scientific name of the whole "D" run. The printed "spenanthera" is merged into *sphenanthera* as a misprint or OCR slip. The Tibetan name comes from the column order below and not from the CSV.

**The glossary, by column position.** `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "D" block (lines 5179 to 5261). The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. The phonetic names run from "Dali" (line 5181) to "Drita sazin" (line 5196) and number twenty-two when each name is counted separately: "Dali" first, "Dhambu kara" second, "Dhatig" third and "Dharyaken" fourth (both on line 5183), "Dhawa" fifth (line 5184). The transliterations (lines 5198 to 5213) begin "da lis", "'dam bu ka ra", "da trig dar ya kan" (line 5200), "dwa ba". The scientific names begin at line 5215 with "Rhododendron laudandum"; line 5217 follows with the five names above, then "Ricinus communis L." (5218), "Corydalis melanochlora" (5219) and "Cassia fistula" (5220). The English names (lines 5222 to 5229) read "-", "Chinese magnolia vine" (5223), "-", "Snake plant", "-", "Castor oil plant", "-", "Golden shower tree". Counted in order, "Dhatig" is the third phonetic name, "da trig" the third transliteration, *Schisandra spenanthera* the third scientific name and "Chinese magnolia vine" the third English name. The count is checked in three places against Clark: "Dhambu kara" is second against *Hippuris vulgaris* (Clark's *hdam.bu.ka.ra*, line 1057, prints "Hippuris vulgaris"), "Dharyaken" fourth against *Lepidium apetalum* (one of the names Clark prints for *dar.ya.kan*, inside the long line 1011), and "Dhawa" fifth against *Arisaema* (Clark's *dva.wa*, line 1056, prints *Arisaema intermedium* and others). The same run is counted in I_1307, I_1346 and I_1233. The glossary does not print the pairing on one row. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text on "Dhatig".* In chapter 19, "The Taste and the Post-Digestive Taste" (contents line 221), line 3205, page 186: "Sedu, larbu, Seyab, Kyurura, Gyashug, Dhatig, curd, buttermilk, chhang, yeast and other similar substances are the group of medicinal substances having a sour taste." In chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents line 225), line 3427, page 206: "Kapae, Dhaiig, Mak and Moen chhara, stop all forms of diarrhea irrespective of their being a hot or cold nature." In chapter 21, "The Group and the Method of Compounding Medicine" (contents line 229), line 3644, page 222: "Kapae, Bilwa, Dhatig, Moen chhara, Mak, Tharam, Naram, Gya kyeg, antelope's blood and Jhakang are the group of medicines that cure diarrhea." The index lists "Dhatig 186,206,222" (line 7321). The text gives no Latin name, description or taste in the entry sense. "Dhaiig" at line 3427 is read as "Dhatig", an OCR form, because the index pages (186, 206, 222) match the three lines. The English "Chinese magnolia vine" is the glossary's only description of the drug.

*Clark 1995, the same Tibetan name.* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, chapter 20 (heading at line 792), line 994: "da.trig: Schizandra: Schizandra sphaerandra Stapf. f. pallida Smith: Rhus semilata: [has seven names. It has sweet and sour tastes and grows in hot areas. The trunk is large and hard with grey bark, a small red flower and berries which resemble the eggs of sheep's lice.]" Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries; everything after the Latin names is inside the bracket, so the entry holds no root-layer sentence on an action. The action is in line 996, after the next entries: "Aegle marmelos, Schizandra, pine root and acorn halt all types of hot and cold diarrhoea." Clark also lists "Schizandra sphaerandra" among the sour medicines (chapter 19, line 766) and among the antidiarrhoeals (chapter 21, line 1222). The Men-Tsee-Khang sentence at line 3427 names "Kapae, Dhaiig, Mak and Moen chhara", which stand in the same order as Clark's *ka.ped* (991), *da.trig* (994), *smag* (995) and *mon.chha.ra* (996), with the same verdict. Neither text states that the two are one drug; the Codex reads them so from the spelling, the order and the action. [USER: verify]

**Identity: the glossary against Clark.** The glossary's *Schisandra* is *spenanthera*, read as *sphenanthera*; Clark's is *sphaerandra*, with a bare genus and a *Rhus* beside it. The two species names differ. Whether they are two identifications of one Tibetan drug or two distinct plants the Codex has not checked. The row stands for the Tibetan drug and not for one species, and the Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Schisandra sphenanthera*, *spenanthera*, *da.trig*, *Dhatig* and "Chinese magnolia vine" found no entry on this binomial. **I_1436** (*Schisandra sphaerandra*, tag FMT) is the sibling entry on the same Tibetan drug, keyed to Clark's binomial and resting on the same lines; the two are counted separately because their spine keys differ and are not merged. **I_215** (*Schisandra chinensis*, tags SBJ and FMT) is the entry on a different species, built on Yang 1998 (Wu Wei, line 2465); its FMT paragraph cites no Clark or Men-Tsee-Khang line, and I did not find *da.trig* or *Dhatig* in it. **I_1307** (*Lepidium apetalum*), **I_1346** (*Rhododendron laudandum*), **I_1233** (*Arisaema intermedium*) and **I_1259** (*Corydalis melanochlora*) quote line 5217 for their own plants, and **I_1416** (*Quercus semecarpifolia*) and **I_1300** (*Hippophae tibetana*) quote the diarrhoea and sour-taste lines. ### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. As a general fact *Schisandra* is a genus of woody vines (family Schisandraceae) bearing clusters of red berries, and species of the genus are known to contain lignans in the fruit. That is a statement about the genus and not a finding of the Tibetan texts, which do not say which species, which part or which preparation is meant. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Men-Tsee-Khang text records two things for "Dhatig": it has a sour taste (line 3205) and it stops all forms of diarrhoea, hot or cold (lines 3427 and 3644). Clark's entry for the same Tibetan name gives the same action (line 996). That is the tradition's own record and not a Codex protocol. The identity is open, so the Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** a sour taste, in the root-layer taste group (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3205). Clark's commentary layer (inside the bracket, line 994) prints sweet and sour tastes for *da.trig*. No potency or post-digestive taste is printed for the drug in either text.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Both translations place the drug in the materia medica as a sour-tasting medicine that stops diarrhoea (Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3205, 3427, 3644; Clark lines 766, 996, 1222). That is a therapeutic place and not a status in rejuvenation or in *bcud len* (essence extraction); I found no "bcud" in either text on disk. The rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23: Clark lines 1264 to 1276; Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3904 to 3945) does not name the drug. It tells the person on the course to "avoid rotten and sour foods" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3931; Clark line 1275 has "avoid rotten, sour, unwholesome foods"); the rule is about foods in general and does not mention this drug, but the drug is in the sour group of the taste chapter, and the Codex notes the juxtaposition without reading the rule as barring it. [USER: verify] The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this drug on the strength of the texts. It is a sour medicine in a list with bael, pine root and acorn that stops diarrhoea, and the one rule about sour things in the rejuvenation chapter is to avoid them during the course. Nothing here supports treating a Schisandra berry as a rejuvenative on the strength of this entry.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for the drug. The identity is open, so no plant should be gathered or taken on the strength of this entry. A medicine that stops diarrhoea is not a treatment for infection or dehydration, and the texts' own regimen puts it under a physician. No conservation information and no safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
