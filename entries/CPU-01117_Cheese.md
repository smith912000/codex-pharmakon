# Cheese (new) — Turos Nearos (Dioscorides 2-79), with Hippace (2-80)
## *New cheese, as the translator headed it ("SUGGESTED: New Cheese"); a milk product made by separating the curd from the whey. With it, folded in as a variety, the "horse cheese" of 2-80. No animal is named for the cheese of 2-79 in the lines read* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The English is the 2000 edition of Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). New cheese is Book 2, chapter 79, headed *TUROS NEAROS*, with the translator's heading "New Cheese", under "Living Creatures" (line 5254; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` locus "2-79 (line 5254)"). I read the whole chapter. It says, in the order printed:

- "New cheese eaten without salt is nourishing, good for the stomach, easy to digest, increasing the flesh [weight gain] and mildly softening the bowels."
- "Some is better than the other, according to the nature of the milk from which it is made."
- "Boiled and strained out, then roasted, it is astringent in the intestines, and applied it is good for inflammation and bruises of the eyes."
- "New salted cheese is more nourishing and if eaten is good for shrinking of the flesh [weight loss]. It is bad for the stomach, upsetting the intestines and the bowels."
- "That which is older is therapeutic for the intestines, and the whey that is made along with cheese is very good nourishment for dogs."

Two points need care. The English says that salted new cheese is "more nourishing" and also "good for shrinking of the flesh [weight loss]"; the bracketed gloss is the translator's, and the two statements sit uneasily together. The printed text is followed here and no resolution is offered. [USER: verify] Second, the chapter does not say which animal's milk is meant. It says only that cheeses differ "according to the nature of the milk from which it is made", and does not rank the milks.

The chapter belongs to a run of milk chapters in Book 2, which I read for context: milk (2-75, *Gala*, line 5234), whey (*Orros galaktos*, 2-76, line 5241: "All milk has whey contained within it"), and *Gala schiston* (2-77, line 5244), which tells how milk is parted by boiling it in a new ceramic jar, stirring with a fresh fig-branch and adding "a wine-cup of vinegar and honey for every half-pint of milk", "thus the whey is parted from the cheesy matter". That is how the text understands the making of the curd. Cheese also appears incidentally elsewhere: goat's fat is given "boiled with polenta, rhoe [4-64] and cheese for dysentery" (2-94, line 5344); wrapped in the leaves of the second cyclamen, cheese "is kept from putrefying" (2-195, line 6266); and the milky juice of a spurge is pickled "mixing lepidium [2-205] and bruised cheese" (4-165, line 9528). These are uses of cheese as an ingredient or as a thing kept, and are not statements about its properties.

### Varieties

**Hippace — horse cheese** (DMM; 2-80, line 5261; `names.csv` locus "2-80 (line 5261)"). The translator's heading is "SUGGESTED: Horse Cheese". The whole chapter is: "That which they call hippace is horse cheese. It has a poisonous smell yet is very nourishing and very similar to that made from pigs' milk. Some have called the horse's rennet by the name of hippace." The English says "horse cheese" and "the horse's rennet"; it does not say "mare" (the working name "mare's-milk cheese" is the batch's own wording, and the word "milk" is not printed for the horse). It is a cheese of the horse, with a bad smell, "very nourishing", and like a pig's-milk cheese, and the name *hippace* is also said to be used by some for the horse's rennet. No use against any complaint is given for it. The chapter sits after the new cheese and before butter (2-81, *Buturon*); it is a variety of cheese by source animal, and the text separates it on that ground.

*Synthesis of the varieties.* The text separates its cheeses on several grounds, and only the last is a separate kind. For the new cheese of 2-79 it separates by age and salt (new, new and salted, older) and by the heat-treatment (boiled, strained and roasted), and it says that cheeses differ "according to the nature of the milk" without naming a milk or ranking any. For *hippace* (2-80) it separates by source animal, the horse, and gives it a bad smell and a high nourishing value, compared with cheese from pigs' milk. The text ranks none of its cheeses first. The one quality the two chapters share is nourishment ("nourishing" for new cheese, "more nourishing" for the salted, "very nourishing" for the horse cheese). The rennet of the horse, named as a second sense of *hippace*, is a different material from a cheese, and the chapter does not settle the point. Other rennets (hare, fawn, seal) are in 2-85 (*Pitua*), outside this entry.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, cheese is the curd of milk: casein and other milk proteins, milk fat, calcium and phosphate, with salt added in salted cheeses and, in aged or ripened cheeses, a changed protein and fat profile and, in some, living microbial cultures. Fresh cheeses retain much of the moisture; salted and aged ones are denser and saltier. Whey, which the text says is separated, holds the whey proteins, lactose and soluble minerals. No quantities are given here because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records new cheese as a food (nourishing, good for the stomach, easy to digest, "increasing the flesh"), and as a roasted dressing for inflamed and bruised eyes; it records new salted cheese as bad for the stomach; it records older cheese as therapeutic for the intestines. Cheese is an ordinary food and needs no protocol from this Codex. The Codex does not recommend the eye use, which is unsafe by modern standards, and offers no regimen built on cheese.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations the text gives: new cheese eaten without salt; new cheese eaten salted; cheese "boiled and strained out, then roasted" and applied to eyes that are inflamed or bruised; older cheese taken for the intestines. For the horse cheese, none. The text gives no amount, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Cheese is a concentrated source of milk protein, fat and calcium, and salted cheeses are high in sodium. People with cow's-milk-protein allergy must avoid it; those with lactose intolerance usually tolerate hard, aged cheeses better than fresh ones. Soft, fresh and unpasteurised cheeses can carry *Listeria*, *Salmonella* and other bacteria. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** 2-79 prints these qualities and actions, with no temperature or moisture grade: new unsalted cheese is nourishing, good for the stomach, easy to digest, increases the flesh, mildly softening to the bowels; roasted, it is astringent in the intestines; new salted cheese is more nourishing, bad for the stomach, and upsetting to the intestines and bowels; older cheese is therapeutic for the intestines. 2-80 prints "very nourishing" and "a poisonous smell" for horse cheese. No further classification is supplied here.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What the chapter offers is a short discipline of attention to the same food in different states: unsalted against salted, new against older, raw against boiled and roasted, and the milk it came from, each with a different effect on the stomach and bowels. It assigns no cultivation status. The horse cheese's "poisonous smell" is a sensory warning given in the chapter and is not a sign of any spiritual quality. The chapters record no amuletic use of cheese.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here makes cheese a cultivation substance. The text's lesson is ordinary and practical: the same food does different things new or old, plain or salted, and the one fault it names, salt upsetting the stomach, is worth taking at face value. A cultivator eats cheese as food and pays attention to what a given cheese does. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Allergy and intolerance:** milk-protein allergy contraindicates all cheese; lactose intolerance varies with the cheese.
- **Hygiene and infection:** unpasteurised and soft cheeses carry a risk of *Listeria* and other bacteria; pregnancy, old age and weakened immunity raise the consequences.
- **Salt:** salted cheeses are high in sodium and unsuitable, in quantity, in hypertension and kidney or heart disease; the text itself says salted new cheese is bad for the stomach.
- **Eye use:** do not apply roasted or any other cheese to the eyes.
- **Horse cheese:** the text itself gives it a "poisonous smell"; no modern source of it is vouched for here.
- **Diet:** the saturated fat in many cheeses matters in lipid disorders and cardiovascular disease.
