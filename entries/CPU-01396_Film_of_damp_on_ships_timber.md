# Film of damp on ships' timber — "The-Film-of-Dampness-which-is-found-on-the-Wood-of-Ships" (Bryan's English rendering of the Ebers drug name; the Egyptian word is not given)
## *A damp film scraped from the wood of ships, listed by Bryan among the mineral remedies and glossed by him "(mould)" in the one recipe where it is used; the source gives no Egyptian name and no further identification* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The locus is Bryan's 1930 translation of the Ebers Papyrus (*The Papyrus Ebers*, C. P. Bryan), Chapter V "Mineral Remedies", the alphabetical drug list (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` lines 1165-1166, printed page 21; name-index row `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` line 12, class mineral, source "Bryan's English identification"). The list prints the name across two lines: "The-Film-of-Dampness-which-is-Found- / on-the-Wood-of-Ships". It sits between "Dough" and "Granite" in Bryan's alphabetical order (the list is alphabetical by Bryan's English head word, not by any Egyptian word).

Bryan's introduction to the chapter says that of the substances grouped as mineral "few of them are prescribed in their crude or native state", that "Antimony, Calamine, Granite, Ink, Indigo, and Sulphur almost exhaust the list", and that for the rest "the source of origin of the drugs is strictly laid down" (line 1137; his examples are Iron-from-Apollonopolis-parva, Leather-from-the-Sandal-maker, Soot-from-the-bet'a-pot and Soda-from-Upper-Egypt). The film of damp on ships' timber is one of those hyphenated drug names that give the source with the material. The same chapter says that the list does not exhaust the mineral drugs, since many others are "completely hidden from us under the guise of meaningless words" (line 1233).

**Where the drug is used.** A search of the whole Bryan text found the name in three places only:

1. The list entry above (lines 1165-1166).
2. Bryan's overview of the papyrus's preparations (page 33; line 1381), where he gives it as one of the "ingenious" mixtures: "another equally useful draught prepared by whipping up the Film-of-Dampness-which-is-found-on-the-Wood-of-Ships, in Froth-of-Beer, for Falling of the Womb". The wording there (a draught, whipped up in beer froth) is Bryan's paraphrase, and the tone ("equally useful") is his own, not the papyrus's.
3. The recipe itself in Bryan's chapter XIV "Diseases of Women" (page 86; lines 2566-2567):

"REMEDY TO ALLOW THE WOMB OF A WOMAN TO SLIP INTO ITS PLACE The-Film-of-Dampness-which-is-found-on-the-Wood-of-Ships (mould) / Rub in Yeast-of-Fermented-Beer and let her drink it."

The parenthesis "(mould)" is Bryan's, not part of the drug name. In the recipe the drug is combined with "Yeast-of-Fermented-Beer" and is to be drunk ("let her drink it"); the recipe is the first in a run of remedies for a displaced womb, the others using wax-ibis fumes, dried excrement fumes and "Oil-of-the-Earth (? Petroleum)" with manure in honey (lines 2569-2574). The wording "Rub in" is as Bryan prints it; the sources retrieved do not say what is rubbed into what, nor how much.

No Egyptian word, transliteration, hieroglyph or papyrus column number is given in the Bryan text for this drug, and none is supplied here. No recitation or spell is printed with the recipe in Bryan's rendering; whether the papyrus attaches one is not known from the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

**Identity.** Two things in Bryan's own text pull in different directions. The list files the drug as a mineral remedy; the recipe glosses it "(mould)", which is a biological film, not a mineral. Bryan gives no argument for either. What the Egyptian word meant is not shown by the sources retrieved, and the identification is, in the terms of the project's rule on Ebers, a modern hypothesis. The list row for this drug is also absent from `_sources/ebers.csv` (searched for "damp", "ship" and "film"), so the Codex source list carries no identification flag for it. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None can be stated from the text. If the film was a mould or other growth on damp wood, as Bryan's "(mould)" suggests, it would be a living or recently living surface growth of fungi, bacteria or algae with wood debris, and its content would vary with the wood, the water and the season. This is a general statement about films on wet timber, not a reading of the papyrus, and no composition is asserted.

---

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The one recipe records the drug in a drink, with beer yeast, for a prolapse or displacement of the womb. The material is a surface growth scraped from timber, of unknown composition; mould can be allergenic and can carry toxins, and a displaced womb is a medical condition. The Codex supplies no modern protocol.

---

### Preparation & Dosing

One preparation is given: the film is taken with "Yeast-of-Fermented-Beer" ("Rub in Yeast-of-Fermented-Beer and let her drink it", line 2567); Bryan's overview describes it as a draught made by "whipping up" the film "in Froth-of-Beer" (line 1381). The two renderings are not the same preparation as printed, and the sources retrieved do not say which better reflects the papyrus. No quantity, ratio, frequency or duration is given in either. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's vital constitution (the *ka*, *ba*, *akh* and *ib* are not addressed in the lines read). The only use recorded here is for a displaced womb. In Egyptian practice a spell and a drug are components of one intervention (*heka*), but no spell is printed with this recipe in Bryan's rendering, and the identity of the material is itself a modern hypothesis.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to take from it for cultivation. It is a single recipe in a translator's rendering for a gynaecological complaint, with a drug that Bryan himself glosses as mould. It is not a recommended practice. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Mould and surface growths:** swallowing scrapings from wet timber risks allergy, infection and toxin exposure.
- **Unidentified material:** the material and its Egyptian name are uncertain; nothing here should be sourced or used on the strength of the English name.
- **Serious conditions:** a displaced or prolapsed womb, or pelvic pain and bleeding, needs medical assessment. Pregnancy is a further reason to take nothing unidentified.
