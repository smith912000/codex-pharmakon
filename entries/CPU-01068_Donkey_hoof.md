# Donkey hoof — Onuches Onon (Ass's Hooves) / bong.buhi.rmig.pa (hoof of a black donkey)
## *The hoof of the domestic donkey or ass, burnt or otherwise prepared* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM, FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-44, *Onuches Onon*, "Ass's Hooves" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 5114). The whole chapter: "Two spoonfuls of an ass's hoof that has been burnt (taken in a drink daily for many days) are said to cure epilepsy. Steeped in oil they dissolve scrofulous tumours [glandular swelling, goitres]; sprinkled on they heal chilblains." The chapter sits in the run of animal parts in book 2: 2-41 lungs of fox (entry III_20), 2-42 ass's liver, 2-45 "Spavins of Horses" (the "hardened substance which grows at their knees and hooves", pounded and drunk with vinegar, said to cure epilepsy), 2-46 "Goats' Hooves" (burnt and rubbed on with vinegar to cure baldness), and 2-47 goat's liver (entry III_21). Dioscorides's wording "are said to cure" marks the epilepsy claim as report, not his own finding; the translation keeps that hedge.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** In Clark's translation of the Explanatory Tantra ch.20, under "Nails" (the ninth of the thirteen animal medicines), after the crocodile claw: "bong.buhi.rmig.pa: the (right) hoof of (a one-year-old black) Donkey: cures urinary retention [and infectious fevers]. Horse's hoof: removes tumors [and is beneficial against nagas]. Horse's ankle bone: cures anterior transposition of the calf muscle [and subdues nagas]." (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 1188; `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` row for *bong.buhi.rmig.pa*, line 228 of that file). The brackets in Clark's translation mark words drawn from the commentary and not the root verse. Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 gives the shorter form: "Donkey hooves cure obstruction of urine, whereas horse hooves cure tumors and the fetlock cures cramps of the calf muscle" (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 3556). The Tibetan text specifies which hoof (the right), which donkey (one year old, black), which is a specification that neither the Greek nor the Men-Tsee-Khang version has. The spine's rGyud-bzhi row is `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 370, "Donkey hooves", id uncertain: yes.

### Active Components

A hoof is a keratinised structure, made mostly of keratin (a fibrous protein rich in sulphur-containing amino acids), with some lipids and mineral. Burning (as Dioscorides directs) converts the organic part to ash and char, leaving mostly mineral matter such as calcium and phosphate compounds. The texts name no active component. No quantities are given here beyond Dioscorides's spoonfuls, because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources record hoof for epilepsy, glandular swellings and chilblains (Dioscorides) and for urinary retention and infectious fevers (Tibetan). The Codex has no protocol for it, and the reasons are plain: epilepsy and infectious fever are conditions for medical care; urinary retention can be an emergency; burnt animal tissue has no established effect on any of them; and using the hoof means killing the animal or taking hooves from a dead one. The Dioscorides entry is best read as an example of how ancient authors passed on an animal-part remedy with the hedge "are said to".

### Preparation & Dosing

Dioscorides gives a dose and a method: "Two spoonfuls of an ass's hoof that has been burnt (taken in a drink daily for many days)" for epilepsy; "steeped in oil" for scrofulous swellings; "sprinkled on" (as a powder) for chilblains (2-44). The Tibetan text specifies the animal, a black donkey one year old, and the part, the right hoof; no method or dose is given in the lines read. No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Keratin is a structural protein, not known to have any systemic medicinal action when swallowed. Burnt animal material is mostly ash and mineral. No anticonvulsant effect of burnt hoof is established. Epilepsy is today treated with anticonvulsant medicines, and the Greek "falling sickness" is the same condition in general terms, though ancient descriptions were wider. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** None is printed for the hoof in 2-44; the chapter gives actions only.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The translated lines print actions only (urinary retention, infectious fevers), with no taste, potency or humour assignment. The same section prints "nagas" (an endnote of Clark's at line 1521 glosses nagas as serpent-spirits; I did not check that it belongs to this chapter) as something horse hoof and horse ankle bone act against; the donkey hoof line does not carry that remark.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. What the chapter offers a reader is a discipline of attention, even in so short a text: the part is named exactly, the preparation is given (burnt), the dose is given (two spoonfuls), and the course is given (daily for many days), and the claim is hedged ("are said to"). The chapter assigns no status beyond the therapeutic.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The hoof is a minor entry in the Explanatory Tantra's "nails" among the thirteen animal medicines, given for urinary retention and, in Clark's brackets, for infectious fevers. The text is exact about the animal (black, one year old) and the part (the right hoof), which is the sort of specification the tradition attaches to its materia medica, but the lines read do not say why. The animal-medicine lines read name no essence-extraction (*bcud len*) use, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition; none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do. In both traditions this is a drug for a complaint, taken for a short, defined purpose, and in the Greek text the claim is hearsay. A cultivator's reading is the attention to specification: which animal, which part, how prepared, for how long.

### Contraindications

- **Epilepsy.** Do not substitute burnt hoof for anticonvulsant medicine; stopping prescribed medicine can cause seizures. Seizures and urinary retention need medical care.
- **Infection.** Hooves from an unknown animal can carry soil organisms, including tetanus spores and anthrax spores (in regions where anthrax occurs). Do not put burnt or raw hoof on open chilblains or wounds. [USER: verify specifics]
- **Dose unknown.** The only dose is Dioscorides's, and its unit is not defined.
- **Ethics and law.** Using hoof means killing the animal or taking hooves from one that has died; donkeys are protected by animal-welfare law in many places.
