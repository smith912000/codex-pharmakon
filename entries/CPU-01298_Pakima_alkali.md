# Pakima alkali — Pakima (Kshara) as printed in Bhishagratna's Sushruta Samhita
## *An alkali (kshara) of the Sushruta group of alkalis, named "Pakima" in Bhishagratna's 1907 English translation; the translator glosses the other alkalis of the list (carbonate of potash, borax) but gives no gloss for this one, so its identity is not fixed in the sources retrieved* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The locus given for this entry is `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt`, line 2089 (Sutrasthana, chapter 46, "the Chapter which treats of food and drink", line 4; the same text is in `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/Bhishagratna_Vol1_djvu.txt`, line 21749). The chapter is Sushruta's account of food and drink, and it opens with a statement that food "forms the chief source of their bodily strength and complexion, as well as of the albuminous (Ojas) principle in their organisms" (lines 10-12), and that the substances of food act "with their specific tastes, virtues, potencies and reactionary or chemical transformations" on the humours and the fundamental principles of the body (lines 15-19). The group of salts, then the group of alkalis, then the metals come in that run.

The passage reads (lines 2087-2095, 2110-2113): "The group of Alkalis.—The different varieties of alkali (Kshara) such as the Javakshara (Carbonate of potash), the Svarjikakshara, the Pakima and the Tankana cure abdominal glands, piles, mesenteric diarrhoea, and gravel and stone in the bladder. All these varieties of alkali should be regarded as possessed of digestant or stomachic properties. Their abuse is known to have brought about cases of haemoptysis." And, after the properties of Javakshara, Svarjikakshara and Usharakshara: "The variety called Pakima tends to reduce obesity, causes a free and increased discharge of urine, thus fully relieving the bladder of all its contents (lit: bladder-cleanser)." The OCR of the sutra text prints the name once as "Pakima" (line 2089) and once as "P^kima" (line 2111); this entry follows the clean spelling. The next alkali, Tankanakshara, is glossed by the translator as borax (line 2114).

So the text gives Pakima two kinds of statement. As one of four alkalis it shares in the group's cures (abdominal glands, piles, mesenteric diarrhoea, gravel and stone in the bladder) and in its warning of abuse (haemoptysis). On its own, it "tends to reduce obesity" and acts as a diuretic and "bladder-cleanser". The neighbouring statements about strength and semen belong to other alkalis, not to Pakima: Javakshara and Svarjikakshara are "anti-spermatopoietic" (line 2100) and Usharakshara is "detrimental to the strength of the body" (lines 2108-2110). These are not transferred to Pakima.

A second locus turns up when the name is searched for in the rest of the Sushruta chapters on disk: `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/chikitsa/SS_Chikitsa_ch19.txt`, line 407 (and `Bhishagratna_Vol2_djvu.txt`, line 19823). Chapter 19 of the Chikitsasthana treats hernia, hydrocele, scrotal tumour, diseases of the genital organ and elephantiasis (*Slipada*; lines 3-6). In the treatment of *Slipada* the text gives a potion of mustard oil or of leaf juice, then continues: "In the same way the juice of Putranjivaka should be prescribed by a physician after a due consideration as to the strength of the patient and of the time. The same juice (i.e., of Putranjivaka) should be taken along with the juice of the bulbs of Kechuka with Pakima (vit) salt. 26." (lines 399-407). Here "Pakima" qualifies a salt, and the translation adds "(vit)". In chapter 46 a different salt, "Vida salt", is described as "a little alkaline (in its taste)" (lines 2048-2049, a few lines before the alkalis), but the sources read do not say that the "Pakima" of chapter 19, the "Pakima" alkali of chapter 46 and Vida salt are one substance. ### Active Components

No text read names a constituent of Pakima. In general terms, the alkalis of Ayurveda (*kshara*) are ash-derived or mineral alkaline salts, and the translator glosses two of its companions in the list as carbonate of potash (Javakshara) and borax (Tankana). No such gloss is given for Pakima, so what it was chemically cannot be said from the sources. Alkaline salts in general are caustic or irritant to tissue in strong solution, and carbonates and borates differ in their toxicity. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved say that Sushruta listed Pakima among the alkalis for abdominal glands, piles, mesenteric diarrhoea and stone, as a reducer of obesity and as a diuretic, and used a "Pakima (vit) salt" with plant juices in elephantiasis. The substance cannot be identified from the sources, alkalis are caustic and can cause serious harm when swallowed, and no part of the sources supports a modern recommendation. The text itself warns that abuse of the alkalis has led to haemoptysis.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources retrieved give one preparation involving Pakima: in the treatment of *Slipada*, the juice of the bulbs of *Kechuka* taken with "Pakima (vit) salt", after the juice of *Putranjivaka* (Chikitsa ch.19, lines 402-407). The quantity of the salt, the amount of juice and the duration are not given in these lines; the phrase "according to his capacity" in line 401 qualifies the preceding potion of mustard oil or leaf juice and is not a dose for the salt. In chapter 46 no preparation is given at all, only the properties. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Alkaline salts such as carbonates and borates are irritants to the gut and to tissue, and some (borates) are toxic when swallowed in quantity; potassium and sodium salts can disturb body fluids and electrolytes. Diuretics reduce fluid and can cause dehydration and salt loss. No alkaline salt has been shown to treat obesity. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Ayurveda (SS):** the group statement gives all four alkalis "digestant or stomachic properties" (lines 2092-2094). For Pakima alone the text prints actions (it reduces obesity, increases urine; lines 2110-2113) but no *rasa*, no *virya* (potency) and no *vipaka* (post-digestive taste). The heat-making potency printed for Usharakshara and the dryness and sharp potency printed for Tankana belong to those alkalis and are not transferred. No other tradition is tagged.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Pakima is named in the food-and-drink chapter, among the salts, alkalis and metals that Sushruta lists for the qualities they impart to food and body (chapter 46, lines 10-19, 2087-2113). That chapter links food in general to the *ojas* principle (lines 10-12), but it does not link Pakima to *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana*, and the passage read is not a *rasayana* (rejuvenation) or *vajikarana* (generative vitality) passage. No *shodhana* (purification) of Pakima is described in the lines read. The text's own caution on alkalis, that "their abuse is known to have brought about cases of haemoptysis", is the opposite of a cultivation claim, and Sushruta says elsewhere in the same list that another alkali, Usharakshara, is "detrimental to the strength of the body", which is not said of Pakima. The *sattva*, *rajas* and *tamas* categories are Samkhya and are not in the passage.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. Pakima is an alkali used in Sushruta's therapeutics for the bladder, the bowels and obesity, and a strong alkali is not food. The honest use for a reader is to know that the text records it, that its identity is uncertain, and that the same passage warns against abuse. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Contraindications

- **Caustic and irritant:** alkalis burn the mouth, throat and gut when swallowed in strength; do not swallow an unidentified alkaline salt.
- **Abuse:** the text itself reports haemoptysis from abuse of the alkalis (lines 2094-2095).
- **Diuretic effect:** forced fluid loss is dangerous in dehydration, kidney disease, heart disease and pregnancy.
- **Bladder stone, piles, diarrhoea, abdominal masses:** these need clinical diagnosis; bleeding, severe pain or retention of urine is an emergency.
- **Weight loss:** a caustic or diuretic is not a safe way to lose weight.
- **Identification:** "Pakima" is Bhishagratna's rendering, with no gloss; the chapter-19 "Pakima (vit) salt" may or may not be the same substance as the chapter-46 alkali.
