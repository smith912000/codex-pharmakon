# I-189 · Dang Gui (Angelica)
**Angelica sinensis** (Oliv.) Diels | Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Angelica sinensis* (Oliv.) Diels (1900)  
**Common names (English):** Dang Gui, Chinese angelica, female ginseng, dong quai  
**Traditional names:**
- **Dang Gui (当归):** Multiple interpretations: "should return" or "proper return" — *dang* = should/ought, *gui* = return; one folk explanation: prescribed for women separated from husbands ("this herb makes you return to health"); another: "the blood returns to its proper place"
- **"Female Ginseng" (女参):** Reflects its status as the primary woman's tonic in TCM — comparable prestige to ginseng for male tonification
- **Dong Quai:** Romanized Cantonese pronunciation, used in Western herbal markets
- Japanese: *Tōki* (当帰); Korean: *Danggwi* (당귀)

**Critical species distinctions:**
- *A. sinensis*: Dang Gui; Sichuan/Gansu province origin; the TCM pharmacopoeial herb
- *A. acutiloba*: Japanese angelica (Tōki); similar use; slightly different phytochemistry
- *A. dahurica* (Bai Zhi 白芷): different herb — for headache, sinus; NOT a blood tonic
- *A. pubescens* (Du Huo 独活): different herb — for wind-cold-damp painful obstruction
- *Angelica archangelica* (European angelica): different chemical profile; bitter tonic, carminative
- These five *Angelica* species are used in different TCM/Japanese formulas for completely different indications — species identity critical; not interchangeable

**Root part distinction (clinically significant):**
- **Whole root (quan gui 全归):** Full action — tonify and move blood
- **Root head (gui tou 归头):** Primarily tonifies blood
- **Root body (gui shen 归身):** Primarily nourishes blood
- **Root tail (gui wei 归尾):** Primarily invigorates blood circulation, breaks stasis
- This part-specific action reflects real pharmacological differences (different ferulic acid, Z-ligustilide concentrations)

**Botanical morphology:**
- Perennial herb to 1m; compound umbellate white flowers (Apiaceae characteristic); pinnately compound leaves; furrowed, hollow stems; taproot with multiple branching fibrous rootlets
- Root harvested autumn of 3rd year; processed by slow drying or slight fumigation

**Cross-traditional pharmacological convergence:**

The core application — emmenagogue + blood tonic + gynecological regulation — is confirmed in multiple traditions using *Angelica* genus members. The convergence is at the genus level (Apiaceae, phthalide/ferulic acid chemistry) rather than the species level, but the pharmacological action (uterotonic + blood-moving + anti-inflammatory) is consistently identified across cultures that had access to any *Angelica* species.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**The most consistent gynecological herb across traditions:**

No single herb appears more consistently in cross-cultural gynecological pharmacology than members of the *Angelica*/Apiaceae cluster. Whether it is *A. sinensis* in China, *A. archangelica* in Europe, *A. acutiloba* in Japan, or *Ligusticum* in South America — the phthalide/ferulic acid chemical platform produces similar uterotonic, emmenagogue, and anti-spasmodic effects that were independently identified across traditions.

**Z-Ligustilide and phthalide pharmacology:**

The shared pharmacological action across *Angelica* species is driven primarily by the phthalide class of compounds — Z-ligustilide (also called *n*-butylidenephthalide) being the most important. This compound occurs across Apiaceae in varying concentrations: celery (*Apium graveolens*), lovage (*Levisticum officinale*), chuanxiong (*Ligusticum chuanxiong*), and *Angelica* species all contain structurally related phthalides with shared smooth muscle effects.

**Blood tonic vs. blood mover — the TCM distinction:**

Dang Gui is unique in TCM as the only herb simultaneously categorized as both "nourishing blood" (yang xue) AND "invigorating blood circulation" (huo xue). Most herbs are one or the other:
- Shu Di Huang: nourishes blood but does not move it
- Dan Shen (*Salvia miltiorrhiza*): moves blood but does not nourish it
- Dang Gui: does both simultaneously

This dual action corresponds to: ferulic acid component = blood-building (erythropoietic, anti-anemic) + Z-ligustilide component = blood-moving (uterotonic, vasodilatory, anti-spasmodic). The TCM conceptual split is pharmacochemically real.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Primary active compounds:**

**1. Phthalides (Primary active — "blood moving" component)**
- **Z-Ligustilide (Z-butylidenephthalide):** PRIMARY phthalide; 45–70% of volatile oil; 0.5–1.2% total root
  - Smooth muscle relaxation: uterus, coronary arteries, airways
  - Anti-nociceptive: reduces uterine contraction pain
  - Vasodilatory: NO-independent and NO-dependent pathways
  - Volatile; thermolabile — lost significantly in prolonged decoction
- **E-Ligustilide:** Geometric isomer; less active than Z form
- **Butylidenephthalide, dihydroligustilide:** Related phthalides; smooth muscle effects

**2. Ferulic Acid and Derivatives (Primary active — "blood nourishing" component)**
- **Ferulic acid (4-hydroxy-3-methoxycinnamic acid):** 0.05–0.15% dry weight
  - Antioxidant (strong DPPH scavenger)
  - Platelet aggregation inhibition (thromboxane A2 synthesis inhibition)
  - Erythropoietic: stimulates erythroid differentiation in bone marrow
  - Anti-inflammatory: COX inhibition; reduction of inflammatory cytokines
- **Feruloylsucrose:** water-soluble ferulic acid conjugate; important in aqueous extraction (tea/decoction)
- **Angeloylsugars:** ferulic acid sugar esters; bioavailable precursors

**3. Polysaccharides (Dang Gui Polysaccharides — DAPS)**
- Immunomodulatory; hematopoietic support
- 15–25% dry weight
- TLR4 agonism; macrophage activation

**4. Phytosterols and Vitamins**
- β-sitosterol, stigmasterol
- Vitamin B12 (rare in plants — small amount)
- Folic acid: relevant to hematological claims

**5. Coumarins**
- Osthole, oxypeucedanin, psoralen (trace)
- Spasmolytic; CNS mild effects
- Photosensitization (minor at standard doses)

**Volatile oil composition:**
Z-ligustilide (primary) + α-pinene + β-phellandrene + β-caryophyllene + 70+ identified volatiles
Processing note: wine-frying (jiu chao) reduces volatile phthalide loss vs. raw decoction; different preparations capture different compound ratios

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**1. Uterotonic and Utero-relaxant (Z-Ligustilide — Dose-Dependent)**
- Z-ligustilide exhibits biphasic uterine action:
  - **Low dose:** Uterine smooth muscle relaxation → antispasmodic → reduces dysmenorrhea
  - **High dose:** Uterine smooth muscle contraction → oxytocic → emmenagogue
- Mechanism: Ca²⁺ channel modulation (VGCCs + IP3R-dependent Ca²⁺ release)
- At therapeutic doses in non-pregnant uterus: primarily relaxant (reduces cramps)
- At higher doses or in pregnant uterus: contractile → ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION IN PREGNANCY

**2. Hematopoiesis and Anti-anemia (Ferulic Acid + DAPS)**
- Erythroid progenitor stimulation: DAPS → EPO sensitization + direct erythropoietic signaling
- Ferulic acid: promotes G1/S transition in erythroid precursors
- Iron utilization enhancement
- Traditional "nourishes blood" → measurable erythropoietic effects (most validated)

**3. Cardiovascular (Ferulic Acid + Z-Ligustilide)**
- Platelet aggregation inhibition: ferulic acid → thromboxane A2 synthesis reduction → anti-platelet
- Vasodilation: Z-ligustilide → smooth muscle relaxation in coronary arteries → increased coronary flow
- NO production enhancement: ferulic acid → eNOS upregulation → vasodilation
- Antioxidant protection of vascular endothelium (ferulic acid)

**4. Anti-inflammatory (Multiple Pathways)**
- Ferulic acid: COX-1/COX-2 inhibition; NF-κB pathway suppression
- Polysaccharides: IL-10 induction; Th1/Th2 balance modulation
- Osthole (coumarin): phosphodiesterase inhibition → cAMP → anti-inflammatory signaling

**5. Immunomodulatory (DAPS)**
- TLR4 agonism → macrophage activation; NK cell enhancement
- Dendritic cell maturation
- Standard immunomodulatory polysaccharide mechanism (shared with astragalus, codonopsis, rehmannia)

**6. Analgesic (Phthalides)**
- Z-ligustilide: µ-opioid receptor partial agonism (very mild)
- Smooth muscle relaxation → relief of visceral (uterine, intestinal) pain
- NF-κB inhibition → reduced inflammatory pain sensitization

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Dysmenorrhea (primary evidence domain):**
- Kotani N et al (2000) *Anesthesiology* 92(5):1258–68: Not specifically Dang Gui alone but Apiaceae phthalide blend
- Multiple Chinese RCTs: Dang Gui preparations vs. NSAIDs for primary dysmenorrhea; mixed results; some non-inferior
- Systematic review (Zhu X et al 2008 *Cochrane* CD004142): Chinese herbal formulas for primary dysmenorrhea — significant symptom reduction vs. placebo; attribution to Dang Gui specifically uncertain in multi-herb formulas

**Anemia:**
- Wang X et al (2014): Dang Gui injection; iron deficiency anemia; N=92; significant hemoglobin improvement vs. control; P<0.001; injection route confounds oral comparison
- Liao JZ et al (1999): DAPS in aplastic anemia adjunct; improved marrow response

**Menopausal symptoms:**
- Hirata JD et al (1997) *Fertility Sterility* 68(6):981–6: Dong quai vs. placebo in 71 postmenopausal women; NO significant improvement in hot flashes, vaginal maturation, endometrial thickness vs. placebo (24-week RCT); importantly this used standardized single-herb extract — TCM practitioners contend Dang Gui should only be used in formula for menopausal symptoms
- This negative trial is frequently cited as disproving estrogenic activity of Dang Gui

**Phytoestrogen question:**
- Dang Gui does NOT contain isoflavones or phytoestrogens
- Ferulic acid has weak ER modulation but no clinically significant estrogenic activity
- The "female ginseng" name does NOT reflect estrogenic mechanism; works via non-estrogenic pathways (uterotonic, anti-spasmodic, hematopoietic)
- ER+ breast cancer: theoretical concern is largely unfounded based on mechanism, but caution maintained pending definitive data

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The woman's herb — 2,000 years of gynecological medicine:**

Dang Gui appears in the *Shennong Bencao Jing* as a superior herb for "irregular menses, uterine flooding, and postpartum blood stasis." Two thousand years later, it remains the most frequently prescribed single herb in Chinese gynecology. The consistency of application across dynasties, across the revolution in political systems, and into modern TCM clinical practice represents one of the longest unbroken clinical records of any herb in the Codex.

**Sichuan and Gansu — the origin terroirs:**

Dang Gui is produced primarily in Gansu province (especially Minxian county, which claims to produce 70%+ of China's Dang Gui) and Sichuan province. Gansu Dang Gui (岷县当归, Minxian) is considered the highest quality — the combination of high-altitude meadows (2,500–3,000m), specific soil mineral profile, and cold winters producing the highest Z-ligustilide content. Regional designation (daodi medicinal material, 道地药材) is critical for clinical efficacy — generic Chinese angelica without origin specification is considered substandard.

**"She Returns When She Takes This":**

One traditional story explaining the name Dang Gui: when a husband goes to war or travels, his wife becomes ill with grief and blood deficiency. The herb prescribed to restore her health is called "the one that makes you return (to health)" or "she returns when she takes this." Whether accurate or apocryphal, this naming narrative encodes the herb's primary clinical context: women's health, blood deficiency from stress or loss, restoration of vitality.

**The Dang Gui-Chuanxiong pair:**

Dang Gui + Ligusticum chuanxiong (川芎, Chuan Xiong) is one of the most classical herb pairs in TCM — the four-ingredient formula *Si Wu Tang* (四物湯, "Four Substance Decoction") = Dang Gui + Shu Di Huang + Chuan Xiong + Bai Shao (white peony) is THE foundational blood-tonifying and blood-regulating formula in Chinese medicine, used continuously since the Tang dynasty (618–907 CE). Both Dang Gui and Chuan Xiong contain Z-ligustilide + ferulic acid; combining them (similar phthalide profile but different ratios) produces additive synergy. Si Wu Tang is one of the most studied TCM formulas in modern pharmacology.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

⚠️ **ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION: PREGNANCY**
Z-ligustilide is a uterine stimulant at higher doses; Dang Gui is classified as a Category A traditional contraindication in pregnancy (strongest level of pregnancy contraindication in TCM); AVOID throughout pregnancy; safe only under specialist supervision if ever considered.

**Primary indications:**
- Primary dysmenorrhea (painful menses)
- Irregular menstruation (delayed, scanty, absent cycles)
- Postpartum blood deficiency and recovery
- Blood deficiency: pallor, fatigue, dizziness, palpitations
- Anemia (iron-deficiency and other forms as adjunct)
- Climacteric and menopausal symptoms (in formulas; not as single herb)
- Chronic constipation with blood deficiency (lubricates intestines)

**Dosing:**
- **Standard dose:** 6–15g dried root as decoction daily
- **Root tail (gui wei) for blood-moving:** Same dose but tail specifically
- **Wine-fried (jiu chao) preparation:** For enhanced circulation, reducing cold nature
- **Duration:** 3–6 months for blood deficiency treatment; acute dysmenorrhea: start 5 days before menses

⚠️ **DRUG INTERACTIONS:**
- **Warfarin:** ⚠️ CLINICALLY SIGNIFICANT — Ferulic acid platelet inhibition + possible warfarin pharmacokinetic interaction → increased bleeding risk; monitor INR; multiple case reports of elevated INR; AVOID concurrent use or closely monitor
- **Antiplatelet drugs (aspirin, clopidogrel):** Additive antiplatelet effect; increased bleeding risk
- **Estrogen-containing medications (OCP, HRT):** Theoretically modulates estrogenic pathways; monitor response; the interaction is complex and not well-characterized
- **Blood thinners generally:** Additive risk; discontinue 2 weeks before surgery

⚠️ **CAUTIONS:**
- **Heavy menstrual bleeding or hemorrhage:** Blood-moving action may worsen; defer or use only tonifying (not moving) preparations under guidance
- **Acute diarrhea:** Can loosen stools; avoid until resolved
- **Hormone-sensitive malignancies:** Despite the "female ginseng" designation, estrogenic mechanism is not established; theoretical caution maintained for ER+ breast/uterine cancer pending clearer data
- **Lactation:** Traditional: appropriate; modern: insufficient data — avoid until resolved

**Quality and standardization:**
- Chinese Pharmacopoeia 2020: minimum 0.1% ferulic acid on dry basis
- Preferred: Minxian (岷县) origin Gansu Dang Gui; daodi certification
- Root should be soft, oily, fragrant with sweet-pungent smell; brittle, odorless = degraded Z-ligustilide
- High-quality volatile oil content means: store in cool, dark, sealed conditions; Z-ligustilide volatilizes readily

**Preparation notes:**
- Traditional decoction: ADD DANG GUI LATE (last 15 minutes of a longer decoction) — preserves Z-ligustilide content which is thermolabile
- Wine-soaking before decoction: enhances phthalide solubility
- Si Wu Tang formula: combine Dang Gui + Shu Di Huang + Chuan Xiong + Bai Shao (equal parts) — the classical blood tonic formula

**Energetic tier:** ◈ Jing (structural/constitutional) + ◉ Qi (circulatory/functional) — dual-tier; simultaneously operates on blood substance/constitution (Jing level) and blood circulation/movement (Qi level)
