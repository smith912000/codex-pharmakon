# Common milkweed (translator's gloss) — Panakes Asklepion, Panaces Aesculapii (Dioscorides 3-56)

## *Asclepias syriaca (the Dioscorides translator's suggestion for chapter 3-56, given beside Thapsia asclepium; the identification is doubtful)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). The binomials below are the translator's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification. **Named only by Dioscorides in this row.**

*3-56 PANAKES ASKLEPION* (heading at line 7036). The translator's line reads "Asclepias syriaca — Milkweed, Silkweed Thapsia asclepium [Loudon] — Deadly Carrot", marked POISONOUS, with the translator's note "Aesculapius is the god of medicine — see 3-106". The text (lines 7039): "Panaces Aesculapij sends a thin stalk of a foot's length (distinguished by knots) out of the earth, around which are leaves similar to marathrum [3-81], yet bigger, rougher, and fragrant; and on the top is a tuft on which are sharp, fragrant flowers of a golden colour. The root is small. The flowers and seeds applied pounded into small pieces with honey, have a medicinal quality suitable for ulcers, pannus [opaque thickening of cornea with veins], and spreading ulcers. For snakebites it is taken as a drink with wine and rubbed on with oil. Some call [this] panaces wild origanum, some again call it cunila (where it is referred to in the section on origanum)."

**Identity.** The translator gives two plants on one line. *Asclepias syriaca* is generally known to be a North American plant, so Dioscorides cannot have described it; the genus name *Asclepias* is built on the Greek name used in these chapters, which is presumably how the gloss arose. The other binomial offered, *Thapsia asclepium*, belongs to the carrot family, and the chapter's description (a tuft of golden, sharp, fragrant flowers, a small root, leaves like marathrum) was not matched to either plant here. The identification of this row is therefore doubtful. [USER: verify]

The chapter belongs to the panaces chapters of Book III (3-55 Heraclean, 3-56 Aesculapian, 3-57 Chironian). The sister chapter 3-106 *Asklepias* (see I_1018) carries the same note "Asclepias syriaca" as an "other usage" and cross-refers to this chapter.

### Active Components

Milkweeds (*Asclepias*) are generally known to contain cardenolide cardiac glycosides and a milky latex. This applies to the genus named in the translator's gloss, not necessarily to the plant Dioscorides described. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The text records the flowers and seeds in honey for ulcers and for the eyes, and a snakebite drink and rub. The identity is doubtful and the plant is marked poisonous. The Codex does not recommend it and gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

Flowers and seeds "pounded into small pieces with honey" applied; a drink with wine and an oil rub for snakebite (lines 7039). No quantity is printed. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Cardenolide glycosides from milkweeds can affect the heart, a general statement about the genus named in the gloss. No study of the plant of this chapter was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** 3-56 prints "fragrant" (leaves, flowers) and "sharp" (flowers). No warming or cooling quality is printed, and the translator marks the chapter POISONOUS.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers is the discipline of attention the cultivation spec describes: how to know the plant (a thin stalk a foot high with knots, rough fragrant leaves, a tuft of golden flowers, a small root), its other names (wild origanum, cunila), and the care to say what it is not. The name carries Asklepios, the god of medicine, as the translator notes, but the text assigns the plant no status. It records no use for the person's vital constitution. The identification is the translator's and doubtful. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator learns from the chapter the habit of reading description before name, since the Latin binomial offered cannot be the plant described. No practice is given.

### Contraindications

The translator marks the chapter POISONOUS, and plants of the genus named in the gloss contain cardiac glycosides. Dioscorides' snakebite use is not advice. Do not use in pregnancy. No dose is given.
