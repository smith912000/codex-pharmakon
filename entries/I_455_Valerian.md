# I-455 | VALERIAN
## *Valeriana officinalis* L.

**Family:** Caprifoliaceae (reclassified 2003 from Valerianaceae; APG III) | **Part Used:** Root and rhizome

**Energetic Tier:** ☿ Shen · ◉ Qi

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Etymology:** Latin *valeriana* — debated origin; most likely from *valere* "to be strong/healthy/well" (as in "vale!" farewell); alternatively from Valeria, a Roman province in present-day Hungary where the plant was abundant; possibly from a personal name *Valerius* as early pharmacist

**Common names:** Garden valerian, garden heliotrope (for flower scent resemblance), all-heal (medieval England), setwall (Old English apothecary name), *herbe aux chats* (French — cat attractant), spikenard (confused with Nardostachys in trade)

**The cat-attractant phenomenon:** Valerian root contains actinidine (iridoid) + isovaleric acid; actinidine elicits rolling, rubbing, and vocalizing in domestic cats nearly identical to catnip (*Nepeta cataria*) response — a documented cross-species behavioral indicator of CNS bioactivity that practitioners historically interpreted as proof of potency

**Species distinctions:**
- *V. officinalis* L. — European/North American; most clinically studied
- *V. wallichii* DC. (= *V. jatamansi* Jones) — Indian *tagara*; Ayurvedic equivalent; slightly different alkaloid profile
- *V. edulis* Nutt. — Mexican/Mesoamerican; used by indigenous peoples
- *V. fauriei* Briq. — Japanese *kanoko-so*; used in Kampo medicine
- Note: *Nardostachys jatamansi* ("spikenard") is frequently confused with valerian in historical texts and modern markets

**Botanical identity:** Erect perennial to 1.5 m; rhizome system producing numerous short stolons; basal leaves pinnate, 15–25 cm; cauline leaves sessile-clasping; small, pale pink to white fragrant flowers in terminal cymose corymbs (June–July); fruit a compressed achene with feathery pappus; diagnostic: crushed root produces unmistakable "dirty socks" odor from isovaleric acid; this odor intensifies dramatically on drying/aging

**Primary active constituents:**
- **Valerenic acid:** 0.2–0.5% dried root; GABA-A PAM at β₂/β₃ subunits; also 5-HT₅A partial agonist; primary standardization marker
- **Hydroxyvalerenic acid:** analog; similar GABA-A activity; present in smaller amounts
- **Isovalerianic acid (isovaleric acid):** characteristic odor compound; mild CNS depression; metabolic product of valepotriate hydrolysis; responsible for "dirty socks" smell
- **Valepotriates (iridoid esters):** valtrate, isovaltrate, didrovaltrate, acevaltrate; 0.5–2.0% in *fresh* root; CRITICALLY UNSTABLE — rapidly hydrolyze during drying, storage, and aqueous extraction; dried commercial preparations contain minimal valepotriates
- **Baldrinal and homobaldrinal:** valepotriate hydrolysis products; in vitro genotoxicity (Bounthanh 1981); no demonstrated genotoxicity at human exposure levels from dried preparations
- **Flavonoids:** hesperidin (5–10 mg/g), linarin; GABA-A modulation; contribute to aqueous extract activity
- **Free GABA:** measurable in aqueous extract; questionable CNS availability (BBB penetration of free GABA limited)
- **Lignans:** olivil, hydroxypinoresinol; minor CNS contributions
- **Glutamine:** GABA precursor; present in extract

**The preparation paradox:** The valepotriates — most pharmacologically active compounds in fresh root (in vitro CNS data) — are essentially absent in dried commercial preparations. Most clinical trials used dried preparations. This means we may be studying preparations depleted of the primary active fraction, which could explain the inconsistency in RCT results. Fresh root preparations preserve valepotriates but cannot be standardized or stored. The clinical evidence base for valerian may underestimate the efficacy of traditional fresh preparations.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**The universal nervine archetype:** Three independent pharmacological traditions — Greek (Dioscorides), Arab (Ibn Sīnā), and Tibetan (FMT) — converged on valerian as the preeminent nervine herb of their respective materia medicae. The Greek *phu*, Arab *sumbul*, and Tibetan *spang spos* represent independent ethnobotanical encounters with the same plant (or closely related species) arriving at identical clinical applications through observation alone, without knowledge of each other's conclusions.

**Ayurvedic *tagara*:** The use of *V. wallichii* in Ayurveda as *tagara* (heart-calming, sleep-promoting, *vata*-pacifying nervine) parallels the European applications exactly. Species substitution with preservation of therapeutic niche across pharmacological systems is a high-confidence marker of genuine activity at the family/genus level.

**The "spirit" application:** Across traditions, valerian appears in the context of disturbances to what each tradition identified as the core mental/spiritual faculty — Greek *thymos* (vital spirit), Arab *ruh* (vital breath), Tibetan *rlung* (wind-mind), Ayurvedic *vata* (air-motion principle). The convergence on a specific type of mental disturbance — agitated, racing, physically restless anxiety with palpitations — across these distinct metaphysical frameworks suggests the observed phenomenology was consistent enough to generate parallel classification.

**Medieval European synthesis:** By the 11th–13th centuries, valerian (*Baldrian* in German = possibly connected to Baldur, Norse god of light and healing) was the central nervous system herb of European medicine. The Canterbury Tales, Dioscorides commentaries, and guild pharmacopeia records all reference it. The persistence across Christian, Jewish, and Islamic medical traditions in medieval Europe (where all three traditions overlapped in centers like Toledo and Salerno) indicates consensus sufficiently strong to cross religious and cultural lines.

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

**Valerenic acid pathway:**

| Compound | Concentration (dry root) | Primary target |
|----------|--------------------------|----------------|
| Valerenic acid | 0.2–0.5% | GABA-A β₂/β₃ PAM; 5-HT₅A agonist |
| Hydroxyvalerenic acid | 0.05–0.15% | GABA-A PAM (similar to valerenic acid) |
| Acetoxyvalerenic acid | 0.02–0.08% | GABA-A activity (less studied) |
| Isovaleric acid | Variable (degradation product) | Mild CNS depression; odor compound |

**Valepotriate iridoids (fresh root only — unstable):**

| Compound | Fresh root concentration | Stability |
|----------|--------------------------|-----------|
| Valtrate | 0.3–0.8% | Hydrolyzes within 24h at room temperature |
| Isovaltrate | 0.2–0.5% | Similarly unstable |
| Didrovaltrate | 0.1–0.3% | Similarly unstable |
| Baldrinal (hydrolysis product) | Increases on drying | Mutagenic in vitro; negligible risk in dried preparations |

**Flavonoids (aqueous extract active fraction):**
- Hesperidin: 5–10 mg/g; GABA-A modulation; contributes to aqueous extract activity
- Linarin: anxiolytic in animal models; potentiates GABA receptor response
- Together these may be the primary drivers of anxiolytic effect in commercial dried-herb preparations

**Free GABA:** Measurable (0.01–0.06%); BBB penetration by exogenous free GABA is limited, but local gut mucosal GABAergic effects and vagus-nerve-mediated CNS signaling cannot be excluded

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

**Valerenic Acid at GABA-A (Primary Demonstrated Mechanism):**
```
Valerenic acid → allosteric binding at GABA-A β₂/β₃ subunit interface
→ Enhances Cl⁻ conductance upon endogenous GABA binding
→ NOT benzodiazepine site (distinct from classical BZD pharmacology)
→ Anxiolytic + sedative WITHOUT BZD site occupation
→ Clinical implication: reduced risk of BZD-type dependence/tolerance
   (though clinical dependence risk still requires assessment)

Lacher et al. 2007 (Mol Pharmacol): valerenic acid demonstrated β₂/β₃
selectivity; no significant activity at α, γ subunits
→ Explains milder sedation relative to benzodiazepines (α₁ subunit mediates
   the hypnotic effect of BZDs — valerian has less α₁ action)
```

**5-HT₅A Serotonin Receptor Modulation:**
```
Valerenic acid → 5-HT₅A partial agonist
→ Role of 5-HT₅A in anxiety and sleep regulation: incompletely characterized
→ May contribute to antidepressant-adjacent effects reported anecdotally
→ May explain effects not fully accounted for by GABA-A mechanism alone
```

**Hesperidin/Linarin Flavonoid Synergy:**
```
Hesperidin + linarin → GABA-A receptor potentiation (rodent models)
→ Synergistic with valerenic acid at GABA-A
→ In aqueous extracts (teas), flavonoids may be primary active fraction
   (valepotriates absent in dried preparations; valerenic acid water-soluble)
→ Explains why aqueous tea preparations have documented clinical effect
```

**Adenosine A₁ Interaction (Emerging):**
```
Aqueous extract components → possible adenosine A₁ modulation
→ A₁ stimulation: sedative, anti-nociceptive, sleep-promoting
→ Mechanism less well-characterized than GABA-A; contributes to sleep
   architecture effects (slow-wave sleep support)
```

**Valepotriate Mechanism (Fresh Root Only):**
```
Valtrate/isovaltrate (fresh root, traditional preparation)
→ Alkylating agent activity: crosslinks DNA in rapidly dividing cells
→ Selective cytotoxicity: may explain traditional febrifuge/antiparasitic uses
→ Too unstable to survive commercial processing; not relevant to dried herb
→ Historical "fresh decoction" preparations fundamentally different pharmacology
```

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Sleep (Primary Indication):**

- **Leathwood 1982** (Pharmacol Biochem Behav; Level B): 128 volunteers; aqueous valerian extract; subjective sleep latency reduced; sleep quality improved; earliest systematic clinical evidence; no placebo-controlled

- **Bent 2006** (Am J Med; Level B systematic review): 16 RCTs; varied preparations and doses; 6 of 8 studies reporting sleep quality showed improvement; objective polysomnographic outcomes less consistent; concluded: "valerian may improve subjective sleep quality without producing side effects" — cautious positive conclusion

- **Fernández-San-Martín 2010** (Sleep Medicine; systematic review Level B): 18 RCTs reviewed; trend toward improvement in sleep quality and latency; significant heterogeneity of preparations problematic for meta-analysis; methodological quality variable

- **Taibi 2009** (Sleep Medicine; Level A, n=16): 16 postmenopausal women with insomnia; 300 mg × 2 weeks; no significant PSG improvements over placebo; *challenges objective sleep benefit at this dose in this population*

**Anxiety:**

- Evidence is primarily mechanistic (valerenic acid GABA-A) + combination product trials (valerian + hops + lemon balm)
- Commission E approved for "nervous restlessness" based on traditional evidence + pharmacological data

**Combination Products:**

- Valerian + hops (*Humulus lupulus*): most common combination; hops contain methylbutenol (2-methyl-3-buten-2-ol), which oxidizes to methylbutanol — sedative metabolite; hop bitter acids also GABA-A active; multiple trials show combination superiority over single herb
- Valerian + lemon balm: Akhondzadeh combination studies; European tradition; additive GABAergic mechanisms (rosmarinic acid GABA-T inhibition + valerenic acid GABA-A PAM)
- Valerian + passionflower + hops (*Dormiplant*, *Sedonium*): commercial combinations with RCT data

**Evidence Summary:**

| Indication | Best Evidence | Grade |
|-----------|---------------|-------|
| Subjective sleep quality | Multiple RCTs (inconsistent) | Level B |
| Sleep latency (subjective) | Leathwood 1982 + others | Level B |
| Objective PSG improvements | Inconsistent; Taibi 2009 negative | Insufficient (mixed) |
| Nervous restlessness | Commission E approval | Level C |
| Anxiety | Mechanistic + traditional | Level C–D |

*The clinical evidence for valerian is notably heterogeneous — likely reflecting the preparation paradox (fresh vs. dried; valepotriate content; extraction solvent) and the standardization challenges of an inherently unstable plant.*

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

**The "cat effect" as ancient quality test:** Practitioners throughout European history observed that potent valerian root attracted cats with behavioral intensity proportional to the root's freshness and potency. This behavioral bioassay — using cat response as a proxy for CNS activity — was a sophisticated traditional quality control mechanism. The cat's behavioral response (rolling, purring, apparent intoxication) provided a visible readout of the root's isovaleric acid / actinidine content, which correlates with freshness and total pharmacological activity. A valerian root that failed to attract cats was considered of inferior quality.

**The Pied Piper theory:** A minority of folklore historians have proposed that the Pied Piper of Hamelin used valerian root (and a flute) to attract rats — rats also respond to valerian's odor — and that this folk memory was then transformed into a children's story. While unverifiable, this narrative encodes an observation: valerian root has demonstrably cross-species behavioral effects in mammals, detectable without modern pharmacology.

**The dirty-socks problem:** Valerenic acid's oxidation during storage generates isovaleric acid, which is responsible for the characteristic pungent odor. Medieval pharmacists observed that older, more pungent valerian was more potent — counterintuitive, because oxidation would presumably degrade active compounds. Modern analysis suggests this paradox may be explained by the fact that isovaleric acid itself has mild sedative properties, so increased odor correlated with increased sedative content in aged/dried preparations, even as the valepotriates degraded. Traditional empirical observations were partially correct for the wrong reasons.

**The 2,000-year prescribing continuity:** Dioscorides' *phu* (77 CE) was prescribed for the same indication — nervine sedative, sleep-inducing, palpitation-calming — that Commission E approved in 1985. The pharmacological rationale changed from humoral (warming the cold, damp phlegmatic temperament) to receptor pharmacology (GABA-A β₂/β₃ modulation), but the observation that drove the prescription remained constant across 19 centuries. Few natural medicines have maintained this prescribing continuity without interruption.

**WWII civilian application:** Valerian was widely distributed in Great Britain during the Blitz (1940–41) as a civilian nerve tonic for bomb-shelter anxiety. The British government's endorsement of a traditional herbal remedy during a public health emergency reflects both the limited pharmaceutical options of the era and the depth of public familiarity with valerian's properties. The herb's commercial availability during wartime austerity (it grew wild throughout Britain) made it a practical choice.

## Section 8: Practical Application

**Primary indications:**
1. Insomnia: difficulty falling asleep; sleep restlessness; early morning awakening
2. Nervous restlessness: daytime anxiety with somatic component (palpitations, muscle tension)
3. Adjunct in benzodiazepine tapering (GABAergic bridge mechanism)
4. Situational anxiety (pre-exam, pre-performance, anticipatory)
5. Menopausal insomnia (often combined with black cohosh or hops)

**Dosing:**
- **Sleep:** 300–600 mg standardized root extract (0.8% valerenic acid) 30–60 min before bed; or 450–900 mg encapsulated dried root
- **Anxiety/restlessness:** 150–300 mg TID (three times daily at intervals throughout day)
- **Tea:** 3–5 g dried root in 200 mL hot water × 10–15 min; strain and drink warm before bed; prepare fresh (never leave to stand as oxidation accelerates)
- **Tincture (1:5, 40–60% ethanol):** 5–10 mL 30–60 min before bed; ethanol preserves valerenic acid better than aqueous extraction
- **Onset:** Sleep benefits may require 2–4 weeks of continuous use for maximum effect; not a one-night acute sedative

**Forms:**
- Standardized extract (0.8% valerenic acid): most consistent dosing; most used in RCTs
- Dried root encapsulated: traditional; valepotriate-depleted but flavonoid-complete
- Tincture: better valerenic acid preservation; strongest traditional form
- Fresh root tincture (rarely available): valepotriate-complete; different pharmacology
- Combination products (+ hops, lemon balm, passionflower): synergistic; multiple clinical trials

**Contraindications:**
- **Pregnancy/lactation:** Insufficient safety data; valepotriate content in fresh preparations raises theoretical concerns; avoid
- **Pre-surgery:** Additive sedation with anesthetic agents; discontinue ≥1 week preoperatively
- **Hepatic impairment:** Isolated hepatotoxicity case reports (likely adulterants/confounders); caution

**Drug interactions:**
- **CNS depressants (benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, alcohol, opioids, antihistamines):** Additive sedation; reduce pharmaceutical dose or use cautiously
- **CYP3A4 substrates:** Weak inhibitory effect in some studies; not as clinically significant as St. John's Wort; caution with narrow therapeutic index drugs
- **Loperamide, morphine:** Possible enhanced CNS depression

**Safety:**
- Generally well-tolerated in adults at therapeutic doses
- Paradoxical stimulation (anxious agitation, restlessness) in ~5–10% — more common in children and elderly; mechanism unknown (possible inverse agonism at some GABA-A subtypes)
- Vivid dreams, nightmares: reported by some users, particularly at higher doses
- Morning grogginess: uncommon at standard doses; more likely with high doses or combination CNS depressants
- Dependence/withdrawal: case reports of withdrawal after prolonged high-dose use (anxiety, tachycardia, restlessness); taper recommended after extended use

**Monitoring:**
- Liver enzymes (AST/ALT) if using long-term at high doses; rare but reported hepatotoxicity

**Synergies:** Hops (complementary GABA-A mechanism via methylbutenol); lemon balm (GABA-T inhibition → increased synaptic GABA); passionflower (additional GABA-A PAM); these triple combinations have both mechanistic rationale and clinical trial data supporting their use for insomnia
