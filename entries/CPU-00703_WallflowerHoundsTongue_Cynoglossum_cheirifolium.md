# Wallflower Hound's Tongue

## *Cynoglossum cheirifolium* L. | Family: Boraginaceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Cooling, moist, demulcent-astringent — by genus-level inference and doctrine of signatures, **not** by attested classification for this species.

**Training Phase:** None. No supportable phase assignment. External/topical only if used at all; internal use is unsafe.

---

### Origin & Tradition

This entry exists to make an honest problem visible rather than to hide it.

*Cynoglossum cheirifolium* is a small, grey-woolly Mediterranean biennial of the borage family — Iberia, southern France, Italy, North Africa (Algeria, Morocco, Tunisia), scattered east. It grows on dry, disturbed, stony ground: roadsides, field margins, garrigue. Densely white-tomentose leaves, deep-blue to purplish or reddish funnel-shaped flowers, and the family's characteristic barbed nutlets that stick to wool and fur. Modern taxonomy has moved it: it is now widely treated as ***Pardoglossum cheirifolium* (L.) E. Barbier & Mathez**, and the recent chemical literature files it under that name. If you search only for *Cynoglossum cheirifolium* you will miss most of what little exists.

**The Doctrine of Signatures — the genus name itself.** *Cynoglossum* is Greek *kynos glossa*, "dog's tongue" — named for the leaf: elongate, tapering, rough-textured, unmistakably the shape and feel of a dog's tongue. This is not a folk etymology attached after the fact; it is the name Dioscorides' tradition carried, and it is one of the cleanest surviving examples of the doctrine of signatures operating **as a naming principle** rather than as a therapeutic one. The species epithet *cheirifolium* means "wallflower-leaved" (*Cheiranthus*) — again pure morphology. Both halves of the binomial are descriptions of a leaf. Neither encodes a medical claim.

That distinction matters, because the doctrine of signatures then did what it always does: it ran forward from the shape to an indication. A tongue-shaped leaf on a rough plant became a remedy for the mouth, the throat, and — by the associative logic of the period — for dog bite. The Latin herbal tradition duly records *Cynoglossa* against dog bites. This is signature-logic all the way down and there is no reason whatever to believe it.

**Classical / Dioscoridean Mediterranean materia medica.** The *Cynoglossum* the classical sources describe is not securely identifiable to *C. cheirifolium*. The historical name-space is shared across several Mediterranean hound's-tongues and the classical descriptions are not diagnostic to species. What the tradition attaches to the name is consistent: a mucilaginous, cooling, mildly astringent wound and demulcent herb — leaf or root pounded into a poultice for burns, scalds, ulcers, piles and slow-healing wounds; internally (in the older sources) for cough and diarrhoea. [USER: verify] — **do not attribute any specific classical passage to *C. cheirifolium* without checking the identification. The safest honest statement is that the classical record is genus-level and probably centres on other species.**

**North African / Maghrebi folk use.** The plant is present and common in Algeria — which is where every phytochemical paper on it comes from — and there are scattered indications of local use as a wound and skin herb, consistent with the family pattern. [USER: verify] — I could retrieve **no ethnobotanical survey documenting a specific, named, traditional indication for *C. cheirifolium***. Present in the flora is not the same as used in the pharmacopoeia. If a Maghrebi use exists, it is undocumented in the literature reachable here, and the entry must not invent one.

**Relationship to *Cynoglossum officinale* — read this carefully.** *C. officinale*, common hound's-tongue, has its own entry in this codex. That is the species with the attested European record: the demulcent/vulnerary "houndstongue" of the herbals, the poultice for burns and haemorrhoids, and the well-characterised pyrrolizidine-alkaloid problem that got it removed from responsible internal use. ***C. cheirifolium* is a different species, and essentially all of the traditional detail that a reader will instinctively import belongs to *C. officinale*, not to this plant.** The two share a genus, a growth habit, a leaf signature and an alkaloid class. They do not share an evidence base. Do not read *C. officinale*'s entry into this one — the whole reason this entry is written separately is to hold that line.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.**

There are two doctrines in play here and they should be graded very differently.

*The naming doctrine* — *kynos glossa*, the leaf that looks like a dog's tongue — is descriptive and **true**. The leaf does look like that. As a field-identification mnemonic it is excellent and it has worked for two thousand years. This is the doctrine of signatures at its best: as a memory system.

*The therapeutic doctrine* — that a tongue-shaped leaf therefore treats the mouth, the throat, or the bite of the animal whose tongue it resembles — is **false**, and should be named as false. The dog-bite indication is sympathetic magic: the plant resembles the dog, therefore it governs the dog, therefore it answers the dog's bite. There is no mechanism, no evidence, and no charitable reading. Rabies is a rhabdovirus. A hound's-tongue poultice does nothing to it. This is a case where the codex must deflate rather than reinterpret.

There is, however, one place where the doctrine lands near real chemistry — and it does so for reasons unrelated to the signature. Boraginaceae are mucilage-rich, and the family's demulcent reputation (comfrey, borage, hound's-tongue alike) tracks a genuine physical property: polysaccharide mucilage forms a hydrated film on abraded tissue. That is why the *wound/burn poultice* use is the only part of the traditional envelope with a plausible physical basis — soothing by occlusion and hydration, not by any specific pharmacology. And it is why the family's topical reputation survived while its internal reputation deserved to die.

Because underneath the mucilage sits the family's real signature, which no tradition perceived: **pyrrolizidine alkaloids**. The comfort of the demulcent is on the surface. The hepatotoxin is in the tissue. The doctrine of signatures gave the herbalist the leaf's shape and told them nothing about the only thing that mattered. That is the lesson of this entry, and it is worth more than the plant is.

### Active Components

The species-specific chemistry is thin but real, and all of it comes from three Algerian studies.

**Pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) — confirmed in this species.** The first study of PAs in *Pardoglossum cheirifolium* (L.) E. Barbier & Mathez used GC-MS on a crude base extract of the whole plant collected in Algeria; **nine compounds were tentatively identified, all new phytochemicals at the species level**, confirming the plant's Boraginaceae placement chemosystematically (*Nat Prod Res*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2020.1721491)). Note the word *tentatively* — GC-MS identification without isolation.

A later study went further, isolating **heliosupine N-oxide** from the *n*-butanol extract of the Algerian plant and characterising it by NMR and ESI-MS/MS, and using LC-DAD-ESI/MS on the methanol extract to propose the presence of **lycopsamine N-oxide, echinatine N-oxide, dihydrolycopsamine, dihydroechinatine, 7-acetyl dihydrolycopsamine, 7-acetyllycopsamine N-oxide, 3′-acetylviridiflorine, vulgarine and heliosupine** (*Nat Prod Res*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2026.2625886)). One isolated-and-characterised PA; the rest proposed from LC-MS.

This is chemically important: **heliosupine is an open-chain diester-type PA**, and diesters are the high-potency structural class (see Scientific Correlation). Lycopsamine-type monoesters are lower-potency. The plant carries both, which means potency-class reasoning cannot rescue it.

**Phenolics and flavonoids.** RP-HPLC-PDA of *C. cheirifolium* leaves identified **rosmarinic acid, ferulic acid, caffeic acid, *p*-coumaric acid, syringic acid, sinapic acid and rutin**. Leaves produced the most phenolics, then flowering aerial parts; the butanolic leaf fraction had the highest total phenolics (78.65 ± 3.58 mg GAE/g DW) and flavonoids (22.15 ± 4.66 mg CE/g DW) (*Nat Prod Res*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2017.1314286)). The 2026 paper adds **glycosylated flavonols** — quercetin and kaempferol glycosides, rutin, kaempferol 3-*O*-rutinoside — and two cinnamic acid glucosides (3,4,5-trihydroxycinnamic acid glucopyranoside; 3,5-dihydroxycinnamic acid diglucopyranoside) ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2026.2625886)).

**Mucilage.** Assumed at family level. **Not quantified for this species in anything retrieved.** Flagged as inference.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**None that can be responsibly recommended.**

This is a plant with a poetic name, a two-thousand-year-old shape-based reputation belonging mostly to its relatives, three chemistry papers, zero pharmacology, zero clinical data, and a confirmed load of hepatotoxic diester pyrrolizidine alkaloids.

There is no Kenetica training-phase slot for it. It is not a recovery herb, not an adaptogen, not a demulcent worth taking, and not a wound herb worth choosing when *Plantago*, *Calendula* and *Aloe* exist, carry actual evidence, and carry no PAs.

If it is used at all, it is a **short-duration topical on intact skin**, and even that is a courtesy to the tradition rather than a recommendation — PAs are absorbed through skin, and the family's topical products (comfrey) are regulated on exactly that basis.

The honest use case for this plant is **as a teaching specimen**: it is the cleanest example in Volume I of a signature that names correctly and prescribes disastrously.

**Dosing.** There is **no established human dose for *Cynoglossum cheirifolium* in any form, by any route, in any tradition, at any time.** Nothing retrieved provides one. Any figure encountered elsewhere is either imported from *C. officinale* or invented. [USER: verify] — treat every number in any secondary source on this plant as suspect until traced.

**Cultivator's notes.** A Mediterranean biennial of dry, stony, alkaline, disturbed ground; full sun; sharp drainage; drought-tolerant; frost-tender to hard frost. Sow the barbed nutlets in autumn where they are to grow — like most Boraginaceae it resents transplanting once the taproot forms. Rosette in year one, flowering stem in year two, then it dies. It self-seeds by burr, which is exactly how its relatives become weeds — *C. officinale* is a declared noxious weed in parts of North America, and the burrs are a genuine problem in wool and in livestock tails. **If you grow it, deadhead before the nutlets ripen.** Wear gloves: PAs are dermally absorbed and the plant is bristly, which means micro-abrasion and absorption in the same gesture. Do not compost the flowering tops into a vegetable bed. Do not plant it where stock graze — PA poisoning of horses and cattle from Boraginaceae is the classic veterinary presentation, and the animals eat it in dried hay when they would refuse it fresh. ### Scientific Correlation

**Grade: F for every traditional indication. D for chemistry (real but sparse and partly tentative). B for the toxicology — which is the only well-supported thing about this plant, and it is a reason not to use it.**

**This species is data-sparse to a degree the reader should understand precisely.** A PubMed search for *Cynoglossum cheirifolium* returns **three papers**. All three. All from Algerian groups, two of them phytochemical inventories and one an in-vitro antioxidant assay. That is the entire indexed biomedical literature on this species. There is no pharmacology, no animal study, no clinical trial, no ethnobotanical survey, no toxicology on the plant itself, and no traditional-use validation of any kind. **This entry cannot be padded and should not be.**

**Antioxidant activity — the only bioassay that exists, and it means little.** DPPH and FRAP on extracts; the butanolic leaf fraction scavenged DPPH with IC₅₀ 0.06 ± 0.003 mg/mL, and the authors conclude the plant "represent[s] a valuable source of natural antioxidants" (*Nat Prod Res*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2017.1314286)). **Grade F for clinical meaning.** DPPH is a colour-change reaction in a cuvette. Any plant with rosmarinic and caffeic acid in it scores well. It predicts nothing in a human, it does not validate a single traditional indication, and it is not a reason to ingest a PA-containing plant. Report it, grade it, move on.

**Chemistry — Grade D, and honestly graded.** Two papers, one genus-relocated name, nine "tentatively identified" PAs by GC-MS ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2020.1721491)), and one properly isolated and NMR-characterised compound — heliosupine N-oxide — alongside nine LC-MS-proposed structures ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2026.2625886)). "Proposed identification" from LC-MS is a hypothesis, not a fact. The PA presence is nonetheless secure — it is confirmed by isolation, and it is what the Boraginaceae family predicts.

**Toxicology — the one strong finding, and it is family/class-level. Named as such.** No toxicology has been done on *C. cheirifolium*. What follows is **pyrrolizidine-alkaloid class data, not species-specific data**, and it applies to this plant only because this plant has been shown to contain PAs of the named structural classes:

- PAs require **metabolic activation by cytochrome P450** to form pyrrole–protein and pyrrole–DNA adducts, producing cytotoxicity and genotoxicity; individual PAs differ in activation pattern and therefore in potency; PAs and their N-oxides are produced by over 6,000 plant species and humans are exposed via PA-containing herbal products and PA-contaminated foods (*Arch Toxicol*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00204-021-03060-w)). **Note the N-oxides — this plant's PAs are largely reported as N-oxides, and N-oxides are covered by this hazard, not exempt from it.**
- Eleven structurally different PAs were tested in CYP3A4-overexpressing HepG2 cells and **primary human hepatocytes** with a genotoxicity battery (γH2AX, p53, alkaline Comet) and benchmark-dose modelling. Genotoxicity was **structure-dependent**; BMD values fell in the **0.1–10 µM range for most cyclic and open diesters**, followed by monoesters. **Retrorsine was the most genotoxic; monocrotaline and lycopsamine the least.** Critically, in primary human hepatocytes **genotoxic effects appeared even in the absence of detectable cytotoxicity** (*Arch Toxicol*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00204-023-03482-8)).

Map that onto this plant. *C. cheirifolium* carries **lycopsamine-type monoesters** (the low-potency end — genuinely the least genotoxic PAs tested) **and heliosupine**, an **open-chain diester** (the high-potency end). The presence of the monoesters is not mitigating when the diester is there too. And the primary-hepatocyte finding — DNA damage without visible cell death — is exactly why "I felt fine on it" is not evidence of anything. PA liver injury is silent, cumulative, and genotoxic; the endpoint is veno-occlusive disease and, in rodents, liver cancer.

**NULLS, stated plainly:**
- **No clinical trial of this species. No case report. No pharmacokinetics. No animal study.**
- **No demulcent, wound-healing, anti-inflammatory or analgesic assay has ever been run on this plant.** The vulnerary reputation is entirely inherited and entirely untested here.
- **No mucilage quantification** for this species.
- **No ethnobotanical documentation** of a specific traditional indication retrieved.
- **No PA content quantification** — the studies identify PAs, they do not tell you how much is in a gram of leaf. So even a risk calculation is impossible.
- The dog-bite indication has, needless to say, **never been tested and never will be**. It is false.

### Contraindications

**⚠️ CONTAINS PYRROLIZIDINE ALKALOIDS — CONFIRMED IN THIS SPECIES, INCLUDING AN OPEN-CHAIN DIESTER (HELIOSUPINE). HEPATOTOXIC AND GENOTOXIC CLASS. INTERNAL USE IS UNSAFE.**

- **Do not ingest, in any form, at any dose, for any duration.** Not as tea, not as tincture, not as a "gentle" traditional infusion, not as a component of a blend. PAs are confirmed present by isolation and LC-MS ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2026.2625886), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2020.1721491)).
- **There is no safe internal dose to calculate**, because PA content in this species has never been quantified. You cannot dose against an unknown.
- **Genotoxicity occurs without cytotoxicity** in primary human hepatocytes (PA class data, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00204-023-03482-8)). Absence of symptoms is not absence of harm. There is no "I'd notice."
- **PA hepatotoxicity is cumulative and delayed.** The clinical endpoint is hepatic sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (veno-occlusive disease); PAs cause liver cancer in rodents ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00204-021-03060-w)). Injury from small repeated exposures is the classic pattern, not from one large one.
- **N-oxides are not safer.** This plant's PAs are reported largely as N-oxides; the class hazard covers PA N-oxides explicitly ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00204-021-03060-w)).
- **Absolute avoidance in pregnancy and lactation.** PAs cross the placenta and enter milk (class property). Infant PA exposure via maternal herbal use is a documented mechanism of harm in this alkaloid class. No exceptions, including topical.
- **Absolute avoidance in children, infants, and in anyone with any liver disease**, hepatitis, cirrhosis, fatty liver, or a history of hepatotoxic drug exposure.
- **Avoid with anything that induces CYP3A4** or with concurrent hepatotoxic drugs — PA bioactivation is P450-mediated ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00204-023-03482-8)).
- **Topical use is discouraged, not endorsed.** PAs are dermally absorbed. The precedent is comfrey, which is restricted to intact skin, limited duration and limited annual exposure precisely because of PAs. **Never apply to broken skin, open wounds, ulcers, burns or abraded tissue** — which unfortunately is the exact traditional indication. The tradition's use case is the one route that defeats the only safety argument for it.
- **Do not confuse with *Cynoglossum officinale***, and do not import *C. officinale*'s data, doses or monographs into this species. Both are PA-bearing; that is the only thing safely transferable.
- **Do not confuse with *Cheiranthus*/wallflower**, which the epithet references. Different family, different plant.
- **Livestock:** PA-bearing Boraginaceae are a recognised cause of fatal hepatic injury in horses and cattle, typically via contaminated hay where the dried plant loses its deterrent taste. Do not let this species into forage or hay. [USER: verify] local veterinary/noxious-weed listing.
- **Regulatory:** many jurisdictions impose strict PA limits on herbal products and foods. This species has no food or supplement status anywhere retrieved. [USER: verify] before any commercial handling.
