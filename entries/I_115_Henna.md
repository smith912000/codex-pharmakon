### Botanical Identity
Deciduous shrub 1–3m; small elliptical leaves; clusters of intensely fragrant white-to-pink flowers; small purple-black drupes; native to North Africa, Middle East, South Asia, and tropical Africa; widely cultivated throughout the tropics for the leaf dye and medicinal applications. The leaf is the primary drug material (contains the highest lawsone content); the root, bark, and flowers have separate pharmacological profiles.

**Lawsone**: The primary compound — 2-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone — is the dye molecule responsible for the orange-red colour of henna staining. It covalently bonds with keratin proteins in skin, hair, and nails, creating permanent staining. This is also the mechanism of several of henna's pharmacological effects — the quinone chemistry is antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory.

**3400 BCE**: The oldest confirmed use of henna as a cosmetic/dye — Egyptian mummies from the pre-dynastic period have henna-stained nails and hair. This makes henna one of the earliest documented plant uses in the Codex for a cosmetic-medicinal application that has continued uninterrupted for 5,400 years.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Dioscorides / Greco-Roman (DMM)
Book I, Chapter 124: "*Kypros* (henna) — astringent; for inflammations of the skin; ulcers in the mouth; the flower for headache and fragrance." Applications: astringent for skin inflammations; oral ulcer treatment; aromatic.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine / Ibn Sīnā (CM)
*Al-ḥinnāʾ* — cooling and drying. Applications: for headache (topical to forehead); for fever (cooling topical); for mouth ulcers; for foot and nail fungal infections; hair dyeing and conditioning; treatment of skin diseases; anti-inflammatory for burns. Ibn Sīnā gives one of the most comprehensive pre-modern treatments of henna's pharmacology.

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (EP)
Henna appears in EP medicinal preparations for skin conditions and as a cosmetic; the Egyptian mummy evidence pre-dates the EP (1550 BCE) by 1,850 years.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)
Preparations identified with henna appear in AT compound formulas for skin conditions and as cosmetic preparations.

### 2.5 Sushruta Samhita (SS)
*Mehndī* (*Lawsonia*) appears in Ayurvedic formulas for skin diseases, fever, and as a hair treatment. Central to Indian cosmetic and medicinal tradition — considered a purifying and cooling plant.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Antifungal / Antimicrobial (Primary — Lawsone)

**Lawsone (2-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone)**:
- Direct fungicidal against dermatophytes (*Trichophyton rubrum*, *T. mentagrophytes*, *Microsporum canis*) — MIC values of 5–50 μg/mL (clinically achievable topically)
- Antibacterial against gram-positive (*Staphylococcus aureus*, including MRSA in some studies) and gram-negative organisms
- Mechanism: the quinone core accepts electrons from respiratory chain components → disrupts bacterial/fungal electron transport chain → cell death
- The nail fungal infection application in CM and SS is pharmacologically grounded — lawsone penetrates nail keratin and reaches subungual dermatophytes

**Dye = Antimicrobial**: The same lawsone molecule that creates the red-orange dye stain on skin and nail is the antimicrobial compound. The covalent keratin-binding that fixes the colour also delivers the antimicrobial agent to a sustained-release depot in the keratin. This is elegant dual-function pharmacology — the cosmetic function and the pharmacological function are the same molecule, the same mechanism.

### 5.2 Anti-Inflammatory / Cooling (Gallic Acid + Ellagic Acid + Quercetin)

**Gallic acid + ellagic acid**: NF-κB inhibition → reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines → anti-inflammatory in skin and mucosal tissue.

**Topical cooling effect**: Henna paste has a genuine evaporative cooling effect on application (the water content cools as it evaporates) combined with the anti-inflammatory compounds — the traditional topical application to the forehead and soles for fever and headache cooling has both a physical (evaporative cooling) and pharmacological (anti-inflammatory) basis.

### 5.3 Oral Antimicrobial / Wound Healing (Astringent Tannins + Lawsone)

**Hennotannic acid + gallic acid + lawsone**:
- Tannin astringency → precipitates surface proteins → forms protective layer over oral ulcers
- Antimicrobial → prevents secondary infection of oral ulcers
- The mouth ulcer application in DMM, CM, and SS is pharmacologically sound

### 5.4 UV Protection (Lawsone)

**Lawsone**: Absorbs UV light strongly (λmax ~340–400nm, the UVA range). This is not merely a dye property — the UV absorption protects underlying keratin and skin cells from UV damage. Historical henna application to hands, feet, and hair provided incidental UV protection in sun-intense environments (North Africa, Middle East, South Asia).

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Antifungal**: In vitro data strongly supports antifungal activity; small clinical studies for tinea/nail onychomycosis show improvement with henna paste. No large RCTs.

**DPPD/PPD allergy in "black henna"**: Natural henna is orange-red; "black henna" temporary tattoos contain para-phenylenediamine (PPD) — a chemical dye not derived from the plant. PPD causes severe contact hypersensitivity (type IV delayed hypersensitivity) including blistering. **This is not a natural henna reaction** — it is an adulterant reaction. Natural pure henna rarely causes sensitisation. This confusion has significantly harmed henna's safety reputation; the distinction must be made.

**Hair conditioning**: Multiple studies confirm lawsone binds to hair protein → improves tensile strength → reduces breakage; the conditioning effect is well-validated.

**Oral antimicrobial**: Small studies confirm activity against *Streptococcus mutans* and *Candida albicans* at concentrations achievable with henna mouthwash preparations.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**5,400 years of continuous use**: The pre-dynastic Egyptian mummies with henna-stained nails represent the oldest documented continuous use of a specific plant application in the Codex. Henna has been used for skin/hair staining and for the same medicinal purposes (skin antiseptic, oral antimicrobial, cooling) for over 5,000 years without interruption. This continuity — from pre-dynastic Egypt through the Ebers Papyrus, through the Greco-Roman period, through Ibn Sīnā, through the Mughal Empire, to modern wedding celebrations across four continents — is unmatched in the Codex for cosmetic-medicinal applications.

**The dye is the medicine**: The integration of aesthetic function (body art, hair colouring, nail decoration) and pharmacological function (antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, UV protective) in henna represents an ancient holistic approach to personal care that was not "medicine" in the Western sense but was not merely cosmetic either. The tradition of mehndi body art at weddings (South Asia, Middle East, North Africa) simultaneously provided antimicrobial protection to the hands and feet in the days before the wedding when intensive food preparation and social contact created infectious exposure, aesthetic beauty marking social transition, and — through the flower oil and paste application to hair — conditioning and perfuming. The functional pharmacology was embedded in the ritual.

**Song of Solomon**: "My beloved is unto me as a cluster of camphire in the vineyards of En-gedi" (Song of Solomon 1:14 KJV). The "camphire" (henna, *Lawsonia*) clusters were famous for their extraordinary perfume — the white flowers of henna produce one of the most intensely fragrant flower scents in the Middle Eastern flora. The En-gedi oasis (now in modern Israel) was known for its henna cultivation in biblical times. The erotic poetry of Song of Solomon choosing henna as a metaphor for the beloved encodes both the sensory intensity of the fragrance and the cultural significance of the plant (associated with beauty, celebration, and the protective anointing of the body).

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

| Form | Dose | Application |
|------|------|-------------|
| Fresh leaf paste (powder + warm water, lemon juice) | Apply topically; leave 2–4 hours | Skin antimicrobial; cooling; cosmetic body art; hair dyeing |
| Leaf decoction (10–15g dried leaf in 500ml water) | Gargle/mouthwash | Oral ulcers; gum inflammation; oral antimicrobial |
| Topical for nails | Paste applied daily for 3–4 weeks | Onychomycosis (nail fungal infection) |
| Bark decoction (5–10g in 500ml, internal) | 50ml 2× daily — only under Ayurvedic supervision | Liver disorders, fever — Ayurvedic internal use |
| Flower infusion (aromatic) | Perfume/aromatic | The intensely fragrant flowers as natural perfume |

**IMPORTANT**: Only use natural henna (orange-red colour). Never use "black henna" products — they contain PPD, a potent contact allergen.

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM (cross-reference)**: Astringent, bitter; cool. Channels: Lung (肺), Liver (肝). Cools heat, astringes, antimicrobial.

**Ayurvedic**: Bitter, astringent; cooling. Reduces Pitta ↓↓, Kapha ↓. *Tvagdoṣahara* (skin disease remover), *Śītalā* (fever-cooling), *Varṇya* (skin complexion-improving). Tridoshic balancing at standard topical doses.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **G6PD deficiency**: Lawsone can cause haemolytic anaemia in G6PD-deficient individuals; avoid large-area topical application and avoid internal use; particularly important in G6PD-common populations (Mediterranean, sub-Saharan African, South Asian origin)
- **Pregnancy (internal)**: Root and bark have emmenagogue properties; leaf topical for hand/hair art is traditional and generally considered safe; avoid internal use
- **"Black henna" (PPD)**: Never use; risk of severe delayed hypersensitivity, permanent sensitisation, and facial/scarring dermatitis
- **Natural henna allergy**: True allergy to natural henna is rare but exists; patch test before large-area application
