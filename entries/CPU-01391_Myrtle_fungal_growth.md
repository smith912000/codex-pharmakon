# Myrtle fungal growth — Murtidanon (Myrtidanum, Dioscorides 1-156)
## *A growth found on the myrtle tree, wart-like and standing out from the trunk; the translation glosses it "Myrtidanum — Fungal growth on Myrtle", and the text itself does not say that it is a fungus* | *(Volume IV — Fungal/Algal, shelved in Volume I)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 1-156, *Murtidanon* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 4673; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 158, class "plant"; the book index sends the reader to page 161). It is the chapter that follows the tree's own chapters, 1-154 *Balaustion* (line 4649) and 1-155 *Mursine* (myrtle, line 4653), and sits in the translation's section on fruit trees. The whole chapter was read:

"Myrtidanum is that which grows on myrtle — unequal and standing out, similar to a wart and the same colour, similar to hands clasping around the body of the myrtle. It is more therapeutic for the bowels than myrtle. Bruised and mixed with hard wine and made into pellets, it is dried in the shade and put in jars to store. It is more effective than the fruit and leaves. It is mixed with stiff ointments, suppositories, baths, poultices, and anywhere there is need of an astringent." (lines 4675-4679; a page break and the number 161 fall in the middle of the passage)

The suggested heading, "Myrtidanum — Fungal growth on Myrtle" (line 4674), is the translator's. The chapter's own description is of something that grows on the myrtle, uneven, wart-like, of the colour of the plant (the text says "the same colour" without naming what it is the same colour as), and clasping the body of the tree "like hands". It does not call the growth a fungus, a gall or a part of the tree. Whether it is a fungus, an insect gall, a swelling of the tree or something else is not settled by the sources retrieved, and this entry is shelved as fungal on the translator's gloss alone. [USER: verify]

**The parent plant.** The preceding chapter, 1-155 *Mursine*, is the translator's "Myrtus communis var romana — Broad-leaved Myrtle" and reports the myrtle's cultivated black and white forms, the herb and fruit as "astringent", and many uses of the fruit, leaves and oil. The comparisons in 1-156 ("more therapeutic for the bowels than myrtle", "more effective than the fruit and leaves") refer to that plant. The myrtle itself is not described here.

### Active Components

None can be stated from the text, and the nature of the growth is not established. In general terms: if the growth is a plant gall, such galls are commonly rich in tannins, which are astringent; if it is a fungus, its chemistry would be that of the particular fungus; and the myrtle tree itself carries an aromatic essential oil and tannins. These are general statements, not readings of the chapter, and none is attributed to *murtidanon*.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records the growth as an astringent for the bowels and for external and mixed preparations. The nature of the material is unknown, the species of fungus (if any) is unidentified, and nothing in the sources supports a modern use. No modern protocol is supplied.

---

### Preparation & Dosing

The one preparation is given in full in 1-156: the growth is "bruised and mixed with hard wine and made into pellets", "dried in the shade" and "put in jars to store". It is then "mixed with stiff ointments, suppositories, baths, poultices". No quantity, no amount of wine and no drinking dose is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM).** The chapter's only quality is its use "anywhere there is need of an astringent". It is said to be "more therapeutic for the bowels than myrtle" and "more effective than the fruit and leaves"; the parent chapter calls the myrtle's herb and fruit "astringent". No warming, cooling, moistening or drying quality is printed for the growth.

---

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. What the herbal offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, adulteration detection), and this short chapter shows it in part: the material is recognised by where it grows (on the myrtle), by its shape (uneven, wart-like, standing out) and colour, it is prepared by bruising, forming into pellets and drying in the shade, and it is stored in jars. No season of gathering, no assay and no adulterant is given. The chapter is therapeutic and carries no cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to take from it for cultivation. The practical lesson is the author's habit of recognition: say where a material grows, what it looks like, and how it is dried and stored. The growth itself is unidentified and is not a recommended material. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Unidentified material:** the growth is not securely identified as a fungus or as anything else; unidentified growths from trees should not be eaten or used as medicine.
- **Fungal hazards:** if it is a fungus, wild fungi can be poisonous, and moulds can contaminate stored pellets.
- **Wine as vehicle:** unsuitable in pregnancy or for those avoiding alcohol.
- **Bowel complaints:** persistent diarrhoea or bleeding needs medical assessment; an astringent does not treat the cause.
- **Plant status:** the legal or conservation status of myrtle trees from which the growth would be taken was not retrieved. [USER: verify]
