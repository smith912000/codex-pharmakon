# Pig's eyes — "the Two Eyes ... of a Pig" (Bryan 1930, Papyrus Ebers)
## *The eyes of a pig (Bryan's "Hog"), named in one Ebers remedy for blindness; no text on disk identifies the animal beyond Bryan's English* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP, AT, DMM, CM, SBJ, SS

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The only locus read is Bryan's account of eye disease in `Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` (about lines 2957-2980, p.104 of Bryan). Bryan writes of blindness: "Only three remedies were given for this calamity. The first, a 'lotion' made by crushing Dried Myrrh in Sour Milk, we may be sure accomplished nothing. The second, however, a poultice of Powdered Onions, most assuredly made the blind man see stars. The third remedy spared the patient somewhat, the disease being attacked through that less-sensitive organ, the ear." The recipe follows under the heading "ANOTHER AGAINST BLINDNESS". In this OCR file its lines are scrambled and read, in order of appearance: "The Two Eyes therefrom / True Collyrium Red-lead Wild Honey / of a Pig; / remove / the water", followed by "Crush, powder, make into one, and inject into the Ear of the Patient. Thereby he will at once recover. When thou hast seen properly to this mixing, repeat this Magic Formula: I HAVE BROUGHT THIS THING AND PUT IT IN ITS PLACE. THE CROCODILE IS WEAK AND POWERLESS. (Twice)." My reading is that the two eyes of a pig, with the water removed, are crushed with true collyrium, red-lead and wild honey; the scrambled layout leaves the grouping of the ingredients uncertain. ### Active Components

An eye consists mostly of water-rich gel (the vitreous), the lens (crystallin proteins), the cornea and sclera (collagen), the retina (nerve tissue) and pigment. The Ebers texts say nothing about composition, and no ingredient list is attached to the eyes in the recipe beyond collyrium, red-lead and honey.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The recipe is a remedy for blindness delivered through the ear, with a spoken formula, and Bryan's own comments treat it with mild scepticism. Nothing in the texts read supports any real effect on sight, and delay in seeking proper eye care is the practical danger. Pig tissue, ear injection and lead compounds (red-lead) all carry risk. The Codex gives no protocol, dose or preparation.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation is the one read: the two eyes of a pig, "remove the water", with true collyrium, red-lead and wild honey, "Crush, powder, make into one, and inject into the Ear of the Patient", with the spoken formula. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Animal eye tissue has no established use as a treatment for blindness. Injecting non-sterile material into the ear can cause infection and damage the eardrum, and red-lead is a lead oxide and is poisonous. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

No quality is printed for pig's eyes in any of the six traditions' texts read. Sushruta's boar-flesh properties (above) belong to the flesh and are not carried over to the eye.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The pig's eyes are one ingredient in a remedy for blindness, with a formula "to be repeated" over the mixing; spell and drug are one intervention (*heka*), and the recipe is incomplete without the words. The words as Bryan prints them address no deity by name; they speak of the crocodile being weak and powerless. The Ebers material is organised by complaint: it records what was done for blindness, not what the substance does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*, and the identity of the animal part is Bryan's English.

### Contraindications

Do not use animal eye tissue, red-lead or any non-sterile material in or near the eye or ear. Raw animal tissue can carry bacteria and parasites. Lead compounds are cumulatively toxic. Pigs are livestock; slaughter-derived parts raise ordinary food-safety, ethical and religious-dietary questions, and no conservation status applies to the domestic pig. Anyone with sudden or progressive loss of sight needs prompt examination by an eye clinician.
