# Tarantula (spider) — Ebers Papyrus, "Tarantula" (Bryan 1930; no Egyptian word given)

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The tarantula is in the Ebers Papyrus as presented in Bryan's 1930 translation (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`), in a Codex source list entry (`Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv`, row 127) that points to Bryan's chapter VII, "Organic Remedies", at line 1404 of the text. That line is in Bryan's list of animals from which the Egyptians took remedies: "Ass Hog Dog Cat Frog Tadpole Scorpion Eel Electric Eel Crab Swordfish Ostrich Locust Tarantula Beetle Bat Wasp Fly Tapeworm" (page 35). Bryan introduces the list as that of "those animals whose identity is not in doubt, and who were called upon to sacrifice themselves in the cause of the Healing Art" (lines 1388-1392), after saying that a complete list "would not be possible since ... the identity of a considerable number of the animals mentioned is still beyond us" (lines 1382-1388). The names.csv row records that the identification is "Bryan's English identification; no Egyptian word or binomial given in this list". The English word "tarantula" is applied to more than one kind of spider, and which animal Bryan means is not given. [USER: verify]

The list says the tarantula is among the animals from which a remedy was drawn, but the lines read do not give the remedy. Bryan's second list, of the parts taken (lines 1408-1415; pages 35-36), runs from hair to tail and is not tied to individual animals, and a search of Bryan's text for "tarantula" finds only the list (line 1404) and one other place. That other place is not a use of the animal. In the chapter on "Domestic Hints" (chapter XXIV, from line 4158; page 165), Bryan writes of the Egyptian farmyard: "We are told what to do to prevent Wasps stinging: we simply take the Fat-of-the-bird-coraciasgarrula and smear ourselves with it. To prevent the Tarantula stinging, however, inunction with Olive Oil was sufficient." (line 4224). So the one concrete passage treats the tarantula as a stinging creature to be guarded against, with olive oil rubbed on the body; Bryan adds that a scorpion is killed by putting a lizard on the fire, and a lizard by burning a scorpion (same line).

So the Ebers record for this entry is two things. The tarantula is named in the list of animals used in medicine, with no preparation, no complaint and no dose. And it is named as a stinger, with an olive-oil inunction as the protective measure. Whether the olive-oil prescription itself is Bryan's summary of a passage of the papyrus or his own paraphrase is not stated in the lines read. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The word "tarantula" in modern English names large hairy spiders, and in some usages a wolf spider. Spiders have a chitin exoskeleton and most make venom, a mixture of peptides and other small molecules, though the effect on people differs widely by species. Nothing in Bryan names an active principle, and none specific to this entry is claimed.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is that the tarantula is named in a list of animals used in remedies, and in a household hint as a creature whose sting is prevented by rubbing the body with olive oil. The Codex gives no protocol. Olive oil is not a protection against any spider's bite, and no remedy made from a tarantula is described in the lines read.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation of the tarantula is given in the sources retrieved. The only preparation in the lines read is the protective one: "inunction with Olive Oil" (line 4224), with no amount and no schedule. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Spider bites vary from harmless to dangerous depending on the species, and a bite with severe pain, spreading redness, swelling, necrosis or systemic symptoms needs medical care. Oil on the skin does not prevent a spider from biting. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's vital constitution. The person in the Egyptian texts is plural (*ka*, *ba*, *akh*, *ib*) and the operative power is *heka*, with spell and drug as parts of one intervention, as the project's cultivation spec records; Bryan makes the general point that "Nowhere ... is the Magic Formula advocated as a cure per se, but it is an essential part of the treatment" (page 41, line 1489). The olive-oil prescription against the tarantula is given in Bryan's summary without a spell, and nothing is tied to a deity. The identity of the animal is itself a modern hypothesis resting on Bryan's English.

### Contraindications

- **Bites:** large spiders can bite; a bite with spreading redness, necrosis, severe pain or general symptoms needs medical care, and olive oil is not a protection.
- **Venom and allergy:** some people react severely to spider material and hairs; the animal is not named to a species, so none can be called safe.
- **Identification:** Bryan gives only an English name; what the Egyptian word meant is not settled in the lines read. [USER: verify]
- **Conservation:** some tarantula species are protected and traded under international rules; the species here is unknown. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** collecting wild spiders for a use that no line read describes is hard to justify.
