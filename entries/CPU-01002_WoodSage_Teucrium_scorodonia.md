# Wood Sage / Wood Germander

## *Teucrium scorodonia* L. | Family: Lamiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Hot and dry in the second degree; bitter, incisive, resolving; a bitter-tonic diaphoretic of the germander (*Teucrium*) class.

**Training Phase:** Purification & Clearing — a bitter resolvent for stagnation, "cold" swellings, and foul wounds; used with restraint because its virtue and its hazard share one chemistry.

---

### Origin & Tradition

Wood sage is a woodland germander — a hairy, sage-like Lamiaceae of dry woods, heaths, and shady banks, its greenish-yellow flowers borne in one-sided spikes, its crushed leaves smelling faintly of garlic (hence *scorodonia*, from Greek *skorodon*, garlic). It is the same **genus as wall germander (*Teucrium chamaedrys*)**, already in the codex, and it inherits that genus's double identity: a prized bitter tonic and vulnerary on one hand, and a documented cause of liver injury on the other. Folk use across Britain and Europe casts it as a bitter "poor man's sage" — a tonic for the stomach, a diaphoretic for fevers and colds, a wound-herb (vulnerary), and a remedy for gout, dropsy, and skin complaints.

**Dioscorides (DMM).** The germanders enter *De Materia Medica* as *teukrion* / *khamaidrys* and the garlic-scented *skordion* (water germander) — bitter, hot herbs used as tonics for the stomach and spleen, as diuretics and emmenagogues, for wound-healing, and against poisons and "obstructions." *T. scorodonia* itself is a northern species not securely named by Dioscorides, but it falls squarely within this Teucrium doctrinal family. Doctrinal category: **hot, bitter, resolving (deobstruent) tonic-vulnerary.** [USER: verify which Dioscoridean germander maps to *scorodonia* — the northern wood sage is likely a later assimilation]

**Avicenna (CM).** The germander of the Arabic tradition is *kamādaryūs* (كماذريوس, from *chamaedrys*) — hot and dry, a bitter deobstruent used for the stomach, spleen, and liver "obstructions," for dropsy, and for chronic fevers and joint pains (gout). *T. scorodonia* is not itself a canonical *Canon* simple but shares the *kamādaryūs* virtues by genus. **Assyrian (AT).** Mesopotamian texts use many bitter and aromatic herbs as tonics and wound remedies, but no secure cuneiform identification with *Teucrium scorodonia* is established; the species' range is largely west-European. **Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** Wood sage's doctrine and its danger are the *same molecule class*, and this is the entry's central lesson. The chemotaxonomic signature of the entire *Teucrium* genus is the **neo-clerodane diterpenoids** — intensely bitter, often furan-bearing compounds (teucrin A, teuflin, teucvidin and relatives). Their bitterness is precisely what a bitter-tonic tradition prizes: bitter principles stimulate salivary and gastric secretion, "wake up" digestion, and give the tonic-deobstruent action the old texts describe. But the *furano*-neo-clerodanes are also **hepatotoxic**: in the liver, cytochrome-P450 (notably CYP3A) oxidises the furan ring to a reactive epoxide/enedial that depletes glutathione, damages hepatocyte proteins, and triggers apoptosis — the mechanism behind the well-documented germander (*T. chamaedrys*) herb-induced liver injury (HILI). So the folk "bitter tonic that opens obstructions of the liver" is, chemically, a genus whose bitter diterpenes *act on the liver* — beneficially at the level of digestive stimulation, but hazardously at the level of reactive-metabolite hepatotoxicity when taken internally, concentrated, or chronically. The doctrine is half-right and half-dangerous, and honest practice must hold both.

### Active Components

- **neo-Clerodane diterpenoids (chemotaxonomic markers):** teucrin A, teuflin, teucvidin, teuflidin and relatives — the bitter principles **and** the hepatotoxic furano-diterpenoids.
- **Flavonoids:** luteolin, apigenin and cirsiliol-type glycosides — antioxidant, anti-inflammatory (the "protective," non-toxic fraction).
- **Iridoid glycosides:** harpagide and 8-*O*-acetylharpagide reported across *Teucrium* — anti-inflammatory.
- **Phenylethanoid glycosides & phenolic acids:** antioxidant, cytoprotective.
- **Essential oil:** small amounts; contributes the faint garlicky/sage aroma.
- **Tannins:** astringency for the vulnerary/wound use.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica, wood sage sits in **Purification & Clearing** but under a **caution flag**. Traditionally it is a bitter digestive tonic, a diaphoretic for feverish colds, and a topical vulnerary and skin-wash; its bitterness makes it a genuine digestive stimulant, and as an *external* wound/skin application (where hepatic exposure is minimal) it aligns with the vulnerary tradition and carries the least risk. **Internal use is where doctrine and hazard collide.** Because *Teucrium* diterpenes are hepatotoxic, sustained internal use as a "liver tonic" — the very indication tradition emphasises — is the highest-risk application and should be avoided outside brief, low-dose, informed use. Kenetica's positioning: treat wood sage primarily as an *external* bitter-vulnerary and a historical study specimen, not as a routine internal tonic; where a bitter digestive is wanted, prefer a non-hepatotoxic bitter. It pairs, conceptually, with the wall-germander entry as a paired lesson in the genus's therapeutic-toxic duality.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the data are genus-rich but *scorodonia*-poor: the hepatotoxicity of the genus is well established, while species-specific pharmacology of wood sage itself is sparse.

- **Genus hepatotoxicity via neo-clerodane diterpenoids (well established).** *Teucrium polium* neo-clerodane-containing fractions were hepatotoxic to HepG2 hepatocytes, while flavonoid/phenolic fractions stripped of the diterpenoids were antioxidant and non-toxic — a clean separation of the toxic principle from the beneficial one (Pacifico et al., *Food Chem* 2012; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2012.06.071)). Furano-neo-clerodanes such as teucrin A from *T. chamaedrys* are the compounds implicated in germander liver injury (Sundaresan et al., *Phytochem Anal* 2006; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/pca.912)), and additional putatively hepatotoxic neo-clerodanes have been isolated across multiple *Teucrium* species (Piozzi et al., *Planta Med* 1997; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-2006-957744)). **Grade: strong for the hazard — the hepatotoxicity flag is well supported at genus level.**
- **Bitter-tonic / antioxidant rationale (moderate, genus-level).** *Teucrium* extracts yield anti-inflammatory iridoids (harpagide, 8-*O*-acetylharpagide) and show significant DPPH radical-scavenging and high phenolic content, giving a real basis for the "tonic/protective" reputation of the *non-diterpenoid* fraction (Abdullah et al., *Molecules* 2022; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27185963)). Notably, that study found the hepatotoxic furano-clerodanes were **not detected** in its particular extract — evidence that diterpenoid content, and thus risk, varies by species, part, and preparation. **Grade: moderate — supports the tonic idea while confirming variable toxicity.**
- **Wood sage (*T. scorodonia*) specifically (sparse).** I retrieved **no controlled clinical trial** and little species-specific pharmacology for *T. scorodonia* this run; its profile is inferred from the genus. The gout/diaphoretic/vulnerary claims remain **traditional and untested** in humans. **Grade: data-sparse — null on species-specific clinical evidence.**

Honest summary: the *risk* side of wood sage (neo-clerodane hepatotoxicity) is far better evidenced than any *benefit*. The bitter-tonic and antioxidant rationale is plausible from the flavonoid/iridoid fraction, but species-specific efficacy for wood sage is essentially unstudied, and internal use should be regarded as hazardous rather than tonic.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Hot and dry in the second degree. Qualities: bitter, incisive, resolving/deobstruent, diaphoretic, diuretic, vulnerary, astringent. Organ affinities (traditional): stomach and spleen (bitter tonic), liver (deobstruent — *this is also the organ of toxicity*), skin and wounds (vulnerary), joints (gout). The energetic "opens obstructions of the liver" claim must be read alongside the modern finding that the genus's bitter principle *injures* the liver.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** Mesopotamian texts use many bitter and aromatic herbs as tonics and wound remedies, but no secure cuneiform identification with *Teucrium scorodonia* is established — the species' range is largely west-European and the entry's own attribution is flagged provisional. Under the standing blocking warning governing Assyrian substance identifications, tablet references and their causal-agency framing of illness, no cultivation status can be asserted, and the identification is too insecure to carry even a firm therapeutic one. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The germanders enter *De Materia Medica* as *teukrion* / *khamaidrys* and the garlic-scented *skordion* — bitter, hot herbs worked as tonics for the stomach and spleen, as diuretics and emmenagogues, for wound-healing and against obstructions; northern wood sage itself is not securely named by Dioscorides and enters the family only by genus analogy, a later assimilation. Even for the germanders he does name, Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system to assign a cultivation status; what the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention — the bitter taste and garlic scent as sensory marks, and the careful identification of one germander against another. That is a practice of assay and provenance, not a vital-body doctrine, and for this species even the therapeutic attribution is by analogy rather than by name. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The germander of the Arabic tradition is *kamādaryūs* (كماذريوس), hot and dry, a bitter deobstruent used for obstructions of the stomach, spleen and liver, for dropsy, chronic fevers and joint pains; *T. scorodonia* is not itself a canonical *Canon* simple and shares these virtues only by genus. The tradition's governing cultivation idea — the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture as a finite endowment whose depletion is ageing — frames the whole regimen and does not attach a cultivation status to a hot-dry bitter resolvent; no *mufarrih* or cardiac-*ruh* role and no *Canon* chapter is cited for it. The germander it belongs to is claimed as a therapeutic deobstruent with no cultivation status recorded, and the species-level claim is itself by analogy. **(b) framed.**

**Practitioner's note.** No tradition here files wood sage as a substance a cultivator takes to build or conserve vitality, and modern chemistry gives a hard reason to keep it that way: the genus's bitter neo-clerodane diterpenoids are hepatotoxic, so the very "liver deobstruent" indication the old texts prize is the highest-risk internal use. The honest cultivator's posture is abstentive and external — use it, if at all, as a topical bitter-vulnerary wash where hepatic exposure is minimal, prefer a non-hepatotoxic bitter when a digestive tonic is wanted, and treat internal long-term use as contraindicated rather than tonic.

### Contraindications

- **HEPATOTOXICITY (primary warning):** *Teucrium* neo-clerodane/furano-diterpenoids cause herb-induced liver injury (as documented for *T. chamaedrys* and *T. polium*). **Do not use internally in anyone with liver disease, and avoid chronic or repeated internal use entirely.** Discontinue at once with any sign of hepatotoxicity (jaundice, dark urine, right-upper-quadrant pain, malaise, raised transaminases).
- **Drug interactions:** avoid combining internal use with other hepatotoxic drugs/herbs, alcohol, or CYP3A-affecting agents that could increase reactive-metabolite formation.
- **Pregnancy / lactation:** avoid — traditional emmenagogue reputation plus hepatotoxic diterpenes; no safety data.
- **Children:** avoid internal use.
- **Preparation variability:** diterpenoid content varies by species, part, and season, so toxicity is unpredictable across sources — a further reason to restrict to external use.
- Prefer external (wash/poultice) applications; regard internal use as high-risk and generally contraindicated.
