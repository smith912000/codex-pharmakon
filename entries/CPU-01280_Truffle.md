# Truffle — Hudnon (HUDNON), "Tuber"
## *An underground, leafless, round, pale-yellow "root" eaten as a vegetable; the translator's heading to Dioscorides 2-175 suggests Tuber album, Rhizopogon album and Choiromyces meandriformis, "White Truffle, False Truffle"; the chapter text itself gives no binomial* | *(Volume IV — Fungal/Algal, shelved in Volume I)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The truffle is chapter 175 of Book Two of the *De Materia Medica*, headed HUDNON, in the section "LACHANA: VEGETABLES" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 6048-6051; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` carries it at line 365 as 2-175, line 6048, and `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 349 has "Hudnon, Rhizopogon album", class plant, an identification not marked uncertain; the book's page index prints "HUDNON 300" and "False Truffle 300"). The translator's heading (line 6050) reads "Tuber album, Rhizopogon album, Choiromyces meandriformis — White Truffle, False Truffle". The whole chapter is two sentences (line 6051): "Tuber is a round, pale, yellow root without leaves or stalk. It is dug up in the spring and is edible eaten either raw or boiled [vegetable]."

That is everything the chapter says. It gives the form of the material (round, pale, yellow, without leaves or stalk), the way it is taken (dug up), the season (spring), and its status as food (edible, raw or boiled). The word "[vegetable]" in square brackets is the translator's. The chapter gives no medicinal indication, no property such as warming or astringent, no preparation as a drug, and no dose. The text calls it a "root", which is how Dioscorides and his time understood a leafless thing that comes out of the ground; the translator's botanical suggestions identify it as a fungus. The three binomials in the heading are alternatives offered by the translator, and they name two different groups of fungi (the genera *Tuber* and *Choiromyces* on the one hand, *Rhizopogon* on the other), so the identification is open. *Its neighbours in the section.* The chapters on either side are also foods. 2-173, TRAGOPOGON (wild salsify), ends "It is an edible herb" (line 6038); 2-174, ORNITHOGALON (star of Bethlehem), says that its bulbous root "is eaten both raw and boiled [vegetable]" (line 6047); 2-176 is SMILAX (line 6052), for which the translator suggests beans and climbing asparagus. The truffle's place is a run of vegetables with no medicinal content. Fungi as a group are treated by Dioscorides at 4-83, MUKETES, as either edible or poisonous (a separate entry of this Codex, `I_868_Mushrooms.md`); that chapter does not name the truffle.

*Agaric, a different entry.* The other fungus in this batch, *agaricon* at 3-1, is given in the text as "said to be a root" and has a long list of medicinal uses (`I_870_Agaric.md`). The truffle chapter has none.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, truffles are the underground fruiting bodies of fungi that live in association with the roots of trees; like other fungi their tissue is made of chitin and glucans, water, a little protein and fibre, and a variety of volatile compounds responsible for the smell for which the true truffles are prized. Because the identification in the text is open, no compound is assigned to the material it describes.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records the truffle as a food and gives it no medicinal use. The Codex therefore has no application for it other than as an entry in the record of what Dioscorides placed in the vegetable section. A cultivator who wants truffles for the table must identify them reliably: many underground and surface fungi look alike, and some are poisonous.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives two ways of taking the truffle, "raw" or "boiled" (line 6051), as a food, and a season for digging, "in the spring". It gives no medicinal preparation and no dose. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. True truffles (the genus *Tuber*) are ascomycete fungi that grow underground in association with the roots of particular trees and are eaten as a delicacy; the "false truffles" are other underground fungi, some of which are not edible. Wild fungi are a common cause of poisoning when misidentified. The text makes no health claim for the truffle, so there is no claim to correlate. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Contraindications

- **Identification:** the description is two lines long and would fit several fungi; do not collect or eat wild fungi from this entry. Misidentification can be fatally poisonous.
- **Allergy:** fungal allergy is possible; stop at once on any reaction.
- **Raw consumption:** the text says "raw or boiled"; raw wild fungi can cause stomach upset, and cooking is the safer rule.
- **Identity:** the text calls the material a "root", and the translator's binomials are alternatives, not one species.
