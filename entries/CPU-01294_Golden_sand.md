# Golden sand — gser.gyis.bye.ma (Clark 1995) / Sergyi Jema (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *A sand-like substance gathered from lake shores, "superior" when yellow, listed among the earth medicines of the Explanatory Tantra; Clark's translation gives no English or scientific equivalent beyond "Golden Sand", and the identity is not otherwise fixed in the sources retrieved* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The loci given for this entry are `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` ("Clark 1995 (line 858)") and `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 160, where the name is spelled "Sergyi Jema", class mineral, identification marked uncertain. Line 858 of `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` opens "gser.gyis.bye.ma: Golden Sand:". It falls under chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (heading at line 792), in the specific section that lists eight kinds of individual substance, "(1) precious [metal] medicines (2) stone medicines (3) earth medicines (4) tree medicines (5) resins (6) plants (7) herbs (8) animal products" (line 802). Golden sand is the first entry of "(3) The Exposition of the Power of Earth Medicines" (line 857).

The whole entry reads (line 858): "[has previously been (wrongly) identified as the small seeds of a tree or of a plant called dwon.poi.rgyug.pa. In actuality it is a sandlike substance obtained from the shores of lakes. The superior type is yellow. The common type is smoky colored, with bitter, hot taste. Nowadays it is found in abundance and so there is little chance of mistaking it. Both types] cure kidney disorders and urinary retention." The square brackets are, by Clark's own statement (line 82), commentary: interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, so the description of the sand, as against the bare statement that it cures kidney disorders and urinary retention, is commentary and not the root text.

Clark states in his preface (line 83) that he gives each substance's Tibetan name first because much Tibetan materia medica "has remained either totally unidentified or only tentatively identified"; for golden sand he gives no scientific name at all, which is consistent with that. The commentary itself records an earlier mistaken identification as the seeds of a plant, and that correction is the only identity information the text offers. `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` lists the row with class "plant", but the line it cites says the substance is sand from lake shores and the spine and `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` class it as mineral; the class in names.csv appears to be an error.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`) carries the same material in a shorter form: "To reveal the efficacy of the soil medicines: Sergyi Jema cures kidney disorders and obstruction of urine." (line 3363). It also lists Sergyi Jema among the diuretics: "Gya tsha, Gyam tsha, Sergi Jema, Digsin, Sugmel and Nyiga are the group of medicines that are diuretics." (line 3652). Clark's chapter 21 has a parallel list: "In the category of oedema medicines are included lake salt, rock salt, golden sand, crab, Smaller Cardamon and (a type of) Mallow leaf (Malva verticillata)." (line 1223). The three passages agree that golden sand belongs with remedies for the kidney, urine and oedema.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. The only physical facts in the sources are those in line 858: it is sandlike, taken from lake shores, yellow in the better kind and smoky in the common kind. In general terms, a shore sand is a mixture of mineral grains whose composition depends on the rocks around the lake. Which mineral or minerals this sand was is not stated, and the name "golden" does not establish any particular one. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved say that the Tibetan Explanatory Tantra lists a lake-shore sand for kidney disorders and urinary retention and for oedema. The substance cannot be identified from the sources retrieved, and the complaints it is said to treat are ones that need diagnosis. The Codex does not recommend swallowing or applying any sand.

The sources retrieved give no preparation for golden sand. They do not say how it was gathered, cleaned or ground, whether it was taken in water, tea or a compound, or how much. Clark's chapter 21 lists it among oedema medicines (line 1223) but the lines read give no recipe. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Kidney disease, urinary retention, oedema:** these can be signs of kidney failure, obstruction, heart failure or infection and need prompt medical assessment; do not substitute a sand.
- **Unidentified material:** the composition of "golden sand" is not established, and a mineral from an unknown source may carry toxic metals or microbial contamination.
- **Ingestion of grit:** sand damages teeth and can injure the gut.
- **Pregnancy and children:** unsuitable.
- **Identification:** the name is a translator's English rendering of a Tibetan name; Clark himself says (preface, line 83) that much of the Tibetan materia medica is only tentatively identified, and the text records that this sand was earlier mistaken for a plant's seeds.
