# Niu bian monkshood — 牛扁 *Niu Bian* (Shennong bencao jing); Yang 1998 prints "Niu Bian (Radix Aconiti Lycoctomi)"
## *A monkshood, Aconitum barbatum Pers. var. puberulum Ledeb. in the standard modern identification of 牛扁; medium confidence, because the classic's text gives no description of the plant and Yang's Latin names the wolfsbane group (Aconiti Lycoctoni), which is a related but different assignment* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Shennong bencao jing among the texts on disk.** The entry rests on Sun Xingyan's 1799 reconstruction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt`) and, as a second witness attributed to him, Yang's 1998 English translation (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`). The identification is ours. (The spine carries the placeholder "Aconitum sp." for the Yang locus of this same monograph, and other monkshood entries from other traditions, for example I_903 Wolf's-bane, *Aconitum lycoctonum*, are not this drug.)

**Toxicity caution.** The plant belongs to the monkshood genus, whose members contain poisonous alkaloids. Nothing here is a recommendation. The classic uses this plant mainly as an external bath and as a veterinary drug; it is not to be taken by mouth on the strength of this entry.

**The Shennong text proper.** The 味 paragraph, in the lower (下) classic, reads 味甘，微寒 ("sweet, slightly cold"). It governs 身皮创热气 ("sores of the body's skin, with heat qi"), says it 可作浴汤 ("can be made into a bath decoction"), 杀牛虱小虫 ("kills cattle lice and small insects") and 又疗牛病 ("also treats diseases of cattle"). The habitat is 生川谷 ("grows in river valleys"). There is no 久服 clause. No toxicity statement is printed in the paragraph. (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, lower classic, entry 牛扁 *niu bian*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 3567.)

**Wu Pu.** No Wu Pu paragraph is printed for this entry.

**Mingyi bielu layer (later).** 《名医》曰：生桂阳 ("grows in Guiyang"). (Line 3569.)

**Sun's note (案).** Sun cites Tao Hongjing: 太常贮，名扁特，或名扁毒, "stored at the Taichang office, it was called Bian Te, or called Bian Du". 扁毒 reads "Bian poison"; Sun's note makes no comment on it. (Line 3571.)

**Yang 1998 (second witness).** Under "Herbs: Inferior Class" (line 3656), Yang prints: "Niu Bian (Radix Aconiti Lycoctomi) is bitter and slightly cold. It mainly treats skin sores and heat qi. It can be used to make bathwater. It kills gadflies and small worms. In addition, it treats cow diseases. It grows in rivers and valleys." Two differences from Sun: Yang has "bitter" where Sun has "sweet" (甘), and "gadflies" where Sun's text has 牛虱, cattle lice. Yang's Chinese source is not on disk, and the second difference may be a rendering choice. [USER: verify]

**Identity.** The text gives only the name and uses: a plant used for the skin and for cattle. "Niu bian" means roughly "ox flat". Tao's alias 扁毒 and the poison-class setting fit a monkshood, but the text does not say so. The modern identification (*Aconitum barbatum* var. *puberulum*) and Yang's Latin (*Aconitum lycoctonum* group) both stay within the genus. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Monkshoods (*Aconitum*) contain diterpenoid alkaloids, some of them highly toxic to the heart and nerves. The alkaloid profile of this particular variety was not retrieved for this entry, and none is asserted. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Classic records an external bath for skin sores, a killer of cattle lice and small insects, and a remedy for cattle disease. The Codex gives no protocol and recommends none. The bath is the only human use the text gives, and absorption of monkshood alkaloids through broken skin is a recognised hazard of the genus.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation is the bath: 可作浴汤 ("can be made into a bath decoction"). No quantity, strength, duration or course is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Monkshood alkaloids act on the sodium channels of heart and nerve tissue; poisoning by monkshood can cause numbness, vomiting, irregular heartbeat and death. The genus is among the better-known poisonous plants. Whether this variety shows any of the effects the text names has not been tested in sources retrieved for this entry. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SBJ (Shennong text proper, Sun ed.).** 味甘，微寒: sweet; slightly cold. (Yang: bitter, slightly cold.) No toxicity statement is printed in the paragraph.
- Class: lower (下). No organ affinity is printed. No other tradition's classification is applied.

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The classic files 牛扁 in the lower grade (下品); its preface (Sun ed. line 2935) says lower drugs are "for treating disease to correspond with earth", "mostly toxic, not to be taken long" (多毒，不可久服). The paragraph has no 久服 clause and no life-nourishing promise; it lists skin sores and the killing of cattle lice. Its place in the lower grade is a warning and not a status. (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, lower classic, entry 牛扁 *niu bian*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 3567.)

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. A lower-grade drug taken as a short external course, or not at all, and in this case used chiefly on animals.

### Contraindications

- **Poison.** Monkshood is poisonous by mouth and can be absorbed through broken skin; do not take it internally, do not apply it to open wounds, and keep it away from children and animals.
- **Identification.** Plants of the genus are easily confused with edible plants and with one another; wild gathering is hazardous.
- **Skin sores and fever.** Need medical assessment.
- **Veterinary use.** The text's use on cattle is historical record only.
