# Snow frog flesh — da.pyid (Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch.20); Daji meat (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011); "Male White Snow Frog" (Clark)
## *The flesh of the "Male White Snow Frog" (Clark's English), for which Clark prints the Latin "Phrynosoma sp." and the commentary name phuhi.rgyal.po; Men-Tsee-Khang prints "Daji meat". The two Latin identifications in the sources do not agree, and the animal is not securely identified* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is the Tibetan *Four Tantras*. In Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; "Clark" below), the materia medica of chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra ends with the medicines of animal origin: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). Meat is the third of the thirteen. In the meat paragraph, after human flesh, snake meat, vulture meat and peacock meat, Clark prints (line 1169):

"da.pyid: Male White Snow Frog: Phrynosomasp.: [has nine names, for example phuhi.rgyal.po.] Its flesh cures cold disorders of the waist and kidneys. [Its urine is a highly recommended aphrodisiac]."

The bracketing matters. Clark's introduction (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets are taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, and those in parentheses from oral instructions. So "has nine names, for example phuhi.rgyal.po" and the remark about the urine come from the commentary layer, and the unbracketed claim is that the flesh "cures cold disorders of the waist and kidneys". The urine is a different part of the animal and is not this entry. "Phrynosomasp." is printed that way in the OCR text and is read here as *Phrynosoma* sp.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`; "MTK" below) gives the parallel sentence at line 3534, in the meat paragraph: "Daji meat cures cold disorders of the kidneys and waist." The two translations agree on the action and differ only in the order of the words and in the name. The link between "Daji" and Clark's *da.pyid* is by sound and by position, not by a printed Tibetan word, and I treat it as probable and not confirmed.

The MTK glossary on animals (lines 7173-7193) has an amphibian table whose columns the OCR has split. The phonetic column reads "Belpa, Chhu ein, Daji, Dikpa, Dikcin, Sam"; the common-name column reads "Frog, Crocodile, Snow Frog, Scorpion, Crab, Otter"; and the scientific-name column reads "Bufo bufo gargarizans Cantor, Crocodylus L., Batrachuporus [sic], Butnus martensi [sic], kansc, pinchonii, Charybdis japonica, Lutra luta Linnaeus". Taken in order, "Daji" pairs with "Snow Frog" and the scientific name in the third place appears to begin *Batrachuperus* (a salamander genus; the OCR spelling is "Batrachuporus", and "pinchonii" is detached). I read that as the glossary's identification for the snow frog, but the columns are not aligned in the text, so the pairing is by order alone. [USER: verify]

That makes two printed identifications for one name, and they point to different animals. Clark's "Phrynosoma" is the genus name of a group of lizards; MTK's third-position name begins as the name of a salamander genus; and the English name in both is "frog". The identity doubt is recorded here and not resolved. Clark's own preface (line 83) says that "where any doubt at all remains regarding the identity of certain animal products the Tibetan names have been included in parentheses with the probable English identification", which is the kind of case this is.

In the meat classification, "frog" is listed among the burrowing animals (Clark line 672, group (h): "the marmot (Marmota bobak), porcupine, frog, snake, Tibetan badger, lizard (Eremias argus), gecko, and scorpion"; MTK line 2918). Neither text says that the snow frog is that "frog", so no group is assigned to it. Clark's preface (line 57) adds that "nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts".

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. Because the animal is not securely identified, no composition can be stated. In general terms, the flesh of any amphibian is mainly water and protein, and the skin of some amphibians carries compounds that are toxic; which, if any, applies here is not known. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text names the flesh of the snow frog as a medicine for cold disorders of the waist and kidneys, and nothing more. The animal is not securely identified, the sources give no preparation, and some amphibians are toxic or protected; the Codex does not recommend taking or eating one. The entry is a record of what the text says.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither text gives a preparation of the flesh or a quantity. The sentence says only that the flesh "cures cold disorders of the waist and kidneys". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. The categories "cold disorder" and "waist and kidneys" belong to the Tibetan frame and do not map onto a modern diagnosis. Some amphibians carry skin toxins, and wild amphibians can carry infections. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** No taste, potency or post-digestive taste is printed for the snow frog in either text. Clark's general statement for the animal-meat section (line 673) is that all meats "are sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste", and that "the flesh of amphibians has both varieties of power", the cool, light and coarse powers of dry-land flesh and the oily, heavy and warm powers of aquatic flesh. MTK gives the same frame (line 2923). Neither text says the snow frog is an amphibian in that sense, and I do not assign it a class. The one stated action is against "cold disorders" of the kidneys and waist.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The frame is the thirteen-part animal section of the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica (Clark line 1148), and in it the snow frog's flesh is a drug against cold disorders of the waist and kidneys. Nothing in the passages read places it in any practice of essence extraction (*bcud len*), and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, so none is asserted. The wider frame is that the three humors are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration (Clark line 51), so disease is at root a spiritual condition, and that food, behaviour, medicine and accessory therapy are the four supports of treatment (line 1299), of which medicine is the third. The chapter on routine behaviour asks the practitioner to refrain from the ten negative actions, the first of which is "taking life" (line 616). The rejuvenation passage (lines 1266-1275) names no animal substance in the lines read. The commentary's remark that the urine is "a highly recommended aphrodisiac" concerns a different part of the animal and carries no grade here.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The text lists the flesh of an animal that cannot be securely identified as a remedy for cold waist and kidney disorders, and Clark notes that few animal products are still used. The Codex does not recommend it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Identity:** the animal is not securely identified; Clark and MTK point to different genera, so do not substitute any frog, toad, salamander or lizard.
- **Toxicity and hygiene:** some amphibians carry skin toxins, and wild amphibians can carry bacteria and parasites.
- **Legal and conservation status:** unknown, because the animal is not identified; amphibians are protected in many regions.
- **Ethics:** the Codex does not recommend taking an animal's life for these uses.
