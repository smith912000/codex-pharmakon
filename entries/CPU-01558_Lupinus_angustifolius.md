# Lupinus angustifolius — Thermos agrios (Dioscorides 2-133, "Lupinus agrestis"); "Wild Lupin" on the translators' line

## *Lupinus angustifolius, the only binomial on the translators' SUGGESTED line for chapter 2-133, glossed "Wild Lupin"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk names this plant under this name. The entry rests on one chapter of one text, and the chapter is a single sentence that refers the reader to the chapter before it. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The heading *2-133. THERMOS AGRIOS* is at line 5657, the SUGGESTED line at line 5659 ("Lupinus angustifolius — Wild Lupin") and the chapter text at line 5660. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 323. The chapter stands in Book Two, in the run of vegetables headed "Lachana: Vegetables" in the running head, after 2-132 *THERMOS EMEROS* (line 5650) and before 2-134 *GONGULIS* (line 5661, the turnip).

The whole chapter reads: "There is a wild lupin that the Latins call lupinus agrestis, similar to that which is sown, yet it is in every way less effective for the same purposes than the sown lupin."

So the chapter says that a wild lupin exists, gives its Latin name (*lupinus agrestis*), says it is "similar to that which is sown" and rates it "in every way less effective for the same purposes". It gives no description, no habitat, no use of its own, no part used and no preparation. The Greek name *thermos agrios* means the wild *thermos*, and the only other name printed is the Latin one. The translators' index lists *Lupinus angustifolius* and *lupinus agrestis* both at page 256 (lines 16388 and 11138), the page of this chapter.

**What the chapter points to.** Everything in 2-133 depends on 2-132 *THERMOS EMEROS* (line 5650), whose translators' identification is *Lupinus albus*, "Egyptian Lupin, Termus". That chapter is a different chapter and is not folded into this entry. It says of the sown lupin that its meal, taken in a linctus with honey or as a drink with vinegar, "expels worms", and that the lupins themselves "steeped and eaten bitter" do the same; that a decoction with rue and pepper helps the splenetic; that it is a warm pack for gangrene, wild ulcers, new scabs and spots and rashes; that in a pessary with myrrh and honey it "extracts the menstrual flow and is an abortifacient"; and that the root boiled in water "expels urine". These are the "same purposes" that 2-133 says the wild lupin serves less well. The Codex does not carry any of them over to the wild plant as a finding. Dioscorides does not say which of the uses the wild lupin shares. [USER: verify]

**Why the identity is only a suggestion.** The chapter describes nothing that would fix a species. The Latin *lupinus agrestis* is a wild form "similar to" the sown one, and the translators' pairing with *Lupinus angustifolius* is theirs alone.

**Overlap.** The chapter 2-133 is not covered by any existing entry (chapter-level search for 2-133, *Thermos agrios*, *lupinus agrestis* and *Lupinus angustifolius*). The sown lupin of 2-132 is covered by the existing I_595 (White Lupine, *Lupinus albus*), whose tag line is EP, AT, DMM, CM. Its DMM paragraph describes *thermos* as a bitter meal for worms and a poultice, and was not checked here against the chapter number. The two entries are not merged, and nothing in I_595 is moved to or from this one. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides means was read. As a general fact, lupin seeds of many species carry bitter alkaloids (the lupin alkaloids), which is why bitter lupin seed is steeped before it is eaten. That is a general statement about the genus. Nothing is stated here about the wild lupin of the chapter or about *Lupinus angustifolius* in particular. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records no use of its own. It records only a ranking: the wild lupin is less effective than the sown one for the same purposes. The Codex gives no protocol. Bitter lupin seed is not a safe food unless it has been properly debittered, and no one should eat or use a wild lupin on the strength of this entry.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. Its one contribution to the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is the habit of ranking a wild form against the cultivated one by effect ("in every way less effective"), and that is a therapeutic ranking and not a vital one. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is one sentence that sends the reader back to the sown lupin, so the honest answer is that there is nothing here to take up.

### Contraindications

Chapter 2-133 prints no warning. Lupin seed can be toxic when it is bitter and not properly prepared, the identification here is only the translators' suggestion, and the chapter gives no preparation, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Lupin is also a known food allergen in people allergic to other legumes, as a general fact. No safety information for pregnancy was read, and 2-132 records the sown lupin as an abortifacient in a pessary. [USER: verify]
