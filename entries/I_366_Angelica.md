# I-366 — Angelica archangelica · Angelica

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Angelica archangelica* L.  
**Synonyms:** *Archangelica officinalis* Hoffm.  
**Family:** Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)  
**Common names:** Angelica (EN); garden angelica (EN); European angelica (EN, to distinguish from Asian species); Angelika / Engelwurz (DE, "angel root"); angélique (FR); angélica (ES); angélique des jardins (FR); kvann (NO/DA — Sami/Norwegian traditional name); hvönn (IS); angelica (IT); qvann (SV, archaic); cherkesski root (RU, informal)

**CRITICAL SPECIES DISTINCTION:**
- *Angelica archangelica* = European angelica (this entry); primarily Northern European; root medicine; Chartreuse/Benedictine liqueur
- *Angelica sinensis* (Dong Quai) — East Asian species; entirely different pharmacological profile and traditional application; much higher clinical evidence; separate future entry as a major TCM herb (one of the four cardinal female tonics in Chinese medicine)
- *Angelica dahurica* (Bai Zhi in TCM) — analgesic; headache; separate TCM entry
- *Angelica pubescens* (Du Huo in TCM) — rheumatic pain; separate TCM entry

Do not conflate European angelica (*A. archangelica*) with Dong Quai (*A. sinensis*) — they share a genus and some coumarins but have fundamentally different therapeutic profiles and evidence bases.

**Parts used:** Root and rhizome (primary medicinal form; highest furanocoumarins and bitter essential oil); seeds (secondary medicinal; some liqueur flavoring); aerial parts / leaf stalks (candied angelica in confectionery; edible vegetable in Sami cuisine)  
**Habitat & distribution:** Biennial or short-lived perennial; native to Northern Europe, Scandinavia, and Northern Russia; grows along riverbanks, coastal cliffs, and in northern forests; the Scandinavian-Arctic distribution is significant — *A. archangelica* thrives in subarctic conditions where few other aromatic medicinal plants survive; cultivated in European physic gardens since at least the 10th century CE; naturalized in Central Europe  
**Physical characteristics:** Impressive plant — can reach 2–2.5 m; large compound umbel flowers; hollow stems; the sheer size distinguishes it from most Apiaceae and likely contributed to its association with angelic power in European mythology

## 3. Convergence Analysis

European angelica's near-zero score in the 9-tradition framework reflects its **geographic pharmacological isolation**: a Northern European plant used by Northern European and Sami peoples, whose medical knowledge was not systematized in any of the nine primary ancient pharmacopoeias. This is not pharmacological obscurity — it is a window into pre-classical Northern European medicine, for which *A. archangelica* represents one of the most prominent documented examples.

The **Christian angelica mythology** is unparalleled among European medicinal plants: according to multiple European chronicles, an angel (sometimes identified as Michael or Raphael) appeared in a plague vision (variously attributed to the 1370 CE plague or earlier) and revealed angelica root as the remedy for plague. This angelic revelation origin story is so deeply embedded in European folk culture that it transformed the plant's pharmaceutical status — angelica was classified as a panacea capable of overcoming all poisons and diseases, particularly epidemic disease, throughout the medieval period.

Modern pharmacology reveals a rational basis for plague use that has nothing to do with angels: angelica's furanocoumarins and essential oil have demonstrated broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity including against *Yersinia pestis* (plague bacillus) in some in vitro studies, and the root's diaphoretic properties may have provided symptomatic relief during fever. The folk pharmacological conclusion (angelica helps plague) was empirically reasonable; the angelic mechanism was the cultural explanation for an observed statistical association.

Angelica's most significant modern pharmacological contribution is as a **flavoring agent in liqueur chemistry**: virtually every classic European aromatic liqueur includes angelica root as a botanical — Chartreuse (130+ botanicals, but angelica is primary), Benedictine, Cointreau, gin (secondary botanical), vermouth, and aquavit variants. This liqueur heritage is not purely flavoring — it encodes centuries of monastic pharmacological compounding where digestive liqueurs were originally medicines dispensed by monks.

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**Digestive bitter / Stomachic:**
Bitter lactone principles (archangelicin, osthole) → activation of bitter taste receptors (TAS2R family) on tongue and gastric enteroendocrine cells → increased gastric acid and digestive enzyme secretion → improved protein digestion and gastric emptying. This is the primary pharmacological mechanism behind angelica root's Commission E approval. The bitter aromatic combination (bitters + carminative volatiles) is the most fundamental functional category in pre-modern European medicine.

**Carminative / Spasmolytic:**
Monoterpene essential oil (phellandrene, α-pinene) → smooth muscle relaxation via calcium channel modulation → reduced GI spasm → gas release. Equivalent mechanism to other Apiaceae carminatives, though the specific terpene composition gives angelica a different receptor profile than fennel/carvone herbs.

**Antimicrobial:**
Furanocoumarins (in light): DNA-crosslinking photochemical mechanism (same as PUVA therapy) → broad-spectrum antimicrobial including *Y. pestis* in vitro. Phellandrene and α-pinene volatile oil → membrane disruption antimicrobial (same mechanism as other Apiaceae). Historical plague-prevention use may reflect a combination of these antimicrobial properties, though clinical validation for any infectious indication is absent.

**Expectorant:**
Phellandrene-rich volatile oil → TRPA1 stimulation on airway neurons → increased mucus secretion and ciliary activity → expectorant effect. Commission E recognized this for respiratory catarrh.

**Anti-inflammatory:**
Osthol and imperatorin → NF-κB pathway inhibition; COX-2 inhibition in vitro. β-Caryophyllene (a minor essential oil component) → CB2 receptor partial agonism → anti-inflammatory without psychoactive effect (same mechanism as in cannabis and black pepper). These mechanisms support traditional use for rheumatic and inflammatory conditions.

**PUVA pharmacology (furanocoumarins):**
Psoralen + UV-A (PUVA) therapy — the dermatological treatment for psoriasis, vitiligo, and cutaneous T-cell lymphoma — uses isolated xanthotoxin/methoxsalen (extracted originally from Apiaceae including angelica relatives). The mechanism: furanocoumarins intercalate DNA → UV-A photoactivation → cyclobutane adduct formation → DNA crosslinks → inhibition of rapidly dividing cells. This pharmaceutical application of Apiaceae furanocoumarins represents a case of traditional photosensitization knowledge (Apiaceae skin phototoxicity was well-known) being reframed as therapeutic photosensitization.

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**Commission E approved (Germany):**
*Angelica root* (*Radix Angelicae archangelicae*) approved for:
- **Dyspeptic complaints** (appetite stimulation; gastric function)
- **Feelings of fullness, flatulence, mild gastrointestinal spasms**
*(Same digestive indication cluster as other Apiaceae)*

**Level A (combination — Iberogast® STW 5):**
Angelica root is one of the nine components in Iberogast®; its bitter-carminative contribution is additive to the combination's demonstrated Level A evidence for functional dyspepsia (see caraway entry I-363 for Iberogast® evidence summary).

**Level B:**
- **Digestive bitter (appetizer):** Clinical tradition confirmed; institutionally documented in German commission E process from published pharmacological and clinical data; no large standalone RCT

**Level C:**
- Respiratory/expectorant: traditional use consistent; mechanism documented; no RCT
- Antimicrobial (plague/infection): laboratory data interesting; no clinical infectious disease trial

**Level D:**
- Furanocoumarins anti-proliferative: in vitro cancer cell lines; no clinical cancer application
- Osthol neurological: animal cognitive/anxiolytic data; no human studies for European angelica specifically

## 7. Phenomenological Record

**The angelic revelation:**
The angelica plague myth — that the archangel Michael or Raphael appeared in a vision and pointed to the plant as a remedy for the great 14th-century plague epidemic — appears in so many independent European chronicles (German, Scandinavian, French, English) that it constitutes a cultural crystallization point. Whatever the original vision (if any), the myth served a real pharmacological-social function: it elevated angelica from regional Sami folk plant to pan-European pandemic medicine in a period of existential crisis. The name *Angelica archangelica* (the species-level archangel reference is unusual in botanical taxonomy) directly records this mythological attribution in the Linnaean binomial.

**Sami ethnobotany — kvann as food-medicine:**
For the circumpolar Sami people of Scandinavia, *A. archangelica* (*kvann/kvan*) is among the most important traditional plants. The fresh hollow stem is eaten raw as a vegetable spring tonic — the only fresh green vegetable available at the end of the long Arctic winter, providing vitamin C (preventing scurvy), calcium, and glycosides after months of fish-and-meat-only diet. The stems were also chewed for respiratory support, steeped as tea for digestive complaints, and used ceremonially. Sami use represents probably 2,000–5,000 years of continuous observation of a plant whose pharmacological properties exactly matched the specific deficiencies of the subarctic winter diet — a perfect example of folk pharmacology calibrated to environmental context.

**Chartreuse and the monastic pharmaceutical tradition:**
Green Chartreuse — produced by Carthusian monks at the Grande Chartreuse monastery near Grenoble, France, according to a manuscript given to them in 1605 (attributed to the Maréchal d'Estrées, possibly using an older recipe) — contains 130 plant ingredients, with angelica root as the most prominent botanical. The recipe has never been fully disclosed. For centuries, Chartreuse served as both medicine (sold as an "Elixir of Long Life" — *Elixir Végétal de la Grande Chartreuse* at 71% ABV, used medicinally drop by drop) and beverage. The distinction between "digestive liqueur" and "medicine" was essentially nonexistent in the European monastic pharmacy tradition — alcohol was the best available solvent for plant bioactives, and liqueurs were therapeutic preparations consumed at low doses. Chartreuse is among the most pharmacologically complex compound preparations derived from traditional plant medicine still in commercial production.

**Candied angelica and pharmaceutical confection:**
The Victorian tradition of candied angelica stem (bright green sugar-preserved stems used as cake decoration) represents the survival of a pharmaceutical preparation — angelica preserved in sugar for long-term storage and easy administration — in the form of a purely decorative confection. The original rationale (angelica sugar-preserved for medicinal use; sweet vehicle masking bitter taste) was lost; the visual tradition persisted as baking decoration stripped of pharmacological context.

## 8. Practical Application

**Therapeutic dose ranges:**

| Form | Dose | Indication |
|------|------|-----------|
| Angelica root tincture (1:5, 45% ethanol) | 1–2 mL TID before meals | Digestive bitters; appetite; functional dyspepsia |
| Angelica root tea (dried root) | 1–2 g/cup, 2–3×/day | Digestive; respiratory catarrh |
| Iberogast® STW 5 | 20 drops TID | Functional dyspepsia (angelica as component) |
| Fresh stem (culinary/Sami use) | 50–100 g fresh | Spring nutritive tonic; vitamin C; digestive |
| Candied stem (confection dose) | Negligible pharmacological effect | Not therapeutic |

**CRITICAL SAFETY — PHOTOTOXICITY:**
- **Do NOT apply fresh angelica plant juice, root extract, or essential oil to skin and then expose to direct sunlight**
- Furanocoumarins (bergapten, xanthotoxin) → contact dermatitis and phototoxic burns under UV exposure; equivalent to a chemical burn
- **Apiaceae phototoxicity** has caused severe blistering burns to people handling fresh stems in sunlight (documented in field botanists, herbalists, and farmworkers)
- Oral therapeutic preparations at normal doses: systemic furanocoumarin levels generally insufficient for significant photosensitization; caution still warranted with intense sun exposure during use
- **Giant Hogweed (*Heracleum mantegazzianum*)** — a toxic invasive Apiaceae species sometimes mistaken for angelica — causes the most severe phototoxic burns of any European plant; never handle unidentified large Apiaceae plants

**Contraindications:**
- **Pregnancy:** Emmenagogue; avoid medicinal doses
- **Renal disease:** Furanocoumarin accumulation; avoid high therapeutic doses
- **Photosensitizing medications (fluoroquinolones, sulfonamides, psoralens):** Additive phototoxicity risk
- **Solar urticaria / photodermatitis:** Contraindicated

**Drug interactions:**
- Anticoagulants (warfarin, heparin): coumarins in angelica have anticoagulant activity; theoretical additive bleeding risk; monitor INR
- Photosensitizing drugs (tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, phenothiazines): additive photosensitization
- CNS depressants (minor): terpene oil has mild sedative properties; minimal clinical significance

**Identification caution:**
Wild-harvested angelica requires expert identification — *Conium maculatum* (hemlock, LETHAL), *Daucus carota* (wild carrot, safe), *Heracleum* species (phototoxic), and *Angelica* are all large white-umbelled Apiaceae species that can be confused. Do not self-harvest without botanical expertise.

**Energetic tier:** ◉ Qi (digestive bitter/carminative primary); minor ◈ Jing (nutritive in Sami spring-tonic context; vitamin C/mineral density in fresh stem)  
**Optimal form:** Tincture (1:5 in 45% ethanol) for standardized digestive bitters; Iberogast® combination for functional dyspepsia; fresh stem (Sami tradition) for spring nutritive tonic use
