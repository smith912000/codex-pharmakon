### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The locus is chapter 4-44 in the 2000 English edition (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, line 8399), between 4-43 and 4-45 RHODIA RADIX (roseroot). The index lists the chapter at page 587 (line 12167) and the Latin index has "Idaea radix 587" (line 16120).

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The chapter reads: "Idaea radix has similar leaves to oxymyrsine near which there grows out (as it were) little tendrils and flowers. The root of this is especially astringent serving as such for those for whom there is need. It is taken in a drink for discharges of the intestines and women's excessive menstrual discharges. It stops all discharges of blood." The translator marks the heading "UNKNOWN". The text therefore gives: the leaf (like oxymyrsine, itself a plant named by Dioscorides and not identified here), small tendrils with flowers, the part used (the root), a quality ("especially astringent"), a manner of use (taken in a drink), and three uses (discharges of the intestines, women's excessive menstrual discharges, and "all discharges of blood"). No habitat is given, and the chapter says nothing more about Mount Ida. The heading means "root from Ida", and Dioscorides does not say which Ida is meant.

Two other places in the text carry the same epithet and should not be mixed with this drug. Chapter 4-38 BATOS IDAIA is the Ida bramble, which the translator glosses as *Rubus idaeus*, red raspberry, and says it is "called rubus idaeus because it grows abundantly in Ida" (line 8326). Chapter 4-147, DAPHNE ALEXANDRINA, lists "idaea" among the synonyms of the Alexandrian laurel (line 9326). Neither is said to be the root of 4-44, and neither is equated with it here.

### Active Components

The text names no constituent and the plant is not identified. In general terms, a root called "especially astringent" is one the ancient authors judged to contract tissue, and astringency in plants is commonly associated with tannins; this is a general fact and not a claim from Dioscorides. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Discharges of the intestines, women's excessive menstrual discharges, and all discharges of blood.
- **What the text does not say.** The plant, the amount of root, the preparation of the drink, the dose or the duration.

Because the plant is unidentified, the Codex gives no protocol and does not recommend any plant for the uses named. Heavy menstrual bleeding and bleeding from any site are medical matters that need proper assessment.
