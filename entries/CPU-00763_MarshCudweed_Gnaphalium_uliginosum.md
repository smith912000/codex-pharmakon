# Marsh Cudweed / Wart-wort / Sushenitsa (Сушеница)

## *Gnaphalium uliginosum* L. | Family: Asteraceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM
**Energetic Classification:** Astringent, slightly bitter, cool; drying; settling/lowering
**Training Phase:** Stabilisation / Sealing (a mild binding and settling corrective, most distinctively used to lower and to heal weeping tissue)

---

### Origin & Tradition

Marsh cudweed is a small, low, grey-woolly annual of damp ground — the edges of ponds and ditches, marshy fields, trampled wet paths and arable hollows across Europe and northern Asia. The whole plant is covered in a dense felt of white-woolly hairs (the "cud-weed"/"cotton-weed" habit typical of *Gnaphalium* and its relatives), which is why it looks silvered and why, dried, it resembles a little wad of felt. In Russian it is **sushenitsa (topyanaya/boloтnaya)** — literally the "dried/marsh herb"; older English names include **wart-wort** (from a folk use against warts) and **cudweed**. It is botanically also placed in *Filaginella uliginosa*; the Asteraceae "cudweeds" are a taxonomically messy group, and this matters for the evidence (see below).

**Western European folk / domestic medicine.** In the western folk record marsh cudweed is a minor astringent and wound herb — used for **loose bowels, mouth and throat complaints, and topically on wounds, ulcers and (per the "wart-wort" name) skin excrescences.** It never rose to prominence in the western herbal canon; it is a genuinely obscure hedgerow-and-marsh remedy, and the western attestation is thin.

**Russian phytotherapy (DMM/CM — the load-bearing tradition).** The serious, formalised use of *Gnaphalium uliginosum* is Russian. It is one of the plants included in the **State Pharmacopoeia of the USSR but not in the European Pharmacopoeia**, and Russian phytotherapy — an official branch of medicine there — used it for a specific and unusual cluster: as an **astringent and antihypertensive, for early/mild arterial hypertension, and for gastric and duodenal ulcer**, as well as **topically for wounds, burns, and varicose/trophic leg ulcers**. According to PubMed, a review of the Russian Pharmacopoeia explicitly lists *Gnaphalium uliginosum* among the medicinal plants unique to Russian practice and flags that much of the underlying data was published only in Russian and never reached the international literature (Shikov et al., *J Ethnopharmacol* 2014, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2014.04.007)). This is the tradition that gives the plant its real identity, and it is honestly documented but under-evidenced by modern standards.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** There is no elaborate esoteric doctrine attached to marsh cudweed; its logic is plain-astringent plus a Russian folk-clinical claim of **"lowering."** The astringent, wound-healing side maps, as with other Asteraceae/tannin herbs, onto its **flavonoids, phenolic acids and tannins** — protein-binding, tissue-tightening, antioxidant chemistry that plausibly underwrites the wound/ulcer and anti-diarrhoeal uses. The distinctive antihypertensive/"lowering" claim has a candidate rationale in the **flavonoid and essential-oil fractions** (vasorelaxant/mild sedative-type activity is a recurring, if weakly-evidenced, theme for flavonoid-rich herbs), but for *this species specifically* the mechanism is essentially **asserted, not demonstrated**. The honest position: the sealing/astringent doctrine has a chemical basis; the blood-pressure-lowering doctrine is a traditional clinical claim awaiting real pharmacology, and is marked as such rather than dressed up.

### Active Components

*Reported for the genus/species in the older and regional literature; species-specific quantified data are limited.*

- **Flavonoids** (gnaphaliin-type and other flavones/flavonols) — the fraction usually credited with the vascular and anti-inflammatory activity.
- **Phenolic acids** — antioxidant/astringent contributors.
- **Tannins** — basis of the astringent, wound-binding action.
- **Essential oil / volatile fraction** — reported constituent; candidate for part of the biological activity.
- **Carotenoids, phytosterols, resins** — minor reported constituents.

According to PubMed, a 2024 phytochemical/GC-MS study of an ethanol extract of *Gnaphalium uliginosum* biomass (stems, leaves, flowers, roots) characterised the extract and demonstrated antimicrobial activity, positioning the plant as a source of bioactive compounds — but its framing is **agricultural (biopesticide) rather than clinical**, and it does not validate the traditional human indications (Davydova et al., *Antibiotics* 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antibiotics13080785)).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Marsh cudweed is filed as a **sealing / settling** agent with a distinctive secondary "lowering" reputation. Its Kenetica use cases, drawn faithfully from the Russian tradition, are: **as a supportive astringent for mild hypertension (the historically notable use), for gastric/duodenal ulcer and gastrointestinal irritation, and topically for slow-healing wounds, burns and varicose/trophic ulcers.** The intended felt effect is *quieting and drying* — a mild binding of weeping or inflamed tissue and, per tradition, a gentle downward/settling action.

It is positioned strictly as **supportive and adjunctive**, never as a primary treatment for hypertension or ulcer disease, both of which are serious conditions requiring proper medical management. The topical wound/ulcer use is the best-matched, lowest-risk application.

**Cultivator's note.** Marsh cudweed is a weedy annual of wet, disturbed, often nutrient-poor ground — easy to find, easy to grow in damp soil, and self-seeding. According to PubMed it is documented as a common agricultural weed and a **moderate host for the root-knot nematode *Meloidogyne hapla*** (Bélair & Benoit, *J Nematol* 1996, [PMID 19277189]) — relevant if cultivating near susceptible vegetable crops. Harvest the flowering aerial parts and dry them well (its own name means "the dried herb").

### Scientific Correlation

The evidence base is **genuinely sparse**, and this must be stated plainly rather than papered over.

- **Modern clinical evidence: essentially none.** There are **no** contemporary randomised controlled trials of *Gnaphalium uliginosum* for hypertension, ulcer, or wound healing retrieved in this search. The antihypertensive reputation rests on **old, Russian-language clinical reports** — e.g. a 1953 paper on "application of *Gnaphalium uliginosum* in hypertension" (Buturlin, *Sov Med* 1953, [PMID 13048837]) and a 1956 pharmacology note (Kulinchenko, *Farmakol Toksikol* 1956, [PMID 13448029]) — neither of which has a retrievable abstract or DOI, and which predate modern methodology. These are real, indexed records, but they are **weak evidence** and are presented as historical attestation only.
- **Regional/pharmacopoeial documentation (supportive of tradition, not of efficacy).** The Russian-Pharmacopoeia review confirms the plant's official status and its traditional antihypertensive/ulcer/wound indications, while explicitly noting the international evidence gap and calling for evidence-based evaluation (Shikov et al., *J Ethnopharmacol* 2014, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2014.04.007)).
- **Antimicrobial (in-vitro, species-specific).** A single modern study demonstrates antimicrobial activity of the ethanol extract against phytopathogenic bacteria and fungi (MICs ~78–156 µg/mL), supporting a general "active-compound-bearing" status and, loosely, the topical wound tradition — but it is an **agricultural biopesticide study**, not a human clinical one (Davydova et al., *Antibiotics* 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antibiotics13080785)).

**Congener caution.** The wider "cudweed"/*Gnaphalium*–*Pseudognaphalium*–*Helichrysum* complex includes species with their own antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective literature. **None of that data is imported here as if it were *G. uliginosum*-specific.** Only findings on *Gnaphalium uliginosum* itself have been used above; where a claim rests on the genus generally, it is flagged.

**Honest grade.** Astringent/wound use: **plausible on general tannin/flavonoid grounds, in-vitro antimicrobial support, no human trials.** Antihypertensive/anti-ulcer use: **historically attested in Russian practice, unvalidated by modern controlled evidence — treat as traditional claim, not established fact.** This is a genuinely data-sparse monograph and is labelled as such.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Taste/nature:** astringent, slightly bitter, cool, drying (DMM/CM, Russian tradition).
- **Action:** binds and seals weeping tissue; traditionally "lowers" (antihypertensive); soothes and heals gastric/skin ulceration.
- **Kenetica reading:** a *mild sealing-and-settling herb* — best matched to weeping wounds/ulcers and, adjunctively and cautiously, to the settling of mild vascular over-activity.

### Contraindications

- **Not a substitute for hypertension or ulcer treatment.** Hypertension and peptic ulcer disease are serious and require proper diagnosis and medical management. Marsh cudweed may only be considered supportive/adjunctive, and internal use for these indications should be under professional supervision — never as a replacement for prescribed therapy.
- **Blood-pressure interactions.** Given the traditional "lowering" reputation, avoid uncontrolled combination with antihypertensive drugs (risk of additive hypotension); monitor if combined. [USER: verify]
- **Asteraceae sensitivity.** As a member of the daisy family, may cause allergic reactions in people sensitive to Asteraceae/Compositae (ragweed, chamomile, etc.).
- **Tannin cautions.** Astringent tannin content can irritate the stomach and reduce absorption of iron/minerals and some drugs with heavy or prolonged internal use; keep internal use modest.
- **Pregnancy and lactation.** No adequate safety data; avoid medicinal use. [USER: verify]
- **Identity/harvest.** Confirm species identification (the cudweeds are easily confused) and harvest from clean, uncontaminated wet ground.
