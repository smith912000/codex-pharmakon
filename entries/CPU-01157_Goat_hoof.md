# Goat hoof — Onuches Aigon (as printed in the Osbaldeston and Wood Dioscorides, "Goats' Hooves")
## *The hooves of goats, burnt; the chapter prints "Goats' Hooves" and gives no binomial* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Goat hoof is chapter 2-46 of *De Materia Medica* in the Osbaldeston and Wood English (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, line 5123, heading "2-46. ONUCHES AIGON"; the entry is also in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`). The whole chapter is one sentence under the heading "Goats' Hooves" (lines 5123-5125): "The hooves of goats burnt and rubbed on with vinegar cure baldness." No quality, season, place of origin, assay or dose is given. The next chapter, 2-47 (goat's liver, printed on the same line as the end of 2-46 in this file), is a separate substance.

The chapter sits in Book Two, which Dioscorides introduces (line 4904) as the book in which "we shall come to discussion both of living creatures and of honey and of milk and of animal fat", followed by cereals and pot herbs. It belongs to a run of hoof chapters. Immediately before it, 2-44 "Ass's Hooves" (line 5114-5116) says two spoonfuls of a burnt ass's hoof "taken in a drink daily for many days are said to cure epilepsy", that steeped in oil it dissolves "scrofulous tumours [glandular swelling, goitres]", and that sprinkled on it heals chilblains; and 2-45 "Spavins of Horses" gives the hardened growth at the horse's knees and hooves "pounded into small pieces and taken in a drink with vinegar" for epilepsy (lines 5118-5121). Among the earlier chapters, a "hippocampus" is burnt and the ashes used to fill "alopecia [baldness] with hair" (line 4925), and the burnt skin of the earth hedgehog is rubbed on with pitch for alopecia (line 4918). Those are different substances and are mentioned only to show that burning an animal part and applying it to the scalp is a recurring form in Book Two.

The bracketed glosses in this translation, such as "[baldness]", are the translators' additions. Whether the Greek means a general falling of the hair or a particular disease is not stated in the file. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

A hoof is the horny, keratinised covering of the toe of an even-toed ungulate. Keratin is a tough structural protein, rich in sulphur-containing cysteine, which is why burning it smells strongly. Burning (charring or ashing) converts the protein largely to carbon and mineral ash, so the burnt material is a different substance from the raw hoof; its composition was not read in any source consulted. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is one line: burnt goat hooves with vinegar, rubbed on, "cure baldness". The Codex offers no protocol. No source read supports the claim, hair loss has many causes that call for a proper diagnosis, and obtaining hooves means slaughtering an animal, which for a hair remedy is hard to justify. A reader who wants to understand what Dioscorides wrote will find the whole of it in the paragraph above.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives a preparation but no dose: the hooves are "burnt and rubbed on with vinegar" (line 5125). No quantity, number of applications, duration, or degree of burning is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] Route: external, rubbed on.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Keratin is a structural protein of hair, nails, horn and hooves; applying burnt keratin or ash to the scalp does not supply anything the hair follicle uses to grow hair. Hair loss in people has many causes (genetic, hormonal, inflammatory, nutritional, drug-related, infectious). Vinegar is dilute acetic acid and can irritate skin. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** No quality (warming, cooling, astringent or otherwise) is printed in the goat-hoof chapter. Dioscorides' general chapter on fats (2-94) states that "all fat is warming, softening and purifying", but that concerns fat and is not applied to hoof. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied here. What he can offer a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, detection of adulteration), and this chapter has none of it: it gives no source herd, no season, and no sign by which good burnt hoof is told from poor. It is a one-sentence recipe for a single complaint. There is no cultivation status in the chapter, nor any amuletic use, so there is nothing to distinguish between recording and endorsing.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. The chapter is a record of what a first-century physician reported for baldness, not a practice, and it offers neither an observational method nor a vital claim. Its use today is as a window onto how Book Two treats the parts of animals.

### Contraindications

- **Not a treatment for hair loss:** no evidence was retrieved that it works; hair loss can be a sign of an underlying condition and may warrant a clinician's assessment.
- **Hygiene and infection:** hooves are dirty, and burnt animal material applied to the scalp, especially if the skin is broken, risks infection; anthrax is a hazard of some animal products in some regions (general fact).
- **Skin irritation:** vinegar, and ash, can irritate or burn the scalp.
- **Legal and animal-welfare status:** goats are domestic livestock; hooves are a by-product of slaughter, not a rare material. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics and religion:** some traditions restrict the use of animal parts; this entry takes no position beyond noting it.
