# Dendrobium longicornu — Pushel, pu shel (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary by column position); "orchid"

## *Dendrobium longicornu, as the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints it (OCR "Dendrobium longicomu", line 5652) as the last scientific name of its "P" run, which column order sets against the name Pushel (pu shel) and the English "Orchid"; Clark 1995 prints pu.shel with Khus-khus grass (Vetiveria zizanioides) first and a different Dendrobium, "Dendrobium curcuminatum", third* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi (Four Tantras), read in two modern translations: the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary for the binomial and its root-text sentence, and Clark 1995 for the Tibetan drug and its commentary. The binomial is the glossary's reading by column position, and Clark's identifications for the same Tibetan name are different, so the botanical identity is open.** Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP, confidence "medium") and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. **Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (row 340) the row is keyed by the scientific name "Dendrobium longicomu", with the locus "Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (line 5652)" and the note "Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". The key is not a first or later name from a list: line 5652 prints this one binomial alone. The OCR reads "rn" as "m", so "longicomu" is read as *longicornu*. The Tibetan name comes from the column order below, not from the CSV.

**The glossary, by column position.** `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "P" run (from line 5578). The phonetic names are sixteen: "Pagoe", "Pang gyen karpo", "Pangpoe", "Pang tsi dowo", "Parpata", "Pawo karpo", "Pawo serpo", "Payak", "Pema gesar", "Phangma", "Phowari", "Phur thal", "Pipiling", "Poekar", "Pukhar mula" and "Pushel" (the last at line 5648, with the transliteration "pu shel" at 5650). **Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *The Explanatory Tantra (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, chapter 20).* In the chapter on the efficacy of medicinal substances (contents, line 225), in the run on herbal medicines, lines 3418 to 3421 read: "Pukhar mula cures baekan and hot disorders. Ruta cures loong-blood disorders and flatulence. ... Pushel is anti-emetic and cures hot disorders associated with baekan disorders." The index (line 7472) gives "Pushel 205". That is the only sentence the text prints about Pushel. It gives no part used, no preparation and no taste.

*Clark 1995, chapter 20 (line 962).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, within the herbs section: "pu.shel: Khus-khus Grass: Vetiveria zizanioides: Dendrobium curcuminatum: Belamacanda chinensis: [has eleven names. It grows on river banks, has stalks similar to kusha grass, and the root smells like camphor]. It halts vomiting, cures phlegm fever [and hemorrhoids, and relieves urinary retention]." Clark's preface (line 82) says that "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary", so the description (river banks, kusha-like stalks, a camphor-smelling root) and the last two indications are the commentary layer. The root-layer clause is "It halts vomiting, cures phlegm fever", which agrees with the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence in its two points (anti-emetic, baekan, which Clark renders "phlegm"). That *Pushel* is *pu.shel* is a reading from the sound of the name and from that agreement. The text does not state it. [USER: verify]

**Identity: the glossary against Clark.** After the English "Khus-khus Grass", Clark prints three Latin names for pu.shel, *Vetiveria zizanioides* (a grass), *Dendrobium curcuminatum* and *Belamacanda chinensis* (printed so), and a commentary that describes a river-bank plant with kusha-like stalks and a camphor-scented root, a description the Codex does not try to match to any one of them. The glossary prints *Dendrobium longicornu* and the English "Orchid". The two Dendrobium names are different species. The Codex does not choose; the spine key follows the glossary, and the row stands for the Tibetan drug and not for one species. The Clark name "pu.tse.shel" in the Picrorhiza entry (hong.len, line 998) is a different drug name.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Dendrobium longicornu*, *Dendrobium curcuminatum*, pu.shel, Pushel, "Khus-khus" and *Vetiveria* found no entry on this Tibetan drug. **I_171** (Noble Dendrobium, *Dendrobium nobile*, tags SBJ, FMT) is the Chinese Shi Hu entry, a different species; its own register says that it carries no Tibetan material and that its FMT tag appears unsupported, so it is not an entry on pu.shel. **I_414** (Ushira, *Vetiveria zizanioides*, tag SS) is the entry on the grass that Clark prints first for pu.shel; it carries no FMT tag. **I_1241** (*Belamcanda chinensis*, She Gan, tag SBJ) is the entry on Clark's fourth name. **I_1288** (*Gentiana jatamansi*) counts the same "P" run and reads "Orchid" as the English of Pushel. Nothing is merged. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. As a general fact, *Dendrobium* is a large genus of epiphytic orchids (Orchidaceae), and the genus is known for alkaloids and polysaccharides in the stems of some species. That is a statement about the genus and not a finding of the Tibetan texts, and the plants Clark names for pu.shel (a grass, an orchid, an iris relative) are not related. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug, Pushel or pu.shel, that is "anti-emetic and cures hot disorders associated with baekan disorders" (Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3420 to 3421) and "halts vomiting, cures phlegm fever" (Clark line 962, outside the brackets). These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation and name no part used for Pushel. Clark's commentary says the root smells like camphor, which is not an instruction. No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Explanatory Tantra classes Pushel by what it does: it is anti-emetic and cures hot disorders associated with baekan (Men-Tsee-Khang), or halts vomiting and cures phlegm fever (Clark). The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this entry places Pushel or pu.shel in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice, and the rejuvenation chapters (Clark chapter 23, lines 1264 to 1276; Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, contents line 235) do not name it. Clark's preface (lines 69 to 71) names the salep orchid root as an example of an essence-extraction ingredient; that is a different orchid, and the Codex does not transfer it to *Dendrobium*. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is an anti-emetic and phlegm-fever drug in the Tibetan texts, its species is not settled between a grass, an iris relative and two orchids, and the orchid's place in essence extraction is attested only for a different orchid. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. The identity is uncertain, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Persistent vomiting and fevers need medical care. Many orchids are protected under international trade rules; no conservation information on this species was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
