# Iron pyrites — Swarna-makshika / Makshika (Sushruta Samhita, Bhishagratna)

## *The metallic mineral that Bhishagratna renders "iron-pyrites", a gold-coloured sulphide ore; the identification is the translator's: his Appendix 3 gives "Iron Pyrites" for Swarna-makshika (id line 33064), and the text of Chikitsa chapter 13 glosses "Makshika (iron-pyrites)". Our confidence in the gloss is medium: it rests on one translator's equation, and the Sanskrit text is not read here* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **CAUTION.** The text treats this as a metal to be prepared before use ("under the same sort of preparation" as Silajatu). This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should take a raw mineral.

**Named only by Sushruta among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 8 November 2026 as an unverified placeholder from the Appendix 3 name list of Bhishagratna's third volume; this entry replaces it, and was written after reading the chapter in which the substance is treated.

**Sushruta (SS).** Three places were read.

*The name list.* In Appendix 3 of the third volume (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`) the Sanskrit names are set in one column, ending with "Swarna-ma'kshika" at line 33046, and the translator's identifications are set in a second column, ending with "Iron Pyrites." at line 33064. The two columns were matched by counting entries from "Soma-raji", which stands against "Vernonia Anthelmintica", through "Snuhi" (Euphorbia Neriifolia), "Srotonjana" (Antimony) and "Svarchika" (Carbonate of Soda), to the last pair. The match is therefore Swarna-makshika to Iron Pyrites.

*The Makshika Kalpa.* The passage is in Chikitsa chapter 13, the chapter on the treatment of diabetes (Madhu-Meha) (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/chikitsa/SS_Chikitsa_ch13.txt`, lines 93 to 101; page 388 of Bhishagratna's second volume, where the volume index prints "Makshika (iron-pyrites)"). It follows the Silajatu section and reads: "The Makshika Kalpa:— The metal known as Makshika (iron-pyrites), which grows in the river Tapi and which copes with the divine ambrosia in its highly therapeutic properties, may be also used in the same way and under the same sort of preparation. The metal is divided into two classes according to its colour, as Svarna-Makshika (gold-coloured) and Rajata-Makshika (silver-coloured). Of these the first has a sweet taste while the second is acid. Both of them prove efficacious in cases of decrepitude, Kushtha, Meha, jaundice and consumption. A person using Silajatu and Makshika (in the manner prescribed above) should refrain from taking pigeon-flesh and Kulattha pulse (during his life-time)."

*A formula.* In Uttara chapter 44 a compound for Pandu-roga names "Tapya (Svarna-makshika)", the parenthesis being the translator's, in the same weight as the other drugs together, with iron-rust (mandura), Chitraka, Vidanga, Pathya and Tri-katu, soaked in cow's urine and made into a lambative with honey (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/uttara/SS_Uttara_ch44.txt`, lines 190 to 195).

### Active Components

The source names no constituent. In general terms, iron pyrites is iron sulphide (FeS2), a brassy, gold-coloured mineral often called fool's gold; on heating or weathering it yields sulphur compounds and iron oxides. The two-fold division by colour in the text, gold and silver, is not a chemical classification and is not translated into one. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Both classes "prove efficacious in cases of decrepitude, Kushtha, Meha, jaundice and consumption" (Chikitsa 13, lines 99 to 101). In the Uttara formula it forms part of a lambative for a serious type of Pandu-roga.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose is given for the Kalpa; the method is by reference to the Silajatu preparation. The dietary taboos (pigeon-flesh and Kulattha pulse) are recorded.

The Codex does not translate Sushruta's terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend taking any mineral.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives the method only by reference: the metal "may be also used in the same way and under the same sort of preparation" as Silajatu (Chikitsa 13, lines 93 to 96), which is "treated (soaked and dried) with appropriate medicinal drugs by stirring it up with the same" (lines 88 to 90). The detail of those drugs lies in the Silajatu passage before it and is not repeated here. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify before any numerical use.] The Uttara formula (lines 190 to 195) gives the proportion "equal to their combined weight" for Tapya; this is a ratio inside one compound and is not a dose.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Pyrite is a common iron sulphide mineral. It is not a nutrient source; when heated it gives off sulphur dioxide, and fine dust is an irritant. Iron deficiency is treated with iron salts under medical advice, and the Pandu-roga of the text is not translated into that or any other diagnosis. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SS.** The Makshika Kalpa prints a taste for the two classes: the gold-coloured is sweet, the silver-coloured is acid (Chikitsa 13, lines 99 to 100). No virya, vipaka or dosha action is printed for it in the passage read, and none is imported.

### Cultivation Register

**Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed.* The substance appears in a chapter on the treatment of Meha, in the sequence that treats Silajatu, and the text names "decrepitude" among the conditions in which it is efficacious. No passage read assigns it to a rasayana or vajikarana chapter, and the word *ojas* is not used of it. Its status is therefore therapeutic, framed by the neighbouring Silajatu section, and a cultivation status is unattested in what was read. The compare-and-contrast of the two colours stays inside Sushruta's own terms.

**Practitioner's note.** A prepared mineral for diabetic and skin disorders, jaundice and wasting, from a text that gives the method only by pointing back to another drug. A cultivator has no safe way to use a raw sulphide, and the tradition's own demand for preparation is the warning. Nothing here supports its use.

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

The Sushruta passage claims efficacy in decrepitude (jara, the wasting of age), kushtha (skin disease), meha (urinary disorders, which the chapter treats under the heading of madhu-meha, sweet urine), jaundice and consumption (yakshma). Mapped to modern anatomy, these fall on the skin, the urinary tract and the metabolic system in diabetes-like states, the liver and bile ducts, and the lungs. The Uttara formula places the material in a preparation for pandu-roga, the pale and anaemic condition, which points at the blood. The two colour classes carry different tastes, the gold-coloured sweet (madhura) and the silver-coloured acid (amla), and the entry notes that no virya, vipaka or dosha action is printed. The claimed sites are therefore the skin, the urinary and metabolic tract, the liver, the lungs, the blood, and the body generally in decrepitude.

### Contraindications

- **Raw mineral.** Not to be eaten; the text itself requires preparation. Do not heat pyrites indoors; the fumes are harmful.
- **Dietary restriction.** The text forbids pigeon-flesh and Kulattha pulse for the user (line 101).
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** No statement in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Conditions named.** Meha, jaundice and consumption are medical conditions; use proper care.
- **Identity.** "Iron pyrites" is the translator's gloss for Makshika; the Sanskrit and the river Tapi are as printed. [USER: verify]
