# Osprey — Phinis (as printed "PHINIS" in the Osbaldeston and Wood Dioscorides), Latin ossifragum, "Ossifrage"
## *The osprey; the translators print "Pandion [Falco] haliaëtus — Osprey, Ossifrage" under the heading, and Dioscorides himself says only that phinis is a bird the Latins call ossifragum* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* Book 2, chapter 2-58 (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, line 5167, in the section headed "LIVING CREATURES" that begins at 2-56, grasshoppers). The whole chapter is two sentences (lines 5167-5169): "2-58. PHINIS / Pandion [Falco] haliaëtus — Osprey, Ossifrage / Phinis is a bird that they call ossifragum in Latin. When given little by little of this in drink it is said to expel stones from urine." The identification line under the heading is the translators' (Osbaldeston and Wood), not Dioscorides's; the English index of the volume lists "Osprey 202", "Ossifrage 202" and "ossifragum 202", all this one chapter. The row in the project's name list is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 248, "PHINIS, 2-58 (line 5167), Pandion [Falco] haliaëtus — Osprey, Ossifrage". I searched the English text for "osprey", "ossifrage" and "haliaet" and found only this chapter and the index.

What the chapter says is little. It names the bird by its Greek name and its Latin name; it does not say which part of the bird is used (flesh, ash, bone or dried body are all possible readings); it does not say how the material is prepared, though "given little by little ... in drink" implies a material that is taken as a drink; and it gives the only indication, expelling stones from urine ("it is said to expel stones", with the hedge "it is said"). Which bird *phinis* and *ossifragum* meant is not settled by anything in the chapter, and I read no other passage in the Dioscorides text that settles it, so the identification with the osprey is the translators'. [USER: verify] The neighbouring chapters of the same section treat the grasshopper (2-56), locust (2-57), lark (2-59, "eaten roasted ... good for those troubled with colic") and swallow (2-60), each as a small bird or insect with a stated use. Chapter 2-58 follows the same short form.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, a bird's body is protein and fat, with bone (calcium phosphate), and the flesh of a fish-eating raptor such as the osprey can accumulate persistent pollutants and heavy metals from its prey. Nothing in an osprey is known to dissolve or expel urinary stones.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records, with the hedge "it is said", that the bird taken in drink little by little expels stones from the urine. The Codex repeats that as a record and does not recommend it. Ospreys are protected wild raptors in many jurisdictions, including under national wildlife law and international agreements on migratory birds and trade, and taking, possessing or selling any part of one may be a criminal offence. Urinary stones that cause pain, blood in the urine, fever or obstruction are a medical emergency requiring diagnosis and treatment. No modern protocol is invented here.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation is the one quoted in 2-58: the material is "given little by little ... in drink". No part of the bird, quantity or vehicle is named. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Urinary stones (calculi) are crystalline deposits, most often of calcium oxalate, calcium phosphate, uric acid or struvite; small stones may pass spontaneously with fluids and pain control, and larger ones are treated by clinicians. Nothing about bird tissue is known to expel them. Wild raptors are carriers of bacteria such as *Salmonella* and of avian influenza viruses. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** None printed. Chapter 2-58 gives no quality (warming, cooling, drying, moistening, astringent) for the osprey. The only property is the stated action, that it "expel[s] stones from urine".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he can offer a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, adulteration detection), and the osprey chapter contains none of it: it gives no place, season, part, test or warning, only the name, the Latin synonym and a hedged single indication. The chapter is therapeutic and assigns no status beyond that. Dioscorides sometimes records amuletic uses while distancing himself, as in the neighbouring swallow chapter 2-60, where stones from young swallows are tied to the arm or neck against epilepsy; no such use is given for the osprey, and recording is not endorsing.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The osprey is a two-sentence chapter naming a bird said to expel urinary stones; it gives no part, preparation or dose, and the animal is a protected raptor in many places. The Codex records it and does not recommend it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Urinary stones:** do not treat a suspected stone with any bird preparation; pain, fever, blood in urine or inability to urinate needs urgent medical care.
- **Law and conservation:** ospreys are protected in many countries and under international agreements; possession of the bird or its parts may be an offence. The Codex does not recommend harming any wild raptor.
- **Contaminants and infection:** a fish-eating raptor can carry pollutants, bacteria and viruses.
- **Identification:** the identification is the translators' and the ancient name (*phinis*, *ossifragum*) is not settled in the sources read.
