# Sindur (limonite) — sindur (Clark 1995, "Limonite: Natural oxide of lead"); Sindura (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011), rGyud-bzhi Explanatory Tantra ch.20

## *A red-yellow mineral of caves and lake shores that the tantra's commentary likens in colour to red lead; Clark's own heading gives two glosses that disagree, "Limonite" (an iron mineral) and "Natural oxide of lead", so the identification is unresolved; the colour clue in the commentary favours a lead oxide, with low to medium confidence* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** If the material is a lead oxide, as the commentary's colour and Clark's gloss suggest, it is a poison. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should take or apply this material.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The Codex's list row is `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 161, "Sindura, , mineral, rGyud-bzhi, Explanatory Tantra ch.20 (Efficacy of Medicinal Substances); Four Tantras of Tibetan Medicine (English trans.)", flagged as an uncertain identification. Two texts were read.

*Clark 1995* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). Chapter 20 has a section on the powers of individual natural substances in eight parts (line 802). Sindur is in part (3), the earth medicines, where it reads (line 859):

"sindur: Limonite: Natural oxide of lead [has six names. The name 'sindur' means ra.chhags or blood, and it is asserted to be the menstrual blood of the dakinis. It forms in caves and on the shores of lakes and is similar in color to red lead, i.e., reddish-yellow]. It heals channel fever and injuries in the vital organs. Limonite also dries pus and blood and is beneficial for burn wounds."

Clark's bracket convention is stated in his introduction (line 82): square brackets carry the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. On that reading the tantra's own words are "It heals channel fever and injuries in the vital organs" and, as Clark continues, drying pus and blood and benefit for burns; the six names, the meaning of the name, the dakini attribution, the habitat and the colour are commentary. The attribution of the name to the "menstrual blood of the dakinis" is therefore the commentary's assertion ("it is asserted to be") and is recorded here without endorsement. Clark's convention was not checked against the Tibetan. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`). In the soil-medicine summary of the Explanatory Tantra (page 199; lines 3365-3373): "Sindura cures hot disorders of the channels, heals wounds of the vital organs, dries up pus and blood of abscesses, and is good for burns." The index lists "Sindura 199" (line 7495). The Men-Tsee-Khang wording differs in detail: "hot disorders of the channels" where Clark has "channel fever", and "abscesses" where Clark has pus and blood in general. The two are the same passage in two translations.

**What is not given.** In the passages read, neither text gives a taste, potency or post-digestive taste for sindur, a dose, a preparation or a formula containing it.

**Identity.** The heading in Clark gives "Limonite" and "Natural oxide of lead" together. They are different materials: limonite, in general mineralogy, is a hydrated iron oxide, brown to yellow, and red lead (the mineral and pigment) is an oxide of lead. The commentary's description, "similar in color to red lead, i.e., reddish-yellow", fits a red-yellow lead oxide, and the name itself (*sindur*, glossed "blood") is a colour name. The Codex's list gives only the transliteration. Clark's "Limonite" and "oxide of lead" cannot both be right. The entry is therefore filed as "sindur, unresolved between a lead oxide and an iron oxide mineral", with the lead-oxide reading favoured by the commentary's colour. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None is stated in the texts. As general facts, not drawn from the texts: red lead is a lead oxide, and limonite is a hydrated iron oxide; lead compounds are cumulative poisons. Which of these, if either, is the material the tantra means is not settled by the sources retrieved.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Possibly toxic.**

- **What the texts say it is for.** Channel fever (Clark) or hot disorders of the channels (Men-Tsee-Khang), injuries or wounds of the vital organs, drying pus and blood (of abscesses, in Men-Tsee-Khang), and burn wounds.
- **What the texts do not say.** No dose, no preparation, no formula, no duration.

The Codex does not translate "channel fever" or "vital organs" into modern diagnoses and gives no protocol. If the material is a lead oxide it is hazardous by any route.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources retrieved give no preparation for sindur. No form (powder, ash, paste), route, measure or duration is given; the Men-Tsee-Khang summary and Clark's entry name actions only. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] As general facts: lead oxides are toxic, accumulate in the body, and are absorbed through broken skin and the gut; iron oxides such as limonite are largely inert pigments. Burns and abscesses need medical care, and lead-containing materials are not applied to them today.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT).** For sindur by itself no taste, potency or post-digestive taste is printed in either text. Clark's entry says the material heals "channel fever" and dries pus and blood; Men-Tsee-Khang says it cures "hot disorders of the channels". The word "hot" is used of the disorder treated, not of the substance. No wind, bile or phlegm (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) action is printed in the passages read. No classification is imported from other systems.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The tantra places the mineral among the earth medicines (Clark part 3, line 802) and gives it only wound- and fever-related actions. The commentary's attribution of its name to the "menstrual blood of the dakinis" is a mythic origin recorded as "asserted"; it is a reputation, not a cultivation status. The one substance of the section called "unsurpassed as an essence-extraction substance" is *brag.zhun* (Clark line 870); nothing like this is said of sindur, and no *bcud len* use of it was found. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to take from it for cultivation. It is a red mineral of uncertain identity, possibly a lead oxide, which the tantra lists for fevers, wounds and burns; no preparation is given. It is not a recommended practice. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Possibly TOXIC (lead).** Do not swallow, inhale as dust or apply to burns or wounds.
- **Burns, abscesses, wounds of the vital organs, fevers.** Medical conditions; use proper care.
- **Unsettled identity:** the translator's "Limonite" and "oxide of lead" disagree; nothing should be collected, bought or used on the strength of the name.
- **Preparation unknown:** Tibetan mineral medicines are traditionally processed by specialists; this entry states no processing. [USER: verify]
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** Lead exposure is especially harmful; no statement in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
