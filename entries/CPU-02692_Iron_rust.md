# Iron rust — IOS SIDEROU (Dioscorides 5-93)

## *Rust of iron, the oxidised crust that forms on the metal; the translator's SUGGESTED line reads "Iron Rust" and the chapter names no other identity; confidence high that the material is iron rust, and distinct from the worm-rust scolecia of 5-92 and the iron slag of 5-94* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **CAUTION.** The translator heads this chapter "CAN CAUSE BLOOD INFECTIONS ON OPEN CUTS". This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and rust should not be put on wounds or taken by mouth.

**Single-text entry.** Iron rust is attested here only in Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica*, book 5, chapter 93, IOS SIDEROU (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, line 10289). It is kept apart from three neighbours: iron itself (CPU-00229, a merged row that carries several traditions), the "rust" called *scolecia* of 5-92 (CPU-02436, a different material, partly man-made from vinegar and brass), and the iron slag of 5-94 (*skoria sideirou*, CPU-02703).

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The chapter reads, in the English translation on disk: "Iron rust is astringent, and applied it stops women's excessive discharges [menstrual flow]. A decoction (taken as a drink) causes inconception [birth control], and rubbed on with vinegar it heals rashes such as measles. It is suitable to use for whitlows, pterygium [membrane on eye], scabbed eyelids, and venereal warts, and it strengthens the gums. Rubbed on it helps gout, and thickens loss of hair [alopecia]." The chapter's last sentence is about iron and not about rust: "Iron made burning hot then quenched in water or wine, and the liquid (taken as a drink) is good for the abdominal cavity, dysentery, spleen, cholera, and diarrhoea." The bracketed glosses and the heading are the translator's. No preparation of the rust itself (grinding, burning) is given in 5-93.

**The neighbour chapters (context only).** Chapter 5-92 describes two kinds of *scolecia*, one mined and one made by beating vinegar, alum and salt in a brass mortar, and says how rust is burnt (broken small, set on coals in a ceramic platter, stirred until ashy). Chapter 5-94 says iron slag is "suitable for as many things as rust from iron but to a lower degree" and, drunk with vinegar and honey, helps against aconite (line 10293). Those statements belong to 5-92 and 5-94 and are not transferred to iron rust here, except that 5-94 shows Dioscorides ranking slag below rust.

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, iron rust is a mixture of hydrated iron oxides and iron oxyhydroxides formed when iron meets air and water; it is insoluble in water, though a vinegar or acid preparation will dissolve a little iron as iron salts. Rust from old objects can also carry whatever else was on the metal, including soil organisms. No analytical source was retrieved. [USER: verify any named constituent before adding it.]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Astringent; for excessive menstrual discharge; as a drink to cause "inconception"; for rashes, whitlows, eye-membrane and scabbed eyelids, venereal warts, gum weakness, gout and hair loss; and, for the quenched-iron liquid, for dysentery, spleen, cholera and diarrhoea.
- **What the Codex does.** It gives no protocol. The translator's own warning (blood infection on open cuts) is reason enough, and rust is also a recognised tetanus-risk surface in modern medicine. The contraceptive claim is a historical report; the Codex does not endorse it and a rust drink is not a method of contraception.

### Preparation & Dosing

Three uses are described and none is given a dose: rust applied dry or "rubbed on with vinegar"; a decoction "taken as a drink"; and iron "made burning hot then quenched in water or wine", the liquid then drunk (line 10289). ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Iron oxides are not a source of absorbable iron in the way iron salts are, and rust is not a medicine in modern practice. Rust on a wound is a recognised concern because rusty objects are often contaminated with soil bacteria, including the organism that causes tetanus; the rust does not cause tetanus by itself. Astringent action on tissues is a general property of metal salts. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records a therapeutic use only. The discipline of attention that Dioscorides does offer shows in the neighbouring chapter, where he distinguishes mined, scraped and manufactured rust by quality (5-92); for iron rust itself he gives no assay, season or provenance in 5-93. No cultivation status is attested.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to practise. Iron rust is a topical and internal remedy in an indication-organised chapter, with an explicit infection warning added by the translator. A cultivator has no use for it, and the recommendation is to leave it as a historical record.

### Contraindications

- **Open wounds and cuts.** The translator's heading warns of blood infection; rust can carry soil bacteria. Do not apply to broken skin.
- **Ingestion.** Do not drink rust decoctions or quenched-iron liquids made from found iron; old iron may carry lead paint, other coatings or contaminants. [USER: verify]
- **Pregnancy and contraception.** The text reports use to stop menstrual flow and to cause "inconception". Neither is a safe or reliable practice.
- **Children, lactation, iron overload.** No statement in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
