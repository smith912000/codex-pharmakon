# Amra (Mango) — Amra (Sanskrit), Ambah / Anba (Arabic-Unani), Mang-guo (Chinese)

## *Mangifera indica* L. | Family: Anacardiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, SBJ, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Ripe fruit — warm and moist (Galenic II); unripe fruit and bark — cold and dry, astringent
**Training Phase:** Foundation

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Sushruta (SS):** In the Ayurvedic surgical corpus the mango (*amra*) is among the most frequently invoked fruit trees, valued whole: ripe fruit as a nourishing, strengthening food (*balya*), unripe fruit and the astringent bark as agents to arrest flux — diarrhoea, excessive bleeding and discharges. The dried flowers and tender leaves appear in preparations for digestive laxity and urinary complaints. The tree's astringency (*kashaya rasa*) governed its clinical logic: what binds tissue binds the loosened humour.

**Shennong / Chinese materia medica (TCM):** Mango (*mang-guo*) entered Chinese medicine along the southern trade and cultivation frontier. The fruit is classed as sweet, sour and cooling, directed at thirst, dryness and a restless stomach; the leaf and kernel were used traditionally for cough and for consolidating the bowels. Its placement among cooling, fluid-generating fruits mirrors the Ayurvedic reading of the ripe fruit as a restorer of depleted moisture.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM):** Transmitted westward through Persian and Arab physicians, the mango (*anba*) is described in the humoral idiom as warming and moistening when ripe, strengthening to the stomach and liver, and — in its unripe, sour state — astringent and appetite-sharpening. The bark and unripe fruit were folded into remedies for looseness of the bowels and for bleeding.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Across all three traditions the mango is a tree of fertility and auspicious abundance, its leaves strung over thresholds at weddings and festivals. The doctrinal signature — a fruit that ripens from hard, sour astringency into sweet, moist fullness — maps precisely onto its pharmacology: the unripe fruit and bark are tannin-rich and genuinely astringent/haemostatic, while the ripe fruit is a carotenoid- and sugar-dense restorative. The plant's most studied molecule, mangiferin, is concentrated in the leaf, bark and kernel — the very "binding" tissues tradition reached for.

### Active Components

- **Mangiferin** — a C-glucosyl xanthone, the signature bioactive; concentrated in leaf, bark, kernel and peel. Antioxidant and anti-inflammatory in preclinical models.
- **Gallotannins and gallic acid** — astringent polyphenols of the unripe fruit and bark; the plausible basis of the traditional binding/haemostatic use.
- **Quercetin, isoquercetin, catechins** — flavonoid antioxidants of leaf and peel.
- **Carotenoids (β-carotene, provitamin A) and vitamin C** — dense in ripe pulp; the nutritive restorative fraction.
- **Mangoferic acid and terpenoid resins** — in bark and unripe fruit.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Amra is a **Foundation-phase** substance: a builder and consolidator rather than a mover. The ripe fruit supplies the practitioner with dense, easily assimilated nourishment and moisture — appropriate where the ground state is depleted, dry or over-drilled. The leaf and bark preparations occupy the opposite pole: astringent, tissue-tightening, used to arrest looseness and to steady a system that is leaking (literally in flux, symbolically in dispersion). The pairing makes Amra a full-spectrum Foundation plant — one part of the tree fills the vessel, the other seals it.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed: Lum et al. (2023), *Frontiers in Pharmacology* — mangiferin (10 and 20 mg/kg, oral) reduced oxidative-stress markers (lowered malondialdehyde, raised glutathione, SOD and catalase) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and improved motor and cognitive measures in a 3-nitropropionic-acid rat model of Huntington's-like injury. DOI: https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2023.1189957.

Khurana et al. (2016), *Pharmaceutical Patent Analyst* — a review of mangiferin as a C-glucosyl xanthone antioxidant, cataloguing preclinical antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic (anticancer) activity across multiple cell models and its modulation of inflammatory signalling. DOI: https://doi.org/10.4155/ppa-2016-0003.

**Evidence grade:** The nutritive value of the ripe fruit is well established. The pharmacological claims for mangiferin rest on **preclinical (in vitro and animal) evidence and mechanistic reviews**; robust human clinical trials are lacking. The traditional astringent/haemostatic use of unripe fruit and bark is consistent with its tannin chemistry but is **traditional, not clinically validated**.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** Fruit — sweet, sour, cooling; generates fluids, quenches thirst, harmonises the stomach.
- **Ayurveda (SS):** Ripe fruit — *madhura* (sweet) *rasa*, *sheeta* to mildly warming *virya*, *madhura vipaka*, nourishing and *balya*; unripe fruit — *kashaya/amla* (astringent/sour) *rasa*, binding.
- **Galenic/Unani (CM):** Ripe — warm and moist (2nd degree), strengthening to stomach and liver; unripe — cold, dry, astringent.

### Contraindications

- Ripe fruit is high in fructose and sucrose — use with caution in diabetes and metabolic dysregulation.
- *Mangifera indica* is in the Anacardiaceae (with poison ivy/cashew); the sap, peel and unripe fruit contain **urushiol-type resorcinols** that can provoke contact dermatitis and perioral rash in sensitised individuals. Handle peel and sap with care.
- Bark and unripe-fruit astringent preparations should not be used long-term or in constipation-dominant states.
