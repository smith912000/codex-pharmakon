# I-495 — Lemon Verbena
## *Aloysia citrodora* Palau

**Family:** Verbenaceae | **Volume:** I — Plant-Based | **Entry:** I-495

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted Name:** *Aloysia citrodora* Palau (1784)
**Common Names:** Lemon verbena, lemon beebrush, verbena, limonaria, luisa, cedron
**Synonyms:** *Lippia citriodora* Kunth (most widely used synonym in older literature); *Aloysia triphylla* (L'Hér.) Britton; *Verbena triphylla* L'Hér.; *Lippia triphylla* (L'Hér.) Kuntze

**Etymology:** *Aloysia* after María Luisa of Parma (1751–1819), Queen of Spain, wife of Charles IV; *citrodora* from Latin *citrus* (citron) + *odora* (fragrant) = citrus-scented; "lemon verbena" = lemon-scented verbena; *limonaria* (Spanish/Portuguese, "lemon-plant"); *luisa* (Spanish, woman's name in honor of the queen); *cedrón* (Argentine/Chilean, "cedron" = lemon-scented)

**Botanical Description:** Perennial shrub or sub-shrub, 1–3 m in favorable climates; deciduous in temperate zones; stems woody, branching; leaves in whorls of 3–4 (characteristic arrangement — taxonomically significant), lanceolate, 5–10 cm, light green, intensely lemon-scented when touched (glandular trichomes on abaxial surface); flowers tiny, white to pale lilac, in terminal and axillary spikes; bloom July–September; tropical/subtropical native requiring frost protection; MOST INTENSELY LEMON-SCENTED plant in commerce — surpasses lemon grass, lemon balm, and lemon thyme in citral content

**Distribution:** Native to South America — western slopes of the Andes, particularly Argentina, Chile, Peru, Bolivia; naturalized in Mediterranean Europe (Spain, Italy, France, Morocco), California; cultivated worldwide in temperate climates as culinary/aromatic herb; introduced to Europe by Spanish and Portuguese colonists ca. 1700

**Plant Part Used:** Leaves (fresh or dried); essential oil distilled from leaves and flowering tops

**Adulterants/Substitutions:** *Melissa officinalis* (lemon balm; far lower citral; different pharmacology; less intense aroma — the most common commercial substitution); *Cymbopogon citratus* (lemon grass; completely different plant; citral-rich but different phytochemical profile); *Aloysia polystachya* (poleo; different aroma; different chemistry)

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

**FAMILY TRANSITION:** *Aloysia citrodora* is the first **Verbenaceae** entry after the long Lamiaceae concentration (I-476 through I-494). The shared bitter iridoids and phenolic acid profile across this order boundary documents the chemical cohesion of the Lamiales.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

### The Columbian Exchange Herb of Maximum Aromatic Potency

Lemon verbena represents the apex of citral concentration among medicinal culinary herbs. While lemon balm (*M. officinalis*, I-478) contains 1–3% citral in its EO, and lemon thyme (*T. × citriodorus*) and lemon grass (*C. citratus*) achieve 70–85% citral in EO, *A. citrodora* achieves 30–35% citral in EO but in a plant whose leaf oil content (0.5–1.2%) combined with citral proportion produces the most powerful lemon aroma per gram of leaf material in practical herb commerce. The scent is immediate, penetrating, and persistent in a way that lemon balm's lighter aroma is not.

Spanish and Portuguese colonists exploring the Andean western slopes in the late 17th century encountered *A. citrodora* already in use among Andean indigenous populations for digestive complaints, nervous conditions, and as a febrifuge. The plant was introduced to Europe by 1700 and reached France, Spain, and Italy within decades, becoming a standard addition to formal garden collections. The French *verveine odorante* and the Spanish *hierba luisa* (named after Queen María Luisa) quickly became popular culinary and medicinal herbs across the Mediterranean.

### The Andean Traditional Medicine Context

Andean indigenous medicine, particularly in Argentina, Chile, Peru, and Bolivia, employs *A. citrodora* (*cedrón* or *hierbaluisa*) primarily for digestive complaints (dyspepsia, nausea, intestinal spasms) and as a calming nervine tea for anxiety and insomnia. The traditional malaria-adjacent use (as a febrifuge) in regions where malaria was endemic has not been pharmacologically validated but reflects the general antipyretic use of aromatic herbs in indigenous medicine.

The pharmacological overlap with *M. officinalis* (lemon balm I-478) is substantial: both herbs are lemon-scented Lamiales (different families) with rosmarinic acid as a major phenolic and citral/geraniol as EO components. The therapeutic overlap in traditional use — both used for nervous tension, insomnia, and digestive complaints — reflects genuine pharmacological convergence across the family boundary.

### Competitive Sports and Anti-Oxidative Stress: Unexpected Contemporary Application

The most clinically documented contemporary application of lemon verbena is in competitive athletes, where standardized verbascoside-containing extracts have shown protection against exercise-induced oxidative stress and muscle damage. This represents a genuinely novel pharmacological application with no traditional precedent — identified through systematic screening of high-antioxidant plant extracts for sports recovery applications. The combination of verbascoside's antioxidant activity with rosmarinic acid's anti-inflammatory action provides a mechanistically coherent basis for exercise recovery that connects ancient digestive herb to contemporary sports nutrition.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

### Essential Oil (Primary Aromatic Fraction)
**Geranial (citral a):** 15–25% of EO; the higher-boiling isomer of citral; dominant contributor to lemon aroma; antimicrobial; anti-inflammatory; melanin synthesis inhibitor (skin lightening in topical application)

**Neral (citral b):** 10–15% of EO; lower-boiling citral isomer; combined geranial + neral = total citral 25–40%; the geranial:neral ratio ~65:35 is characteristic of *A. citrodora* and can be used as authentication marker

**Limonene:** 5–15%; monoterpene hydrocarbon; gentle solvent; anxiolytic (olfactory/limbic mechanism)

**β-Caryophyllene:** 10–20%; sesquiterpene; CB2 partial agonist; anti-inflammatory; recurring Lamiales compound across all entries in this region

**Geraniol:** 3–8%; terpene alcohol; antimicrobial; skin sensitizer at high concentrations (must dilute for topical use)

**Linalool:** 1–5%; anxiolytic terpene alcohol; shared with lavender (I-483), marjoram (I-488), catnip (I-490)

**1,8-Cineole:** 1–5%; AChE inhibitor; mucolytic; shared with rosemary (I-480), sage (I-481)

**trans-β-Ocimene, cis-β-ocimene:** aliphatic monoterpenes; fresh herbal note; minor

### Phenolic Fraction
**Verbascoside (Acteoside):** 1–3% dry weight; the dominant phenolic; phenylethanoid glycoside; the same compound documented in mullein (I-473), betony (I-492), horehound (I-493); antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; antiviral; hepatoprotective; this compound is the primary active constituent in the athlete oxidative stress studies

**Rosmarinic acid:** standard Lamiales phenolic; anti-inflammatory; antiviral; GABA-T inhibitor; the 19th entry in the Codex to feature rosmarinic acid as a primary phenolic

**Luteolin, apigenin:** flavone anti-inflammatory/GABA-A modulatory compounds; standard Lamiales profile

**Caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid:** hydroxycinnamic acid antioxidants

### Iridoids
**Thymol methyl ether:** minor; related to iridoid biosynthesis pathway

**Nepetalactone trace:** connects to catnip (I-490) via iridoid chemistry shared within Lamiales

### Mucilage/Polysaccharides
Minor mucilage fraction; mild demulcent contribution to digestive soothing effect

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Antioxidant / Anti-Inflammatory Mechanism

**Verbascoside → free radical scavenging + NF-κB inhibition:**
Verbascoside (acteoside) demonstrates DPPH radical scavenging capacity superior to trolox (water-soluble vitamin E analog) on molar basis; inhibits NF-κB activation in inflammatory cell models; Nrf2 pathway activation → endogenous antioxidant enzyme upregulation (superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase); the Nrf2 mechanism is relevant to exercise-induced oxidative stress where reactive oxygen species generation exceeds antioxidant buffering capacity

**Rosmarinic acid → COX/LOX dual inhibition:**
Standard Lamiales anti-inflammatory mechanism; reduces prostaglandin and leukotriene synthesis; contributes to both the anti-inflammatory and digestive anti-spasmodic effects

### 5.2 Anxiolytic / Sedative Mechanism

**Linalool → GABA-A PAM:** shared mechanism with lavender (I-483); mild positive allosteric modulation at BZD site; contributes to traditional nervine use

**Limonene → limbic modulation:** olfactory limonene inhalation reduces cortisol in human subjects (Komori 1995, *Neuroimmunomodulation* 2(3):174-180); anxiolytic effect mediated through olfactory-limbic pathway without BZD-site activity; relevant to the aromatic nervine tradition

**Rosmarinic acid → GABA-T inhibition:** standard Lamiales nervine mechanism

**Combined aromatherapy mechanism:** the intensely lemon-citral aroma of *A. citrodora* provides one of the most potent natural limonene/citral olfactory stimuli available; the olfactory anxiolytic mechanism may contribute significantly to the traditional nervine applications

### 5.3 Digestive / Antispasmodic Mechanism

**Citral → smooth muscle relaxation:**
Citral (geranial + neral) relaxes intestinal smooth muscle via calcium channel antagonism; relevant to the primary traditional indication (digestive antispasmodic, carminative)

**Rosmarinic acid + luteolin → GI anti-inflammatory:**
Anti-inflammatory activity at GI mucosal level; relevant to irritable bowel component of digestive complaints

**Combined mechanism:** citral smooth muscle relaxation + rosmarinic acid anti-inflammatory = dual mechanism for the traditional digestive antispasmodic and carminative applications

### 5.4 Antimicrobial Mechanism

**Citral + geraniol → membrane disruption:**
Same mechanism as thymol/carvacrol (I-482/I-486) but targeting gram-positive bacteria at somewhat higher MIC values; citral demonstrates antifungal activity against *Candida* spp. in vitro; relevant to traditional use for infectious GI complaints

**Geraniol → H. pylori:** geraniol demonstrates anti-*H. pylori* activity in vitro; potential relevance to gastric ulcer applications

### 5.5 Exercise Recovery Mechanism

**Verbascoside → mitochondrial protection:**
Verbascoside reduces exercise-induced mitochondrial DNA oxidation; protects mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes from ROS-mediated inactivation; relevant to reducing exercise-induced muscle fatigue and recovery time

**Combined antioxidant mechanism:** verbascoside (primary) + rosmarinic acid + flavones create a multi-mechanism antioxidant response relevant to the oxidative stress generated by intensive exercise

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### 6.1 Exercise Recovery / Oxidative Stress

**Evidence Level: A (RCTs in healthy athletes)**

- Carrera-Quintanar L et al. 2012 (*Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition* 9:47): Standardized *A. citrodora* extract (400 mg daily, verbascoside standardized) vs placebo in amateur athletes; n=55; 21 days; reduced exercise-induced muscle damage markers (creatine kinase, lactate dehydrogenase) and oxidative stress biomarkers (TBARS, 8-isoprostane); improved recovery time (p<0.05)

- Funes L et al. 2011 (*European Journal of Nutrition* 50(5):395-403): *A. citrodora* polyphenol extract in trained male cyclists; n=33; 3-week intervention; improved antioxidant status; reduced neutrophil count (marker of exercise-induced inflammation); maintained muscle performance

- Carrera-Quintanar L et al. 2015: Follow-up study confirming results; mitochondrial protection markers

These represent among the best-controlled clinical trials for any Lamiales herb in the Codex, conducted under sports science RCT standards with objective biochemical endpoints.

### 6.2 Digestive / Carminative

**Evidence Level: C (traditional evidence; mechanistic support)**

- No RCTs for digestive indications
- Traditional use across South American, Spanish, French, and North African traditions for dyspepsia, nausea, intestinal cramps; Level C
- EMA herbal monograph (2013): Traditional use indication for symptomatic relief of mild digestive complaints including meteorism and spasms

### 6.3 Anxiolytic / Sedative

**Evidence Level: C (traditional; mechanistic support)**

- No RCTs for anxiety or sleep indication
- Traditional use for nervous tension and insomnia across multiple cultures; Level C
- EMA herbal monograph (2013): Traditional use indication for mild nervous tension and sleep disturbance

### 6.4 Antimicrobial

**Evidence Level: D (in vitro)**

- Multiple in vitro studies confirming antimicrobial activity of *A. citrodora* EO against various pathogens; no clinical trials

### 6.5 Regulatory Status

- **EMA (European Medicines Agency) Herbal Monograph 2013:** Traditional use for (1) symptomatic relief of mild digestive disorders; (2) mild nervous tension and sleep disturbance; oral use (tea and tincture); well-established use status for digestive indication
- **Commission E (Germany):** NOT monographed
- **ESCOP:** NOT monographed
- **FDA (USA):** GRAS status for food/flavoring use; no drug approval
- **Food use:** Widely used culinary herb; standard ingredient in herbal teas, cocktails, cordials; safe at food quantities

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

### 7.1 Queen María Luisa and the Naming of a Continent's Herb

The naming of *Aloysia citrodora* in honor of Queen María Luisa of Parma reflects the plant's arrival in Europe at the height of Spanish colonial botanical exploration. The 18th-century Spanish and Portuguese colonial botanical programs — which brought back cacao, vanilla, quinine, coca, and dozens of other South American plants — were among the most significant pharmacological discovery programs in history. Lemon verbena arrived in this stream, named for the reigning Spanish queen at the time of its introduction (ca. 1767–1800). Unlike many politically-named plants, the name *Aloysia* has remained stable through multiple taxonomic revisions (unlike the species epithet, which shifted between *triphylla*, *citriodora*, and back through several nomenclatural revisions).

### 7.2 The French *Verveine* Tradition

In France, *verveine* (lemon verbena tea) achieved a cultural status comparable to chamomile in Germany or lemon balm in the UK — the universal, safe, delicious "calming tea" of the French domestic pharmacy. The French *tisane de verveine* is consumed daily by millions of people across France, Belgium, and the French-speaking world as an after-dinner digestive and calming drink. Unlike many traditional medicines whose popularity has declined with pharmaceutical competition, French *verveine* tea consumption has remained robust into the 21st century — a testament to the genuine pleasure of the experience combined with genuine mild therapeutic benefit.

The distinction between *verveine odorante* (*A. citrodora*, lemon verbena) and *verveine officinale* (*Verbena officinalis*, common vervain) is culturally significant: in French pharmacy, both are called *verveine*, but they are entirely different plants. The common name conflation has historically created confusion about which plant's pharmacological studies apply to which traditional use — a reminder that common name disambiguation is essential in pharmacognosy.

### 7.3 *Cedrón* and Andean Healing

In the Andean countries — particularly Argentina and Chile — *cedrón* (*A. citrodora*) holds a place in domestic medicine analogous to its French role, but with stronger emphasis on cardiac calming ("for the heart") and fever management. Argentine herbalists specifically recommend *cedrón* for "nervous heart" conditions (*corazón nervioso*) — palpitations, anxiety-related tachycardia, nervous tension — a cardiovascular-nervine application consistent with the plant's linalool + limonene olfactory anxiolytic mechanism and rosmarinic acid GABA modulation. The "heart" application reflects traditional understanding of the heart as a seat of emotional experience as much as a mechanical pump, and the nervine-anxiolytic mechanism addresses both levels simultaneously.

### 7.4 The Athlete's Herb — Ancient Plant Meets Contemporary Science

The discovery of lemon verbena's exercise recovery properties through systematic antioxidant screening of culinary herbs represents a model for contemporary ethnopharmacology: traditional use (digestive-nervine herb) → systematic pharmacological screening → novel indication identification (sports recovery) → controlled clinical trials → validated new application. This progression produced the strongest clinical evidence in the plant's pharmacological record for an indication that has no traditional precedent whatsoever. It also validates the general principle that pharmacologically active plants have activities that transcend the indications for which they were traditionally used — the activity profile is a property of the chemistry, not of the traditional use context.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Indications (Evidence-Based, Within Safety Parameters)

**Exercise Recovery / Antioxidant Support:**
Standardized extract (verbascoside standardized); Level A (RCTs); appropriate for athletes with intensive training schedules; reduces exercise-induced oxidative stress and muscle damage markers; dose: 400 mg standardized extract daily during training periods

**Digestive Complaints (Dyspepsia, Bloating, IBS-type cramping):**
Herb tea; EMA traditional use monograph; Level C-B; appropriate for mild to moderate functional digestive complaints; pleasant palatability makes compliance excellent

**Mild Anxiety / Nervous Tension / Sleep Support:**
Herb tea; EMA traditional use monograph; Level C; appropriate mild anxiolytic/sedative; pleasant aroma provides olfactory anxiolytic contribution; safe for long-term use

**Culinary / Flavoring:**
Herb tea, culinary flavoring; GRAS; safe; widely consumed

### 8.2 Preparations and Dosing

**Herb Tea:**
- 2–4 g dried leaves (or 4–8 g fresh leaves) in 250 mL just-below-boiling water (90°C preferred; high heat degrades EO); steep 5–8 minutes; cover
- 1–3 cups daily; after meals for digestive application; evening for sleep/anxiolytic
- EMA traditional use dose: 1.5 g dried leaf 3–4 times daily

**Standardized Extract (Exercise Recovery):**
- Dose used in RCTs: 400 mg daily, standardized to 14% verbascoside; continue for minimum 3 weeks to observe effects
- Best initiated 1–2 weeks before intensive training period

**Tincture:**
- 1:5 in 40% ethanol; 1.5–3 mL three times daily

**Essential Oil (External Only):**
- Dilute to 0.5–1% in carrier oil for topical aromatic application (higher concentrations irritate skin due to geraniol/citral sensitization potential)
- Avoid sensitization: do not use undiluted on skin; patch test recommended

**Aromatherapy:**
- 2–3 drops *A. citrodora* EO in diffuser for olfactory anxiolytic effect; limonene + linalool inhalation; 20–30 minutes per session

### 8.3 Contraindications and Safety

**CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- **Known citral/linalool skin sensitization:** Geraniol and citral are documented contact sensitizers in patch-test positive individuals; avoid high-concentration topical applications; herb tea internal use safe even in sensitized individuals (sensitization is contact, not systemic)
- **Renal disease:** Mild diuretic effect; monitor if significant renal impairment

**DRUG INTERACTIONS:**
- **Sedatives/CNS depressants:** Mild additive effect from linalool/limonene mechanism; low clinical significance at tea doses
- **Diabetic medications:** Mild hypoglycemic effect (citral → insulin sensitization in animal models); monitor blood glucose if used heavily alongside oral hypoglycemic agents

**PREGNANCY:** No documented teratogenicity; moderate use (1–2 cups tea daily) generally considered safe; avoid high-dose supplemental extracts; avoid EO internal use

**SAFETY:** Excellent safety profile; centuries of culinary and medicinal use without significant toxicity; EMA traditional use classification (14 years historical use) applies

### 8.4 Energetics

**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi + ☿ Shen (mild)
**Thermal Quality:** Cool-moist (Galenic: cool, slightly drying based on aromatic nature but moistening via mucilage/demulcent); one of the few "cooling" aromatic herbs despite tropical origin
**Directional Action:** Downward-bearing (digestive, carminative); harmonizing (nervine — calming without sedating at typical doses)
**South American Traditional Classification:** *Refrescante* (refreshing/cooling); *calmante* (calming); *digestiva* (digestive)

### 8.5 Quality and Authentication

**Botanical Authentication:**
- Morphological: whorled leaves (3–4 per node; characteristic); intensely lemon-scented when touched; no other common herb matches this combination
- GC-MS EO: geranial 15–25% + neral 10–15% (total citral 25–40%); geranial:neral ratio ~65:35; confirms *A. citrodora*; distinguish from *M. officinalis* (lemon balm; citral 1–3% EO only)
- HPLC for verbascoside content: minimum 0.5% dry herb for medicinal quality; 1%+ for standardized extract

**Commercial Notes:**
- *Aloysia citrodora* and *Lippia citriodora* are the same species; both names appear on commercial products
- Frequent substitution with lemon balm (*M. officinalis*) in commercial "lemon herb" products — entirely different plant, different pharmacology, different clinical evidence; GC-MS authentication reveals the substitution immediately (lemon balm citral 1–3% EO vs lemon verbena citral 25–40% EO)
- Fresh plant: significantly more aromatic than dried; if available, use fresh at double the dried weight; drying volatilizes EO significantly
