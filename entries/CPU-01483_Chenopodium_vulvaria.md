# Chenopodium vulvaria — Tragion (Dioscorides 4-49)

## *Chenopodium vulvaria (the translators' suggestion; "Stinking Motherwort")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Identity doubt.** The SUGGESTED line (line 8441) reads "Chenopodium vulvaria, Tragium germanicum — Stinking Motherwort". The chapter describes a plant that "grows only in Crete", has leaves, stems and seed like lentiscus but smaller, and "a liquid similar to gum". Whether a goosefoot fits that description was not checked, and the translators do not say why they chose it. The English name "Stinking Motherwort" is theirs; the chapter prints no smell. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-49. TRAGION* at line 8440, text at line 8442.

The chapter reads: "Tragium grows only in Crete. It has leaves, stems and seed similar to lentiscus [1-90] but all smaller. It has a liquid similar to gum. The leaves, seed and fluid (applied with wine) draw out arrowheads, splinters, and all things fastened within. A decoction (taken as a drink) cures slow painful urination, breaks stones in the bladder, and induces the menstrual flow. A teaspoonful is taken. They say that wild goats that have been shot feed on this herb and put out the arrows."

So the text gives a habitat (Crete only), a comparison with lentiscus (the tree of chapter 1-90, which was not read for this entry), a gum-like fluid, external use with wine to draw out arrowheads, splinters and lodged things, and an internal decoction for difficult urination, bladder stones and bringing on the menses. The dose is printed: "A teaspoonful is taken" (the chapter does not say of what, a decoction or the fluid). The last sentence is hearsay ("They say") that wounded wild goats eat the herb and expel the arrows. The translators' index lists *Chenopodium vulvaria* at page 591.

**Overlap.** No entry covers this chapter. The next chapter, 4-50 Tragion allo (line 8443, glossed *Artemisia vulgaris*), is a separate plant and is not attributed to this row. 4-51 Tragos (line 8446) is already I_990 *Tragus berteronianus*, which reports 4-50 as separate. The chapters share the stem *tragion*/*tragos* and nothing else.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records an external drawing application for arrowheads and splinters, and a decoction for difficult urination, bladder stones and bringing on the menses. These are the text's claims, together with a report of wounded goats. The Codex gives no protocol: the identification is doubtful, and the internal use is an emmenagogue and a stone remedy for which a modern reader should seek medical care.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-49 gives the leaves, seed and fluid "applied with wine" externally, and a decoction "taken as a drink" internally. The only quantity printed is "A teaspoonful is taken". The chapter does not say what the teaspoonful is of or how often. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No study of the plant was opened. Bladder stones and difficult urination need medical diagnosis, and no drink is known to this entry to break bladder stones. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes: provenance (Crete only), a comparison for recognition (lentiscus, smaller), the fluid to look for, and which parts are used for which purpose. The goat anecdote is explicitly hearsay and shows the habit of recording a report as a report. The chapter assigns the plant no status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a wound-and-urinary remedy in the record, with a doubtful identity, and the answer is to leave it as history.

### Contraindications

The internal use is said to induce the menses, so it must not be used in pregnancy. Painful or obstructed urination and suspected bladder stones need medical assessment. The plant's identity is doubtful, so no wild plant should be gathered or drunk on this basis, and a wound with something lodged in it needs clinical care. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
