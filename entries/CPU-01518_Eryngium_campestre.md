# Eryngium campestre — Erungion (Dioscorides 3-24)

## *Eryngium campestre [Linnaeus], the last of four names on the translators' SUGGESTED line ("Eryngium, Iringus [Fuchs], Eryngium vulgare [Bauhin], Eryngium campestre [Linnaeus] — Common Eryngo, Field Eryngo, Eryngium")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed under the chapter heading, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line gives four forms: a pre-Linnaean name credited to Fuchs ("Eryngium, Iringus"), Bauhin's *Eryngium vulgare*, Linnaeus's *Eryngium campestre*, and the English names. The row takes the Linnaean binomial. The spine already carries a separate row for *Eryngium vulgare* ("Common Eryngo", also Dioscorides), which is Bauhin's form from the same line. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *3-24. ERUNGION* at line 6726, SUGGESTED lines at 6727 and 6728, text from line 6729 to 6741 (page numbers and plate captions are printed inside it, and the list of foreign names continues after them). The chapter stands in Book Three, "Roots", in the group headed "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants", after 3-23 *TRAGAKANTHE* (tragacanth) and before 3-25 *ALOE*. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 430.

The chapter reads, in sequence. "Eryngium is one of the prickly plants. The new leaves are stored in brine and eaten as vegetables." The plant is then described: leaves "broad and rough in the circumference, and aromatic to the taste"; as it grows it becomes "prickly at the furthest points of the stalks, on the tops of which are little round heads surrounded with very sharp prickles like a star, hard all around"; the colour "can be green, pale, white, or sometimes azure [blue]". "The root is long, broad, black on the outside and white within, the thickness of a big finger or thumb. It is sweet smelling and aromatic, and grows in fields and rough places."

The uses follow. "It is able to warm, and expels urine and the menstrual flow. Taken as a drink it dissolves griping and gaseousness. It is good with wine for liver complaints, those bitten by venomous creatures, and as an antidote for those who have taken a deadly drink. It is taken in a drink for the most part with one teaspoonful of pastinaca [3-59] seed." Two further statements are marked differently: "It is said that used as a personal ornament or rubbed on someone it dissolves tubercles [growths]", and "The root (taken as a drink with honey water) cures tetanus and epilepsy."

The names printed are *erynge*, *eryneris*, *caryon*, *gorginium*, *hermium*, *origanum chlunium*, *myracanthum* and *moly*, then, by people, the Egyptians' *crobysus*, the Magi's *siserti*, the Romans' *capitulum cardui* (some say *carterae*), the Dacians' *sicupnoex*, the Spaniards' *ciotocapeta* and the Africans' *cherdan*, *herba montana* or *chida*. The text only lists *moly* as a name here and does not discuss it. The cross-reference "pastinaca [3-59]" points to the chapter *STAPHULINOS AGRIOS, STAPHULINOS KEPAIOS* (line 7065), whose SUGGESTED line gives carrot and parsnip names. It is not read into this entry beyond that pointer.

*Another mention.* In the chapter on mountain nardus (line 3624, Book One) Dioscorides says the nardus has "stalks and leaves like eryngium but smaller, yet not sharp and prickly". That is a comparison used to describe another plant, and it adds the statement that eryngium is sharp and prickly.

### Active Components

No analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. The genus *Eryngium* belongs to the carrot family, whose members are often aromatic, and the chapter itself calls the leaves and root "aromatic". Nothing further is stated here about this plant's composition. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records an edible spring leaf and a root drunk in wine or honey water for urine, menstrual flow, wind, liver complaints, venomous bites, poisoning, tetanus and epilepsy. The Codex gives no protocol. Several of these are emergencies (venomous bites, poisoning, tetanus, epilepsy) that need urgent medical care. A root used to bring on the menstrual flow is also not suitable in pregnancy.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations. The new leaves are "stored in brine and eaten as vegetables". The root is "taken as a drink", for the most part with wine and "one teaspoonful of pastinaca [3-59] seed", and for tetanus and epilepsy "with honey water". The only quantity printed is that one teaspoonful of pastinaca seed, which is a quantity of the companion seed and not of the eryngium root. No dose of the root is given in the sources retrieved. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one quality, that the root "is able to warm". It prints tastes and smells: the leaves are "aromatic to the taste", the root "sweet smelling and aromatic". No wetness, dryness or degree is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a careful description of the plant at two stages (the soft new leaves that are eaten, the prickly heads of the grown plant), of its root cut open (black outside, white within), of its smell, and of where it grows (fields and rough places). It also contains an item he reports without vouching for: "It is said that used as a personal ornament or rubbed on someone it dissolves tubercles". The spec records that Dioscorides sometimes records amuletic uses while distancing himself, and recording is not endorsing. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is the habit of describing a plant at each stage and by smell and cut surface, and of keeping what is "said" apart from what the author states. The text offers nothing more.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. The text says the root expels the menstrual flow, so it should not be used in pregnancy. Tetanus, epilepsy, poisoning and venomous bites need immediate medical care and must not be left to a home preparation. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
