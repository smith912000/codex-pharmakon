# Iris nepalensis — Dre dru, dres 'bru (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary, by column position)

## *Iris nepalensis, as the binomial printed in the scientific-name run of the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary's "D" block; the Tibetan name "Dre dru" is set against it by column position only, and Clark 1995 prints no Iris nepalensis* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, and on one printed binomial in one of its two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. Only Men-Tsee-Khang prints *Iris nepalensis* (line 5253). A search of Clark 1995 for "Iris nepalensis" found nothing; Clark prints other irises under other Tibetan names (see below). A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk for the species name found no *Iris nepalensis*. A search for the bare word *Iris* finds many hits in those texts, which are other species or other senses of the word, and none is attributed to this row. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 269) the key is the glossary's own scientific name, *Iris nepalensis*, at "Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (line 5253)", with the note "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". Line 5253 prints two binomials, "Iris nepalensis Thlaspi arvense L.", and the key is the first of the two on that line. The glossary gives one binomial per entry, so this is not a first-of-several-names case as in Clark.

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "D" block, lines 5179 to 5261).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs, and the pairing of a Tibetan name with a binomial is not printed in one row.

The phonetic names run from "Dali" (line 5181) to "Drita sazin" (line 5196). Counting each name separately (line 5183 holds two, "Dhatig Dharyaken"; line 5188 holds five; line 5189 holds two), there are twenty-two. The nineteenth is "Dre dru" (line 5193), after "Drakya hawo" (line 5192) and before "Drega" (line 5194) and "Dremai" (line 5195). The transliterations (lines 5198 to 5213) include "brag skya ha bo", "dres 'bru" and "bre ga" (lines 5210 to 5212), so "dres 'bru" is the transliteration of "Dre dru".

The scientific names begin at line 5215. Counted in the same way, the nineteenth is "Iris nepalensis" and the twentieth "Thlaspi arvense L." (line 5253), the twenty-first "Iris ensata" (line 5254) and the twenty-second "Fragaria nubicola" (line 5259). The English names include "Iris" (line 5256). So "Dre dru" stands against *Iris nepalensis*, "Drega" against *Thlaspi arvense*, "Dremai" against *Iris ensata* and "Drita sazin" against *Fragaria nubicola*. The count can be checked against Clark on two neighbours: Clark's *bre.ga* (line 1104) is *Thlaspi arvense*, the twentieth, and Clark's *brag.skya.ha.wo* (line 1068) is *Corallodiscus*, which stands eighteenth ("Corallodiscus batalin", line 5247). The pairing of the nineteenth, "Dre dru" with *Iris nepalensis*, is by position between two neighbours that check, and is not printed in a row. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text on "Dre dru".* The name occurs once in the running text, in chapter 21, "The Group and the Method of Compounding Medicine" (contents, line 229), in a list of groups of medicines that cure specific disorders. Line 3638 reads: "La tsi, Shingkun, Gokya, Soma raza, Thangtrom, Langthangtse, Jitanga, Dre dru, snail shell, Phur thal, Cinshingdru, Dhawa, Yherma and Tuma are the group of medicines that cures cm disorders." The disorder word is printed "cm", which is not a word in the text read. The Codex does not resolve it. The index lists "Dre dru 222" (line 7348). The line is a group list, not a statement about this drug alone, and it gives no action, taste, power or part used for it. That the "Dre dru" of this list is the glossary's "Dre dru" rests on the identical spelling. [USER: verify]

*What Clark prints for the Tibetan name.* Clark 1995 does not print *dres 'bru* or *dre dru* as a Tibetan name; the nearest are *dres.ma* (line 1048, "Iris ensata Thumb: Iris decora: Iris lactea Pall.", whose petals "are anthelmintic, relieve cramps") and, in chapter 21, "seed of Iris ensata" in the anthelmintic category (line 1221). Neither names *Iris nepalensis*. Whether the glossary's "Dre dru" and Clark's *dres.ma* are one drug in two forms is not said by the texts read; the Codex does not decide it. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** I_1108 (*Iris ensata*, tag FMT) is the entry on Clark's *dres.ma* and the glossary's "Dremai". It also takes "Dre dru" (line 3638) into its text and its title as a form of the same drug, "a reading from the sound of the names and from the agreement on cramps and anthelmintic use" (its words), and says the glossary columns "do not settle" which phonetic name belongs to which scientific name. It does not count them place by place. By the count above, "Dre dru" stands against *Iris nepalensis* and "Dremai" against *Iris ensata*, so the two entries divide the two names that I_1108 groups. This entry does not edit I_1108; the difference is reported for a decision. I_1233 (*Arisaema intermedium*) and I_1278 (*Ferula narthex*) quote the line 3638 list for other names in it. No entry on *Iris nepalensis* was found. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Iris* is a genus of the iris family (Iridaceae), and that isoflavonoids are commonly found in the genus. Which part was used is not said, and the glossary gives the drug no description, so no more is stated.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The only use the Tibetan text prints for the name is its place in a list of medicines for a disorder whose name the print has garbled (line 3638). No action of its own is recorded. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and the entry does not support any use.
