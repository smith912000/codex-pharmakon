# KLUMENON — Klumenon (Clymenon, clumenion), Dioscorides *De Materia Medica* 4-13

## *Klumenon (Latin Clymenon), a herb with a four-sided stalk, bean-like stem and plantain-like leaves, and little pods like iris; the translator marks it UNKNOWN, so the identification stays open; confidence low* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder from the name index (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`: "KLUMENON, 4-13 (line 8107), UNKNOWN"); this entry replaces it. A search of the Dioscorides text for *clymenon*, *clumenion* and *Klumenon* found the chapter at 4-13 (lines 8107 to 8113 including a page break) and the same names in the alias list of the next chapter, 4-14 *Periklumenon* (line 8117), which the translator glosses as honeysuckle (*Lonicera periclymenum*).

**Dioscorides (DMM).** Book 4, chapter 13, `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 8107 to 8113, in the Book 4 section "Other Herbs & Roots". The translator's gloss is "UNKNOWN". The chapter reads: "Clymenon sends out a foursquare stalk similar to that of the bean, and leaves similar to those of plantain. It has little pods on the stalk (nodding together) similar to iris and the curled tufts of the polypus. That on the hills is the best. It is all juiced together with the root. The juice (taken as a drink) is good for throwing-up blood, for abdominal cavities, and for excessive bloody discharges. It is astringent and cooling, and it stops flows of blood that come out of the nostrils. The leaves or the pods, pounded into small pieces and applied to new wounds, bring them effectively to a scar." The page break "555 / BOOK FOUR: OTHER HERBS & ROOTS" falls inside the paragraph in the e-text.

**Names.** The chapter closes with a list of other names: "calycanthemom, periclymenon, helyophthes, hepatitis, smilax, anatolicon, dyticon, or merginem; the Romans call it volucrum, or volucrum maius; the Egyptians call it oxiui, clymenon, or clumenion, and they also call it agonon." Several of these names (*periclymenon*, *calycanthemon*, *hepatitis*, *clymenon*, *volucrum maius*) also appear in the alias list of chapter 4-14, the following chapter, so the two chapters overlap in naming, and the Codex's separate entry for 4-14 (if there is one) should be read with this one. Whether Dioscorides meant two plants or one is not decided here. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The text names no constituent and the plant is not identified, so no composition can be stated. Dioscorides' "astringent and cooling" suggests, in general terms, a herb rich in tannin-type astringent substances, but this is only a reading of the quality word and not a finding. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** The juice, drunk, for "throwing-up blood", for "abdominal cavities" (the translator's phrase) and for "excessive bloody discharges"; it is said to stop nosebleed. The leaves or pods, pounded and applied, bring new wounds "effectively to a scar" (4-13).
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no duration, no instruction for gathering beyond "that on the hills is the best".

Bleeding of this kind is a medical emergency and the Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The whole plant is juiced together with the root; the juice is drunk. For wounds the leaves or pods are pounded small and applied (4-13). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** An astringent, cooling herb used for bleeding and wounds, its identity unknown. Nothing in the record supports a cultivation use.
