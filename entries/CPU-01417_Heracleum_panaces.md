# Heracleum panaces — Panakes herakleion, Panaces heracleum (Dioscorides 3-55); Oinos panakites (5-72)

## *Heracleum panaces* (the Dioscorides translators' suggested identification; fig-leaved cow parsnip) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Identity note.** This plant is named only by Dioscorides (DMM). The binomial is not Dioscorides' own: it is the suggestion printed on the SUGGESTED line of the English translation by Osbaldeston and Wood (2000). The translators list *Heracleum panaces* ("Fig-leaved Cow Parsnip") beside *H. sphondylium*, *H. gummiferum*, *H. pubescens* and *H. pyrenaicum*, so even the translators do not settle on one species. [USER: verify] The substance the chapter is mainly about is the dried juice, opopanax, which has existing entries in this book (I_176 and I_613). This row records the plant under the translators' *Heracleum* gloss.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Source: `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`.

*3-55 PANAKES HERAKLEION* (heading at line 7021; text lines 7023 and 7034). Dioscorides says it "grows in abundance in Boeotia, and Psophis in Arcadia. It is carefully cultivated in gardens for the benefit that comes from the juice", and also in Cyrene, Libya and Macedonia. The description: rough green leaves on the ground "very near to those of the fig, jagged five-fold in the circumference"; a very high stalk "like a ferula" with white down; a long tuft on top "like dill"; yellowish flowers; seed that "smells sweet and acrid"; "many white strong-smelling roots" with thick bark and a somewhat bitter taste. The chapter also gives a harvest method: the root is juiced "after being cut when the stalks are newly emerged", a white juice that dries saffron-coloured outside; the leaves are laid on a hollowed piece of ground and picked up when dry; the stalk is cut at harvest time and juiced the same way.

The chapter is largely a discipline of assay. The best roots are "stretched out, white, dry, not worm-eaten, hot to the taste, and aromatic"; the seed from the middle of the stalk is better than that from the sprigs. The best juice is "the most bitter to the taste", white and somewhat red inside and saffron outside, "smooth, fat, brittle", melting quickly and strongly scented; "the black and soft is worthless as it is adulterated with ammoniacum [3-98] or wax". The test: "Being rubbed in water with the fingers tests it, for the counterfeited dissolves and becomes similar to milk."

*5-72 OINOS PANAKITES* (heading at line 10158). A wine prepared from "panax [opopanax, 3-55]", described under Preparation & Dosing.

### Active Components

The plant is a member of the carrot family (Apiaceae). Genus *Heracleum* is generally known to contain furanocoumarins, which can make skin sensitive to sunlight, and aromatic resins and essential oil. The constituents of the plant or juice Dioscorides describes were not read in any source. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The chapter's uses are medicinal and gynaecological (including abortifacient uses) and are given as Dioscorides' own claims, not as a Codex protocol. The Codex gives no protocol for the plant or its juice. The identification is a translators' gloss and is not settled.

### Preparation & Dosing

*3-55.* "Taken as a drink with honey and water (or wine) it is good for periods of acute fevers and chills, convulsions, hernia, pains in the side, coughs, griping, parasitic diseases in the bladder, and slow painful urination." Dissolved with honey it "induces the menstrual flow, is an abortifacient, and scatters gaseousness and hardness in the womb". It is an ointment for hip pains; "mixed with [medicines for] removal of fatigue, and with head medicines"; it "breaks carbuncles all around", and rubbed on with raisin clusters is good for gout. It soothes toothache when put in tooth cavities and "is rubbed on as a sight-restorer for the eyes". Mixed with pitch it is a plaster for bites of mad dogs; the shaved root applied to the vulva "is an abortifacient"; with honey it is effective for old ulcers and "covers exposed bones with flesh". The seed taken with wormwood [3-26] induces the menstrual flow, with aristolochia [3-4, 3-5, 3-6] it is good for bites of poisonous beasts, and as a drink with wine it is given for constriction of the womb.

*5-72 (wine).* "Put one ounce of panax [opopanax, 3-55] into four and a half litres of must [for several months], and then pour it into another jar." It is said to be good for convulsions, hernias, bruises and orthopnoea; it reduces the thickness of the spleen, and is good for griping, sciatica, slow digestion, dropsy and bites of venomous creatures; it "expels the menstrual flow". This is the only quantity in the entry. No dose for drinking is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Apiaceae gum-resins and furanocoumarin-containing *Heracleum* species are generally known to be skin photosensitisers. This is a general statement about the family and genus; no study of this species was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** 3-55 prints "It is warming and softening, and reduces the intensity of symptoms." The root is "hot to the taste" and the juice "most bitter to the taste". No degree system is printed in the chapter.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. The chapter gives a discipline of attention: where the plant grows, how to know leaf, stalk, seed and root, when to cut, how to gather the juice, how to judge the best juice and detect adulteration with ammoniacum or wax by rubbing it in water. The statement that it is "carefully cultivated in gardens" is horticultural fact about the source of the juice and not a cultivation status for the person. The identification with *H. panaces* is the translators' gloss. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the source read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. What the chapter offers is the habit of testing a drug before use: the water-rubbing test for adulterated juice. The answer for the person is to abstain, since the uses recorded include abortifacient ones and the identification is unsettled.

### Contraindications

Historical uses recorded here include abortifacient uses and are not advice. Do not use in pregnancy. Plants of the genus *Heracleum* can cause skin reactions in sunlight. The identity of the plant and of the juice is uncertain, and no safe dose is given in the sources retrieved. Seek qualified care for any of the complaints named.
