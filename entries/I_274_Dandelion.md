# I-274: Taraxacum officinale (Dandelion)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-274 | Asteraceae (Compositae)**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Taraxacum officinale* F.H.Wigg. (agg.)
**Family:** Asteraceae

**Common Names:**
- English: Dandelion, Blowball, Lion's Tooth, Fairy Clock
- French: *Pissenlit* — "wet the bed" — direct reference to diuretic action (the most candid folk name in botanical medicine)
- German: *Löwenzahn* (lion's tooth) — leaf morphology
- Italian: *Tarassaco*, *Dente di leone*
- Spanish: *Diente de león*
- Arabic: *Hindabā barrī* (هندباء برية) — wild chicory/dandelion (Cichorium/Taraxacum often conflated in Arabic texts)
- Mandarin: *Pú gōng yīng* (蒲公英) — one of the most important TCM anti-infectious herbs

**Etymology:**
"Dandelion" = Old French *dent de lion* (lion's tooth), referencing the deeply toothed leaves. *Pú gōng yīng* (dandelion) in Chinese: *pú* = cattail/spread; *gōng* = duke/chief; *yīng* = *yīng táo* (cherry) — alternate explanation: "spreading flower with flying seeds." The French *pissenlit* (piss-in-bed) is perhaps the most pharmacologically honest common name in any language — it directly describes the herb's most obvious clinical effect.

**Taxonomy Note:**
*Taraxacum officinale* is taxonomically complex — an apomictic polyploid aggregate with hundreds of microspecies across the Northern Hemisphere. For medicinal purposes, the *officinale* aggregate is treated as a single taxon; all are pharmacologically equivalent.

**Morphology:**
Perennial; radical rosette; deeply lobed basal leaves; hollow flowering scapes with single bright yellow composite flower head; spherical seed head (the "blowball" / "fairy clock") dispersing achenes with white pappus parachutes.

**Parts Used:**
- **Leaves:** Diuretic primary; higher vitamin C, K₁, A; bitter compounds
- **Root:** Liver/hepatoprotective primary; inulin-rich; prebiotic; deeper bitter action
- **Flowers:** Antioxidant; mild; edible (dandelion wine, fritters)
- **Whole plant:** Combined applications; commonly used as leaf + root combination in preparations

**Global Distribution:** *T. officinale* is one of the most widely distributed vascular plants — naturalized on all inhabited continents; invasive in lawns across North America, Europe, Australia, South America; native to Eurasia.

**Weed or Medicine?**
The cultural positioning of dandelion as a "weed" in modern North American and European lawn culture represents a complete reversal of its traditional status as a valuable food-medicine. Traditional European societies harvested dandelion leaves as a spring vegetable (bitter green; one of the first spring greens available), the root as a coffee substitute (roasted dandelion root = caffeine-free coffee analog), and the flowers for wine and syrup. The modern "weed" label reflects agrochemical-lawn culture priorities, not plant value.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domains:**
1. **Diuretic / renal** — All accessible traditions: promotes urination; kidney; edema; urinary tract; *pissenlit* = clinical confirmation across cultures
2. **Liver / hepatoprotective / biliary** — CM, SBJ, SS, EP, AT: liver; bile flow; "obstruction of liver"; jaundice; bitter digestive
3. **Anti-inflammatory / anti-infectious** — SBJ (primary): *pú gōng yīng* for breast abscess, lymphadenitis, boils — heat and toxin resolution
4. **Nutritive tonic / spring bitter** — All European traditions: early spring nutritive tonic; vitamins; minerals; bitter digestive stimulant

**Divergence Between East and West:**
- **Western traditions (CM, EP, AT, European folk):** Emphasize diuretic + hepatoprotective + digestive bitter
- **TCM (SBJ):** Emphasizes anti-infectious + heat-clearing; used for breast mastitis, abscess, snake bite — more acute infectious applications; distinct from Western hepatodiuretic emphasis
- This represents genuine pharmacological breadth: the same plant covers different primary applications depending on cultural framing; modern research validates BOTH diuretic AND anti-inflammatory mechanisms

**Energetic Classification:**
- ◉ Qi (functional): Primary — diuretic renal Qi; biliary Qi flow; heat-clearing
- ◈ Jing (structural): Secondary — hepatoprotective (liver structural); nutritive (vitamins, minerals)

**Primary Tier: ◉ Qi** — functional diuretic + biliary + anti-inflammatory Qi regulation

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Sesquiterpene Lactones (Root + Leaf Bitters)

**1. Taraxacin**
- Sesquiterpene lactone; primary bitter compound; eudesmanolide class
- Choleretic (bile flow); digestive bitter; bitter index > 100

**2. Taraxacerin**
- Related sesquiterpene lactone; anti-inflammatory; NF-κB inhibition

**3. Taraxacoside** — acylated sterol glycoside; anti-inflammatory

### Triterpenes and Sterols

**4. Taraxerol, Taraxasterol, Pseudotaraxasterol**
- Pentacyclic triterpenes; anti-inflammatory; liver-protective
- Characteristic of *Taraxacum*; chemotaxonomic markers

**5. β-Amyrin** — anti-inflammatory triterpene

### Flavonoids

**6. Luteolin-7-O-glucoside** — primary flavonoid; anti-inflammatory; estrogenic (weak)
**7. Apigenin-7-O-glucoside** — GABA-A PAM (mild anxiolytic at higher doses)
**8. Chrysoeriol** — anti-inflammatory flavonoid

### Polysaccharides (Root)

**9. Inulin — 12–45% root dry weight (highest in autumn harvest)**
- Prebiotic FOS; bifidogenic; SCFA production
- Dandelion root = one of the richest natural inulin sources (comparable to Jerusalem artichoke and chicory root)
- Root should be harvested autumn for maximum inulin; spring for maximum bitter principle

### Vitamins and Minerals (Leaf)

**10. Vitamin A (as β-carotene) — 5,854 IU/100g** (exceptional; higher than most greens)
**11. Vitamin C — 35 mg/100g** (spring green anti-scurvy source)
**12. Vitamin K₁ — 778 μg/100g** (among the highest of any food plant)
**13. Folate — 27 μg/100g**
**14. Iron — 3.1 mg/100g** (significant non-heme iron)
**15. Potassium — 397 mg/100g** (critically important for diuretic applications — see below)

### Other

**16. Caffeic acid derivatives (chlorogenic acid)** — antioxidant; hepatoprotective
**17. Coumarins (minor)** — scopoletin; mild anti-spasmodic
**18. Phytosterols** — β-sitosterol; cholesterol competition

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### 1. Diuretic (Primary Mechanism — Unique for a Herbal Diuretic)

**The Potassium-Sparing Diuretic Herb:**
Most pharmaceutical diuretics (thiazides, loop diuretics) deplete potassium — requiring potassium supplementation in long-term use. Dandelion leaf is unique among diuretic herbs because it:
- Increases urinary output (potassium-mediated aquaporin pathway; caffeic acid + flavonoid mechanism)
- **Provides potassium simultaneously** (397 mg K⁺/100g leaf)
- Net K⁺ balance: the K⁺ lost in increased urine is partially replaced by K⁺ from consuming the herb

This "built-in K⁺ replacement" is a pharmacological elegance that exceeds synthetic diuretics' side effect profile.

**Mechanism detail:**
- Luteolin, apigenin: inhibit aldosterone-mediated Na⁺ reabsorption in distal tubule → increased urinary Na⁺ + water → diuresis
- Caffeic acid: mild inhibition of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase in renal tubule → increased Na⁺ excretion

### 2. Hepatoprotective and Choleretic
- Taraxacin/taraxacerin (sesquiterpene lactones): choleretic — increase bile secretion and flow
- Triterpenes (taraxerol, taraxasterol): hepatocyte membrane protection; antioxidant within liver
- Anti-fibrotic: TGF-β1 reduction (similar mechanism to silymarin, lesser potency)
- Inulin: prebiotic → bifidogenic → butyrate production → enterohepatic axis improvement → indirect liver benefit

### 3. Anti-Inflammatory (TCM Heat-Clearing Application)
- NF-κB inhibition (taraxacerin, luteolin): reduces TNF-α, IL-6
- Anti-staphylococcal: chlorogenic acid + taraxerol inhibit *S. aureus* (validates TCM abscess/mastitis application)
- Antimicrobial: moderate broad-spectrum MICs against *E. coli*, *S. aureus*; relevant to TCM infection-clearing
- Anti-estrogenic (high dose): luteolin anti-estrogenic at high concentrations → may explain TCM breast application (estrogen-driven mastitis; anti-inflammatory + anti-estrogenic)

### 4. Pre-Biotic (Root Inulin)
- 12–45% root inulin: one of richest natural prebiotic sources
- Well-established bifidogenic effect (same FOS/inulin mechanism as leek, Jerusalem artichoke, chicory)
- Enterohepatic benefit: improved microbiome → improved bile acid metabolism → improved liver function

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Diuretic:**
- RCT (Clare 2009, n=17): Dandelion leaf extract → statistically significant increased urinary frequency and volume over 5-hour period vs. baseline
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (one RCT; mechanistically solid; traditional convergence 6+ traditions)

**Anti-Inflammatory / Anti-oxidant:**
- Multiple in vitro studies; one small human pilot: dandelion extract (3.5 g/day × 4 weeks) → reduced oxidative stress markers
- **Evidence Grade: Level D** (in vitro) to Level C (pilot human data)

**Prebiotic:**
- Root inulin class: Level A evidence for bifidogenic effect (same as chicory root, Jerusalem artichoke inulin)
- **Evidence Grade: Level A** (inulin class)

**Glycemic Control:**
- In vitro: dandelion leaf extract inhibits α-glucosidase and α-amylase (digestive enzymes)
- Animal models: glucose-lowering effect
- **Evidence Grade: Level D** (in vitro); Level C (traditional)

**No high-quality RCT** for primary indications (hepatoprotective, anti-infectious) yet published.

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The Resurrected Spring Weed:**
The dandelion phenomenology in modern Western culture is paradoxical: one of the most widely herbicided plants in the world is also one of the most nutritionally dense and pharmacologically active common weeds. The experience of gathering dandelion from an unsprayed lawn or meadow in early spring — the bright yellow flowers, the distinctive milk when stems are broken, the earthy-bitter taste of young leaves — reconnects contemporary food culture to the foraging traditions that sustained human nutrition for millennia.

**Taste Profile:**
- Young spring leaves: bitter, slightly nutty, fresh-green; best in salads with acidic dressing (counterpoints bitterness)
- Late-season leaves: more intensely bitter; better cooked (blanched, sautéed with olive oil and garlic — traditional Italian *cicoria* preparation)
- Root decoction: dark, slightly caramelized, bitter-sweet; roasted root = coffee analog (no caffeine; same viscosity; inulin provides body)
- Flowers: sweet, mild, slightly grassy; eaten raw; made into wine (sugar + dandelion flowers + citrus → traditional European dandelion wine)

**The Diuretic Experience:**
The French name *pissenlit* reflects genuine, strong, well-known diuretic effect. Eating a significant quantity of fresh dandelion leaves (100–200 g in a salad) produces measurable increase in urinary frequency within 2–3 hours — a pharmacological effect legible to anyone who has eaten a traditional European dandelion salad. This is the most immediately palpable pharmacological effect in all of the food-medicine herbs in the Codex.

**TCM Anti-Infectious Context:**
*Pú gōng yīng* is used in TCM for breast conditions — particularly mastitis (breast abscess, blocked ducts). Patients describe fresh dandelion root poultice applied to the breast and hot dandelion decoction drunk simultaneously producing noticeable pain and heat reduction within 1–2 days. The combined anti-inflammatory + anti-staphylococcal + diuretic (fluid movement) mechanisms provide a coherent pharmacological explanation.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Fresh leaves (diuretic + nutritive):**
- 50–200 g fresh leaves (salad or cooked): significant diuretic effect; exceptional micronutrient delivery
- Spring harvest preferred: young leaves less bitter; highest vitamin content
- Dress with olive oil + lemon/vinegar: fat enhances β-carotene and K₁ absorption (fat-soluble vitamins)

**Leaf tea:**
- 4–10 g dried leaf in 250 mL water; steep 10 minutes; 3 cups/day: clinical diuretic dose
- Standard European herbal dose: 4–10 g leaf 3× daily

**Root decoction:**
- 2–8 g dried root in 250 mL water; decoct 10–15 minutes; 1–2 cups daily: liver + prebiotic + bitter digestive
- Autumn-harvested root contains peak inulin (40–45%); spring root contains more bitter compounds
- Roasted root: excellent coffee substitute; roast at 180°C until dark brown; powder; brew as coffee

**Root extract (standardized):**
- Root extract 1:5 (25% ethanol): 5–10 mL 3× daily
- Root powder capsule: 500–1,000 mg 3× daily

**TCM application:**
- *Pú gōng yīng* decoction: 9–30 g fresh or dried whole plant; anti-infectious
- Poultice: fresh crushed plant on breast abscess, boil, or inflamed area

### Foraging Guidelines
- **Verify no herbicide application:** Do NOT harvest from lawns that have been sprayed (herbicide absorption in leaves); roadside dandelions may accumulate heavy metals
- **Harvest in spring** for best taste and vitamin content; young leaves before flower stalk emergence
- **Dig root in autumn** for maximum inulin

### Safety Profile

**Excellent safety profile.** Widely consumed as food for millennia.

**Drug Interactions:**
- **Diuretic drugs (thiazides, furosemide):** Additive diuretic effect; electrolyte monitoring; but dandelion's K⁺ content partially offsets K⁺ depletion from thiazides
- **Warfarin:** Vitamin K₁ (778 μg/100g) — among the HIGHEST K₁ of any food; significant dietary change (eating large amounts of dandelion) can affect INR substantially; maintain dietary consistency; inform anticoagulation clinic
- **Lithium:** Diuretic effect may concentrate lithium; theoretical; monitor lithium levels if adding significant dandelion supplementation
- **Anti-diabetic:** α-glucosidase inhibition + glucose lowering; additive with anti-diabetic medications; monitor blood glucose

**Contraindications:**
- **Bile duct obstruction:** Choleretic activity; bile duct stones or obstruction — contraindicated (stimulating bile flow against a block)
- **Gallbladder disease (acute):** Avoid in acute cholecystitis
- **Asteraceae allergy:** Cross-reactive with ragweed, chrysanthemum; rare IgE-mediated reactions

**Pregnancy / Lactation:**
- Food quantities safe
- Medicinal doses: limited data; general caution with high-dose leaf/root preparations
- Traditional galactagogue use; likely safe at food doses during lactation
