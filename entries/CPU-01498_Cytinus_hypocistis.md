# Cytinus hypocistis — Upokustis, Upokistis etera (Dioscorides 1-127)

## *Cytinus hypocistis (the translators' suggestion; "Hypocistis"), a parasitic plant that Dioscorides says grows around the roots of the cistus* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant as a drug. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The line reads "Cytinus hypocistis — Hypocistis", and below it the translators add a note: "parasitic plant — contains gallic acid". That note is the translators', not Dioscorides', and no source for it was opened. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *1-127. UPOKUSTIS, UPOKISTIS ETERA*, heading at line 4463, SUGGESTED line at 4464, translators' note at 4465, text at line 4466. It stands in Book One among the gums and trees.

The whole chapter reads: "Hypocistis grows around the roots of the cistus [rock roses] like cytinus on the pomegranate. Some of it is yellow, some green, some white. It is juiced like acacia, but some dry and break it, then steep and boil it, and do other things to it in the same way as lycium [1-132]. It is similar in strength to acacia, yet more astringent and drying — good for those troubled with colic, dysentery, blood-spitting, or women's excessive discharges — taken either as a drink or infused. It is called robethrum, or cytinus by some. The Africans call it phyllesade."

So Dioscorides gives no botanical description beyond its habit, growing around the roots of the cistus as cytinus (the pomegranate flower-calyx, 1-152) does on the pomegranate, and its colours: some yellow, some green, some white. He gives two ways of preparing it, juicing it as is done with acacia, or drying and breaking it, then steeping and boiling it as is done with lycium (1-132). Its strength is compared with acacia but he says it is more astringent and drying. It is said to be good for colic, dysentery, blood-spitting and women's excessive discharges, drunk or infused. The other names printed are *robethrum* and, "by some", *cytinus*, and the African name *phyllesade*.

**Other places in the same text.** The drug is named three times outside its own chapter, each time as a comparison or a substitute. In the chapter on *schinos* (mastic tree) the text says that the juice of the bark "may be used instead of acacia and hypocistis [1-127]" (line 4219). In the passage on preparing juices (line 6615), "Lycium [1-132], wormwood [3-26], hypocistis [1-127] and herbs similar to these are boiled and stirred around as previously" described. In the chapter on asarum (line 3628) the flower is said to be "an azure [blue] colour like cytinus [1-127] or hyoscyamus [4-69]". The first two bear on use and preparation; the third is a colour reference to a cytinus flower, which sits ill with the colours listed for hypocistis in 1-127 (yellow, green, white), so the Codex does not read the cross-reference as certain to mean the same thing. ### Active Components

Beyond the translators' note, no constituent of the plant was read. The note says "contains gallic acid"; gallic acid is a plant phenolic acid known generally as a tannin component, which fits Dioscorides' repeated description of the drug as astringent, but no source for the note was opened and no composition is asserted here. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records an astringent juice or infusion of a parasitic plant for colic, dysentery, spitting blood and women's excessive discharges. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol: spitting blood and dysentery need medical care, and the plant is a parasite on a wild shrub, so it is neither a cultivated nor a readily verified plant.

### Preparation & Dosing

1-127 gives these preparations: "juiced like acacia"; or "dry and break it, then steep and boil it, and do other things to it in the same way as lycium [1-132]"; and "taken either as a drink or infused". The text for lycium (1-132) was not read for this entry, so what the "other things" are is not stated here. [USER: verify] No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints the drug as "similar in strength to acacia, yet more astringent and drying". No warming or cooling quality is printed. The "other kind" (*etera*) in the heading is not described in the text, which has a single paragraph. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter's offer to a cultivator is the discipline of attention: knowing where the thing grows (round the roots of the cistus), how its colours vary (yellow, green, white), how it is turned into a drug (juiced, or dried, broken, steeped and boiled), and how strong it is judged against a known standard (acacia, but more astringent and drying). It assigns the plant no status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is an astringent remedy for bowel, bleeding and discharge complaints, with no amounts, so it is history and not a practice. What it teaches is how Dioscorides ranks a drug by comparison with another one, its strength set against acacia's.

### Contraindications

Colic, dysentery, blood-spitting and heavy discharges are conditions that need medical care, and a home preparation must not replace it. A strongly astringent drug is not harmless merely because it is old, and no safety information was read. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
