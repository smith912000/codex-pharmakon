# I-510 | Goldenseal (*Hydrastis canadensis* L.)

**Volume I — Plant-Based Substances**
**Family:** Ranunculaceae
**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, mucous membrane toning) primary | ◈ Jing (bitter tonic) secondary

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary binomial:** *Hydrastis canadensis* L. (1759)

**Synonyms & historical names:**
- *Warneria canadensis* Mill. (1768)
- Historical: Indian paintbrush (inappropriate — frequently confused with *Castilleja* species), eye balm, eye root, orange root, jaundice root, ground raspberry, golden seal, yellow puccoon

**Common names:**
- English: Goldenseal, yellow root (common misnomer shared with *Xanthorhiza simplicissima*), gold seal, orange root
- German: *Kanadische Gelbwurzel*, *Hydrastis*
- French: *Hydraste du Canada*
- Italian: *Idrastide*
- Spanish: *Sello de oro*
- Cherokee: *Nv-no-hi-lo-hi* (path plant — grows along forest paths); also used as yellow dye
- Iroquois: *Oseragondi* (skin helper)

**Etymology:** *Hydrastis* = Greek *hydor* (water) + *drastes* (worker; active) — water worker; *canadensis* = from Canada. "Goldenseal" — from the golden-yellow color of the rhizome cross-section + the indented "seal" (scar pattern) left by previous year's stem bases. "Orange root" describes the same color in the root cortex. The brilliant golden-yellow color = berberine alkaloid chromophore (same compound that colors *Berberis* yellow).

**Botanical authentication:**
- Low-growing woodland perennial 15–40 cm; single stem; 2–3 large palmately lobed leaves (5–7 lobes, wrinkled surface); solitary white flower (April–May; no petals — only sepals; stamens prominent); fruit: raspberry-like aggregate of red berries; **rhizome:** knotted, golden-yellow internally (berberine coloration), wrinkled externally with circular stem scars = "seals"; characteristic intensely bitter taste
- Authentication critical: *H. canadensis* is CITES Appendix II listed (at-risk wild species); must verify cultivation origin; wild-harvest creates serious conservation concern
- Color test: cut rhizome exposes brilliant golden-yellow interior (berberine); if pale/white internally → adulterated
- Adulterants: *Coptis chinensis* (goldthread/黄连 — contains berberine; sometimes sold as goldenseal substitute; pharmacologically similar but distinct species), *Xanthorhiza simplicissima* (yellow-root), *Caltha palustris* — verify species

**Conservation status:**
*Hydrastis canadensis* is:
- CITES Appendix II: international commercial trade requires permit; export documentation required from country of origin
- NatureServe: "Vulnerable" across native range (Eastern North America deciduous forest understory)
- Primary threat: wild-harvest for supplement industry (estimated 90%+ historically wild-harvested before CITES listing)
- Cultivation now commercially available; prescribers should specify "organically cultivated" to avoid contributing to wild-population depletion

**Convergence note:** Goldenseal itself (*H. canadensis*) is known only to North American indigenous traditions. However, the primary pharmacologically active alkaloid — berberine — is produced by multiple plant species across unrelated families (*Berberis* Berberidaceae, *Coptis* Ranunculaceae, *Hydrastis* Ranunculaceae, *Chelidonium* Papaveraceae) distributed across North America, Europe, Asia, and the Indian subcontinent. Each culture independently identified the bitter yellow alkaloid-containing plant in their region as a mucous membrane tonic, antimicrobial, and bitter digestive — suggesting berberine's pharmacological signature is detectable at the level of whole-plant use in multiple traditions.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

The berberine alkaloid family represents one of the most striking examples of pharmacological convergence in the Codex at the chemical rather than species level. Independent traditions discovered that yellow-colored bitter roots with isoquinoline alkaloids share antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, mucous membrane-toning, and bitter digestive properties:

- **North American:** *Hydrastis canadensis* (goldenseal) — Cherokee, Iroquois, Eclectic physicians
- **Chinese:** *Coptis chinensis* 黄连 (*huáng lián*) — one of the oldest Chinese medicinal herbs; Shennong Bencao Jing
- **Ayurvedic/Tibetan:** *Berberis aristata* (*dārvī*); *Berberis* spp. (*se-ba*) — fever, skin, eye, bitter digestive
- **European:** *Berberis vulgaris* (barberry) — European folk tradition; bitter digestive; UTI; jaundice
- **Mesoamerican:** *Mahonia* spp. (Oregon grape — related to *Berberis*) — Pacific Northwest indigenous

The chemical unifier across all these uses: **berberine** (isoquinoline alkaloid, 0.5–6% in various species). This is parallel to the rosmarinic acid pattern established in Lamiales entries — one key compound discovered independently by multiple traditions across different plant genera and families, in each case through observation of the same yellow color + extreme bitterness + antimicrobial + mucous membrane activity combination.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Isoquinoline alkaloids (primary pharmacologically active fraction):**
- **Berberine** (5,6-dihydro-9,10-dimethoxybenzo[g]-1,3-benzodioxolo[5,6-a]isoquinolinium): 2–4% DW in rhizome (0.5–6% depending on analysis); primary active alkaloid; YELLOW chromophore (absorbs visible light ~430 nm); protoberberine-type isoquinoline
- **Hydrastine** (1.5–4% DW): phthalide isoquinoline alkaloid (distinct class from berberine); selective α1-adrenergic receptor agonism → vasoconstriction; uterine contractor; unique to *Hydrastis* (absent from *Coptis*, *Berberis*) — **this is the distinguishing phytochemical feature of goldenseal vs. berberine-only alternatives**
- **Canadine** (tetrahydroberberine, 0.5–1.0% DW): protoberberine; sedative via GABA-A modulation and dopamine D2 antagonism; present in other berberine-source plants
- **Berberastine**: minor protoberberine
- **Hydrastinine**: oxidation product of hydrastine; hemostatic (uterine); used historically as hemostatic agent

**Chlorogenic acid and phenolic acids:** 0.5–2% DW; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory

**Resin fraction:** 5–10% DW; bitter; contributes to mucous membrane activity

**Meconine** (trace): narcotic coumarin; extremely minor

**PhEur standardization:** *Hydrastis rhizoma* — ≥2.0% hydrastine and ≥3.0% berberine (calculated on dried basis); these two alkaloids together serve as both quality marker and fingerprint distinguishing *H. canadensis* from berberine-only adulterants (which lack hydrastine).

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Berberine — multiple converging mechanisms:**

1. **Antimicrobial:**
- Intercalates bacterial DNA (same mechanism as acridine dyes); particularly effective against gram-positive organisms and *Chlamydia trachomatis*
- Inhibits bacterial topoisomerase II and IV
- Anti-biofilm: disrupts *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Candida albicans* biofilm formation
- MIC against *S. aureus*, *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *Shigella*, *Vibrio cholerae*: 16–256 μg/mL (consistent with topical but not necessarily systemic antibacterial at oral doses)
- Anti-*H. pylori*: clinical evidence; combination with antibiotics shows synergy

2. **Anti-diarrheal / antisecretory:**
- Reduces *Vibrio cholerae* and *E. coli* enterotoxin-induced fluid secretion (inhibits cyclic AMP elevation in intestinal epithelium)
- Anti-inflammatory in gut mucosa
- Multiple clinical trials in cholera and infectious diarrhea (Bengal, Bangladesh, China): berberine as adjunct to oral rehydration therapy significantly reduces stool frequency and duration

3. **Hypoglycemic:**
- AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase) activation → increased GLUT4 expression → improved insulin sensitivity
- Inhibition of mitochondrial complex I (metabolic, not toxic at relevant doses)
- Clinical evidence for Type 2 diabetes comparable to metformin (berberine 1500 mg/day vs metformin 1500 mg/day RCTs — Zhang 2008, Yin 2008)

4. **Lipid-lowering:**
- PCSK9 inhibition → increased LDL receptor expression → reduced LDL-C
- Mechanism independent of statins; additive with statins; cardiovascular benefit in multiple RCTs (berberine 1000 mg/day: LDL-C -23%, TG -35%, Zhang 2008)

5. **Anti-inflammatory:**
- NF-κB inhibition
- COX-2 suppression
- NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition
- IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6 reduction

6. **Mucous membrane toning (astringent + anti-inflammatory + antimicrobial triad):**
The combination of mild astringency (resin + tannin fraction), berberine antimicrobial activity, and berberine anti-inflammatory effects produces the "mucous membrane tonic" clinical effect consistent across all traditional applications (eye, throat, gut, vagina, skin). No single mechanism explains this; the triad is the mechanism.

**Hydrastine — unique goldenseal mechanism:**
- α1-Adrenergic receptor agonism → arteriolar constriction → hemostasis (nosebleed, postpartum hemorrhage — traditional Eclectic use)
- Uterotonic at higher doses → CONTRAINDICATED in pregnancy
- Mild CNS stimulant (small doses); CNS depressant (large doses)
- Differentiates goldenseal from berberine alone preparations — hydrastine adds the hemostatic dimension

**"Drug masking" myth:**
A persistent internet claim: goldenseal masks urine drug tests (opiate/cannabis). This is pharmacologically FALSE. No credible study has demonstrated goldenseal consumption alters drug test results. The myth originated from a 1900 novel (*Stringtown on the Pike* by John Uri Lloyd) as a fictional plot device. Goldenseal does not affect urine drug immunoassay results. It has no relationship to opiate or cannabis metabolism.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Berberine for Type 2 Diabetes — Level A:**
1. **Zhang Y et al. 2008** (*Metabolism* 57:712-717) — n=116, T2DM; berberine 500 mg TID vs metformin 500 mg TID; equivalent HbA1c reduction, FBG reduction, postprandial glucose; Level A

2. **Yin J et al. 2008** (*Metabolism* 57:652-661) — n=36, T2DM; berberine 500 mg TID; HbA1c reduced by 2.2% from baseline (7.5→5.6%); FBG -20%, 2h PG -28%; Level A (small)

3. **Dong H et al. 2012** (*Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine*): meta-analysis 14 RCTs; berberine significantly superior to placebo for FBG, HbA1c; comparable to oral hypoglycemics; Level A

**Berberine for Dyslipidemia — Level A:**
1. **Zhang H et al. 2008** (*Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism* 93:2559-2565) — n=91; berberine 500 mg TID; LDL-C -23.8%, TG -35.1%, TC -12.2% vs placebo; Level A

2. **Meng S et al. 2015** (*Phytomedicine* 22:1194-1201) — meta-analysis 27 RCTs; berberine significantly reduces LDL-C, TC, TG; Level A aggregate

**Infectious diarrhea — Level A:**
- **Rabbani GH et al. 1987** (*Lancet* 329:1361-1364) — n=400, enterotoxigenic *E. coli* diarrhea; berberine 400 mg single dose reduced stool volume 48% at 24 hours vs placebo; Level A
- Multiple cholera adjunct studies (India, Bangladesh); berberine 400 mg reduces duration and stool output

***Goldenseal herb* (not isolated berberine) for upper respiratory tract infection — Level C:**
- Traditional Eclectic and North American indigenous use; Commission E does not have an approved monograph; EMA: traditional use only
- No RCTs for goldenseal (vs berberine) for upper respiratory indications
- Anti-mucous membrane infection mechanism plausible (berberine + hydrastine antimicrobial in mucosa)

**Eye infections (topical berberine) — Level B:**
- Multiple clinical studies (India, Egypt): berberine eye drops (0.2% solution) for trachoma and bacterial conjunctivitis; significant improvement vs placebo; Level B
- Consistent with traditional Cherokee eye wash use

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Cherokee dye and medicine:**
The Cherokee used golden yellow root powder as a facial dye for ceremonial and warfare purposes — the berberine chromophore producing a vivid golden-yellow color on skin. This dual use (dye + medicine) is documented across North American eastern woodland tribes and represents one of the clearest traditional demonstrations of berberine's visibility as a chemical marker — the yellow that says "medicinal."

**The Eclectic Pharmacopoeia:**
The 19th-century Eclectic medical movement built goldenseal into one of its signature preparations. John Uri Lloyd's *Specific Medication* (1870s) and *King's American Eclectic Dispensatory* established goldenseal's clinical profile through extensive case observation. By 1900, goldenseal was one of the top five selling "patent medicines" in North America. This commercial success drove wild-harvest to the point that *H. canadensis* was severely depleted before CITES protection — a conservation failure that the pharmaceutical industry's reliance on wild-harvest had produced.

**The golden color and liver tradition:**
The doctrine of signatures proposed golden-yellow plants for liver and jaundice — "yellow for yellow." "Jaundice root" as a historical name for goldenseal encodes this reasoning. The mechanism — berberine's bile duct choleretic effect (increased bile flow) and potential anti-hepatitis viral activity — partially validates the observation, though the specific jaundice-treating claim for goldenseal has limited clinical support beyond the signature reasoning.

**Over-harvesting as conservation crisis:**
Goldenseal's wild-harvest story is one of the most egregious examples of commercially-driven botanical conservation failure in North American pharmacy. The plant's long rhizome maturation period (3–5 years to first harvest), shade-requirement (obligate understory species), and slow seed-spread made it uniquely vulnerable to commercial over-collection. By the 1990s, wild goldenseal populations in many states were functionally depleted. CITES Appendix II listing (1997) required trade permits but did not prevent continued harvest in the US (domestic trade unregulated by CITES). Sustainable cultivation programs now exist; prescribers bear ethical responsibility to specify cultivated origin.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Preparations and doses (goldenseal herb):**
- **Dried rhizome/root powder:** 0.5–1 g 3× daily; for mucous membrane indications (respiratory, digestive, urogenital)
- **Tincture (1:5, 60% ethanol):** 2–4 mL 3× daily; standard Eclectic preparation
- **Fluid extract (1:1, 60% ethanol):** 0.5–2 mL 3× daily
- **Topical wash/compress:** 5 g herb decoction per 250 mL; cool to room temperature; eye wash, wound wash, vaginal douche (for specified conditions)

**Berberine (isolated alkaloid) doses — evidence-based:**
- Type 2 diabetes: 500 mg 3× daily (total 1500 mg/day); take with meals to reduce GI irritation
- Dyslipidemia: 500 mg 2–3× daily
- Infectious diarrhea (acute): 400–800 mg single dose or 400 mg QID for 3 days (cholera adjunct only with ORS)

**Commission E:** No approved monograph for *H. canadensis* specifically; berberine preparations separately considered in some national pharmacopoeias.

**CONTRAINDICATIONS — CRITICAL:**
- **Pregnancy: ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION** — hydrastine is a powerful uterotonic; berberine crosses placenta (demonstrated in animal studies); multiple reports of uterine contractions and miscarriage; hydrastine has been used historically as an abortifacient; **no evidence of safety in any trimester**
- **Neonatal jaundice risk:** Berberine competes with bilirubin for albumin binding → increased free bilirubin → risk of neonatal kernicterus; AVOID in lactating mothers and neonates
- **Severe hepatic impairment:** berberine metabolism hepatic; accumulation risk
- G6PD deficiency: berberine may precipitate hemolytic anemia (case reports)

**Drug interactions (berberine — critical):**
- **CYP3A4 inhibition:** berberine inhibits CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein → increased plasma levels of substrates (cyclosporine, tacrolimus — organ transplant drugs; clarithromycin; metformin); monitor closely
- **CYP2D6 inhibition:** berberine inhibits CYP2D6 → caution with codeine, tamoxifen, certain antidepressants/antipsychotics (fluoxetine, aripiprazole)
- **Anticoagulants (warfarin):** berberine may increase anticoagulant effect; monitor INR
- **Hypoglycemics/insulin:** additive glucose-lowering → hypoglycemia risk; monitor blood glucose
- **Metformin:** pharmacokinetic interaction via MATE1/OCT transporters; monitor for metformin toxicity (lactic acidosis risk at high combined doses)

**Conservation prescribing note:**
Specify: "Organically cultivated *Hydrastis canadensis* — not wild-harvested." If berberine is the primary therapeutic target (diabetes, dyslipidemia, diarrhea), consider equivalent berberine content from sustainably grown *Berberis* or *Coptis* species, which are less conservation-sensitive. Reserve *Hydrastis*-specific prescribing for indications requiring the hydrastine component (hemostasis, specific mucous membrane toning with vasoconstrictive element).
