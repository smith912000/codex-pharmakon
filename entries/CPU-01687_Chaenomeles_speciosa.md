# Chaenomeles speciosa — Seyab, bse yab (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary); "Tibetan quince"

## *Chaenomeles speciosa, as the binomial printed in the scientific-name run of the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary, and set there against "Seyab", "bse yab" and the English "Tibetan quince" by column position only; Clark 1995 prints bse.yab under Chaenomeles tibetica and Chaenomeles lagenaria, and not under this binomial* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under this binomial only by the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. Both Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The extraction note reads: "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 353). The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (lines 5717 to 5760).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "S" block. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. The phonetic names include "Sewai metok" (line 5715) and then "Seyab / Shelta Shhimthikle Shhu khen / Shingkun" (lines 5717 to 5719). The Tibetan transliterations follow: "bse yab / shel ta / zhim thig le / zhu mkhan / shing kun" (lines 5721 to 5725). The scientific names are in a later run (lines 5727 to 5765), where several lines hold more than one name. Counting from the end of the run, "Ferula asafetida" (line 5765) falls against "Shingkun", "Symplocus paniculata" (line 5764) against "Shhu khen", "Rabdosia ragosa" (line 5758) against "Shhimthikle", "Pinus sp." (line 5758) against "Shelta", and "Chaenomeles speciosa" (line 5757) against "Seyab". The English column prints "Tibetan quince" (line 5760) in the same place. Clark's own gloss for this drug in chapter 21 reads "Tibetan quince [bSe.yab ...]" (line 1215, below), so the English name agrees across the two texts. The count rests on order and not on rows, and the Codex reads the glossary as pairing "Seyab" with *Chaenomeles speciosa* by column position, not as a printed row. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* Three places name Seyab. First, in the taste-group lists, Seyab is in the sour group: "Sedu, larbu, Seyab, Kyurura, Gyashug, Dhatig, curd, buttermilk, chhang, yeast and other similar substances are the group of medicinal substances having a sour taste" (the sentence runs from line 3205 to line 3209; the lines between are the page number and running head). Second, in the efficacy passage of chapter 20 (line 3394): "Seyab cures baekan and hot disorders." Third, in the compounding groups of chapter 21 (line 3614): "Seyab, Manu, Wosu, Tarbu, Upel, Sedu, Gakya and Kyurura are the group of medicines that cure hot disorder associated with baekan." The printed index (line 7487) lists "Seyab 186,202,221,267". The text gives no Latin name in any of these passages, so reading the "Seyab" in them as the glossary's "Seyab" rests on the identical spelling. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, the same Tibetan name under other binomials.* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance". The entry in the materia medica run of the Explanatory Tantra (line 908) reads:

"bse.yab: Chaenomeles tibetica: Chaenomeles lagenaria: [The hKhrungs.dpe text says, “It is a large tree with a large leaf and a white flower. Its pods contain kernels similar to those of the Bengal Kino tree. It has sour and slightly sweet taste and is of nectarlike benefit in ear diseases. The leaf, trunk and flower resemble the apple tree. One type has a fruit similar to an unripe walnut and does not change color in autumn.”] It cures phlegm and fever."

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Everything in the brackets, including the quotation from the *hKhrungs.dpe* text, is the commentary layer. The root-text layer, as far as the brackets mark it, is the Tibetan name, the two Latin names (Clark's identifications and not the root text's) and the clause "It cures phlegm and fever". Two further Clark lines name the drug: the sour-taste group in chapter 19 (line 766) lists "Chaenomeles tibetica", and chapter 21 (line 1215) lists, among medicines that cure phlegm-fever combinations, "Tibetan quince [bSe.yab, Chaenomeles lagenaria (Loise)]". Clark prints no *Chaenomeles speciosa*. The Tibetan name is the same in the two texts and the binomials differ; the Codex does not choose between them.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Chaenomeles*, *bse.yab*, *Seyab* and "Tibetan quince" found no entry on this plant. I_1252 (*Chaenomeles tibetica*), written in this batch, is the sibling row on Clark's binomial for the same Tibetan name. I_538 (Papaya) names *mu gua* (*Chaenomeles*) in passing to separate the Chinese quince from the papaya, and is not an entry on it. I_1178 (*Rosa macrophylla*) quotes the phonetic names of this same glossary block ("Seyab" among them) to say that they do not include *Saegoe*, and is not an entry on this plant. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Chaenomeles* is a genus of the rose family (Rosaceae) that bears hard, sour, quince-like fruit, and that such fruit is generally known to be acidic. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained, and the Tibetan name stands against two or more binomials. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Men-Tsee-Khang text records a sour-tasting drug, "Seyab", that "cures baekan and hot disorders" (line 3394), and Clark records *bse.yab* as curing "phlegm and fever" (line 908). The commentary quoted by Clark adds that it is "of nectarlike benefit in ear diseases". These are the texts' claims and not Codex protocols, and they are attached to this binomial only by a column position. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** The taste is "sour" by the group list (Men-Tsee-Khang, lines 3205 to 3209; Clark chapter 19, line 766), and the commentary Clark quotes at line 908 says "sour and slightly sweet taste". The commentary is the bracketed layer and the group lists are the root-text layer, so the two are not the same claim. No power (hot or cool) and no post-digestive taste is printed for this drug in the lines read.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Tibetan texts class this drug by taste (sour) and by what it cures: "baekan and hot disorders" in the Men-Tsee-Khang text and "phlegm and fever" in Clark. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the passages cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a sour fruit-tree drug for phlegm and fever in the Tibetan texts, its Latin name varies between the two texts, and this binomial reaches it only through a glossary column. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. The identity rests on a glossary column position, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Fever that does not settle needs medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
