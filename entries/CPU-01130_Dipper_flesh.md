# Dipper flesh — rba.byi (Clark's transliteration), "White-throated Dipper" (Men-Tsee-Khang's English)
## *The flesh of the white-throated dipper as a remedy in the Tibetan Explanatory Tantra. Clark prints "rba.byi: Cinclus cinclus (a white-throated aquatic bird)"; the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints "White-throated Dipper Cinclus cinclus"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The text on disk is Barry Clark's 1995 *The Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). Chapter 20, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (line 792), says that "The power of medicines is twofold: (a) the power of taste and (b) the natural power [of the substance]" (line 794), and its "specific section" divides the individual substances into eight kinds, the eighth being "(8) animal products" (line 802). Clark's introduction explains his layout: "I have presented first the Tibetan name of each substance as a precise reference for scholars... Thereafter I have given each entry an English name where available and in almost all cases it is followed by at least one scientific name" and, where the identity of an animal product is in doubt, "the Tibetan names have been included in parentheses with the probable English identification" (line 83).

The dipper's entry is the last of a run of small creatures at the end of the animal section, after the heading "Droppings" (line 1198) and a list of insects and other small animals, and before the closing remarks on the "medicinal horses" (sugar and honey) that carry compounds. It reads (line 1202):

"chhu.byi (colloquial chhu.yer): Water rat (amphibious rodent with long snout and tail): rba.byi: Cinclus cinclus (a white-throated aquatic bird): Its flesh and that of water rat cure meat poisoning."

So the Tibetan name of the bird is printed as *rba.byi*, its scientific name as *Cinclus cinclus*, the translator describes it only as "a white-throated aquatic bird", and the action is that its flesh, and the water rat's, "cure meat poisoning". The text does not say whether the flesh is eaten fresh, dried or burnt. Clark's brackets mark interpolations from the commentaries (line 82); this entry has none, so its words are the text's own as Clark gives them. The headings in the printed text do not separate the dipper from the preceding "Droppings" block, and I have not guessed at a different grouping.

The second English text on disk, the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`), gives it as the end of a sentence (line 3573): "The meat of water birds and white-throated dippers cure meat poisoning." Its animal-group introduction reads, "There are thirteen medicinal substances derived from animals. They are the horn, bone, flesh, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, fur, urine, feces and the entire body of an animal" (line 3503), and its glossary lists "White-throated Dipper Cinclus cinclus" (line 6922). The Men-Tsee-Khang version pairs the dipper with "water birds" in general, where Clark pairs it with the water rat; I report the difference and do not reconcile it. [USER: verify]

Clark's introduction notes that "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57). The Men-Tsee-Khang preface says that "the use of animal products was negligible", that "animal products in Tibetan medicine have been completely substituted by equally effective herbal substances", and that their potencies are given "to preserve the understanding of the therapeutic values of animal products" (line 687).

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, bird flesh is muscle: protein with variable fat, iron-containing myoglobin and B vitamins. Nothing more specific can be said without a source.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text records the flesh of a small aquatic bird as a remedy for meat poisoning. The Codex does not recommend it. Meat poisoning (food poisoning from spoiled or contaminated meat) is treated today with fluids and medical care, the dipper is a wild protected bird in most places, and the tradition's own present-day practice, as its translators describe it, has moved to herbal substitutes.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives no preparation for the dipper. It does not say whether the flesh is eaten fresh, dried, ashed or taken in a drink, and the lines read give no quantity. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Eating more meat does not treat food poisoning from meat, and eating the flesh of a wild bird adds its own infection risk. Food poisoning is managed by rehydration and, in severe cases, medical treatment. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** None printed for this item. Chapter 20 says that medicines act by taste and by natural power, and lists eight powers (heavy, oily, cool, blunt, light, coarse, hot, sharp; Clark line 796). The dipper entry (line 1202) prints no taste, power or post-digestive taste. Clark's introduction says that the later *Crystal Orb* and *Crystal Rosary* give each medicine's tastes, potencies and qualities (line 57), but that text is not on disk, so none is supplied here. Clark's diet chapter (chapter 16) says of the meats it lists that they "are sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste", and gives powers by class, for instance that "The flesh of aquatic animals has oily, heavy and warm powers" (line 673). The dipper is not named in that chapter (a search of the text for "Cinclus" finds only line 1202), so the class qualities are not applied to it. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for the dipper's flesh in the lines read. Chapter 20 assigns it a single therapeutic action against meat poisoning, and says nothing about essence extraction (*bcud len*) or any other practice. The Men-Tsee-Khang preface records a Buddhist objection to causing harm to any being as the reason animal products have been replaced by herbs (line 687), which is the tradition's own comment on animal products in general and not a cultivation status for this one.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The dipper's flesh is one clause among small animals at the end of an animal list, and its tradition describes animal products as largely replaced. The honest use of the entry is as a record. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Meat poisoning:** food poisoning with severe vomiting, blood in the stool, fever or dehydration needs medical care; do not treat it with wild-bird flesh.
- **Hygiene:** wild birds can carry *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter* and avian influenza viruses, and raw or undercooked flesh is an infection risk.
- **Law and conservation:** the white-throated dipper is a wild songbird protected in most jurisdictions; the Codex does not recommend sourcing it.
- **Ethics:** the Codex does not recommend killing or harming wild animals for these uses, and the Tibetan translators themselves record a Buddhist objection to harming any being.
