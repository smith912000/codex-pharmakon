# Great willowherb, codlins and cream / Epilobium hirsutum — Lusimachion (Dioscorides 4-3); Onagra (4-118)

## *Epilobium hirsutum* (the Dioscorides translators' suggested identification, "Apple Pie, Codlins and Cream" in 4-3 and "Onagrade, Great Willowherb, Apple Pie, Codlins and Cream" in 4-118) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides.** This entry stands on one text, Dioscorides (DMM). It was added 2026-10-09 (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP).

**Identity note.** The binomial belongs to the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood) and not to Dioscorides. In 4-3 it is the first of two identifications: "*Lysimachia purpurea* [Fuchs], *Epilobium hirsutum* [Linnaeus] — Apple Pie, Codlins and Cream" and "*Lysimachia lutea* [Fuchs], *Lysimachia vulgaris* [Linnaeus] — Common Yellow Loosestrife", followed by "see 4-118". In 4-118 the SUGGESTED line reads "Onagra [Italian], *Epilobium hirsutum*" and is followed by "see 4-3". The two chapters describe two different shrubs and are cross-referenced by the translators only. The text of 4-3 (flowers "red or a golden colour", a marsh plant with willow-like leaves) can fit a loosestrife as well as a willowherb, so the identification is doubtful. *4-3 LUSIMACHION* (heading at line 8020; text from line 8024, interrupted by a page break and the plate captions "Polygonum bistorta after FAGUET — 1892" and "Lysimachia lutea from FUCHS — 1545"). The text: "Lysimachia sends out thin stalks a foot high (or even higher) at the joints of which thin leaves emerge, similar to those of the willow, astringent to the taste. The flowers are red or a golden colour. It grows in marshy places and near water. The juice of the leaves is astringent, and a liquid medicine of it, enema, or suppository, is good for throwing-up blood and dysentery. In a pessary it stops women's excessive menstrual discharges. The herb is effective stuffed in the nostrils for flows of blood. It is also a wound herb and staunches blood. The smoke (inhaled) has very sharp fumes so that it both drives away snakes, and kills flies. It is also called lytron." The chapter before it in the text is betony, which is also called astringent.

*4-118 ONAGRA* (heading at line 9085; text line 9088). The text: "Onagra is a good-sized shrub like a tree, with leaves like the almond tree, but broader and like those of the lily. The large flowers are like roses. The root is white and long, and dried gives off the smell of wine. It grows in hilly places. A dilution of the root taken as a drink by the wild living creatures is able to make them tame. Smeared on, it soothes wild ulcers. It is also called oenothera, or onuris." The claim that a root drink makes wild animals tame is Dioscorides' report, and he does not vouch for it.

The translation's index lists the binomial at pages 544 and 668 (index entry at line 15633), the pages of these two chapters.

### Active Components

Willowherbs (*Epilobium*) are generally known to be rich in tannins and other polyphenols, which accords with the astringency 4-3 states. The constituents of the plants named in the chapters were not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. For 4-3 Dioscorides records an astringent herb for bleeding (blood-spitting, dysentery, excessive menstrual flow, nosebleed) and for wounds, with a smoke against snakes and flies. For 4-118 he records a root that soothes "wild ulcers" and is reported to tame animals. These are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-3: the juice of the leaves, as a liquid medicine, enema or suppository; a pessary; the herb stuffed into the nostrils; the smoke inhaled. 4-118: the root smeared on ulcers. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Astringent herbs rich in tannins are generally known to tighten tissue and reduce small bleeding and secretions. This is a general statement and no study was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** 4-3 prints the leaves as "astringent to the taste" and the juice as "astringent". No warming, cooling or other Galenic quality is printed in either chapter.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. The chapters offer a discipline of attention: how to know the plant (thin stalks a foot high with willow-like leaves and red or golden flowers by water; or a shrub with almond-like leaves, large rose-like flowers and a white long root that smells of wine when dried), where it grows (marshy places; hilly places) and what it is called. They record no status for the person. The identification is a translators' gloss.

**Practitioner's note.** The practice in these chapters is recognition by habit and habitat, and the dry root's smell of wine as an assay. Nothing gives a cultivator a practice for the person. The identification is doubtful, so no use is recommended.

### Contraindications

No contraindication is printed in the chapters read. The text's own uses include a pessary for menstrual flow and an enema, so avoid in pregnancy; do not self-treat bleeding, which needs medical assessment. The identification is doubtful, so the plant should not be gathered on the strength of these chapters. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
