# Aristolochia griffithii — Bhaleka, ba le ka (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary by column position); "Indian birthwort"

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *The root and Explanatory Tantra (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, chapter 20).* In the chapter on the efficacy of medicinal substances (contents, line 225), in the run on herbal medicines, line 3423 reads: "Bhaleka cures hot disorders of the lungs, liver and vessel organs." The index (line 7267) gives "Bhaleka 205". That is the only sentence the text prints about Bhaleka. It gives no part used, no preparation and no taste.

*Clark 1995, chapter 20 (line 976).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`: "ba.le.ka: Aristolochia saccata Wall (a type of birthwort): Aristolochia contorta: Akebia quinata: Menispermum dahuricum: [has six names and is a twining shrub similar to clematis which has no fruit nor flower. It has bitter taste, coarse power and cures phlegm, painful infectious fever and blood disorders]. Birthwort also cures fevers of the lungs, liver and vessel organs." Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, so the description, taste, power and the three indications are the commentary layer, and the outside sentence agrees with the Men-Tsee-Khang line in its three organs. That *Bhaleka* is a form of *ba.le.ka* is a reading from the sound of the name and from that agreement. The text does not state it. [USER: verify]

**Identity: the glossary against Clark.** Clark prints four binomials for ba.le.ka (*saccata*, *contorta*, *Akebia quinata*, *Menispermum dahuricum*) and none is *griffithii*. The glossary prints *griffithii* and none of Clark's four. The Codex does not choose. The spine key follows the glossary; I_1234 follows Clark's second binomial. The row stands for the Tibetan drug and not for one species.

**Overlap.** **I_1234** (*Aristolochia contorta*, ba.le.ka, tag FMT) is the entry on the same Tibetan drug and the same two passages (Clark line 976, Men-Tsee-Khang line 3423), and it already names this glossary line and this spine row. The two entries are not merged here. **I_1011** (*Aristolochia clematitis*) and **I_1012** (*Aristolochia pistolochia*) are Dioscorides entries. **I_035** (Birthwort, *Aristolochia clematitis*) and **I_1373** (*Aristolochia indica*, Sushruta) are other traditions' birthworts. None is an entry on this binomial. No entry on *Aristolochia griffithii* or on "Indian birthwort" under a Tibetan tag was found beyond I_1234. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general, well-established fact that plants of the genus *Aristolochia* contain aristolochic acids, which are nephrotoxic and carcinogenic in humans. That is a statement about the genus and not a finding of the Tibetan texts, and the identity of the Tibetan drug is not settled between the genus's species and other families (Clark's *Akebia* and *Menispermum* are not birthworts). [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug, Bhaleka, that "cures hot disorders of the lungs, liver and vessel organs" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3423), and in Clark's commentary layer phlegm, painful infectious fever and blood disorders. These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. Because the genus *Aristolochia* contains aristolochic acids, and the identity of the drug is uncertain, the Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** the Men-Tsee-Khang line calls the disorders it cures "hot disorders" (line 3423) and prints no taste or potency for the drug. Clark's line 976 prints "bitter taste, coarse power" inside the square brackets (commentary layer), for ba.le.ka. No other quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Explanatory Tantra classes Bhaleka by what it does: in the Men-Tsee-Khang line, it cures hot disorders of the lungs, liver and vessel organs. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. The rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, "Maintaining Health through Rejuvenation", contents line 235, and Clark's chapter 23, lines 1264 to 1276) was read for the name and does not name Bhaleka or ba.le.ka, and no line read puts it among the essence-extraction (*bcud len*) ingredients. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a fever-curing drug in the Tibetan texts, its species is not settled, and the genus it may belong to is a known kidney and cancer hazard. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. Plants of the genus *Aristolochia* contain aristolochic acids, a general, well-established hazard to the kidneys and a cancer risk, and the Tibetan drug may or may not be such a plant. The botanical identity is uncertain, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Fevers of the lungs, liver and vessel organs need medical care.
