# Chalcitis — chalkitis, calcitis (Dioscorides, Osbaldeston and Wood 2000)
## *Chalcitis, a brass-coloured mineral of the copper workings; the edition glosses it "Calcite — Anhydrous Carbonate of Lime, Calcium Carbonate", but the text itself says it looks "like brass" and lists it with the mineral veins of a metal mound, so the identification is open* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP, AT, DMM, CM

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** 5-115 CHALKITIS (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10426-10428), under the book's "Metallic Stones" heading, glossed "SUGGESTED: Calcite — Anhydrous Carbonate of Lime, Calcium Carbonate". The whole chapter: "Calcitis is preferred which looks like brass, brittle, without stones, and is not old, and furthermore with somewhat long glittering veins. It is astringent, warming and scab forming, purging away filth around the eye corners and the eyes. It is one of those things that are mildly astringent. It is good (with the juice of a leek) for erysipela [streptococcal skin infection], herpes [viral skin infection], and discharges of blood from the womb and nostrils. The powder of it is good for repressing abnormal growths on the gums, and gangrenous ulceration of the cheeks and tonsils. Burnt, pounded into small pieces and mixed with honey, it is far more effective for eye medicines. It reduces and cleans rough eyelids that have grown hard, and put in like a suppository it takes away fistulas [ulcers]." Related chapters refer to it: 5-84 (cadmia) says that in the mound where cadmia is found "are found (as it were) some veins of chalcitis, misy, sory, melanteria, coeruleus, chrysocolla, calcanthus and diphryges" (line 10223); 5-106 (*cyanus*, "formed from brass minerals") is "burnt like chalcitis" (line 10379); 5-114 (*chalkanthon*, copper sulphate; see that entry) ends "It is burnt, as we will show [below] in calcitis" (line 10422); 5-116 *psorikon* is two parts calcitis to one of cadmia, pounded with vinegar and sealed in a jar covered in dung for forty days "in the hottest days" (lines 10429-10432); 5-117 *misy* "has the same strength as chalcitis [5-115], and is burnt the same way" (line 10437); and the alum chapter 5-123 says alums are "burnt and roasted like chalcitis" (line 10472). The edition also glosses chalcitis as calcium carbonate in these cross-references (for example at lines 10379 and 10431), though the "brass" colour and its place among metal-mound minerals point to a copper-bearing mineral. ### Active Components

If chalcitis is the brass-coloured copper-iron mineral the Greek text's colour and company suggest, it is a copper iron sulphide and, on roasting, yields copper and iron oxides and sulphur dioxide; if it is calcite as the edition glosses it, it is calcium carbonate. The text does not settle which, and so no composition is asserted here for the material Dioscorides described.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. In the sources the substance is an astringent mineral for the eyes, mouth and wounds, burnt and powdered, plus a cross-reference for how other minerals are roasted. It is not a cultivation substance and, if copper-bearing, it is toxic to swallow or to put in the eye. The Codex offers no protocol, dose or preparation.

### Preparation & Dosing

DMM 5-115: the powder is used on the gums and ulcerated cheeks and tonsils; "Burnt, pounded into small pieces and mixed with honey" for eye medicines; with leek juice for erysipelas and herpes; as a suppository for fistulas. The method of burning is not given in this chapter, which is referred to by 5-106, 5-114, 5-117 and 5-123. 5-116 gives a preparation of *psorikon* from it with cadmia. No quantity is given for any use. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Copper minerals and their roasting products are irritant and toxic, and calcium carbonate is an antacid and a mild abrasive; which applies depends on the unsettled identification. Roasting sulphide minerals releases sulphur dioxide. No clinical studies were retrieved for the traditional uses. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here for a cultivator to take or do. Chalcitis is, in the one text that treats it, an astringent mineral for eyes, gums and ulcers, whose identification is uncertain even in the edition that prints it. The honest lesson is the assay discipline: know exactly what the material is before using it, and where that cannot be known, do not. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

If the material is copper-bearing, it is toxic and corrosive; it must not be put in the eye, swallowed or placed in the vagina or rectum as the Greek suppository uses describe. Roasting sulphide minerals releases irritant and toxic fumes. Because the identity of Dioscorides' chalcitis is uncertain, modern materials sold under the name cannot be assumed to be the same thing. Purchase and use of traditional mineral preparations are regulated in many places.
