# Thladiantha harmsii — gser.gyi.phud.bu, Sergyi phuebu (rGyud-bzhi; Clark 1995 prints Thladiantha harmsii Cogn., the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary sets Luffa aegyptiaca against the name by position)

## *Thladiantha harmsii Cogn., the only Latin name Clark 1995 prints for the Tibetan name gser.gyi.phud.bu (line 900); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary sets a different gourd-family binomial, Luffa aegyptiaca, against "Sergyi phuebu" by column position (line 5745), and that reading has its own entry, I_1311. This entry stands for Clark's reading of the same Tibetan drug* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta text folders on disk for *Thladiantha* found no hit. The row was added from a printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 57) the row is keyed by the **Tibetan name** *gser.gyi.phud.bu*, with the identification "Thladiantha harmsii Cogn" and the locus "Clark 1995 (line 900)". The string prints a single Latin name, so the spine's binomial is neither a first nor a later one of several; it is the only name Clark gives. The Men-Tsee-Khang binomial for the same Tibetan name, *Luffa aegyptiaca*, has its own row (names.csv line 351, from glossary line 5745) and its own entry, **I_1311**, which reads the same Clark and Men-Tsee-Khang lines from the other side. The two entries are one Tibetan drug under two binomials, one from each edition. This is the point the batch note raised (that CPU-01801 is Clark's binomial for the drug of I_1311).

**Identity doubt.** The two editions disagree on the plant. Clark gives *Thladiantha harmsii*; the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary gives *Luffa aegyptiaca* ("Sponge gourd") by position. Both are gourd-family genera as the Codex understands it (not checked against a flora). Which plant the Tibetan texts meant, or whether the two editors identified two different plants under one name, cannot be told from the lines read. Clark's preface says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" (line 83) and that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect" (line 68). The Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note (line 686) says that its botanical terms rest on the information of its own Materia Medica Department. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (lines 900 to 903).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792). Clark's list of the eight sections (line 802) names "(6) plants"; the text read prints no "(6)" heading, and the entry stands between "(5) Resins" (line 879) and "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). It follows *gser.gyi.me.tog* (line 899) and precedes *dug.mo.nyung* (line 904). A page number (93) is printed at line 901, in the middle of the entry. The entry reads:

"gser.gyi.phud.bu: Thladiantha harmsii Cogn: [has a fruit similar to a headless sbur.wa beetle. It has bitter taste, coarse power, and cures poisoning, phlegm-bile combinations and has an emetic action. This plant is also a creeper with a lined, green petiole and (fruit-) stalk and yellow flower resembling an acorn. The superior type has a bitter, black fruit with coarse power, whilst the common one has a yellow fruit. Both types] act as an emetic in bile disorders."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Almost all of the entry is therefore the commentary layer: the beetle-like fruit, the taste and power, the poisoning and phlegm-bile uses, the creeper's description and the two types. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the Latin name, which is Clark's identification and not the root text's, and the single clause "act as an emetic in bile disorders". The line gives no part used (the "fruit" and the "fruit-stalk" belong to the description) and no preparation. Clark's name index lists *gser.gyi.phud.bu* (line 1768) and his Latin index lists *Thladiantha harmsii* (line 1652).

*Clark 1995, chapter 21, the emetics (line 1224).* The compounding chapter ("The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines", heading at lines 1205 and 1206) names the drug under its Latin name only: "The class of emetics contains pericarp of Soapnut, Hill Dockleaf, Thistle (Cirsium sp.), Sweet Flag Root, Thladiantha harmsii Cogn., Fragaria nilgeerensis (a type of mock strawberry), seed of Sophora moorcroftiana, Pyrola incarnata and mustard seed." The line gives no Tibetan name, so the link to *gser.gyi.phud.bu* is Clark's own: the same binomial and the same emetic role.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3392).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225; colophon at line 3583), in the run that begins "The efficacy of these medicinal substances for healing disorder is as follows" (line 3380). Line 3392 reads, in the middle of a longer passage: "Sergyi metok cures hot disorders of the tripa and the vessel organs. Sergyiphuebu acts as emetic and bring up tripa disorders." ("Sergyiphuebu" is the OCR's joined spelling.) The index lists "Sergyi phuebu 202" (line 7485), and the page number 202 is printed at line 3395. Clark's "emetic action" and "emetic in bile disorders" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "acts as emetic and bring up tripa disorders" agree on the emetic. They are two translators' wordings, and the Codex does not treat "bile disorders" and "tripa disorders" as one term. The running text gives no Latin name, no taste and no part used.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "S" block).* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate runs. Four consecutive phonetic names are "Sergyi metok" (transliteration "gser gyi me tog", line 5707), "Sergyi phuebu" ("gser gye phud bu", line 5709), "Ser kue" ("gser skud", line 5713) and "Sewai metok" ("se ba'i me tog", line 5715). In the scientific run the matching four are "Herpetospermum pedunculosum Golden flower" (line 5743), "Luffa aegyptiaca" (5745), "Lethariella semanderi" (5749) and "Rosa branonii" (5753); the English names are "Golden flower", "Sponge gourd" (5747), "Golden dodder" (5751) and "Musk rose" (5755). Counted in order, "Sergyi phuebu" is the second of the four, as are *Luffa aegyptiaca* and "Sponge gourd". The glossary does not print *Thladiantha* at all. The neighbours fit the Tibetan names: Clark prints *gser.gyi.me.tog* with *Herpetospermum caudigerum* as the second of its two binomials (line 899). The pairing is by position. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** **I_1311** (*Luffa aegyptiaca*, tag FMT) is the entry for the same Tibetan drug under the Men-Tsee-Khang binomial; the two are not merged. **I_1280** (*Fragaria nilgeerensis*) and **I_1360** (*Sophora moorcroftiana*) quote Clark's emetic class (line 1224) for their own plants. A search of the entries folder for *Thladiantha*, *Sergyi phuebu* and *gser.gyi.phud.bu* found no other entry. I_824 (Loofah, *Luffa cylindrica*, tags SBJ and SS) and I_594 (Ridge gourd, *Luffa acutangula*, tags CM, SBJ and SS) are *Luffa* entries of other species from other traditions, and neither cites the Tibetan texts.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Thladiantha* belongs to the gourd family (Cucurbitaceae) and that many members of the family are bitter, the bitterness often owing to the triterpenoid cucurbitacins. Whether that applies to the Tibetan drug is not shown by the lines read: Clark's commentary calls the plant bitter with "coarse power", and which plant is meant, and which part, is not settled. No statement about this drug's constituents is made. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record an emetic: a drug that Clark says acts "as an emetic in bile disorders" (line 903), whose commentary adds uses against poisoning and "phlegm-bile combinations", and that Men-Tsee-Khang says "acts as emetic and bring up tripa disorders" (line 3392). These are the texts' claims in their own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and nothing in this entry should be taken by mouth. Inducing vomiting is not a safe self-treatment, and a bitter gourd-family drug described with "coarse power" should not be tried on the strength of a materia medica line.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's compounding chapter places the drug in the class of emetics (line 1224) and gives no recipe. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

It prints no post-digestive taste. The Men-Tsee-Khang lines read (3392, 5709) print no taste, power or post-digestive taste for *Sergyi phuebu*.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it is an emetic in bile disorders, and chapter 21 lists it among the emetics. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names (*gser.gyi.phud.bu*, "Sergyi phuebu", *Thladiantha*) found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in Men-Tsee-Khang's rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23, colophon at line 3950) or in Clark's chapter 23, "Normal health" (lines 1264 to 1276), and the herb entries of Clark's chapter 20 that mention essence extraction (lines 971, 1043, 1078, 1103, 1121 and 1144) do not include it. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is an emetic with no preparation and no dose, and its Latin name differs between the two editions. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug beyond the commentary's bitterness and "coarse power". It is an emetic, and inducing vomiting can be dangerous: it can cause dehydration and aspiration, and it is not appropriate for poisoning, pregnancy or weak persons without medical advice. The identity is open between two genera, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. It is a general fact that many bitter gourd-family plants are toxic in quantity. No conservation information was read. [USER: verify]
