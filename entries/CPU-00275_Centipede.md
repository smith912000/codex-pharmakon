# Centipede (Wu Gong) — skolopendra (Dioscorides) / Sata-padi, Sata-padaka (Bhishagratna, Kalpasthana ch.8) / 吳公 Wu Gong (project Shennong list)
## *The centipede, a many-legged venomous arthropod; no text on disk prints a binomial, Dioscorides' skolopendra is glossed as a sea creature ("Sea Centipedes, Millepedes"), and the Shennong row gives the Chinese name only* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM, SBJ, SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** 2-16 SKOLOPENDRA (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 4979-4981; `extract/names.csv` row for line 4979), glossed by the edition "Myriapoda, Cheilopoda — Sea Centipedes, Millepedes". The whole chapter: "The sea scolopendra boiled in oil and rubbed on removes hair [depilatory], but when touched it breeds itching." That is a sea animal, and the edition itself puts "Sea Centipedes" and "Millepedes" together, so the chapter is not the land centipede of the Chinese and Sanskrit entries. The land creature occurs as something to be treated: the unripe fig with wild poppy leaves "applied with ervum [2-129, 2-131] and wine ... good against the bites of rodents, spiders, centipedes and millipedes" (1-185, line 4883); asphodel seed and flowers "(taken in a drink of wine) are an extraordinary remedy against the strikes of millipedes, centipedes and scorpions" (2-199, line 6300); and salt "for the bites of centipedes or millipedes with honey and vinegar" (5-126, line 10492). A separate chapter, 2-37 KOUBARIDES (lines 5088-5091), is glossed "Oniscus asellus — Common Woodlouse", 'Millipede' [old English]; creatures "that are found under water vessels ... with many feet that roll themselves up round when they are touched", taken in wine "help painful urination and yellow jaundice", rubbed on with honey help tonsillitis, and warmed in a pomegranate rind with rosaceum are dropped in the ears for earache. That is a woodlouse and is not claimed as the centipede. The tag therefore rests on the sea scolopendra chapter and the sting remedies. [USER: verify]

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** No Avicenna locus was found. `_sources/avicenna.csv` has no centipede row (its animal rows are coral, locust, Spanish fly and musk) and no Avicenna text is on disk. No *Canon* chapter reference exists to give. The attestation rests on the spine row. [USER: verify]

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`) lists "Animals: Superior Class", "Middle Class" and "Inferior Class" in its contents (lines 770-776), but the text layer is readable only to PDF p.104 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/README.md`), so no animal monograph could be read. `_sources/shennong.csv` has a row "吳公" (Wu Gong, line 346), Chinese characters only, with no pinyin gloss, binomial or class; the next row is 馬陸 (Ma Lu). That these characters name the centipede is the list's own assumption and is not shown in any page read. Nothing of what the Chinese text says of the centipede was read. [USER: verify]

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The centipede is a poisonous creature in *Kalpasthana* ch.8, "insects ... the measures, etc. to be adopted in cases of insect-bite" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/kalpa/SS_Kalpa_ch08.txt` lines 4-7). Insects are divided by temperament; the *Sata-padaka* (centipede) is listed among the twenty-four families of fiery, *Pittaja* temperament, whose poison "tends to derange and aggravate the bodily Pitta" (lines 30-52). The section on the *Sata-padi* (centipede) (lines 161-169 and following): "The S'ata-padi (centipede) species is divided into eight kinds, viz., the Parusha (rough), Krishna (black), Chitra (of variegated colours), Kapilika (tawny brown), Pitaka (yellow), Rakta (red), S'veta (white) and the Agni-prabha (resembling fire in virtue). A bite by any of these insects is attended with swelling, pain and a burning sensation in the heart. A bite by one of the Sveta or the Agni-prabha species is marked by all the aforesaid symptoms as well as by violent epileptic fits, an intolerable burning sensation and eruptions of white pustules." Treatment (lines 302-304): "An Agada composed of Kumkuma, Tagara, S'igru, Padmaka and the two kinds of Rajani, pasted with water, proves curative in the case of a bite by a S'ata-padi (centipede)." In *Kalpasthana* ch.3 the poison of the *Satapadi* is located "in the bristles (Suka) and the head" with several other creatures (`kalpa/SS_Kalpa_ch03.txt` lines 50-54). The centipede is not used as a drug in the lines read.

### Active Components

Centipede venom is a mixture of proteins, peptides, enzymes and small amine compounds that cause local pain, swelling and sometimes systemic effects; the bite of a large species can be serious. The dried whole animal also contains proteins and fats, but the sources read here treat the centipede as a poisoner and not as a drug, so the relevant composition is the venom.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. In the sources read the centipede is a hazard to be treated (Sanskrit, and the Greek sting remedies), a sea creature rubbed on to remove hair (Greek 2-16), and an unread Chinese entry. Nothing here supports taking or applying a centipede. The Codex offers no protocol, dose or preparation, and gives no first-aid instruction: a centipede bite is a medical matter.

### Preparation & Dosing

Only preparations the texts give. DMM 2-16: the sea scolopendra boiled in oil and rubbed on. SS: no preparation of the centipede; the preparation is an *agada* of *Kumkuma*, *Tagara*, *Sigru*, *Padmaka* and the two *Rajani*, pasted with water, for the bite (ch.8 lines 302-304). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Centipede bites are painful and usually local; large tropical species can cause more serious illness, and treatment today is supportive. The Greek depilatory claim for a sea animal and the Sanskrit herbal pastes have no established basis. No clinical studies were retrieved for the traditional uses. [USER: verify]

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed.* The centipede is claimed by the toxicology of *Kalpasthana* as a venomous creature, graded by kind and treated, and not as a substance of *rasayana* or *vajikarana* (no centipede occurs in the lines of *Chikitsasthana* ch.27-30 searched). No *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* claim is printed for it.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here for a cultivator to take or do. The centipede is, in the sources read, a hazard whose poison is graded and treated. If the sources offer a discipline, it is the Sanskrit one: know the creature and its kinds, and see a physician for a bite. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Centipede bites can be painful and, in children, people with allergy and with large species, dangerous; dried centipede preparations are unstandardised and may be adulterated or contaminated, and venom allergy is possible. They are not safe to take. Wild collection and trade of arthropods may be regulated in some places; the status of any particular species has not been checked here. [USER: verify]
