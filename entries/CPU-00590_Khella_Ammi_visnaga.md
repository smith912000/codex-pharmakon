# Khella — Toothpick Weed, Bishop's Weed, Visnaga, Pick-Tooth

## *Ammi visnaga* (L.) Lam. | Family: Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)

**Tradition Tags:** EP, AT, DMM, CM, SS

**Energetic Classification:** Warming, Dry (2nd degree) — opening, deobstruent, antispasmodic

**Training Phase:** Tempering — an agent that widens narrowed channels and steadies contractile tissue under load

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ebers (EP):** The Nile Delta is the botanical homeland of *Ammi visnaga*, and the plant belongs to the Egyptian pharmacopoeic tradition preserved in the corpus of pharmaceutical papyri. Egyptian folk medicine, in a lineage claimed to reach back to pharaonic practice, used decoctions of the ripe fruits (schizocarps) for painful urination and for the passage of gravel and stones — the "spasm of the ureter" that khellin was later shown to relax. The dried compound umbels, with their stiff radiating rays, were used as literal toothpicks, which is the origin of both the English "toothpick weed" and the persistence of the plant in household use across North Africa.

**Assyrian (AT):** Cuneiform medical tablets from the Mesopotamian tradition record umbelliferous seeds among carminative and "wind-releasing" remedies for colic and gripping abdominal pain. Attribution of a specific tablet to *Ammi visnaga* is philologically uncertain — Akkadian plant names rarely map cleanly onto modern species — so this entry treats the Assyrian tag as a general umbellifer-for-colic lineage rather than a pinned identification.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** In *De Materia Medica*, Dioscorides describes *ammi* among the warming, diuretic, seed-drugs of the carrot family, prescribed for gripes, for promoting urine, and applied with honey. The classical materia medica did not distinguish the modern species with pharmacological precision; the Greek *ammi* is a cluster of related apiaceous seeds valued for opening obstruction and moving stagnant fluid — the same functional signature that modern chemistry traces to the chromone and furanochromone content.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM):** In the *Canon of Medicine*, the Unani tradition placed warming, drying umbellifer seeds in the class of *muhallil* (resolvent) and *mufattih* (deobstruent) agents that open the passages, break stones, and relieve the spasm of hollow organs. Khella persisted in Unani practice for renal colic and for "tightness" of the chest — a striking anticipation of its dual coronary and ureteral smooth-muscle actions.

**Sushruta (SS):** Ayurvedic and allied South Asian practice absorbed *Ammi* seeds (as *yavani*-adjacent umbellifers) into the deep*ana*/dipana carminative group for *vata* obstruction, colic, and difficult urination. As with the other classical traditions, the identification is functional rather than strictly botanical.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Khella is the archetype of the *opener of narrowed passages*. Every tradition that used it reached for the same image — a plant that relieves the cramp of a hollow tube, whether ureter, bronchus, or the coronary vessel feeding the heart. This is not coincidence: the same furanochromone chemistry that relaxes ureteral smooth muscle to let a stone pass also relaxes bronchial and coronary smooth muscle. The doctrinal signature — "that which lets what is stuck move through" — maps with unusual fidelity onto a real, unified pharmacology of smooth-muscle relaxation and channel dilation. In the Kenetica frame, khella is the substance of *patency*: the restoration of flow through a channel that spasm has closed.

### Active Components

The pharmacologically defining constituents of *Ammi visnaga* are furanochromones, principally **khellin** and **visnagin**, concentrated in the ripe fruit. These are the historical lead structures for an entire lineage of synthetic drug development: khellin's chromone scaffold informed the design of **sodium cromoglycate (cromolyn)**, the mast-cell-stabilising antiasthmatic, and the benzofuran/chromone medicinal-chemistry tradition that surrounds **amiodarone**. A second key class is the pyranocoumarins, above all **visnadin** (and the related samidin/dihydrosamidin), which carry much of the coronary-vasodilator and calcium-modulating activity. The plant also contains **khellol glucoside**, flavonoids, and a volatile-oil fraction. Furanochromones and pyranocoumarins together account for the antispasmodic, coronary-dilating, bronchodilating, and diuretic-lith-passing profile attested across traditions.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Khella is a Tempering-phase agent for the *contractile channels* — the smooth-muscle tubes whose spasm blocks flow. Its training use is framed around three channel systems: coronary (steadying the vessel that feeds the working heart), bronchial (widening the airway under respiratory load), and urinary (relaxing the ureter to let gravel clear). The Kenetica reading is that khella teaches a tissue to hold *open tone* rather than clenching — patency under stress rather than reflexive constriction. It is not a stimulant and not a sedative; it is a resolver of spasm, deployed when the presenting pattern is "the channel has closed and flow must be restored." It pairs conceptually with breath and cardiovascular conditioning work rather than with acute exertion.

Khellin has a narrow tolerability window: doses used in mid-20th-century angina trials frequently produced nausea, vomiting, and dose-limiting side effects, which is a principal reason it was superseded by better-tolerated synthetic drugs. Treat all doses as requiring professional verification.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:

- Bagouri (1949), *Journal of Pharmacy and Pharmacology* — early demonstration of the coronary vasodilator action of the crystalline principles (khellin/visnagin, "visammin") of *Ammi visnaga*, documenting the effect that underlay its mid-century use in angina pectoris. DOI: [https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2042-7158.1949.tb12396.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2042-7158.1949.tb12396.x)
- Abukhalil et al. (2021), *Journal of Biochemical and Molecular Toxicology* — in a rat model, visnagin protected against isoproterenol-induced myocardial injury by attenuating oxidative stress and inflammation (lowering CK-MB, LDH, troponin-I, ROS, MDA, NF-κB p65, TNF-α, IL-6) and upregulating Nrf2/HO-1 and PPARγ signalling. DOI: [https://doi.org/10.1002/jbt.22906](https://doi.org/10.1002/jbt.22906)

**Evidence grade:** *Moderate-historical for the coronary/antispasmodic pharmacology of the isolated compounds; preclinical for the modern cardioprotective mechanism.* The vasodilator and antispasmodic actions of khellin/visnagin are genuinely established (and drove real drug development — cromolyn's chromone lineage, and the chromone/benzofuran medicinal-chemistry space around amiodarone). Modern controlled clinical trials of the whole herb are sparse; recent mechanistic work is animal/in-vitro. Khellin's own clinical use faded largely because of poor tolerability, not lack of effect.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Unani/Galenic:** Warm and dry (≈2nd degree); *mufattih* (deobstruent), *muhallil* (resolvent), *mudir-e-baul* (diuretic), antispasmodic of the hollow organs.
- **Ayurvedic (functional, as an umbellifer seed):** *Rasa* — pungent/bitter (katu/tikta); *Virya* — heating (ushna); *Vipaka* — pungent (katu); action *vata-kapha* reducing, dipana/deepana (kindling), mutrala (diuretic).
- **TCM analogue:** No canonical *Ben Cao* entry; by function it sits with warm, acrid, qi-moving/channel-opening seed-drugs that "free the water passages" and relax gripping pain.

### Contraindications

Khellin and visnagin are **furanochromones and pyranocoumarins — photosensitising**. Ingestion or topical use can markedly increase sensitivity to UV light; the related compound in the same plant family (and khellin itself) has been used deliberately with UV in vitiligo phototherapy, which flags the real risk of phototoxic burns and pigmentation change with sun exposure. Prolonged or high-dose use has been associated with **nausea, vomiting, appetite loss, dizziness, and reversible cholestatic/pseudo-allergic hepatic effects and elevated liver enzymes**. Avoid in pregnancy and lactation (insufficient safety data; smooth-muscle activity). Caution with **hepatic impairment**. Potential additive effects with **calcium-channel blockers, nitrates, and other vasodilators/antihypertensives**, and theoretical interaction with photosensitising drugs. *Ammi visnaga* must not be confused with the closely related, highly toxic *Ammi majus* or with poison hemlock relatives — misidentification within the Apiaceae is dangerous. Do not self-treat cardiac or renal-colic symptoms with this herb; these are conditions requiring medical assessment.
