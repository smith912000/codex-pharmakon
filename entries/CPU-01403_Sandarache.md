# Sandarache — Sandarache (as printed in the chapter heading), "sandarac" in the translator's gloss, Dioscorides 5-122
## *A red, brittle, sulphur-smelling mineral found in the same mines as arsenicum (5-121), roasted as arsenicum is; the translator's suggestion names a tree gum ("Sandarac — Arar Tree, Sandarach Tree Callitris quadrivalvus, Thuya articulata") and then adds that 5-121 shows it is a mineral, not a gum* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-122, heading "SANDARACHE" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 10461; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 895, "Sandarac — Arar Tree, Sandarach Tree Callitris quadrivalvus, Thuya articulata — Juniper GumTree", class mineral; the book index sends the reader to page 805, line 12485, and lists "Sandarach Tree 805", line 17381). The whole chapter was read. Under the heading the translator prints "SUGGESTED: Sandarac — Arar Tree, Sandarach Tree Callitris quadrivalvus, Thuya articulata — Juniper GumTree" (line 10462) and then a note: "5-121 indicates however, this sandarac is a mineral, not a gum." (line 10463). The text itself (line 10464) reads:

"Sandarac is most highly valued which is fully red, brittle, easily pounded into small pieces, and pure — looking like cinnabar [5-109] in colour, and also having a sulphurous smell. It has the same properties and method of roasting as arsenicum. Used with rosin it heals loss of hair [alopecia], and with pitch it removes leprous nails. It is good with oil for lice infestations, and with grease it dissolves small swellings. It is also good with rosaceum for ulcers in the nostrils and mouth, and for other pustules [1-53], as well as for venereal warts. It is given with mead [honey wine] to those who spit up rotten matter, and it is made into an inhalant with rosin for old coughs, the smoke drawn through a funnel into the mouth. Licked in with honey it clears the voice, and it is given with rosin in a catapotion [pill] to the asthmatic."

The chapter therefore gives: a rule for choosing the best (fully red, brittle, easily pounded, pure, the colour of cinnabar, with a sulphurous smell); a statement that its properties and its roasting are those of arsenicum; and a run of uses, each tied to a vehicle (rosin, pitch, oil, grease, rosaceum, mead, honey). The bracketed [1-53] in the printed text is the translator's cross-reference for rosaceum (the rose oil of book 1), not a reference to a use of sandarac.

**The pointer to 5-121.** The translator's note sends the reader to the arsenicum chapter, *ARSENIKON* 5-121 (line 10456). That chapter is headed "POISONOUS" and the translator's suggestion is "Orpiment — Yellow Arsenic, Realgar — Arsenic disulphide". It opens: "Arsenicum is found in the same mines as sandaracha [?] [5-122]. The best is crusty, gold in colour and with crusts like fish scales (as it were) lying one over another, and it is not mixed with any other material." (line 10459). It continues that there is a second kind "in clumps and like sandaracha in colour", brought from Pontus and Cappadocia, and gives the roasting: "place it in a new ceramic jar, set it over burning coals and stir it continuously; and when it is red-hot and has changed colour, cool it, beat it finely, and put it in jars. It is antiseptic, astringent, and scab forming with a burning, strong, biting strength, and it is one of those medicines that repress abnormal growths and make the hair fall out." (line 10460). Since 5-122 says sandarac "has the same properties and method of roasting as arsenicum", these are the roasting method and the properties the chapter means.

**A tension in the text as printed.** 5-122 says sandarac heals loss of hair when used with rosin, while the properties it is said to share with arsenicum include making "the hair fall out" (line 10460). The chapters do not reconcile these, and nothing retrieved explains it; it is recorded here as printed, not resolved. [USER: verify]

**Identity.** The two source rows disagree. `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 895 gives the class as mineral, but `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 848 reads "Sandarache, Callitris quadrivalvus, plant, DMM V, no" (that is: plant, identification not flagged uncertain), which follows the tree-gum half of the translator's suggestion and conflicts with the translator's own note and with the chapter. The chapter's clues (red, brittle, easily pounded, cinnabar-coloured, sulphurous smell, found with arsenicum, roasted as arsenicum is) point to a mineral, and the translator accepts that. A red arsenic sulphide would fit the clues, but neither the chapter nor the translator's note says so for 5-122, and that reading is an inference of this entry, not a source statement. Which material Dioscorides meant is not settled by the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None can be stated from the chapter. The chapter names no composition. It ties sandarac to arsenicum by shared mines, shared properties and a shared roasting, and it gives it a sulphurous smell. If the material is an arsenic sulphide mineral (an inference, see Identity), it would contain arsenic and sulphur, and arsenic compounds in general are toxic. If it were the tree gum named in the translator's suggestion, it would be a resin; the translator rules that out from 5-121. No composition is claimed for this entry.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records sandarac as an arsenicum-like mineral used with vehicles on skin, hair, nails, lice, warts, mouth and nose ulcers, and, taken by mouth or inhaled as smoke, for coughs, the voice, the asthmatic and those who spit up rotten matter. Several of those uses involve swallowing or inhaling a material the text places beside a substance it labels poisonous, and it is probable (not stated by the chapter) that the material is an arsenic compound. The Codex supplies no modern protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations, each by its vehicle and without amounts (line 10464): with rosin for loss of hair; with pitch for leprous nails; with oil for lice; with grease for small swellings; with rosaceum for ulcers of the nostrils and mouth, other pustules and venereal warts; with mead for those who spit up rotten matter; an inhalant with rosin for old coughs, "the smoke drawn through a funnel into the mouth"; licked in with honey for the voice; and a pill (*catapotion*) with rosin for the asthmatic. The roasting is by reference to arsenicum: a new ceramic jar over burning coals, stirred continuously, taken off when red-hot and changed in colour, cooled, beaten fine and stored in jars (5-121, line 10460). No proportions, no quantities and no durations are given for any of these. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM).** The chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality for sandarac. It prints one rule of equivalence, "the same properties and method of roasting as arsenicum", and the arsenicum chapter prints the properties: "antiseptic, astringent, and scab forming with a burning, strong, biting strength", one of the medicines "that repress abnormal growths and make the hair fall out" (5-121, line 10460). Those are the qualities the chapter lends to sandarac by reference; no others are printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. What the herbal offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, sensory assay, adulteration detection), and this chapter shows a small part of it: the best sandarac is "fully red, brittle, easily pounded into small pieces, and pure", the colour of cinnabar, with a sulphurous smell, so it is judged by eye, by crumbling and by smell; and its place in the book is fixed by its companion, arsenicum, found in the same mines and roasted in the same way. The book does not say when it is gathered. The chapter assigns it no cultivation status, only therapeutic uses.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to take from it for cultivation beyond the discipline the chapter shows: judge a mineral by colour, brittleness and smell, and know what it is found beside. The text itself keeps it next to a substance it labels poisonous, and several of its uses (smoke drawn into the mouth, a pill, a lick with honey) are internal. It is not a recommended practice. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Probable arsenic compound:** the chapter ties sandarac to arsenicum (labelled "POISONOUS" in 5-121). Do not swallow, inhale as smoke or dust, or apply to broken skin or mucous membranes. Arsenic compounds are toxic and carcinogenic.
- **Inhalation:** the chapter's own funnel inhalant draws the smoke of a heated mineral into the mouth; this is dangerous and is not endorsed.
- **Unsettled identity:** the source rows disagree (mineral in names.csv, plant in dioscorides.csv). Nothing should be collected, bought or used on the strength of the name; the translator's note says the sandarac of this chapter is a mineral, not a gum, so a gum sold under the name "sandarac" is not the material the chapter describes.
- **Conflicting hair statements:** the text both heals and causes hair loss by the properties it names; do not use it for hair.
