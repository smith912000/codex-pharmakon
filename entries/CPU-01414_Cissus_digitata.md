# Wild grape / Cissus digitata — Oinanthinon (Dioscorides 1-56); Wine of Wild Grapes (5-14)

## *Cissus digitata* (the Dioscorides translators' suggested identification, "Wild Grape, Sorrel Vine", listed together with *Vitis labrusca*, "Wild Grapes") | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides.** This entry stands on one text, Dioscorides (DMM). It was added 2026-10-09 (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP). The text gives almost nothing about the plant itself, and this entry is short.

**Identity note.** The binomial belongs to the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood) and not to Dioscorides. In both chapters the SUGGESTED line reads "*Cissus digitata* — Wild Grape, Sorrel Vine *Vitis labrusca* — Wild Grapes", two plants side by side. The text speaks only of "the wild grape". The same translators also list *Cissus digitata* as an "other usage" for 2-210 KISSOS (ivy), for which their main identification is *Hedera helix* (line 6429, see below). Whether *Cissus digitata* is meant by any of these chapters is not established by the text, and the identification is doubtful. *1-56 OINANTHINON* (heading at line 3986; text line 3989). This chapter is in Book One's oils and ointments. The whole chapter: "Oenanthemum. Having dried the sweet-smelling shoots or buds of the wild grape, put it into oil of unripe olives and stir it around, churn it upside down and leave it so for two days. Afterwards strain it out and store it. It is astringent, equivalent to rosaceum [1-53], except it neither loosens nor softens the bowels. That which carries the smell of the shoots or buds is the most approved of." The oil is therefore a scented oil infused with the dried shoots or buds of the wild grape, and the quality printed is "astringent".

*5-14 Wine of Wild Grapes* (heading at line 9842; text line 9844). The whole chapter: "The black wine from the wild grape is astringent. It is good for flowing intestines and the stomach and many other things that require astringency and contraction."

*2-210 KISSOS* (heading at line 6426; the translators' "other usage" at line 6429). The chapter is about ivy ("Cissus has many differences ... three most particular kinds, for some is white, some black and another helix"), its main SUGGESTED identification is *Hedera helix*, and the translators' "other usage" lists *Cissus quadrangularis* ("Edible Stemmed Vine") and *Cissus digitata* ("Wild Grape, Sorrel Vine"). It states that "All cissus is sharp and astringent". The ivy uses of that chapter are the subject of I_136 and are not attributed to this row.

### Active Components

Grape-family plants are generally known to contain tannins and other polyphenols, which fits the astringency the text states. The constituents of the plant named in the chapters were not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records an astringent scented oil of the wild grape's shoots (1-56) and an astringent black wine of the wild grape for the intestines and the stomach (5-14). These are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

1-56: the dried shoots or buds of the wild grape are put in oil of unripe olives, stirred and churned, left for two days, strained and stored. 5-14: no preparation is given beyond "black wine from the wild grape". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Astringent plant materials rich in tannins are generally known to tighten tissue and reduce secretions. This is a general statement and no study was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** 1-56 prints "astringent", "equivalent to rosaceum [1-53], except it neither loosens nor softens the bowels". 5-14 prints "astringent".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. Chapter 1-56 offers a small discipline of attention: dry the shoots or buds, infuse, strain, and prefer the oil that carries the smell of the shoots. They record no status for the person.

**Practitioner's note.** The practice in these chapters is the infusion and the test by smell. Nothing gives a cultivator a practice for the person, and the identification is doubtful, so no use is recommended.

### Contraindications

No contraindication is printed in the chapters read. Astringent preparations are generally unsuited to constipation, and wine is a problem for anyone avoiding alcohol. The identification is doubtful, so the plant should not be gathered on the strength of these chapters. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
