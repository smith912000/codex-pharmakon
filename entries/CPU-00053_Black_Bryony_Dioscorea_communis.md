### Origin & Tradition

Black bryony is a vigorous twining vine of European hedgerows with glossy, heart-shaped leaves, strings of shining red berries in autumn, and a large, dark-skinned, fleshy tuberous root that gave it its country names — "black bryony," "lady's-seal." Despite the shared name, it is **botanically unrelated to white bryony** (*Bryonia alba*, a cucurbit); black bryony belongs to the yam family (*Dioscoraceae*) — indeed the genus *Dioscorea* is itself named for Dioscorides — and the two "bryonies" were grouped by the old herbalists by appearance and by their shared acridity, not by kinship. (Modern taxonomy moved it from *Tamus communis* to *Dioscorea communis*.)

Dioscorides and the classical-into-folk tradition treat both bryonies among the acrid, dangerous plants, valuing the root chiefly as a **powerful external counter-irritant and resolvent.** The grated fresh root, rubbed on the skin, produces redness, warmth and irritation — and on this basis it was a famous folk application for **bruises, chilblains, rheumatic and "cold" joint pains, and to disperse the discolouration of black eyes and bruises** ("a remedy for bruises" being its most enduring reputation). The irritant action draws blood to the surface (rubefacient/counter-irritant), the traditional logic for easing deep-seated cold pain.

Internally the root was regarded as an emetic and drastic purgative and was used as such in older practice, but its acridity and toxicity made internal use dangerous and it is essentially obsolete as an internal medicine. The **berries are toxic** and a recognised cause of childhood poisoning. Black bryony thus sits firmly in the specialist/external register: a useful traditional counter-irritant whose internal use is best left in the historical record.

**No internal home dose is given. The root and berries are acrid and toxic; only cautious external use has a traditional place, and even that irritates skin.**

- **Fresh root poultice/rub (external):** grated fresh tuber applied to intact skin over bruises/painful joints, removed before it blisters; a counter-irritant, used briefly and with care. [USER: verify traditional method; warn against application to broken skin.]

Safety framing: the raphides/saponins cause burning, blistering and inflammation on prolonged skin contact; ingestion causes oral/GI burning, vomiting and toxicity. Keep the attractive red berries away from children.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM / Galenic humoral degree:** Hot and Dry in a high degree — an acrid, biting, "drawing" plant; classed with the irritant/dangerous herbs and used to heat and draw the surface.
- **Folk-European mapping:** the bruise-and-chilblain counter-irritant; "lady's-seal"; grouped with white bryony by acridity despite unrelated botany.
- **Kenetica Qi/Shen tier:** ◉ **Qi** — on the acrid, surface-moving, counter-irritant axis (draw blood and disperse cold stagnation), used at the skin rather than internally.

### Contraindications

- **Broken/sensitive skin.** The irritant root blisters and inflames; do not apply to broken skin or leave on long.
- **Internal use — avoid.** Acrid emetic/purgative toxicity; obsolete and dangerous internally.
- **Children.** The red berries are an attractive, toxic poisoning hazard; the root is irritant.
- **Pregnancy — avoid.** Acrid/irritant plant with traditional drastic action; avoid medicinal use. [USER: verify.]
- **Allergy/sensitisation.** Contact dermatitis likely with repeated handling.
