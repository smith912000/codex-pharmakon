# Meadowsweet

## *Filipendula ulmaria* (L.) Maxim. | Family: Rosaceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM
**Energetic Classification:** Cool, dry, astringent-and-relaxing; descending; anti-febrile at the surface, binding in the gut
**Training Phase:** Consolidation — a settling and cooling agent; the herb you take to come down, not to go up

---

### Origin & Tradition

Meadowsweet stands to head height in wet ground — ditch, fen, damp meadow, riverbank — with pinnate leaves and a foaming, irregular cream-white cyme that smells of almond and antiseptic at once. It is common, unmistakable, and it is the most historically consequential plant in this volume.

**Nomenclature, and the fact that made the twentieth century.** The plant was long placed in ***Spiraea*** — as *Spiraea ulmaria* L. — before transfer to *Filipendula*. That obsolete genus name is fossilised inside the most-consumed drug in human history. **Aspirin** is *a-* (acetyl) + *-spir-* (*Spiraea*) + *-in* (conventional drug suffix). The whole global pharmacology of salicylates carries this plant's discarded name in its middle syllable. Salicylate chemistry was worked out across willow (*Salix*, which gave *salicin* its name) and meadowsweet together, and **salicylaldehyde was isolated from the flower buds of *Spiraea ulmaria* in the 1830s** — the standard account credits work of that decade, and this book flags the specific attribution and date as `[USER: verify]`, since no primary historical source was retrieved this run. What *is* verified here is the chemistry that made the isolation possible: **salicylaldehyde and methylsalicylate are the main salicylates of the inflorescence**, in both early and late blooming stages (Ložienė et al. 2023, *Plants*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants12020300)). The nineteenth-century chemists were not mistaken about where to look.

The English names carry the rest. **Meadowsweet** — probably *mead-sweet*, the herb used to flavour mead, rather than "sweet of the meadow." **Bridewort** — strewn at weddings, and woven into bridal garlands. **Queen of the Meadow** — its stature in wet grassland. It was a **strewing herb** of the first rank, valued above most others for the scent released when trodden. This matters chemically: the smell is the volatile salicylate fraction, so the strewing use is a use of the same compounds as the medicine.

**Northern & Central Europe (DMM) — the classical indications.** Four, consistently: **fever**, **pain**, **rheumatism**, and — the one that makes this entry — **stomach complaints**. Hyperacidity, heartburn, gastritis, "sour stomach," and gastric ulcer. The plant is one of the standard European folk antacids. Contemporary ethnopharmacological work restates the tradition's range: meadowsweet is described as used in traditional medicine for rheumatic, inflammatory and tumour-related disease, among others (Andonova et al. 2024, *Antioxidants*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox13101200)), and the genus is credited with antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, **gastroprotective**, immunomodulatory and anticancer properties (Istomina et al. 2026, *Curr Issues Mol Biol*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/cimb48060640)). A Russian ethnopharmacology group explicitly set out to test the traditional oncological use (Bespalov et al. 2018, *J Ethnopharmacol*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2018.08.013)).

**Slavic and Siberian usage (CM).** The plant survives as a **beverage**, not merely a drug — meadowsweet floral tea is a functional beverage across its range, and comparative work covers *F. ulmaria* alongside *F. camschatica*, *F. denudata* and *F. stepposa* as tea sources (Olennikov et al. 2016, *Molecules*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules22010016)). The daily-drinkable dose form is itself evidence about the plant's tolerability at traditional strength — and, as shown below, about the limits of that tolerability.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.**

Meadowsweet's doctrine is not a signature. Nobody looked at the flower and inferred a use. The doctrine here is **empirical and correct**: this plant reduces fever, pain, and rheumatic inflammation — and modern medicine agrees, because modern medicine took the molecule and sells it by the tonne. Fever/pain/rheumatism is one of the great vindications of folk indication in this book. There is nothing to deflate.

**The paradox is the entry.** Take the tradition's four indications and set the fourth against the other three:

- Meadowsweet contains salicylates. Salicylates are the parent chemistry of aspirin.
- **Aspirin ulcerates the stomach.** It is among the commonest causes of drug-induced gastric erosion and upper-GI bleed, by COX-1 inhibition, prostaglandin suppression, and loss of mucosal defence. This is not contested.
- Yet **the whole herb is traditionally a stomach remedy** — given *for* hyperacidity and ulcer, the exact injury its own signature molecule causes.

The tradition is asserting that a plant cures what its active constituent inflicts. Either the tradition is wrong, or **the plant is not its active constituent** — and the second is the interesting claim, because it is the claim this book exists to test.

The proposed resolution is the **matrix argument**: meadowsweet delivers salicylate embedded in a large **tannin and flavonoid** load that a purified acetylated salicylate does not have, and the matrix offsets the salicylate's gastric cost. That is not hand-waving here, because a mechanism was actually found — and it is not the mechanism usually offered.

**Rugosin D, rugosin A, rugosin A methyl ester (a novel compound), and tellimagrandin II** — four **ellagitannins**, the main components of the ethyl acetate extract of meadowsweet **petal** — are potent **non-competitive inhibitors of recombinant human histidine decarboxylase (HDC)**, with Kᵢ values of approximately **0.35–1 µM**, nearly equal to the substrate-analogue inhibitor histidine methyl ester (Kᵢ = 0.46 µM) (Nitta et al. 2012, *Food Chem*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2012.10.074)). HDC makes histamine. Histamine drives gastric acid secretion via parietal-cell H2 receptors — the target of an entire drug class. **A histamine-synthesis inhibitor in the flower is a mechanistically coherent antacid**, and the authors themselves name stomach ulceration as a histamine-mediated condition these agents might address. So the doctrinal paradox has a candidate answer: the flower carries *both* an acid-promoting irritant (salicylate) and an acid-suppressing tannin fraction (ellagitannins → less histamine), and the folk preparation is the one that delivers them together. The tradition's stomach indication may be tracking a real antagonism inside the plant.

Now the deflation, and it is severe, because this is where the doctrine gets dangerous rather than merely charming.

**The matrix does not reliably protect anyone.** A previously healthy **4-year-old boy** was admitted in **hypovolaemic shock with severe gastrointestinal bleeding**; endoscopy showed erosions and ulcerations of the lower oesophagus and a duodenal erosion. The only product he had taken was a **herbal syrup containing *Filipendula ulmaria* and *Salix* spp.**, given by his paediatrician for a mild cold with fever. Quali-quantitative analysis **confirmed salicylates in the syrup**; the Naranjo algorithm showed a **probable** causal relation. He required intensive care and recovered. **The product was withdrawn from the Italian market** (Moro et al. 2011, *Ann Ist Super Sanita*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4415/ANN_11_03_07)).

Read that against the doctrine. The tradition says meadowsweet protects the stomach. A real child nearly died of gastrointestinal haemorrhage after taking it. The case is confounded — a multi-herb syrup, willow also present, a pre-existing hiatus hernia and probable reflux — and it is a single report, and it should not be inflated into a general prohibition. But it is a **retrieved human harm signal pointing in exactly the opposite direction from the doctrine**, and it disposes of any claim that whole-plant meadowsweet is inherently gastric-safe. The matrix hypothesis is a laboratory finding about an enzyme. The bleeding was a child in shock. Where those two conflict, this book weights the child.

The honest doctrinal verdict: **fever, pain, and rheumatism — the tradition is right and the world agrees.** **Stomach — the tradition has a real candidate mechanism, no clinical evidence whatsoever, and one probable case of the precise opposite outcome.** Treat the gastroprotective reputation as *unproven and possibly inverted in vulnerable people*. The plant that named aspirin also inherits aspirin's liabilities, and the folklore did not know that because folklore could not see a duodenum.

### Active Components

- **Salicylates (volatile).** **Salicylaldehyde** and **methylsalicylate** are the main salicylates of the inflorescence and dominate the essential oil across *Filipendula* species (Ložienė et al. 2023, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants12020300); Olennikov et al. 2016, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules22010016)). Note that these are **not aspirin** — not acetylated, and the plant is a prodrug source, not a dose of aspirin.
- **Salicylic acid.** Present in the plant and a **major phenolic** of the dry tincture at **18.84 mg/g dry extract** — the single largest identified phenolic there (Andonova et al. 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox13101200)). Higher in flowers, fruits and roots than elsewhere (Savina et al. 2023, *Molecules*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules28083512)).
- **Ellagitannins — the gastroprotective candidates.** **Rugosin D, rugosin A, rugosin A methyl ester, tellimagrandin II** in petal (Nitta et al. 2012, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2012.10.074)); **Tellimagrandins I and II** confirmed independently in aerial parts (Pannakal et al. 2023, *Molecules*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules28176349)). Ellagitannins are credited with the plant's antioxidant activity (Olennikov et al. 2016).
- **Flavonoids — quercetin derivatives dominate:** quercetin 3-O-rutinoside (rutin), quercetin 3-β-D-glucoside, and **quercetin 4'-O-glucoside — spiraeoside — found only in the flowers and fruits** (Savina et al. 2023). The name is another fossil of *Spiraea*. Rutin at 9.97 mg/g de and quercetin at 4.47 mg/g de in dry tincture (Andonova et al. 2024).
- **Tannins.** Highest in **fruits (38.3 mg/g)**; catechins and proanthocyanidins concentrate in **roots** (45.1 and 3.4 mg/g) (Savina et al. 2023).
- **Other phenolics:** *p*-coumaric acid (6.80 mg/g de), rosmarinic acid (4.01), vanillic acid (3.82) (Andonova et al. 2024); chlorogenic acid in upper leaves, ellagic acid in lower leaves, gallic and caftaric acids in flowers and fruits (Savina et al. 2023). Ursolic acid, pomolic acid, kaempferol glycosides, naringenin and β-sitosterol also identified (Pannakal et al. 2023).
- **Polysaccharides:** galactans/arabinogalactans with starch-type glucans and galacturonans as minor components; the water-soluble fraction shows anti-complement activity (Olennikov et al. 2016).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

A **consolidation-phase** agent: cooling, descending, settling. Nothing about it opens anything. It is the herb of the come-down and the cooling-off.

1. **Fever and aching (DMM, CM).** The best-founded use. A diaphoretic, antipyretic, analgesic infusion for feverish, aching illness.
2. **Rheumatic and inflammatory pain (DMM).** The classical indication that the salicylate chemistry directly explains.
3. **Gastric complaints (DMM) — carry with a warning, not a recommendation.** Traditional; mechanistically plausible via HDC inhibition; **clinically unevidenced**; and shadowed by a probable human bleed. If used at all, use the **flower infusion** — the tannin/ellagitannin-rich preparation the tradition actually specifies — never a concentrated or standardised salicylate extract, which discards the matrix and keeps the hazard.
4. **Astringent gut action.** The tannin load supports a mild binding effect on loose stool.

**As a cultivator's plant — and here the cultivator makes the medicine.** Unlike eyebright, meadowsweet is easy: a hardy, unfussy perennial of **wet ground**. Give it damp-to-boggy, neutral-to-alkaline soil in sun or part shade; it tolerates heavy clay and seasonal flooding and will not thrive dry. Propagate by **division in autumn or spring** (fast, keeps the chemotype) or from seed sown fresh in autumn with cold stratification. It self-seeds freely in wet ground and can colonise — site it where spread is acceptable.

The decisive point is **harvest timing**, and it is quantified. The **early blooming stage (BBCH 62–63) yields 1.2–2× more volatile oil than the late stage (BBCH 65–67/72–73)** — but the **late-stage dried raw material yield per hectare is 18–56% higher**, while **essential oil yield per hectare is 1.5–1.6× higher at early flowering** (Ložienė et al. 2023, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants12020300)). So: **harvest early for potency, late for bulk.** For a medicinal preparation trading on the volatile salicylates, take the inflorescence at early bloom. That study also found **no statistically significant relationship between habitat parameters and the measured plant parameters** — a useful null: the cultivator's *timing* matters more than the cultivator's *site*.

But population genetics do matter. Chemical composition is **chemotype-driven and varies between populations**, shaped by phytosociological habitat and geographical distance, with salicylates named as the compounds determining market quality (Stawarczyk et al. 2021, *Molecules*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules26175172)). Practical instruction: **propagate vegetatively from a known-good clone** rather than trusting seed from mixed provenance.

And know your organ. The plant is not chemically uniform: **flowers and upper leaves** for flavonoids and antioxidant strength; **flowers and fruits** for spiraeoside and salicylic acid; **fruits** for tannin; **roots** for catechins and proanthocyanidins (Savina et al. 2023, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules28083512)). Flower is the medicinal organ of the tradition, and the chemistry endorses that choice.

### Scientific Correlation

- **Antipyretic / analgesic / antirheumatic — Grade B, by mechanism and derivative, not by trial.** The chemistry is beyond dispute: the plant contains salicylaldehyde, methylsalicylate and free salicylic acid (Ložienė et al. 2023; Andonova et al. 2024; Savina et al. 2023), and salicylate pharmacology is among the best-characterised in medicine. But state the limit precisely: **no randomised controlled trial of *F. ulmaria* in fever, pain, or rheumatic disease was retrieved this run.** The grade rests on the molecule's established pharmacology and the plant's confirmed content of it — an inference, not a demonstration. Aspirin works; that is not the same as this tea working.
- **Gastroprotective doctrine — Grade C for mechanism, Grade D for outcome, with a contrary human signal.** Positive: four petal ellagitannins are potent non-competitive HDC inhibitors at Kᵢ ≈ 0.35–1 µM (Nitta et al. 2012, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2012.10.074)) — an in vitro recombinant-enzyme assay, which is a long way from a human stomach. Negative: **no clinical trial of meadowsweet in gastritis, hyperacidity, or ulcer was retrieved.** Contrary: the paediatric hypovolaemic-shock GI bleed with confirmed salicylates and probable Naranjo causality (Moro et al. 2011, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4415/ANN_11_03_07)). **The best-known doctrinal claim about this plant has zero clinical support and one probable case of the opposite.** That is the honest state of it.
- **Anticarcinogenic — Grade C (animal).** The strongest efficacy data retrieved, and it is in rats. Meadowsweet decoction given as drinking water after methylnitrosourea exposure produced statistically significant reductions versus MNU control: overall tumour incidence and multiplicity down **1.4× and 2.9×**; colon tumour incidence and multiplicity down **2.0× and 2.8×**; malignant tumours at other sites down **2.2× and 3.0×** (Bespalov et al. 2018, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2018.08.013)). Genuinely notable. Also: a rodent chemoprevention model, one group, one laboratory, no human data. Do not let a cancer claim off this leash.
- **Antioxidant — Grade B (in vitro, consistent, replicated).** Confirmed across four methods, best by CUPRAC at **10,605.91 µM TE/g de** (Andonova et al. 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox13101200)); confirmed independently by DPPH/ABTS/FRAP with upper leaves, flowers and fruits the best sources (Savina et al. 2023). Robust in the tube. In vitro antioxidant capacity has a poor record of predicting clinical benefit, and this book does not treat it as a therapeutic claim.
- **DNA-protective / antiproliferative — Grade D.** First report of protection against oxidative DNA damage and antiproliferative effect against HepG2 (Andonova et al. 2024). Cell line; first report.
- **Antibacterial — Grade C.** Fruit and flower extracts showed activity (Savina et al. 2023). In vitro.
- **Anti-diabetic / enzyme inhibition — Grade D.** Teas inhibited amylase, α-glucosidase and AGE formation (Olennikov et al. 2016). In vitro beverage screen.
- **Anti-complement / immunomodulatory — Grade D.** Water-soluble polysaccharide fraction only; "possible" per authors (Olennikov et al. 2016).
- **Skin barrier / renewal — Grade D.** Fractions containing quercetin, kaempferol glycosides, ursolic and pomolic acid, naringenin, β-sitosterol and Tellimagrandins I–II upregulated barrier and renewal genes in normal human epidermal keratinocytes (Pannakal et al. 2023, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules28176349)). Keratinocyte gene expression; **industry-authored** (L'Oréal), which is not disqualifying but is disclosed.
- **Plant immunity genomics — not a medicinal claim.** 27 thaumatin-like protein genes identified in the meadowsweet genome; six differentially expressed under *Bipolaris sorokiniana* infection (Istomina et al. 2026, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/cimb48060640)). Relevant to the cultivator, not the patient.

*Overall:* the historically decisive plant in this volume, with an unimpeachable claim on the origin of salicylate medicine — and, astonishingly, **not one human clinical trial retrieved for any indication.** The molecule it gave the world has been tested on millions. The plant itself has been tested on nobody. The single retrieved human datum is a poisoning.

*All citations above were retrieved from PubMed.*

### Traditional Energetic Classification

| Axis | Reading |
|---|---|
| Temperature | Cool |
| Moisture | Dry (astringent), with a diaphoretic release at the surface |
| Taste | Astringent, faintly sweet-aromatic; almond-medicinal on the nose |
| Direction | Descending and settling; outward at the skin in fever |
| Organ affinity | Stomach; joints; skin |
| Doctrine of gesture | Cooling of heat that has risen — the fever brought down, the sour stomach quieted |

The energetic reading is coherent: astringency and cooling are exactly what tannin and salicylate respectively deliver. The tradition described the chemistry accurately in the only vocabulary it had.

### Contraindications

**⚠️ This is a salicylate plant. Every salicylate caution applies, and they are not theoretical.**

- **Aspirin / NSAID allergy or intolerance — contraindicated.** Cross-reactivity is expected, not speculative.
- **Salicylate-sensitive asthma / aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD) — contraindicated.** Salicylates can precipitate severe bronchospasm in this population. This is a life-threatening reaction, not a sensitivity.
- **Children — do not give for febrile illness. Reye's syndrome.** Salicylates in children with viral fever are associated with Reye's syndrome — an encephalopathy with hepatic failure, and it kills. The retrieved case is exactly this scenario and exactly this failure of judgment: a **paediatrician prescribed a salicylate-containing herbal syrup to a 4-year-old for a cold with fever**, and the child arrived in **hypovolaemic shock with GI bleeding**; the product was withdrawn from market (Moro et al. 2011, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4415/ANN_11_03_07)). "It is a herb, marketed as a food, given by a doctor" protected that child from nothing. **Meadowsweet is not a paediatric fever remedy. Do not use it as one.**
- **Anticoagulants and antiplatelets — avoid.** Warfarin, DOACs, aspirin, clopidogrel. Additive bleeding risk. Also avoid with other NSAIDs.
- **Existing peptic ulcer, gastritis, GORD, hiatus hernia, or prior GI bleed — avoid**, notwithstanding the traditional gastric indication. The retrieved case involved a child with hiatus hernia and probable reflux. **The people the doctrine most recommends this plant to are the people most likely to be harmed by it.** That inversion is the central safety point of this entry.
- **Before surgery — discontinue** (bleeding risk), consistent with standard salicylate practice. Timing per clinician.
- **Pregnancy and lactation — avoid.** Salicylates in late pregnancy carry recognised concerns. No safety data retrieved for the herb.
- **Renal or hepatic impairment; G6PD deficiency; gout** — salicylate cautions apply. `[USER: verify]` against a current pharmacopoeial monograph.
- **Dose is uncontrolled and unknowable.** Salicylate content is **chemotype- and population-dependent** (Stawarczyk et al. 2021, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules26175172)) and varies by **blooming stage** (Ložienė et al. 2023) and **plant organ** (Savina et al. 2023). Two jars of meadowsweet may differ severalfold in salicylate. **You cannot know your dose.** For a plant whose hazards are dose-dependent, this is the practical reason to keep to weak flower infusions and stay away from concentrated extracts.
- **Do not combine with willow, poplar, or wintergreen** — or with any other salicylate-bearing herb. The retrieved harm case was a **combination product containing both *Filipendula* and *Salix***. Stacking salicylate herbs is how the ceiling gets breached.
