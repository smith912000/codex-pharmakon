# Memphite stone — Memphites lithos (the translation prints "Memphitis stone"), Dioscorides 5-158
## *A pebble-sized, "fat" stone of various colours from near Memphis in Egypt, said to cause numbness without danger when pounded and smeared on a place to be cut or burnt; the translator gives no identification ("UNKNOWN")* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-158, *Memphites lithos* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 10652; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 931, translator identification "UNKNOWN", class guess mineral; list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 884, "Memphites Liihos" [sic], mineral, flagged as an uncertain identification; the book index sends the reader to page 822 as "Memphitis stone"). The whole chapter was read. It reads (line 10655):

"Memphitis stone is found in Egypt near Memphis — the size of a pebble, fat, and of various colours. It is said that this (pounded into small pieces and smeared on any place that shall be cut or burnt), causes numbness [anaesthetic] without danger."

The chapter thus gives a place (Egypt, near Memphis), a size (that of a pebble), a texture ("fat"), a colour note (various colours), and one use: pounded into small pieces and smeared on a place "that shall be cut or burnt", it "causes numbness ... without danger". The word "[anaesthetic]" is the translator's bracket, not Dioscorides'. The claim is introduced with "It is said that", so Dioscorides reports it at second hand and does not vouch for it. The chapter gives no assay, no adulterant, no burning method and no quantity.

**Neighbouring chapters.** The chapter before it, 5-157 (*sapheiros*, whose translator's suggestion is "Sapphire — previously Lapis Lazuli"), and the one after, 5-159 (*selenites*), do not refer to it.

**Identity.** The translator gives no identification ("UNKNOWN"), and the Codex list marks the identification uncertain. The chapter's clues (pebble-sized, "fat", various colours, from near Memphis) do not name a mineral, and the chapter does not say that the numbing effect has been observed by the author. Which material Dioscorides meant is not settled by the sources retrieved. A separate tradition's text, the Ebers Papyrus in Bryan's translation, lists "Stone-from-Memphis" among its mineral drugs (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` lines 1208 and 3299); that is a different text and this entry is not tagged with it, and the two are not claimed to be the same material. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides reports, at second hand ("It is said"), that an unidentified Egyptian stone, pounded and smeared on, numbs a place to be cut or burnt "without danger". The material cannot be identified, and numbing a surgical or burnt site with an unknown powder is not a sound practice: an unidentified mineral rubbed into a wound or burn risks infection and poisoning, and the claim of safety is the source's, not the Codex's. The Codex supplies no modern protocol.

### Contraindications

- **Unidentified material:** the identification is uncertain; an unknown stone should not be applied to wounds or burns.
- **Infection:** pounded mineral powder on a fresh cut or burn is non-sterile.
- **False safety:** the chapter's "without danger" is a reported claim and is not supported by any test in the sources retrieved; numbing a site can hide pain that signals damage.
- **Burns and wounds:** burns and cuts that need cutting or cautery need medical care.
