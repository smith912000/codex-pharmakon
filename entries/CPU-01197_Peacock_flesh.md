# Peacock flesh — peacock meat, as printed in Clark's translation of the rGyud-bzhi
## *The meat of the peacock, a large pheasant-family bird; named in the Tibetan materia medica of animal origin and in its diet chapter* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan Tradition (FMT).** The text is Barry Clark's 1995 translation of the rGyud-bzhi, file `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`. Peacock flesh is attested at two loci.

1. The materia medica. Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (heading at line 792), section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), sub-group "Meat" (line 1161). The line (1168) reads: "Peacock meat: [has seventeen names]. It subdues bile disorders, poisoning [and spirits]." The same line adds the parenthesis on the feather as a test for poisoning, treated in the entry for Peacock feather. `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 346 ("Peacock meat", animal, id_uncertain "yes") points to this chapter.
2. The diet chapter. In the discussion of meats, Clark's text says: "Peacock flesh is beneficial in eye diseases, hoarseness and for rejuvenation." (line 678). The word "rejuvenation" is the translator's rendering and stands in a list with two plain indications; no Tibetan term is printed beside it.

Clark says in his preface (line 80) that he "interpolated liberally from the Blue Aquamarine commentary", and the brackets mark added matter. In line 1168 the phrase "[has seventeen names]" and "[and spirits]" are therefore commentary-derived, and the root-text sentence is "It subdues bile disorders, poisoning." That reading of the bracket convention is an inference. [USER: verify]

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3529) has the parallel sentence: "Peacock meat cures tripa disorders and is an antidote against poison." It has no "spirits" and no eye, hoarseness or rejuvenation statement in that passage; I did not find the diet-chapter sentence in that edition. [USER: verify]

Where the peacock sits in the meat classification: Clark's line 672 puts it first in section "(a) Creatures which dig with their claws" (with white grouse, partridge, jackdaw and hill birds). The Men-Tsee-Khang text says the first three groups "are those that dwell on dry land" (line 2923) and that peacock heads the claw-digging birds (line 2899).

### Active Components

Poultry-type meat is mainly protein with some fat, B vitamins and minerals. The text names no active compound. Peafowl meat is a food, and the properties above are the tradition's, not a pharmacological finding.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The Tibetan text says peacock meat "subdues bile disorders, poisoning", and in the diet chapter that it is "beneficial in eye diseases, hoarseness and for rejuvenation". The Codex does not recommend eating peacock for any of these, and gives no protocol. Peafowl are protected, sacred or regulated in some countries (see Contraindications), poisoning is an emergency that no meat treats, and "rejuvenation" is a claim in the translation that no source retrieved explains.

### Preparation & Dosing

The lines give no preparation (roasted, boiled, dried) and no dose; the diet chapter treats it as a meat in the ordinary sense. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] General fact: no meat is a recognised treatment for poisoning or for eye disease, and "rejuvenation" has no clinical definition.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Tibetan: the materia medica line prints actions only. The diet chapter supplies group rules. Clark says (line 673) that the meats of the eight sections "are sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste", and that "The flesh of dry land animals has cool, light and coarse powers which eliminate wind and phlegm fevers". Peacock is in section (a) (line 672), and the Men-Tsee-Khang text places the first three sections on dry land (line 2923), so the group rule would apply to it by classification. The peacock is not given a separate taste or power in the lines read. [USER: verify] No other tradition is tagged.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan (FMT).** *(b) Framed; the text uses the word "rejuvenation" but gives no cultivation status.* Clark's diet chapter says peacock flesh is "beneficial in eye diseases, hoarseness and for rejuvenation" (line 678). That is the only item here that approaches a cultivation claim. It sits in a food list, as one indication among three; it is not placed in a rejuvenation chapter. The Explanatory Tantra's own passage on rejuvenation, chapter 23 "Normal health" (lines 1264-1275), calls rejuvenation "fourfold: (a) benefits, (b) environment, (c) working basis and (d) mode of practice", prescribes oil therapy, a bath and a purgative compound as preliminaries, and gives five compounds; it does not name the peacock (a text search of the Clark file for "peacock" finds only lines 111, 112, 678, 726, 1168 and 1193). The Oral Instruction Tantra's chapter 90, "rejuvenation and essence-extraction compounds" (listed at line 133), is not in the file in detail and was not read. No *bcud len* (essence-extraction) role is printed for the flesh. The materia medica line (1168) is therapeutic (bile disorders, poisoning), and its spirit clause is commentary-derived and bracketed. The translator's "rejuvenation" is recorded as the text's own word and not expanded into any subtle-body claim. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing is sourced beyond a word in a food list. A cultivator does not need peacock meat, and the text does not require it. The Codex records that Clark's translation lists it as beneficial "for rejuvenation" among other things, and it does not carry that into practice.

### Contraindications

Peafowl are protected or culturally revered in some countries, and killing or trading them can be illegal; check the law of the jurisdiction. [USER: verify] Wild and farmed birds can carry food-borne bacteria (including *Salmonella* and *Campylobacter*), so any poultry meat needs thorough cooking. Do not rely on any food for poisoning; suspected poisoning is an emergency. This entry gives no medical advice.
