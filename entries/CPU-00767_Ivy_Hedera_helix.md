# Ivy — لبلاب (Lablāb) / Κισσός (Kissos) / लता (Latā) / 常春藤 (Chángchūnténg)
## *Hedera helix L. — Common Ivy / English Ivy*
**Tradition Tags:** AT · DMM · CM · SS
**Energetic Classification (TCM):** ◉ Qi
**Training Phase:** `[RESET]`

### Origin & Tradition

Ivy was the sacred plant of Dionysus/Bacchus in Greek-Roman mythology — those crowned with ivy were thought to be protected from intoxication (a folk belief that ironically reflects the reality that ivy saponins impair alcohol absorption). Its pharmacological identity centers on a unique saponin complex (hederacoside C → α-hederin) that provides the most clinically validated natural bronchosecretolytic (mucus-loosening expectorant) activity of any botanical currently in European clinical medicine. Ivy leaf dry extract (standardized preparations) are licensed pharmaceutical drugs for bronchitis and cough in Germany and across the EU — the only herbal cough preparation with formal pharmaceutical registration in multiple European countries.

**AT — Assyrian (Kīppu / related climbing plant preparations in Nineveh tablets):** Ivy-related preparations appear in Assyrian medical texts for respiratory and analgesic applications. The climbing habit and distinctive leaf shape made ivy easily identifiable and documented in Mesopotamian medical records.

**DMM — De Materia Medica (Κισσός — Kissos):** Dioscorides provides extensive ivy coverage in Book II. He distinguishes black-berried ivy, white-berried ivy, and other types. Primary indications: anti-diarrheal (berries — toxic caution); topical anti-inflammatory (leaves for headache, burns, skin conditions); emmenagogue; the berry juice as a purging emetic; and the surprising "preventive against drunkenness" — worn as a crown or taken before drinking (the saponin alcohol-interaction belief).

**CM — Canon of Medicine (لبلاب — Lablāb):** Ibn Sina classifies ivy as warm in the second degree and dry. He notes: astringent; anti-diarrheal (berries — with toxicity caution); topical anti-inflammatory; and the analgesic topical for headache (cold ivy poultice applied to the forehead — a traditional headache remedy). He distinguishes the topical leaf applications (safe) from the berry preparations (potentially toxic at higher doses).

**SS — Ayurveda (documented via later Unani-Ayurvedic overlap; *Hedera* species in Kashmir and Himalayan materia medica):** Ivy preparations appear in Himalayan regional Ayurvedic medicine. Classification: Katu-Tikta rasa, Ushna virya, Vata-Kapha shamaka. Actions: Kasahara (antitussive-expectorant); respiratory antimicrobial; anti-inflammatory.

### Active Components

**Saponins (primary pharmacological class):**
- **Hederacoside C** — the primary triterpenoid saponin; the prodrug converted in the body to **α-hederin**; the confirmed bronchosecretolytic mechanism: α-hederin activates β2-adrenergic receptors in airway smooth muscle → bronchodilation; simultaneously stimulates surfactant secretion (the lung surface tension-reducing fluid) → liquefies mucus → expectorant; the precise molecular mechanism of ivy leaf's cough medicine action
- **Hederacoside D, E, F, G, H, I, J, K** — minor hederacoside variants; contribute to the activity spectrum
- **α-Hederin** — the active aglycone; directly bronchodilatory; the confirmed pharmaceutical active
- **Saponin mixture** — disrupts cell membranes at high concentrations (the toxicity mechanism of berries and raw juice)

**Flavonoids:**
- **Rutin, quercetin, kaempferol, luteolin** — anti-inflammatory; antioxidant; contribute to the mucosal anti-inflammatory activity

**Polyacetylenes (berries — toxic):**
- **Falcarinol, falcarindiol** — present primarily in berries and leaves; cytotoxic; responsible for the poisoning risk from raw ivy berries; destroyed by drying and extraction processes

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**1. Bronchosecretolytic expectorant ([RESET] — respiratory illness recovery)**
Ivy leaf dry extract is the European Union's most evidenced botanical cough medicine with pharmaceutical registration (Prospan®, Hedelix®, and other standardized preparations). The α-hederin β2-adrenergic mechanism provides:
- Bronchodilation (opens narrowed airways)
- Mucus liquefaction (makes thick mucus thinner and easier to clear)
- Anti-spasmodic (reduces cough reflex threshold without suppressing it — the mucus is cleared naturally)

This is the most appropriate respiratory expectorant for productive cough with thick mucus from cold-Phlegm or heat-Phlegm patterns (the preparation works for both). For practitioners with training-interrupting bronchitis or persistent productive cough, ivy leaf extract is the first-line botanical before considering pharmaceutical options.

**2. Anti-bacterial (respiratory pathogens)**
α-Hederin demonstrates direct antimicrobial activity against respiratory pathogens (*Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae*) at concentrations achievable in airway mucus. The dual expectorant + antimicrobial mechanism parallels the most effective combination approaches in respiratory medicine.

### Preparation & Dosing

**Standardized ivy leaf extract (pharmaceutical-grade — preferred):**
35mg ivy leaf dry extract (standardized to hederacoside C equivalent; Prospan® or equivalent); 2–3x/day for acute bronchitis. Syrup preparations (5–7mL) are the most bioavailable. **Use standardized preparations only** — the toxicity of non-standardized preparations varies widely.

**Ivy leaf tea (traditional — lower dose, monitoring required):**
0.3–0.8g dried ivy leaf (NOT berries — only the dried leaf from established pharmaceutical preparations) in 150mL hot water; steep 10 minutes. The traditional preparation is less reliable than standardized extract due to variability in saponin content.

**Duration:** Acute bronchitis: 7–14 days. Chronic cough: up to 4 weeks, then reassess.

### Scientific Correlation

- **Bronchosecretolytic (α-hederin):** Kayser O, Linden A (2001): α-hederin mechanism as β2-adrenergic agonist + surfactant secretion stimulator confirmed; Brenk-Franz K et al. review: clinical evidence summary for ivy leaf extract
- **Clinical efficacy (bronchitis):** Hecker M et al. (2002): ivy leaf syrup significantly superior to placebo for acute bronchitis symptoms in children (RCT); Cwientzek U et al. (2011): ivy leaf equivalent to ambroxol (standard pharmaceutical expectorant) in adult acute bronchitis (non-inferiority RCT)
- **Pharmaceutical registration:** Commission E (Germany) positive monograph for ivy leaf; ESCOP monograph; HMPC (EU) positive opinion for traditional use in productive cough; one of the few botanical medicines with full pharmaceutical registered status in the EU

### Contraindications

- **Raw ivy berries — TOXIC** — berries contain concentrated polyacetylene toxins (falcarinol) and high-dose saponins; raw berries cause vomiting, diarrhea, and potentially cardiac effects; never ingest raw berries; pharmaceutical preparations use only the leaf extract at controlled saponin concentrations
- **Raw leaf juice** — can cause contact dermatitis (falcarindiol sensitization) and GI irritation; only use dried standardized leaf preparations
- **Pediatric — verified preparation required** — several reports of adverse effects from non-standardized ivy leaf preparations in children; only use licensed pharmaceutical preparations in pediatric use
- **Pregnancy** — saponin effect on cellular membranes; avoid during pregnancy
- **Kidney disease** — high-dose saponins are renally processed; caution in renal impairment
