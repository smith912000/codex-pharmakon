# Hedge Bindweed

## *Calystegia sepium* (L.) R.Br. | Family: Convolvulaceae

**Also known as:** Bearbind, Devil's Guts, Rutland Beauty, Old Man's Nightcap, Great Bindweed, Wild Morning Glory, Hedge Lily, Withywind

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Cold, Dry, Downward-Moving, Draining (per the classical purgative reading — see *Traditional Energetic Classification*)

**Training Phase:** Not a Kenetic training substance. Filed as a **doctrinal-lineage and research-thread entry** — retained for the coherence of the Convolvulaceae purgative complex and for the calystegine research line, not for practitioner use.

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Calystegia sepium* is the great climbing bindweed of European hedgerows, ditches and river floodplains — a rhizomatous perennial that twines counter-clockwise (a genuinely fixed, species-typical helical handedness) up any support it meets, throwing white or pink-striped funnel flowers the size of a hen's egg. It is circumboreal, weedy, and famously impossible to eradicate: the brittle white rhizomes fragment under the spade and each fragment regrows. Its ecology is well documented — it is one of the characteristic companion species of Central European riverine sedge stands (*Carex buekii* communities), where it climbs through the tall herb layer (Więcław et al., 2019, *Scientific Reports*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-47563-0)).

**A frank statement on the evidence, up front.** This entry is **data-sparse on the ethnomedical side**, and the Codex will not pretend otherwise. Hedge Bindweed has no substantial, well-attested independent materia medica tradition of its own. What it has is a *position within a tradition* — the Convolvulaceae purgative complex — and a genuine, active modern research thread that has nothing to do with purging. Most historical statements attached to *C. sepium* in secondary herbals are either (a) generic convolvulus-purgative doctrine applied by family resemblance, or (b) confusions with its relatives. The Codex reports the doctrinal frame honestly and marks every specific attribution for verification.

**European Domestic Materia Medica (DMM) — "Bearbind", "Devil's Guts", "Scammony of the hedges"**

The European placement of Hedge Bindweed is **derivative, not primary**. The Convolvulaceae gave the old pharmacopoeias three of their most important drastic purgatives — Scammony (*Convolvulus scammonia*), Jalap (*Ipomoea purga*), and Turpeth (*Operculina turpethum*) — all of them resin drugs, all traded, all monographed in detail. Against that background, the native European bindweeds were read as **local, weaker, adulterant-grade members of the same purgative genus**: plants with the right family, the right milky-resinous root, and a recognisably similar (if cruder and less reliable) action. The vernacular name **Devil's Guts** encodes both the ropy indestructible rhizome and the churning bowel-action; **Bearbind** encodes the strangling growth habit.

The classical indication follows from the family doctrine rather than from independent observation: the **root/rhizome** taken as a purgative and **cholagogue** — a "hydragogue" evacuant intended to draw watery stool and bile downward, used in the humoral frame for dropsy, jaundice, "obstruction of the liver", and costiveness. [USER: verify] the specific attribution of these indications to *C. sepium* against a primary source — Gerard, Parkinson, Culpeper, or a regional folk-botany survey — rather than a modern compilation. The Codex's position is that the family-level doctrine is securely attested; the species-level indication for *C. sepium* specifically is **weakly attested and probably back-formed**.

**Chinese Medicine**

*Calystegia sepium* and closely allied *Calystegia* taxa appear in the Chinese ethnobotanical record, and the genus is a recognised source of resin glycosides in Chinese phytochemical work — the hexasaccharide resin glycosides *calysepins* were isolated from *C. sepium* aerial parts by a Chinese group and characterised in detail (Lv et al., 2022, *J Nat Prod*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jnatprod.2c00008)). That establishes the plant's presence and chemical study in the Chinese materia medica orbit. **However:** the Codex cannot, this run, verify a specific classical CM indication, pinyin drug name, channel entry, or *Ben Cao* citation for *C. sepium*. The **CM** tag is therefore carried as **[USER: verify]** — it should be either substantiated with a named source (drug name + text) or downgraded. Note the standing confusion risk: 打碗花 (*dǎ wǎn huā*) is applied in Chinese sources to *Calystegia* species generally, and the boundary between *C. sepium*, *C. hederacea* and *C. pubescens* in the folk record is not clean. Do not let a genus-level name license a species-level claim.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

The doctrine here is unusually legible, and it maps onto two *different* real chemistries — one of which the tradition knew about and one of which it could not possibly have known about. That split is the whole interest of this entry.

*The signature of the twining vine.* The bindweed's habit — a plant that is all rope and gut, that winds, constricts, tangles and cannot be pulled out — reads, in the doctrine-of-signatures grammar, as a plant of **the intestines**. "Devil's Guts" is a signature name: the rhizome *is* a gut, white, ropy, coiling underground. A plant shaped like a bowel is a plant that acts on the bowel. And in the wet, cold, low ditch-habitat the plant favours, the humoral reading assigns it cold and moist origins with a downward, watery, draining action — precisely the "hydragogue" profile.

*Where the doctrine is right.* The purgative reading is **not** fantasy. It has a real chemical referent: Convolvulaceae roots and aerial parts carry **resin glycosides** (glycoresins) — amphipathic macrolactone-forming oligosaccharides of hydroxy fatty acids. These are the actual purgative principle of Scammony and Jalap, and *C. sepium* genuinely makes them: the calysepins are 27-membered macrolactone hexasaccharides built from one D-quinovose, four D-glucose and one L-rhamnose, acylated with 2-methylbutanoic and nilic acids (Lv et al., 2022, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jnatprod.2c00008)). So the doctrine "this is a lesser member of the scammony kind, and it purges" is **chemically coherent** — the family shares the pharmacophore. The tradition, reasoning by family resemblance and taste, arrived at a real compound class.

*Where the doctrine must be deflated.* Two deflations, both necessary.

First: **coherent is not the same as effective, safe, or dosable.** That *C. sepium* contains resin glycosides does not establish that it contains them at a therapeutically usable concentration, in a reproducible ratio, or with an acceptable margin. Scammony and Jalap were traded precisely because the native bindweeds were *not good enough* — variable, crude, and unpleasant. The doctrinal frame that makes *C. sepium* legible as a purgative is the same frame that correctly ranked it as third-rate. Modern purgative pharmacology has retired the entire drastic-hydragogue class; the indications it served (dropsy, "liver obstruction") were miscategorised conditions treated by a mechanism — violent fluid loss — now understood as harm, not cure.

Second, and more interesting: **the doctrine is silent on the compound that actually matters.** The genuine modern research thread in *Calystegia* is not the resins at all. It is the **calystegines** — polyhydroxylated nortropane alkaloids named for this very genus. These are sugar-mimetic glycosidase inhibitors: they look like sugars, they sit in the active sites of carbohydrate-processing enzymes, and they do something the purgative doctrine has no vocabulary for. No signature reading could have found them. This is the honest shape of the relationship between doctrine and chemistry: the tradition's symbolic logic tracked one real compound class (which turned out to be a dead end therapeutically) and was completely blind to the other (which is where the live science is). The vine that "looks like a gut" contains molecules that "look like a sugar" — and only the second resemblance is doing real pharmacological work.

### Active Components

**Resin glycosides (glycoresins) — the purgative principle.**
- **Calysepins I–VII** — species-specific to *C. sepium*, isolated from aerial parts. Hexasaccharide resin glycosides with 27-membered macrolactone rings; sugar core of one D-quinovose + four D-glucose + one L-rhamnose; partial acylation by (2*S*)-methylbutanoic and nilic acids, with the isomers differing chiefly in acylation position. Calysepin IV showed cytotoxicity against A549 lung carcinoma cells (IC50 5.2 μM) — an *in vitro* cell-line result only (Lv et al., 2022, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jnatprod.2c00008)).
- Family-level context: this is the same structural class as the resins of Scammony, Jalap and Turpeth. **Congener data — do not import as species-specific.**

**Calystegines — polyhydroxy nortropane alkaloids.**
- Named for the genus. Nortropane (N-demethylated tropane) skeletons bearing three to five hydroxyl groups. Principal named members: **calystegines A3, B1, B2, B3, C1**.
- *C. sepium* root cultures are the organism in which calystegine **biosynthesis** was worked out: ¹⁵N-tropinone feeding established tropinone and pseudotropine as pathway intermediates, with maximum calystegine formation ~0.4 mg/day/g biomass (Scholl, Schneider & Dräger, 2003, *Phytochemistry*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0031-9422(02)00544-7)). The pathway entry enzyme is putrescine N-methyltransferase (PMT), cloned from Convolvulaceae as well as Solanaceae (Teuber et al., 2007, *Plant Mol Biol*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11103-006-9126-7)).
- ⚠️ **Species-specificity caveat.** The detailed *activity* data for individual calystegines below come from material isolated from **other** plants (potato, *Ipomoea carnea*, edible Solanaceae). *C. sepium* is established as a calystegine *producer* and as the model for the *biosynthesis*; the Codex is **not** asserting a quantified calystegine profile for wild *C. sepium* rhizome. That number, to our knowledge, is not something this run retrieved.

**Storage proteins and lectins.**
- An abundant cytoplasmic **β-amylase** (EC 3.2.1.2) from resting rhizomes, characterised and cloned; argued to function as a cytoplasmic vegetative storage protein (Van Damme et al., 2001, *Eur J Biochem*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.0014-2956.2001.02584.x)).
- **Calystegia sepium agglutinin (Calsepa)** — a mannose-binding plant lectin, now used as a routine laboratory reagent. It appears as one of four high-mannose-specific lectins on lectin microarrays used to profile glycosylation changes in hepatocellular carcinoma dedifferentiation (Takayama et al., 2020, *BMC Cancer*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-020-6699-5)). Note carefully: this is the plant serving as a **source of a reagent**, not as a medicine. It is the plant's most genuinely useful contribution to modern biomedicine, and it is entirely non-therapeutic.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Kenetica has no use case for this plant. It is not a training substance at any phase.**

This is stated plainly because the temptation to invent one is real. Kenetic practice concerns trained modulation of attention, arousal and interoceptive discrimination. A drastic hydragogue purgative offers nothing to that project. Violent enterically-driven fluid loss is not a "clearing", a "grounding", or a "downward reset" — it is dehydration and electrolyte derangement with a folk-theoretical gloss. Traditions that used purging as a preparatory rite were operating inside a humoral cosmology whose premises Kenetica does not share, and the Codex will not smuggle that cosmology back in by calling the same action by a nicer name.

**What the entry is for:**

1. **Doctrinal completeness.** Hedge Bindweed sits between **Field Bindweed** (*Convolvulus arvensis*) and **Jalap** (*Ipomoea purga*) in the codex, both already filed. Holding all three lets the reader see a doctrine propagate across a family by resemblance, and see exactly where that propagation was chemically justified (shared resin glycosides) and where it was mere inference (the species-level indications). This is a worked example of how to read a family-resemblance claim.

2. **A live research thread the practitioner should know exists.** Calystegines are real glycosidase inhibitors with real pharmacology. That thread runs toward carbohydrate-metabolism modulation and chemical-chaperone biology — not toward anything a person should be brewing. Knowing the thread exists is the point; acting on it is not.

**For the cultivator.** Practical note, offered honestly: **do not plant this.** *C. sepium* is a rhizomatous perennial of the first rank of invasiveness. Every rhizome fragment left in the soil regenerates; it climbs and smothers standing crops and is a recognised perennial weed of arable systems (documented among the perennial weeds targeted in glyphosate-resistant soybean trials — Sarpe et al., 2001, *Meded Rijksuniv Gent*, PMID 12425098). If you have it, you did not choose it. If a specimen is needed for study, it is far more sensibly kept as a **root culture** — which is, tellingly, exactly how the calystegine biosynthesis work was done. The interesting chemistry has been accessed through sterile root culture, not through the garden, for twenty-five years. The cultivator's guidance here is a containment protocol, not a growing protocol: a sunk impermeable barrier or a sealed container, and vigilance.

| Calystegines as the toxic principle in Convolvulaceae livestock poisoning | **REFUTED for calystegines — the toxin is swainsonine** | This is the entry's most important null. *Ipomoea carnea* causes a genuine lysosomal storage disease in goats (de Balogh et al., 1999, *J Vet Diagn Invest*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/104063879901100310)) — and the plant contains both swainsonine **and** calystegines B2/C1. The natural inference is that both contribute. It was tested and it was wrong: in human lymphoblast culture, swainsonine at 0.1 μM cut α-mannosidase activity ~60%, while calystegines B2 and C1 showed **no** β-glucosidase inhibition up to 1 mM and at 100 μM *increased* activity 1.5–1.6-fold, apparently acting as **chemical chaperones**. The authors concluded calystegines "seem to have little risk of inducing intoxication of livestock" (Ikeda et al., 2003, *J Agric Food Chem*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf035003k)). **Potent inhibition in the test tube reversed to enhancement in living cells.** Record this. |

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Humoral (European, derivative):** Cold and dry in the second-to-third degree by the purgative reading; hydragogue and cholagogue; strongly **downward-moving** and **draining**; classed with the *drastics* rather than the *laxatives*. The plant's wet ditch habitat was read as the source of its watery-evacuant virtue. Assigned by family analogy to Scammony rather than by independent observation of this species. **[USER: verify]** any degree-of-quality assignment against a primary text.

### Contraindications

⚠️ **The Codex does not recommend internal use of *Calystegia sepium* under any circumstance, at any dose, for any indication.**

- **Drastic purgation and GI toxicity.** The resin glycosides are the purgative principle and are chemically established in this species (Lv et al., 2022). The drastic hydragogue class produces violent watery evacuation with cramping, colic, tenesmus and, at excess, haemorrhagic enteritis. Deaths from the traded members of this class (Scammony, Jalap) are documented in the historical record; there is no reason to assume the local relative is benign, only that it is *less potent and less predictable* — which is worse, not better, since unpredictability is what kills.
- **No verified dose exists.** Content is unquantified and certainly variable by part, season, population and habitat. There is no basis for titration.
- **Dehydration and electrolyte loss.** The mechanism *is* fluid loss. Hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia, hypovolaemia. Compounding risk with diuretics, cardiac glycosides (hypokalaemia potentiates digitalis toxicity), corticosteroids, or any pre-existing renal or cardiac compromise.
- **Pregnancy — absolute contraindication.** Drastic purgatives are reflex uterine stimulants and were historically employed as abortifacients. Do not use. Do not use while breastfeeding.
- **Absolute contraindications:** intestinal obstruction or suspected obstruction, undiagnosed abdominal pain, appendicitis, inflammatory bowel disease, GI bleeding, children, the elderly, the debilitated, anyone with renal or cardiac disease.
- **Misidentification.** *Calystegia* species (*C. sepium*, *C. silvatica*, *C. pubescens*, *C. soldanella*) intergrade and hybridise; *Calystegia* is confused with *Convolvulus arvensis* in casual identification; and Convolvulaceae in general includes plants of real toxicity. Genus-level identification is not sufficient for any purpose here.
- **Livestock and grazing.** The genuine, documented Convolvulaceae poisoning risk to livestock is the **swainsonine**-mediated lysosomal storage disease of *Ipomoea carnea* (de Balogh et al., 1999) — a different genus and a different toxin. Do **not** transfer that specific hazard to *C. sepium*; equally, do not read the calystegine "all-clear" (Ikeda et al., 2003) as a safety clearance for a plant whose resin content is uncharacterised.
- **Note on the calystegine research thread.** Nothing in the glycosidase-inhibitor literature constitutes evidence that consuming this plant is beneficial. The most rigorous work found low intestinal inhibition, no Caco-2 transport (Jocković et al., 2013), and cellular *enhancement* rather than inhibition (Ikeda et al., 2003). The interesting molecule is not a reason to eat the plant that makes it.
