# Tapeworm — Ebers Papyrus, "Tape-worm", "a Black Tapeworm" (Bryan 1930; no Egyptian word given)
## *The tapeworm, as an animal ingredient in the Ebers remedies; Bryan prints "Tapeworm" in his list of animals and "a Black Tapeworm" in a hair prescription, with no Egyptian word and no binomial* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The tapeworm is in the Ebers Papyrus as presented in Bryan's 1930 translation (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`), in a Codex source list entry (`Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv`, row 132) that points to Bryan's chapter VII, "Organic Remedies", at line 1404 of the text. That line is the last item of Bryan's list of animals from which the Egyptians took remedies: "Ass Hog Dog Cat Frog Tadpole Scorpion Eel Electric Eel Crab Swordfish Ostrich Locust Tarantula Beetle Bat Wasp Fly Tapeworm" (page 35). Bryan introduces the list (lines 1388-1392) as the animals "whose identity is not in doubt, and who were called upon to sacrifice themselves in the cause of the Healing Art", and he says that to compile a complete list "would not be possible since ... the identity of a considerable number of the animals mentioned is still beyond us" (lines 1382-1388). The names.csv row records that the identification is "Bryan's English identification; no Egyptian word or binomial given in this list".

The tapeworm is named as an ingredient in two hair prescriptions, both quoted by Bryan. In the same chapter (page 33, line 1377) he lists, among the odd preparations, "another Hair-dye which combines a Tape-worm with the Hoof-of-an-Ass and the Vulva-of-a-Bitch". In the chapter on the hair (line 4057; the OCR prints the page as 165, but the neighbouring pages are numbered 156 and 157, so the page number is uncertain [USER: verify]) the prescription is given as: "Hoof-of-an-Ass, roasted, Vulva-of-a-Bitch, a Black Tapeworm, and the uauitworm-which-is-found-in-dung: the whole cooked in Oil and Gum and rubbed well into the scalp!" Bryan sets it, with the womb-of-a-cat prescription, against two milder-sounding ones ("Bile-of-Many-Crabs" and "Dried-Tadpoles-from-the-Canal"), which "are mild, however, compared to two others" (lines 4055-4057); the context, from line 4048, is "The remedies for dyeing grey hairs". The "uauitworm" is Bryan's transliteration of a worm found in dung, and no more is said of it in the lines read.

The tapeworm also appears in the text as a disease to be driven out, not an ingredient. Bryan's chapter on indigestion treats "Roundworms and Tapeworms" (pages 56-57, lines 1951-1995): "The Tapeworm proved more obstinate. Wormcakes were recommended first. One cake was composed of Herbs-of-the-Field and Natron, baked into a cake with Cow's Bile. Another was compounded of Herbs-of-the-Field and Heart-of-the-mesa-Bird, along with Honey, Wine, and Sweet Beer. Or a draught could be used." The draught lists "Red-lead gentet-plant, neswi-corn, ta-bread", "Petroleum" and "Sweet Beer" ("Stir, powder, strain, and take for one day"). "And desperate cases could resort to a poultice", headed "TO DRIVE OUT THE DISEASES CAUSED BY THE TAPEWORM", whose ingredients Bryan gives as "Resin-of-Acanthus", "Blossoms-of-the-Peppermint", "Wild Lettuce" and a "t'as-plant". These are remedies against the worm and use no tapeworm, so they are not uses of the animal.

### Active Components

Tapeworms are flatworms (cestodes) that live in the gut of animals, made of a head with attachment organs and a chain of body segments, with a tough outer layer (tegument). Nothing in Bryan names an active principle, and none specific to this entry is claimed.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is that a tapeworm was one ingredient of a hair preparation, with ass hoof, bitch vulva and a dung worm, cooked in oil and gum and rubbed into the scalp. The Codex gives no protocol. A tapeworm is a parasite, and handling parasite-bearing material is a hazard; hair colour and hair growth have safe modern means.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation given is that of the hair remedy: the ingredients are roasted (the hoof), then "the whole cooked in Oil and Gum and rubbed well into the scalp" (line 4057). No quantity, no schedule and no dose is given in the lines read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Tapeworms are intestinal parasites of mammals, fish and other hosts, and their eggs or larval cysts can infect people who handle or ingest contaminated material. Applying worm material to the scalp has no recognised effect on hair colour or growth. Tapeworm infection in people is diagnosed and treated with antiparasitic drugs. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's vital constitution. The person in the Egyptian texts is plural (*ka*, *ba*, *akh*, *ib*) and the operative power is *heka*, with spell and drug as parts of one intervention, as the project's cultivation spec records; Bryan makes the general point that "Nowhere ... is the Magic Formula advocated as a cure per se, but it is an essential part of the treatment" (page 41, line 1489). None of the tapeworm prescriptions read carries a spell in Bryan's rendering, and none is tied to a deity. The identity of the substance is itself a modern hypothesis resting on Bryan's English.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a list entry and a hair-dye ingredient from a corpus of complaints, with an uncertain identity, no dose and a real infection hazard. The honest use of the entry is as a record of what Bryan reports.

### Contraindications

- **Parasite infection:** tapeworm material can carry eggs or larval cysts; do not handle or apply it.
- **Scalp:** raw animal material on the scalp risks infection and allergy.
- **Tapeworm infection:** suspected infection (segments in stool, abdominal symptoms) needs diagnosis and antiparasitic treatment, not a folk remedy.
- **Identification:** Bryan gives only an English name; what the Egyptian word meant is not settled in the lines read. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** no animal needs to be harmed, but deliberately cultivating or collecting a parasite for a hair preparation has no supported benefit.
