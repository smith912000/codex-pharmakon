# Wild yak blood — "The blood of wild yak" (Clark 1995); "The blood of wild yaks" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011); no Tibetan name is printed in the blood line
## *The blood of the wild yak; Clark's gloss for the wild yak's horn entry gives "Takin: Wild yak: Budorcas taxicolor", and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary pairs "Wild yak" with Bos grunniens, so the animal's identity is not settled by the texts* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The Explanatory Tantra (*bshad rgyud*), chapter 20, "Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", has a section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" listing thirteen kinds of animal product, among them blood (Clark 1995, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` lines 1147-1148). Under the sub-heading "Blood" the entries run: deer blood "is anthelmintic, halts bleeding from the womb [and prevents hangovers]"; goat blood "cures venereal diseases, smallpox, [brown phlegm and leprosy]"; and then, in the same paragraph (line 1173): "The blood of wild yak and of Hodgson's antelope (gtsod): halts diarrhoea." Pig blood, donkey blood, the blood from a rooster's comb and womb blood follow. The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` lines 3539-3540) reads: "The blood of wild yaks and antelopes stop diarrhea." The two translations agree on the substance, the blood of the wild yak, and on the single action, halting diarrhoea. Clark adds the Tibetan name *gtsod* for the antelope only; neither text prints a Tibetan name for the wild yak in this line.

The identity of the wild yak is a real question. In the horn paragraph of the same chapter, Clark prints "rgod.gyag: Takin: Wild yak: Budorcas taxicolor: [has ten names and is also known as hbrong]. Its horn increases [body] heat, disperses tumors, [and dries (accumulations of) pus in the abdomen]" (line 1158), so his glosses for *rgod.gyag* run takin, wild yak, *Budorcas taxicolor*. His classification of meats lists "wild yak (takin)" among the larger hill and game animals (line 672). The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary (lines 7084-7088) has "Yak" with "Bos domesticus mutus" and "Wild yak" with "Bos grunniens", in an OCR-split two-column list where the pairing is by order and not certain. [USER: verify] Whether the blood line refers to the animal Clark calls the takin, or to a bovine wild yak, cannot be settled from the English texts. This is the reason the entry's identity is flagged.

The name list `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` has rows for deer, goat, donkey and menstrual blood (lines 357-360) and none for wild yak blood, so the attestation rests on Clark's line and the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence. Clark's preface (line 83) warns that Tibetan materia medica is often "either totally unidentified or only tentatively identified" and that for animal products he has put the Tibetan names beside the probable English identification where doubt remains.

*The wild yak as food, not folded in.* The animal's meat, a different material, is a separate matter in the same Tantra: "Wild yak meat eliminates cold disorders of the stomach and liver and boosts the digestive heat" (Clark line 679), it is among the foods for phlegm disorders (lines 176, 783 and 1415), and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same (lines 1107, 3249 and 4433). The horn is a third material (line 1158 above). In the Sushruta Samhita (Bhishagratna), *Chamara* (yak) is named among the *Kulacharas* (shore-dwellers) of the *Anupa* (marsh-frequenting) group in Sutrasthana ch.46 (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` lines 658-676), and its flesh is "demulcent, cures an attack of cough, is sweet in taste and digestion and subdues the deranged Vayu and Pittam" (line 712; list row `_sources/sushruta.csv` line 242). That is the flesh of the yak, not the blood of the wild yak. Sushruta is not on this entry's tag line. None of these is the blood.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, blood is a fluid tissue of water, plasma proteins, red cells (rich in haemoglobin and iron), white cells, salts and sugars, and it clots when exposed to air. Nothing links any constituent to the action the Tantra gives, which is to halt diarrhoea.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts say the blood of the wild yak halts diarrhoea. The Codex does not recommend it. Diarrhoea can be a symptom of serious infection or dehydration and needs fluids and, where severe, medical care, and raw animal blood carries real infection risk. The wild yak is a wild animal, and taking or trading wild animals and their products is regulated or banned in many countries; if the animal is the takin, the same applies. The entry exists to record what the Explanatory Tantra says.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither translation gives a preparation, vehicle, quantity or course for wild yak blood; it does not say whether the blood was taken fresh, dried or in a compound. The "medicinal horses" at the end of the chapter (Clark line 1202), raw cane sugar, crystal sugar and honey, are named as vehicles for compounded medicines in general; the text does not say that blood was taken with any of them. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Fresh animal blood can carry bacteria, viruses and parasites, including those of diseases that pass from animals to people, and drinking it is a recognised infection risk. The first treatment of diarrhoea is replacing fluid and salts, and persistent or bloody diarrhoea needs clinical assessment. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** No taste, post-digestive taste or potency is printed for wild yak blood; its only printed action is to halt diarrhoea. The Tantra's rule for meats, that all are sweet in taste and post-digestive taste (Clark line 673), is about flesh, and the statement for wild yak meat that it "eliminates cold disorders of the stomach and liver and boosts the digestive heat" (line 679) belongs to the meat. Neither is carried over to the blood.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Wild yak blood is named in the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica of animal products with a single therapeutic action (Clark line 1173). The wider frame is real but does not reach it. In Clark's account the three humors are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration (line 51) and these three are "the three causes" of wind, bile and phlegm disorders (line 147), so disease is at root a spiritual condition and medicine is one support; the chapter on routine behaviour asks the practitioner to refrain from the ten negative actions, the first of which is "taking life" (line 616). The rejuvenation passage in chapter 23 (lines 1266-1275) does not name wild yak blood, and the lines read give it no *bcud len* use. The tradition has no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The wild yak's blood is a one-line diarrhoea remedy in a list of thirteen kinds of animal product, of an animal the English texts do not fix, and the tradition's first precept is against taking life. The Codex does not recommend it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Infection and hygiene:** raw blood of wild animals can carry pathogens; do not drink or apply it.
- **Diarrhoea:** severe, bloody or prolonged diarrhoea, or any diarrhoea in infants, older people or the ill, needs medical care and fluid replacement.
- **Identity:** the Tibetan text may mean the takin or a bovine wild yak; do not infer which animal is meant.
- **Conservation and law:** taking or trading wild bovids and their products is regulated or banned in many countries; do not buy or collect them.
- **Ethics:** the Tibetan source itself lists "taking life" first among the ten negative actions (Clark line 616).
