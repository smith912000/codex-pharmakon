# I-508 | Ginkgo (*Ginkgo biloba* L.)

**Volume I — Plant-Based Substances**
**Family:** Ginkgoaceae (sole surviving member; living fossil)
**Energetic Tier:** ☿ Shen (cognitive, cerebrovascular) primary | ◉ Qi (vascular, anti-platelet) secondary

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary binomial:** *Ginkgo biloba* L. (1771; originally *Ginkgo* Kaempfer 1712)

**Synonyms & historical names:**
- *Salisburia adiantifolia* Sm.
- *Pterophyllus salisburiensis* J.Nelson
- Historical: *Yajiao* (duck-foot tree — Chinese morphological name)

**Common names:**
- English: Ginkgo, maidenhair tree, ginkgo biloba
- German: *Ginkgobaum*, *Fächerblattbaum* (fan-leaf tree)
- French: *Ginkgo*
- Japanese: *Ichō* (銀杏 = silver apricot — seed appearance); *Ginkyō* (Silver apricot)
- Chinese: 银杏 (*yín xìng*, silver apricot — seed); 白果 (*bái guǒ*, white fruit — seed); 公孙树 (*gōng sūn shù*, grandfather-grandson tree — longevity; tree planted by grandfather, fruit harvested by grandson)
- Korean: 은행나무 (*eunhaengnamu*)
- Sanskrit: Insufficient documentation for *Ginkgo* specifically in Ayurvedic tradition

**Etymology:** *Ginkgo* derives from the Japanese romanization of the Chinese 銀杏 (*yín xìng* → Japanese *ginkyō* → Kaempfer's transcription "Gingko" → Latinized "Ginkgo"). *Biloba* = two-lobed (the distinctive bilobed fan-shaped leaf, divided by central notch). The genus name preserves a 300-year-old phonetic transcription error: Engelbert Kaempfer (1712) wrote "Ginkgo" rather than "Ginkyo"; Linnaeus perpetuated the misspelling which is now the accepted nomenclature.

**Botanical authentication — the living fossil:**
- *Ginkgo biloba* is the sole living representative of division Ginkgophyta, an ancient gymnosperm lineage dating to the Permian (~270 million years ago); its sister lineages are entirely extinct; the modern species is morphologically identical to *Ginkgo* specimens from Jurassic fossil beds (150 million years) — hence "living fossil"
- Dioecious tree (male and female separate trees); tall, distinctive columnar form; fan-shaped leaves with central notch (bilobed) and parallel venation (no midrib — unique among broad-leaved trees; characteristic dichotomous venation); brilliant yellow autumn color
- Female tree: produces seed resembling small plum/apricot with fleshy outer layer; **inner seed kernel = edible (roasted); outer fleshy layer = toxic** (contains butyric acid + ginkgotoxin); characteristic rancid smell of fallen female seeds (butyric acid)
- Commercial cultivation: male trees preferred (no malodorous seeds); gynkgo = primary urban ornamental (pollution-tolerant, pest-resistant, drought-tolerant)
- **Autumn leaf harvest** = optimal pharmaceutical harvest (peak ginkgo flavone glycoside content)

**Phylogenetic isolation:**
Ginkgo biloba is so phylogenetically distant from all other plants in the Codex (division Ginkgophyta vs Magnoliophyta/angiosperms; separated ~300 million years) that its chemical profile is essentially unique — the ginkgo flavone glycosides and ginkgolides are not found in any other plant family. This uniqueness is pharmacological as well as phylogenetic.

**Convergence Score: 1/9** (SBJ ✓; Japanese traditional medicine = second major tradition outside matrix)

**Convergence note:** Ginkgo's single confirmed convergence score reflects geographic restriction to East Asia (China, Japan, Korea) rather than pharmacological insignificance. The Chinese Pharmacopoeia has listed ginkgo seed for centuries, and Japanese Kampo medicine has parallel documentation. Beyond East Asian traditions, ginkgo was entirely unknown until Portuguese traders introduced it to Europe in the early 17th century. The subsequent European scientific and pharmaceutical development of *G. biloba* leaf extracts — not the traditional seed preparations — constitutes one of the most commercially successful botanical pharmaceutical developments in history, generating global annual sales exceeding $1 billion.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

Ginkgo represents a paradox in the Codex: low traditional cross-cultural convergence but one of the most globally recognized and RCT-researched botanical preparations. The explanation is that the traditional Chinese use (seed = *bái guǒ*) is for pulmonary and urinary conditions, while the modern European pharmaceutical development (standardized leaf extract = EGb 761) targets cerebrovascular and cognitive applications for which no substantial traditional record existed — effectively creating a new pharmacological indication for the plant that was not part of the traditional knowledge base.

The chemical uniqueness of ginkgo is matched by its pharmacological uniqueness: ginkgolides (diterpene lactones) are potent and specific PAF (Platelet Activating Factor) receptor antagonists — the most potent natural PAF antagonists known; no other plant family contains ginkgolides. PAF is a major phospholipid mediator of inflammation, platelet aggregation, and endothelial activation; ginkgolide B's PAF antagonism provides anti-inflammatory, anti-platelet, and vascular-protective effects via a mechanism distinct from all other botanical anti-inflammatory agents in the Codex.

The bilobalide constituent provides neuroprotective effects (GABA-A modulation + inhibition of excitotoxicity) that are also pharmacologically unique within the botanical pharmacopoeia.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Ginkgo flavone glycosides (flavonoids specific to Ginkgo biloba):**
- **Quercetin-3-rutinoside (rutin)**, quercetin-3-glucoside, quercetin-3-rhamnoside
- **Kaempferol glycosides** (kaempferol-3-glucoside, kaempferol-3-rutinoside): primary components
- **Isorhamnetin glycosides**: secondary
- **Myricetin glycosides**: trace
- **Amentoflavone** (biflavone): 0.02–0.05% DW; MAO inhibitor (weak); antidepressant contribution (same biflavone as in *Hypericum*, I-501)
- **Bilobetin, ginkgetin, isoginkgetin** (biflavones): 0.1–0.5% DW; anti-inflammatory
- PhEur standardization for EGb 761: **22–27% total flavone glycosides** (expressed as flavone aglycones after acid hydrolysis)
- Ratio: quercetin : kaempferol : isorhamnetin = approximately 1:1.2:0.2 (serves as authentication fingerprint)

**Terpene lactones (pharmacologically unique):**
- **Ginkgolide A, B, C, J, M** (diterpene cage-structured lactones with *tert*-butyl group unique in natural products):
  - Ginkgolide B: most potent PAF (Platelet Activating Factor) receptor antagonist; IC50 ~1 nM for PAF binding inhibition; specific for PAF receptor over all other lipid mediator receptors; reduces platelet aggregation, reduces allergic bronchospasm (PAF role in asthma), reduces ischemia-reperfusion injury
  - Ginkgolide A, C: less potent PAF antagonism
  - Total ginkgolides: 0.2–0.9% DW in leaves
- **Bilobalide** (sesquiterpene lactone):
  - 0.1–0.5% DW
  - Mechanism: GABA-A receptor modulator (negative allosteric modulator at picrotoxin site → slightly anxiogenic at high doses; at therapeutic doses, different effect profile); glutamate NMDA receptor antagonism → neuroprotection; inhibits excitotoxic cell death; mitochondrial protective
  - Key neuroprotective constituent; synergistic with ginkgolides
- PhEur standardization for EGb 761: **5–7% total terpene lactones** (2.8–3.4% ginkgolides, 2.6–3.2% bilobalide)

**Alkylphenols (TOXIC — must be removed):**
- **Ginkgolic acids** (C13:0, C15:0, C15:1, C17:1 anacardic acid homologs): 0.5–10% in leaves; HIGHLY ALLERGENIC (contact dermatitis); cytotoxic; mutagenic in vitro
- PhEur maximum permitted: **<5 ppm ginkgolic acids** in standardized extracts
- Urushiol-cross reactive (same phenol class as poison ivy; significant sensitization potential)
- This is a critical quality parameter: raw ginkgo leaf preparations may contain dangerous ginkgolic acid levels; only PhEur-compliant standardized extracts (EGb 761 or equivalent) are acceptable pharmaceutical products

**EGb 761 — the pharmacological standard:**
Dr. Willmar Schwabe GmbH's EGb 761 is the most studied ginkgo extract globally: 50:1 concentration ratio (50 kg leaves → 1 kg extract); 22–27% flavone glycosides; 5–7% terpene lactones; <5 ppm ginkgolic acids; consistent pharmacokinetic profile; used in >80% of clinical trials cited in this Codex entry. Using non-standardized ginkgo tea, non-standardized extracts, or raw leaves is not equivalent and not clinically validated.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**1. PAF receptor antagonism (ginkgolide B — defining mechanism):**
Platelet Activating Factor (1-O-alkyl-2-acetyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine) is a potent phospholipid mediator triggering platelet aggregation, leukocyte activation, bronchoconstriction, and endothelial dysfunction. Ginkgolide B competitively antagonizes the PAF receptor (PAFR/PTAFR) with nanomolar affinity — the most potent natural PAFR antagonist characterized. Effects:
- Reduced platelet aggregation → anti-thrombotic (ADDS to aspirin/anticoagulant risk)
- Reduced inflammatory leukocyte activation → anti-inflammatory
- Reduced endothelial activation → vascular protective
- Potential bronchodilatory benefit in allergic asthma (PAF role in bronchoconstriction)

**2. Free radical scavenging / antioxidant (flavone glycoside-mediated):**
Quercetin, kaempferol, and isorhamnetin glycosides are potent antioxidants (DPPH IC50 in μM range); scavenge superoxide, hydroxyl, and peroxyl radicals generated during ischemia-reperfusion. In ischemic/hypoxic brain: ROS production → neuronal membrane lipid peroxidation → cell death. Ginkgo flavonoids reduce this oxidative cascade. Relevant to both dementia pathology (oxidative stress component) and acute cerebrovascular ischemia.

**3. Bilobalide — neuroprotection via multiple targets:**
- GABA-A receptor: negative modulator at picrotoxin binding site → reduces GABAergic inhibition (paradoxical, potentially anxiogenic in high doses; complex dose-dependent effects)
- NMDA receptor: partial antagonism → neuroprotection against glutamate excitotoxicity
- Mitochondrial protection: bilobalide stabilizes mitochondrial membrane potential; reduces cytochrome c release (anti-apoptotic); Chandrasekaran et al. 2001 demonstrated bilobalide ≥ 10 μM fully prevents staurosporine-induced apoptosis in neurons
- Neurotrophic: BDNF mRNA upregulation (cell culture studies)

**4. Vasodilatory effects:**
- NO (nitric oxide) bioavailability increase: flavonoids preserve NO by scavenging superoxide that would otherwise destroy NO → improved microvascular circulation
- Direct vasodilation: flavonoids modulate eNOS activity
- Relevant to peripheral vascular disease and cerebrovascular insufficiency

**5. Inhibition of blood viscosity:**
- Anti-platelet (ginkgolide B, PAF antagonism): reduces platelet aggregation
- Rheological improvement: reduced fibrinogen levels in some studies; improved erythrocyte deformability
- Combined: improved microvascular perfusion in conditions of impaired circulation

**Serotonin modulation (controversial, incomplete evidence):**
Some studies suggest ginkgo flavonoids inhibit serotonin reuptake or modulate 5-HT receptor sensitivity; not established as a primary mechanism; may contribute to antidepressant effects observed in some trials.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Cognitive decline / dementia (mild-to-moderate) — Level A:**

1. **Le Bars PL et al. 1997** (*JAMA* 278:1327-1332) — landmark US trial; n=309, mild-to-moderate AD + vascular dementia; EGb 761 (120 mg/day) vs placebo, 52 weeks; statistically significant improvement on ADAS-Cog and GERRI (caregiver-assessed function) — first RCT demonstrating cognitive stabilization with ginkgo; Level A

2. **Kanowski S et al. 1996** (*Phytomedicine* 3:3-14) — n=216, dementia; EGb 761 240 mg/day; significant improvement on cognitive testing vs placebo; Level A

3. **Vellas B et al. 2012** (*Lancet Neurology* 11:851-859) — "GuidAge" trial; n=2,854; EGb 761 240 mg/day vs placebo, 5 years; primary outcome (progression from memory complaint to dementia): NOT significantly different; NEGATIVE primary outcome; consistent with difficulty showing prevention (vs treatment of established decline)

4. **Tan MS et al. 2015** (*Journal of Alzheimer's Disease* 43:589-603) — Meta-analysis 9 RCTs; EGb 761 consistently superior to placebo on cognitive measures in dementia; Level A aggregate

5. **Hashiguchi M et al. 2015** (*BMC Complementary and Alternative Medicine* 15:12) — Meta-analysis; EGb 761 significantly improves MMSE and ADAS-Cog vs placebo; Level A

**Current regulatory position:** EGb 761 is registered as a pharmaceutical drug in Germany (Tebonin), France (Tanakan), and multiple European countries for "cerebral insufficiency," cognitive decline, dementia, tinnitus, and peripheral arterial disease. It is NOT approved by FDA for dementia prevention or treatment (insufficient US RCT evidence by FDA standards).

**Tinnitus — Level A:**
- **Rejali D et al. 2004** (*Journal of Laryngology & Otology* 118:679-681) — EGb 761 vs placebo for tinnitus; significant reduction in tinnitus severity; Level A (smaller trial)
- Multiple European studies consistent; WHO traditional use monograph supports this indication
- Cochrane 2013 (Hilton M): inconclusive due to heterogeneity; positive trend

**Peripheral arterial disease / intermittent claudication — Level A:**
- **Pittler MH, Ernst E 2000** (Cochrane Review): meta-analysis of 8 RCTs; ginkgo superior to placebo for pain-free walking distance in intermittent claudication; Level A

**Altitude sickness prevention — Level B:**
- Multiple RCTs; EGb 761 240 mg/day vs placebo for high-altitude travel; mixed results; some positive; mechanism: improved microvascular perfusion at altitude

**Dementia prevention (healthy older adults) — Negative (Level A):**
- **DeKosky ST et al. 2008** (*JAMA* 300:2253-2262) — "GEM" trial; n=3,069 older adults without dementia; EGb 761 240 mg/day vs placebo, 6.1 years; NO reduction in dementia incidence (primary outcome); Negative Level A

**Antidepressant / anxiety (adjunct) — Level B:**
- EGb 761 as augmentation to antidepressants: several Level B studies; ginkgo may improve antidepressant response in some patients; insufficient evidence for standalone antidepressant recommendation
- Possible sexual dysfunction reversal associated with SSRI use (several case series; not confirmed in RCTs)

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The grandfather-grandson tree:**
The Chinese name 公孙树 (*gōng sūn shù* = grandfather-grandson tree) encodes the profound temporal scale of ginkgo: trees mature slowly (decades before producing seeds), live for extreme lengths of time (some documented specimens >3,000 years), and were traditionally planted at birth with the expectation that grandchildren would harvest the first nuts. This temporal perspective is pharmacologically resonant — the ginkgolide chemical class took ~250 million years of gymnosperm evolution to produce, and the medical applications took most of human civilization to partially characterize.

**Hiroshima's survivors:**
The atomic bombing of Hiroshima on August 6, 1945 destroyed most of the city within 2 km of the hypocenter. Among the few living things that survived within 1 km: six *Ginkgo biloba* trees at Housenbou Temple. All six resprouted the following spring (1946) — ginkgos can regenerate from the base after total crown destruction. These trees, now designated "A-bomb survivor trees" (*hibakujumoku*), continue to grow in Hiroshima. The ginkgo's radiation resistance is attributed to its rapid DNA repair mechanisms, antioxidant capacity, and bark chemistry — though this is speculation; the actual survival mechanism has not been studied systematically.

**Engelbert Kaempfer and the Western discovery:**
Engelbert Kaempfer (1651–1716), a German physician and naturalist employed by the Dutch East India Company, observed ginkgo trees in Japan in 1691–1692 and brought the first seeds to Europe (Leiden botanical garden, ~1730). His 1712 publication *Amoenitatum Exoticarum* introduced *Ginkgo* to European botany. The typo "Ginkgo" (for "Ginkyo") persists in the Linnaean naming to this day. Kaempfer's transfer of ginkgo from East Asia to Europe initiated a 200-year process of naturalization that made ginkgo the most widely planted ornamental tree in temperate urban environments worldwide — setting the stage for the 20th-century pharmaceutical development of EGb 761 in European phytomedicine.

**Goethe and the bilobed leaf:**
Johann Wolfgang von Goethe (1749–1832) was fascinated by ginkgo's bilobed leaf — a single leaf divided into two lobes — and composed the poem *Ginkgo biloba* (1815, in the *West-Eastern Divan*) framing the divided-yet-united leaf as a metaphor for the mystical union of self and other in love. The poem is engraved on a ginkgo leaf specimen sent to Marianne von Willemer. Goethe's engagement with ginkgo's morphology reflects the Romantic fascination with *Urform* — archetypal plant forms — and positions ginkgo's unique leaf as philosophically significant. The bilobed leaf is now the global symbol of the ginkgo pharmaceutical brand.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Standardized EGb 761 — clinical doses:**
- **Cognitive decline / dementia:** 240 mg EGb 761/day (120 mg twice daily or 240 mg once daily); minimum 12 weeks before assessing response; evidence base strongest at this dose
- **Tinnitus:** 120–240 mg/day; evidence for tinnitus at 120 mg (Rejali 2004) and 240 mg
- **Peripheral arterial disease:** 120–160 mg/day
- **Vertigo:** 120 mg/day (European phytomedicine standard)

**Preparation requirements:**
- Use only **PhEur-compliant standardized extract** (EGb 761 or equivalent): 22–27% flavone glycosides + 5–7% terpene lactones + <5 ppm ginkgolic acids
- Raw leaves, non-standardized products, teas: NOT equivalent; ginkgolic acid exposure risk; unpredictable active content
- PhEur *Ginkgonis extractum siccum normatum* = quality standard

**German Commission E approved (1994):**
- Symptomatic treatment of deficiency of cerebral blood flow (vertigo, memory impairment, difficulty concentrating, headache)
- Peripheral arterial occlusive disease (Fontaine stage IIb: intermittent claudication)
- Vertigo and tinnitus of vascular and involutional origin

**EMA Well-established use:** Memory, concentration, vertigo, tinnitus (adults, 240 mg/day EGb 761).

**CONTRAINDICATIONS — CRITICAL:**
- **Anticoagulant/antiplatelet therapy (warfarin, aspirin, clopidogrel, heparin, NOACs):** additive bleeding risk via ginkgolide B PAF antagonism + flavonoid antiplatelet effects; ginkgo is CONTRAINDICATED in combination with anticoagulants without medical supervision; case reports of serious bleeding (intracranial hemorrhage, subdural hematoma)
- **Pre-operative:** discontinue 2 weeks before surgery (bleeding risk)
- **Epilepsy:** theoretical lowering of seizure threshold (bilobalide GABA-A negative modulation); avoid in epilepsy or with proconvulsant medications
- Pregnancy and lactation: insufficient safety data; avoid
- Ginkgolic acid allergy (urushiol cross-reactivity): avoid all ginkgo preparations

**Drug interactions (critical tier):**
- Warfarin: elevated INR → hemorrhage risk; multiple case reports; AVOID combination
- Aspirin: additive antiplatelet → bleeding risk; case series; use with caution; may combine with monitoring
- Clopidogrel: additive antiplatelet; avoid
- MAO inhibitors: theoretical serotonin risk (flavonoid MAO inhibition); case reports with phenelzine (limited)
- Cyclosporine: potential CYP3A4 induction (minor); monitor
- SSRIs/antidepressants: theoretical serotonin augmentation; generally used as adjunct in clinical practice but document and monitor

**TCM seed preparation (*bái guǒ*) — separate tradition:**
- White ginkgo seeds (roasted kernel): 10–15 seeds/day; for cough, asthma (reducing sputum), vaginal discharge (astringent); NOT for cognitive indications
- Ginkgotoxin in seeds: raw seeds toxic; roasting reduces toxicity; maximum 10 seeds/day in adults (ginkgotoxin = 4-O-methylpyridoxine; GABA synthesis inhibitor → convulsions at high doses; children especially sensitive; death from seed overconsumption documented)
- Seed preparations are entirely different from leaf extracts (EGb 761); different chemistry, different indications, different safety profile
