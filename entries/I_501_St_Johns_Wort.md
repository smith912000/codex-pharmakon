# I-501 | St. John's Wort (*Hypericum perforatum* L.)

**Volume I — Plant-Based Substances**
**Family:** Hypericaceae (formerly Clusiaceae/Guttiferae)
**Energetic Tier:** ☿ Shen (neuromodulatory) primary | ◉ Qi (anti-inflammatory, wound-healing) secondary

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary binomial:** *Hypericum perforatum* L. (1753, *Species Plantarum*)

**Synonyms & historical names:**
- *Hypericum officinarum* Crantz
- *Hypericum vulgare* Lam.

**Common names:**
- English: St. John's Wort, Perforate St. John's Wort, Klamath weed, goatweed
- German: *Johanniskraut* (John's herb), *Tüpfelhartheu*
- French: *Millepertuis perforé*
- Italian: *Iperico*
- Spanish: *Hipérico*, *Hierba de San Juan*
- Arabic: *Dawa al-junun* (medicine of madness) — *Hypericum* spp.
- Chinese: 贯叶连翘 (*guàn yè lián qiào*, "penetrating-leaf forsythia")
- Sanskrit: *Manasimitra* (friend of the mind) — *Hypericum* spp.

**Botanical authentication:**
- Erect perennial herb 30–100 cm; stems with two raised longitudinal ridges; bright yellow pentamerous flowers with black glandular dots on petals and sepals; leaves opposite, sessile, with pale oil glands visible when held to light ("perforatum" = perforated, from translucent oil glands); stamens numerous, united in three bundles (characteristic Hypericaceae); fruit a trilocular capsule
- Black dots on petals and sepals = hypericin-containing glands; translucent leaf dots = hyperforin-containing essential oil glands; presence of both = authentication
- Harvest: aerial parts at peak bloom (June 24, feast of St. John the Baptist — hence "St. John's Wort"); optimal hypericin content at full flowering
- Globally naturalized; native to Europe, West Asia, North Africa; considered invasive in North America, Australia (toxic to livestock — photosensitization via hypericin in pale-skinned animals)

**Taxonomy note:** Hypericaceae contains ~550 species in *Hypericum*. *H. perforatum* is the pharmacological standard; *H. calycinum* (rose of Sharon), *H. androsaemum* (tutsan), and *H. olympicum* are ornamentals with different chemical profiles. The medicinal species is *perforatum* specifically.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

*H. perforatum* represents one of the most extensively documented medicinal plants in the Western tradition and the single most RCT-validated botanical for mood disorders. The convergence across DMM, CM, and Ayurvedic/Tibetan traditions on "melancholy," nerve pain, and wound healing maps directly onto modern pharmacology: hyperforin's neurotransmitter reuptake inhibition explains the mood effects; hypericin's photodynamic activity explains the antimicrobial/antiviral wound properties; both fractions contribute to anti-inflammatory effects relevant to nerve pain.

The name "St. John's Wort" encodes a medieval European synthesis: the red blood-like exudate when flowers are crushed (hypericin + pseudohypericin in hypericin glands) linked the plant to blood, wounds, and the feast of John the Baptist (June 24 — harvest timing). The older designation *herba daemonis fuga* ("fleeing of demons") directly names the archaic European psychiatric indication.

The pharmacological explanation for cross-traditional psychiatric use emerges from hyperforin: a phloroglucinol derivative that inhibits reuptake of serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine, GABA, and glutamate via a non-transporter mechanism (ion conduction inhibition; activation of TRPC6 channels). This multi-neurotransmitter reuptake profile is genuinely unusual — no synthetic antidepressant shares it — and may explain both clinical activity and the distinctive phenomenological reports across traditions.

The CYP3A4/P-glycoprotein induction by hyperforin (clinically significant at therapeutic doses) was not known to any traditional system but represents the most consequential pharmacokinetic interaction in herbal medicine — rendering St. John's Wort a drug interaction hazard of the highest practical importance.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Primary active constituents:**

**Naphthodianthrone pigments (hypericins):**
- Hypericin: 0.03–0.3% DW; concentrated in black glandular dots of petals, sepals, stamens; absorbs maximally at 545 nm (red); photosensitizing chromophore; generates singlet oxygen under UV — photodynamic mechanism
- Pseudohypericin: 0.2–1.0% DW; predominant naphthodianthrone; same photodynamic properties
- European Pharmacopoeia (PhEur) standardizes to ≥0.08% total hypericins calculated as hypericin
- Biosynthetically derived from emodin anthrone dimerization via oxidative coupling

**Phloroglucinol derivatives (hyperforins):**
- Hyperforin: 2–4% DW in fresh flowering herb; 0.1–3.0% in commercial dried preparations (highly unstable; oxidizes readily; principal source of preparation variability)
- Adhyperforin: minor congener with comparable activity
- Hyperforin is the primary antidepressant-active constituent; standardization to ≥0.3% hyperforin correlates with clinical efficacy in RCTs
- Mechanism: TRPC6 calcium channel activation → altered sodium conductance → non-transporter-mediated reuptake inhibition; distinct from classical SSRI mechanism

**Flavonoids:**
- Hyperoside (quercetin-3-galactoside): 0.7–1.5% DW
- Rutin (quercetin-3-rutinoside): 0.3–1.7% DW
- Amentoflavone (biflavone): 0.01–0.05% DW; MAO inhibitor (competitive; reversible; IC50 ~1 μM); initially proposed as primary antidepressant mechanism (now superseded by hyperforin evidence)
- Isoquercitrin, quercitrin, quercetin: additional flavonol glycosides
- I3,II8-biapigenin: second biflavone; anxiolytic via GABA-A affinity

**Xanthones:**
- 1,3,6,7-tetrahydroxyxanthone (kielcorin and congeners): trace; MAO inhibitory activity

**Essential oil (EO):**
- 2-methyl-3-buten-2-ol: 0.1–0.3% DW; sedative-active
- α-pinene, β-pinene, caryophyllene, geraniol: terpene composition
- EO <1% total

**Tannins:**
- Proanthocyanidins (oligomeric): 3–12% DW; wound-healing astringency; anti-inflammatory
- Catechins: monomeric flavan-3-ols

**Phenolic acids:**
- Chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid, ferulic acid: 1–3% DW

**Preparation-dependent composition matrix:**
| Preparation | Hypericin | Hyperforin | Stability notes |
|-------------|-----------|------------|-----------------|
| Fresh herb | 0.2–0.3% | 2–4% | Best hyperforin content |
| Dried herb (standard) | 0.1–0.3% | 0.5–2% | Hyperforin oxidizes during drying |
| Ethanolic extract (60%) | Concentrated | Concentrated (if fresh → extraction) | Gold standard for RCTs |
| Aqueous tea infusion | Present | Minimal (insoluble in water) | Not equivalent to extracts |
| LI 160 extract (60% EtOH, 5:1 ratio) | Standardized 0.3% hypericin | ~5% | Neuraxpharm; most-studied extract |
| WS 5570 extract (80% MeOH) | Standardized 0.2% hypericin | ≥5% hyperforin | Schwabe; high hyperforin |
| WS 5572 extract (50% EtOH) | Standardized 0.3% hypericin | ~0.5% hyperforin | Low hyperforin; reduced interaction risk? |

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Primary mechanism — hyperforin (TRPC6-mediated multi-neurotransmitter reuptake inhibition):**
Hyperforin activates Transient Receptor Potential Canonical 6 (TRPC6) channels, causing Na⁺ influx. This depolarization-independent mechanism reduces the sodium gradient that normally drives neurotransmitter reuptake transporters (SERT, DAT, NET). Result: inhibition of serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine, GABA, and glutamate reuptake simultaneously — a pharmacological profile no synthetic drug replicates. Muller et al. 1998 (*Pharmacopsychiatry* 31 Suppl 1:16) established hyperforin as the primary antidepressant constituent. Chatterjee SS et al. 1998 confirmed TRPC6 as the molecular target.

**Secondary mechanism — hypericin photodynamic activity:**
Under UV/visible light, hypericin generates reactive oxygen species (singlet ¹O₂, superoxide anion, hydroxyl radical) via Type I/II photodynamic reactions. This explains:
- Antimicrobial: effective against *S. aureus*, *E. coli*, Candida spp. under light exposure
- Antiviral: HIV, herpes simplex, influenza inactivation in vitro (light-dependent)
- Wound healing: photobiomodulation at non-photodynamic doses
- **Phototoxicity:** the same mechanism causes cutaneous photosensitization in animals and potentially humans at high doses (clinical significance debated at oral doses; topical + sunlight = confirmed risk)

**Tertiary mechanism — MAO inhibition:**
Amentoflavone competitively inhibits MAO-A and MAO-B (IC50 ~1 μM amentoflavone). This was the original proposed antidepressant mechanism (Suzuki 1984). Now considered secondary: amentoflavone concentrations in clinical extracts are too low after absorption to achieve significant in vivo MAO inhibition. Clinically relevant MAO inhibition from *H. perforatum* is unlikely at standard doses. However, this mechanism remains incompletely excluded and contributes to drug interaction caution with serotonergic drugs.

**CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein induction (clinically dominant pharmacokinetic mechanism):**
Hyperforin activates the Pregnane X Receptor (PXR/NR1I2), upregulating CYP3A4 mRNA/protein expression and P-glycoprotein (MDR1/ABCB1) transport. This is the most clinically significant action of *H. perforatum* — not therapeutic but as a drug interaction mechanism:
- CYP3A4 induction: accelerated metabolism of ~50% of all drugs (cyclosporine, indinavir, warfarin, oral contraceptives, digoxin, amitriptyline, irinotecan, imatinib, midazolam, and hundreds more)
- P-gp induction: reduced intestinal absorption and increased efflux of substrate drugs
- Onset: 10–14 days of regular use; resolution: ~2 weeks after discontinuation
- Magnitude: similar to rifampicin (the benchmark CYP3A4 inducer) for some substrates

**Anti-inflammatory mechanisms:**
- NF-κB inhibition (hyperforin)
- COX-2 suppression (flavonoids, hyperforin)
- IL-6 and TNF-α reduction
- Relevant to historical "nerve pain" indications

**GABA-A effects:**
- I3,II8-biapigenin: GABA-A PAM (anxiolytic)
- Hyperforin GABA reuptake inhibition: increased synaptic GABA
- Together: contributes to anxiolytic component documented in clinical studies

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Depression — Major depressive disorder (mild-to-moderate):**

**Level A — RCTs and meta-analyses:**

1. **Linde K et al. 1996** (*BMJ* 313:253-258) — First systematic Cochrane-style review; 23 RCTs, n=1,757; *H. perforatum* significantly superior to placebo (RR 2.67, 95% CI 1.78–4.01) and equally effective as standard antidepressants for mild-to-moderate depression; side effect profile significantly more favorable than tricyclics

2. **Shelton RC et al. 2001** (*JAMA* 285:1978-1986) — n=200, mild-to-moderate MDD; LI 160 (900 mg/day) vs placebo; HDRS outcome: no significant difference from placebo (negative trial; controversial — design using HDRS response threshold ≥50% reduction criticized as inappropriately stringent)

3. **Hypericum Depression Trial Study Group 2002** (*JAMA* 287:1807-1814) — n=340, moderate MDD; *H. perforatum* vs sertraline vs placebo; neither St. John's Wort nor sertraline separated from placebo on HDRS full response at 8 weeks; both superior to placebo on secondary measure (CGI); interpreted as trial failing to demonstrate activity of either treatment (sertraline's failure = validation issue with this trial design)

4. **Linde K et al. 2005** (*British Journal of Psychiatry* 186:99-107) — Updated meta-analysis, 37 trials; consistent superiority over placebo for mild-to-moderate depression; equivalent to standard antidepressants with fewer side effects; not demonstrated effective for severe MDD

5. **Kasper S et al. 2006** (*European Archives of Psychiatry and Clinical Neuroscience* 256:539-547) — WS 5570 (600 mg/day) vs placebo, mild-to-moderate MDD; significantly superior on HDRS, HAMD-17, CGI (Level A)

6. **Rahimi R et al. 2009** (*Progress in Neuro-Psychopharmacology & Biological Psychiatry* 33:118-127) — Meta-analysis 13 RCTs, moderate depression; significantly superior to placebo; equivalent to SSRIs; fewer adverse effects, better tolerability

**Current consensus (2024):** *H. perforatum* at standardized doses (300 mg extract TID, standardized to 0.3% hypericin ± 5% hyperforin) is effective for mild-to-moderate depression; NOT demonstrated effective for severe MDD or severe anxiety; supported by Commission E, ESCOP, EMA, WHO, and Cochrane reviews for indicated population.

**Anxiety — Level B:**
- Woelk 2000: *H. perforatum* vs valium for anxiety (small study); comparable
- Multiple studies show secondary anxiolytic effects in depressed patients; insufficient evidence as primary anxiolytic indication separate from depression

**Menopausal symptoms — Level B:**
- Uebelhack R et al. 2006 (*Obstetrics & Gynecology* 107:247-255): *H. perforatum* + isoflavones vs placebo; reduced hot flash frequency and severity (Level A)
- Multiple Level B studies for climacteric symptoms; MenoChi standardized extracts marketed

**Somatic pain (nerve pain) — Level B:**
- Traditional indication for sciatica, neuralgia; limited RCT data; anti-inflammatory mechanism plausible; insufficient for evidence-based recommendation alone

**Topical wound healing — Level B:**
- St. John's Wort oil (flowers macerated in olive oil → hypericin-rich red oil); burns, abrasions; traditional European first-aid; limited controlled trials but consistent historical and mechanistic basis

**Drug interactions — CRITICAL Level A evidence:**
- Piscitelli SC et al. 2000 (*Lancet* 355:547-548): *H. perforatum* reduced indinavir AUC by 57% — HIV treatment failure risk (landmark interaction report)
- Multiple studies: cyclosporine reduction → organ transplant rejection (serious, potentially fatal)
- Oral contraceptive failure → breakthrough bleeding, unintended pregnancy (documented case series)
- Warfarin INR reduction → thromboembolic risk
- Digoxin levels reduced ~25%
- Irinotecan AUC reduced 42% → chemotherapy failure risk

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The "demon-fleeing herb" — archaic psychiatric understanding:**
The medieval European designation *fuga daemonum* ("flight of demons") is not mere superstition. In pre-modern European medicine, "demons" and "melancholy" occupied overlapping conceptual space. Paracelsus (1493–1541) systematized this, recommending *Johanniskraut* for "phantasmata" — hallucinatory and melancholic states. The German physician Johann Weyer (1515–1588) prescribed it for psychotic-adjacent conditions. The historical trajectory from "demon-fleeing" → "nerve remedy" → "antidepressant" tracks a 2,000-year refinement of essentially the same observation.

**Dioscorides (65 CE):** "*Hyperikon* ... applied to burns, it relieves the ache; drunk in wine it expels bile, and is good for those bitten by venomous beasts." The "bile" reference encodes humoral understanding of melancholy (Greek *melas cholē* = black bile; the humor of depression). The wound-healing and anti-toxic themes persist across all subsequent uses.

**Ibn Sīnā's Avicennian synthesis:** Al-Qānūn specifies *Hypericum* for "paralysis of nerves," sciatica, and the condition he terms *sawdā* (black bile excess = Galenic melancholy). This is a remarkably accurate anticipation of the modern antidepressant and peripheral neuropathy indications — documented 1,000 years before neuroscience.

**The red oil tradition:** The brilliant red-orange oil obtained by macerating fresh flowers in olive oil for several weeks (hypericin dissolves into lipid phase) became Europe's most widely used external wound remedy from antiquity through the 19th century. The red color was cosmologically significant — blood-like, signaling wound affinity (doctrine of signatures coinciding with validated topical activity). The oil was called *Oleum Hyperici* in Latin pharmacopoeias and appears in virtually every European materia medica from Galen onward.

**Photosensitization paradox:** Livestock grazing heavily on *H. perforatum* develop severe photosensitization (skin necrosis, ear sloughing in pale-skinned animals) — the "Klamath weed" problem in California's Klamath River basin after 19th-century introduction. This toxicity is exploited in photodynamic cancer therapy research (hypericin as photosensitizer). The same compound that makes it a wound healer externally (where light exposure activates antimicrobial ROS) makes it a phototoxic hazard systemically. This dose- and route-dependent duality is a recurring Codex theme.

**Phenomenology of hyperforin's mechanism:** Users of St. John's Wort describe effects different from SSRIs — less "emotional blunting," more "lightening" quality. This is pharmacologically consistent with multi-neurotransmitter reuptake inhibition (serotonin + dopamine + norepinephrine simultaneously) vs selective serotonin reuptake alone. The dopamine component may preserve motivational hedonia that pure SSRIs sometimes suppress. This phenomenological distinctiveness has been noted in clinical trials through CGI observer-rated "naturalness" of response.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Standardized preparations (evidence-based doses):**
- LI 160 (Remotiv/Jarsin 300): 300 mg TID (standardized 0.3% hypericin, ~5% hyperforin) — most studied extract; equivalent to 2–4.5 g dried herb/day
- WS 5570 (Neuroplant): 300 mg TID or 600 mg QD (0.2% hypericin, ≥5% hyperforin)
- WS 5572: 300 mg TID (0.3% hypericin, ~0.5% hyperforin — lower CYP induction?)
- Onset: 4–6 weeks for mood effects; trial duration minimum 6 weeks before assessing response

**Traditional preparation:**
- Tea/infusion: 2–4 g dried herb per 250 mL water; steep 10 minutes; 1–3 cups/day; NOTE: hypericin extracts well; hyperforin does NOT (poorly water-soluble) — tea lacks primary antidepressant constituent; appropriate for mild tension, traditional use, but not equivalent to standardized extract for depression
- Macerated oil: fresh flowers packed in olive oil, sealed, exposed to sun 4–6 weeks; classic red-orange color indicates hypericin; topical only; burns, abrasions, bruises

**Commission E (German Commission E Monograph, 1984/updated):** APPROVED for:
- Internal: psychovegetative disturbances, depressive moods, anxiety and/or nervous unrest
- External: blunt injuries, wounds, myalgia (oil preparation)
- NOT approved for: severe depression, bipolar disorder

**EMA/HMPC Monograph:** Well-established use for mild-to-moderate depressive episodes (adults); traditional use for minor skin lesions, nerve pain, mild anxiety.

**CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- Pregnancy and lactation (insufficient safety data; avoid)
- Concurrent use of drugs with narrow therapeutic index metabolized by CYP3A4 or P-gp substrates (this is the critical contraindication — the list is vast):
  - Immunosuppressants (cyclosporine, tacrolimus) → ABSOLUTE contraindication
  - Antiretrovirals (protease inhibitors, NNRTIs) → ABSOLUTE contraindication
  - Oral contraceptives → HIGH risk; use additional barrier methods
  - Anticoagulants (warfarin, acenocoumarol) → monitor INR; likely interaction
  - Antineoplastics (irinotecan, imatinib, docetaxel) → ABSOLUTE contraindication
  - Digoxin → monitor levels
  - Sertraline, paroxetine, other SSRIs → serotonin syndrome risk (theoretical; case reports)
  - Triptans → serotonin syndrome risk
  - Voriconazole, posaconazole → antifungal failure risk
  - Amitriptyline, nortriptyline → reduced levels
- Bipolar disorder: risk of mania induction (as with all antidepressants)
- Bipolar history without mood stabilizer coverage: avoid

**Photosensitivity precaution:** Fair-skinned individuals on high-dose standardized extracts should limit intense UV exposure; clinically significant phototoxicity at standard doses is uncommon but reported.

**Duration:** No established time limit for depression treatment (Commission E previously listed 4–6 weeks; current EMA allows longer for established depression); valepotriate concern (from valerian) does not apply here.

**Authentication markers for quality:**
1. Red/dark red extract color (hypericin pigmentation)
2. PhEur certificate: ≥0.08% total hypericins in dried herb
3. Hyperforin content declared (critical for depression efficacy; absent from many commodity products)
4. Species verification: *H. perforatum* (black dots present on petals/sepals/stamens)

**CYP interaction disclosure requirement:** Any practitioner recommending St. John's Wort is professionally obligated to conduct a full drug interaction review. No other botanical in the Codex has equivalent interaction breadth or severity.

**Pharmacological classification context:** *H. perforatum* is the only botanical substance in this Codex that has been directly compared to FDA/EMA-approved pharmaceuticals (SSRIs, tricyclics) in RCTs and found equivalent for the indicated population. This distinction is pharmacologically and clinically significant. The evidence base meets Level A standard by any reasonable methodology — Commission E, Cochrane, EMA, and WHO all confirm this.
