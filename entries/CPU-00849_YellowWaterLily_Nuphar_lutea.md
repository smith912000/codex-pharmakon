# Yellow Water Lily — Brandy-bottle, Yellow Pond-lily, Nenuphar, *nūfar* (Perso-Arabic)

## *Nuphar lutea* (L.) Sm. | Family: Nymphaeaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, SBJ
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry (Galenic/Unani); astringent and refrigerant
**Training Phase:** Damping — cooling and binding an over-heated, over-excited system

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian:** Aquatic and marsh plants appear in the Mesopotamian pharmacy as cooling, binding remedies for fluxes and inflammations. Water-lily rhizomes fit this refrigerant-astringent category, used as poultices and washes; species-level identification from the tablets is approximate and should be read as tradition rather than a pinned botanical claim.

**Avicenna (Qanun):** *Nūfar* (nenuphar) is a named cooling agent in the Perso-Arabic materia medica, classed as cold-moist to cold-dry and used to quiet heat, temper excess sexual heat, calm palpitations of "hot" origin, and check fluxes. The anaphrodisiac reputation — a cooling herb to damp inflamed desire — is at its clearest here.

**Dioscorides (De Materia Medica):** The Greek herbal records *nymphaia* with a strongly astringent root, prescribed for fluxes and dysentery and reputed to cool sexual appetite when taken in wine — an early and explicit statement of the anaphrodisiac and astringent uses that persisted into European herbalism.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The water lily is the archetypal plant of cooled, still water — floating, closing at night, rooted in cold mud. The doctrine-of-signatures reading (a cold, watery plant to cool a hot, watery excess) aligns unusually well with the pharmacology: *Nuphar* rhizome concentrates unusual sulphur-bearing sesquiterpene thioalkaloids, and modern work finds these same molecules acting on inflammatory and proliferative signalling. The symbolic "damping" identity is, again, echoed by measurable chemistry rather than being purely poetic.

### Active Components

- **Sesquiterpene thioalkaloids** — the signature chemistry of the genus, including nupharidine-type and **thiobinupharidine** dimers such as **6,6′-dihydroxythiobinupharidine (DTBN)**; nupharolutine is among the characterised rhizome alkaloids.
- These sulphur-containing dimeric alkaloids are the presumed carriers of the antimicrobial, antiparasitic and immunomodulatory activity.
- Tannins and mucilage in the rhizome account for the classical astringency.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Positioned in the **Damping** phase as a cold, binding agent for an inflamed, over-stimulated state. Traditional uses cluster around three actions: **astringent** (checking fluxes, toning lax mucosa, topical wound and skin washes), **anaphrodisiac** (the Unani/Dioscoridean cooling of excess sexual heat), and **antimicrobial** (topical application to inflamed or infected tissue). In Kenetica it is a corrective rather than a daily tonic — reserved for states of heat and excess, used briefly and topically by preference, given the toxicity profile below.

The anaphrodisiac reputation is worth reading through both a doctrinal and a pharmacological lens. In the humoral schema, sexual desire was a "hot, moist" impulse of the generative faculty, so a drug classed as cold and drying was the logical corrective — and the water lily, a plant rooted in cold water and crowned with a closed, flask-like fruit (the English "brandy-bottle"), read to the medieval eye as a signature of continence and containment. This is the same symbolic logic that attached anaphrodisiac claims to the white and blue water lilies of the *Nymphaea* genus, and the monastic tradition that reputedly used such preparations to temper the appetites of the cloister. Pharmacologically the rhizome's sesquiterpene thioalkaloids are genuinely bioactive membrane-active compounds, but there is no controlled evidence that they suppress libido in humans; the anaphrodisiac use is best treated as a doctrinally-driven tradition rather than a demonstrated effect. What the chemistry does support is the astringent and topical-antimicrobial direction — the tannin-rich, alkaloid-bearing rhizome is a plausible wound wash and mucosal astringent, which is the use most defensible on modern grounds and the one Kenetica foregrounds.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, *Nuphar lutea* extracts and their nupharidine alkaloids show genuine, if preclinical, bioactivity. El-On et al. found a methanolic rhizome/leaf extract potently active against *Leishmania major* promastigotes and intracellular amastigotes in vitro (IC50 ≈ 2 µg/mL) with synergy alongside paromomycin, attributing activity to a thermostable, uncharged alkaloid fraction ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2009.01.011)). Waidha et al. showed that the dimeric thioalkaloid DTBN selectively inhibits cysteine proteases, most strongly cathepsin S (IC ≈ 3.2 µM), via a proposed covalent interaction at the catalytic cysteine ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules26164743)). Ozer et al. reported that semi-purified *Nuphar* leaf extract (nupharidines) suppressed NF-κB signalling and reduced experimental lung-metastatic load in a murine melanoma model, synergising with cisplatin ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.7150/jca.18435)).

**Evidence grade: PRECLINICAL / TRADITIONAL.** The antimicrobial, antiparasitic and immunomodulatory data are real but confined to in vitro and animal models; the classical astringent and anaphrodisiac uses are traditional. No controlled human trials are cited, and none should be inferred.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Cold and dry; astringent, refrigerant, anaphrodisiac, flux-checking.
- **TCM (by functional analogy):** cooling, astringent; would be read as clearing heat and consolidating — no canonical classical entry is claimed.
- **Ayurvedic (attributed by energetics):** *rasa* astringent/bitter; *virya* cooling (shita); *vipaka* pungent; pitta-pacifying, potentially vata-aggravating and kapha-neutral in the cold-dry direction.

### Contraindications

- **⚠️ Alkaloid toxicity.** The sesquiterpene thioalkaloids are bioactive at low doses; internal use carries a real risk of **cardiovascular and neurological toxicity** and must not be self-administered. Prefer external preparations; keep internal use expert-supervised or avoid entirely.
- **Pregnancy — avoid.** Both the alkaloid toxicity and the traditional anaphrodisiac/cooling and flux-altering profile make it unsuitable in pregnancy; avoid as a firm precaution.
- Not for paediatric internal use. Avoid combining with cardioactive or neuroactive drugs given the alkaloid profile.
- Wide chemotypic variation means potency is unpredictable across sources — a further reason to avoid casual internal dosing.
