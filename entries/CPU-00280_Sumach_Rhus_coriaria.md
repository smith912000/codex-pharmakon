### Origin & Tradition

**Greco-Roman (Dioscorides):** Dioscorides described *rhous* in *De Materia Medica*, distinguishing the culinary/tanning sumach (the fruit-clusters and the ground seed sprinkled on food, "the sumach used on meat") from the leather-tanner's *rhous* (*rhous byrsodepsikē*, from which the epithet *coriaria* — "of the tanners" — derives). He recorded its use as an astringent for looseness of the bowels, for bleeding, for weeping sores, and as a mouth and gum wash. This is a genuinely Mediterranean, not Chinese, materia medica: *R. coriaria* is native to the Mediterranean basin and the Near East, and has no place in the classical Chinese canon.

**Islamic / Unani (Avicenna):** *Summāq* appears in the *Canon of Medicine* classed as cold and dry, a strong *qābiḍ* (astringent/binding) agent. It was used to check diarrhoea and dysentery, to strengthen a weak or "loose" stomach, to stop haemorrhage, and as a gargle for relaxed gums and sore throat — all applications that follow logically from its high tannin load. The dried, ground drupes remain a defining sour spice of Levantine, Persian and Anatolian cooking (a key component of the za'atar blend), where the sourness comes largely from malic and other organic acids rather than from citrus.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — the Binding of the Loosened:** Sumach's doctrinal signature is *contraction*. In humoral terms it draws scattered, over-relaxed tissue back to tone — the weeping gut, the bleeding surface, the flaccid gum. Its deep crimson drupe and its historical role in *tanning* (turning slack raw hide into firm, preserved leather) make the symbolism unusually literal: the same tannins that "fix" a hide "fix" an over-loose membrane. In Kenetica this reads cleanly as a `[RESET]` agent — a substance of consolidation and tone-restoration after a phase of dispersion or leakage, not of stimulation.

### Active Components

- **Hydrolysable tannins (gallotannins)** — the dominant astringent principle; the drupes and leaves are tannin-rich, which is exactly why the plant was a historic tanning material.
- **Organic acids** — malic, citric and tartaric acids give the fruit its characteristic sourness.
- **Anthocyanins** — cyanidin- and delphinidin-glycosides responsible for the deep red pericarp.
- **Flavonoids and phenolic acids** — quercetin, myricetin and gallic-acid derivatives contributing to the marked antioxidant capacity.
- **Fixed oil in the seed**, plus fibre in the whole-fruit powder.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Sumach is a **consolidating, tone-restoring `[RESET]` agent** with a secondary **metabolic `[BUILD]`** role emerging from modern trial data. Traditionally it is the astringent of choice for over-loose states: mild diarrhoea, relaxed gums, minor surface bleeding, a "weak" stomach. In the applied Kenetica framing it belongs to a recovery block — used as a culinary seasoning to bring sourness, polyphenols and gentle astringency to a meal rather than as a high-dose extract.

The genuinely modern layer is glycaemic and lipid support: pooled trial evidence (below) points to reductions in fasting glucose, insulin and insulin resistance, and a favourable shift in HDL/Apo-A1 — which is why sumach earns a cautious `[BUILD]` tag as a dietary adjunct in metabolic-syndrome-type contexts. This is an **adjuvant seasoning**, not a treatment.

### Scientific Correlation

*Based on articles retrieved from PubMed:*

- **Meta-analysis, glycaemic parameters (metabolic syndrome).** Ghafouri et al. (2021), *Phytomedicine* — nine RCTs, 526 participants. Sumac significantly decreased fasting blood sugar (SMD −0.28; 95% CI −0.54 to −0.02), insulin (SMD −0.67; 95% CI −0.99 to −0.36) and HOMA-IR (SMD −0.79; 95% CI −1.24 to −0.34), with **no** significant effect on weight, BMI, HbA1c, lipids or blood pressure. Authors stress heterogeneity and urge caution. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2021.153661)
- **Meta-analysis, blood lipids.** Akbari-Fakhrabadi et al. (2018), *Complementary Therapies in Medicine* — systematic review of RCTs; individual meta-analyses of total cholesterol, HDL, LDL and triglyceride showed **no** significant difference between sumac and control, and the authors conclude no definite lipid claim can be made given the small number of trials. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctim.2018.07.001)
- **RCT, HDL/Apo-A1.** Hajmohammadi et al. (2017), *Journal of Integrative Medicine* — double-blind placebo-controlled trial, 80 hyperlipidaemic patients, 6 weeks. Sumac significantly raised HDL-cholesterol and apolipoprotein-A1 (P = 0.001) but did not significantly reduce total cholesterol, LDL or triglycerides (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02295293). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joim.2017.12.007)

**Evidence grade:** *Emerging clinical* for glycaemic/insulin-resistance endpoints (positive but heterogeneous meta-analysis); *inconclusive clinical* for lipids (one positive RCT on HDL/Apo-A1, but pooled lipid analyses null). Astringent/traditional uses remain traditional-grade. No large definitive trial exists; claims must stay adjunctive and hedged. Attribution: findings from PubMed.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Cold & dry, 2nd degree; strong astringent (*qābiḍ*), stomach-strengthener, haemostatic.
- **Dioscoridean (Greek):** Binding/styptic; internal for flux, external for sores and bleeding.
- **TCM:** Not classically used — omit.
- **Ayurvedic:** Not a classical dravya of the Sanskrit materia medica — omit.

### Contraindications

- **Anacardiaceae family** — related to poison ivy/oak/sumac (*Toxicodendron*) and to cashew/mango; *R. coriaria* is the edible, non-urushiol culinary sumach, but genuine tree-nut/Anacardiaceae-sensitive individuals should approach with caution. (Do **not** confuse with toxic North American *Toxicodendron vernix*, "poison sumac.")
- **High tannin load** — large or prolonged doses may reduce non-haem iron absorption and cause GI irritation or constipation; keep to culinary quantities.
- **Diabetes / hypoglycaemics** — given the glycaemic effect signalled in trials, monitor if combining supplement-level doses with glucose-lowering drugs to avoid additive hypoglycaemia; this is a theoretical interaction warranting attention.
- **Pregnancy/lactation** — culinary amounts are traditional foods and broadly regarded as safe; concentrated supplement doses are not established in pregnancy, so default to food-level use. `[USER: verify]`
- Not a substitute for statins or antidiabetic therapy.
