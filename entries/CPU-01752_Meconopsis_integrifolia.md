# Meconopsis integrifolia — smug.chhung.mdan.yon, Mukchhung dhenyon (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Meconopsis integrifolia, the one binomial Clark 1995 prints for smug.chhung.mdan.yon, which he calls "a type of Blue Poppy"; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints only the genus, "Meconopsis sp.", beside Mukchhung dhenyon* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and on a species name that only one of them prints.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk for *Meconopsis* found nothing. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 141) the Tibetan name *smug.chhung.mdan.yon* is recorded at Clark line 1028 with the identification "a type of Blue Poppy; Meconopsis integrifolia" and no "key uses the first" note. The key, *Meconopsis integrifolia*, is therefore the **only** binomial Clark prints for the name (the first item, "a type of Blue Poppy", is an English phrase), and not one of several. The Men-Tsee-Khang text gives no species. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", which is not the case for this line.

**Identity doubt.** Clark gives one species; Men-Tsee-Khang gives the genus alone; the species therefore rests on Clark's identification. The drug is tied in both editions to the blue poppy (Clark: "It has the same uses [as blue poppy]"; Men-Tsee-Khang: "has the same efficacy as Tsher ngon"), so its identity is bound to that of *tsher.sngon*, whose own binomial, *Meconopsis horridula*, is treated in I_1131. The Codex does not state which species the Tibetan texts meant. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1028).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry follows *tsher.sngon* (line 1027) and is followed by *gser.skud* (line 1029). It reads:

"smug.chhung.mdan.yon: a type of Blue Poppy: Meconopsis integrifolia: [is called by some smug.chhung.ber.mgo and has a green sword-shaped leaf. It has a brown flower with yellow stamens and resembles blue poppy in being covered with fine thorns, whilst its taste is slightly bitter]. It has the same uses [as blue poppy]."

The entry it points back to (line 1027) reads: "tsher.sngon: Blue Poppy: Meconopsis horridula Hook f. et Thoms: [has four names and grows on high, rocky hills and mountains. It has a small sword-like green leaf, brown petiole, attractive blue flower with yellow stamens and is rendered very coarse by a covering of fine thorns. Blue poppy has a bitter taste and] heals skull fractures, sustains bone resin [and cures bone fever]." Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. In the line for this drug, everything is inside the brackets (the commentary layer) except the Tibetan name, the English phrase, the binomial, which is Clark's identification and not the root text's, and the sentence "It has the same uses". Even "[as blue poppy]" is Clark's interpolation. The root text, as far as Clark's brackets mark it, therefore gives this drug no action of its own and refers to the previous entry, whose root-text layer is "heals skull fractures, sustains bone resin". The line gives no part used and no preparation. Clark's name index lists the alternative name *smug.chhung.ber.mgo* (line 1730) and the Latin index lists *Meconopsis integrifolia* (line 1614). A different line, *ut.pa.la* (line 892, "Blue Lotus: Nelumbo nucifera: Meconopsis"), prints a bare genus for another Tibetan name and is not read as this drug.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428). Line 3433 reads, in a run of short sentences: "Tsher ngon heals fractured bone and protects spongy bone. Muk chung dhenyon has the same efficacy as Tsher ngon." The page number 207 is printed at line 3434. The sentence is the same device as Clark's: the drug is given the action of the blue poppy. The index does not list "Muk chung dhenyon" under either spelling (a search of the text for "Muk chhung", "Muk chung" and "Mukchhung" found only lines 3433 and 5492). Clark's "heals skull fractures, sustains bone resin" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "heals fractured bone and protects spongy bone" are two translators' wordings of the blue poppy's action; the Codex does not treat "skull fractures" and "fractured bone", or "bone resin" and "spongy bone", as one term.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "M" block).* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints the last row of the block on one line (line 5492): "Mukchhung dhenyon smug chung mdan yon Meconopsis sp." That is the phonetic name, the transliteration (Clark's *smug.chhung.mdan.yon*, in a different transliteration scheme) and the scientific name together, so the pairing needs no counting. The glossary prints no English name for it: the English names of the block (lines 5494 to 5498) are "Elecampane Elecampane", "Bastard teak", "Kind of ginger", "Calendula, marigold" and "Oak tree", for other rows. The glossary's two other *Meconopsis sp.* places are the "U" block's "Upal" and "Upal ngonpo" (line 6013, with the English "Light blue poppy"), which are other Tibetan names, and its *Meconopsis horridula* against "Tsher ngon" (line 5994), treated in I_1131.

**Overlap.** I_1131 (*Meconopsis horridula*, tag FMT) treats the preceding Clark entry (*tsher.sngon*, line 1027) and the glossary's *Meconopsis horridula* line, and quotes line 1028 as a different drug that "ties its uses to this one"; no entry covers line 1028 itself. I_043 (Blue Himalayan poppy, *Meconopsis torquata*, tag FMT) mentions *smug.chhung.mdan.yon* as a congener in a cultivation paragraph graded absent and does not give the drug an entry. I_1301 (*Incarvillea younghusbandii*) prints the "U" block's *Meconopsis sp.* names for its neighbours. No other entry on *Meconopsis integrifolia* or the name was found. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Meconopsis* is a genus of the poppy family (Papaveraceae) and that poppy-family plants characteristically contain isoquinoline alkaloids; Clark's commentary describes the plant as "covered with fine thorns", a description of its look and not a constituent. Which alkaloids this species holds, and whether the Tibetan drug is this species, are not shown by the lines read, so no statement about this drug's constituents is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug with "the same uses" (Clark, line 1028) and "the same efficacy" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3433) as the blue poppy, which heals skull fractures and sustains bone resin (Clark, line 1027) or heals fractured bone and protects spongy bone (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3433). These are the texts' claims in their own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. A fracture needs medical care, and a poppy-family plant should not be taken as a bone remedy on the strength of this entry.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 1028) prints "its taste is slightly bitter". No power and no post-digestive taste is printed. The blue poppy's own line (1027) prints "bitter taste". Men-Tsee-Khang prints no taste or power for the name in the lines read. The only action printed for the drug is "the same uses" as the blue poppy (Clark) and "the same efficacy" (Men-Tsee-Khang).

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug only by reference to another: it has the same uses as the blue poppy, which heals skull fractures and sustains bone resin. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the chapter 20 lines cited above, the glossary and the name index, and not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23 in both editions); no line read places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name, not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a bone drug by reference to the blue poppy in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and the species is Clark's identification alone. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Fractures and bone injury need medical assessment; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Because the identity is uncertain, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
