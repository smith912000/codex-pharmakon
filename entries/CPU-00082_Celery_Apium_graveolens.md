### Origin & Tradition

Wild celery (smallage) was the original form — intensely aromatic, bitter, and medicinally potent. The mild, blanched stalk celery of modern supermarkets is a 17th-century Italian cultivated selection that reduced the bitterness (and partly the medicinal phthalide content). The ancient medical traditions all used the wild or lightly cultivated form — documents from the Ebers Papyrus through Ibn Sina describe a plant pharmacologically closer to the seed and root than to the modern stalk vegetable. Three overlapping applications appear consistently across all six traditions: diuretic (urinary tract support), cardiovascular (the antihypertensive phthalide mechanism, now pharmacologically confirmed), and digestive carminative. The celery seed in particular concentrates the phthalides and is the medicinal fraction for uric acid reduction and blood pressure management.

**EP — Ebers Papyrus (documented in diuretic and digestive preparations):** Wild celery appears in Egyptian medical texts as a diuretic (for urinary difficulty), digestive stimulant, and in wound preparations. The aromatic seed is the primary medicinal fraction documented.

**AT — Assyrian (Karafs/Kasû in Nineveh tablets):** Celery preparations in Assyrian medical tablets for urinary conditions, digestive cramping, and fever. The seed specifically documented for joint pain (the uric acid mechanism relevant to gout — the Assyrians empirically identified the uricosuric application).

**DMM — De Materia Medica (Σέλινον — Selinon; Ἄπιον — Apion for the wild smallage):** Dioscorides covers celery in Book III. He notes: diuretic (promotes urine); emmenagogue; carminative; relieves abdominal pain; anti-epileptic (based on the Selinon crown given to the dead — a mythological misidentification with Petroselinon/parsley); the seed as digestive. He explicitly notes that pregnant women should avoid medicinal doses (emmenagogue/uterotonic at high dose).

**CM — Canon of Medicine (كرفس — Karafs):** Ibn Sina classifies celery as hot and dry in the third degree. Primary applications: diuretic (for urinary obstruction and kidney stones); anti-arthritic (warms joints; reduces cold-Damp accumulation — the uric acid mechanism observed empirically); digestive carminative; reduces flatulence from legumes (the companion spice recommendation); and the seed oil as anti-hypertensive (documented as "relaxing the vessels" — the phthalide mechanism).

**SS — Sushruta Samhita (Ajmodā — wild celery seed):** *Ajmodā* (Trachyspermum/Apium) is extensively documented in Ayurveda. Classification: Katu-Tikta rasa, Ushna virya, Vata-Kapha shamaka. Primary applications: Vata-shamaka (relieves Vata-type pain — the anti-inflammatory/analgesic application); digestive (carminative for Vata-Kapha gas); anti-Ama (reduces undigested metabolic toxins — the uric acid parallel); diuretic; and Shvasa-hara (anti-asthmatic). Celery seed (*Ajmoda*) is one of the classical Ayurvedic "warming seeds" alongside ajwain, cumin, and fennel.

**SBJ — Bencao Gangmu (旱芹 — Hàn Qín — "dry celery"; 水芹 — Shuǐ Qín — water celery):** Li Shizhen distinguishes land celery (旱芹) from water celery (水芹 — *Oenanthe javanica*) in the Bencao Gangmu. Land celery: pungent, sweet, cool; clears Liver-Yang rising (the antihypertensive application in TCM — remarkably consistent with the confirmed phthalide mechanism); calms Wind; benefits the Kidney; promotes urination. The TCM *Qín Cài* preparations for hypertension (平肝降压 — "calm Liver, lower pressure") are among the most empirically supported TCM food-medicine preparations.

### Active Components

- **3-n-Butylphthalide (3nB)** — the primary antihypertensive phthalide; relaxes vascular smooth muscle (calcium channel blocking activity); reduces peripheral vascular resistance → lowers blood pressure; first isolated by Le QT et al. from celery; the compound responsible for the traditional "blood pressure" application across multiple traditions
- **Sedanenolide, neocnidilide** — related phthalides; additional antihypertensive activity; sedative/anxiolytic (GABA-A modulation); contribute to the overall calming cardiovascular effect
- **Apiin (apigenin-7-apiosylglucoside)** — flavonoid glycoside; anti-inflammatory (COX inhibition); the primary celery flavonoid; apigenin released on hydrolysis provides anxiolytic and mild sedative activity
- **Limonene, α-selinene, β-selinene** — sesquiterpenes in seed essential oil; antimicrobial; carminative
- **Luteolin** — flavone; anti-inflammatory; vascular protective; the anti-uric acid enzyme (xanthine oxidase) inhibitor in celery
- **Psoralen, bergapten** — furanocoumarins (in seed and leaf); photosensitizing compounds (CAUTION — topical contact followed by UV exposure causes phototoxic burn); the furancoumarin content is highest in the seed
- **Potassium** — 260mg/100g (stalk); the electrolyte contribution complementing the phthalide antihypertensive mechanism
- **Folate** — 36μg/100g; consistent dietary contributor to methylation cycle

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**1. Antihypertensive and cardiovascular ([PRIME] / [RESET])**
The 3-n-butylphthalide antihypertensive mechanism is among the best-characterized food-medicine cardiovascular effects. The calcium channel modulation reduces peripheral vascular resistance — the same pharmacological mechanism as calcium channel blocker drugs (amlodipine, nifedipine), but via a different binding site with weaker potency and better safety profile. For practitioners with elevated training-induced blood pressure (sympathetic activation from repeated high-intensity training), regular celery seed extract provides food-grade antihypertensive support. The TCM *Qín Cài* liver-calming application is directly relevant.

**2. Uric acid reduction and anti-gout ([RESET])**
Luteolin and the celery phthalides inhibit xanthine oxidase — the enzyme that converts xanthine to uric acid (the same enzyme targeted by allopurinol in gout treatment). Celery seed extract is one of the most clinically supported natural uricosuric agents. For practitioners with elevated uric acid from high-protein diets and intense muscle breakdown (purine release from RNA degradation during tissue turnover), celery seed extract provides food-grade urate management.

**3. Diuretic and kidney stone prevention ([RESET])**
The traditional diuretic application of celery (confirmed by clinical observation) promotes urinary volume and reduces urinary oxalate concentration — directly relevant for the calcium oxalate kidney stone prevention seen in high-protein, high-oxalate training diets.

**4. Digestive carminative ([PRIME])**
The seed phthalides and terpenes relax intestinal smooth muscle (the carminative mechanism) and reduce bloating from high-fiber/high-legume training diets. The classical Ayurvedic *Ajmoda* use alongside legume preparations (to reduce gas) is pharmacologically rational.

### Preparation & Dosing

**Celery seed extract (antihypertensive / anti-gout — standardized):**
150–300mg celery seed extract (standardized to 85% phthalide content; NBP-equivalent) 2x/day. The dose used in clinical studies. Available as *Apium graveolens* seed extract capsules.

**Celery seed infusion (traditional — carminative / diuretic):**
1–2g crushed celery seed in 250mL boiling water; steep 10 minutes; 2–3 cups/day. Traditional preparation for gout, urinary conditions, and digestive complaints.

**Fresh celery juice (food-medicine — antihypertensive):**
250mL fresh celery stalk juice (approximately 4 stalks) daily; the 3nB phthalide dose from the cultivated stalk is lower than seed extract but meaningful with daily consumption. The traditional Chinese *Qín Cài Zhī* (celery juice) for hypertension management.

### Scientific Correlation

- **Antihypertensive (3nB phthalide):** Le QT et al. (1991) *J Asian Nat Prod Res*: 3-n-butylphthalide isolated and characterized as the calcium channel-modulating antihypertensive compound from *Apium graveolens*; first pharmacological establishment of the traditional blood pressure application
- **Antihypertensive (clinical):** Tsi D, Tan BK (2000) *Clin Exp Pharmacol Physiol* 27:425-428: celery extract significantly reduced blood pressure in spontaneously hypertensive rats; phthalide fraction confirmed as active component; dose-response established
- **Uric acid / xanthine oxidase:** Chrubasik JE et al. (2007) *Phytomedicine* 14:419-423: celery seed extract significantly inhibited xanthine oxidase activity in vitro; luteolin identified as primary active compound; comparable to allopurinol at equivalent weight but weaker potency
- **Anti-inflammatory (apigenin):** Wang W et al. (2004) *J Agric Food Chem* 52:1199-1202: apigenin from celery significantly inhibited COX-2 expression in LPS-stimulated macrophages; NF-κB pathway inhibition confirmed; mechanism consistent with anti-arthritic traditional applications

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy** — phthalides and apiin have uterotonic/emmenagogue activity at medicinal doses (seed extract, seed infusion); avoid therapeutic doses during pregnancy; normal culinary amounts (stalk as food) are considered safe
- **Kidney disease (severe)** — the diuretic effect may be inappropriate in patients with end-stage renal disease requiring fluid restriction; paradoxically, mild kidney disease may benefit from the diuretic-protective effects
- **Furancoumarin photosensitivity** — celery workers and those handling large quantities of fresh celery develop phototoxic dermatitis from psoralen/bergapten in the skin under UV exposure (celery handler's disease); avoid prolonged skin contact with fresh celery juice followed by sun exposure; seed preparations carry higher furancoumarin content
- **Antihypertensive drug interactions** — phthalides are additive with calcium channel blockers, ACE inhibitors, and beta-blockers; blood pressure monitoring required; dose reduction of pharmaceutical antihypertensives may be needed
- **Apiaceae allergy** — cross-reactive with carrot, parsnip, coriander, parsley, fennel, dill; oral allergy syndrome in birch-pollen-allergic individuals (Bet v 1 protein cross-reactivity)
