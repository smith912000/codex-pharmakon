# Smilax tracheia — SMILAX TRACHEIA (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 4-144); "Smilax trachea" in the translator's text

## *Rough smilax, a prickly climber of the Mediterranean; the translator's SUGGESTED line reads "Smilax-aspera [Fuchs, Linnaeus] — Prickly Ivy, Rough Bindweed", and the identification here is that one, of medium confidence at species level, since the description is short and the older authorities named are Renaissance and Linnaean* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

> **CAUTION.** The chapter records an antidote claim and a claim about giving the herb to a newborn. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should rely on this plant against poison or give it to a child.

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk, under this name.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder; this entry replaces it. The text on disk is the 2000 English edition (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`); the name index row reads "SMILAX TRACHEIA, 4-144 (line 9294)" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 722).

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* Book 4, chapter 4-144 (text line 9294; the translator's page 691, "Book Four: Other Herbs & Roots"). The full chapter reads: "Smilax trachea has leaves similar to periclymen [4-14], with many sprigs, thin and prickly like paliurus or rubus, and it is wrapped around trees creeping (as it were) above and beneath. It bears a clustered fruit like a grape bunch, which ripens red, somewhat gently biting to the taste; the root is hard and thick. It grows in marshy rough places. A decoction of the leaves and fruit is an antidote for deadly poisons, taken as a drink beforehand (or afterwards). It is said that this herb, beaten and given to drink to a newborn small child, prevents hurt by any poisonous medicine. It is cut for antidotes against poison."

So the chapter gives a description (prickly, climbing, red clustered fruit, hard thick root, marshy rough ground), one use (a decoction of leaf and fruit as an antidote to deadly poisons, before or after), a hearsay use ("It is said ...", for a newborn) and a statement that the plant is cut for antidote-making. The chapter does not name the root as the part used; it names the leaves and fruit.

**The neighbouring smilaxes.** The next chapter, 4-145 *Smilax leia* (line 9297), is a different plant, a smooth climber without prickles, whose seed "taken as a drink ... is said to cause many troublesome dreams"; its SUGGESTED line is a bindweed. Book 2 has a garden *smilax* (2-176, line 6052) glossed as a bean. Two clematis chapters compare their climbers to smilax: 4-7b *Klematis etera* (line 8072, marked POISONOUS in the translation), which "winds around trees like smilax [4-144, 4-145]", and 4-182 *Klematitis* (line 9671), which "winds around trees like smilax". The vinegar chapter, 5-21 *Oxus* (line 9878), lists vinegar, with salt, "against mushrooms, ixia [3-103] and smilax", without saying which smilax. These are other chapters and are not folded into this one.

**Overlap with an existing row.** The China Root entry (`I_645_ChinaRoot_Smilax_china.md`) remarks that Dioscorides' *smilax trachea* is the Mediterranean *Smilax aspera* and describes it as a root given in wine as an alexipharmic. The chapter on disk (above) names leaves and fruit, and does not mention wine or the root as the part used; that entry's wording is not borne out by 4-144 and should be rechecked. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** An antidote to "deadly poisons" (decoction of leaves and fruit), and a report that it protects a newborn from "any poisonous medicine".
- **What the text does not say.** No amount of leaf or fruit, no volume of water, no length of boiling, no named poison.

The Codex gives no protocol. A claimed antidote to unnamed poisons is not a treatment: poisoning is an emergency for a poison service or a hospital.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation: "A decoction of the leaves and fruit", "taken as a drink beforehand (or afterwards)". For the newborn the herb is "beaten and given to drink". ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. *Smilax aspera* is a prickly evergreen climber of the Mediterranean region with red berries, and the genus is known for saponin-bearing roots. There is no general antidote to "deadly poisons" in a plant decoction, and the chapter's claim is not supported as stated. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested; Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured for this entry. What the chapter does offer a cultivator is a discipline of attention: a description by which the plant can be told (leaf like *periclymen*, thin prickly sprigs, red clustered fruit, hard root, marshy ground) and a warning, by contrast with *Smilax leia* in the next chapter, that two climbers called *smilax* are not the same. He records the newborn use with "It is said", which is recording and not endorsing.

**Practitioner's note.** A prickly climber named in an antidote claim. Nothing supports cultivation use. The useful lesson is the one the text itself teaches: identify by description, and do not trust a hearsay antidote.

### Contraindications

- **Poisoning.** An emergency; seek medical care. Do not rely on any plant decoction.
- **Newborns and young children.** The chapter's report of giving it to a newborn is a record only. Do not give it to a child.
- **Identity.** The species is the translator's suggestion; two further plants carry the name *smilax* in the same book and a third (a garden bean) in Book 2. [USER: verify]
