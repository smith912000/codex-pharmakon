# Matricaria inodora — Bouphthalmon (Dioscorides 3-156, "Buphthalmum"); "False Chamomile, Scentless Mayweed" on the translators' line

## *Matricaria inodora [Linnaeus], the last of four names on the translators' first SUGGESTED line for chapter 3-156 (Buphthalmum, Oculis bovis [Fuchs], Chamaemelum inodorum [Bauhin]), glossed "False Chamomile, Scentless Mayweed"; a second block, "[other usage]", adds Buphthalmum graveolens "Ox-eye", Buphthalmum salicifolium "Yellow Ox-eye Daisy" and Anthemis arvensis "Corn Chamomile"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk names this plant under this name. The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The SUGGESTED block offers one group of names for the chapter and then a second group marked "[other usage]", which is a list of other plants that bear the name today. The entry takes the Linnaean-tagged name of the first line. The translators add a note that "It is believed that the buhphthalmum of Pliny is a species of anthemis [Loudon]" (line 7868, spelled so in the text). The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The heading *3-156. BUPHTHALMON* is at line 7864, the SUGGESTED block at lines 7865 to 7867, the note on Pliny at line 7868 and the chapter text at lines 7869 and 7870. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 562. The chapter stands in Book Three ("Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants" in the running head), after 3-155 *PARTHENION (AMARAKON)* (line 7856, the stinking chamomile or mayweed) and before 3-157 *PAIONIA ARREN, PAIONIA THELEIA* (line 7871, the peonies). The translators' index lists *Buphthalmum* at page 529 (line 15117).

The chapter has two paragraphs. The first reads: "Buphthalmum sends out somewhat slender tender little stalks, leaves like marathrum [3-81], yellowish flowers — bigger than anthemis, similar to eyes (from which it is named). It grows in fields and around towns. The flowers of this (pounded into small pieces with wax ointment) dissolve oedema and hard lumps. They say that a decoction (taken as a drink after coming out from a bath) causes the jaundiced to have a good colour for some time."

The second reads: "You must gather buphthalmum while the moon is increasing. It is used against fears, devils, enchantments, and poisons (turning aside these things); and if anyone chews the root (and afterwards spits it out) it immediately stops mucus, eases toothache, and loosens the bowels. It is also called cachlan, or balsamina, the Magi call it haemorrha, some, genitura Mercurii, semen incorruptibile, or mnesitheos; the Romans call it kappacorania, and the Africans, narat."

So the chapter describes the plant (slender tender stalks, leaves like marathrum, the cross-reference being to 3-81, and yellowish flowers "bigger than anthemis", from which the name "ox-eye" is explained, as the flowers are "similar to eyes"), where it grows (fields and around towns), and its uses: the flowers pounded with wax ointment for oedema and hard lumps, and, on report ("They say"), a bath-time drink for the jaundiced. The second paragraph adds the instruction to gather it "while the moon is increasing", its use "against fears, devils, enchantments, and poisons", and a use of the chewed and spat-out root for mucus, toothache and the bowels. The other names are *cachlan* and *balsamina*, the Magi name *haemorrha*, the names *genitura Mercurii*, *semen incorruptibile* and *mnesitheos* given by "some", the Roman *kappacorania* and the African *narat*. The text does not say who "some" are.

**Recorded, not endorsed.** The gathering rule ("You must gather ... while the moon is increasing") and the use "against fears, devils, enchantments, and poisons" are in the voice of the text. They are recorded. The translators' glossary (line 3509) says that in Dioscorides' time the name *Magi* was used for those claiming occult powers of Babylonian or Oriental origin, which is the source of the name *haemorrha* here. The text does not say that Dioscorides himself tested or endorses the lunar rule or the protective use, and the Codex does not turn either into a practice.

**Other places in the same text.** Three other chapters touch the name. 2-192 *PTARMIKE* (sneezewort) is described with "a little head like anthemis arvensis" (line 6204), and after a page break the text resumes "[3-156], small, round, sharp according to the smell" (line 6217), so the translators cross-refer to this chapter for the head. 4-58 *CHRUSANTHEMON* (line 8510) lists *bupthalmum* among its other names (line 8515) and has several of the same elements: flowers that give "the jaundiced a good colour in good time given to drink after they have spent a long time in the baths", gathering "before the rising of the sun", and plants that "are hung around the neck, being good for averting women witches and all enchantments". 4-33 *SIDERITIS* (line 8297) says "Osthenes says buphthalmum" among its names. Those are different chapters and different plants, and nothing from them is attributed to this one. The name was shared, and the identity here is a suggestion. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** The chapter 3-156 is not the subject of any existing entry (chapter-level search for 3-156, *Buphthalmon*, *Matricaria inodora* and *Buphthalmum*; the string 3-156 matches I_150 only inside a page range of a journal citation). 3-155 *Parthenion* is covered by I_976 (*Anthemis cotula*) and is not merged. 4-58 *Chrysanthemon* has no entry of its own, although I_888 and I_1094 mention the word *Chrysanthemon* in other contexts. None is merged. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. As a general fact, the chamomile and mayweed relatives belong to the daisy family (Asteraceae), a large family in which many members have essential oils or bitter lactones, and some people are allergic to them. That is a statement about the family and not a finding about *Matricaria inodora*. The scentless mayweed gets its Latin species name from the lack of scent, but the chapter prints no scent for its plant. Nothing is stated here about its composition. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a flower paste for swellings and lumps, a bath-time drink for jaundice, a chewed root for mucus, toothache and the bowels, and a protective use against fears, devils, enchantments and poisons. These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol. Jaundice and lumps need medical assessment, and the protective use is a recorded belief.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives four preparations: the flowers "pounded into small pieces with wax ointment" and applied; "a decoction (taken as a drink after coming out from a bath)"; the root chewed and spat out; and a plant used "against fears, devils, enchantments, and poisons" with no method stated. The only rule of timing is to gather the plant "while the moon is increasing". The text gives no quantity for any of them. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 3-156 prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. The qualities printed are of form and colour: "somewhat slender tender little stalks", "yellowish flowers — bigger than anthemis".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. What it gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a field description (stalks, leaves, flowers, habitat) and a timing instruction for gathering ("while the moon is increasing"). It also records a protective use "against fears, devils, enchantments, and poisons", and a set of Magi names. The spec notes that Dioscorides sometimes records amuletic uses while distancing himself, and recording is not endorsing. The text here records the use and the lunar rule without comment, and the Codex neither endorses them nor builds a practice on them. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** What the chapter offers a cultivator is the habit of attention, a plant known by its habit and habitat and gathered by a stated rule of time. The protective use is a recorded belief and the lunar rule is a recorded instruction, and neither is a practice the Codex recommends. Nothing else here is to take up.

### Contraindications

Chapter 3-156 prints no warning. Jaundice and lumps need medical care and must not be left to a bath-time drink or a wax-ointment paste. The identification is only the translators' suggestion among several plants of the daisy family, some of which cause skin reactions, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. The chapter gives no dose, and no safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
