**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** 5-144 AIMATITES LITHOS, "Haematite — Iron Sesquioxide" (line 10583 in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`): "The best haematite stone is brittle and has a deep colour, or is black and hard and even, not containing any impurities or veins. It is astringent, somewhat warming, and reduces the intensity of symptoms. With honey it wears off scars and scabs on the eyes. With woman's milk it helps sore eyes and broken bloodshot eyes. It is taken in a drink with wine for painful urination, and women's discharges [menstrual flow] with pomegranate juice, and for spitting-up blood. Eye salves are made of it, and suppositories and touchstones suitable for eye complaints." It "is burnt like Phrygian [dyer's] stone, only the wine omitted", and is "properly burnt when it is mildly light and swollen up with bubbles". The chapter then describes a counterfeit made from a piece of *schistos* heated in ash and tested on a touchstone, and how to tell it: "this has straight veins but haematites does not", and the real stone has "a deeper" colour "like cinnabar". Last, "haematites is made from lodestone [magnetic oxide of iron] burnt very well; but that which occurs naturally is dug out of mines in Egypt". Elsewhere cinnabar is said to have "the same properties as haematite" but to be "more astringent and blood staunching" (5-109, line 10394). 5-145 SCHISTOS LITHOS (the neighbouring stone) is said to have "the same uses as haematite only with less strength".

### Active Components

Hematite is iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3, a hard, dense mineral that is steel-grey to red-brown in mass and gives a red-brown streak and powder. It is insoluble in water and body fluids, which is why it is also a pigment (red ochre is a related, earthier iron oxide and a different substance in the Greek text, 5-111 and 5-112). Counterfeits and lodestone (magnetite, Fe3O4), which Dioscorides says is burnt to make it, are chemically different iron oxides.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources use hematite as an astringent for eyes and bleeding in Greek practice, against "demonic influx" and spirits in the Chinese text, and as a lymph-drying and fracture-healing stone in the Tibetan text, always in the physician's hands and with no dose. The mineral is inert, and the Codex offers no protocol, dose or preparation. "Touchstones suitable for eye complaints" (5-144) are named without explanation.

### Preparation & Dosing

Only preparations the texts give. DMM 5-144: burnt (as for Phrygian stone, minus wine) until mildly light and puffed with bubbles; with honey for eye scars, with woman's milk for sore eyes, in wine for painful urination and in pomegranate juice for menstrual discharge, made into eye salves, suppositories and touchstones. SBJ: none printed. FMT: none printed in the line. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Hematite is insoluble and poorly absorbed, so internal action is unlikely, and it is not itself a known medicine; its mild astringency as a fine powder on tissue is plausible but not established. Inhaled mineral dust is a hazard. No clinical studies were retrieved for the traditional uses. [USER: verify]

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: Inferior Class.* Dai Zhe stands under the heading "Jades and Stones: Inferior Class". The preface (lines 391-396) says the inferior class "mainly treat[s] disease and correspond[s] to earth. They are usually toxic and cannot be taken for a long time. If one intends to eliminate cold and heat and evil qi, break accumulations and gatherings, and cure disease, one should base [one's efforts] on the inferior class." This is the grade for a defined course against a defined complaint, then stopped, and it is the opposite of the long-term "nourishing life" superior grade. The line itself promises no longevity and says nothing of *jing*, *qi* or *shen* beyond "toxic and evil qi in the abdomen".

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* The stone is in the second section of Explanatory Tantra ch.20, as a medicine whose powers are on lymph, bone and brain. **Practitioner's note.** Nothing here for a cultivator to take. Hematite is, in these sources, a physician's stone for the eyes and bleeding (Greek), a short-course antidemonic drug of the lowest Chinese grade, and a lymph-drying stone (Tibetan). The only discipline on offer is Dioscorides': know the stone by its look, test it against a touchstone, and distrust the counterfeit. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Hematite is inert as a solid, but powders and "ash" preparations are unstandardised and may contain other metals; fine mineral dust is a lung hazard. The Chinese inferior-class grade is the tradition's own warning against long use ("usually toxic and cannot be taken for a long time"). Ritual and spirit-related uses are ritual, not therapy.
