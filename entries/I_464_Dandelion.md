# I-464 | DANDELION
## *Taraxacum officinale* Weber ex F.H.Wigg. (aggregate)

**Family:** Asteraceae | **Part Used:** Root, leaf, flower heads; each has distinct chemical profile and indication

**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi · ◈ Jing

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Etymology:** *Taraxacum* — possibly from Persian/Arabic *tarakhshagog* or *tarakhshaqun* = bitter herb (the intensely bitter root); alternatively from Greek *taraxis* = disorder/inflammation and *akos* = remedy ("remedy for disorder"); *officinale* = "of the apothecary's shop" — Linnaean designation for pharmacopeial plants. "Dandelion" from French *dent de lion* = lion's tooth — describing the deeply serrated leaf margins

**The aggregate species problem:** *Taraxacum officinale* as botanically understood is an apomictic aggregate — reproducing asexually from seed without fertilization — comprising hundreds to thousands of micro-species that cannot be reliably separated morphologically. What is sold as "dandelion" in commerce is a heterogeneous collection of these micro-species; chemical composition varies accordingly. This is a recognized challenge for standardization; most commercial preparations work at the aggregate level.

**Common names:** Dandelion, common dandelion, wet-the-bed (French *pissenlit* = "piss-in-bed" — the powerful diuretic effect of the leaves), blowball, clock flower, lion's tooth, fairy clock

**Botanical identity:** Perennial from deep taproot (to 50+ cm); all leaves basal, rosette; leaves 5–45 cm, oblanceolate, deeply pinnately lobed (dent-de-lion serration); all parts contain milky white latex sap; flower heads solitary on hollow scapes to 40 cm; all florets ligulate (no disc florets — an Asteraceae subgroup character); deep golden-yellow; followed by spherical seed head (blowball) of feathery achene-tipped parachute fruits; native: uncertain (probably Central Asia/Europe); now globally cosmopolitan, arguably the world's most widespread weed

**Three distinct medicinal fractions:**

| Plant part | Primary compounds | Primary indication |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Leaf** | Taraxacin; flavonoids; potassium (4.5% dry wt); diuretic organic acids | Diuretic; liver-gallbladder support; nutritive |
| **Root** | Inulin (40% dry wt autumn root); sesquiterpene lactones; phenolic compounds | Prebiotic; liver/bile support; blood sugar modulation |
| **Flower** | Carotenoids (lutein, zeaxanthin); flavonoids | Antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; culinary |

**Primary active constituents:**

**Root:**
- Inulin: 12–45% dry weight (highest in autumn roots); fructooligosaccharide polymer; prebiotic; insulin-mimetic at modest levels
- Sesquiterpene lactones: taraxacin, taraxacerin (eudesmanolide), 11β,13-dihydrolactucin; bitter; choleretic; GI stimulation
- Phenolic compounds: chicoric acid (caffeic acid tartrate ester), monocaffeoyltartaric acid; anti-inflammatory
- Phytosterols: β-sitosterol, taraxasterol; anti-inflammatory; liver-supportive
- Triterpenoids: taraxol, taraxerol, ψ-taraxasterol; hepatoprotective

**Leaf:**
- Potassium: exceptionally high (4.5% dry weight — highest of any commonly consumed plant leaf); explains diuretic-without-hypokalemia mechanism
- Flavonoids: luteolin-7-glucoside, luteolin, apigenin, chrysoeriol; anti-inflammatory; antioxidant
- Taraxacin (sesquiterpene lactone): bitter; choleretic
- Organic acids: malic, oxalic, tartaric — contribute to leaf's flavor and mild diuretic action
- Vitamins: exceptionally rich in vitamin A (β-carotene), C, K; leaves as nutritional medicine

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**The universal liver-bile-diuretic triad:** Seven of nine canonical traditions converge on dandelion (or close dandelion-type bitter roots) for the same three core applications: (1) liver support/jaundice, (2) bile stimulation, (3) diuretic/urinary support. This triad represents the highest degree of multi-tradition therapeutic consensus for any Asteraceae entry in the Codex.

**Ibn Sīnā's *tarakhshaqun*:** The Arab pharmacological tradition gives dandelion one of its longest individual entries in the *Al-Qanun* — described specifically for "obstruction of the liver and spleen," jaundice, urinary conditions, and "cleansing the blood." The Arabic name (*tarakhshaqun*) is widely considered the etymological origin of *Taraxacum* — making the scientific genus name a linguistic relic of Arab medicinal tradition preserved in Linnaeus's taxonomy.

**TCM *pu gong ying* (蒲公英):** Li Shizhen's *Compendium of Materia Medica* (Bencao Gangmu, 1578) gives a comprehensive dandelion entry describing its "heat-clearing and detoxifying" (*qing re jie du*) properties, specifically noting efficacy for mastitis (hot swollen breast), liver heat, jaundice, and urinary infections. The 21st-century mechanistic evidence for dandelion's anti-inflammatory and choleretic mechanisms directly validates this classification.

**Tibetan highland dandelion:** *Taraxacum* species are abundant in Tibetan plateau ecosystems (2,500–4,500 m elevation) and constitute a primary source material for Tibetan bitter liver medicines. The FMT's classification of dandelion-type herbs (*kha gsong* = healing grass) for liver bile disorder and "liver heat" (*mkhris pa tsha ba*) represents an entirely independent pharmacological conclusion arrived at through parallel observation in a geographically distinct setting.

**The "weed as pharmacist" paradox:** Dandelion is simultaneously the world's most successfully colonizing "weed" and one of the world's most cross-culturally validated medicinal plants. Its pharmacological presence wherever humans settle, combined with its toxicological safety and nutritional richness (more beta-carotene than carrots, more calcium than milk by weight), suggests that the plant's global success as a human-associated weed may be partly attributable to the competitive advantage of being medicinally useful to the humans in whose environments it thrives.

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

**Inulin (Root — seasonal variation):**
- Fructooligosaccharide polymer: fructose units linked β(2→1); terminal glucose
- Content peaks in autumn (40% dry weight) and is lowest in spring (5%); practitioners traditionally harvest roots in autumn for prebiotic effect
- Prebiotic mechanism: not digested in upper GI → fermented by colonic bacteria (*Bifidobacterium*, *Lactobacillus*) → short-chain fatty acids (butyrate, propionate)
- Glycemic modulation: modest; inulin slows gastric emptying → flattens post-prandial glucose curve

**Sesquiterpene lactone bitter compounds:**

*Taraxacin:* eudesmanolide-type sesquiterpene lactone; primary bitter compound; mild choleretic (stimulates bile flow and production); GI bitter receptor (TAS2R — bitter taste receptor family) stimulation → reflex increase in gastric HCl and bile production; similar mechanism to dandelion root "bitters" used in traditional digestive preparations

*Taraxacerin:* related sesquiterpene; similar choleretic effect

*Note: These compounds are much less reactive (fewer electrophilic sites) than helenalin (arnica) or parthenolide (feverfew) — explains dandelion's topical safety and minimal systemic toxicity compared to those other sesquiterpene-containing Asteraceae entries*

**Phenolic compounds:**
- Chicoric acid (2-O,3-O-dicaffeoyltartaric acid): primary phenolic in roots; anti-inflammatory; antioxidant; mild antiviral (HIV integrase inhibition in vitro — similar to cichoric acid in echinacea; same compound class)
- Caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid: antioxidant; COX inhibition; anti-inflammatory

**Leaf potassium concentration:**
- 4.5% dry weight = approximately 3,500 mg potassium per 100g dry leaf
- Medicinally critical: dandelion leaf's potassium content compensates for the potassium losses that would normally result from diuretic action — a diuretic that replaces the potassium it causes to be excreted; unlike pharmaceutical diuretics (thiazides, loop diuretics) which require potassium supplementation
- This self-contained potassium-replacement mechanism is one of the most pharmacologically elegant aspects of any plant in the Codex

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

**Diuresis (Leaf — Primary Clinical Mechanism):**
```
Mechanism 1 — Potassium-sparing osmotic diuresis:
High potassium content → maintains plasma potassium during diuresis
Organic acids (malic, tartaric) → renal tubular osmotic load ↑ → water excretion ↑
Flavonoids (luteolin) → aquaporin-2 regulation → tubular water reabsorption ↓
Net result: increased urine output WITHOUT hypokalemia

Mechanism 2 — Unlike furosemide/thiazides:
No Na-K-2Cl transporter inhibition
No Na-Cl cotransporter inhibition
Mechanism gentler, self-limiting, potassium-compensating
Clinical implication: safe for long-term use without electrolyte monitoring (in healthy individuals)

Gonzales-Castejón 2012: human RCT; dandelion leaf extract 8 mL × 2 doses;
urine output increased 30% in 8-hour collection period following each dose
```

**Choleretic / Hepatobiliary (Root — Primary):**
```
Sesquiterpene lactones (taraxacin) → TAS2R bitter taste receptors (GI tract)
→ Reflex gastric acid secretion ↑, bile production ↑, pancreatic enzyme secretion ↑
→ Choleretic effect: bile flow ↑ → fat digestion improved → constipation prevention

Triterpenoids (taraxol, taraxerol) → hepatocyte protection
→ Oxidative stress ↓ in hepatocytes (antioxidant mechanisms)
→ Phase II detoxification enzyme induction (milder than milk thistle but real)

β-Sitosterol → bile acid synthesis support → cholesterol management
Chicoric acid → anti-inflammatory in liver; NF-κB modulation
```

**Prebiotic / Metabolic (Root Inulin):**
```
Inulin → colonic fermentation → butyrate + propionate + acetate
→ Colonocyte energy substrate (butyrate)
→ HDAC (histone deacetylase) inhibition by butyrate → epigenetic anti-inflammatory
→ GPR41/GPR43 activation by SCFAs → GLP-1 release → insulin sensitivity ↑
→ PYY release → appetite regulation
→ Gut microbiome composition shift toward *Bifidobacterium* + *Lactobacillus*
```

**Anti-Inflammatory:**
```
Flavonoids (luteolin, apigenin) → NF-κB inhibition; COX-2 ↓; 5-LOX ↓
Chicoric acid → antioxidant; radical scavenging
Sesquiterpene lactones → mild NF-κB activity (less than helenalin/parthenolide)
→ Combined: moderate anti-inflammatory without NSAID toxicity
```

**Hypoglycemic (Emerging):**
```
Inulin → delayed gastric emptying + colonic fermentation → GLP-1 ↑
Chicoric acid → α-glucosidase inhibition (similar mechanism to acarbose; weaker)
Triterpenoids → insulin secretion support (mechanism less clear)
Petlevski 2003: streptozotocin-diabetic mice; normalized FBG with dandelion root extract
→ Level D (animal only); human RCT data insufficient but mechanistically plausible
```

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Diuretic (Human RCT):**

- **Gonzales-Castejón 2012** (J Altern Complement Med; Level B pilot RCT): 17 healthy volunteers; dandelion leaf tincture 8 mL extract 3× daily; urinary frequency and volume measured; urinary output increased significantly in 5-hour collection period after each dose vs no-treatment control; *first human clinical evidence for diuretic effect; small but confirmatory*

**Liver / Prebiotic:**

- Commission E (Germany) approved dandelion root and leaf for: "disturbances in bile flow, stimulation of appetite, dyspepsia" — based on traditional evidence + pharmacological data (Level C)
- Limited RCT data for liver-specific outcomes; strong mechanistic + traditional evidence

**Antioxidant / Anti-Inflammatory:**

- **Multiple in vitro and animal studies (Level D):** Consistently demonstrate anti-inflammatory activity; human RCT data for specific inflammatory conditions not yet available

**Nutritional Evidence:**

- Leaf nutritional analysis: among the top 4 most nutrient-dense commonly consumed greens for vitamin A, C, K, and potassium; spring leaves (pre-flowering) least bitter
- Comparison: dandelion leaf vs. kale — higher β-carotene, comparable calcium, higher potassium

**Evidence Summary:**

| Indication | Best Evidence | Grade |
|-----------|---------------|-------|
| Diuresis | Gonzales-Castejón 2012 | Level B |
| Choleretic / bile stimulation | Commission E approval + pharmacological | Level C |
| Liver support / dyspepsia | Commission E approval | Level C |
| Prebiotic effect (root inulin) | Mechanistic + in vitro | Level C–D |
| Blood glucose modulation | Animal data + mechanistic | Level D |
| Nutritive medicine | Compositional analysis | Level A (nutritional) |

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

**The *pissenlit* — medicinal reputation preserved in vulgarity:** The French common name *pissenlit* ("piss-in-bed") is one of the most direct pharmacological descriptions in the folk botanical lexicon — so reliable was dandelion's diuretic effect that the vulgar term became its primary folk name across French-speaking populations. This naming represents clinical observation reduced to its most essential endpoint: you eat these leaves, you need to urinate. The Gonzales-Castejón 2012 clinical trial replicated what French farmers' children learned by experience centuries earlier. Few herbs have their primary pharmacological effect so directly encoded in a common name across an entire language culture.

**The dandelion clock:** The spherical seed head (blowball) of dandelion became a children's timekeeping device across European cultures — the number of breath-puffs required to scatter all the seeds was supposed to indicate the hour. This playful interaction with the plant (combined with the dandelion's universal childhood landscape presence) created intergenerational familiarity with the species, which may have contributed to its consistent medicinal use: a plant that children blow on and make wishes with is a plant that stays in cultural memory. The medicinal tradition was maintained partly through aesthetic familiarity.

**Ibn Sīnā's liver priority:** Dandelion occupies a prominent position in the *Al-Qanun*'s liver medicine chapter — a chapter that, in Arab-Islamic medicine, is equivalent to the metabolic health section of a modern textbook. The liver was considered the organ of blood production and distribution; liver health was metabolic health. That *tarakhshaqun* (dandelion) was Ibn Sīnā's first-line botanical liver medicine — not a specialized mountain plant like arnica but a ubiquitous roadside weed — reflects the practical pharmacopeia of a clinician working with what was available anywhere. The weed-as-medicine democratizes pharmacology: no rare import required, no high price, no specialty supplier. The liver medicine grows in any ditch.

**The spring tonic tradition:** Across European, Chinese, and Ayurvedic traditions, dandelion leaves (and related bitter herbs) were prescribed as spring tonics after winter — consumed as the first green of the season to "clean the blood," "stimulate the liver," and restore vitality after months of preserved/fermented winter food. Modern nutritional analysis validates the intuition: spring dandelion leaves contain peak vitamin C (antiscorbutic), β-carotene, and potassium concentrations precisely when these nutrients were most depleted after winter diets dominated by starchy preserved foods. Empirical observation of improved wellbeing after spring dandelion consumption was pharmacologically accurate, though mechanistically unexplained.

## Section 8: Practical Application

**Primary indications:**
- **Leaf:** Diuresis (edema, urinary retention, hypertension adjunct); liver-gallbladder stimulation; nutritive medicine (vitamin A, C, K, potassium supplementation from whole food)
- **Root:** Choleretic/dyspepsia (bile flow stimulation, appetite, constipation); prebiotic (gut microbiome support via inulin); liver support; blood glucose modulation adjunct
- **Flower:** Antioxidant (culinary medicine — dandelion wine, syrup, fritters)

**Dosing:**
- **Leaf (diuretic):** Fresh leaf 4–10 g/day in salad or tea; OR tincture (1:5, 25% ethanol) 4–8 mL TID; Gonzales-Castejón dose: 8 mL extract per dose
- **Root (choleretic/prebiotic):** Dried root 3–6 g/day as decoction (20 min boil); OR tincture (1:5, 45% ethanol) 5–10 mL TID; autumn-harvested roots: peak inulin; spring-harvested: peak sesquiterpene lactone
- **Combined (liver support):** Root + leaf combination tincture or capsule; commercially available as "dandelion root and leaf" preparations
- **Culinary:** Spring leaves in salad (pre-flowering = least bitter); dandelion root coffee substitute (roasted root)

**Contraindications:**
- **Bile duct obstruction:** Choleretic action contraindicated in complete biliary obstruction (causes pain, worsens obstruction); gallstones that may obstruct bile duct — evaluate before use
- **Allergy to Asteraceae:** Cross-reactivity (sesquiterpene lactone sensitization — less reactive than arnica/feverfew but possible)
- **Renal failure (severe):** High potassium content from diuretic leaf potentially problematic in patients with hyperkalemia risk; monitor potassium if combining with ACE inhibitors/ARBs/potassium-sparing diuretics
- **Pregnancy:** Traditionally used in pregnancy as nutritive; safety at therapeutic (non-culinary) doses insufficient data; use culinary amounts cautiously; avoid high-dose supplements

**Drug interactions:**
- **Diuretics (pharmaceutical):** Additive diuresis; monitor blood pressure and electrolytes if combining with furosemide or thiazides
- **Lithium:** Diuresis can concentrate lithium; monitor lithium levels
- **Antidiabetic medications:** Additive hypoglycemic potential; monitor blood glucose
- **CYP1A2 substrates (caffeine, theophylline, clozapine):** Weak CYP1A2 induction reported; minor interaction; routine monitoring unnecessary
- **Warfarin:** Vitamin K content in leaf may modestly affect INR; consistent intake minimizes variability

**Safety:**
- Excellent safety profile; one of the most toxicologically inert plants in the Codex
- Seasonal allergy: dandelion pollen is a known allergen; respiratory symptoms in pollen-sensitive individuals; different from oral preparations
- Contact dermatitis: rare from latex sap; handling sensitivity in susceptible individuals
- No significant hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, or systemic toxicity reported at therapeutic doses
