# Leopard's bane — خانق النمر (*khāniq al-namir*, 'choker of the leopard'); also 'killer of the leopard' (قاتل النمر)

## *A poisonous climbing plant with ivy-like leaves and bean-like pods, from Dioscorides as quoted by Avicenna; compare Dioscorides 4-81 (apocynon), where the translator's suggestion is *Apocynum venetum*, a suggestion only (confidence moderate for the Dioscoridean plant, low for the modern species)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** CM

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** The Canon describes this plant as a deadly poison that kills dogs, wolves, foxes and leopards, and says it is not used internally or externally. Historical record only; nobody should take it.

**Single-text entry.** Named only by Avicenna's *Canon* among the texts on disk (with Dioscorides's chapter on disk as a reading aid for the description only). Source file: `Sources/CM_Avicenna/text/IbnSina_Qanun_OpenITI_Shamela0010706.txt`.

**Loci (CM).** The spine row points to line 19190, a blank-heading stub: a heading with no name followed by 'It is the killer of the leopard, and it has been said about it' (هو قاتل النمر وقد قيل فيه; lines 19190-19192). That stub is a back-reference. The substance itself is described under the heading *khāniq al-namir* (خانق النمر), Arabic text line 19182, entry to line 19189 (the next heading is *khilāf*, line 19194). This entry reports both.

**Dioscorides quoted (CM).** 'Dioscorides said: a plant with long thin stems hard to crush; its leaves resemble those of ivy (*labālib*) but are softer, with a sharper tip and a heavy smell, moist with a sticky yellow moisture; its fruit resembles the pods of the bean, a finger long, and inside are small, hard, black seeds' (lines 19183-19187). The English Dioscorides on disk (4-81 APOCUNON, line 8766 of `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, read for this entry) has the same bean-like pod, small hard black seeds, a heavy scent and a viscous yellow juice, and says that the leaves put into bread kill dogs, wolves, foxes and panthers. So the Canon is evidently drawing on that chapter; the leaf of ivy and the willow stem differ slightly, and the match is moderate, not certain.

**What the text says (CM).** Properties: the leaf of this plant, mixed with fat and baked in bread and fed to wolves, dogs, foxes and leopards (النمور), kills them; it weakens their strength at the moment they eat it; and 'it is not used, neither internally nor externally' (ولا يستعمل لا داخلا ولا خارجا; lines 19187-19189). Poisons: 'a deadly poison; it is said that if brought near a scorpion it subdues it' (lines 19189-19190). The Canon thus gives it no medicinal use.

### Active Components

The text names no constituent. In general terms, the *Apocynum* group (dogbane family) is known to contain cardiac glycosides. This is general botany, not from the Canon, and is not asserted for this plant. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Toxic.** The text says it kills dogs, wolves, foxes and leopards and is a deadly poison. Do not ingest or handle the plant or its sap.
