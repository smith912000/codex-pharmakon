# Psoricum — Psorikon (ψωρικόν), "Psoricum" in the translation (Dioscorides 5-116)
## *A prepared product, not a found mineral: two parts of "calcitis" to one of cadmia, pounded with vinegar, matured in a dung-covered jar and burnt; the translator's suggestion is "Calcium Carbonate & Cadmium Earth — Psoricum"* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek Tradition (DMM).** The main locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-116 *PSORIKON* in the 2000 English translation (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10429-10431; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 889; book index "Psoricum 802", line 17162; list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 842, "Psoricum, , mineral, DMM V, id_uncertain yes"). The translator's heading is "SUGGESTED: Calcium Carbonate & Cadmium Earth — Psoricum."

Dioscorides' account, read in full, is one paragraph that is entirely a recipe:

"Psoricum is made of two parts of calcitis [calcium carbonate] mixed with one of cadmia [5-84], and pounded into small pieces with vinegar. It must be tightly shut up in a ceramic jar covered in dung for forty days, in the hottest days, so it becomes sharper, and it is good for whatever calcitis is. Some mix the materials in equal proportions, beat them in wine, and do the same as before. They burn it by placing it in a new ceramic jar and setting it over burning coals. Measure the burning this way: those full of moisture must become without bubbles and perfectly dry, and the rest must change into a more flourishing colour. When it is like blood (or red ochre) deep within, then it must be taken off. Blow off the filth on top of it and put it in jars. It is roasted on coals that are blown [with bellows] until it becomes a pale yellow, or in a ceramic pot over burning coals and turned often until it is on fire and changes colour." (line 10431)

**Reading the chapter.** (1) Composition: "two parts of calcitis" to "one of cadmia", pounded with vinegar; "some" use equal parts and wine. (2) Maturing: shut in a ceramic jar "covered in dung for forty days, in the hottest days, so it becomes sharper". (3) Use: "good for whatever calcitis is", so its indications are those of chalcitis (5-115), which is "astringent, warming and scab forming" and is used for the eyes, mouth and growths (line 10426). The chapter gives no indications of its own. (4) Burning: in a new ceramic jar on coals; endpoint tests by the look of the material ("without bubbles and perfectly dry"; "like blood (or red ochre) deep within"; "pale yellow" for the bellows-blown roast); the surface "filth" is blown off and the product stored in jars. No place of origin and no choice of the best kind are given, because it is a made product.

**Cross-references.** (1) Cadmia, 5-84 (line 10220), the second ingredient, is described there as "binding", filling sores, stopping discharges of the pores, drying and drawing to a scab, and creating "a new skin on malignant ulcers"; its preparation by burning and quenching in wine or vinegar is described in that chapter and was read only in part. (2) The misy chapter, 5-117 (line 10437), says misy is "burnt the same way" as chalcitis "without psoricum [5-116] being produced from it": psoricum is therefore something made from calcitis and cadmia and not from misy.

**Identity.** The translator brackets "calcitis" as "calcium carbonate". Yet the chapter on chalcitis (5-115) describes a material that "looks like brass", with "glittering veins" (line 10426), which is not a description of calcium carbonate; the translator's "Calcite — Calcium Carbonate" heading for that chapter is a suggestion. The ingredients, and so the product, are therefore not settled by the text. The list row marks the identity uncertain. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Stated generally. If the ingredients are the copper-bearing chalcitis and zinc-bearing cadmia of the neighbouring chapters, the product after burning would be a mixture of metal oxides and salts; if calcite is meant, it would burn to lime. The text gives no composition, and the gloss is in doubt (above), so none is asserted for psoricum.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The chapter is a workshop recipe for a roasted mineral preparation whose only stated use is to serve "for whatever calcitis is". It is a metal-working product with the hazards of its ingredients, and the sources give no protocol for taking or applying it. No modern protocol is supplied.

---

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations are the recipe itself (loci above): two parts calcitis to one cadmia (or equal parts), pounded with vinegar or wine; matured forty days in a dung-covered jar in the hottest days; burnt in a new ceramic jar on coals until dry and "like blood (or red ochre) deep within", or roasted with bellows to pale yellow; the surface blown clear and the product stored in jars (line 10431). No dose and no route of use are given in this chapter. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM).** No quality of its own is printed. The chapter says psoricum is "good for whatever calcitis is" and becomes "sharper" with maturing (line 10431). Chalcitis is printed "astringent, warming and scab forming" (line 10426); that is stated of chalcitis, not of psoricum. No degree of hot, cold, moist or dry is printed for psoricum.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers is a discipline of attention, and this chapter is a clear case: it gives no cultivation but a method, with endpoints that can be seen (no bubbles, perfectly dry, "like blood (or red ochre) deep within", pale yellow) and a note of what variants exist ("some" use equal parts and wine). The chapter is therapeutic by reference to chalcitis and no cultivation status is assigned; no amuletic use is recorded.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here for a cultivator to make or take. What carries over is the habit of testing a process by an observable endpoint and of recording the variants other workers use. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Toxicity:** copper, zinc and other metal compounds in the ingredients are irritant and poisonous if swallowed.
- **Fumes:** burning and roasting metal-bearing minerals and cadmia gives off irritant fumes; ores can carry arsenic, lead and, in the case of some zinc ores, cadmium. The recipe should not be followed.
- **Wounds and eyes:** an astringent metal calx applied to eyes, gums or ulcers can injure tissue.
- **Identity:** the ingredient identification is unsettled; the name cannot guarantee composition.
