# Bell metal — Kansya (Kāṃsya), "Indian bell-metal"
## *An alloy metal, rendered "Indian bell-metal" by Bhishagratna, the translator of the Sushruta Samhita; the text itself gives no composition* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** Bell metal appears in the Sushruta Samhita under the name *Kansya*, which the translator glosses "Indian bell-metal" (in the Uttara-tantra the same word is spelt "Kamsya" and "Kcimsya" in the OCR text). It is named in the Sutrasthana, chapter 46, a chapter that opens as the discourse "on food and drink (Annapana-Vidhi-madhyaya)" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` lines 1-6). Near its end the chapter turns to the alkalis and then to "The Metal group" (line 2121). The line the spine cites is line 2131: "Kansya Indian bell-metal) has a bitter taste, and acts as a liquefacient agent. It subdues the Kapham and Vayu, and is beneficial to the eyesight." (The opening parenthesis is missing in the machine-read text; the sentence is quoted as it stands. The same wording is in `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/Bhishagratna_Vol1_djvu.txt` line 21791.)

The metals around it in the same passage give the frame in which the text places it (lines 2121-2141). Gold "has a sweet and agreeable taste, acts as a tonic or restorative elixir, imparts rotundity to the body, and subdues the action of all the three deranged humours"; silver "has an acid taste, is laxative and cooling"; copper "has a sweet and astringent taste, and acts as a liquefacient and corrosive agent"; iron "generates Vayu, is cooling in its potency, allays thirst"; zinc and lead "are vermifugal, as well as liquefacient and corrosive". Bell metal is the only one of these given a bitter taste. The text does not say what bell metal is made of.

Sushruta's other mentions, all in the Uttara-tantra (the part on eye, ear, nose and head disease), show bell metal used as a material and as a comparison:

- **As a drug in an eye salve.** In chapter 12 (the chapter on the blood-origin type of *abhishyanda*, ophthalmia) a compound of "Chandana, Kumuda, Patra, Sila-jatu, Kumkuma, powders of dead iron and dead copper, Tuttha (Sulphate of copper), lead, bell-metal, Rasanjana and the resinous exudation of a Nimba tree taken in equal parts" is pasted with honey and made into "large Vartis (sticks)", applied to the eye "as an Anjana" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/uttara/SS_Uttara_ch12.txt` lines 66-74). A few lines on in the same chapter (line 264) "the rust of bell-metal pasted with clarified butter" is given as an alternative Anjana.
- **As a vessel.** In chapter 18 (the Kriyakalpa chapter, on preparations for eye disease in general) the vessels for the different Anjanas and their rods are to be "made of gold, silver, horn, copper, Vaidurya, bell-metal and iron respectively (in accordance with the different tastes of the drugs the Anjanas are made of)" (`SS_Uttara_ch18.txt` lines 446-451), and, in the same chapter, an Anjana "of bitter taste should be placed in a vessel made of bell-metal" (line 496). A lamp-black Anjana is prepared "by collecting lamp-black on a vessel made of Indian bell-metal" and mixing it with Yashtimadhu, Saindhava, Tagara and roots of Eranda and Brihati (lines 712-720).
- **In a translator's note.** A footnote in chapter 54 (`SS_Uttara_ch54.txt` line 219) says that oil cooked with the Surasadi group of drugs "should be placed in a pot of Indian bell-metal", and that when the inner surface of the pot turns blue by oxidation the oil is stirred and mixed with the blue part and used as an errhine. This is the translator's footnote, not the verse.
- **As a colour or sound.** The text uses bell metal as a comparison: a speck "coloured like the Indian bell-metal" on the white of the eye (`SS_Uttara_ch04.txt` line 46); a patch "coloured like bell-metal" in the Pittaja form of *linganasha* (`SS_Uttara_ch07.txt` line 156); and a cough with "a peculiar sound resembling that of broken Indian bell-metal" (`SS_Uttara_ch52.txt` lines 17-22).

### Active Components

No text read gives a composition for bell metal. In general terms, bell metal is a bronze: an alloy of copper with a large proportion of tin. Old alloys of this kind sometimes also carried lead, zinc or other metals, and the exact make-up varies with the maker. The "rust" and the blue film the text mentions are the corrosion products that form on copper-rich alloys in air and moisture; these are copper compounds. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Sushruta text records bell metal as a bitter, liquefacient metal said to subdue Kapha and Vata and to be good for the eyes, as an ingredient and a rust in eye salves, and as the vessel in which bitter eye salves were kept. The Codex does not recommend swallowing or applying it. The eye is a sensitive organ; copper-bearing corrosion products are irritant and poisonous, and no part of the text read supports a modern use.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives the preparations above and no more: the equal-parts compound made into sticks with honey and applied as an Anjana (Uttara ch.12, line 70); the rust of bell metal pasted with clarified butter as an alternative Anjana (ch.12, line 264); the vessel preparation of lamp-black (ch.18, lines 712-720). "Equal parts" is the only proportion given; no weight, quantity or number of applications is printed for the bell-metal items in the passages read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. The text does not describe how bell metal itself was made, purified or burnt to a powder; the "dead" (calcined) metals named beside it in ch.12 are given no method in the lines read. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Bronze, the copper-tin alloy family to which bell metal belongs, has been used for bells, vessels and tools since antiquity. Copper and its salts are toxic when swallowed in quantity and irritate skin and eyes; the green and blue films on old bronze (verdigris and related salts) are copper compounds and are not safe to put in the eye. Alloys made with lead are a lead hazard. Copper and brass vessels can release copper into acid foods and drinks. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Ayurveda (SS):** The text prints, for *Kansya*: taste *bitter*; action *liquefacient* (translator's term; "acts as a liquefacient agent"); effect on the humours *subdues Kapham and Vayu*; and *beneficial to the eyesight* (Sutra ch.46, line 2131). No *virya* (potency) is printed for it in this passage, in contrast with copper, iron, gold and silver, which have "cooling" or similar named in the same stretch. No *vipaka* is printed. The Uttara-tantra connects the metal with bitter taste in the vessel rule (ch.18, lines 446-451 and 496).

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status found.* The substance sits in the *dravyaguna* frame of the Sutrasthana (taste, action, effect on the humours) and in the Uttara-tantra's eye pharmacy. In the same passage of Sutra ch.46 gold is called "a tonic or restorative elixir"; bell metal is not, and in the passages read the text does not name it in a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* context, and nothing in them speaks of *ojas*. The text's use of it as a vessel is a point of method (each salve in the vessel suited to its taste), not a vital-substrate claim.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. Bell metal is a classical bitter metal of eye pharmacy, and the Codex does not recommend taking it or putting it in the eye. What the text teaches is a matching rule: a vessel and a rod chosen to suit the taste of what they hold. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Toxicity:** copper-rich alloys and their corrosion products (blue and green films, rust) are poisonous if swallowed and irritant to the eye and skin; do not take bell metal by mouth, and do not put its powder, rust or any alloy into the eye. Old alloys may also contain lead.
- **Cross-tradition note, not a Sushruta statement:** Clark's translation of the Tibetan *rGyud-bzhi* ch.20 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 841) says of *bshah.dkar* (stannic ore): "is mixed with copper to make bell metal". That line names bell metal and so is a candidate locus for tagging FMT; it has not been added to the tag line.
- **Vessels:** a bell-metal vessel is not suitable for acid foods or for storing medicines intended to be swallowed.
- **Pregnancy and children:** any copper-bearing preparation is unsuitable.
- **Identity:** the translator's "Indian bell-metal" is the only identification given; the text states no composition.
