# Soybean — Da Dou (大豆), Soja, Shu (菽), Glycine, Edamame (fresh)

## *Glycine max* (L.) Merr. | Family: Fabaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, SBJ, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Cool and moist (Galenic: cold in the first degree, moist in the first) — a nourishing, temperate legume that the humoral tradition read as thickening and cooling to an over-heated constitution.
**Training Phase:** Foundation / Nourishment — a base-tier substance used to build reserve and steady the endocrine terrain rather than to provoke acute change.

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Shennong (Chinese materia medica — CM):** The soybean, *Da Dou*, is among the "five grains" (*wu gu*) of the earliest Chinese agronomy and appears in the dietary-medicinal stream that the *Shennong Bencao Jing* tradition organised. Classical practice distinguished the yellow soybean (*huang dou*) as a Spleen-and-Blood nourisher from the black soybean (*hei dou*, *Semen Sojae Nigrum*), which was directed to the Kidney and used to "move water" and settle wind. The most medicinally specific form is the fermented preparation **Dan Dou Chi** (*Semen Sojae Praeparatum*) — soybeans fermented with mugwort and other herbs — classically given to release the exterior and relieve the "vexation and oppression" of a lingering febrile disease. Fermentation, in the doctrinal reading, transforms a heavy nourishing bean into a light, dispersing, upward-and-outward remedy.

**Sushruta / Ayurveda (SS):** Within the South Asian materia medica the soybean sits among the *shimbi* (leguminous) foods — heavy (*guru*), building, and *kapha*-forming when eaten to excess, yet valued as a vegetarian source of strength-giving nourishment (*bala*). Ayurvedic dietetics treats pulses as grounding and mineral-rich but flatulent, requiring correction by fermentation, sprouting, or warming spices — a practical anticipation of the modern observation that fermentation degrades the oligosaccharides and phytate that make raw soy hard to digest.

**Avicenna / Unani (AT):** The Unani tradition inherited the bean-and-pulse pharmacology of Galen and Dioscorides. Beans of this class were classed as cooling and moist, nourishing but "windy" (flatulent), thickening to thin humours, and were prescribed cooked and well-seasoned to temper their heaviness. Avicenna's *Qanun* treats the legumes as foods-that-are-also-medicines — the paradigm to which soy belongs.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Every tradition read soy as a *nourishing but heavy* substance requiring transformation — fermentation, sprouting, spicing — to release its virtue. Modern pharmacology gives this intuition a mechanism: the raw bean carries protein, phytate and oligosaccharides that fermentation cleaves, while the isoflavones bound as glycosides are hydrolysed to their more bioavailable aglycone forms (genistein, daidzein) by microbial and gut β-glucosidases. The old rule "transform the bean before you trust it" tracks a real change in what the body can absorb.

### Active Components

- **Isoflavones** — genistein, daidzein, and glycitein, present largely as glucoside conjugates (genistin, daidzin). These are the phytoestrogens: diphenolic molecules that bind oestrogen receptors, with relative selectivity for ERβ.
- **Equol** — a downstream metabolite of daidzein produced only by certain gut bacteria; "equol producers" (a minority of people) may respond differently to soy.
- **Soy protein** — a complete plant protein (~36–40% of the dry bean), the basis of tofu, tempeh, and soy protein isolate.
- **Lecithin / phospholipids**, unsaturated oils, and **saponins**.
- **Oligosaccharides** (raffinose, stachyose) and **phytic acid** — the antinutritional / flatulent fraction reduced by fermentation.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Soy is filed as a **Foundation-phase nourisher with a secondary endocrine-modulating action.** Its practical uses in the Kenetica framing are (1) as a temperate, complete-protein base food for building reserve, and (2) as a mild phytoestrogenic support for the perimenopausal transition and for cardiovascular-lipid terrain. It is not a fast-acting or heroic remedy; it works over weeks of dietary intake, consistent with its classification as a food-medicine. The fermented form (Dan Dou Chi / tempeh / miso) is preferred where digestibility or a lighter, dispersing action is wanted.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the phytoestrogen and lipid effects of soy have both been examined in pooled clinical data.

- A meta-analysis and systematic review of phytoestrogens for menopausal symptoms (15 RCTs) found that phytoestrogens produced a significantly greater reduction in hot-flush frequency than placebo (pooled mean difference in hot flushes favouring treatment, p < 0.005), while showing no significant effect on the broader Kupperman index and no significant excess of side-effects (Chen, Lin & Liu, *Climacteric*, 2014; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3109/13697137.2014.966241)).
- On lipids, a meta-analysis of 11 RCTs found soy isoflavones significantly lowered serum total cholesterol (−0.10 mmol/L, ~1.77%, p = 0.02) and LDL cholesterol (−0.13 mmol/L, ~3.58%, p < 0.0001), with larger reductions in hypercholesterolaemic subjects (Taku et al., *Am J Clin Nutr*, 2007; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/85.4.1148)).
- A counter-weight in the literature: an earlier meta-analysis found no dose-response relation between soy-associated isoflavones and LDL/HDL change, attributing cholesterol effects more to the protein matrix than to isoflavones per se (Weggemans & Trautwein, *Eur J Clin Nutr*, 2003; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ejcn.1601628)).

**Evidence grade: moderate.** Effects are real but modest in magnitude, and the literature genuinely disagrees on how much is isoflavone versus protein. Claims should stay proportionate: soy modestly reduces hot-flush frequency and modestly lowers LDL — it is not a hormone-replacement substitute.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** *Da Dou* — sweet, neutral-to-cool; enters Spleen and Large Intestine (black soybean → Kidney). Actions: tonify Spleen, nourish Blood, clear heat, promote urination/move water (black form). **Dan Dou Chi** — acrid, sweet, slightly cold; enters Lung and Stomach; releases the exterior, relieves irritability/oppression.
- **Ayurveda:** Rasa — *madhura* (sweet); Guna — *guru* (heavy), *snigdha* (unctuous); Virya — *shita* (cooling); Vipaka — *madhura* (sweet). *Kapha*-increasing, *vata*-provoking (flatulent) unless fermented/spiced.
- **Galenic / Unani (Avicennian):** cold and moist; nourishing, thickening, flatulent; corrected by cooking and warming carminatives.

### Contraindications

- **Oestrogen-sensitive conditions — caution.** Because isoflavones are phytoestrogens with ER-binding activity, concentrated isoflavone supplements should be used cautiously (and under medical guidance) by people with a history of oestrogen-receptor-positive breast cancer or other hormone-sensitive conditions. Dietary soy is generally regarded differently from high-dose extracts, but concentrated supplementation warrants clinician oversight. [USER: verify current oncology guidance for the individual.]
- **Thyroid.** Soy isoflavones can act as goitrogens and may interfere with thyroid function or with levothyroxine absorption, particularly where iodine intake is marginal or in overt hypothyroidism. Separate soy intake from thyroid medication and monitor.
- **Soy allergy.** Soy is a recognised major food allergen; contraindicated in soy-allergic individuals (risk from anaphylaxis to urticaria).
- **Raw/undercooked soy.** Contains trypsin inhibitors and lectins — must be thoroughly cooked or fermented.
- **Infants.** Soy-based formula and high isoflavone exposure in infancy is a specialist decision, not a self-directed one.
