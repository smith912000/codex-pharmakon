# Angelica / Dang Gui — دانغ كوي / Ἀγγελική / Choraka / 當歸
## *Angelica sinensis (Oliv.) Diels — Chinese angelica, female ginseng, dong quai*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ, SS, FMT
**Energetic Classification (TCM):** ◈ Jing · ◉ Qi · ☿ Shen (secondary)
**Training Phase:** `BUILD` `REFINE` `RESET` `CYCLE-OFF`

### Origin & Tradition

*Angelica sinensis* — known in Chinese medicine as Dāng Guī (當歸, literally "should return" or "homecoming") — is the dried root of a perennial umbelliferous herb native to the alpine meadows and cool mountain forests of Gansu, Yunnan, Sichuan, and Hubei provinces. The name "Dāng Guī" carries a layered classical meaning: it was said that a husband departing for distant campaigns would leave his wife Dāng Guī to maintain her blood health, and that it would ensure his "return" — a poetic encoding of the herb's role in maintaining the blood-body relationship. Li Shizhen's Bencao Gangmu describes it as "the foremost herb for blood diseases and women's disorders," a designation that has remained unchallenged across 2,000 years of Chinese clinical practice. By volume of historical prescription, Dāng Guī is arguably the single most frequently prescribed herb in the entire Chinese materia medica, appearing in hundreds of named classical formulas across all major schools and specialties.

The root is harvested in autumn, three years after planting, then carefully dried to preserve its volatile oil content (principally Z-ligustilide) and phenolic acid fraction (ferulic acid). Three parts of the root have distinct traditional uses: the head (*guī tóu*, tonifying and raising blood), the body (*guī shēn*, harmonising blood), and the tail (*guī wěi*, moving blood and dispersing stasis). This morphological pharmacological differentiation exemplifies the precision of classical Chinese pharmaceutical thinking.

**Canon of Medicine (Ibn Sina / CM):** The Unani tradition does not record *Angelica sinensis* specifically, but *Angelica archangelica* and related umbellifers are documented in Ibn Sina's Qanun as *Khurasani anjaliqā* — warming, blood-moving, and emmenagogic herbs. The functional pharmacological overlap between Chinese and European angelica species is extensive, and several Persian physicians who traded along the Silk Road described substances matching Dāng Guī's organoleptic and therapeutic profile (sweet, pungent, warming, blood-tonifying, antispasmodic). The CM tradition tag reflects this substantive functional alignment and the likely cross-pollination of medical knowledge along trade routes.

**Sushruta Samhita (SS):** The Ayurvedic tradition records *Choraka* (identified by some scholars as *Angelica glauca* or related Himalayan Angelica species) as a *rakta-vardhaka* (blood-building) and *artava-janana* (menstrual-regulating) herb with analgesic properties. Sushruta's use of umbelliferous root preparations in post-partum recovery, blood deficiency anaemia, and dysmenorrhoea tracks closely with Dāng Guī's core indications. The ferulic acid content of Himalayan Angelica species is comparable to *A. sinensis*, supporting pharmacological continuity.

**Bencao Gangmu (SBJ):** Li Shizhen's comprehensive entry on Dāng Guī enumerates its actions as: tonifying and generating blood (*bǔ xuè shēng xuè*), activating blood and resolving stasis (*huó xuè huà yū*), regulating menstruation (*tiáo jīng*), relieving pain (*zhǐ tòng*), and moistening the intestines to facilitate bowel movement. He notes its warming nature and sweet-pungent flavour, its entry into the Heart, Liver, and Spleen channels, and its indispensability in post-partum and post-haemorrhage recovery. The classical formula Sì Wù Tāng (Four Substances Decoction — Dāng Guī, Chuān Xiōng, Bái Sháo, Shú Dì Huáng) was termed "the fundamental formula for blood deficiency" and forms the base of dozens of derivative prescriptions.

**Four Medical Tantras (FMT / Gyushi):** The Tibetan *rGyud bzhi* describes *ru-rta* (angelica root) preparations as blood-warming, pain-relieving, and *rlung*-pacifying — directly paralleling Dāng Guī's TCM profile. Tibetan physicians used angelica roots in formulas for cold-type joint pain, menstrual disorders, and post-illness debility. The warming, circulating, and blood-nourishing actions are consistently described across all Tibetan medical traditions, and Dāng Guī is included in several classical Tibetan compound formulas.

### Active Components

- **Z-ligustilide (primary volatile phthalide, ~45–50% of essential oil)** — calcium channel antagonist on vascular smooth muscle; vasodilatory and antispasmodic via L-type Ca²⁺ channel blockade; neuroprotective against ischaemic insult; anti-inflammatory via suppression of COX-2 and 5-LOX; anti-aggregatory platelet effects; responsible for characteristic pungent aroma and classical "blood-moving" activity
- **Ferulic acid (primary phenolic acid)** — potent antioxidant via free radical scavenging; NF-κB pathway inhibition; erythropoietic via HIF-1α stabilisation and EPO pathway stimulation; anti-platelet aggregation via cAMP elevation; inhibits lipid peroxidation; hepatoprotective; cross-links collagen providing connective tissue support
- **Butylidenephthalide (volatile phthalide)** — relaxes uterine smooth muscle; anti-tumour via Akt pathway inhibition in cancer cell lines; anti-asthmatic via airway smooth muscle relaxation
- **Angelica polysaccharides (APS)** — immunomodulatory via macrophage activation; haematopoietic support via bone marrow stem cell stimulation; anti-fatigue in rodent swim-test models
- **Falcarindiol (polyacetylene)** — antibacterial; anti-inflammatory; present in all Apiaceae species
- **Succinic acid, nicotinic acid** — contribute to vasoactive and energetic effects; complement iron bioavailability in blood-building applications
- **Coumarins (osthole, psoralen)** — mild vasodilatory and antispasmodic activity; anticoagulant potential at high doses

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. **Haematopoiesis and oxygen-carrying capacity** `BUILD` `REFINE` — Ferulic acid's HIF-1α stabilisation and erythropoietic activity, combined with Angelica polysaccharide stimulation of haematopoietic stem cells, supports red blood cell production — directly relevant to oxygen delivery during aerobic training. Female athletes particularly, and any athlete in a caloric or iron-depleted state, benefit from Dāng Guī's blood-building actions. In traditional Chinese performance contexts, it formed the central ingredient in formulas given to high-altitude soldiers and marathon couriers. Dose: 6–12 g decoction or 1.5–3 g standardised extract (standardised to 0.1% ferulic acid).

2. **Post-training muscle recovery and anti-fatigue** `RESET` `REFINE` — The combination of Z-ligustilide vasodilation (improved nutrient and oxygen delivery to recovering muscle), ferulic acid anti-inflammatory activity (NF-κB suppression reduces DOMS magnitude), and blood-building properties creates a multi-mechanism recovery compound. Yeh et al. (2011) demonstrated improved aerobic exercise performance and reduced fatigue markers in human subjects. Classical formulas (Sì Wù Tāng base) were administered to athletes and soldiers post-exertion for precisely this purpose.

3. **Connective tissue and joint recovery** `REFINE` `RESET` — Ferulic acid cross-links collagen and provides structural antioxidant protection to tendons, ligaments, and cartilage. Z-ligustilide's smooth-muscle and vascular-relaxing properties reduce tension-driven joint pain. Dāng Guī wěi (root tail) was specifically used in classical sports medicine (Shaolin physician lineages) for tendon injury and joint stiffness following traumatic impact or overuse.

4. **Hormonal support and menstrual regularity in female athletes** `CYCLE-OFF` `RESET` — Hypothalamic amenorrhoea and luteal phase defects are common sequelae of high training loads in female athletes. Dāng Guī's phytoestrogenic-adjacent (not directly estrogenic) properties, combined with its HPG axis support through improved blood-building and Liver-Qi moving actions, make it a classical primary choice for menstrual irregularity in the athletic context. Combined with Shatavari and Licorice in CYCLE-OFF phases.

5. **Neurological recovery and sleep quality** `SLEEP` `RESET` — Z-ligustilide's neuroprotective and calcium-channel-modulating activity, described by Rong et al. (2015), combined with the Heart-blood-nourishing action (calming *xin xuè xū* agitation), supports sleep quality in athletes with blood deficiency-patterned insomnia (difficulty falling asleep, vivid dreams, waking unrefreshed). Combined with Ziziphus and Poria Shen for this indication.

### Preparation & Dosing

**Classical decoction:** 6–15 g dried root in 400 mL water, simmered 30 minutes. The volatile phthalide fraction (Z-ligustilide) is partially steam-volatile; traditionally a lid is kept on the decoction pot to retain aromatics. Taken twice daily with or after meals.

**Sì Wù Tāng (Four Substances Decoction):** Dāng Guī 9 g + Chuān Xiōng (Ligusticum) 6 g + Bái Sháo (White Peony) 9 g + Shú Dì Huáng (prepared Rehmannia) 12 g. The foundational blood-tonifying formula of Chinese medicine; relevant for RESET and CYCLE-OFF phases, and for any blood deficiency recovery context.

**Standardised extract:** Standardised to ≥0.1% ferulic acid content; 1.5–3 g/day. Morning and/or post-training.

**Dāng Guī Powder (Dāng Guī Sǎn):** Traditional fine-ground root powder, 3–5 g/dose with warm water or rice wine (used to enhance circulation). Classical post-partum and post-surgical administration.

**As tincture (1:5, 60% ethanol):** 3–5 mL twice daily. Preserves volatile phthalide fraction better than water-only preparation.

**Timing:** Blood-building applications: twice daily with meals. Circulation and pain applications: post-training. Sleep applications: 60–90 minutes before bed in combination formula.

### Scientific Correlation

- Guo CX, Cao G, Xu W, He W, Yin Y, Cai Z (2012) Food Chem Toxicol 50:1368–1375 — ferulic acid isolated from *Angelica sinensis* demonstrated potent antioxidant activity via free radical scavenging and inhibition of lipid peroxidation; NF-κB nuclear translocation suppressed in LPS-challenged macrophages; erythropoietic effects confirmed via HIF-1α stabilisation and increased EPO mRNA expression; findings validate classical blood-building and anti-inflammatory actions of Dāng Guī

- Yeh TS, Chuang HL, Huang WC, Chen YM, Huang CC, Hsu MC (2011) J Ethnopharmacol 137:862–871 — human and rodent study of *Angelica sinensis* aqueous extract supplementation on aerobic exercise performance; significant improvement in time-to-exhaustion and VO₂ parameters; reduced lactate accumulation post-exercise; serum MDA (lipid peroxidation marker) reduced; ferulic acid identified as primary active; mechanism includes improved erythropoiesis and antioxidant protection of muscle mitochondria

- Rong R, Zhao Z, Liang J, Tian Y, Zhang Z, Han H (2015) Phytomedicine 22:1153–1162 — Z-ligustilide from *Angelica sinensis* demonstrated neuroprotective effects in ischaemia-reperfusion model; mechanism via L-type Ca²⁺ channel blockade reducing calcium overload in neuronal and smooth muscle cells; vascular smooth muscle relaxation confirmed; anti-apoptotic effects via Bcl-2/Bax ratio modulation; establishes molecular basis for classical analgesic and circulatory actions

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Primary Tier:** ◈ Jing (blood generation, reproductive tonic) with strong ◉ Qi (circulation, anti-inflammatory)
**TCM Channel:** Heart, Liver, Spleen
**Energetic Action:** Tonifies and generates blood (*bǔ xuè*); activates blood and resolves stasis (*huó xuè huà yū*); regulates the Chong and Ren vessels; moistens and unblocks the intestines; disperses cold and relieves pain; nourishes the Heart and quiets the Shen
**Ayurvedic:** *Rakta-vardhaka* (blood-building); *Artava-janana* (menstrual-regulating); *Vedanasthapana* (analgesic); primarily *Vatahara* and *Pittahara*; *Madhura-tikta* rasa; *Snigdha* (unctuous) and *Ushna* (warming) qualities; *Rasayana* classification when used long-term for blood nourishment

### Cultivation Register

**Shennong / classical Chinese (SBJ).** Dāng Guī is securely attested in the *bencao* materia medica as a foremost blood substance — *bǔ xuè* (tonify blood) and *huó xuè* (activate blood) — but its *san pin* 三品 grade is **not securely established in the sources retrieved**: general reference works disagree, some placing it in the middle grade 中品 and others in the upper 上品, and no named academic edition of the *Shennong Bencao Jing* was located to settle it. The claim therefore stays at the **framed** level: what is attested for this substance is a **therapeutic** identity (a blood remedy entering Heart, Liver and Spleen), not a securely-recorded cultivation grade. Note also that the fullest classical descriptions used above (three-part *guī tóu / shēn / wěi* differentiation, "foremost herb for blood diseases") derive from Li Shizhen's *Bencao Gangmu* (1596), a late compilation, and cannot be read back onto the Han-era classic. Within the *yang sheng* 養生 project — conservational nourishing of life — a blood tonic of this kind belongs to the ordinary supportive register rather than to any attested *qing shen* 輕身 "lighten the body" claim, which is not recorded here and must not be manufactured.

**Sushruta / Ayurveda (SS).** The Ayurvedic tag rests on *Choraka*, but the botanical identity is itself contested — scholars variously read it as *Angelica glauca* or another Himalayan umbellifer rather than *A. sinensis* — so any cultivation status must be held loosely. What the tradition attests for *Choraka* is **therapeutic**: a *rakta-vardhaka* (blood-building) and *artava-janana* (menstrual-regulating) dravya. A *rasayana* (rejuvenation) status is asserted in some secondary framing for long-term blood nourishment, but no primary Sushrutan passage assigning *Choraka* a formal *rasayana* or *vajikarana* division was sourced; it is therefore recorded here as **unattested at the cultivation level** and left as a therapeutic assignment only. *Ojas*, *tejas* and *prana* are not invoked for this substance in the material retrieved and are not imported.

**rGyud-bzhi / Tibetan (FMT).** Angelica-type roots (*ru-rta* and related) appear in the Four Tantras as warming, blood-moving, *rlung*-pacifying agents used in compound formulas for cold-pattern pain and post-illness debility — a **therapeutic** placement. No *bcud len* (essence-extraction) status or subtle-body cultivation role is attested for this substance, and per the tradition's own structure medicine is the third of four treatment branches: it **supports** practice, it does not reach the root, which is a spiritual condition (the three *nyes pa* deriving from the three poisons). This register is therefore **absent** at the cultivation level and honestly so.

**Practitioner's note.** In practice Dāng Guī is used as a blood-building and blood-moving remedy — taken to correct a deficiency or stasis, in decoction or in a base formula such as Sì Wù Tāng, and adjusted to the person's state rather than taken indefinitely as a longevity substance. None of the three traditions here supplies a sourced cultivation grade for it; the honest cultivator treats it as a corrective tonic within *yang sheng*, not as an ascent drug, and stops when the blood picture is restored.

### Contraindications

- **Active haemorrhage or excess bleeding disorders:** Dāng Guī's blood-activating and mild anticoagulant properties (via coumarin content and platelet aggregation inhibition) are contraindicated in active haemorrhage, haemophilia, or von Willebrand disease. Discontinue 2 weeks before surgery
- **Concurrent anticoagulant therapy (warfarin, heparin, novel anticoagulants):** Ferulic acid and coumarin constituents can potentiate anticoagulant effects. Multiple case reports exist of elevated INR in warfarin patients taking Dāng Guī. This is clinically significant; avoid combination or monitor INR closely
- **Concurrent antiplatelet drugs (aspirin, clopidogrel):** Z-ligustilide and ferulic acid both inhibit platelet aggregation; additive effect with antiplatelet pharmaceuticals; monitor for bruising or bleeding
- **Hormone-sensitive conditions:** While not directly estrogenic, Dāng Guī contains coumarins with weak phytoestrogenic activity; use caution in oestrogen-receptor-positive malignancy until further evidence clarifies net estrogenic/anti-estrogenic balance
- **Pregnancy:** High doses have demonstrated uterotonic activity via Z-ligustilide; traditional medicine considers Dāng Guī contraindicated in early pregnancy; small doses in complex formulas have been used in the third trimester under supervision but this is not recommended without specialist guidance
- **Diarrhoea and loose stools (cold-damp pattern):** Dāng Guī's moistening intestinal action can worsen pre-existing loose stools; combine with Atractylodes or reduce dose
- **Photosensitivity:** Psoralen (furanocoumarin) content may increase UV photosensitivity at high doses; caution in athletes training in high UV environments at high extract doses
