# Dracocephalum heterophyllum — Ngoga, sngo sga, "Dragonhead" (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary)

## *Dracocephalum heterophyllum, as printed with the English "Dragonhead" in the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary directly after the name "Ngoga" and its transliteration "sngo sga"; Clark 1995 prints no line for this binomial or this name* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and by the Men-Tsee-Khang text alone.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. Clark 1995 (`Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`) prints no *Dracocephalum heterophyllum*, no *sngo.sga* and no "Ngoga"; its only *Dracocephalum* is "Dracocephalum tangutium Maxim" as the second Latin name for *pri.yang.ku* (line 1005), a different Tibetan name, and the index of Latin names lists "Dracocephalum tangutium" (line 1578). The entry therefore rests on one glossary pairing and one running-text sentence. The extraction note reads: "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 325). The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (lines 5500 to 5537).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "N" block (the letter at line 5500). The phonetic names are "Naga gesar Naga pukhpa" (line 5501), "Nalaesham" (5502), "Nampar" (5503), "Naram" (5504) and "Ngoga" (5505), six in all. The transliterations follow, one under another: "na ga ge sar" (5507), "nA ga pu+Sh+pa" (5513), "na le sham" (5517), "mam par" (5523), "na rams" (5529) and "sngo sga" (5535). Each is followed by its scientific name and English name: "Bombax ceiba" and "Silk-cotton tree" (5509, 5511), "Bombax ceiba" and "Silk-cotton tree" again (5513, 5515), "Piper nigrum" and "Black pepper" (5519, 5521), "Senecio dianthus" and "White ragwort" (5525, 5527), "Plantago lanceolata" and "Ribwort plantain" (5531, 5533), and, last, on one line directly after "sngo sga": "Dracocephalum heterophyllum Dragonhead" (line 5537). Counting in order, "Ngoga" is the sixth phonetic name and the last, and the sixth scientific name is *Dracocephalum heterophyllum*. The Codex therefore reads the glossary as pairing "Ngoga" with *Dracocephalum heterophyllum*. The binomial is not on the same line as the name, so it remains a pairing by order, though in this stretch the transliteration, the binomial and the English name follow each other without a gap. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text on "Ngoga".* The name is printed once in the text and once in the index, and the text gives no Latin name there. The passage is in the dietary material of Chapter 16, "Dietetics" (contents, line 209, which gives the chapter page 158 to the next chapter at page 176), on the printed page 170 (the page marker is at line 3050). Line 3046 reads: "Cooked nettle leaves help to treat loong disorders and generate heat, but aggravate baekan and tripa disorders. Cooked Champa and Baekhur generate heat and stop diarrhea. Cooked Dhawa pacifies loong disorders and dries up abscesses, but it also aggravates baekan and tripa disorders. Cooked Neu though harmful to the eyes, is effective against constipation. Cooked Moennae marpo pacifies all three nyepa. Cooked Kyab and Khurmang are cool in quality and treats hot disorders. Cooked Ngoga cures hot disorders associated with tripa and headache." The same line goes on with cooked bean leaves, mustard leaves, radish and garlic. The index (line 7453) lists "Ngoga 170". The name is not printed in chapter 20 (the efficacy of medicinal substances), in the taste lists of chapter 19 or in the compounding groups of chapter 21. The sentence treats Ngoga as a cooked leaf food with a therapeutic effect, and gives no medicinal preparation. [USER: verify]

*A near neighbour.* The glossary also prints "Dracocephalum tanguticum Dragon head" (line 5972), against a different Tibetan name; the block it stands in prints "Tiyangku" (line 5898) and "pri yang ku" (line 5901), and Clark's *pri.yang.ku* (line 1005) names *Dracocephalum tangutium* as the second of two Latin names. That is another drug, treated in I_230, and it is not this one.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Dracocephalum heterophyllum*, "Ngoga" and *sngo sga* found no entry on this plant. I_230 (*Dracocephalum tanguticum*, tagged FMT) and I_566 (*Dracocephalum moldavica*) are entries on other species of the genus; neither was read as a source here. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Dracocephalum* is a genus of the mint family (Lamiaceae), that its species are aromatic herbs, and that mint-family herbs are generally known to carry volatile oils. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Men-Tsee-Khang text records that "Cooked Ngoga cures hot disorders associated with tripa and headache" (line 3046). It is a statement in the dietary chapter about a cooked leaf, attached to this binomial only by a glossary pairing, and it is not a Codex protocol. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation the text gives is the word "cooked" (line 3046): the passage speaks of cooked leaves. It gives no part used beyond that, no method of cooking, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** No taste, power or post-digestive taste is printed for Ngoga in the lines read, and Clark prints no line for it. The only classification is by effect: it "cures hot disorders associated with tripa" (line 3046). The same sentence says of its neighbours that cooked Kyab and Khurmang "are cool in quality", and that is not said of Ngoga.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Men-Tsee-Khang text places Ngoga in its dietetics chapter, as a cooked leaf that cures hot disorders associated with tripa and headache. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the sentence and the glossary row cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic and dietary only, and the link from this binomial to that framing rests on a glossary pairing.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a cooked-leaf food that the Men-Tsee-Khang text says cures a hot tripa disorder and headache, with no preparation beyond "cooked" and no dose, and its Latin name rests on a glossary pairing. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. The identity rests on a glossary pairing, so no plant should be gathered, cooked or eaten on the strength of this entry. Headache that is sudden, severe or persistent needs medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
