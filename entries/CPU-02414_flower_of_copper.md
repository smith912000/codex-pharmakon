# Flower of copper — Chalkou Anthos (CHALKOU ANTHOS), "Flowers of Brass" (Dioscorides); also called psegma

## *The small, brittle, red granules thrown off when molten brass is chilled with water, used as a biting bowel, nasal and eye remedy; the translator's gloss is "Flowers of Brass" and the identification as a copper oxide scale or granule is ours, from the text's account of how it is made, of low confidence* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** The text calls this material "excessively biting" and gives a dose that "extracts thick fluids". Copper and brass compounds are poisonous and corrosive. This entry is a historical record only, and nobody should take or apply this material.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** This is a single-text entry. The chapter is 5-88 CHALKOU ANTHOS in Book Five of the *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10256-10258; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` carries it as 5-88, line 10256, translator's identification "Flowers of Brass", class mineral; `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 815 has "Chalkou Anthos", mineral). The Greek *chalkos* is rendered "brass" in the translation; the copper entry (Copper / Tong, CPU-00700, `II_96_Copper_Tong.md`) gives the chapter two short mentions ("therapeutic for the bowels" and the forty-grain dose) in a list of brass products, and notes that the "brass" equals copper only on the spine's reading. A search of Sushruta, Shennong, rGyud-bzhi and Ebers on disk found no separate attestation under this name, and none is claimed. The row began as an unverified placeholder on 13 October; this entry replaces it.

*Naming and choice.* The chapter (line 10258) opens: "The flowers of brass, which some of the ancients called psegma [ramentum — shavings] are best if they are very brittle and very red when crushed finely; and like millet in size — small, heavy and mildly glittering — and besides, not containing any dust of brass with which it is counterfeited. The powder spreading out when crushed by the teeth allows one to discern it."

*Making.* "It is made as follows. When the brass that is melted in metallic furnaces runs through the colanders of the adjoining pipes into the receptacles, those who supervise take away the scum, and pour on the purest water intending to cool it. The previously mentioned flower of brass is 'spit out' (as it were) as a result of this sudden thickening and hardening." So the "flower" is what the molten metal throws off when it is suddenly cooled with water at the furnace.

*Action and use.* "This is therapeutic for the bowels, represses abnormal growths, and wears off those things that darken the sight of the eyes as it is excessively biting. If forty grains are given it extracts thick fluids. It consumes abnormal growths of flesh in the nostrils, and with wine it represses pustules on the perineum. White flowers of brass are blown into the ears through a pipe for deafness that has lasted a long time. It is beaten finely and applied with honey to repress the tonsils and uvula."

So the text names a red granular kind and a "white" kind; gives uses by mouth (a bowel remedy and a purgative-like drink at forty grains), in the nose (to consume overgrowths), on the skin (with wine), in the eyes, as a blown powder in the ear, and with honey on the tonsils and uvula.

*The chapter next to it.* 5-89 LEPIS, "Brass Scales" (line 10259), is a different material (the scale from worked brass nails) with its own entry (Copper scales, CPU-02411, `II_175_copper_scales.md`), and its washed fine fraction is called there "the flower of squama". That phrase is a name used inside 5-89 for a part of the scale; it is not the 5-88 material, and the text keeps them in separate chapters.

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, the granules and scum that form when molten copper alloy meets water are mostly copper and copper oxides, with zinc or tin from an alloy. The "white" kind may carry zinc oxide, but the text does not say. Copper oxides and salts are astringent, irritant and, in quantity, caustic. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a recommended practice. Toxic.** The text records a furnace by-product used as a biting remedy in many places: bowel, nose, eye, ear, throat. The Codex gives no protocol and does not translate the text's "abnormal growths", "pustules on the perineum" or "deafness" into modern diagnoses. A caustic copper powder is unsafe in the eye, nose or ear.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these preparations (line 10258): finely crushed and tested between the teeth; a dose "forty grains" that "extracts thick fluids"; with wine for pustules on the perineum; the white kind blown through a pipe into the ear; beaten finely and applied with honey to the tonsils and uvula. The forty grains is the only quantity the chapter gives. The unit "grain" is the translator's word and its weight in the text is not given; it is not converted here. **Do not use this dose.** The text gives no frequency or duration. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Copper salts taken by mouth cause vomiting and purging, which fits in kind the text's "extracts thick fluids", and they damage the liver and kidney in larger amounts. They are corrosive to mucous membranes, which fits "excessively biting" and the nasal overgrowths. Copper compounds also have an antimicrobial effect on surfaces. None of this makes the material safe. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** The chapter prints "excessively biting" (line 10258) as its one quality. It prints no heat or moisture grade and no degree.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: the grain size, the colour, the brittleness, a test between the teeth to detect a dust of brass used as a counterfeit, and an account of exactly how the furnace makes it. The chapter assigns no cultivation status, and the material has only a therapeutic one.

**Practitioner's note.** A caustic furnace by-product of the ancient metal shop, used in the old pharmacy for its bite. Nothing here supports ingestion or any cultivation use. The Codex takes from the chapter the habit of testing for adulteration, and nothing else.

### Contraindications

- **TOXIC AND CAUSTIC.** Do not swallow it, and do not put it in the eyes, nose, ears or throat.
- **Metal fume.** Brass fume and dust at a furnace cause metal-fume fever and injure the lungs.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** No statement in the sources retrieved; given the toxicity, avoid. [USER: verify]
- **Identity.** The reading as a copper oxide granule is ours and is of low confidence. [USER: verify]
