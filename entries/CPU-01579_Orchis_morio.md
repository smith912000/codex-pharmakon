# Orchis morio — Orchis eteros, testiculus alter, serapias (Dioscorides 3-142)

## *Orchis morio [Linnaeus], the last of the names on the first group of the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-142 (which also prints Triochis-serapias mas [Fuchs] and Triorchis mas minor [Brunfels] — "Green Winged Orchid"; a second group with Triorchis foemina [Fuchs], Orchis fucum [Bauhin], Ophrys insectifera [Linnaeus] and Ophrys apifera [in Sprague] — "Bee Orchid"; and the other usage Serapias lingua, Serapias cordigera — "Serapias")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED block (lines 7771 to 7773) offers three groups: the "Green Winged Orchid" (two older names and *Orchis morio*), the "Bee Orchid" (four names, two of them in the genus *Ophrys*), and the other usage *Serapias*. These are different plants, and the Codex does not choose between them; the spine row takes the *Orchis morio* name from the first group. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-142. ORCHIS ETEROS* ("the other orchis"), heading at line 7770, text at line 7774, in Book Three among the roots of the *akanthoda*. The whole chapter reads: "Testiculus alter (also called serapias — as Acreas does for the many uses of the root) has leaves similar to leek, somewhat long but broader and fat, winding around in the wings; little stalks twenty centimetres long, the flowers almost purple. The root (similar to testicles) lies under, and applied is able to dissipate oedema, clean ulcers, and repress herpes [viral infection]. Smeared on it destroys fistulas, and soothes inflamed parts. Sprinkled on dry it stops nomae [grazer disease, eats away muscle, tissue and bones], and a decoction (taken as a drink with wine) cures the intestines. There is a similar story told of this as there is of the dog's stone [cyanosorchis 3-141]."

The chapter gives a description for recognition (leaves like leek, long, broader and fat, "winding around in the wings"; little stalks of twenty centimetres; almost purple flowers; a root "similar to testicles" that "lies under") and a list of uses, nearly all external: the root applied dissipates oedema, cleans ulcers and represses herpes; smeared on it "destroys fistulas" and soothes inflamed parts; sprinkled on dry it stops *nomae*. The one internal use is the decoction drunk with wine, which "cures the intestines". The bracketed glosses ("viral infection"; "grazer disease, eats away muscle, tissue and bones") are the translators' and are quoted as printed. The chapter names an earlier writer, "Acreas", for the many uses of the root; no other text of his was read. It closes with the sentence that links it to 3-141: "a similar story told of this as there is of the dog's stone". The text does not say what the story is, and the Codex does not fill it in from 3-141, which tells a story of offspring.

**The chapters around it.** 3-141 *ORCHIS* (line 7761), the translators' military orchid, is a root "eaten (boiled) like bulbus" and carries the sex-of-offspring report. 3-143 *SATURION* (line 7775) and 3-144 *SATURION ERUTHRONION* (line 7784) are separate chapters, both with an aphrodisiac report. The Codex carries no statement of one chapter over to another.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter id 3-142 and the Greek names *orchis eteros*, *testiculus alter* and *serapias* found no existing entry on this chapter. I_1140 (*Orchis bifolia*, chapter 3-143) quotes part of 3-142 as a neighbour, in a paragraph on the neighbouring chapters, and does not treat it as a subject. No entry is duplicated.

### Active Components

The composition of the root Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, the terrestrial orchids (Orchidaceae) of Europe bear underground tubers, and orchid tubers are known to be rich in a mucilaginous starch-like carbohydrate. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a root applied to swellings, ulcers, fistulas and inflamed parts, sprinkled on a spreading ulcer, and drunk in wine for the intestines. The Codex gives no protocol for any of these. A fistula, a spreading ulcer and herpes are conditions for medical care, and the identification is the translators', among three different plants. Many wild orchids are protected, and their tubers are not to be dug on the strength of this chapter.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives four preparations: the root "applied"; the root "smeared on"; the root "sprinkled on dry" (the text does not say how it is dried or prepared); and "a decoction (taken as a drink with wine)". It gives no quantity for any of them. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that a plant root laid on an ulcer or fistula is not a treatment for it. The identification is a translators' suggestion among three groups of plants, so the chapter does not show that the plant it describes is *Orchis morio*.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no taste, and no warming, cooling, drying, moistening or astringent quality for 3-142.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant (leek-like leaves, broader and fat, winding in the wings; twenty-centimetre stalks; nearly purple flowers; a testicle-like root lying beneath) and how to tell it from its sister chapter by name, since it is the "other" orchis. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a root used outwardly and, in a decoction with wine, inwardly. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a wound and swelling root with no stated quantity. The useful lesson is Dioscorides' method of naming a twin plant by contrast with the first, and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Fistulas, spreading ulcers and herpes need medical assessment, and wine with a root of unknown dose is not harmless. Many wild orchids are protected by law in many places and their tubers must not be dug; the particular status was not researched. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
