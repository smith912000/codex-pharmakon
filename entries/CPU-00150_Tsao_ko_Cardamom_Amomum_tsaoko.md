### Origin & Tradition

Tsao-ko (草果, Cǎo Guǒ — literally "grass fruit") is the large, dark-brown, smoke-dried fruit of *Amomum tsaoko*, a robust ginger-family perennial of the wet subtropical valleys of Yunnan, Guangxi and northern Vietnam. It must not be confused with green cardamom (*Elettaria cardamomum*, I_053/I_054) nor with Cambodian cardamom (*Amomum kravanh*, I_048): although all three are aromatic Zingiberaceae fruits used to transform dampness, tsao-ko is by far the largest, the most pungent-bitter, and the most warming, and its fruits are characteristically cured over wood smoke, giving the dried pod a distinctive camphoraceous, faintly smoky aroma quite unlike the bright sweetness of green cardamom.

In the Chinese materia medica tsao-ko belongs to the category of **aromatic herbs that transform dampness** (化湿药, huà shī yào). Its classical role is to dispel **cold-dampness** lodged in the Middle Burner — the digestive centre — when that cold-damp obstruction produces fullness and distension of the epigastrium, nausea, a thick greasy tongue coat, and loss of appetite. Because cold-damp stagnation in the centre is also a recognised setting for the classical syndrome of **malarial disorders (瘧疾, nüè jí)** — intermittent fevers and chills with a damp, turbid quality — tsao-ko earned a long-standing reputation as an antimalarial-adjunct herb, almost always combined rather than used alone. It appears in this role in formulae such as **Dá Yuán Yǐn (達原飲, "Reach the Membrane Source Decoction")**, a Ming-dynasty febrile-disease formula in which tsao-ko, *Areca* seed (Bing Lang) and *Magnolia* bark (Hou Po) together break through turbid damp-cold obstruction said to lodge in the "membrane source" between exterior and interior.

Beyond medicine, tsao-ko is one of the defining culinary spices of southwestern China and the borderland kitchens of the Tibetan plateau's eastern margin — a near-constant component of red-braised (hong shao) meat dishes, spiced broths and the long-simmered marinades (lu shui) of Yunnan and Sichuan, where its warming, dampness-cutting character is valued precisely in cold, damp mountain climates. This continuity between kitchen and pharmacy is characteristic of the warming aromatics: the same property that cuts the greasiness of braised meat is the property that, in the clinic, cuts damp-cold stagnation of the digestion.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Within the Kenetica framework tsao-ko is a **cold-damp digestive corrective** — narrower and more forceful than the broad Spleen-Qi tonics. Where Bái Zhú (I_022) builds and stabilises the digestive centre over time, tsao-ko is an acute, aromatic intervention: it is reached for when the athlete or practitioner presents with the specific picture of **cold-damp stagnation in the centre** — heavy epigastric fullness after eating, nausea, a thick white greasy tongue coat, sluggish appetite, and a sense of cold, waterlogged heaviness rather than heat.

This picture is common in two Kenetica contexts. First, during **[BUILD]** phases of very high caloric intake in cold or damp conditions, where the digestive apparatus is overwhelmed and food sits undigested ("damp accumulation"): tsao-ko's prokinetic, carminative essential oil helps move the obstruction, restore appetite and reduce post-prandial heaviness, improving the actual extraction of the nutrients being consumed. Second, during **[RESET]** phases after illness or in damp climates, where a turbid, cold digestive sluggishness lingers: a short aromatic course clears the coat and reopens appetite.

Tsao-ko is **not a tonic** and not for long-term daily use — it is a corrective. Its drying, aromatic action is appropriate for short courses against an identifiable cold-damp pattern, then withdrawn. It is almost always combined: with *Magnolia* bark and *Citrus* peel for damp-fullness, or within warming digestive formulae rather than taken alone. Its classical antimalarial-adjunct role has no direct training analogue and is recorded here as tradition, not as a self-treatment claim — malaria is a medical emergency treated with proven antimalarial drugs.

### Preparation & Dosing

- **Standard decoction:** the dried fruit is lightly crushed or the seed-cluster broken to release the aroma; conventional materia medica decoction range is on the order of **3–6 g** per day, decocted with other herbs. [USER: verify the exact range against an authoritative CM materia medica before relying on a number.]
- **Roasted/dry-fried (chao):** dry-frying is sometimes used to temper the harshness and strengthen the Spleen-warming action.
- **Culinary:** 1–2 whole pods added to braises, broths and lu-shui marinades, lightly toasted first; removed before serving. This is the safest everyday route and the one most users will actually use.
- **Pairing:** classically combined with *Magnolia officinalis* bark (Hou Po) and *Citrus* peel (Chen Pi) for damp-fullness; with *Areca* seed (Bing Lang) and other herbs in Dá Yuán Yǐn for turbid damp-cold febrile patterns.
- **Timing:** with or just before meals for the digestive indication.

No isolated high-dose extract is recommended for self-use; tsao-ko's place is as a culinary spice and a short, combined clinical course.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**TCM Organ-Channel Assignment:**
- **Tsao-ko (Cao Guo):** Acrid (pungent), warm; enters the Spleen (脾) and Stomach (胃) channels.
  - Primary action: **dries dampness and dispels cold** in the Middle Burner; warms and unblocks the digestive centre.
  - Secondary action: **checks malarial disorders** (nüè jí) — used in formulae for intermittent fever-and-chill patterns with a turbid, damp-cold quality.

**Five-Phase / Element Context:** an Earth-phase (土) agent acting through the warming, drying, aromatic mode rather than the tonifying mode — it removes the cold-damp obstruction that prevents Earth (the Spleen) from transforming food, rather than supplementing Earth directly. Functionally it clears the ground that a tonic like Bái Zhú then builds upon.

**Tibetan/borderland (FMT) parallel:** as a warming, digestion-kindling spice of the cold, damp eastern-plateau kitchens, tsao-ko occupies the same niche as the warming, *agni*-like digestive aromatics — taken to counter the cold, heavy, *Bad-kan*/Kapha-like dampness of high, wet climates. **Ayurvedic parallel:** functionally analogous to the warming *deepana–pachana* (appetiser–digestive) spices used against *Mandagni* (weak digestive fire) and *Ama* (undigested damp residue) — the same cold-damp-stagnation symptom cluster of heaviness, nausea and coated tongue.

### Contraindications

- **Yin deficiency / heat patterns:** tsao-ko's warm, drying, aromatic nature is contraindicated where there is dryness, thirst, red tongue without coat, night sweats or interior heat — it will aggravate Yin depletion.
- **Damp-heat (as opposed to damp-cold):** the herb is for **cold**-damp; in damp-heat patterns (yellow greasy coat, heat signs) it is inappropriate without modifying herbs.
- **Pregnancy:** strongly aromatic, Qi-moving warming herbs are used cautiously in pregnancy; avoid medicinal doses without practitioner supervision. Culinary amounts in food are conventional.
- **No substitute for antimalarial treatment:** the traditional antimalarial-adjunct role does **not** make tsao-ko a treatment for malaria, which is a medical emergency requiring proven antimalarial drugs.
- **Overuse:** as a drying corrective rather than a tonic, prolonged high-dose use can deplete fluids; use short courses against an identified cold-damp pattern, then stop.
- **Allergy:** Zingiberaceae-family sensitivity is rare but possible.
