# Cardamine praetensis — Iberis, "cardamantica" (Dioscorides 1-188)

## *Cardamine praetensis* (the spelling printed by the Dioscorides translators, as the last of four names on the first SUGGESTED line for 1-188, attributed to Linnaeus; glossed with *Cardamine amara*, "Bitter Cress", and the candytuft *Iberis numidica* and *I. amara*) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry.** This plant is named only by Dioscorides (DMM). It was added 2026-10-09 from the extraction list (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) at the locus 1-188, line 4895.

**Identity note.** The binomial is the translators' (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) suggestion and not Dioscorides' identification. The SUGGESTED lines (4896 to 4898) read: "Nasturtium agreste [Fuchs], Hiberis [Brunfels], Nasturtium pratense [Bauhin], Cardamine praetensis [Linnaeus]", then "Cardamine amara — Bitter Cress, Large Bitter Cress [other usage] Candytuft — Iberis numidica", then "Clown Mustard, Bitter Candytuft — Iberis amara". The spelling *praetensis* is as printed; the Linnaean species name is ordinarily spelled *pratensis*, and the spine row carries the printed spelling. [USER: verify] The list names a bitter-cress and two candytufts, all of them crucifers. The chapter itself names no feature that separates them. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is the last of Book One, following 1-187 PERSEA, and the preface to Book Two comes directly after it.

*1-188 IBERIS* (heading at line 4895; text at line 4899). The whole chapter reads: "Iberis or cardamantica has leaves similar to nasturtium [2-185], more flourishing in the spring, the length of it as much as a foot or somewhat smaller. It grows in untilled places. In the summer it puts out a milky flower and then it is more effective. It has two roots similar to nasturtium — warming and caustic. They are good for those diseased with sciatica if a poultice similar to a plaster is made of it with salted lard of a sow (and so applied and let lie) for four hours. Afterward let the patient be put into a bath and rubbed on the places affected with oil and wine dipped in wool."

The chapter gives: two names (*iberis*, *cardamantica*); leaves like *nasturtium* (2-185), best in spring, a foot long or a little less; a habitat (untilled places); a milky flower in summer, when the plant is "more effective"; two roots, "warming and caustic"; and one use, a poultice of the roots made "similar to a plaster" with salted sow's lard, left on for four hours, for sciatica, after which the patient is bathed and rubbed with oil and wine on wool. It gives no internal use.

The comparison chapter 2-185 (*nasturtium*) was not opened for this entry beyond the citation; it is printed in the text as a likeness only.

### Active Components

Bitter-cresses and candytufts are crucifers (Brassicaceae), a family generally known for glucosinolates, which release pungent, irritant isothiocyanates when the tissue is crushed. That fits a root that the chapter calls "caustic", but no analysis of the plant Dioscorides meant was read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records an external poultice of the roots with lard for sciatica. A material called "caustic" is a skin irritant, and the Codex gives no protocol for it. The identification with a particular bitter-cress or candytuft is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

One preparation is printed (1-188): the roots made into a poultice "similar to a plaster" with the "salted lard of a sow", applied and left "for four hours", then the patient is "put into a bath and rubbed on the places affected with oil and wine dipped in wool". The four hours is a time, not a dose. No quantity of root or lard is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Pungent crucifer roots are generally known as skin irritants (rubefacients) when applied crushed, which is consistent with the chapter's word "caustic". No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the roots are "warming and caustic" (line 4899). No other quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. The chapter offers the discipline of attention: the likeness to *nasturtium*, the season ("more flourishing in the spring"), the habitat (untilled places), and a timing for use ("In the summer ... then it is more effective"), then a procedure with a set time and an after-care bath. It records one external remedy and no status as a long-term substance. The identification is the translators' and the list is open. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice. It is a counter-irritant poultice for a physician's use, from a plant that cannot be placed between several crucifers. The abstentive answer is the correct one.

### Contraindications

The chapter calls the roots "caustic". Any caustic poultice left on skin for hours can blister or burn it, and none of the above is advice. The identification is uncertain, so no plant should be gathered or applied on the basis of this chapter.
