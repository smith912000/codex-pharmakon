# Bulbus — بلبوس (Avicenna, *Canon* Book II)

## *A small edible bulb with narcissus-like bulbs, leek-like leaves and violet-like flowers; the Arabic name is a transliteration of the Greek *bolbos*. The identification is ours, from the Arabic description and a comparison with Dioscorides' *bolbos edodimos* (2-200), and is of low confidence at species level; the tassel hyacinth (*Leopoldia comosa*) that the spine row proposes is a modern guess not stated in either text* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** CM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Avicenna's *Canon* among the texts on disk, under this Arabic name.** This is a single-text entry. The Arabic text is `Sources/CM_Avicenna/text/IbnSina_Qanun_OpenITI_Shamela0010706.txt`; the heading is بلبوس (*bulbūs*), Arabic text line 10993. A search of the other texts on disk for the Arabic name found nothing for this drug. The row is a heading found in the Canon's Book II alphabetical materia medica and replaces an unverified placeholder.

**Avicenna, *Canon* of Medicine (CM).** (Canon Book II, entry *Bulbūs*, Arabic text line 10993–11010). *What it is:* "small edible onions resembling the narcissus bulb, its leaves like leek leaves and its flowers like the violet; among them is a kind that provokes vomiting". Avicenna adds that some said it is *al-zayz*, others that it is of the mallow-like *ṭalkhabyāz* group, and that it seems to be *anāʿīs*, and that these meanings should be brought here [USER: verify these three readings]. *Temperament:* close to the onion, "perhaps dry in the first degree with a superfluous moisture". *Actions:* it causes wind, disperses, and roughens the tongue. *Cosmetic:* smeared on freckles, especially in the sun; on scars of ulcers; with egg yolk on warts; with *sikanjabīn* on milk-crust sores. *Wounds:* it is said that roasted with the heads of *ṣīr* fish and sprinkled on chin sores it removes them. *Joints:* a plaster with vinegar for gout and joint pains, alone for sprained sinew, for a crushed nail or ear, and with *sawīq*. *Head:* a remedy for scurf and scalp sores, and applied to unbroken scalp wounds. *Eye:* alone or with egg yolk for a blow to the eye, and with vinegar for the lacrimal angle. *Digestive:* the sweet red kind is good for the stomach, with honey as a plaster for stomach pains; the bitter is better, helps digest food and increases nourishment, though it is not a praiseworthy food, especially raw, and when not digested it causes colic and wind. *Excretion:* it arouses sexual desire (يهيج الباه). The wording is close to Dioscorides' chapter *bolbos edodimos* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 6301, 2-200), which also has the red edible bulb 'good for the stomach', harsh to tongue and tonsils, aphrodisiac, windy, and applied with egg yolk or honey to freckles; the Canon entry does not name Dioscorides, so the link is ours. The translator's suggested identification at 2-200 is *Gagea lutea* (Yellow Star of Bethlehem) and for the vomiting kind *Scilla amoena*; neither is a settled species. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms bulbs of the lily family store starch and mucilage, and some contain irritant or toxic compounds; the vomiting kind the text mentions would be such. No analytical source was retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Freckles, scars, warts, scalp scurf, joint and sinew pains (as a plaster), eye complaints, stomach pains, digestion, and sexual desire.
- **What the text does not say.** The species, the dose, and which kind is the vomiting kind in practice.

The Codex does not translate the Canon's terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend this material.

### Preparation & Dosing

Preparations in the text are all poultices or salves: with vinegar, honey, egg yolk, *sikanjabīn* or *sawīq*; roasted with fish heads for chin sores; eaten raw or cooked. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only: a number of bulbs of the lily family are irritant and some are emetic, which agrees in kind with the text's vomiting kind. The species identification is open, so no single plant can be assumed safe. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **CM.** Close to the onion, "perhaps dry in the first degree with a superfluous moisture" (lines 10996-10997). The entry also calls it windy and dispersing.

### Cultivation Register

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The *Canon* entry is organised by the headings of the materia medica (what it is, choice, temperament, actions, then organ by organ), and in the lines read it assigns this material no place in the preservation of innate heat and radical moisture and no status as a life-lengthening drug; nothing is imported from outside the lines read.

**Practitioner's note.** An edible bulb used mostly externally, in the tradition of Dioscorides' *bolbos*. The text itself says it is not a praiseworthy food, and no cultivation status is attested.

### Contraindications

- **Emetic kind and unidentified species.** The text says one kind provokes vomiting. Do not eat any wild bulb on the basis of this entry.
- **Indigestion.** The text says that when it is not digested it causes colic and wind.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** No statement in the lines read. [USER: verify]
- **Medical conditions named.** These are medical conditions; use proper care.
