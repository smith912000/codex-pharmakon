# I-380 | Fang Feng / Siler (*Saposhnikovia divaricata*)

**Volume I — Plant-Based | Family: Apiaceae**
**Convergence Score: 3/9 | Energetic Tier: ◉ Qi**

---

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Binomial:** *Saposhnikovia divaricata* (Turcz.) Schischk., 1951
**Former synonyms:** *Ledebouriella divaricata* (Turcz.) Hiroe; *Ledebouriella seseloides* (Hoffm.) Wolff; *Siler divaricatum* Benth. & Hook.f. (most commonly used pharmaceutical synonym; "Siler" persists in English pharmaceutical literature)
**Family:** Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)
**Subfamily:** Apioideae

**Monotypic genus note:** *Saposhnikovia* is essentially a monotypic genus — *S. divaricata* is the only recognized species, making species and genus pharmacologically identical. Named for the Russian botanist Alexander Saposhnikov (1831–1893).

**Common names:**
- Chinese: 防风 (*Fáng Fēng*) — literally "prevent wind"; one of the most semantically direct names in the Chinese materia medica: 防 (*fáng*) = "to guard, to prevent, defense"; 风 (*fēng*) = "wind" (in both meteorological and pathological TCM sense). The name encodes its primary pharmacological function: protection against Wind pathogen (防 = guard, 风 = Wind).
- English: Siler (pharmaceutical); Chinese Siler Root; Ledebouriella (older pharmaceutical)
- Japanese: 防風 (*Bōfū*) — Japanese reading; important Kampo herb
- Korean: 방풍 (*Bangpung*)
- Vietnamese: Phòng phong
- Pharmaceutical drug name: *Saposhnikoviae Radix* (current Pharmacopoeia of PRC); also *Ledebouriellae Radix* (older pharmacopoeial editions)

**Botanical description:** Perennial herb; root fleshy, carrot-like, brown-grey external, white internal, strongly aromatic; stem erect 30–80cm, branched; leaves bipinnately or tripinnately compound with linear-lanceolate segments — distinctively narrow segments differentiating it from most Apiaceae; flowers white, umbels (June–August); fruits ovate, winged. Dry grassland, steppe, and hillside habitat; native to Northeast China (Manchuria), Inner Mongolia, Korea, Eastern Russia — a steppe/grassland plant. Distributed throughout Chinese provinces north of the Yellow River.

**Parts used:** Dried root (pharmaceutical); older roots preferred (5+ years in traditional quality criteria); head section (防风头, *Fáng Fēng Tóu*) considered highest grade; fibrous tail sections (防风须) also used.

**Adulteration concern:** Multiple plants are sold as Fang Feng in Chinese markets: *Peucedanum japonicum* (滨海前胡, Coastal Hogfennel), *Ligusticum brachylobum* (短叶藁本), and other Apiaceae with similar root morphology. Modern HPLC verification (prim-O-glucosylcimifugin as marker compound) is necessary for genuine *S. divaricata* confirmation.

## 3. Convergence Analysis

Fang Feng's 3/9 convergence score again reflects its geographic restriction to East Asian pharmacology rather than its pharmacological importance within that tradition. Within the East Asian medicinal system, Fang Feng is among the most frequently prescribed herbs — appearing in hundreds of classical formulas as a fundamental wind-dispelling agent. It represents a distinct pharmacological category in TCM thought: the "Wind-expelling, surface-releasing" herb that neither primarily warms (like ephedra/Ma Huang), nor primarily cools (like peppermint/Bo He), but functions as a balanced defender of the exterior against Wind in any thermal guise.

The TCM category of Wind (风 *fēng*) as pathogen is a complex construct that maps onto several modern concepts simultaneously: viral upper respiratory infections (wind-cold/wind-heat = common cold/influenza), allergic reactions (wind urticaria = allergic urticaria), neurological conditions with tremor/tic components (internal wind), rheumatic/joint pain from weather exposure (wind-damp-bi syndrome). Fang Feng's utility across these disparate modern categories reflects the breadth of the Wind pathogen concept — and correspondingly broad pharmacological activity (antiviral, anti-allergic, analgesic, possible anticonvulsant) in the one herb.

The chromone compounds (prim-O-glucosylcimifugin, cimifugin) are specific to *S. divaricata* and have no direct parallel in European Apiaceae — representing genuinely unique phytochemistry that the East Asian tradition identified and employed. This makes Fang Feng not merely a geographic variant of Western Apiaceae pharmacology but a distinct pharmacological entity.

## 4. Phytochemical Composition

**Chromones (most distinctive compound class — specific to this genus):**
- **Prim-O-glucosylcimifugin** (升麻苷; the primary chromone glycoside): the standard marker compound for quality control of *S. divaricata*; anti-inflammatory (NF-κB inhibition); analgesic; anti-allergic; 0.1–0.5% DW in commercial roots
- **4'-O-glucosyl-5-O-methylvisamminol**: chromone glucoside; anti-inflammatory; co-marker compound
- **Cimifugin** (升麻素): aglycone of glucosylcimifugin; more bioavailable than glycoside form; anti-inflammatory; smooth muscle relaxant; analgesic
- **Sec-O-glucosylhamaudol**: additional chromone; anti-inflammatory
- **Deltoin, hamaudol**: minor chromone compounds
- Total chromone content: 0.3–1.5% DW; chromones essentially specific to *Saposhnikovia*/*Ledebouriella* complex — distinguishing this herb pharmacochemically from all other Apiaceae in the Codex

**Coumarins:**
- **Psoralen, bergapten** (trace furanocoumarins): phototoxicity concern minimal at typical dosing
- **Scopoletin, umbelliferone** (simple coumarins): anti-inflammatory, antispasmodic
- **Imperatorin** (also in Bai Zhi): shared furanocoumarin; analgesic, spasmolytic

**Polyacetylenes:**
- Falcarinol, falcarindiol (shared with carrot, celery): TRPV1 agonism; anti-tumor preclinical

**Volatile oil:**
- α-Pinene, β-bisabolene, bornyl acetate: monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes contributing to aromatic character and minor anti-inflammatory activity; total 0.1–0.4% DW

**Polysaccharides:**
- Saposhnikovia polysaccharides: immunomodulatory; NF-κB pathway modulation; relevant to the "exterior-strengthening" concept (immune defense enhancement at mucosal surfaces)

**Phenolic acids:**
- Ferulic acid (minor), caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid (lower than in Dong Quai or Chuanxiong)

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**Anti-inflammatory mechanism (primary — chromones):**
Prim-O-glucosylcimifugin + cimifugin → NF-κB transcription factor inhibition → reduced TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, PGE₂ production → anti-inflammatory effect relevant to upper respiratory inflammation, allergic reactions, and rheumatic joint pain. The chromone mechanism is distinct from the furanocoumarin anti-inflammatory mechanism in Bai Zhi (though both ultimately target NF-κB) — different chemical scaffolds reaching the same target via different molecular interactions.

**Anti-allergic mechanism:**
Chromone compounds → inhibition of mast cell degranulation → reduced histamine release → anti-allergic effect relevant to urticaria (hives), allergic rhinitis, and wind-pattern skin reactions. This mechanism parallels cromoglicate (sodium cromoglycate, Intal®), the pharmaceutical anti-allergic drug that is itself a synthetic chromone analog — making Fang Feng's chromone pharmacology a natural chromone anti-allergic analogous to pharmaceutical cromoglicate.

**Analgesic mechanism:**
Cimifugin + imperatorin → Ca²⁺ channel blockade in nociceptors + NF-κB anti-inflammatory → combined central and peripheral pain reduction. Animal models: formalin test (Fang Feng extract reduces writhing at both early/neurogenic and late/inflammatory phases); hot plate test (central component); acetic acid writhing (inflammatory pain model).

**Antispasmodic/anticonvulsant mechanism:**
Chromone smooth muscle relaxant activity + GABA-A modulatory effects (proposed for chromone class; less studied in *S. divaricata* specifically); traditional use for tremors, tics, and convulsions aligns with possible anticonvulsant mechanism. Not confirmed by modern pharmacological study to the same degree as anti-inflammatory action.

**Immunomodulatory mechanism:**
Saposhnikovia polysaccharides → macrophage activation + cytokine modulation → innate immune enhancement at mucosal barriers. TCM concept "strengthening the exterior" (*固表*, *gù biǎo*) maps onto this: polysaccharide-enhanced mucosal immunity = reinforced physical/immune barrier against external pathogen penetration.

**Antiviral mechanism:**
Preliminary evidence (in vitro): extracts show activity against influenza A, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV); mechanism partially via direct viral inhibition, partially via immune enhancement. Consistent with traditional use in early-stage viral respiratory infection (wind-cold/wind-heat exterior patterns).

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**Wind-damp-Bi syndrome (rheumatoid/osteoarthritis pain):**
- **Level B** — Multiple Chinese hospital RCTs for rheumatoid arthritis using Fang Feng in formula (Juan Bi Tang: Fang Feng + Qiang Huo + Dang Gui + Chuan Xiong + other herbs); significant pain reduction vs. placebo; Fang Feng's isolated contribution uncertain in formula context

**Urticaria (wind-type allergic skin reaction):**
- **Level B** — Chinese RCTs for chronic urticaria: Fang Feng-based formulas (Xiao Feng San, Fang Feng Tong Sheng Wan) significantly reduce urticaria frequency and severity vs. antihistamines alone, with comparable or superior outcomes in some trials. Anti-allergic chromone mechanism provides pharmacological rationale.

**Upper respiratory infections (wind-cold pattern):**
- **Level B/C** — Traditional use validation studies for early-stage viral URTI; combination formulas with Fang Feng show reduction in duration and severity; difficult to isolate Fang Feng's contribution; pharmacological rationale (antiviral preclinical data, immunomodulatory polysaccharides) is reasonable

**Yu Ping Feng San (Jade Windscreen Powder) — Level B:**
The most famous formula containing Fang Feng: 玉屏风散 (*Yu Ping Feng San*) — Huang Qi (*Astragalus membranaceus*) + Bai Zhu (*Atractylodes macrocephala*) + Fang Feng. A three-herb formula specifically for prevention of recurrent upper respiratory infections in people with Wei Qi (defensive Qi) deficiency. Multiple Chinese clinical trials for recurrent URTIs, childhood respiratory allergy, and prophylaxis in immunocompromised patients show benefit. This formula has generated more clinical evidence than Fang Feng alone — the formula effect rather than single-herb effect is the relevant clinical unit.

**Overall:** Significant clinical evidence in formula context (Level B); limited single-herb RCT data; Yu Ping Feng San represents one of the most studied classical Chinese formulas with clinically relevant applications in modern immunology practice.

## 7. Phenomenological Record

**The name as pharmacological instruction:**
防风 (*Fáng Fēng*) — "Prevent Wind" — is unusual among plant names in that the name itself constitutes a clinical instruction. Most plant names describe morphology, habitat, or taste; Fang Feng describes a therapeutic action. This naming convention reflects its status in TCM as the prototypical wind-expelling herb — so central to this therapeutic category that its name became synonymous with the action.

This naming tradition has clinical significance: when a TCM practitioner reads a classical formula and encounters 防风, they immediately understand it is addressing Wind pathogen. The name functions as a pharmacological descriptor embedded in classical formula texts, allowing rapid interpretation of formula intent without separate annotation.

**Shennong Bencao Jing — upper-grade classification and Wind taxonomy:**
The original *Bencao* text for 防风: "防风，味甘，温。主大风头眩痛，恶风，风邪，目盲无所见，风行周身，骨节疼痹，烦满。久服轻身。" — "Fang Feng, sweet, warm; governs great wind with head dizziness and pain, aversion to wind, wind pathogen, blindness with inability to see, wind traveling throughout the body, painful obstruction of joints and bones, oppressive fullness. Long-term use lightens the body."

The "great wind" (*大风 dà fēng*) mentioned includes what later TCM texts identify as leprosy — reflecting the pre-germ-theory understanding of severe infectious/wind conditions as wind pathogen penetration. The anti-inflammatory and possibly antibacterial mechanisms of chromone-bearing plants may have provided some symptomatic benefit that reinforced traditional attribution.

**Yu Ping Feng San — Jade Windscreen:**
The metaphor in the formula name 玉屏风散 is pharmacologically precise: a jade screen (玉屏风 — a decorative lacquered screen panel used in Chinese architecture to block wind and create privacy) is both beautiful and protective. The formula creates an energetic windscreen — strengthening the external defensive barrier so that Wind pathogen cannot penetrate. Huang Qi (Astragalus) provides the structural substance of the screen; Bai Zhu (Atractylodes) provides the anchoring foundation; Fang Feng disperses any Wind that manages to approach the exterior. The formula became so central to preventive medicine in classical TCM that it is still standard prescription in contemporary Chinese hospital immunology departments for recurrent respiratory infections in children.

**Modern clinical adoption in Japan:**
Japanese Kampo medicine, working within a regulated pharmaceutical framework, employs 防風 (*Bōfū*) in multiple standard Kampo formulas covered by Japanese health insurance. Particularly notable: 防風通聖散 (*Bōfū Tsūshō San*, Fangfeng Tongsheng San) — a classical 18-herb formula including Fang Feng, used in modern Japanese medicine for metabolic syndrome, obesity, and inflammatory skin conditions. This formula is available as an insurance-covered pharmaceutical in Japan, representing one of the most commercially successful classical TCM formulas in modern Western-adjacent medicine.

**Wind concept as unified pathophysiological framework:**
The breadth of conditions addressed by Fang Feng (URTI, joint pain, urticaria, tremors, convulsions) seems medically incoherent to Western trained eyes. The TCM Wind pathogen framework explains the pattern: Wind is characterized by rapid movement, unpredictability, and preference for the "upper body" and "exterior" — exactly the characteristics of: (1) viral infections (rapid spread, unpredictable symptoms, respiratory = upper body); (2) allergic urticaria (rapidly moving itching "running" across skin — 风疹块 "wind-blister lumps"); (3) neurological tremors (wind = movement, tremor = internal wind); (4) migratory joint pain (moves location = wind-damp-bi). The unifying concept is clinically operational even if the mechanistic explanation differs from modern pathology.

## 8. Practical Application

**Clinical prescribing principles (TCM):**
- Standard dose: 3–10g dried root in decoction daily; used almost exclusively in formula (rarely as single herb)
- Key formulas:
  - **玉屏风散 (Yu Ping Feng San)**: recurrent URTI prevention; allergic rhinitis; Wei Qi deficiency — 12g Huang Qi + 6g Bai Zhu + 6g Fang Feng (traditional ratio 2:1:1 or 3:1:1)
  - **防风通圣散 (Fang Feng Tong Sheng San)**: wind-heat with interior heat accumulation; obesity; inflammatory skin conditions; large 18-herb formula
  - **消风散 (Xiao Feng San)**: wind-type urticaria, eczema, allergic dermatitis — Fang Feng + Jing Jie + Chan Tui + various supporting herbs
  - **独活寄生丸 (Du Huo Ji Sheng Wan)**: wind-cold-damp Bi syndrome (joint pain, stiffness)
- Pattern requirement: Wind pathogen (exterior — wind-cold or wind-heat) OR interior Wind (tremors, tics, muscle spasm); Wei Qi deficiency (preventive use); Wind-damp-Bi (rheumatic joint pain)

**Contraindications:**
- **Blood deficiency or Yin deficiency generating internal Wind:** Fang Feng is warming/dispersing and does not nourish blood or Yin; using it alone for internal wind from blood/Yin deficiency would worsen the underlying deficiency. Pair with blood-nourishing herbs if Wind from deficiency is a component.
- **Excessive sweating/spontaneous sweating:** Wind-dispersing herbs promote perspiration; Fang Feng can worsen excessive sweating in Wei Qi deficiency if not balanced by Huang Qi/Bai Zhu (as in Yu Ping Feng San, where the ratio of Huang Qi stabilizes the exterior while Fang Feng keeps Wind at bay)

**Drug interactions:**
- Relatively few documented interactions; chromones do not significantly inhibit major CYP enzymes at therapeutic doses (unlike imperatorin in Bai Zhi)
- Mild anti-inflammatory effect: additive with NSAIDs; unlikely to be clinically significant at herbal doses
- Monitor in patients on immunosuppressants (polysaccharide immunomodulatory activity could theoretically interact)

**Adulteration and quality:**
- Request HPLC verification for prim-O-glucosylcimifugin ≥0.24% (Pharmacopoeia of PRC 2020 standard) when sourcing commercially
- Visual: genuine roots have distinctive narrow pinnate leaves when whole; dried root with circular cross-section showing yellow-white interior rings (chrysanthemum pattern — 菊花心 *júhuā xīn*) is a traditional quality marker
- Geographic origin: Inner Mongolia and Hebei Province historically highest quality; "Guan Fang Feng" (关防风, Northeast/Manchurian Fang Feng) considered superior to "Chuan Fang Feng" (Sichuan-sourced substitutes)

**Preparation:**
- Standard decoction: 3–10g in 300ml water, simmer 20–25 min; mild aromatic character; drink 2× daily
- Granule extract: convenient; standardized
- Patent formula preparations: Yu Ping Feng San and Fang Feng Tong Sheng San available as standardized granules, pills, and capsules — most patients use these rather than decoct raw herb

**Energetic classification:**
- Tier: **◉ Qi** — functions at the Wei Qi (defensive Qi) level; unlike herbs that nourish deeper structural Qi or Blood, Fang Feng addresses the exterior Qi boundary; dispersing rather than tonifying; moves Qi (and by extension disperses Wind-pathogen) at the surface layer
- Thermal nature: slightly warm (微温); flavor: sweet-pungent (甘辛); organ meridians: Urinary Bladder (主表), Liver (内风), Spleen
- Classical characterization: 祛风解表，胜湿止痛，止痉 — expels Wind and releases the exterior; overcomes dampness and stops pain; stops convulsions
