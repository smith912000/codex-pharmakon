# Blue actinolite — mthing.rgyus (Clark 1995, "indigo tendon"); Thinggyue (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)

## *The blue type of the stone rdo.rgyus ("stone tendon"), which Clark glosses "Actinolite" (with "Stibine" as a second gloss in the same heading) and, in the entry for *rdo.chhu*, calls "Actinolite asbestos"; the Tibetan name means "indigo tendon". It is said to heal disorders of the ligaments and tendons. Identification: a blue fibrous stone of the actinolite group, by the translator's English glosses only, with the asbestiform form named in Clark; the mineralogy is not established by the texts; confidence low* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **CAUTION.** Clark glosses the blue stone as "Actinolite asbestos". Asbestiform actinolite is a fibrous mineral whose dust, once breathed in, causes serious lung disease, including cancer. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should handle, grind or take this stone.

**Single-text entry** in the sense that only the Four Tantras tradition, in two English renderings, names the substance. The two texts read are Barry Clark's 1995 *The Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20) and the Men-Tsee-Khang edition of 2011 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`). The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder, "blue actinolite (thinggyue)", from `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 151 ("Thinggyue", the Men-Tsee-Khang phonetic form).

**rGyud-bzhi, Clark 1995 (FMT).** Chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", Clark line 792 onward) gives the powers of stone medicines at line 816 onward. Two entries name the blue stone. At line 846: "rdo.rgyus (lit. 'stone tendon'): Actinolite: Stibine: A smooth, white stone with the texture of a tendon. The blue type is called mthing.rgyus (lit: 'indigo tendon') and the green type is called spang.rgyus (lit. 'meadow tendon'). Carbonate of chalk and the three types of actinolite heal [disorders of] the ligaments [and tendons]." Here the sentence about colour types sits outside the square brackets, which in this translation mark commentary from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree (translator's note, line 82), so the three colour types and the action on ligaments and tendons are given in the root text's own voice, and the "carbonate of chalk" in the same sentence refers to the entry above it (*ba.nu*, line 845). At line 839, the entry for another stone: "rdo.chhu (lit. 'stone water'): A type of Actinolite: [has two names and bears a rippling design. It is found around hot springs. The Malaya commentary states that the white type is called rdo.rgyus (lit. 'stone tendon') whilst the blue type is called mthing.rgyus (lit. 'indigo tendon') or Actinolite asbestos. The yellow type] heals fractures." That is commentary, and it is the only place in the sources where the blue stone is called "Actinolite asbestos", and it is a note under a different stone, *rdo.chhu*, which the Malaya commentary relates to *rdo.rgyus*. The three colour types (white, blue, green) are therefore three stones that the text groups under one action.

**rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (FMT).** The efficacy of stone medicines (line 3356): "Bhanu, Dhogyue and Thinggyue heals ligament." The phonetic *Thinggyue* corresponds to Clark's *mthing.rgyus*, and *Dhogyue* to *rdo.rgyus* by sound; the pairing with *Bhanu* (Clark's *ba.nu*, carbonate of chalk) matches Clark's sentence, so the two renderings agree. The pairing of names is an inference from sound and from the shared sentence, not stated in either text. [USER: verify] The index (line 7510) lists "Thinggyue 199".

**What the entry is not.** It is not the Shennong stone *Yang Qi Shi* (Yang's "Actinolitum", filed at II_127), which belongs to a different tradition and is not claimed here. The white and green types (*rdo.rgyus*, *spang.rgyus*) have no row of their own on the spine.

A search of the Sushruta, Dioscorides and Ebers texts on disk found nothing for this stone, and none is claimed.

### Active Components

The sources name no constituent. In general terms, actinolite is a calcium-magnesium-iron silicate of the amphibole group, which can form fibres, and in its fibrous form it is a regulated asbestos. Whether the Tibetan stone is actinolite is Clark's gloss, and the same heading also gives "Stibine" (antimony sulphide), so even the gloss is not settled. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Asbestiform amphiboles, including actinolite asbestos, are recognised causes of asbestosis, lung cancer and mesothelioma when their fibres are inhaled. Stibnite (antimony sulphide), the other gloss in the heading, is also toxic. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan medicine, rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* This stone has no cultivation status in the texts read; it is a stone medicine for the ligaments and tendons. The frame it sits inside is the tantra's own account of disease: Clark's text says that "the sole cause of all disease is said to be ignorance due to lack of understanding of the meaning of selflessness" (line 440), that "from ignorance arise the three poisons of attachment, hatred and closedmindedness whence are produced in turn the humors wind, bile and phlegm" (line 441), and that the humors are "rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration respectively" (line 51). A stone medicine acts on a disorder that the text places downstream of that root cause. No source retrieved assigns the stone a place in essence-extraction (*bcud len*) or any practice, and the Codex asserts none.

**Practitioner's note.** A stone for the ligaments and tendons, and a hazard if it is fibrous. A cultivator should not handle it. Nothing in the record supports a cultivation use.
