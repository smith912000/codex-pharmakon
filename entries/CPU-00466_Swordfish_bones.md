# Swordfish bones — "Swordfish", "Bone-of-the-Sword-fish", "Bones-of-the-Swordfish" (Bryan's English renderings of the Ebers Papyrus; no Egyptian word is printed)
## *The bones of the fish Bryan calls "Swordfish"; Bryan prints no Egyptian word and no binomial, so the spine's "Xiphias gladius or Tetrapturus sp." is the list-maker's gloss and is not read from the text* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian Tradition (EP).** The locus is Bryan's 1930 English *The Papyrus Ebers*. "Swordfish" stands in Bryan's list of the animals "whose identity is not in doubt" in Chapter VII "Organic Remedies" (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1404, after "Crab" and before "Ostrich"; name-index row `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` line 124, class animal, "Bryan's English identification; no Egyptian word or binomial given in this list"). The spine row reads "Swordfish bones", "Xiphias gladius or Tetrapturus sp.", note "Ebers; Wikipedia/Ebers Papyrus; id-uncertain" (`_sources/ebers.csv` line 91). Wikipedia was not used. The parts-of-animals list (lines 1405-1416) includes "Bones" but ties it to no animal.

**Where the bones are prescribed.** The Bryan text on disk was searched for "sword". Besides the list and two passages irrelevant to the drug (a Roman sword, line 386; a sword cut on a skeleton, line 671), the bones occur twice.

- **Pain in the head, lines 2100-2108**, in Chapter XI "Minor Medicine" (heading lines 2090-2091). Bryan notes that the headache remedies are "repulsive messes, their sole redeeming feature in our eyes being that they were not to be taken internally but rubbed in externally" (line 2093). The prescription (line 2100 heading, 2102 ingredients, 2108 direction): "REMEDY TO DRIVE OUT THE PAIN IN THE HEAD / Inner-of-Onions Fruit-of-the-am-tree Natron setseft-seeds Bone-of-the-Sword-fish, cooked Redfish, cooked Skull-of-the-Crayfish, cooked Honey abra-ointment ... Smear the Head therewith for four days." The bone is one of eight or nine ingredients and is "cooked"; the printed text carries no quantities.
- **Milk for a woman about to suckle, line 2515**, in Chapter XIV "Diseases of Women" (heading lines 2501-2502): "To produce Milk in the breast of a woman about to suckle a child, Bones-of-the-Swordfish were warmed in Oil and her backbone smeared therewith." This is Bryan's summary in his own words, not a transliterated line of the papyrus; the next sentence gives a second remedy for the same aim (a bread of soured durra with the poppy-plant, eaten).

**Related items, printed separately, not counted as the swordfish.** Other fish are named elsewhere (the "Shadfish": "Skull-of-the-Shadfish, cooked in Oil" for a splinter, line 2344; the "Redfish" in the headache compound); they are different items.

**What is not said.** The sources retrieved do not say how the bones were cooked or prepared, what the "Redfish" was, or how much of any ingredient entered the headache compound. Nothing says that the Egyptian scribe meant a swordfish; Bryan's list is of animals "whose identity is not in doubt", but he prints no reasoning for this one. The Ebers material is indication-organised, so the identity of any named substance is a modern hypothesis. The 1987 translation and the Egyptian word could change any reading above. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None can be stated for the material as the papyrus names it. In general terms, fish bone is mostly calcium phosphate (as hydroxyapatite) in a collagen matrix. Nothing in the sources retrieved says that any component acts on a headache or on lactation. This is a general statement, not a reading of Bryan.

---

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved show cooked fish bone in a head ointment and bones warmed in oil and rubbed on the backbone of a nursing mother. Neither is supported as a treatment, and the headache compound contains natron and other ingredients not assessed here. No modern protocol is supplied and none is implied.

### Preparation & Dosing

Only what Bryan prints: bone cooked and combined with the other ingredients, smeared on the head for four days (lines 2102-2108); bones warmed in oil and smeared on the woman's backbone (line 2515). No quantity is printed. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's vital constitution, and the uses found are an external head ointment and an external rub for lactation. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); no recitation is printed with either prescription in Bryan's summary, which is no proof the papyrus gives none. The person as plural (*ka*, *ba*, *akh*, *ib*) is not engaged by this entry. No deity is attributed.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. The record is a fish bone in a head ointment and in a rub for a nursing mother, with no quantity and no cultivation role. A cultivator has nothing to do with it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** bone and other animal material put on skin, especially broken skin, risks infection.
- **Co-ingredients:** the headache compound includes natron and other ingredients not assessed here.
- **Breastfeeding:** nothing should be put on or near a nursing mother or child on the strength of these sources; lactation difficulty needs proper advice.
- **Conservation and law:** swordfish are subject to fishing quotas and conservation management in parts of their range; the present status was not retrieved. [USER: verify]
