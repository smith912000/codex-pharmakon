# Plant ash (twig ash) — Tephra Klematine (τέφρα κληματίνη), "Cinis sarmentitius" (Dioscorides 5-135)
## *The ash of burnt twigs or prunings, which the chapter does not tie to one plant; the translator's suggestion is "Ash from twigs or prunings"* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek Tradition (DMM).** The main locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-135 *TEPHRA KLEMATINE* in the 2000 English translation (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10536-10538; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 908; book index "TEPHRA KLEMATINE 813", line 12684, "Cinis sarmentitius 813", line 15361, and "Lye 182, 813", line 16398). The translator's heading is "SUGGESTED: Ash from twigs or prunings." It stands among the earth and stone chapters of Book 5, between gypsum (5-134) and alcyonium (5-136).

Dioscorides' account, read in full, is:

"Cinis sarmentitius is caustic. Smeared on with goose grease or oil it is good for bruised sinews, dislocated joints and knotted nerves. With saltpetre [potassium nitrate] and vinegar it represses fleshy abnormal growths in the scrotum, and smeared on with vinegar it heals the bites of snakes or dogs. It is mixed with scab-forming medicines. Lye is made from it, good for precipitations [antidotes to mercury poisoning], and [the effects of] mushrooms, used with vinegar, salt and honey." (line 10538)

**Reading the chapter.** (1) The one quality is "caustic". (2) External uses: with goose grease or oil on bruised sinews, dislocated joints and "knotted nerves"; with saltpetre and vinegar on fleshy growths "in the scrotum"; with vinegar on bites of snakes or dogs; as an ingredient "mixed with scab-forming medicines". (3) Lye "is made from it" and is used against "precipitations" (which the translator glosses as antidotes to mercury poisoning) and against the effects of mushrooms, "with vinegar, salt and honey". (4) The chapter does not say which plant the twigs come from, how the ash is burned, or how the lye is made.

**Related passages.** (1) In the passage on the *syca* trees the lye of their ashes is described: "Lye is made from ashes of the burnt branches of the wild and cultivated syca trees. You must steep the ashes long and often. It is good both for caustic medicines and gangrenous parts, for it cleans and removes things which are superfluous" (line 4887); the chapter goes on to give internal and external uses which were not read in full here. (2) Under the plant *achras* (wild pear): "Ash from the wood effectively helps those suffocated from eating mushrooms" (line 4763). These are ashes of named trees and are not the same chapter; they show the antidote use for mushrooms recurring. They are cited as parallels and not as part of 5-135.

**Identity.** The Latin *cinis sarmentitius* means, in general Latin, ash of cut shoots or prunings, and the Greek *klematine* is not glossed in the text read; the translator's English is "Ash from twigs or prunings". The material is therefore the ash of an unspecified woody plant, and its qualities depend on the plant, which the text leaves open. The Codex's list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 861 reads "Tephra Klematine,Ash from,plant,DMM V,no": the gloss has been cut at its comma and the class entered as "plant". [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Stated generally. Wood ash is mostly calcium carbonate with potassium salts (potash, chiefly potassium carbonate), phosphates and other minerals, and a little unburnt carbon. Leached with water it gives an alkaline solution of potassium carbonate and hydroxide (lye), which is caustic. This fits the chapter's "caustic" in kind. The text gives no composition, and the plant is not named.

---

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The chapter records an alkaline ash and its lye, applied to the skin for joints, growths and bites and taken as an antidote for mushrooms and mercury. Alkaline lye is a caustic hazard, and using it as an antidote to mushroom or mercury poisoning is not supported; poisoning needs emergency care. No modern protocol is supplied.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations in the chapter are: ash "smeared on with goose grease or oil"; "with saltpetre and vinegar"; "with vinegar"; "mixed with scab-forming medicines"; and lye "used with vinegar, salt and honey" (line 10538). The way of burning and of leaching is not given here; the *syca* passage says the ashes are steeped "long and often" for lye (line 4887). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers is a discipline of attention; in this chapter that shows chiefly in its plain statement of a quality ("caustic") and of the vehicles that temper it (goose grease, oil, vinegar). The chapter is therapeutic and no cultivation status is assigned; no amuletic use is recorded.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here for a cultivator to take. What carries over is the habit of naming a material's hazard first and noting what it is mixed with. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Caustic injury:** wood ash and especially lye burn skin, eyes and mucous membranes; swallowing lye can damage the gullet and stomach.
- **Poisoning:** the chapter's antidote uses (mushrooms, "precipitations" or mercury) are not supported; suspected poisoning needs emergency care, not lye.
- **Broken skin and growths:** applying alkali to open wounds, bites or growths delays healing and risks infection.
- **Source plant:** the plant is not named in the chapter, and ash from treated or painted wood contains metals and other contaminants.
