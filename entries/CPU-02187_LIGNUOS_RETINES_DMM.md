# LIGNUOS RETINES — Lignuos Retines (Dioscorides 1-93)

## *A product of resin: resin boiled or burnt in a jar until dry and brittle, and the soot taken from resins, as the translators' subtitle says ("Soot from Resin"); not the raw resin of 1-92; the tree is not fixed by the chapter, which makes the identification of the source tree open; confidence in the product, medium; in the tree, low* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM) among the texts on disk.** Single-text entry. The chapter is *1-93. LIGNUOS RETINES* in Book One, "Gums from Trees", `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 4246 (translators' index: page 94, line 12234; the translators' own subtitle is "SUGGESTED: Soot from Resin"). The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder, and a first-pass check proposed that it duplicated the pine-resin row (CPU-00029). The audit disagreed: 1-93 is a manufactured product of resin, not the raw resin, which is chapter 1-92 (*Retina Allas*). This entry keeps them apart.

**Greek, Dioscorides (DMM).** The chapter describes a manufacture and its uses. The manufacture: "All liquid resin is burnt in a jar containing four times as much resin as the amount of the liquid that is to be poured into it. You must (when you have put four and a half litres of resin and two gallons of rain water into a jar) boil it over a coal fire gently, always stirring it until it is without any smell and brittle and dry, and as it were yields to the fingers. At last having cooled it, it must be put up in a bottle made without pitch, and so it becomes extraordinarily white, but first you must strain all the resin, melting it so that the filth may be separated from it. It is also burnt without water on coals — gently at first but more violently when it begins to thicken — then place many coals under and boil it without intermission for three days and three nights until the remains have the predicted properties. Then (as it is said) bottle and store it. It is enough to boil the dry resin for one whole day and then store it."

The uses: "When thus burnt these are good to give a sweet smell to warm compresses and remedies to remove fatigue, as well as to colour ointments. Soot is also taken from resins as well as thus [1-81] which is good mixed with medicines to make the eyelids pleasing, for gnawed-around eye corners, for the membranes of the eyelids, and for weeping eyes. Ink with which to write is also made from it."

**The neighbouring chapters.** Chapter 1-92 (*Retina Allas*, line 4240) is the raw moist and dry resin of pine, pitch tree, larch and cypress. Chapters 1-85 (*Lignuos Skeuasia*, line 4191) and 1-96 (*Lignuos Ugras Pisses*, line 4265) share the first word of the heading with this one; only their headings were read for this entry. The translators' bracket [1-81] refers to the frankincense chapter (thus), which is not part of the row.

**What the sources do not give.** The tree is not fixed: chapter 1-92 names several, and 1-93 says only "resin". The meaning of the Greek heading is not explained in the translation. The word *Lignuos* may be related to the soot or smoke of the product, but that is an inference. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

No constituent is named. In general terms, boiling or burning a conifer resin drives off the volatile terpenes, leaving a hard, brittle residue (the rosin type of material), and the soot from burning resin is carbon. These are stated of the class of material only, and the Codex does not claim that Dioscorides' product is any particular modern product. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** To give a sweet smell to warm compresses and to remedies against fatigue, to colour ointments, as a soot mixed with medicines for the eyelids and eye corners and for weeping eyes, and for making writing ink.
- **What the text does not say.** An amount for any use, or which tree.
- The Codex does not recommend burning resin, putting soot or burnt resin near the eye, or any of these uses. The eye use in particular is a historical record only.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives a manufacturing method in the translators' volumes (four and a half litres of resin and two gallons of rain water; boiled gently; or burnt on coals for three days and nights; or boiled dry for one day), not a dose. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Soot is carbon, and heating resin changes its volatile fraction. Applying soot or hot resin products to the eye is not safe practice today. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek, Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The chapter is a craft recipe for a product, with cosmetic, fragrance and ink uses, and an eye remedy. Dioscorides offers no subtle-body system, and none is imported. What the chapter does show is the discipline of attention that the Codex reads in Dioscorides: the proportions, the straining of the resin to remove filth, the choice of an unpitched bottle, and the time of boiling.

**Practitioner's note.** A craft recipe, not a cultivation substance. The only thing for a cultivator here is the care of the method.

### Contraindications

- **Burns and fire.** Boiling resin for days over coals is a fire and burn hazard, a general caution.
- **Eyes.** Do not put soot or any resin product in the eye. The text's eye use is a historical record, not advice.
- **Fumes.** Burning resin gives off smoke and irritant vapours; indoor use is unwise.
- **Allergy.** Conifer resins are a known cause of contact allergy. [USER: verify]
- **Identity.** The source tree is not fixed by the chapter.
