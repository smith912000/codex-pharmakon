# Luffa aegyptiaca — Sergyi phuebu, gser gye phud bu (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Luffa aegyptiaca, the binomial the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary sets by column position against Sergyi phuebu (English name "Sponge gourd"); Clark 1995 prints a different binomial, Thladiantha harmsii, for the same Tibetan name gser.gyi.phud.bu* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and on a binomial that only one of the two editions prints.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk for *aegyptiaca* found nothing, and for the genus *Luffa* found only two lines in the botanical appendix of Bhishagratna's Sushruta, Vol. III (`Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`, lines 31621 and 32682), both printing "Luffa Cylindrica", a different species. Nothing in those texts is read as a mention of this drug. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 351) the key *Luffa aegyptiaca* is taken from the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary line 5745, with the note "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". The key is therefore not the first or a later one of several names in one line: it is the only binomial the glossary prints at that place, it is not printed by Clark at all, and the pairing with a Tibetan name is the Codex's own reading by position, set out below. Clark's binomial for the same Tibetan name, *Thladiantha harmsii* Cogn., has its own row (names.csv line 57; spine CPU-01801, which has no written file at the time of this entry).

**Identity doubt.** The two editions disagree on the plant. Men-Tsee-Khang's scientific column gives *Luffa aegyptiaca* by position; Clark gives *Thladiantha harmsii*. Both are gourd-family genera as the Codex understands it (not checked against a flora). Which plant the Tibetan texts meant, or whether the two editors identified two different plants under one name, cannot be told from the lines read. The entry stands for the Tibetan drug. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (lines 900 to 903).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792). Clark's list of the eight sections (line 802) names "(6) plants"; the text read prints no "(6)" heading, and the entry stands between "(5) Resins" (line 879) and "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). It follows *gser.gyi.me.tog* (line 899). A page number (93) is printed at line 901, in the middle of the entry. The entry reads:

"gser.gyi.phud.bu: Thladiantha harmsii Cogn: [has a fruit similar to a headless sbur.wa beetle. It has bitter taste, coarse power, and cures poisoning, phlegm-bile combinations and has an emetic action. This plant is also a creeper with a lined, green petiole and (fruit-) stalk and yellow flower resembling an acorn. The superior type has a bitter, black fruit with coarse power, whilst the common one has a yellow fruit. Both types] act as an emetic in bile disorders."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Almost all of this entry is inside the brackets, that is, in the commentary layer: the beetle-like fruit, the taste and power, the poisoning and phlegm-bile uses, the creeper's description and the two types. Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the binomial, which is Clark's identification and not the root text's, and the single clause "act as an emetic in bile disorders". The line gives no part used and no preparation. Chapter 21 of Clark, on compounding, lists the drug in a class (line 1224): "The class of emetics contains pericarp of Soapnut, Hill Dockleaf, Thistle (Cirsium sp.), Sweet Flag Root, Thladiantha harmsii Cogn., Fragaria nilgeerensis (a type of mock strawberry), seed of Sophora moorcroftiana, Pyrola incarnata and mustard seed." Clark's name index lists *gser.gyi.phud.bu* (line 1768) and the Latin index lists *Thladiantha* (line 1652).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428; the run begins on an earlier page). Line 3392 reads, in the middle of a longer passage: "Sergyi metok cures hot disorders of the tripa and the vessel organs. Sergyiphuebu acts as emetic and bring up tripa disorders." ("Sergyiphuebu" is the OCR's joined spelling.) The index lists "Sergyi phuebu 202" (line 7485), and the page number 202 is printed shortly after the passage (line 3395). Clark's "emetic action" and "emetic in bile disorders" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "acts as emetic and bring up tripa disorders" agree on the emetic; they are two translators' wordings, and the Codex does not treat "bile disorders" and "tripa disorders" as one term. The running text gives no Latin name, no taste and no part used.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "S" block, lines 5669 to 5770).* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate runs. Four consecutive phonetic names are "Sergyi metok" (transliteration "gser gyi me tog", line 5707), "Sergyi phuebu" ("gser gye phud bu", line 5709), "Ser kue" ("gser skud", lines 5711 and 5713) and "Sewai metok" ("se ba'i me tog", line 5715). In the scientific run the matching four are "Herpetospermum pedunculosum Golden flower" (line 5743), "Luffa aegyptiaca" (5745), "Lethariella semanderi" (5749) and "Rosa branonii" (5753); the English names are "Golden flower", "Sponge gourd" (5747), "Golden dodder" (5751) and "Musk rose" (5755). Counted in order, "Sergyi phuebu" is the second of the four and *Luffa aegyptiaca* with "Sponge gourd" the second of the four. The neighbours fit the Tibetan names: Clark prints *gser.gyi.me.tog* with a *Herpetospermum* as the second of its two binomials (line 899), and *gser.skud* with *Cuscuta sinensis* first (a dodder), so the counting is consistent at the points where Clark allows a check. The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Thladiantha*, *Sergyi phuebu* and *gser.gyi.phud.bu* found no entry on this drug. The only entry naming *Thladiantha* is I_1280 (*Fragaria nilgeerensis*), which quotes Clark's emetic list (line 1224). I_824 (Loofah, *Luffa cylindrica*, tags SBJ and SS) and I_594 (Ridge gourd, *Luffa acutangula*, tags CM, SBJ and SS) are *Luffa* entries of other species from other traditions, and neither cites the Tibetan texts. I_1311 and the future entry for *Thladiantha harmsii* (CPU-01801) are one Tibetan drug under two binomials, one from each edition. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Luffa* and *Thladiantha* both belong to the gourd family (Cucurbitaceae), that the mature fruit of a *Luffa* dries to a fibrous network, and that many members of the family are bitter, the bitterness often owing to the triterpenoid cucurbitacins. Whether that applies to the Tibetan drug is not shown by the lines read: Clark's commentary calls the plant bitter with "coarse power", and which plant is meant, and which part, is not settled. No statement about this drug's constituents is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record an emetic: a drug that Clark says acts "as an emetic in bile disorders" (line 903) and that Men-Tsee-Khang says "acts as emetic and bring up tripa disorders" (line 3392). These are the texts' claims in their own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and nothing in this entry should be taken by mouth. Inducing vomiting is not a safe self-treatment, and a drug listed with poisoning uses and a bitter, coarse commentary description should not be tried on the strength of a glossary line.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's compounding chapter places the drug in the class of emetics (line 1224) and gives no recipe. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (lines 900 and 903) prints "bitter taste, coarse power", and "a bitter, black fruit with coarse power" for the superior type. No post-digestive taste is printed. Men-Tsee-Khang prints no taste or power for the name in the lines read. The actions printed are curing poisoning and phlegm-bile combinations and acting as an emetic, and, in the root-text layer, acting as an emetic in bile disorders (Clark); acting as emetic and bringing up tripa disorders (Men-Tsee-Khang).

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it is an emetic in bile disorders, and its commentary adds poisoning and phlegm-bile combinations. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the chapter 20 and chapter 21 lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes, and not in the rejuvenation chapter (Clark chapter 23; Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23); no line read places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name, not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is an emetic in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and the two editions name two different plants for it. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. It is described as an emetic and as acting on poisoning, so it should be treated as a drug that provokes vomiting; poisoning and vomiting need medical assessment, and no preparation or dose is given. No plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Because the identity is uncertain, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case.
