# Balm of Gilead / Balsamodendron gileadense — Balsamon, opobalsamum, xylobalsamum (Dioscorides 1-18)

## *Balsamodendron gileadense, the first of several binomials the Dioscorides translators suggest for chapter 1-18 (also Commiphora opobalsamum; "Balm of Gilead, Balm of Mecca")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). **Named only by Dioscorides in this row.** The binomial is the translators' suggestion, not Dioscorides' own identification.

*Overlap, stated first.* Chapter 1-18 is the same Dioscorides chapter that the spine's I_525 *Balsam of Mecca / Commiphora opobalsamum* (tagged EP, AT, DMM, CM) draws on for its DMM paragraph. The two binomials sit together in the translators' list for this one chapter. *1-18 BALSAMON* (heading at line 3703). The SUGGESTED line (lines 3705 to 3707): "Opalobalsamum, Balsamodendron gileadense, Balsamodendron opalobalsamum, Commiphora opalobalsamum, Amyris gileadensis — Balm of Gilead, Balm of Mecca Amyris kataf, Commiphora kataf, Balsamodendron kataf — Balsam of Kataf".

The tree (line 3708): "The tree balsamum is noted, similar in size to lycium [1-132] or pyracantha [1-169, 1-170], with leaves like rue [3-52, 3-53, 4-98] but a great deal paler and much more flourishing. It grows only in Judaea in a certain valley and in Egypt." The part of the shrub that is thin is "called theriston". "Opobalsamum is the juice exuded by the tree when it is cut with iron nails in the heat of the hottest days. But it drops so little that every year they can get no more than six or seven congii [three litre units approximately] of it, and a weight of it is sold in that place for double its weight in silver." The wood is "called xylobalsamum". The best juice is "new, with a strong smell, pure and not inclining to sweetness, dissolving easily, smooth, astringent, and a little biting to the tongue".

Adulteration and assay occupy much of the chapter. It lists the ointments that are mixed into the juice, and then the tests: "if the unmixed is dropped on a woollen cloth and afterwards washed out it makes no stain or spot on it, but that which is counterfeited sticks. The pure when put into water or milk is easily diffused and turns like milk, but that which is counterfeited swims on the top like oil." Of the fruit it says to choose that "which is yellow, full, great, heavy, biting in taste and hot in the mouth", and that a seed "like hypericum [3-171]" brought from Petra is used to counterfeit it, which one can tell because it is "bigger, and empty with no strength, and tastes of pepper".

Uses (lines 3715): "The juice has the most strength as it heats the most, cleaning away things that darken the pupils, and curing abrasions around the vulva applied with waxy ointments and rosaceum [1-53]. It expels the menstrual flow and the afterbirth, is an abortifacient, and rubbed on dissolves chills and the filthy matter of boils. Taken as a drink it is a concoction for rejuvenation and moving urine. Given with milk it is also good for difficult breathers and those who have taken a drink of aconitum [4-77, 4-78]; also for those bitten by snakes." Of the fruit: "good for pleurisy, pneumonia, coughs, sciatica, epilepsy, vertigo, asthma, griping, difficulty in conception, and for those bitten by snakes". Of the wood: "the same virtues the fruit has but to a lesser degree", and "Generally the juice of the balsamum has the most strength, next to that the fruit, but the wood has the least strength of all."

### Active Components

Balsams are generally known as aromatic resinous exudates, rich in volatile oils and resin acids. The composition of the juice Dioscorides describes was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records the juice as the strongest part, a heating, cleansing and emmenagogue material that is also an abortifacient, a drink "for rejuvenation and moving urine", and an antidote in milk for those who had drunk *aconitum*. These are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The tree is a rare, valued resource, and the Codex does not recommend any use of it.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations the text gives: the juice by tapping with iron nails; the juice mixed into ointments; the juice with waxy ointments and *rosaceum* for vulval abrasions; the juice "taken as a drink" and "given with milk"; the fruit "taken in a drink"; fumes for women's inhalations; and the wood "boiled in water and taken as a drink" or boiled for hip baths. No quantity of the medicine is printed. The only quantity is the yield of the tree, "six or seven congii" a year. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Resinous balsams are generally known to have antiseptic and irritant properties, and a resin applied to skin can cause contact allergy. This is a general statement about the class and no study of this tree was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** "The juice has the most strength as it heats the most" (line 3715). The best juice is "astringent, and a little biting to the tongue" (line 3708). The fruit is "hot in the mouth". The wood has the least strength.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and the chapter assigns the tree no status. The translation prints the juice as a drink "for rejuvenation", but that word sits inside a list of therapeutic uses, the Greek term was not read, and it is not treated here as a cultivation status. What the chapter gives a cultivator is the fullest discipline of attention in the book: provenance (a valley in Judaea, and Egypt), season of gathering (the heat of the hottest days), the grade order (juice, then fruit, then wood), the sensory assay, and the detection of counterfeits by the wool-cloth and water-or-milk tests. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** What a cultivator takes from this chapter is the method of provenance and adulteration testing, not a practice with the balsam. The abstentive answer is the honest one, because the tree is rare and genuine material is hard to know.

### Contraindications

The text records the juice and the fruit as emmenagogues and abortifacients, so they must be avoided in pregnancy on its own showing. The antidote use against *aconitum*, and the use for snakebite, are historical claims and neither is a safe home remedy. Resins can irritate the skin and mucous membranes. The text itself warns that counterfeits are common, so authenticity cannot be assumed. The tree is a rare plant in the wild and is treated as a protected resource in many places, and this is a general statement and not from the text. [USER: verify]
