# Takin (wild yak) horn — rGyud-bzhi, "rgod.gyag" (rgod.gyag; also known as hbrong), wild yak horn
## *The horn of the animal Clark prints as "Takin: Wild yak: Budorcas taxicolor"; Men-Tsee-Khang prints "wild yak horn"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The horn of the rgod.gyag is entered in the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, the chapter on the powers of medicines, in Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). It falls in the section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), whose thirteen headings are "horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148), and under the first, "Horn". The entry, in the line numbered 1158 (it follows that of the ram), reads: "rgod.gyag: Takin: Wild yak: Budorcas taxicolor: [has ten names and is also known as hbrong]. Its horn increases [body] heat, disperses tumors, [and dries (accumulations of) pus in the abdomen]." So the Tantra's own action is that the horn increases heat and disperses tumours, and the third action (dries pus in the abdomen) is in Clark's brackets, which look like interpolation from the commentary he describes in his introduction (line 80, "interpolated liberally from the Blue Aquamarine commentary"), although the lines read do not state the bracket convention outright. [USER: verify] The identification "Takin: Wild yak" is Clark's: he gives the animal two English names and one binomial, *Budorcas taxicolor*. In his meat classification he again writes "wild yak (takin)" among the larger hill and game animals (line 672).

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3508) has the item as "Wild yak horn generates heat and dissolves tumors." (the OCR prints the words as "WildyaA: horn"). The two translations agree: the horn heats and the tumours are dispersed or dissolved. Men-Tsee-Khang does not carry the pus action. Its glossary of animals lists a "Wild yak" (line 7086) beside scientific names that include "Bos grunniens" (a different species and genus from Clark's), but the glossary is an OCR of columns and the pairing of name and binomial rests on position, so it is not used to identify the animal. [USER: verify]

The animal is named elsewhere in the Tantra for its other parts. Clark's "Blood" section (line 1173) says: "The blood of wild yak and of Hodgson's antelope (gtsod): halts diarrhoea." (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3539, reads "The blood of wild yaks and antelopes stop diarrhea.") The meat is stated at Clark line 679: "Wild yak meat eliminates cold disorders of the stomach and liver and boosts the digestive heat." (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 2931: it "relieves cold disorders of the liver and stomach, and generates digestive heat".) Wild yak meat is also named among the foods for phlegm disorders (Clark lines 176 and 783: "Wild yak meat, fish, mutton and honey are beneficial [in phlegm disorders]"). Those are meat and blood, not the horn.

### Active Components

Horn of the bovid family is a keratin sheath over a bony core. Keratin is a structural protein, with some minerals in the bone core. Nothing in the lines read names an active principle for the horn, and none specific to this entry is claimed.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is a pair of actions of the horn: it increases body heat and disperses tumours, with Clark's bracketed extension to abdominal pus. The Codex gives no protocol. Tumours and abdominal pus are matters for clinical assessment, and the takin is a protected animal (see Contraindications). Nothing is recommended.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation, route or dose is given in the sources retrieved: Clark and Men-Tsee-Khang state the action only, and neither says whether the horn is powdered, burnt, boiled or used in a compound. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Horn is keratin, the same protein as hair and nails, and it has no recognised medicinal action on heat, tumours or abdominal infections. A new or growing lump, and any abdominal pus or abscess, needs prompt clinical examination. The takin (*Budorcas taxicolor*) is a hoofed mammal of the Himalayan region and is a protected species. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** One quality is printed in action form: the horn "increases [body] heat" (Clark) or "generates heat" (Men-Tsee-Khang). No taste, potency or humoral action is printed for the horn. The general statement that animal meats are "sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste" (Clark line 673) concerns flesh and is not applied to horn. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* Neither translation gives takin or wild yak horn any cultivation framing. It is a line in the list of horns, with a heating action and a tumour-dispersing action. The frame around it is the tradition's own: the project's cultivation spec records that the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) are derived from the three poisons and that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root. That point comes from the spec and not from any line about the horn. No source read attaches the horn to *bcud len* (essence extraction), and there is no formal Tibetan category of substances for subtle-body practice. Clark's remark (line 57) that "only very few animal products are used" nowadays is a note on usage, not on status.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a list entry for the horn of a protected wild animal, with no preparation and no dose; the actions named (heat, tumours) are therapeutic claims and not cultivation claims. The honest use of the entry is as a record of what the Tantra says.

### Contraindications

- **Tumours and abscesses:** any lump or abdominal pus needs clinical assessment; do not rely on this line.
- **Legal and conservation status:** the takin and wild yak are protected and their horn and parts are controlled in trade in many countries and under international trade rules; the identification rests on the translators' names and binomial and the two translations differ in how they name the animal. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** killing or trading in a protected wild animal for a substance with no supported benefit is hard to justify.
