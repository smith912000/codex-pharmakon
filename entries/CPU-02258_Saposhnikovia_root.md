# Saposhnikovia root — 防风 (Fang Feng); also Hui Yun, Hui Cao, Bai Zhi, Ping Feng

## **Saposhnikovia divaricata* (Turcz.) Schischk. (syn. *Ledebouriella divaricata*), the root; the identification is the modern standard one for 防风 and is of high confidence, but the Shennong text itself describes no part of the plant beyond its habitat and Wu Pu's notes* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

### Origin & Tradition

*Fang feng* is named only by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts on disk. It is a single-text entry; no other tradition in the Codex is tagged here.

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** In Sun Xingyan's 1799 reconstruction the entry 防风 stands in the upper classic (卷一 上经; `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 761, 味 paragraph; name line 759). The Shennong text proper reads: "bitter, warm, nontoxic" (味苦，温，无毒). It "mainly treats" (主) "great wind, dizzy head and pain, aversion to wind, wind evil, blindness with nothing seen, wind moving through the whole body, aching impediment of the bone joints, and vexing fullness" (大风、头眩痛，恶风风邪，目盲无所见，风行周身，骨节疼痹，烦满). A variant is noted: the *Yulan* quotation has 痛 "pain" for 疼痹. Then: "Taken long, it lightens the body" (久服，轻身). "One name: Tong Yun" 铜芸, "Bronze Rue" in Yang (the *Yulan* variant reads 芒). "It grows in rivers and marshes" (生川泽).

*Wu Pu layer* (3rd c.; line 763). Aliases 回云, 回草, 百枝, 百韭, 百种 (one alias is lost to a missing glyph in this e-text). Wu Pu reports the older authorities' flavours: "Shennong, Huangdi, Qibo, Tongjun, Leigong, Bian Que: sweet, nontoxic; Mr Li: slightly cold." This disagrees with the 味 paragraph above, which says bitter; the e-text does not resolve it. Wu Pu adds a plant description: leaves come in the first month, "fine and round, blue-black, yellow-white"; the flower is yellow in the fifth month; the fruit is black in the sixth; the root is gathered in the third and tenth months and dried in the sun, and that of Langya is good.

*Mingyi bielu layer* (later additions; line 765). Aliases 茴草, 百枝, 屏风 ("screen against wind"), 百蜚. Habitat: Shayuan, and Handan, Langya, Shangcai. The root is gathered in the second and tenth months and dried in the sun.

*Sun's note* (案, line 767) cites the *Fanzi jiran*: "fang feng comes from the Sanfu; the white is good." He adds nothing else.

*Drug-relations table* (Sun's edition, appended per class; the terms are introduced at line 4117: 相须, 相使, 相畏, 相恶, 相反, 相杀). Fang feng appears in it as: "dislikes (恶) dried ginger, veratrum, 白蔹 and genkwa; kills the poison of aconite (杀附子毒)" (line 4321); as envoy (使) to 术 (line 4273); and as something that 附子 "fears" (畏, line 4443) and 山茱萸 "dislikes" (line 4505). These are statements about combination, not about use on its own.

**Yang 1998 as second witness.** Yang's English (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt` line 2351) gives "Fang Feng (Radix Ledebouriellae Divaricatae) is sweet and warm. It is nontoxic," with the same indications, "Protracted taking may make the body light," and "Tong Yun (Bronze Rue)." Yang's flavour is sweet, as in Wu Pu's report; Sun's text says bitter. Yang's footnote 141 (line 2361) is his own later commentary: it ascribes all the listed conditions to "wind" and says the root "can either promote or stop sweating depending upon what other medicinals it is combined with." That is Yang's gloss on later authorities (Zhang Zhicong, Ye Gui, Xu Dachun), not Shennong text.

**Grade check.** The extraction that created the spine row recorded this as "Middle Class" because the nearest class heading above Yang's page 62 reads so; the page itself carries no heading. Sun's edition files it in the upper classic and the 久服 clause fits that class, so the upper grade is used here.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

This is a historical record of one text, not a recommended practice.

- **What the text says it is for.** "Wind" complaints in the old sense: head dizziness and pain, aversion to wind, joint aching, blindness, vexing fullness (line 761).
- **What the text says it does to the person.** "Taken long, it lightens the body" (久服，轻身). This is a classical effect category; it is not weight loss and has no pharmacological equivalent.
- **What the text does not say.** It gives no dose, no preparation, no duration of "long" taking, and no cautions beyond the relations table above.

A modern reader should not read the indications as treatments for dizziness, vision loss or joint disease; those conditions need medical assessment. No Kenetica protocol is derived from this entry.

### Preparation & Dosing

The Shennong text gives no preparation and no dose. Wu Pu and the Mingyi layer give only the harvest: root, third/second and tenth months, dried in the sun (lines 763, 765). The relations table (line 4321) says it "kills the poison of aconite", which is a combination statement and is not read as a method. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Scientific Correlation

The root of *Saposhnikovia divaricata* (*fangfeng*) is a standard Chinese crude drug, and several other umbelliferous roots have been sold under the same name. Whether it relieves any condition named in the Shennong text has not been tested in sources retrieved for this entry. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SBJ (Sun 1799).** Bitter, warm, nontoxic (味苦，温，无毒; line 761). Wu Pu's report from the older authorities: sweet, nontoxic; Mr Li: slightly cold (line 763).
- **SBJ (Yang 1998).** Sweet and warm, nontoxic (line 2351).
- The two witnesses differ on the flavour (bitter against sweet) and the reconstruction does not settle it. No channel or organ assignment is given in the Shennong text; Yang's footnote 141 supplies later vocabulary which is not attributed to the classic.

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested.* The entry stands in the upper classic (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 761). The Shennong preface in Sun's edition (line 43) defines the upper drugs as those that "nourish life to correspond with heaven, are nontoxic, and do not harm a person when taken much or long," for one who wishes to "lighten the body and add qi, not age and extend the years." The entry's own 久服 clause is narrower than that frame: it promises one thing only, *qing shen* (轻身), "lighten the body." It does not promise added qi, no ageing or extended years. The claim is an attested classical category and is not rendered as a pharmacological action.

**Practitioner's note.** The classic grants this root the long-term standing of an upper drug, and records it with a defined list of "wind" complaints. It gives no dose, form or length of "long taking," and its own relations table has the root disliking several named drugs. A cultivator has nothing here to turn into a regimen; the entry is a record of what the classic said.

### Contraindications

- **Dose and form unknown.** The text gives none; do not infer one from modern products.
- **Identification.** Material sold as *fangfeng* may be a different umbelliferous root. [USER: verify]
- **Combinations.** The classic's relations table lists drugs the root "dislikes" (dried ginger, veratrum, genkwa, 白蔹); veratrum and genkwa are toxic drugs and are not to be used.
- **Serious conditions.** The indications include blindness and loss of vision, and "wind" conditions; these need medical care.
- **Pregnancy and lactation.** No statement is made in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
