### Active Components

Lapis lazuli is a rock, not a single mineral. Its blue comes from lazurite, a sodium-calcium aluminosilicate containing sulfur, and it usually carries calcite and flecks of pyrite. Finely ground, it has been the source of the pigment ultramarine. The stone is chemically stable and not soluble in body fluids to any significant degree; most health concerns come from impurities and from the way it is powdered.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. In the sources the stone is an ingredient of eye salves and cataract remedies (Egyptian), a drink for scorpion-touch and internal ulcers (the Greek, under the edition's gloss), and an unread Assyrian list row. Nothing here supports swallowing powdered stone. The Codex offers no protocol, dose or preparation.

### Scientific Correlation

Lapis lazuli is a stable silicate rock with no known pharmacological action; ground stone applied to the eye would be an abrasive and non-sterile. Its modern use is as a gemstone and as the pigment ultramarine. No clinical studies were retrieved for the traditional uses. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Powdered stone is not sterile and, applied to the eye, can scratch the cornea and cause infection; it must not be put in the eye. Swallowing powdered stone has no sourced benefit. Commercial "lapis" powders and "ultramarine" may be dyed, synthetic or contaminated with other minerals; the pigment trade is regulated in some places. The Greek identification is uncertain (the edition glosses it both as sapphire and as lapis lazuli), so what Dioscorides recommended may not be this stone.
