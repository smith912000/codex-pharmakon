# Agrimony — Church Steeples, Cocklebur, Herba Eupatorii, Sticklewort

## *Agrimonia eupatoria* L. | Family: Rosaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM

**Energetic Classification:** Warm and dry in the first-to-second degree (Galenic); a binding, drying astringent that "closes and strengthens" lax tissue.

**Training Phase:** Foundation — a gentle, broadly tolerated astringent and tonic suited to early, everyday practice.

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian (AT):** Astringent, wound-closing field herbs of the rose and steeple-flowered groups appear among the Mesopotamian remedies for "flowing" complaints — open sores, loose bowels, and bleeding. The tall yellow spikes of the agrimony type were among the drying, binding plants gathered for external dressing and for washes of the mouth and throat.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM):** In the Galenic-Islamic corpus, agrimony (*ghafith* in the Arabic tradition, a name long associated with hepatic herbs) was esteemed as a **liver medicine** and an astringent tonic — used for obstruction and weakness of the liver, for jaundice, and to bind loose humours. The *Qanun*-derived dispensatories placed such warm, drying, mildly bitter herbs among the deobstruents that "open the liver" while tightening slack tissue.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** *De Materia Medica* describes *eupatorion* — a plant whose leaves, pounded and applied, healed wounds and whose decoction was taken for complaints of the liver and for dysentery. The species epithet *eupatoria* preserves this Dioscoridean lineage directly, and the twin traditional roles — **wound-healing astringent** and **liver herb** — descend unbroken from the Greek text.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The flower's ranked yellow spike ("church steeples") points upward like a spine held straight; folk usage made agrimony a herb of *binding and holding together* — of wounds, of the voice, of a slack gut. Kenetica reads the astringency as its doctrine: a Foundation herb that teaches *tone* — the drawing-in and firming of tissue that has grown lax or weepy. Its symbolism of steadiness matches its tannins' literal action of contracting and sealing surfaces.

### Active Components

Agrimony's activity is dominated by **hydrolysable and condensed tannins** — notably **agrimoniin** (a dimeric ellagitannin), together with catechin, procyanidin B3, and ellagitannins — which account for its characteristic astringency. Alongside these sit a rich set of **flavonoids and phenolic acids**: rutin, hyperoside, isoquercitrin, astragalin, cynaroside, isovitexin, apigenin and quercetin glycosides, plus caffeic and other phenolic acids. Small amounts of volatile oil, bitter principles, and silica complete the profile. The tannins provide the surface-binding and antimicrobial actions; the flavonoids and phenolic acids underlie the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Kenetica assigns agrimony a **Foundation astringent-tonic** role. Its tannins contract and firm mucous membranes, making the traditional preparations well suited to **sore throat and hoarseness** (as a gargle), **minor wounds and grazes** (as a wash or compress), and **mild loose bowel** where a gentle drying binder is wanted. The flavonoid fraction supports a broader tonic and antioxidant use — a mild, safe herb for the everyday maintenance work of the early phases. It is the archetype of "tone without force": it steadies and closes rather than purges or stimulates.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, agrimony's traditional profile is supported by a growing pharmacological literature. Malheiros et al. (2022), *Journal of Ethnopharmacology*, in an integrative review of *Agrimonia eupatoria*, catalogue its phenolic composition (agrimoniin, rutin, hyperoside, catechin, procyanidin B3 among others) and report antioxidant, antimicrobial, antidiabetic, antinociceptive and anti-inflammatory activities; they note that two small clinical studies of the infusion indicated hepatoprotective and cardiovascular/metabolic benefit, while cautioning that human evidence remains limited. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2022.115498). Kováč et al. (2022), *Molecules*, review the flavonoids and tannins (including those characteristic of agrimony-type Rosaceae) as antimicrobial and antibiofilm agents relevant to oral infections — a mechanistic basis for the traditional throat and mouth gargle. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules28010158). Complementary work on the closely related *Agrimonia pilosa* by Park et al. (2024), *Molecules*, isolated anti-inflammatory flavonoids (quercetin, apigenin and kaempferol glycosides) with strong nitric-oxide-inhibitory activity, supporting the anti-inflammatory role attributed to the genus. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules29020283).

**Evidence grade:** Phytochemistry is well characterised (high confidence). Bioactivity is supported by strong preclinical and mechanistic data; human clinical evidence is early and small. Graded: **preclinical/traditional with emerging clinical support** — appropriate for gentle, established traditional uses, not for disease-treatment claims.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM (by analogy):** *xian he cao* (*Agrimonia pilosa*, the East-Asian congener) is a genuine Chinese materia medica herb — astringent, "restrains bleeding," stops dysentery; *A. eupatoria* is read here through that astringent, blood-stanching lens.
- **Ayurveda (by analogy):** predominant *kashaya* (astringent) rasa; *virya* mildly warming; *vipaka* pungent; pacifies *kapha* and controls *pitta*-type oozing/laxity — mapped by property, not from a classical entry.
- **Galenic / Unani (attested):** warm and dry, astringent and mildly bitter; a liver deobstruent and tissue-binder.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** The Mesopotamian remedies record astringent, wound-closing field herbs of the rose and steeple-flowered groups for "flowing" complaints — open sores, loose bowels, bleeding — with the tall yellow spikes among the drying, binding plants used for external dressing and mouth-and-throat washes. This is a therapeutic, agency-facing use, not a cultivation status: the binomial identification of Assyrian plant names is insecure, the standing blocking warning applies to every such identification, and no framing of the herb's action on a vital constitution is attested. **(c) absent.**

**Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* describes *eupatorion* — pounded leaves for wounds, the decoction for liver complaints and dysentery — and the epithet *eupatoria* preserves that lineage, though the exact identity of Dioscorides' *eupatorion* is itself debated among scholars and cannot be pinned to modern *Agrimonia eupatoria* with certainty. Either way Dioscorides carries no subtle-body doctrine into which a cultivation status could be placed; what the tradition gives a cultivator is his discipline of attention — the assay of a genuine astringent, the distinction of the true herb from look-alikes, the judgement of provenance and preparation. That is a real practice, not a vital-body status. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM).** In the Galenic-Islamic corpus agrimony (*ghāfith*, a name long tied to hepatic herbs) was esteemed as a liver medicine and astringent tonic — warm and dry, mildly bitter, a deobstruent that "opens the liver" while binding slack, weepy tissue. This is a humoral-therapeutic classification: the herb is worked against hepatic obstruction, jaundice and loose humours. The Unani cultivation idea proper — conservation of innate heat and radical moisture across the whole regimen — is not a status the tradition assigns to this drying deobstruent, and no *mufarriḥ* or gladdening-of-the-*rūḥ* role is claimed for it. The attestation is real but therapeutic. **(b) framed** — attested as liver-tonic and astringent, no cultivation status sourced.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator uses agrimony as a Foundation astringent: a gargle for a hoarse or sore throat, a wash for a graze or weeping skin, a gentle drying binder for a slack gut — the archetype of "tone without force," steadying and closing rather than purging or stimulating. It is mild and broadly tolerated, well suited to the everyday maintenance work of the early phases, but it is not a longevity or rejuvenative substance in any of the three traditions and none assigns it a cultivation status. The honest practice is to reach for it to firm lax tissue for a defined stretch, keep the strong internal course short because the tannins are hard on iron and the gut, and expect tone, not transformation.

### Contraindications

- Generally well tolerated in traditional food-herb amounts. Its high **tannin** content means strong or prolonged internal use may cause gastric irritation and can **reduce absorption of dietary iron and of some medicines** — separate dosing from iron supplements and oral drugs by ~2 hours.
- Astringent/drying: use cautiously in constipation and in marked dryness of the tissues.
- Traditional texts caution against use in acute obstructive biliary disease without professional guidance; discontinue if abdominal pain worsens.
- Insufficient safety data in **pregnancy and lactation** — avoid medicinal-strength preparations [USER: verify].
- Possible additive effect with antihypertensive and antidiabetic medication (agrimony has shown hypotensive and hypoglycaemic activity) — monitor if combined.
- Discontinue and seek advice if an allergic (Rosaceae) reaction occurs.
