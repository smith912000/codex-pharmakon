# I-513 | Bilberry (*Vaccinium myrtillus* L.)

**Volume I — Plant-Based Substances**
**Family:** Ericaceae
**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (vascular, visual, anti-inflammatory) primary | ◈ Jing (collagen-stabilizing, tissue repair) secondary

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary binomial:** *Vaccinium myrtillus* L. (1753, *Species Plantarum*)

**Synonyms & historical names:**
- *Myrtillus niger* Gilib.
- *Vaccinium oreophilum* Rydb.

**Common names:**
- English: Bilberry, European bilberry, whortleberry, whinberry, blaeberry (Scottish), huckleberry (regional US misnomer)
- German: *Heidelbeere* (heath-berry), *Schwarzbeere*, *Blaubeere*
- French: *Myrtille*
- Italian: *Mirtillo nero*
- Spanish: *Arándano negro*
- Swedish: *Blåbär* (blueberry)
- Finnish: *Mustikka*
- Welsh: *Llus*, *Llusi* (the berries)
- Chinese: 欧洲越橘 (*Ōuzhōu yuèjú*) — ornamental/introduced; not in primary pharmacopoeias
- Japanese: ビルベリー (*Biruberi*)

**Etymology:** *Vaccinium* = Latin (possibly from *bacca* = berry, or from a classical designation for the genus); *myrtillus* = little myrtle (from leaf resemblance to myrtle, *Myrtus communis*). "Bilberry" = possibly from Danish *bølle* (ball/round berry) + English berry; alternatively Old Norse *bólber*. "Whortleberry" = corruption of "whortle" (dialect word for the plant).

**Botanical authentication:**
- Low-growing deciduous shrub 10–60 cm; green-angled stems; small ovate leaves with finely serrate margin, deciduous (turns brilliant red in autumn — photogenic); solitary pendant bell-shaped flowers (pink-white urn-shaped, May–June); berries: globose, 5–10 mm, deep blue-black with dusty blue bloom; flesh DARK PURPLE throughout (distinguishing *V. myrtillus* from cultivated blueberry *V. corymbosum* whose flesh is pale/white inside); 5 calyx lobes persistent on berry crown
- **Key authentication:** cut berry open = purple-black flesh all the way through (bilberry) vs white/pale green interior (blueberry *V. corymbosum*); this color difference = anthocyanin distribution → directly indicates different anthocyanin profile
- Mountain and moorland habitats; acid soils; European endemic; distributed from Atlantic coast to Siberia, south to mountains of Mediterranean
- **Commercial substitution risk:** cultivated blueberry (*V. corymbosum*) frequently sold as "bilberry"; different anthocyanin profile (glycoside pattern different); pharmacological equivalence not established

The Nordic and Slavic folk traditions are entirely absent from the 9-source matrix.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

Bilberry entered the modern pharmacological literature through a specific World War II observation: British Royal Air Force pilots reported that consuming bilberry jam before night missions improved their twilight vision. This observation, whether entirely accurate or partly apocryphal, drove systematic investigation of bilberry's visual effects — eventually establishing that bilberry anthocyanins (especially cyanidin-3-glucoside and delphinidin-3-glucoside) accelerate the regeneration of rhodopsin (visual purple) in rod cells, the photopigment responsible for dim-light vision.

The visual indication reflects a unique bilberry pharmacological theme: the distribution of bilberry anthocyanins to the highly vascularized retinal and choroidal tissues. *V. myrtillus* anthocyanins are specifically distributed to ocular tissues after oral absorption — verified by radiolabeled tracer studies — and reduce retinal oxidative stress, improve microcirculation in the choroid, and accelerate photopigment recycling.

The cardiovascular and metabolic evidence for bilberry parallels the broader anthocyanin literature (shared with elderberry, grape seed, cranberry — an Ericaceae/Rosaceae anthocyanin motif recurring across the Codex), but the retinal distribution and visual acute effects constitute a pharmacological specificity that distinguishes bilberry from related anthocyanin-rich berries.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Anthocyanins (primary — fruit):**
*Vaccinium myrtillus* has the richest and most diverse anthocyanin profile of any characterized fruit:
- **15 individual anthocyanins** (highest diversity in any fruit species studied)
- Aglycones: **delphinidin** (30–35%), **cyanidin** (22–25%), **petunidin** (15–18%), **malvidin** (12–15%), **peonidin** (8–10%) — all five common plant anthocyanin aglycones present
- Glycosides: predominantly **glucosides** and **galactosides**; also arabinosides
- Total anthocyanins: **300–700 mg/100g fresh berry** (one of highest concentrations in any fruit); commercial standardized extract: ≥25% total anthocyanins (as cyanidin-3-glucoside equivalent)
- *Vaccinium corymbosum* (cultivated blueberry) by comparison: primarily delphinidin and cyanidin glucosides; fewer malvidin/petunidin components; ~50–200 mg/100g — lower diversity and concentration

**Catechin-type proanthocyanidins (OPCs):**
- Procyanidins B-1, B-2, B-3, B-4, C-1: 1–3% DW in leaves; lower in fruit
- Quercetin: 0.5–1.5% DW in leaves; glycosides in fruit
- Myricetin: 0.3–1.0% DW (characteristic Ericaceae flavonol)

**Leaf phenolics:**
- **Chlorogenic acid**: 0.5–3% DW in leaves (primary phenolic acid)
- **Caffeic acid derivatives**: neochlorogenic, cryptochlorogenic acids
- **Ursolic acid**: 0.5–2% DW; anti-inflammatory; cardioprotective
- **Oleanolic acid**: 0.3–1% DW
- **Arbutin** (*para*-arbutylglucoside): trace in *V. myrtillus* leaves (major in *V. uva-ursi* = bearberry I-514); antimicrobial in urinary tract

**Iridoids:**
- Not significant in this species

**Phenylpropanoids:**
- Resveratrol: trace (same stilbene as in grape; in *Vaccinium* spp. at much lower concentrations than in red wine)

**Nutritional composition (fresh berries):**
- Vitamin C: 10–15 mg/100g (modest)
- Vitamin E: 0.3–0.5 mg/100g
- Potassium: 77 mg/100g
- Manganese: 0.3 mg/100g
- Fiber: 2.4 g/100g; pectin component

**PhEur standardization:** *Myrtilli fructus siccus* — ≥1.0% total anthocyanins expressed as cyanidin-3-glucoside; *Myrtilli folium* — ≥1.0% total flavonoids as hyperoside.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Retinal/visual mechanisms (unique to bilberry among Codex entries):**

1. **Rhodopsin regeneration acceleration:**
Rhodopsin (visual purple) = 11-cis-retinal + opsin; photoactivation converts 11-cis-retinal to all-trans-retinal (isomerization) → signal transduction → bleaching (pale state). Resynthesis from all-trans-retinal requires enzymatic recycling. Bilberry anthocyanins (cyanidin-3-glucoside, delphinidin-3-glucoside) interact with the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) enzyme cycle to accelerate rhodopsin resynthesis after bleaching. Result: faster dark adaptation; improved dim-light vision recovery. Mechanism confirmed in retinal tissue culture studies; animal studies; human trials.

2. **Choroidal microcirculation improvement:**
Anthocyanins selectively accumulate in the highly vascularized uveal tract (choroid, ciliary body); improve capillary permeability parameters; reduce microhemorrhage in retinal capillaries. Relevant to diabetic retinopathy and macular degeneration prevention.

3. **Antioxidant protection of photoreceptors:**
Rod and cone photoreceptors are among the most oxidatively stressed cells in the body (high polyunsaturated fatty acid content in outer segments + photon bombardment generating ROS). Bilberry anthocyanins reach retinal tissue and reduce lipid peroxidation in photoreceptor outer segments. Mechanism: singlet oxygen quenching; hydroxyl radical scavenging; transition metal chelation.

**Cardiovascular/vascular mechanisms:**

1. **Endothelial NO enhancement:**
Anthocyanins increase eNOS expression and activity → increased NO production → vasodilation; reduced endothelial inflammation (reduced ICAM-1, VCAM-1, E-selectin expression). Mechanism: Akt phosphorylation via PI3K pathway.

2. **Platelet aggregation inhibition:**
Anthocyanins reduce platelet aggregation via COX-1 inhibition (reduced TXA₂); ADP receptor antagonism; cAMP elevation in platelets. Additive with aspirin at some concentrations.

3. **LDL oxidation prevention:**
Anthocyanins intercalate into LDL particles (lipophilic interaction with the lipid bilayer) → prevent Cu²⁺-mediated LDL oxidation (primary step in atherosclerotic foam cell formation). IC50 for LDL oxidation: bilberry extract 0.1–0.5 μg/mL (very potent).

4. **Collagen cross-link stabilization:**
OPCs (proanthocyanidins) bind collagen fibrils, inhibit collagenase and elastase degradation → improved vascular wall structural integrity. Same mechanism as witch hazel (I-506) and grape seed OPCs.

**Anti-inflammatory:**
Delphinidin: potent NF-κB inhibitor (IC50 20 μM); reduces COX-2 expression; suppresses iNOS. Myricetin: 5-LOX inhibition (same mechanism as bilberry's Ericaceae cousin bearberry).

**Antidiabetic (leaf fraction):**
Chlorogenic acid (dominant in leaves): inhibits intestinal glucose-6-phosphatase → reduced postprandial glucose absorption; GLUT-2 transporter inhibition in intestinal epithelium. Relevant to traditional use of bilberry leaves for diabetes (European folk medicine predating insulin).

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Visual function / dark adaptation — Level A/B:**

1. **Jayle GE et al. 1965** (*Annals of Ocular Medicine* 198:729): n=40; bilberry extract (Tegens); improved dark adaptation threshold vs placebo; Level A (early methodology)

2. **Cluzel C et al. 1970** (*C.R. Societe de Biologie* 164:2357): bilberry anthocyanins accelerated rhodopsin regeneration in vitro and in vivo (rabbit); Level B

3. **Muth ER et al. 2000** (*Alternative Medicine Review* 5:164-173): n=18, healthy volunteers; no significant improvement in night vision, visual acuity, or contrast sensitivity at 3 weeks (180 mg anthocyanins/day); NEGATIVE; Level A — challenged the World War II claim

4. **Canter PH, Ernst E 2004** (*Survey of Ophthalmology* 49:38-50) — systematic review; 12 trials evaluated; 5/12 positive, 7/12 negative or mixed; insufficient evidence to confirm visual acuity benefits in healthy subjects; recommends more rigorous RCTs

5. **Heterogeneous literature:** Consistent evidence for improved night vision in subjects with pre-existing deficiency (darker-than-normal adaptation); inconsistent evidence in healthy controls; disease-specific evidence (retinal disorders) more promising

**Chronic venous insufficiency — Level A:**
- **Ghiringhelli C et al. 1978** — early double-blind trial; bilberry extract vs placebo; significant reduction in ankle circumference and edema; Level A (early methodology)
- **Gatta L 1988**: n=47; bilberry + Vit E vs control; improved CVI symptoms (heaviness, pruritus, edema); Level A
- Multiple similar European trials; consistent positive outcomes for CVI symptoms

**Diabetic retinopathy — Level B:**
- **Perossini M et al. 1987** — bilberry extract in diabetic retinopathy; reduced microaneurysms, hemorrhages on fundoscopy vs placebo; Level B (small, Italian registry)
- **Scharrer A, Ober M 1981** (*Klin Monbl Augenheilkd* 178:386): diabetic and hypertensive retinopathy; bilberry extract improved vascular parameters; Level B

**Blood glucose (bilberry leaf, traditional use) — Level C:**
- European folk use of bilberry leaf tea for diabetes predates insulin; historical record extensive; no modern RCTs for leaf preparations specifically
- Chlorogenic acid mechanism well-established for glucose modulation

**Cognitive function — Level B:**
- Emerging evidence for blueberry (*V. corymbosum*) anthocyanins in cognitive aging (UK Brain Banks; Rendeiro et al. 2013 *Journal of Nutrition*); bilberry-specific cognitive data limited but mechanistically consistent

**Commission E (1994):** APPROVED for:
- Fruit (*Myrtilli fructus*): nonspecific acute diarrhea; mild inflammation of oral/pharyngeal mucosa
- Leaf (*Myrtilli folium*): NOT approved (insufficient evidence; hypoglycemic risk)
**Commission E does NOT have approved monograph for bilberry extract for visual or CVI indications despite widespread European use — these indications are supported by traditional use + level B evidence, not Commission E approval.**

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**World War II pilots and the bilberry legend:**
The story that RAF pilots ate bilberry jam before night raids during the Battle of Britain (1940) — and subsequently had improved night vision — is documented in multiple sources though its precise origin is disputed. Whether the observation was systematic (pilot survey) or anecdotal (individual accounts), it triggered what became the first sustained scientific investigation of a food plant for specific visual function improvement. The pharmacological mechanism discovered (rhodopsin regeneration acceleration) provides a plausible basis for the observed effect, even if controlled trial results in healthy subjects have been mixed.

This episode illustrates a broader Codex pattern: military medicine as a source of pharmacological observation. Military settings provide large populations of relatively homogeneous age/health subjects under consistent dietary and environmental conditions — ideal for observing differential effects of dietary components. The bilberry-RAF story, the scurvy-citrus connection (Royal Navy; James Lind's trial 1747), and the pellagra-niacin connection (American Civil War prison camps) all represent military medicine generating dietary pharmacology insights.

**Scandinavian foraging tradition:**
In Scandinavia, Finland, and Russia, bilberry (*Vaccinium myrtillus*) foraging is a cultural institution embedded in the legal right of common access (*Allemansrätten* in Swedish, *Jokamiehenoikeus* in Finnish). Every citizen has the legal right to forage bilberries (and other forest products) regardless of land ownership. This democratic access to medicinal-nutritive wild plants has created cultures where bilberry consumption is routine, seasonal, and multigenerational — potentially explaining the epidemiological associations observed between Nordic diets and cardiovascular health that have been attributed partly to high polyphenol berry consumption.

**The dark flesh of the bilberry:**
Medieval European dye-makers valued bilberry juice as a purple-red textile dye (unstable; faded; never became a major commercial dye). The berries' staining power — which turns teeth, lips, and fingers deep blue-black on eating — is the visible expression of the same anthocyanin concentration that makes bilberry pharmacologically distinctive. Traditional foragers recognized high-staining intensity as a marker of good-quality berries; this folk authentication heuristic aligns with the modern analytical finding that anthocyanin concentration is the primary quality marker for pharmaceutical bilberry preparations.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Standard preparations and doses:**
- **Standardized extract (25% anthocyanins):** 80–160 mg 2–3× daily (most clinical evidence dose range); equivalent to ~80–120 g fresh berries per dose — far more than dietary consumption; oral bioavailability of anthocyanins: 0.5–2% (very low; peak plasma concentration 20–30 minutes post-ingestion)
- **Fresh berries:** 50–150 g/day; dietary; antioxidant and cardiovascular support; insufficient concentration for specific pharmacological indications
- **Dried berries:** 20–40 g/day (≈1/3 fresh weight); traditional anti-diarrheal dose; high tannin + anthocyanin; boiling the dried berries increases tannin release (astringent anti-diarrheal effect)
- **Bilberry leaf tea:** 3–5 g dried leaf per 250 mL water; traditional for diabetes; NOT Commission E approved for internal use; folk use only; monitor blood glucose if using

**Commission E approved dose (diarrhea):**
- Dried berries: 20–60 g/day (may be taken as concentrated decoction); internal use for acute diarrhea
- Topical: diluted decoction for mouth ulcers, throat inflammation

**Visual/retinal use (traditional + Level B evidence):**
- 160 mg standardized extract (25% anthocyanins) twice daily; maintain for minimum 4 weeks before assessing visual benefit; long-term use supported by absence of toxicity

**CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- Bilberry leaf (internal): hypoglycemia risk in diabetics; Commission E does not approve leaf for internal use; monitor blood glucose if used
- Anticoagulant therapy: mild antiplatelet effect (additive with aspirin/warfarin); monitor at high doses
- Pregnancy: fruit at dietary doses = safe; high-dose extract = insufficient data; avoid pharmacological doses

**Drug interactions:**
- Anticoagulants (warfarin, heparin, NOACs): mild antiplatelet/anticoagulant contribution; monitor at therapeutic extract doses
- Antidiabetic medications/insulin: bilberry leaf chlorogenic acid may enhance glucose lowering; monitor blood glucose
- Iron supplements: anthocyanin/tannin iron chelation → separate timing by 2 hours

**Comparison with cultivated blueberry (*V. corymbosum*):**
- *V. myrtillus*: higher anthocyanin concentration (300–700 vs 50–200 mg/100g); wider anthocyanin diversity (15 vs 5–8 compounds); deeper flesh color; more intense pharmacological activity per gram
- *V. corymbosum*: larger fruit; available year-round; commercially standardized; growing blueberry-specific clinical evidence base; mechanistically similar but not pharmacologically identical
- For standardized pharmaceutical preparations: specify *V. myrtillus* (bilberry); for dietary supplementation: cultivated blueberry acceptable
