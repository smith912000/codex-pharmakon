# Psimuthios — printed PSIMUTHIOS, "cerussa" (white lead), Dioscorides *De Materia Medica* 5-103; also called sacyx when washed like cadmia

## *Cerussa, white lead, the white pigment-and-medicament that Dioscorides describes as made by exposing a plate of lead to the fumes of sharp vinegar; the identification is the translator's ("SUGGESTED: Cerussa — White Lead", printed under the heading) and the text's own process (lead plate, vinegar, a white product) supports it; confidence high. ### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** The chapter carries the translator's flag "TOXIC", and Dioscorides himself ends it by saying that cerussa is "among those things which taken internally kill". White lead is a lead compound. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should take, apply or handle this substance.

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk, under this Greek name.** This is a single-text entry. The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-103, heading "PSIMUTHIOS" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 10354; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 876, "DMM, PSIMUTHIOS, 5-103 (line 10354), Cerussa — White Lead TOXIC, mineral"; list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 829; the book index gives "PSIMUTHIOS 796", line 12475, and "Cerussa 796", line 15222). The whole chapter (lines 10354 to 10362) was read. The row is a separate substance from the lead-metal row (`CPU-00892`), which covers the metal; Dioscorides treats cerussa in its own chapter, after the lead chapters 5-95 to 5-100.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The chapter gives a manufacturing recipe first and the properties last.

*Manufacture (line 10357).* "Pour the sharpest vinegar into a broad-mouthed jar or ceramic urn, and fasten a lead plate to the mouth of the jar with a little reed mat placed underneath." Covers are placed on top "so that the vinegar cannot evaporate before it is dissolved, and falls down distilled". The pure liquid on top is strained out, the "viscous stuff" is poured into a jar and dried in the sun, then ground in a hand mill or pounded and "sarced [scraped]", the pounding and scraping being repeated "three or four times". "That which is sarced [scraped] first is the best, to be used in eye medicines, and the next best follows next, and so on." A variant uses a bullrush mat fastened toward the middle of the jar so that it touches the vinegar; the mouth is corked and sealed, and after some days the cover is taken off, and "when it is all dissolved they repeat the procedures previously described". To form lozenges it is kneaded with sharp vinegar, shaped and dried in the sun. "The things mentioned here must be done in the summer for then it becomes white and effective."

*Winter making and provenance (line 10358 onward).* It "is also made in the winter, the ceramic pots set over furnaces, baths, or chimneys. The warmth is carried from above, the same as the sun." The best is "that which is made in Rhodes, Corinth or in Lacedaemonia"; next is "that which comes from Puteoli".

*Roasting and burning (line 10362).* The text has two treatments. In one, a new "Ostracean jar (especially an Athenian)" is placed over light burning coals, cerussa pounded into small pieces is sprinkled on and stirred continuously, and "when it is ashy in colour take it out, cool it, and use it". In the other, "if you want to burn it" the pounded pieces go into a hollow platter over live coals and are stirred "with a stick of ferula until it is similar in colour to sandarac [5-122]", then taken out and used.

*Washing and the name sacyx.* "If cerussa is washed the same way as cadmia [5-84] some call this sacyx" (line 10362).

*Properties (line 10362).* "Mixed with stiff ointments, plasters called lipara, and with lozenges [tablets, but not to take internally]" it is "cooling, pore-closing, softening, filling, reduces the intensity of symptoms; and furthermore gently represses abnormal growths, and forms new skins; it is among those things which taken internally kill." The bracket "tablets, but not to take internally" is the translator's gloss.

*Cross-references read.* Chapter 5-97 (lead slag, line 10316) uses cerussa as the comparison for appearance: the best lead slag "looks like cerussa [white lead ore]" (line 10316), and Eretrian earth (5-171, line 10721) "is washed like cerussa". Both are comparisons of appearance or washing method and add no property.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Toxic.**

- **What the text says it is for.** An ingredient of eye medicines (the first, finest scrapings), and of ointments, plasters and lozenges that are "cooling, pore-closing, softening, filling" and that "form new skins" and repress growths.
- **What the text says against it.** The last clause: it is among the things that, "taken internally", kill.
- **What the text does not say.** It names no disease by name for cerussa itself, apart from "eye medicines" in the manufacture paragraph, and gives no measure of how much to apply.

The Codex does not translate the properties into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend lead compounds in any form.

### Preparation & Dosing

Preparations given by the text: making (lead plate over sharp vinegar, summer or winter), sieving into grades, lozenges kneaded with vinegar, roasting in an Ostracean jar until ashy, burning on live coals until sandarac-coloured, and washing as for cadmia (line 10357 to 10362). These describe how Dioscorides's materials were prepared and are not instructions. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Lead and its compounds are cumulative toxins: ingestion, inhalation of dust or fume, or prolonged skin contact can cause lead poisoning, with damage to the nervous system, blood and kidneys, and children and pregnancy are especially vulnerable. White lead was long used as a pigment and cosmetic and has been largely withdrawn for those uses because of the harm. Dioscorides's closing warning that it kills when taken internally agrees in kind with this. The classic's effects ("cooling", "pore-closing", "filling") are the author's qualities and are not translated into modern pharmacology. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is attested here. What the chapter does show a cultivator is a discipline of attention: the grade of the scrapings (first sarced is best), the season of making (summer, for whiteness), the provenance ranking (Rhodes, Corinth, Lacedaemonia, then Puteoli), and the explicit warning that the same substance is fatal internally. That is craft knowledge about a material, with no claim about the person's vital constitution.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. The record is a manufacturing description and a warning, and the only lesson it supports is the one Dioscorides gives: know exactly what the material is and refuse it for the body.

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

The chapter's claimed sites are the eyes, the skin and its surface layers, and the pores. As an ingredient of eye medicines, the finest scrapings are placed in the eye. In ointments, plasters and lozenges, the material is said to be cooling, pore-closing, softening and filling, and to form new skins and gently repress abnormal growths, which maps to the skin surface, the pores, the wound bed and abnormal tissue overgrowth. The chapter's qualities are cooling, closing, softening and filling, and it prints no further degree of hot, cold, moist or dry, no rasa and no TCM class, so none is imported. Its named preparations concern the making and washing of the material rather than the body. No internal organ, channel or humour is named as a target of the substance, and no cultivation centre is attested.

- **TOXIC.** The chapter flags it, and white lead is a lead compound. Do not ingest it, apply it to broken skin, or make it. Dust and fume from grinding, pounding and burning are an additional exposure.
