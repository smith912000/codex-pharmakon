# Eagle stone (aetites) — Aetites lithos (AETITES LITHOS), "Eagle Stone"
## *A hollow stone nodule that rattles when shaken, worn tied to the arm and thigh in pregnancy and childbirth, used as a test for a thief and pounded into a warming ointment for epilepsy; the translator's heading reads "Eagle Stone — Iron Oxide", with the note "hollow nodule of argilaceous Iron Oxide with a loose nucleus"* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The eagle stone is chapter 161 of Book Five of the *De Materia Medica*, in the section "METALLIC STONES", headed AETITES LITHOS (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10667-10670; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` carries it as 5-161, line 10667, with the translator's identification "Eagle Stone — Iron Oxide hollow nodule of argilaceous Iron Oxide with a loose nucleus", class mineral; `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 887 has "Aetites Liihos", mineral, identification marked uncertain; the book's page index prints "AETITES LITHOS 823" and "Eagle Stone 823"). The Greek name *aetites* is printed beside the English "Eagle Stone"; the text does not itself say why the stone is named after the eagle. The whole chapter is five sentences (line 10670). It says:

*A sign of the stone.* "Aetites stone when shaken sends out a sound as if it were pregnant with another stone." The stone is therefore recognised by its sound: a stone with something loose inside it.

*In pregnancy.* "It prevents miscarriage when the wombs are slippery, tied around the left arm."

*In childbirth.* "At the time of delivery [in childbirth] take it from the arm and tie it around the thigh and she shall bring forth without pain."

*As a test for a thief.* "It exposes a thief if it is put into the bread offered him, for he that stole is not able to swallow down the things chewed. They say that aetites boiled together with meat will betray a thief, for he who stole shall not be able to swallow that which was boiled with it." The first sentence is stated flatly; the second, about boiling the stone with meat, is introduced with "They say".

*For epilepsy.* "Pounded into small pieces and used in a wax ointment made of cyprinum [1-65], gleucinum [1-67] or any of those things that warm, it helps epilepsy considerably." The two oils named are cross-referenced by chapter number in the translation; they are other substances, not read further here.

*Its neighbours.* The two chapters on either side of it are also stones worn on the body. Jasper (5-160, lines 10664-10666): "All are said to be amulets used as personal ornaments, and to speed delivery [in childbirth] tied to the thigh." Ophites (5-162, lines 10671-10674; translator's gloss "Serpentine"): "Any of them (tied about the body) is good for snakebites and headaches." In all three the stone is worn on the body; the eagle stone alone is also put in food and powdered into an ointment. The text does not say that tying a stone on is Dioscorides's own recommendation as against a custom he records.

The chapter gives no place of origin, no colour, no grade or ranking, no method of preparation beyond pounding, and no dose.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent beyond the translator's gloss. In general terms, the object described is a natural concretion of iron oxides or iron oxyhydroxides bound with clay minerals, hollow, with a smaller loose piece inside that rattles when shaken; such nodules form in sedimentary rock, and the rattling core is a feature of how they form. Iron oxides are the rust-coloured pigments of ochre and haematite. Concretions of this kind may carry other metals as impurities. The identification of the Greek stone is the translator's opinion, and the chapter does not state a composition. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records a stone worn as an amulet in pregnancy and labour, offered to a suspect as a test, and powdered in a warm ointment for epilepsy. The Codex does not recommend any of these. No stone prevents miscarriage or eases childbirth, a "swallowing test" for a thief is an ordeal that accuses an innocent person as readily as a guilty one, and epilepsy is a serious illness that needs clinical care. The entry exists to record what the text says and how its author words it.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives one preparation: the stone "pounded into small pieces and used in a wax ointment made of cyprinum [1-65], gleucinum [1-67] or any of those things that warm" (line 10670). No proportion of stone to ointment, no frequency, no quantity and no dose is given for that ointment, and no dose or weight is given for the bread or meat tests. The amulet use needs no dose: the stone is tied on the left arm and then moved to the thigh. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Iron oxide concretions are common in sedimentary rocks; their rattling hollow nodules are a known natural form. Iron oxides are used as pigments (ochre, haematite, red oxide). There is no evidence that carrying a stone affects miscarriage or labour, or that a stone in food reveals a thief; a "swallowing test" can work only through the fear of the person tested. Epilepsy is treated by medicines for which there is clinical evidence; no evidence supports a stone ointment. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** The text prints no taste, no hot or cold, dry or moist rating and no degree for the eagle stone. Its only quality-like entry is that the wax ointment in which it is used is made with oils "that warm" (line 10670); that describes the oils, not the stone. No energetic classification is given.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is asserted here. The chapter records an amuletic stone, as he does elsewhere for jasper and ophites, but it makes no claim about the person's constitution, vitality or long life. What the chapter offers a practitioner is a way of recognising the stone (it sounds as if "pregnant with another stone" when shaken), and a placing of it among things worn on the body. Recording an amuletic use is not the same as endorsing it: the one place where the wording shifts is "They say" at the meat test.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The eagle stone is a rattling nodule that Dioscorides records as an amulet for childbirth, a test of a thief and an ointment for epilepsy. The Codex does not recommend any of those uses. What the chapter teaches is how a hollow stone is recognised, and how a physician reports what is said of a stone without vouching for all of it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy and labour:** a stone tied to the arm or thigh does not prevent miscarriage or ease delivery; bleeding, pain or slow labour needs a midwife or doctor, not an amulet.
- **Epilepsy:** do not use a stone ointment in place of treatment; seizures need clinical assessment and do not respond to such a preparation.
- **Thief test:** do not use food or a stone to test anyone; it is an ordeal, it harms innocent people, and swallowing a stone is a choking and injury risk.
- **Ingestion and ointments:** a pounded stone is a rough mineral dust; do not swallow it or rub it into broken skin, which risks infection and metal contamination.
- **Identification:** "Eagle Stone — Iron Oxide" is the translator's gloss; the text identifies the stone by its sound.
