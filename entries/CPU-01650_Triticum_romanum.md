# Triticum romanum — Olura, olyra (Dioscorides 2-113)

## *Triticum romanum [Bedevian], the third name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 2-113, which also prints "Olyra" and "Spelta"; no English gloss is printed on that line* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk lists the plant under this name (the name indexes in `Sources/*/extract/names.csv` and the list files in `_sources/` were searched; the wheat entries of other traditions are under other names and are listed in the Overlap paragraph below). The entry rests on one very short chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading and credited there to "[Bedevian]", and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *2-113. OLURA* at line 5459, SUGGESTED line at 5461 ("Olyra, Spelta, Triticum romanum [Bedevian]"), and the text at line 5462. The chapter stands in Book Two, in the section "Frumentacea: Cereals" (from page 229; line 31), after *2-112. KRIMNON* (line 5454) and before *2-114. ATHERA* (line 5463). The page number 232 follows the next chapters (line 5473). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 303, and the translators' index prints "Triticum romanum 232" (line 17940).

The whole chapter reads: "Olyra [grain] is very similar to zea but somewhat less nourishing. It is also made into bread and crimnum [porridge] is similarly made of it."

That is all the chapter says, and it is shorter than any chapter in the batch. It has no description of the plant, no habitat and no sensory note. It defines the grain by comparison with another grain (*zea*, which is "very similar", and "somewhat less nourishing"). It records two uses: it is made into bread, and a porridge (*crimnum*) is "similarly made of it". It records no medical use.

**The chapters the comparison depends on (labelled cross-reference, brief).** The chapter cannot be understood without its neighbours, and the Codex reads them only to place this one. 2-111 *ZEIA* (lines 5446 to 5449) says there are "two kinds of zea" and that it "is more nourishing than barley, pleasant-tasting to the mouth, but made into bread it is less nourishing than wheat". 2-112 *KRIMNON* (lines 5454 to 5458) says that *crimnum* is "the coarsest meal that is made from zea and wheat", "abundantly nourishing and easy to digest", and that "that from zea is more astringent to the bowels". 2-114 *ATHERA* (lines 5463 to 5465) says it is made from zea ground very small, a "spoonmeal" suitable for children, "good in plasters". Those are the claims of other chapters and are not attributed to olyra here. The translators print *Triticum zea*, *Triticum spelta* and *Triticum dicoccum* on those chapters' lines, and the Codex does not place olyra among them.

**Overlap.** A chapter-level search of the entries folder for 2-113, the Greek heading, *Olyra*, *Triticum romanum* and *Spelta* found no entry on this chapter. The neighbouring cereal chapters 2-112 *KRIMNON* and 2-114 *ATHERA* have no entry of their own either; I_897 (*Melampyrum pratense*, cow wheat) names them in its line 12 among the cereal chapters. I_243 and I_476 (both *Triticum aestivum*, tagged EP, AT, DMM, CM, SS and SBJ) are entries on bread wheat; they speak of Dioscorides' wheat in general terms (I_243 line 19, I_476 line 17) and cite no chapter number. I_918 (emmer wheat, *Triticum dicoccum*) is tagged EP only. These entries share a genus with this one and the Codex does not read them as the same plant. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. It is a general fact that *Triticum* is a genus of cereal grasses (Poaceae) and that the grain of wheats is rich in starch and contains the protein gluten. The chapter's "nourishing" is a judgement of food value and not a statement of composition. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the translators' "Triticum romanum". [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a grain for bread and porridge and nothing more. It records no remedy and no medical claim, and the Codex draws no protocol from it. Wheat grains contain gluten, and people with coeliac disease or wheat allergy cannot take them; that is a general fact and not a claim of the chapter.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives two preparations by name: bread, and the porridge *crimnum* made "similarly" to the one made from zea. It gives no recipe, no quantity and no method. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that wheats (including the hulled wheats such as emmer and spelt) are staple foods rich in carbohydrate, and that gluten-containing grains are unsafe in coeliac disease. This is a statement about the cereals in general and is not a verification of the chapter's "olyra".

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 2-113 prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality and no taste. Its only graded statement is that the grain is "somewhat less nourishing" than zea.

### Contraindications

Chapter 2-113 prints no warning. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no grain should be bought or eaten on the strength of this entry. Wheat-family grains contain gluten and are unsafe in coeliac disease and wheat allergy. The chapter gives no dose and no safety information for pregnancy or children. [USER: verify]
