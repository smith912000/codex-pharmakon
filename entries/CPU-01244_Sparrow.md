# Sparrow (Passer montanus) — rGyud-bzhi, "Sparrow: Passer montanus" (mchhil.pa)
## *The sparrow, as a meat, and a bird of the Tantra's meat classification; Clark prints "mchhil.pa: Sparrow: Passer montanus" and a second bird "nas.zan bird (lit. 'barley feeder'): Asian sparrow"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The sparrow is entered in the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, the chapter on the powers of medicines, in Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). It falls in the section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), under the heading "Meat". The entry, at line 1170, reads: "mchhil.pa: Sparrow: Passer montanus: [has seven names]. nas.zan bird (lit. 'barley feeder'): Asian sparrow: [has ten names]. rtsangs.pa: a type of Gecko: Agama himalayana sacra: [has eleven names, for example sder.chhags. The flesh of] these three creatures increases semen, [is aphrodisiac, tonic and anodyne, and drains pus.]" So the Tantra here treats the sparrow, the "barley feeder" bird and a lizard (Clark's "gecko") together, and the one action it states for the three is that the flesh increases semen; the bracketed additions (aphrodisiac, tonic, anodyne, drains pus) are Clark's interpolation from the commentary he describes in his introduction (line 80, "interpolated liberally from the Blue Aquamarine commentary"), though the lines read do not state the bracket convention outright. [USER: verify]

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3536) puts the same grouping more briefly: "The meat of sparrows and mountain-dwelling lizards increase semen." It names the sparrows and the lizards and does not name the "barley feeder" bird. The Tibetan wording is not in the files read, so no closer match is claimed.

The sparrow also appears in the meat classification of the chapter on food. Clark's group (b) of the nine kinds of meat is of "creatures which excavate the earth with their beaks; these include parrot, cuckoo (Cuculus canorus), stockdove, magpie, Pomatorhinus swinhoei and sparrow" (line 672); Men-Tsee-Khang has "The parrot, cuckoo, pigeon, magpie, Tibetan lark and sparrow are the group of birds which use their beak to dig for food" (line 2905). Clark then says that meats in general are "sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste" (line 673). A separate sentence on the flesh of particular animals follows: Clark (line 678), "Flesh of chicken and sparrow increases semen and is beneficial for wounds"; Men-Tsee-Khang (line 2931), "Chicken meat and meat from a sparrow increase semen production and heals wounds." This is the one place where the Tantra gives the sparrow a use beyond semen: it is beneficial for wounds.

Men-Tsee-Khang's glossary of birds lists two sparrows with scientific names: "Passer moutanus S." (line 6887, spelling as printed, for *Passer montanus*, under the English "Sparrow" at line 6862) and "Passer domesticus Hodgon" (under a second "Sparrow" at line 6918). The glossary is an OCR of columns and the names are paired with the English by position, not by a line that links them. [USER: verify] Of the two, Clark's binomial, *Passer montanus*, agrees with the first. The Tibetan name is not paired with either glossary entry (project `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, rows 401 and 403).

### Active Components

Sparrow meat is muscle: mostly water and protein, with little fat, and the bird is small. Nothing in the lines read names an active principle, and none specific to this entry is claimed.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is two actions of the flesh: it increases semen, and it is beneficial for wounds. The Codex gives no protocol. Wild small birds are protected in many countries, the meat has no recognised effect on semen or on wounds, and the Tantra gives no preparation or dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation, route or dose is given in the sources retrieved: Clark and Men-Tsee-Khang state the action only. Neither says whether the flesh is eaten fresh, cooked or in a compound. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Wild-bird meat can carry bacteria such as *Salmonella* and parasites, and it must be cooked thoroughly. Sparrow meat has no recognised effect on semen production or on wound healing; wounds heal by the body's own repair and by cleaning, and a wound that is deep, dirty or slow needs care from a clinician. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The sparrow is among the meats of the Tantra's nine groups; the general statement is that all such meats are "sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste" (Clark line 673). Clark then gives qualities for dry-land, aquatic and amphibian meats and for birds of prey (cool, light and coarse; oily, heavy and warm; both; coarse, light and sharp). The lines read do not say which of those habitat classes the sparrow belongs to, and none is applied to it. For the sparrow itself the text prints an action but no taste, potency or humoral action beyond the general sweet taste. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* Neither translation read gives the sparrow any cultivation framing. "Increases semen" is a therapeutic action of the flesh, stated in the animal-origin chapter and the food chapter, and it is not a status. The frame around it is the tradition's own: the project's cultivation spec records that the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) are derived from the three poisons and that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root. That point comes from the spec and not from any line about the sparrow. No source read attaches the sparrow to *bcud len* (essence extraction), and there is no formal Tibetan category of substances for subtle-body practice. Clark's remark (line 57) that "only very few animal products are used" nowadays is a note on usage, not on status.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a list entry for wild-bird meat, with no preparation and no dose; the action named for it (more semen) is not a cultivation claim, and no benefit is supported. The honest use of the entry is as a record of what the Tantra says.

### Contraindications

- **Infection and parasites:** wild-bird meat can carry bacteria and parasites; it must be cooked through.
- **Wounds:** deep, dirty or non-healing wounds need clinical care; do not rely on this line.
- **Legal and conservation status:** small wild birds are protected in many countries and their capture and sale are controlled; the species named rests on the translators' binomials. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** killing wild birds for a substance with no supported benefit is hard to justify, and Clark's omens section (line 431) values saving animals from slaughter; that passage is about merit and omens, not about medicine.
