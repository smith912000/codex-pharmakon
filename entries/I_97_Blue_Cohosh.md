## Section 1 — Nomenclature

### Primary Scientific Name
*Caulophyllum thalictroides* (L.) Michx. — Blue Cohosh (Berberidaceae)

### Nomenclature Table

| Tradition | Language | Name | Transliteration | Meaning |
|-----------|----------|------|-----------------|---------|
| Greek (Botanical) | Ἑλληνική | — | *Caulophyllum* | From *caulos* (stem) + *phyllon* (leaf) — "stem-leaved plant"; the leaf appears to emerge from the stem |
| Abenaki (BM) | — | — | Kohosh | Algonquian root meaning "knobby" — refers to the rhizome's appearance; gives the common name "cohosh" (shared with black cohosh *Actaea racemosa* and red cohosh — all knobbly rhizomes) |
| Algonquian (BM) | — | — | Squaw Root / Papoose Root | Colonial-era names encoding the gynaecological application; "squaw" is considered offensive in modern usage |
| English | — | Blue Cohosh / Blue Ginseng / Yellow Ginseng | — | "Blue" from the bluish waxy coating on the leaves and berries; "Ginseng" — the appearance of the root clusters |

### Botanical Identity
Perennial woodland herb 30–90cm; single large compound leaf (3–9 leaflets); inconspicuous yellow-green flowers (March–April, before leaf emergence); distinctive blue-black berry clusters; bluish-green waxy appearance; grows in moist rich woodland, especially in deciduous forest understory; native to eastern North America. The root and rhizome are the drug material. Named alongside black cohosh (*Actaea racemosa*, I_68), though pharmacologically distinct. Blue cohosh was one of the primary North American indigenous uterine medicines.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 North American Indigenous / Eclectic Medicine (BM)

Widespread indigenous use across eastern North America:
- **Cherokee, Menominee, Ojibwe, Potawatomi, Meskwaki**: The root tea used for uterine spasm (antispasmodic), to promote labour contractions (oxytocic/partus preparation), and to regulate menstruation
- **Cherokee**: Specifically used as a "squaw tea" (now offensive — traditional women's medicine designation) for labour induction and dysmenorrhoea
- **American Eclectic physicians**: 19th century extensive use for uterine inertia (delayed/weak labour), dysmenorrhoea, and threatened miscarriage — both antispasmodic and oxytocic applications documented, apparently contradictory but dose-dependent

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Oxytocic / Uterine Stimulant (Primary)

**Caulosaponin + methylcytisine**: 
- **Caulosaponin**: Directly stimulates uterine smooth muscle contraction — a saponin-mediated mechanism independent of oxytocin receptors; produces dose-dependent contractions in vitro and in vivo
- **Methylcytisine**: Nicotinic receptor agonist → ganglionic stimulation → indirect uterotonic effect; also causes vasoconstriction (the mechanism behind adverse cardiac events)

The oxytocic mechanism explains both the labour-induction traditional use AND the absolute pregnancy contraindication before term.

### 5.2 Antispasmodic (Paradox Resolved by Dose)

**Magnoflorine**: Smooth muscle relaxant at low doses (calcium channel antagonism) → antispasmodic for uterine spasm/dysmenorrhoea.

The apparent paradox of blue cohosh being both antispasmodic (for dysmenorrhoea) and oxytocic (for labour induction) is dose-dependent:
- Low doses: antispasmodic (magnoflorine-dominant) → dysmenorrhoea
- Higher doses: oxytocic (caulosaponin + methylcytisine-dominant) → labour stimulation

This biphasic dose-response is the mechanistic explanation for the Eclectic physicians' apparently contradictory applications.

### 5.3 Anti-Rheumatic (Berberine + Taspine)

**Berberine + taspine**: Anti-inflammatory through AMPK and NF-κB pathways. The anti-rheumatic application is pharmacologically coherent.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Labour induction**: Used by midwives and herbalists in North America as a labour-preparation herb ("partus preparator") taken in the final weeks of pregnancy. **Safety concern**: Multiple case reports of neonatal adverse events (cardiac ischaemia, stroke, myocardial infarction in neonates) following maternal blue cohosh use for labour induction. The oxytocic + vasoconstrictive mechanism (caulosaponin + methylcytisine) can cause uteroplacental insufficiency and fetal distress. **This is a clinically significant safety concern supported by case report evidence** — not a theoretical risk.

**Dysmenorrhoea**: No RCT data. Traditional evidence from Eclectic physicians for low-dose antispasmodic use.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The named-after-women plant**: "Squaw Root" and "Papoose Root" (colonial-era names, now considered offensive) encoded the most critical piece of traditional knowledge: this is the plant used in childbirth. The indigenous naming preserved the application — women's medicine for labour. The names crossed into the Eclectic physician literature, were eventually recognised as offensive and replaced with the botanical "blue cohosh," but the application remained. The pharmacological knowledge — transferred from indigenous midwives to Eclectic physicians to modern herbal practitioners — followed a chain of women-centred transmission across 200+ years. The plant's pharmacology is coherent with the traditional application; the safety concern (neonatal cardiac events) was not detectable at the population level until case report surveillance identified the pattern in the 1990s–2000s.

**The cohosh confusion**: Blue cohosh (*Caulophyllum thalictroides*) and black cohosh (*Actaea racemosa*, I_68) share a common name element ("cohosh" — Algonquian for knobby root) but are pharmacologically completely different:
- Black cohosh: serotonergic; anti-hot-flush; NOT oxytocic; safe in ER+ breast cancer
- Blue cohosh: oxytocic; uterine stimulant; significant neonatal safety concern

Products labelled simply "cohosh" may contain either or both. This is one of the most clinically significant nomenclature confusions in herbal medicine — where mixing them up has documented adverse consequences (neonatal cardiac events from inadvertent blue cohosh in labour).

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM (cross-reference)**: Bitter, acrid; warm. Channels: Liver (肝), Uterus (胞宮). Moves Blood, stimulates uterine contractions, disperses Cold stagnation.

**Ayurvedic (cross-reference)**: Bitter, pungent; warming. Reduces Kapha ↓↓. *Artavajanana* (emmenagogue/oxytocic). Not a classical Ayurvedic plant.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Pregnancy (except managed labour induction under skilled supervision)**: ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION before term — neonatal myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiac ischaemia case reports. This is not a theoretical risk. Do not use in self-medication during pregnancy
- **Cardiovascular disease**: Methylcytisine vasoconstriction — contraindicated in hypertension, coronary artery disease, Raynaud's
- **Nicotinic medication interactions**: Methylcytisine nicotinic agonism — potential interactions with nicotinic receptor-active medications
- **Anagyrine (teratogenic alkaloid)**: Teratogenicity confirmed in livestock; human relevance uncertain but precautionary avoidance in pregnancy reinforced by this mechanism
- **Confusion with black cohosh**: Ensure correct species identification — the safety profiles are opposite
