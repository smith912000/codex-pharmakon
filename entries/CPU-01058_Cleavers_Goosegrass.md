*3-104 APARINE* (heading at line 7475; text at line 7477). The translator's identification is "Aparine vulgaris [Bauhin], Galium aparine — Catch Grass, Cleavers, Goosegrass, Sticky Willy". The text reads: "Aparine has many little square rough branches. The leaves are at distances lying about in a circle (like those of rubia [dyer's madder]). The flowers are white; the seed hard, white, round, somewhat hollow in the middle (like a navel). The herb sticks to cloths, and the shepherds use it instead of a strainer for milk, for taking out hairs with it. The seed, stalks and leaves are juiced (taken as a drink with wine) to help those bitten by harvest spiders and snakes. The juice dropped in ears cures earache. The herb (taken in pounded swines' grease) dissolves scrofulous tumours [glandular swelling] and goitres. It is also called ampelocarpum, omphelocarpum, philanthropum, and ixos." The text file's indexes list it at page 488 (lines 12014, 14740, 15188 and 15811).

*4-96 GALLION* (heading at line 8893; text at line 8895), the neighbouring plant of the same genus by the translator's gloss, "Galium verum [Linnaeus] — Ladies' Bedstraw, Rennet". The text says that it is "called this because coagulates milk instead of rennet", that its leaves are "very similar to aparina yet straight", and that the flower "is smeared on for burns from fire, and it stops flows of blood". It is a different chapter and a different species, and it is reported only because the text compares it with *aparine*. It is not attributed to this row.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records the juice with wine for bites by harvest spiders and snakes, the juice in the ear for earache, and the herb pounded in swines' grease for scrofulous tumours and goitres. Clark records a medicine for jaundice and bile disorders. These are the texts' own claims and not a Codex protocol. The Codex gives no protocol for this plant.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. The chapter is a discipline of attention in the sense of the cultivation spec: it tells how to know the plant (many small rough square branches, leaves in rings like madder, white flowers, a hard round seed hollowed like a navel, a herb that sticks to cloth), a use made of it by shepherds (a milk strainer), and a list of names, and it relates the plant to madder by the leaves. It records uses for bites, the ear and swellings and assigns the plant no status.

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra classes the plant by what it cures (jaundice and bile disorders), with a botanical description (a square petiole, a seed that adheres to clothing, red pods with black seeds). The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice and not reaching the root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. Nothing read assigns the plant a place in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. The Tibetan name is shared with *Artemisia scoparia* in Clark's entry. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the sources read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant beyond the attention the Dioscorides chapter asks for: learn to know it by its square stem, its leaf rings and its clinging seed before using the name. The Tibetan entry says what it cures and nothing more. No long-term regimen is attested in either text.

### Contraindications

No safety data were read. Cleavers is a common weed and the plant is generally regarded as low in toxicity, but handling it can irritate the skin of some people through its hooked hairs. None of the traditional uses above is advice, and the snake-bite and spider-bite uses in Dioscorides are not a treatment: seek urgent medical care for any bite. Seek a qualified practitioner, and seek ordinary medical care for jaundice, which is a sign of liver or bile disease.
