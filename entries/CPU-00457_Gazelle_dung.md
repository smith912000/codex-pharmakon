# Gazelle dung — "Gazelle's dung" (Bryan's English rendering of the Ebers Papyrus)
## *The droppings of the animal Bryan calls "Gazelle"; Bryan prints no Egyptian word and no binomial, so the spine row's "Gazella sp." is the list-maker's gloss* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian Tradition (EP).** The locus is Bryan's 1930 English *The Papyrus Ebers*, Chapter VII "Organic Remedies (animals)". "Gazelle" stands in Bryan's list of the animals "whose identity is not in doubt" (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1398, between "Stag" and "Ram"; name-index row `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` line 111, class animal, "no Egyptian word or binomial given in this list"). The parts-of-animals list (lines 1405-1415) includes "Excrement" but ties it to no animal. The spine row reads "Gazelle dung, Gazella sp." with the note "Ebers; Wikipedia/Ebers Papyrus" (`_sources/ebers.csv` line 78). Wikipedia was not used.

**Where the dung is prescribed.** The Bryan text on disk was searched for "gazelle" and "dung". The dung occurs in two places.

- **A hair preparation, lines 4030-4031**, in Chapter XXII "The Hair" (heading line 3993). Bryan writes that "other preservatives and restoratives are frankly and positively revolting, such as a concoction of Writing-fluid, Fat-of-the-Hippopotamus, and Gazelle's dung; and another which is a vile concoction of Vulva, Phallus, and the Black Lizard". The first concoction follows the six-fat baldness pomade (lines 4023-4029). Bryan's sentence does not say which hair complaint it is for; the sentence that follows speaks of remedies "which aim at once at preserving the hair and removing grey hairs". The summary prints no quantity. [USER: verify]
- **A granary device, lines 4236-4240**, in Chapter XXIV "Domestic Hints" (heading line 4158): "TO PREVENT THE RODENTS EATING DURRA IN THE GRANARY. Gazelle's dung Put on the fire in the Granary. Cover the wall and the floor with Mice-excrement and with their urine. That will prevent the durra being eaten." The wry comment after it ("It would certainly prevent it being eaten by a good many of us") is Bryan's. This is a household pest measure, not a medicine; the dung is burnt in the granary.

**Related items, not counted as the dung.** "Gazelle's Ears" occurs in a remedy to dry wounds in the chapter on the ear, nose and mouth, line 3066 ("Head-of-the-amamu-animal Gazelle's Ears Tortoise-shell annek-plant"). The dung of the "Antilope" is a separate item in an eye remedy (line 2735; see the Antelope dung entry), and "Goat's dung" and "Cat's dung" are separate items. Bryan does not say that any of these dungs is the same as another.

**What is not said.** The sources retrieved do not say how the dung was collected or prepared, how much was used, or why it was chosen. The Ebers material is indication-organised, so the identity of any named substance is a modern hypothesis. The Ghalioungui 1987 glossary was not opened and has no gazelle row in `_sources/ebers.csv`.

### Active Components

None can be stated for the material as the papyrus names it. In general terms, herbivore dung is mostly undigested plant fibre with bacteria, and it can carry intestinal parasites and pathogens. Nothing in the sources retrieved says that gazelle dung contains anything that acts on hair or on rodents; burning dung produces smoke, and the papyrus gives no reason for the device. This is a general statement, not a reading of Bryan.

---

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved show gazelle dung in a hair concoction with writing-fluid and hippopotamus fat, and burnt in a granary against rodents. No quantity is legible in either. Dung is a hygiene risk when applied to the scalp, and the gazelle is a wild animal in many range states. No modern protocol is supplied and none is implied.

### Preparation & Dosing

Only what Bryan reports: dung combined with writing-fluid and hippopotamus fat for the hair (line 4030); dung "put on the fire in the Granary" (line 4239). No quantity is printed for either. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's vital constitution, and the two uses found are a cosmetic for the hair and a household pest measure. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*). The one spoken text in the hair chapter, "TO CHARM AWAY ALOPECIA O Shining One, Thou who hoverest above! [...]" (lines 4069-4072), is attached to a different mixture (iron, red-lead, onions, alabaster, honey) and not to the dung. Bryan prints no recitation with either use of the dung, though he summarises, and that is no proof the papyrus gives none. The person as plural (*ka*, *ba*, *akh*, *ib*) is not engaged by this entry. No deity is attributed.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. The record is a dung in a hair mixture and a dung burnt in a granary, with no quantity and no cultivation role. A cultivator has nothing to do with it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** animal dung on the scalp or on broken skin carries infection risk.
- **Toxic co-ingredients:** writing-fluid (ink) is among the ingredients named with it in the hair passage, and its composition is not given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Smoke:** burning material in a closed store produces smoke; this should not be reproduced.
- **Conservation:** gazelle species are protected in parts of their range; the animal is not identified in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
