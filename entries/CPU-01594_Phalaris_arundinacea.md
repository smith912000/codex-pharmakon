# Phalaris arundinacea — Phalaris, canary grass (Dioscorides 3-159)

## *Phalaris arundinacea, the first of two binomials on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-159 (which also prints Phalaris canariensis — "Canary Grass, Phalaris"); a knotty, reed-like grass with white millet-like seed* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter, and no other tradition on disk is tagged for it. The binomials are the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. Two species are printed (a reed canary grass and the cultivated canary grass of bird-seed) and the row takes the first; the text does not say which, if either, is meant. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-159. PHALARIS*, heading at line 7888, the SUGGESTED line at line 7889 and the text at line 7890, in Book Three among the roots (the running head near it reads "ROOTS of AKANTHODA or PRICKLY PLANTS", on the translators' page 531). The whole chapter reads:

"Phalaris sends out many little stalks from slender useless roots — the breadth of two hands, knotty, similar to reeds, resembling those of zea [Triticum zea], yet more slender and sweet in taste. The leaves are similar to those of zea. The white seed is abundant like millet, and somewhat long. The herb (cut and juiced with water or wine and so taken as a drink) is able to be effective for disorders of the bladder and sperm; a spoonful of a decoction (taken as a drink with water) is good for the same purposes."

The description is mostly of the plant's form: many small stalks "the breadth of two hands" (a height, as printed), knotty like reeds but more slender than those of *zea*, leaves like *zea*, and abundant, white, "somewhat long" seed "like millet". The bracket "[Triticum zea]" is the translators' identification of *zea* (a cereal), not Dioscorides'. The roots are "slender" and "useless", and the stalks are "sweet in taste", which is the only taste printed. The uses are a single sentence in two parts: the herb, cut and juiced with water or wine, taken as a drink for "disorders of the bladder and sperm", and "a spoonful of a decoction" taken with water for the same. The phrase "sperm" is the translators' English. The chapter gives no other name or synonym list, no deity and no amuletic use; it does not mention the seed as food or as a drug, only as part of the description.

**The chapters around it.** 3-158 *LITHOSPERMON* (line 7884) comes before it and 3-160 *ERUTHRODANON* (line 7892, dyer's madder in the translators' suggestion) follows; both are separate chapters and the Codex does not carry their statements over to 3-159.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter id 3-159 and for the names *phalaris* and "canary grass" found no existing entry on the same chapter or the same names. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The composition of the grass Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about a particular species. As a general fact, *Phalaris* is a genus of grasses (Poaceae); the seeds of canary grass are widely used as bird feed, and some *Phalaris* species are known to contain indole alkaloids such as gramine, which affect grazing livestock. No constituent is named in the chapter. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records one use: the juice or a decoction of the grass drunk for disorders of the bladder and of the seed. The Codex gives no protocol for it. Urinary and reproductive complaints need medical assessment, and grasses cannot be told apart by a short description.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives two preparations, with locus (line 7890): the herb "cut and juiced with water or wine and so taken as a drink", and "a spoonful of a decoction (taken as a drink with water)". The spoonful is the only measure printed, and it refers to the decoction; no quantity of juice, no frequency and no duration is stated. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that canary grass seed is a common bird feed and that no grass juice is a treatment for bladder or reproductive disease. The identification is a translators' suggestion between two species, so the chapter does not show that the plant it describes is *Phalaris arundinacea*.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one taste: the stalks are "sweet in taste". It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality for 3-159.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter is a description of form and a single therapeutic sentence. Its sensory observation is the sweetness of the stalks; it names no provenance, no season of gathering, no assay for quality and no adulteration. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a medicinal herb, and the text records no amuletic use. No cultivation framing is attested.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The abstentive answer is the honest one: a grass described by its stalks and seed, and drunk for two complaints.

### Contraindications

Bladder and reproductive complaints need medical care and not a grass juice. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion between two species, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry; grasses of this genus may carry alkaloids that are harmful to animals. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
