# Euphorbia chamaesyce — Chamaisuke (Dioscorides 4-170)

## *Euphorbia chamaesyce, the first of two binomials on the translators' SUGGESTED line ("Euphorbia chamaesyce, Euphorbia massilensis — Crenated Annual Spurge")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed under the chapter heading, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line prints two binomials, *Euphorbia chamaesyce* and *Euphorbia massilensis*, with the English name "Crenated Annual Spurge", and a cross-reference line, "see 3-96". The row takes the first binomial. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-170. CHAMAISUKE* at line 9583, SUGGESTED line at 9584, cross-reference at 9585, text from 9586 to 9590 (a page number and a section heading are printed inside it). The chapter stands in Book Four, "Other Herbs & Roots", after 4-169 *PEPLION* and before 4-171 *SKAMMONIA* (scammony). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 748.

The whole chapter reads: "Chamaesyce sends out branches four fingers long, lying upon the ground, round in a circle, full of juice, with leaves like lens [lentils], similar to peplus [above], little, thin, close to the earth. The seed is under the leaves, round, like peplus. It has neither flower nor stalk, and the root is thin and useless. The branches (pounded into small pieces) with wine are able to lessen pains about the womb. Applied as a pessary and smeared on they take away oedema, hanging warts, and myrmecias [wart resembling an anthill]. Eaten boiled, they loosen the bowels, and the juice does the same things. Furthermore, smeared on, it helps a scorpion strike. It is good rubbed on with honey for dullness of sight, darkening of the vision, new liquid discharges from the eyes, scars, and specks or small clouds in the eye. It grows in dry rocky places. Some call it syce, or papaver spumeum."

So the chapter has a description of a low plant lying flat on the ground in a circle, with small lentil-like leaves and round seed beneath them, no flower and no stalk, and a thin useless root. It has a list of uses: womb pain (drunk in wine), swellings and warts (as a pessary and an external application), loosening the bowels (the boiled branches eaten, or the juice), scorpion sting, and a group of eye complaints (rubbed on with honey). It also has a habitat, dry rocky places, and two alternative names.

*The chapters it points to.* The cross-reference "see 3-96" leads to *EUPHORBION*, the resin chapter (line 7412), which is a different substance and is read in the entry for *Euphorbia amygdaloides* (I_1083). The comparison "similar to peplus [above]" points to 4-168 *PEPLOS* (line 9576), whose SUGGESTED line gives *Euphorbia peplus* and which prints the same two alternative names, *syce* and *papaver spumeum*. The two chapters therefore share both names and several features (a small spreading plant full of white juice, with round seed under the leaves). They are separate chapters, with separate descriptions and uses, and the entry does not merge them. The tithymal chapter (4-165, heading line 9515) gives the larger group of spurges. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

No analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. It is well established that members of the genus *Euphorbia* have a milky latex that is irritant to skin and eyes. The chapter calls the branches "full of juice" and does not call the juice milky or white (as it does for peplus and the tithymals). Nothing further is stated here about the composition of this plant. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records an inner use for womb pain and loosening the bowels, an external use for warts, swellings and a scorpion sting, and a use on the eyes. The Codex gives no protocol. A spurge juice applied to the eyes or to the womb as a pessary is a hazard, since spurge latex is an irritant, and nothing here supports any of these uses.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations. The branches are pounded small and taken "with wine". They are "applied as a pessary and smeared on". They are "eaten boiled", and the juice does the same. For the eyes the preparation is "rubbed on with honey". No quantity, frequency or duration is given for any use. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality and no taste. The one quality printed is that the branches loosen the bowels when eaten boiled.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a plain description for recognition: a plant that lies on the ground in a circle, with branches four fingers long, small leaves, seed beneath them, no flower or stalk, and a root of no use, found in dry rocky places. The chapter's comparison with peplus shows the habit of placing a plant against a neighbour. It is not a cultivation status, and the identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is a modest one, the habit of learning a plant by its habit of growth and by what it lacks (no flower, no stalk, a useless root). The text offers nothing more.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Spurge juice is an irritant of skin, eyes and mucous membranes, so it should not be put in the eye, taken by mouth or used as a pessary. A scorpion sting, eye disease and womb pain need medical care. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read, and a pessary used for womb pain is a reason for caution. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
