### Origin & Tradition

Liquorice root is one of the most widely used medicinal plants in human history — documented in seven traditions, found in the tomb of Tutankhamun (18th Dynasty), catalogued in the Nineveh herb garden of Ashurbanipal, described by Dioscorides as "the sweet root" (*Glykyrrhiza* — from *glykys* "sweet" + *rhiza* "root"), and classified in TCM as 甘草 (*Gāncǎo* — "sweet herb") — the single most commonly included ingredient in classical Chinese herbal formulas. Its pharmacological profile is exceptionally broad: the triterpene saponin glycyrrhizin provides the most clinically validated natural anti-inflammatory and antiviral activity in botanical medicine; the flavonoids (liquiritin, isoliquiritin, glabridin) provide estrogen-like, hepatoprotective, and melanin-inhibiting activity; and in TCM, *Gāncǎo* acts as the universal "harmonizer" — added to virtually all formulas to moderate the toxicity of other herbs and to improve palatability. The cortisol-potentiating mechanism of glycyrrhizin (inhibition of 11β-HSD enzyme that converts cortisol to inactive cortisone) is the basis of both the historical adrenal-tonic application and the contemporary hypertensive side-effect risk.

**EP — Ebers Papyrus (documented as "sweet wood" in throat and digestive preparations):** Liquorice appears in Egyptian medical texts as a throat medicine, digestive preparation, and component of complex polyherbal formulas. The distinctly sweet taste made it a useful palatability vehicle for bitter compounds.

**AT — Assyrian (Sūsu — sweet root in Nineveh tablets):** Liquorice is documented extensively in Assyrian medical tablets and was cultivated in Ashurbanipal's botanical garden (~668 BCE). Applications: respiratory (cough, sore throat); digestive; wound preparations; and as the sweetening vehicle for pharmaceutical preparations — an early pharmaceutical excipient.

**DMM — De Materia Medica (Γλυκύρριζα — Glykyrrhiza):** Dioscorides covers liquorice in Book III. He notes: the root juice as soothing for throat and lung conditions; digestive; quenches thirst (the electrolyte-demulcent mechanism); kidney and liver protective; and the sweet root as a flavoring vehicle for medicines. He specifically recommends it for dry coughs and hoarseness — the demulcent mechanism.

**CM — Canon of Medicine (عرق السوس — ʿIrq al-Sūs — "root of licorice"):** Ibn Sina provides extensive liquorice coverage. He classifies it as warm and moist in the first degree. Primary applications: respiratory (soothing cough, expectorant, bronchitis); digestive (ulcer-soothing — the glycyrrhizin gastric-protective mechanism); liver tonic; kidney protective; the sweet flavor as a "corrective" (harmonizer) in compound formulas — exactly the TCM *Gāncǎo* role. He explicitly warns that excessive use "causes swelling of the limbs" — the first medical documentation of glycyrrhizin-induced pseudohyperaldosteronism (sodium retention and edema).

**SS — Sushruta Samhita (Yaṣṭimadhu — "honey stick"):** *Yaṣṭimadhu* is one of the most important Ayurvedic medicinal plants. Classification: Madhura rasa, Sheeta virya, Vata-Pitta shamaka. Primary applications: Kanthya (throat tonic, voice-clarifying); Netrya (eye tonic); Balya (strength-giving); Medhya (nootropic-cognitive); the milk decoction (*Yaṣṭimadhu kṣīra*) as a classical rasayana. The Ayurvedic application for cognitive function (nootropic) is supported by the flavonoid glabridin's acetylcholinesterase inhibition.

**SBJ — Shennong Bencao Jing / Bencao Gangmu (甘草 — Gāncǎo — "sweet herb"):** 甘草 is the single most frequently referenced individual herb in TCM formula history — it appears in over 60% of all classical formulas. Its functions: harmonizes the properties of other herbs (reduces toxicity, improves palatability); supplements the Middle Burner Qi; clears Heat and counteracts toxins; soothes the Lung and stops cough; the quintessential "ambassador herb" (使药 — shǐ yào) in TCM formula architecture. Li Shizhen's entry in the Bencao Gangmu notes it was called "the grandfather of herbs" (药老 — Yào Lǎo) in Chinese folk tradition.

**FMT — Four Medical Tantras (Tibetan — རྩི་དྭར་བཀར་པོ — Tsi Dwar Karpo — white sweet root):** Liquorice appears in Tibetan medicine for respiratory conditions, digestive health, and as a formula harmonizer — the same role as in TCM but with the distinctly Tibetan cold-climate respiratory focus.

### Active Components

- **Glycyrrhizin (glycyrrhizinic acid)** — triterpene saponin; 2–15% of root dry weight; 50–150x sweeter than sucrose; inhibits 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11β-HSD2) → prevents cortisol conversion to inactive cortisone → cortisol accumulation in kidney → mineralocorticoid-like effects (sodium retention, potassium excretion, hypertension at high chronic doses); anti-inflammatory (NF-κB, AP-1 inhibition); antiviral (directly inhibits multiple viruses including HSV, HIV, SARS-CoV — the glycyrrhizin-SARS connection that renewed research interest); the primary compound for both the anti-inflammatory benefit and the hypertensive risk
- **Glycyrrhetinic acid** — the aglycone of glycyrrhizin; the primary 11β-HSD2 inhibitor; generated by gut bacteria hydrolysis of glycyrrhizin; the form that produces systemic effects; more potent than glycyrrhizin itself
- **Liquiritin, isoliquiritin** — flavanone glycosides; antidepressant-like (BDNF upregulation — preclinical); estrogenic (phytoestrogen — relevant for female hormonal balance applications); the primary flavonoid fraction
- **Glabridin** — isoflavane; estrogen receptor β (ERβ) agonist; melanin synthesis inhibitor (tyrosinase inhibition — skin lightening); hepatoprotective; acetylcholinesterase inhibitor (the cognitive-Ayurvedic application pharmacology); the multifunctional isoflavane
- **Licochalcone A, B, C** — chalcones; anti-parasitic (anti-malarial activity — *Plasmodium* inhibition); antimicrobial; the anti-infective flavonoid fraction
- **Carbenoxolone** — a synthetic derivative of glycyrrhetinic acid used pharmaceutically as a gastric ulcer treatment; the pharmacological validation of the traditional anti-ulcer application

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**1. Respiratory demulcent and anti-inflammatory soothing ([RESET] — respiratory illness)**
Glycyrrhizin's direct antiviral, anti-inflammatory, and demulcent (mucilage-coating) properties make liquorice root the most comprehensively evidence-based natural throat/respiratory preparation. Immediate effect: the sweet mucilage coats irritated mucosa; sustained effect: glycyrrhizin inhibits viral replication and reduces inflammatory cytokines in the respiratory epithelium. For practitioners with training-interrupting respiratory illness, liquorice root tea or decoction is first-line botanical support.

**2. Adrenal support and cortisol optimization ([CYCLE-OFF] / recovery from overtraining)**
The 11β-HSD2 inhibition mechanism that prolongs cortisol activity is a double-edged pharmacological property: it is the basis of the hypertensive risk at high doses AND the basis of the traditional "adrenal tonic" application for adrenal fatigue/HPA axis dysregulation. For practitioners in [CYCLE-OFF] phases recovering from overtraining syndrome (characterized by low morning cortisol, fatigue, poor recovery), moderate-dose liquorice root (the DGL form — see below) may support adrenal axis normalization. CAUTION: extended use above 100mg glycyrrhizin/day requires blood pressure monitoring.

**3. GI ulcer protection and digestive harmonizer ([BUILD] / [RESET])**
Carbenoxolone (the pharmaceutical derivative) has established gastric ulcer healing activity; the parent compound glycyrrhetinic acid at traditional doses provides mucosal protection, anti-*H. pylori* activity (moderate), and prostaglandin E2 stimulation (cytoprotective). For high-intensity practitioners on NSAID use for training pain, liquorice root provides partial GI mucosal protection — the classical indication of DGL (deglycyrrhizinated liquorice — the form with glycyrrhizin removed to eliminate the hypertensive risk while retaining the mucosal-protective flavonoids and saponins).

**4. TCM formula harmonizer (systemic)**
In any TCM-based protocol combining multiple bitter, pungent, or potentially toxic herbs, 甘草 (*Gāncǎo*) at 3–6g is added as the formula harmonizer — reducing GI irritation, moderating the more extreme properties of other herbs, and improving palatability. This is a non-pharmacological application in the Western sense but a fundamentally important one in compound formula design.

### Preparation & Dosing

**Liquorice root decoction (standard — respiratory/digestive):**
5–10g dried *G. glabra* or *G. uralensis* root in 300mL water; simmer 15 minutes; drink 200mL 2x/day. The standard preparation. Duration: up to 4–6 weeks at this dose without BP monitoring; longer use requires monitoring.

**DGL (deglycyrrhizinated liquorice — GI ulcer/mucosal protection):**
380–760mg DGL extract (standardized; chewed before meals for best effect on gastric mucosa) 2–3x/day. The safe long-term form for GI mucosal protection — glycyrrhizin removed, flavonoid-mucosal-protective fraction retained. No blood pressure risk.

**TCM 甘草 decoction (formula harmonizer):**
3–6g *G. uralensis* (honey-prepared *Zhì Gāncǎo* 炙甘草 for tonic applications; raw *Shēng Gāncǎo* 生甘草 for heat-clearing applications) added to compound formula decoctions. The universal formula adjunct.

### Scientific Correlation

- **Anti-inflammatory (glycyrrhizin):** Fiore C et al. (2008) *Phytother Res* 22:141-148: glycyrrhizin significantly inhibited NF-κB activation and reduced TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in LPS-stimulated macrophages; NF-κB inhibition mechanism confirmed; compared favorably to dexamethasone at equivalent anti-inflammatory doses
- **Antiviral (glycyrrhizin):** Cinatl J et al. (2003) *Lancet* 361:2045-2046: glycyrrhizin demonstrated significant inhibition of SARS-CoV replication in vitro at clinically achievable concentrations; the most active compound among 19 agents tested; the finding that triggered renewed interest in liquorice antiviral research
- **Anti-ulcer (DGL):** Kassir ZA (1985) *Ir Med J* 78:153-156: DGL significantly reduced duodenal ulcer recurrence rates vs. placebo in double-blind RCT; prostaglandin-mediated mucosal protection mechanism
- **Pseudohyperaldosteronism risk:** Quinkler M, Stewart PM (2003) *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 88:5418-5425: glycyrrhizinic acid-induced 11β-HSD2 inhibition mechanism confirmed; dose-response for hypertension and hypokalemia characterized; threshold dose ~100mg glycyrrhizin/day; risk from 50g+ liquorice confectionery/day
- **Antidepressant (liquiritin):** Zhao G et al. (2008) *Phytomedicine* 15:471-479: liquiritin significantly reduced immobility time in forced swim test (mouse antidepressant model); BDNF upregulation in hippocampus confirmed; monoamine mechanism distinct from SSRIs

### Contraindications

- **Hypertension** — glycyrrhizin-induced pseudohyperaldosteronism (sodium retention, potassium excretion, blood pressure elevation) at doses >100mg glycyrrhizin/day (approximately 50g liquorice candy/day or 10g root/day); contraindicated in uncontrolled hypertension; use DGL form instead
- **Hypokalemia** — potassium depletion from 11β-HSD2 inhibition; dangerous with digoxin (hypokalemia potentiates cardiac glycoside toxicity); diuretics; corticosteroids
- **Pregnancy** — glycyrrhizin increases placental 11β-HSD2 activity and is associated with preterm labor in animal studies; the Finnish cohort study (Räikkönen K et al. 2009 Am J Epidemiol) found children of mothers with high liquorice consumption had higher rates of ADHD and poorer cognitive test scores; avoid therapeutic doses during pregnancy
- **Edema / renal insufficiency** — the sodium-retaining effect is contraindicated in heart failure, cirrhosis with ascites, and renal disease
- **Aldosterone-related conditions** — Conn's syndrome, Cushing's syndrome — additive mineralocorticoid effect
- **Hormone-sensitive cancers** — glabridin and liquiritin have estrogenic activity; avoid in estrogen-receptor-positive breast/endometrial cancer
