# I-254: Cinnamomum verum / zeylanicum (Ceylon Cinnamon / True Cinnamon)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-254 | Lauraceae**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Cinnamomum verum* J.Presl (syn. *C. zeylanicum* Nees)
**Family:** Lauraceae

**Common Names:**
- English: Ceylon Cinnamon, True Cinnamon, Sri Lanka Cinnamon
- Arabic: *Qirfa* (قرفة) — also *Dār-al-ṣīnī* (دار الصيني) — "Chinese bark" (confusing because it refers to Cassia in early trade)
- Persian: *Dār-chīnī* — "Chinese bark/wood" (reflecting that cinnamon/cassia reached Persia via Chinese trade routes)
- Sanskrit: *Tvak* (त्वक्) — "bark" (from *tvac*, skin); also *Daruchini*
- Hindi: *Dalchini* (from Sanskrit via Persian)
- Mandarin: *Ròu guì* (肉桂) — "flesh cassia"; also refers to the genus broadly
- Ancient Greek: *Kinnamōmon* (κιννάμωμον) — possibly from Phoenician/Hebrew *qinnamon* (*קִנָּמוֹן*)
- Hebrew: *Qinnamon* (קִנָּמוֹן) — mentioned in Exodus 30:23 as an ingredient in the sacred anointing oil; and Song of Songs 4:14
- Latin: *Cinnamomum* (direct from Greek/Semitic)

**Critical Disambiguation — Cinnamon vs. Cassia:**
This is one of the most commercially important botanical confusions in herbal medicine:

| Feature | Ceylon Cinnamon (C. verum) | Cassia Cinnamon (C. cassia, C. aromaticum) |
|---------|----------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| Origin | Sri Lanka (primary) | China, Vietnam, Indonesia |
| Coumarin content | Very low (<0.004% dry weight) | High (0.45–6.97% dry weight) |
| Taste | Sweet, delicate, mild | Pungent, spicy, sharp |
| Bark texture | Thin, multi-layered quills | Single thick rolls |
| Coumarin health concern | Negligible at normal doses | Hepatotoxic at large doses (warfarin activity) |
| US/UK "cinnamon" in stores | Often Cassia | Often Cassia |
| EU regulation | Preferred for medicinal use | Restricted in supplements due to coumarin |
| Price | Higher | Lower |
| Primary traditional medicine | Ayurveda, CM, DMM, CM | TCM (C. cassia); distinct species |

**For this Codex entry:** *C. verum* (Ceylon Cinnamon) is the primary entry. Where Cassia (C. cassia / C. aromaticum) is documented separately in traditional medicine, this is noted but detailed C. cassia pharmacology requires Entry I-255.

**Morphology (C. verum):** Small to medium evergreen tree; 10–15 m; smooth greyish bark; oval to lanceolate glossy leaves; cream flowers; purple berries. Commercial production: inner bark of young branches (2–3 years old) scraped and rolled into quills as it dries.

**Part Used:** Inner bark (quills/sticks); bark powder; essential oil; bark extract; leaf oil (lower quality).

**Growth Region:** Native to Sri Lanka (the primary global source); also cultivated in India (Kerala, Tamil Nadu), Madagascar, Seychelles, Indonesia.

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Essential Oil (0.5–2.5% bark; 0.5–1.5% leaves)

**1. (E)-Cinnamaldehyde (trans-Cinnamaldehyde) — 55–90% of bark oil**
- Phenylpropanoid aldehyde; primary bioactive; responsible for characteristic cinnamon aroma
- Mechanisms:
  - **Anti-diabetic (primary):** Inhibits protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B) → enhances insulin receptor signaling; AMPK activation (parallel to metformin mechanism); GLUT4 translocation enhancement; post-prandial glucose reduction
  - **Antimicrobial:** Membrane disruption; strong antifungal (Candida MIC 0.125 mg/mL); biofilm inhibition
  - **Anti-inflammatory:** NF-κB inhibition; TNF-α reduction
  - **TRPA1 agonism:** "Warming" sensory activation; anti-nociceptive via TRPA1 desensitization
  - **Aldehyde reactivity:** Some bioactivity from direct protein/enzyme covalent modification; also explains potential sensitization (aldehyde reactive group)
- Bioavailability: Rapidly absorbed; metabolized to cinnamic acid and hippuric acid (urinary markers)

**2. Eugenol — 1–10% (bark); 70–95% (leaf oil)**
- As documented in clove (I-253) and Tulsi (I-252)
- Bark oil low in eugenol; leaf oil high — explains why bark (low eugenol, high cinnamaldehyde) is medicinal preference

**3. β-Caryophyllene — 2–6%**
- CB2 anti-inflammatory (as in multiple previous entries)

**4. Linalool — 2–5%**
- Anxiolytic GABA-A (as in lavender, cardamom entries)

**5. α-Pinene, β-Pinene, p-Cymene — minor terpenes**

### Non-Volatile Constituents

**6. Procyanidins (Type A and Type B) — 1–5%**
- Oligomeric proanthocyanidins; primary anti-diabetic activity in aqueous extracts
- Mechanism: Insulin receptor phosphorylation enhancement; GLUT4 translocation
- **Water-soluble:** These are the primary anti-diabetic actives in cinnamon *tea/powder* (without essential oil)
- Different mechanism from cinnamaldehyde (which requires lipophilic extraction)

**7. Coumarin — VERY LOW in C. verum (<0.004% vs. 0.45–6.97% in cassia)**
- Hepatotoxic at high doses; anticoagulant (warfarin precursor); **this is why C. verum is the safe choice**
- EU RASFF (food safety) has issued multiple warnings for cassia-containing supplements exceeding coumarin limits

**8. Catechin, Epicatechin — flavan-3-ols**
- Antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; additional anti-diabetic contribution

**9. Cinnamate esters (methyl cinnamate, cinnamyl acetate)**

**10. Mucilaginous polysaccharides — demulcent; mild GI soothing**

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### 1. Insulin Sensitization / Anti-Diabetic (Multi-Mechanism)

**A. Procyanidin-mediated insulin receptor activation:**
- Procyanidins activate insulin receptor tyrosine kinase (IR-TK) → autophosphorylation of the insulin receptor → mimics insulin signaling WITHOUT insulin
- GLUT4 glucose transporter translocation to cell surface → enhanced glucose uptake by muscle and adipose tissue
- This mechanism is independent of insulin secretion (not a secretagogue) — works in insulin resistance where insulin is present but signaling is impaired

**B. Cinnamaldehyde PTP1B inhibition:**
- PTP1B (protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B) normally terminates insulin signaling by dephosphorylating the insulin receptor
- Cinnamaldehyde inhibits PTP1B → prolongs active insulin receptor phosphorylation → enhanced insulin sensitivity
- PTP1B is an active pharmaceutical target; cinnamaldehyde's mechanism is identical to some investigational PTP1B inhibitor drugs

**C. AMPK activation:**
- Both procyanidins and cinnamaldehyde activate AMPK (adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase), the cellular energy sensor
- AMPK activation → increased glucose uptake, fatty acid oxidation, reduced gluconeogenesis (same pathway as metformin)

**D. Glycogen synthase kinase-3 (GSK-3) inhibition:**
- Reduces hepatic glucose output
- Enhances glycogen synthesis in muscle

**Combined:** These four complementary mechanisms make cinnamon one of the pharmacologically most sophisticated natural anti-diabetic agents in the Codex.

### 2. Antimicrobial (Cinnamaldehyde Primary)
- Cell membrane disruption (aldehyde group + phenylpropanoid scaffold)
- ATP leakage; protein denaturation
- Outstanding antifungal: Candida MIC 0.031–0.125 mg/mL; effective against Aspergillus
- Anti-biofilm at sub-MIC concentrations — clinically relevant for oral and vaginal Candida

### 3. Anti-inflammatory (NF-κB / COX-2)
- Cinnamaldehyde: NF-κB IκBα stabilization → reduced inflammatory cytokine transcription
- Procyanidins: COX-2 modulation; TNF-α reduction
- Combined: Effective anti-inflammatory at oral doses achievable from 1–6 g/day cinnamon

### 4. Anti-Alzheimer's / Neurological (Research-Stage)
- Cinnamaldehyde inhibits tau protein aggregation (tau aggregation is pathological in Alzheimer's): IC₅₀ 45 μM
- Epigallocatechin + procyanidins: Beta-amyloid aggregation inhibition
- AChE inhibition: mild (cinnamaldehyde; proanthocyanidins)
- Animal model: Oral cinnamon extract reduces cognitive decline in Alzheimer's mouse models
- Human data: Not yet available; Level D

### 5. Lipid Regulation
- Reduces fasting triglycerides; modestly reduces LDL cholesterol
- Mechanism: PPAR-α activation → fatty acid oxidation; hepatic lipogenesis inhibition
- Clinical trials show consistent trends; meta-analyses Level B

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

### Type 2 Diabetes — Primary Evidence Base

**Khan et al. 2003 (Diabetes Care — landmark study):**
- N=60 Pakistani T2DM patients; cinnamon (Cassia species — note: C. cassia used, not C. verum) 1, 3, or 6 g/day × 40 days vs. placebo
- Fasting blood glucose reduced 18–29% across all three doses (dose-equivalent results — ceiling effect)
- Triglycerides reduced 23–30%; Total cholesterol reduced 12–26%; LDL reduced 7–27%
- **Note:** Used C. cassia (high coumarin); long-term safety concern at 6 g/day; C. verum equivalent doses would avoid coumarin concern

**Allen et al. 2013 (Annals of Family Medicine — systematic review):**
- 10 RCTs, 543 patients, 1–6 g cinnamon/day
- Significant fasting glucose reduction (mean −24.59 mg/dL vs. placebo; p<0.001)
- Significant lipid improvements
- Heterogeneity across studies; Level B

**Leach & Kumar 2012 (Cochrane-style systematic review):**
- 6 RCTs; mixed results; overall positive trend but heterogeneous preparations
- Conclusion: "Insufficient evidence to support or refute"; calls for higher-quality trials
- Level B (consistent direction; quality limitation)

**Davis et al. 2019 (Nutrition Research meta-analysis):**
- 18 RCTs; cinnamon consistently reduced fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, fasting insulin, insulin resistance (HOMA-IR)
- Effect size: HbA1c −0.27% reduction (modest but consistent)
- Level B consensus

### Overall Clinical Evidence Summary:

**Cinnamon and Type 2 diabetes:** Level B; consistent positive direction across 18+ RCTs; magnitude modest but consistent; most appropriate as ADJUNCT to standard T2DM management, not replacement. C. verum preferred for safety (low coumarin).

**Lipids:** Level B; triglyceride reduction most consistent effect.

**Anti-infective:** Level D (in vitro); no clinical RCTs for systemic infections.

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

Cinnamon's phenomenological signature is perhaps the most universally familiar of any botanical medicine — it is the defining sensory experience of warmth in human spice culture across all documented civilizations. Its phenomenological profile is simultaneously culinary, medicinal, and sacred.

**Oral (powder/tea/quills):**
- Immediate: Sweet warmth spreading from mouth downward; the quintessential "warming spice" sensation from TRPA1 activation by cinnamaldehyde; much gentler than clove, ginger, or pepper
- 15–30 minutes: GI warmth; anti-spasmodic effect on GI smooth muscle; reduction of gas and bloating
- 30–90 minutes (anti-diabetic effect): Post-prandial glucose blunting measurable; subjectively experienced as more sustained energy and reduced post-meal fatigue
- Chronic (4+ weeks): Insulin sensitivity improvements become apparent subjectively — more stable energy through the day; reduced "sugar crash" after meals

**Sacred/ceremonial phenomenology:**
The use of cinnamon in Exodus 30:23 for the sacred anointing oil (alongside myrrh, calamus, and cassia) positions cinnamon at the intersection of the pharmaceutical and the divine in multiple traditions. The Egyptian pharaonic embalming use (cinnamon bark detected in mummy preparations), the Greek temple offerings of *kinnamōmon*, and the Ayurvedic ritual preparations of *tvak* all involve cinnamon in the preservation of sacred bodies and spaces. The warmth, preservation, and antimicrobial properties of cinnamaldehyde that make cinnamon effective at prolonging biological tissue integrity (embalming) and resisting microbial decay are the same properties that make it a universal digestive and metabolic support.

**Cultural continuity unmatched in Volume I:**
From Exodus (1400 BCE) to Ibn Sīnā's Canon (1025 CE) to today's T2DM clinical trials (2003 onwards): cinnamon's pharmacological narrative spans 3,400+ years without interruption. The same warming carminative described in the oldest botanical texts continues to show metabolic benefit in contemporary RCTs. This temporal arc of consistent pharmacological observation is equaled by very few substances in human history.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Anti-diabetic (primary evidence use):**
- *C. verum* (Ceylon cinnamon): 1–6 g/day as powder in food or capsules; divide into 2–3 doses
- Clinical trial doses: 1, 3, or 6 g/day (Khan 2003); 1 g/day sufficient (equivalent results at all doses)
- Form: Powder in water, sprinkled on oatmeal, in capsule; water extract (aqueous) captures procyanidins well
- **C. verum strongly preferred** over cassia at these doses due to coumarin safety margin

**Tea:**
- 1 cinnamon quill (approximately 2 g) in 250 mL water; simmer 10–15 minutes; steep 10 more minutes covered
- 1–3 cups daily; pleasant, effective delivery
- Cinnamon tea extracts both water-soluble procyanidins (anti-diabetic) and volatile cinnamaldehyde (antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory)

**Anti-inflammatory / general metabolic:**
- 1 tsp (approximately 2.5 g) C. verum powder/day in food
- EU tolerable upper intake level for C. verum: No formal UL (negligible coumarin); practical upper limit ~6 g/day
- C. cassia: EU recommends limiting to 0.1 mg coumarin/kg body weight/day → approximately 0.5–1 g cassia per day maximum for adults (compare to 6 g/day C. verum safety margin)

**Essential oil (aromatherapy/topical only):**
- Aromatherapy: 1–2 drops in diffuser; warming/stimulating (not for relaxation)
- Topical (diluted): 0.5–1% in carrier oil for local warming/antimicrobial
- **NEVER ingest essential oil neat:** Cinnamaldehyde is hepatotoxic at high doses; skin sensitizer undiluted

### Dosing by Indication

| Indication | Form | Dose |
|------------|------|------|
| Type 2 DM (adjunct) | C. verum powder/capsule | 1–3 g/day with meals |
| Post-prandial glucose | Add to meals | ½–1 tsp powder with high-glycemic foods |
| Digestive/carminative | Tea | 1 quill in 250 mL; 1–3 cups/day |
| Antimicrobial (oral/GI) | Tea or extract | 3 cups tea/day or 500 mg extract 3×/day |
| Lipid management | Powder/capsule | 1–3 g/day; expect 8–12 weeks |

### Safety Profile

**C. verum: Very safe at typical doses.** The coumarin safety concern is primarily for C. cassia (Cassia cinnamon).

**Adverse effects (C. verum at therapeutic doses):**
- **Contact sensitization (cinnamaldehyde):** Cinnamaldehyde is the most common cause of cinnamon allergy; contact urticaria and allergic contact dermatitis; test patch for topical; rarely affects oral consumption at normal doses
- **GI upset:** Nausea, diarrhea at >6 g/day; dose-dependent
- **Hepatotoxicity:** At very high doses or extended use (C. verum: rare; C. cassia: more concern due to coumarin)

**Drug Interactions (CRITICAL for diabetic patients):**
- **Antidiabetics (metformin, insulin, sulfonylureas, SGLT2 inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors):** ADDITIVE GLUCOSE LOWERING — hypoglycemia risk; **CRITICAL: Monitor blood glucose and adjust medication doses if adding cinnamon to antidiabetic regimen**; the insulin-sensitizing mechanism of cinnamon is additive with virtually all classes of antidiabetics
- **Anticoagulants:** Cinnamaldehyde has mild antiplatelet activity; C. cassia coumarin is a warfarin precursor; INR monitoring; C. verum low coumarin reduces but does not eliminate concern
- **Antibiotics:** In vitro synergism with antibiotics against resistant organisms; no clinical interaction data

**Contraindications:**
- Cinnamaldehyde contact allergy (allergy testing recommended before regular topical use)
- Hepatic disease (high doses of any cinnamon preparation)
- Pregnancy (high dose): Uterotonic at pharmacological doses; culinary use safe; medicinal doses >3 g/day: discuss with physician
