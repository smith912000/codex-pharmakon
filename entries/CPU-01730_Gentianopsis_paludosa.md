# Gentianopsis paludosa — lchags.tig, Chaagtik (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Gentianopsis paludosa (Monro) Ma, the first of two names Clark 1995 prints for lchags.tig (then Lomatogonium sp.), called "Iron coloured gentian" in the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary, which prints the spelling "Gentianopsus paludosa"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT).** This entry rests on one tradition's texts, in two modern editions: Clark 1995 (the row's locus) and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011. The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. No other text read for this entry names the plant: a search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk (including the three Bhishagratna volumes) for the genus names *Gentianopsis* and *Gentiana* found no *Gentianopsis*, and found Dioscorides 3-3 GENTIANE (line 6541, glosses *Gentiana lutea* and Yellow Gentian) and Yang 1998's Long Dan (line 1936) and Qin Jiao (line 2494). These are other genera or species and are not attributed to this row. Clark's preface (line 83) says that more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (row 132) the Tibetan name *lchags.tig* is recorded at Clark line 1009 with the identification "Gentianopsis paludosa (Monro) Ma". The key is the first name Clark prints; Clark gives a second, "Lomatogonium sp.", which is a genus only.

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1009).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The line before it (1008) is *zangs.tig*, Clark's "red gentian"; the line after it (1010) is *gser.tig*, a *Swertia*. It reads:

"lchags.tig: Gentianopsis paludosa (Monro) Ma: Lomatogonium sp.: [has two names and grows in clumps. It has small leaves, (long) blue flowers (with pointed petals) and thin, black petioles. Its taste is bitter and it cures wound fevers and infectious fevers]. Both red gentian and Gentianopsis cure bile fevers."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries; those in parentheses come from the oral instructions of Dr. Yeshe Donden and Dr. Pema Dorje. Inside the square brackets (commentary layer) are the number of names, the habit, the leaves, the flowers, the petioles, the bitter taste and the clause "it cures wound fevers and infectious fevers". Outside them (root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the two Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the last sentence, which cures "bile fevers" by "red gentian" and "Gentianopsis" together. That sentence ties this drug to the preceding line's "red gentian" (*zangs.tig*), which is recorded in the entry for *Gueldenstaedtia multiflora* (I_1298, in this batch).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* Line 3428 (page 206), in the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines", ends: "Zangtik and Chaagtik cure hot disorder associated with tripa." The index lists "Chaagtik 206" (line 7290). The Tibetan name is therefore the same pair that Clark treats together. Clark's "bile fevers" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "hot disorder associated with tripa" are the two translations' wordings, and the Codex does not treat them as one term.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary.* The "C" block prints its columns in separate runs. Eight phonetic names are printed at lines 5130 to 5133 ("Chaga", "Chaagtik", "Champa", "Chang tsher", "Chawa", "Che tsha", "Chhuma tsi", "Chhurugpa"), eight transliterations at lines 5135 to 5140 (the second is "lcags tig"), and eight scientific names at lines 5142 to 5146 ("Zingiber officinalis", "Gentianopsus paludosa", "Malva botka", then "Cryptothladia polyphylla Angelica glauca Ranunculus tanguticus" on one line, "Rheum nobile" and "Helerpestes cymbblar"). The English column (lines 5148 to 5160) includes "Ginger", "Iron coloured gentian" (line 5158), "Blue mallow", "Thistel", "Buttercup" and "Himalayan rhubarb". Counted in order, the second phonetic name, "Chaagtik", stands against the second scientific name, "Gentianopsus paludosa", and "Iron coloured gentian" stands second in the English column after "Ginger". The last positions are consistent: "Chhuma tsi" against *Rheum nobile* and "Himalayan rhubarb", "Chhurugpa" against "Helerpestes", a variant of Clark's *Halerpestes sarmentosa* for *chhu.rug* (line 1032). The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position. [USER: verify] The two Tibetan texts agree on the Tibetan name, the genus and the species.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder found no entry on *Gentianopsis paludosa*. I_1176 (*Rheum nobile*) and I_1231 (*Angelica glauca*) quote the neighbouring "C" block lines or the name "Chaagtik" when treating their own subjects. I_315 (*Gentiana crassicaulis*) names the neighbouring *zangs.tig* as a bare *Gentiana* sp. in its cultivation text. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Gentianopsis* belongs to the gentian family (Gentianaceae), a family in which bitter secoiridoid glycosides, of which gentiopicroside is an example, are characteristic; Clark's commentary does record a "bitter" taste. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained or which part was used.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "cures wound fevers and infectious fevers" and, with the red gentian, "bile fevers" (Clark, line 1009), and that "cure[s] hot disorder associated with tripa" together with *Zangtik* (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3428). These are the texts' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 1009) prints "Its taste is bitter". No power or post-digestive taste is printed. Men-Tsee-Khang prints no taste or power for this name in the lines read. The actions printed are the curing of wound and infectious fevers and, with the red gentian, of bile fevers (Clark), and of hot disorder associated with tripa (Men-Tsee-Khang).

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it cures wound fevers, infectious fevers and bile fevers, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text pairs it with *Zangtik* for hot disorder associated with tripa. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the sentences cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a bitter drug for fevers in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one.
