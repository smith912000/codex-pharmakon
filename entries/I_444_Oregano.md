## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary name:** Oregano, Wild marjoram
**Synonyms:** *Origanum* (Greek), Rigani (Greek modern — *ρίγανη*), *Origan* (French), *Origano* (Italian)
**Binomial:** *Origanum vulgare* L.
**Authority:** Linnaeus 1753
**Related species/subspecies:** *O. vulgare* subsp. *hirtum* (Greek oregano — highest thymol/carvacrol; primary medicinal); *O. majorana* L. (sweet marjoram — gentler, linalool/sabinene-dominant; different pharmacological profile); *O. compactum* (Moroccan oregano — high carvacrol)

**Botanical description:** Perennial herb 20–80 cm. Stems square, often purplish, hairy. Leaves ovate, opposite, glandular-dotted, 1–4 cm. Flowers in corymbs, white-pink-purple, tiny tubular, 4–5 mm. Highly aromatic. Distinguishing character from marjoram (*O. majorana*): oregano leaves more pungent, veins hairy beneath; flower bracts purple-tinged (green in marjoram); taste more bitter/peppery vs. marjoram's sweeter profile.

**Critical quality note:** Most US-sold "oregano" has very low essential oil content (thymol/carvacrol) because it is harvested from cold-climate cultivars or is actually *Lippia graveolens* (Mexican oregano — different family: Verbenaceae) rather than true *Origanum*. Greek and Turkish oregano (*O. vulgare* subsp. *hirtum*) has highest essential oil quality. Essential oil content should be ≥2.5% (European Pharmacopoeia) but many commercial products are <0.5%.

**Pharmacognostic parts used:** Aerial parts, dried (*Origani herba*); essential oil (*Origani aetheroleum*)

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

Oregano and thyme (I-443) are pharmacological near-twins — both Lamiaceae, both thymol/carvacrol-dominant, both with strong antimicrobial and respiratory applications. The primary distinguishing pharmacological feature: **oregano contains higher carvacrol relative to thymol** (especially Greek/Turkish subsp. *hirtum*) and has stronger anti-parasitic traditional documentation.

**Carvacrol vs. thymol — the structural isomer difference:**
Carvacrol (5-isopropyl-2-methylphenol) and thymol (5-methyl-2-isopropylphenol) are positional isomers — same atoms, different arrangement. Both are phenolic monoterpenes with overlapping but slightly different receptor affinities and membrane interactions. Carvacrol shows somewhat greater potency against gram-negative bacteria and parasites; thymol somewhat more active against gram-positive organisms and fungi. This subtle chemical difference creates slight clinical differentiation: oregano slightly preferred for gut infections and parasites (carvacrol emphasis); thyme slightly preferred for respiratory (thymol emphasis in standard preparations).

**Za'tar — the spice blend as pharmacological vehicle:**
Middle Eastern *za'tar* spice blend (oregano + sumac + sesame + salt) consumed daily with olive oil on bread is one of the most pharmacologically dense daily foods in traditional Mediterranean diet. The daily dose of polyphenols (rosmarinic acid, thymol/carvacrol, sumac anthocyanins, sesame lignans) represents sustained low-dose anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial pharmacology.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Essential oil (Greek/Turkish subsp. *hirtum*):**

| Compound | Concentration | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| Carvacrol | 50–80% | Primary antimicrobial; anti-parasitic; antifungal; anti-inflammatory |
| Thymol | 3–20% | Antimicrobial; bronchospasmolytic (shared with thyme) |
| p-Cymene | 5–15% | Precursor; analgesic; membrane disruptor |
| γ-Terpinene | 3–10% | Antioxidant; precursor to carvacrol/thymol |
| Linalool | 1–5% | Anxiolytic; anti-inflammatory |
| β-Caryophyllene | 1–5% | CB2 agonist; anti-inflammatory (shared with black pepper, clove) |

**Phenolic acids and flavonoids:**
- Rosmarinic acid: 1.0–3.0% (higher than thyme — important antioxidant)
- Luteolin: 0.3–1.0%
- Apigenin: minor
- Eriodictyol: minor

**β-Caryophyllene note:**
β-Caryophyllene (sesquiterpene) is present in oregano oil (and black pepper, clove, cannabis leaves). It is a selective CB2 cannabinoid receptor agonist — the only dietary/herbal compound with demonstrated CB2 agonism at nutritionally relevant doses. CB2 activation: anti-inflammatory (macrophage), bone metabolism, pain modulation. This makes oregano (alongside black pepper and clove) a dietary source of phytoendocannabinoid-like activity.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**1. Carvacrol — antimicrobial membrane disruption**
Carvacrol mechanism identical to thymol (I-443): phenolic ring integrates into bacterial/fungal cell membranes → membrane depolarization → ATP depletion → cell death. Carvacrol shows slightly greater membrane permeabilization than thymol (lower MIC against gram-negatives). Active against: E. coli, Salmonella, Campylobacter (food pathogens), Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, MRSA (biofilm penetration), Candida, Aspergillus, and — critically — intestinal parasites (Giardia, Blastocystis).

**2. Anti-parasitic mechanism (Giardia, Blastocystis)**
Carvacrol and thymol against *Giardia lamblia*: disrupt trophozoite membrane integrity, reduce adhesion disk function, inhibit cyst wall formation. IC₅₀ carvacrol vs. *Giardia* trophozoites: ~0.03 mg/mL (achieved in GI tract with adequate oral dose). This is the basis for oregano oil's use as a natural anti-parasitic.

**3. β-Caryophyllene — CB2 agonism**
β-Caryophyllene binds CB2 receptors (Ki ~155 nM) without CB1 activity (no psychoactivity). CB2 in macrophages and gut-associated lymphoid tissue: modulates inflammatory cytokine production, reduces NF-κB activation. Animal models: β-caryophyllene reduces inflammatory pain and metabolic syndrome markers. Human data sparse but mechanistically interesting.

**4. Anti-inflammatory (carvacrol + rosmarinic acid)**
Carvacrol: NF-κB nuclear translocation inhibition (IC₅₀ ~10 μM). NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition. PPAR-γ activation (metabolic anti-inflammatory). Rosmarinic acid: COX-1/2 inhibition; 5-LOX inhibition; complement activation reduction. Combined: multi-pathway anti-inflammatory relevant for IBD and systemic inflammation.

**5. Antifungal (carvacrol/thymol vs. Candida)**
*Candida albicans* MIC carvacrol: 0.12–0.5 mg/mL. Ergosterol binding + membrane disruption + biofilm prevention. Comparable to fluconazole (MIC 0.25–8 mg/mL) for many *Candida* strains. Anti-MRSA biofilm: carvacrol penetrates biofilm at sub-MIC concentrations.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### Intestinal Parasites (Small Pilot)

**Level B: Force 2000 (Phytotherapy Research, n=14)**
Oregano oil (emulsified, 200 mg/day) × 6 weeks in patients with intestinal parasites (Blastocystis hominis, Entamoeba hartmanni, Endolimax nana).
Result: Complete resolution of Blastocystis in 8/11 patients (73%); E. hartmanni resolved 50%; significant GI symptom improvement.
Limitation: Small, uncontrolled, unblinded. No RCT replication to date.

### SIBO and IBS-like Symptoms

**Level B: Siegel 2011 (Global Advances in Health and Medicine)**
Herbal therapy including oregano oil vs. rifaximin for SIBO.
Result: Herbal therapy non-inferior to rifaximin for SIBO normalization. Oregano oil component had significant in vitro activity against the SIBO pathogens.

### Oral / Food-borne Antimicrobial

**Level D: Multiple laboratory studies**
Carvacrol/thymol effective against food-borne pathogens including E. coli O157:H7, Salmonella, Campylobacter. Commercial food preservation using oregano essential oil validated in multiple food science studies. Not translated to clinical trials of oral antimicrobial efficacy.

### Anti-inflammatory

**Level B: Avci 2014 (Multiple citation — in vitro / small pilot)**
Rosmarinic acid-rich oregano extract: significant reduction in inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6) in small pilot of metabolic syndrome patients. No RCT.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Za'tar and the daily medicine:**
In Lebanon, Jordan, Palestine, and Syria, za'tar with olive oil eaten with flatbread is breakfast for millions daily. The polyphenol load in this meal (carvacrol + thymol from oregano; gallic acid + anthocyanins from sumac; tyrosol + hydroxytyrosol from olive oil; sesaminol from sesame) is among the highest of any traditional breakfast food. Regular za'tar consumption has been associated with cognitive preservation in observational studies of elderly Lebanese populations. Whether this is the za'tar polyphenols, the olive oil, the Mediterranean diet pattern overall, or healthy-user bias is unclear — but the pharmacological rationale is sound.

**"Joy of the mountain" — the Greek etymology:**
*Origanum* derives from Greek *oros* (mountain) + *ganos* (brightness/joy) = "joy/brightness of the mountain." This evocative name describes both its preferred habitat (dry sunny Mediterranean mountain slopes) and its sensory quality — the intense aromatic brightness when walking through oregano scrub on a warm Greek hillside is qualitatively distinct. The Greek cultural embedding of oregano as the scent of mountains, joy, and summer feeds into its inclusion in virtually every Greek culinary and folk-medicinal context.

**The pizza oregano paradox:**
American "oregano" applied liberally to pizza and Italian-American cooking is typically *Origanum vulgare* subsp. *vulgare* — the weaker, low-carvacrol temperate subspecies. True Mediterranean oregano (subsp. *hirtum*) is dramatically more pungent. The American pizza-oregano cultural association has zero therapeutic significance; the Greek/Turkish medical oregano (subsp. *hirtum*) used in essential oil preparations has genuine clinical relevance. The same plant name covering 10% vs. 80% carvacrol content represents one of the most significant quality disparities in common botanical medicines.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Anti-parasitic / intestinal antimicrobial:**
- Emulsified oregano oil: 200 mg/day (standardized to ≥70% carvacrol); divided in 2 doses with meals
- Duration: 4–6 weeks for intestinal parasite management
- Always purchase Greek or Turkish origin; verify ≥70% carvacrol by GC analysis on label
- Not a replacement for medical diagnosis and conventional anti-parasitic treatment; adjunct use

**Immune support / upper respiratory infections:**
- Oregano oil 150–300 mg/day (as above) during acute infections
- Throat spray: 2 drops oregano oil in 30 mL water + 1 mL honey; spray into throat 3× daily
- Steam inhalation: 4 drops in bowl of hot water; inhale steam

**Culinary integration:**
- Liberal use of high-quality dried Greek oregano in Mediterranean-style diet provides daily carvacrol/rosmarinic acid dose
- Amounts typically used in cooking (1–3 g per serving) are pharmacologically subtherapeutic but contribute to overall polyphenol load

**Topical antifungal:**
- 2–3% dilution in carrier oil for skin fungal infections
- Avoid undiluted essential oil on skin (irritant at full concentration)

**Safety:**
Generally safe at culinary and standard therapeutic doses.

**Contraindications:**
- Pregnancy: Avoid medicinal doses (emmenagogue/uterotonic at high doses; culinary safe)
- Iron absorption: Thymol/carvacrol may reduce non-heme iron absorption; take iron supplements 2 hours away from oregano preparations
- Hypersensitivity: Cross-reactivity with thyme, sage (Lamiaceae family) possible

**Drug interactions:**
- Anticoagulants: Mild antiplatelet (rosmarinic acid) — monitor
- Cyclosporin: Oregano oil may inhibit CYP3A4 — potential cyclosporin level increase; avoid high-dose concurrent use
