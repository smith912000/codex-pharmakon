# I_130 — Agrimonia eupatoria | Agrimony / Church Steeples / Cocklebur / Liverwort

**Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry:** I_130
**Convergence Score:** 3/9 traditions
**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (hepatic/astringent) | ◈ Jing (structural — collagen/wound)

## Section 1 — Nomenclature

| Register | Name |
|----------|------|
| Scientific | *Agrimonia eupatoria* L. (Rosaceae) |
| DMM (Greek) | Eupatorion (Book IV.41) — Dioscorides attributes discovery/naming to Mithridates Eupator (King of Pontus, 132–63 BCE), the great pharmacological experimenter; also Agrimonia (Latinized form adopted from Greek) |
| CM (Arabic/Persian) | Gharīqūn / Aghrīmūniyā; Ibn Sīnā discusses under liver and skin conditions |
| European folk | Church Steeples (for slender tapering flower spike); Cocklebur (for hooked fruit burrs); Sticklewort; Liverwort (for liver applications — though unrelated to botanical "liverworts"); Philanthropos (Greek: "lover of man," for clinging burrs) |
| Latin | Agrimonia; Eupatorium (= named for Eupator); Herba benedicta (Blessed herb) |
| Welsh | Llysiau'r dryw (Druid's herb — again, Celtic sacred plant attribution) |
| German folk | Odermennig; Leberkraut (Liver herb) |
| Chinese | Xiāncáo (仙草) in some regional usages; more precisely Lóng yá cǎo (龍牙草 — Dragon's tooth herb) for *A. pilosa* (Asian species); *A. eupatoria* not in classical SBJ |

**Botanical identity:** Perennial herb to 90cm; pinnate leaves, grey-green hairy; terminal raceme of small yellow 5-petalled flowers; fruit a burr with hooked bristles that cling to clothing and animal fur — primary seed dispersal mechanism and source of folk names "Cocklebur" and "Philanthropos" (loves everyone by clinging to them). Widely distributed across Europe, North Africa, and western Asia; grows in hedgerows, field margins, woodland edges.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Score: 3/9** (DMM · CM · European folk tradition confirmed)

Agrimony demonstrates the "liver herb" convergence pattern: wherever the plant was known in the Mediterranean/Islamic world, liver complaints were the primary application. The convergence is driven by the tannin-flavonoid combination that genuinely supports hepatobiliary function.

**Mithridates attribution** (both DMM and classical sources): King Mithridates VI Eupator of Pontus (d. 63 BCE) was the ancient world's most obsessive pharmacologist, experimenter with antidotes, and self-medicator against poisons (he reportedly developed immunity to many poisons through progressive micro-dosing — the "mithridatism" practice). Dioscorides attributes *Eupatorium* naming to him, as does Pliny. Several plants received the "Eupator" designation as royal pharmacological stamp. This attribution may reflect genuine Pontic court medical use, or may be the ancient equivalent of a royal endorsement label.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Tannins (primary active class, 4–10% total):**
- Agrimoniin — ellagitannin; principal astringent; potent antioxidant; antitumor (in vitro)
- Pedunculagin, agrimonolide (lactone tannin)
- Gallotannins: gallic acid esters
- Hydrolysable tannins → ellagic acid on hydrolysis

**Flavonoids:**
- Quercetin, kaempferol, luteolin, apigenin — free aglycones and glycosides
- Rutin — capillary-protective
- Vitexin, isovitexin — anti-inflammatory

**Triterpenes:**
- Ursolic acid (1–2%) — hepatoprotective; anti-inflammatory
- Oleanolic acid — hepatoprotective
- Tormentic acid (agrimony-specific)

**Phenolic acids:**
- Ellagic acid, caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, p-coumaric acid

**Volatile oil (trace):**
- α-Pinene, β-pinene, limonene — mild antimicrobial; aromatic

**Vitamins:**
- Vitamin K (significant — explains hemostatic application)
- Vitamin B complex (thiamine, B₆)

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Astringent / Wound healing (primary):**
- High tannin content → protein precipitation on wound surfaces → protective pellicle formation → reduced bleeding → antimicrobial barrier
- Agrimoniin: potent ROS scavenger protecting wound tissue from oxidative damage
- Vitamin K: cofactor for clotting factor synthesis → systemic hemostatic contribution with regular use
- Combined effect explains consistent wound, dysentery, and menorrhagia applications

**Hepatobiliary:**
- Ursolic acid + oleanolic acid: hepatoprotective (Nrf2 activation; hepatocyte membrane stabilization)
- Agrimoniin: inhibits lipid peroxidation in liver cell models
- Quercetin: bile acid transport modulation; mild choleretic
- Combined effect validates traditional liver tonic classification

**Anti-inflammatory:**
- Quercetin, kaempferol, luteolin: COX-1/2 inhibition; 5-LOX inhibition; NF-κB inhibition
- Agrimoniin: complement system modulation → anti-inflammatory in autoimmune models
- Tormentic acid: anti-inflammatory (unique to Rosaceae tannin-bearing plants)

**Antimicrobial:**
- Tannin fraction: bacterial membrane disruption; protein binding; MRSA activity in vitro (agrimoniin)
- Phenolic acids: broad-spectrum antimicrobial in vitro
- Explains traditional use for throat infection (gargle), infected wounds, GI infections (dysentery)

**Antidiabetic:**
- Agrimoniin: PPAR-γ agonist (in vitro); insulin sensitization
- Quercetin: SGLT2 inhibition; α-glucosidase inhibition → postprandial glucose reduction
- Ursolic acid: insulin mimetic (IRS-1/PI3K pathway activation in fat cell models)

**Antitumor (in vitro):**
- Agrimoniin: apoptosis induction in cancer cell lines (multiple tumor types)
- Tormentic acid: anti-proliferative
- No clinical translation yet; significant in vitro signal

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Regulatory status:**
- Commission E (Germany): Approved monograph for agrimony aerial parts. Indications: diarrhea; mild inflammation of oral mucosa and pharynx. Explicitly hepatobiliary applications are considered traditional but not in the formal Commission E indication.
- EMA: THMP listing; supports Commission E indications

**Clinical trials:**
- Limited formal RCT data for human subjects
- Diabetic animal model: agrimoniin blood glucose reduction — compelling animal data; no human RCT
- Antitumor: agrimoniin in vitro studies at multiple centers; pre-clinical animal data; no clinical trials published

**Traditional validation (strong):**
- Consistent liver + wound + astringent application from Dioscorides (1st century CE) through modern European herbal medicine represents approximately 2,000 years of clinical observation
- Gerard (1597): "It is a singular good herb to cleanse the liver and the blood." Consistent with Dioscorides 1,600 years earlier.
- Culpeper (1653): Primary liver remedy; recommended for jaundice, "obstructions of the liver"

**German phytotherapy:** Agrimony tea widely sold in Germany; among the top-20 medicinal herbs by commercial volume in German natural pharmacy retail.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The Mithridates connection — royal pharmacological tradition:**
King Mithridates VI Eupator (132–63 BCE) is one of antiquity's most fascinating figures: simultaneously ruthless military tactician and obsessive pharmacologist. According to ancient sources, he spent his life developing antidotes to all known poisons, testing them on prisoners, and self-administering progressive doses to build immunity. Pliny records that when he was finally defeated by Pompey and attempted suicide by poison, the poison failed to kill him — he had rendered himself immune. He died by the sword instead. The naming of agrimony after him (*Eupatorion*) places this mild, safe herb in a biographical tradition of extreme pharmacological experimentation — an irony characteristic of botanical history.

**"Philanthropos" (Greek: lover of mankind):**
The burr-fruit's clinging hooks earned the plant a name that means it "loves" all who pass by. In medieval botanical literature this became an ethical botanical attribute: the plant that clings to and accompanies humans everywhere is pharmacologically useful to them everywhere. The folk name represents the ancient principle of pairing botanical behavior with medicinal virtue.

**Culpeper's Jupiter herb:**
Nicholas Culpeper (1653) assigns agrimony to Jupiter (warm, moist, beneficial) and uses it as his principal liver remedy. His popular English-language herbal democratized agrimony use throughout the British Isles in the 17th century — it became a standard cottage garden herb specifically because Culpeper made it accessible to ordinary people outside the professional apothecary tradition.

**Wound herb of the Hundred Years' War:**
Medieval military surgeons regularly packed agrimony into wounds; the astringent tannins formed a protective surface (essentially a natural wound dressing) while the antimicrobial phenolics reduced infection. Field medicine in medieval Europe relied heavily on a triad of wound herbs: agrimony (tannin astringent), yarrow (I_034, hemostatic), and plantain (demulcent/antimicrobial). This folk battlefield pharmacopoeia was remarkably effective within its pharmacological limits.

**Bach Flower Remedy:** Agrimony — "for those who hide their troubles behind a cheerful face." Edward Bach's characterization (1930s) captures something of the plant's herbal personality: the mild, reliable, unassuming herb that quietly does its work without drama — precisely as reliable as its human archetype was supposed to appear untroubled.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Modern therapeutic applications:**

1. **Diarrhea / GI astringent:** 3g dried aerial parts as infusion 3× daily; or 2–3mL tincture (1:5 in 25%) 3× daily; tannin astringent reduces intestinal inflammation and fluid loss; appropriate for acute diarrhea and irritable bowel
2. **Sore throat / tonsillitis gargle:** Strong infusion (4g/200mL), gargle 30 seconds 4–6× daily; tannin + antimicrobial action; long-standing folk and Commission E application
3. **Liver/gallbladder support:** 2–3g infusion 2× daily, before meals; include in hepatobiliary formula with dandelion and milk thistle
4. **Wound care (topical):** Cooled decoction (5g/200mL) as compress or wash; gentle for skin wounds, ulcers, eczema
5. **Menorrhagia (heavy menstrual bleeding):** Traditional application; tannin astringent + Vitamin K hemostatic synergy; use throughout cycle; combine with lady's mantle (*Alchemilla*)

**Dose:**
- Infusion: 2–3g dried herb in 150–200mL water, steep 10 min; 3–4 cups daily
- Tincture (1:5 in 25%): 2–4mL 3× daily
- Maximum Commission E dose: 3g per dose, 3× daily

**Contraindications:** None established. Commission E lists no known risks, side effects, or interactions at therapeutic doses. Among the safest herbs in the European pharmacopoeia.

**Cautions:**
- Tannin content may reduce iron absorption if taken simultaneously with iron supplements — take 2 hours apart
- Theoretical additive anticoagulant effect from Vitamin K may counter anticoagulant medications (warfarin) — monitor INR with regular use
- No contraindication in pregnancy at culinary/tea doses (unlike many herbs); medicinal doses in pregnancy should be discussed with qualified practitioner
