# Wild Basil Thyme — Kakuti · Kākutī · Kekik-otu · Mountain Mint

## *Ziziphora clinopodioides* Lam. | Family: Lamiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM, FMT
**Energetic Classification:** Hot and dry in the second (to third) degree — a pungent aromatic *muḥallil* (resolvent) and *mufattiḥ* (deobstruent), warming to the stomach and heart.
**Training Phase:** Restoration / Circulation — a digestive-cardiac aromatic used to settle the gut, open the chest, and "calm the heart" in the recovery phase.

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Ziziphora clinopodioides* is a low, densely aromatic subshrub of the mint family, carpeting dry mountain slopes from Anatolia and the Iranian plateau across the Caucasus into Central Asia and western China. Crushed, it releases a sharp mentholic-peppery scent — the sensory signature of a pulegone- and menthone-rich essential oil. Across every tradition that grew on those slopes it acquired the same cluster of uses: a carminative for the belly, a tonic for the heart, and a remedy for cough and cold "wind."

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian cuneiform materia medica (AT).** The Akkadian herbal corpus lists numerous aromatic *šammu* (plant drugs) used as carminatives, fumigants, and stomach remedies, and Lamiaceae aromatics of this warming, digestive class plausibly sit among them. However, secure one-to-one identification of *Ziziphora* with any specific cuneiform plant-name is not established. **Dioscorides / De Materia Medica (DMM).** Dioscorides catalogued a family of pungent mountain mints — *kalaminthē* and its kin — as heating, digestive, and diuretic drugs that "warm and thin" (resolve) thick humours, promote menstruation, and settle the stomach. *Ziziphora*, morphologically and aromatically a small *Calamintha*-type mint, belongs to exactly this Dioscoridean class of hot, thinning aromatics. [USER: verify whether *Ziziphora* is identified with a specific Dioscoridean entry or is a regional member of the *kalaminthē* group — treat as class-level attestation.]

**Avicenna / Unani-Persian tradition (CM).** In Persian and Unani practice the plant is *kākutī* / *kakuti-ye kuhi* (mountain kakuti). It is classed as hot and dry, a *muqawwī-ye qalb* (cardiotonic / heart-strengthener) and *muqawwī-ye meʿde* (stomach-strengthener), given as a carminative for flatulent colic and indigestion, for palpitation and "weakness of the heart," and as an expectorant for cough. This is the doctrinal core: a warming aromatic that simultaneously drives out digestive "wind" and fortifies the heart.

**The Four Tantras / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** In the Tibetan and adjacent Central-Asian systems, warming aromatic mints of this type are used against cold-phlegm and *rlung* (wind) disorders of the upper body — cough, chest congestion, and digestive stagnation — the same warm-carminative logic in a different humoral vocabulary. [USER: verify the specific Tibetan/Sowa-Rigpa name if citing a named text.]

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The unifying doctrine is heat that *moves* — the aromatic-pungent quality read across all four systems as a warming, dispersing, wind-expelling force. Map this onto the essential-oil chemistry and the doctrine is almost literally correct. The volatile fraction is dominated by **pulegone, menthone, isomenthone, 1,8-cineole and menthol** — small, lipophilic monoterpenes that are volatile (hence the "warmth" the nose registers), spasmolytic on gut smooth muscle (hence the carminative "expulsion of wind"), and antimicrobial (hence use in gastrointestinal upset and cough). The traditional word "carminative" is a precise humoral description of what a spasmolytic terpene does: it relaxes intestinal smooth muscle, allowing trapped gas to pass — "wind driven out by warmth." The menthol/cineole fraction produces the cooling-then-warming counter-irritant sensation and mild bronchodilation that underlies the cough/chest use.

The "calming the heart" doctrine is the more interesting mapping. Read symbolically, a fragrant mountain herb that steadies palpitation is a *cardiac calmative*. Read chemically, the picture is genuinely dual and the doctrine deserves both credit and deflation. On one hand, the **flavonoid fraction** of *Ziziphora* (chrysin, acacetin, luteolin/apigenin rutinosides, rosmarinic acid) has real, measured cardioprotective and antioxidant activity in animal models (below) — a defensible "tonic for the heart." On the other hand, pulegone-rich mint oils are not benign cardiotonics: at high dose the same volatile that warms the chest is hepatotoxic and can depress rather than tone. The tradition's instinct to use the *flavonoid-bearing aqueous/herb preparation* (infusions, decoctions) for the heart — rather than the concentrated oil — turns out to be pharmacologically wise, even if the reasoning was humoral. The doctrine is right about the herb and wrong about the isolated oil.

### Active Components

- **Essential oil (the pungent-warming fraction):** **pulegone** (frequently the dominant constituent), **menthone / isomenthone**, **pulegone-derived menthofuran**, **1,8-cineole**, **menthol**, **piperitone**, **p-cymene**, **limonene** — proportions vary widely by chemotype, altitude, and harvest. This fraction carries the antispasmodic, antimicrobial, and counter-irritant/expectorant activity — *and* the toxicity.
- **Flavonoids (the cardioprotective fraction):** chrysin, acacetin, genkwanin, kaempferide, apigenin/luteolin and their rutinosides, cynaroside, linarin — the "Ziziphora clinopodioides flavonoids" (ZCF) studied for cardiovascular effects.
- **Phenolic acids:** rosmarinic acid, caffeic-acid derivatives — antioxidant.
- **Monoterpene glucosides:** ziziphorosides A–C and related — some with anti-inflammatory (NO-inhibitory) activity.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Ziziphora is the Codex's **aromatic digestive-cardiac resolvent** of the Restoration/Circulation phase — reached for after heavy meals, in flatulent colic, and where digestive stagnation coexists with a sense of chest tightness or nervous palpitation. Its Kenetica role is deliberately restricted to the **gentle herb preparation** (infusion of the dried aerial parts), exploiting the flavonoid antioxidant/cardioprotective fraction and the mild spasmolytic terpene action while keeping pulegone exposure low. It is a supportive, subtractive digestive — not a stimulant, not a chronic daily tonic, and explicitly **not** to be used as a concentrated essential oil internally.

### Scientific Correlation

Based on articles retrieved from PubMed, the *cardiac* and *antioxidant* half of the traditional doctrine has the better modern support; the specific "carminative/cough" essential-oil claims rest more on general terpene pharmacology than on dedicated human trials, and the safety caveat is well-documented.

**Cardiotropic / "tonic for the heart" (doctrine — supported at animal/mechanistic level).** Whaley et al. (2023) validated the traditional coronary-disease use of *Z. clinopodioides* subsp. *bungeana*: multiple extracts improved contraction and output indices in a rat chronic-heart-failure model comparably to the reference drug (fosinopril/Monopril), and the authors isolated the responsible chemistry — monoterpenoid derivatives, megastigmane glycosides, and flavonoids (pinocembrin, chrysin, acacetin, luteolin rutinosides, rutin) plus rosmarinic acid ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2023.116660)). Wu et al. (2020) showed the total flavonoids (ZCF) attenuated atherosclerosis in rats, lowering TG/TC/LDL-C and down-regulating the VEGF/AKT/NF-κB pathway ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopha.2020.110399)). Li et al. (2018) found the flavonoids protected cardiomyocytes against ischemia-reperfusion/oxidative injury in vitro and in a rat model, preserving mitochondrial membrane potential and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity and reducing malondialdehyde ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1155/2018/8495010)). Liu et al. (2023) mapped the anti-atherosclerotic flavonoids (cynaroside, linarin, kaempferide, acacetin, genkwanin, chrysin) onto MMP9/TP53/AKT1/SRC targets by network pharmacology with in-vitro validation ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12013-023-01151-2)). Together these give the "muqawwī-ye qalb" doctrine a real mechanistic spine — antioxidant, lipid-lowering, endothelial-protective flavonoids — while confirming the effect is carried by the flavonoid/aqueous fraction, not the pungent oil.

**Anti-inflammatory chemistry (supports the carminative/resolvent picture).** Furukawa et al. (2012) isolated ziziphorosides A–C and known constituents from *Z. clinopodioides* and found several (a shizonepetoside and flavones) potently inhibited nitric-oxide production in LPS/IFN-γ-activated macrophages ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1248/cpb.60.397)) — a plausible correlate of the herb's traditional use against inflamed, irritated digestive and respiratory tissue.

**Carminative / antispasmodic / antimicrobial (doctrine — plausible, mechanism-level, human data thin).** The dominant essential-oil constituents — pulegone, menthone, 1,8-cineole, menthol — are, as a class, well-established smooth-muscle spasmolytics, antimicrobials, and (cineole/menthol) mild expectorant counter-irritants. This is a sound mechanistic rationale for the carminative and cough uses, but it is a class inference; dedicated controlled human trials of *Ziziphora* for dyspepsia or cough were not retrieved this run. **Honest grade:** the digestive/cough doctrine is pharmacologically reasonable but clinically under-evidenced.

**Evidence grade:** Moderate for cardiovascular/antioxidant activity (multiple animal + mechanistic studies, no human RCTs); mechanism-level/plausible for carminative-antispasmodic-antimicrobial use; the pungent-warming doctrine correctly identifies the volatile terpenes, and the "calm the heart" doctrine correctly (if unwittingly) tracks the flavonoid fraction.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Unani / Galenic (CM):** hot and dry, 2nd–3rd degree; *muqawwī-ye qalb* (cardiotonic), *muqawwī-ye meʿde* (stomach-strengthener), carminative, deobstruent (*mufattiḥ*), expectorant.
- **Dioscoridean (DMM):** heating, thinning/resolving aromatic of the *kalaminthē* (mountain-mint) class — digestive, diuretic, emmenagogue.
- **Assyrian (AT):** warming aromatic carminative/fumigant class — attested at class level; specific identification not established. [USER: verify]
- **Tibetan (FMT):** warming aromatic for cold-phlegm and wind of the chest/stomach. [USER: verify]
- **Ayurveda:** not classically attested for this species (it is a Central Asian / Perso-Anatolian drug, outside the core Ayurvedic materia medica).
- **TCM:** not a classical Chinese materia medica drug, though used in Uyghur/Central-Asian traditional medicine within China.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** The cuneiform *materia medica* records warming aromatic *šammu* used as carminatives, fumigants and stomach remedies, and a Lamiaceae aromatic of this class would sit comfortably among them — but no secure one-to-one identification of *Ziziphora* with any named cuneiform plant is established, and under the standing blocking warning for Assyrian substance identifications, deity attributions and tablet references, none is asserted here. Illness in this corpus is classified by causal agency (divine hand, ghost, oath) rather than by any doctrine of a vital constitution a drug might build; even were the identification secure, the framing would be therapeutic and apotropaic, not cultivational. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *Ziziphora* belongs to the Dioscoridean class of hot, thinning mountain mints — the *kalaminthē* kin — heating and resolving drugs that warm the stomach, thin thick humours, promote menstruation and move the belly. Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system into which such a drug could be graded, so there is no cultivation status to assign; the Galenic three-spirits scheme is later and post-Dioscoridean. What the tradition offers a cultivator of this herb is instead his discipline of attention — recognising the true pungent-aromatic chemotype by its sharp mentholic-peppery assay, gathering it in season from the dry mountain slope where the oil runs strong, and distinguishing it from the weaker garden mints. That is a real practice of provenance and sensory judgement, not a vital-body doctrine. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** Here the plant is *kākutī* (mountain kakuti), classed hot and dry and worked as *muqawwī-ye qalb* (heart-strengthener) and *muqawwī-ye meʿde* (stomach-strengthener), a carminative for flatulent colic and an aromatic for palpitation and "weakness of the heart." The strongest cultivation idea in the Unani corpus is the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture — ageing understood as the depletion of a finite vital endowment, so that longevity practice is conservation rather than acquisition. That frame belongs to the whole regimen, not to this single warming aromatic: the sources retrieved attest *kākutī* as a cardiotonic and digestive drug but record no cultivation grade for it, and no *mufarrih* (gladdening / cardiac-*ruh*) constituent role from the *Kitāb al-Adwiya al-Qalbiyya* is claimed here, nor are *Canon* chapter references invoked. Its standing is therapeutic — a strengthener of the heart's function in illness — not a formal status a practitioner takes to husband the innate heat. **(b) framed:** attested therapeutically, no cultivation status sourced.

**Tibetan (FMT).** In Sowa Rigpa and the adjacent Central-Asian systems, warming aromatic mints of this type are directed against cold-phlegm (*bad kan*) and *rlung* (wind) disorders of the chest and stomach — cough, congestion, digestive stagnation. The three *nyes pa* derive from the three poisons, so pathology is at root a spiritual condition, and medicine is only the third of the four treatment branches: it supports practice without reaching the root. Crucially, Sowa Rigpa has no formal "substances for subtle-body practice" category, and *bcud len* (essence-extraction) is a defined practice within the Four Tantras (Gerke) to which no source retrieved assigns *Ziziphora*. The plant is a humoral corrective for cold-wind of the upper body, not a graded cultivation drug. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a longevity substance, and the tradition that comes closest — Unani, with its cardiotonic *kākutī* — is describing repair of a weakened heart, not a discipline for husbanding vitality. What a cultivator actually does with *Ziziphora* is narrow and defensive: take the mild water infusion of the dried herb in short courses after heavy meals or in flatulent, chest-tight digestion, exploiting the flavonoid-bearing aqueous fraction that carries the measured cardioprotection while keeping pulegone exposure low. The concentrated essential oil is emphatically *not* a cultivation form — it is hepatotoxic and abortifacient, the same pulegone that poisons in pennyroyal — so the honest cultivator's discipline here is one of restraint: the gentle preparation, short courses, and a full stop, never the oil internally.

### Contraindications

- **Pulegone hepatotoxicity — the central safety concern.** *Ziziphora*'s essential oil is pulegone-rich, and pulegone is the toxic principle of pennyroyal. Pulegone is bioactivated by hepatic cytochrome P450 to the reactive metabolite **menthofuran** and further electrophiles that deplete glutathione and bind liver proteins, causing centrilobular hepatic necrosis; the mechanism has been characterised over decades (Gordon & Khojasteh, 2014, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3109/03602532.2014.990032)). Human pennyroyal poisonings document severe, sometimes fatal hepatotoxicity, with measurable pulegone/menthofuran in serum (Anderson et al., 1996; moderate-to-severe toxicity from ≥10 mL of pennyroyal oil, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-124-8-199604150-00004)). **Do not ingest concentrated *Ziziphora* essential oil.** Favour dilute water infusions and short courses; avoid in hepatic impairment.
- **Pregnancy — avoid.** Pulegone is a classical abortifacient and the pennyroyal case literature links it to abortion attempts, maternal hepatotoxicity, and death. **Contraindicated in pregnancy**; avoid the oil in lactation.
- **Paediatric / high-dose culinary caution.** Keep to food-level and dilute-tisane exposure; do not give the oil to children.
- **Drug interactions.** Potential additive hepatic burden with other hepatotoxic agents and with CYP-modulating drugs; theoretical additive effects with antihypertensive/lipid-lowering therapy given the cardiovascular activity — monitor.
