# Massicotite — stang.zil (Clark 1995) / Tangzil (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *A black stone that breaks into sparkling needle-like pieces, one of three "zil" stones of the Explanatory Tantra; "Massicotite" is Clark's English rendering and the sources retrieved do not define the word* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The locus given for this entry is `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, row "Clark 1995 (line 830)". There is no row for it in `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv`: a search of that list for "stang", "zil" and "massic" finds only "Serzil" (line 139) and "Ngulzil" (line 140), the other two stones of the group, each marked as an uncertain identification. Line 830 of `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` reads: "stang.zil: Massicotite: [has four names and is a black stone which breaks into sparkling needlelike pieces. It is so dark in color that it is said to resemble the hair of the Lord Buddha]." It falls under chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (heading at line 792), in "(2) The Powers of Stone Medicines" (line 816). The square brackets are, by Clark's own statement (preface, line 82), commentary taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries.

The line itself states no action. The action comes from the next entries. Clark's list continues with two more stones of the same name-group (line 831): "gser.zil: Vermiculite: Litharge: Mineral Oxide of Lead: [resembles gold ore and has various shapes. It is a soft stone which breaks into long, sparkling needle-like pieces]", and (line 832) "dngul.zil: Mineral Sulphate of Calcium: Carbonate of Lime: [varies in shape and is softer than silver ore. It has a greenish hue and breaks into needle-like pieces. All three types of zil] remove discoloration of the bone." So the statement of use covers the three *zil* together, and Massicotite shares it only as the first of the three.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`) has the same grouping in its list of stone medicines: "Tangzil, Serzil and Ngulzil give color to bones." (line 3356). Its name index lists "stang zil" as the Tibetan form of "Tangzil" (lines 5873-5875) and cites page 199 (line 7508). The two translations do not say the same thing: Clark has "remove discoloration of the bone", the Men-Tsee-Khang rendering has "give color to bones". Both are English renderings of one Tibetan statement that is not on disk; which is closer cannot be told from the sources retrieved. [USER: verify against the Tibetan]

Two cautions on identity. First, the same Tibetan name is used for something else: in the tree medicines, camphor is said to have three types, "shel.ga.bur, which is white and brittle like ice, stang.zil, which is bright yellow with folds, and mang.ga.bur" (Clark line 873). That is a camphor resin, not the black stone, and nothing in the lines read connects the two. Second, in the entry for malachite (line 849), the commentary says that "according to Zur.mkhar" malachite "is a type of stang.zil (see above)", while "others maintain that it is a heavier type of mdung.rtse". So even within the commentary the boundary of stang.zil is not fixed. The word "Massicotite" is not defined in the sources; the lead oxide known in English as massicot is a yellow mineral, whereas the stone described here is black, so the English name is not an obvious match to the description. ### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. The only physical facts in the sources are those in line 830: black, very dark, breaks into sparkling needle-like pieces. Clark's English gloss for the next stone in the list is a lead oxide (line 831), and for the third a calcium sulphate or carbonate (line 832), but no gloss for stang.zil itself states a composition. A black stone that splits into needles is not enough to name a mineral. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved say that the Tibetan Explanatory Tantra lists three needle-breaking stones, of which stang.zil is one, as remedies for discoloration of the bone. The stone cannot be identified with confidence, one of its companions in the list is glossed as a lead oxide, and the complaint it treats is not a clinical condition that can be recognised today. The Codex does not recommend ingesting or applying any of these stones.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Stones glossed as lead oxides are toxic when taken by mouth, and lead accumulates in bone and other tissue. "Discoloration of the bone" is not a recognised modern clinical finding, and no mineral is known to alter the colour of bone. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for stang.zil in the lines read. The chapter-20 entry gives a physical description, the aside about the Buddha's hair as a comparison for colour, and a shared use for the bone. Clark does record which substances are named for essence-extraction elsewhere in the chapter (calcite, line 853; heart-leaved moonseed, line 971; mineral exudate, line 870); stang.zil is not among them. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted. The reference to the hair of the Lord Buddha is a simile for how dark the stone is and does not give the stone a religious status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The record is a short description of a black, needle-breaking stone and one shared statement about bone colour, with a name whose English rendering is doubtful. If the stone is a lead mineral, as one of its companions is glossed, it is toxic. The honest conclusion is a gap: no identification, no preparation and no dose can be taken from the sources. ### Contraindications

- **Possible lead content:** an item in the same list is glossed as a lead oxide, and lead is a cumulative poison; do not take or apply an unidentified lead-bearing stone, powder or "traditional medicine".
- **Unidentified material:** the identity of "Massicotite" is not established in the sources read.
- **Bone disease:** changes in bone need diagnosis and imaging, not a mineral.
- **Pregnancy and children:** unsuitable; children and the fetus are the most vulnerable to lead.
- **Name confusion:** the same Tibetan name, stang.zil, is also used in the text for a type of camphor resin (Clark, line 873); the two should not be confused.
