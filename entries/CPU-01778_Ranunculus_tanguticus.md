# Ranunculus tanguticus — Che tsha, lce tsha, lche.tsha (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Ranunculus tanguticus, the third of three binomials on line 5144 of the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary, which column order sets against "Che tsha" (lce tsha, English "Buttercup"); Clark 1995 prints lche.tsha with Ranunculus acris and Ranunculus pulchellus, and no Ranunculus tanguticus* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Shennong (Yang 1998), Sushruta, Ebers and Assyrian texts on disk for *Ranunculus* found only Yang line 1705, "Shi Long Rui (Semen Ranunculi Sclerati)", a drug of another species, which is not read as this one. The Dioscorides text prints *Ranunculus* only in translators' SUGGESTED lines for Greek drugs (for example lines 6363 to 6365, 6453 and 8511 to 8512), which are also not read as this plant. The row was added from a printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 250) the row is keyed by the **scientific name** "Ranunculus tanguticus", with the locus "Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (line 5144)" and the note "Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". Line 5144 prints **three** binomials on one line ("Cryptothladia polyphylla Angelica glauca Ranunculus tanguticus"). The key is the **third**, a later name on the line and not the first. Clark prints no *Ranunculus tanguticus*, so there is no Clark row for this binomial.

**Identity doubt.** The binomial is set against the Tibetan name by column position only, as set out below. Clark's *lche.tsha* (line 1119) prints *Ranunculus acris* first and *Ranunculus pulchellus* second. The two editions therefore agree on the genus and differ on the species, and the Codex does not choose. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" and (line 68) that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect". The Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note (line 686) says that its botanical terms rest on the information of its own Materia Medica Department, and it names no earlier flora. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "C" block).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The phonetic names are eight (lines 5130 to 5133): "Chaga", "Chaagtik", "Champa", "Chang tsher", "Chawa", "Che tsha", "Chhuma tsi" and "Chhurugpa". The transliterations (lines 5135 to 5140) are "bca'sga", "lcags tig", "lcam pa" (printed "learn pa"), "spyang tsher", "lca ba" (printed "lea ba"), "lce tsha" (printed "Ice tsha"), "chu ma rtsi" and "chu rug pa". The OCR spellings are quoted as they stand. The scientific names are eight (lines 5142 to 5146): "Zingiber officinalis", "Gentianopsus paludosa" (both on 5142), "Malva botka" (5143), "Cryptothladia polyphylla", "Angelica glauca", "Ranunculus tanguticus" (all three on 5144), "Rheum nobile" (5145) and "Helerpestes cymbblar" (5146). Counted in order, "Che tsha" is the sixth phonetic name, "lce tsha" the sixth transliteration and *Ranunculus tanguticus* the sixth scientific name. The English column (lines 5158 to 5160) has six names for the eight rows ("Ginger", "Iron coloured gentian", "Blue mallow", "Thistel", "Buttercup", "Himalayan rhubarb") and "Buttercup" stands next before the name that goes with *Rheum nobile*. The count is checked on two neighbours by Clark: "Chhuma tsi" is seventh against *Rheum nobile* (Clark's *chhu.ma.rtsi*, line 1079, whose first binomial is *Rheum nobile*) and "Chhurugpa" is eighth against *Halerpestes* (Clark's *chhu.rug (sbal.lag)*, line 1032, "Halerpestes sarmentosa"). The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position only. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), line 3493: "Subka heals necrosis, generates heat and drains chhuser. Che tsha and E mong have the same efficacy as the Subka." The glossary prints "dbyi mong" with "Clematis Montana" and "Mountain clematis" (lines 5268 to 5272). In chapter 21, "the groups and methods of compounding medicines" (colophon at line 3689), line 3623 (page 221) lists "Che tsha" in the group that cures "cold disorders associated with baekan": "... Dali, Zira nagpo, Lalaphue, E mong, Subka, Che tsha, Tsabru tsha, Gya tsha, Gyam tsha, Ra tsha and Thai tsha are the group of medicines that cure cold disorders associated with baekan." The index lists "Chetsha 211,221" (line 7290). In chapter 19, the taste groups, line 3215 lists "Che tshawa" among "the group of medicinal substances having a hot taste". That spelling is not the one the index registers, and the Codex does not read it as the same name. [USER: verify] "Chhuser" and "baekan" are Tibetan terms that the editors keep untranslated (note at line 686).

*Clark 1995, the same Tibetan name (read through the name, not the binomial).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997), line 1119: "lche.tsha: Ranunculus acris: Ranunculus pulchellus: [has six names, for example ser.hdab.lnga.pa, bong.bu.lan.tshra, etc., and grows in shady meadows. The superior or female type has a lobed, rough, pale green leaf and a large, yellow five-petalled flower. The inferior or male type has a large, thick leaf shaped like a frog's foot.] Ranunculus removes cold tumors, diphtheria and quinsy, etc., and dries oedema fluid." Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, so the names, habitat and two types are the commentary layer, and the sentence "Ranunculus removes cold tumors ..." is outside the brackets. The next entry (line 1120) ends: "Ranunculus and Clematis both have the same use as Anemone", where Anemone (*srub.ka*, line 1118) "halts necrosis, increases digestive heat, drains lymph [accumulations and removes cold tumors]". In chapter 21 (heading at line 1205), line 1217 lists "Ranunculus pulchellus" in "the category of medicines which cure cold phlegm conditions". The two editions agree in kind that this drug goes with *Clematis* (*E mong*) and with the drug Clark calls *srub.ka* and the Men-Tsee-Khang running text calls *Subka*, and that it is a drug for cold conditions. The Codex reads *Che tsha* and *lche.tsha* as one drug from the spelling and that agreement; neither text says so. Clark's name index prints the Tibetan name as "c(h)he.tsha" (line 1692) and the Latin names *Ranunculus acris* and *Ranunculus pulchellus* (line 1636), and no *Ranunculus tanguticus*. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Ranunculus tanguticus*, *lche.tsha*, *Che tsha* and *Subka* found no entry on this drug. I_1313 (*Malva botka*), I_1231 (*Angelica glauca*), I_1294 (*Gentianopsis paludosa*) and I_1176 (*Rheum nobile*) print other binomials of the same glossary "C" block. ### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Ranunculus* is a genus of the buttercup family (Ranunculaceae), that *Clematis* and *Anemone*, the genera named in the neighbouring lines, belong to the same family, and that many members of the family are known to be irritant to skin and mucous membranes when fresh. Nothing is stated here about this species or about the Tibetan drug's composition.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "removes cold tumors, diphtheria and quinsy, etc., and dries oedema fluid" (Clark, line 1119) and that has "the same efficacy as the Subka", which "heals necrosis, generates heat and drains chhuser" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3493). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only through column position, and they are not Codex protocols. Diphtheria, quinsy and tumours need medical care, and the Codex gives no protocol and no dose for any of them.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary describes leaves and flowers and does not say which part is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's line 1119 prints no taste, power or post-digestive taste for *lche.tsha*. The Men-Tsee-Khang lines read give its place in the group that cures cold disorders associated with baekan (line 3623), which is a use group and not a taste, and print "Che tshawa" in the hot-taste group (line 3215) under a spelling the Codex does not read as the same name. The colours and leaf shapes in Clark's commentary are descriptions of the plant and not qualities.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it removes cold tumours and dries oedema fluid (Clark), and it works like the *Subka* (Men-Tsee-Khang). The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names (*lche.tsha*, *Che tsha*, *Ranunculus*) found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, colophon at line 3950), and the herb entries of Clark's chapter 20 that mention essence extraction (lines 971, 1043, 1078, 1103, 1121 and 1144) do not include it. Its framing is therapeutic only, and its link to this binomial rests on column position.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a drug for cold tumours and swellings in short sentences, with no preparation and no dose, and the Latin name is a positional guess among at least three. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. It is a general fact that many buttercup-family plants blister skin on contact and are toxic if eaten fresh, but that is not shown for this species in the lines read. Diphtheria, quinsy and tumours need medical care, the identity is open, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
