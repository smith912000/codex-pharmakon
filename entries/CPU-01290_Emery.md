# Emery (smyris) — Smuris lithos (SMURIS LITHOS), "Smyris lapis"
## *A hard polishing stone that jewellers use on gems and that is added to antiseptic and caustic medicines and used for moist gums and for cleaning the teeth; the translator's heading reads "opaque crystallised Alumina — Adamantine Spar — Emery, Corundum"* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Emery is chapter 166 of Book Five of the *De Materia Medica*, in the section "METALLIC STONES", headed SMURIS LITHOS (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10692-10695; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` carries it as 5-166, line 10692, with the translator's identification "opaque crystallised Alumina — Adamantine Spar— Emery, Corundum", class mineral; `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 892 has "Adamantine Spar— Emery", mineral, identification marked uncertain; the book's page index prints "SMURIS LITHOS 824", "Smyris lapis 824", "Emery 824" and "Corundum 824"). The translator's heading (line 10694) reads "SUGGESTED: opaque crystallised Alumina — Adamantine Spar— Emery, Corundum".

The whole chapter is two sentences (line 10695): "Jewelers to polish their stones use Smyris lapis. It is good for antiseptic and caustic medicines, and for moist gums and cleaning the teeth."

So the text says three things. First, a craft use: jewellers polish their stones with it, so it is a stone that is harder than the stones polished. The text does not say so in words; the inference is drawn from the use and is stated here as an inference. Second, a pharmaceutical use: it is "good for antiseptic and caustic medicines". The wording does not say how it is used in them, whether as a powder mixed into the preparations or as something else; "antiseptic" is the translator's word for the medicines. Third, a dental use: it is good for "moist gums" and for "cleaning the teeth", which, with its use as a polisher, fits an abrasive tooth powder, but the text does not say it is a powder or how it is applied.

The chapter gives no provenance, no colour or grade to choose by, no method of grinding or washing, no adulteration test, no dose, and no warning. It is one of the short mineral chapters of the book, and nothing further about emery was found by searching the text for the names "smyris", "smuris" and "emery" (only the chapter and the index entries).

*Its neighbours.* The next-but-one chapter, 5-168 AKONE (whetstone, lines 10703-10706, with the translator's gloss "Whetstone"), is another stone used for working edges: "That which comes off the Naxian whetstone (when iron is rubbed on it) fills up bald patches with hair, and prevents maidens' breasts growing big. Taken as a drink with vinegar it reduces the spleen, and is good for epilepsy." That is a different stone with its own uses, and the two are named together only so that they are not confused; the text does not link them.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent beyond the translator's gloss. In general terms, corundum is crystalline aluminium oxide, one of the hardest common minerals (the gem forms are ruby and sapphire); emery is a natural, grey-black mixture of corundum with iron oxides such as magnetite or haematite. It is used as an abrasive and polishing powder. The identification of the Greek stone is the translator's. The text calls it only a stone that jewellers use. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records a hard stone used by jewellers and added to caustic and antiseptic medicines and to preparations for the gums and the teeth. The Codex does not recommend any of these uses. A hard abrasive powder wears tooth enamel and cuts the gum, and no part of the text gives a method that could be followed safely.

The only forms of use named are as a jeweller's polisher and in "antiseptic and caustic medicines" and for "moist gums and cleaning the teeth". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Emery and corundum are abrasives; emery paper and emery powder are still made for grinding and polishing metals and stones. Abrasive powders applied to teeth wear the enamel and are not used in modern dentistry for that purpose. Fine mineral dust is a lung hazard when inhaled. ### Contraindications

- **Teeth and gums:** abrasive powders wear enamel and cut the gums; do not apply emery, corundum or any stone dust to the teeth or gums.
- **Dust:** do not inhale mineral dust; grinding or sanding emery makes fine dust that damages the lungs.
- **Caustic medicines:** the text places the stone in caustic preparations; caustics burn tissue and are not to be improvised.
- **Ingestion:** do not swallow abrasive powders.
- **Identification:** "Adamantine Spar — Emery, Corundum" is the translator's gloss; the text identifies the stone by the craft that uses it.
