### Botanical Identity
Deciduous tree 8–18m; feathery pinnate leaves; greenish-yellow flowers; smooth pale greenish-yellow spherical fruit 2–3cm diameter with 6 vertical furrows; flesh extremely sour and astringent fresh, turning sweet after swallowing (the *yú gān zǐ* "remaining sweetness" quality). Native to tropical and subtropical India, extending to Southeast Asia, China. The fruit is one of the richest natural sources of vitamin C on Earth: 445–900mg/100g (fresh), stable due to tannin-vitamin C complex that protects against oxidative degradation — unlike synthetic ascorbic acid, amla vitamin C persists through cooking and drying.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Sushruta Samhita / Classical Ayurveda (SS)

*Āmalakī* in Charaka Samhita (the primary Ayurvedic text):
> *"Sour, sweet, bitter, astringent, pungent — contains five of the six tastes (only salt absent). Reduces Pitta and Vata; balances Kapha. The supreme rasāyana — the single best fruit for longevity. Benefits the eyes, promotes hair growth, strengthens the immune system, builds ojas. The mother who nurses the constitution."*

*Āmalakī* is ranked as the **single most important individual fruit** in the entire Ayurvedic pharmacopoeia — a distinction it shares with no other substance. It is simultaneously the most important of the three fruits of Triphala (the formulation of *Terminalia chebula*, *Terminalia bellirica*, and *Phyllanthus emblica*) and the stand-alone supreme tonic.

### 2.2 Four Medical Tantras / Tibetan (FMT)

*Skyu ru ra* in Tibetan medicine:
> *"Sour, cooling, heavy. The supreme cooling fruit. Clears bile heat, benefits the eyes, strengthens the body, promotes longevity. Of the three fruits of *ril bu gsum*, this is the chief."*

Tibetan medical cosmology elevates *skyu ru ra* to divine origin — described as having fallen from the hand of the Medicine Buddha (*Sangye Menla*).

### 2.3 Shennong Bencao Jing / TCM (SBJ)

*Yú gān zǐ*:
> *"Sour, sweet; cooling. Clears heat, transforms phlegm, promotes fluid secretion, relieves cough, benefits the throat. Primarily used for sore throat, dry cough with heat, and heat-related digestive conditions."*

The Chinese application is more limited than the Ayurvedic and Tibetan — focused on respiratory/throat and cooling.

### 2.4 Canon of Medicine (CM — minor)

Ibn Sīnā describes *amlaj* as cooling, useful for liver heat, and as an antioxidant fruit — its inclusion in Unani pharmacopoeia reflects South Asian trade influence on Islamic medicine.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Antioxidant (Primary — Superior to Isolated Vitamin C)

**Emblicanin A+B + vitamin C complex**: Emblicanins are the highest-ORAC plant compounds yet identified. Their unique property: they protect vitamin C from oxidation (tannin-ascorbate complex) AND provide independent radical scavenging. This explains why amla vitamin C is more potent and stable than isolated ascorbic acid — the complete fruit chemistry is required.

**ORAC (oxygen radical absorbance capacity)**: Amla fruit: ~3000 µmol TE/g — among the highest of any food plant. Emblicanin B alone: DPPH IC50 ~0.7µg/mL (comparable to reference standard BHT at 0.72µg/mL).

### 5.2 Hepatoprotective

**Gallic acid + corilagin + ellagic acid**: Hepatocyte antioxidant protection; inhibit CYP2E1 (toxic oxidation pathway); upregulate hepatic GSH (glutathione); inhibit lipid peroxidation. Demonstrated protective against CCl4, alcohol, and paracetamol-induced hepatotoxicity.

### 5.3 Anti-Ageing / Rasāyana Mechanisms

**Ellagic acid + emblicanins**: Inhibit collagen-degrading MMP (matrix metalloproteinase) → preserve skin collagen. Activate Nrf2 → upregulate antioxidant enzymes. Preliminary: SIRT1 activation suggesting telomere-protective effects (the *rasāyana* longevity mechanism).

### 5.4 Anti-Diabetic

**Gallic acid + ellagic acid**: α-Glucosidase inhibition → reduced post-prandial glucose. Quercetin: AMPK activation → insulin sensitisation. Amla significantly reduces blood glucose in multiple animal models and several human trials.

### 5.5 Hair Growth

**Tannins + vitamin C**: Inhibit 5α-reductase → reduce scalp DHT (androgenetic alopecia mechanism). Stimulate proliferation of dermal papilla cells. Traditional amla oil for hair and the modern trichological application converge. Cosmetic use of amla in hair preparations is among the most ancient documented cosmetic practices.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Lipids**: Thilakchand et al. (2013) review: Multiple RCTs confirm significant reduction in LDL cholesterol, triglycerides, and total cholesterol with amla extract. Normalization of HDL/LDL ratio.

**Diabetes**: Akhtar (2011): Amla extract (1g/day) significantly reduced fasting blood glucose in T2DM vs metformin controls (non-significant difference from metformin at same glucose endpoint).

**Gastric**: Multiple studies: Amla extract inhibits *H. pylori* adherence; reduces gastric acid secretion; protects gastric mucosa. Non-inferior to ranitidine for gastric acid reduction in one pilot trial.

**Inflammation**: Multiple animal model studies: amla significantly reduces CRP, TNF-α, IL-6. Human trial data limited but consistent with mechanistic predictions.

**Vitamin C absorption**: Unique finding: amla vitamin C absorbed 35% more efficiently than synthetic ascorbic acid (tannin-mediated slower release → higher intestinal residence time → greater absorption).

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The five-taste fruit**: Classical Ayurvedic pharmacology classifies all substances by taste (*rasa*) — and a food/medicine possesses at most 3-4 tastes. *Āmalakī* has five of the six classical tastes (sour, sweet, bitter, astringent, pungent — only salt absent). In the Ayurvedic system, a substance with five tastes simultaneously covers virtually every physiological sphere — each taste corresponds to different tissue levels and dosha effects. The five-taste attribution is not mystical: the complex polyphenol chemistry of amla produces sour (ascorbic acid), sweet (glucose, fructose — revealed on longer chewing), bitter (tannins), astringent (tannins binding salivary proteins), and pungent (terpenoids). The Charaka Samhita's five-taste description is accurate organoleptic pharmacology.

**Dhātrī — the divine nurse**: The epithet *dhātrī* (nurse/mother) given to amla reflects a pharmacological observation: amla supports tissue building, antioxidant defence, immune function, and liver protection simultaneously — nurturing multiple organ systems. A plant whose range of action encompasses so many fundamental physiological processes that it "nurses the body like a mother" is the Ayurvedic conceptual framework for what pharmacology would call pleiotropic tonic.

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Sour, sweet, bitter; cooling. Channels: Liver (肝), Lung (肺), Stomach (胃). Clears heat, generates fluids, nourishes Yin.

**Ayurvedic**: Sour, sweet, astringent; cooling. Reduces Pitta ↓↓, reduces Vata ↓, balances Kapha. The supreme *Pitta* rasāyana. *Cakṣuṣya* (eye-beneficial), *Keśya* (hair-beneficial), *Yakrit* (hepatic), *Rasāyana* (rejuvenating).

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Low stomach acid (hypochlorhydria)**: High sour content — monitor with achlorhydria
- **Anticoagulants**: Possible platelet inhibition — monitor INR with warfarin
- Very high doses (>5g pure extract): Laxative effect
- **Diarrhoea (active)**: Avoid until resolved — cooling and slightly laxative
- Among the safest substances in the Codex; consumed as daily food throughout South Asia
