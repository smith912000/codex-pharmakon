# Lepisorus waltonii — brag.spos, Drakpoe, the "queen fern" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Lepisorus waltonii ching, the first of two identifications Clark 1995 prints for brag.spos (the other is Lepisorus scolopendrium); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary sets a different binomial, Lepisorus clathratus, beside Drakpoe by column position* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. Only Clark prints *Lepisorus waltonii* (line 1069 and the Latin-name index, line 1609). A search of Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 for "waltonii" found nothing. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk for the genus name *Lepisorus* found nothing. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 168) the Tibetan name *brag.spos* is recorded at Clark line 1069 with the identification "Lepisorus waltonii ching; Lepisorus scolopendrium" and the note "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The key, *Lepisorus waltonii*, is therefore the first of the two names Clark prints, and not a later one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance". Both of Clark's names are *Lepisorus* species, so the doubt is about the species and not the genus, as far as Clark goes.

**Identity doubt.** The two Tibetan editions do not agree on a species. Clark prints *waltonii* and *scolopendrium*; the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary, by column position, sets *Lepisorus clathratus* beside "Drakpoe" (see below). The Codex cannot say from the sources read which species the Tibetan texts meant, and the entry stands for the Tibetan drug. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1069).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). It reads:

"brag.spos: Lepisorus waltonii ching: Lepisorus scolopendrium: [has four names, for example, btsun.mo.re.ral, etc. It has a swordlike, narrow, yellow leaf with four lobes at the base covered with dewdrops. (At least two similar subtypes have now been classified.)] Lepisorus heals wounds, dries pus, sustains bone resin, [cures channel fevers and is beneficial for injuries and enlargement of the diaphragm]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, and those in parentheses from the oral teaching of two named doctors. Inside the square brackets (the commentary layer) are the number of names, the name *btsun.mo.re.ral*, the leaf, and the clauses "cures channel fevers and is beneficial for injuries and enlargement of the diaphragm". The remark that "at least two similar subtypes have now been classified" is in parentheses inside the brackets, so it is oral instruction in Clark's terms. Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the two Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the sentence "Lepisorus heals wounds, dries pus, sustains bone resin". The word "Lepisorus" in that sentence is Clark's own word for the drug. The line gives no taste, no power, no part used and no preparation. Clark does not gloss *btsun.mo.re.ral* in the line, and the Codex does not translate it.

*Clark 1995, the fern gloss (line 1084).* The entry for *ldum.bu.re.ral* (line 1084), on a drug identified as *Drynaria propinqua* and *Polystichum squarrosum*, ends with a gloss that "is said to be the 'king fern', whilst brags.spos (Lepisorus) is the 'queen fern' and brags.skya.ha.wo (Corallodiscus) is the 'minister fern'". The same trio is therefore ranked in Clark's text, with this drug second. The preceding entry, *re.ral* (line 1083), gives *rgyal.po.re.ral* with the gloss "lit. 'king fern'", and *brag.skya.ha.wo* (line 1068) gives *blon.po.re.ral* with "minister fern".

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* In chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), line 3448 reads, in the middle of a longer passage: "Drakpoe treats wounds, dries up pus and protects spongy bone." The index lists "Drakpoe 209" (line 7347). The glossary's transliteration "brag lcham brag spos" (line 5208) shows that "brag spos" is Clark's *brag.spos*. Clark's "heals wounds, dries pus, sustains bone resin" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "treats wounds, dries up pus and protects spongy bone" agree on wounds and pus and are worded differently on the third ("bone resin" and "spongy bone"); the Codex does not treat the third pair as one term. That the "Drakpoe" of the running text is the same drug as Clark's *brag.spos* rests on the transliteration, the order and the agreement in kind. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "D" block, lines 5179 to 5261).* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate runs. Counting each name separately, "Drakpoe" (line 5190) is the sixteenth of twenty-two phonetic names, and the sixteenth scientific name is "Lepisorus clathratus" (line 5246), with the English "Pyrrosia leaf" (line 5249). The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position. The count is treated in I_1308. The glossary prints no *waltonii* and no *scolopendrium*. The two Tibetan texts agree on the Tibetan name, that it is a *Lepisorus*, and that it is a wound drug; they differ on the species, and the Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** The same Tibetan drug is treated in I_1308 (*Lepisorus clathratus*, the glossary's binomial for "Drakpoe", written in this batch). The two entries are one drug under two binomials, one drawn from each edition; they are not merged, and each says so. A search of the entries folder for *Lepisorus*, *brag.spos* and *Drakpoe* found these other entries that quote the line, without being entries on the drug: I_1269 (*Drynaria propinqua*) and I_1225 (*Adiantum pedatum*) quote the line 1084 gloss and, in I_1269, line 1069 itself; I_1270 (*Dryopteris fragrans*) mentions *brag.spos* as the "queen fern" in checking its own count; I_1248 (*Caragana gerardiana*) quotes the glossary line beside *Lepisorus clathratus*. No entry on *Lepisorus waltonii* or *Lepisorus scolopendrium* was found. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Lepisorus* is a genus of ferns (family Polypodiaceae in the usual treatment, which the Codex has not checked against a flora), and that ferns bear neither flowers nor seeds. Clark's commentary describes the plant by its leaf only. Which part was used is not said, and no general statement about constituents is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "heals wounds, dries pus, sustains bone resin" (Clark, line 1069), whose commentary adds "cures channel fevers and is beneficial for injuries and enlargement of the diaphragm", and "treats wounds, dries up pus and protects spongy bone" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3448). These are the texts' claims in their own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and does not translate the Tibetan terms into modern diagnoses. Wounds with pus need medical care.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** No taste, power or post-digestive taste is printed for this drug in the lines read. Clark's commentary (line 1069) describes the leaf only, and Men-Tsee-Khang prints none. The actions printed are healing wounds, drying pus and sustaining bone resin (Clark) and treating wounds, drying up pus and protecting spongy bone (Men-Tsee-Khang). The "queen fern" rank (Clark, line 1084) is a ranking among three ferns and is not a quality.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it heals wounds, dries pus and sustains bone resin, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text gives a parallel set of actions. The "queen fern" in Clark's gloss (line 1084) names a place in a trio of ferns, "king", "queen" and "minister", and is not a cultivation status. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes, and not in the rejuvenation material; no line read places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name, not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a wound and pus drug in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and the species behind the Tibetan name differs between the two editions. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Wounds, pus and fever need medical assessment; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Because the editions differ on the species, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read.
