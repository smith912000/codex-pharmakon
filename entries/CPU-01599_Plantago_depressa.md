# Plantago depressa — tha.ram, Tharam, tha rams (rGyud-bzhi); "Greater plantain"

## *Plantago depressa Willd, the first of two binomials Clark 1995 prints for tha.ram (with Plantago majorvulgaris latifolia var. asiatica), and the one the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints in its scientific-name column, where a nearby English column prints "Greater plantain"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts in this row.** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and the row's binomial is the first of two on Clark's line. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed names (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1123).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads: "tha.ram: Plantago depressa Willd: Plantago majorvulgaris latifolia var. asiatica: [has four names, for example be.khur, and grows in fields and by the roadside. It has a glossy, green lobed leaf, grows tall and erect, has a green petiole and a green inflorescence. One sub-species has a pale green leaf, a yellow multiple flower and is without petiole.] Plantago has sweet and astringent tastes, heals wounds and dries lymph accumulations."

Clark's preface (line 82) says "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". So the whole bracketed part of the entry (four names, habitat, description, the pale-leaved sub-species) is the commentary layer. What stands outside the brackets is the root-text layer as Clark gives it: "Plantago has sweet and astringent tastes, heals wounds and dries lymph accumulations." The text thus gives two tastes and two actions, and no power.

Three more Clark lines name the drug. In the same section, the closing sentence of the larkspur entry (line 1128) reads: "Plantago, Polygonum alopecuroides and larkspur halt diarrhea." In chapter 21, "The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines" (heading at line 1205), line 1222 reads: "The class of anti-diarrhoeals consists of both wild and cultivated types of Bael fruit, Schizandra sphaerandra, Acorn, powdered root (of, for example, old pine tree), Plantain (Plantago depressa), Plantago lanceolata, Bastard Teak, the blood of Hodgson's antelope and Larkspur (Delphinium grandiflora Linn.)". In the section on prepared foods (line 696 begins it) line 698 reads: "Stew of mallow and Plantago depressa increases digestive heat and stops diarrhoea." Clark's name index prints *tha.ram* (line 1703) and *Plantago depressa* (line 1629).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (Explanatory Tantra, glossary).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate blocks. The scientific name "Plantago depressa" is at line 5960 and the English "Greater plantain" at line 5962. In the phonetic column "Tharam" is at line 5888, and in the Tibetan column "tha rams" at line 5891. The glossary does not print them on one row, and the columns hold different numbers of entries, so the pairing of "Tharam" with "Plantago depressa" is not printed. The Codex does not state that the glossary pairs them. A separate glossary entry, "Plantago lanceolata", stands at line 5531 with "Ribwort plantain" at line 5533. [USER: verify]

The Men-Tsee-Khang text uses the name in two places and its index. Chapter 20, line 3493: "Tharam, Naram and Jhakang stops diarrhea." Chapter 21, line 3644: "Kapae, Bilwa, Dhatig, Moen chhara, Mak, Tharam, Naram, Gya kyeg, antelope's blood and Jhakang are the group of medicines that cure diarrhea." The index (line 7510) gives "Tharam 211, 222". That *Tharam* is a form of *tha.ram* is a reading from the sound of the name and from the agreement on diarrhoea, and is not stated in the text. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *tha.ram*, *Tharam* and *Plantago depressa* found no existing entry on this drug. Three entries touch the same Clark lines or genus: III_197 (Tibetan antelope blood) quotes line 1222, the anti-diarrhoeal class, in which both the blood and *Plantago depressa* stand; I_676 (*Plantago lanceolata*) is the entry on Clark's second plantain in the same class, and carries no FMT tag at present; and I_189 and I_469 (*Plantago major*) are entries on the common plantain, which Clark's second binomial for this drug ("Plantago majorvulgaris latifolia var. asiatica") and the glossary's English "Greater plantain" resemble in name only. Neither carries an FMT tag, and whether the Tibetan drug is that plant is not settled by the lines read. None of them is an entry on *tha.ram*.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Plantago* is a genus of the plantain family (Plantaginaceae) and that plantain leaves and seeds are generally known to contain mucilage. Which species and which part the Tibetan drug is (Clark names the plant, and line 698 a stew) is only as stated above. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the Tibetan drug. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a plant of sweet and astringent taste that heals wounds and dries lymph accumulations, that halts diarrhoea (Clark lines 1128 and 1222, Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3493 and 3644) and that, stewed with mallow, increases digestive heat and stops diarrhoea (Clark line 698). These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for wounds or diarrhoea, and the botanical identity is uncertain.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give one preparation for *tha.ram*: a stew of mallow and *Plantago depressa* (Clark, line 698). No quantity, frequency or duration is given there or elsewhere in the lines read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark line 1123 prints "sweet and astringent tastes" outside the square brackets. It prints no power (hot, cool, heavy or light). The Men-Tsee-Khang text places *Tharam* among the medicines that stop or cure diarrhoea (lines 3493 and 3644). No other quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes the plant by its tastes and by what it does: sweet and astringent, healing wounds, drying lymph accumulations and halting diarrhoea, and it appears in a food (the stew, line 698) and in the anti-diarrhoeal class (line 1222). The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this entry places *tha.ram* in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. In the lines read, the essence-extraction mentions near this entry are for other plants (for example at lines 853, 870 and 971), and *tha.ram* is not named there. Its framing is therapeutic and dietary only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a wound-healing, lymph-drying and anti-diarrhoeal drug, and the plant of a stew, in the Tibetan texts, and nothing more can be said from them. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. Diarrhoea that persists needs medical care, since dehydration is a general risk, and wounds that do not heal need assessment. The botanical identity is uncertain (two binomials in Clark), so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
