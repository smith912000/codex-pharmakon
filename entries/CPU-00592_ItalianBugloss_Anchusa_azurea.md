# Italian Bugloss — Large Blue Alkanet, Italian Alkanet, Hamham

## *Anchusa azurea* Mill. | Family: Boraginaceae

**Tradition Tags:** EP (Ebers), AT (Assyrian), CM (Avicenna), DMM (Dioscorides)
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and Moist (first–second degree) — a cooling, softening, demulcent agent in the Galenic-Unani scheme
**Training Phase:** Foundation

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ebers Papyrus (EP):** The Egyptian medical papyri record numerous cooling, emollient plant applications for inflamed skin, burns, and "hot" swellings — soothing poultices of mucilaginous herbs laid on heat and sores. The blue-flowered borages of the eastern Mediterranean, of which *Anchusa* is a conspicuous member, belong to this class of softening, wound-dressing plants used to draw heat from injured skin.

**Assyrian (AT):** Mesopotamian recipes call on rough-leaved, red-rooted herbs for washes and dressings on burns and ulcers. The alkanet-type roots (*Anchusa* and its relatives) yield red pigments long associated in the region's practice with wound and skin preparations, tying the plant to a tradition of topical dressings for hot, damaged tissue.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** In *De Materia Medica*, Dioscorides describes several *ankhousa* (alkanet/anchusa) species, valued chiefly for the red dye of their roots and for external applications — the root, worked with oil or wax, applied to burns, ulcers, and inflammations, and taken in some preparations for complaints of the spleen and for the bites of venomous creatures. The emphasis is emphatically on the root and on external, cooling use.

**Avicenna (CM):** The *Qanun* places the demulcent, cooling borages among remedies that moisten and soothe hot, dry conditions, cool inflamed surfaces, and gladden the heart — the humoral portrait attached across the family to *Borago* and its blue-flowered kin. *Anchusa azurea* inherits this cooling-emollient classification within the Unani scheme.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The plant's vivid azure flower and blood-red root gave it a doctrine-of-signatures dual reading: the blue for cooling and calming (the "gladdening" borage temperament), the red root for the blood and for wounds. In Kenetica, Italian bugloss is a **Foundation-phase** plant precisely because its action is gentle, cooling, and restorative rather than forceful — a base-layer soother. The symbolism is partly borne out: the aerial parts are genuinely rich in antioxidant phenolics and the root in red naphthoquinone pigments (shikonin-type) with real wound-relevant activity. But the same family carries a serious hidden hazard (see Contraindications), so the "gentle" reading applies to *external* use, not to casual internal consumption.

### Active Components

*Anchusa azurea* combines the demulcent, phenolic-rich aerial chemistry of the borages with the pigmented-root chemistry of the alkanets — and, as a Boraginaceae member, the characteristic alkaloid hazard of the family.

- **Phenolic acids** — notably **rosmarinic acid**, plus caffeic/hydrocaffeic and chlorogenic acids; **rutin** and other flavonoids in the shoot.
- **Allantoin** — the tissue-repair-associated compound common to the family.
- **Naphthoquinones** — **shikonin/alkannin-type** red pigments in the root.
- **Fatty acids and further flavonoids/phenols** in the aerial parts (LC-MS characterised).
- **⚠️ Pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs)** — the Boraginaceae signature class; a genuine hepatotoxic hazard that governs all internal-use guidance below.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Italian bugloss is a **Foundation-phase**, principally **topical** plant: a cooling, emollient, antioxidant dressing for hot, damaged, or inflamed skin — burns, minor wounds, and irritated surfaces. The evidence base and the tradition agree most strongly here. Its role in Kenetica is a base-layer soother: it does not move, purge, or stimulate; it calms and supports repair of surface tissue. Internal use as a demulcent "blood-cooler" is traditional but is deliberately **not recommended** in this system because of the pyrrolizidine-alkaloid liver risk. Treat it as a skin remedy, not a tea.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:

- **Al-Qaisi et al. (2024), *Burns*** — a methanolic extract of *A. azurea* aerial parts, formulated as 1% and 10% ointments, significantly accelerated burn-wound healing in Wistar rats (78.6% and 84.8% contraction at 12 days) with histological evidence of collagen deposition and re-epithelialisation and no dermal toxicity; the extract scavenged 89.78% of DPPH at 500 µg/mL (IC50 213.6 µg/mL) and shifted IL-6/IL-10 cytokine balance. DOI: [10.1016/j.burns.2024.05.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.burns.2024.05.001).
- **Boussoualim et al. (2022), *Anti-inflammatory & Anti-allergy Agents in Medicinal Chemistry*** — phenolic fractions of *A. azurea* showed dose-dependent antioxidant (DPPH, β-carotene bleaching), anti-hemolytic, and in-vivo anti-inflammatory (reduced ear oedema) activity, supporting the plant's traditional Algerian medicinal use (locally "hamham"). DOI: [10.2174/1871523020666211201162917](https://doi.org/10.2174/1871523020666211201162917).
- **Dresler et al. (2017), *Pharmaceutical Biology***  — a comparative Boraginaceae survey including *A. azurea* quantified shared secondary metabolites (allantoin, rosmarinic acid, rutin, chlorogenic/hydrocaffeic acids in shoots; shikonin in roots), characterising the phytochemical profile the family shares. DOI: [10.1080/13880209.2016.1265986](https://doi.org/10.1080/13880209.2016.1265986).

**Evidence grade:** The topical wound-healing, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions are supported by **preclinical animal and in-vitro data** — promising but not clinically validated in humans. The internal/humoral uses are **traditional**. The pyrrolizidine-alkaloid hazard is a **class-level, well-established** toxicological concern for Boraginaceae, not a hypothetical one.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Cold and Moist, first–second degree. Demulcent, emollient, refrigerant; "gladdening" borage temperament, cooling to hot/dry states.
- **TCM (analogical):** cool, sweet/bland, moistening — no classical entry; classified by property analogy to cooling demulcents.
- **Ayurvedic (analogical):** *rasa* sweet (madhura) and slightly bitter (tikta); *virya* cooling (shita); *vipaka* sweet; pitta-pacifying, potentially kapha-increasing. Analogy only; not a classical dravya.

### Contraindications

**⚠️ PYRROLIZIDINE-ALKALOID HEPATOTOXICITY — THE PRIMARY INTERNAL HAZARD.** As a member of the **Boraginaceae**, *Anchusa azurea* belongs to a family well documented to contain **pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs)**, a class of compounds that are hepatotoxic and can cause **hepatic veno-occlusive disease (sinusoidal obstruction syndrome)**, are genotoxic/carcinogenic in animal models, and are teratogenic. Regulatory limits on PA intake exist for exactly this reason.

- **Do not take internally** as a tea, infusion, or unpurified extract, and especially **not chronically**. Cumulative low-dose exposure is the dangerous pattern.
- **Contraindicated in pregnancy and lactation** in any internal form — PAs cross the placenta and pass into milk.
- **Contraindicated in liver disease** or with hepatotoxic medication.
- **Prefer external use.** Topical application to intact-margin skin is the evidence-supported route; even then avoid application to large open wounds where systemic absorption is plausible. [USER: verify PA content of any product before internal use.]
- **Identification / adulteration:** blue-flowered borages are easily confused; ensure correct identification and avoid wild internal use.
