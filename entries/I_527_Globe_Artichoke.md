# I-527 | Globe Artichoke

**Binomial:** *Cynara cardunculus* L. var. *scolymus* (L.) Fiori
**Synonyms:** *Cynara scolymus* L. (widely used synonym, accepted in PhEur); *Cynara cardunculus* L. subsp. *scolymus* (L.) Hegi
**Family:** Asteraceae (Cardueae/Cynareae)
**Common Names:** Globe artichoke; cultivated artichoke; *Artischocke* (German); *Artichaut* (French); *Alcachofa* (Spanish); *Carciofo* (Italian)
**Part Used:** Leaf (*Cynarae folium* PhEur); inflorescence receptacle (edible food part — separate pharmacological consideration)
**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (hepatobiliary, lipid-regulatory) + ◈ Jing (constitutional lipid metabolism)

---

## I. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

The genus *Cynara* derives from Greek *kyon/kynaros* (dog) — possibly referring to the dog-tooth-like involucre spines, or from the Greek legend that Zeus transformed a maiden named Cynara into a thistle-like plant when she refused his advances. The cultivated globe artichoke (*C. cardunculus* var. *scolymus*) is a domestication of the wild cardoon (*C. cardunculus* L. var. *sylvestris*) from the Mediterranean, developed primarily in Italy and Spain. The Arabic name *al-kharshuf* → Spanish *alcachofa* → Italian *carcioffo* traces the Moorish agricultural transmission of artichoke cultivation into Iberian and Italian farming during the medieval period.

**Taxonomy note:** PhEur and much pharmacological literature use *Cynara scolymus* L. (Linnaeus's original species-level designation) while current botanical taxonomy treats cultivated artichoke as a variety of *C. cardunculus*. Both names refer to the same plant; PhEur *Cynarae folium* specifies *C. scolymus* L. or *Cynara cardunculus* L. The traditional Latin binomial *C. scolymus* is retained here as the PhEur reference.

**Pharmaceutical part distinction:**
- **LEAF** (*Cynarae folium*): bitter compounds, phenolic acids — the pharmaceutical raw material; Commission E and EMA approved.
- **INFLORESCENCE RECEPTACLE** (the edible "artichoke heart"): lower phenolic content, fleshy, food; the food part is pharmacologically distinct from the pharmaceutical leaf.
- **ROOT:** historical use for diuresis; not Commission E standardized.

## III. Convergence Analysis

Globe artichoke sits immediately adjacent to milk thistle (I-526) in the Asteraceae Cardueae/Cynareae sequence — both are thistles, both are hepatobiliary herbs, both have Mediterranean-Islamic cross-traditional convergence on liver function. The pharmacological distinction is important:

| Feature | Milk Thistle (I-526) | Globe Artichoke (I-527) |
|---|---|---|
| Primary actives | Flavonolignans (silymarin) | Phenolic acids (cynarin, CGA) |
| Primary mechanism | Hepatoprotective (membrane stabilization, antifibrotic) | Choleretic (bile production/flow) + hypolipidemic |
| Clinical strength | Severe hepatotoxicity, cirrhosis (Level A) | Dyslipidemia, dyspepsia (Level B) |
| Traditional framing | "Nourish/protect liver" | "Open/deobstruct liver/bile" |
| Energetic tier | ◈ Jing (constitutional regeneration) | ◉ Qi (functional flow) |

The traditional distinction between "strengthening" (milk thistle role in Galenic terminology) and "opening/deobstructing" (artichoke role) maps onto the pharmacological distinction between protective/regenerative mechanisms vs. flow-enhancing/lipid-metabolic mechanisms. The traditional herbalists achieved mechanistically accurate differentiation without chemistry.

## IV. Phytochemical Composition

### A. Caffeoylquinic Acid Derivatives — Primary Actives

**Cynarin (1,3-dicaffeoylquinic acid; 1,3-O-dicaffeoylquinic acid):** The signature *Cynara* compound; 0.5–2.0% DW leaf; bitter taste; primary historical marker for artichoke preparations. Hydrolysis to chlorogenic acid + caffeic acid occurs readily in aqueous extract and in vivo. Cynarin specifically induces a "water-sweet taste after eating," the "artichoke effect" — a sensory phenomenon due to cynarin blocking sweet-taste receptors, causing everything consumed after artichoke to taste sweet (the artichoke "taste paradox" documented by Linda Bartoshuk in the 1970s). This gustatory effect is irrelevant to hepatobiliary pharmacology but is pharmacologically real.

**Chlorogenic acid (5-O-caffeoylquinic acid; CGA):** 1.0–4.0% DW leaf — the highest CGA levels in any PhEur-standardized herb; antioxidant (hydrogen atom transfer and single electron transfer); anti-diabetic (α-glucosidase inhibition, SGLT1 inhibition); anti-inflammatory; choleretic. CGA is the primary HPLC marker for PhEur quality assessment. **PhEur (*Cynarae folium*): ≥0.8% chlorogenic acid** (expressed as chlorogenic acid, MW 354.3).

**Isochlorogenic acids (3,4-, 3,5-, 4,5-dicaffeoylquinic acids):** Additional caffeoylquinic acid isomers; anti-inflammatory; antioxidant.

### B. Flavonoids (0.5–2.0% DW)

**Luteolin-7-O-glucoside (cynaroside):** Primary flavonoid; anti-inflammatory (NF-κB, COX-2); hepatoprotective (cytochrome P450-mediated protection); antioxidant.

**Luteolin-7-O-rutinoside (scolymoside):** Characteristic artichoke flavonoid; bile acid synthesis regulation.

**Apigenin-7-O-glucoside, luteolin aglycone:** Anti-inflammatory; apigenin-GABA-A PAM (recurring Codex compound).

**Cynaroside (luteolin-7-glucoside) as secondary PhEur quality marker:** ≥3.0% flavonoids expressed as luteolin-7-glucoside in some PhEur specifications.

### C. Sesquiterpene Lactones (Bitter Compounds)

**Cynaropicrin** (guaianolide sesquiterpene lactone; 0.5–2.5% DW): Primary bitter compound; responsible for the intensely bitter taste of artichoke leaf; stimulates bitter taste receptors on the tongue → vagal reflex → bile production (choleretic reflex); NF-κB inhibition (α-methylene-γ-lactone Michael acceptor, as in helenalin/parthenolide); contact allergen potential (lower than helenalin but present — Asteraceae cross-sensitivity applies).

**Grosheimin, aguerin B:** Minor sesquiterpene lactones; additional bitter contribution.

### D. Polyphenols (Total)

Total polyphenol content of artichoke leaf: 4–8% DW expressed as gallic acid equivalents — among the highest total polyphenol contents for a standardized herbal medicine in the Codex. The combination of high CGA + luteolin + cynaropicrin delivers a broad-spectrum antioxidant and anti-inflammatory profile.

### E. Inulin (Root and Stem — Less in Leaf)

**Inulin (β-2,1-fructan; prebiotic):** 15–20% DW in root/stem; lower in leaf pharmaceutical material; prebiotic effect (selective *Bifidobacterium* + *Lactobacillus* feeding); insulin-sensitizing (fecal microbiome modification); relevant for NAFLD and MetS indication synergy.

### F. Bitter Acids

**Artichoke acids (caffeic acid conjugates):** Additional bitter phenolics contributing to the choleretic reflex.

## V. Mechanisms of Action

### A. Choleretic (Bile Production Stimulation)

**Cynarin + cynaropicrin bitter receptor mechanism:** Bitter compounds (especially cynaropicrin) activate bitter taste receptor type 2 (TAS2R) family on the tongue and possibly in the GI tract → gustatory reflex activation → vagal stimulation → increased hepatic bile production and secretion. This is the "bitters" choleretic mechanism shared by artichoke, dandelion (I-518), gentian, and other traditional digestive bitters — but artichoke has the strongest evidence and standardized pharmaceutical form.

**Direct hepatocyte mechanism:** Cynarin and CGA directly stimulate hepatocyte bile acid secretion (MRP2/ABCC2 transporter induction) and increase HMG-CoA reductase expression modulation → bile acid synthesis changes → altered hepatic lipid processing.

**Clinical correlate:** Increased bile flow → improved fat emulsification and absorption → reduced bloating and flatulence from fat maldigestion → the traditional dyspeptic indication of artichoke is mechanistically based on this choleretic enhancement.

### B. Hypolipidemic (Primary Evidence-Based Indication)

**HMG-CoA reductase inhibition (partial, indirect):** Luteolin and CGA inhibit HMG-CoA reductase (rate-limiting enzyme in cholesterol biosynthesis) — not competitively (unlike statins) but via AMPK pathway activation → reduced hepatic cholesterol synthesis. Effect is less potent than statins but meaningful in moderate dyslipidemia.

**PCSK9 downregulation:** Emerging evidence that artichoke extract reduces PCSK9 expression → increased LDL receptor expression → enhanced LDL-C clearance. Mechanism distinct from and potentially complementary to statins (which upregulate PCSK9 as a compensatory response, reducing statin efficacy).

**Increased cholesterol conversion to bile acids:** Enhanced bile acid synthesis from cholesterol → reduced circulating LDL pool. Choleretic and hypolipidemic mechanisms are interconnected.

**Triglyceride reduction:** Multiple trial observations of TG reduction; mechanism: PPAR-α activation (CGA) → increased fatty acid β-oxidation → reduced hepatic TG synthesis and VLDL secretion.

### C. Antioxidant (Hepatic)

CGA and luteolin scavenge ROS directly (very high ORAC values for CGA-rich extracts) and induce hepatic Nrf2 → HO-1, NQO1, and GST expression → enhanced antioxidant enzyme defense in hepatocytes. This mechanism contributes to the mild hepatoprotective effect (not as potent as silymarin) and the NASH-relevant antioxidant benefit.

### D. Anti-inflammatory (NF-κB via Cynaropicrin)

Cynaropicrin's α-methylene-γ-lactone alkylates p65-Cys38 → NF-κB suppression → reduced hepatic and systemic inflammatory cytokines. Less potent than helenalin (arnica) or parthenolide (feverfew) at equivalent concentrations — cynaropicrin is metabolized and diluted by the time it reaches systemic circulation from GI absorption — but contributes to local GI anti-inflammatory and hepatic anti-inflammatory effects.

### E. Prebiotic / Gut-Liver Axis

The inulin fraction (present in whole artichoke food preparations; lower in pharmaceutical leaf extract) modulates gut microbiome → increased SCFA production (butyrate, propionate) → improved gut barrier function → reduced LPS translocation from gut to liver via portal circulation → reduced hepatic Kupffer cell activation → reduced NASH progression. This is the contemporary gut-liver axis mechanism applicable to the food use of artichoke; it is less relevant for standardized leaf extract preparations that contain less inulin.

## VI. Clinical Evidence

### A. Dyslipidemia (Level A)

**Englisch W et al. (2000)** *Arzneimittelforschung* 50(3): 260–265. Double-blind RCT, n=143 patients with total cholesterol >280 mg/dL; artichoke dry extract (1,800 mg/day corresponding to 18 mg cynarin equivalent) vs. placebo; 6 weeks.

**Results:** Total cholesterol reduced by 18.5% in artichoke group vs. 8.6% in placebo group (P<0.05). LDL-C reduced by 22.9% vs. 6.3% (P<0.01). TG and HDL-C: non-significant changes. **Level A evidence for LDL-C reduction in moderate hypercholesterolemia.**

**Rondanelli M et al. (2011)** *Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine* 17(2): 143–150. RCT, n=67 overweight dyslipidemic adults; artichoke extract 2,700 mg/day; 8 weeks; LDL-C −12.5%, TG −12.9% vs. placebo (P<0.05 for both). Level B/A (smaller).

**Meta-analysis (Sahebkar 2018):** 5 RCTs, n=468 participants; pooled effect: LDL-C −0.40 mmol/L (95% CI −0.56 to −0.24); TC −0.59 mmol/L; TG −0.11 mmol/L; HDL-C non-significant. **Level A (pooled): statistically significant and clinically modest LDL-C reduction (~10–18%) in moderate hypercholesterolemia.**

*Clinical positioning:* Artichoke extract is not a statin substitute for high-risk CVD patients. In mild-to-moderate hypercholesterolemia where pharmacological treatment is not yet indicated (LDL-C 130–160 mg/dL without high CVD risk), artichoke extract provides a pharmacologically validated and well-tolerated alternative to watchful waiting or lifestyle modification alone.

### B. Dyspepsia / Functional GI (Level A)

**Holtmann G et al. (2003)** *Aliment Pharmacol Ther* 18(11–12): 1099–1105. RCT, n=247 patients with functional dyspepsia; artichoke leaf extract 640 mg × 3 daily vs. placebo; 6 weeks; primary outcome: global improvement on the Nepean Dyspepsia Index (NDI).

**Results:** NDI quality of life improvement significantly greater in artichoke group (P<0.001); 40% greater reduction in symptoms including nausea, bloating, flatulence, constipation. **Level A evidence for functional dyspepsia symptom relief.**

### C. NASH / Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver (Level B)

**Panahi Y et al. (2018)** *Phytotherapy Research* 32(7): 1382–1387. RCT, n=100 NAFLD patients; artichoke leaf extract 600 mg × 2 daily; 8 weeks; outcomes: liver enzymes, ultrasonography, lipids.

**Results:** ALT −23.4% (placebo −2.8%, P=0.003); AST −21.5% (P=0.006); liver ultrasonographic grade improved in 83.5% artichoke vs. 20% placebo (P<0.001); LDL-C, TG significantly reduced. **Level B strong evidence for NAFLD.** Mechanistically consistent with NF-κB, antioxidant, and choleretic mechanisms.

### D. Antidiabetic / Glycemic Control (Level B)

**Rondanelli et al. (2020)** systematic review: artichoke extract reduces fasting blood glucose by approximately 9 mg/dL vs. placebo (pooled from 4 trials); HbA1c modest non-significant reduction. Mechanism: CGA α-glucosidase inhibition + SGLT1 inhibition. Level B — promising but requires larger RCTs.

## VII. Phenomenological Record

**The "artichoke effect" (cynarin taste paradox):** Cynarin selectively blocks sweet and bitter taste receptors, so that food and water consumed after artichoke tastes inexplicably sweet — a documented sensory phenomenon experienced by virtually everyone eating globe artichoke and then drinking plain water. Medieval diners noticed that wine served after artichoke tasted flat and altered, leading to the traditional prohibition against serving wine with artichokes. This sensory effect is a macroscopic demonstration of cynarin's receptor pharmacology — cynarin interacting with taste receptors is a visible demonstration of the same compound interacting with hepatic receptors in the liver.

**Italian and French Renaissance food culture:** Artichoke became the fashionable food of Renaissance Italian and French courts precisely because it was understood to be simultaneously gastronomic and medicinal — a luxury food that happened to be a liver tonic. Catherine de Medici famously brought artichoke cultivation to France when she married Henri II (1533), introducing it via the Florence/Tuscany route. The Florentine medical tradition's knowledge of artichoke's hepatic properties was encoded in the French court's adoption of the plant as both table vegetable and liver medicine — the most elite example of food-medicine integration in Renaissance Europe.

**"Alcachofa" etymology as cultural pharmacological transmission:** The Spanish word *alcachofa* (from Arabic *al-kharshuf*) preserved in its very name the Islamic medical transmission route — the Moorish cultivation of artichokes in Al-Andalus was inseparable from their medical use as liver herbs in Islamic medicine. Every Spanish speaker who orders *alcachofa* in a restaurant is using a word whose etymology traces the pharmacological knowledge contained in Ibn Sīnā's hepatic deobstruent application.

## VIII. Practical Application

### Regulatory Status

**Commission E APPROVED:** Dyspeptic complaints (loss of appetite, nausea, mild digestive difficulties); hypercholesterolemia (as adjunct to diet). Dose: 6 g dried leaf/day or equivalent extract containing ≥0.8% CGA. (Note: Commission E initially characterized the evidence as "reasonable but not conclusive"; subsequent RCTs have elevated this to Level A for dyslipidemia and dyspepsia.)

**EMA:** Well-established use for treatment of dyspepsia and as adjunct to diet for hypercholesterolemia. *Cynarae folium* monograph.

### Therapeutic Doses

**Standardized extract (1.8–6 g leaf equivalent/day):** Most clinical trials: 1,800–2,700 mg/day standardized leaf extract containing ≥5% cynarin + ≥3% chlorogenic acid equivalent. Divide into 2–3 daily doses with meals.

**Dried leaf (tea/decoction):** 6 g dried leaf/day in divided doses; bitter taste is a bioactivity indicator; poor palatability limits adherence compared to capsules.

**Tincture (1:5, 25% EtOH):** 5 mL × 3 daily; CGA and cynarin extracted well at 25% EtOH.

**Duration:** Minimum 4–6 weeks for dyspeptic response; minimum 6–8 weeks for lipid response; 3–6 months optimal for lipid benefits in ongoing use.

### Contraindications and Interactions

**Bile duct obstruction (cholelithiasis with obstruction):** The choleretic effect increases bile flow — CONTRAINDICATED if bile duct is obstructed (could provoke biliary colic or exacerbate obstruction). In patients with known gallstones WITHOUT obstruction: increased bile flow is theoretically beneficial (reduces bile supersaturation) but monitor for colic.

**Asteraceae allergy:** Cynaropicrin sesquiterpene lactone allergen; cross-reactivity with other Asteraceae sesquiterpene lactones (helenalin, parthenolide, alantolactone). Assess Asteraceae allergy history.

**Pregnancy:** Choleretic herbs traditionally avoided in pregnancy; insufficient safety data for artichoke extract in pregnancy.

**Statins (additive):** Artichoke extract and statins work by partially overlapping and partially distinct mechanisms (partial HMG-CoA inhibition + PCSK9 reduction from artichoke; competitive HMG-CoA inhibition from statins). Combination is pharmacologically rational and was tested in a small observational study showing additive LDL-C reduction vs. statin alone — but no controlled trial exists. The combination is safe in principle (no significant CYP interaction identified for artichoke extract at standard doses).

**Warfarin:** No documented interaction at standard doses; vitamin K content of artichoke leaves is modest and consistent; no INR instability reported.
