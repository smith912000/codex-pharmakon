# Misy — Misu (μίσυ), "Misy" in the translation (Dioscorides 5-117)
## *A hard, gold-coloured mineral found in Cyprus and Egypt, chosen for its glistening broken face; the translator's suggestion is "Misy — Copiapite — yellow translucent Hydrous Silicate of Iron"* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek Tradition (DMM).** The main locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-117 *MISU* in the 2000 English translation (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10435-10437; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 890; book index "Misy 803" and "misy 781", line 16613; list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 843, "Misu, , mineral, DMM V, id_uncertain yes"). The translator's heading is "SUGGESTED: Misy — Copiapite — yellow translucent Hydrous Silicate of Iron."

Dioscorides' account, read in full, is one paragraph:

"Cyprian misy must be chosen — looking like gold, hard, and if broken a golden colour, glistening like a star. It has the same strength as chalcitis [5-115], and is burnt the same way without psoricum [5-116] being produced from it, differing both in excess and defect. That from Egypt (compared to others) is the best and most effective, but for eye medicines it is not comparable to that previously mentioned." (line 10437)

**Reading the chapter.** (1) Choice: Cyprian misy "looking like gold, hard", and golden and "glistening like a star" when broken. (2) Strength: "the same strength as chalcitis", so the actions of the neighbouring chapter apply by reference (below). (3) Preparation: "burnt the same way" as chalcitis, and no psoricum is produced from it; the phrase "differing both in excess and defect" is printed as above and is not explained in the lines read. (4) Provenance: Egyptian misy is called "the best and most effective" compared with others, yet "for eye medicines" not comparable "to that previously mentioned"; which kind "previously mentioned" refers to is not clear from the text, since the Cyprian kind is the only one named before it. [USER: verify]

**The chalcitis chapter it refers to.** 5-115 *Chalkitis* (line 10426) says chalcitis is "astringent, warming and scab forming, purging away filth around the eye corners and the eyes", and "good (with the juice of a leek) for erysipela, herpes, and discharges of blood from the womb and nostrils", its powder "good for repressing abnormal growths on the gums, and gangrenous ulceration of the cheeks and tonsils"; burnt, pounded and mixed with honey it is "far more effective for eye medicines", and "put in like a suppository it takes away fistulas" (line 10426). Those are chalcitis's actions; misy is said to share its strength, but the misy chapter lists no indications of its own. The burning method itself is not described in 5-115 in the lines read.

**Other chapters that name misy.** (1) The psoricum chapter, 5-116 (line 10429), is a prepared product of calcitis and cadmia; misy is said not to yield it (above). (2) 5-118 *Melanteria* (line 10438) is "sharp like misy". (3) 5-119 *Soru* (line 10441): sory that "does not glisten when broken (like misy)" is thought to be another kind and weak. This shows the glisten of the broken face as a test. (4) In the cadmia chapter 5-84 (line 10223) misy is one of the materials said to be found as veins in the same mound over Solis with chalcitis, sory, melanteria, "coeruleus", chrysocolla, calcanthus and diphryges.

**Identity.** The translator's gloss is "Copiapite — yellow translucent Hydrous Silicate of Iron". Copiapite is, in modern mineralogy, a hydrated iron sulphate, not a silicate, so the two parts of the gloss do not agree with each other. The chapter's own description (gold colour, hard, glistening broken face, found with copper minerals, same strength as chalcitis) is too thin to identify the mineral. The list row marks the identity uncertain. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Stated generally. Iron and copper sulphate minerals that weather out of sulphide ores are soluble, acid and astringent, and the roasted forms are oxides. The text gives no composition and the gloss is internally inconsistent (above), so no composition is asserted for misy.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The chapter records a mineral of "the same strength as chalcitis", an astringent, scab-forming eye and mouth material in the neighbouring chapter, burnt before use. Putting a burnt mineral in the eye or on the gums is not supported by anything retrieved, and the sources give no protocol. No modern protocol is supplied.

---

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation stated is burning "the same way" as chalcitis (line 10437); for chalcitis the preparations in 5-115 are burning, pounding small and mixing with honey for eye medicines, and the powder for the gums, and a suppository form for fistulas (line 10426). Whether those apply to misy beyond "same strength" is not said. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM).** Misy has no printed quality of its own. The chapter says it has "the same strength as chalcitis" (line 10437); chalcitis is printed "astringent, warming and scab forming" (line 10426). Melanteria is "sharp like misy" (line 10440). No degree of hot, cold, moist or dry is printed for misy.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers is a discipline of attention, and the misy chapter shows it: choice by colour, hardness and the glistening of the broken face, rival origins ranked (Cyprus and Egypt), a preparation tied to a related material, and a statement that it does not give psoricum. The sory chapter reuses the glisten test (line 10443). The chapter is therapeutic and no cultivation status is assigned; no amuletic use is recorded.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here for a cultivator to take. What carries over is the discipline of choosing by a broken face and of knowing what a material is not (it is not psoricum; sory without the glisten is a weak kind). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Toxicity:** metal sulphates are irritant and some are poisonous; copper-bearing material can cause vomiting and tissue injury if swallowed.
- **Eye and mouth:** astringent or caustic mineral applied to eyes, gums, tonsils or rectum can injure tissue.
- **Burning:** roasting sulphate or sulphide minerals releases sulphur dioxide and, where ores carry arsenic, arsenic fumes.
- **Identity:** the identification is a suggestion and the gloss disagrees with modern usage; the name cannot guarantee composition.
