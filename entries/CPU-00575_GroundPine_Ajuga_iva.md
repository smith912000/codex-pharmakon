# Ground Pine / Ivette — Musk Bugle, Herb Ivy, Chendgoura (Maghreb)

## *Ajuga iva* (L.) Schreb. | Family: Lamiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM

**Energetic Classification:** Warm and dry in the second degree (Galenic); a bitter, resolving, deobstruent herb that "opens obstruction" and dries cold, sluggish humour.

**Training Phase:** Purification — a bitter metabolic herb suited to the clearing and regulating work of the middle phases.

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian (AT):** The low, resinous, pine-scented bugle-worts of the Near Eastern hills fall among the bitter "resolving" herbs gathered in Mesopotamian practice for obstruction, for chronic sluggish complaints, and for febrile disorder. Their pungent, pine-like scent marked them as drying and penetrating in the herbalist's sensory taxonomy.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM):** In the Galenic-Islamic tradition the *chamaepitys* / ground-pine group (Arabic *kamafitus*) was classed among the hot, dry, deobstruent bitters used for cold obstructions of the liver and joints, for "cold" rheumatic pain, for jaundice and for chronic fever. Avicennan pharmacology valued such herbs for cutting thick, cold humour and opening blocked passages — a description that maps onto the plant's modern reputation as a metabolic regulator.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** *De Materia Medica* describes *chamaepitys* — "ground-pine," so named for its resinous pine scent — recommending the decoction in wine for complaints of the liver and kidneys, for jaundice, and for the expulsion of retained fluids; taken over days it was held to relieve sciatica and chronic aches. *Ajuga iva* and its relatives inherit this Dioscoridean ground-pine identity across the Mediterranean.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The plant hugs the ground yet smells of pine — a lowly herb carrying the resinous, resolving virtue of the conifer. In North African folk medicine it is a near-panacea (*chendgoura*), taken for "sugar," blood pressure, and general vitality. Kenetica reads it as a **Purification** herb: bitter, resolving, and quietly regulating rather than dramatic — a plant that clears and rebalances the metabolic ground on which later work is built. Its phytoecdysteroids, structurally echoing insect and plant "moulting" hormones, fit the symbolic theme of shedding and renewal.

### Active Components

*Ajuga iva* is chemically characteristic of the genus: it contains **phytoecdysteroids** (ecdysterone / 20-hydroxyecdysone and analogues), **neo-clerodane diterpenoids** (the bitter *ajugarin*-type and iridoid-associated principles), and **iridoid glycosides** (harpagide, reptoside and relatives). Overlaying these are the Lamiaceae **polyphenols** — flavonoids such as apigenin derivatives and the flavanone glycoside naringin, together with phenolic acids. The phytoecdysteroids and clerodanes are the signature "insect-hormone-mimic" and bitter constituents; the flavonoids and iridoids contribute antioxidant and metabolic-regulatory activity. This chemistry underpins the plant's traditional antidiabetic, hypolipidaemic and hypotensive reputation.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Kenetica places *Ajuga iva* as a **Purification-phase metabolic regulator**. Its best-attested traditional and experimental role is as a **North African antidiabetic and lipid-lowering herb** — the aqueous infusion of the whole aerial plant taken to moderate blood sugar and blood lipids. Within the training arc it serves the clearing work: a bitter, gently resolving tonic used to steady metabolic terrain (glucose, lipids, sluggish digestion) rather than to force any single organ. It is a herb of adjustment and gradual regulation, not of acute intervention.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the antidiabetic reputation of *Ajuga iva* has repeated experimental support. Boudjelal et al. (2015), *Planta Medica*, prepared the traditional aqueous infusion of *A. iva* aerial parts and found that oral dosing (300 mg/kg) produced a significant fall in blood glucose in alloxan-diabetic rats — reported as more effective than the reference drug glibenclamide — and characterised the herb's flavonoid-rich phenolic profile (naringin and apigenin derivatives). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0035-1546006). El-Hilaly et al. (2006), *Journal of Ethnopharmacology*, showed that acute and sub-chronic oral administration of a lyophilised aqueous whole-plant extract significantly lowered plasma cholesterol and triglycerides (alongside glucose) in streptozotocin-diabetic rats, and prevented diabetic weight loss. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2005.11.023). A companion study, El-Hilaly et al. (2007), *Pakistan Journal of Pharmaceutical Sciences* (PMID 17604246; no DOI assigned), found that continuous intravenous infusion of the extract reduced glucose, cholesterol and triglycerides in diabetic rats by mechanisms the authors likened to insulin sensitisation.

**Evidence grade:** Consistent **preclinical (animal-model) and traditional** evidence for antidiabetic and hypolipidaemic activity; robust controlled **human clinical** trials are lacking. Graded: preclinical/traditional — promising and coherent, but not a substitute for prescribed diabetes therapy.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM (by analogy):** a bitter, cooling-to-neutral "clearing and resolving" herb that drains damp-heat and moves obstruction — mapped by property, not from a classical Chinese entry.
- **Ayurveda (by analogy):** predominant *tikta* (bitter) rasa; *virya* warming; *vipaka* pungent; reduces *kapha* and *meda* (fat tissue) — consistent with its lipid- and glucose-lowering use; mapped by property.
- **Galenic / Unani (attested):** hot and dry, bitter, deobstruent; opener of cold obstructions of liver and joints (the *chamaepitys* class).

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** The pine-scented resolving bitters of the Mesopotamian hills were gathered by the *asû* and read within an aetiology of causal agency — illness classed by the hand that sent it (god, ghost, sworn oath) as much as by humour — so a drug is placed partly by what agency it opposes rather than by any action on a vital constitution. For *Ajuga iva* specifically the identification is not secure, and the standing blocking warning is in force for every Assyrian substance identification and tablet reference; no cultivation status can honestly be asserted. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides gives *chamaepitys* — the ground-pine named for its resinous scent — a straightforward therapeutic account: the decoction in wine for the liver and kidneys, for jaundice, for the expulsion of retained fluids, taken over days for sciatica and chronic ache. He carries no subtle-body system into which a cultivation status could be set; *pneuma* and the Galenic three spirits are later and post-Dioscoridean. What the tradition offers a cultivator here is a discipline of attention — recognising the true ground-pine by its pine scent and low habit, judging the season and provenance of the aerial parts, and guarding against the several plants that share the *chamaepitys* name. That is a real practice of assay, not a vital doctrine. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** In the Galenic-Islamic corpus the ground-pine group (*kamāfītūs*) is classed hot and dry, a deobstruent bitter that cuts thick cold humour and opens the blocked passages of liver and joints. The strongest cultivation idea available in Unani is the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture — the finite vital endowment whose depletion *is* ageing, so that longevity is conservation rather than acquisition — but that is a frame of the whole regimen, not a status the tradition assigns to this warming resolvent, and no *mufarrih* / cardiac-*ruh* role is recorded for it in the retrieved sources. The attestation is therapeutic. **(b) framed:** claimed by the tradition, no cultivation status sourced.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a substance a cultivator takes to build vitality. Across all three traditions *Ajuga iva* is a bitter, drying, deobstruent worked to cut sluggish cold humour, open obstruction and steady metabolic terrain — the North African *chendgoura* infusion for "sugar," lipids and blood pressure. Its phytoecdysteroid "moulting-hormone" chemistry makes the Purification theme of shedding and renewal an apt symbolic reading, but that is a Kenetica gloss, not a status any of the three source traditions assigns. Use it as a clearing bitter for a defined metabolic job, over a course, and then stop — and never in place of prescribed diabetes therapy.

### Contraindications

- **Diabetes interaction — important:** *Ajuga iva* has demonstrated **hypoglycaemic activity**. Anyone taking insulin or oral hypoglycaemics who uses it risks **additive hypoglycaemia**; blood glucose must be monitored and medical advice sought. Do not replace prescribed diabetes treatment with the herb.
- May add to the effect of **antihypertensive and lipid-lowering** medication — monitor if combined.
- Contains bitter clerodane diterpenoids and phytoecdysteroids; safety of concentrated or prolonged use is not established. Avoid high or extended dosing without professional guidance.
- Insufficient safety data in **pregnancy and lactation** — avoid medicinal use [USER: verify].
- Discontinue before surgery (potential effect on blood glucose).
- Not for use in children at medicinal strength; keep to qualified guidance.
