# Tiger bone — "Tiger bone" (Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch.20); "Tiger bone" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *The bone of the tiger. Clark prints "Tiger bone" with no Tibetan word and no Latin name; the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary on animals gives* Panthera tigris *L for "Tiger"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is the Tibetan *Four Tantras*. In Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; "Clark" below) the materia medica of chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra ends with the medicines of animal origin: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). Bone is the second of the thirteen, and the bones are listed in one long paragraph at line 1160. In it, after human skull, human bone and the weather-beaten shoulder-blade, comes: "Dragon bone: [has sixteen names]. It halts necrosis and heals glandular growths. Tiger bone: [has fifteen names]. It increases bone resin."

Clark's introduction (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets are taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, and those in parentheses from the oral instructions of Dr. Yeshe Donden and Dr. Pema Dorje. So "has fifteen names" is a commentary-layer note, and "It increases bone resin" is the unbracketed claim. Clark does not define "bone resin" in the passage. The same word is used of other medicines in his translation: pine resin "retains bone resin and bone lymph" (line 926) and a plant at line 1014 "maintains bone resin"; and bone is one of the seven bodily constituents he lists (line 145: "essential nutriment, blood, flesh, fat, bone, marrow and semen"). I do not equate "bone resin" with any of these, and this entry does not translate it.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`; "MTK" below) gives the parallel sentence at line 3523 in a different wording: "Tiger bone protects the spongy bone." The two translations agree that tiger bone acts on the bone, and differ in the words: Clark has it "increase" the "bone resin", and MTK has it "protect" the "spongy bone". The Tibetan underlying both is not printed in either, so I cannot say which rendering is closer. [USER: verify]

The tiger appears in other places in the same books, none of which concerns the bone as a medicine. Clark's meat classification (line 672, group (e), carnivorous animals) lists "the tiger, leopard, bear..." and MTK's classification (line 2912) lists "the tiger, leopard, black bear, Tibetan brown bear, snow leopard, wolf, lynx, fox, dhole (Tibetan wild-dog) and monkey" as "carnivorous wild animals". The MTK glossary on animals (line 7013, with the Latin at line 7015) gives "Tiger: *Panthera tigris* L". Clark also prints a plant named *stag.sha* ("lit. 'tiger flesh'") at line 1017, for which Clark gives the Latin names *Potentilla discolor*, *Calophaca crassicaulis* and *Oxytropis chiliophylla*; it is a different material. The list `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 335 has "Tiger bone" with the source "rGyud-bzhi, Explanatory Tantra ch.20 (Efficacy of Medicinal Substances); Four Tantras of Tibetan Medicine (English trans.)", marked identification uncertain; the text lines above are what I rely on, and the list row only agrees with them.

On other traditions: Sushruta's food chapter names the tiger among the cave-dwelling mammals (*Guhashaya*) whose flesh is described, and says nothing of the bone (Sutrasthana ch.46, lines 534-543). That is a separate material (the flesh) and is not folded into this entry. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Bone is mainly a mineral phase of calcium phosphate (hydroxyapatite) laid on a framework of collagen protein, with marrow and a little fat. These are general facts about bone from any large animal. The Tibetan texts name no constituent and no mechanism for the action claimed, and this entry supplies none.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The record is one claim: the bone acts on "bone resin" (Clark) or protects "the spongy bone" (MTK). The Codex does not recommend it, and there is a plain reason: the tiger is an endangered species, and the trade in tiger bone and its products is prohibited in law and fuels poaching. No protocol is offered, and no substitute is proposed, since the texts give none.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither text gives a preparation (powdered, burnt, in a compound), a quantity or a dose for this bone. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] Clark remarks that "nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57), a remark that applies to the whole animal section.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Bone from any animal is a calcium phosphate and collagen tissue; eating it does not deliver bone to the body. Bone problems in humans (fracture, osteoporosis, infection, tumour) are assessed and treated medically. The tiger is listed in CITES Appendix I and is endangered in the wild. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi.** No taste, potency or post-digestive taste is printed for this bone in the passages read (Clark line 1160; MTK line 3523). The only quality attached to it is the action: it "increases bone resin" (Clark) or "protects the spongy bone" (MTK). Neighbouring bones carry their own stated actions, such as dragon bone "halts necrosis", but these are not extended to this one.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The frame is the thirteen-part animal section of the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica (Clark line 1148), and in it the bone is a drug for a stated action on the bone (line 1160). Nothing in the passages read places it in any practice of essence extraction (*bcud len*), and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, so none is asserted here. The tradition's wider frame is that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching the root; this entry adds nothing to that.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports cultivation use. The bone appears as one sentence in a list of bones, with an action on the bone itself, in a section of the materia medica that Clark says is little used today. A cultivator has nothing to take from this entry; the honest practice is abstention, which also spares an endangered animal.

### Contraindications

- **Law and conservation:** the tiger is an endangered species and trade in its bone is prohibited in most countries; possession or purchase is unlawful and drives poaching. A product sold as tiger bone is also likely to be something else.
- **Hygiene and infection:** unprocessed animal bone can carry bacteria and parasites; it should not be eaten or applied.
- **Bone disease:** fractures, loss of bone density and bone pain need medical assessment; do not delay it for an animal remedy.
- **Identification and translation:** the English wording of the action differs between the two translations, and the Tibetan was not read; no claim here is made beyond the two sentences quoted.
