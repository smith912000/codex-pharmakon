# I-462 | CALENDULA
## *Calendula officinalis* L.

**Family:** Asteraceae | **Part Used:** Flower heads (ligulate + tubular florets); petals (ligules) alone in some preparations

**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi · ◈ Jing

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Etymology:** *Calendula* from Latin *calendae* = the first day of every month (calends) — the plant flowers continuously throughout most of the year in temperate climates, apparently through multiple calendar cycles; some authors derive it from *calendarium* = "account book" (the debt fell due on each calend); *officinalis* = "of the apothecary's shop" — the standard Linnaean designation for plants in official medical use

**Common names:** Pot marigold, garden marigold, English marigold, Mary's gold, Mary bud, gold bloom, common marigold

**Critical nomenclature alert — MARIGOLD CONFUSION:** "Marigold" commonly refers to THREE entirely distinct plants:
- *Calendula officinalis* (pot marigold): THIS ENTRY; medicinal; Asteraceae; Old World
- *Tagetes erecta* / *T. patula* (African/French marigold): ornamental; Asteraceae; New World; different chemistry
- *Tagetes lucida* (Mexican marigold): used in SA traditional medicine; also New World
*Calendula and Tagetes are not interchangeable medicinally. Most topical medicinal preparations specify Calendula. "Marigold" in Latin American traditional medicine typically = Tagetes.*

**Botanical identity:** Annual or short-lived perennial to 50 cm; branching from base; leaves oblong-lanceolate, slightly succulent, with short soft hairs, alternate; flower heads solitary, terminal, 4–7 cm diameter; all florets orange-yellow to deep orange (varieties from pale yellow through deep orange selected for ornamental use; medicinal varieties typically deep orange); ray florets: 15–25, ligulate, spreading; disc florets: tubular, orange; strongly aromatic when crushed (resinous-musky); native: uncertain (possibly Southern Europe/North Africa; widely cultivated since antiquity); invasive in some Mediterranean and temperate regions

**Primary active constituents:**

| Compound class | Examples | Concentration | Primary activity |
|---------------|----------|---------------|-----------------|
| Triterpenoid saponins | Oleanolic acid glycosides (calendulosides A-F, oleanolide) | 2–4% | Anti-inflammatory; antifungal; antiviral |
| Flavonoids | Isorhamnetin-3-O-glucoside, isorhamnetin-3-O-rutinoside, quercetin, narcissin | 0.3–0.8% | Anti-inflammatory; antioxidant; antiviral |
| Carotenoids | Lutein (dominant), zeaxanthin, β-carotene, lycopene, β-cryptoxanthin | 0.01–0.03% | Antioxidant; wound healing; skin repair; anti-inflammatory |
| Polysaccharides | Arabinogalactan, rhamnoarabinogalactan | 15–20% | Immunostimulatory; wound healing fibroblast activation |
| Essential oil | α-Cadinene, δ-cadinene, α-muurolene, carvone, β-farnesene | 0.02–0.03% | Antimicrobial; aromatic |
| Sterols | β-Sitosterol, stigmasterol, taraxasterol | 0.5–1.5% | Anti-inflammatory; membrane component |
| Resin | Triterpenoid resins | 5–6% | Topical skin-protective; occlusive layer |
| Phenolic acids | Caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, ferulic acid | trace | Antioxidant |
| Calendulin | High-MW polysaccharide | significant | Emollient; wound healing |

**Carotenoid orange — dual function:** The deep orange color of calendula flowers is produced by an exceptionally high carotenoid content (among the richest of any flower). These carotenoids serve simultaneously as visual pollinator attractants, photoprotectants for the plant's photosynthetic apparatus, and pharmacologically active wound-healing and anti-inflammatory compounds — a single evolutionary adaptation serving three biological functions.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**The universal wound-healing yellow flower:** Three of the most geographically distant ancient traditions (Greek/Dioscorides, Arab/Ibn Sīnā, Egyptian/Ebers Papyrus) all document yellow composite flowers — highly likely *Calendula* — for wound healing and skin inflammation. This convergence on topical applications (the most empirically straightforward to validate — visible wound healing is observable without laboratory methods) is among the strongest signals in the Codex for a topical herbal medicine.

**The Egyptian cosmetic-pharmacological synthesis:** The Ebers Papyrus includes skin-care preparations alongside medical treatments in ways that suggest the Egyptians understood the boundary between cosmetic and medicinal preparation as fluid. Yellow flower preparations appear in both contexts — consistent with modern understanding of calendula's dual role as cosmetic ingredient (skin-brightening carotenoids, emollient resin) and pharmacological agent (anti-inflammatory triterpenoids, wound-healing polysaccharides). The ancient integration of beauty and medicine in calendula use predates modern "cosmeceutical" classification by 3,500 years.

**Ibn Sīnā's skin-healing classification:** The *Al-Qanun*'s categorization of *qarandal* preparations specifically for wound healing, skin inflammation, and jaundice (liver-clearing application) aligns with both the anti-inflammatory flavonoid/triterpenoid content and the hepatoprotective triterpenoid activity now being studied in oncology contexts. Arab medicine's dual categorization of the herb as both wound medicine and liver medicine anticipated modern phytochemical understanding of oleanolic acid's anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective properties.

**The "Tagetes substitution" in SA traditions:** South American traditional medicine uses *Tagetes* species (African marigold relatives) for applications paralleling calendula's Old World role — wound healing, skin care, antimicrobial. This is not the same plant but represents parallel pharmacological solution-finding: different continents, different marigold-family species, same therapeutic niche. The "marigold" category as wound-healing orange-yellow composite flower recurs independently across civilizations.

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

**Triterpenoid saponin profile:**

*Oleanolic acid glycosides (calendulosides):*
- Calenduloside A: 3-O-glucuronide; anti-inflammatory; antifungal
- Calenduloside B: 3-O-glucuronide-28-O-glucoside; similar activity
- Calenduloside F: most pharmacologically studied
- Oleanolic acid (aglycone): direct anti-inflammatory; hepatoprotective (NF-κB ↓, TGF-β ↓ in hepatic stellate cells); antimicrobial; antiviral against HSV

*Note: saponin concentration highest in dried flowers vs fresh; standardized extracts typically specify saponin content (≥3% as oleanolic acid equivalents)*

**Carotenoid quantification:**
- Lutein: dominant (40–60% of total carotenoids)
- β-Carotene: provitamin A → retinol conversion in skin
- Zeaxanthin, lycopene, β-cryptoxanthin: antioxidant; anti-inflammatory
- Carotenoids extracted in oil-based preparations (carotenoids are lipophilic); aqueous/tea preparations contain negligible carotenoids — this explains why oil macerations are preferred for topical wound-healing preparations

**Polysaccharide wound-healing fraction:**
- Arabinogalactan (high MW): macrophage activation; fibroblast stimulation
- Rhamnoarabinogalactan: complement pathway activation; immunostimulatory
- These water-soluble polysaccharides are present in aqueous extracts and teas; important for topical wound-rinse applications

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

**Wound Healing (Primary Topical Mechanism):**
```
1. ANTI-INFLAMMATORY:
Triterpenoid saponins (oleanolic acid glucosides)
→ COX-2 inhibition (IC₅₀ ~10–50 μM for oleanolic acid)
→ NF-κB transcription factor inhibition → pro-inflammatory cytokine production ↓
→ Edema resolution → improved wound environment for healing

Flavonoids (isorhamnetin, quercetin)
→ 5-LOX inhibition → LTB₄ ↓ → neutrophil recruitment ↓
→ Antioxidant → oxidative stress ↓ in wound bed

2. FIBROBLAST ACTIVATION:
Polysaccharides → fibroblast proliferation stimulation
→ Collagen type I/III synthesis ↑
→ Wound matrix formation acceleration

β-Sitosterol → collagen cross-linking enzyme (lysyl oxidase) activity support
→ Tensile strength of healing tissue increases

3. EPITHELIALIZATION:
Carotenoids (β-carotene → retinol/retinoic acid locally)
→ Keratinocyte proliferation + differentiation
→ Epithelial coverage of wound bed accelerated

Oleanolic acid derivatives → EGF (epidermal growth factor) receptor potentiation
→ Keratinocyte migration ↑

4. ANTIMICROBIAL:
Essential oil + saponins → broad antimicrobial activity
Oleanolic acid → active vs Staphylococcus aureus (MIC 50–200 μg/mL)
Flavonoids → antifungal vs Candida albicans
→ Reduces microbial biofilm in chronic wounds
```

**Anti-Inflammatory (Systemic/Oral):**
```
Oleanolic acid (oral bioavailability: 5–15% after absorption)
→ NF-κB pathway inhibition systemically
→ Hepatoprotective: stellate cell TGF-β1 ↓ → anti-fibrotic
→ Anti-tumor: cell cycle arrest (p21 ↑, cyclin D1 ↓) in cancer cell lines
```

**Radiation Dermatitis Protection:**
```
Carotenoids → ROS (reactive oxygen species) quenching
→ Radiation-induced lipid peroxidation ↓
Flavonoids → DNA damage repair pathway support
→ UV/ionizing radiation skin damage mitigation
Polysaccharides → skin barrier repair → keratinocyte integrity ↑
Pommier 2004 (J Clin Oncol): 41% vs 63% grade ≥2 dermatitis
```

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Radiation Dermatitis (Oncology — Primary RCT):**

- **Pommier 2004** (Journal of Clinical Oncology; Level A): 254 breast cancer patients receiving post-operative radiation; calendula cream vs trolamine (Biafine); Grade ≥2 acute dermatitis: 41% (calendula) vs 63% (trolamine) (p<0.001); pain score significantly lower; quality of life better; *calendula now used in multiple cancer centers for radiation skin care — Level A evidence for this indication*

**Wound Healing:**

- **Duran 2003** (Rev Bras Enferm; Level B): episiotomy wound healing; calendula cream vs petrolatum gauze; calendula group: superior healing at days 3, 6, and 10; less redness, edema, discharge; significant

- **Naeini 2020** (J Wound Care; Level B): chronic wound care; calendula cream vs petroleum jelly; wound area reduction significantly greater in calendula group over 4 weeks

**Diaper Dermatitis (Pediatric):**

- **Panahi 2012** (J Dermatolog Treat; Level A): 66 neonates with diaper rash; calendula ointment vs aloe vera gel; severity scores at 10 days: calendula significantly superior; neonatal use well-tolerated

**Oral Mucositis (Chemotherapy):**

- **Babaee 2013** (J Altern Complement Med; Level A): 40 patients on 5-FU chemotherapy; calendula mouthwash vs placebo; WHO mucositis grade significantly lower in calendula group; time to first mucositis episode extended

**Evidence Summary:**

| Indication | Best Evidence | Grade |
|-----------|---------------|-------|
| Radiation dermatitis | Pommier 2004 (254 patients, RCT) | Level A |
| Wound healing (topical) | Duran 2003; Naeini 2020 | Level B |
| Diaper dermatitis | Panahi 2012 | Level A |
| Oral mucositis (chemotherapy) | Babaee 2013 | Level A |
| Episiotomy healing | Duran 2003 | Level B |
| Anti-inflammatory (systemic) | Mechanistic + limited oral trials | Level C–D |

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

**Calendula's unbroken commercial continuity:** Few medicinal plants have maintained continuous commercial production for as long as calendula. From ancient Egyptian cosmetic preparations through Roman dye use, through medieval monastery garden cultivation, into 16th-century apothecaries (where it was listed as *Flores Calendulae* or *Flos Calendulae*), through 19th-century Eclectic medicine (where it was used for hemorrhage control and wound healing), to contemporary cosmeceutical formulations — the commercial chain is essentially unbroken across 3,500+ years. No marketing campaign or clinical trial created this continuity; empirical validation of wound-healing efficacy maintained it across generations.

**The Roman dye and food plant:** Calendula petals were used in Roman cuisine (as saffron substitute, "poor man's saffron") and as dye for textiles. The same carotenoids that color the petals color food and fabric — and now validated as wound-healing anti-inflammatory compounds. Roman soldiers reportedly carried dried calendula petals for use in battlefield wound care, consuming any remainder as food coloring in camp cooking. A single plant serving as simultaneous food, dye, and battlefield medicine encodes efficiency in resource use that has modern pharmacological explanation: the carotenoid pigments responsible for all three applications are also the primary photoprotective and wound-healing compounds.

**The Pommier study's oncological impact:** The 2004 Journal of Clinical Oncology trial comparing calendula cream to trolamine (the pharmaceutical standard) for radiation dermatitis represents a watershed moment for plant medicine's integration into mainstream oncology. That *Calendula officinalis* — a common garden plant — outperformed a purpose-designed pharmaceutical preparation in a well-powered Journal of Clinical Oncology trial was unexpected enough to generate significant clinical attention. Multiple cancer centers now include calendula preparations in radiation skin care protocols as a result. This represents one of the most direct translations of traditional wound-healing knowledge into evidence-based oncology supportive care.

**"Like the sun" — the sympathetic doctrine:** Medieval herbalists categorized calendula as a "solar herb" — its opening and closing with sunlight (calendula flower heads open in morning sunshine and close at evening) made it, under the Doctrine of Signatures, a plant governed by the sun's energy and therefore appropriate for inflammatory (hot/fiery) conditions. This cosmological classification — while mechanistically inaccurate in its reasoning — correctly directed practitioners toward an anti-inflammatory application that modern pharmacology validates through COX-2 inhibition and NF-κB modulation. The wrong theory produced the right treatment recommendation.

## Section 8: Practical Application

**Primary indications:**
1. **Radiation dermatitis prevention/treatment** (Level A evidence; first-line consideration)
2. Wound healing: lacerations, abrasions, burns, post-surgical incisions, episiotomy
3. Dermatitis: contact, atopic, diaper dermatitis
4. Oral mucositis (chemotherapy-induced): mouthwash preparation
5. Sunburn and UV skin damage (topical; carotenoid photoprotection)
6. Antifungal (topical; Candida, tinea — adjunct)

**Dosing:**
- **Radiation dermatitis:** Apply calendula cream (minimum 2% extract standardized to ≥3% saponins) to treatment area after each radiation fraction and throughout the day × entire radiation course; Pommier protocol
- **General wound care:** Apply calendula cream or ointment 2–4× daily to clean wound; occlusive preparation preferred for dry wounds; open preparation for wet wounds
- **Oral mucositis rinse:** Aqueous calendula extract (2% w/v) as oral rinse 4–6×/day during chemotherapy; swish 30 seconds, expectorate
- **Topical diaper area:** Calendula ointment at each diaper change; Panahi protocol (30 mg extract/g ointment base)
- **Calendula oil (topical):** 1 part dried flowers macerated in 5 parts olive oil × 4–6 weeks; strain; apply directly or incorporate into cream base; preferred for carotenoid-rich preparation

**Forms:**
- Standardized cream/ointment (≥2% extract standardized for saponins): most clinical evidence
- Calendula oil (olive oil or jojoba macerate): superior carotenoid delivery; best for dry/chronic skin conditions
- Tincture (1:5, 60% ethanol): appropriate for oral mucositis rinse (diluted); antifungal applications
- Aqueous decoction: wound-rinse; oral rinse; contains polysaccharides and flavonoids
- Calendula hydrosol: gentle; suitable for sensitive skin and infants
- Avoid: "marigold" preparations that do not specify *Calendula officinalis* — may be *Tagetes* (different chemistry)

**Contraindications:**
- **Allergy to Asteraceae:** Cross-reactivity risk with ragweed, chrysanthemum, chamomile (see I-461)
- **Oral use — pregnancy:** Historical emmenagogue/uterotonic properties; AVOID oral consumption in pregnancy; topical use at standard concentrations generally considered safe
- **Contact allergy:** 2–4% incidence of contact sensitization; patch-test before broad topical application in sensitive individuals

**Drug interactions:**
- **Sedatives (oral doses):** Mild sedative potential reported; additive with pharmaceutical CNS depressants
- **Antidiabetic medications:** Hypoglycemic potential in animal studies; theoretical interaction; monitor blood glucose if using high-dose oral preparations
- *Topical applications:* minimal systemic absorption; drug interaction risk negligible

**Safety:**
- Topical: excellent safety profile; well-tolerated by neonates (Panahi 2012); suitable for sensitive skin
- Oral at therapeutic doses: generally safe; mild GI upset at high doses
- No significant systemic toxicity documented at therapeutic doses in any tradition or clinical trial

**Quality markers:**
- Deep orange color: indicates high carotenoid content
- Resinous touch: indicates adequate triterpenoid/resin content
- Standardization to: ≥3% oleanolic acid saponins AND carotenoids specified
- Third-party heavy metal testing: Calendula accumulates heavy metals from contaminated soils; essential for commercial preparations
