# I-309: Tanacetum vulgare (Tansy)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-309 | Asteraceae (Compositae)**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Tanacetum vulgare* L.
**Family:** Asteraceae

**Common Names:**
- English: Tansy, Bitter Buttons, Cow Bitter, Golden Buttons
- German: *Rainfarn* ("pure fern"), *Wurmfarn* (worm fern)
- French: *Tanaisie*, *Herbe aux vers* (worm herb)
- Latin: *Tanacetum* — possibly from Greek *athanasia* ("immortality") — the plant's use in preserving corpses
- Arabic: *Rainfān* (رينفان) — adapted from European name

**Etymology:**
The name *athanasia* (immortality) enters through the preservation tradition — tansy was used in medieval Europe to pack corpses and preserve meat from flies and insects (via thujone's insecticidal properties). *vulgare* = "common/ordinary." The *Rainfarn* German name reflects both the fern-like feathery leaves and the purity-preservation association. The "buttons" English common name refers to the distinctive flat-topped yellow button flowers without ray florets — tansy's most visually distinctive feature.

**Disambiguation from Feverfew:**
*Tanacetum vulgare* (tansy) and *Tanacetum parthenium* (feverfew, I-299) are closely related in the same genus. Tansy is larger, more aromatic, thujone-dominant, historically more toxic. Feverfew is parthenolide-dominant, safer, NF-κB mechanism. They are not interchangeable.

**Morphology:**
Perennial; 60–150 cm; strongly aromatic (camphor-thujone odor); pinnately divided feathery leaves (fernlike); dense flat-topped corymbs of small yellow button flowers (no ray petals — buttons); rhizomatous; common in waste ground, roadsides throughout Europe and naturalized North America.

**Parts Used:** Aerial parts (leaves + flowers) — historically; seeds; essential oil distilled from aerial parts.

**The Corpse Herb:**
Tansy's historical use in mortuary practice is one of the most distinctive niche uses in the Codex. Medieval European corpses were wrapped in tansy to repel flies and slow putrefaction — a documented practice with a genuine mechanism (thujone insecticidal/fumigant activity). This preservative use gave rise to *athanasia* (immortality) — a case where functional preservation of the body was linguistically encoded as transcendence of death.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domains:**

1. **Anthelmintic (worm-expelling)** — DMM, CM, AT, European folk; thujone + bitter mechanism; **most consistent historical use**
2. **Emmenagogue / abortifacient** — CM, European folk; DANGEROUS — uterotonic + thujone convulsant at therapeutic doses
3. **Insect repellent / insecticidal** — European folk; thujone mechanism; valid external use
4. **Preservation / antimicrobial** — European mortuary tradition; thujone + camphor
5. **Bitter digestive** — DMM, CM; secondary to anthelmintic

**The Safety Inflection Point:**
Tansy represents a category of traditional herb where historical use at "therapeutic" anthelmintic doses was genuinely dangerous. Pre-modern therapeutic margins were narrow — the dose required to expel worms was close to the toxic dose producing convulsions. Abortifacient use killed women. Multiple historical toxicological records confirm tansy convulsive poisoning and death from medicinal doses. Commission E explicitly declines to approve tansy — this is one of the rare cases where the traditional indication (anthelmintic) has been superseded by modern safer alternatives while the herb itself retains no modern approved therapeutic role.

**Energetic Classification:**
- ◉ Qi (functional): Historically primary — bitter-anthelmintic; emmenagogue
- External use: insect repellent valid

**Primary Tier: ◉ Qi (historical bitter anthelmintic — now obsolete therapeutically)**

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Essential Oil (Thujone-Dominant Chemotype)

**1. β-Thujone (35–70% EO depending on chemotype) — PRIMARY compound**
- GABA-A receptor antagonist (same mechanism as α-thujone in wormwood — I-292)
- **Proconvulsant** at moderate doses; potentially lethal at high doses
- Historical anthelmintic mechanism: paralysis of intestinal parasites via neurotoxicity
- **Narrow therapeutic margin:** Dose sufficient for anthelmintic effect → dangerous systemic toxicity
- Insecticidal: disrupts insect nervous system (GABA-A mechanism conserved across arthropods)

**2. Camphor (5–25% EO)**
- Antimicrobial; insect repellent; preservative
- Contributes to characteristic odor; respiratory stimulant in low doses

**3. Borneol, Umbellulone** — EO minor components; antimicrobial

**Note on Chemotypic Variation:**
*T. vulgare* has documented thujone-free chemotypes (primarily camphor-dominant) in some populations. The thujone-free chemotype ("daisy-type") is pharmacologically distinct and safer — but most commercially available tansy is thujone-dominant without chemotype verification.

### Sesquiterpene Lactones

**4. Tanacetin, Chrysanthenyl acetate** — Asteraceae class; mild anti-inflammatory; bitter receptor stimulation

### Flavonoids

**5. Luteolin, Apigenin, Chrysanthemin** — anti-inflammatory; antioxidant; lower potency than constituent EO

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### Anthelmintic (Historical — Obsolete)
- **β-Thujone → GABA-A antagonism in parasites + host** → spastic paralysis of intestinal worms + expulsion
- **Problem:** Parasite GABA-A and human GABA-A have same sensitivity — anthelmintic dose = convulsant dose margin is narrow
- Modern anthelmintics (mebendazole, albendazole, praziquantel) selectively target parasite-specific mechanisms without host CNS toxicity — tansy is obsolete for this indication

### Proconvulsant / Toxic
- **β-Thujone → GABA-A chloride channel blockade** → reduced inhibitory neurotransmission → excitatory CNS dominance → seizures
- **Dose-dependent:** low doses = mild CNS stimulation; moderate doses = tremors; high doses = tonic-clonic seizures; lethal doses reported

### Insect Repellent / Insecticidal (Topical — Valid)
- **β-Thujone + Camphor → arthropod GABA-A + octopamine receptor disruption** → insect motor dysfunction
- Documented repellent activity against flies, moths, ants; fumigant effect in closed spaces
- External use is the one pharmacologically valid and safe contemporary application

### Emmenagogue / Abortifacient (Dangerous)
- β-Thujone → uterine smooth muscle stimulation; combined with anthelmintic CNS toxicity → historically lethal abortifacient doses

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Anthelmintic:**
- Historical use; now obsolete; safer alternatives exist
- **Evidence Grade: Level C** (traditional use only; not recommended)

**Insect Repellent:**
- Controlled studies demonstrate repellent activity against house flies, stable flies, moths
- Topical cloth sachets and dried herb bundles shown effective
- **Evidence Grade: Level C** (valid; mechanism-supported)

**Any Internal Use:**
- Commission E: **NOT approved** — insufficient evidence of efficacy AND documented safety concerns
- ESCOP: no monograph
- **Evidence Grade: Level C** (traditional) — but CONTRAINDICATED due to safety profile

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The Insecticide Garden:**
Medieval European households used tansy strategically — strewn on floors (flies avoid thujone-aromatic surfaces), packed with stored linens (moth repellent), rubbed on meat (fly deterrent), grown near kitchen doorways (thujone vapor repels insects). This was systematic applied pharmacology before the chemical era — recognition that the plant's volatile compounds had insecticidal properties. The practice worked. The aroma of tansy in a medieval farmhouse or church was a functional bio-insecticide, and the plant's survival in European kitchen gardens long after its therapeutic use declined reflects this persistent utility.

**The Easter Tansy Cake:**
Medieval English Easter custom involved eating "tansy cakes" — small pancakes or cakes flavored with tansy juice. The symbolism was purification (bitter herb = Passover reference + Lenten purification) and spring tonic (bitter stimulating digestion after Lenten fasting). This custom persisted until the 18th century in rural England. The tansy-cake tradition demonstrates how toxic plants were integrated into ritual food culture in small, symbolically meaningful doses — below the clinical toxicity threshold but fulfilling cultural-religious purification functions.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Contemporary Use

**INTERNAL USE: CONTRAINDICATED**
- Thujone content makes internal use unsafe at anthelmintic doses
- No Commission E approval; no modern clinical indication justifies risk
- Historical anthelmintic use superseded by modern safe anthelmintics
- Historical emmenagogue/abortifacient use is documented as lethal

**EXTERNAL USE (Valid Applications):**
- **Insect repellent:** Dried herb sachets in wardrobes/drawers (moth repellent); fresh sprigs near doorways/windows; rubbed on skin carefully (patch test for Asteraceae allergy)
- **Garden companion planting:** Tansy planted near crops repels aphids, beetles, flies
- **Dried flower arrangements:** Buttons retain structure and repellent volatile for months

### Safety Profile

**HIGH RISK for internal use.**

**Thujone Toxicity:**
- Proconvulsant; minimum lethal dose in humans not precisely established but historical fatalities at medicinal doses documented
- Epilepsy: ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION (any dose)
- Children: ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION
- Pregnancy: ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION (emmenagogue + thujone convulsant)
- Liver disease: caution

**Asteraceae Allergy:**
- Sesquiterpene lactone contact sensitization
- Less common than arnica/feverfew sensitization but present

**Drug Interactions:**
- Antiepileptic drugs: pharmacodynamic antagonism (thujone reduces seizure threshold)
- CNS depressants: complex interaction (thujone proconvulsant may be masked then unmasked)

**Contraindications (Internal):**
- All internal use in clinical practice
- Epilepsy, pregnancy, children, liver disease are absolute contraindications if any internal exposure considered
