# Sea horse — *Hippokampos* (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 2-3)
## *"Sea Horse — Hippocampus", the Latin name printed with the heading; the text calls it "a little living creature of the sea"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica*, in the 2000 English edition (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`; "text" below). The chapter is 2-3, headed *HIPPOKAMPOS*, at line 4919 (the spine's locus, `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`: "2-3 (line 4919)", translator's identification "Sea Horse — Hippocampus from DAVIS — 1907", class animal). The words "from DAVIS — 1907" and the repeated "Sea Horse — Hippocampus" at line 4921 are the edition's plate caption, an illustration credit and not part of Dioscorides' text. The Latin word *Hippocampus* appears again in square brackets at line 4924 ("Hippocampus [Latin] — Sea Horse"), a translator's note.

The whole chapter, line 4925, reads: "Hippocampus is a little living creature of the sea that is burnt and the ashes used either in goose grease, liquid pitch, or ointment amaracinum [1-68]. Rubbed on it fills up alopecia [baldness] with hair." The chapter has one preparation, burning the animal to ashes, with three vehicles for the ashes (goose grease, liquid pitch, or the ointment *amaracinum*, which the translator refers to chapter 1-68), and one use, rubbing it on bald patches to restore the hair. The "Hippocampus" of Dioscorides is a small sea animal whose description is the single phrase "a little living creature of the sea"; the translator's equation with the sea horse is the identification the chapter rests on. [USER: verify]

The sea horse chapter belongs to the opening run of Book 2 and shares its use with its neighbours. Chapter 2-2 (*Echinos chersaios*, the hedgehog, line 4915) says that "the burnt skin of the earth hedgehog is good for alopecia [baldness] rubbed on with moist pitch" (line 4918); the same ailment and the same method (burnt animal material in a pitch vehicle) are given there. Chapter 2-4 (*Porphura*, "Purpura", line 4926) follows with a shellfish whose burnt remains "dry and clean teeth, repress excrescent flesh, and draw boils and heal them". Both are chapters of burnt sea or land creatures with a topical use.

No other tradition on the tag line, and no text outside Dioscorides on disk, was found naming the sea horse. A whole-word search of the Sushruta, Ebers, Tibetan and Assyrian texts on disk for "sea horse" and "seahorse" returned nothing.

### Active Components

The bony plates of a sea horse are mostly calcium phosphate and protein, and burning an animal leaves mainly its mineral ash. These are general facts. The chapter names no constituent, and this entry names none.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The use recorded is a topical one, burnt ashes rubbed on bald patches. Sea horses are a group of species that are protected and traded under international controls, and wild collection for medicine is a leading threat to several of them. The Codex's application layer has nothing to add, and no protocol is offered.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives the preparation: the animal "is burnt and the ashes used either in goose grease, liquid pitch, or ointment amaracinum" and "rubbed on" the bald place (line 4925). No quantity, number of animals or frequency is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. Chapter 2-3 is two sentences, a preparation and a topical use for baldness. It records no provenance, season or assay such as makes the discipline-of-attention frame useful elsewhere in the work, and it assigns no status to the person taking the remedy.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing in the source for a cultivator to use, and the animal is better left alone. What the chapter does show is the method that the opening of Book 2 repeats: burn the material, mix the ash into a fat or pitch, and rub it on the place affected.

### Contraindications

The preparation is a topical ash in a fat or pitch vehicle, and pitch can irritate the skin; the text gives no caution. Sea horses are listed in international trade controls (CITES) and are threatened by collection for medicine, so wild-sourced material is unethical and often illegal to trade. These are general cautions and not claims from the text.
