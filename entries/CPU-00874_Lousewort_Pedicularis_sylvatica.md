### Origin & Tradition

*Pedicularis sylvatica* is a low, sprawling biennial or short-lived perennial of damp acid heath, wet grassland, flushes and the drier margins of bogs across western and central Europe — Britain, Ireland, Iberia, France, the Low Countries, Scandinavia and the western Alps. It forms a loose rosette from which several decumbent, often reddish stems radiate; the leaves are pinnately dissected into small toothed lobes, giving the fern-like texture typical of the genus. The flowers are pink to rose-purple, strongly two-lipped, with a hooded upper lip (galea) and a broad three-lobed lower lip — the classic bee-pollinated Orobanchaceae architecture. It flowers late spring into summer and is a reliable indicator of unimproved, nutrient-poor, seasonally wet ground.

The botanically decisive fact — and the one that governs everything in this entry — is that *P. sylvatica* is a **root hemiparasite**. It photosynthesises normally and looks entirely self-sufficient above ground, but below ground it forms **haustoria**, specialised organs that penetrate the roots of neighbouring plants and tap their xylem for water, dissolved minerals and nitrogen. It is a generalist, attaching opportunistically to grasses, sedges, rushes and forbs in the surrounding sward. This is the same strategy used by two species already held in the Codex: **Red Bartsia (*Odontites vernus*, CPU-00852)** and **Yellow Rattle (*Rhinanthus minor*, CPU-00926)**. All three belong to the reorganised Orobanchaceae, all three parasitise grassland roots, and all three carry the same practical warning for the cultivator: **the chemistry of a hemiparasite is not fully its own.** Composition varies with host identity, host condition, soil and season, and host-derived compounds — including host secondary metabolites and any accumulated toxins — can be drawn into the parasite's tissues. A batch of lousewort from a heath is not chemically interchangeable with a batch from a different heath.

Its ecological weight is real and measurable. According to PubMed, Demey and colleagues (2013, *Oecologia*) ran a removal experiment across sites infested with *P. sylvatica* and with *Rhinanthus angustifolius*, and found the two hemiparasites behaved very differently: removing *Rhinanthus* raised total plant biomass by about 24%, while removing *Pedicularis* had no significant effect — but *Pedicularis* produced roughly twice the litter of *Rhinanthus*, returning nitrogen equivalent to about 30% of the amount removed by mowing. Lousewort is therefore a nutrient-cycling agent as much as a parasite. This is a genuine, species-specific, peer-reviewed finding about *P. sylvatica* — and it is worth noting plainly that it is an **ecological** finding, not a medicinal one.

**On the tradition tags.** The tags **CM** (Avicenna, *Canon of Medicine*, Unani stream) and **DMM** (the Dioscoridean/Greek herbal stream) are carried on this entry from the Codex index, but I could not substantiate a primary-text attestation naming *Pedicularis sylvatica* specifically. Both Dioscorides and Avicenna wrote in and about the eastern Mediterranean and Persian floras; *P. sylvatica* is an Atlantic-European heathland plant largely outside that range. The overwhelming likelihood is that these tags are **genus- or name-inheritance** — a later herbalist mapping a classical *pedicularis*-type entry (a name applied loosely to louse-associated plants, including *Delphinium staphisagria*, the classical stavesacre and a genuine antiparasitic) onto this northern species. Do not repeat the CM/DMM attestation as fact for *P. sylvatica* without a primary citation. **[USER: verify]**

**The name is the doctrine — and the doctrine is false.** "Lousewort", and the Latin genus name *Pedicularis* (from *pediculus*, "little louse"), record an old European stock-keepers' belief that pasture carrying this plant bred lice in the grazing animals, and that eating it caused lousiness in sheep and cattle. This is a **folk aetiology**, not a pharmacological action. State it plainly: *no known constituent of* Pedicularis *causes ectoparasite infestation, and no plant can generate lice in a mammal.* The real correlation is almost certainly ecological — *P. sylvatica* thrives on poor, wet, unimproved ground, which is also the ground on which underfed and thin-fleeced livestock are most parasite-burdened. The plant marked the poor pasture; the poor pasture produced the lousy sheep; the name blamed the marker. It survives in the Codex as a **signature/name story** — a case study in how a plant acquires a doctrine from correlation — and explicitly **not** as an indication for use. Treat it as a teaching artefact.

### Active Components

There is no published compound-by-compound phytochemical profile specific to *Pedicularis sylvatica* that I could retrieve. What follows is **genus-level (congener) chemistry** and must be labelled as such wherever it is repeated.

Across the genus (~600 species), according to PubMed the recurring classes are **iridoid glycosides**, **phenylpropanoid / phenylethanoid glycosides (PhGs)** of the verbascoside (acteoside) type, **lignan glycosides**, **flavonoids**, **phenolics** and **alkaloids** (Li et al., 2014, *Am J Chin Med*; Yatoo et al., 2017, *Biomed Pharmacother*).

The nearest well-characterised European congener is *P. kerneri*, an alpine species from the Dolomites. According to PubMed, Venditti et al. (2015, *Nat Prod Res*) isolated from it the iridoid glucosides **aucubin, monomelittoside, plantarenaloside, euphroside, mussaenosidic acid and 8-epiloganic acid**, together with the phenylethanoid glycosides **verbascoside, leucosceptoside A and echinacoside**. This is a European alpine *Pedicularis* — **a congener, not *P. sylvatica*** — but it is the most defensible template for what a European lousewort's polar fraction is likely to contain. Note also from that work that the genus shows **chemotypes**: composition varies between populations. Combined with hemiparasitic host variability, this makes any fixed "constituent profile" for *P. sylvatica* untenable.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Signature Study only.** *P. sylvatica* enters the Codex as a **field-diagnostic and doctrinal specimen**, not a working remedy.

Its Kenetic uses are three:
1. **Indicator of ground.** Its presence reads the site: acidic, wet, unimproved, low-fertility, unfertilised. For a cultivator surveying land for other heath species, lousewort is a reliable soil-and-water signal.
2. **Hemiparasite triad.** Studied alongside Red Bartsia (CPU-00852) and Yellow Rattle (CPU-00926), it teaches the central lesson of parasitic materia medica: **provenance determines chemistry**. Any protocol built on a hemiparasite must specify host community, not just species.
3. **Doctrine dissection.** The louse story is the cleanest available example of a name generating a mechanism that does not exist. Kenetica uses it to train the discipline of separating *what a plant is called* from *what a plant does*.

It has **no** Kenetic internal use, no assigned protocol, and no dosing schedule.

| Photographic / site record | **Permitted** | Preferred method — the plant is a habitat indicator, not a crop |

Safety caveats: hemiparasite tissue may carry host-derived compounds, so even a "known" batch is not a controlled input. There is no pharmacopoeial monograph, no standardised extract and no human dosing literature for *P. sylvatica*. Any figure presented elsewhere as a lousewort dose should be treated as unsourced until a primary reference is produced.

**Conservation note:** *P. sylvatica* depends on unimproved wet heath, a habitat in steep decline across its range. Wild collection is discouraged on conservation grounds alone.

### Scientific Correlation

Each claim graded.

- **Root hemiparasitism, haustorial connection to host roots — ESTABLISHED (species-specific).** Documented directly for *P. sylvatica*.
- **Significant litter-mediated nitrogen return to the sward; no significant biomass release on removal — ESTABLISHED (species-specific, single well-designed field study).** Ecological, not medicinal.
- **Chemistry containing iridoid and phenylethanoid glycosides — PROBABLE, CONGENER/GENUS-LEVEL.** Demonstrated in other *Pedicularis*, including the European alpine *P. kerneri*. **Not verified in *P. sylvatica*.**
- **Antioxidant, hepatoprotective, antimicrobial, antitumour, anti-fatigue activity — PRECLINICAL, CONGENER ONLY.** Reported for other species and for isolated compounds (verbascoside, isoacteoside), in vitro and in animal models. **Do not attribute any of it to *P. sylvatica*.**
- **Species-specific medicinal evidence for *P. sylvatica* — NULL.** No clinical trials. No human data. No species-specific pharmacology retrieved.
- **"Breeds lice in livestock" — FALSE AS MECHANISM.** No supporting evidence of any kind; biologically implausible.
- **Classical CM/DMM attestation for this species — UNSUBSTANTIATED.** **[USER: verify]**

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Kenetica records **no reliable classical energetic assignment** for *Pedicularis sylvatica*. Where northern European practice placed the louseworts at all, it was by habitat logic — a plant of cold, wet, sour ground read as cold and moist — but this is an **inference from station**, not a sourced doctrine, and it is recorded here as inference only.

Within Kenetica's own frame the plant is classified functionally: **Dependent / Derived** — a substance whose character is contingent on what it is attached to. It shares this class with Red Bartsia and Yellow Rattle. The teaching is that a Dependent substance cannot be given a fixed energetic signature at all, because its signature is partly its host's.

### Contraindications

- **Do not take internally.** There is no established safe dose, no toxicology and no clinical data for *P. sylvatica*.
- **Hemiparasitic contamination risk.** Tissue may carry host-derived secondary metabolites, including toxins from parasitised neighbours. A safe-looking sward is not a guarantee of safe tissue.
- **Genus-level toxicity signals.** Congener studies in the genus include cytotoxicity findings (brine shrimp lethality in *P. groenlandica* extracts), and some *Pedicularis* species have been reported to accumulate toxic compounds. Absence of species data is not evidence of safety. **[USER: verify specific accumulator species against a primary toxicology source before citing.]**
- **Pregnancy, breastfeeding, paediatric use:** contraindicated by default — no data.
- **Livestock:** the historical louse doctrine is false, but that is not a clearance for feeding; grazing toxicology has not been established here.
- **Misidentification risk:** the dissected foliage and pink two-lipped flowers can be confused with *Pedicularis palustris* (marsh lousewort) and with young *Rhinanthus*. Confirm before any handling protocol.
- **Conservation:** protected or declining across parts of its range; check local status before collection.
