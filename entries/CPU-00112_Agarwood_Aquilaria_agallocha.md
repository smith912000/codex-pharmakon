# Agarwood — aloeswood, oud, eaglewood, Chen Xiang, agaru

## *Aquilaria agallocha* Roxb. (*A. malaccensis*) — the resin-soaked heartwood, warming qi-descender and calmer of the spirit

### Origin & Tradition

Agarwood is among the most precious aromatic substances on earth — the dark, dense, resin-impregnated heartwood that forms in *Aquilaria* trees of South and Southeast Asia in response to wounding and fungal infection. Healthy *Aquilaria* wood is pale and worthless; only when the tree is infected does it lay down the fragrant oleoresin ("agar") that, over years, produces the prized dark, sinking wood. This rarity (and the resulting overharvesting that has made wild *Aquilaria* endangered and CITES-protected) has made oud one of the costliest natural materials, central to Islamic, Buddhist and Hindu devotional incense and to luxury perfumery ("oud").

In medicine the heartwood is valued across the Eastern traditions. In **Chinese Medicine** **Chen Xiang** (沉香, "sinking fragrance," for the heavy wood that sinks in water) is a warming, aromatic herb that **moves and descends qi** — used for cold-type epigastric and abdominal pain and distension, for rebellious qi (hiccup, belching, vomiting, wheezing from "qi not grasped by the kidneys"), warming the middle and directing qi downward. In **Ayurveda** Agaru is a warming, aromatic, *kapha-vata*-reducing remedy for digestion, respiration, skin and as a nervine; in **Unani** *ud* is a cardiac and nervine tonic and aromatic. Across all, the fragrance is regarded as calming and centring to the spirit (Shen/mind), the basis of its meditative and devotional use.

The fragrance and activity arise from a complex of **sesquiterpenes and chromones** unique to resinous *Aquilaria* wood.

### Varieties

The two Arabic texts on disk grade agarwood by origin, colour, weight and above all by whether it sinks. Both are read in the Arabic (OpenITI editions; see `Sources/CM_Avicenna/text/` and `Sources/TBN_Prophetic/text/`).

**al-Mandalī** (CM; *Canon* Book II, letter ʿayn, entry *ʿūd*, Bulaq vol. 1 p. 613, line 16545). Avicenna ranks it first: "the best of its kinds is *al-ʿūd al-mandalī*, brought from the middle of India" (أجود أصنافه العود المندلي ويجلب من وسط بلاد الهند). "The *mandalī* is good throughout."

**al-Hindī** (CM; same entry). Next, "the one called *al-hindī*, which is a mountain wood from the roots"; it "is preferred to the *mandalī* because it does not breed lice and clings longer to clothes" (ويفضل على المندلي بأنه لا يولد القمل وهو أعبق بالثياب). Ibn Qayyim (TBN) ranks *al-hindī* first of all.

**al-Samandūrī** (CM; same entry). "Among the best agarwood is *al-samandūrī*, from Sufāla, a land of China at the far end of India." Its best is "the blue, heavy, hard, much-watered, thick wood with no white in it, that lasts on the fire; some prefer the black to the blue."

**al-Qamārī** (CM; same entry; TBN). "Then *al-qamārī*, from Sufāla of India." Its best is "the black, clean of white, heavy on the fire, thick, much-watered." Ibn Qayyim ranks it third, after *hindī* and *ṣīnī*.

**al-Ṣanfī** (CM; same entry). "A kind from Sufāla." Avicenna gives a name only.

**Lesser kinds** (CM; same entry). "After that *al-qāqullī*, *al-barī*, *al-quṭfī* and *al-ṣīnī*, also called *al-qashmūrī*, which is moist and sweet; and below these *al-jalāʾī*, *al-mānṭāqī*, *al-lawāmī* and *al-rabṭānī*." Names only. Ibn Qayyim ranks *al-ṣīnī* second.

**Kinam / Kyara** (no classical locus on disk). *Kinam* (Vietnamese *kỳ nam*; Japanese *kyara*) is the modern trade name for the very highest agarwood grade: a soft, resin-saturated wood traded at many times the price of ordinary oud. Neither Avicenna nor Ibn Qayyim uses the word, and no text on disk names it, so nothing here is attributed to them. Two points are offered as leads, not as attestations: the trade associates kinam with *Aquilaria crassna* and the old Champa coast of central Vietnam [USER: verify]; and Arabic geographers' *Ṣanf* is commonly identified with Champa and *Qimār* with the Khmer lands, which would make *al-ṣanfī* and *al-qamārī* the classical names nearest to kinam's source region [USER: verify; identification only, not an equation of grades]. The medieval test that best matches what kinam is prized for is Avicenna's: "the best agarwood is the one that sinks most in water, and the floating one is lifeless" (فأفضل العود أرسبه في الماء والطافي عديم الحياة).

**A trap in the hadith.** The Prophetic hadith that praise *al-ʿūd al-hindī* for "seven cures, among them pleurisy" are not about agarwood. Ibn Qayyim states plainly that *al-ʿūd al-hindī* is of two kinds, "one used in medicines, which is *kust*, also called *qust*" (costus, I_080), and "one used in perfume, called *al-uluwwa*" (TBN; *al-Ṭibb al-Nabawī*, letter ʿayn, entry *ʿūd*, line 6004). Only the *uluwwa* is agarwood.

*Synthesis of the varieties.* Avicenna separates agarwood by place of origin and ranks *mandalī* first, with *hindī* close and preferred for wear; Ibn Qayyim, writing three centuries later, ranks *hindī*, *ṣīnī*, *qamārī*, *mandalī* in that order. Both put the same physical test above origin: dark or blue, hard, heavy, oily, free of white, and sinking. Light wood that floats is the lowest grade in both. Kinam, a later trade grade, is not ranked by either text.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. **Move and descend qi (CM Chen Xiang core use).** For cold-type epigastric/abdominal pain and distension and for rebellious qi (hiccup, belching, vomiting, wheezing) — warming the middle, directing qi down.
2. **Warming digestive / carminative.** For cold, stagnant, painful digestion.
3. **Calming the spirit (Shen).** The fragrance/wood for restlessness and anxiety; meditative/devotional aromatic.
4. **Respiratory (kidney-grasping qi).** For wheezing/dyspnoea of the "qi not grasped" pattern. [USER: verify.]
5. **Nervine/cardiac tonic (Unani/Ayurveda).** Aromatic nervine and cardiac use.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Chinese Medicine mapping:** **Chen Xiang** — acrid, bitter, warm, aromatic; Spleen, Stomach, Kidney; moves/descends qi, warms middle, stops vomiting, aids kidney grasping of qi.
- **Ayurvedic/Unani mapping:** Agaru/ud — warming, aromatic; *kapha-vata* reducing; digestive, nervine, cardiac, calming.

### Cultivation Register

**Dioscorides / Greek (DMM).** Dioscorides records *agallochon* (ἀγάλλοχον) as an aromatic wood carried from India and Arabia — chewed for the breath, its decoction taken for the stomach and mouth. That is the whole of it: an imported aromatic with digestive indications. *De Materia Medica* carries no subtle-body system to graft onto it, and none should be manufactured here. What the Greek tradition genuinely offers a cultivator of this wood is a discipline of attention — the assay of a costly, heavily-adulterated material by scent, density and origin, exactly the connoisseurship the oud trade still demands. **(c) — no cultivation status is attested; the tradition supplies discernment, not a vital assignment.**

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** The *Canon* itself (Book II, entry *ʿūd*, Bulaq vol. 1 pp. 613-614, line 16545) gives agarwood as "hot and dry in the second degree, as I think"; "subtle, opening obstructions, breaking wind, removing excess moisture, and strengthening the viscera and all the organs." For the head, "agarwood benefits the brain greatly and strengthens the senses" (العود ينفع الدماغ جدا ويقوي الحواس); for the joints, "it strengthens the nerves (*aʿṣāb*) and benefits them"; for the stomach, "drunk at a dirham and a half it removes putrid moisture from the stomach and strengthens it, and strengthens the liver"; and "chewing it sweetens the breath greatly." These are the textual basis for the brain and nerve claims made of agarwood in this entry. They are humoral claims in Avicenna's own terms, not modern neurology. The doctrine of *innate heat and radical moisture* is the honest frame for a warming, moisture-clearing aromatic: it conserves and steadies the vital faculty rather than adding to it. The *Canon* entry does not use the word *mufarriḥ* (gladdening) of agarwood; that word is attested in the Prophetic-medicine text (TBN) below, so it is not published here as an Avicennan category. **(b) — tonic actions for the brain, senses, nerves and viscera are attested in the text, but no substance-specific cultivation status is.**

**Chinese (SBJ).** *Chen Xiang* 沉香 enters the Chinese materia medica late — first recorded in the *Mingyi Bielu* (Tao Hongjing, 420–589 CE) as the South Sea trade carried it inland — and so is **absent from the *Shennong Bencao Jing*, where the *san pin* 三品 grades that would confer a cultivation status are assigned.** It therefore carries no upper/middle/lower-grade *yang sheng* standing in the foundational classic. Its recorded role is therapeutic and directional: acrid, bitter, warm; moves and descends qi, warms the middle, aids the Kidney's grasping of qi. **(b) — attested in the materia medica but with no *san pin* grade recorded; a therapeutic, not a cultivation, status.**

**Ayurveda (SS).** *Agaru* is documented as a warming, *kapha-vata*-reducing aromatic — digestive, nervine, and prized as incense. It is not attested in the sources retrieved as a named *rasayana* (rejuvenation) or *vajikarana* (generative) drug; its standing is that of a therapeutic and fragrant *dravya*, not a formal rejuvenative discipline. **(b) — claimed by the tradition, but framed as therapeutic/aromatic with no *rasayana* status attested.**

**Tibetan (FMT).** Agarwood (*a-ga-ru*) is a valued constituent of Tibetan incense and compound formulas, reaching the plateau through Indian and Silk Road transmission. Its devotional and fumigatory use is real and historically attested; but the Four Tantras define **no formal "substances for subtle-body practice" category**, and none is asserted for it here. **(c) — ritual and compound use attested; no cultivation status.**

**Prophetic medicine (TBN).** Two hadith in the canonical collections place agarwood, *al-uluwwa*, in the Prophet's own practice and in the description of Paradise. Nāfiʿ reports that "when Ibn ʿUmar fumigated, he fumigated with unblended *uluwwa*, and with camphor placed with the *uluwwa*, and said: thus did the Messenger of God fumigate" (Muslim, *Ṣaḥīḥ*, no. 2254, Kitāb al-Alfāẓ). The people of Paradise are described as having "censers of *uluwwa*" (مجامرهم الألوة), and Abū al-Yamān glosses the word: "meaning agarwood" (Bukhārī, *Ṣaḥīḥ*, Kitāb Badʾ al-Khalq, chapter on the description of Paradise; Muslim, *Ṣaḥīḥ*, no. 2834). Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya, *al-Ṭibb al-Nabawī* (letter ʿayn, entry *ʿūd*), then gives the medicine: "hot and dry in the third degree; it opens obstructions, breaks wind, removes excess moisture, strengthens the viscera and the heart and gladdens it (*yufarriḥuhu*), benefits the brain, strengthens the senses, binds the bowel, and helps incontinence of urine arising from cold of the bladder." Quoting Ibn Samjūn, he adds that mixing camphor with agarwood in fumigation "has a medical meaning, the correction of each by the other," and that fumigation tends "the substance of the air," "one of the six necessary things on whose soundness the soundness of bodies depends." In this tradition, then, burning agarwood is a Prophetic practice (*sunna*) and a care of the air, the first of the six non-naturals, and taking it inwardly is a heart-gladdening tonic. **(b) — a sunna of fumigation and a *mufarriḥ* action are attested in named texts; the tradition assigns no cultivation grade, and none is added here.**

**Ritual note (across traditions).** Agarwood's pre-eminence as Buddhist, Islamic and Hindu devotional incense is securely attested and recorded here as historical liturgical practice. That devotional standing is not the same as a subtle-body *cultivation* status, and it is not converted into one.

**Practitioner's note.** What a cultivator actually does with agarwood is burn it and sit with it — a settling, centring aromatic for meditation and devotional attention, and internally a small warming dose to move and descend stuck, cold qi. No tradition surveyed grants it a long-life *cultivation grade*; the honest use is aromatic and conservational, and the first practical discipline it demands is the discernment of genuine resin-rich wood from the adulterated (and CITES-protected) trade.

### Contraindications

- **Heat patterns / yin-deficiency with heat (CM).** A warming aromatic is contraindicated in heat/dryness-from-deficiency.
- **Pregnancy.** Medicinal use [USER: verify].
- **Sustainability / authenticity (CITES).** Wild *Aquilaria* is endangered/CITES-protected; prefer sustainably cultivated/inoculated sources; adulteration is rife.
- **Allergy / sensitisation.** Possible with the oil.
