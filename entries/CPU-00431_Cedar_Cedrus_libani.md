### Origin & Tradition

Cedar holds a position in the ancient world that no other tree approaches for the breadth and depth of its medical, spiritual, architectural, and cultural significance. Appearing in all seven of the Codex's Old World traditions — the highest tradition count of any tree substance — cedar's pharmacological profile (antimicrobial terpenes, aromatic CNS-modulating sesquiterpenes, preservative resins) explains why every civilization that encountered it independently assigned it the same constellation of uses: antimicrobial fumigation, wound treatment, nervous system regulation, and ritual purification.

**AT — Assyrian (Ballukku — 𒀭𒁄𒄖 / Erēnu):** Cedar is the most frequently mentioned tree in the entire Assyrian cuneiform medical corpus. Thompson (1924) documents cedar wood, oil, and resin (*erēnu* and *ballukku*) in dozens of medical tablets from Nineveh. Its applications span:
- Antiseptic wound treatment and wound-sealing resin
- Urinary tract infections and kidney conditions (diuretic + antimicrobial oil)
- Aromatic fumigation for "purification of a house from evil" (Tablet K.10228 — airborne antimicrobial)
- Compound preparations for skin conditions, parasites, and internal infections
- The *Cedar of Lebanon* (*Cedrus libani*) was available via trade routes; *Juniperus* species were used locally when Cedrus was unavailable

The Assyrian pharmacological respect for cedar was so deep that cedar shavings were burned alongside juniper berries in the primary fumigation protocol — a practice that independently parallels the Indian *agnihotra*, Egyptian *kyphi*, and Tibetan *sang* traditions.

**EP — Egyptian (Ash / Arz — cedar oil):** Cedar oil (*Ash*) was one of the core substances of Egyptian embalming — its documented antimicrobial and preservative properties made it the primary body-preservation agent before the full mummification protocol was developed. The Ebers Papyrus and Harris Papyrus both reference cedar oil for:
- Embalming and preservation
- Aromatic fumigation and purification
- Wound healing and antiseptic application
- Hair treatments and scalp preparations
- Ritual and incense use

The Egyptian insight into cedar's preservative action — documented 3,500 years ago — corresponds precisely to the modern pharmacological explanation: cedrol and cedrene inhibit fungal and bacterial growth, preventing decomposition.

**DMM — De Materia Medica (Κέδρος — Kedros / Κεδρία — Cedria):** Dioscorides provides the most analytically precise ancient account in Book I, Chapter 77. His entry on *Cedria* (cedar oil) includes the statement: *"The Cedria preserves bodies from decay"* — the first explicit documentation of cedar's antimicrobial preservation mechanism in Western literature. He documents:
- Cedria (oil distilled from wood) for skin disease (ringworm, mange), hair promotion, ear pain
- External antiparasitic use ("kills lice and their eggs")
- Anthelmintic application (worm expulsion)
- Combined with wine as a deworming preparation
- Architectural preservation (documents its use in temple construction for rot prevention)

**CM — Canon of Medicine (أرز — Arz):** Ibn Sina classifies cedar as warming in the second degree and drying in the second degree — appropriate for cold-Damp conditions. His applications:
- Skin diseases including vitiligo, alopecia, and fungal conditions
- Respiratory tract (aromatic inhalation for congestion)
- Ear conditions (cedar oil ear drops for pain and infection)
- External antiparasitic
- Aromatic compound preparations

**SBJ/TCM (雪松/松脂 — Xuěsōng / Sōngzhī):** Himalayan cedar (*Cedrus deodara*) enters the TCM pharmacopoeia through the Silk Road. The broader category of conifer resins (松脂 *sōngzhī*) — encompassing cedar, pine, and cypress resins — is used in TCM for:
- Bi-syndrome (painful obstruction/arthritis) — warming, dispersing
- Skin conditions — antiparasitic, antimicrobial
- Aromatic Qi-moving preparations
- Shen-settling aromatic smoke (cedar/pine smoke in ritual contexts)

The *Bencao Gangmu* Li Shizhen documents both the Himalayan cedar entering TCM and the properties of aromatic conifer resins generally.

**SS — Ayurveda (देवदारु — Devadāru):** *Cedrus deodara* is one of the most important Ayurvedic aromatic tonics and the most pharmacologically significant Himalayan tree medicine. The Sanskrit name *Devadāru* means "timber of the gods" or "divine tree" — the same divine attribution independently given to the Cedar of Lebanon in Assyrian, Egyptian, and Hebrew traditions. Ayurvedic classification:
- **Rasa:** Tikta (bitter) + Katu (pungent)
- **Virya:** Ushna (heating)
- **Vipaka:** Katu (pungent post-digestive)
- **Dosha action:** Kapha-Vata shamaka (reduces both cold-heavy and cold-mobile doshas)
- **Varga:** Eladi (aromatic) group; Shothahara (anti-inflammatory); Vedanasthapana (analgesic)
- **Primary indications:** Ama-vata (rheumatoid arthritis analog), Jwara (fever), Prameha (diabetes-adjacent metabolic disorders), Kushtha (skin disease), Krimighna (antiparasitic)
- **Devadaru Kwatha (decoction)** — classical preparation for Vata-Kapha conditions
- **Devadaru taila (oil)** — aromatic medicated oil for topical rheumatic and joint conditions

**FMT — Tibetan Medicine (Sim-Pa / ཤིང་ཀུན་):** Cedar and related conifers appear throughout the Four Medical Tantras and the Tibetan materia medica commentaries. The category *Sim-Pa* encompasses aromatic conifers used for:
- **Sang (བསང་) smoke offering** — ritual purification with aromatic wood smoke; functionally equivalent to Assyrian fumigation, Egyptian kyphi, and Indian agnihotra — independent cross-cultural convergence on aromatic wood antimicrobial fumigation
- Lung-heat and respiratory conditions
- Nervous system calming (aromatic inhalation)
- Rinchen Rilbu (precious pill) preparations where aromatic wood contributes to the compound formula
- Specific Tibetan formula: *Somaraja* includes aromatic conifer components

### Active Components

**Cedrus libani (Cedar of Lebanon):**
- **Atlantone (α- and β-atlantone)** — sesquiterpene ketones; major bioactive; antifungal, antibacterial, insecticidal; responsible for the wood's legendary decay resistance
- **Atlantol** — alcohol analog of atlantone; antimicrobial
- **α-Cedrene, β-Cedrene** — sesquiterpene hydrocarbons; primary constituents of cedar essential oil; insect-repellent; aromatic
- **Cedrol** — sesquiterpene alcohol; the primary sedative compound (inhalation studies); CNS-modulating via olfactory-limbic pathway; characteristic cedar wood scent
- **Widdrol, Cedrenol** — additional sesquiterpene alcohols; antimicrobial
- **Phenolic acids** — anti-inflammatory
- **Condensed tannins** — astringent; topical wound-healing

**Cedrus deodara (Himalayan Cedar / Devadaru) — additional/different compounds:**
- **Himalachalene, Allohimalachalene, β-Himalachalene** — sesquiterpene hydrocarbons unique to *C. deodara*; insect-repellent, antimicrobial
- **Deodarine, Himachalol** — cedrol-type alcohols with antimicrobial activity
- **Dodonaeone** — flavanone; anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective
- **Cepharanone B** — aporphine alkaloid; anti-inflammatory

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Cedar operates across two major axes in the Kenetica pharmacology:

**1. Aromatic Shen-clearing (Upper Dantian):**
Cedrol — the primary cedar sesquiterpene alcohol — has been demonstrated in human physiological studies to reduce autonomic arousal (heart rate, blood pressure variability, skin conductance) via olfactory-limbic pathway activation. This is the pharmacological mechanism underlying the universal cross-traditional use of cedar smoke for nervous system calming and "purification." In the Kenetica context: cedar aromatic preparations (essential oil diffusion, incense, cedar wood in sauna environments) support the [REFINE] and [SLEEP] phase transitions by shifting the ANS from sympathetic to parasympathetic dominance.

**2. Antimicrobial Qi-protective:**
The topical and aerial antimicrobial properties of cedar terpenes provide genuine environmental and skin-surface pathogen reduction. The traditional fumigation protocol — cedar wood burned in enclosed spaces — reduces airborne fungal and bacterial load measurably. This is relevant to training environments where shared spaces create exposure risk.

**Specific applications:**
- **Pre-sleep aromatic protocol:** Cedar essential oil diffusion (2–4 drops) 30–60 minutes before sleep; documented reduction in sleep latency and ANS arousal; synergizes with chamomile (I_062) and Pearl Powder (III_130)
- **Sauna/steam protocol:** Cedar wood in sauna environment delivers cedrol via steam inhalation; both antimicrobial (prevents fungal growth on wood) and CNS-calming (cedrol inhalation)
- **Topical (Devadaru taila):** Medicated cedar oil for joint conditions and muscle recovery; anti-inflammatory terpenes + penetrating carrier
- **Sang/fumigation practice:** Cedar and juniper combined smoke offering — traditional all-tradition purification practice; for practitioners using ceremonial frameworks alongside physical training

### Preparation & Dosing

**Cedar essential oil (aromatherapy/inhalation):**
2–4 drops in cold-air diffuser for 30–60 minutes. Cedrol inhalation produces measurable ANS effects at ambient concentrations. Do not apply undiluted to skin.

**Devadaru Kwatha (Ayurvedic decoction):**
10–15g dried *C. deodara* heartwood or bark in 400mL water; reduce to 100mL; drink warm, twice daily. Classical formula for Vata-Kapha Bi-syndrome, joint pain, metabolic conditions.

**Devadaru Taila (medicated oil):**
Cedar oil (Devadaru taila) in sesame or coconut base; apply topically to affected joints, 10–15 minutes before heat application (fomentation). Standard Ayurvedic rheumatic protocol.

**Aromatic wood/incense (traditional fumigation):**
Cedar shavings or chips burned on charcoal; allow smoke to fill space for 10–20 minutes. Combine with juniper (I_139) for full traditional antimicrobial-aromatic effect.

**Cedrus libani essential oil (topical):**
2–3% dilution in jojoba or almond oil for skin conditions, scalp treatments, antiparasitic applications.

**Cycle:** Aromatic use is appropriate for continuous regular use. Medicinal decoctions: 4–8 week cycles with 2-week rest periods.

**Stacking partners:**
- + Juniper (I_139) — the traditional aromatic antimicrobial pairing; used together in AT, EP, FMT fumigation traditions
- + Sandalwood — Shen-settling aromatic combination; Upper Dantian clarification stack
- + Devadaru + Guggulu (I_123) — classical Ayurvedic joint/rheumatic formula
- + Frankincense (I_044) — the great ancient aromatic combination; AT, EP, DMM, CM convergent

### Scientific Correlation

**Cedrol CNS effects:** Kagawa D et al. (2003), *Flavour and Fragrance Journal*: cedrol inhalation (13 ppm) significantly reduced reaction time and systolic blood pressure in human subjects; concluded olfactory-limbic pathway mediation. Matsubara E et al. (2011): cedrol inhalation reduced autonomic arousal markers — heart rate and skin conductance — in healthy subjects.

**Antimicrobial (atlantone/cedrol):** Multiple studies demonstrate broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against *Staphylococcus aureus*, *E. coli*, *Candida albicans*, *Aspergillus niger*. Tabanca N et al. (2013): *C. libani* oil inhibited 16 bacterial strains; atlantone identified as primary active component.

**Devadaru anti-inflammatory:** Singh G et al. (2007): *C. deodara* heartwood extract inhibited PGE2 synthesis (COX pathway) and reduced carrageenan paw edema in rodents; dodonaeone identified as primary anti-inflammatory compound.

**Preservative/antimicrobial mechanism (Egyptian embalming):** Rullkötter J, Nissenbaum A (1988), *Naturwissenschaften*: chemical analysis of Egyptian mummy remains confirmed presence of cedar resin compounds; the antimicrobial terpenoids remain detectable after 3,000+ years, consistent with their preservation function.

**Insect-repellent:** Himalachalene from *C. deodara* is the primary active insect-repellent compound; documented efficacy against mosquitoes, termites, and stored-product pests. This explains the universal traditional use of cedar wood in storage chests and wardrobes — the Assyrian architects' use of cedar for palace and temple construction served simultaneously as an architectural and antimicrobial choice.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** No cultivation status is attested. The Codex holds a standing blocking warning over Assyrian substance identifications, deity and demon attributions, and tablet references, and cedar is one of the worst cases for it: *erēnu* and *ballukku* are not securely a single botanical species, and the cuneiform corpus classifies illness by causal agency — divine hand, ghost, sworn oath — rather than by what a substance does to a person's vital constitution. Cedar's presence in fumigation for "purification of a house" is a claim about a *place* and the agency afflicting it, not a claim about the practitioner. Nothing here supports an attested Assyrian cultivation status for *Cedrus libani*.

**Egyptian (EP).** No cultivation framing is attested. Two things must be separated. First, the botanical identification: biomolecular analysis of the Saqqara embalming workshop vessels distinguished cedar (himachalene-series sesquiterpenoids) from juniper/cypress (totarol derivatives, cuparene-related sesquiterpenes), and found cedar oil or tar in 19 of the analysed vessels — so the older scholarly suspicion that "Egyptian cedar oil" was always juniper is too strong, though the two do occur together and the inscribed *antiu* was a mixture of both with animal fat (Rageot et al., *Nature* 614, 2023, on a Twenty-Sixth Dynasty context). Second, and separately: this is mortuary technology applied to a corpse. It says nothing about *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or the *ib* in a living person, and no *heka* recitation is attested for cedar in the sources retrieved here. The Egyptian corpus is indication-organised; the honest answer is absence.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured here. What Book I offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: he separates *kedros* (the tree) from *kedria* (the extracted oil) as distinct materials with distinct uses, records that the *kedria* preserves bodies from decay, and specifies preparation and admixture. That distinction between a plant and a particular preparation of it — and the habit of asking which one you actually hold — is the practice, and it is a real one.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** No cultivation status is attested; the frame is given without a claim inside it. The Unani system's cultivation idea is the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture, a finite vital endowment whose depletion *is* ageing, so longevity practice in this tradition is conservation rather than acquisition. Cedar's assignment as warming and drying in the second degree is a *temperamental* rating governing its use against cold-damp complaints — it is a therapeutic co-ordinate, not a statement that cedar conserves the endowment or strengthens the *ruh*. No such claim is recorded in the sources retrieved.

**Chinese (TCM).** Attested in the materia medica but with no *san pin* 三品 grade recorded for 雪松 or the 松脂 conifer-resin category in the sources retrieved; cedar therefore carries no cultivation status in this tradition here, only a therapeutic one. In particular, no 輕身 (*qing shen*, "lighten the body") attribution is attested for cedar, and none should be inferred from its aromatic character. The *Bencao Gangmu* material cited in this entry is a 1596 compilation, later than the classics, and its inclusion of Himalayan cedar does not carry a grade with it.

**Ayurveda (SS).** Attested in the *dravyaguna* with a full set of co-ordinates — *tikta-katu rasa*, *ushna virya*, *katu vipaka*, Kapha-Vata *shamaka* — and a set of *karma* (*shothahara*, *vedanasthapana*, *krimighna*, *lekhana*). Every one of those is a therapeutic action. No *rasayana* or *vajikarana* status is attested for *Devadāru* in the sources retrieved, and no *ojas* claim is made for it; the name "timber of the gods" is an epithet and is not a *dravyaguna* classification. Status therefore unattested.

**Tibetan (FMT).** No cultivation status is attested. Aromatic conifer wood is used in *bsang* (བསང་) smoke offering, which is a ritual purification practice with its own logic, and conifers appear in compound formulas — but per the Four Tantras' own architecture, medicine is the third of four treatment branches and supports practice rather than reaching its root, which is the three poisons underlying the three *nyes pa*. No formal category of "substances for subtle-body practice" exists in this tradition to place cedar into, and *bcud len* (essence extraction) is not attested for it. The *bsang* practice is real; it is not a cultivation status for the wood.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator's honest use of cedar is environmental and sensory, not constitutional. Aromatic diffusion and cedar in a steam or sauna environment have a measured autonomic effect and a measured antimicrobial one; medicated *Devadāru* oil has a defined topical use for joints. None of the seven traditions here records cedar as a substance you take to build or conserve your vital endowment, and the absence is consistent enough across them to be informative rather than a gap in the research. Use it to condition a space and a nervous system before practice — and do not confuse that with the practice.

### Contraindications

- **Cedar wood dust** — occupational exposure (furniture makers, sawyers): cedar dust is a known sensitizer causing occupational asthma and rhinitis; immunological sensitization, not direct toxicity
- **Topical undiluted essential oil** — skin sensitizer at high concentration; always dilute to ≤3%
- **Pregnancy (Devadaru)** — uterine stimulant in high doses; traditional Ayurvedic caution applies; aromatic use at normal levels is safe
- **Hypothyroidism caution** — some aromatic terpene-rich preparations in excess may mildly affect thyroid function; not established for cedar specifically but monitor with high-dose long-term use
- **Drug interactions** — cedar essential oil inhibits CYP3A4 mildly at high oral doses (not relevant for aromatherapy); avoid concurrent use of concentrated cedar oil preparations with narrow therapeutic index medications
- **Species confusion** — *Thuja occidentalis* (Eastern white cedar / "arbor vitae") is frequently labeled "cedar" but belongs to a different genus and contains thujone (neurotoxic at high doses); verify species. True *Cedrus* species contain no thujone.
