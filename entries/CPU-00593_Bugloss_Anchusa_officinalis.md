# Common Bugloss — Alkanet, Ox-tongue, Lisan al-thawr, Buglosson

## *Anchusa officinalis* L. | Family: Boraginaceae

**Tradition Tags:** EP, AT, DMM, CM
**Energetic Classification:** Warm and moist (temperate, tending to the first degree) — a sanguine, blood-friendly herb in the Galenic scheme
**Training Phase:** Foundation

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ebers (EP):** The Boraginaceae of the Nile margins — rough-leaved, blue-flowered herbs grouped by Egyptian scribes with the "tongue plants" for the shape and texture of their foliage — appear in the Ebers material among poultice herbs applied to inflamed skin and stubborn sores. The genuine record is general rather than binomial-precise: Egyptian materia medica named plants by leaf-form and habitat, and the ox-tongue herbs sat among the cooling, moistening topical drugs used to soften hardened tissue.

**Assyrian (AT):** The cuneiform herbal tablets of Ashurbanipal's library catalogue rough, blue-flowered "tongue herbs" among remedies for the heart and blood — reflecting the doctrine of signatures that ran through Mesopotamian practice, in which a red-rooted, blood-red-yielding plant was read as a remedy for the sanguine humour. Anchusa roots yield a red pigment (the alkannin/shikonin family shared across the Boraginaceae), which underwrote both a dye trade and a medical symbolism of blood and vitality.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** In *De Materia Medica*, Dioscorides describes *ankhousa* — the ox-tongue and alkanet herbs — with the red root used in wine for jaundice and kidney complaints, and the root steeped in oil applied to burns, ulcers and skin eruptions. He distinguished several kinds by root colour and leaf roughness, and the red-rooted alkanets were prized as much for their dye as for their emollient topical use.

**Avicenna (CM):** In the *Qanun fi'l-Tibb*, Ibn Sina treats *lisan al-thawr* ("ox-tongue") as a herb of the sanguine temperament — gladdening the heart, thinning the blood's melancholic residues, and used in cordial confections intended to lift the spirits. Persian and later Unani practice carried the ox-tongue herbs forward as exhilarant cordials, a use that blurred botanically across *Anchusa*, *Borago* and *Echium* — all rough, blue-flowered Boraginaceae treated as near-interchangeable "buglosses."

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Bugloss sits at the meeting of two ancient readings. The red root spoke of blood, and so the plant was tied to the sanguine humour and to cordial, heart-gladdening medicine. The rough, tongue-shaped leaf gave it the "bugloss" name (Greek *bous* + *glossa*, "ox-tongue") and grouped it with the demulcent, softening herbs. Modern phytochemistry partly redeems the first reading: the Boraginaceae are rich in rosmarinic acid and other caffeic-acid esters with genuine anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity, so the "blood-friendly, inflammation-soothing" symbolism maps onto a real class of active molecules — even as the same family carries a hidden hepatotoxic burden the ancients could not see.

### Active Components

- **Rosmarinic acid** and related caffeic-acid esters — the dominant phenolic acids of the Boraginaceae, responsible for antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity.
- **Naphthoquinone pigments** of the alkannin/shikonin type in the root — the source of the red dye and of documented topical wound-supporting activity in the wider family.
- **Mucilage and allantoin-type constituents** — underwriting the demulcent, tissue-softening topical use.
- **Pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs)** — low but real concentrations typical of the Boraginaceae; the decisive safety-limiting constituents (see Contraindications).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Bugloss enters Kenetica strictly as a **Foundation-phase topical and symbolic herb**, never as an internal tonic. Its role is skin-surface: a cooling, softening application over inflamed, chafed or reddened tissue during the early conditioning phase, where the practitioner is learning to attend to the body's surface signals. Its cordial, "spirit-lifting" heritage is retained as a symbolic anchor for the Foundation theme of steadiness and good cheer — but the internal cordial use of the tradition is deliberately **not** carried forward, because the Boraginaceae PA burden makes habitual internal use unsafe.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed: the phenolic chemistry that underlies bugloss's traditional soothing reputation is well characterised at the family level. Petersen & Simmonds (2003), *Phytochemistry*, review rosmarinic acid — an ester of caffeic acid abundant in the Boraginaceae — and document its antiviral, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities. DOI: [https://doi.org/10.1016/s0031-9422(02)00513-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0031-9422(02)00513-7).

The safety-limiting side of the same family is equally documented. Kopp et al. (2019), *Planta Medica*, describe methods to deplete pyrrolizidine alkaloids from herbal extracts, noting their hepatotoxic, pneumotoxic, genotoxic and cytotoxic effects and citing a tolerable daily intake set as low as 0.007 µg/kg body weight. DOI: [https://doi.org/10.1055/a-0961-2658](https://doi.org/10.1055/a-0961-2658).

**Evidence grade:** Traditional / preclinical. No controlled clinical trials of *Anchusa officinalis* itself for any indication were retrieved. Activity is inferred from family-level phenolic chemistry; the PA hazard is well established across the Boraginaceae. Treat all claims of internal benefit as unproven and outweighed by the hepatotoxicity risk.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic/Unani:** Warm and moist in the first degree; a sanguine cordial herb (*lisan al-thawr*), exhilarant, blood-thinning of melancholic residues.
- **Ayurvedic (by analogy, not classical attribution):** *rasa* — madhura (sweet), tikta (bitter); *virya* — cooling on the surface; *vipaka* — madhura. Pitta-pacifying topically.
- **TCM:** No classical Chinese usage; the alkannin-bearing Boraginaceae most nearly parallel the "cool the blood, resolve toxin" surface category, but this is analogical only.

### Contraindications

- **Pyrrolizidine-alkaloid hepatotoxicity — do not take internally on a repeated or habitual basis.** As a member of the Boraginaceae, *Anchusa officinalis* contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids capable of causing hepatic veno-occlusive disease, and with genotoxic and potentially carcinogenic activity. Regulatory tolerable-intake limits are extremely low.
- **Contraindicated in pregnancy and lactation** — PAs cross the placenta and enter breast milk.
- **Contraindicated in existing liver disease** and with concurrent hepatotoxic drugs.
- **Topical use only** is the safest form; even then, avoid application to broken skin where systemic PA absorption may occur, and avoid prolonged daily use.
- Not for infants or children internally under any circumstance.
