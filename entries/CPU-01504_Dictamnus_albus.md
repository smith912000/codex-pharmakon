# Dictamnus albus — Diktamnon (Dioscorides 3-37)

## *Dictamnus albus (the translators' first suggestion; "White Dittany, Gas Plant, Candle Plant, Fraxinella"), with Dictamnus fraxinella and, in a second group of names, Origanum dictamnus, "Dittany of Crete"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant as a drug. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The SUGGESTED line (lines 6841 and 6842) reads "Dictamnus albus, Dictamnus fraxinella — White Dittany, Gas Plant, Candle Plant, Fraxinella", and then a second group, "Origanum dictamnus, Dictamnus creticus, Amaracus dictamnus — Dittany of Crete". Dioscorides' own words are "Dictamnus is a Cretian herb", and the second group is the one carrying "Dittany of Crete". The translators therefore name two different plants for the one chapter, and the Codex does not choose between them. The entry takes its binomial from the spine row and the first-named suggestion only. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-37. DIKTAMNON*, heading at line 6840, text from line 6843, continuing after the translators' illustration pages at line 6853 and ending at line 6856. It stands in Book Three, "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants", after 3-36 *pulegium*.

The chapter reads, in its opening: "Dictamnus is a Cretian herb — sharp, smooth, similar to pulegium [3-36]. It has bigger leaves, downy, with a kind of woolly adherence, but it bears neither flower nor seed. It does all the things that the cultivated pulegium does but much more forcibly, for not only taken as a drink but also applied and inhaled as smoke, it expels dead embryos." It continues: "They say that goats in Crete having fed on the herb reject arrows if shot [wounds]. The juice rubbed on (alone or with polenta) is cleansing. The herb is applied to cure prickles under the feet, or [on] the rest of the body. It is also effective for a painful spleen, for it lessens it. They gather it in the summer and the fall. The root warms those who taste it. It is also a birth-hastener, and the juice (taken as a drink with wine) helps those bitten by snakes. Such is the strength of this herb that even the smell drives away poisonous beasts, and the touch kills them. The juice dropped into a wound caused by iron, or the bite of a poisonous beast (and as well as dropping it on, if it is taken in drink), immediately cures."

A parenthetical paragraph follows in the translation: "(Rub dried dictamnus in your hands until it is similar to meal, throw in a drop of wine and apply it to your body. It is good against all snakes. It first cleans ulcers and rotten, gangrenous ulcerations, and then it fills them up. If one is pricked apply this to him and immediately you shall help him. Having made meal of it, apply it for the spleen and disorders from inflammation in hidden places. Dig up the herb in the spring, the hot seasons, and in the autumn.)" The brackets are the translators', and the chapter does not say why the text is set off in them. [USER: verify] The names printed are *pulegium sylvestre, embactron, beluacos, artemedion, creticus, ephemeron, eldian, belotocos, dorcidium, elbunium*, and the Roman *ustilago rustica*.

So the chapter describes a sharp, smooth, downy-leaved Cretan herb with no flower or seed (as written), stronger than cultivated pulegium, and gives it a long list of actions: expelling a dead embryo (drunk, applied or as inhaled smoke), hastening birth, cleansing and filling ulcers, lessening a painful spleen, and treating snake and beast bites and iron wounds. It also records, with "They say", a Cretan goat story: goats that have fed on it reject arrows. The strength claim is strong ("even the smell drives away poisonous beasts, and the touch kills them"). Recording is not endorsing; the Codex does not take these as facts. The gathering seasons given are the summer and the fall in the main text and the spring, the hot seasons and the autumn in the bracketed paragraph; the two are not the same list and the text does not reconcile them. [USER: verify]

**Other chapters on the same name (not merged).** 3-38 *PSEUDODIKTAMNOS* (line 6857) says the "bastard" form grows widely, is less sharp, and "does the same things as dictamnus, but is not similarly effective". 3-39 *DIKTAMNOS ALLO* (line 6864), "another kind of dictamnus" from Crete, has leaves like sisymbrium, a black soft flower like wild origanum, and is "effective for all things (as that above) but somewhat less biting"; it is mixed with plasters and antidotal medicines. 5-57 *OINOS DIKTAMNITES* (line 10074) is a wine of four teaspoons of dittany in must, for squeamish stomach, wastes and the menstrual flow. None of the three has an entry in the Codex (chapter-level grep); I_976 (*Anthemis cotula*) quotes a SUGGESTED line sharing the name *Dictamnus creticus* with a different chapter, and IV_09 (Theriaca) has a table row for "Cretan dittany" with no Dioscorides chapter cited. The translators' index lists *Dictamnus albus* at pages 404, 408 and 771; 404 is the page of the start of 3-37 and 771 the page of 5-57. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a strong Cretan herb used to expel a dead embryo, to hasten birth, to cleanse ulcers, to reduce a painful spleen and against snake bites and wounds, plus a goat-and-arrow tale and a claim that its touch kills poisonous beasts. These are the text's claims, some hedged with "They say". The Codex gives no protocol: the uses for the uterus are abortifacient and obstetric, snake bites and iron wounds are medical emergencies, and the identification is uncertain between two different plants.

### Preparation & Dosing

3-37 gives these preparations: a drink; the herb applied; the herb inhaled as smoke; the juice rubbed on alone or with polenta; the juice taken "as a drink with wine"; the juice dropped into a wound; and, in the bracketed paragraph, dried herb rubbed in the hands "similar to meal", with "a drop of wine" applied to the body, or the meal applied for the spleen. The only quantity is "a drop of wine". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints the herb as "sharp, smooth", and "The root warms those who taste it". It also says the plant does what pulegium does "much more forcibly". No drying or moistening quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: provenance (Crete), a leaf description (bigger than pulegium, downy and woolly), gathering seasons (given twice, differently), the taste test (the root warms the taster) and comparison with a cultivated herb. He records a goat tale and a claim about the smell repelling beasts, keeping the distance of "They say"; recording is not endorsing. It assigns the plant no status. The identification is the translators' and is two plants. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. Its uses are obstetric, abortifacient, wound and antivenom remedies with no amounts, so it is history and not a practice. The two-seasons list and the two-plants suggestion are reminders of how loosely the identification and the text are held together.

### Contraindications

The chapter's principal uses expel an embryo, hasten birth and treat snake bites and wounds, none of which is for self-treatment; an abortifacient is not safe for pregnancy, and snake bites and deep wounds need medical care. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion of two different plants, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
