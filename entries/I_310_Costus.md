# I-310: Saussurea costus (Costus / Indian Costus)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-310 | Asteraceae (Compositae)**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Saussurea costus* (Falc.) Lipsch.
**Synonyms:** *Aucklandia costus* Falc.; *Saussurea lappa* (Decne.) C.B.Clarke; *Aucklandia lappa*
**Family:** Asteraceae

**Common Names:**
- English: Costus, Indian Costus, Kuth, Kushtha
- Arabic: *Qust al-Hindi* (قسط الهندي) — "Indian costus"; *Qust al-Bahri* (قسط البحري) — "sea costus" (white variety)
- Sanskrit/Ayurveda: *Kushtha* (कुष्ठ) — one of the most frequently mentioned herbs in classical Ayurveda
- Chinese: *Mù Xiāng* (木香) — "fragrant wood" — though *Mù Xiāng* sometimes refers to *Aplotaxis auriculata* or several related species
- German: *Kostuswurzel* (costus root)
- Tibetan: *Ru rta* — one of the key aromatics in Tibetan medicine

**Etymology:**
*Saussurea* — named for the Swiss botanist and naturalist Horace-Bénédict de Saussure (1740–1799). *costus* — from Greek *kostos* / Latin *costus*, an ancient trade name for this root imported via the spice routes. *kushtha* in Sanskrit means "skin disease medicine" — its primary Ayurvedic designation. *Qust al-Hindi* (Indian costus) distinguishes it from European costus (*Costus speciosus*), a different plant.

**The Hadith Documentation:**
*Qust al-Hindi* is specifically mentioned in Sahih Bukhari (9th century CE) as a prophetic medicine recommended by Muhammad for seven diseases. This constitutes the most specific and authoritative documentation of any plant in Islamic medicine. The hadith records *qust al-hindi* and *qust al-bahri* as recommended for: pleurisy, throat ailments, and general therapeutic use. This single textual reference drove centuries of trade demand for Indian costus root throughout the Islamic world.

**CITES Status:**
*Saussurea costus* is listed on CITES Appendix I (highest protection) due to overharvesting in its native Himalayan habitat. The Kashmir/Pakistan/Northwest India populations are severely threatened. Trade in *S. costus* root requires CITES permits. Alternatives: *Saussurea obvallata* (brahma kamal) or cultivated Costus species from outside Appendix I.

**Morphology:**
Perennial alpine herb; 1–2 m; grows 2,500–4,000 m altitude in Himalayan meadows; large basal leaves; dark purple thistle-like flowers; distinctive strongly aromatic root (earthy, balsamic, faintly sweet odor); harvested in 3rd year.

**Parts Used:** Root (dried) — the only part with documented pharmacological activity and commercial value.

**The Ancient Spice Route Molecule:**
Costus root is documented in every major pre-modern Old World pharmacological tradition precisely because it was a **trade commodity** moving along spice routes from the Himalayan source to Egypt, Assyria, Greece, Rome, Arabia, and China for at least 3,500 years. Unlike herbs that achieved convergence through parallel discovery (multiple civilizations independently finding the same plant in their local flora), costus convergence is driven by **actual physical movement of the root** along the ancient incense-spice-medicine trade networks. The aroma — distinctive, powerful, earthy-balsamic — made it recognizable and valued across cultures.

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Sesquiterpene Lactones

**1. Dehydrocostus lactone (DHC) — PRIMARY compound**
- Eudesmanolide sesquiterpene lactone; 2–3% dried root
- **5-LOX inhibition:** Reduces leukotriene synthesis → anti-inflammatory + bronchodilator
- **NF-κB inhibition:** Potent; similar mechanism to parthenolide (I-299) and helenalin (I-300)
- **Anti-spasmodic:** Relaxes bronchial and intestinal smooth muscle
- **Anti-cancer (emerging):** In vitro and in vivo activity against multiple tumor lines; clinical not validated

**2. Costunolide — MAJOR compound**
- Germacranolide sesquiterpene lactone; co-dominant with DHC
- **Anti-inflammatory:** NF-κB + COX inhibition
- **Vasodilator:** Endothelium-dependent relaxation
- **Anti-fungal:** Membrane disruption
- Strong Asteraceae sensitizer (α-methylene-γ-lactone class)

**3. Dihydrocostunolide, Isodehydrocostus lactone** — related sesquiterpene lactones; anti-inflammatory

### Essential Oil

**4. Costic acid (40–50% EO)** — characteristic aromatic; carminative
**5. α-Costol, β-Costol** — alcohol sesquiterpenoids; anti-bacterial; aromatic

### Sterols

**6. β-Sitosterol** — anti-inflammatory; adaptogenic

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### Anti-Inflammatory / Bronchodilator
- **Costunolide + DHC → 5-LOX inhibition** → reduced leukotriene B4 + C4 → anti-asthmatic + anti-inflammatory throughout respiratory tract
- **Costunolide → bronchial smooth muscle relaxation (Ca²⁺ channel modulation)** → bronchodilator effect
- Combined anti-inflammatory + bronchodilatory = explanatory mechanism for respiratory traditional use

### Digestive
- **Costic acid EO + sesquiterpene lactones → carminative** (inhibit GI smooth muscle spasm) + bitter receptor stimulation → digestive enzyme secretion
- **Anti-spasmodic:** DHC relaxes intestinal smooth muscle → reduces cramping

### Immunomodulatory
- **Costunolide → NF-κB inhibition** → reduced TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6
- **Anti-cancer exploration:** DHC induces apoptosis in multiple cancer cell lines; mechanism via mitochondrial pathway + NF-κB

### Antimicrobial
- Costic acid EO + costunolide → broad-spectrum anti-bacterial + anti-fungal in vitro

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The Incense Root:**
Costus root has one of the most distinctive aromatic signatures in the pharmacopoeia — earthier than sandalwood, warmer than vetiver, with a balsamic-animal undertone unlike anything in European botanical medicine. In Islamic incense tradition (*bakhoor*), costus root is used as a fixative — its heavy aromatic molecules slow the volatilization of other fragrance components, extending the incense's burn time. In Ayurvedic *dhupa* (medicated smoke), kushtha is used in fumigation for skin diseases and as a room purifier. The same aromatic compound (costic acid and relatives) serves simultaneously as medicine, fragrance, and ritual object across cultures — a trinity of functions that explains its 3,500-year trade persistence.

**The CITES Paradox:**
*Saussurea costus* is now listed on CITES Appendix I — the same appendix as tigers and elephants — due to catastrophic overharvesting driven by religious demand (*qust al-hindi* in Islamic practice), perfume industry demand, and Ayurvedic pharmaceutical demand. The plant that traded for 3,500 years along every major route is now at risk of extinction in its Himalayan alpine meadow habitat. Each unit of traditional continuity has a conservation cost. The CITES listing creates a pharmacological-theological tension: *qust al-hindi* specifically recommended in authenticated hadith, but its harvest now threatens species survival. Islamic jurisprudence (*fiqh*) scholars are increasingly examining whether conservation-driven substitution (*badal*) is permissible.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Root Powder:**
- Traditional: 1–3 g dried root powder; with honey or water; 2–3× daily
- Decoction: 5 g root per 500 mL water; simmer 20 minutes; strain; 1–2 cups daily

**Topical:**
- Paste of root powder + oil: traditional Ayurvedic skin preparation; anti-fungal; anti-inflammatory

**CITES Compliance:**
- Only purchase *S. costus* root from sources with verified CITES permits
- Consider *Saussurea obvallata* (brahma kamal) or cultivated alternatives where available
- Declining to purchase uncertified *qust al-hindi* directly supports species conservation

### Safety Profile

**Generally safe at traditional doses.**

**Asteraceae Sesquiterpene Lactone Allergy:**
- Costunolide + DHC: potent Asteraceae sensitizers in the α-methylene-γ-butyrolactone class
- Contact dermatitis risk with prolonged skin contact (root powder paste)
- Cross-reactivity with arnica, feverfew, parthenium; screen for Asteraceae allergy

**Drug Interactions:**
- Anticoagulants: theoretical interaction (platelet effects); monitor
- Immunosuppressants: NF-κB modulation may interact

**Contraindications:**
- Asteraceae allergy
- Pregnancy: emmenagogue properties; AVOID medicinal doses
- CITES restrictions: verify sourcing legality
