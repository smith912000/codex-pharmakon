# Glass — zujāj (زجاج)

## *Manufactured silicate glass; the identification is the plain meaning of the Arabic word and is secure, but the text does not say which kind of glass* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** CM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Avicenna's *Canon* among the texts on disk.** Searches of the Arabic Canon for the word found other uses of the word *zujāj* as a simile for clear things (clear urine, "glassy" phlegm) and one entry on a plant used to polish glass (*ḥashīshat al-zujāj*, line 13223), but only one entry in which glass is itself the drug: Book II, entry *zujāj*, Arabic text lines 12572 to 12577. The entry has no "what it is" section. It does not say how glass is made, which kind is meant, or where it comes from. Everything below is what those six lines say.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** Temperament: "hot in the first [degree], dry in the second" (حار في الأولى يابس في الثانية). Actions and properties: "in it is astringency (*qabḍ*) and subtlety (*laṭāfa*)". For the head: "it purifies medicines when one washes with it, and it polishes (*yajlū*) the teeth" (ينقي الأدوية إذا غسل به ويجلو الأسنان). For the eye: "it polishes the eye and removes its whiteness, and the burnt is stronger" (يجلو العين ويذهب بياضها والمحرق أقوى). For the excretory organs: "the powdered and the burnt of it are very beneficial for stone of the bladder and kidney when given to drink with wine" (المسحوق والمحرق منه نافع جدا لحصاة المثانة والكلية إذا سقي بشراب). The word *sharāb* can mean wine or any drink; the edition does not decide. No dose is given. The entry quotes no other author.

### Active Components

Glass is a non-crystalline solid made by melting silica (sand) with fluxes and stabilisers; ordinary glass is a soda-lime silicate. The text says nothing about composition. The word "burnt" (*muḥraq*) is not explained. [USER: verify what calcining glass would mean in the source tradition.]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The text records powdered or burnt glass as a drink for urinary stone and as an eye polish. Swallowing glass powder is regarded as hazardous, and putting it in the eye can injure it. The Codex does not recommend any use of this material as a medicine.

### Preparation & Dosing

The entry names three forms: glass used to wash medicines; powdered glass; burnt glass. The powder and the burnt form are "drunk with sharāb" for stone (line 12576 to 12577), and the burnt form is said to be stronger in the eye (line 12575). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Scientific Correlation

Glass is chemically inert in the body and is not dissolved by the gut; there is no established mechanism by which swallowed glass would break up a urinary stone. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify.]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Avicenna: hot in the first degree, dry in the second, with astringency and subtlety (lines 12573 to 12574). No other tradition is tagged.

### Cultivation Register

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** The entry is a short list of external and eliminative uses and says nothing of the vital faculties. The Canon's frame of innate heat and radical moisture is not applied to glass in the lines read. **(c) Absent: no cultivation framing is attested for glass.**

**Practitioner's note.** There is no cultivation use here, and the one thing a practitioner can take from the entry is a caution: the medieval texts did list fine mineral and manufactured powders as drinks, and the Codex records them as history.

### Contraindications

- Powdered glass swallowed or applied to the eye is a physical hazard (cuts, abrasion); this is a plain warning, not a quotation of the text.
- Urinary stone with pain or obstruction needs medical care.
