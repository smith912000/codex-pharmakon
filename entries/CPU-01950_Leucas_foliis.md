# Leucas foliis — Leukas (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 3-113); the Latin is the translator's label, not a species

## *A herb Dioscorides calls leukas, "of the hill [wild]" and cultivated, whose seed is drunk and smeared on against the venom of poisonous creatures; the 2000 English edition's SUGGESTED line prints "Leucas foliis rotundus, Phlomis biflora [Roxburgh]; Leucas indica — Leucas", so the genus is Leucas (mint family) on the translator's say-so, and the species is not settled: confidence low* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The text is the 2000 English edition of *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). The row's name, "Leucas foliis", is not a binomial. It is the start of the translator's pre-Linnaean phrase-name *Leucas foliis rotundus* ("Leucas with round leaves"), printed in the SUGGESTED line at line 7544 and again in the book's index (line 16295: "Leucas 496 Leucas foliis rotundus 496 Leucas indica 496"). The row was opened on 13 October as an unverified placeholder from that string.

**Dioscorides (DMM).** *Locus: Book 3, chapter 113, heading LEUKAS (line 7543).* The chapter is three sentences (lines 7545–7546): "Leucas of the hill [wild] is broader-leaved than the cultivated. The seed is sharper, more bitter, and worse-tasting in the mouth, yet it is more effective than the cultivated. Both of them (smeared on and taken as a drink) are good with wine against the venom of poisonous creatures, especially those of the sea." The brackets are the translator's. So the text knows two kinds, a wild kind of the hills and a cultivated kind, and gives their difference in leaf, seed and strength, and a single use, against venom, with wine. Nothing more is said: no root, flower, habitat, harvest, or name in other languages.

**Identity.** The SUGGESTED line offers *Phlomis biflora* [Roxburgh] and *Leucas indica*, and the phrase-name. *Phlomis biflora* Roxb. is, as far as the Codex knows, an Indian plant [USER: verify]; Dioscorides is describing a Mediterranean herb known to Greek readers, so the Indian species are a post-Linnaean guess by the translator. The chapter has too few characters to decide. Its only decisive character, the "seed" that is "sharper, more bitter", cannot be tied to a genus. The Codex therefore records the identification as *Leucas* sp., possibly in the mint family (Lamiaceae), with low confidence. [USER: verify] The name *leukas* means "white" in Greek, but the text does not say what is white. Whether it is related to *leukas* in other chapters was not checked.

**Position in the book.** The chapter stands in Book 3, among the roots and herbs, in a run of short chapters; the neighbouring chapter before it is a germander (*chamaedrys*), which the text describes at length (lines 7541–7542). The page break after it (lines 7547–7552) carries the plate captions and is not part of the chapter.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.** The sourced content is one use: both kinds, smeared on and drunk with wine, are "good ... against the venom of poisonous creatures, especially those of the sea". The Codex gives no protocol. Venomous stings and bites are medical emergencies, and nothing here should replace emergency care.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives the route only: "smeared on and taken as a drink", "with wine". No quantity, part dose or duration is printed. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. No herbal drink is a recognised treatment for envenomation; bites and stings from venomous animals need prompt medical care, and in some cases specific antivenom. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming or cooling quality and no taste for the plant as a whole. It prints that the seed of the wild kind is "sharper, more bitter, and worse-tasting" than the cultivated, and that the wild kind is "more effective". No other quality is given.
- No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured here. The chapter says nothing about long life, strength of constitution or any practice. What it does show is the discipline of attention that the book offers: a comparison of two kinds by leaf and by the taste of the seed, with a plain statement that the worse-tasting wild seed is the stronger.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a three-sentence chapter on an unidentified herb used against venom, and the one thing to learn from it is the habit of noting how wild and cultivated kinds differ before relying on either.

### Contraindications

- **Venomous bites and stings:** emergencies; seek medical care first. Do not rely on this line.
- **Identity:** the plant is not identified, so no plant should be gathered, eaten or applied on the strength of this entry.
- **Wine:** the ordinary cautions for alcohol apply.
- **Pregnancy, children:** no statement in the sources retrieved. Avoid. [USER: verify]
