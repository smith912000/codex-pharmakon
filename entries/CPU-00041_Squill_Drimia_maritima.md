### Origin & Tradition

Squill is one of the oldest documented medicines of the Mediterranean — a great fleshy bulb, sometimes the size of a man's head, growing on the sandy coasts and dry hillsides of the region. It appears in the Egyptian Ebers material and is treated at length by Dioscorides in Book II, who already records the whole sweep of its later use: as a powerful **expectorant** for thick chest congestion, as a **diuretic** for dropsy (oedema), as a digestive and "incising" agent that cuts through viscid humours, and — crucially — with explicit warnings of its acridity and the need to prepare it carefully (roasting, drying, or compounding with honey as **oxymel of squill** and **vinegar of squill**). The Pythagoreans and later authors hung squill bulbs over doorways as a protective charm, and the plant carried a strong apotropaic and ritual reputation alongside its medicine.

The deepest thread in squill's history is its action on **dropsy** — the fluid-overload state that, in many cases, was the oedema of congestive heart failure. For more than two thousand years squill was a frontline remedy for dropsy, and we now understand why: the bulb contains **cardiac glycosides** (notably **scilliroside** and **proscillaridin**) that act on the heart in the same broad manner as the foxglove's digitalis — strengthening the failing heartbeat and, by improving cardiac output, relieving the secondary fluid retention. Squill was thus a genuine cardiotonic-diuretic long before the mechanism was known, and proscillaridin entered modern pharmacy as a purified cardiac glycoside.

Squill's other famous face is as **"red squill" rat poison.** A red-bulbed variety, rich in scilliroside, was a classic rodenticide — lethal to rats (which cannot vomit and so cannot expel it) while comparatively safer to other animals that can vomit out a toxic dose. This dual identity — life-saving cardiac-respiratory medicine and deliberate poison — makes squill a paradigmatic specialist substance: powerful, useful, and dangerous in exactly the same chemistry.

### Active Components

- **Scilliroside** — a bufadienolide cardiac glycoside; the principal toxic principle, especially concentrated in **red squill**; the basis of the rodenticidal action; potent cardiac/emetic toxicity.
- **Proscillaridin A (and related bufadienolides)** — cardiac glycosides of the white squill; inhibit the membrane **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase**, raising intracellular calcium and increasing the force of cardiac contraction (positive inotropy) — the digitalis-type cardiotonic mechanism. Proscillaridin has been used as a purified cardiac drug.
- **Glucoscilliphaeoside and other minor bufadienolides** — additional cardioactive constituents.
- **Mucilage and sapogenins** — contributing to the expectorant/irritant action on the bronchial mucosa.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Recorded as **historical and specialist material.** Squill is not a self-administered herb; its cardiac glycoside content gives it a narrow therapeutic margin.

1. **Cardiotonic for dropsy/heart failure (historical/specialist).** The defining use: strengthening the failing heart and relieving the associated oedema — the action later refined into purified proscillaridin.
2. **Expectorant for chronic bronchitis (specialist).** In small, carefully prepared doses (oxymel of squill) as a stimulating expectorant for thick, hard-to-raise phlegm.
3. **Diuretic (specialist).** For fluid overload, largely via the cardiac action.
4. **Rodenticide (red squill).** The scilliroside-rich red bulb as a classic rat poison — documented, non-medical.
5. **Emetic (historical).** Larger doses are violently emetic — a property both exploited and feared.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM / Galenic humoral degree:** Hot and Dry in a high degree — an acrid, "incising," forceful agent that cuts viscid humours and drives out water; the classical texts emphasise its strength and the need to temper it.
- **Folk-Mediterranean mapping:** the dropsy and chest remedy par excellence; also apotropaic (door-charm).
- **Kenetica Qi/Shen tier:** ◉ **Qi** for the forceful expectorant/diuretic "moving water and phlegm" action, with a ☿ **Shen** ascription for the direct, life-or-death action on the heartbeat — squill reaches into the cardiac/vital register, which is why it is specialist.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian (EP).** Squill sits in the Egyptian material as a chest-and-water remedy, and the Ebers corpus is indication-organised — it records what was given for a complaint, not what the bulb does to a person's *ka*, *ib* or vital constitution. No operative *heka* recitation is attested for squill in the retrieved sources, and its identity within the Egyptian plant-names is itself a modern hypothesis rather than a secured reading. There is therefore no cultivation status to state here: the tradition offers a therapeutic use, not a doctrine of what the bulb builds in the person. Cultivation status: **(c) absent**.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* Book II gives squill a full therapeutic account — expectorant, diuretic, "incising" of viscid humours — and lays great weight on preparation: roasting the acrid bulb, drying it, compounding it as oxymel and vinegar of squill to temper its strength and standardise a small dose. Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system into which a cultivation status could be assigned; what he offers a cultivator is exactly this discipline of attention — the assay of a dangerous plant, the tempering of its acridity, the judgement of dose. The Pythagorean door-charm and apotropaic reputation he and others record is a use noted at arm's length, not a vital-body doctrine assigned to the drug. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status; the real inheritance is the craft of preparation.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** Squill (*isqīl* / *'unṣul*) is treated as a hot and dry, forceful acrid drug — an expectorant, diuretic and deobstruent that cuts thick humours. The strongest cultivation idea in the Unani corpus, the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture, is a longevity frame of the whole regimen and is not a status the tradition assigns to a sharp evacuant of this kind; no *mufarrih* or cardiac-*ruh* role is claimed for it in the retrieved sources. Its standing here is therapeutic, and pointedly a drug to be used sparingly and stopped, not a substance taken to nourish the vital endowment. **(b) framed:** attested therapeutically, no cultivation status sourced.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a substance a cultivator takes to build vitality — and the traditions themselves say as much by hedging it with preparation and warning. Squill is specialist cardiac-glycoside material with a narrow margin: across the Egyptian, Greek and Unani records it is a forceful expectorant and diuretic for defined, serious complaints, carefully tempered before use and never taken long. The cultivator's discipline with squill is Dioscorides' discipline of attention — knowing what the bulb is, respecting the poison in the medicine, and leaving the dose to a qualified hand.

### Contraindications

- **Cardiac glycoside toxicity — absolute caution.** Narrow margin; arrhythmogenic in excess; contraindicated alongside digitalis/other cardiac glycosides and where their toxicity is a risk (e.g. hypokalaemia, which potentiates glycoside toxicity).
- **Existing arrhythmia / heart block.** Contraindicated.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding — avoid.**
- **Renal impairment.** Glycoside accumulation risk; avoid.
- **Mucosal/skin irritation.** The fresh bulb is acrid and irritant; handle with care.
- **No unsupervised use.** Specialist framing throughout.
