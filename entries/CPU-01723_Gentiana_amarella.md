# Gentiana amarella — sum.chu.tig, Sumchutik (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Gentiana amarella, the second of two binomials Clark 1995 prints for sum.chu.tig (after Saxifraga pasumensis Marq. et Shaw. f. integrifolia Jesir)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under this binomial only by Clark.** No other text read for this entry names this species. The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk (including the three Bhishagratna volumes) for the genus name *Gentiana* found Dioscorides 3-3 GENTIANE (line 6541, the translator's glosses *Gentiana lutea* and Yellow Gentian) and Yang 1998's Long Dan (Radix Gentianae Scabrae, line 1936) and Qin Jiao (Radix Gentianae Macrophyllae, line 2494). These are other species and are not attributed to this row. Clark's preface (line 83) says that more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance. Here the line prints two, of which the first is a *Saxifraga* (a different family); the spine took the second (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 129). The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1004).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry follows *kyi.lche* (line 1003) and precedes *pri.yang.ku* (line 1005). It reads:

"sum.chu.tig: Saxifraga pasumensis Marq. et Shaw. f. integrifolia Jesir: Gentiana amarella: [grows in rocky meadows and has small, flat green leaves like the beak of the rko.ma bird. It has clusters of small, pale yellow flowers similar to lotuses and reddish-yellow furry stems]. It cures liver fever and bile fever."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the brackets (commentary layer) are the habitat, the leaves, the flowers and the stems. Outside them (root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the two Latin names, which are Clark's identifications, and the sentence "It cures liver fever and bile fever." The commentary does not say which of the two Latin names it describes; the Codex records the description and does not decide between them.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* Line 3428 reads: "Sumchutik cures hot disorders of the gall bladder and liver." The index lists "Sumchutik 206" (line 7499). The action agrees in kind with Clark's "liver fever and bile fever". The Codex does not equate *hot disorder* with *fever*. The glossary lists the phonetic name "Sumchutik" (line 5785) with the transliteration "sum cu tig"; no Latin name could be paired with it by position in this reading, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text does not print *Gentiana amarella*.

**Overlap.** I_1229 (*Anemone tatrasepala*) quotes the glossary block that ends with "Sumchutik". I_316 (*Gentiana straminea*) and I_315 (*Gentiana crassicaulis*) are entries on other gentians, and the former covers the neighbouring Clark entry *kyi.lche*. The spine has no row for the *Saxifraga* named first. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Gentiana* is a genus of the gentian family (Gentianaceae) and *Saxifraga* a genus of another family (Saxifragaceae), so the two names Clark prints point to unrelated plants. Bitter secoiridoid glycosides are characteristic of the gentians in general, but Clark's commentary for this entry records no taste. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained or which part was used.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "cures liver fever and bile fever" (Clark, line 1004) and "cures hot disorders of the gall bladder and liver" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3428). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only by Clark's second of two names, and they are not Codex protocols. Liver disease is a medical matter and the Codex offers no advice for it.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] Clark's commentary describes habitat, leaves, flowers and stems and does not say which part is taken.

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. The identity is open between a gentian and a saxifrage, so a study of one would not bear directly on the other.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it cures liver fever and bile fever, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same in kind. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the sentences cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and the link from this binomial to that framing rests on Clark's second choice between two.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a drug for liver and bile fever in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and its Latin name is one of two that Clark offers, the first from another family. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. The identity is open between plants of different families, liver disease needs medical care, and no preparation or dose is given, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
