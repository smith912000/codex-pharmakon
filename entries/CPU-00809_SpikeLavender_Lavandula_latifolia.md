### Origin & Tradition

Spike lavender — *aspic*, *lavande aspic*, "oil of spike" — is the broad-leaved, sun-loving lavender of the low Mediterranean hills, and it is the loud, camphoraceous sibling of the gentle true lavender (*Lavandula angustifolia*). Where true lavender smells sweet, floral, and sedative, spike smells sharp, medicinal, and *cool-burning* — a eucalyptus-and-camphor top note over the lavender base. That single olfactory difference is the whole entry: the two plants look alike and share a genus, but their essential oils are chemically opposite in the axis that matters, and every tradition that used them well kept them apart. Spike was the *worker's* lavender — the painter's varnish solvent ("oil of spike" thinned varnishes and lacquers and cleaned brushes for centuries), and simultaneously the stimulating, decongesting, antiseptic rub for cold chests, stiff joints, and dull heads. True lavender soothed; spike *moved*.

**AT — Assyrian (aromatic *Lamiaceae* / lavender-class incense-herb, [native name USER: verify]):** Assyrian medicine made heavy use of aromatic mint-family herbs as fumigants, chest and head applications, and warming embrocations. A camphoraceous lavender-type aromatic fits that pharmacological slot, but no cuneiform line can be securely pinned to *L. latifolia* specifically. `[USER: verify — genus/family-level at best; the western-Mediterranean spike lavender may not have reached Mesopotamia as a distinct named drug.]`

**DMM — De Materia Medica (nard/*stoichas*-adjacent aromatics):** Dioscorides describes several warming aromatic Lamiaceae — notably *stoichas* (referred to *Lavandula stoechas*, French lavender) — as heating, chest-opening, and bile-moving, and he treats the "nard/aspic" aromatics as warming nervine-tonics. Classical authors did not cleanly separate *L. latifolia* from these; the specific "oil of spike / aspic" identity crystallised in the medieval and Renaissance Mediterranean. So the DMM attribution here is **class-level**: spike lavender belongs to Dioscorides' warming, decongesting, aromatic-nervine drug-family, but is not unambiguously named in the text. `[USER: verify the exact Greek referent.]`

**CM — Canon of Medicine (أُسْطُوخُودُوس — *Usṭūkhūdūs*, the lavender/stoechas group; زَيْت الأَسْبِيك — spike/aspic oil):** *Usṭūkhūdūs* is a major nervine in Unani medicine — classed hot and dry, a "broom of the brain" (*jārūb al-dimāgh*) that sweeps cold phlegmatic humours from the head and nerves. Indications for the lavender group: warming the brain and nerves; relieving cold headache, torpor, and phlegmatic dullness; decongesting the chest and expelling cold phlegm (expectorant); warming embrocation for cold joint and nerve pain; and antiseptic dressing. The camphoraceous spike/aspic oil sits at the strongly-heating, stimulating end of this *Usṭūkhūdūs* spectrum — the opposite pole from the calming preparations later associated with sweet lavender.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.**
The traditions read spike lavender as *warming, quickening, and dispersing* — a stimulant to a cold, sluggish head and chest, and a heating rub for cold pain. This is where distinguishing the two lavenders becomes a chemistry lesson, because the doctrine tracks a real molecular fork:

- **True lavender (*L. angustifolia*)** is dominated by **linalool** and **linalyl acetate** — GABAergic-leaning, anxiolytic, sedative monoterpenes. Its doctrine is *cooling, calming, downward*.
- **Spike lavender (*L. latifolia*)** is dominated instead by **1,8-cineole (eucalyptol)** and **camphor**, with comparatively little linalyl acetate. Cineole is a mucolytic, bronchodilating, mucociliary-stimulating expectorant; camphor is a warming TRP-channel counter-irritant, rubefacient, and CNS stimulant. Its doctrine is *warming, stimulating, upward-and-outward*.

So when Unani calls spike a heating "broom of the brain" and a decongestant while treating sweet lavender as calming, it is, in folk-sensory language, describing the **cineole/camphor vs. linalool** split. The warming-stimulant "signature" the physicians tasted and smelled is literally the higher oxide/ketone fraction. The doctrine is a well-aimed piece of chemistry — with one critical correction the ancients could not make: the very camphor that carries the "stimulating warmth" is, at the wrong dose, a convulsant neurotoxin (see Contraindications).

### Active Components

**Essential oil (the therapeutic fraction) — the defining profile vs. true lavender:**
- **1,8-Cineole (eucalyptol)** — a major constituent of *L. latifolia* oil (reported on the order of ~25–35% in Spanish/Mediterranean material); mucolytic and mucociliary-stimulating expectorant, bronchodilator, anti-inflammatory (NF-κB/TLR4-axis suppression), antimicrobial. **This is the compound largely absent from sedative *L. angustifolia*.**
- **Camphor** — present at a substantial level in *L. latifolia* (reported ~10–18% in some Mediterranean oils); warming counter-irritant/rubefacient, mild local analgesic (TRPV1/TRPM8 activity), CNS stimulant — **and the toxic principle.**
- **Linalool** — still a major component (spike oil is often ~35–50% linalool), giving the lavender base note, antimicrobial and mildly anxiolytic — but here overlaid by cineole+camphor rather than by linalyl acetate.
- **Comparatively low linalyl acetate** — the sweet, sedative ester that dominates *L. angustifolia* is minor in spike, which is the single most important compositional difference.
- Minor **α-pinene, limonene, α-terpineol, β-caryophyllene, borneol** — antimicrobial/anti-inflammatory supporting terpenes.

**Non-volatile background:** rosmarinic and other phenolic acids, flavonoids — antioxidant/anti-inflammatory constituents shared across Lamiaceae.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**1. Stimulating expectorant / chest decongestant ([RESET])**
A steam inhalation or a diluted chest rub of spike lavender oil for a cold, congested, phlegmy chest or blocked sinuses — the cineole-driven mucolytic, mucociliary-accelerating, bronchodilating action. This is the sharp, "clearing" application that distinguishes spike from sedative true lavender, which is not an expectorant.

**2. Warming antirheumatic / recovery rub ([RESET])**
A well-diluted topical rub over cold, stiff, aching joints and muscles after training or in cold-damp stiffness — the camphor+cineole counter-irritant warming-analgesic combination. Produces the reddening, warming, "the pain lifts to the surface" sensation the traditions describe.

**3. Aromatic stimulant for cold, dull-headed torpor ([PRIME])**
Brief inhalation of the sharp camphoraceous aroma as a quickening, focusing counterpoint — the Unani "broom of the brain" role — when the state is heavy, foggy, and cold. **Use spike here specifically because you do *not* want the sedation of true lavender.**

**4. Topical antiseptic dressing**
Diluted spike oil as an antimicrobial wash/dressing for minor skin infection, drawing on the demonstrated antibacterial activity of *L. latifolia* oil (below).

### Preparation & Dosing

| Preparation | Method | Typical amount | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steam inhalation (expectorant) | Add oil to hot (not boiling) water, tent and inhale 5–10 min | 2–4 drops in a bowl | Eyes closed; stop if it irritates the airway |
| Chest/joint rub (topical) | Dilute in carrier oil (max ~2–3% for adults) | ~2–3% = ~12–18 drops per 30 mL carrier | **Adults only**; patch-test; keep off mucous membranes |
| Aromatic inhalation (stimulant) | Sniff from bottle or tissue | 1–2 brief inhalations | Do not over-expose — camphor headache/nausea if overdone |
| Antiseptic wash | Diluted oil in carrier or in a hydrosol dressing | ~1–2% dilution | External, intact-skin-adjacent minor wounds only |

**Dosing honesty:** camphor content makes an absolute upper limit essential. Keep total spike-lavender oil exposure conservative; the numbers above are traditional-aromatherapy ranges, not clinically validated therapeutic doses — `[USER: verify]`, and **never** approach these amounts in children or pregnancy.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:

- **Composition — the defining cineole/camphor vs. linalool/linalyl-acetate split (validates the entire "warming stimulant vs. sedative" doctrine):** Carrasco A et al., *Planta Medica* 2015 — GC/MS of Spanish *L. latifolia* vs. *L. angustifolia* found spike oil dominated by linalool (~35–51%), **eucalyptol (~26–32%)** and **camphor (~10–18%)**, whereas true lavender was dominated by linalool + **linalyl acetate (~21–36%)** with the camphor/cineole fraction minor; lipoxygenase-inhibitory (anti-inflammatory) and antioxidant activity were also shown. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0035-1558095). **Grade: strong, direct-to-species** for the compositional claim that is the crux of this entry.
- **Antimicrobial activity of *L. latifolia* oil (validates antiseptic use):** Saifi M et al., *Chem Biodivers* 2025 — Algerian *L. latifolia* oil (1,8-cineole/limonene/camphor–rich) was the most effective of the tested lavender oils against multiple *Bacillus cereus* strains. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/cbdv.202501821). Roller S et al., *J Altern Complement Med* 2009 — *L. latifolia* among lavender oils inhibiting MSSA and MRSA by direct contact. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1089/acm.2008.0268). **Grade: moderate — real antibacterial activity in vitro; efficacy as a clinical antiseptic not established.**
- **1,8-Cineole as expectorant / respiratory anti-inflammatory (validates the decongestant use — via the marker compound):** Begrow F et al., *Adv Ther* 2012 — 1,8-cineole (as a marker of a standardised cineole/limonene/pinene phytomedicine) accelerated mucociliary clearance in vivo. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12325-012-0014-z). Qiu XY et al., *Int Immunopharmacol* 2024 — eucalyptol/limonene/pinene capsules reduced airway inflammation, mucin (MUC5AC/5B) and TLR4 signalling in an LPS bronchitis model. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intimp.2024.111571). **Grade: moderate — strong for the isolated marker compound; indirect for whole spike-lavender oil**, which merely happens to be cineole-rich.
- **⚠️ Camphor neurotoxicity (deflates the "stimulant is harmless" reading; grounds the safety flag):** Salcedo YE et al., *BMC Pediatr* 2025 — a 1-year-old developed generalised tonic-clonic seizures after camphor ingestion, requiring ICU care. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-025-05488-w). Ferreira LO et al., *Sci Rep* 2020 — camphor reliably induced tonic-clonic seizures with epileptiform electrocorticography in rats. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-75309-w). **Grade: strong for the hazard.**

**Honest overall grade — MODERATE, with a hard safety qualifier.** The composition that underpins the traditional distinction is solidly established (Carrasco 2015). The antimicrobial and expectorant rationales are supported at the compound/in-vitro level but not by clinical trials of spike-lavender oil itself. The "stimulating warmth" doctrine is chemically real — and the same chemistry (camphor) is the reason the plant is dangerous if misused. Do not upgrade "traditionally stimulating" to "safe tonic."

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Primary Tier:** ◉ Qi — warming, upward-and-outward aromatic disperser.
**TCM Analogy:** Functionally near *Bo He* (薄荷, mint) crossed with a warm aromatic opener — an acrid, aromatic head-and-chest clearer; the camphor/cineole warmth also echoes *Zhang Nao* (樟脑, camphor) itself as a topical dispersing counter-irritant.
**Organ-Channel Map:** Lung (disperses cold phlegm, expectorant, opens the chest), Head/Brain-orifices (the Unani "broom of the brain," clears cold-phlegm torpor), channels/joints (warming dispersal of cold-damp).
**Energetic Action:** Warms, quickens, and disperses; opens the pores and the chest; clears cold phlegm; stimulates rather than sedates — **the deliberate opposite of *L. angustifolia*'s calming, downward action.**
**Greco-Arabic:** Hot and dry — a strongly heating aromatic nervine/expectorant at the stimulant pole of the *Usṭūkhūdūs* group.

### Contraindications

- **⚠️⚠️ Camphor toxicity — the governing safety fact:** spike lavender oil is camphor-rich. Camphor is a documented convulsant; ingestion has caused tonic-clonic seizures (case and animal evidence above). **Never take internally. Never use on or near infants and young children** (facial/chest application can cause laryngospasm and seizures). **Contraindicated in pregnancy and breastfeeding.** Contraindicated in anyone with epilepsy or a seizure history.
- **Do not confuse with true lavender:** *L. latifolia* is **not** interchangeable with sedative *L. angustifolia*. Using spike where calming/anxiolytic lavender is intended gives the opposite (stimulating) effect and adds camphor risk.
- **Topical only, always diluted:** never apply neat; keep the total oil low (camphor limit) and off mucous membranes, eyes, and broken skin. Patch-test first.
- **Airway reactivity:** cineole/camphor vapour can trigger bronchospasm in asthmatics and reactive airways — introduce cautiously or avoid.
- **Keep the bottle secured:** as with all camphor-bearing oils, accidental pediatric ingestion is a medical emergency.
