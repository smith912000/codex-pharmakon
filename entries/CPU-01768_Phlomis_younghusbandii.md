# Phlomis younghusbandii — Lugmur, lug mur (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Phlomis younghusbandii, the first of four binomials the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints on one line (5448), and the sixth of the nine scientific names of its "L" block, which the Codex reads by position against the phonetic name "Lugmur" and the transliteration "lug mur"; Clark 1995 prints two other Phlomis species (kawaguchii, tuberosa) for lug.mur and not this one* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and under this binomial on the Men-Tsee-Khang edition alone, set against the Tibetan name by column position and not on one printed line.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Ebers, Assyrian, Sushruta and Shennong text on disk for *Phlomis* found only Dioscorides lines for other drugs: chapter 4-104 (line 8953), whose translators' names are *Phlomis fructiosa* (as printed) and *Phlomis floccosa* (line 8961), and chapter 3-113, *Leukas* (line 7543), whose SUGGESTED line (7544) lists "Phlomis biflora". Those are the translators' identifications for Greek drugs and are not read as this one. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 312) the key *Phlomis younghusbandii* is taken from the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary line 5448 with the note "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". Line 5448 prints **four** binomials on one line ("Phlomis younghusbandii Corydalis meifolia Pedicularis siphonantha Pedicularis longiflora"). The key is the **first** of the four on the line, and the sixth of the block's nine scientific names. Each of the other three has its own row. Clark does not print *Phlomis younghusbandii*; the spine row for Clark's own *lug.mur* binomial is *Phlomis tuberosa* (CPU-01767, I_1331).

**Identity doubt.** The glossary's binomial is set against the name by position only. Clark's line for *lug.mur* prints two other species of the same genus. The two editions therefore agree on the genus and differ on the species, and the Codex does not choose. The glossary's English column carries seven names for the block's nine binomials (lines 5450 to 5454: "Celery", "Henbane", "Clove", "Lesser starwort", "Mountain turkeycom", "Lousewort", "Yellow lousewort"), and none of the seven is a name for a *Phlomis*, so the glossary gives this slot no English name. The Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note (line 686) says that the botanical terms in their text "are based on the information procured from the Materia Medica Department of this institute"; it names no flora or earlier work. **Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "L" block, lines 5433 to 5454).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. The phonetic names are nine (lines 5435 to 5437): "Lalaphue", "Langthangtse", "Lishi", "Looh dhue dorje", "Lugchhung", "Lugmur", "Lugnyal", "Lugru mukpo" and "Lugru serpo". The transliterations (lines 5439 to 5441) include "lug mur" (line 5441). The scientific names are nine (lines 5445 to 5448): "Cnidium monnieri", "Hyoscyamus niger L.", "Syzygium aromaticum", "Codonopsis thalictrifolia", "Aster strachei", "Phlomis younghusbandii", "Corydalis meifolia", "Pedicularis siphonantha" and "Pedicularis longiflora". Counted in order, "Lugmur" is the sixth phonetic name and *Phlomis younghusbandii* the sixth scientific name. The count is checked on three neighbours by Clark: "Lalaphue" is first against *Cnidium monnieri* (Clark's *la.la.phud*, line 896, which prints *Cnidium monnieri* as its second binomial), "Lugchhung" is fifth against *Aster strachei* (Clark's *lug.chhung*, line 1105) and "Lugnyal" is seventh against *Corydalis meifolia* (Clark's *lug.ngal*, line 1106). The glossary does not print the pairing in one row. It is positional and agrees with Clark in genus, because Clark's *lug.mur* (line 1062) names two *Phlomis*. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3446).* Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428): "Dhambu kara cures hot disorder of the lungs, liver, and channels. Drita sazin drain pus, blood and chhuser. Jhapo tsitsi stops menstrual bleeding. Shumosa dries up lung abscesses and stops diarrhea. Nyiwa and Lugmur cure heart bum and the common cold." "Bum" is the printed OCR spelling and is quoted as it stands; "chhuser" is a Tibetan term kept untranslated (the editors' note, line 686). The index lists "Lugmur 209" (line 7428). The sentence gives no part used and no preparation. The name is not in the taste groups of chapter 19 and is not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23).

*Clark 1995, the same Tibetan name (read through the name, not the binomial).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997), lines 1062 to 1065: "lug.mur: (Leucas) Phlomis kawaguchii Murata: Phlomis tuberosa: [is also known as pi.pi.lug.mur. It has furry, yellowish-green leaves, square petioles and brown flowers. The fruit has sweet taste and cures lung disorders. Both Codonopsis and Leucas] cure chest fevers and common cold." The page break "106" is on line 1063. Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, so the description, the taste and the lung action are the commentary layer, and "cure chest fevers and common cold" is outside the brackets. It is a clause shared with the preceding drug (*snyi.wa*). Clark's index of Tibetan names lists *lug.mur* (line 1758). The Codex reads the glossary's "Lugmur" and Clark's *lug.mur* as one drug from the spelling and from the agreement in kind of the two actions ("the common cold" in both); neither text says so.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Phlomis younghusbandii*, *lug.mur* and *Lugmur* found no entry on this plant. I_1331 (*Phlomis tuberosa*, CPU-01767) is the sibling entry from the Clark line. I_1054 (*Codonopsis convolvulacea*) quotes the same Clark lines for the preceding drug. I_1327 and I_1328 (the two *Pedicularis* rows) print the same glossary line 5448, and I_1258 (*Corydalis meifolia*), I_1256 (*Codonopsis thalictrifolia*), I_1255 (*Cnidium monnieri*) and I_1022 (*Aster strachei*) print the neighbouring binomials of the same "L" block. The three are not merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Phlomis* is a genus of the mint family (Lamiaceae). Which compounds this species or the Tibetan drug holds is not shown by the lines read, so no statement about its composition is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Men-Tsee-Khang records that "Nyiwa and Lugmur cure heart bum and the common cold" (line 3446), and Clark records, for the same Tibetan name, "chest fevers and common cold" (line 1065). These are the texts' claims in their own terms, attached to this binomial only through a column position, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. A fever or a chest illness needs medical care, and no herb should be used for either on the strength of this entry.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Men-Tsee-Khang running text gives the drug one clause, shared with another drug, and Clark's commentary layer gives it a description and a sweet taste. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, lines 3900 to 3950; Clark's chapter 23 is headed "Normal health", lines 1262 to 1276), and Clark's materia medica names other drugs as essence-extraction ingredients (for example lines 853, 971 and 1144), none of them this one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name and not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a common-cold drug in one shared sentence, set against a Tibetan name by column position, with no preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one.
