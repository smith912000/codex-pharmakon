# Eel — "Eel" (Bryan 1930, Ebers Papyrus; no Egyptian word printed in the list)
## *The eel, as Bryan's English identification names it; the list gives no Egyptian word and no binomial* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The only text on disk for this tradition is C. P. Bryan's 1930 English *The Papyrus Ebers* (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`). In his chapter VII, "Organic Remedies", Bryan says that a full list of the animal materia medica "would not be possible", because "the identity of a considerable number of the animals mentioned is still beyond us", and then lists those "whose identity is not in doubt, and who were called upon to sacrifice themselves in the cause of the Healing Art" (lines 1385-1392). The list is alphabetical by group and runs through the frog, tadpole and scorpion to "Eel Electric Eel Crab Swordfish Ostrich" (line 1404). The eel is therefore attested in this tradition by one thing only: a name in Bryan's list. The extract row (`Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv`, row 121, "Bryan 1930, Ch. VII Organic Remedies (animals) (line 1404)") records the same and adds that Bryan's list gives "no Egyptian word or binomial". The Egyptian word, the fish it names, and its identity as an eel are not stated in anything on disk, and identification is a modern hypothesis in this corpus because the papyrus is organised by complaint, not by substance.

I searched the whole Bryan text for the word "eel" (as a word, any case). It occurs in the list at line 1404 and in one recipe, the electric eel's head, at line 2231. I found no recipe in Bryan's text that names the plain eel. Bryan's text is a summary with selected quotations, not a full translation, so this proves only that his book does not quote one. Whether the papyrus itself has one cannot be said from the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

The one recipe that touches the group is printed under Bryan's discussion of burns (lines 2226-2231):

"TO PREVENT BURN WOUNDS / A Frog / Warm in Oil and rub therewith. / ANOTHER The Head-of-the-Electric-Eel Warm in Oil and apply to the part of the body."

Bryan lists "Eel" and "Electric Eel" as two separate heads in the animal list, and the extract file has them as two rows (121 and 122, both citing line 1404). The recipe names the electric eel, not the eel; it is not carried over to the plain eel here. A second source list on disk (`_sources/ebers.csv`, line 90, "Electric eel head", with the identification *Malapterurus electricus*, Nile electric catfish, marked identification-uncertain) shows the identification of the electric eel is itself disputed, and again it concerns that animal and not the eel. The Codex entry for the electric ray (III_88) is a Dioscorides entry and is unrelated.

The Ebers material here has no Egyptian text, no spell and no deity attributed to the eel.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, an eel is a fish: its flesh is mainly water and protein with a high fat content, its body is covered in a mucus layer, and the blood serum of freshwater eels is toxic when eaten raw and is made harmless by cooking. Nothing about the Egyptian animal's identity or preparation can be added without a source.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources read give the eel no use at all, only a place in a list of animals the papyrus drew on. The nearest recipe, a burn remedy using the head of the electric eel, is a different animal and the Codex does not recommend it; a burn needs cooling with clean water and medical assessment, and an animal head warmed in oil is a source of infection. The honest use of this entry is as a record of an attested name with no recorded practice behind it.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation of the plain eel is given in the sources retrieved. The one nearby recipe, quoted above, is "Warm in Oil and apply to the part of the body", and it concerns the electric eel's head. No dose, quantity or length of course is given for any of it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Burns are treated today by cooling, clean dressings and, for deep or extensive burns, urgent medical care; an applied fish or animal part has no standing as a treatment and risks infection. Raw eel blood contains a toxin that is destroyed by heat. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Egyptian (EP):** None. Bryan's list and the one recipe quoted give no quality (hot, cold, moist, dry) for the eel or the electric eel. The Ebers corpus is organised by complaint and records what was done for a condition, not a classification of the substance.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. For the eel it records less than that, a name in a list. The Egyptian practice of treating spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*) cannot be applied, because no recipe naming the plain eel was found, so there is no recitation to keep or lose. No deity is attributed to the eel.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The eel is a name in an Egyptian animal list and the sources read give it no use. The Codex does not recommend handling or taking animal material on this basis. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Raw eel blood or flesh:** the blood of freshwater eels is toxic raw; do not eat any eel uncooked.
- **Burns:** the only nearby recipe is a burn remedy (electric eel head in oil); do not put animal material on a burn, which needs cooling and medical care.
- **Infection and hygiene:** fresh animal parts carry bacteria and parasites; do not apply them to broken skin.
- **Conservation:** some eel species are endangered; the Codex does not recommend taking any animal for these uses.
