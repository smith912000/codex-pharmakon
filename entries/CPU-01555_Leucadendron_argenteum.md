# Leucadendron argenteum — Leuke (Dioscorides 1-109)

## *Leucadendron argenteum, the first of two suggestions on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 1-109 ("Silver Tree"; the second line prints Melaleuca leucadendron and Melaleuca cajputi, "Punk Tree, Cajeput Oil Tree", and Myrtus leucadendron is printed with the first)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (lines 4353 and 4354) reads "Leucadendron argenteum, Myrtus leucadendron — Silver Tree" and then, on a second line, "Melaleuca leucadendron, Melaleuca cajputi — Punk Tree, Cajeput Oil Tree". The translators therefore offer plants of different genera for one chapter and do not choose between them, and the chapter gives no description of the tree that could decide. The row takes the first name and was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP). It has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] As general botanical knowledge, not a statement of any text read here, the silver tree is a plant of southern Africa. Whether the translators mean it as Dioscorides' own tree is a point for the person who verifies the row. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *1-109. LEUKE*, heading at line 4352, SUGGESTED line at 4353, text at line 4355. It stands in Book One, between *1-108. MELIA* (marked "? POISONOUS" by the translators) and *1-110. MAKER*, and just before the running page head "GUMS from TREES" that precedes *1-110*. The translators' index gives *Leucadendron argenteum* and *Melaleuca leucadendron* at page 109 (lines 16292 and 16531).

The whole chapter reads: "One ounce of the bark of the leuke tree (taken as a drink) helps sciatica [pains in hips; sciatic nerves] and slow painful urination. Taken as a drink with a mule's kidney it is reported to take away conception [abortifacient]; and the leaves of it taken in a drink with wine (after the menstrual flow) are said to do the same. The lukewarm juice of the leaves is good dropped in the ears for earache. The filaments which put out at the first sprouting of the leaves, bruised small and rubbed on with honey, cure moisture of the eyes. Some say that the bark of the white and the black leuke, cut into pieces of a small size and scattered (or as it were sown) in beds that are dunged, brings forth edible mushrooms at any time of the year."

So the chapter is entirely about uses and not about the tree. The bark, in a drink, is for sciatica and slow painful urination, with the one stated quantity, "one ounce". The bark in a drink "with a mule's kidney", and the leaves in wine "after the menstrual flow", are reported ("it is reported", "are said") to take away conception. The lukewarm leaf juice is dropped in the ear for earache, and the first-sprouting filaments of the leaves, bruised and rubbed on with honey, are for "moisture of the eyes". The last sentence, introduced by "Some say", is a report of a cultivation trick: small pieces of the bark of the "white and the black leuke", scattered in dunged beds, bring forth edible mushrooms in any season. The chapter gives the tree no other name, no habitat and no description. It does name two kinds, white and black, in that last sentence only.

**Names shared with other chapters.** A different chapter carries a similar name: *4-184. AMPELOS LEUKE* (white bryony), which the existing entry I_998 notes as a neighbouring chapter and which is not this plant. No text of it is read into this entry. The chapter that stands next to this one, 1-108 *MELIA*, is the one the translators flag as "? POISONOUS" ("the thin membranous scales of the wood (taken in a drink) are reported to be deadly"), and that flag is not printed for 1-109.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a tree whose bark, in a drink, is for sciatica and slow painful urination, whose bark and leaves are reported to "take away conception", whose leaf juice is dropped in the ear and whose sprouting filaments are rubbed on for the eyes. It also reports a way of raising mushrooms. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol. The abortifacient use in particular is not a practice the Codex supports, and the identification is the translators' across different genera.

### Preparation & Dosing

1-109 gives these preparations, each with its locus in the one chapter: "one ounce of the bark … taken as a drink" for sciatica and slow painful urination; the bark "taken as a drink with a mule's kidney" and the leaves "taken in a drink with wine (after the menstrual flow)", both reported to take away conception; "the lukewarm juice of the leaves" dropped in the ears; the first filaments "bruised small and rubbed on with honey" for the eyes; and bark "cut into pieces of a small size and scattered" in dunged beds for mushrooms. The only quantity is "one ounce", and the chapter does not say whether that is the dose for one drink or for a course. No frequency or duration is given. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter gives no status to the tree beyond that of a drug. The one place that touches cultivation in the ordinary sense is the reported trick of raising mushrooms from scattered bark in dunged beds, which is a horticultural report introduced by "Some say" and not a cultivation of the person. The chapter offers little even of the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: the parts used are named (bark, leaves, leaf juice, first filaments), and a white and a black kind are mentioned, but there is no description, place or season. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this tree. It is a list of remedies for the hip nerve, urination, ears and eyes, two reports of ending a pregnancy, and one reported mushroom trick, with a single stated quantity. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Its reported abortifacient uses make the plant unsuitable in pregnancy, and no other safety information was read. Sciatica, difficult urination, earache and eye disease need medical assessment, and a home preparation must not replace it. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion across different genera, so no tree should be used on the strength of this entry, and mushrooms must never be raised or eaten on the strength of a reported trick. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
