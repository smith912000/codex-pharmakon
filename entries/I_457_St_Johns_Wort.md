# I-457 | ST. JOHN'S WORT
## *Hypericum perforatum* L.

**Family:** Hypericaceae (formerly Clusiaceae/Guttiferae) | **Part Used:** Aerial flowering tops (harvested at peak bloom)

**Energetic Tier:** ☿ Shen · ◉ Qi

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Etymology:** "St. John's Wort" — *wort* = Old English for herb/plant; traditionally harvested on or around St. John the Baptist's feast day, June 24, when the plant is in full flower; the red oil expressed from the flower buds was associated with the blood of St. John the Baptist in medieval Christian symbolism. *Perforatum* = "perforated" — referring to the translucent oil gland cavities visible in the leaves when held to bright light, appearing as small holes or perforations

**Common names:** Common St. John's wort, perforate St. John's wort, tipton's weed, klamath weed (Pacific Northwest agricultural pest), goat weed, amber, rosin rose

**Botanical diagnostic features (identification critical due to 450+ Hypericum species):**
1. **Translucent oil glands:** small clear "holes" when leaf held to light — oil-filled lysigenous cavities producing volatile terpenoids
2. **Black marginal glands:** along leaf and sepal margins — hypericin-containing secretory nodules; diagnostic for *H. perforatum*
3. **Five yellow petals** with black dots along margins (hypericin glands); **five sepals** with black-tipped margins
4. **Stamens in three fascicles** (united at base into 3 bundles) — key genus-level character
5. **Creeping rhizome:** erect stems arising from persistent root; grows in disturbed soils, meadows, roadsides throughout temperate regions

**Botanical identity:** Perennial herb to 1 m; stems erect, two-ridged longitudinally (diagnostic); leaves opposite, elliptic to linear-oblong, 1–3 cm, sessile; flowers 2–3 cm, bright yellow, numerous in terminal cymes; in full summer bloom the plant appears as a mass of golden yellow (June–August in Northern Hemisphere)

**Primary active constituents:**

| Compound | Concentration | Primary role |
|----------|---------------|-------------|
| Hyperforin | 2–5% dry herb | PRIMARY antidepressant mechanism: TRPC6 reuptake inhibition |
| Adhyperforin | 0.2–1.9% | Analogous TRPC6 activity; more stable than hyperforin |
| Hypericin | 0.03–0.3% | Photosensitizer; earlier (incorrect) attribution as primary antidepressant |
| Pseudohypericin | 0.2–0.9% | Photosensitizer; related naphthodianthrone |
| Amentoflavone | 0.01–0.05% | MAO-A/B inhibition; poor oral bioavailability (high first-pass) |
| Hyperoside | 0.5–2.0% | Anti-inflammatory flavonoid |
| Rutin | 0.3–1.7% | Antioxidant; capillary protection |
| Isoquercitrin | 0.3–1.5% | Anti-inflammatory |
| Xanthones | trace | Anti-anxiety; MAO inhibition |
| Chlorogenic acid | trace | Antioxidant |

**The hyperforin stability problem:** Hyperforin is the primary antidepressant compound, yet it is highly unstable — degrades on exposure to air, heat, and light. A properly formulated commercial extract (Li 160/WS 5570) stabilized hyperforin through encapsulation; poorly manufactured or improperly stored products may be nearly devoid of hyperforin while retaining hypericin (which is stable and easily measured). Products standardized to hypericin alone (old standard) may contain negligible active hyperforin.

**Modern standardization requirement:** Products should specify BOTH 0.3% hypericin AND 3–5% hyperforin to ensure clinical potency.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**The melancholy thread:** Two traditions with unambiguous identification — Dioscorides and Ibn Sīnā — both specify St. John's Wort explicitly for what they called "melancholy" (*melancholia* = Greek, literally "black bile" disease; Arabic equivalent *malankhuliya*). This semantic continuity across ~1,000 years and two medical paradigms (Greek humoral → Arab humoral) for the same indication is the most direct form of convergence possible in historical pharmacology.

**Dioscorides' complete description (De Materia Medica, Book III, Chapter 155):** He describes *hyperikon* with sufficient botanical precision to confirm the identification: "It grows in hedges and roadsides... The leaves bruised and applied with oil are good for burning... taken internally for 40 days it is said to relieve pain of the sciatic nerve, and to expel foreign things from the body." The wound-healing application, nerve-pain application, and internal use align with modern evidence (anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antidepressant).

**Ibn Sīnā's extension:** Identifies *farasyun* (Hypericum) as effective for "the black bile disease and for pain of the sciatic nerve" — directly replicating Dioscorides' clinical observation through independent Ottoman-era clinical practice 1,000 years later. That a physician working in Islamic Persia independently confirmed the same primary application as a Greek physician in Rome — without access to Dioscorides in many cases (Al-Qanun was written from original clinical observation) — is convergent evidence of the highest quality.

**The "nerve oil" application across European folk traditions:** Red oil prepared by macerating the flowers in olive oil — turning deep crimson red from hypericin — was used across Mediterranean, Germanic, Celtic, Slavic, and Nordic folk traditions for wound healing, nerve injuries, burns, and "melancholy." The geographic spread of an identical preparation across independent European folk traditions before any central pharmacopeia coordination demonstrates the robustness of the empirical observation.

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

**Hyperforin (primary antidepressant compound):**
- Acylphloroglucinol derivative; unique structural class
- 2–5% in standardized extract; 0.2–1% in some poorly processed products
- Mechanism: TRPC6 cation channel activation (see Section 5)
- Instability: oxidizes within 2–3 weeks in improperly stored products; accounts for 80%+ degradation without encapsulation
- Dimethylallyl-substituted phloroglucinol skeleton — biosynthetically derived from acetyl-CoA + isoprenoid units; not a simple flavonoid

**Hypericin/Pseudohypericin (photosensitizer class):**
- Naphthodianthrone pigments; red fluorescence
- Primary photosensitizer: absorbs UV-A/B → generates reactive oxygen species → phototoxic skin reactions
- Historical confusion: early 1990s hypothesis that hypericin = MAO inhibitor = antidepressant mechanism; now known incorrect
- Hypericin IS the antiviral compound (in vitro activity vs. HIV, HSV); clinical antiviral significance unclear
- Standard marker for commercial quality (0.3%) but NOT the therapeutic mechanism for depression

**Amentoflavone (biflavonoid):**
- Biflavonoid derived from two apigenin units; potent MAO-A/B inhibitor in vitro
- IC₅₀ MAO-A: ~0.5–2 μM (highly potent in vitro)
- PROBLEM: first-pass metabolism >97%; plasma levels after oral dosing negligible
- At therapeutic oral doses, amentoflavone does NOT meaningfully inhibit MAO-A in vivo
- This resolves the original "MAOI mechanism" hypothesis for SJW — the MAOI compound is present but not bioavailable

**Flavonols and phenolic acids:**
- Hyperoside (quercetin-3-galactoside): NF-κB inhibition; anti-inflammatory
- Rutin: antioxidant; capillary-protecting; anti-inflammatory
- Chlorogenic acid: antioxidant
- These contribute to anti-inflammatory and antioxidant profile but are not the primary antidepressant mechanism

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

**TRPC6 Channel Activation (Primary Antidepressant Mechanism — Müller 2003):**
```
Hyperforin → TRPC6 (Transient Receptor Potential Canonical 6) cation channel activation
→ Na⁺ influx through TRPC6 at nerve terminal membrane
→ Na⁺ accumulation dissipates proton (H⁺) gradient across vesicle membrane
→ Vesicular monoamine transport (VMAT2) requires H⁺ gradient to function
→ VMAT2 impaired → monoamines cannot efficiently re-enter storage vesicles
→ Effective inhibition of neuronal reuptake: serotonin, dopamine, NE, GABA, glutamate
→ ALL FIVE NEUROTRANSMITTERS simultaneously affected

*This mechanism is entirely distinct from SSRIs (which block SERT directly),
SNRIs (SERT + NET blockade), TCAs (SERT + NET + muscarinic blockade),
and MAOIs (MAO-A/B enzyme inhibition)*
*No direct transporter binding — transporter-independent reuptake inhibition
via vesicle energetics disruption*
```

**CYP3A4 + P-gp Induction (CRITICAL drug interaction mechanism):**
```
Hyperforin → Pregnane X Receptor (PXR) nuclear receptor activation
→ PXR translocates to nucleus → binds PXR response elements
→ CYP3A4 gene transcription ↑ 3–8× (dose and duration-dependent)
→ P-glycoprotein (MDR1/ABCB1) gene transcription ↑ 2–4×
→ Combined effect: dramatically accelerated metabolism + enhanced efflux
   of virtually all CYP3A4 substrates + P-gp substrates

Onset: 7–14 days to full induction
Offset: 7–14 days after discontinuation (enzyme/transporter de-induction)
Clinical impact: 50–70% reduction in plasma concentrations of affected drugs
```

**Photosensitization:**
```
Hypericin/pseudohypericin → UV-A/B photon absorption
→ Singlet oxygen generation
→ Reactive oxygen species → tissue damage at UV-exposed skin
→ Phototoxic reactions: erythema, blistering (severe cases)
→ Dose-dependent: minimal risk at standard oral doses in non-photosensitive individuals;
   risk increases with high doses, fair skin, intense UV exposure, topical application
```

**Anti-inflammatory:**
```
Hyperforin → NF-κB pathway modulation
→ IL-6 ↓, TNF-α ↓, COX-2 expression ↓
→ Antioxidant flavonoids (hyperoside, rutin) → radical scavenging
→ Contributes to historical wound-healing and nerve-pain applications
```

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Depression (Primary Indication):**

- **Linde 2008** (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews; Level A — definitive): 29 RCTs; n=5,489; mild–moderate major depression; SJW vs placebo: RR 1.48 (95% CI: 1.23–1.77); SJW vs standard antidepressants: comparable efficacy; adverse event rates: significantly lower for SJW than TCAs and SSRIs; *conclusion: SJW is superior to placebo and comparable to standard antidepressants for mild–moderate depression with a more favorable side-effect profile*

- **Hypericum Depression Trial Study Group 2002** (JAMA; Level A): n=340; HAMD-17; SJW (900–1,500 mg/day, 0.3% hypericin) vs sertraline (50–100 mg/day) vs placebo; 8 weeks; PRIMARY OUTCOME: neither SJW nor sertraline significantly separated from placebo; frequently cited as "SJW doesn't work" — *but:* population had MODERATE-SEVERE depression (mean HAMD-17 = 22.9); both SJW and sertraline response rates (~26% and ~25%) trended above placebo (~19%) but were underpowered to show significance; this trial demonstrated difficulty separating any antidepressant from placebo in this severity range, not SJW inefficacy specifically

- **Philipp 1999** (BMJ; Level A): SJW 1,050 mg/day vs imipramine 100 mg/day; 8 weeks; n=263; HAMD-17 equivalent outcomes; significantly fewer adverse effects with SJW; demonstrates non-inferiority to TCA for moderate depression

- **Woelk 2000** (BMJ; Level A): SJW 600 mg/day vs imipramine 75 mg/day; 6 weeks; HAMD-17 equivalent; SJW better tolerability

**Critical interpretation of evidence:**
- Effective indication: **mild–moderate major depression** (HAMD-17 ≤20)
- Insufficient evidence for: **moderate–severe depression** (HAMD-17 ≥22; HDTS 2002 trial)
- Not indicated: **severe or melancholic depression** (hospitalization-level severity)
- Evidence grade: Level A for target indication (mild–moderate) — one of the best-supported herbal treatments for any psychiatric indication

**Evidence Summary:**

| Indication | Best Evidence | Grade |
|-----------|---------------|-------|
| Mild–moderate major depression | Linde 2008 Cochrane (29 RCTs) | Level A |
| Comparable to TCAs/SSRIs (mild–moderate) | Philipp 1999; Woelk 2000 | Level A |
| Moderate–severe depression | HDTS 2002 (negative) | Insufficient |
| Wound healing (topical) | Traditional + in vitro | Level C |
| Nerve pain (topical) | Traditional + mechanistic | Level C |
| Somatization disorder | Müller 2003 Level B study | Level B |

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

**The red oil and the medieval wound surgeon:** *Oleum Hyperici* — St. John's Wort flower macerated in olive oil until the oil turns blood-red from hypericin — was the primary wound oil of medieval European surgery. Military surgeons who accompanied Crusader armies used it for sword wounds; herbalists recommended it for burns. The oil's red color, arriving just as the herb flowers on the feast day of John the Baptist, was interpreted as providential: a plant that bled its medicinal substance on the saint's feast day, for healing the wounds of holy warfare. This narrative, entirely mythological, preserved accurate pharmacological information (the preparation IS anti-inflammatory and promotes wound healing) through a religious mnemonics system.

**Dioscorides to Commission E: 1,900 years of documentation:** The same plant, the same indication (melancholy/depression equivalent), the same harvest timing (summer solstice ± 2 weeks), documented by Dioscorides (77 CE), Galen (2nd century), Hildegard of Bingen (12th century), Paracelsus (16th century), Culpeper (17th century), the British Pharmacopeia (19th century), Commission E (1984), and finally the Cochrane Collaboration (2008). The path from ancient humoral melancholy to modern HAM-A scores runs through 1,900 years of clinical observation that modern pharmacology ultimately validated through TRPC6 mechanism elucidation. This is the longest evidence chain for a single antidepressant treatment in the history of pharmacology.

**The cyclosporin crisis (1999–2000):** A heart transplant recipient in Switzerland taking St. John's Wort experienced acute organ rejection. Investigation revealed CYP3A4 induction had reduced cyclosporin levels to sub-therapeutic concentrations. The subsequent investigation revealed that this same mechanism affected antiretrovirals (HIV patients experiencing virological failure while taking SJW), oral contraceptives (unintended pregnancies reported to WHO adverse event database), warfarin, digoxin, and irinotecan. The UK Medicines Control Agency issued a Dear Doctor letter in February 2000 — one of the first major regulatorily-actioned herbdrug interactions in history. SJW's drug interaction profile is now the standard example used in all pharmacology courses to illustrate herbal-pharmaceutical interactions, inductive mechanisms, and the PXR nuclear receptor system.

**The "klamath weed" agricultural war:** In California and the Pacific Northwest, *H. perforatum* — introduced from Europe and completely naturalized — became a serious agricultural pest devastating to livestock. Cattle grazing on SJW develop *hypericism*: photosensitization, blistering, and behavioral abnormalities from hypericin + sun exposure. The USDA successfully introduced *Chrysolina quadrigemina* (a European leaf beetle, Chrysomelidae) as a biocontrol agent in 1947 — one of the first successful classical biological control programs in American agriculture — dramatically reducing rangeland infestation. The same plant that healers cultivated for 2,000 years was simultaneously an agricultural enemy requiring biological warfare. The duality of plant as medicine and pest is encoded in both "St. John's Wort" (healing) and "klamath weed" (noxious) naming.

## Section 8: Practical Application

**Primary indications:**
1. Mild–moderate major depression (HAMD-17 ≤20; PHQ-9 ≤15)
2. Subsyndromal/seasonal depression
3. Anxiety with depressive component (somatization)
4. Perimenopausal mood changes
5. Topical: wound healing, burns, nerve pain (neuralgia), bruising

**Dosing:**
- **Depression:** Standardized extract 300 mg TID (900 mg/day total; 0.3% hypericin + 3–5% hyperforin); minimum 4–6 week trial; optimal assessment at 8–12 weeks
- **Alternative dosing:** 900 mg once daily (pharmacokinetically equivalent; improved adherence)
- **Mild anxiety/subsyndromal:** 300–600 mg/day; same standardization requirement
- **Topical wound/nerve oil:** *Oleum Hyperici* — macerate 1 part dried flowers in 3 parts olive oil × 4–6 weeks in sunlight; apply to wounds, burns, nerve pain 2–3×/day

**Standardization requirements (non-negotiable):**
- Must specify BOTH hypericin (≥0.3%) AND hyperforin (≥3%)
- Hyperforin-stabilized formulations (encapsulated or oil-based): required for potency
- Avoid products with only hypericin standardization — may contain negligible hyperforin

**CRITICAL DRUG INTERACTIONS — the most clinically significant of any herbal medicine:**

| Drug Category | Specific Examples | Interaction | Consequence |
|--------------|-------------------|-------------|-------------|
| Calcineurin inhibitors | Cyclosporin, tacrolimus | CYP3A4 induction → 50% level reduction | **Transplant rejection** |
| Antiretrovirals | Indinavir, nevirapine, efavirenz | CYP3A4 + P-gp induction → 50–70% level reduction | **HIV virological failure; resistance** |
| Oral contraceptives | Ethinylestradiol + progestins | CYP3A4 induction → 40–50% level reduction | **Breakthrough pregnancy** |
| Anticoagulants | Warfarin | CYP2C9 + CYP3A4 induction → reduced INR | **Thrombosis risk** |
| Cardiac glycosides | Digoxin | P-gp induction → reduced absorption | **Loss of cardiac control** |
| Cancer chemotherapy | Irinotecan, imatinib | CYP3A4 induction → dramatic level reduction | **Treatment failure** |
| Antifungals | Voriconazole | CYP3A4 induction → level reduction | **Fungal treatment failure** |
| Transplant drugs | Everolimus, sirolimus | CYP3A4 + P-gp | **Rejection risk** |
| SSRIs/SNRIs | All classes | Combined serotonin increase | **Serotonin syndrome** |
| Triptans | Sumatriptan, rizatriptan | Serotonin syndrome risk | **Serotonin syndrome** |

*Practical rule: SJW is CONTRAINDICATED with all drugs with narrow therapeutic index, all immunosuppressants, all antiretrovirals, and all serotonergic pharmaceuticals. This is not a minor precaution — it is an absolute clinical restriction.*

**Contraindications:**
- **All patients on transplant immunosuppressants:** ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION
- **HIV patients on antiretrovirals:** ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION
- **All patients on oral contraceptives (if contraception is critical):** HIGH-RISK CONTRAINDICATION
- **Patients on SSRIs/SNRIs/MAOIs/triptans:** CONTRAINDICATED (serotonin syndrome risk)
- **Pregnancy:** CONTRAINDICATED — limited safety data; alkaloid content; theoretical teratogenicity
- **Bipolar disorder:** CONTRAINDICATED — may precipitate manic episode (as can all antidepressants)
- **Severe depression:** Insufficient evidence; pharmaceutical management required; risk of delaying effective treatment
- **Pre-surgery:** Discontinue ≥5 days preoperatively (serotonin, bleeding, anesthetic interactions)

**Photosensitivity management:**
- At standard doses (900 mg/day), photosensitivity is uncommon in normally pigmented skin
- Fair-skinned individuals: limit UV exposure, use SPF ≥30 during sunny months
- High-dose use (>1,800 mg/day): increased photosensitivity risk; minimize sun exposure
- Topical application: strong photosensitizer — do NOT apply before UV exposure

**Onset and duration:**
- Antidepressant onset: 2–4 weeks (comparable to pharmaceutical antidepressants)
- Full effect: 6–8 weeks minimum trial required before efficacy assessment
- Drug interaction onset: CYP3A4/P-gp induction begins at 3–5 days, reaches maximum at 14 days; persists 7–14 days after discontinuation — patients switching off SJW to pharmaceutical antidepressants must wait 2 weeks for CYP3A4 to de-induce

**Monitoring:**
- No routine laboratory monitoring for depression indication in otherwise healthy patients
- If patient discovers need for transplant medication, antiretroviral, or anticoagulant therapy: discontinue SJW immediately and wait 2 weeks before starting new drug; inform prescribing physician
