# I-275: Calendula officinalis (Calendula / Pot Marigold)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-275 | Asteraceae (Compositae)**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Calendula officinalis* L.
**Family:** Asteraceae

**Common Names:**
- English: Calendula, Pot Marigold, Garden Marigold
- Latin: *Calendula* — from *Calendae* (first day of the month) — because it blooms at the start of each new month nearly year-round in Mediterranean climates
- German: *Ringelblume* (ring flower)
- French: *Souci des jardins*, *Fleur de calendula*
- Arabic: *'Uqḥuwān* (أقحوان) — also used for chamomile (genera often conflated in traditional texts)
- Hindi: *Zergul* / *Genda phool* (गेंदा फूल) — though *genda* usually = Tagetes marigold (distinct genus; avoid confusion)
- Sanskrit: *Sthalapadma* — "land lotus" — referring to the large flower

**Critical Distinction from Other "Marigolds":**
*Calendula officinalis* (pot marigold) ≠ *Tagetes* species (African/French marigold). *Tagetes* is commonly called "marigold" in gardening; *Calendula* is "pot marigold" or "calendula." They are different genera with different chemistry:
- *Calendula*: resin-rich; calendulin; flavonoids; triterpenoids; deep orange; medicinal
- *Tagetes*: lutein source; aromatic; used as pesticide; edible flower; limited medicinal record

**Morphology:**
Annual; 30–60 cm; deeply hairy stems; alternate leaves; bright orange-yellow composite flower heads with ray (ligulate) and disc florets; highly resinous to touch; flowers profusely from spring to frost (validating the *calendae* monthly bloom tradition).

**Parts Used:** Flower heads (both ray and disc florets); primarily the ligulate ray florets in commercial preparations; fresh or dried.

**Color Indicator:**
The deep orange color of high-quality calendula flowers indicates high carotenoid content (lutein, zeaxanthin, β-carotene). Pale yellow cultivars are pharmacologically weaker. Traditional quality assessment: "the darker the orange, the stronger the medicine."

**European Wound Healing Tradition:**
Calendula's primary historical role in European medicine is as the wound herb par excellence. Used in the American Civil War (confederate and union field hospitals) for dressing battlefield wounds. Used throughout WWI in European military medicine. The continuous "war medicine" application (acute wound care) across centuries reflects its consistent antimicrobial + vulnerary (wound-healing) combination.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domains:**
1. **Wound healing / vulnerary** — CM, AT, European folk, Eclectic: the dominant application; skin wounds, burns, ulcers, surgical wounds
2. **Anti-inflammatory (topical)** — All accessible traditions: skin inflammation; dermatitis; rashes; sunburn
3. **Antimicrobial** — Eclectic, European: infected wounds; antiseptic; antimicrobial
4. **Menstrual / hormonal** — European folk: emmenagogue; menstrual regulation; threatened miscarriage (traditional); galactagogue

**Pharmacological Duality:**
Calendula's pharmacology divides clearly between:
- **Topical applications** (primary; wound, skin, mucous membrane): extremely well validated
- **Internal applications** (secondary; lymphatic, digestive, menstrual): less studied

The topical evidence base is exceptional; internal evidence is primarily traditional.

**Energetic Classification:**
- ◉ Qi (functional): Primary — topical healing Qi; wound resolution; lymphatic Qi
- ◈ Jing (structural): Secondary — tissue regeneration; structural wound healing; skin structural repair

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Triterpenoids (Primary Active — Resins)

**1. Oleanolic acid glycosides (Calendulosides)**
- Major triterpenoid saponins; A–I series
- Anti-inflammatory: NF-κB inhibition; PGE₂ reduction
- Tissue regeneration: fibroblast proliferation stimulation

**2. Ursolic acid**
- Pentacyclic triterpenoid; anti-inflammatory; anti-proliferative
- Promotes epithelial cell proliferation → wound closure acceleration

**3. Betulin, betulinic acid** — anti-inflammatory; anti-tumor

### Flavonoids

**4. Isorhamnetin-3-O-glucoside** — primary flavonoid; anti-inflammatory; antioxidant
**5. Narcissin (Isorhamnetin-3-rutinoside)** — anti-inflammatory
**6. Quercetin glycosides** — anti-inflammatory; antioxidant

### Carotenoids

**7. Lutein** — primary carotenoid; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; skin repair; eye-protective
**8. Zeaxanthin** — macular pigment; antioxidant
**9. β-Carotene** — Vitamin A precursor; epithelial regeneration

### Polysaccharides

**10. Fructans** — immunomodulatory; wound healing support
**11. Calendula polysaccharide PS-I** — anti-tumor; macrophage activation; complement activation

### Essential Oil (Minor)

**12. α-Cadinol, β-cadinene** — sesquiterpene alcohols; antimicrobial
**13. Linalool, geraniol** — antimicrobial; anti-inflammatory

### Resins (Major Quality Determinant)

**14. Calendulin** — primary resin; non-specific but contributes to wound-healing; protective film formation

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### 1. Wound Healing (Primary Mechanism — Multi-Component)

**Phase 1 — Hemostasis:** Resinous compounds promote wound adhesion and platelet aggregation initiation

**Phase 2 — Inflammation (control):**
- Triterpenoids (oleanolic acid, ursolic acid): NF-κB inhibition → controlled inflammatory phase
- Flavonoids: COX-2 inhibition; 5-LOX inhibition → reduced PGE₂ and leukotrienes
- Anti-inflammatory without completely suppressing healing-necessary inflammation (unlike corticosteroids)

**Phase 3 — Proliferation (key calendula contribution):**
- Ursolic acid + calendulosides: fibroblast proliferation stimulation → granulation tissue formation
- Triterpenoids: keratinocyte migration acceleration → epithelial closure
- β-Carotene → Vitamin A activity: essential for epithelial differentiation; accelerates re-epithelialization

**Phase 4 — Remodeling:**
- Anti-fibrotic triterpenoids: prevent excessive scarring; scar quality improvement
- Carotenoid antioxidants: protect new tissue from oxidative damage

### 2. Antimicrobial (Wound Antiseptic)
- Calendar essential oil (α-cadinol, β-cadinene): membrane disruption; *S. aureus* MIC 0.3-3 mg/mL
- Triterpenoids: anti-*Candida* activity
- Resinous compounds: physical barrier to bacterial colonization

### 3. Anti-Inflammatory (Topical + Systemic)
- Isorhamnetin + quercetin glycosides: COX-1/COX-2 dual inhibition
- Oleanolic acid: NF-κB inhibition; cytokine reduction
- Combined mechanisms: potent topical anti-inflammatory comparable to 1% hydrocortisone in some models

### 4. Lymphatic / Anti-Edema
- Lymphagogue properties attributed traditionally; mechanism: mild stimulation of lymphatic contractility (flavonoid/triterpenoid VEGF-C modulation proposed)
- Reduces tissue edema around wounds and in lymphatic stasis conditions

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Wound Healing:**
- **Post-surgical wound care (episiotomy):** RCT (Pommier 2004, n=254): calendula cream vs. standard care → significantly faster episiotomy wound healing; reduced pain; trend toward reduced infection
- **Leg ulcers/venous ulcers:** Multiple controlled studies showing improved healing time vs. control
- **Radiation dermatitis:** Major RCT (Pommier 2004, n=254 breast cancer patients): calendula cream significantly superior to trolamine (standard) for preventing acute radiation dermatitis during radiotherapy — primary outcome p < 0.001
- **Evidence Grade: Level A** (wound healing; multiple RCTs; radiation dermatitis landmark trial)

**Oral Mucositis (Chemotherapy):**
- RCT: calendula mouthwash vs. placebo → significant reduction in chemotherapy-induced oral mucositis
- **Evidence Grade: Level B**

**Contact Dermatitis / Eczema:**
- Multiple comparative studies; calendula cream vs. petrolatum jelly; comparable or superior
- **Evidence Grade: Level B**

**Vaginal Candidiasis:**
- Calendula ovule/cream vs. standard antifungal: small RCT; comparable efficacy
- **Evidence Grade: Level B**

**Anti-Tumor (In Vitro):**
- Polysaccharides and triterpenoids: apoptosis induction in multiple cancer cell lines
- **Evidence Grade: Level D** (in vitro; no clinical trials)

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The Wound Healer's Herb:**
Calendula has the most specific and universally agreed phenomenological profile of any topical plant medicine — it heals wounds, soothes skin, and reduces inflammation in a manner that is both immediately perceivable and quantifiably accelerated. This clarity of application and observable effect makes calendula one of the most satisfying herbs to work with clinically.

**Sensory Profile:**
- Flowers fresh: intensely aromatic; resinous-slightly-herbal; sticky to the touch (resin)
- Orange color: deeply saturating orange-gold; visually distinct from weaker yellow cultivars
- Calendula-infused oil: golden-orange; warm, slightly earthy, herbaceous; stains fabric
- Cream/ointment: silky; lightly aromatic; immediate cooling sensation on inflamed skin

**Topical Application Experience:**
- On wounds: immediate calming of itch and burning; over 24–48 hours, perceptible tissue repair; less weeping; cleaner wound margins
- On eczema/dermatitis: rapid symptom relief of itch and redness within hours; may require repeat application
- On radiation burns: the landmark RCT data mirrors the subjective experience — patients describe significantly less pain and skin breakdown compared to other preparations
- On nipple soreness (breastfeeding): calendula cream is a traditional and clinically supported treatment; immediate soothing; antimicrobial; facilitates continued breastfeeding

**The Radiation Dermatitis Clinical Context:**
Oncology nurses who have used calendula cream on patients undergoing breast radiotherapy describe: dramatic difference in skin integrity compared to trolamine (standard institutional product); patients can tolerate the full radiation course without treatment breaks due to skin breakdown; reduced pain; maintained skin integrity allows complete dosing.

**Pregnancy Association:**
Traditional European use associates calendula with reproductive health — as emmenagogue (regulating periods), as galactagogue (stimulating milk), and paradoxically as a herb to avoid in early pregnancy (uterotonic at high doses). This dual use — stimulating for menstrual regularity; galactagogue postpartum; cautious in early pregnancy — mirrors the hormonal context of its phytoestrogenic minor components.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Infused oil (most versatile base):**
- Pack dried calendula flowers loosely in jar; cover completely with carrier oil (olive or sunflower)
- Infuse in warm place (or solar infusion): 4–6 weeks
- Alternatively: gentle heat extraction 60°C × 4 hours
- Uses: basis for creams, ointments, balms; direct topical application; massage for lymphatic drainage

**Calendula cream/ointment (commercial or prepared):**
- Apply 3–5× daily to wounds, dermatitis, burns, episiotomy; thin layer
- Commercial: Weleda, A.Vogel, many European and organic skincare brands
- Clinical trials use commercial standardized products

**Tea / infusion (internal):**
- 1–2 g dried flowers in 250 mL water; steep 10 minutes; 2–3 cups daily
- Internal: lymphatic; digestive; menstrual; anti-inflammatory; mild
- Note: less dramatic effect internally than topically; primarily lymphatic-digestive

**Tincture:**
- 1:5 in 25% ethanol: 2–4 mL 3× daily for internal use; diluted 1:3 with water for wound rinse

**Mouthwash:**
- 1:5 tincture diluted 1:4 with water: rinse and gargle for oral mucositis, aphthous ulcers, gum disease

**Nipple cream (breastfeeding):**
- Pure calendula cream (avoid lanolin or petroleum base if possible)
- Apply after each feed; safe for infant — wipe excess before next feeding
- Preferred by many lactation consultants over pharmaceutical preparations for nipple soreness

### Safety Profile

**Excellent safety profile for topical use. Very safe internally at recommended doses.**

**Topical adverse effects:**
- Allergic contact dermatitis: Asteraceae family allergy (ragweed, chamomile cross-reactive); patch test before large-area application in sensitive individuals; uncommon but possible
- Staining of fabric and surfaces (carotenoids): caution with fabric and linens

**Drug Interactions:**
- **Sedatives (CNS depressants):** Internal calendula has mild sedative properties; additive with benzodiazepines, alcohol (very minor at standard doses)
- **Immunosuppressants:** Polysaccharide immunostimulation (minor); theoretical caution in transplant patients (lower concern than astragalus)

**Contraindications:**
- **Asteraceae allergy** (ragweed, chamomile, chrysanthemum, echinacea family): cross-reactive; avoid
- **Pregnancy (internal medicinal doses):** Emmenagogue + potentially uterotonic at high internal doses; external topical use considered safe
- **Pre-surgery:** Mild antiplatelet properties (isorhamnetin, quercetin); discontinue supplemental doses 1 week pre-operatively
