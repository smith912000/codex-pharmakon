# Wild Peony — Male Peony / Paeonia / The Physician's Flower
## *Paeonia mascula* (L.) Mill. — Wild Peony, Male Peony, Balkan Peony; native names: Greek *paiōnía árrēn* ("male peony"), Latin *paeonia mas*, Arabic *fāwāniyā* / *'ūd al-ṣalīb* ("cross-wood," from the crucifix-shaped root beads), Italian *peonia selvatica* | Family: Paeoniaceae

**Tradition Tags:** EP, AT, DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification (Galenic/Unani):** Warm in the first-to-second degree, Dry in the second degree (*ḥārr yābis*) — an attenuating, opening, antispasmodic root, distinct from the cooling *Paeonia lactiflora* (Bái Sháo) of the Chinese tradition

### Origin & Tradition

Of every plant in this Codex, the peony carries the plainest claim to being *the* archetypal medicine of the West: it is named for **Paeon** (Παιάν / Παιήων), the physician of the Olympian gods in Homer's *Iliad*, who healed Hades and Ares of their wounds. The plant that bears the healer's name was, for the classical world, the flower of medicine itself — and its most famous indication was one of the most feared disorders of antiquity: the "falling sickness," epilepsy.

**DMM (Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica*, c. 50–70 CE):** Dioscorides gives peony (*paiōnía*) in two forms — *árrēn* (male, our *P. mascula*, with undivided leaves) and *thḗleia* (female, *P. officinalis*, with divided leaves). He directs the **root**, taken orally or dried and powdered, for uterine disorders and to promote menstruation after childbirth (an emmenagogue and post-partum cleanser), for jaundice and kidney/bladder pain, and for gastric complaints. The male root, worn or administered, is specifically associated in the classical and post-classical reception with **epilepsy in children** and with nightmares.

**AT (Avicenna, *al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb*, c. 1025 CE):** In the *Qānūn*, *fāwāniyā* is a warm, dry, attenuating root. Avicenna records the **amulet** tradition explicitly: peony root or its seeds hung about the neck of a child subject to *ṣar'* (epilepsy / the falling sickness). He also records internal use for uterine and menstrual complaints, for spasm and cramp, and for disorders attributed to "melancholic" and convulsive humours. The pairing of *external amulet* and *internal antispasmodic* is important and is preserved faithfully into the European herbals (through Galen, the Arabic transmission, and later Culpeper, who still lists peony "against the falling sickness").

**BM (Assyrian / Mesopotamian tradition):** Mesopotamian medicine treated convulsive and "seizing" illnesses (rendered with terms such as *bennu* / *AN.TA.ŠUB.BA*, the "falling" disease) within a largely incantatory and amulet-based frame, and root-and-stone amulets against seizure were part of that world. Whether *Paeonia* specifically figures in the surviving cuneiform plant lists is not securely established, and no Akkadian binomial for peony should be asserted [USER: verify against a current Mesopotamian materia medica concordance]. The defensible point is cultural continuity: the peony-amulet-against-epilepsy that Dioscorides and Avicenna record sits within a much older Near Eastern amulet tradition for the falling sickness.

**EP (Ebers Papyrus / Egyptian tradition, c. 1550 BCE):** The Egyptian papyri treat convulsive and "trembling/falling" conditions and use a broad amulet-and-plant apparatus, but a secure identification of *Paeonia mascula* in the Egyptian materia medica is not available; the genus is Mediterranean/Balkan-centred rather than Egyptian. The Egyptian tag is best read as the general antiquity of amulet-and-root therapy for seizure, not as a specific peony monograph [USER: verify species attribution].

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Two doctrines converge on one molecule. The first is the **anti-epilepsy amulet**: a root worn to ward off the "falling sickness" and "lunacy" (the disorders classical medicine tied to the moon and to convulsive humours). The second is the **antispasmodic emmenagogue root**: taken internally to relax cramping and move stuck menstruation. Both doctrines are, at their core, claims about *quieting abnormal excitation* — of the brain (seizure), of the mind (lunacy/melancholy), and of smooth muscle (uterine cramp). Modern phytochemistry supplies a single unifying candidate: **paeoniflorin**, the monoterpene glycoside that is the signature active of the entire *Paeonia* genus and the same compound that carries the reputation of Chinese Bái Sháo (*P. lactiflora*) and Chì Sháo. Paeoniflorin is genuinely anticonvulsant, neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory, and smooth-muscle-relaxant in modern animal pharmacology, with proposed actions on adenosine A1 receptors, GABAergic/neuroinflammatory tone, and calcium-dependent contraction. So the ancient doctrine maps with striking fidelity onto real chemistry: the plant the Greeks named for their divine physician, and hung on epileptic children, does contain a neuroactive compound with authentic anticonvulsant pharmacology. **But the map has two honest gaps.** First, the *amulet* route is pharmacologically inert — a worn root delivers no paeoniflorin across the skin; only ingestion of the root could plausibly deliver active compound, and the tradition's most confident anti-seizure claim is for the route (wearing) that cannot work. Second, essentially all of the modern anticonvulsant evidence is for **isolated paeoniflorin or for *P. lactiflora* preparations**, not for *P. mascula* whole-root taken orally, and none of it is clinical. The doctrine points at a real molecule; it does not yet prove the folk remedy.

### Active Components

- **Paeoniflorin** — Monoterpene glycoside; the defining active of *Paeonia*. Documented in modern pharmacology as neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory (NF-κB, TLR4, NLRP3 modulation), antioxidant, smooth-muscle-relaxant (calcium-influx inhibition), and anticonvulsant in animal seizure models; proposed adenosine A1 receptor agonism and effects on monoaminergic and HPA-axis signalling. This is the compound onto which the entire anti-epilepsy / antispasmodic doctrine maps.
- **Albiflorin** — Glycosidic isomer of paeoniflorin; central analgesic and antidepressant-adjacent activity in animal work; contributes to the neuroactive fraction.
- **Oxypaeoniflorin, benzoylpaeoniflorin** — Paeoniflorin analogues; anti-inflammatory and antioxidant.
- **Paeonol** (chiefly a tree-peony bark constituent, minor in *mascula* root) — phenolic ketone; anti-inflammatory, mast-cell-stabilising.
- **Gallotannins and gallic acid** — Astringent polyphenols; antioxidant; underlie the "drying," astringing Galenic character of the root and its haemostatic/wound reputation.
- **Paeoniflorin content note** — Concentration and glycoside profile differ between *P. mascula*, *P. officinalis*, and the pharmacopoeial *P. lactiflora*; quantitative data for *P. mascula* specifically are sparse, so potency should not be assumed equal to standardised Bái Sháo [USER: verify].

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. `[REFINE]` **Antispasmodic / smooth-muscle relaxation.** On the paeoniflorin mechanism (calcium-influx inhibition in visceral smooth muscle), peony root sits in the same functional family as Bái Sháo for cramp-type and menstrual smooth-muscle spasm — though for reliable, evidence-backed dosing the Codex directs practitioners to the far better-characterised *P. lactiflora* (see I_026) rather than wild *P. mascula*.
2. `[RESET]` **Neuroinflammation and excitatory-stress buffering.** Paeoniflorin's NF-κB/NLRP3 and glutamate-excitotoxicity actions provide a rationale for using peony-genus preparations as anti-neuroinflammatory support during high-arousal, over-reached training states. Framed as genus-level rationale, not as a seizure treatment.
3. `[REFINE]` **Historical / symbolic anchor.** The peony's status as *the* physician's flower makes it the Codex's emblem of the "quieting of abnormal excitation" doctrine; its practical Kenetica dosing defers to standardised *P. lactiflora*.

**Explicit clinical boundary:** peony is **not** an antiepileptic drug and must never be used to treat, replace medication for, or self-manage epilepsy or any seizure disorder. The anticonvulsant pharmacology is preclinical and compound-level only.

The raw root, seeds, and flowers of *Paeonia* are considered mildly toxic if eaten in quantity; the plant is a medicinal root to be dosed, not a food. Do not exceed traditional low-gram root quantities, and prefer the characterised *P. lactiflora* extract when paeoniflorin action is actually the goal.

### Scientific Correlation

Evidence grade: **MODERATE for the compound, LOW for the folk remedy.** Paeoniflorin has real, reproducible anticonvulsant/neuroprotective pharmacology; whole *P. mascula* root taken orally (let alone worn) has essentially none of its own, and there are no controlled clinical trials for the traditional anti-epilepsy use.

According to PubMed:
- Tao F, Cai Y, Deng C, Chen Z, Shen Y, Sun H (2022), "A narrative review on traditional Chinese medicine prescriptions and bioactive components in epilepsy treatment," *Annals of Translational Medicine* 11(2):129 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.21037/atm-22-3306)) — lists **paeoniflorin** among the principal TCM bioactive compounds with antiepileptic rationale, while explicitly cautioning that the field lacks standardised treatments and adequate clinical trials — an honest ceiling on the claim.
- Lee S, Ryu SM, Kim DH, Lee YE, Lee SJ, Kang S, Kim JS, Lee SI (2022), "Neuroprotective effect of Geijigadaehwang-tang against trimethyltin-induced hippocampal neurodegeneration," *Journal of Ethnopharmacology* 296:115451 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2022.115451)) — a **paeoniflorin- and albiflorin-containing** herbal formula inhibited trimethyltin-induced **seizures** in mice at oral doses of 180 and 540 mg/kg and reduced hippocampal neuronal death via anti-inflammatory (NF-κB, NLRP3) and antioxidant pathways. Note: this is a multi-herb formula, so it evidences the *genus/compound* rationale, not *P. mascula* specifically.
- Ma W, Ren H, Meng X, Liu S, Du K, Fang S, Chang Y (2024), "A review of the ethnopharmacology, phytochemistry, pharmacology, pharmacokinetics and quality control of *Paeonia lactiflora* Pall.," *Journal of Ethnopharmacology* 335:118616 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2024.118616)) — confirms paeoniflorin as the focal bioactive of the genus with neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity, and that the historical anti-spasmodic/emmenagogue uses have mechanistic support; also flags that constituents beyond paeoniflorin remain understudied.
- Wang XL, Feng ST, Wang YT, Chen NH, Wang ZZ, Zhang Y (2021), "Paeoniflorin: A neuroprotective monoterpenoid glycoside with promising anti-depressive properties," *Phytomedicine* 90:153669 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2021.153669)) — summarises paeoniflorin's neuroprotective, HPA-axis-normalising, monoaminergic and BDNF-upregulating actions, again in **pre-clinical** models, concluding that clinical confirmation is still needed.

The synthesis is therefore a genuine and rather beautiful case of doctrine-meeting-molecule: an ancient anti-epilepsy amulet plant contains a real neuroactive, anticonvulsant glycoside. The deflation the evidence demands is equally clear — the amulet route is inert, the oral whole-*mascula* remedy is unvalidated, and every positive study is either compound-level or on the sister species *P. lactiflora*, none of it clinical for epilepsy.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Warm (1st–2nd degree), Dry (2nd degree); attenuating, opening (*mufattiḥ*), antispasmodic, emmenagogue. **Attested** (Avicenna, Dioscorides). Note the contrast with *P. lactiflora*, which the Chinese tradition classes as **cool** — a genuine cross-tradition divergence in energetics for closely related species.
- **TCM:** *P. mascula* is not a Chinese pharmacopoeial herb; the genus is represented by Bái Sháo (*P. lactiflora*, cool, sour-bitter, Liver/Spleen) and Chì Sháo. For *P. mascula* specifically, **not attested**.
- **Ayurvedic:** *Paeonia mascula* is not a classical Ayurvedic dravya; a *rasa/guṇa/vīrya/vipāka* profile is **not attested** and is not invented here.

### Contraindications

- **NOT a treatment for epilepsy or seizures.** Under no circumstances substitute peony for prescribed antiepileptic medication or use it to self-treat a seizure disorder; the anticonvulsant data are preclinical and compound-level only. Abrupt reliance on an unproven herbal in place of medication risks status epilepticus.
- **Amulet distinction:** the historical worn-root amulet delivers no active compound and has no pharmacological effect; it is recorded as history, not therapy.
- **Root toxicity:** raw root, seeds and flowers of *Paeonia* are mildly toxic in quantity, causing gastrointestinal upset; the root is a dosed medicine, not a food. Do not exceed traditional low quantities [USER: verify safe dose].
- **Pregnancy — contraindicated.** Peony root is a classical emmenagogue with documented uterine/smooth-muscle activity for the genus; avoid in pregnancy. Caution also in lactation (insufficient data).
- **Anticoagulant/antiplatelet co-use:** paeoniflorin and gallotannins show mild antiplatelet activity in vitro; monitor if combined with anticoagulant medication.
- **Species/quality confusion:** commercial "peony" material varies across *P. mascula*, *P. officinalis*, *P. lactiflora* and tree peony (*P. suffruticosa*) with different chemistry; for reliable paeoniflorin dosing use standardised *P. lactiflora* (I_026), not wild-collected *mascula*.
