# Wayfaring Tree

## *Viburnum lantana* L. | Family: Adoxaceae (formerly Caprifoliaceae)

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Cooling / astringent (traditional attribution; inferred from astringent use — see note)
**Training Phase:** Foundation — Signature Study

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Viburnum lantana* — the Wayfaring Tree — is a deciduous European shrub of hedgerows, woodland edges, and calcareous scrub, native across central and southern Europe into North Africa and western Asia, and naturalised elsewhere. It grows to four or five metres, with felted, wrinkled leaves (the underside grey with dense stellate trichomes) and flat corymbs of small creamy-white flowers that ripen into drupes turning from green through red to black. The name "Wayfaring Tree" reflects its habit of lining old travellers' tracks on chalk. It propagates from seed (slow, often two winters to germinate) and from cuttings or layering; it tolerates dry alkaline soils that defeat many shrubs, which is its main horticultural virtue.

Traditionally, the **bark and leaves** are the parts noted for use — **astringent**, historically employed as a **gargle or mouthwash** and for minor astringent purposes (styptic, folk oral care). The **fruit is mildly toxic raw** and is not a food in the way that, say, processed *V. opulus* "gilaburu" juice is in Anatolia.

**A crucial distinction — do not import cramp-bark reputation.** The medicinally famous Viburnums are the **congeners** *Viburnum opulus* ("cramp bark") and *Viburnum prunifolium* ("black haw"), which carry a long reputation as **uterine antispasmodics** and smooth-muscle relaxants. *V. lantana* does **not** share that reputation to any comparable degree; it is a **minor astringent** by comparison. Any antispasmodic or uterine claim in the herbal literature belongs to the congeners, not to the Wayfaring Tree — Kenetica labels this explicitly so a cultivator or compounder never substitutes *V. lantana* where *V. opulus*/*V. prunifolium* was intended.

**Named-tradition attestations.** The tags **CM** (Avicenna, *Canon of Medicine* / Unani stream) and **DMM** (the Dioscorides/Greek herbal stream) are applied here with an explicit caveat. The identity of the plant Dioscorides and later classical writers called "viburnum" is **botanically uncertain** — the Latin *viburnum* in Virgil and the Greek herbal tradition cannot be confidently mapped to *V. lantana* specifically rather than to another shrub. Treat the DMM and CM attestations as **[USER: verify]**: the astringent-gargle use is genuinely attested for the Wayfaring Tree in the European folk-herbal record, but the specific classical (Dioscorides/Avicenna) lineage should be confirmed against a primary source before it is printed as established doctrine.

### Active Components

Phytochemistry specific to *V. lantana* is real but modest, and dominated by the genus-typical classes:

- **Iridoid glycosides** — isolated specifically from *V. lantana* var. *discolor* (Tomassini & Brkić, 1997).
- **Tannins** — the basis of the astringency; the bark/leaf are the astringent parts.
- **Polyphenols and flavonoids** — including compounds such as chlorogenic acid and biflavonoids (e.g. amentoflavone) reported across the genus, with strong antioxidant capacity for *V. lantana* leaf and branch (Altun et al., 2008; Erdogan-Orhan et al., 2010).
- **Coumarins** — attributed to the genus in the traditional phytochemical literature (species-specific confirmation for *V. lantana* is thin — treat as genus-level).
- **Saponins, essential oils, proteins** — the *V. lantana* drupe was found richer in essential oils, saponins, and proteins than *V. opulus*, which was richer in carotenoids, polyphenols, flavonoids, steroids, and pectins (Konarska & Domaciuk, 2017).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Kenetica files *V. lantana* as a **Signature Study / traditional astringent**, deliberately deflated from the medicinal reputation of its cousins. Its verifiable pharmacology is limited to:

- **Astringency** — a tannin-based tissue-tightening action, the mechanistic basis for the traditional gargle/mouthwash and styptic use. This is a chemical-class inference (tannins are astringent), not a species-specific clinical demonstration.
- **Antioxidant capacity** — well demonstrated in vitro for leaf and branch extracts.
- **Weak cholinesterase inhibition** — present but notably weaker than the congener *V. opulus* (see below).

The honest use-case is **narrow and topical/local**: a minor astringent for oral/mucosal folk use. It is **not** an antispasmodic, **not** a "women's herb," and **not** an internal remedy of established efficacy. Where the literature offers a therapeutic Viburnum action, it almost always tested *V. opulus* or *V. prunifolium* — which is the correct cross-reference, not a licence to use *V. lantana*.

### Scientific Correlation

Grading each claim by retrieved evidence:

- **Astringency (tannin-based): TRADITIONAL / SIGNATURE.** Mechanistically plausible (tannins are astringent) and consistent with folk gargle use, but no species-specific clinical trial exists. Grade: traditional.
- **Antioxidant activity: SUPPORTED (in vitro).** *V. lantana* leaf and branch water extracts show strong DPPH and superoxide scavenging (Altun et al., 2008); leaf/branch extracts show high antioxidant power alongside phenolic content (Erdogan-Orhan et al., 2010). Grade: in vitro only.
- **Acetylcholinesterase inhibition: WEAK / SECONDARY.** In a head-to-head study *V. opulus* leaf methanol extract was the strong AChE inhibitor (up to ~93% at 200 µg/mL); *V. lantana* was comparatively the **antioxidant**, not the cholinesterase, performer (Erdogan-Orhan et al., 2010). Grade: weak for *V. lantana*.
- **Iridoid/phytochemical presence: SUPPORTED.** Iridoid glucosides isolated from *V. lantana* var. *discolor* (Tomassini & Brkić, 1997); comparative fruit chemistry documented (Konarska & Domaciuk, 2017).
- **Uterine / smooth-muscle antispasmodic action: NULL for *V. lantana* — belongs to the CONGENER.** The antispasmodic/hypotensive smooth-muscle activity is documented for ***Viburnum opulus***, via the compound **viopudial** (Nicholson, Darby & Jarboe, 1972 — a hypotensive and smooth-muscle antispasmodic from *V. opulus*, tested on uterus and other smooth muscle). This is **congener (other-species) data** and must NOT be attributed to the Wayfaring Tree. Grade for *V. lantana*: NULL / not demonstrated.
- **General medicinal efficacy (internal): NULL.** No clinical evidence for *V. lantana* specifically.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

European folk practice treats the Wayfaring Tree as **cooling and astringent** — the drying, tissue-tightening quality of a tannin-rich bark/leaf suited to relaxed, weeping, or bleeding tissue (gargles, styptic use). As with the classical attestation, this energetic label is **inferred from the astringent indication pattern** rather than quoted from a sourced classical text; treat it as a working classification pending **[USER: verify]**.

### Contraindications

- **Raw fruit is mildly toxic / emetic.** The genus contains cyanogenic and other irritant/toxic constituents in unripe or raw fruit; do not eat raw berries.
- **No established internal dose.** Only external/oral-rinse folk use has any traditional footing; internal use is unsupported and unquantified.
- **Do NOT substitute for cramp bark / black haw.** *V. lantana* lacks the demonstrated antispasmodic action of *V. opulus* / *V. prunifolium*; substituting it where those are indicated is a therapeutic failure hazard.
- **Pregnancy/lactation, paediatric use:** no safety data — avoid. (Note the irony: the antispasmodic "women's" reputation attaches to the congeners, not this plant.)
- **Astringent load:** high-tannin preparations can irritate the gut and impair absorption of iron and some alkaloidal drugs if taken internally.
