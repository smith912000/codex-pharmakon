# Parietaria officinalis — Elxine, Helxine, pellitory of the wall (Dioscorides 4-86)

## *Parietaria officinalis [Linnaeus], one of several names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-86 (which also prints Helxine, Parietaria [Fuchs] and Parietaria officinarum et Dioscorides [Bauhin] — "Pellitory of the Wall", and, marked "other usage", Helxine soleirolii and Soleirolia soleirolii — "Mind-your-own-business, Mother of Thousands"); a wall plant* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter, and no other tradition on disk is tagged for it. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line prints two distinct plants: the pellitory-of-the-wall group (*Parietaria*), which the row takes, and, under "other usage", the garden plant *Soleirolia soleirolii* ("Mind-your-own-business"). The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-86. ELXINE*, heading at line 8799, the SUGGESTED line at line 8800, the first part of the text at line 8801 and the end at line 8813, in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"). The text is cut in the middle of a sentence by two plate pages, "Galeopsis minor after FUCHS" and "Gallium after FUCHS" (lines 8802 to 8812, with the translators' page numbers 636 to 638), and resumes at line 8813. The whole chapter, joined, reads:

"Helxine grows in mounds and walls. It has thin little stalks, somewhat red, rough leaves similar to mercury [4-191]; and around the stalks (as it were) sharp little seeds, catching hold of cloths. The leaves are astringent and cooling, as a result (smeared on) they heal erysipela [streptococcal skin infection], venereal warts, dehydration, pannus [opaque thickening of cornea with veins] that is beginning, all types of inflammation, and oedema. The juice of it (mixed with cerussa [white lead ore] and smeared on) helps erysipela [streptococcal skin infection] and herpes [viral skin infection]; and taken with cyprinum [1-65] and goat's grease, it helps gout in the feet. As much as a wine cupful of the juice (sipped) helps those who have coughed a long time, is an effective gargle and ointment for inflamed tonsils, and dropped in the ears with rosaceum [1-53] is good for earache. It is also called sideritis, parthenium, heraclia, asyria, agrestis hygiena, clibadium, or polyonymon."

The description gives a habitat (mounds and walls), reddish thin stalks, rough leaves compared with *mercury* (4-191), and seeds that cling to cloth, which is a feature of the pellitory group and one of the better descriptive clues in the chapter. The qualities are two, "astringent and cooling", and the uses are mostly external: leaves smeared on for skin inflammations, warts, the early film on the eye and swellings, juice mixed with white lead for skin eruptions, juice with an ointment and goat's grease for gout. One internal use is given: a wine cupful of the juice "sipped" for a long cough. The synonym list includes *sideritis*, which is also the heading of a separate chapter, 4-33 *SIDERITIS* (line 8291, the mountain woundwort of the translators), so the same name labels two chapters. The text gives no deity and no amuletic use.

**The chapters around it.** 4-87 *ALSINE* (chickweed or stitchwort in the translators' suggestion) follows at line 8814 and is described there as "a herb similar to helxine [4-86] but lower", able to "do the same things as helxine"; it is a separate chapter and the Codex does not carry its statements over to 4-86. 4-39 *ELXINE KUSSAMPELOS* (line 8338) is a different "helxine" (the translators suggest bindweed) and is not this plant.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter id 4-86 and for the names *helxine*, *elxine*, *parietaria* and "pellitory" found no existing entry on the same chapter. The hits are cross-references only: I_1132 (*Mercurialis annua*) cites 4-86 inside its own chapter text ("similar to those of helxine [4-39, 4-86]"); I_1116 (*Lemna polyrrhiza*, 4-88) places its own chapter after 4-86 and 4-87; I_1074 (*Elatine paludosa*) tells *elatine* from helxine by leaf size; I_649 (*Stellaria media*, chickweed) places chickweed in the *helxine*/*alsine* group and I_977 (*Achillea ageratum*) quotes the page break of 4-39; I_1000 (*Anagallis arvensis*) also matched the search, and I_1067 (*Delphinium peregrinum*) matched only on "Pellitory of Spain", a different plant (*Anacyclus pyrethrum*); I_892 (*Common Honeysuckle*) prints "helxine major" among its names. None is a duplicate of this chapter. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The composition of the wall plant Dioscorides describes was not read. As a general fact, *Parietaria* belongs to the nettle family (Urticaceae) but does not sting, and its pollen is a well-known cause of hay fever and allergic asthma in the Mediterranean region. No constituent is named in the chapter. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records an astringent, cooling leaf smeared on for erysipelas, warts, the early film on the eye and swellings, and a juice with white lead, ointment and goat's grease for skin eruptions and gout, and sipped for coughs, tonsils and earache. The Codex gives no protocol for any of these. One preparation uses white lead ore (*cerussa*), a poison, and the Codex does not recommend it.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives several preparations, with locus (lines 8801 and 8813): the leaves "smeared on"; the juice "mixed with cerussa [white lead ore] and smeared on"; the juice "taken with cyprinum [1-65] and goat's grease" for gout in the feet; "as much as a wine cupful of the juice (sipped)" for a long cough, as a gargle and ointment for the tonsils; and the juice "dropped in the ears with rosaceum [1-53]". The wine cupful is the only measure given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved for the other preparations. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that astringent plant preparations tighten tissues, that white lead is toxic and should never be applied or taken, and that erysipelas, eye changes with vessels on the cornea, long-standing cough and inflamed tonsils need medical assessment. The identification is a translators' suggestion between two different plants, so the chapter does not show that the plant it describes is *Parietaria officinalis*.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints two qualities: "The leaves are astringent and cooling". It prints no degree and no drying or moistening quality for 4-86.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives a student of attention is a recognition description: the habitat on walls, the reddish stalks, the leaves compared with a named herb, and the sharp seeds that catch hold of cloth. It gives no season of gathering, no storage and no test of quality. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a medicinal leaf and juice, and the text records no amuletic use. No cultivation framing is attested.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The one thing to take from it is the habit of recognising a plant by where it grows and by a plain physical trait (here, seeds that cling to cloth). The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

One preparation in the chapter contains white lead ore, which is poisonous; do not use it. Erysipelas, eye disease, tonsillitis and earache need medical care. People with hay fever or asthma should be aware that the plant's pollen is a common allergen. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion between two distinct plants, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
