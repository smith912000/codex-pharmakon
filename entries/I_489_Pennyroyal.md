# I-489 — Pennyroyal
## *Mentha pulegium* L.

**Family:** Lamiaceae | **Volume:** I — Plant-Based | **Entry:** I-489

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted Name:** *Mentha pulegium* L. (1753)
**Common Names:** Pennyroyal, European pennyroyal, squaw mint, mosquito plant, pudding grass
**Synonyms:** *Pulegium vulgare* Mill., *Mentha pulegium* var. *genuina* Briq.

**Etymology:** *pulegium* derives from Latin *pulex* (flea), reflecting the plant's ancient use as a flea repellent; "penny" may derive from *pulegium* corruption or from Old English *pulioll* (thyme-like plant); "royal" from *regale* (of the king), indicating highest quality of the herb

**Botanical Description:** Aromatic perennial herb, 10–40 cm, strongly prostrate to ascending; stems square (characteristic Lamiaceae); leaves ovate to elliptic, 1–3 cm, glandular-dotted, aromatic when crushed; flowers pale lilac to violet in dense, distinct whorls; calyx tubular with hairy throat; bloom July–October; strongly peppermint-like aroma but sharper, more menthone-like

**Distribution:** Native to Europe, North Africa, Middle East; naturalized globally in temperate zones; prefers wet habitats, stream banks, moist meadows; now rare in parts of native range

**Plant Part Used:** Aerial parts (herb); essential oil (EO) distilled from aerial parts

**Adulterants/Substitutions:** *Mentha × piperita* (peppermint); *Hedeoma pulegioides* (American pennyroyal; different species but similar folk use and pulegone content)

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

### The Emmenagogue Paradox

Pennyroyal represents one of history's most dangerous medicinal plants, where the gap between traditional use and safe practice is widest. The cross-traditional consensus — striking across Mediterranean, Near Eastern, and indigenous American traditions — centers on a single, consistent application: stimulation of menstrual flow and promotion of uterine contraction. This consensus is pharmacologically real; the mechanisms are documented and the effects are genuine. The danger is that the dose producing therapeutic emmenagogue effects overlaps dangerously with the dose producing severe hepatotoxicity and fetal demise.

Dioscorides (*De Materia Medica* II.27) describes *glechon* (pennyroyal) as promoting menstruation, facilitating labor, and expelling the afterbirth — applications reflecting uterotonic activity. He also notes its use in wine for digestive complaints, as a flea repellent, and for respiratory conditions. Ibn Sīnā echoes these emmenagogue applications in the Canon, emphasizing the plant's "opening" and "dispersing" qualities (*mufattiḥ* and *muḥallil*) that were understood to move stagnant humors — a framework that maps, at the pharmacological level, to smooth muscle stimulation.

The persistent cross-cultural use of pennyroyal as an abortifacient — particularly in North American indigenous traditions and in European folk medicine through the 20th century — is documented in both ethnobotanical records and, tragically, in the forensic medicine literature. Multiple deaths from pennyroyal EO ingestion for attempted self-induced abortion are documented in the 20th-century clinical record. The mechanism is not abortion through uterine stimulation alone; it is hepatic failure from pulegone metabolites that kills before abortion completes.

### The Flea-Repellent Etymology

The botanical name *pulegium*, from Latin *pulex* (flea), preserves one of the plant's oldest documented uses. Pliny the Elder (NH XX.53) dedicates considerable space to pennyroyal as the preeminent flea repellent of the ancient Mediterranean world. The insecticidal and insect-repellent activity of pulegone has been confirmed in modern bioassays; pulegone disrupts insect cholinesterase function at concentrations below its mammalian toxicity threshold, providing a genuine therapeutic window absent in its emmenagogue applications.

### The Dose Collapse Problem

Historical practitioners worked with herb teas and crude preparations where pulegone concentration was modest and variable. Modern pennyroyal EO concentrates pulegone 800–1,000-fold relative to a weak tea infusion. When 20th-century users applied EO at volumes comparable to traditional decoction doses, they unknowingly crossed the hepatotoxic threshold. The Sullivan 1979 case and subsequent reports confirmed what pharmacokinetics predicts: there is no safe internal dose of pennyroyal EO.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

### Essential Oil (Primary Active Fraction)
**Pulegone** (8-p-menthen-3-one): 80–92% of EO; monocyclic monoterpene ketone; the primary active constituent and primary toxicant; responsible for both therapeutic emmenagogue activity and hepatotoxic metabolite cascade

**Isopulegone:** minor constituent; structural isomer

**Menthone:** variable; 1–5%; biosynthetically related precursor

**Isomenthone:** trace to 3%

**Pulegol:** trace alcohol

**d-Limonene:** ~1–3%

### Phenolic Fraction
**Rosmarinic acid:** standard Lamiaceae constituent; anti-inflammatory; GABA-T inhibitor (shared with lemon balm, sage, rosemary, thyme, lavender, peppermint, spearmint, oregano, hyssop, marjoram — universal Lamiaceae marker across entries I-476 through I-488)

**Luteolin, apigenin:** flavone anti-inflammatory and mild GABA-A modulatory compounds

**Caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid:** hydroxycinnamic acid antioxidants

### Pulegone Metabolism — The Hepatotoxicity Cascade

The toxicological significance of pennyroyal is inseparable from its CYP450-mediated metabolic activation:

```
Pulegone
  → (CYP3A4, CYP2E1)
  → Menthofuran [primary hepatotoxic intermediate]
     → (further oxidation)
     → p-Cresol [reactive metabolite]
     → Mintlactone, Isomintlactone [minor metabolites]
  → Pulegone epoxide [reactive alkylating agent]
  → Glutathione conjugates (GSH depletion)
```

Menthofuran is directly hepatotoxic and a potent mechanism-based inactivator of CYP2E1, creating a self-amplifying toxicity: as menthofuran accumulates, it inhibits its own further metabolism, concentrating the reactive epoxide pool. GSH depletion below 30% of normal triggers hepatocyte necrosis through oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction — identical in mechanism to acetaminophen hepatotoxicity, and similarly mitigated by N-acetylcysteine if administered within the narrow early window.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Uterotonic/Emmenagogue Mechanism

**Pulegone → prostaglandin synthesis stimulation:** Pulegone and its metabolites increase PGE₂ and PGF₂α synthesis, promoting uterine smooth muscle contraction. The mechanism involves CYP450-mediated reactive intermediates that stimulate arachidonic acid release from membrane phospholipids via phospholipase A₂ activation.

**Direct smooth muscle effects:** Pulegone at low concentrations acts as a mild smooth muscle stimulant through calcium channel-facilitated mechanisms, mechanistically distinct from the VGCC *blockade* seen with menthol (I-484).

**Dose separation collapse:** Emmenagogue doses (herb tea) and hepatotoxic doses (EO) appear separated in principle, but individual CYP2E1 variation, alcohol induction of CYP2E1, and cumulative dosing erase this separation in practice. The perceived safety of "just an herb tea" has caused fatalities when concentrated decoctions or repeated dosing were employed.

### 5.2 Insecticidal/Repellent Mechanism

Pulegone inhibits invertebrate acetylcholinesterase at concentrations below mammalian toxicity threshold, consistent with its selective insect toxicity. Contact irritant and olfactory deterrent effects contribute to flea and mosquito repellency. The relationship between *pulex* and *pulegium* preserves a genuinely pharmacological observation: pulegone's insecticidal selectivity is real at the concentrations encountered in plant surface contact.

### 5.3 Carminative/Antispasmodic Mechanism

At herb-tea concentrations, pulegone and related monoterpenes provide mild smooth muscle relaxation in the gastrointestinal tract via calcium channel antagonism — the same carminative mechanism as peppermint but far weaker and without therapeutic advantage over safer *Mentha* species.

### 5.4 Hepatotoxicity Mechanism (Critical Safety)

**CYP2E1/3A4 activation → menthofuran:** Key first step; rate-limiting in toxicity; explains why CYP2E1 inducers (ethanol, fasting, obesity, isoniazid) dramatically increase risk

**Menthofuran → CYP2E1 inactivation:** Suicide substrate; amplifies metabolite accumulation in a positive feedback loop

**GSH depletion:** Reactive intermediates conjugate GSH; GSH <30% normal = oxidative crisis

**Mitochondrial dysfunction:** Uncoupling + reactive oxygen species → hepatocyte necrosis

**Centrilobular necrosis pattern:** Same hepatic zonal pattern as acetaminophen; confirmed in autopsy reports

**Antidote:** N-acetylcysteine (NAC) — same protocol as acetaminophen overdose; must be given within 8–16 hours of ingestion for maximum effect; IV NAC preferred

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### 6.1 Emmenagogue/Abortifacient — No Safe Evidence

**Evidence Level: D (case reports of toxicity; controlled trials ethically prohibited)**

Multiple case reports and forensic medicine literature document:

- Sullivan et al. 1979 (*Lancet*): First published American case — pennyroyal EO ingestion for attempted abortion → fulminant hepatic failure, death; autopsy: centrilobular necrosis
- Anderson et al. 1996 (*Veterinary and Human Toxicology* 38(5):368-371): Two cases of severe hepatotoxicity from pennyroyal EO; one fatality; one survival with early NAC therapy
- Key finding: Effective abortion dose equals or exceeds severely hepatotoxic dose; no therapeutic window exists for EO

No RCTs exist, are planned, or are ethically possible for abortifacient endpoints.

### 6.2 Flea/Insect Repellent

**Evidence Level: B (laboratory; limited human field validation)**

- Pulegone demonstrates insect repellency in controlled laboratory assays against *Aedes aegypti* (dengue vector), *Anopheles* spp. (malaria vector), and fleas
- Comparative efficacy against DEET: inconsistent; some assays show comparable short-duration efficacy; field duration markedly inferior to DEET
- No RCT for human application
- Safety concern: topical pulegone penetrates intact skin; dermal absorption occurs; hepatotoxic concern at high-frequency application

### 6.3 Carminative/Digestive

**Evidence Level: C (traditional evidence only)**

Herb-tea preparations (dried herb, not EO) have traditional evidence for digestive discomfort, but no clinical trials exist and no advantage over safer *Mentha* species (peppermint I-484, spearmint I-485) can be demonstrated.

### 6.4 Regulatory Status

- **Commission E:** NOT approved; monograph not granted due to hepatotoxicity
- **ESCOP:** NOT monographed
- **FDA:** No drug approval; warning letters issued regarding EO products marketed for abortion induction
- **EU Regulation 1334/2008 (food flavors):** Maximum pulegone 25 mg/kg food products; 20 mg/kg in beverages; exception: mint confectionery 250 mg/kg
- **EFSA 2014:** Scientific opinion on pulegone; tolerable daily intake established at 0.04 mg/kg bw/day

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

### 7.1 The Abortifacient Legacy and Its Costs

The history of pennyroyal is inseparable from reproductive autonomy and its costs. For at least two millennia, pennyroyal was the most widely known herbal abortifacient in the Western pharmacopoeia — documented in Roman elegiac poetry (Ovid), medieval herbal manuscripts, and early modern midwifery texts. Its persistence in this role into the 20th and 21st centuries has resulted in documented fatalities in contexts where safer alternatives were unavailable or unknown.

The tragedy of pennyroyal lies not in its toxicity per se, but in the collapse of the dose-response separation that traditional practitioners assumed existed. A concentrated decoction in historical settings differed from a modern commercial EO by orders of magnitude in pulegone concentration. When modern users applied EO at decoction-equivalent volumes, the hepatotoxic threshold was crossed. The 1979 Sullivan case confirmed what the pharmacology predicts.

### 7.2 Etymology as Pharmacological Archive

The name *pulegium* (from flea-*pulex*) and the plant's consistent use as a flea repellent across Mediterranean, European, and North American traditions represents a genuinely accurate pharmacological observation preserved in folk knowledge. The insecticidal activity of pulegone against *Ctenocephalides felis* (cat flea) and *Pulex irritans* (human flea) has been confirmed in bioassays — the etymology preserves a valid clinical observation. This is the safest traditional application.

### 7.3 "Squaw Mint" — Colonial Naming and Indigenous Pharmacology

The common name "squaw mint" reflects both the widespread use of American pennyroyal (*Hedeoma pulegioides*) among North American indigenous women for menstrual regulation and the deeply derogatory colonial naming conventions applied to plants used in women's reproductive medicine. The name is not acceptable in contemporary usage. The pharmacological observation embedded in it deserves documentation: multiple North American indigenous traditions used both native *Hedeoma* species and introduced *M. pulegium* for menstrual regulation, providing cross-cultural validation of the emmenagogue mechanism that predates European pharmacology's interest in the plant.

### 7.4 Pudding Grass and Culinary Safety

The English common name "pudding grass" derives from the plant's culinary use as a flavoring in black puddings (blood sausages) and pork preparations. Medieval English cookbooks reference *peniroial* as a common pork stuffing herb, serving both as flavor and, practically, as a flea deterrent in meat preparation. This culinary use at low concentrations (dried herb in food quantities) is pharmacologically distinct from medicinal use — pulegone content per serving of food is below any threshold of concern. The culinary and medicinal use represent two entirely different dose ranges with non-overlapping pharmacological effects.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Indications (Evidence-Based, Within Safety Parameters)

**Flea Repellent (Topical Herb — Not EO):**
Dried herb sachets in pet bedding; fresh herb scattered in areas of flea activity; diluted herb infusion as topical wash. *Thymus vulgaris* (thyme I-482) and *Lavandula angustifolia* (lavender I-483) provide comparable or superior flea repellency with substantially better safety profiles — prefer these.

**No other indications are recommended** due to the availability of safer alternatives for all traditional applications and the absence of any approved regulatory indication.

### 8.2 Preparations and Dosing

**Herb Tea (historical; not recommended as preferred treatment):**
- 1–2 g dried herb in 150 mL boiling water, 10 minutes
- Maximum 3 cups/day; maximum 1 week duration; NOT during pregnancy; NOT with hepatic disease; NOT with EO

**Essential Oil (INTERNAL USE ABSOLUTELY CONTRAINDICATED):**
- No safe internal dose established
- Multiple fatalities documented at doses as low as 10–30 mL of EO
- External use only: maximum 1% dilution in carrier oil; avoid mucous membranes; avoid with pregnancy; limit skin contact area and duration

### 8.3 Contraindications and Safety

**ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- **Pregnancy:** Abortifacient at supramedicinal doses; potentially lethal to mother at abortifacient doses; DO NOT USE under any circumstances during pregnancy
- **Hepatic disease (any):** Metabolic activation to menthofuran requires hepatic CYP enzymes; pre-existing hepatic compromise dramatically increases toxicity risk
- **Children under 12:** No safety data; heightened sensitivity to monoterpene ketones documented in children (see also hyssop pinocamphone I-487, sage thujone I-481)
- **Internal essential oil use:** No safe dose; NEVER administer pennyroyal EO internally

**RELATIVE CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- **CYP2E1 inducers concurrent:** Ethanol (significant induction at habitual drinking levels), isoniazid, fasting (upregulates CYP2E1); avoid pennyroyal in any form in these contexts
- **CYP3A4 inducers concurrent:** Rifampin, carbamazepine, St. John's Wort; may increase pulegone activation rate

**MONITORING IN SUSPECTED INGESTION:**
Immediate emergency department referral; liver function panel; AST/ALT/bilirubin; N-acetylcysteine protocol per acetaminophen toxicity protocol; poison control consultation

**PREFERRED ALTERNATIVES (always superior):**
- Digestive carminative: *M. × piperita* (I-484), *M. spicata* (I-485)
- Flea repellent: *L. angustifolia* (I-483), *T. vulgaris* (I-482), DEET-based products
- Emmenagogue/menstrual support: medical evaluation; evidence-based botanical alternatives

### 8.4 Energetics

**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (circulatory/functional)
**Thermal Quality:** Warm-moving (Galenic: hot, dry)
**Directional Action:** Downward-bearing (emmenagogue, descending); dispersing
**Classical Temperament:** Among the most heating and dispersing of the *Mentha* genus; contrasts sharply with cooling menthol of peppermint (I-484)

### 8.5 Quality and Authentication

**Botanical Authentication:** GC-MS analysis of EO: pulegone 80–92% confirms *M. pulegium*; distinguish from *H. pulegioides* (American pennyroyal; pulegone 10–30%; different risk profile but same hepatotoxic concern); *M. × piperita* (menthol 30–50%; not pulegone dominant — a critical safety authentication in either direction)

**Adulteration risk:** Commercial EO market; adulteration with synthetic pulegone; species substitution in both directions (diluted pennyroyal sold as pennyroyal; pennyroyal sold as peppermint — the second direction being a dangerous adulteration to the end user). GC-MS CoA mandatory for any pennyroyal product.
