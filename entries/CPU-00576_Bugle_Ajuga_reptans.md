# Bugle — Bugleweed, Carpet Bugle, Middle Confound, Sicklewort

## *Ajuga reptans* L. | Family: Lamiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT (Assyrian), DMM (Dioscorides), CM (Avicenna)
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry in the second degree — a binding, astringent, "closing" herb in the Galenic-Unani scheme.
**Training Phase:** Purification

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian (AT):** The cuneiform materia medica of the Ashurbanipal collection catalogues a family of low, creeping "wound herbs" gathered from damp meadow and woodland margins and pounded into poultices for bleeding cuts and ulcerating sores. *Ajuga reptans* — a mat-forming Lamiaceae of exactly those habitats across the Fertile Crescent's northern reach — sits comfortably within this class of stem-the-blood, close-the-flesh plants applied fresh to a wound and bound in place.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** In *De Materia Medica*, Dioscorides describes bugle-type plants of the "chamaipitys / bugula" grouping as astringent herbs that consolidate wounds, check haemorrhage, and are laid on fresh injuries and applied to the mouth and throat for inflamed, ulcerated tissue. The plant earned its long European by-names — *consolida media* ("middle confound"), *herba carpentariorum* ("carpenters' herb"), and sicklewort — precisely because it was the field dressing reached for when a blade slipped.

**Avicenna (CM):** The Unani tradition carried forward through Avicenna's *Qanun* classed such creeping astringent herbs as cold, dry, and "retentive": cooling heat, drying excess moisture, binding relaxed tissue, and arresting fluxes of blood. Bugle's role as a wound-consolidator and mouthwash for slack, bleeding gums fits this humoral logic directly — a plant that gathers and holds what the body is losing.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Bugle's signature is *containment*. It runs flat and knits the ground into a continuous living mat by rooting from its runners — a plant that literally closes open surfaces. Tradition read this creeping, self-suturing habit as the outward sign of an inward virtue: the power to draw the lips of a wound together and hold them. Modern work supplies a mechanism where symbol once stood alone. According to PubMed, *Ajuga reptans* herb extract measurably shortens bleeding time and closes experimental wounds, so the doctrine of "the herb that binds" now has a testable correlate rather than resting on resemblance.

### Active Components

- **Phytoecdysteroids** — insect-moulting-hormone analogues concentrated in *Ajuga*; from *A. reptans* var. *reptans*, novel C-29 ecdysteroids (reptanslactone A and B, sendreisterone) alongside 24-dehydroprecyasterone and breviflorasterone have been isolated and structurally resolved.
- **Iridoid glycosides** — characteristic of the genus, associated with the plant's bitter, astringent, anti-inflammatory character.
- **Phenolics and hydroxycinnamic acids** — HPLC of the herb identifies roughly twenty phenolic constituents, plus flavonoids and tannins carrying the astringent action.
- **Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone)** — detected in the herb, a plausible contributor to the observed local haemostatic effect.
- **Amino and organic acids, ascorbic acid** — sixteen amino acids and a spread of organic acids reported on assay.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In the Purification phase, Bugle is a *closing and clearing* agent — the herb turned to when a surface is open, weeping, or slack and needs to be drawn shut and settled. Its traditional and evidenced actions cluster around three uses: topical wound consolidation and control of minor bleeding; an astringent gargle or mouthwash for inflamed, spongy, bleeding gums and sore throat; and a mild internal bitter-astringent for loose, relaxed tissue states. It is a herb of *finishing* rather than *forcing* — it does not purge or stimulate; it binds, dries, and consolidates, making it a natural companion to the phase's work of sealing what earlier stages have opened.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:

- Maliuvanchuk et al. (2025), *Plants (Basel)* — aqueous and aqueous-ethanolic *A. reptans* herb extracts were phytochemically profiled (16 amino acids, 20 phenolics, vitamin K1, tannins, flavonoids) and shown to be practically non-toxic, hepatoprotective in CCl4-induced hepatitis, moderately anti-inflammatory in carrageenan oedema, significantly haemostatic (bleeding time reduced by 40.6%), and wound-healing (complete closure by day 9), with activity against Gram-positive bacteria. DOI: https://doi.org/10.3390/plants14020219
- Ványolós et al. (2009), *Journal of Natural Products* — isolation and full structural elucidation of three new C-29 ecdysteroids (reptanslactone A, reptanslactone B, sendreisterone) plus two known ecdysteroids from *A. reptans* var. *reptans*, confirming the species as a source of structurally diverse phytoecdysteroids. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1021/np800708g

**Evidence grade:** Preclinical and traditional. Wound-healing, haemostatic, hepatoprotective and anti-inflammatory findings rest on animal-model and in vitro data from a single recent extract study; the phytochemistry (ecdysteroids, phenolics) is well characterised. No controlled human trials. Traditional wound and astringent uses are of high historical confidence; clinical efficacy is not established.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Cold and dry (second degree); binding, retentive, consolidating; used to arrest fluxes and close wounds.
- **TCM (by analogy, not classical):** would map to a cooling, astringent, blood-stanching herb clearing damp-heat from the surface; not a canonical Chinese materia medica plant.
- **Ayurvedic (by analogy):** *rasa* — tikta (bitter) and kashaya (astringent); *virya* — shita (cooling); *vipaka* — katu (pungent); pacifying to pitta and kapha in excess-moisture, bleeding presentations. Not a classical Ayurvedic dravya.

### Contraindications

- Internal medicinal use is poorly standardised; keep to short courses and traditional doses. Uncertain material — [USER: verify] any internal protocol.
- Vitamin K1 content is a theoretical concern for patients on vitamin-K-antagonist anticoagulants (e.g. warfarin); avoid concentrated internal preparations without clinical oversight.
- Avoid in pregnancy and lactation for want of safety data.
- Topical use should be on minor, clean wounds only; deep, infected, or extensive wounds require medical care, not herbal poultice.
- Possible cross-reactive sensitivity in those allergic to Lamiaceae.
