**Avicenna, *Canon* of Medicine (CM).** (Canon Book II, entry *Ūnūmālī*, Arabic text line 10378–10386). *What it is (الماهية):* "a very hot oil, thick like honey and thicker than it, that oozes from the trunk of a sweet *Tadmurī* tree" (دهن حار جدا ثخين كالعسل وأثخن منه يتحلب من ساق شجرة تدمرية حلوة); *Tadmur* is Palmyra. *Choice:* the best is the clearest, thickest and oldest. *Temperament:* hot and moist, its heat greater than its moisture. *Wounds:* of benefit in ulcerated scab (الجرب المتقرح) as a salve and a plaster. *Joints:* it benefits joint pains. *Head:* "in it is drowsiness and sluggishness" (فيه إسبات وتكسيل). *Eye:* good for dimness of the eye applied as a kohl. *Excretion:* it purges. The passage ends by telling those purged not to be alarmed, and adds that the person must not sleep upon it, "as is said" (فيما يقال). The wording of the last sentences is difficult in this e-text. ### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

Per Avicenna's Canon, Onomali is a hot and moist material, with heat greater than moisture and no degree printed. Its stated actions reach the skin, the joints, the head, the eyes and the bowels. As a salve or plaster it benefits ulcerated scab, and the entry lists joint pains as a use. The Canon says it causes drowsiness and sluggishness, which the text places in the head. Applied as a kohl it is said to help dimness of the eye. The purge is said to expel raw humours and to slacken, an effect on the bowels. The entry assigns this material no place in preserving innate heat or radical moisture. The temperament and the purge belong to the Canon's humoral language, and the Codex records them as historical only, with no mapping to any modern condition.
