# Catnip — Catmint, Field Balm, Nep, *nabruqu* (Assyrian herbals), *faranjmushk* (Perso-Arabic tradition)

## *Nepeta cataria* L. | Family: Lamiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, SBJ, CM
**Energetic Classification:** Warm and dry in the second degree (Galenic/Unani); diaphoretic and carminative
**Training Phase:** Settling — the parasympathetic down-shift before deeper work

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian (Nineveh tablets):** The cuneiform herbal corpus recovered from Ashurbanipal's library lists numerous aromatic Lamiaceae used as calmatives, carminatives and febrifuges, prepared as infusions and poultices. Catmint belongs to this broad Mesopotamian pharmacy of "cooling the belly and quieting the head," though tablet-to-species identification is necessarily approximate and should be read as genus-level tradition rather than a pinned botanical claim.

**Avicenna (Qanun fi'l-Tibb):** In the Perso-Arabic materia medica, aromatic mints of the *faranjmushk* / *nammam* group are classed as warm-dry, prescribed to disperse cold flatulence, provoke sweating in fevers, promote menstruation and settle the stomach. The energetic reading — a warming, dispersing, drying herb — maps closely onto catnip's later European use.

**Dioscorides (De Materia Medica):** The Greek herbal tradition describes *kalaminthe*/*nepeta* types as heating herbs that expel wind, provoke sweat and bring on the menses; the emmenagogue reputation recorded here is the origin of the enduring pregnancy caution below.

**Shennong / Chinese materia medica:** *Jing jie* (schizonepeta, a closely allied Lamiaceae) occupies the classic role of a warm, acrid surface-releasing herb used for early wind-cold fevers, sore throat and skin eruptions — the same diaphoretic logic that European herbalists applied to *N. cataria*. The parallel is functional and cross-traditional rather than a claim of identical species.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Catnip sits at a revealing pharmacological hinge — it is a sedative to one animal and a stimulant to another. The famous feline intoxication is triggered by nepetalactone acting through the olfactory pathway, while in humans the warm infusion is a mild relaxant and diaphoretic. The doctrine-of-signatures reading (a herb that "calms the beast") is, unusually, echoed by real chemistry: the same iridoid that excites cats is a potent arthropod deterrent, so the plant's symbolic identity as a threshold guardian — repelling pests, quieting nerves, opening the pores — is grounded in a single measurable molecule.

### Active Components

- **Nepetalactone** (iridoid monoterpenoid) — the dominant volatile, present as *cis,trans* and *trans,cis* stereoisomers whose ratio defines the plant's chemotype. Responsible for both the feline response and the insect-repellent activity.
- **Caryophyllene** (sesquiterpene) — secondary volatile that modulates and, in mixtures, enhances repellency.
- Iridoid biosynthesis proceeds via the enzymes GES, ISY and MLPL, confirmed in planta (see Scientific Correlation).
- Rosmarinic acid and other Lamiaceae polyphenols; trace tannins accounting for the mild astringency.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Positioned in the **Settling** phase as a gentle parasympathetic primer. Traditionally taken as a warm evening infusion to ease restlessness, soothe digestive cramping and colic, and encourage a light sweat at the onset of a feverish chill. In the historical children's-remedy tradition it was the go-to for infant colic and low fevers — a use to be treated as traditional practice, not as a validated paediatric indication (see Contraindications). Its Kenetica role is a low-intensity nervine and carminative: it loosens the grip of a busy digestive-nervous axis before deeper contemplative or somatic work, without sedation heavy enough to dull attention.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the best-evidenced pharmacology of *N. cataria* is arthropod repellency rather than human sedation. Birkett et al. showed that catmint essential oil and purified nepetalactone isomers repelled *Anopheles gambiae* malaria mosquitoes with potency comparable to DEET, and also deterred ixodid ticks and poultry mites, with a notable synergy between the two nepetalactone isomers ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phytochem.2010.09.016)). The biosynthetic route to nepetalactone was confirmed in planta by Palmer et al., who used virus-induced gene silencing to establish the roles of the GES, ISY and MLPL enzymes ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00425-022-04012-z)).

**Evidence grade: TRADITIONAL for the calmative / carminative / diaphoretic and paediatric-colic uses** — these rest on long cross-tradition practice, not on controlled human trials, and no such trial is cited here. **The insect-repellent activity is EXPERIMENTALLY SUPPORTED** at the bioassay level. Human clinical efficacy for anxiety, colic or fever should be regarded as unproven pending trials.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Warm and dry (2nd degree); dispersing, diaphoretic, carminative, emmenagogue.
- **TCM (by functional analogy to *jing jie*):** Acrid, slightly warm; enters Lung and Liver; releases the exterior, disperses wind.
- **Ayurvedic (attributed by energetics, not classical text):** *rasa* pungent/bitter; *virya* heating (ushna); *vipaka* pungent; kapha- and vata-settling in small warm doses, potentially vata-aggravating if over-dried.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy — caution/avoid.** Catnip carries a documented emmenagogue and traditional uterine-stimulant reputation across the Dioscoridean and Unani traditions; it should be avoided in pregnancy as a precaution.
- **Paediatric use** was traditional but is not clinically validated; modern practice is conservative with aromatic nervines in infants. Treat as [USER: clinician-guided].
- May potentiate sedatives and is mildly diaphoretic — use care alongside CNS depressants or in dehydration.
- Handling the fresh plant or oil can provoke intense behavioural responses in cats.
