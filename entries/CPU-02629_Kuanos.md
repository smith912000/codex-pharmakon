# Kuanos — ΚΥΑΝΟΣ (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 5-106); Latin "cyanus", "Cyanus" as printed in the translation

## *A blue copper-containing mineral product from Cyprus, formed "from brass minerals" and made from shore sand according to Dioscorides; the translator's suggested identification is cyanochroite, a blue hydrous sulphate of copper and potassium. The identification is the translator's and ours is of low to medium confidence: the text gives a colour ("full colour"), a place (Cyprus), a copper link ("brass minerals") and a way of treating it, and nothing that fixes a species. The English name "blue copper frit" in the spine row is a modern convention, not a phrase in the text. [USER: verify]* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** Dioscorides describes this material as corrosive and ulcerating, and the translator's suggested identity is a copper salt. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nothing here is to be swallowed, applied to the body, or handled as a remedy.

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The row began as an unverified residual on the spine (CPU-02629), taken from the name index of the English translation; the chapter was opened and read for this entry. A search of the English text for "cyanus" and "kuanos" found the chapter itself (line 10376), the book index entries (line 12202, "KUANOS 798"; line 15534, "Cyanochroite 798", "Cyanus 372, 798"), and a different plant, the cornflower *Centaurea cyanus* (chapter at line 6582, page 372), which shares the Latin spelling and is a separate entry on the spine (CPU-00656). The two must not be confused: the mineral is in book 5, the metallic stones, and the plant in book 4.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-106, *Kuanos* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading at line 10376, suggested identification at line 10378, chapter text at line 10379; the name-index row is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` row 879, class guess mineral; the list row in `_sources/dioscorides.csv` at line 832 carries a wrong class and a garbled identification and is not relied on). The translator's heading reads "SUGGESTED: Cyanochroite — blue hydrous Sulphate of Copper and Potassium". The whole chapter reads:

"Cyanus, formed from brass minerals, is found in Cyprus. The largest quantity is made from sand at the shore that is found near some hollow places of the sea, like burrows. This is also the best. That which has a full colour must be chosen. It is burnt like chalcitis [calcium carbonate] and washed like cadmia [5-84]. It has the ability to repress, is a little corrosive, forms scabs, and is ulcerating."

Four things are stated. (1) Origin: formed from "brass minerals" and found in Cyprus; the bracketed word "brass" is the translator's rendering of the Greek metal term and the text goes no further. (2) Manufacture: the larger quantity is "made from sand at the shore" near hollows "like burrows" by the sea. That wording makes the best and largest supply a made product, not simply a dug stone, and that is why the spine carries the word "frit" for it; the text does not use that word. (3) Selection: the one to choose is the one "of full colour". The colour itself is not named in the chapter; "blue" comes from the translator's suggestion and from the Latin name. (4) Treatment and action: it is burnt "like chalcitis" and washed "like cadmia [5-84]", and its actions are to repress, a little corrosive, scab-forming, ulcerating.

**Neighbouring chapters.** The chapter sits in the metallic stones of book 5, between 5-105 *Armenion* ("Armenian stone", suggested blue copper carbonate, chosen "smooth, an azure colour, very even, without stones, and very brittle") and 5-107 *Indikon* (indigo). 5-104 *Chrusokolla* (chrysocolla, marked TOXIC in the translation, "gently corrosive and a little biting", one of the medicines that "cause vomiting and are strong enough to kill") is the nearest copper-green neighbour. Those chapters are the context for a reader, not evidence about Kuanos; Dioscorides does not say that Kuanos shares their strength. The cross-reference to chalcitis is to 5-115 (calcium carbonate by the translator's gloss, "like brass" in appearance), whose burning method is given elsewhere in book 5; no burning instructions for chalcitis were read for this entry beyond what 5-115 and 5-116 print.

### Active Components

The text names no constituent. In general terms, if the translator's suggestion is right, the material is a hydrated sulphate of copper and potassium; copper salts of this kind are generally blue to blue-green, soluble in water to some degree, and astringent and irritant to tissue, and soluble copper compounds are toxic if swallowed. These are general statements about copper sulphates, not findings about this product. The text's own description (made from shore sand, "formed from brass minerals") fits a prepared copper-bearing frit or pigment better than a simple mineral, but that reading is ours. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Toxic.**

- **What the text says it does.** It "has the ability to repress", is "a little corrosive", "forms scabs", and "is ulcerating". It names no disease, no part of the body, and no preparation for use on the body.
- **What the text does not say.** No indication, no dose, no vehicle, no duration, and no instruction to take it by mouth.

The Codex does not translate these actions into modern conditions, gives no protocol, and does not recommend this material. A corrosive copper product has no place in a cultivation practice, and the only honest use of this entry is to record that Dioscorides listed it, how he said to pick and prepare it, and that he judged it corrosive.

### Preparation & Dosing

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Copper sulphates and related copper salts are blue or blue-green solids; they are used industrially and in agriculture, are astringent and irritating to skin and mucous membranes, and cause vomiting and organ damage if swallowed in quantity. Cyprus has a long copper-mining history, which is consistent with Dioscorides placing the material there. The "ulcerating" and "scab-forming" actions he records are in kind with the irritant effect of soluble copper salts on tissue. [USER: verify] The identification as cyanochroite is the translator's, and mineral cyanochroite is a rare species, so the ancient product need not have been that mineral. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body or vital system, and nothing in the chapter links this material to the person's constitution, to long life, or to any practice of self-cultivation. The chapter is a craftsman's note on origin, choice and finishing, followed by a short list of harsh actions. What it does offer a cultivator is a discipline of attention: where the material comes from (Cyprus), how to tell the good lot (full colour), and how it is finished (burnt, washed). That is the extent of what the text supports, and it is a discipline of provenance and assay, not a cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** A corrosive copper product recorded for its caustic and scab-forming action. There is no cultivation use, and a cultivator should not handle it, burn it, or take it. Treat the entry as a record of how a first-century materia medica described a mineral drug.

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

Little to map: the text names no body part, organ, tissue or disease. Its actions are given as to repress, to be a little corrosive, to form scabs, and to be ulcerating. In modern terms these describe an effect on surface tissue, such as skin or a mucous lining where a salt would be applied, but the chapter itself places none of them in an organ. The entry records that the chapter gives no quality of the hot, cold, moist or dry kind and no taste, so the classical energetic frame is absent. The only other statement is that the material is burnt and washed in the manner of two other substances, which is a finishing method and not a body claim. No channel, humour or cultivation centre is named, and no part of the body is named as its target.

### Contraindications

- **TOXIC.** The text marks it corrosive and ulcerating; copper salts are toxic if swallowed. Do not ingest it. Do not apply it to broken skin or the eyes.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children, the weak.** No statement in the sources retrieved. Given the toxicity, avoid entirely. [USER: verify]
- **Hazard in preparation.** Burning copper compounds can release irritating fumes and dust. The entry gives no safe method and none should be inferred.
- **Identity.** The translator's identification is a suggestion. Cyanochroite is a rare mineral and a shore-sand product would be a different material; the actual ancient product is not fixed by the text. [USER: verify]
- **Name confusion.** Do not confuse with the cornflower (*Centaurea cyanus*, book 4), which shares the Latin "cyanus".
