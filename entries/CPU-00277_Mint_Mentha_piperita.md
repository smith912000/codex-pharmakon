# Peppermint / Mint — na'na' (Arabic mint) · Greek "mínthē" (classical mint, broad sense)

## *Mentha × piperita* L. | Family: Lamiaceae

### Origin & Tradition

**Greco-Arab (Avicenna / CM):** Mint (*na'na'*) is a staple of the Greco-Arab digestive and aromatic tradition carried through Dioscorides and Avicenna's *Canon* — used as a carminative, stomachic, and cooling aromatic for nausea, weak digestion, and to "settle" the stomach. The classical mint concept was broad and pre-Linnaean; the peppermint we standardise on today (*M. × piperita*, prized for high menthol) is a **post-classical hybrid** that stabilised in cultivation centuries later. So the honest reading is: the *tradition* of medicinal mint is genuinely ancient and Avicennan; *this particular high-menthol cultivar* is the modern pharmacological workhorse that inherits, rather than literally is, that tradition.

**Chinese (parallel, not the same species):** *Bò he* (*Mentha haplocalyx*) plays the cool, wind-heat-dispersing, Liver-coursing role in TCM — a convergent use of a mint on cooling/dispersing indications.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Cooling Breath:** The Codex reads mint as the archetypal **coolant that also relaxes**. Menthol produces a genuine cold sensation (TRPM8 agonism) without lowering temperature — a doctrinal image of *apparent* cold that is really a change in signalling, not substance. Its smooth-muscle relaxant action makes it the settle-agent par excellence: the breath that cools the head and unclenches the gut. Where warming drugs carry fire (see Mugwort, I_329), mint is their polar `[RESET]` — the exhale that releases spasm.

### Active Components

- **Menthol** — dominant monoterpene alcohol; TRPM8 (cold-receptor) agonist and smooth-muscle relaxant (acts partly via calcium-channel blockade) — the basis of the antispasmodic/carminative action.
- **Menthone, menthyl acetate, 1,8-cineole, limonene** — supporting volatile fraction.
- **Rosmarinic acid and flavonoids** — antioxidant/anti-inflammatory phenolics in the leaf.
- **Peppermint essential oil** — the concentrated therapeutic fraction used in IBS trials, typically enteric-coated for targeted release.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Positioned as a `[RESET]` gut-settling / cooling-dispersing agent. Its strongest evidence base is **antispasmodic relief of IBS symptoms and abdominal pain** via enteric-coated peppermint oil. Secondary uses: carminative for functional dyspepsia/nausea (traditional), aromatic cooling for the head. Kenetica frames peppermint oil as a **first-line-adjacent, low-risk antispasmodic** for IBS-type gut symptoms, with the caveat that adverse events (notably reflux) are real.

### Scientific Correlation

Attribution: findings from PubMed.

- **Meta-analysis — efficacy in IBS.** In 10 RCTs (1030 patients), peppermint oil beat placebo for global IBS symptoms (RR of not improving 0.65, 95% CI 0.43–0.98; NNT 4) and abdominal pain (RR 0.76, 95% CI 0.62–0.93; NNT 7), but adverse events were more frequent (RR 1.57, 95% CI 1.04–2.37) and the authors rated quality of evidence as very low (Ingrosso et al., 2022, *Aliment Pharmacol Ther*) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/apt.17179).
- **Large RCT — mixed/nuanced.** A double-blind trial (n=190, Rome IV IBS) found neither small-intestinal- nor ileocolonic-release peppermint oil met the primary FDA/EMA endpoints for abdominal-pain response or overall relief; however, small-intestinal-release oil significantly improved secondary measures of abdominal pain, discomfort, and IBS severity, with more (mild) adverse events (Weerts et al., 2019, *Gastroenterology*) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.gastro.2019.08.026).

**Evidence grade:** IBS symptom relief — **clinical (RCT/meta-analysis), but evidence quality is low and endpoint-dependent.** Net: a real but modest, imperfectly consistent antispasmodic effect. Carminative/aromatic uses — traditional.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM tier (analogue *bò he*):** cool, acrid, aromatic; LUNG + LIVER. Actions: disperse wind-heat, clear head/eyes/throat, course Liver qi, vent rashes.
- **Greco-Arab (Avicenna):** cooling, carminative, stomachic.
- **Dantian:** middle (gut-settling) and upper (head-cooling).

### Contraindications

- **GERD / reflux / hiatus hernia** — menthol relaxes the lower oesophageal sphincter; reflux is the most reported adverse event (Ingrosso 2022, Weerts 2019). Enteric coating mitigates but does not eliminate.
- **Infants and young children** — menthol can cause laryngospasm/apnoea; avoid neat oil near the face of infants.
- **G6PD deficiency** — caution with menthol.
- **Bile-duct obstruction / severe hepatic impairment** — caution.
- **Pregnancy/lactation** — culinary amounts fine; therapeutic oil doses `[USER: verify]`.
- **Interactions** — as a CYP substrate/inhibitor in vitro, high-dose oil may theoretically affect co-administered drugs; clinically minor at food doses.
