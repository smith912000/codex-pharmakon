# Anemarrhena asphodeloides — Zhi Mu 知母 (rhizome) (Shennong Bencao Jing)

## *Anemarrhena asphodeloides, as the pharmaceutical Latin "Rhizoma Anemarrhenae Asphodeloidis" that Yang 1998 prints for the entry Zhi Mu* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Shennong text (SBJ).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The entry is in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`). The Latin name is the translator's pharmaceutical Latin and was matched to the binomial by stem in the extraction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv`, row 113). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Chinese characters are those of the Codex's own list of Shennong names (`_sources/shennong.csv`, line 151: "Anemarrhena Rhizome (知母 Zhī Mǔ)"), not of Yang's English text. [USER: verify]

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *Zhi Mu, PDF page 70.* The page marker `[[p70]]` is at line 2637 and the entry is at line 2646. No class heading is printed on this page. The governing heading is "Herbs: Middle Class" at line 2592 on the page marked `[[p69]]` (line 2591), and the next heading, "Herbs: Middle Class" again, is at line 2665 on `[[p71]]` (line 2664). The entry therefore stands under the middle-class heading by position (fifty-four lines and one page break below it), not directly beneath it. The text reads (lines 2646 to 2653):

"Zhi Mu (Rhizoma Anemarrhenae Asphodeloidis)156 is bitter and cold. It is nontoxic, mainly treating wasting thirst and heat in the center. It eliminates evil qi [in the treatment of] puffy swelling of the limbs, precipitates water, supplements insufficiency, and boosts the qi. Its other name is Chi Mu (Ant Egg Mother). Another name is Lian Mu (Linking Mother). It is also called Ye Liao (Wild Water Weed), Di Shen (Earth Ginseng), Shui Shen (Water Ginseng), Shui fun (Water Dredger), BUD Mu (Goods Matrix), and Ti Mu (Cicada Mother). It ~ows in rivers and valleys."

The scan has damaged three places in this passage ("Shui fun", "BUD Mu" and "~ows"), which are quoted as printed in the text file, not corrected. The names "Di Shen" and "Shui Shen" are names the text prints; the text does not say they relate this plant to any other. [USER: verify]

Yang's note 156 (lines 2659 to 2662) is the translator's modern commentary and not part of the classic: "Anemarrhena drains fire through disinhibiting urination. Therefore, it treats wasting thirst, vexatious fever, alternating cold and heat, etc. It is a well known yin-boosting medicinal, but it should not be taken as a yin-supplementing one because taking too much of it may actually damage yin." The note puts these actions and this warning in the present tense and does not say they are the classic's.

*A name that recurs.* The same Romanised name "Zhi Mu" is printed as an "other name" of a different entry (line 2391, on the page marked `[[p63]]`: "Its other name is Zhi Mu (Gratitude to Mother)"). That is not this plant, and the Codex does not treat it as one.

*The class headings.* Yang's table of contents prints "Herbs: Middle Class" (line 716). The preface (lines 385 to 390) says of the middle class: "There are 120 medium class medicinals which are used as ministers. They mainly nurture personality and correspond to humanity. They may or may not be toxic, and [therefore,] one should weigh and ponder before putting them to their appropriate use. If one intends to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion, one should base [one's efforts] on the middle class." Yang's commentary (lines 358 to 362) says that middle-class medicinals "are able to cultivate personality or modify temperament" and that "Since this class of medicinals may be toxic, their prescription requires care."

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Anemarrhena*, *Zhi Mu* and *asphodeloides* found no entry on this plant. It is named only in formula prose or cross-references of three entries: I_092 (*Dioscorea*, Shan Yao, line 33, in the Zhi Bai Di Huang Wan formula), I_343 (Gypsum) and I_345 (Salt). None is an entry on Zhi Mu.

### Active Components

General composition, stated without numbers: *Anemarrhena asphodeloides* is a plant of the asparagus family and is generally known to be rich in saponins. This is a general statement about the genus and not about the text's Zhi Mu. The composition of the rhizome as the Shennong text meant it was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Shennong text lists the entry's actions in its own terms (wasting thirst, heat in the center, puffy swelling of the limbs, precipitating water, supplementing insufficiency, boosting the qi), which are not modern diagnoses. These are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The translator's note adds a warning against taking too much. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: the middle class (中品), by position.* The class is assigned by the printed heading "Herbs: Middle Class" (line 2592, `[[p69]]`), repeated at the top of the next page (line 2665, `[[p71]]`). The entry on `[[p70]]` has no heading directly above it, so the grade rests on its position between the two. This is a printed class, and not a promise of cultivation use. In the preface (lines 385 to 390) the middle class mainly "nurture[s] personality", may or may not be toxic, and is the class on which one bases the effort "to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion". It is not the class the preface ties to making the body light and prolonging life (lines 352 to 355, 383 to 384), which is the superior class.

The entry's own words fit that frame and go no further: "supplements insufficiency, and boosts the qi" are recorded in the text's terms, and the entry says nothing of protracted taking, of making the body light or of prolonging life. It says it is "nontoxic". The entry asserts no *jing*, *shen* or *neidan* action, and none is added here.

**Practitioner's note.** A middle-class entry in the Chinese scheme: for controlling disease and supplementing, weighed before use, and not the class of long-term life-nourishing drugs. The entry does not promise a cultivation outcome, and the translator's own note warns that too much may damage yin. Nothing here supports taking this rhizome as a cultivation practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The Shennong entry prints "It is nontoxic" and no other warning. The preface says of the middle class generally that it "may or may not be toxic" and calls for weighing before use. Yang's note 156 says that "taking too much of it may actually damage yin". The identity rests on a translator's Latin name, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Wasting thirst and swelling need medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
