### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The English text on disk is C. P. Bryan's 1930 *The Papyrus Ebers*, a translation with summaries (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`); the Egyptian words are not printed. The spine's list row is `_sources/ebers.csv` line 96 ("Alabaster", id uncertain: yes), citing "Ghalioungui 1987 trans., Glossary I (Medicaments)", which is not on disk and was not read. The names row is `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` line 2: "Bryan 1930, Ch. V Mineral Remedies (line 1137)", forms listed "Alabaster | Meal-of-Alabaster | Dust-of-Alabaster". Bryan's Chapter V prints them in his alphabetical list of mineral remedies (line 1137). His own sentence before the list: "few of them are prescribed in their crude or native state", and "the source of origin of the drugs is strictly laid down" (for example "Soot-from-the-bet'a-pot"). The prescriptions found by searching the text for "Alabaster" are these. (1) "Remedy to drive away the wheals from blows" (line 2385: honey, cow's brain, mason's clay, linseed-water, date-juice, "Cook, and apply as a plaster"), and its "Another": "Dust-of-Alabaster, Dust-of-a-Statue, Granite, Fresh Milk. Anoint therewith" (line 2391). (2) "To charm away alopecia" (line 4069), which begins with an invocation as Bryan prints it, "O Shining One, Thou who hoverest above! O Xare! O Disc of the Sun! O Protector of the Divine Neb-apt!", then "To be spoken over: Iron, Red-lead, Onions, Alabaster, Honey. Make into one and give against" (lines 4069-4072). Bryan's comment (line 4073) is that "we have long discarded the Red-lead, Onions, Alabaster, and Honey, as of doubtful benefit." (3) In the cosmetics section, a face remedy: "Water-from-the-qebu-plant, Meal-of-Alabaster, Fresh abt-grain. Mix in Honey, make into a pap, mix in Human Milk, and anoint the face therewith" (about lines 4107-4112). (4) "Remedy to beautify the skin": "Meal-of-Alabaster, Meal-of-Natron, Sea-salt, Honey. Mix into one in this Honey and anoint the body therewith" (lines 4141-4143). The Ebers uses are therefore: a dust for blows, a mixed medicine spoken over for hair, and two cosmetics. Bryan's English "Alabaster" is his identification; whether the Egyptian word means the stone known today as alabaster is a modern hypothesis. [USER: verify]

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 5-153, *Alabastrites Lithos*, "Alabaster — Lime Sulphate", "Onyx-banded quartz similar to Agate" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10630-10633; the index row is `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 879). The whole chapter: "Alabastrites stone (which is called onyx) used burnt with rosin or pitch dissolves hard lumps. With stiff ointment it eases disorders of the stomach, and it is therapeutic for the gums." The chapter gives no source, quality grade, or dose. The neighbouring chapters are 5-152 *Morochthos* and 5-154 *Thuites*.

### Active Components

Alabaster is a fine-grained, translucent, banded stone. The soft stone sold under this name today is mostly gypsum (hydrated calcium sulphate), and the harder banded calcium carbonate, onyx marble, is also called "alabaster" in the older literature; the translator's two glosses above name both. This entry does not decide which stone either text meant. Burning gypsum drives off water and gives a plaster-like powder; burning calcium carbonate gives quicklime. The sources name no active component and give no quantities.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The texts record alabaster as a ground stone in plasters, anointings, a pap for the face, and a spoken-over mixture for hair; and, in Greece, as a burnt stone in ointments for hard lumps, stomach disorders and the gums. The Codex has no protocol for any of this and offers none. Ground stone in the mouth or on the skin has no verified benefit in anything read here; burnt stone can be caustic; and Bryan himself calls the alopecia remedy "of doubtful benefit".

### Preparation & Dosing

Egyptian: the dust or meal of alabaster mixed with dust of a statue, granite and milk and "anointed"; with iron, red-lead, onions and honey "made into one" and given (the alopecia charm); with water of the qebu-plant, abt-grain, honey and human milk as a face pap; with natron meal, sea-salt and honey as a body salve. Greek: alabastrites "burnt with rosin or pitch" for hard lumps, "with stiff ointment" for the stomach and for the gums (5-153). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Gypsum (calcium sulphate) and calcium carbonate are chemically ordinary minerals; calcium carbonate is an antacid, and gypsum is used in plasters and casts. Fine mineral dust is a hazard to the lungs when breathed in. Nothing in the sources read ties alabaster to a healing effect on wheals, hair, skin or gums. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Alabaster is an ingredient for a complaint or a cosmetic; the prescriptions read say nothing about what it does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. The Ebers material is indication-organised: the identity of each named substance is a modern hypothesis, and the English forms are Bryan's. One prescription is a spell and a drug together. In the alopecia charm (line 4069) the invocation is addressed, as Bryan prints it, to "O Disc of the Sun" and others; the mixture of iron, red-lead, onions, alabaster and honey is "to be spoken over". That is the pattern of *heka*: spell and drug as one intervention, so that dropping the recitation loses half the prescription. It is the only prescription for alabaster in which Bryan prints words to be spoken; the others read give none, and since he summarises, that is not proof that none existed. No deity is identified here, and none is claimed. [USER: verify]

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. The chapter records no amuletic use. What it offers a cultivator is a small discipline of attention: the stone's name and the other name it carried ("which is called onyx"), and the preparation (burnt, with rosin or pitch, or in stiff ointment). The chapter is therapeutic and assigns the stone no cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do. Alabaster is a stone used in ancient plasters and cosmetics, and it earns its place in the Codex as a worked case of Egyptian drug-and-spell treatment (the alopecia charm), not as something to apply or to take. A cultivator can take from it the point that one prescription is incomplete without its recitation, and leave the stone alone.

### Contraindications

- **Dust.** Fine stone dust is irritating to eyes and lungs; avoid breathing it in.
- **Burnt stone.** Burnt calcium carbonate is quicklime and burnt gypsum is plaster; both can burn or set on moist skin and mucous membranes and should never be put in the mouth, eyes or on open skin.
- **Ingestion.** Ground stone should not be swallowed; the Egyptian alopecia mixture is "given" (taken) per Bryan's text, and this is not advised.
- **Pregnancy and children.** No safety data from the sources read. [USER: verify]
- **Dose unknown.** No dose is given for any use in the sources read.
- **Heritage and law.** Worked alabaster is cultural property in some places, and quarrying or collecting may be regulated. [USER: verify]
