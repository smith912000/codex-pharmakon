# Paliurus aculeatus — Paliouros (Dioscorides 1-121)

## *Paliurus aculeatus, the first of four binomials on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 1-121 (which also prints Paliurus australis, Paliurus spina-Christi and Rhamnus paliurus — "Christ's Thorn, Jerusalem Thorn, Garland Thorn"); a prickly shrub* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter, and no other tradition on disk is tagged for it. The binomials are the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. Four names are printed and the row takes the first. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *1-121. PALIOUROS*, heading at line 4433, the SUGGESTED line at lines 4434 to 4435 and the text at line 4436, in Book One among the trees (the running head near it reads "GUMS from TREES"). The whole chapter is one paragraph:

"Paliurus is a well-known shrub, prickly and strong. The fat seed is found blackened with fungus. Taken in a drink it helps coughs, breaks stones [urinary] in the bladder, and is good for the stings of snakes. The leaves and root are therefore astringent; a decoction of them (taken as a drink) regulates the bowels, draws out urine, and is good against poison and the bites of venomous beasts. The root pounded into small pieces and applied as a plaster dissolves all new tubercula [nodules] and oedema."

Three things are worth stating about what the paragraph does and does not say. First, the description is two phrases long: a "well-known shrub, prickly and strong", and a seed that is "fat" and "found blackened with fungus". It gives no leaf, flower, fruit colour, height or habitat, and it relies on the reader already knowing the plant. The identification therefore rests almost entirely on the name and on the translators' suggestion. Second, the one quality printed is astringency, and it is attached to the leaves and root: the word "therefore" in "The leaves and root are therefore astringent" is Dioscorides' (in the translation) and links the quality to the uses that follow. Third, the chapter gives no names in other languages, no synonym list and no "the Magi call it" sentence, unlike many of the later chapters. No deity and no amuletic use is named.

**The chapters around it.** The chapter after it is 1-122 *OXUAKANTHA* (line 4437), a prickly tree with a red fruit; it is a separate chapter and the Codex does not carry its statements over to 1-121.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter id 1-121 and for the names *paliurus*, *paliouros* and "Christ's Thorn" found no existing entry on the same chapter. Two Ziziphus entries share the English name "Christ's Thorn" only in a different sense: I_675 (*Christ's Thorn Jujube, Ziziphus spina-christi*) and I_692 (*Lotus Jujube, Ziziphus lotus*, which refers to I_675). Neither cites chapter 1-121, and *Ziziphus* is a different genus from *Paliurus*; they are not duplicates, though the shared English name could cause confusion. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The composition of the shrub Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about a particular species. As a general fact, *Paliurus* belongs to the buckthorn family (Rhamnaceae), a family of often thorny shrubs, and the astringency Dioscorides records is the kind of taste that tannin-bearing plant parts commonly give. No constituent is named in the chapter. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records the seed drunk for coughs, for the stone in the bladder and against snake stings; a decoction of leaves and root drunk to regulate the bowels, to draw out urine and against poison and venomous bites; and the root applied as a plaster to dissolve new nodules and swelling. The Codex gives no protocol for any of these. The plant cannot be identified with confidence from the chapter alone, and no one should gather or take a thorny shrub on the strength of a two-line description and a translators' suggestion.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives three preparations, with locus (line 4436): the seed "taken in a drink"; a decoction of the leaves and root "taken as a drink"; and the root "pounded into small pieces and applied as a plaster". No dose, no volume and no duration is given for any of them. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that astringent plant preparations act on tissues through tannins and similar compounds, and that a herbal drink does not dissolve bladder stones or neutralise snake venom; snakebite and urinary stones need medical care. The identification is a translators' suggestion, so the chapter does not show that the plant it describes is *Paliurus aculeatus*.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one quality: "The leaves and root are therefore astringent". It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality for 1-121 and no degree.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The cultivation spec says that what he offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, adulteration detection. This chapter gives very little of that. It names no place, no season of gathering, no test of quality and no sign of adulteration; its one observation of the material is that the seed is found "blackened with fungus". The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a medicinal shrub, and the text records no amuletic use. No cultivation framing is attested.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this shrub. The only thing a student can take from it is the habit of noting the condition of a seed (here, blackened with fungus) before judging a material. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Urinary stones, snakebite and poisoning are conditions for urgent medical care, not for a thorny shrub's seed or root. The chapter's uses to "draw out urine" and to regulate the bowels are not something to attempt on this identification. The plant is spiny, so handling it risks puncture wounds. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion among four binomials, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
