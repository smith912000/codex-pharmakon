# Liquorice — *Glycyrrhiza glabra* L.

**Family:** Fabaceae | **Class:** plant | **Volume:** I · Plant | **Codex id:** CPU-00060
**Attested in:** Dioscorides · Avicenna · Sushruta · Ebers Papyrus · Assyrian tablets

---

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

*Glycyrrhiza glabra* L. is a perennial herbaceous legume native to the Mediterranean basin, western and central Asia, and northern Africa. The genus name derives from the Greek *glukyrrhiza* — "sweet root" — coined by Theophrastus and formalised by Dioscorides. The English "liquorice" reaches back through Anglo-Norman *licoresse* and Medieval Latin *liquiritia*, itself a corruption of *glycyrrhiza*. Synonyms in use across traditions include: *sus al-sus* (Arabic/Persian), *yashtimadhu* or *madhuka* (Sanskrit), *zhi gan cao* (Chinese, for the prepared form of *G. uralensis*, the closest congener).

The medicinally active material is the dried root and stolon. The plant reaches 1–1.5 m in height, bearing pinnate leaves and pale violet flowers. Commercial cultivation is concentrated in Spain, Italy, Turkey, Iran, Afghanistan, and China. The species should be distinguished from *G. uralensis* Fisch. (Chinese licorice) and *G. inflata* Bat., though all three share the principal active glycoside, glycyrrhizin.

## 2. Cross-Traditional Presence

### (a) Assyrian Tablets (c. 2000 BCE)
Liquorice appears in cuneiform pharmacopoeias from Mesopotamia, documented by Thompson (1930) as a constituent of prescriptions for bruising, swelling, and systemic illness. [1] This represents one of the earliest written attestations of any single plant substance in a therapeutic context. The Assyrian material frames the root as a cooling, demulcent agent, compounded with other plant and mineral ingredients. Specific tablet numbers and transliteration remain a specialist domain; the claim of Assyrian attestation rests on Thompson's 1930 philological work and subsequent secondary synthesis by Fiore et al. (2005). [1]

### (b) Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE)
The Ebers Papyrus, an Egyptian hieratic medical text of 110 pages comprising some 842 prescriptions and remedies, includes liquorice among polyherbal preparations for internal conditions. [2] The Egyptian medical tradition, as synthesised in this document, employs liquorice in compound formulae rather than as a simple. The framing is empirical-magical in character; specific indications attributed to the papyrus include preparations for the stomach and chest, consistent with the demulcent properties recognised across traditions. [1]

### (c) Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica* (c. 50–70 CE)
Pedanius Dioscorides, a Greek physician in the Roman army, catalogued liquorice systematically in his five-volume *De Materia Medica*. He attributes to the root the power to relieve "burning of the stomach, disorders in the chest and liver, diseases of the bladder and kidney." He notes that the root, melted in the mouth with a draught of *passum* (raisin wine), quenches thirst; applied topically, it heals wounds; and chewed, it benefits the stomach. [3] Dioscorides is also credited with codifying the name *Glycyrrhiza* from Greek roots. His text dominated European pharmacopoeia for more than 1,500 years.

### (d) Avicenna (Ibn Sina), *Al-Qanun fi al-Tibb* (Canon of Medicine, c. 1025 CE)
Ibn Sina cites liquorice (*sus al-sus*) for diseases of the kidneys, bladder, and respiratory tract. He records — as later relayed by Ibn al-Baytar — that it "makes the voice clear and reduces hoarseness." [1] The Canon situates liquorice within a humoral framework (see §9), classing it as moist and temperate, suitable for conditions of dryness and heat. Respiratory and renal indications in Avicenna's account closely parallel those of Dioscorides, a convergence noted by Fiore et al. (2005) as indicative of shared pharmacological reality rather than mere textual borrowing. [1]

### (e) Sushruta Samhita & Charaka Samhita (Ayurvedic tradition, c. 1st–2nd century CE)
In Ayurvedic *dravyaguna* (materia medica), liquorice is known principally as *Yashtimadhu* ("sweet stick"; *yashti* = stick, *madhu* = honey/sweet) and sometimes as *Madhuka*. It is classified by rasa (taste: madhura/sweet), guna (qualities: guru/heavy, snigdha/unctuous), virya (potency: sheeta/cooling), and vipaka (post-digestive taste: madhura). Both the Charaka and Sushruta Samhitas list it among herbal *rasayanas* — substances that promote longevity, strengthen *ojas* (vital essence), and support *agni* (digestive fire). [4][5] Primary classical indications include Vata disorders, cough, sore throat, peptic complaints, and skin inflammation. The Charaka Samhita includes *yashtimadhu* in formulations for *kasa* (cough) and *svasa* (dyspnoea), making the respiratory convergence with Dioscorides and Avicenna explicit.

## 3. Convergence Analysis

Across five independent traditions separated by geography and epistemology, three therapeutic domains recur with notable consistency: (1) respiratory demulcency and voice clarity, (2) gastric and mucosal protection, and (3) renal and urinary tract soothing. Fiore et al. (2005) treat this cross-cultural convergence as evidence of reproducible pharmacological effect rather than textual diffusion, given that the Ayurvedic and Mesopotamian records pre-date Greek systematisation. [1] The convergence on adrenal-adjacent and fluid-regulatory effects — not articulated in any pre-modern tradition but now explicable through glycyrrhizin's inhibition of cortisol catabolism (§5) — is a post-hoc phytochemical coherence, not a pre-modern claim.

---

## 4. Phytochemistry & Composition

Approximately 400 compounds have been isolated from *G. glabra* roots, of which around 300 are flavonoid-class molecules. [6] Principal bioactive constituents:

- **Glycyrrhizin** (glycyrrhizinic acid): a triterpenoid saponin glycoside constituting approximately 2–25% of root dry weight (mean c. 10%); approximately 50 times sweeter than sucrose. [7] It is the primary compound responsible for both therapeutic and adverse adrenal-mineralocorticoid effects.
- **18β-Glycyrrhetinic acid** (glycyrrhetinic acid, GA): the active aglycone metabolite of glycyrrhizin, generated by intestinal microbial hydrolysis. Poorly absorbed as intact glycyrrhizin; GA is the systemically bioavailable form. [7]
- **Glabridin**: the principal isoflavone, constituting 0.08–0.35% of dry root weight. Demonstrates antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and GABA-modulatory activity. [7]
- **Liquiritigenin, isoliquiritigenin, liquiritin, isoliquiritin**: chalcone and flavanone glycosides with demonstrated estrogenic receptor activity (liquiritigenin) and anti-inflammatory effects.
- **Licochalcones A, B, D, E**: prenylated chalcones with antiviral and antimicrobial properties.
- **Coumarins and volatile oils**: minor constituents.

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**Anti-inflammatory.** Glycyrrhizin inhibits phospholipase A2 and suppresses prostaglandin E2 and leukotriene synthesis. It also inhibits HMGB1 (high-mobility group box 1 protein), a late mediator of systemic inflammation. [7]

**Gastroprotective / mucosal.** Glycyrrhizin raises endogenous prostaglandin concentrations in the gastric mucosa, stimulating mucus secretion, stabilising surface epithelial cells, and inhibiting acid hypersecretion. Carbenoxolone, a hemisuccinate derivative of glycyrrhetinic acid, was the first synthetic exploitation of this mechanism, demonstrating endoscopic ulcer healing in clinical trials from the 1960s onward (see §6). [8]

**Pseudo-aldosteronism mechanism.** Glycyrrhetinic acid competitively inhibits 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11β-HSD2), the enzyme that converts cortisol to the inactive cortisone in renal tubular cells. Accumulation of cortisol at mineralocorticoid receptors produces sodium retention, potassium excretion, hypertension, and hypokalemia — the pseudoaldosteronism syndrome — at high or prolonged doses. [9]

**Antiviral.** Glycyrrhizin interferes with viral membrane binding and inhibits viral replication by modulating HMGB1-DNA interactions. In vitro activity demonstrated against influenza, HSV, SARS-CoV, and HIV-1 replication, though clinical translation remains limited. [7]

**GABA modulation.** Glabridin potentiates GABA-A receptor activity, producing anxiolytic-adjacent sedative effects. [7]

## 6. Clinical Evidence

Evidence quality is uneven. The most robust signal is for GI mucosal protection; other indications rest on small trials or in vitro data.

**Peptic ulcer / GI mucosa (moderate evidence).** Carbenoxolone, derived from glycyrrhetinic acid, demonstrated statistically significant endoscopic healing of gastric and duodenal ulcers in controlled trials; a duodenal ulcer trial found complete healing in 14 of 21 carbenoxolone patients versus 7 of 23 placebo patients. [8] Deglycyrrhizinated liquorice (DGL) — produced by removing glycyrrhizin to eliminate systemic risk — has shown benefit in peptic ulcer models, though evidence for DGL specifically is less uniform. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis (Efficacy of *Glycyrrhiza glabra* on peptic ulcer disease, *Journal of Ethnopharmacology*-adjacent source) examined available RCT data; high-quality independent replication remains needed. [10]

**Oral mucositis (moderate evidence, single RCT).** A randomised clinical trial found that topical *G. glabra* extract significantly reduced oral mucositis severity in patients undergoing head and neck radiotherapy. [11]

**Hepatitis / liver protection (preliminary).** Intravenous glycyrrhizin (doses ranging 80–1,600 mg/day) has been studied in chronic hepatitis C with some evidence of biochemical improvement; evidence is insufficient for practice recommendation without further RCT data. [7]

**Pseudoaldosteronism / hypertension risk (well-documented harm).** This is among the best-characterised adverse effects of any herbal preparation in the literature. Clinical presentation includes peripheral oedema, hypertension, hypokalemia, and suppressed plasma renin activity. Risk is dose- and duration-dependent; onset has been observed with as little as 6 g liquorice root daily over 30 days in susceptible individuals. [9] The effect is fully reversible on cessation. Risk factors include: older age, female sex, baseline hypertension, hypoalbuminaemia, constipation, and concurrent potassium-wasting diuretics. [9]

## 7. Phenomenological Record

Historical accounts — most prominently in Dioscorides and Avicenna — describe a soothing, sweetening, and voice-clearing action experienced as immediate and local. Chewing the root was reported to quench thirst and diminish a sense of gastric burning. The Ayurvedic tradition emphasises a cooling, unctuous quality that is subjectively perceived as calming to inflamed or dry mucous membranes. No psychoactive or significantly altered-state phenomenology is recorded in any tradition; the experiential register is demulcent and restorative rather than entheogenic.

## 8. Practical Application

**Forms.** Dried root (chewed or decocted); standardised extract (solid or liquid, typically standardised to glycyrrhizin content); DGL (deglycyrrhizinated liquorice) tablets or powder; topical preparations.

**Indicative dosing.** The European Scientific Cooperative on Phytotherapy (ESCOP) and similar bodies generally permit up to 5–15 g dried root per day for short-term use in gastric complaints. DGL is used at 380–1,140 mg per dose before meals for GI applications, with the glycyrrhizin-free profile mitigating systemic risk.

**Glycyrrhizin safety ceiling.** The Dutch Nutrition Information Bureau (DNIB) sets a conservative upper limit of 100 mg glycyrrhizin per day; the Scientific Committee on Food (SCF) of the European Commission set 200 mg/day as the threshold above which mineralocorticoid effects become probable. [9] A dose of 2 mg/kg body weight per day of glycyrrhizic acid did not produce measurable aldosterone or blood pressure effects in healthy volunteer studies. [9] Duration matters: Japanese Kampo guidelines contraindicate preparations containing ≥2.5 g liquorice root per day in patients with aldosteronism, myopathy, or existing hypokalemia. [9]

**Contraindications and interactions.** Hypertension; hypokalaemia; cardiac arrhythmia; chronic renal or hepatic impairment; concurrent diuretics (especially thiazides and loop diuretics); corticosteroids (additive mineralocorticoid effect); digoxin (hypokalaemia potentiates toxicity). Pregnancy: avoid therapeutic doses. Carbenoxolone is no longer widely prescribed due to its mineralocorticoid side-effect burden.

## 9. Spiritual & Esoteric Layer

Within the Galenic-Islamic humoral framework, liquorice is classified as moist and temperate in the second degree, suited to constitutions and conditions characterised by dryness and bilious or choleric excess. Avicenna's Canon attributes its benefit in chest and throat complaints to its capacity to moderate dry heat in the respiratory passages — a humoral rationale that maps, with notable coherence, onto the demulcent and anti-inflammatory mechanisms now established pharmacologically. [1]

In Ayurvedic cosmology, *Yashtimadhu* is classed as a *rasayana* — one of a category of substances understood to renew the bodily tissues (*dhatus*), enhance *ojas* (the subtle essence of vitality and immunity), and extend healthy lifespan. [4] Its *sheeta* (cooling) *virya* and *madhura* (sweet) rasa make it a harmoniser of *Pitta* excess and a nourisher of *Vata*-depleted tissues. It appears in Charaka's formularies for mental fatigue and as a voice-preserving herb for those whose practice requires sustained speech — a detail preserved in Avicenna's account across traditions. These framings are presented as the interpretive categories of their respective traditions, not as empirical claims.

---

## 10. Functional–Anatomical Correlate

*Glycyrrhiza glabra* exerts its principal documented effects across three functional systems:

**Adrenal / endocrine axis.** The inhibition of 11β-HSD2 by glycyrrhetinic acid constitutes a pharmacological modulation of cortisol-cortisone interconversion, producing effects that mimic mild hyperaldosteronism. This is both the plant's most dangerous liability at high dose and — at low physiological doses — a proposed mechanism for its tonic and adaptogenic reputation in traditional contexts.

**Gastrointestinal mucosa.** Prostaglandin-mediated mucus stimulation, anti-inflammatory inhibition of COX-derived mediators, and direct surface demulcency confer well-documented protective and healing actions on the gastric and intestinal epithelium.

**Respiratory epithelium.** Expectorant, anti-inflammatory, and demulcent actions on bronchial and pharyngeal mucosa account for the universally attested indication in cough, dysphonia, and chest complaints.
