# Kiang (wild ass) — *Kyang* (Men-Tsee-Khang glossary transliteration); "wild ass", *Equus kiang* (Men-Tsee-Khang) / *Equus hemionus* (Clark)
## *The wild ass of the Tibetan plateau, as a meat in the Tibetan materia medica; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints "Wild Ass" with* Equus kiang, *and Clark's* Quintessence Tantras *prints "wild ass (Equus hemionus)"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The spine row rests on the glossary of animals in the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`; `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, "Equus kiang", line 404, citing glossary line 7001). In that glossary the English column prints "Wild Ass" first (line 6995) and the transliteration column "Kyang" first (line 6975), with the Latin *Equus kiang* at line 7001; the OCR splits the columns, so the pairing is by order, and names.csv records that no Tibetan name is paired to the row.

*In the meat classification.* Explanatory Tantra ch.16 (principles of diet) sorts meats into nine sections (Clark 1995, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, lines 659-672). Section (d) is "Larger hill and game animals include saiga antelope ..., spotted deer ..., wild goat, wild boar, buffalo, rhinoceros, bamboo forest tiger ..., wild ass (Equus hemionus), wild yak (takin) and wild dzo" (Clark line 672). Men-Tsee-Khang's list reads "The goral, spotted deer, Himalayan tahr, boar, buffalo, black rhinoceros, wild ass, wild yak and wild Zho are the group of larger herbivorous wild animals" (lines 2907-2911). Meats in general "are sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste" (Clark line 673).

*On the flesh.* "The rump meats of horse, wild ass and donkey dry up pus and eliminate disorders of the kidneys and waist, as well as cold disorders and lymphatic ailments" (Clark line 674). Men-Tsee-Khang: "The meat of animals having rounded hoof like the horse, wild ass, donkey etc., stops pus formation, relieves cold disorders of the kidneys and waist, and helps against chhuser disorders" (line 2931). These are the only two places in the files read where the wild ass is named in the text of the Tantra; searches of Clark and of Men-Tsee-Khang for "wild ass" and "kiang" returned the lines above, the glossary rows and Clark's index entries ("Equus hemionus", line 1583; "Wild ass", line 1661), and no entry for the wild ass in the chapter 20 lists of animal products.

**Identity.** The two translations print different Latin names for "wild ass": *Equus hemionus* (Clark) and *Equus kiang* (Men-Tsee-Khang). The Tibetan text is not in the files read, and the passages give no description of the animal. Which wild ass the Tantra means, and whether the kiang is treated as a species or a kind of *hemionus* here, is not settled. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Wild equid meat is lean red meat: mostly water and muscle protein with little fat, and a source of iron, zinc and B vitamins. No analysis was read for the Tibetan materia medica item, and the species is not settled. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts name the wild ass's rump meat in a sentence with the horse and donkey, as drying pus and relieving cold disorders of the kidneys and waist. The Codex gives no protocol. The kiang lives in the wild on the plateau; hunting and trade in wild animals are regulated or prohibited in many countries; and there is no sourced preparation or dose. A reader who eats no meat has nothing to adopt.

### Preparation & Dosing

None is given for the wild ass beyond "rump meat" and "meat" in the two sentences. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Wild game can carry zoonotic infections and parasites, and equid meat can carry *Trichinella*; thorough cooking with hygienic handling is the standard precaution. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Printed qualities: all meats have a sweet taste and a sweet post-digestive taste (Clark line 673; Men-Tsee-Khang line 2923). For the rump meat of horse, wild ass and donkey: dries pus, removes kidney and waist disorders, cold disorders and lymphatic disorders (Clark line 674); the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence adds that the meat stops pus formation and helps against *chhuser* disorders. The wild ass is in the group of larger hill and game animals. The Tantra's separate qualities for dry-land, wet-place and both groups depend on group order, and one group line is unreadable in the OCR (Men-Tsee-Khang line 2915), so the animal's place in that scheme is not asserted. ### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The wild ass is a meat in the Tantra's diet chapter, in a sentence it shares with horse and donkey. Clark's introduction says the three humours are "rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration respectively" (line 51), so disease is not only bodily in this tradition; but nothing says the wild ass treats that root. The essence-extraction (*bcud len*) compounds Clark's introduction mentions are made from plants, flowers, calcite and mineral exudate (line 69), not from game meat. There is no category of substances for subtle-body practice in this tradition, and none is claimed.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice. Wild ass is one name in a list of game animals and one in a sentence on rump meat; a cultivator has no regimen to take from it.

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** wild game must be cooked through; equid meat can carry parasites.
- **Identity:** the Latin name differs between the two translations; do not substitute a species on this basis. [USER: verify]
- **Legal and conservation status:** wild equids are protected in many countries, and trade in their products may be controlled; status for the species intended was not checked. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics and religion:** killing an animal for food or medicine is a moral question for many traditions; this entry takes no position beyond recording the text.
