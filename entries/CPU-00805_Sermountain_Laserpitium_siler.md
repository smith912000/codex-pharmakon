# Sermountain — Laser / Sili / Sermountain Hartwort

## *Laserpitium siler* L. | Family: Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)

**Tradition Tags:** AT · CM · DMM

**Energetic Classification:** Warming, Drying (Third Degree) — a hot, dispersing aromatic in the Galenic reckoning

**Training Phase:** Purification

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Dioscorides (De Materia Medica):** The Greek and Roman aromatic-umbellifer tradition prized a family of resinous, pungent "laser"-type plants whose seeds and roots were used as warming carminatives, emmenagogues and digestive stimulants. *Laserpitium* — the very name preserved in the modern genus — sat at the centre of this group. The mountain-dwelling *Laserpitium siler*, the "sermountain" or "siler montanum" of later herbals, supplied an acrid, spicy seed employed to break wind, provoke urine and menses, and warm a cold, sluggish stomach. It belongs to the same doctrinal category as the more famous (and probably extinct) *silphium*/*laser* of Cyrene, and classical writers were themselves uncertain where one "laser" ended and another began. That uncertainty is genuine and worth stating plainly: the identity of the ancient laser-resins is debated, and *L. siler* is best understood as a surviving member of the broad aromatic-umbellifer tradition rather than as a confirmed identity for any single ancient drug.

**Avicenna (Qanun) / Unani:** The Arabic-Persian materia medica carried the Greek umbellifer pharmacy forward, classing hot aromatic seeds of this type as warming to the stomach and uterus, dissolving of "windy" swellings, and opening of obstructions. Sermountain-type seeds fit the Unani category of *kāsir-i-riyāḥ* (wind-breaking) carminatives used for colic, flatulence and cold digestive complaints.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian:** The cuneiform plant lists record numerous aromatic umbellifer seeds used as fumigants, carminatives and warming remedies; a hot mountain hartwort of this kind belongs among them, though — as with much of the Assyrian pharmacopoeia — the exact botanical identification behind any one Sumerogram is provisional.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Sermountain embodies the classical archetype of the *warming aromatic seed* — a plant read by the doctrine of qualities as "hot and dry," and used to move what is stagnant, cold and damp. That doctrinal reading maps onto real pharmacology: the plant's activity resides in a volatile essential oil rich in terpenoids, the same chemical class that gives caraway, fennel, cumin and the other kitchen umbellifers their carminative, antispasmodic and mildly antimicrobial reputation. The "heat" of the humoral tradition is, in modern terms, the pungent, membrane-active terpene fraction; the "opening of obstructions" is the smooth-muscle-relaxing, gas-dispersing action of those same oils on the gut. The symbolic lineage that ties sermountain to the legendary silphium is a story about scarcity and value — the ancient laser was worth its weight in silver — but pharmacologically the honest picture is of a competent minor carminative, not a lost panacea.

### Active Components

The pharmacologically relevant fraction is the **essential (volatile) oil** of the fruit and root, dominated by terpenoids. According to PubMed, gas-chromatographic analysis of *Laserpitium siler* subspecies oils found profiles dominated either by **oxygenated sesquiterpenoids** — notably cubebol, 4-*epi*-cubebol and cubenol — or by **monoterpene hydrocarbons** such as β-phellandrene, limonene and γ-terpinene, depending on the subspecies and provenance (Basile et al., 2022). The genus *Laserpitium* more broadly is also a known source of **sesquiterpene lactones** and coumarins, compound classes typical of the Apiaceae. Reported minor constituents follow the usual umbellifer pattern (phenylpropanoids, furanocoumarins), though quantitative species-specific data are limited.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Sermountain is filed in the **Purification** phase as a warming digestive aromatic — a "mover" used to disperse cold stagnation, relieve flatulent colic, and stimulate a torpid appetite. In the Kenetica framing it is a secondary, supporting agent rather than a primary remedy: the kind of pungent seed added to a formula to warm and carry, comparable in role to caraway or lovage. Its traditional emmenagogue reputation places it, with appropriate caution, among the warming seeds historically used to promote delayed menses — a use that also underlies its contraindication in pregnancy.

### Scientific Correlation

Based on articles retrieved from PubMed, species-specific pharmacological data on *Laserpitium siler* are **sparse and confined largely to phytochemistry**:

- Basile et al. (2022), *Molecules* — GC×GC-MS characterisation of *Laserpitium siler* subspecies essential oils, showing one oil rich in oxygenated sesquiterpenoids (cubebol 35%, 4-*epi*-cubebol 23%, cubenol 13%) and another dominated by monoterpene hydrocarbons (β-phellandrene 42%, limonene 24%), and evaluating **insecticidal activity** against stored-product pests, with the sesquiterpenoid-rich oil the more biocidal. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27030588).

For biological context — explicitly **not** species-specific to *L. siler* — the sesquiterpene lactones characteristic of the genus and wider Apiaceae/Asteraceae are reviewed as a class:

- Quintana & Estévez (2018), *Current Pharmaceutical Design* — review of cytotoxic sesquiterpene lactones and the α-methylene-γ-butyrolactone pharmacophore underlying their bioactivity. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2174/1381612825666190119114323). (General class review; cited for mechanism, not as evidence for this species.)

**Evidence grade: traditional / preclinical (phytochemical).** The warming-carminative and emmenagogue uses rest on long classical attestation and on the plant's terpene-rich essential-oil chemistry, which is consistent with mild antispasmodic and antimicrobial activity by analogy to related umbellifers. There are **no human clinical trials** for *L. siler*, and the only species-specific experimental work identified concerns essential-oil composition and insecticidal, not medicinal, endpoints. Claims beyond "plausible minor carminative" are not supported.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Hot and dry (to the third degree); carminative, emmenagogue, diuretic, stomachic — a warming disperser of cold and wind.
- **Ayurvedic (by analogy, not attested for this species):** the profile corresponds to *katu* (pungent) taste, *ushna* (heating) *virya*, and *vata*-*kapha*-reducing action typical of aromatic umbellifer seeds; this is an inference from chemistry and Galenic parallel, not a documented Ayurvedic listing.
- **TCM:** not a classical Chinese materia medica item; no traditional Chinese classification.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** The cuneiform *šammu* lists carry many aromatic umbellifer seeds worked as fumigants, carminatives and warming remedies, and Mesopotamian medicine partly classified a substance by the causal agency — divine hand, ghost, oath — it was set against. But the botanical identity behind any one Sumerogram is provisional, and a hot mountain hartwort of this type can only be placed among that class, not securely matched to *Laserpitium siler*. Under the standing blocking warning for every Assyrian substance identification, tablet reference and agency attribution, no agency-opposing role and no cultivation status can be claimed for this species. Cultivation status: **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Sermountain sits at the heart of the classical "laser" tradition of resinous, pungent aromatic umbels, worked as a warming carminative, emmenagogue and diuretic — a therapeutic profile, and Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system in which a substance could be given a cultivation status. What the tradition offers a cultivator is instead a discipline of attention, sharpened here by a real problem the ancients admitted themselves: they were uncertain where one "laser" ended and another began, and *L. siler* is best read as a surviving member of the broad aromatic-umbel group rather than a confirmed identity for any single ancient drug. Knowing the true plant, guarding against the conflation with the legendary Cyrenaic *silphium*, and assaying the acrid seed are the practice — provenance and judgement, not a vital-body doctrine. Cultivation status: **(c) absent.**

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The Arabic-Persian pharmacy carried the Greek umbel doctrine forward, classing hot aromatic seeds of this type as *kāsir-i-riyāḥ* (wind-breaking) carminatives that warm the stomach and uterus, dissolve windy swellings and open obstructions. That is a therapeutic frame; the one genuine Unani cultivation idea — conserving the finite innate heat and radical moisture whose depletion *is* ageing, so that longevity is guarding an endowment rather than acquiring it — is a doctrine of the whole regimen, not a status assigned to a third-degree warming disperser. No *mufarrih* (*ruh*-gladdening cardiac) role is claimed, no *Canon* chapter is cited, and sermountain enters by its class of hot wind-breaking seeds rather than as a species Avicenna named. The tradition claims the plant therapeutically but records no cultivation status for it: **(b) framed.**

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator treats sermountain as a minor warming mover, not as anything taken to build vitality — a pungent seed added to disperse cold stagnation, relieve flatulent colic and stir a torpid appetite, comparable in role to caraway or lovage. The symbolic lineage tying it to the silver-priced silphium is a story about scarcity, not about longevity: pharmacologically and doctrinally the honest picture is a competent secondary carminative used short-term and in formula. Its emmenagogue reputation is a caution, not a cultivation virtue — avoid in pregnancy, keep the concentrated oil out of unsupervised internal use, and reach for it to warm and carry, then stop.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy — avoid.** Traditional emmenagogue reputation; warming aromatic seeds of this class are classically used to promote menstruation and are inappropriate in pregnancy.
- **Concentrated essential oil** should not be taken internally without professional supervision — terpene-rich umbellifer oils can irritate mucous membranes and, in excess, stress the kidneys.
- **Apiaceae furanocoumarins** raise a theoretical risk of **photosensitisation** with skin contact and sun exposure, as in related umbellifers.
- **Botanical-identity risk:** the Apiaceae contains several deadly members (hemlock, water hemlock); wild-gathered "sermountain" must be identified by an expert, never by common name alone.
- Evidence for safety is traditional only; there are no modern toxicology or interaction studies for this species.
