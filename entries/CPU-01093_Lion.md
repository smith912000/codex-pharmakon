# Lion — Lion / Fat-of-the-Lion (Bryan 1930, Ebers) / Sengye (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary)
## *Lion (Panthera leo L., as the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints it); Bryan gives only "Lion"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP, FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

The lion enters the Codex as an animal named in two texts, and in neither is it a well-documented medicine. The honest summary is a fat in one Egyptian hair remedy and a name in a Tibetan list. Nothing here is a recommendation.

**Egyptian (EP).** Bryan's 1930 introduction to the Ebers Papyrus (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`, "Organic Remedies", page 34-35 in the OCR) lists the animals "whose identity is not in doubt, and who were called upon to sacrifice themselves in the cause of the Healing Art". The list is printed in a column and opens with "Lion" (line 1390), followed by the hippopotamus, crocodile, ox, cow, calf, mouse and the rest. The same passage lists the parts the animals "were forced to yield", among them "Fat" and "Tallow" (the lines after 1390). The only prescription found that names the lion is in Bryan's chapter on the hair (about line 4025), headed "ANOTHER FOR THE GROWTH OF THE HAIR ON A HEAD WHICH IS BECOMING BALD": "Fat-of-the-Lion, Fat-of-the-Hippopotamus, Fat-of-the-Crocodile, Fat-of-the-Cat, Fat-of-the-Serpent, Fat-of-the-Egyptian-Goat. Make into one and rub the head of the Bald One therewith." Bryan introduces the group of remedies as the "desperate remedies" resorted to when simple oils had failed, and he treats the list with open amusement (lines 4015-4040). No quantity is printed and no recitation is reported. The list row `_sources/ebers.csv` line 72 ("Lion fat", "Ebers (baldness pomade)") agrees with this. No lion product other than the fat was found, and the Egyptian word is not given in Bryan's list (`Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` line 92: "no Egyptian word or binomial given in this list").

**Tibetan (FMT).** The locus is the "Glossary on Animals" at the end of the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` lines 7120-7130). The OCR prints a column of Tibetan names (Shawa, Sengye, Sah, Semuk, Seru) beside a column of English names (Deer, Lion, Snow Leopard, Black Rhinoceros, Rhinoceros) and a column of binomials (Cervus elaphus, Panthera Leo L., Uncia uncia, Rhinoceros bicornis L., and so on). The pairing of *Sengye* with "Lion" rests on the order of the columns, which is an OCR reading, and is marked [USER: verify]. The glossary is a list of names; it gives no use. In the running text the lion appears once as food: Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 730), in the Explanatory Tantra's list of unwholesome combinations, "It is unwholesome to eat (lion's) meat roasted on a fire of barberry (wood)". The Men-Tsee-Khang text (line 3114) renders the same item as "meat of haritra roasted over a Kyerpa fire", and its note (line 3133) says that "there are different views ... Some identify it as parrot while others think of it as lion, rabbit, patridge etc." So the one therapeutic-text appearance of a lion in this tradition is a warning, and the identification of the animal is disputed in the translation itself. The lion appears elsewhere in the Tibetan texts only as imagery (a physician "in the manner of a lion", a posture, the "Lion of Speech" meditation, the instrument *seng.ge.kha*, "lion's mouth") and these are not the animal as a substance.

**Noticed but not tagged.** Two other texts name the lion. Dioscorides 2-90 *Stear Taureion, Pardaleion kai Leonteion* ("Bulls' Suet etc.") (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 5317) says of the preparation of bulls' suet that "In the same way panthers', lions', wild boars', camels', horses', and other similar fats are prepared." In 2-94 *Steaton Dunamis* (line 5344) he adds: "All fat is warming, softening and purifying; yet that of bulls, cows and calves is somewhat astringent. The lions' is similar to these and they say it is an antidote to defend against those who intend treachery." (In the same chapter it is bears' fat, not lions', that "is thought to make hair that was destroyed by alopecia grow again".) Sushruta, *Sutrasthana* ch.46 (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` lines 536-545), puts the lion (the OCR prints "Hon") among the cave-dwelling mammals of the *Guhashaya* family, whose flesh "is sweet, heavy, demulcent and strength-giving ... heat-making in its potency, and proves beneficial in diseases affecting the eyes and anus"; this is a statement about the family, not a prescription for the lion. Neither tradition is tagged on this entry's spine row; the lines are recorded here so the information is not lost.

### Active Components

No chemistry is given by any text read. In general terms, animal fat (tallow, depot fat) is mostly triglycerides, a mixture of saturated and unsaturated fatty acids, with smaller amounts of cholesterol and fat-soluble vitamins; muscle meat is protein, water and fat. Nothing is known from the sources here about the composition of the lion fat the Egyptian scribe meant, or whether a lion's fat differs usefully from that of the other five animals in the same recipe.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Egyptian recipe is a six-fat pomade for baldness, and the sources say nothing about whether it worked. The Tibetan appearance is a food warning. The lion is also a protected animal: it is not obtainable lawfully as a medicine, and its fat has no known property that other legal fats lack. Kenetica gives it no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation given is the Egyptian one (Bryan, about line 4025): the fat of the lion mixed with the fat of the hippopotamus, crocodile, cat, serpent and Egyptian goat, "made into one", and rubbed on the head of the bald person. No quantities and no duration are given in the passage read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Egyptian (EP):** none printed; the Ebers material is organised by complaint and gives no qualities for its ingredients.

**Tibetan (FMT):** none printed for the lion. The glossary gives only the name; the single running-text mention is a warning about an unwholesome preparation.

**Greek (DMM, not tagged here):** Dioscorides 2-94 (line 5344) prints fat in general as "warming, softening and purifying" and the lion's, like the bull's and cow's, as "somewhat astringent" (the lions' fat "is similar to these").

**Ayurveda (SS, not tagged here):** the *Guhashaya* family statement quoted above (sweet, heavy, demulcent, strength-giving, heat-making in potency) belongs to the family and not to the lion by name.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* The lion is named once in a list of animal sources (Bryan, `PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1390) and once as one of six fats in a baldness pomade (about line 4025). The Ebers material records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); Bryan reports no recitation for the pomade, and since Bryan summarises, that does not show the papyrus gives none. Substance identity in an indication-organised corpus is a modern hypothesis. No deity is attributed.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* The lion is a name in the Glossary on Animals (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, line 7130) and, in Clark's rendering only, an item in a list of unwholesome foods (Clark 1995, line 730; the other translation reads the item as *haritra* and records a dispute over its identity). No therapeutic use, and no *bcud len* use, was found. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted.

### Contraindications

- **Conservation and law:** the lion is a protected species (listed on the CITES appendices and assessed as threatened by the IUCN, with Asiatic and African populations treated differently). Trade in its fat or parts is restricted or illegal in most countries. [USER: verify current status]
- **Ethics:** killing a protected top predator for a remedy of unknown benefit cannot be defended.
- **Hygiene and infection:** raw animal fat and carnivore tissue can carry bacterial and parasitic pathogens; the carcass of a wild animal can carry zoonotic disease.
- **Topical use:** rubbing unprocessed animal fat on the scalp risks infection and contact irritation.
- **Food:** the Tibetan text lists lion's meat roasted over barberry wood as unwholesome; the identification is disputed.
