# Downy Hemp-nettle

## *Galeopsis segetum* Neck. | Family: Lamiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Cool-neutral, dry. Mildly astringent, "strengthening" (mineral-tonic in the traditional reading).

**Training Phase:** Restoration. A convalescent respiratory herb in the tradition. See Scientific Correlation before deploying it as anything.

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Galeopsis segetum* Neck. (syn. *G. dubia* Leers, *G. ochroleuca* Lam.) — **downy hemp-nettle**, **yellow hemp-nettle** — is an annual of the Lamiaceae found on sandy, acid, disturbed ground and arable margins across western and central Europe, with a stronghold in the Rhineland, Belgium and northern France. It is soft-hairy (hence *downy*), bears pale yellow flowers, and is in decline across much of its range as arable weed floras have been simplified.

**The name is the first correction this entry has to make.** It is a "nettle" that **does not sting**. *Galeopsis* is Lamiaceae — mint family — and carries no stinging trichomes; the resemblance to *Urtica* is a matter of leaf shape and rough pubescence and nothing else. "Hemp-nettle" compounds the error with a second false likeness (*Cannabis*). The plant is neither a nettle nor a hemp, and the vernacular name has misled collectors for centuries. Compare *Lamium album*, white dead-nettle — the same joke, played by the same family.

**Mediaeval–Renaissance and early-modern materia medica (DMM)**
This is a **pulmonary** herb, and unusually among European folk drugs its reputation is documentary and traceable rather than immemorial. German **Gelber Hohlzahn** ("yellow hollow-tooth"); Dutch **raai**; French **galéopside**. The pharmacy name is **Herba Galeopsidis**, and the drug's fame rests on two branded eighteenth/nineteenth-century preparations:

- **"Lieber's tea"** (*Liebersches Kräuter-Auszehrungs-Kraut*) — a proprietary herbal mixture sold against **Auszehrung**: consumption, wasting, phthisis.
- **"Blankenheim tea"** (*Blankenheimer Tee*), named for the Eifel district that supplied the herb.

The classical indication set is narrow and consistent: **chronic cough, bronchitis, bronchial catarrh, hoarseness, and — the claim that sold it — pulmonary consumption**. It was a **convalescent's** drug: taken over weeks by the wasted and the coughing, drunk as a daily tea, given to the tubercular.

**The doctrine attached to it is explicitly mineral.** The tradition holds that *Galeopsis* is rich in **silicic acid**, and that silicic acid **strengthens lung tissue** — that the lung of the consumptive is structurally weakened, and that supplying silica rebuilds it. This is not vague vitalism; it is a mechanistic claim with a named compound and a named target tissue, and it is the whole basis of the plant's use.

**Contemporary / folk-clinical (CM)**
*Herba Galeopsidis* persists in German-speaking folk practice as a silica-bearing "lung tea", usually blended with *Equisetum arvense* (horsetail — the other great silica herb), *Plantago lanceolata* and *Thymus*. The same silica doctrine has migrated sideways into modern supplement marketing, where it now attaches to hair, nails, skin and connective tissue as much as to lung.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**
This is the most instructive entry in the sequence, because the doctrine is **not** a piece of pre-scientific poetry. It is a *falsifiable mechanistic hypothesis*, stated in chemical language, and it is **wrong at the step that matters** — which is a far more interesting failure than a vague one.

Unpack it. The doctrine has three links:

1. ***Galeopsis* contains silicic acid.** — **Plausible and probably true.** High silica accumulation is a well-known trait of certain plant families; *Equisetum* is the textbook case. That *Galeopsis* accumulates silica is the traditional claim and is chemically unremarkable as a botanical fact. **However: no analysis quantifying silicic acid in *G. segetum* was retrieved this run.** The codex therefore records this as *traditionally asserted, chemically plausible, unverified here*. 2. **Silicon is involved in connective tissue.** — **Arguable at the level of general nutrition, and irrelevant here.**
3. **Therefore drinking silica-rich tea strengthens diseased lung tissue.** — **This step is false, and it is false in three independent ways.**

Take the third link seriously, because it is where the tradition's rigour collapses:

- **Solubility and absorption.** The silica in plant tissue is largely polymerised, insoluble biogenic silica — phytoliths. What is absorbed from the gut is monomeric orthosilicic acid, and only a fraction. Most of the silica in the cup passes through. The dose that reaches blood is not the dose in the herb.
- **Distribution.** Absorbed silicon is excreted renally with considerable efficiency. There is no mechanism by which orally-ingested silicic acid is *targeted to the lung*. The doctrine requires a delivery system it never specifies and biology does not provide.
- **The pathology is wrong.** Tuberculosis is an infection. The lung of the consumptive is not *structurally deficient in silicon*; it is being destroyed by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*. Supplying a building material does not address a pathogen. **The doctrine mistook the rubble for the cause.**

And then there is the fact that turns this doctrine from merely wrong into darkly instructive. **Silica's actual, best-documented relationship with the human lung is that inhaled crystalline silica causes silicosis** — a progressive, irreversible fibrotic lung disease, and a potent risk factor for tuberculosis. The literature linking silica to the lung is enormous and it runs in **precisely the opposite direction** to the doctrine: a PubMed search this run for silica, silicosis and pulmonary fibrosis returned **335 indexed records**, an entire occupational-medicine discipline built on silica *destroying* lung tissue.

The route of administration is different — inhaled crystalline dust is not swallowed plant phytolith, and it would be sloppy to pretend the tea causes silicosis. **It does not, and this entry does not claim it does.** But the doctrinal irony stands and should be stated: the tradition selected a lung remedy on the theory that silica builds lung tissue, and silica is the archetypal substance for which medicine has documented lung tissue being **unbuilt**. The symbolic logic — *give the weak lung a hard, structural, mineral substance and it will become strong* — is sympathetic magic wearing a chemical vocabulary. Naming a real compound does not make an argument scientific. **That is the lesson of this entry, and it is worth more than the plant is.**

### Active Components

**This section is short because the data do not exist.**

**A PubMed search for "Galeopsis segetum" this run returned zero records.** Not few — **zero**. There is no phytochemical characterisation, no pharmacological study, no toxicological assessment, and no clinical data for this species indexed in PubMed. Everything below is **genus-level congener data, named as such, and must not be read as a *G. segetum* value.**

**Genus-level chemistry (*Galeopsis* spp. — NOT *segetum*):**
- **Iridoid glucosides** — the genus marker. *G. angustifolia*: harpagide, acetylharpagide, and 8-epi-loganin, with iridoids at **4.5% of crude extract** (Venditti et al., *Nat Prod Res*; PubMed, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2012.724066)). *G. bifida*: ajugoside and 8-acetylharpagide, both first reports for the genus (Zhang et al., *Zhongguo Zhong Yao Za Zhi*; PubMed — no DOI indexed; PMID 12774403). *G. tetrahit*: an iridoid glucoside, "gluroside", isolated and structurally elucidated (Sticher & Weisflog, *Pharm Acta Helv*; PubMed — no DOI indexed; PMID 1208610).
- **Flavonoids** — *G. angustifolia* at **16.7% of crude extract**, chiefly isoscutellarein derivatives including a novel 3'-hydroxy-isoscutellarein glycoside ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2012.724066)). *G. bifida*: chrysoeriol, hederagenin, daucosterol, martynoside and several flavone glucosides (PMID 12774403).
- **Alkaloids** — **stachydrine** and other alkaloids detected **in *G. ladanum* seeds** (Aparicio et al., *Med Clin (Barc)*; PubMed — no DOI indexed; PMID 10074634). See Contraindications; this is not an incidental finding.

**Silicic acid — the doctrinal active — is not quantified in any retrieved source, for this species or any *Galeopsis*.** The single most important compound in this entry's traditional pharmacology is, in the retrieved literature, **absent**. [USER: verify]

*A search-hygiene note for future editors:* querying "Galeopsis" in PubMed returns *Galeopsis porcellanicus* — a **New Zealand bryozoan**, an animal, sharing the genus name by homonymy. It is not a plant and has nothing to do with this entry. The genus's PubMed footprint is small enough that this marine invertebrate is a meaningful fraction of it.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**The honest recommendation is that this plant has no evidence-based use in Kenetica, and the entry exists to say so.**

Its traditional role is clear: a **convalescent respiratory tea**, drunk over weeks by those recovering from or wasting with chronic lung disease. If a practitioner chooses to work with it in that role, they should be explicit — with themselves and with anyone else — that they are practising **history, not pharmacology**. It is a mild, palatable, mucilaginous-astringent Lamiaceae tea. It is a warm drink for someone who is coughing. Warm drinks help people who are coughing, and there is no need to invent a mineral mechanism to explain that.

What it must **never** be is a tuberculosis drug, and the reason is not academic. TB is curable with antimicrobial therapy and lethal without it. The historical use of *Herba Galeopsidis* against consumption occurred in a world with no alternative; **that excuse is not available now.** A practitioner who treats, or allows a patient to treat, suspected TB with lung tea has not made a therapeutic error — they have withheld treatment from a communicable, fatal, curable infection.

The plant's real value to this codex is **as a specimen of doctrinal failure**. It demonstrates, better than any plant in the volume, that a tradition can name a real compound, propose a real mechanism, target a real tissue, sustain a commercial preparation for a century — and be **completely wrong**, because it never checked whether the compound arrives, whether it acts, or whether the disease is the kind of thing it could act on. Fumitory (CPU-00749) got the organ wrong. *Galeopsis* got the **entire causal architecture** wrong while sounding more modern than either.

**Cultivator's note.** *G. segetum* is an annual of poor, sandy, acid, disturbed ground — it wants the conditions most growers spend their lives correcting. Sow *in situ* in spring on light soil; do not enrich, do not water heavily, do not transplant. It behaves as a cornfield weed because it is one, and its decline across Europe is a decline of the arable margins it needs.

**Its serious cultivation interest is conservation, not pharmacy.** As a declining arable-weed species it has a place in a conservation bed or a deliberately impoverished margin, and that is a better reason to grow it than any in the traditional literature. Harvest aerial parts in flower and dry fast in shade if the herb is wanted.

**A note on the genus's agronomy that bears on wild collection:** *G. tetrahit* has documented herbicide resistance — an MCPA-resistant field biotype with a resistance factor of 3.3, governed by at least two nuclear genes, and cross-resistance to fluroxypyr at a factor of 8.2 (Weinberg et al., *J Agric Food Chem*; PubMed, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf061803u)). This is congener data about a different species, and it tells you nothing pharmacological. It tells you something practical: **hemp-nettles live in sprayed fields and survive being sprayed.** Wild-collected arable-margin *Galeopsis* is agrochemical-exposed material by default. Do not gather from field edges.

**Every figure in this table is traditional consensus. None is trial-derived. There is no established safe or effective human dose for *Galeopsis segetum*.** The entire quantitative pharmacology of this plant, in the indexed literature, is **empty**.

Note the internal contradiction the tradition never resolved: **if silicic acid is the active, the infusion is the wrong preparation.** Polymerised plant silica is poorly extracted into a ten-minute water infusion and poorly absorbed if it is. The tradition's chosen method is inconsistent with the tradition's stated mechanism. **Nobody checked.** That is the entry in miniature.

### Scientific Correlation

**GRADE: NULL — everything, for this species.**

This must be stated without hedging. **A PubMed search for *Galeopsis segetum* this run returned zero indexed records.** There is no evidence for or against any claim made about this plant. It has not been tested and found wanting; **it has not been tested.** The codex's obligation here is to say so and stop, not to fill the vacuum with congener data wearing a *segetum* label.

**GRADE: NULL — the pulmonary claim. Deflate completely.**
No study — for *G. segetum* or any *Galeopsis* — tests the herb against cough, bronchitis, bronchial catarrh, tuberculosis, or any respiratory endpoint, in any model, at any dose. The plant's **entire reason for existing in the materia medica** has no retrieved support whatsoever. Two named commercial preparations, a century of sales, a specific disease target, and **not one study**. Commercial longevity is not evidence; it is evidence of commerce.

**GRADE: NULL — the silica-strengthens-lung doctrine. Deflate, and note the direction of the real evidence.**
Silicic acid is not quantified in this species in any retrieved source. No retrieved study tests silicon supplementation for any pulmonary endpoint. The mechanism fails at absorption, at distribution, and at the pathology, as set out in the Doctrinal layer. And the substantial literature that *does* connect silica to the lung — 335 records for silica/silicosis/pulmonary fibrosis in this run's search — documents silica **causing** irreversible fibrotic lung disease by inhalation. **The doctrine is not merely unsupported. The only well-evidenced silica–lung relationship runs the other way.** The distinction in route of administration is real and is not being elided; the point is that the doctrine's central intuition — silica is good for lungs — is the opposite of what medicine actually knows about silica and lungs.

**GRADE: WEAK, congener only — genus chemistry is real but tells us nothing therapeutic.**
*Galeopsis* is a genuine iridoid/flavonoid genus: harpagide and acetylharpagide at 4.5% and flavonoids at 16.7% of crude extract **in *G. angustifolia*** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2012.724066)); ajugoside and 8-acetylharpagide **in *G. bifida*** (PMID 12774403). Harpagide is a compound of some pharmacological interest elsewhere. **None of this is *G. segetum* data, none of it is respiratory data, and none of it constitutes support for any traditional claim.** It is listed because a phytochemical vacuum should be labelled, not furnished.

**GRADE: MODERATE — and it is a *toxicology* signal, not an efficacy one.**
The one substantive human clinical finding retrieved for this genus is an **adverse** one. A **rhabdomyolysis outbreak in Spain** was traced to eating **quail that had fed on *Galeopsis ladanum* seeds** — 20 patients with myalgia, raised muscle enzymes and myoglobinuria; 6 required hospital care; outcomes benign. A murine model was developed (rats fed such quail had higher creatine kinase than controls, p = 0.0588 — near significance, and the authors say so). **Stachydrine and other alkaloids were detected in the seeds**, and an unidentified alkaloid in the quail meat; the authors conclude a plant alkaloid is the probable toxin (Aparicio et al., *Med Clin (Barc)*; PubMed — no DOI indexed; PMID 10074634). This is a documented instance of *coturnism*, transmitted through a food chain.

Read this carefully. It concerns **seeds**, of a **different species**, **via quail**, and it **does not demonstrate that *G. segetum* aerial-part tea is toxic**. It is not evidence against the tea. But it establishes that this genus contains alkaloids capable of causing **human rhabdomyolysis** at second hand — and it does so in a genus whose only other retrieved human signal is a 1950 Russian note titled simply **"Toxicity of *Galeopsis speciosa*"** (Beloruchev, *Gig Sanit*; PubMed — no abstract available, no DOI; PMID 15427856). [USER: verify — the source is inaccessible in this run and its content is unknown; it is recorded here as a flag, not a finding.]

**The summary position.** For a plant with a century-old commercial reputation as a lung remedy, the indexed literature contains: **zero efficacy studies, zero species-specific chemistry, zero toxicology — and two adverse signals at genus level.** That ratio is the finding.

### Contraindications

- **Do not use for tuberculosis, suspected tuberculosis, or any serious respiratory illness.** This is the entry's principal warning and it is a warning about *omission*, not toxicity. TB is communicable, curable, and fatal untreated. Persistent cough, haemoptysis, night sweats, weight loss, or fever require **urgent medical assessment**. The historical indication of this plant is the single most dangerous thing about it.
- **The genus contains alkaloids that have caused human rhabdomyolysis.** *G. ladanum* seed alkaloids, including stachydrine, poisoned 20 people through quail meat (PMID 10074634). Aerial parts of *G. segetum* are not the same material — but a genus with a documented alkaloid-mediated human myotoxicity outbreak is not a genus to treat casually, and the absence of *segetum* toxicology is an absence of *study*, not a clean result.
- **A 1950 report titled "Toxicity of *Galeopsis speciosa*" exists and could not be assessed this run.** Unresolved. [USER: verify]
- **No toxicology, no pharmacokinetics, no safety data of any kind for this species.** Nothing retrieved. This is the honest statement and it should not be softened into "generally considered safe" — a phrase that here would mean only "nobody looked".
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children — avoid.** No data whatsoever. Prohibition on grounds of ignorance.
- **Do not wild-collect from arable margins.** Hemp-nettles are herbicide-exposed field weeds with documented resistance in the genus ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf061803u)) — meaning they survive spraying and are still standing there when you find them. Assume agrochemical residue.
- **Do not concentrate.** No tincture, no extract, no standardised preparation. Concentrating a plant with no phytochemistry and a genus-level alkaloid myotoxicity signal is not herbalism.
- **Identification.** *Galeopsis* species are separated on calyx, corolla colour and stem characters and are routinely confused with each other and with *Lamium* and *Stachys*. Given that the genus's one human toxicity signal is species-specific (*ladanum* seed), **"it's a hemp-nettle" is not an identification.** Note also that *G. segetum* seed should not be collected or used at all — the toxicity signal in this genus is a **seed** signal.
- **Silica supplementation is not a therapeutic strategy for lung disease.** Stated as a contraindication because the doctrine is still in circulation and now sells supplements.
