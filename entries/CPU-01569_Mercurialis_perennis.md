# Mercurialis perennis — Kunokrambe, Cynia (Dioscorides 4-192)

## *Mercurialis perennis [Linnaeus], the last of the names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-192 (which also prints Cynocrambe, Mercurialis sylvestris mas and Cynocrambe mas [Fuchs]); "Dog's Mercury [Mabberley]"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 9736) lists four names for the chapter, with the English "Dog's Mercury [Mabberley]", and a second line, "[other usage] Thelygonum cynocrambe — Dog's Cabbage". So the translators themselves record that the Greek name has been read as another plant by other users. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-192. KUNOKRAMBE*, heading at line 9735, in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"). The whole chapter (line 9738) reads: "Cynia (or cynocrambe) sends out small tender stems two feet high, somewhat white, with whitish leaves at distances, like mercuria annua [4-191] or cissus. The seed lying by the leaves is little and round. Both the stalk and the leaves (taken as a decoction or as vegetables) are able to move the bowels. The water from their boiling expels bile and watery stuff. It is also called the wild male linozostis; the Africans call it harmas, and some, asumeslabon."

Dioscorides describes the plant by its stems (small, tender, two feet high and whitish), by its leaves, set apart on the stem, and by its seed, small and round and lying by the leaves. He compares the leaves to those of *mercuria annua* (4-191) and to *cissus*. The uses printed are two: the stalk and leaves, taken as a decoction or eaten as vegetables, "are able to move the bowels", and the water of their boiling "expels bile and watery stuff". That is the same pair of actions that 4-191 gives for its plant. He also names it "the wild male linozostis", so the chapter ties it by name to the plant of 4-191, which is the subject of I_1132. It prints two other names, the African *harmas* and *asumeslabon*, as names only. Where the 4-191 chapter says the Africans call that plant *asumes*, this one gives *asumeslabon*; the lines read do not explain the relation of the two forms.

**Chapters that point here.** The translators' cross-reference in 3-140 *PHULLON* (heading at line 7757) reads "see 4-192" (line 7759), under the suggestion "Bryophyllum calycinum, Bryophyllum pinnatum — Air Plant, Floppers, Life Plant". That is a different plant, treated in I_1029, and the pointer is the translators' own, not a statement that the two chapters concern the same plant. The index lists "Mercurialis perennis 738" (line 16605).

### Active Components

The composition of the plant Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, *Mercurialis perennis* (dog's mercury) is a woodland plant of the spurge family (Euphorbiaceae) and is generally regarded as poisonous when eaten. That is a general statement and not a finding for the plant of this chapter. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a plant of small tender stems, eaten as a vegetable or taken as a decoction to move the bowels, and a boiling-water drink that expels bile and watery stuff. These are the text's claims. A plant that the text says moves the bowels and that its modern name marks as poisonous is not something to eat on the strength of this chapter, and the Codex gives no protocol. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives two preparations: "taken as a decoction or as vegetables" (the stalk and the leaves), and "the water from their boiling". It gives no quantity or dose for either; the one measure, two feet, is the height of the plant. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Greek name has been applied to more than one plant, as the translators' own second line shows, so a modern fact about dog's mercury would not by itself bear on the remedy recorded. It is a general, well-established fact that plants regarded as poisonous should not be used as vegetables.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying, moistening or astringent quality and no taste for 4-192. It prints only the actions, bowel-moving and expelling of bile and watery stuff.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant (small tender whitish stems of two feet, whitish leaves set at distances, small round seed lying by the leaves) and how it stands to a plant like it, since he places it by name as "the wild male linozostis" and by leaf beside *mercuria annua*. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a vegetable and a drug for the bowels. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a bowel-moving plant with no stated quantity. The useful lesson is Dioscorides' habit of telling a plant by its likeness to a sister plant, and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Dioscorides gives the plant as a bowel-mover that "expels bile and watery stuff", so it should not be self-treated, and no safety information for pregnancy was read. The plant is generally regarded as poisonous when eaten, whatever the chapter says of vegetables. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion with other readings of the Greek name, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
