# Pinus tabulaeformis — sgron.shing, Doen shing; "Yew-leaf Fir", "Pine tree" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Pinus tabulaeformis, the only binomial on its line in the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary (5237, with the English "Pine tree" at 5239), which the Codex reads by position against the phonetic name "Doen shing"; Clark 1995 prints it as the third of four Pinus binomials for sgron.shing ("Yew-leaf Fir")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and on a species name that both editions print, but set against the Tibetan name in one of them (Men-Tsee-Khang) by column position and not on one printed line.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Ebers, Assyrian, Sushruta and Shennong text on disk for *Pinus* found other species and other drugs: Dioscorides prints *Pinus* names only in translators' lines for the pine chapters (for example lines 4195 to 4263), Bhishagratna's Sushruta volume 3 prints "Pinus Longifolia" in two lists of plant names (lines 32847 and 32991), and a search of the Shennong text (Yang 1998) for "Pinus" found none. None is read as this drug. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. **Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 266) the key *Pinus tabulaeformis* is taken from the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary line 5237 with the note "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". It is the **only** binomial on that line, so it is neither the first nor a later one of several there. Clark's own row for *sgron.shing* (`names.csv` line 112) is keyed by the Tibetan name and the **first** of the four Latin names, *Pinus picea*, with the note "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". In Clark's line, therefore, *Pinus tabulaeformis* is a **later** name, the third of four (*Pinus picea*, *Pinus massionana*, *Pinus tabulaeformis*, *Pinus silvestris*, as printed). The spine has rows for *Pinus tabulaeformis* (this entry) but, as far as the files read show, none for *Pinus picea* or *Pinus massionana*; *Pinus sylvestris* has an older entry (I_185).

**Identity doubt.** Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" and (line 68) that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect". Here four species of one genus are given for one Tibetan name, so the name does not fix the species, and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary's English "Pine tree" is a genus-level name. The two editions agree on a pine and on the binomial's presence, and the Codex does not choose among the four. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (lines 979 to 982).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792). The entry stands after "(5) Resins" (line 879) and before "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997); the file prints no "(6)" heading, although the opening list of sections (line 802) names "(6) plants". It follows *ga.dur* (line 977) and *stab.seng* (line 978), is split by a page break (the page number "99" is on line 980), and precedes *skyer.pa* (Indian barberry, line 983). It reads:

"sgron.shing: Yew-leaf Fir: Pinus picea: Pinus massionana: Pinus tabulaeformis: Pinus silvestris: [has three names, for example rasna, etc. It has hot taste and dry, coarse power. It has a large trunk, scattered needles, a long, scented cone and yields a resinous oil upon combustion]. It cures wind-phlegm combinations, cold lymph disorders [and dispels tumors. The root is beneficial in gynecological disorders]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The names, the description, the taste and power, the tumour action and the root remark are inside the brackets, and are the commentary layer. "It cures wind-phlegm combinations, cold lymph disorders" is outside the brackets and is the root-text layer as Clark's marks stand. Clark's General Index lists "Yew-leaf Fir" (line 1667) and his Latin index lists *Pinus tabulaeformis* (line 1626). A search of his index of Tibetan names found no *sgron.shing* line.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "D" block, lines 5179 to 5262).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. The phonetic names run from "Dali" (line 5181) to "Drita sazin" (line 5196); line 5188 reads "Dhong ga Dhugmo nyung Dhumtag Dhurji Doen shing". The transliterations include "dur byid sgron shing" (line 5206). The scientific names begin at line 5215 and end, in this run, with "Euphorbia helioscopia Sun spurge" (line 5235) and "Pinus tabulaeformis" (line 5237) with "Pine tree" (line 5239). Counted in order, "Doen shing" is the thirteenth phonetic name (counting "Dhatig" and "Dharyaken" as two) and *Pinus tabulaeformis* is the thirteenth scientific name. The count is checked on its neighbours by Clark: "Dhong ga" is ninth against *Cassia fistula* (Clark's *dong.ga*, line 990), "Dhugmo nyung" is tenth against *Holarrhena antidysenterica* (Clark's *dug.mo.nyung*, line 904) and "Dhumtag" is eleventh against *Strychnos nux-vomica* (Clark's *ldum.stag*, line 1022). The thirteenth also agrees with Clark's own line, because Clark prints *Pinus tabulaeformis* for *sgron.shing*. The glossary does not print the pairing in one row; several lines hold more than one name (lines 5183, 5188 and 5217), and the count rests on order. The twelfth, "Dhurji", is the subject of I_1274. *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (lines 3423 to 3427).* Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), in the run of efficacies that opens at line 3380, before the herbal run (line 3428). The sentence is split by a page break (the page number "205" is on line 3424): "Doen shing cures the combined disorder of baekan and loong, and chhuser disorders associated with cold disorders." "Chhuser" is a Tibetan term kept untranslated (the editors' note, line 686). The index lists "Doen shing 205" (line 7343). The name is not in the taste groups of chapter 19 and is not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23). Clark's "wind-phlegm combinations, cold lymph disorders" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "combined disorder of baekan and loong, and chhuser disorders associated with cold disorders" agree in kind as the two translators render the humours and the cold qualifier; the Codex records that and does not set "lymph" and *chhuser* equal.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Pinus tabulaeformis*, *sgron.shing* and *Doen shing* found these. I_1274 (*Euphorbia helioscopia*) states the glossary count that puts "Doen shing" against *Pinus tabulaeformis* in one sentence and gives the drug no entry. I_102 (*Abies fabri*, FMT) says that *sgron.shing* is a different drug from that fir and cites it only to exclude it. I_185 (*Pinus sylvestris*, tags include FMT) gives a Tibetan paragraph with no line cited, and the lines read for this entry do not show the claims it makes (see the data note in the report); *Pinus sylvestris* is the fourth Latin name of this same Clark line. I_1335 (*Pinus griffithii*) is the entry on the different resin drug *shel.ta*. I_1304 (*Jatropha glandulifera*) covers the *dur byid* name that stands beside *sgron.shing* in the glossary's transliteration (line 5206). Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Pinus* is the pine genus of the conifer family (Pinaceae) and that pines yield an oleoresin, a mixture of volatile terpenes and resin acids; Clark's commentary says only that the plant "yields a resinous oil upon combustion". Which compounds the Tibetan drug holds, and which of the four species it is, is not shown by the lines read, so no statement about its composition is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "cures wind-phlegm combinations, cold lymph disorders" (Clark, line 982) and "cures the combined disorder of baekan and loong, and chhuser disorders associated with cold disorders" (Men-Tsee-Khang, lines 3423 to 3427); Clark's commentary adds that it "dispels tumors" and that its root "is beneficial in gynecological disorders". These are the texts' claims in their own terms, attached to this binomial only through the name, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. A tumour or a gynaecological complaint needs medical care, and no plant should be used for either on the strength of this entry.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary names the root as the part "beneficial in gynecological disorders" and says the plant "yields a resinous oil upon combustion", which describes the plant and is not a preparation. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's line 979 prints, inside the brackets (the commentary layer), "hot taste and dry, coarse power". No post-digestive taste is printed. The Men-Tsee-Khang lines read (3423 to 3427, 5188) print no taste or power. The qualities attach to the Tibetan name *sgron.shing* in Clark and are not printed beside the glossary's binomial.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The materia medica classes this drug by what it does: it cures wind-phlegm combinations and cold lymph disorders, and the commentary adds tumours and the root's use. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, lines 3900 to 3950; Clark's chapter 23 is headed "Normal health", lines 1262 to 1276), and Clark's materia medica names other drugs as essence-extraction ingredients (for example lines 853, 971 and 1144), none of them this one. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this tree. It is a hot, dry drug for cold-type disorders in one short sentence in each edition, one of four candidate species for a Tibetan name, with no preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. The commentary calls it hot in taste, and the texts give it for cold-type disorders; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Tumours and gynaecological disorders need medical care. Pine resin and turpentine can cause skin irritation and allergic reaction. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
