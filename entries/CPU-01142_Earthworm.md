# Earthworm — Entera Ges (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 2-72), "Lumbricus — Earthworms"; "Worms from the soil"
## *The earthworm, as Dioscorides' chapter 2-72 is headed in the Osbaldeston English: "Lumbricus — Earthworms" is the edition's gloss on the Greek heading, and the chapter's own words are "Worms from the soil"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM, SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The text on disk is the 2000 English edition of Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). The chapter is 2-72, headed "ENTERA GES" with the English "Lumbricus — Earthworms" (line 5223; the row in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, "2-72 (line 5223)"). It sits in Book Two, which the author's preface (line 4904) describes as dealing with "living creatures and of honey and of milk and of animal fat". In the run of small creatures it follows the skink (2-71) and precedes the shrew mouse (2-73) and the house mouse (2-74). The edition's headings for these are glosses by the translators; the text of 2-72 itself says only "Worms from the soil". The edition prints no "SUGGESTED" identification line for it. The whole chapter, in the English, reads (lines 5225-5229):

"Worms from the soil (pounded into small pieces and applied) glue together sinews that are cut apart. They dissolve tertians [fevers with paroxysms every third day]. Boiled with goose grease they cure diseases of the ears, dropped in there. Boiled together with oil and poured into the opposing ear they help toothaches. Pounded into small pieces and taken in a drink with passum [raisin wine] they expel urine."

So the chapter gives five uses, in this order:

1. *Cut sinews.* Pounded and applied, the worms "glue together sinews that are cut apart".
2. *Tertians.* The worms "dissolve tertians"; the bracketed gloss, "fevers with paroxysms every third day", is the edition's. The chapter does not say how they are to be given for this, whether applied or taken.
3. *Ear diseases.* Boiled with goose grease, and "dropped in there" (into the ear).
4. *Toothache.* Boiled with oil and poured "into the opposing ear"; I read this as the ear on the side opposite the aching tooth, but the English does not say so. [USER: verify]
5. *Urine.* Pounded and taken in a drink with passum, they "expel urine".

The chapter gives no description of the worm, no place or season of gathering, and no assay or sign of adulteration, which Dioscorides gives for many plant and mineral materials. It uses the plain voice for all five uses; it has none of the hedging words ("said to", "thought to", "reported") that he uses elsewhere in the same book for remedies he does not vouch for (for example "are said to cure epilepsy" in the ass's-hoof chapter, 2-44, line 5116).

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms the earthworm's body is mostly water, with protein and fat, and it contains a coelomic fluid and a mucus on its skin; earthworms live in soil and can hold soil bacteria, parasites and, in contaminated soil, heavy metals in their tissues. Nothing more specific can be said without a source.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records the earthworm as an application for cut sinews, a remedy for tertian fevers, ear drops with goose grease or oil, and a drink with raisin wine to expel urine. The Codex does not recommend any of them. A cut tendon needs surgical repair, malarial fevers need diagnosis and treatment, and drops of boiled animal material in the ear risk infection. Urinary retention can be an emergency.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations are those quoted: worms pounded into small pieces and applied; worms boiled with goose grease and dropped into the ear; worms boiled with oil and poured into the opposite ear; worms pounded and taken in a drink with passum. The chapter gives no number of worms, no weight, no volume and no length of course. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. A fever that returns every third day is a pattern of malaria, which is caused by a parasite and is treated with antimalarial drugs; earthworms are no treatment for it. Severed tendons do not heal by an applied animal paste. Soil animals can carry bacteria, including tetanus spores, and parasites; and putting any non-sterile material into the ear carries a risk of infection and of damage if the eardrum is perforated. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** None printed for this chapter. The English of 2-72 gives no quality (warming, cooling, drying, astringent) for the earthworm. Other chapters of the same book print qualities for other materials, for example "All fat is warming, softening and purifying" (line 5344), but that statement is about fat and is not carried to the worm.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied here. The chapter is five short remedies. It assigns the earthworm no cultivation status, and no passage read connects it to *pneuma* or to any practice. Unlike many of his plant and mineral chapters it offers not even a discipline of attention (no provenance, season, assay or adulteration), so the entry has nothing to say under that heading either.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** The Canon entry *kharāṭīn* is brief, and Avicenna's temperament is his own inference: «يجب فيما أقدر أن يكون حارا», "as far as I can judge, it must be hot". Under wounds, the crushed worms are bound on nerve wounds and not removed for three days, when it is "very useful". Under the head, a decoction with goose fat helps earache and is dropped in with oil on the opposite side. Under the digestive organs, given with *ṭilā'* (boiled-down wine) it cures jaundice. Under the excretory organs, crushed fine and given in the same wine it provokes urine and helps stone (*Canon* Book II, entry *kharāṭīn*, Arabic text line 19358). No degree is given and no dose. The entry is wound, ear, jaundice and stone medicine, and it carries no statement about innate heat, radical moisture or the conservation of life; the heat it ascribes is a therapeutic quality. **(b) — Therapeutic actions attested; no cultivation status.**

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The paragraph for 蚯蚓 (earthworm) reads 味咸，寒 ("salty, cold"). The classic files it in the lower grade (下品); its preface says lower-grade drugs treat disease, are toxic, and are not for long use. It governs "snake conglomerations" (主蛇瘕), expels the "three worms", hidden corpse illness, "ghost infusion" and gu poison (去三虫、伏尸、鬼注、蛊毒), and kills long worms (杀长虫). It adds 仍自化作水 ("it also turns itself into water") and the habitat 生平土 ("grows in level ground"). There is no 久服 ("taken long") clause. The Mingyi bielu, not the Shennong text, gives the alias 土龙 ("earth dragon") and the month for taking it. The paragraph is a flavour, nature and indication list with no status for the person, so the register is (b). (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, lower classic, entry 蚯蚓 *qiuyin*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 3867)

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The chapter is a list of five remedies for injuries and complaints and the Codex does not recommend them. The honest use of the entry is as a record. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Cut tendons and deep wounds:** need surgical and medical care; do not apply animal tissue.
- **Fever:** a recurring fever, particularly after travel to a malarial region, needs urgent medical testing.
- **Ear:** do not put boiled animal material or oil in the ear if there is pain, discharge or a possible perforation; seek medical care.
- **Urinary retention:** an inability to pass urine is an emergency.
- **Infection and hygiene:** worms from soil carry bacteria (including tetanus spores) and parasites; do not eat them.
- **Ethics:** the Codex does not recommend harming any animal for these uses.
