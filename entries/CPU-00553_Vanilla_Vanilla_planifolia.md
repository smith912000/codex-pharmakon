### Origin & Tradition

*Vanilla planifolia* is a perennial climbing orchid native to the lowland tropical forests of eastern Mexico and Central America. It is a vine — a thick, fleshy, green-stemmed liana that scrambles up host trees by adventitious roots, photosynthesising in its stems as much as its leaves. It is, by a wide margin, **the only orchid of major economic significance in world agriculture**; the Orchidaceae is one of the largest plant families on earth and contributes essentially nothing else to the food supply.

The **BM** tag denotes the Badianus manuscript — the *Libellus de Medicinalibus Indorum Herbis*, compiled at the Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco in **1552**, and known as the Codex de la Cruz-Badiano. Its structure is unusual and important for how we weight it: the herbal knowledge was supplied by **Martín de la Cruz**, an indigenous Nahua physician, and rendered into Latin by **Juan Badiano**, a Nahua translator. It is therefore an indigenous Mesoamerican medical text, but one filtered through Latin and through the interests of its Spanish recipients — a translated, mediated source, not a transparent window onto pre-conquest practice.

The Nahuatl name is **tlilxochitl**, conventionally glossed "black flower" — from *tlīlli* (black, and also ink or soot) and *xōchitl* (flower). The name describes the cured pod, not the bloom: the living flower is a pale greenish-yellow, and it is the fermented, blackened, oil-sheened bean that earns the epithet. This is a small but instructive case of a traditional name encoding the *processed* article rather than the living plant.

Domestication is generally credited first to the **Totonac** of the Veracruz region, with the Aztecs acquiring vanilla through tribute and trade rather than cultivating it themselves — a sequence that fits the general Mesoamerican pattern for lowland tropical crops. Its principal documented use was as a flavouring for the elite cacao beverage: vanilla belongs, historically, with the aromatic additives that made *cacahuatl* a prestige drink of the Nahua nobility rather than a common food. **Cross-reference the Cacao entry** for the beverage complex itself.

First, the specific identification of any given Badianus entry with *Vanilla planifolia*: Nahuatl-name-to-modern-species mapping in the Badianus manuscript is **genuinely contested** across the scholarly literature, and identifications made by different commentators disagree. Second, any specific medicinal indication attributed to tlilxochitl in the manuscript. This entry does **not** assert a particular Badianus prescription, because no such prescription was verified during this compilation. The tradition claim made here is limited to what is broadly supportable: Mesoamerican domestication, the Nahuatl name, and use as a flavouring of the elite cacao drink.

### Active Components

- **Vanillin** (4-hydroxy-3-methoxybenzaldehyde) — the dominant aroma compound and the principal phenolic. According to the *Food Chemistry* study below, "vanillin was the main phenolic."
- **Glucovanillin** (β-D-glucovanillin) — the **odourless glucoside precursor** stored in the green pod. This is the substrate of the entire curing process.
- **Vanillyl alcohol** — a secondary contributor; the same study identifies vanillin as forming by two routes, "β-D-glucovanillin hydrolysis **and** vanillyl alcohol oxidation." The single-pathway account is therefore incomplete.
- **Ethyl vanillin and its glucoside precursors** — historically assumed to be purely synthetic markers of adulteration. The 2025 *Molecules* study explicitly explored "the potential natural presence of ethyl vanillin and its corresponding glucoside precursors" in authentic pods. Treat the "ethyl vanillin proves fraud" heuristic as **under revision**.
- **Minor volatile organic compounds** — a broad accessory aroma fraction (the 2025 study profiled these as candidate biomarkers of botanical and geographical origin). These are why real vanilla and synthetic vanillin do not smell the same.
- **Guaiacol, α-cubebene, β-pinene** — detected in cultures of bean-colonising bacteria metabolising glucovanillin.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Vanilla is filed as **Foundation — Signature Study**, and its lesson is the mirror image of Lapacho's. Where Lapacho teaches that a celebrated doctrine can be empty, Vanilla teaches that **a traditional process can be exactly, mechanistically correct** — that a craft procedure worked out empirically over centuries can turn out to encode a precise enzymatic reaction.

The practitioner's use case is:

1. **The worked example of a validated traditional process.** The killing/sweating/drying cure is not superstition or mere drying. It is a controlled enzymatic hydrolysis, and the practitioner should be able to draw the chemistry.
2. **A culinary and olfactory material.** Vanilla's honest place in the Codex is as a food and aromatic, not a drug.
3. **A refusal exercise in the opposite direction from Lapacho** — resisting the temptation to medicalise something because it is pleasant and traditional.

**Refusals of the received doctrine, entered here as corrections:**

- **"Vanilla is an aphrodisiac."** REFUSED. No supporting evidence was retrieved. This claim descends from 18th–19th century European pharmacy and has no verified basis. It should not be repeated.
- **"Vanilla is a nervine / antidepressant / anxiolytic."** REFUSED as unsupported by anything retrieved here. Pleasant odours have psychological effects; that is not a pharmacological indication for a spice.
- **"Vanillin's antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties make vanilla therapeutic."** REFUSED as a leap. Vanillin's preclinical activity is real (below) but was demonstrated at rodent doses and in engineered delivery systems, not from culinary vanilla exposure.
- **"The curing enzyme is the plant's own β-glucosidase."** **PARTIALLY REFUSED — and this is a genuine correction.** See below.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Form | Material | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cured pod ("bean") | Whole cured fruit | The culinary article. Split, seeds scraped, pod infused. Food use; no medicinal dose exists. |
| Vanilla extract | Ethanolic maceration of cured pods | Food use. Note the ethanol content is itself relevant to the dermatitis discussion below. |
| Green (uncured) pod | Fresh fruit | **Odourless and of no culinary value.** Not a usable article. |
| Isolated vanillin | Purified compound | Flavour chemistry and research use. Rodent studies below used **30, 100 and 300 mg/kg** — animal doses; **not human doses, and not to be extrapolated.** |

**No human therapeutic dose is given, because none exists in the retrieved literature.** Vanilla is a food. Dosing language should not be applied to it.

**The cure itself — the actual "preparation" of consequence:**

1. **Killing** — the harvested green pod is scalded or otherwise shocked, arresting vegetative development and breaking down cell compartmentation.
2. **Sweating** — warm, humid holding, during which the enzymatic liberation occurs.
3. **Slow drying**, then **conditioning** — months of storage during which the aroma matures.

The biochemistry is confirmed and quantified: the *Food Chemistry* study found that high hydrostatic pressure treatment increased vanillin formation and β-D-glucosidase activity, with up to a **400% increase in β-D-glucosidase activity at 100–300 MPa**, attributed to "tissue decompartmentalization, and conformational changes induced by pressure." Decompartmentation is the whole principle: the enzyme and its substrate sit in separate compartments in the intact pod, and the cure's job is to bring them together under conditions that let the reaction run to completion.

**Cultivator's note — the pollination problem.** Outside its native range, *Vanilla planifolia* sets no fruit, because its natural pollinator is absent. Every commercial vanilla bean produced in Madagascar, Réunion, Indonesia or Uganda is the product of **manual pollination**, flower by flower, by hand, within the few hours the bloom is receptive. This single fact — not the curing, not the orchid's slow maturation — is the primary determinant of vanilla's price and its position as one of the most expensive agricultural commodities on earth. It also means vanilla cultivation outside Mesoamerica is not agriculture in the ordinary sense but a labour-intensive craft. **[USER: verify]** the identity of the native pollinator before naming a specific bee genus in print; the attribution to *Melipona* is widely repeated but has been challenged, and no source confirming it was retrieved this run.

**Adulteration economics.** Synthetic vanillin — manufactured from petrochemical or lignin feedstocks — is orders of magnitude cheaper than pod-derived vanilla, which creates sustained economic pressure toward fraud. The authentication literature reflects an arms race: the 2025 *Molecules* study states plainly that "increasingly sophisticated fraud techniques necessitate ongoing refinement of analytical methods." Stable isotope ratio analysis of carbon and hydrogen in vanillin is the established discriminating tool. **No specific percentage of adulterated product is claimed here, as no figure was verifiable.**

### Scientific Correlation

**Grading each claim:**

- **The curing doctrine — CONFIRMED, mechanism established.** Vanillin is liberated from glucovanillin by β-D-glucosidase during curing. Directly demonstrated. This is the entry's strongest evidential claim, and the hook survives verification intact.
- **The curing doctrine, first amendment — vanillin has TWO formation routes.** The *Food Chemistry* study identifies both glucovanillin hydrolysis and vanillyl alcohol oxidation. The single-pathway story taught in most flavour-chemistry summaries is a simplification.
- **The curing doctrine, second amendment — the enzyme is not purely the plant's. CONFIRMED CORRECTION.** Chen et al. (2015, *Applied and Environmental Microbiology*) showed that glucovanillin disperses from the inner to the outer part of the bean during curing, that "enzymatic hydrolysis was found to occur **on the surface** of the vanilla beans," and that transcripts of the β-D-glucosidase gene (*bgl*) **of colonizing microorganisms** were detected. The colonisers were predominantly *Bacillus*; GFP-tagged isolates were shown to colonise beans; and the isolates metabolised glucovanillin as sole carbon source within 24 hours, producing vanillin as the major volatile. The authors conclude that colonising *Bacillus* isolates produce the β-D-glucosidase mediating hydrolysis. **The cure is therefore partly a microbial fermentation, not solely a plant-autolytic process.** The traditional practice is even more sophisticated than the standard account gives it credit for — the sweating stage is managing a microbial community.
- **Vanillin antioxidant / anti-inflammatory activity — PRECLINICAL.** Described as established background in the rodent work below; no human trial retrieved.
- **Vanillin antiurolithic activity — PRECLINICAL (rat + in silico).** Khan et al. reported dose-dependent reduction of hyperoxaluria, hypercalciuria and renal crystal counts at 30–300 mg/kg in ethylene-glycol-induced hyperoxaluric rats, with molecular docking (binding affinity −7.6 kcal/mol). **Docking scores are computational predictions, not evidence of clinical effect.** The authors' framing of this as "a safe, effective and non-invasive option in modern medicine" is an overstatement of what a rat model supports, and the Codex records it as such.
- **Vanillin antibacterial / wound-healing activity — PRECLINICAL, and delivery-system dependent.** Shafeeq et al. loaded vanillin into Soluplus nanomicelles within a 3D-printed alginate-fucoidan scaffold, achieving inhibition zones of 21.4 ± 1.15 mm against *S. aureus* and 23.2 ± 0.9 mm against *E. coli*, with complete wound healing at 14 days in a rat model. **Note carefully: this is an engineered nanomicellar dressing. It is congener-adjacent evidence — the active is genuine vanillin, but the result belongs to the formulation, not to vanilla.**
- **Aphrodisiac / nervine / mood effects in humans — NULL.** No evidence retrieved. Graded NULL and refused above.
- **Any human clinical trial of vanilla or vanillin — NULL for the indications discussed here.** None retrieved.
- **"Vanillism" as a named occupational syndrome — NOT VERIFIED.** Per standing instruction, this hook is **partially refused.** A targeted PubMed search for vanillism returned **zero results**, and the Codex will not print the term as though it denoted a documented clinical entity. What *is* supportable is narrower: vanilla appears in the contact-dermatitis literature as one of several plant-derived flavouring allergens. Ramachandran et al. list "cinnamon, vanilla, citrus peels, and Balsam of Peru" among possible allergens in alcoholic beverages in a two-patient case report of systemic contact dermatitis — in which the authors concluded the likely culprit was **Balsam of Peru, not vanilla.** That is weak, indirect evidence of allergenic potential and nothing more. **[USER: verify]** the classical vanillism literature (early 20th-century occupational dermatology, likely not PubMed-indexed) before restoring the term.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Vanilla resists placement in the humoral systems. It has no Ayurvedic or TCM classification of any antiquity, having reached Asia only through post-Columbian trade. Nahua medicine had its own hot/cold axis, but the Badianus manuscript — a Latin translation produced for a Spanish audience — does not preserve that framework in recoverable form, and this compilation verified no energetic assignment for tlilxochitl within it.

Where 18th- and 19th-century European pharmacy classed vanilla as warming, stimulant and aphrodisiac, that classification was applied by analogy to other aromatic exotics and carried the era's general assumption that costly fragrant imports must be tonic. It is **inferred, not traditional testimony**, and the Codex records it only to mark it as unreliable.

The honest energetic observation is organoleptic: the cured pod is sweet-aromatic, warm-smelling, resinous and faintly balsamic — and the association between that sensory register and "warming" is the whole basis of the European classification. That is a real perceptual fact about the material and a poor basis for a therapeutic doctrine.

### Contraindications

**Vanilla is a food, and at culinary exposures it carries no meaningful toxicological concern.** The cautions below are correspondingly minor and are recorded for completeness rather than because vanilla is hazardous.

- **Allergic contact dermatitis — real but poorly characterised.** Vanilla appears in the contact-dermatitis literature as one of a group of plant-derived flavouring allergens alongside cinnamon and Balsam of Peru. Individuals with known Balsam of Peru sensitivity are the relevant risk group, as cross-reactivity within this fragrance/flavour cluster is the recognised pattern. **Note honestly that the case report retrieved attributed the reaction to Balsam of Peru, not vanilla.**
- **Occupational handler exposure** — plausible on the above basis, but the specific syndrome historically called "vanillism" was **not verifiable in PubMed this run** and is not asserted here. Handlers of large quantities of cured pods reporting skin complaints should be patch-tested rather than diagnosed from folklore.
- **Vanilla extract contains ethanol**, typically at substantial concentration. Relevant for anyone avoiding alcohol — those in recovery, some religious observances, and where it might interact with disulfiram or metronidazole. This is a real and frequently overlooked point.
- **Pregnancy and lactation** — culinary vanilla is unproblematic. **Isolated vanillin at pharmacological doses has no safety data in pregnancy and should not be used.**
- **Do not treat vanillin's preclinical profile as licence for supplementation.** The antiurolithic, antibacterial and anti-inflammatory findings are rodent, in-vitro and formulation-dependent. There is no established human dose, no human safety dataset at pharmacological exposure, and no indication.
- **Adulterated product carries unknown exposure.** Material sold as vanilla extract may contain synthetic vanillin, ethyl vanillin, caramel colour or undeclared additives. Where composition matters, isotope-ratio-authenticated material is the only reliable answer.
- **No contraindication is claimed for culinary use in any population**, including children — and the Codex declines to manufacture one.
