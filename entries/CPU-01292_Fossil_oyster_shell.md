# Fossil oyster shell (ostracites) — Ostrakites lithos (OSTRAKITES LITHOS), "Ostracites stone"
## *A shell-like, crusty, brittle stone used by women to remove hair, taken in wine to stop the menstrual flow, and applied with honey to inflamed breasts and cheek ulcers; the translator's heading reads "Fossilised Oyster Shells"* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Ostracites stone is chapter 165 of Book Five of the *De Materia Medica*, in the section "METALLIC STONES", headed OSTRAKITES LITHOS (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10688-10691; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` carries it as 5-165, line 10688, with the translator's identification "Fossilised Oyster Shells", class mineral; `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 891 has "Ostrakites Lithos", mineral, identification marked uncertain; the book's page index prints "Ostracites 824" and "Fossilised Oyster Shells 824"). The translator's line (line 10690) reads "SUGGESTED: Fossilised Oyster Shells". The chapter is five sentences (line 10691):

*What it is like.* "Ostracites stone is like a shell, crusty and easy to break." The stone is described by its appearance and its brittleness. The text does not say that it is a shell turned to stone; the translator's gloss does.

*As a depilatory.* "Women use it instead of pumice stone for taking off hair." The chapter on pumice has its own entry in the Codex (II_16); here the text only says that this stone is used as a substitute for it, by women, to remove hair.

*On the menstrual flow and conception.* "A teaspoonful (taken as a drink with wine) stops the menstrual flow. If anyone drinks two teaspoonfuls after the menstrual flow it prevents conception." Two amounts are therefore given: one teaspoonful, in wine, for stopping the flow; two teaspoonfuls, drunk "after the menstrual flow", to prevent conception. The text does not say how the stone is prepared before it is measured by the spoon (whether powdered or burnt), what quantity of wine is used, how long the effect is said to last, or how often it may be repeated. The word "teaspoonful" is the translator's, and the Greek measure behind it is not given in the English text. [USER: verify]

*On the breasts and cheeks.* "Applied with honey it soothes inflamed breasts, and stops gangrenous ulceration of the cheeks."

The chapter gives no place of origin, no colour, no grade or ranking, no test of the genuine stone, and no warning.

*The word elsewhere.* A kind of cadmia is called *ostracitis* in the cadmia chapter (5-84, line 10222), "slender and mostly black, with an earthy or baked clay outside"; that is a form of cadmia and is not this stone. The clay tile (5-178, *ostraka*) is another substance again and has its own entry. No other chapter was found that names ostracites, searching the text for "ostracit" and "fossil".

*Other traditions.* A search of the Tibetan text on disk (Clark 1995, ch.20) finds fossils of other creatures in the stone medicines, namely "man(y)jira" (fossil muscovite), "phag.mgo" (pig's head fossil) and "bye.mgo" (sparrow's head fossil) at lines 825-827 of `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, but none is an oyster shell, and they are not treated as the same substance here.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent beyond the translator's gloss. In general terms, oyster shell is largely calcium carbonate in the form of calcite, with a small amount of organic matter. In a fossil shell the original shell material may have been partly or wholly replaced by other minerals, such as silica, so a "fossil oyster" is not always chemically the same as a living shell. Calcium carbonate is an antacid and a source of calcium. The identification of the Greek stone is the translator's, and the text describes only its look and brittleness. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records a crusty stone taken in wine to stop menstrual flow, claimed (when taken after the flow) to prevent conception, and used on the skin as a depilatory and with honey on the breast and cheeks. The Codex does not recommend any of these. A stone dose does not stop bleeding or prevent pregnancy, and relying on it is a risk. The entry records what the text says and the doses it gives.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these amounts and forms, all at line 10691: "A teaspoonful (taken as a drink with wine)" to stop the menstrual flow; "two teaspoonfuls after the menstrual flow" as a drink, to prevent conception; the stone used for removing hair "instead of pumice stone"; and "applied with honey" to inflamed breasts and to gangrenous ulceration of the cheeks. No method of grinding or burning, no volume of wine, no proportion of honey, no frequency and no duration is given. Apart from the teaspoonful amounts above, no dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Calcium carbonate is the main ingredient of antacids and of calcium supplements, and ground shell is a common source of it. There is no evidence that swallowing a ground stone or shell stops menstrual bleeding or prevents conception; pregnancy is prevented by established contraceptive methods. Large or prolonged intakes of calcium carbonate can cause constipation and raise blood calcium. Pumice, which the text says it replaces for hair removal, is an abrasive volcanic glass; abrasion, not any drug action, is how it removes hair. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** The text prints no taste, hot or cold, dry or moist rating or degree for ostracites. The only quality-like words are "crusty" and "easy to break" (line 10691), which describe how the stone looks and breaks. No energetic classification is given.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is asserted here. The chapter describes the stone by its look and brittleness, in the manner of an identification, but it gives no provenance to rank, no assay and no adulteration test, so even the discipline of attention that other chapters offer is thin. It makes no claim about the person's constitution, vitality or long life, and its uses are depilatory, gynaecological and local.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The fossil shell is a depilatory stone and a doubtful drink for the menses that Dioscorides lists without comment. The Codex does not recommend using it as a contraceptive or to stop bleeding. What the chapter shows is how an ancient physician sets a stone beside a more familiar one (pumice) to say how it is used. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Contraception:** the text says two teaspoonfuls prevent conception; there is no evidence for this and it must not be relied on. Use an established contraceptive method.
- **Menstrual bleeding:** do not take any stone to stop a period; heavy, prolonged or unexpected bleeding needs medical assessment, and bleeding in pregnancy is an emergency.
- **Pregnancy:** a stone drunk "to stop the flow" may be taken in early pregnancy without the person knowing; there is no evidence of its safety, and it is unsuitable.
- **Skin and breasts:** do not apply stone dust and honey to infected, inflamed or ulcerated skin; breast inflammation and spreading ulcers need clinical care, and an infected breast in breastfeeding is an emergency if there is fever.
- **Hair removal:** rubbing with an abrasive stone damages skin and can infect it.
- **Calcium:** repeated large amounts of calcium carbonate cause constipation and raised blood calcium.
- **Identification:** "Fossilised Oyster Shells" is the translator's gloss; the text identifies the stone only by its look and brittleness.
