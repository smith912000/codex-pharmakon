# Pancratium maritimum — Panicration, the little squill (Dioscorides 2-203)

## *Pancratium maritimum, the first of two binomials on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 2-203 (which also prints Scilla pancratium — "Sea Pancratium Lily, Sea Daffodil"); a bulb Dioscorides sets beside the squill* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter, and no other tradition on disk is tagged for it. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *2-203. PANICRATION*, heading at line 6336, the SUGGESTED line at line 6337 and the text at line 6338, in Book Two among the "herbs with a sharp quality" (a running head after it). The whole chapter reads:

"Pancratium (also called the little squill) has a pale red or pale purple root similar to the great bulbus, and a bitter burning taste. The leaves are similar to lilies but longer. It has a similar strength and preparation as the squill and the same dose [is to be taken of it]. It is good for the same disorders but has a milder nature than squill. As a result the root (juiced and mixed with flour of ervum [2-129, 2-131] and formed into tablets) is effective given with honey water for the spleen, and dropsy."

The chapter is built on a comparison. It does not give the plant's own account of uses; it refers to the squill (the chapter just before, *2-202. SKILLA*, heading at line 6314) for "strength", "preparation", "dose" and "disorders", and says only that the little squill is milder. What is its own: a root "pale red or pale purple", resembling the "great bulbus" (a bulb, the chapter does not say which); a taste "bitter" and "burning"; leaves "similar to lilies but longer"; and a single preparation, the juiced root mixed with ervum flour into tablets and given with honey water for the spleen and for dropsy. The chapter gives no habitat, no season and no other name than "the little squill". The Greek heading and the first words show that Dioscorides thinks of the plant as a smaller kin of the squill.

**What the squill chapter adds.** The Codex reads the end of 2-202 (lines 6330 to 6335) to see what "the same dose" might mean. That passage describes how squill pieces are dried threaded on linen for making oil, wine and vinegar of squill, and gives, for squill, "Thirty grains (taken as a syrup with honey) is sufficient", and a caution that squill "must be avoided by those who have an inward ulceration". The first part of 2-202 was not read in full for this entry. Whether 2-203 means the thirty-grain figure when it says "the same dose" is not stated by the chapter; the Codex records the cross-reference and does not decide it.

**The chapters around it.** 2-204 *KAPPARIS* (the caper) follows at once and is a separate chapter; it is not carried over to this entry.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter id 2-203 and for the names *pancratium*, *panicration* and *pancration* found no existing entry on the same chapter. I_1145 (*Origanum heracleoticum*) mentions the word "pancration" only because it is one of the other names printed at the end of 3-31 *STOICHAS* (heading at line 6796; the name sits at line 6807: "It is also called syncliopa, alcibiades, pancration or styphonia"), which is a different plant, and it is not a duplicate. ### Active Components

The composition of the bulb Dioscorides describes was not read. As a general fact, *Pancratium* belongs to the Amaryllidaceae, a family whose bulbs are known to contain alkaloids (such as lycorine) and to be bitter and irritant when eaten; the squill proper (*Drimia maritima*) is a different family and contains cardiac glycosides. No constituent is named in the chapter. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a bitter, burning bulb prepared like squill, used for the spleen and dropsy, and judged milder than squill. The Codex gives no protocol for it. The bulb is described in the same terms as an acrid, potentially toxic drug, and the identification is a translators' suggestion; no one should gather or eat a wild bulb on this basis.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation of its own, with locus (line 6338): the root "juiced and mixed with flour of ervum and formed into tablets", given "with honey water" for the spleen and dropsy. It gives no number: its dose is "the same dose [is to be taken of it]" as squill, the bracketed words being the translators'. For squill the neighbouring chapter prints "Thirty grains (taken as a syrup with honey)" (read in 2-202, lines 6330 to 6335), but that sentence is about squill. No dose for pancratium is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that bitter, burning bulbs of the lily-like families can be strongly irritant or poisonous when eaten, and that dropsy (oedema) and an enlarged spleen are signs of disease that need medical assessment. The identification is a translators' suggestion, so the chapter does not show that the plant it describes is *Pancratium maritimum*.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints a taste ("bitter" and "burning") and a comparative strength ("a milder nature than squill"). It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality for 2-203.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter is short and entirely therapeutic; its one move that a student of attention can notice is the comparison itself: the root is known by colour, taste and leaf, and set against a better-known drug as its milder kin. It names no provenance beyond the comparison, no season of gathering, no assay and no adulteration. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a medicinal root, and the text records no amuletic use. No cultivation framing is attested.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this bulb. The abstentive answer is the honest one: it is a bitter, burning bulb described as a weaker version of a drug that the same book says is to be avoided in some conditions.

### Contraindications

The bulb is described as bitter and burning, and the book's squill is cautioned against in inward ulceration (2-202). Dropsy and a swollen spleen are conditions for medical care. Wild bulbs of this kind should not be eaten or tableted. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
