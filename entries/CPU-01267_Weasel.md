# Weasel — GALE KATOIKIDIOS, "the household weasel" (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 2-27)
## *The household weasel, which the translation's gloss gives as Putorius nivalis, family Mustelidae; the weasel's blood and burnt body are also used in the chapter* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Chapter 2-27 of *De Materia Medica*, headed GALE KATOIKIDIOS, stands in Book 2 among the "Living Creatures" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 5043-5046; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 217, which records the translator's identification "Putorius nivalis, Mustelidae — Weasel"). I read the whole chapter. It reads, in the English of this edition:

"The household weasel is burned over flames after the bowels are taken out, salted, and afterwards dried in the shade and kept a long time. It is a very effective remedy (taken as a drink of two teaspoonfuls in wine) against all kinds of snakes. It is taken the same way as an antidote to poisoning. The belly of the weasel is stuffed with coriander and kept until it is old, then taken in a drink to help those bitten by poisonous beasts, and the epileptic. It is burnt complete together in a pot and the ashes rubbed on with vinegar for gout. The blood rubbed on helps scrofulous tumours [glandular swelling, goitres]. It is also good for epilepsy."

The chapter therefore gives four preparations and a run of conditions. The preparations are the whole animal gutted, burned over flames, salted and dried in the shade (kept a long time) and taken in wine; the belly stuffed with coriander and kept until old, taken in a drink; the whole animal burnt in a pot, the ashes rubbed on with vinegar; and the blood, rubbed on. The conditions are snakebite, poisoning, the bites of "poisonous beasts", epilepsy, gout, and scrofulous tumours (the bracketed gloss "glandular swelling, goitres" is the translator's). The chapter sits among a run of creatures used against venom: the next chapter after it, 2-28, makes frogs "antidotes against the poisons of all snakes" (line 5050), and the mullet is said to heal the hurt of the sea dragon, scorpion and spider (line 5020).

The index of the same edition lists "blood of a weasel" at pages 526 and 600 and "blood of the weasel" at page 600 (line 10865), which are not the pages of chapter 2-27 in the edition's own listing (the index puts GALE KATOIKIDIOS at page 194, line 12147). I searched the text for "weasel" and found only chapter 2-27. Whether the index points to uses under other headings, such as the weasel's blood in a compound, I could not tell. [USER: verify]

*Other texts on disk, not folded in.* The Tibetan Explanatory Tantra lists the weasel with the carnivorous animals in its classification of meats ("the tiger, leopard, bear, snow bear, snow leopard, wolf, lynx, fox, hill dog and weasel", Clark 1995, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 672); that is a food classification of the animal's flesh and not a medicinal entry, and the line is reported to the editor as a positive locus for a possible FMT tag, not applied. In Yang's 1998 Shennong translation a note explaining "spiritual matter" says that "weasels and foxes were believed to be possessed by malevolent spirits which might negatively afflict human beings" (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt` line 1087); it is a translator's note on another substance, not an entry for the weasel. The Sushruta list has *Nakula* (mongoose; `_sources/sushruta.csv` line 228), an animal I do not identify with the weasel. None of these traditions is on this entry's tag line.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. Dioscorides' preparations use the whole dried or burnt animal, the blood, and coriander. In general terms, a dried and salted animal is protein and salt; burning an animal produces ash, which is mainly mineral. Nothing links any constituent to the uses given.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records the weasel as a snakebite and antidote remedy, and as a drink or application for epilepsy, gout and glandular swellings. The Codex does not recommend any of it. Snakebite and poisoning are emergencies that need prompt medical care, epilepsy is managed with proper treatment, and a salted, burned or blood-based animal preparation adds infection risk and offers nothing of known value. Weasels are wild animals protected in many places. The entry exists to record what Dioscorides says.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations are those quoted from line 5046. The only quantity Dioscorides' English gives is for the first: "taken as a drink of two teaspoonfuls in wine" (a rendering by the translator, not a verified measure). The coriander-stuffed belly, the ash with vinegar and the blood have no quantity or course. The preservation steps are exact: gut, burn over flames, salt, dry in the shade, keep a long time. No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Snakebite is treated with antivenom and supportive care, not with animal-derived drinks; epilepsy is a seizure disorder treated by clinicians; gout is a disorder of uric acid deposition in the joints; a "scrofulous tumour" in the old sense is a chronic swelling of lymph glands, often tuberculous. Wild mammals can carry infections that pass to people, and salting and drying does not make an animal body safe to take. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM):** None is printed. Chapter 2-27 gives no quality (warming, cooling, drying, astringent) for the weasel, only the preparations and the indications quoted above.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. The Galenic three spirits are later than Dioscorides, and he assigns the weasel no status beyond therapy. What the chapter offers a practitioner is a discipline of attention to preparation: the animal is gutted, burned, salted, dried in the shade and *kept a long time*; the stuffed belly is kept "until it is old". The chapter gives no sensory assay, no place of origin and no mark of a good specimen, so there is nothing to test the quality of the weasel against. Dioscorides sometimes records amuletic uses while distancing himself; none is recorded in 2-27. The uses are therapeutic, for poison, epilepsy, gout and swellings, and the chapter assigns no cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The weasel is a snakebite and epilepsy remedy in a book of "living creatures", prepared by gutting, burning, salting and long keeping, and the Codex does not recommend it. If anything is to be taken from the chapter it is the habit of recording exactly how a substance was prepared and how long it was kept. No dose beyond the translator's "two teaspoonfuls in wine" is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Snakebite and poisoning:** emergencies; call emergency services; do not delay for an animal remedy.
- **Epilepsy:** do not replace prescribed treatment with any animal preparation.
- **Infection and hygiene:** burnt, salted or blood-based preparations from wild animals can carry bacteria and parasites.
- **Skin and open lesions:** do not rub blood or ash on broken skin or swellings.
- **Conservation, law and ethics:** weasels are wild animals; taking them may be illegal, and the Codex does not recommend it.
