# Myrobalan citrina — Balanos Murepsike / Balaninon Elaion, the "Arabic myrobalan" and its oil (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 1-40 and 4-160); the Latin is the translator's first candidate

## *The nut and expressed oil Dioscorides calls balanos (balanos aurepsike, "balanum auripsike"), a tree fruit "like hazelnut" whose kernel yields a liquid used for ointments; the 2000 English edition's SUGGESTED lines for both chapters print "Myrobalan citrina, Terminalia citrina — Hara Nut Tree" first, with Balanites aegyptica (Egyptian balsam, desert date) as the second candidate. The Codex row follows the first name; the text's own details (Ethiopia, Egypt, Arabia, Petra; kernel pressed for an ointment oil) point as readily to Balanites, so the identification is of low confidence and the row is kept apart from Terminalia chebula (Haritaki)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The text is the 2000 English edition of *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). "Myrobalan citrina" is not Dioscorides' word; it is the translator's pre-Linnaean phrase-name, printed in the SUGGESTED lines of 1-40 (line 3873) and 4-160 (line 9477), and in the index (line 16647: "Myrobalan citrina 41, 715"; "Terminalia citrina 41, 715", line 17838). The row was opened on 13 October as an unverified placeholder from that string. An audit on 30 October kept it as a separate entry from the chebulic myrobalan (CPU-00095, `I_124_Haritaki_Terminalia_chebula.md`).

**Dioscorides (DMM), 4-160.** *Locus: Book 4, chapter 160, heading BALANOS AUREPSIKE (line 9475), "acorns which are plucked" (line 9479, translator's gloss).* The text (lines 9480–9484): "Balanum auripsike is the fruit of a tree like myrica, similar to hazelnut. That within is pressed like bitter almonds, and it yields a liquid that they use for precious ointments instead of oil. It grows in Ethiopia, Egypt, Arabia, and in Petra, a town in Judaea. That which is new, full, white, and easily peeled is the best." The uses follow: "This, pounded into small pieces and a teaspoonful taken in a drink with posca [hot drinks] reduces the spleen, and it is also laid on it with lolium [2-116, 4-140] meal. It is used with honey and water on gout. Boiled with vinegar it raises out scabies [itchy parasitical disease] and leprosy. It is used with saltpetre [potassium nitrate] for vitiligines [form of leprosy] and black scars. With urine it takes away freckles, varicose veins, sunburn, and pustules on the face. With honey water it induces vomiting, and loosens the intestines, but is very bad for the stomach. The oil (taken as a drink) is astringent to the bowels. The bark is more astringent. The dregs that are left from that which was pounded and strained out are mixed with sebaceous treatments for roughness and itchiness." (the last sentence is on the next page, line 9486). Bracketed glosses are the translator's.

**Dioscorides (DMM), 1-40.** *Locus: Book 1, chapter 40, heading BALANINON ELAION, "oil of balanos" (line 3871).* The text (lines 3876–3877): "In the same way oil balaninum is prepared. It has strength to clean spots, freckles, down on the face, and darkening cataracts and it purges the intestines. It is bad for the stomach. Poured in with goose fat it is good for earache, ear resonance and tinnitus." "In the same way" refers back to the oils of the chapters before it (the neighbouring chapter 1-39, bitter-almond oil, lines 3859–3860, describes pounding and pressing); the text of 1-40 itself gives no method. Its SUGGESTED line lists three things: *Myrobalan citrina*; *Quercus infectoria* and *Quercus lusitanica* ("Acorns from Quercus species — Gall Oak"); and *Balanites aegyptica* ("Thorn Tree, Egyptian Balsam, Zachum Oil Tree"); and refers to 4-143 and 4-160. The two chapters therefore treat the nut and its oil.

**A third mention.** In the date-palm chapter 1-148 (line 4604) the fruit is said to be "similar to the Arabic myrobalan [1-40, 4-160]". So Dioscorides' own cross-reference calls this fruit the "Arabic myrobalan"; the Latin *Terminalia* is not in his text.

**Identity.** The Greek *balanos* means an acorn-like nut. The 2000 edition gives *Terminalia citrina* first for both chapters; the same edition gives *Balanites aegyptiaca* second. Nothing in the two chapters decides between them. The places named (Ethiopia, Egypt, Arabia, Petra) are all in the range of the desert date, a thorny tree with an oil-bearing kernel [USER: verify]; *Terminalia citrina* is, as far as the Codex knows, a tree of southern Asia [USER: verify]. The Codex therefore records low confidence, and neither is stated as fact. [USER: verify] The Haritaki entry (*T. chebula*) is a separate subject and is not merged here.

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, Terminalia fruits are known to be rich in tannins, which are astringent, and the kernel of the desert date is oil-bearing. Which applies to Dioscorides' nut depends on the identification, which is not settled. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.** Dioscorides records the nut as a spleen remedy, a skin cleanser (spots, freckles, scabies, vitiligo), a drunk emetic and purgative that is "very bad for the stomach", and the oil as a precious ointment base and an eardrop (with goose fat). The Codex gives no protocol and recommends nothing. The emetic and purgative actions are the ones that matter for safety, and the text itself warns of the stomach.

### Preparation & Dosing

*Quoted from the text, with loci.* 4-160: the kernel "pounded into small pieces and a teaspoonful taken in a drink with posca [hot drinks]" for the spleen (line 9482); laid on the spleen with lolium meal; "with honey and water" for gout; boiled with vinegar for scabies and leprosy; with saltpetre for vitiligo and black scars; with urine for freckles, sunburn and facial pustules; "with honey water" it induces vomiting and loosens the bowels; the oil is drunk (astringent to the bowels); the bark is "more astringent"; the dregs go into "sebaceous treatments". 1-40: the oil, applied for spots, freckles, facial down and "darkening cataracts"; "poured in with goose fat" for earache and tinnitus. The only measured dose is the teaspoonful in 4-160; no other dose, duration or quantity is given. No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Tannin-rich fruits are astringent. Many oil-bearing seeds yield an oil used in cosmetics and ointments. Emetic and purgative preparations can cause dehydration and electrolyte loss. Nothing in the chapters is a recognised treatment for leprosy (Hansen's disease), which needs specific medical therapy, or for cataract, tinnitus or gout. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** no warming or cooling quality is printed for the nut or oil in either chapter. 4-160 prints that the oil is "astringent to the bowels" and the bark "more astringent"; 1-40 prints that the oil "purges the intestines" and is "bad for the stomach"; 4-160 prints that the nut "loosens the intestines, but is very bad for the stomach".
- No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured here. The two chapters assign no status for long life or the person's constitution, and 4-160 itself warns that the nut is "very bad for the stomach". What the chapters offer a cultivator is a discipline of attention: 4-160 records the provenance (Ethiopia, Egypt, Arabia, Petra) and a sensory assay of the best nut ("new, full, white, and easily peeled"), and notes that the oil goes to precious ointments.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a record of an imported nut and oil, with a stomach warning in the text itself, and an identification that the translator leaves open between a southern-Asian tree and a desert tree.

### Contraindications

- **Stomach:** the text itself says the nut and oil are "bad" or "very bad for the stomach".
- **Emetic and purgative action:** vomiting and loosening of the bowels can dehydrate; do not use.
- **Leprosy, scabies, vitiligo, cataract, tinnitus, gout:** medical conditions; use proper care.
- **Urine and saltpetre applications:** unsanitary or irritant; the Codex does not endorse them.
- **Identity:** low confidence; do not gather or use any tree on the strength of this entry.
- **Pregnancy, children:** no statement in the sources retrieved. Avoid. [USER: verify]
