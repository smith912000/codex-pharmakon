# I-269: Glycyrrhiza glabra / uralensis (Licorice / Gan Cao)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-269 | Fabaceae (Leguminosae)**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomials:**
- *Glycyrrhiza glabra* L. — Mediterranean/Western Licorice (primary European and CM species)
- *Glycyrrhiza uralensis* Fisch. — Ural Licorice (*Gān Cǎo* 甘草; primary TCM species)
- *Glycyrrhiza inflata* Bat. — Chinese licorice; minor

**Family:** Fabaceae (Leguminosae)

**Common Names:**
- English: Licorice, Liquorice (British), Sweet Root
- Mandarin: *Gān Cǎo* (甘草) — "sweet grass" — one of the most important herbs in all TCM
- Arabic: *'Irq al-sūs* (عرق السوس) — "root of the Sus [plant]"; Sūs = historical name; also *'Ūd al-sūs*
- Greek: *Glykyrrhiza* (γλυκύρριζα) — "sweet root" (glykys = sweet; rhiza = root); this Greek name became the Latin genus name
- Hindi: *Mulethi* (मुलेठी)
- Sanskrit: *Yashtimadhu* (यष्टिमधु) — "sweet stick"
- Tibetan: *Shing mngar* — "sweet wood"

**Etymology:**
The Greek *glykyrrhiza* (sweet root) is the direct source of both the genus name and the English "liquorice" / "licorice" (via Old French *licoresse*, Latin *liquiritia*, distortion of *glycyrrhiza*). Multiple independent naming traditions converge on the same descriptor — sweetness — because glycyrrhizin (the primary saponin) is 50× sweeter than sucrose, the most distinctive organoleptic property of the root.

**Species Distinction:**
- *G. glabra*: Mediterranean basin, Middle East, Afghanistan, Central Asia; classical Western and Islamic medicine species; smooth leaflets (*glabra* = "smooth"); primary confectionery species
- *G. uralensis*: Siberia, Mongolia, Northern China; TCM primary species; "Ural" = Ural Mountains; slightly different glycyrrhizin concentration and flavonoid profile

**Morphology:**
Herbaceous perennial; 1–1.5 m; pinnate leaves; pale blue-violet pea flowers; horizontal stolons and vertical roots — the medicinal part; roots 3–5 years to harvest maturity; may run several meters horizontally and reach considerable depth.

**Parts Used:** Root and rhizome (dried; raw as *Sheng Gan Cao* in TCM; honey-prepared as *Zhi Gan Cao* in TCM — different applications).

**TCM Processed Form (*Zhi Gan Cao* — Honey-Fried Licorice):**
In Chinese medicine, *Zhi Gan Cao* (炙甘草, honey-baked licorice) has distinct properties from raw *Sheng Gan Cao* (生甘草):
- *Sheng Gan Cao*: Clears heat; detoxifies; more anti-inflammatory; higher liquiritin content
- *Zhi Gan Cao*: Strengthens Spleen Qi; tonifies; "harmonizes all herbs in a formula"; gentler and more tonic; less anti-inflammatory; *Zhi Gan Cao Tang* — a classical cardiac arrhythmia formula

**8/9** — exceptional; maximum possible within geographic range. All 8 accessible Old World traditions confirm licorice as a primary medicinal herb.

**Status as "Harmonizer" Across Traditions:**
The concept of licorice as an herb that "harmonizes" or "modifies" other herbs appears independently in:
- TCM: *Gān Cǎo* harmonizes harsh herbs; reduces toxicity of other herbs; appears in >50% of classical formulas
- Ayurveda: *Yashtimadhu* as *anupāna* (carrier herb) that enhances absorption of other drugs
- Islamic medicine: used as excipient in compound medications; improves palatability

This cross-traditional "harmonizer" role is unique among major herbs and reflects glycyrrhizin's dual anti-inflammatory + sweet-masking properties: it literally makes bitter preparations more palatable while reducing inflammatory damage potential.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domains:**
1. **Respiratory / anti-tussive / expectorant** — All 8 traditions: cough; throat; bronchitis; mucus liquefaction; the most consistent single application
2. **Anti-inflammatory / anti-ulcer** — All traditions: gastric; oral; systemic; validated by glycyrrhizinic acid mechanism
3. **Liver / hepatoprotective** — DMM, CM, SBJ, SS: liver support; liver disease prevention
4. **Tonifying / qi-supplementing** — SBJ, SS, FMT: energy; vitality; adrenal support
5. **Detoxifying** — SBJ, CM, DMM: modifies toxicity of other herbs/drugs; antidote properties

**Unique Indication:**
- **Pseudo-aldosterone effect (licorice-induced):** Glycyrrhizin inhibits 11β-HSD2 (11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2), preventing cortisol from being inactivated → elevated effective mineralocorticoid activity → sodium retention, potassium loss, hypertension. This is the basis of both the traditional "strengthening" and "warming" effects AND the primary adverse effect of excess licorice. This mechanism was discovered in 20th century endocrinology but was empirically observed in traditional medicine as a "warming-strengthening" effect on the body.

**Energetic Classification:**
- ◉ Qi (functional): Primary — Lung Qi (respiratory); Spleen Qi (digestive/tonifying)
- ◈ Jing (structural): Secondary — hepatoprotective; adrenal adaptogenic
- ⊕ Yuan (constitutional): Tertiary — *Zhi Gan Cao* in cardiac formulas; adrenal HPA axis modulation

**Primary Tier: ◉ Qi** — multi-system Qi harmonization; universally applicable

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Triterpenoid Saponins (Primary Active Class)

**1. Glycyrrhizin (Glycyrrhizinic Acid) — 2–25% dry root weight**
- The defining compound of licorice; responsible for sweetness (50× sucrose) and most pharmacological effects
- Triterpenoid saponin glycoside: glycyrrhetic acid (aglycone) + 2 glucuronic acid moieties
- After oral administration: gut bacteria cleave glucuronic acids → 18β-glycyrrhetinic acid (primary active form)
- **11β-HSD2 inhibition** (mineralocorticoid side effect mechanism)
- **NF-κB inhibition** (primary anti-inflammatory mechanism)
- **Antiviral:** inhibits multiple viruses; SARS-CoV entry inhibition demonstrated

**2. 18β-Glycyrrhetinic Acid (GA)**
- Active metabolite; primary systemic form after GI absorption
- 11β-HSD2 inhibitor: key for both therapeutic and adverse effects

### Flavonoids

**3. Liquiritin (Liquiritigenin-4'-glucoside)**
- Primary flavanone of licorice; antidepressant-like effects (serotonin pathway modulation)
- Estrogenic activity (phytoestrogenic — weak agonist); relevant to menopausal applications
- MAO-A inhibition (mild)

**4. Isoliquiritin (Chalcone glycoside)**
- Estrogenic; antidepressant; anti-platelet

**5. Glabridin (Isoflavone — *G. glabra* specific)**
- Tyrosinase inhibitor → skin whitening / hyperpigmentation treatment (cosmetic application)
- Anti-atherosclerotic; LDL oxidation inhibition; anti-inflammatory
- Estrogenic (ERα agonist; stronger phytoestrogen than liquiritin)

**6. Licoflavone A, B, C** — anti-microbial; anti-cancer (apoptosis)

**7. Licochalcone A** — specific to *G. uralensis*; antibacterial; anti-leishmanial; anti-malarial

### Other

**8. Glycyrrhizol** — anti-fungal

**9. Polysaccharides (Glycyrrhiza polysaccharides)**
- Immunomodulatory; complement activation; macrophage activation; validates "harmonizing immune system" applications

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### 1. Anti-Inflammatory (Glycyrrhizin / GA / NF-κB)
- Glycyrrhizin: NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation inhibition → reduced TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8
- 18β-GA: inhibits iNOS (inducible nitric oxide synthase) → reduced inflammatory NO production
- Combined COX-2 downregulation; PGE₂ reduction
- Validates anti-inflammatory use across all 8 traditions (respiratory, GI, hepatic)

### 2. Pseudo-Aldosterone / Mineralocorticoid (11β-HSD2 Inhibition)
- 18β-GA: irreversibly inhibits 11β-HSD2 (enzyme that inactivates cortisol → cortisone in kidney)
- Cortisol (abundant) now acts on mineralocorticoid receptors (normally restricted to aldosterone access)
- Effect: sodium retention; potassium loss; increased blood pressure
- **Traditional observation:** "Warming, strengthening, energy-increasing" effects = clinical experience of elevated cortisol activity
- **Adverse effect:** hypertension, hypokalemia, pseudohyperaldosteronism at >100 mg glycyrrhizin/day (see Safety)
- **Therapeutic application:** Addison's disease (adrenal insufficiency); postural hypotension; chronic fatigue with hypocortisolism — using the 11β-HSD2 inhibition therapeutically

### 3. Antiviral
- Glycyrrhizin: inhibits viral protein synthesis; membrane-active; disrupts viral attachment
- Documented activity: SARS-CoV (2003), MERS-CoV, influenza A, HIV-1, EBV, herpes simplex
- Intravenous glycyrrhizin used in Japanese hospitals for viral hepatitis (chronic hepatitis B, C)

### 4. Hepatoprotective
- Glycyrrhizin IV (Stronger Neo-Minophagen-C): significantly reduces hepatic inflammation in chronic viral hepatitis; reduces progression to cirrhosis
- Mechanism: NF-κB inhibition in hepatocytes; antiviral; anti-fibrotic (TGF-β reduction)
- Level B for oral; Level A for IV formulation (Japanese pharmaceutical product)

### 5. Respiratory / Expectorant
- Glycyrrhizin: increases bronchial secretion viscosity reduction → mucolytic
- Liquiritin: smooth muscle relaxation → bronchodilation
- Anti-inflammatory effect on bronchial mucosa; reduces mucosal edema

### 6. Antidepressant / Anxiolytic (Liquiritin)
- Liquiritin: MAO-A inhibition (mild) + serotonin pathway modulation
- Reduces forced swim test immobility in rats (validated antidepressant-like effect)
- TCM "Gan Mai Da Zao Tang" (Licorice, Wheat, Jujube Decoction): classical antidepressant/anxiolytic formula; liquiritin primary active

### 7. Gastric / Anti-Ulcer
- Glycyrrhizin + polysaccharides: protective mucus layer stimulation (mucus secretagogue)
- 18β-GA: inhibits *H. pylori* growth (MIC 2–16 μg/mL)
- DGL (Deglycyrrhizinated Licorice): glycyrrhizin removed → anti-ulcer activity maintained via mucus stimulation; cardiovascular side effects eliminated

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Exceptional evidence base for a traditional herb.**

**Hepatoprotective (Chronic Viral Hepatitis B/C):**
- **Intravenous Glycyrrhizin (Stronger Neo-Minophagen-C / SNMC):** Multiple Japanese RCTs; significantly reduces ALT/AST; reduces fibrosis progression; reduces hepatocellular carcinoma risk
- **Evidence Grade: Level A** (IV formulation; multiple RCTs; Japanese Pharmaceutical standard of care)
- Oral: smaller studies; less consistent
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (oral)

**Gastric Ulcer / GERD:**
- **DGL (Deglycyrrhizinated Licorice) chewable tablets:** RCT showing comparable efficacy to cimetidine for peptic ulcer (Kassir 1985); multiple positive trials
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (DGL)

**Antidepressant / Anxiety (*Gan Mai Da Zao Tang*):**
- Multiple Chinese RCTs for depression and anxiety; most in Chinese literature; moderate quality
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (traditional formula RCTs)

**Aphthous Ulcer (Oral):**
- Licorice mucoadhesive patch: RCT showed reduced pain and healing time for mouth ulcers
- **Evidence Grade: Level B**

**Respiratory (Expectorant, Anti-tussive):**
- Long traditional use; few modern isolated RCTs; included in multiple respiratory formulas studied
- **Evidence Grade: Level C**

**Anti-Hypertensive (Inverse — Adverse at High Dose):**
- High-dose licorice (>100 mg glycyrrhizin/day) *causes* hypertension — this is established
- **Evidence Grade: Level A** (for adverse cardiovascular effect)

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The Sweetest Medicinal Root:**
Licorice's defining sensory quality — the sweetness — is pharmacologically active, not incidental. Glycyrrhizin's 50× sucrose sweetness creates an immediate palatability that has driven licorice into confectionery (licorice candy, anise candy, black licorice) simultaneously with its medical applications. This dual food-medicine identity means many people have experienced licorice's acute pharmacological effects without ever taking it as a medicine.

**Oral Experience:**
- Raw root: extraordinary sweetness; clean, slightly earthy sweetness; then trailing anise-like note; sweet lingers for 15–30 minutes (glycyrrhizin slow oral absorption from mucosa); mild astringency
- Decoction: sweet, mildly bitter-sweet; warming; soothing to throat immediately on contact; the anti-tussive effect on sore throat (coat-and-soothe) is subjectively rapid
- Black licorice candy (glycyrrhizin present): distinct flavor; love-or-hate response; the 50× sweetness plus anise creates distinctive flavor memory; consumption patterns are often habitual

**TCM Formula Experience (*Zhi Gan Cao Tang* — Cardiac Arrhythmia):**
Patients with irregular heartbeat (palpitations, irregular pulse) given *Zhi Gan Cao Tang* (Honey-Fried Licorice Decoction) classically report: within 2–4 weeks, the sense of cardiac irregularity diminishing; pulse smoothing; reduced fatigue. This is among the few classical TCM formulas with direct cardiac electrophysiological effects (glycyrrhizin K⁺ channel effects; licorice + ginger + jujube + other components).

**The "Harmonizer" Experience:**
Herbalists and practitioners who work with TCM compound formulas report that *Gān Cǎo* reliably makes bitter formulas more palatable, reduces nausea and GI discomfort from harsh herbs, and subjectively "rounds" the effects of the formula. Whether this is pharmacodynamic (anti-inflammatory cushioning), pharmacokinetic (CYP modulation affecting absorption of other herbs), or palatability-compliance mediated is difficult to disentangle — and may not need disentangling.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Root decoction:**
- 3–6 g dried root in 300 mL water; simmer 15–20 minutes; drink as tea
- Daily dose: 1–2 cups; duration: 4–8 weeks for anti-inflammatory/respiratory
- **DOSE-LIMIT CRITICAL:** Do not exceed 5–6 g/day of whole root (equivalent to ~100 mg glycyrrhizin) for chronic use; acute/short-term use can tolerate more

**DGL (Deglycyrrhizinated Licorice) — for GI/ulcer:**
- 380–1,140 mg DGL extract: chewed before meals; ulcer / GERD / gastritis
- Cardiovascular effects removed by deglycyrrhizination; safe for hypertensives and those with potassium sensitivity
- Chewable preferred over capsule for ulcer application (mucosal contact important)

**Licorice throat lozenges:**
- OTC; 10–50 mg glycyrrhizin per lozenge; anti-tussive; sore throat; safe at standard doses
- Short-course use (< 2 weeks): no concern; avoid habitual long-term use

**TCM compound formula (practitioner-guided):**
- *Zhi Gan Cao* (honey-fried): 3–6 g in formula; tonic applications; harmonizer
- *Sheng Gan Cao* (raw): 3–6 g in formula; anti-inflammatory; heat-clearing

**IV Glycyrrhizin (pharmaceutical):**
- 100–200 mg/day IV: chronic viral hepatitis (SNMC, Japan); medical supervision only; not self-administered

### Dose Limits and Safety Monitor

**Glycyrrhizin Thresholds:**
- **Safe chronic**: < 100 mg/day glycyrrhizin (= ~5–6 g dried root)
- **Risk zone**: 100–400 mg/day glycyrrhizin; BP and K⁺ monitoring
- **High risk**: > 400 mg/day; pseudohyperaldosteronism risk significant; UK FSA TDI = 1.5 mg/kg/day

**European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) position:**
- Licorice confectionery containing > 100 mg glycyrrhizin per portion must carry warnings in EU
- "Persons with hypertension should avoid consuming large quantities of licorice"

### Safety Profile

**CRITICAL adverse effect: Pseudohyperaldosteronism at excess doses.**

**Signs and symptoms:**
- Hypertension (sometimes severe; > 180/110 mmHg)
- Hypokalemia (muscle weakness; cardiac arrhythmia; K⁺ < 3.0 mEq/L)
- Water retention (edema, weight gain)
- Metabolic alkalosis

**Populations requiring extra caution:**
- Pre-existing hypertension
- Heart failure (fluid retention additive)
- Renal insufficiency (impaired potassium handling)
- Corticosteroid users (additive cortisol enhancement)
- Diuretic users (K⁺ depletion additive with thiazides/loop diuretics)
- Pregnancy: glycyrrhizin associated with premature birth in prospective Finnish cohort (Räikkönen 2017); **AVOID > 100 mg glycyrrhizin/day in pregnancy**

**Drug Interactions (Critical):**
- **Antihypertensive drugs:** Antagonism — licorice raises BP while drugs lower it; large amounts may overcome antihypertensive therapy
- **Digoxin:** Hypokalemia from licorice increases digoxin toxicity risk (hypokalemia sensitizes myocardium to digoxin); **AVOID combination with significant licorice consumption**
- **Corticosteroids:** Additive anti-inflammatory but also additive sodium retention and K⁺ loss
- **Spironolactone / Eplerenone (aldosterone antagonists):** Pharmacological opposition; licorice-induced pseudo-aldosteronism may overcome these medications

**DGL (Deglycyrrhizinated Licorice):**
- Most cardiovascular and electrolyte adverse effects eliminated by removing glycyrrhizin
- DGL safe for hypertensives; safe in pregnancy (at standard doses)
