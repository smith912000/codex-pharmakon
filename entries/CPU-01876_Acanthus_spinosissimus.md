# Acanthus spinosissimus — Akantha agria AKANTHA AGRIA, wild acantha (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 3-20); Romans: acantha sylvestris, spina agrestis

## *Acanthus spinosissimus, the white-spined acanthus, a spiny, thistle-like Mediterranean herb; the identification is the English edition's ("Acanthus spinosissimus — White-spined Akantha"), the species is of medium confidence only, since the chapter describes the plant just as "wild" and "prickly", and it is kept apart from Acanthus spinosus (CPU-01877) because the edition lists them under different chapters* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named by Dioscorides among the texts on disk, in chapter 3-20.** This is a single-text entry. The row was added on 13 October as an unverified placeholder from the Dioscorides name list (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 426). The name list marks it "animal"; that is an extraction error, and the spine has the class corrected to plant. A first pass this month proposed merging the row with CPU-01877 (*Acanthus spinosus*); the audit disagreed, and the two stay apart. This entry replaces the placeholder. A search of the other texts on disk for the Greek name and for *Acanthus* found nothing else for this plant.

**Dioscorides, De Materia Medica (DMM).** The chapter is 3-20, AKANTHA AGRIA, in Book Three, `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading at line 6698. The editor's line reads: "Acanthus spinosissimus — White-spined Akantha". The whole chapter is short (lines 6699-6701): "The Romans call acantha sylvestris by the name of spina agrestis. There is also a wild acantha, similar to carduus nutans [musk thistle] — prickly, shorter than the garden variety that is cultivated. The root of this affects as many things as the previous one."

Three things follow from the text alone. (1) The chapter is built as an appendix to the one before it: "wild" (*agria*) against the "garden variety that is cultivated" of 3-19. (2) The only description is that the plant is "similar to carduus nutans", prickly, and shorter than the garden kind. (3) The uses are by reference. "The previous one" is 3-19, AKANTHA ERPEKANTHA, where the root is described as follows (heading at line 6689): "The roots underneath are viscous, mouldy, reddish and long. Applied, they are good for burns and dislocations. Taken in a drink they encourage urine and stop discharges of the bowels. They are good for tuberculosis of the lungs, hernia, and convulsions. It is also called melamphyllon, pasderota, acanthus topiaria, mamolaria, or craepula." The wild kind's root is said to "affect" as many things, which is Dioscorides' claim that the two roots are of similar power; the text does not list a use of its own.

The editor's name "Acanthus spinosissimus" is a modern botanical suggestion. The text itself would fit any short, prickly, thistle-like wild plant, and the edition gives no further reasoning. Where the 3-19 plant is a garden plant with leaves "far broader, longer than lettuce, divided like those of eruca", the wild kind is not described at all beyond "prickly" and "shorter". ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Only that the root "affects as many things as the previous one" (3-20), which by reference means burns, dislocations, urine, loose bowels, tuberculosis of the lungs, hernia and convulsions (3-19, as read above).
- **What the text does not say.** It gives no use of its own for the wild kind, no preparation, and no dose.

The Codex does not translate the classic's terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend this plant.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives no preparation of its own. By reference to 3-19 the root is applied (for burns and dislocations) or taken in a drink. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any dose from this entry.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. *Acanthus* species are spiny herbs of the Mediterranean and beyond, grown in gardens for their leaves, and the Greek *akantha* is also the root of the word for a thorn. The identification of Dioscorides' wild *akantha* with a particular species of the genus is a matter of editorial suggestion. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and 3-20 offers none. The chapter is a brief note that the garden plant has a wild counterpart which is prickly and shorter and which has the same root-power. What it shows a practitioner is how the work of telling plants apart ran in his book: by habitat (wild or garden), by size, by comparison with a better-known plant (*carduus nutans*), and by the Roman name. That is a discipline of attention and not a cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports any cultivation use. The chapter's one lesson is to keep a wild plant and a garden plant apart even when the text says their roots do as much.

### Contraindications

- **Identity.** The species is an editorial suggestion of medium confidence, and many unrelated spiny plants might fit "prickly, shorter". Do not gather a plant on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
