# Sea urchin — *Echinos thalassios* (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 2-1), "Echinus from the sea"
## *"Echinus species — Sea Urchin", the translator's identification; the text speaks of the "raw shell" of "Echinus from the sea"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica*, in the 2000 English edition (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`; "text" below). The chapter is 2-1, headed *ECHINOS THALASSIOS* ("sea echinus"), at line 4911, the first chapter of Book 2, the book of living creatures (the spine's locus, `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`: "2-1 (line 4911)", translator's identification "Echinus species — Sea Urchin", class animal).

The whole chapter, line 4914, reads: "Echinus from the sea is good for the stomach, good for the intestines, and diuretic. The raw shell roasted well should be mixed with washing medications made for psoriasis. Burnt, it cleans foul ulcers and represses abnormal growths on the flesh." The chapter gives three kinds of use. The creature is "good for the stomach, good for the intestines, and diuretic", with no preparation named for that use, so how it was taken is not stated. The shell, "raw ... roasted well", is an ingredient in "washing medications made for psoriasis". And the burnt shell "cleans foul ulcers and represses abnormal growths on the flesh". The word *psoriasis* is the translator's and the chapter does not describe the condition. [USER: verify]

The next chapter, 2-2 (*Echinos chersaios*, "Erinaceous genus — Hedgehog", line 4915), is the land counterpart under the same Greek word *echinos*, and is a separate substance: "the burnt skin of the earth hedgehog is good for alopecia [baldness] rubbed on with moist pitch" and "the dried flesh (taken in a drink with honey or vinegar and honey) helps inflamed kidneys, water under the skin [dropsy], and those who have convulsions, elephantiasis, or cachexy" (line 4918). The two chapters share a name, not a substance, and the hedgehog is not treated here. The edition's plate caption "Hedgehog — Echinus species after DAVIS — 1907" (line 5024) belongs to the illustrations, not to the text.

No other tradition on the tag line, and no text outside Dioscorides on disk, was found naming the sea urchin. A whole-word search of the Sushruta, Ebers, Tibetan and Assyrian texts on disk for "sea urchin" returned nothing.

### Active Components

The test (shell) of a sea urchin is mostly calcium carbonate with a small organic matrix; roasting or burning calcium carbonate leaves calcium oxide and ash. The soft parts are the gonads (roe) and body fluids of an echinoderm. These are general facts. The chapter names no constituent, and this entry names none.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records the sea urchin as a remedy for the stomach, intestines and urine, and its burnt shell for ulcers and skin. Calcined shell is an irritant caustic when it becomes lime, and a wild-gathered marine animal carries the infection and toxin risks of raw shellfish. The Codex's application layer has nothing to add, and no protocol is offered.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives two preparations: the shell "roasted well" and mixed with washing medications (line 4914), and the shell burnt (line 4914). No quantity, no vehicle other than the unspecified "washing medications", and no dose or duration are given. For the internal use no preparation is named at all. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The general fact offered is that the shell is largely calcium carbonate, an antacid and an alkali when burnt. No action on the stomach, intestines, urine or skin is claimed here for it.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides.** No quality (warming, astringent, or the like) is printed for the sea urchin in chapter 2-1, and none is supplied here. The chapter states actions only ("good for the stomach, good for the intestines, and diuretic").

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. Chapter 2-1 states uses and two preparations; it records no provenance, season or assay such as makes the discipline-of-attention frame useful elsewhere in the work, and it assigns no status to the person taking the remedy. The one small instruction in it, that the shell be "roasted well", is a matter of preparation, not of cultivation.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing in the source for a cultivator to use. The chapter is a short note on a digestive and skin remedy from a wild marine animal, and the honest practice is to read it as history.

### Contraindications

Raw or poorly handled shellfish and echinoderms carry bacterial and algal-toxin risks, and wild-gathered urchins can be contaminated. Burnt shell is caustic and should not be applied to ulcers or raw flesh. Some sea urchin species have venomous spines, and several are over-harvested. The roe of some species is a common allergen. These are general cautions and not claims from the text.
