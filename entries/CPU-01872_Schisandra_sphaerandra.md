# Schisandra sphaerandra — da.trig (Clark 1995), Dhatig (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011) (rGyud-bzhi); printed "Schizandra sphaerandra"

## *Schisandra sphaerandra, spelled "Schizandra sphaerandra Stapf. f. pallida Smith" in Clark 1995, where it is the second of three names printed for the Tibetan drug da.trig (line 994, after the bare genus "Schizandra" and before "Rhus semilata") and is named again by itself in two of Clark's lists (lines 766 and 1222); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text prints no Latin name for the drug in its running text, and its glossary, by column position, sets another Schisandra against the same Tibetan name* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi (Four Tantras), read in two modern translations, Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011. The binomial *Schisandra sphaerandra* is Clark's alone; the Men-Tsee-Khang text names the drug only by its Tibetan name, and the botanical identity is open.** Both texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk found no *Schisandra sphaerandra*: Yang 1998 prints "Wu Wei (Fructus Schisandrae Chinensis)" at line 2465, a different species named in a different tradition. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP, confidence "medium") and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 122) the row is keyed by the Tibetan name *da.trig*, with the identification "Schizandra; Schizandra sphaerandra Stapf. f. pallida Smith; Rhus semilata", the locus "Clark 1995 (line 994)" and the note "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The first item of the identification is the bare genus "Schizandra", which is not a binomial; the spine binomial is the next item, the first species-level name, with the spelling normalised from "Schizandra" to "Schisandra" and the authority and the form (*f. pallida*) dropped. So the key is the first binomial and not the first name in the row. Clark's third name, *Rhus semilata*, has no spine row.

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, chapter 20 (heading at line 792), the run between "(5) Resins" (line 879) and "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997); no "(6)" heading is printed in the text on disk.* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, line 994: "da.trig: Schizandra: Schizandra sphaerandra Stapf. f. pallida Smith: Rhus semilata: [has seven names. It has sweet and sour tastes and grows in hot areas. The trunk is large and hard with grey bark, a small red flower and berries which resemble the eggs of sheep's lice.]" Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Everything after the Latin names is inside the bracket, so the entry itself holds no root-layer sentence on an action. The Tibetan name, the English name and the three Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's (his preface, line 83, says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance"), stand outside. The action is given in a sentence that follows the bracket of the *mon.chha.ra* entry two lines below and covers the group, line 996: "Aegle marmelos, Schizandra, pine root and acorn halt all types of hot and cold diarrhoea." This stands outside the brackets and names the genus alone. Clark's name index lists *da.trig* (line 1705), and his Latin index lists "Schizandra" and "Schizandra sphaerandra" (line 1644) and "Rhus semilata" (line 1639).

*Clark 1995, two lists that print the full binomial.* In chapter 19, "Principles of Medicines: Tastes and Post-Digestive Tastes" (heading at line 747), under "Groups of Medicines Having the Same Taste" (line 764), line 766: "The group of medicines having sour taste includes pomegranate, sea buckthorn (Hippophae rhamnoides), Chaenomeles tibetica, emblic myrobalan, Indian juniper, Schizandra sphaerandra, curd, whey and curd 'culture', grain beer, etc." In chapter 21, "The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines" (heading at line 1205), line 1222: "The class of anti-diarrhoeals consists of both wild and cultivated types of Bael fruit, Schizandra sphaerandra, Acorn, powdered root (of, for example, old pine tree), Plantain (Plantago depressa), Plantago lanceolata, Bastard Teak, the blood of Hodgson's antelope and Larkspur (Delphinium grandiflora Linn.)". These are the only places where Clark prints *Schizandra sphaerandra* in running text, without the authority.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (read through the Tibetan name, not the binomial).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. In chapter 19, "The Taste and the Post-Digestive Taste" (contents line 221), line 3205, page 186: "Sedu, larbu, Seyab, Kyurura, Gyashug, Dhatig, curd, buttermilk, chhang, yeast and other similar substances are the group of medicinal substances having a sour taste." In chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents line 225), line 3427, page 206: "Kapae, Dhaiig, Mak and Moen chhara, stop all forms of diarrhea irrespective of their being a hot or cold nature." In chapter 21, "The Group and the Method of Compounding Medicine" (contents line 229), line 3644, page 222: "Kapae, Bilwa, Dhatig, Moen chhara, Mak, Tharam, Naram, Gya kyeg, antelope's blood and Jhakang are the group of medicines that cure diarrhea." The index lists "Dhatig 186,206,222" (line 7321). The text gives no Latin name for the drug. "Dhaiig" at line 3427 is read as "Dhatig", an OCR form, because the index pages (186, 206, 222) match the three lines.

*The two translations read as one drug.* Clark's closing sentence (line 996) names "Aegle marmelos, Schizandra, pine root and acorn" as the medicines that halt diarrhoea, and the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence at line 3427 names "Kapae, Dhaiig, Mak and Moen chhara" and gives the same verdict (stop all forms of diarrhea, hot or cold). Clark's *ka.ped* (line 991, "a type of Bael Fruit: Aegle marmelos"), *da.trig*, *smag* (line 995, "Powdered Root of, for example, old pine trees") and *mon.chha.ra* (line 996, "Indian Oak (Acorn)") stand in the same order as the four Men-Tsee-Khang names. Both texts also put the drug in the sour group (Clark line 766; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3205) and in the group of antidiarrhoeals (Clark line 1222; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3644). Neither text states that the two are one drug; the Codex reads them so from the spelling, the order and the action. [USER: verify]

**The glossary, by column position.** The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints "Dhatig" third among its "D" names (line 5183) and "da trig" third among the transliterations (line 5200), and its third scientific name is *Schisandra spenanthera* (line 5217), with the English "Chinese magnolia vine" (line 5223). By position the glossary therefore sets a different Schisandra name from Clark's against the same Tibetan drug. That reading is made in full in I_1437, the sibling entry, and rests on column order. [USER: verify]

**Identity: Clark's list against the glossary.** Clark prints a bare genus, one named species and form of *Schisandra*, and a *Rhus*. The glossary prints *Schisandra* as *spenanthera* (read as *sphenanthera*). The two species names differ, and Clark's own lists spell the drug as *sphaerandra* alone. Whether the two names are two identifications of one Tibetan drug or two distinct plants the Codex has not checked, and the row stands for the Tibetan drug and not for one species. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Schisandra sphaerandra*, *Schizandra*, *da.trig*, *Dhatig*, *Rhus semilata* and "Chinese magnolia vine" found no entry on this binomial. **I_1437** (*Schisandra sphenanthera*, tag FMT) is the sibling entry on the same Tibetan drug, keyed to the glossary's binomial, resting on the same Clark line (994) and the same Men-Tsee-Khang lines (3205, 3427, 3644); the two are counted separately because their spine keys differ and are not merged. **I_215** (*Schisandra chinensis*, tags SBJ and FMT) is the entry on a different species, built on Yang 1998 (Wu Wei, line 2465); its FMT paragraph cites no Clark or Men-Tsee-Khang line, Clark's own *Schizandra chinensis* row is a different Tibetan name (*sra.hbras*, line 952), and I did not find *da.trig* or *Dhatig* in it. **I_1416** (*Quercus semecarpifolia*, *mon.chha.ra*), **I_1163** (*Plantago depressa*), **I_1337** (*Polygonum alopecuroides*), **I_1336** (*Pinus tabulaeformis*) and **I_1300** (*Hippophae tibetana*) quote the same diarrhoea or sour-taste lists for their own drugs, and **I_025** (Bael, *Aegle marmelos*, tags Avicenna, Sushruta, rGyud-bzhi) is the entry on the first of the four drugs named in line 996. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. As a general fact *Schisandra* is a genus of woody vines (family Schisandraceae) bearing clusters of red berries, and the best-known species, *Schisandra chinensis*, is known to contain lignans in its fruit. That is a statement about the genus and not a finding of the Tibetan texts, which do not say which species, which part or which preparation is meant, and Clark's commentary describes a tree with a "large and hard" trunk. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record two things for this drug: it has a sour taste (Clark line 766; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3205) and it halts diarrhoea of both hot and cold kinds (Clark lines 996 and 1222; Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3427 and 3644). Clark's commentary layer adds sweet and sour tastes and a hot-climate habitat (line 994). That is the tradition's own record and not a Codex protocol. The identity is open, so the Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** a sour taste, in the root-layer taste groups (Clark line 766; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3205). Clark's commentary layer (inside the bracket, line 994) prints sweet and sour tastes. No potency or post-digestive taste is printed for the drug in either text.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Both translations place the drug in the materia medica as a sour-tasting medicine that halts diarrhoea (Clark lines 766, 996, 1222; Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3205, 3427, 3644). That is a therapeutic place and not a status in rejuvenation or in *bcud len* (essence extraction). The rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23: Clark lines 1264 to 1276; Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3904 to 3945) does not name the drug; it tells the person on the course to "avoid rotten, sour, unwholesome foods" (Clark line 1275) and to "avoid rotten and sour foods" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3931). Those are rules about foods in general and do not mention this drug, but the drug is in the sour group of the taste chapters, and the Codex notes the juxtaposition without reading the rule as barring it. [USER: verify] The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this drug on the strength of the texts. It is a sour medicine that stops diarrhoea, in a list with bael, pine root and acorn, and the one rule about sour things in the rejuvenation chapter is to avoid them during the course. Nothing here supports treating a Schisandra berry as a rejuvenative on the strength of this entry.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for the drug. The identity is open, so no plant should be gathered or taken on the strength of this entry. A medicine that halts diarrhoea is not a treatment for infection or dehydration, and the texts' own regimen puts it under a physician. No conservation information and no safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
