# Dung beetle — bying.bying.thu.lus ("Dung beetle and Scarab beetle", Clark's English); Jingjing Thulu ("Wingless Cockroach", Men-Tsee-Khang's glossary)
## *Insects found under dung, as Clark's translation of the Tibetan Explanatory Tantra glosses the headword bying.bying.thu.lus. Clark prints no binomial for the headword; the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary translates the corresponding name differently, as a wingless cockroach* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The text on disk is Barry Clark's 1995 *The Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). Chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (line 792), says that "The power of medicines is twofold: (a) the power of taste and (b) the natural power [of the substance]" (line 794). Its Specific Section (line 801) lists the individual substances in eight kinds, the eighth being animal products. The dung beetle is in the run of droppings and insects at the end of that section, after the sub-heading "Droppings" (line 1199). The lines are these, in Clark's English:

"byang.pa: Beetle: Mylabris phalerata: Mylabris cichorii Fabricius: [has four names]. It purges all channel disorders. Crab: [has nine names], and chhu.sbur: Tibetan ant: Both dispel urinary retention. (Tibetan ant in particular is a mild purgative of channel disorders). bse.sbur: Tibetan cockroach: Blatta orientalis L.: Periplaneta americana: Geotrupes laevistriatus: (a large, black, malodorous, nocturnal insect): [is beneficial in septic fevers and for lte.mkhad, a children's disease]. bying.bying.thu.lus: Dung beetle and Scarab beetle: both types found under dung: [has five names and relieves urinary retention. These two types of insect] and Tibetan cockroach are antispasmodic."

So the one action printed outside brackets is that the two kinds of insect found under dung, "and Tibetan cockroach", are "antispasmodic". The words "has five names and relieves urinary retention" are in square brackets. Clark says in his introduction that square-bracket interpolations are taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries (line 82), so the urinary-retention action and the count of five names come from commentary, not from the root line. Clark prints no Tibetan names for the five names, and no binomial for the headword *bying.bying.thu.lus*. (The binomial *Geotrupes laevistriatus* is printed in the cockroach line, for *bse.sbur*, and is not the headword of this entry.)

*A difference between the translations.* The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`) gives the passage (lines 3572-3573): "Blister bettle cleanse disorders of the channels. Crabs and water beetles cure obstruction of urine. Dark beetles and wingless cockroaches relieve abdominal cramps. Tumai bu stops bleeding." Its glossary pairs the names "Jingjing Thulu" with "Wingless Cockroach" and "Sebur" with "Dark Beetle" (lines 7164-7168). By the sound of the names, *Jingjing Thulu* looks like Clark's *bying.bying.thu.lus* and *Sebur* like his *bse.sbur*, but that is my observation, since neither text states the equivalence. If it holds, the two translators identify the headword differently: Clark as a dung or scarab beetle, Men-Tsee-Khang as a wingless cockroach; and for the second insect Clark gives "Tibetan cockroach" and Men-Tsee-Khang "dark beetle". The action ("antispasmodic" in Clark, "relieve abdominal cramps" in Men-Tsee-Khang) is consistent between the two. I do not resolve the identification, and the entry uses Clark's "dung beetle" only because that is the name the batch list carries. [USER: verify]

Clark's introduction says that "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57). The Men-Tsee-Khang preface says that the "use of animal products was negligible" and that animal products "have been completely substituted by equally effective herbal substances", their potencies being given "to preserve the understanding of the therapeutic values of animal products" (line 687).

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms a beetle's body is made of protein and chitin, the polysaccharide of the insect exoskeleton, with fat and water. Nothing more specific can be said without a source, and the species is not settled (see above).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text records the dung beetle and scarab among the "antispasmodic" insects, and Clark's bracketed commentary adds relief of urinary retention. The Codex does not recommend it. Abdominal cramp and urinary retention are conditions for ordinary medical assessment, and urinary retention in particular can be an emergency. An insect collected from dung carries gut bacteria and parasites from the dung. The tradition's own translators say that animal products have largely been replaced by herbal substances.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives no preparation for the dung beetle. It does not say whether the insect is dried, burnt, powdered or eaten, or whether it is used whole, and the lines read give no quantity. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Dung beetles (the scarab family) feed on and breed in animal dung and are well known for rolling and burying it. They can carry bacteria and parasite eggs from the dung on their bodies. No insect is an established treatment for spasm or for urinary retention. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** None printed for this item. Chapter 20 says that medicines act by taste and by natural power, and lists eight powers (heavy, oily, cool, blunt, light, coarse, hot, sharp; Clark line 796). The dung-beetle line (line 1199) prints no taste, power or post-digestive taste, and neither does the Men-Tsee-Khang passage (lines 3572-3573). Clark's introduction says that the later *Crystal Orb* and *Crystal Rosary* give each medicine's tastes, potencies and qualities, but that text is not on disk.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for the dung beetle in the lines read. Chapter 20 gives it one therapeutic action, shared with two other insects, and says nothing about essence extraction (*bcud len*) or any other practice. The Men-Tsee-Khang preface records a Buddhist objection to causing harm to any being as the reason animal products have been replaced by herbs (line 687); that is the tradition's comment on animal products in general. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The dung beetle is a clause in a list of insects, the translators disagree about which insect is meant, and the tradition describes animal products as largely replaced. The honest use of the entry is as a record. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Abdominal cramp and urinary retention:** seek medical assessment; urinary retention can be an emergency.
- **Infection and hygiene:** insects collected from dung can carry bacteria and parasites; do not eat or apply them.
- **Allergy:** insects contain proteins that can cause allergic reactions in some people, including those allergic to shellfish or dust mites.
- **Ethics and conservation:** the Codex does not recommend killing any animal for these uses, and the Tibetan translators themselves record a Buddhist objection to harming any being. Dung beetles are ecologically useful, and no conservation status is asserted for the unidentified species here.
