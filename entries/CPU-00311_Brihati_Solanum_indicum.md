### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda (Suśruta & the classical corpus).** Bṛhatī — "the great one," from *bṛhat*, large/mighty — is the **greater** member of the classical nightshade dyad *Bṛhatī–Kaṇṭakārī* and, with its lesser sibling, one of the two *kṣudra-mūla* (lesser roots) of the **Daśamūla**, the ten-root formula that is arguably the most-used compound base in all of Ayurveda. Where Kaṇṭakārī (*S. xanthocarpum*) is the small, thorn-defended nightshade, Bṛhatī is the larger, more erect nightshade of the pair. Suśruta and Caraka place it among the **kāsahara** (anti-tussive), **śvāsahara** (anti-dyspnoeic) and **dīpanīya** (digestive-kindling) dravyas; it is used for kāsa (cough), śvāsa (dyspnoea), jvara (fever), aruci (anorexia/loss of appetite), krimi (worms), hṛdroga (heart disease) and Vāta disorders, and is a standard component of the Daśamūla decoctions used in fever, respiratory illness and postpartum/convalescent Vāta care.

Its synonyms encode both size and fierceness: **Mahoṭikā** and **Bṛhatī** ("the great"), **Siṃhī** ("lioness"), **Duṣpradharṣiṇī** ("hard to assail," for the prickles). The Bṛhatī–Kaṇṭakārī pair — the greater and the lesser — is a recurring doctrinal dyad, the two magnitudes of the same nightshade principle deployed together for the breath and the digestive fire.

**Siddha and folk practice.** *S. indicum* fruit and root are used across South Asian folk medicine for cough, asthma, colic, and fever; the fruit is eaten as a vegetable in parts of Africa and Asia. The Gadewar study (below) notes the traditional use of roots, leaves and fruits "in the treatment of cough, asthma and rhinitis."

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Greater of the Twinned Roots:** Bṛhatī's doctrine in the codex is *magnitude and the restoration of fire*. It is the "great" and "lioness" nightshade — larger, more upright, more forceful than its thorny lesser twin — and where Kaṇṭakārī opens the breath, Bṛhatī additionally *rekindles the digestive fire (agni)* that has gone cold in kapha-heavy and convalescent states. The pairing of the two nightshades is the codex's model of the twinned remedy: the same essential nature — hot, bitter, penetrating, defended — expressed in two sizes, so that the practitioner reaches for the lesser or the greater according to the depth and force required. Bṛhatī is the greater reach: it opens the channel *and* relights the hearth beneath it.

### Active Components

*S. indicum* carries the characteristic **Solanaceae steroidal alkaloids** — **solasodine**, **solasonine**, **solamargine** and related glycoalkaloids — together with steroidal saponins, sterols, and phenolic acids, notably **chlorogenic acid** (the standardised marker in the antihypertensive work below). Flavonoids and a range of volatile phytochemicals (identified by GC-MS in extract studies) are also present. Solasodine is again the principal alkaloidal marker.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Bṛhatī is the codex's **greater breath-and-fire** substance: used where cold-wet (kapha) obstruction of the chest coexists with a *cold, sluggish digestive fire and convalescent Vāta depletion*. It opens the airway and mobilises secretions (like its lesser twin) but is reached for when the picture also includes poor appetite, weak digestion, and the aftermath of fever or exhaustion — the dīpana (kindling) dimension is what distinguishes it from Kaṇṭakārī. It belongs to the respiratory-opening and digestive-kindling phases and functions as the greater half of the Daśamūla root-dyad.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the pharmacology of *S. indicum* is preclinical but reasonably diverse. Gadewar et al. (*Current Issues in Molecular Biology*, 2023) evaluated a methanolic fruit extract in streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats at 100 and 200 mg/kg for 30 days and reported dose-dependent reduction of blood glucose (more marked at 200 mg/kg), α-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibition, lowering of serum cholesterol and triglycerides, and modulation of hepatic antioxidant enzymes — offering preliminary scientific support for the plant's traditional metabolic uses. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/cimb45020058)

A separate study on the closely related *S. indicum* ssp. *distichum* (Bahgat et al., *Fundamental & Clinical Pharmacology*, 2008) found that a chlorogenic-acid-standardised ethanolic fruit extract prevented and partly reversed L-NAME-induced hypertension in rats, providing "pharmacological evidence for the antihypertensive activity … for the first time," while noting that chlorogenic acids likely play only a minor role and other constituents are probably responsible. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1472-8206.2008.00627.x)

**Evidence grade: LOW–PRELIMINARY — animal/in vitro only.** The antidiabetic, antihyperlipidaemic and antihypertensive signals come from rodent and in vitro models; there is no controlled human trial for *S. indicum* comparable to the Kaṇṭakārī asthma studies. The subspecies caveat (distichum) matters. Claims are attributed to PubMed-indexed sources and are not established human outcomes.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Rasa (taste):** *Tikta* (bitter), *Kaṭu* (pungent).
- **Guṇa (qualities):** *Laghu* (light), *Rūkṣa* (dry), *Tīkṣṇa* (penetrating).
- **Vīrya (potency):** *Uṣṇa* (heating).
- **Vipāka (post-digestive effect):** *Kaṭu* (pungent).
- **Prabhāva (specific action):** *Dīpana–śvāsahara* — the coupling of digestive-fire kindling with respiratory opening that marks it out from Kaṇṭakārī.
- **Doṣa action:** *Kapha-Vāta-śāmaka*; Pitta-aggravating in excess.
- **Karma (actions):** *Kāsahara* (anti-tussive), *Śvāsahara* (anti-dyspnoeic), *Dīpana–Pācana* (kindles and digests), *Aruciघna* / anti-anorexic, *Kṛmighna* (anthelmintic), *Hṛdya* (cardiac tonic), *Jvaraghna* (antipyretic).
- **Srotas (channels):** *prāṇavaha* (respiratory) and *annavaha* (digestive); secondarily *raktavaha*.
- **Gaṇa membership:** the greater of the two *kṣudra-mūla* (lesser roots) of the **Daśamūla**; classed among Caraka's kāsahara, śvāsahara and dīpanīya dravyas.

### Contraindications

- Heating, sharp and kindling — caution in Pitta excess, hyperacidity, peptic ulceration and active GI inflammation.
- Contains Solanaceae steroidal glycoalkaloids (solasodine/solasonine/solamargine) — potentially toxic in excess; keep to traditional doses and avoid unripe/green fruit in quantity.
- Avoid in pregnancy without qualified supervision.
- Taxonomic caution: *S. indicum sensu lato* is a confused complex, and the antihypertensive data derive from the *distichum* subspecies — do not over-generalise across the aggregate.
- Not a substitute for medical management of diabetes, hypertension or dyslipidaemia; the metabolic evidence is animal-only.
