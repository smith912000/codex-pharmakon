# Crocus — as printed by Bryan 1930 (Ebers papyrus); forms Crocus-from-the-South, Crocus-from-the-Hills, Crocus-from-the-Delta

## *A plant called "Crocus" in Bryan's English, listed in his Chapter VI with three place-qualified forms; Bryan prints no Egyptian word and no botanical name in the lines read, so the species is a modern hypothesis. Identification: the genus Crocus (saffron crocus, C. sativus) is the usual guess, with the autumn crocus (Colchicum) also proposed by other writers; Bryan lists "Saffron" as a separate head word, which argues against simply merging the two. Confidence: low* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

> **IDENTITY CAUTION.** If "Crocus" were the autumn crocus (*Colchicum*), the plant is highly poisonous. The identity is not settled, and this entry is a historical record only. Nobody should gather or take a crocus-like plant on the strength of the name.

**Egyptian Papyrus Ebers (EP).** Bryan's Chapter VI, "Plant Remedies", lists "Crocus" with the forms Crocus-from-the-South, Crocus-from-the-Hills and Crocus-from-the-Delta (line 1270; names.csv row, "Bryan's English identification"). The recipes found by searching the Ebers text are these.

1. **Crocus-from-the-South, in a urinary remedy.** Under Bryan's heading "The Urinary System", after "To regulate the urine" (Xet-plant mixed in fresh milk and applied to the phallus), a second recipe, "Another", opens with "Crocus-from-the-South" and further ingredients that run into OCR-damaged columns of figures; the text lines I read break off and the full list is not recoverable from this file (line 2421 and the lines after it). 2. **Crocus-from-the-Hills and Crocus-from-the-Delta, in a remedy "to force out the urine".** The list reads: "Crocus-from-the-Hills, Crocus-from-the-Delta, *abu*-plant-from-Upper-Egypt, *abu*-plant-from-Lower-Egypt, Berry-of-the-*uan*-tree, Fresh Gruel, Linseed, *uam*-seeds" (lines 2465 to 2468). Bryan places this among the remedies he describes as medical treatment of stricture, in place of urethral dilatation (lines 2462 to 2464).
3. **Crocus in the eyes.** "A simple poultice of Incense and Crocus, equal parts, often sufficed to clear up Bloodshot Eyes" (line 2747). If that failed, a more complicated poultice followed.
4. **Crocus in the teeth.** In Bryan's discussion of strengthening the teeth by chewing, "Crocus-in-Sweet-Beer was one of the remedies recommended" (lines 3143 to 3144). For "Blisters-in-the-Teeth" a plaster "included Incense, Crocus, Cyperus, Onions, the Wood-of-Aloes, and Water, not to mention a variety of herbs of whose name and nature we are still in ignorance" (line 3145).

No description of the plant, and no statement of which part is used, was found in the lines read. ### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, the saffron crocus (*Crocus sativus*) is the source of the spice saffron, whose colour and aroma come from carotenoid-derived compounds (crocin, picrocrocin, safranal). The autumn crocus (*Colchicum*) is a different plant that contains the alkaloid colchicine, which is toxic. Which, if either, the Egyptian recipes used is not known. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Identity unresolved.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Urinary complaints (a topical application to the phallus, and a remedy "to force out the urine"), bloodshot eyes, and the teeth and gums (chewed in beer, and in a plaster).
- **What the text does not say.** No quantity beyond "equal parts" in the eye poultice, no part of the plant, no preparation beyond mixing and poulticing.

The Codex gives no protocol. Because the plant may be a poisonous *Colchicum*, no crocus-like plant should be substituted for these recipes.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations found are a topical application, a mixed remedy, a poultice of incense and crocus in equal parts, a chewing remedy in sweet beer, and a plaster for the teeth. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any recipe from this entry.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Saffron is a spice and food colouring; *Colchicum* alkaloids are poisonous, and colchicine is used in modern medicine only under close medical control. The Ebers terms (forcing out the urine, blisters in the teeth) are not translated here into modern diagnoses. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Identity and toxicity.** "Crocus" may be a *Colchicum*, which is poisonous. Do not gather or ingest any crocus-like plant.
- **Urinary retention, eye and dental problems.** These are medical conditions needing proper care.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** No statement in the sources retrieved. Saffron in medicinal amounts is generally advised against in pregnancy. [USER: verify]
- **Companion ingredients.** The recipes combine crocus with incense, onions, aloes-wood, cyperus and other materials; their safety is not addressed here.
