# Wolf — "wolf" (Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, as a class of carnivorous animal); Canis Lupus (Men-Tsee-Khang glossary on animals)
## *The wolf as an animal named in the Tibetan Four Tantras: a member of the carnivorous animals in the meat classification, with its stomach, tongue and droppings as separate medicines. Neither translation prints a Tibetan word for the animal itself, and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary gives the Latin name* Canis Lupus | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is the Tibetan *Four Tantras*. The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`; "MTK" below) has a glossary on animals in which "Wolf" is printed at line 7023 and the Latin "Canis Lupus" at line 7027. The OCR has split the columns of that glossary, so the Tibetan and phonetic names are not paired to the English and Latin, and no Tibetan word for the wolf is available in the sources read. The Latin identification is the glossary's own.

The wolf does not have a flesh entry or a whole-animal entry in the materia medica of chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra. What the texts give is its place in the classification of meats and three parts:

- **Meat classification.** In Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; "Clark" below), group (e) of the meats reads: "Carnivorous animals include the tiger, leopard, bear (Selenarctos thibetanus), snow bear (Ursus arctos), snow leopard (Panthera pardus), wolf, lynx, fox, hill dog and weasel" (line 672). MTK's parallel list is at line 2912: "The tiger, leopard, black bear, Tibetan brown bear, snow leopard, wolf, lynx, fox, dhole (Tibetan wild-dog) and monkey are the group of canivorous wild animals."
- **Stomach and tongue.** Clark line 1170: "sbyang.kihi.pho.wa: Wolf's stomach: [has twelve names] It increases digestive heat and digests undigested food, [especially meat]. Its tongue removes swellings of the tongue." MTK lines 3535-3536: "Wolf stomach generates digestive heat and helps the digestion of all undigested food particles. Wolf tongue reduces swelling of the tongue." (Clark's introduction, line 82, says that square-bracketed words are from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. I do not split the Tibetan name Clark prints for the stomach entry.)
- **Stool.** Clark line 1199: "Wolf stool and droppings of Gong.mo: Tetragallus himalayensis: [all three] reduce swellings." MTK line 3568: "The feces of dogs, wolves and the Tibetan snow cock reduce swelling."
- **Qualities of the group's meat.** Clark attributes the qualities to the birds of prey ("coarse, light and sharp powers which increase the digestive heat, disperse tumors, increase flesh and eliminate all cold disorders", line 673). MTK says at line 2931: "The meat of birds, carnivorous animals and birds of prey that feed on raw meat has rough, light and sharp qualities, and therefore generates digestive heat, destroys tumors, increases weight and eliminates all cold disorders." The two translations differ on whether the statement reaches the carnivores, and I cannot say which is closer to the Tibetan. Neither text says that wolf meat was eaten or used.

The stomach, the tongue and the stool are separate substances with their own entries in this Codex (Wolf stomach, Wolf tongue, Wolf dung). The wolf appears under one more homonym: "Yellow wolfsbane" in MTK's plant glossary (line 5157) is a plant and not this animal.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent of the wolf. In general terms, the wolf is a wild carnivorous canid, and its flesh is protein, fat and mineral like that of other mammals. Nothing more specific can be said without a source.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts name the wolf as a carnivorous animal and give group qualities for the meat of such animals, and give actions for its stomach, tongue and stool as separate medicines. The Codex does not recommend any wolf product. The wolf is protected or restricted in much of its range, and wild carnivore tissue carries parasite and infection risks. Clark notes in his introduction that "nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57). This entry is a record of how the text classifies the animal.

### Preparation & Dosing

The texts give no preparation of the wolf itself and no quantity. The group statement in MTK line 2931 describes the meat's qualities; the parts have their own entries. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. The qualities "rough, light and sharp" and the categories "tumors" and "swellings" belong to the Tibetan frame and have no direct counterpart in modern physiology. Wild canids can carry parasites and infections, among them rabies and tapeworm cysts, that pass to people. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** No taste, potency or post-digestive taste is printed for the wolf alone. For the meats of the animals in the classification, Clark line 673 says that all are "sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste". For the group of carnivorous animals, MTK line 2931 gives "rough, light and sharp qualities", which "generate" digestive heat, destroy tumors, increase weight and eliminate all cold disorders. Clark gives the same powers for the birds of prey only. The wolf's parts have their own statements in their entries.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The wolf is named in the Explanatory Tantra as a carnivorous animal in the meat classification (Clark line 672; MTK line 2912) and as the source of three medicines, the stomach, the tongue and the stool (Clark lines 1170, 1199). None of this is a cultivation status. Nothing in the passages read places the wolf in any practice of essence extraction (*bcud len*), and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, so none is asserted. The wider frame is that the three humors are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration (Clark line 51), so disease is at root a spiritual condition, and that food, behaviour, medicine and accessory therapy are the four supports of treatment (line 1299), of which medicine is the third. The chapter on routine behaviour asks the practitioner to refrain from the ten negative actions, the first of which is "taking life" (line 616). The rejuvenation passage (lines 1266-1275) names no animal substance in the lines read.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The tradition classes the wolf among the carnivores and records three of its parts as drugs for digestion and swellings, and its own behavioural teaching begins with refraining from taking life. The Codex does not recommend any wolf product. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** tissue from wild carnivores can carry parasites and viruses (rabies, tapeworm cysts); do not handle or eat it.
- **Legal and conservation status:** wolves are protected or restricted in much of their range, and the status differs by country and population; do not obtain any wolf product without lawful, documented origin.
- **Ethics:** the Codex does not recommend harming any animal for these uses.
