# Stratiotes aloides — Stratiotes o en tois udasin, "Stratiotes which grows in the water" (Dioscorides 4-102)

## *Stratiotes aloides, the first name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-102, glossed "Water Soldier"; the line also gives Pistia stratiodes (the printed spelling), "Water Lettuce, Tropical Duckweed"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** Among the traditions tagged here, one text names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain, and the translators print a second candidate on the same line (below). The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-102. STRATIOTES O EN TOIS UDASIN* at line 8934, SUGGESTED line at 8935, text at line 8936. The SUGGESTED line reads "Stratiotes aloides — Water Soldier Pistia stratiodes — Water Lettuce, Tropical Duckweed". It stands in Book Four, "Other Herbs & Roots", after *4-101. POTAMOGEITON* and before *4-103. STRATIOTES CHILIOPHULLOS* (line 8937). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 682, and the translators' index prints "Stratiotes aloides 652" (line 17781).

The whole chapter reads: "Stratiotes which grows in the water is called this because it swims on the waters and lives without a root. It has a leaf similar to that of sempervivum [4-89 to 4-91] yet bigger. It is cooling and stops bloody discharges from the kidneys. A decoction (taken as a drink and also smeared on with vinegar) keeps wounds, erysipela [streptococcal skin infection] and oedema uninflamed. It is also called river stratiotes, the Egyptians call it tibus, and the Magi, the blood of a cat."

The description is brief and rests on a habit of growth: the plant "swims on the waters and lives without a root". The text gives that as the reason for the name. The leaf is compared with that of *sempervivum* (the chapters 4-89 to 4-91, which are not read for this entry) and is "bigger". The quality printed is that it is "cooling". Two uses follow: it "stops bloody discharges from the kidneys", and a decoction, drunk and also smeared on with vinegar, "keeps wounds, erysipela ... and oedema uninflamed". The chapter prints the names *river stratiotes*, the Egyptian *tibus* and the "Magi" name "the blood of a cat". The last two are names recorded by Dioscorides and not texts of those peoples.

**Two plants on the translators' line.** *Stratiotes aloides* (water soldier) and *Pistia* (water lettuce) are listed on one line, and the translators do not say which they prefer. They are two different plants. The Codex keeps the first, as the spine does, and does not decide between them. The name index of the Sushruta text on disk has one row, `Sources/SS_Sushruta/extract/names.csv` line 527, that pairs the Sanskrit-derived OCR word "Kmubhik" with "Pistia Stratiotes" in the glossary of Bhishagratna's Vol. III (Appendix 3, lines 32073 and 32105 of `Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`). The row's own note says that the pairing was proposed from two OCR columns and checked by script. It is another species (*Pistia*), it is a name list and not a passage of use, and it is not used here as an attestation. No tag is added.

**Overlap.** A chapter-level search of the entries folder for 4-102 found no entry on the chapter. I_1168 (*Potamogeton natans*, 4-101) and I_1138 (*Myriophyllum spicatum*, 4-115) mention 4-102 only as a neighbouring chapter. The chapter next to it, 4-103 (*Stratiotes chiliophyllos*, yarrow), is covered by I_770 and shares the word *stratiotes* only. I_1138 records that the names *stratiotice* and *millefolium* belong to other chapters. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. It is a general fact that *Stratiotes aloides* is a freshwater plant of the frogbit family (Hydrocharitaceae) that grows in rosettes at or below the water surface, and that *Pistia* belongs to the arum family (Araceae), a family in which calcium oxalate crystals are common. Which of the two, if either, is Dioscorides' plant is not known. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a cooling plant for bleeding from the kidneys, and a decoction to keep wounds, erysipelas and swelling uninflamed. These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. Blood in the urine is a sign for medical assessment, and erysipelas is a bacterial skin infection that needs treatment (the translators' bracket in the text calls it "streptococcal skin infection"). The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation: "A decoction (taken as a drink and also smeared on with vinegar)". No quantity, frequency or duration is printed, and the chapter does not say which part of the plant is boiled. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that blood in the urine has many causes and needs medical assessment, and that erysipelas needs medical treatment.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 4-102 prints one quality, that the plant "is cooling". It prints no taste and no moist or dry quality.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a field mark that is easy to check in the water (the plant floats and has no root), a leaf compared with a known plant, and a plain statement of its quality (cooling). The name is explained from the habit. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The identification is the translators', and they print two. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is that the name is explained by something one can observe in place, a plant that floats and has no root. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 4-102 prints no warning. The identification is uncertain between two unrelated plants, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Bleeding from the kidneys and infected skin need medical care and must not be left to a decoction. Wild aquatic plants can carry contamination from the water they grow in, and the Codex has not read a source on that for this plant. The text gives no dose and no safety information for pregnancy or children. [USER: verify]
