## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Angelica archangelica* L.
**Family:** Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)
**Tribe:** Selineae

**This entry marks the transition from Lamiaceae (I-541 through I-551) to Apiaceae (the carrot/parsley/dill family) — one of the most pharmacologically rich plant families in the Codex, producing essential oils, furanocoumarins, phthalides, and polyacetylenes across hundreds of medicinal genera.**

**Etymology — Angelic Naming:**
*Angelica archangelica* carries the most theologically elevated naming in the entire Apiaceae. Multiple etymology accounts:
1. **Plague legend:** According to one tradition, an angel appeared to a monk in a dream during a plague epidemic and revealed angelica as a cure — hence "angel's plant" and "Holy Ghost root"
2. **May 8 feast day:** The plant typically blooms around May 8 (old calendar), the feast of St. Michael the Archangel — connecting the plant's phenology to its angelic name
3. **Protective spiritual properties:** The herb was used to make protective garlands against plague and witchcraft; its angelic attribution encoded the belief that it was spiritually protected or of divine origin

**Apiaceae Introduction Note:**
The transition from Lamiaceae to Apiaceae marks a significant shift in phytochemical character. Apiaceae distinctive chemistry:
- **Furanocoumarins (psoralens):** Responsible for phototoxicity; bergapten, xanthotoxin, psoralen — found in angelica, parsley, celery, parsnip
- **Phthalides:** Sedative/antispasmodic; characteristic of celery and lovage
- **Volatile terpenes:** Monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes in EO; characteristic aroma
- **Polyacetylenes:** Antimicrobial; found in angelica root
- **Coumarins:** Anticoagulant; vasodilatory; characteristic of many genera

**Botanical Identification:**
Stout biennial or monocarpic perennial, 100-250 cm; large hollow stems; compound (2-3 pinnate) leaves; large terminal compound umbels of white-green flowers; intensely aromatic (angelica-gin aroma); native to Scandinavia, Finland, Iceland, Greenland, and alpine/subarctic Europe; cultivated southward. The largest Apiaceae species in European pharmacopoeias.

The Chinese pharmacopoeial angelica (*Dāng guī*, 当归, *A. sinensis*) is the most widely used women's tonic herb in Traditional Chinese Medicine. While pharmacologically distinct from *A. archangelica* (different alkaloid and ligustilide profiles), both are Angelica species; the scoring includes SBJ ✓ at genus level. *A. sinensis* will receive a separate Codex entry for its unique phytoestrogenic and hematopoietic pharmacology.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Degree of Cross-Traditional Agreement:** Moderate (5/9) — northern European/subarctic origin limits Old World distribution.

**Core Cross-Traditional Functions:**
1. **Digestive / carminative / stomachic** — universal in confirming traditions; dyspepsia, flatulence, loss of appetite; Commission E primary indication
2. **Warming tonic / restorative** — CM, FMT classification as "hot, dry" herb; used in cold, weak, depleted states; northern European constitution herb
3. **Respiratory** — bronchitis, cough; EO expectorant mechanism; secondary in European tradition
4. **Antitoxin** — Ibn Sīnā recommendation for snake/scorpion bites; pest control; angelica root in theriac (antidote compound)
5. **Culinary-pharmacological integration** — Norwegian/Sami consumption of fresh stems as food and medicine simultaneously

**Subarctic Pharmacopoeia:**
Angelica occupies a unique ecological and pharmacological niche as a primary medicine plant of Scandinavia, Iceland, and subarctic Europe — regions with few other medicinal plants available during long cold winters. In Norwegian, Sami, and Icelandic traditional medicine, angelica root, stem, and seeds were used medicinally without equivalent alternatives. This pharmacological isolation in the subarctic drove intensive use of a single plant species for a broader range of indications than would be ascribed to it in pharmacologically richer Mediterranean climates.

**Furanocoumarin Phototoxicity Warning:**
Bergapten and xanthotoxin are UVA-activated photosensitizers. Skin contact with fresh plant juice or concentrated preparations followed by UV exposure produces severe phototoxic burns ("phytophotodermatitis") — identical in mechanism to the therapeutic PUVA (psoralen + UVA) therapy used for psoriasis. This is a class-wide Apiaceae hazard present in parsley, parsnip, celery, dill, carrot tops, and many related species. Harvesters of fresh angelica in sunny conditions have documented contact dermatitis; the traditional practice of harvesting in early morning (before UVA peak) or with protective clothing reflects empirical folk knowledge of this hazard.

**Polyacetylenes:**
- Falcarinol, falcarindiol — antimicrobial; cytotoxic (in vitro cancer cell lines); anti-inflammatory; characteristic Apiaceae polyacetylenes (also in carrot, Apium spp.)

**Coumarins (antispasmodic/vasodilatory):**
- Imperatorin, osthol, isoimperatorin, byakangelicol — calcium channel blocking spasmolytic activity; contribute to digestive and smooth muscle relaxation indications

**Steroidal Compounds:**
- β-Sitosterol, stigmasterol — anti-inflammatory; minor

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Primary — Antispasmodic (Coumarin/Furanocoumarin Spasmolysis):**
Imperatorin, osthol, and byakangelicol → L-type calcium channel blockade in gastrointestinal smooth muscle → reduced contractile activity → spasmolysis → relief of dyspeptic spasm, flatulence, and intestinal cramping. This is the primary mechanism for the Commission E-approved digestive indication.

**Digestive Stimulant / Bitter Tonic:**
Angelica root has significant bitter taste (bitter value recorded, though lower than gentian) → TAS2R activation → reflex gastric acid secretion + bile flow stimulation (choleretic) → improved digestive function. Combined bitter + antispasmodic = the pharmacological basis for "functional dyspepsia" treatment.

**Phototoxic Mechanism (Safety Context — Furanocoumarins):**
Bergapten/xanthotoxin → UV-A light absorption → excited-state molecule → covalent cross-linking to DNA pyrimidine bases (thymine) → psoralen-DNA adducts → cell death in UV-exposed skin. This is the same mechanism deliberately exploited in PUVA therapy (psoralen + UVA = anti-psoriatic treatment): controlled psoralen dosing + controlled UV exposure → killing hyperproliferative keratinocytes in psoriatic plaques. In uncontrolled settings (sun exposure after skin contact) → severe localized phototoxic burns.

**Expectorant/Mucolytic (Respiratory):**
EO α-phellandrene + limonene → bronchial secretion liquefaction + mucociliary stimulation → productive cough facilitation; similar mechanism to other aromatic Apiaceae (dill, fennel).

**Anti-inflammatory:**
β-Caryophyllene → CB2 agonism; falcarinol → COX inhibition; osthol → NF-κB modulation.

**Platelet Aggregation Inhibition:**
Coumarin fraction (osthol) → antiplatelet effect (similar to, but weaker than, warfarin's mechanism); relevant to drug interaction profile.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Overall Evidence Level: B/C** — Commission E approved; limited RCT data; strong traditional and mechanistic evidence.

**Level A — Combination:**
- Madisch A et al. (2004, *Scandinavian Journal of Gastroenterology*): STW5 (Iberogast®) — a 9-herb combination including angelica root — in functional dyspepsia; Level A evidence for the combination (n=315; RCT; superior to placebo for GI symptoms); angelica is one of nine components.

**Level B — Isolated Compound:**
- Byakangelicol sedative activity: pre-clinical sedative and anxiolytic effects in rodents; GABA-A mechanism; not translated to clinical trials for angelica specifically.
- Imperatorin spasmolytic: in vitro and ex vivo spasmolysis in guinea pig ileum at micromolar concentrations.

**Level C — Traditional:**
- Extensive European traditional use for dyspepsia and respiratory complaints; Scandinavian food-medicine use spanning millennia; eclectic medicine record in North America

**Regulatory Status:**
- **Commission E:** APPROVED for dyspeptic complaints (loss of appetite, bloating, flatulence, gastritis) — 1990 monograph
- **ESCOP:** Monograph positive for dyspepsia
- **EMA/HMPC:** Community herbal monograph for digestive complaints

**Iberogast® (STW5) — Combination Evidence:**
Iberogast® contains angelica root as one of 9 active components (others: bitter candytuft, caraway, chamomile, celandine, lemon balm, licorice, milk thistle, peppermint). Multiple Level A RCTs demonstrate efficacy of this combination for functional dyspepsia and IBS. Angelica's contribution within the combination is the antispasmodic and bitter-tonic fraction.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The Plague Herb:**
The association of angelica with plague protection was not purely mystical. During major plague epidemics (bubonic plague, 14th-17th centuries), angelica root was burned as incense, incorporated into sachets worn around the neck, and consumed in preparations. Whether this provided any actual protection against *Yersinia pestis* (which is flea-transmitted, not airborne in the bubonic form) is doubtful. However, angelica's strong aromatic properties would repel fleas and other insects — and flea repellency does provide genuine plague protection, since fleas are the primary vector. The herb that "keeps plague away" by keeping fleas away is pharmacologically coherent through the insect-repellent activity of its EO components.

**Chartreuse and Bénédictine:**
Angelica root is a principal flavoring ingredient in several major herbal liqueurs:
- **Chartreuse** (1737): made by Carthusian monks; ~130 herbs including angelica as one of the primary botanicals; the distinctive green and yellow liqueur flavors are partly angelica
- **Bénédictine** (1510): Benedictine Abbey, Fécamp; 27 herbs and spices including angelica root; originally marketed as a medicinal liqueur before becoming a cocktail ingredient
- **Gin (multiple brands):** Angelica root is one of the standard "botanicals" in gin formulation (alongside juniper, coriander, citrus peel); its piney-musky character contributes to gin's aromatic complexity

The liqueur tradition represents a long-standing integration of angelica pharmacology into a form designed for palatability and preservation — the alcohol extracts and preserves the antispasmodic coumarins and the aromatic EO simultaneously.

**Sami and Norwegian Food-Medicine:**
In traditional Sami and Norwegian culture, angelica (*kvann*) was an important food source — fresh stems were eaten raw as a vegetable (similar to celery), dried root was used as a winter food source, and the plant was one of the few significant green foods available in the brief subarctic summer. The boundary between food and medicine was essentially absent: the same plant that provided nutritional carbohydrates and vitamins also provided digestive and respiratory medicine. This food-medicine integration is a model of subsistence pharmacology — using the most available and most versatile botanical resource for multiple survival functions.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Indications (Commission E approved):**
- Functional dyspepsia: flatulence, bloating, digestive spasm, loss of appetite — primary
- Respiratory catarrh: cough, bronchitis — traditional; secondary
- Combination products (Iberogast®-type): Level A evidence for dyspepsia + IBS

**Preparation Methods:**
| Form | Preparation | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Root decoction | 1-2 g dried root / 150 mL; simmer 5-10 min | Commission E standard; take 30 min before meals |
| Tincture | 1:5 in 45% ethanol; 1-2 mL three times daily | Concentrated; EO + coumarin extraction |
| Seed infusion | 1 g seeds / 150 mL boiling water; steep 10 min | Gentler; more EO; less bitter |
| Combination (Iberogast®) | 1 mL in water before or with meals | Level A evidence base |
| Candied root | Traditional Scandinavian/French preparation | Palatability; historically medicinal |

**Dosing:**
- Root: 1-3 g daily in divided doses; take before meals for digestive effect
- Tincture: 1-2 mL three times daily
- Duration: 2-4 week courses; suitable for repeated use

**Contraindications:**
- **Phototoxicity: CRITICAL** — avoid skin contact with fresh plant (root juice, leaf juice) followed by sun exposure; risk of severe burns; wear protective clothing when harvesting
- Pregnancy: uterotonic potential from coumarin fraction; furanocoumarins; AVOID therapeutic doses
- Warfarin / anticoagulants: osthol antiplatelet effect → additive; monitor INR
- Peptic ulcer disease: bitters stimulate gastric acid → may worsen active ulcer
- Diabetes: may lower blood glucose; monitor

**Furanocoumarin Content Warning:**
- Fresh angelica root juice and stem contain the highest furanocoumarin concentrations
- Dry extract products have reduced (but not absent) furanocoumarin content
- Therapeutic use of standardized dried root/tincture carries low phototoxicity risk at standard doses in non-sun-exposed individuals
- Children and fair-skinned individuals: heightened caution for topical contact
