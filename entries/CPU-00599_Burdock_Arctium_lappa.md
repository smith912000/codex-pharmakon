# Burdock — Niu Bang Zi (牛蒡子, TCM seed), Bardana, Gobō (Japanese root), Great Burdock

## *Arctium lappa* L. | Family: Asteraceae (Compositae)

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM, TCM, SS
**Tags (previous line, retained for reference):** DMM, CM, SBJ, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry (Galenic); cooling, cleansing, and detoxifying
**Training Phase:** Purification

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Dioscorides (DMM):** The Greek herbal tradition knew the large-leaved burrs of *Arktion* / *Personata* and used the root and leaves for cleansing wounds, drawing out heat, and treating swellings and skin complaints. Burdock passed from Dioscorides into the European herbal mainstream as a premier "blood-cleansing" and skin herb, a role it held through the medieval and early-modern herbals.

**Avicenna (CM):** In the Unani *Qanun* stream, burdock (bardana) sits among the cooling, dissolving, and purifying drugs used for hot skin eruptions, abscesses, and to promote the clearing of morbid humours through urine and sweat.

**Shennong / TCM (SBJ):** Chinese medicine uses two distinct parts. The seed (fruit), **Niu Bang Zi (牛蒡子)**, is a classic acrid-bitter, cold herb that "disperses wind-heat," clears heat and toxin, benefits the throat (for sore, swollen throat and mumps), and vents rashes such as measles. The root, **Niu Bang (gobō)**, is widely eaten as a food and used as a cooling, moistening, detoxifying tonic. This dual food-and-medicine identity is one of burdock's defining features.

**Sushruta / Ayurveda (SS):** The Ayurvedic surgical tradition and later nighantus employ burdock-type bitter-cooling roots as *rakta-shodhana* (blood-purifying) and diuretic agents for pitta-driven skin disorders — consistent across all four traditions in the "cooling cleanser" role.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The burr that clings to fur and cloth — the inspiration for hook-and-loop fastening — makes burdock the emblem of *seizure and release*: a plant that grips in order to be carried, then lets its cargo go. In Kenetica this reads as the Purification-phase dynamic of binding to what must be cleared and escorting it out. The symbolism is grounded in real pharmacology: burdock root's inulin and the seed's lignans genuinely act on hepatic and inflammatory "clearance" pathways.

### Active Components

- **Inulin** — a fructan storage carbohydrate abundant in the root; a prebiotic fibre underlying burdock's traditional gut and "detox" reputation.
- **Arctigenin and arctiin** — dibenzylbutyrolactone lignans concentrated in the seed (Niu Bang Zi); the principal bioactive lignans, with anti-inflammatory, antifibrotic and antitumour activity in preclinical work.
- **Caffeoylquinic acids** (chlorogenic acid and derivatives) — antioxidant phenolics of the root.
- **Polyacetylenes and sesquiterpenes** — contributing antimicrobial and bitter qualities.
- **Sterols and mucilage** — supporting the moistening, demulcent food quality of the root.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Burdock is a cornerstone **Purification-phase** herb and, unlike columbine or arum in this volume, a genuinely usable one. The root is deployed as a gentle, food-grade cooling cleanser: supporting digestion via inulin, promoting mild diuresis and diaphoresis, and forming the base of Purification-phase broths and decoctions. The seed (Niu Bang Zi) is reserved for the acute "vent heat from the surface" indication — sore, swollen throat and heat-type skin eruptions — where its acrid-cold dispersing action is used short-term. The Kenetica logic treats root as *foundation-cleanse* (sustained, nourishing) and seed as *acute-vent* (brief, dispersing).

### Scientific Correlation

Burdock's seed lignan arctigenin is the best-studied constituent, with a growing preclinical literature.

- According to PubMed, Zhai et al. (2026), *Chemical Biology & Drug Design*, reported that arctigenin alleviated osteoarthritis pain in a rat model by suppressing chondrocyte pyroptosis via the POU2F1/GRB10 axis, lowering pro-inflammatory cytokines and NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD markers. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/cbdd.70300
- According to PubMed, Xia et al. (2025), *Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry*, showed that arctigenin attenuates hepatic stellate cell activation by suppressing endoplasmic-reticulum stress and restoring lipid homeostasis via ERAD, supporting an antifibrotic role in liver disease. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jafc.5c01366
- According to PubMed, Chen et al. (2025), *Toxicology Letters*, characterised the metabolic activation of arctigenin: CYP3A generates a reactive quinone-methide intermediate that covalently binds hepatic protein and produced cytotoxicity at 50 μM in mouse hepatocytes — a genuine hepatotoxicity signal relevant to high-dose isolated-compound use. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.toxlet.2025.111785

**Evidence grade: Preclinical (strong mechanistic), with cross-tradition convergence.** Anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects of arctigenin are well demonstrated in animal and cell models; human clinical evidence for whole-herb burdock remains limited. The toxicology data (Chen et al.) counsel against high-dose isolated arctigenin.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** Niu Bang Zi — acrid, bitter, cold; enters Lung and Stomach. Actions: disperse wind-heat, vent rashes, clear heat/toxin, benefit throat.
- **Ayurveda:** *Tikta* (bitter) rasa, *sheeta* (cold) virya, *katu* vipaka; *pitta*-pacifying, *rakta-shodhana* (blood-purifying) and *mutrala* (diuretic).
- **Galenic / Unani:** Cold and dry; dissolving, deobstruent, and diaphoretic.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The large-leaved burrs of *Arktion* / *Personata* are given a therapeutic account — root and leaves for cleansing wounds, drawing heat, and treating swellings and skin complaints — and Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system to assign the plant a cultivation status. What the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention: recognising the burr-plant, distinguishing root from leaf, judging the cleansing preparation. That is provenance and correct working, not a vital-body doctrine. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** In the *Qanun* stream burdock (*bardana*) sits among the cooling, dissolving, purifying drugs used for hot skin eruptions and abscesses and to clear morbid humours through urine and sweat. This is a deobstruent-therapeutic use; the tradition's actual cultivation idea — conservation of innate heat and radical moisture as a finite endowment — is a longevity frame of the whole regimen, not a status assigned to a cooling purgative herb, and none is claimed. **(b) framed:** attested and used within the humoral scheme, no cultivation status sourced.

**Chinese medicine — post-classical (TCM).** Chinese practice uses two parts: the seed *Niu Bang Zi* (牛蒡子), an acrid-bitter, cold herb that disperses wind-heat, vents rashes and benefits the throat; and the root *Niu Bang* (gobō), eaten as a food. The seed's action is dispersing and short-course — a disease-treating (治病) function rather than the conservational project of *yang sheng* 養生 — and no *san pin* 三品 grade is recorded for this species in the sources retrieved; nor is the seed's earliest secure attestation the classical *Shennong Bencao Jing* stratum. It therefore carries a therapeutic, not a cultivation, status, and the root's standing is that of a food. **(b) framed**, with no grade asserted.

**Ayurveda (SS).** The attestation here is by type rather than by a named classical dravya: the surgical tradition and later *nighantus* employ bitter-cooling, blood-purifying (*rakta-shodhana*) and diuretic roots of the burdock kind for pitta-driven skin disorders, but *Arctium lappa* itself is not a species of the classical Sushrutan corpus. *Rakta-shodhana* is in any case a therapeutic-purificatory action, not the formal rejuvenative disciplines (*rasayana* / *vajikarana*), and no such rejuvenative assignment is recorded for this plant. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator uses this one at the surface, to clear rather than to build. The root (gobō) is food-grade and can carry a sustained, gentle Purification-phase cleanse — a cooling, inulin-bearing broth or decoction — while the seed is reserved for the brief, acute job of venting heat from a sore throat or a hot rash, then stopped. Nothing across the four traditions makes it a longevity or vitality-building substance; the toxicology on high-dose isolated arctigenin reinforces the "whole-root, moderate, time-limited" reading.

### Contraindications

- **Asteraceae allergy:** May cross-react in people sensitive to ragweed, daisies, chrysanthemums; can cause contact dermatitis.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** Traditionally cautioned (potential uterine and diuretic activity); avoid without professional supervision.
- **Diuretic and hypoglycaemic interaction:** May add to the effect of diuretics and blood-glucose-lowering drugs — monitor if diabetic.
- **Isolated arctigenin at high dose:** Preclinical hepatotoxicity via reactive-metabolite formation (Chen et al.). Whole-root food use is not implicated, but concentrated seed/lignan supplements should be used cautiously and not with hepatotoxic co-medication.
- **Misidentification hazard:** Wild burdock root can be confused with the toxic belladonna (*Atropa*) root when foraging — a documented cause of poisoning. Source only verified material.
