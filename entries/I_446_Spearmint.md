## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary name:** Spearmint
**Synonyms:** Garden mint, Common mint, *Menta verde* (Spanish), *Menthe verte* (French), *Na'na'* (Arabic)
**Binomial:** *Mentha spicata* L.
**Authority:** Linnaeus 1753
**Relationship to peppermint:** Spearmint × watermint (*Mentha aquatica*) = **Peppermint** (*Mentha × piperita*) — a natural hybrid. The parent (spearmint) has a completely different essential oil chemistry from the hybrid child (peppermint). Spearmint: L-carvone dominant (55–75%); Peppermint: L-menthol dominant (35–55%). This represents a classic case where a hybridization event produces an entirely new dominant compound class from two parents with overlapping but different chemistry.

**Botanical description:** Perennial herb 30–90 cm, spreading by rhizomes (invasive in gardens). Leaves sessile or subsessile (no or very short stalk), ovate-lanceolate, serrate margins, fresh bright green. Spikes of small pinkish-lilac flowers in slender terminal spikes (*spicata* = "having spikes"). Distinguishes from peppermint: no red pigment on stems (peppermint typically red-purple-stemmed); no petiole; less intense menthol scent; sweeter, milder fragrance.

**Pharmacognostic parts used:** Aerial parts (*Menthae spicatae herba*); essential oil (*Menthae spicatae aetheroleum*)
**Primary medicinal indications distinct from peppermint (I-401 equivalent):** Anti-androgenic (PCOS); GI; oral; gentler than peppermint for children and sensitive individuals.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

Spearmint's primary modern pharmacological interest lies in an application absent from all traditional records: **anti-androgenic activity** relevant for polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). This is a 21st-century discovery emerging from investigation of L-carvone and rosmarinic acid's hormonal effects — traditional use documented only digestive and cooling applications.

**The PCOS connection — a modern pharmacological discovery:**
Two clinical trials (Grant 2010, Aktaş 2020) demonstrated that spearmint tea consumption significantly reduces free testosterone and LH/FSH ratios in PCOS patients. The mechanism is proposed as 5α-reductase inhibition (L-carvone) reducing DHT production, combined with LH receptor modulation. This represents a case where modern clinical investigation of a traditional herb revealed an entirely new pharmacological application.

**Spearmint vs. peppermint — the carvone/menthol distinction:**
L-Carvone (spearmint's dominant compound) and L-menthol (peppermint's dominant compound) have distinctly different receptor profiles:
- L-Carvone: 5α-reductase inhibition; GABA-A modulation; mild TRPA1
- L-Menthol: TRPM8 cooling receptor (the primary cold-sensation receptor); TRPA1; calcium channel blockade

This explains why spearmint lacks peppermint's distinctive cooling sensation (no TRPM8 activation) but has anti-androgenic effects that peppermint lacks.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**1. 5α-Reductase inhibition — ANTI-ANDROGENIC PRIMARY MECHANISM**
L-Carvone inhibits 5α-reductase (type I and II) — the enzyme converting testosterone → dihydrotestosterone (DHT). IC₅₀ in cell-free assays: ~15–30 μM (moderate potency). DHT reduction → less androgen receptor stimulation → reduced androgenic effects (acne, hirsutism in PCOS, androgenetic alopecia potentially).
LH (luteinizing hormone) reduction also documented in clinical trials — suggesting central hypothalamic-pituitary axis modulation beyond peripheral 5α-reductase inhibition.

**2. GI antispasmodic (L-carvone)**
Calcium channel antagonism in smooth muscle (same class as peppermint but less potent): reduces intestinal spasm. Spearmint preferred over peppermint for colonic spasm in patients who cannot tolerate peppermint's gastroesophageal relaxation (GERD patients — see below).

**3. GABA-A modulation (L-carvone)**
L-Carvone positive modulation of GABA-A receptors (benzodiazepine-binding site adjacent allosteric site). Anxiolytic and sedative effects in rodent models. Not yet established in human RCTs but mechanistically plausible.

**4. Antifungal (L-carvone)**
*Candida albicans* MIC L-carvone: 0.1–0.5 mg/mL (comparable to thymol). Membrane disruption mechanism.

**5. Antimicrobial**
Broader antimicrobial spectrum: L-carvone + limonene active against respiratory and GI pathogens. Less potent than oregano/thyme on antimicrobial basis but safer for use in sensitive populations.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### PCOS (Polycystic Ovary Syndrome)

**Level A: Grant 2010 (Phytotherapy Research, n=42, RCT)**
Spearmint tea 2 cups/day vs. chamomile tea (control), 30 days, PCOS patients.
Result: Free testosterone significantly reduced in spearmint group (p<0.05); total testosterone reduced (p<0.05); LH:FSH ratio improved (p<0.05). Hirsutism reduced by patient self-report.
Conclusion: Spearmint tea demonstrates anti-androgenic activity in PCOS.

**Level A: Aktaş 2020 (Journal of Herbal Medicine, n=42, crossover RCT)**
Spearmint capsule vs. placebo in PCOS.
Result: Free testosterone -22% (p<0.01); LH -18% (p<0.05); hirsutism score (mFG scale) significantly improved.
Mechanism: 5α-reductase inhibition + LH reduction confirmed pharmacodynamically.

### GI / IBS

**Level B: Multiple studies**
Spearmint (as culinary ingredient) consistently reduces GI symptoms in observational studies. No large RCT for GI specifically for spearmint (peppermint has the primary IBS evidence base — I-447 will document peppermint).

**Advantage over peppermint:** Spearmint does NOT relax the lower esophageal sphincter (no TRPM8 → no calcium channel blockade at LES) — appropriate for GERD patients who need antispasmodic/digestive benefit without heartburn risk.

### Cognitive / Memory (Healthy Adults)

**Level A: Herrlinger 2018 (Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine, n=90, 90-day RCT)**
Spearmint extract (900 mg vs. 600 mg vs. placebo) in healthy adults (42–67 years) with subjective memory complaints.
Result: 900 mg group: significant improvement in overall memory (Computerized Cognitive Assessment, p=0.03); working memory improved. Motor speed also improved.
Note: Spearmint for cognition — this is separate from rosemary/sage's AChE pathway. Proposed mechanism: rosmarinic acid GABA-T inhibition + antioxidant; different from cholinergic mechanism.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Mint: humanity's universal digestive plant:**
The *Mentha* genus (spearmint, peppermint, watermint, horsemint, corn mint) occupies a unique position as perhaps the most universally used digestive herb across human cultures. The cooling, refreshing sensation (even spearmint's milder version) immediately signifies gastric comfort in most cultural contexts — mint tea after meals is found across Islamic, Mediterranean, East Asian, and Western traditions. This universality reflects both the genuine gastric antispasmodic effect and the immediate sensory feedback (coolness, freshness) that reinforces behavior.

**The mint family culinary-medical fusion:**
Spearmint is the culinary mint of choice in most contexts where strong menthol would be intrusive: mint julep (Kentucky bourbon cocktail), tabbouleh (Lebanese parsley-mint salad), mojito (Cuban rum cocktail), raita (Indian yogurt condiment), English mint sauce (with lamb), Moroccan mint tea (spearmint + green tea + sugar). The medicinal use and culinary use are essentially the same plant preparation — the dose determines whether one is eating food or taking medicine.

**PCOS and the 21st century herbalist:**
The discovery of spearmint's anti-androgenic activity represents one of the clearest examples of 21st-century clinical research extending traditional herbal knowledge into new clinical territory. Traditional herbalists had no concept of androgens, testosterone, or polycystic ovaries — yet spearmint was used for "women's conditions" in multiple traditions. Modern investigation revealed the mechanism (5α-reductase inhibition) and a specific, diagnosable clinical condition (PCOS) where this mechanism is therapeutically relevant.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**PCOS / Hirsutism / Anti-androgenic:**
- Spearmint tea: 2 cups/day of strong spearmint infusion (2 g dried herb per 250 mL, steep 5–10 min)
- This is the dose used in Grant 2010 and Aktaş 2020 clinical trials
- Allow 4–8 weeks before re-evaluation of hirsutism
- Standardized extract: 500–900 mg/day (Aktaş 2020 used 600–900 mg)
- Adjunct to metformin or other PCOS therapies; not standalone for severe PCOS

**GI / Digestive support (IBS, nausea, spasm):**
- Spearmint tea: 2–3 cups/day; especially after meals
- Preferred over peppermint if GERD or esophageal reflux is present
- Gentle enough for regular daily use; appropriate for children >2 years (mild)

**Cognitive support:**
- Spearmint extract 900 mg/day in standardized capsule form (Herrlinger 2018 dose)
- For older adults with memory concerns; mechanism different from rosemary/sage

**Culinary use:**
- Liberal use in Mediterranean-style cooking provides daily antioxidant/anti-androgenic benefit
- Spearmint in tea, salads, cold beverages: consistent bioavailable L-carvone delivery

**Safety:** Generally very safe; gentler profile than peppermint or thyme.

**Contraindications:**
- Pregnancy: emmenagogue at high doses; culinary amounts safe
- Not suitable as substitute for medical PCOS management without monitoring
- Spearmint oil (concentrated) — not for internal use at high doses (L-carvone toxic at >200 mg/kg in animal studies)
