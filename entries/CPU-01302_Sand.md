# Sand — Ammos (as printed in the 2000 English edition of Dioscorides)
## *Sand, the translator's gloss under 5-167 AMMOS; the text means sand "heated by the sun on the shores", and gives no mineral, colour or locality beyond that* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus for this entry is *De Materia Medica* 5-167 *Ammos* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, line 10700; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row "5-167 (line 10700)"; `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 893, class mineral). The translator's gloss under the heading is "Sand" (line 10701). It stands in Book 5 after 5-166 *Smuris Lithos* (line 10692) and before 5-168 *Akone*, the whetstone (line 10703); 5-170 *Ge*, earth, follows at line 10711.

The whole chapter is one sentence and a half (line 10702): "Sand heated by the sun on the shores dries up the moisture of dropsy in bodies when they are covered all over with it, except the head. It is roasted for dry warm packs instead of millet or salt." The word "dropsy" is the translator's English for the swelling of the body from fluid; the sentence does not name organs. The method is spelled out only in outline: the patient is covered with sun-warmed sand over the whole body except the head, and the same sand can be roasted and used in dry warm packs as a substitute for millet or salt.

So the chapter gives two uses of one material, in two forms. The first is a sun-heated shore sand used as a cover for the body in dropsy. The second is a roasted sand used in the way millet or salt is used in dry warm packs (the words "instead of millet or salt" suggest that those two were the usual materials for such packs). It gives no preference between kinds of sand, no place other than "the shores", no time of day, no duration, no number of applications, and no instruction on how to cover the body.

The word "sand" appears in other chapters of Book 5 in a different way, as a raw material in the making of other substances: "spuma argenti or froth of silver is made from sand called molibditis [molybdenite — disulphide of molybdenum] blown in a furnace" (5-102 *Litharguros*, line 10342), and Cyprian *cyanus* is made "from sand at the shore that is found near some hollow places of the sea" (5-106 *Kuanos*, line 10379). Those chapters concern other substances, and the Greek words for "sand" there have not been checked here; they are mentioned only to separate this chapter from them. [USER: verify]

No other tradition is tagged for this entry. The Ebers (Bryan) text contains the word "sand" (lines 3592 and 3649 of `Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`), but only in a diagnostic remark on a person with a hardness in his left side ("He has dug sand-bags and lifted sand", line 3592) and in a description of how a finding moves "under thy fingers like grains of sand" (line 3649). Neither is a remedy made of sand, only the line or two around each was read, and neither has been added as a locus.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, shore sand is a loose mixture of mineral grains, most often quartz (silicon dioxide) with smaller amounts of other minerals and shell fragments, and its composition varies from beach to beach. It is insoluble and chemically inert in the body, and its therapeutic action in the chapter, if any, is physical: it holds heat. Sun-heated sand reaches temperatures that depend on the place and the season, and the text gives none. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved say that Dioscorides recommended a patient with dropsy be covered with sun-heated shore sand, except the head, and that roasted sand served for dry warm packs in place of millet or salt. Burying a swollen or ill person in hot sand risks burns, heat injury and dehydration, and swelling of the body from fluid is a sign of heart, kidney or liver disease that needs diagnosis. The Codex does not recommend the practice. The chapter supports a historical entry and nothing more.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations the text gives are two: sand heated by the sun on the shore and applied over the body except the head; and sand roasted for dry warm packs (line 10702). It does not say how hot, for how long, how often, how deep the sand is, or what the pack is made of. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Heat applied to the body makes a person sweat and can temporarily shift fluid; it does not remove the cause of oedema. Hot sand causes burns and heat illness, and hot-sand cover on a person with heart or circulatory disease is dangerous. Sand is inert, and a dry warm pack of any granular material acts by holding and releasing heat. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** the chapter prints one quality: sand "dries up the moisture of dropsy" (line 10702). That is an action, and it implies drying, but the chapter does not print a taste, a degree of heat or cold, or any other rating, and no qualities are transferred from the salt chapters. No other tradition is tagged, so no other classification is offered.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for sand. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, adulteration), and this short chapter gives one small element of it: the sand is to be taken "heated by the sun on the shores", so the place and the heat source are named. It gives no test of the sand and no season. The chapter is therapeutic and assigns no cultivation status. No amuletic use is recorded.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The entry records a Greek external treatment for swelling of the body, a covering of sun-warmed shore sand, and a use of roasted sand in place of millet or salt for dry warm packs. It is a plain warming measure, not a regimen, and the Codex does not recommend burying the body in hot sand. The one practical lesson is the limit of the record: the chapter is short and gives no quantities. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Burns and heat illness:** sun-heated or roasted sand can burn skin and cause heat stroke; covering the body is unsafe for the very young, the old, those with diabetes or poor sensation, and anyone with a heart or circulatory condition.
- **Swelling of the body (dropsy, oedema):** this is a symptom of heart, kidney, liver or other disease and needs a clinician; heat is not a treatment for its cause.
- **Covering the body:** burial in sand can restrict breathing and movement; it is not safe to try.
- **Hygiene:** shore sand can contain microbes, sharp fragments and contaminants and is unsuitable on broken skin.
- **Identification:** "sand" is a translator's gloss for the Greek *ammos*; the text does not say which kind of sand was meant.
