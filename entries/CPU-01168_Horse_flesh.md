# Horse flesh — Ashva (horse), the flesh of the horse and of other "unbifurcated-hoofed" animals (Ekashapha)
## *The meat of the horse, as one of the domestic animals whose flesh the Sushruta Samhita describes in its chapter on food and drink* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The locus is Sushruta Samhita, *Sutrasthana* chapter 46, the chapter on food and drink (*Annapana-vidhi*), in Bhishagratna's English (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt`; `_sources/sushruta.csv` line 230, "Ashva (horse)", animal, "Sutrasthana ch.46 (Annapanavidhi); Bhishagratna trans. vol.1"). The horse is named in three places in that chapter.

First, in the opening of the meat section (line 384 onward): "The flesh of animals such as those which are aquatic in their habits (Jaleshaya), or frequent marshy lands (Anupa), or dwell in villages (Gramya), or are carnivorous in their habits (Kravyabhuja), or are possessed of unbifurcated hoofs (Ekashapha), or dwell on high ground (Jangala), is generally used as food. Of these each succeeding kind is superior to the one immediately preceding it in the order of enumeration" (lines 384-392). The *Ekashapha* group, the animals with undivided hoofs, is therefore one of six divisions of edible flesh; by the text's own order each is superior to the one before it, so it is ranked above the village (*Gramya*) and carnivorous (*Kravyabhuja*) animals and below the high-ground (*Jangala*) animals. A second passage lists the animals in a different order, ending with those "possessed of unbifurcated hoofs", and says that "the flesh of each preceding one is lighter and tends to give rise to a lesser secretion from the internal organs than the one immediately following it in the order of enumeration" (lines 951-960); in that list the hoofed group comes after the dry-land, marsh, domesticated and carnivorous animals and before those that dart on prey, dwell in holes, are long-legged, eat by pricking and scatter their food with the claws, so by the passage's rule it is heavier than the first four and lighter than the rest.

Second, the "Domestic Animal Group" (lines 614-617): "Animals such as horses, mules, cows, bullocks, asses, camels, goats, sheep, and Medapuchhas (fat tailed or Turkish sheep) etc., belong to the group of domestic animals (Gramyas)." The metrical text that follows gives the properties of the group: "The flesh of domestic animals is possessed of constructive, tonic and appetising properties, is sweet in taste and digestion. It destroys the deranged Vayu and produces the Kapham and Pittam" (lines 621-625).

Third, the specific line (lines 646-649): "The flesh of an animal with unbifurcated hoofs (Ekashapha such as, the horse and the mule etc.), has a slightly saline taste, and is possessed of properties similar to those of mutton." The mutton line just before gives the comparison: "The flesh of sheep (mutton) is constructive, tonic and heavy, and generates the Pittam and Kapham" (lines 637-639).

A fourth mention concerns what to drink afterwards. In the after-drinks passage (lines 2826-2851): "Triphala wine should be taken after the flesh of an animal with unbifurcated hoofs (Ekashapha)."

The chapter also gives general rules for any flesh. "Dried or putrid flesh, as well as the flesh of a diseased, old, emaciated, poisoned, or snake-bitten animal, or of one of extremely tender years or struck with a poisoned dart or weapon, or of that which has fed on unnatural food, should be carefully avoided" (lines 869-874), and the metrical text lists the harms (lines 887-896). These rules are not horse-specific.

The text does not give the Sanskrit word for the horse in the English line; "Ashva" is the spine's and the list file's name. [USER: verify the Sanskrit as printed in the original]

### Active Components

Horse meat, like other mammalian muscle, is mostly water and protein, with fat that varies with the animal and the cut, and it contains iron, B vitamins and zinc. It generally has a lower fat content than beef from fattened cattle, though that varies. No analysis is given in the sources, and no figures are offered.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The Sushruta passages class horse flesh as an ordinary food of the *Ekashapha* and domestic groups, with properties like those of mutton. They do not present it as a medicine for a named disease. The Codex's application layer adds no protocol.

It is not a recommended practice to take horse flesh for any therapeutic purpose on this authority. The text offers no dose, no preparation and no indication. In many countries the sale or eating of horse meat is lawful and ordinary, in others it is restricted or taboo, and in many communities the horse is a companion or working animal whose slaughter for food is ethically unacceptable. Those are matters of law, custom and conscience that this entry records and does not decide.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives no preparation for horse flesh by name. Its general instructions for meat, cooking methods and after-drinks apply to the group; the specific after-drink for *Ekashapha* flesh is Triphala wine (lines 2849-2851). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] General facts: horse meat is a source of protein and iron, as other red meats are. Like other meats it must be cooked thoroughly. Horses can carry parasites such as *Trichinella* and, as with any meat, bacteria, so inspection and cooking matter. The Ayurvedic properties given above (sweet, saline, like mutton) belong to the *dravyaguna* framework and have no modern equivalent that can be tested.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS), as printed in ch.46: the flesh of domestic animals is "sweet in taste and digestion" and "destroys the deranged Vayu and produces the Kapham and Pittam" (lines 621-625); the flesh of the *Ekashapha* (the horse and the mule) "has a slightly saline taste" and properties "similar to those of mutton" (lines 646-649), where mutton is "constructive, tonic and heavy" and "generates the Pittam and Kapham" (lines 637-639). The text prints no *virya* (potency) or *vipaka* line for horse flesh separately; the "sweet in digestion" statement for the domestic group is the only post-digestive quality in the lines read.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Horse flesh is attested in the *Sutrasthana* food-and-drink chapter as a food of the *Ekashapha* and domestic groups. It is not named in the lines read as a *rasayana* (rejuvenation) or *vajikarana* (generative vitality) substance. The chapter opens by saying that food is the source of bodily strength and complexion "as well as of the albuminous (Ojas) principle in their organisms" (line 12), but that is a statement about food in general and is not applied to horse flesh. The word "horse" does appear in the *vajikarana* chapter (*Chikitsasthana* ch.26), but only in similes: the remedies make a man "sexually as strong as a horse (Vaji)" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/chikitsa/SS_Chikitsa_ch26.txt` lines 24, 148), not as a prescription of horse flesh. The sattvic, rajasic and tamasic food lists are Samkhya-era material, not Sushruta, and are not used.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. The text treats horse flesh as ordinary meat, with a taste, an effect on the humours and a wine to follow it; it gives no cultivation status, and the horse in the *vajikarana* chapter is only a figure of speech. The honest reading is that this is a diet entry, and a diet entry about an animal that many people would not eat.

### Contraindications

- **Not a recommended therapeutic practice.** This is a historical record of how a classical text classed a meat.
- **Legal and cultural:** the eating of horse meat is lawful in some countries and prohibited or taboo in others; check local law. [USER: verify]
- **Hygiene:** raw or undercooked meat carries bacterial and parasitic risk; cook thoroughly.
- **Quality rules in the text:** the chapter itself says to avoid dried or putrid flesh and the flesh of diseased, old, emaciated, poisoned or snake-bitten animals (lines 869-874).
- **Medicines in the animal:** horses treated with certain veterinary drugs (for example phenylbutazone) are barred from the human food chain in many jurisdictions; do not eat an animal of unknown history. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** many people regard the horse as a companion animal; this is a matter for the reader's conscience.
