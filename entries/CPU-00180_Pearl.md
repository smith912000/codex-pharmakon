# Pearl — Pearl (rakta mutika, gandza mu.tig, wa.lu.mu.tig and the other types) and Mother of Pearl (Clark 1995) / "Pearl (Pearl Oyster)" and "Freshwater Mussel Pearl" (project rGyud-bzhi list)
## *Pearl and its shell; Clark's text names seven pearl types and only one of them comes from "a sea creature ... which resembles an oyster", and the binomials Pinctada martensii and Cristaria plicata are the project's list, not the text's* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Explanatory Tantra ch.20 (Clark 1995, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`) divides the powers of individual natural substances into eight sections: "(1) precious [metal] medicines (2) stone medicines (3) earth medicines (4) tree medicines (5) resins (6) plants (7) herbs (8) animal products" (line 802). Pearl is in the first section, among the precious metals and gems, between turquoise and mother of pearl (line 812). The entry runs: "Pearl: [pearl has seven names. The first type, rakta mutika, is held to be supremely precious, because Buddhas have manifested as the red creature from which red pearl is obtained. The second type, gandza mu.tig, is sparkling white and comes from inside the skull or tusk of elephant. The third type of pearl is wa.lu.mu.tig, which is bright blue and comes from South India. It is found inside the leaves of the du.ya tree after the rain has fallen. The fourth type, ram.pa.mu.tig, is slightly greenish and is the size of a pea. In East India it is found in the leaves of the banana tree after rain has fallen. The fifth type, smad.mu.tig, is bright red and comes from the West of Hor, where it is found in the brain of a species of snake." (line 812). "The sixth type of pearl, shing.pi.ka.mu.tig, varies in size and has a pale yellow radiance. It comes from the stomach of a sea creature called spang.rtsi, which resembles an oyster and is found around Sri Lanka." (line 813). "All types of pearls without eyes are of male gender and those having many layers are considered female. Pearl] prevents loss of cerebral fluid and cures all types of poisoning." (line 814). Only six of the seven types are described in the lines read. Clark's introduction (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets are taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, so the seven names and the type descriptions are commentary, and the sentence outside the brackets, with the mother-of-pearl line, is the root text's. Of the seven types, four are said to come from a creature or an animal body (the red creature, the elephant's skull or tusk, the snake's brain, the sea creature); two from leaves after rain; one from South India without a source given. None of the lines names a mollusc as the source of an ordinary white pearl. *Mother of pearl*: "Mother of Pearl has three names and its power is not unlike that of pearl" (line 815). The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` lines 3330-3333) gives the power in short form: "Pearl stops cerebrospinal fluid discharge and acts as an antidote against poison. Shells has the same potency as that of pearl." The project's list `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` has four rows that were folded together here: "Pearl (Pearl Oyster)" with the binomial *Pinctada martensii* (line 98), "Freshwater Mussel Pearl" with *Cristaria plicata* (line 104, flagged id-uncertain), "Pearl" (line 126, class mineral, the ch.20 reference) and "Shells" (line 127, the mother of pearl line). The two binomials are the list's identifications; no text on disk prints them, and the mussel row has no passage that was found in Clark or the Men-Tsee-Khang text (a search of both for "mussel" returned nothing). ### Active Components

Pearl is calcium carbonate (aragonite) in thin layers with an organic protein matrix (conchiolin), made by a mollusc around an irritant. Mother of pearl is the inner shell layer of the same type. The sources read give no composition and describe pearls from several kinds of origin (see above).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text lists pearl in the section on precious substances and states two actions, preventing loss of cerebral fluid and curing poisoning. Nothing in the lines read assigns it a longevity or rejuvenation action, in contrast to gold in the same section ("Gold promotes longevity, strengthens the aged", Clark line 804). The entry offers no protocol, dose or preparation. Pearls from the wild and from farmed shellfish are commercial goods, and the Codex does not recommend taking powdered pearl.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation is given in the pearl entry of Explanatory Tantra ch.20. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Pearl and shell are mostly calcium carbonate; as a powder they are a calcium source and a mild antacid. There is no established effect on "loss of cerebral fluid", and pearls do not detoxify poisons. Powdered shell can carry heavy metals or contaminants. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- FMT: the pearl entry prints no taste, potency or post-digestive taste. The text says the power of mother of pearl is "not unlike that of pearl" (Clark line 815) and the Men-Tsee-Khang text that shells have "the same potency" (lines 3332-3333). Elsewhere in the same section the entries for copper and iron print taste and potency (Clark lines 806-810); none is printed for pearl.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* The medicine is the third of the four treatment branches: it supports practice and does not reach the root of the three poisons, which are at root a spiritual condition. Pearl is claimed in the section of precious substances in Explanatory Tantra ch.20 and given two therapeutic actions; no longevity, rejuvenation or *bcud len* action is attested for pearl in the lines read, and no pearl occurs in the rejuvenation compound named in Clark's later chapter (a search of Clark for "pearl" returned lines 812-815 and the index only). The first pearl type is explained by a religious account ("Buddhas have manifested as the red creature from which red pearl is obtained", line 812); that is the commentary's explanation of its preciousness, not a cultivation status. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here for a cultivator to take. The text calls pearl precious and gives it a poison and fluid-loss use, and then moves on. The text's own gloss, that the pearls come from many strange origins, is a caution against assuming a single material. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Pearl powder and shell powders are unstandardised and may be contaminated with heavy metals or bacteria; they should not be taken as an antidote to any poisoning, and a poisoning needs emergency care. Collecting pearl oysters and mussels may be restricted for conservation reasons, and the freshwater mussel (if the list's identification is right) is at risk in some places; the status has not been checked here. [USER: verify] The traditional origins of some pearl types from a snake's brain or an elephant's skull are not a basis for sourcing anything from those animals.
