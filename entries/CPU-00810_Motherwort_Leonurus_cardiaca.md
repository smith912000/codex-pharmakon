# Motherwort — Leonuri Cardiacae Herba, Lion's Tail, Herba Cardiaca

## *Leonurus cardiaca* L. | Family: Lamiaceae (Labiatae)

**Tradition Tags:** CM, SBJ, SS, FMT, EF
**Tags (previous line, retained for reference):** CM, DMM, SBJ, SS, FMT
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry (Galenic); bitter, moving-and-settling to the heart
**Training Phase:** Settling / Heart-Steadying (Kenetica) — calms an over-driven system

---

### Origin & Tradition

Motherwort is the herb of the steadied heart. Its Latin epithet *cardiaca* — "of the heart" — and its common name across a dozen European languages both preserve the same twin reputation: a remedy for the racing, fluttering, anxious heart, and a remedy for the labouring uterus. The bitter, square-stemmed European species (*Leonurus cardiaca*) and its East Asian sibling are two distinct but parallel medicines, and the encyclopaedia keeps them separate on purpose.

**⚠️ Nomenclature — read first.** Western "motherwort" is *Leonurus cardiaca* L. The Chinese herb **Yì Mǔ Cǎo (益母草)** — literally "benefit-mother herb" — is **properly *Leonurus japonicus*** Houtt. (older texts: *L. heterophyllus / L. artemisia*), a closely related but botanically **different species**. They share the *Leonurus* genus, overlapping alkaloid chemistry (leonurine, stachydrine) and a shared uterine reputation, but their classical monographs and dose traditions are not interchangeable. Where this entry cites Chinese doctrine it refers to Yì Mǔ Cǎo / *L. japonicus*; the binomial header and Western material refer to *L. cardiaca*.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** The Greco-Roman tradition knew *Leonurus*-type labiates as bitter cardiac and uterine herbs, used to quiet palpitation and "to bring on the courses" (promote menstruation) — the earliest Western statement of its dual heart-and-womb action.

**Shennong / Chinese (SBJ):** Yì Mǔ Cǎo (*L. japonicus*) is a cornerstone gynaecological herb of the Chinese materia medica, classically used to "invigorate blood, regulate menstruation, reduce swelling," and to move the *blood* of the lower burner postpartum. Its very name marks it as the mother's herb.

**Sushruta / Ayurvedic (SS):** *Leonurus* enters the wider Ayurvedic and folk pharmacopoeia of the subcontinent as a bitter *emmenagogue* and cardiac calmative, aligned with its Greek and Chinese uses.

**rGyud-bzhi / Tibetan (FMT):** The Four Tantras tradition employs bitter Lamiaceae herbs of this group as blood-moving and heart-settling agents, consistent with the cross-tradition consensus.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Motherwort sits at the crossing of two "hollow, rhythmically contracting muscular organs" — the heart and the uterus. The tradition intuited a single herb for both; modern pharmacology finds a real thread, since its constituents act on smooth and cardiac muscle contractility and on autonomic tone. The symbol — the lion's calm, maternal steadiness — matches a mild negative-chronotropic, calcium-antagonist profile: an herb that slows and settles what is racing.

### Active Components

- **Leonurine (an alkaloid)** — associated with uterotonic and vasoactive effects; the constituent that most defines the "mother's herb" reputation.
- **Stachydrine** — a betaine alkaloid, blood-moving in TCM pharmacology; used as a marker compound in refined extracts.
- **Phenolic acids and flavonoids** — chlorogenic acid, orientin, quercetin, hyperoside, rutin — antioxidant and mitochondrially active.
- **Bitter iridoids, diterpenes, and volatile oil** — contributing the characteristic bitterness and calmative action.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica, motherwort is a **Settling / Heart-Steadying** herb: indicated where the practitioner presents with a fast, pounding, anxious heart — palpitation of nervous rather than structural origin, tension-driven tachyarrhythmia, the somatic signature of an over-driven sympathetic system. It is used to lower the "gain" on an over-excited cardiovascular loop without sedating the mind heavily. Secondarily it is a menstrual-regulating and postpartum blood-mover in the traditional sense. It pairs with slower nervine tonics in the Settling phase and is explicitly withheld in phases where uterine quiescence is required (see Contraindications).

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, there is genuine mechanistic support for the traditional cardiac claim. Ritter et al. showed that a refined *Leonurus cardiaca* extract (LCRE), applied to isolated rabbit hearts and single-cell models, reduced left-ventricular pressure, increased coronary flow, prolonged the PQ interval, and acted on multiple electrophysiological targets — blocking L-type calcium current (I_Ca.L), reducing the repolarising I_Kr, and prolonging action-potential duration — a profile "used for centuries as a remedy against tachyarrhythmia" now given a plausible calcium-antagonist basis ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0029-1240602)). Bernatoniene et al. found that *L. cardiaca* polyphenols (chlorogenic acid, orientin, quercetin, hyperoside, rutin) partially uncouple cardiac mitochondria and attenuate reactive-oxygen-species generation, mechanisms consistent with cardioprotection ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0034-1368426)). A note of caution comes from Micota et al., who observed that at sub-inhibitory concentrations the extract has direct **anticoagulant** properties and can modulate *Staphylococcus aureus* virulence in both host-favourable and host-unfavourable directions — a reminder that "plant-derived polyphenols are not always beneficial" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1099/jmm.0.000332)).

**Evidence grade: MODERATE (preclinical).** Cardiac electrophysiological and antioxidant mechanisms are demonstrated in animal and cell models; robust human clinical trial data are limited. The uterotonic reputation is traditional and pharmacologically plausible but not established by controlled human trials — treat the pregnancy contraindication as precautionary-but-serious.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM (Yì Mǔ Cǎo / *L. japonicus*):** acrid and bitter, slightly cold; enters Heart, Liver, and Bladder channels; "invigorates blood, regulates menstruation, reduces swelling, promotes urination."
- **Ayurveda (SS):** bitter (*tikta*) rasa, cooling (*virya*); emmenagogue and cardiac calmative; *pitta*-neutral, *kapha*-reducing.
- **Galenic / Unani:** cold and dry; bitter cardiac and uterine deobstruent, "gladdening the heart" while promoting the menses.

### Cultivation Register

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The herb carries a Galenic–Unani humoral grading — cold and dry, a bitter cardiac and uterine deobstruent said to "gladden the heart" while promoting the menses. That "gladdening" gestures toward the *mufarrih* cardiac-drug idea, but that category belongs to the *Kitāb al-Adwiya al-Qalbiyya*, not the *Canon*, and no sourced attestation places this European plant there; no constituent list is offered. The tradition's proper cultivation idea — conservation of innate heat and radical moisture across the regimen — is not assigned to a cold-dry deobstruent. Claimed humorally, with no cultivation status sourced: **(b) framed.**

**Chinese — Bencao (SBJ).** The classical Chinese attestation is not for this plant. The Chinese "mother's herb," Yì Mǔ Cǎo 益母草, is *Leonurus japonicus* — a botanically distinct species whose monograph, as the entry's own nomenclature warning states, is not interchangeable with *L. cardiaca*. For that sibling species the tradition's use is 活血調經 (invigorate blood, regulate menstruation), a blood-moving therapeutic function of the lower burner, not a *yang sheng* 養生 or *san pin* 三品 cultivation status. None of it transfers to this entry's Western species, which the classical Chinese materia medica does not treat. **(c) absent** for the tagged species.

**Ayurveda (SS).** *Leonurus* enters the wider Ayurvedic and folk pharmacopoeia of the subcontinent as a bitter (*tikta*) herb of cooling (*virya*), *pitta*-neutral and *kapha*-reducing, worked as an emmenagogue and cardiac calmative. This is a *dravyaguna* profile — a therapeutic and dietetic placement — and no *rasayana* (rejuvenation) or *vajikarana* (generative vitality) status is recorded for it; nor is any action on *ojas*, *tejas*, or *prana* attested. Claimed within the tradition's substance-quality scheme, with no rejuvenative cultivation status: **(b) framed.**

**Tibetan (FMT).** The entry's Four Tantras attribution is group-level and inferential — "bitter Lamiaceae herbs of this group... consistent with the cross-tradition consensus" — rather than a species-specific *rGyud-bzhi* attestation of this plant. Sowa Rigpa carries no formal "substances for subtle-body practice" category to assign in any case; medicine there is the third of the four treatment branches, supporting practice rather than reaching the root of the three *nyes pa*. No cultivation status is attested. **(c) absent.**

**European folk / phytotherapy (EF).** This plant's documented Western medicinal record is post-classical. The genus name *Leonurus* and the epithet *cardiaca* are botanical Latin coined after antiquity, and the species enters the European pharmacopoeia not through any securely-attested classical source but through the sixteenth-century herbals — Fuchs (1543) and Bock (1577) — from which the living tradition descends: Culpeper, the nineteenth-century Eclectics, and, in the present, the EMA HMPC monograph (2010). Across that lineage "motherwort" / *Herba Cardiaca* is worked as a cardiac-nervine and uterine herb — a bitter, cold-and-dry deobstruent taken to steady the racing, anxious heart and to promote the menses, folk-glossed as "gladdening the heart." European folk medicine and phytotherapy carry no formal subtle-anatomy or vital-substrate doctrine, so there is no European vital-cultivation status to assign; what the tradition records is a therapeutic and restorative placement, not a vital-body one (Adams et al. 2012, *J. Ethnopharmacol.*; Wojtyniak et al. 2013, *Phytother. Res.*). Claimed within a therapeutic-humoral scheme, with no European cultivation doctrine to invoke: **(b) framed.**

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator uses motherwort as a settling herb, not a building one: it is taken in short, defined courses to lower the gain on a racing, anxious heart — an infusion or tincture of the bitter aerial parts — and, in supervised traditional practice, as a postpartum blood-mover. It is withheld in pregnancy and around anticoagulants. Nothing across these traditions marks it as a longevity or rejuvenative substance; its work is to calm and to move, then to stop.

### Contraindications

⚠️ **PREGNANCY — CONTRAINDICATED.**

- **Uterotonic / emmenagogue.** Motherwort has a long cross-tradition reputation (Greek, Chinese, Ayurvedic) as a uterine stimulant and menstrual promoter, and leonurine is associated with uterotonic activity. **Do not use in pregnancy** — it may stimulate uterine contraction. Avoid throughout gestation; its postpartum blood-moving use is a separate, supervised application.
- **Anticoagulant / bleeding risk.** The extract shows direct anticoagulant activity in vitro; use caution with anticoagulant/antiplatelet drugs and around surgery, and in those with bleeding disorders or heavy menstrual bleeding.
- **Cardiac drug interaction.** Because it acts on cardiac calcium current, conduction (PQ prolongation), and heart rate, it may interact with cardiac glycosides, beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers, and antiarrhythmics. Anyone with a diagnosed arrhythmia or structural heart disease should use it only under qualified supervision, not as self-treatment.
- Not a substitute for medical management of any cardiac condition.
