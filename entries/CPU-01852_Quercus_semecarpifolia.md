# Quercus semecarpifolia — mon.chha.ra, Moen chhara, "Indian Oak (Acorn)" (rGyud-bzhi, Clark 1995 line 996; Men-Tsee-Khang glossary line 5490 and running text by name)

## *Quercus semecarpifolia, the spelling that the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints (line 5490) as the only binomial of its line, which column order sets against "Moen chhara" (mon cha ra) and the English "Oak tree"; Clark 1995 prints the same drug under mon.chha.ra as "Indian Oak (Acorn)" with the spelling "Quercus semicarpifolia Sm" followed by two other species, Quercus lanuginosa and Quercus acutissima (line 996)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of `Sources/` for the spellings *semecarpifolia* and *semicarpifolia* found them only in the two Tibetan texts and in the Tibetan `names.csv`, so no other tradition in the repository's sources names this species. The row was added from a printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` the drug has **two** source rows. The batch locus is line 320: keyed by the **scientific name** "Quercus semecarpifolia", locus "Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (line 5490)", with the note "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". The second is line 123, keyed by the **Tibetan name** *mon.chha.ra*, locus "Clark 1995 (line 996)", with the translator identification "Indian Oak (Acorn); Quercus semicarpifolia Sm; Quercus lanuginosa; Quercus acutissima" and the note "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The spine binomial *Quercus semecarpifolia* is therefore the **first binomial** of Clark's identification, though the first name of the entry as a whole is the English "Indian Oak (Acorn)"; in the glossary it is the only binomial of its line. The two spellings, Clark's "semicarpifolia" and the glossary's "semecarpifolia", are merged in the spine row.

**Identity doubt.** Clark names three oaks for one drug. Her preface says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" (line 83) and that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect" (line 68). The Men-Tsee-Khang editors say (line 686) that names of medicinal substances are kept in phonetic Tibetan. The glossary binomial is set against the Tibetan name by column position, and the English column agrees ("Oak tree"). The identification is therefore supported as an oak by both editions, and as *Q. semecarpifolia* by both; whether the Tibetan drug is that species and not one of Clark's other two is open. **Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 996).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792). The text file does not carry the sub-heading "(6)"; the entry stands after "(5) Resins" (line 879) and just before "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997), after the entries for *da.trig* (Schizandra) and *smag* (powdered root of old pine trees). It reads:

"mon.chha.ra: Indian Oak (Acorn): Quercus semicarpifolia Sm: Quercus lanuginosa: Quercus acutissima: [has six names. It has a rounded tea plant-like leaf, small thorns and fruits similar to nutmeg. The black type yields a blood(-like resin)]. Aegle marmelos, Schizandra, pine root and acorn halt all types of hot and cold diarrhoea."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, those in round brackets from the oral instructions of two physicians. So the number of names, the leaf, the thorns, the fruit and the resin are the commentary layer. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the English and the three identifications, which are Clark's, and the sentence that Bael fruit, Schizandra, pine root and acorn "halt all types of hot and cold diarrhoea". Clark's name index lists *mon.chha.ra* (line 1727), "Indian Oak (acorn)" (line 1600), and the Latin index lists *Quercus semicarpifolia*, *Q. lanuginosa* and *Q. acutissima* (line 1634).

*Clark 1995, chapter 19, the astringent group (line 770).* Under "Groups of Medicines Having the Same Taste" (line 764): "The group of medicines having astringent taste includes (white) sandalwood, chebulic myrobalan, beleric myrobalan, blue Utpala flower (Nelumbo nucifera), meadow cranesbill, powdered (pine)root (Padus asiatica), acorns and Tibetan tamarisk."

*Clark 1995, chapter 21, the class of anti-diarrhoeals (line 1222).* In the chapter on compounding (heading at lines 1205 and 1206): "The class of anti-diarrhoeals consists of both wild and cultivated types of Bael fruit, Schizandra sphaerandra, Acorn, powdered root (of, for example, old pine tree), Plantain (Plantago depressa), Plantago lanceolata, Bastard Teak, the blood of Hodgson's antelope and Larkspur (Delphinium grandiflora Linn.)."

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "M" block).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. The phonetic names of the block are eleven: "Mak" and "Manu" (line 5456), "Manu pata" and "Manzira" (5464), "Maru tse", "Men ga", "Metok lugmig" and "Metok serchen" (5471 to 5474), **"Moen chhara" (line 5486)**, then "Mukchhung" and "Dhenyon" (5492). The transliterations are also eleven, from "smag" (5458) to **"mon cha ra" (5488)** and "smug chung", "mdan yon" (5492). The scientific names are ten: "Metroxylon sagu" (5461), "Inula racemosa" (5462), "Inula helenium" (5469), the OCR "Amnoites" (5469), "Butea monosperma" (5481), "Hedychium spicatum" (5482), "Aster himalaicus" (5483), "Calendula officinalis" (5484), **"Quercus semecarpifolia" (line 5490)** and "Meconopsis sp." (5492). Counted in order, "Moen chhara" is the ninth phonetic name, "mon cha ra" the ninth transliteration and *Quercus semecarpifolia* the ninth scientific name. The English column skips some slots but runs in the same order: "Elecampane", "Elecampane" (5494), "Bastard teak" (5495, which Clark's anti-diarrhoeal list also names, beside Maru tse), "Kind of ginger" (5496), "Calendula, marigold" (5497) and **"Oak tree" (5498)**, which follows the Calendula of the eighth entry and so falls on the ninth. The first entry does not fit: "smag" reads *Metroxylon sagu* here, where Clark's *smag* is the powdered root of old pine trees (*Padus asiatica*), so the two editions differ on that name; the Codex records the difference and does not resolve it. The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position only. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, chapter 19, the astringent group (line 3220).* Chapter 19 is "The Taste and the Post-Digestive Taste" (contents, line 220): "The group of medicinal substances having an astringent taste includes Tsendhen, Arura, Bharura, Upel, Gadhur, Mak, Moen chhara, Vombu and other similar substances." The list stands name for name beside Clark's astringent group (line 770): sandalwood, chebulic and beleric myrobalan, Utpala, meadow cranesbill, powdered pine root, acorns, Tibetan tamarisk, so "Moen chhara" falls where Clark's "acorns" do. The printed index lists "Moen chhara 188, 206, 222" (line 7442).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, chapter 20, the efficacy (line 3427).* In "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225): "Kapae, Dhaiig, Mak and Moen chhara, stop all forms of diarrhea irrespective of their being a hot or cold nature." The sentence stands name for name beside Clark's "Aegle marmelos, Schizandra, pine root and acorn halt all types of hot and cold diarrhoea" (line 996).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, chapter 21, the class (line 3644).* In "The Group and the Method of Compounding Medicine" (contents, line 229): "Kapae, Bilwa, Dhatig, Moen chhara, Mak, Tharam, Naram, Gya kyeg, antelope's blood and Jhakang are the group of medicines that cure diarrhea." The order follows Clark's class (line 1222): Bael fruit wild and cultivated, Schizandra, Acorn, powdered root, Plantain, Plantago lanceolata, Bastard Teak, antelope blood, Larkspur.

The two editions therefore agree in kind and in order: the drug is an oak fruit, in the astringent taste group and in the class that stops diarrhoea. The Codex's own list of rGyud-bzhi names (`_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv`) has no row for this drug under oak, acorn, Moen chhara or *Quercus*. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Quercus* is the oak genus (Fagaceae) and that oak fruit and bark contain tannins, which are astringent; these are statements about the genus and not findings of the Tibetan text. Nothing is stated here about what this drug contains. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that is in the astringent group (Clark line 770; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3220), in the class that stops diarrhoea (Clark line 1222; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3644), and one of four substances that "halt all types of hot and cold diarrhoea" (Clark line 996; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3427). These are the texts' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. Persistent diarrhoea needs medical care.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read name the drug and give no part beyond Clark's "Acorn" and "fruits", no preparation, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. The anti-diarrhoeal class is a list of names and gives no compound, no method and no dose. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it is in the astringent group and halts diarrhoea of both hot and cold kinds. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names (*mon.chha.ra*, *Moen chhara*, "acorn", *Quercus*) found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, colophon at line 3950; Clark's chapter 23, "Normal health", lines 1262 to 1276, which carries the rejuvenation passage), and the herb entries of Clark's chapter 20 that mention essence-extraction compounds (lines 971 and 1144) do not include it. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is an astringent oak fruit placed with the anti-diarrhoeals, with no preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one: nothing here supports gathering or taking it.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Astringent, tannin-rich plant material can constipate and irritate the stomach (a general fact about the class and not a finding of the text). Diarrhoea that is bloody, prolonged or in a child needs medical care and not an astringent. No preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No conservation information and no safety information for pregnancy were read. [USER: verify]
