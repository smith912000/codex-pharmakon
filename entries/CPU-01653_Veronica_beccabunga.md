# Veronica beccabunga — Sion to en odasin, "sium that grows in the waters" (Dioscorides 2-154); "Brooklime"

## *Veronica beccabunga [Linnaeus], the last of the names in the first group on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 2-154, glossed "Brooklime [Mabberley]"; the line also prints Sium and Anagallis-aquatica, and "other usage" lines give Sium latifolium ("Water Parsley, Water Parsnip"), Sium falcaria and Sium siculum* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk lists the plant under this name (the name indexes in `Sources/*/extract/names.csv` and the list files in `_sources/` were searched). The entry rests on one short chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line gives *Sium* and *Anagallis aquatica* first, and then *Veronica beccabunga*, and the "other usage" lines give three further water-parsnip names; they are different plants, and the Codex does not choose among them. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *2-154. SION TO EN ODASIN* at line 5859, SUGGESTED lines at 5860 to 5862, and the text at line 5863. The chapter stands in Book Two, in the section "Lachana: Vegetables" (from page 243; line 31), after *2-153. ARNOGLOSSON, ARNOGLOSSON MIKRON* (line 5833) and before *2-155. SISUMBRION* (line 5864). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 344, and the translators' indexes print "Veronica beccabunga 280" and "Sium aquaticum 280" (lines 17963 and 17562). A different index, a short one, prints "Veronica beccabunga 442" (line 11942). Page 442 carries a plate captioned "Brooklime Veronica beccabunga after FAGUET — 1888" (line 7120), which stands in Book Three between *3-66. KAROS* and *3-67. ANETHON*. It is a plate on a page of other chapters, and the text of this chapter does not mention brooklime or a veronica. The Codex does not attribute the plate to any chapter.

The whole chapter reads: "Sium aquaticum is a little shrub which is found in the water — upright, fat, with broad leaves similar to hipposelinum [3-78], yet somewhat smaller and aromatic — which is eaten (either boiled or raw) to break stones [kidney, bladder] and discharge them. Eaten they also induce the movement of urine, are abortifacient, expel the menstrual flow, and are good for dysentery. (Crateuas speaks of it thus: it is a herb like a shrub, little, with round leaves, bigger than black mint, similar to eruca [2-170]). It is also called anagallis aquatica, schoenos aromatica, as well as a sort of juncus odoratus, darenion, or laver."

The chapter describes the plant in one sentence: a "little shrub" found "in the water", "upright, fat", with "broad leaves similar to hipposelinum, yet somewhat smaller and aromatic". It is a plant that is eaten, "either boiled or raw". The uses named are breaking stones in the kidney or bladder "and discharge them", moving the urine, being "abortifacient", expelling the menstrual flow, and dysentery. A bracketed passage reports a second description from a named earlier writer: "Crateuas speaks of it thus: it is a herb like a shrub, little, with round leaves, bigger than black mint, similar to eruca". The names printed are *anagallis aquatica*, *schoenos aromatica*, *juncus odoratus*, *darenion* and *laver*. The description in the text, with "aromatic" leaves and broad leaves like those of *hipposelinon*, does not by itself decide between the translators' candidates, and the Codex does not try to.

**Overlap.** A chapter-level search of the entries folder for 2-154, the Greek heading, *Sium aquaticum*, *Veronica beccabunga* and *Brooklime* found no entry on this chapter. Two entries share names with it. I_1192 (*Sium angustifolium*, chapter 3-75 *ELEIOSELINON*) is a different chapter, and the name "Laver vulgo dicitur [Fuchs]" printed on its SUGGESTED line is the same Latin name as the word *laver* in this chapter's names. I_1000 (*Anagallis arvensis*, chapter 2-209 *ANAGALLIS*) is a different chapter, and this chapter prints *anagallis aquatica* both on its SUGGESTED line and among its names. The neighbouring chapter 2-155 *SISUMBRION* (line 5864) is glossed as water mint (*Mentha aquatica*). Nothing is merged.

The chapter's "aromatic" is a sensory note and not a statement of composition. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the translators' brooklime. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records an edible water plant used to break and pass stones, to move urine, to bring on the menstrual flow and to stop dysentery, and it calls the plant "abortifacient". These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. Kidney and bladder stones, dysentery and an unwanted or wanted pregnancy are conditions that need medical care, and the Codex gives no protocol for any of them. Plants that grow in water can also carry contamination, and the identification here is only a suggestion.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation: the plant "eaten (either boiled or raw)". It gives no quantity, no frequency, no duration and no method. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a habitat (in the water), a posture and build ("upright, fat"), a leaf comparison for recognition (like *hipposelinon*, somewhat smaller, aromatic), and a second description credited by name to Crateuas, which the author sets beside his own in brackets. Setting a named earlier witness beside the author's own is a part of the discipline. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The identification is the translators', and they print several unlike plants. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is the use of two descriptions side by side, the author's and the earlier writer's, to hold a recognition. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 2-154 prints no warning, though it calls the plant "abortifacient" and says it expels the menstrual flow, so pregnancy is a clear concern that the text does not otherwise address. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, and the translators offer several unlike plants, so no plant should be gathered or eaten on the strength of this entry. Water plants can carry contamination, and some water-side plants are poisonous, which the chapter does not address. The text gives no dose and no safety information for children or for any medicine taken at the same time. [USER: verify]
