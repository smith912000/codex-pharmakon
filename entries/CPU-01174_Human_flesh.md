# Human flesh — sha.chen ("great flesh"), as printed in Clark's translation of the rGyud-bzhi
## *Muscle tissue of a human being, as named in the Tibetan materia medica of animal origin and in the dietary advice for wind disorders* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan Tradition (FMT).** The text is Barry Clark's 1995 translation of the rGyud-bzhi, file `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`. Three places in it name human flesh.

1. Materia medica of animal origin. Under "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), in the "Meat" group, the text reads: "Human flesh: [also known as sha.chen, lit. 'great flesh'] heals sores, cures wind disorders, poisoning and septic conditions" (lines 1162-1163; the sentence runs across a page break). The project list `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` (line 343) carries the entry as "Misha", class animal, source "rGyud-bzhi, Explanatory Tantra ch.20". The text I read prints "sha.chen" and not "Misha", so the list's transliteration is unconfirmed. [USER: verify]

2. Diet for wind disorders. In the chapter headed "5. Methods of Treatment" (line 170), under "DIET", the recommended diet for wind disorders "(includes) meat of horse, donkey and marmot, one-year-old (dried) meat, human flesh,1 seed oil, one-year-old butter, raw cane sugar, garlic, onion, milk ..." (line 174). Clark's note 1 to that chapter reads: "The best type is of a hero who died in battle, but the flesh of any violent person would suffice" (Clark, notes to Chapter 5, line 1525). This is what the translation says; I read no further explanation of why that qualification is given. [USER: verify]

3. Direct therapeutic techniques. In the chapter headed "30. Direct Therapeutic Techniques" (line 1409), the treatment of a wind condition lists "heavy, oily, smooth, warm-powered dietary factors such as raw cane sugar, grain beer, old butter, mutton stored [for one year], the flesh of marmot, horse, donkey and human, garlic and onion, etc." (line 1411).

All three loci agree that the Tibetan text uses human flesh against wind (*rlung*) disorders. Which of the Four Tantras holds loci 2 and 3 is not stated in the lines I read. [USER: verify]

Clark says in his introduction (line 57): "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts." This entry is a record of what is documented, not of a present practice.

### Active Components

Human flesh is skeletal muscle with its associated fat and connective tissue. In general terms it is protein (mostly myofibrillar), water, lipids and minerals, as are the muscles of other mammals. No figures are given. It may also contain whatever pathogens the person it came from carried.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The Codex does not recommend eating, applying or otherwise using human flesh, and it offers no protocol. Tibetan medical sources did record it, in the contexts above, and a reader needs to be able to find that. The reasons for not using it today are plain: eating human tissue risks infection, including prion disease; obtaining it involves illegality and the violation of a body; and no source retrieved supplies preparation, screening or dose. Anyone seeking a "warm, heavy, oily" food for the wind pattern that the Tibetan text describes can see the same Clark passages (lines 174 and 1411), which name ordinary foods, such as sesame oil, old butter, mutton, garlic and onion, in the same list.

### Preparation & Dosing

The materia medica line gives no preparation and no dose. The diet passage at line 174 lists "one-year-old (dried) meat" as a separate item before "human flesh", and I cannot tell from the text whether that qualifier also governs human flesh. The only preparation-like statement is the note on the "best type" at line 1525, which concerns the source of the flesh. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] General facts only: eating human tissue can transmit prion disease and other infections, and the practice is prohibited or forbidden by law and custom nearly everywhere.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Tibetan: the materia medica line prints actions only (sores, wind disorders, poisoning, septic conditions). It prints no taste or power for human flesh. Clark's general statement at line 673 says that all meats "in the above eight sections" are sweet-tasting with a sweet post-digestive taste; the sections listed at line 672 name animals but not humans, so it is not carried over here. At line 1411 human flesh is listed among foods that Clark's text characterises collectively as "heavy, oily, smooth, warm-powered" for wind disorders. That is a group description, not a property printed for this item alone. No other tradition is tagged.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* Human flesh appears in the animal-origin materia medica (line 1162), in the diet for wind disorders (line 174) and in the treatment of wind (line 1411). In each place its role is therapeutic or dietary against a named humoral disorder. No cultivation status, no essence-extraction (*bcud len*) role and no rejuvenation role is printed for it in the lines read. (Rejuvenation is printed for peacock flesh at line 678, not for this.) The note at line 1525 shows that the tradition qualified the source of the flesh, but I read no cultivation reason for that. Its status here is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. A cultivator does not use this substance. The text records it against wind disorders; the Codex preserves the record and does not carry it into practice.

### Contraindications

Not a recommended practice. Consuming human flesh carries a risk of transmitting prion disease (such as kuru) and bacterial and viral infection. Using human remains is unlawful in most jurisdictions and ethically unacceptable without explicit lawful consent. The source qualifier "a hero who died in battle ... any violent person" (line 1525) is recorded only as history; it is not a standard to follow. For wind-type complaints, seek a qualified clinician.
