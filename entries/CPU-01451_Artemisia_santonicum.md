# Santonian wormwood — Apsinthion triton santonion (Dioscorides 3-28)

## *Artemisia santonicum (the Dioscorides translator's suggestion for chapter 3-28, "Holy Wormwood", with A. glacialis)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). The binomials below are the translator's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification. **Named only by Dioscorides in this row.** The chapter is three sentences long.

*3-28 APSINTHION TRITON SANTONION* (heading at line 6773). The translator's line reads "Artemisia santonicum — Holy Wormwood Artemisia glacialis — Silky Wormwood, Glacier Wormwood". The text (line 6775): "There is a third kind of wormwood that grows abundantly in Galatia (or rather Gallia) near the Alps, which they call by the place's name — santonicum, giving it this surname from its growing in the country of Sardonis. It is similar to wormwood, not as seedy, somewhat bitter, and able to do the same things as the Seriphian [3-27]."

The chapter has no use of its own. It refers back to 3-27 (see I_1013), where the "Seriphian" (sea) wormwood is boiled with honey, rice, sapa or lentils for threadworms and roundworms, and which is also named *santonicum* by the Romans (line 6769). The "third kind" counts after the ordinary wormwood (3-26) and the sea wormwood.

**Identity.** The translator's binomial is one of two on a single line; *A. glacialis*, an alpine plant, is the other. The text itself says only that the place name gives the plant its name and that it grows near the Alps. The place spelling "Sardonis" is printed in the text and was not checked. Because 3-27 also gives *santonicum* as a Roman name, the two chapters may overlap. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

*Artemisia* species are generally known to contain volatile oils and bitter sesquiterpene lactones. The name "santonin" for one such compound, historically used against roundworms, derives from the Latin plant name, but whether the plant of Dioscorides' chapter is the source was not read. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The text records a bitter wormwood that does what the sea wormwood does. The Codex does not recommend the plant as a practice. No protocol is given.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation is given in 3-28 itself, which refers to 3-27 (boiled with honey, rice, sapa or lentils; no quantity printed). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Wormwoods in general have a long history as bitter herbs against intestinal worms, a general statement. No study of *A. santonicum* was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** 3-28 prints "somewhat bitter" and refers to 3-27, where the sea wormwood is "astringent with some heating" and "bad for the stomach". Whether those qualities pass across is not stated.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers is the discipline of attention the cultivation spec describes: the place (Galatia or Gaul near the Alps), the way the plant's name follows its country, a comparison with common wormwood (less seedy, somewhat bitter), and the instruction to read it beside the sea wormwood. It records no use for the person's vital constitution and assigns no status. The identification is the translator's. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator learns from this very short chapter how Dioscorides handles a plant he sets beside another: by naming where it grows and what it resembles. There is no practice to take up.

### Contraindications

Wormwood-group herbs in large amounts can cause toxicity, a general statement. Do not use in pregnancy. No traditional use is advice, and no dose is given.
