# Nasturtium — Nasturtium (Ebers Papyrus, Bryan 1930 English)

## *A plant named by Bryan's English translation of the Ebers Papyrus in his list of plant remedies; the Egyptian word is not given in the text on disk. The English "Nasturtium" most probably stands for a cress (*Nasturtium* is the Latin genus name of watercress), and cannot be the garden nasturtium, a New World plant unknown in Egypt. Identification: a cress; basis: the name and the plant's geography; confidence: low* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Ebers Papyrus (EP).**

1. **The list of plant remedies.** The English translation by Bryan (1930) prints "Nasturtium" as one entry in the alphabetical list under "Chapter VI, Plant Remedies" (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1312, between "Mint-of-the-Mountains / Splinters-of-the-Mulberry-tree" and "Onions"). The list is a bare list of English names with no indication, no recipe and no Egyptian word. Bryan introduces it with the remark that the list of minerals "by no means" exhausts the pharmacopoeia (line 1233).
2. **The one recipe found.** In Bryan's chapter XX, "The Heart and Circulatory System" (line 3302), Bryan summarises a dozen remedies "to put the Heart into proper working order and make it take up nourishment" and says that one of them is "a 'drink' which is composed of Fat Flesh, Figs, Berries, Incense, Caraway, Nasturtium, Goose-grease, Writing-fluid, and Sweet Beer" (line 3434). He calls these remedies "tougher propositions" than the simpler ones. This is Bryan's summary and not the papyrus text itself, and no quantity is given. [USER: verify against the papyrus formula]

### Active Components

The plant is not identified, so no composition can be stated from the sources. In general terms, the cresses of the mustard family (Brassicaceae) are known for pungent sulphur-containing compounds, which give watercress its peppery taste; that is a statement about the family, not about this drug.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Watercress is a common edible aquatic plant, and the garden nasturtium (*Tropaeolum*) is a different plant native to the Americas, so it cannot be what an ancient Egyptian text names. The identification of the Egyptian drug is, in any case, a modern hypothesis. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]
