## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Cannabis sativa* L.
**Synonyms:** *Cannabis indica* Lam. (now generally subsumed into *C. sativa*); *Cannabis ruderalis* Janisch. (now subsumed)
**Family:** Cannabaceae

**Common Names:**
- English: Cannabis, Hemp, Marijuana, Ganja (from Sanskrit *gañjā*)
- Sanskrit/Ayurveda: *Vijayā* (विजया — "victory"), *Bhāng* (भांग — leaf preparation), *Gañjā* (गञ्जा — flowers), *Charas* (चरस — resin)
- Arabic: *Hashīsh* (حشيش — dried plant material), *Qinnab* (قنّب — hemp)
- Chinese: *Dà Má* (大麻 — "great hemp"); *Huo Ma Ren* (火麻仁 — hemp seeds, medicinal in TCM)
- Persian: *Bang* (بنگ) — given to Sanskrit *bhāng*
- Greek: *Kannabis* (κάνναβις) — Herodotus documented Scythian cannabis vapor baths
- Hebrew: *Qaneh bosem* (קנה בשׂם) — "aromatic reed" — possibly in Exodus 30:23 as anointing oil ingredient

**Taxonomic Note:**
The single-species model (*C. sativa* encompassing *indica* and *ruderalis*) is now broadly accepted scientifically; the "sativa/indica" distinction used in commercial cannabis markets is chemotype/phenotype-based, not taxonomically valid. Legal hemp (*Cannabis sativa* with <0.3% THC in US; <0.2% in EU) is the same species as drug cannabis — differentiated by breeding for cannabinoid profile, not taxonomic status.

**The Cannabinoid System Discovery:**
The pharmacological significance of *Cannabis* extends beyond the plant itself — its primary compounds led to the discovery of the **endocannabinoid system (ECS)**, one of the most important neuromodulatory systems identified in the 20th century. Raphael Mechoulam's isolation of THC (1964, Hebrew University of Jerusalem) initiated decades of research that revealed CB1 and CB2 receptors, endogenous ligands (anandamide/AEA; 2-arachidonoylglycerol/2-AG), and the ECS's role in regulating pain, appetite, mood, memory, immune function, and neuroinflammation throughout mammalian biology. The plant's pharmacology thus opened a window into fundamental mammalian neurobiology.

**Hemp vs. Drug Cannabis (Legal Framework):**
- **Hemp:** THC ≤0.3% (US); ≤0.2% (EU); legal for cultivation; CBD-rich; fiber, seed, CBD oil
- **Drug Cannabis:** THC >0.3%; scheduled substance in most jurisdictions
- **CBD:** Legal status varies globally; FDA-approved for epilepsy (Epidiolex®); Schedule V or unscheduled in many contexts
- This entry addresses the full phytochemical profile; safety and legal context must be applied jurisdiction-specifically

**Parts Used:**
- Female flower tops/buds: highest cannabinoid concentration (THC/CBD)
- Leaves: lower cannabinoid concentration; traditional culinary/medicinal preparations (*bhāng*)
- Seeds/hemp seeds: protein, omega-3/omega-6, negligible THC
- Hemp seed oil: essential fatty acids; no significant cannabinoids
- Resin (*charas*, hashish): concentrated cannabinoids

**6/9** — strong Old World multi-tradition convergence; one of the most consistently documented psychoactive medicinals in ancient pharmacopoeia.

**The Scythian Vapor Bath:**
Herodotus (c. 440 BCE, *Histories* IV.75) describes Scythian ritual cannabis use — throwing cannabis seeds and flowers onto heated stones in felt tents, inhaling the vapor, crying out with pleasure. This constitutes the first documented description of cannabis inhalation for psychoactive effect in Western historical literature, 2,400 years before modern recreational cannabis culture. Archaeological confirmation: Scythian burial mounds (*kurgans*) in Ukraine and Russia have yielded felt-covered bronze cauldrons containing carbonized cannabis seeds and hemp — physical corroboration of Herodotus' account.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domains:**

1. **Analgesic / anti-nociceptive** — DMM, CM, SBJ, SS, FMT; CB1 + CB2 mechanism; **Level A (neuropathic pain)**
2. **Anti-epileptic (CBD)** — modern primary; **Level A; FDA Epidiolex® approved**
3. **Anti-nausea / anti-emetic (THC)** — SS, CM; CB1 mechanism; **Level A (chemotherapy-induced)**
4. **Anxiolytic / sedative** — traditional across traditions; CBD serotonergic + THC biphasic
5. **Appetite stimulant (THC)** — SS; CB1 orexigenic; Level A (cancer/AIDS-associated anorexia)
6. **Anti-inflammatory** — all traditions; CB2 + endocannabinoid mechanism; Level B
7. **Spiritual / entheogenic** — SS, FMT, Scythian; psychoactive tradition

**The Endocannabinoid System:**
The discovery of the ECS (CB1: 1990, Matsuda; CB2: 1993, Munro; anandamide: 1992, Devane/Mechoulam; 2-AG: 1995) fundamentally changed understanding of mammalian neurobiology. Key facts:
- CB1 receptors: brain + peripheral nervous system; highest density in basal ganglia, hippocampus, cerebellum, prefrontal cortex
- CB2 receptors: primarily immune cells + peripheral tissues; anti-inflammatory
- Endocannabinoids (AEA, 2-AG): retrograde neurotransmitters — released from postsynaptic cell; act on presynaptic CB1 to modulate neurotransmitter release (a signaling direction opposite to most neurotransmitters)
- THC is a CB1/CB2 agonist; CBD is a partial CB1 inverse agonist + CB2 modulator + TRPV1 agonist + 5-HT1A agonist + GPR55 antagonist — a complex polypharmacological profile

**CBD vs. THC — Clinical Distinction:**
| Property | CBD | THC |
|----------|-----|-----|
| Psychoactive | No | Yes (CB1 agonist) |
| FDA-approved indication | Epilepsy (Epidiolex®) | Nausea/cachexia (Marinol®/dronabinol); nabilone |
| Anxiolytic | Yes (at moderate doses) | Anxiogenic at high doses |
| Addiction potential | Very low | Moderate (cannabis use disorder risk) |
| Schedule | V (Epidiolex®); varies by form | Schedule I (federal US, except pharmaceutical forms) |

**Energetic Classification:**
- ☿ Shen (neuromodulatory): Primary — psychoactive; anxiolytic; anti-epileptic; analgesic (CNS)
- ◈ Jing (constitutional): Secondary — deep ECS modulation; appetite/metabolic
- ◉ Qi (functional): Tertiary — anti-inflammatory; anti-nausea

**Primary Tier: ☿ Shen primary (endocannabinoid neuromodulator)**

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Cannabinoids (>100 identified)

**1. Δ9-Tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) — PRIMARY psychoactive compound**
- CB1 partial agonist (high efficacy) + CB2 partial agonist
- **Analgesic:** Central + peripheral pain modulation
- **Anti-emetic:** CB1 in NTS (nucleus tractus solitarius); vomiting reflex suppression
- **Appetite stimulation:** Hypothalamic CB1 → orexigenic
- **Psychoactive:** Euphoria, altered perception, cognitive modulation, anxiety (biphasic: low doses anxiolytic; high doses anxiogenic)
- **Addiction potential:** Cannabis use disorder in ~9% of users; ~17% adolescent users

**2. Cannabidiol (CBD) — PRIMARY non-psychoactive compound**
- Complex polypharmacological profile:
- **CB1 negative allosteric modulator (NAM)** → attenuates THC psychoactive effects when co-present
- **CB2 agonist** → anti-inflammatory
- **TRPV1 agonist** → pain modulation; desensitization
- **5-HT1A agonist** → anxiolytic; antidepressant
- **GPR55 antagonist** → anti-epileptic
- **VDAC (voltage-dependent anion channel) modulation** → anti-seizure
- **FDA approved (Epidiolex®):** Dravet syndrome + Lennox-Gastaut syndrome epilepsy

**3. Cannabinol (CBN)** — THC oxidative degradation product; mild sedation; pain

**4. Cannabigerol (CBG)** — non-psychoactive; antimicrobial; anti-inflammatory; "stem cell cannabinoid"

**5. Δ8-THC** — less potent psychoactive than Δ9; anti-emetic; less anxiogenic; found naturally in small quantities; synthetic production from CBD widely marketed (legal grey area)

**6. THCA, CBDA** — acid precursors (non-activated); decarboxylation → THC, CBD (via heat)

### Terpenoids (>200 identified)

**7. Myrcene** — primary monoterpene; sedating; "couch-lock" effect; synergistic with THC (entourage)
**8. Limonene** — anxiolytic; antidepressant; anti-fungal
**9. Linalool** — anxiolytic; shared with lavender (I-248)
**10. Pinene (α + β)** — bronchodilator; memory enhancement (may offset THC memory impairment)
**11. Caryophyllene (β-caryophyllene)** — CB2 agonist; anti-inflammatory; shared with black pepper, cloves

### The Entourage Effect
The hypothesis (Mechoulam, Russo) that whole-plant cannabis preparations have synergistic effects greater than isolated cannabinoids alone — terpenoids, flavonoids, and minor cannabinoids modifying primary cannabinoid activity. Supported by clinical observations and in vitro data; not fully validated in clinical trials comparing whole-plant vs. isolate for specific indications.

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### Anti-Epileptic (CBD)
- **CBD → GPR55 antagonism** → reduced Ca²⁺ mobilization → decreased neuronal excitability
- **CBD → TRP channel (TRPV1, TRPA1) activation** → neuronal desensitization
- **CBD → adenosine A1 receptor enhancement** → increased inhibitory adenosine signaling
- **CBD → allosteric GABA-A modulation** → enhanced inhibitory tone
- Epidiolex® RCTs: ≥50% seizure reduction vs. placebo in drug-resistant Dravet and Lennox-Gastaut syndromes

### Analgesic (THC + CBD)
- **THC → CB1 agonism** → reduced presynaptic substance P and CGRP release → reduced pain signal transmission
- **THC → CB1 in PAG (periaqueductal gray)** → endogenous opioid release → descending pain inhibition
- **CBD → TRPV1 desensitization** → reduced peripheral nociceptor sensitization

### Anti-Emetic (THC)
- **THC → CB1 agonism in NTS** → suppresses vomiting reflex
- Dronabinol (synthetic THC) = FDA-approved for chemotherapy-induced nausea/vomiting (CINV) and AIDS-associated anorexia

### Anxiolytic (CBD)
- **CBD → 5-HT1A agonism** → anxiolytic (shared with buspirone mechanism)
- **CBD (low-moderate doses)** → anxiolytic; **THC (high doses) → anxiogenic** — cannabinoids are dose- and receptor-dependent for anxiety

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Epilepsy (CBD / Epidiolex®):**
- **Devinsky et al. 2017** (*NEJM*): RCT; CBD (20 mg/kg/day) vs. placebo; Dravet syndrome; n=120; 39% seizure frequency reduction vs. 13% placebo; p<0.001
- **Thiele et al. 2018** (*Lancet*): Lennox-Gastaut RCT; CBD 20 mg/kg/day; 41.9% drop seizure reduction vs. 17.2% placebo; p=0.002
- FDA approved Epidiolex® June 2018 for both syndromes; subsequently approved for tuberous sclerosis complex (2020)
- **Evidence Grade: Level A** (multiple large RCTs; FDA-approved)

**Nausea / Anorexia (THC/Cannabinoids):**
- Dronabinol (Marinol®) FDA-approved 1985 for CINV; FDA-approved 1992 for AIDS-related anorexia
- Multiple RCTs supporting cannabinoid anti-emetic efficacy
- **Evidence Grade: Level A** (FDA-approved pharmaceutical THC)

**Neuropathic Pain:**
- Multiple systematic reviews (Aviram & Samuelly 2017; Mucke et al. 2018 Cochrane): cannabinoids significantly reduce neuropathic pain vs. placebo
- Canadian Pain Society: cannabis recommended for neuropathic pain (Level B–A)
- **Evidence Grade: Level A** (meta-analyses; systematic reviews; multiple RCTs)

**Anxiety (CBD):**
- Bergamaschi et al. 2011: CBD significantly reduced anxiety in simulated public speaking test (n=24; RCT)
- Multiple open-label and controlled studies
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (multiple controlled studies; some RCTs)

**Multiple Sclerosis Spasticity:**
- Nabiximols (Sativex®): 1:1 THC:CBD oromucosal spray; approved in UK, EU, Canada for MS spasticity
- **Evidence Grade: Level A** (multiple RCTs; approved pharmaceutical)

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The Endocannabinoid Homeostasis Paradox:**
Cannabis works because mammalian biology already has the receptors — CB1 and CB2 were not created for THC; THC is a plant mimic of endogenous anandamide. The human ECS regulates: pain threshold, appetite, mood, memory consolidation, immune response, neuroprotection, stress response, sleep, reproductive function. Cannabis's pharmacological power comes from its ability to flood this endogenous system with exogenous agonists — producing effects ranging from profound analgesia to euphoria to sedation to anxiety, depending on dose, cannabinoid ratio, set, and setting. The plant did not evolve these compounds for human benefit; the trichome resin evolved as a UV-protectant and antimicrobial surface barrier — human pharmacology is an accidental consequence of structural similarity to endocannabinoids.

**The Mechoulam Legacy:**
Raphael Mechoulam (1930–2023), the "father of cannabis research," spent 60+ years systematically unpacking the chemistry of cannabis at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. He isolated and synthesized THC (1964), CBD (1963), anandamide (1992, with Devane and Hanus), and 2-AG (1995). His work created the field of endocannabinoid pharmacology. That a plant used for 5,000+ years across six pharmacological traditions had its primary active mechanism identified only in the second half of the 20th century — because adequate analytical chemistry didn't exist earlier — demonstrates the temporal gap between empirical observation and mechanistic understanding that runs through the entire Codex.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Medical Cannabis / CBD Applications

**Epidiolex® (CBD) — Epilepsy:**
- Prescription only; FDA-approved for Dravet syndrome, Lennox-Gastaut, tuberous sclerosis
- Starting: 2.5 mg/kg twice daily; target: 5–10 mg/kg twice daily
- Available in US, UK, EU through prescription

**Nabilone / Dronabinol (Synthetic THC Pharmaceuticals):**
- **Nabilone (Cesamet®):** 1–2 mg twice daily; CINV; Schedule II (Canada, UK)
- **Dronabinol (Marinol®):** 2.5–10 mg twice daily; CINV; AIDS anorexia; Schedule III (US)
- Prescription required

**Nabiximols (Sativex®):**
- THC:CBD 1:1 oromucosal spray; 2.7 mg THC + 2.5 mg CBD per spray
- MS spasticity: up to 12 sprays/day; prescription in approved countries
- Available UK, EU, Canada; not FDA-approved US

**CBD Supplements (Non-Prescription):**
- Legal hemp-derived CBD in many jurisdictions; variable quality and labeling accuracy
- For sleep/anxiety: 25–75 mg CBD; dose-dependent; inter-individual variation marked
- Quality: third-party tested products essential (contamination with THC and heavy metals documented in unregulated market)

### Safety Profile

**CBD:**
- Favorable safety profile; FDA-approved
- Drug interactions: **CYP3A4 + CYP2C9 inhibition** — clinically significant interactions with warfarin, clobazam, valproate, statins
- **Warfarin: CBD significantly increases INR** — monitor closely; dose reduction may be needed
- **Valproate/clobazam:** Increased levels with CBD co-administration; monitor
- Hepatotoxicity: elevated liver enzymes at high doses (Epidiolex® doses); monitor LFTs
- AVOID in pregnancy and breastfeeding (insufficient data; precautionary)

**THC:**
- **Cannabis Use Disorder:** ~9% lifetime users; ~17% adolescent users; psychiatric dependence
- **Psychosis:** THC (especially high-potency concentrate) associated with psychosis risk; absolute contraindication in personal or family history of psychotic disorders
- **Adolescent neurodevelopment:** Chronic use during adolescence (before age 25) associated with structural and functional brain changes; cognitive impairment
- **Cardiovascular:** Tachycardia; increased MI risk in first hour after use in predisposed individuals
- **Driving:** Significantly impairs driving; legally equivalent to DUI in most jurisdictions
- **Lung (smoked):** Chronic smoking → bronchitis; wheeze; no confirmed carcinoma link (unlike tobacco) but respiratory irritant

**Drug Interactions (Cannabis):**
- CNS depressants: additive (opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol)
- Anticoagulants: CBD CYP2C9 inhibition → INR increase
- Antiepileptics: CBD interactions with clobazam, valproate, stiripentol

**Contraindications:**
- Schizophrenia / psychotic disorders (THC): ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION
- Pregnancy / breastfeeding (all forms): AVOID
- Adolescents (<18): AVOID recreational THC; Epidiolex® with physician supervision for epilepsy
- Cardiovascular disease (THC): Caution; tachycardia/MI risk
- Driving or operating machinery: CONTRAINDICATED (THC)
