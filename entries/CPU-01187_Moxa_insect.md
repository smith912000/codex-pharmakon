# Moxa insect (chrysalis) — spru.mahi.hbu (Clark 1995) / Tumai bu (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *An insect Clark glosses "Moxa insect: Chrysalis: (a long insect found on crops)"; no binomial is printed in the line read, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text gives it only as "Tumai bu"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** In the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`) lists the thirteen animal-origin medicines (about lines 1148-1149): horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body. Its entry sits at the end of the Droppings section, which in Clark's text includes a run of insects and small creatures (line 1199): "byang.pa: Beetle: Mylabris phalerata ... It purges all channel disorders. Crab ... and chhu.sbur: Tibetan ant: Both dispel urinary retention. ... bse.sbur: Tibetan cockroach ... is beneficial in septic fevers ... bying.bying.thu.lus: Dung beetle and Scarab beetle ... relieves urinary retention. ... spru.mahi.hbu: Moxa insect: Chrysalis: (a long insect found on crops): stops bleeding from the veins [or arteries]." The square-bracketed words are Clark's own insertions, marking words he takes from the commentary and not from the root verse. The next line (about 1200) goes on to the slug, which "stops discharge of sinus fluid". The list row that created this entry, `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 381, reads "Tumai bu", class animal, source "rGyud-bzhi, Explanatory Tantra ch.20 (Efficacy of Medicinal Substances)".

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, lines 3572-3574) gives the same run: "Blister bettle cleanse disorders of the channels. Crabs and water beetles cure obstruction of urine. Dark beetles and wingless cockroaches relieve abdominal cramps. Tumai bu stops bleeding. Snails stop cerebrospinal fluid discharge." Its index lists "Tumai bu" at page 217. The two translations agree on one action, stopping bleeding; Clark's "from the veins [or arteries]" is his bracketed addition. That Clark's "Moxa insect: Chrysalis" and the Men-Tsee-Khang "Tumai bu" are the same item rests on their identical position in the list and the identical action; neither prints the other's name, and the list row gives only "Tumai bu", so the equation is mine. ### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, an insect pupa or chrysalis is mostly protein and chitin, with lipid, water and, in some species, defensive or pigment compounds; the chemistry depends on the species, which is not identified here.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text records the insect as a drug that stops bleeding. The Codex repeats that as a record. It does not recommend using an insect, chrysalis or any animal material to treat bleeding. Bleeding that does not stop with direct pressure, or is heavy, from a vessel, or after injury, needs emergency care. The species is not identified, so no safety statement about the material itself can be given. No modern protocol is invented here.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Haemostasis depends on pressure, platelets and the clotting cascade, and applying insect material to a bleeding wound adds infection risk without any known haemostatic action. Bleeding from a vein or artery is an emergency whose treatment is direct pressure, surgical or clinical control and, if needed, transfusion. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Contraindications

- **Bleeding:** do not rely on any insect or animal preparation to stop bleeding; heavy or uncontrolled bleeding needs emergency care.
- **Infection:** applying insect material to a wound can introduce bacteria and spores, including tetanus.
- **Identification:** the insect is not identified to species in the lines read; unknown insects can be toxic, irritant or allergenic.
- **Ethics and conservation:** the Codex does not recommend collecting wild animals for these uses.
