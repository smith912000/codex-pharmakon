### Origin & Tradition

*Sapindus mukorossi* is a deciduous tree of the Sapindaceae — the soapberry family — native to the foothills of the Himalaya and widely cultivated across northern India, Nepal and parts of China. Its small, round, amber fruits dry to a sticky, translucent shell around a hard black seed, and that shell is the famous part: rubbed in water it raises a soft, copious lather. To the North-Indian and Himalayan household it is **reetha** or **aritha** (Sanskrit *arishtaka*), the natural soap used for centuries to wash hair, delicate fabrics, wool and silk, and to clean and brighten jewellery and metalwork.

In Ayurveda the fruit pericarp is **arishtaka**, valued both as a cleanser and as a medicine. It was used to wash the scalp and hair — promoting cleanliness, treating dandruff and scalp complaints, and reputed to strengthen the hair — and entered preparations for skin conditions. Internally and as an errant (nasal) and emetic agent, the saponin-rich fruit was used cautiously: in small amounts as an expectorant to loosen phlegm, and in classical practice as an emetic or for parasitic and skin complaints. Its foaming, surface-active nature made it a natural fit for cleansing both the body's surfaces and its mucous passages.

The functional chemistry is **saponins** — soap-forming triterpenoid glycosides concentrated in the fruit shell. Saponins are amphipathic molecules: one end water-loving, the other oil-loving, so in water they lower surface tension, emulsify oils and foam. This single property explains the whole spread of the plant's uses — surfactant cleanser for hair, skin and textiles; emulsifier; mild expectorant (loosening respiratory mucus); and, in concentrated internal doses, gastric irritant and emetic. The same molecules that lift grease from cloth irritate the gut lining if swallowed in quantity.

Today *Sapindus mukorossi* enjoys a global second life as a renewable, biodegradable natural surfactant — "soap nuts" sold for eco-laundry, shampoos and cosmetics — and as a source of saponins for research and industry. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. **Natural surfactant cleanser (external — primary).** Lather from the fruit shell washes hair, scalp, skin, wool, silk and delicate fabrics, and cleans jewellery — the everyday traditional use, gentle and biodegradable.
2. **Scalp and hair care (Ayurveda).** Used to cleanse the scalp, address dandruff and scalp complaints and as a traditional hair wash; often combined with shikakai and amla in hair preparations.
3. **Expectorant (internal, small dose).** Saponins loosen and help expel respiratory mucus — a traditional use for productive cough and congestion in modest amounts.
4. **Emetic / cleansing (traditional, cautious).** In larger internal doses the saponins irritate the stomach and provoke vomiting; used as such in classical practice and for some skin/parasitic indications. Not for casual use.
5. **Skin applications & insect-repellent/pesticidal (folk & research).** Topical use for skin complaints; saponin extracts studied as surfactant, foaming and mild pesticidal agents.

### Preparation & Dosing

**External cleansing use is benign and [GENERAL]; internal use should stay modest because concentrated saponins are gastric irritants.**

- **Wash / shampoo (external).** Soak or simmer the deseeded fruit shells in water to release a saponin-rich, foaming liquid; use as hair wash, body wash or laundry liquid. The mainstay use — gentle and safe externally (avoid eyes).
- **Decoction (internal, expectorant).** Small amounts of a dilute fruit decoction used traditionally as an expectorant. [USER: verify the exact traditional dose from an Ayurvedic source; do not state a number unverified.]
- **Powder.** Dried pericarp powdered for hair packs and skin preparations (external) and, cautiously, for traditional internal formulae.
- **Avoid the seed kernel** for cleansing — the surfactant value is in the shell/pericarp.

Safety framing: keep the lather out of the eyes (saponins sting mucous membranes). Internal use in more than small amounts can cause nausea, vomiting and gastric upset — the same surfactant action that cleans cloth irritates the gut.

### Contraindications

- **Eye contact.** The lather stings; keep soapnut wash out of the eyes and rinse thoroughly if exposed.
- **Internal overuse.** More than small internal amounts irritate the stomach and provoke nausea/vomiting; avoid large doses.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** Avoid medicinal internal use (the emetic/irritant potential of saponins); external cleansing use is the traditional norm. [USER: verify pregnancy guidance from an authoritative source.]
- **Sensitive or broken skin.** Patch-test; surfactant saponins can irritate very sensitive or damaged skin.
- **Children — internal.** Avoid medicinal internal dosing; external washing is fine.
- **General:** treat as a cleanser first and a cautious internal remedy second — the same surface activity that makes it a soap makes concentrated internal use irritant.
