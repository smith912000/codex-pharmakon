# Chinese wild ginger — Xi Xin 细辛 (Shennong bencao jing); alias Xiao Xin 小辛, "small pungent"

## *Asarum heterotropoides F.Schmidt, Chinese wild ginger (xi xin), a small woodland herb whose root and rhizome are the drug; the identification is ours, from the Chinese name and the standard modern identification, and is of medium confidence at species level, since other Asarum species (A. sieboldii is also used) go by the same name and the texts give only a few features of the plant* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts on disk, under this Chinese name.** The only text read for this entry is Sun Xingyan's 1799 reconstruction, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt`. Its other edition on disk, Yang 1998, also lists the drug (names.csv line 63, "Xi Xin", pharmaceutical Latin "Herba Asari Cum Radice"); it is the same tradition and is not read here. Two other texts on disk touch the genus, and neither is tagged here. Dioscorides' *asaron* (DMM 1-9, "Asarum europaeum — Asarabacca", spine entry I_016) is the European species, a different plant from a different tradition. Clark's rGyud-bzhi lists *Asarum heterotropoides* as one of four binomials for the Tibetan drug *rta.rmig* (`Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, line 1094; spine entry I_1273), which the Codex keeps as a Tibetan drug of uncertain identity and not as this one.

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** The entry is 细辛 in the upper classic (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, upper classic, entry 细辛 *xi xin*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 509). The Shennong paragraph reads: 味辛，温 ("pungent, warm"); 主咳逆 ("governs counterflow cough"), 头痛脑动 ("headache with stirring of the brain"), 百节拘挛 ("cramping of the hundred joints"), 风湿痹痛 ("wind-damp painful obstruction") and 死肌 ("dead flesh"); 久服，明目、利九窍，轻身、长年 ("taken long: brightens the eyes, frees the nine orifices, lightens the body, lengthens the years"); alias 一名小辛 ("also called small pungent"); habitat 生山谷 ("grows in mountain valleys").

The Wu Pu layer (《吴普》曰, third century, line 511) says: 细辛，一名细草 ("also called thin grass"; the Yulan reads 小辛); and records the authorities' flavours: "Shennong, Huangdi, Leigong, Tongjun: pungent, slightly warm; Qibo: non-toxic; Li shi: slightly cold" (神农、黄帝、雷公、桐君：辛，小温；岐伯：无毒；李氏：小寒). It then gives the only description of the plant in the entry: 如葵叶，色赤黑，一根一叶相连, "like a mallow leaf, red-black in colour, one root joined to one leaf" (the Yulan adds that the root is gathered in the third and eighth months).

The Mingyi bielu layer (line 513) adds: 生华阴。二月、八月采根，阴干, "grows at Huayin; the root is gathered in the second and eighth months and dried in the shade".

Sun Xingyan's note (案, line 515) is brief: the Guangya gives "xi tiao" and "shao xin" as names of xi xin; the Shan hai jing's central mountains section says there is much "shao xin" on Mount Fuxi, which Guo Pu says is xi xin; the Guanzi (Diyuan chapter) pairs "xiao xin" with "da meng"; and the Fanzi jiran says xi xin comes from Huayin and the white-coloured is good. These are the commentators' and not the Shennong text's.

An appendix of drug pairings at the end of the volume has the line 细辛 曾青、东根为使；恶野狼毒、山茱萸、黄 [a character is missing]；畏滑石、硝石；反藜芦, "xi xin: zeng qing and dong gen are its envoys; it dislikes ye lang du, shan zhu yu and huang [?]; it fears hua shi and xiao shi; it is opposed to li lu" (line 4301). It is a later appendix. The words "opposed to" (反) are the appendix's, and the preface (line 4117) says that where drugs are in these relations they should not be combined; the Codex draws no further conclusion.

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, the root of wild gingers contains a pungent volatile oil whose components include methyleugenol and safrole. The genus *Asarum* belongs to the family Aristolochiaceae, in which aristolochic acids (kidney-toxic and cancer-causing) occur; reports vary with species and plant part, and the root is the part used. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The classic records a pungent, warm root for cough, headache, cramped joints, painful obstruction and dead flesh, and, when taken long, for brightening the eyes, freeing the nine orifices, lightening the body and lengthening the years. These are the classic's categories and the Codex does not translate them into modern diagnoses. It gives no protocol and no dose. See Contraindications.

### Preparation & Dosing

The Wu Pu and Mingyi layers give the harvest months and the drying (in the shade); the Shennong paragraph names no part or preparation. The preface (line 4121) says medicines differ in suitability for pills, powders, decoction, wine or paste, with no rule for this one. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. The root of *Asarum* species is used in modern Chinese pharmacy and contains aromatic volatile compounds. The genus is in a plant family with documented kidney and carcinogenic risks from aristolochic acids. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The classic's claims (lightening the body, lengthening the years) have no modern pharmacological equivalent and are not translated into one.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**SBJ:** flavour and nature 味辛，温, "pungent, warm" (line 509). The Wu Pu layer records "pungent, slightly warm" for Shennong and the other named authorities, "non-toxic" for Qibo and "slightly cold" for Li shi (line 511), a disagreement the Codex does not settle.

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested.* The paragraph for 细辛 reads 味辛，温 and files the root in the upper grade (上品). The classic's preface (line 43) says the upper medicines "nourish life, correspond to heaven, are non-toxic; taken much or long they do not harm people; for those who wish to lighten the body and benefit qi, not age and extend the years, base it on the upper classic" (主养命以应天，无毒。多服、久服不伤人。欲轻身益气，不老延年者，本上经). The root's own 久服 clause reads 久服，明目、利九窍，轻身、长年, "taken long: brightens the eyes, frees the nine orifices, lightens the body, lengthens the years", which includes the lightening of the body and long years named in the preface. The register is therefore (a). This records the classic's grade and wording and not a claim that the plant does these things. The Wu Pu layer's report that some authorities called it non-toxic and others slightly cold or warm is a disagreement over its nature and does not change its grade. (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, upper classic, entry 细辛 *xi xin*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 509)

**Practitioner's note.** The classic grades this root as a long-use, life-nourishing drug, and that grade is its whole cultivation status. A cultivator reading the entry should not take it as licence for long use: no dose, preparation or course length is given, the plant belongs to a family with known toxic constituents, and the species behind the name is not settled by the text. The honest use of the entry is as a record of how the classic framed the drug.

### Contraindications

- **Aristolochic acids:** the genus is in the family Aristolochiaceae, in which kidney-damaging and carcinogenic aristolochic acids occur; the extent in the root of any species used here is not stated in any source read. Do not take wild ginger long-term on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
- **Identity:** other wild gingers, including European asarabacca (I_016), are not interchangeable with this drug.
- **Appendix cautions:** the appendix of pairings says xi xin is "opposed to" li lu (a different plant of its own toxicity) and "fears" certain minerals (line 4301); these are historical pairings and not a modern interaction list.
- **Pregnancy and nursing:** no safety information was read. [USER: verify]
- **Quantities:** no dose or duration is given; "taken long" is not a basis for a regimen.
