# Friar's cowl — Arisaron, Arisarum (Dioscorides 2-198)

## *Arum arisarum (the Dioscorides translator's suggestion for chapter 2-198, with Arisarum vulgare; "Aris, Friar's Cowl")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). The binomials below are the translator's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification. **Named only by Dioscorides in this row.** The chapter is two sentences long.

*2-198 ARISARON* (heading at line 6277). The translator's line reads "Arum arisarum, Arisarum vulgare — Aris, Friar's Cowl". The whole text (line 6280): "Arisarum is a small little herb with a root the size of an olive tree's, but it is sharper than aron [arum]. As a result (rubbed on) it prevents gangrenous ulceration of the cheeks. Salves are made from it that are effective for fistulas, but either put in or applied it destroys the genitals of any living creature."

"Aron" is the arum of the chapters just before (the line 6272 context belongs to the preceding herb chapter, which says it is "edible because it is not oversharp"). The heading *Herbs with a sharp quality* runs over the page, which places this plant among the sharp or acrid herbs.

**Overlap.** The spine already has *Arum italicum* (I_515) and *Arum maculatum* (I_516), which belong to the other arum chapters. This row is the small plant *arisaron* only.

### Active Components

Members of the arum family generally contain calcium oxalate crystals and other irritants that make the tissues acrid. That is a general statement about the family. Whether it holds for this species was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The text records an acrid root rubbed on to prevent ulceration of the cheeks, salves for fistulas, and a warning that it destroys the genitals of any living creature. The Codex does not recommend the plant, and no protocol is given.

### Preparation & Dosing

Rubbed on the cheeks; made into salves for fistulas; "either put in or applied" it is destructive (line 6280). No salve recipe or quantity is printed. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Acrid aroids can cause burning and swelling of mouth and skin on contact, a general statement about the family. No study of this plant was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** 2-198 prints "sharper than aron" and places the plant in the section of herbs with a sharp quality. No warming or cooling quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers is the discipline of attention the cultivation spec describes: how to know the plant (a small herb with an olive-sized root), its relation to the arum (sharper), and the plain warning of what its sharpness does. It records no use for the person's vital constitution and assigns no status. The identification is the translator's. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here gives a cultivator a practice. The record is a caustic root with a warning attached, and the abstentive answer is correct.

### Contraindications

Acrid and caustic. Do not eat or apply. The text itself says it destroys the genitals of any living creature when put in or applied. Contact with the plant's sap can blister skin and mucous membranes, a general statement about the family. No traditional use above is advice.
