# Tibetan antelope horn — *gtsod* (Hodgson's antelope) horn (Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch.20)
## *The horn of the animal the Tibetan text calls* gtsod*; Clark prints "Hodgson's Antelope" with the Latin name* Pantholops hodgsoni *(printed* Pantholopshodgsoni*), the animal English calls the Tibetan antelope or chiru* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is the Tibetan *Four Tantras*. In Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; "Clark" below) the materia medica of chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra ends with the medicines of animal origin: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). Horn is the first of the thirteen, and the horns are listed in lines 1151-1158.

The entry at line 1155 reads: "*gtsod*: Hodgson's Antelope: Pantholopshodgsoni: [has seven names. It heals wounds and is oxytocic (facilitates childbirth)]." The next line (1156) is the entry for the gazelle *dgo.wa* (the subject of the following entry, III_199), and ends with the sentence that carries this horn's claim: "The horn of Hodgson's antelope and Procapra halt diarrhoea." That sentence is outside the square brackets. Clark's introduction (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets are taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries and those in parentheses from the oral instructions of Dr. Yeshe Donden and Dr. Pema Dorje. So the bracketed "heals wounds and is oxytocic" belongs to the commentary layer; the text does not say, in what I read, whether those two actions are those of the animal's horn or of the animal. The "halts diarrhoea" statement is the one the text attaches to the horn.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`; "MTK" below) has a parallel sentence at line 3508: "The horns of antelopes and gazelles cure diarrhea." MTK names the antelope and the gazelle together and does not separate them by Tibetan name in that sentence. Its glossary on animals (line 7064, with the Latin at line 7066) gives "Antelope: *Pantholops hodgsoni* Abel". Both translations agree on the action. *Pantholops hodgsonii* is the Tibetan antelope or chiru in English; that is a general fact and not a statement of the Tibetan text.

Two cautions on identity. First, the Latin name is Clark's, not the Tibetan text's. Second, in Clark's dietary chapter (line 672) the same Latin name is attached to a different Tibetan animal: "saiga tatarica (*Pantholops hodgsoni*)" among the game animals. The *rgya* horn entry (III_166, Saiga antelope horn) discusses that muddle. For *gtsod*, Clark's horn entry (line 1155), his blood entry (line 1173) and the MTK glossary all give *Pantholops hodgsoni*, so the identification within this material is consistent in the sources read, but it is a translator's identification. `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` has no row for *gtsod*, and no row for this horn was found in `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv`. The spine's locus is Clark line 1155. No other tradition's text on disk was found to name this horn.

The same animal's blood is a separate material in the Codex (III_197, Tibetan antelope blood), with its own one-line claim, also for diarrhoea.

### Active Components

Horn of the bovid type is mainly keratin, a structural protein, over a bony core that carries calcium phosphate. These are general facts about horn. The Tibetan texts name no constituent and no mechanism for the actions claimed, and this entry supplies none.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The record is one claim: the horn halts diarrhoea, in company with the horn of the gazelle *Procapra*. The Codex does not recommend using it. The animal is protected, the trade in its products is prohibited under international agreement, and diarrhoea has safer remedies, the first of which is replacing lost fluid. No protocol is offered.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither text gives a preparation (powdered, burnt, in a compound), a quantity or a dose for this horn. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] Clark remarks that "nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57), a remark that applies to the whole animal section.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Diarrhoea can cause dehydration and loss of salts, and the main treatment is oral rehydration; persistent or bloody diarrhoea, or diarrhoea in infants, the elderly or the ill, needs medical assessment. Keratin is a largely indigestible protein, and nothing in the sources links it to an anti-diarrhoeal action. The Tibetan antelope was hunted heavily for its fine under-wool, and the species is listed in CITES Appendix I. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi.** No taste, potency or post-digestive taste is printed for this horn in the passages read (Clark lines 1155-1156; MTK line 3508). The only quality attached to it is the action "halts diarrhoea". Other horns in the same passage carry stated qualities (takin horn "increases [body] heat", Clark line 1158), but these are not extended to this one.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The frame is the thirteen-part animal section of the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica (Clark line 1148), and in it the horn is a drug for a stated action, halting diarrhoea (line 1156). Nothing in the passages read places it in any practice of essence extraction (*bcud len*), and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, so none is asserted here. The tradition's wider frame is that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching the root; this entry adds nothing to that.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports cultivation use. The horn appears as part of one line in a list of horn medicines, with a single action, in a section of the materia medica that Clark says is little used today. A cultivator has nothing to take from this entry; the honest practice is abstention, which also spares a protected animal.

### Contraindications

- **Law and conservation:** the Tibetan antelope is a protected species and trade in its products is prohibited in most countries; a horn sold as this material cannot be trusted to be what the text means, and buying it feeds poaching.
- **Diarrhoea:** needs fluid replacement and, if severe, bloody or prolonged, or in infants and the frail, medical care; do not delay it for an animal remedy.
- **Identification:** the animal is taken from Clark's gloss and the MTK glossary, and Clark's own Latin names are confused elsewhere; this entry does not say that any product on sale is the *gtsod* of the text.
- **Hygiene:** unprocessed animal material from the wild carries a risk of infection.
