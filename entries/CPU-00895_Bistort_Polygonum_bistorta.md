# Bistort / Snakeweed / Adderwort / Easter-ledges / Quan Shen (拳参) / Wiesenknöterich / Serpentaria

## *Polygonum bistorta* L. (*Bistorta officinalis* Delarbre) | Family: Polygonaceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM, SBJ

**Energetic Classification:** Strongly astringent, cool, dry; binding and contracting, downward and inward — the archetypal European binder

**Training Phase:** Foundation — a first-rank astringent, simple in action, safe in short courses, with a well-defined tannin ceiling

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Polygonum bistorta* — increasingly published as *Bistorta officinalis* — is a perennial of wet meadows, upland pastures and damp woodland across Europe and temperate Asia, with a dense pink flower-spike and a thick, dark, contorted rhizome. Everything about this plant's name is a description of that rhizome. **Latin *bis-torta*, "twice-twisted".** The rhizome does not grow straight; it doubles back on itself in a tight S-curve, dark-skinned and red-fleshed when cut. Every vernacular name across Europe records the same reading of the same shape: **snakeweed**, **adderwort**, **serpentaria**, **serpentary dragonwort**. In Chinese medicine it is **Quan Shen (拳参)** — "fist root", the same knuckled, doubled form read as a clenched hand rather than a serpent.

**This entry exists to hold two things apart that the tradition fused**: what the shape *suggested*, and what the root actually *does*. They are not the same, and the honest verdict runs in opposite directions.

**Dioscoridean / European Materia Medica (DMM)**
The classical and early-modern European materia medica placed bistort among the first rank of **binders**. Its indications are consistent, narrow, and entirely of one class: **dysentery and the bloody flux; all fluxes and looseness of the belly; excessive menstrual bleeding; spitting of blood; ulceration of the mouth and gums; relaxation of the uvula and the throat; wounds requiring closing.** The doctrine is *contraction*: the root was understood to draw tissue together, to close what was gaping and stop what was running. This is the plant's real and most durable career, and it is expressed with unusual precision — bistort is not a herb of vague "tonic" reputation, it is a herb given for one physical action.

Alongside this, and inseparable from the name, ran the **serpent doctrine**: the twisted root as a remedy against snakebite and against poison generally, on the reading that the plant's form declared its office. Bistort's names in half a dozen European languages encode this claim.

**Traditional Chinese Medicine (CM)**
**Quan Shen (拳参)** is a recognised item of the Chinese materia medica, classified among the herbs that **clear heat and resolve toxin (清热解毒)**. Its stated actions: clears heat and toxin, cools the blood, stops bleeding, calms wind, and dries dampness — given for dysentery with heat signs, for sores and abscesses, for bleeding, for mouth and throat ulceration, and for **snakebite**. Note the convergence: **two unconnected traditions, on two ends of Eurasia, independently gave this rhizome for dysentery, for bleeding, for mouth ulcers, and for snakebite.** The overlap of the astringent indications is strong evidence of a real observed effect. The overlap of the snakebite indication is a more interesting problem, addressed below. **Signature, Bath & Domestic Register (SBJ)**
Bistort is a **food** as well as a drug in Britain — the young leaves are the principal ingredient of the Cumbrian and northern English **Easter-ledge pudding**, eaten at Lent, giving the plant its names *Easter-ledges*, *Passion dock*, and *Patience dock*. This is the plant's domestic anchoring and it matters: a rhizome-drug whose leaves are a seasonal pudding sits closer to the kitchen than the dispensary, and its harm profile reflects that. The rhizome entered the household register as a **gargle and mouthwash** for spongy gums, loose teeth and mouth ulcers, and as a **wash and poultice** for ulcerated skin — the exact topical uses that modern work has since examined directly.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

Two doctrines. One is folklore dressed as pharmacology. The other is one of the cleanest doctrine-to-molecule mappings in the European materia medica. Kenetica separates them without hedging.

**⛔ The serpent signature — DEFLATED.** *Bis-torta*. Twice-twisted. The root is snake-shaped, therefore the root cures the snake's bite. This is **doctrine of signatures reasoning in its purest and most transparent form** — the inference runs entirely from morphology to indication, with no observational step in between. There is no mechanism by which a rhizome tannin neutralises a viperid or elapid venom, and no evidence retrieved here that bistort does so. Kenetica states it flatly: **the snakebite indication is a naming artefact. The shape generated the claim; the claim generated the name; the name has been propping up the claim ever since.** The plant is not an antivenom and must never be used as one — a live snakebite is a medical emergency, and any delay caused by a herbal application is itself the injury.

But the deflation has an honest and instructive tail. A Danish screening study examined **88 plant species used as Chinese folk remedies for snakebite**, on the reasoning that **bacterial infection is one of the main secondary complications of snakebite**. *Polygonum bistorta* was among the crude extracts showing MIC values below 200 μg/mL against test bacteria, and high-resolution bacterial-growth-inhibition profiling demonstrated that **tannins were the principal growth inhibitors** in bistort and in most of the active plants (Liu, Nielsen, Staerk & Jäger, *J Ethnopharmacol* 2014; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2014.07.019)). So: bistort applied to a snakebite wound would have done something — it would have astringed the tissue and inhibited the bacteria colonising it. **It would have addressed the sepsis and not the venom.** The tradition's *action* had partial value; the tradition's *explanation* was wrong; and the shape of the root had nothing to do with either. That is the whole lesson of the signature doctrine in one plant.

**✅ The astringent doctrine — CONFIRMED, and mapped precisely.** The tradition read bistort as a contractor of tissue, and the reading is chemically exact. Bistort rhizome is one of the most tannin-dense drugs in the European pharmacopoeia, carrying both **hydrolysable tannins (galloyl glucose derivatives)** and **condensed tannins (procyanidins, flavan-3-ol derivatives)** — an infusion was found to contain principally **galloyl glucose derivatives, procyanidins and chlorogenic acid** (Pawłowska et al., *J Ethnopharmacol* 2020; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2020.113077)). The pharmacology of a tannin is not obscure: tannins are polyphenols that **cross-link and precipitate proteins**. Applied to a mucosal or wounded surface, they bind surface proteins and form a condensed, contracted, relatively impermeable protein layer. That layer physically reduces exudation and capillary bleeding, deprives colonising bacteria of a moist protein substrate, and blunts sensory nerve endings.

**"Draws the tissue together and stops the running" is not a metaphor. It is a literal, macroscopic description of protein precipitation, arrived at by observation, centuries before the chemistry had a name.** The mouth-ulcer gargle, the bloody-flux decoction, the wound wash, the gum astringent, and the TCM "stops bleeding" indication are all one mechanism, applied to five surfaces. The tradition generalised correctly *because the mechanism is physical and surface-acting* — and this is also precisely why the doctrine's reach **stops at the surface**. Where bistort's tannins can touch a membrane, the doctrine holds. Where the claim requires systemic absorption and a receptor-level effect, it collapses — as the diabetes data below demonstrate with unusual clarity.

### Active Components

- **Hydrolysable tannins — galloyl glucose derivatives (gallotannins)** — the dominant class in aqueous infusion; the protein-precipitating principle (Pawłowska et al. 2020; Bauer et al. 2023)
- **Condensed tannins — procyanidins, flavan-3-ol derivatives** — bistort's tannin content is described as "mostly condensed flavan-3-ol derivatives" in the traditional characterisation, with the infusion work confirming procyanidins as a major constituent class (Pawłowska et al. 2020)
- **Catechins** — flavan-3-ol monomers (Bauer et al. 2023)
- **Chlorogenic acid** — a major infusion phenolic (Pawłowska et al. 2020)
- **Flavonoids and further phenolics** — profiled by HPLC-DAD-MS across *Polygonum*/*Persicaria* taxa with chemophenetic markers identified (Pawłowska et al., *Phytochemistry* 2023; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phytochem.2023.113605))
- **Starch** — the rhizome's bulk; the reason the leaves and root have a food history
- Several infusion constituents were **isolated and identified from this plant material for the first time** in the 2020 work — the phytochemistry is still being completed

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Bistort enters Kenetica as the **reference European astringent** — a Foundation-phase herb whose value is that it does one thing, does it physically, and does it where you put it.

**Primary use cases (surface-acting, doctrine-aligned):**
- **Mouth and throat**: gargle or mouthwash for aphthous ulceration, spongy or bleeding gums, sore throat with a relaxed, boggy quality. The most defensible use of the plant.
- **Skin**: wash, compress or poultice for weeping, ulcerated, or superficially infected skin — the use for which modern work provides direct support.
- **Bowel**: short-course decoction for acute uncomplicated looseness where a binder is wanted. **Bistort binds; it does not treat the cause of a flux.**

**What Kenetica refuses:**
- **Snakebite. Absolutely and without qualification.**
- **Systemic or metabolic indications.** See the null below.
- **Long courses.** A tannin is a tool, not a tonic.

Bistort's Foundation placement is earned by its *legibility*: the practitioner can taste the mechanism. The mouth puckers, and that puckering is the drug acting on the practitioner's own mucosa exactly as it will act on the patient's. Few herbs teach their own pharmacology so directly.

### Scientific Correlation

**The astringent/topical doctrine → CONFIRMED, and confirmed by a study designed to test exactly this question.** The 2020 Warsaw work explicitly set out to justify or refuse the traditional topical use, noting that supporting reports were scarce. Its findings: the aqueous infusion contains mainly galloyl glucose derivatives, procyanidins and chlorogenic acid; **the infusion and its isolated constituents modulated the release of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, TNF-α and IL-8 from LPS-stimulated human neutrophils**; and **both the extract and the isolated compounds displayed antimicrobial activity against skin pathogens**, with MIC and MBC values established. The authors conclude that the study **"justifies the traditional topical application of common bistort"** (Pawłowska et al., *J Ethnopharmacol* 2020; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2020.113077)). This is as good as ethnopharmacological validation gets for a folk topical. **Grade: STRONG for topical anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial action; in vitro, but mechanistically aligned with the route of use — a topical agent tested against the cells and organisms it would actually meet.**

**Note the honest caveat inside the positive result**: the same study reports that the extract and compounds **"affected the viability and apoptosis of healthy cells."** A protein-precipitating agent is cytotoxic to the tissue it contacts at sufficient concentration. This is the astringent's dose ceiling made visible, and it is why bistort is a short-course and correct-strength herb rather than a benign one.

**Antibacterial → CONFIRMED, and attributed to the right molecules.** Crude *Polygonum bistorta* extract showed MIC below 200 μg/mL against *Bacillus subtilis*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Escherichia coli* or *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, with biochromatographic profiling demonstrating **tannins as the main bacterial growth inhibitors** (Liu et al., *J Ethnopharmacol* 2014; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2014.07.019)). **The doctrine's active principle and the doctrine's chemistry are the same substance. Grade: CONFIRMED, in vitro.**

**Anti-virulence → PROMISING, with a real in vivo safety signal.** An ethanol extract of *Bistorta officinalis* at a subinhibitory 50 μg/mL inhibited *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* biofilm formation (~50%), **reduced pyocyanin production by 59%**, and **completely halted swarming motility**, with anti-quorum-sensing activity and LasR interference exceeding that of its dominant isolated constituents — and **the bioactive concentrations were not toxic in the zebrafish model** (Jovanović et al., *Molecules* 2020; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules25081811)). This is a genuinely interesting result: the whole extract outperformed its own major compounds, and an in vivo toxicity check was actually run. **Grade: PROMISING; supports the wound/skin register; not yet clinical.**

**⛔ MAJOR NULL — the metabolic claim collapses in vivo.** This is the entry's most valuable negative. An aqueous *Bistorta officinalis* root extract (BODE) looked excellent in vitro across **multiple independent targets in glucose homeostasis**: α-amylase inhibition (IC₅₀ 81.5 μg/mL), α-glucosidase inhibition (IC₅₀ 8.4 μg/mL), moderate DPP-4 reduction, and significant inhibition of the intestinal glucose transporter SGLT1 in Caco-2 cells in Ussing chambers. HPLC-MS confirmed gallotannins, catechins and chlorogenic acid. **And then it failed twice: BODE supplementation did not confirm any antidiabetic effect in *Drosophila melanogaster*, and BODE failed to reduce blood glucose in chicken embryos.** The authors' own conclusion: **BODE "is probably not a suitable candidate for developing a pharmaceutical against diabetes mellitus"** (Bauer et al., *J Physiol Pharmacol* 2023; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.26402/jpp.2023.1.04)). **Grade: NEGATIVE in vivo, despite four positive in vitro targets.**

Kenetica flags *why* this null was predictable, because the reason is the entry's whole doctrine: **tannins are promiscuous protein binders. In a test tube they will inhibit almost any enzyme you offer them — that is not selective pharmacology, it is the astringent mechanism producing false positives on an assay plate.** Bistort inhibits α-glucosidase for the same reason it puckers the mouth. The doctrine of contraction predicts the topical successes *and* predicts the systemic failure. A drug that works by precipitating proteins on contact does not become a metabolic agent simply because an enzyme assay is also a protein in a dish. **The tradition never claimed bistort was a systemic drug. The tradition was right.**

**⛔ SNAKEBITE → REFUTED as venom therapy** (see Doctrinal Layer). No antivenom activity was retrieved. The only relevant finding is antibacterial action against the secondary infection (Liu et al. 2014). **Grade: the signature-derived indication is REJECTED.**

### Contraindications

⚠️ **TANNIN LOAD — the governing caution. Bistort is among the most tannin-dense drugs in the European materia medica, and the tannin is the drug.**

- **⛔ NEVER USE FOR SNAKEBITE.** Snakebite is a medical emergency requiring antivenom. There is no evidence bistort affects venom. **Any delay to definitive care caused by applying this herb is itself the harm.** The plant's names are not evidence. Call emergency services.
- **Iron absorption — a real and predictable interaction.** Tannins chelate non-haem iron and inhibit its absorption. **Contraindicated or strictly separated in anaemia, in pregnancy, in menstruating women with low ferritin, in vegetarians and vegans, and in anyone taking iron supplements.** If used, separate by ≥2 hours from iron-containing meals or supplements. This is the most likely real-world harm from bistort and it is silent.
- **Absorption interference generally.** Tannins bind dietary and pharmaceutical proteins and alkaloids. **Do not take with medication.** Separate from all oral drugs by ≥2 hours.
- **GI irritation, nausea, constipation.** Concentrated or prolonged internal tannin use causes gastric irritation, nausea, and rebound constipation. **Contraindicated in gastritis, peptic ulcer, GORD, and in constipation.**
- **Do not use in chronic or undiagnosed diarrhoea.** Binding a flux is symptomatic. **Bloody stool, fever, persistent diarrhoea beyond 48 hours, diarrhoea in the very young or very old, suspected dysentery or *C. difficile*, or diarrhoea after travel or antibiotics all require diagnosis, not an astringent.** Suppressing an infective flux can be actively harmful.
- **No prolonged internal use.** Kenetica limit: courses of days, not weeks. Chronic high tannin intake is associated with concern regarding upper-GI mucosal effects. [USER: verify — long-term tannin toxicology is contested and should not be overstated]
- **Local cytotoxicity at strength.** The infusion affected the viability and apoptosis of healthy cells in vitro (Pawłowska et al. 2020). Use the stated strengths; do not concentrate topicals in the belief that stronger is better.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** avoid internal use — the iron-chelation issue alone is disqualifying in pregnancy, and no safety data exist. Topical use [USER: verify].
- **Children:** avoid internal use.
- **⚠️ Heavy-metal accumulation — a wildcrafting hazard.** *Bistorta officinalis* was identified among herbaceous plants at a multiple trace-element-contaminated site in a bioaccumulation survey (Nworie, Qin & Lin, *Toxics* 2019; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/toxics7010003)). **Do not wildcraft bistort rhizome from roadsides, industrial ground, former mining land, or reclaimed sites.** A rhizome is a root drug and a root drug carries what the soil holds.
- **Correct identification required.** The Polygonaceae are a genus-tangle of similar species with different profiles (*Persicaria maculosa*, *P. hydropiper*, *Polygonum aviculare*); phenolic profiles differ measurably between them (Pawłowska et al. 2023). *P. hydropiper* is acrid and irritant and is **not** interchangeable with bistort.
