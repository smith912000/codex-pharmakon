# Forsythia suspensa — Lian Qiao 連翹 (fruit) and Qiao Gen (root) (Shennong Bencao Jing)

## *Forsythia suspensa, as the pharmaceutical Latin Yang 1998 prints for two separate entries, "Fructus Forsythiae Suspensae" for Lian Qiao and "Radix Forsythiae Suspensae" for Qiao Gen; for the second Yang's note says the identity is "now uncertain" and "provisional"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Shennong text (SBJ).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. Both entries are in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`). The Latin names are the translator's pharmaceutical Latin and were matched to the binomial by stem in the extraction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv`, rows 183 and 203). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain, and for Qiao Gen the translator himself says it is not. The row was added from the printed names (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *Lian Qiao, PDF page 97.* The page marker `[[p97]]` is at line 3609 and the heading "Herbs: Inferior Class" is printed at line 3610, directly above the entry at line 3611:

"Lian Qiao (Fructus Forsythiae Suspensae)224 is bitter and balanced. It mainly treats cold and heat, mouse fistulas, scrofulas, welling abscesses and swellings, malign sores, goiters and tumors, bound heat, and gu toxins. Its other name is Yi Qiao (Strange Beauty). Another name is Zhi (Hub). It is also called Jian Hua (Orchid Flower), She Gen (Broken Root), and San Lian (Three Honesties). It grows in mountains and valleys."

Yang's note 224 (line 3628) is the translator's modern commentary and not part of the classic: "This medicinal is able to drain fire, eliminate damp heat, disperse congealed blood and gathered qi in various channels, disinhibit water, and free the flow of the channels. It is used to kill parasites, relieve pain, disperse swelling, discharge pus, and resolve toxins. In modern times, it is among the most frequently used medicinals for various sorts of sores." The note puts these actions in the present and in "modern times". It does not say they are the classic's.

*Qiao Gen, PDF page 102.* The page marker `[[p102]]` is at line 3781 and the entry is at line 3790. No class heading is printed on this page. The governing heading is "Herbs: Inferior Class" at line 3749 on the page marked `[[p101]]` (line 3748), and the next section heading, "Woods: Superior Class", is at line 3809 on `[[p103]]` (line 3808), so the entry falls inside the herbs of the inferior class by position. The text reads:

"Qiao Gen (Radix Forsythiae Suspensae)240 is bitter and cold. It mainly precipitates heat qi, boosts yin essence, makes the facial complexion attractive, and brightens the eyes. Protracted taking may make the body light and slow aging. It grows in plains and swamps."

Yang's note 240 (line 3799) reads: "Because this medicinal fell out of use by at least the 7th century CE, its identity is now uncertain. The pharmacological identification given here should only be regarded as provisional."

So the Shennong text has two separate entries under two names, the fruit (Lian Qiao) and the root (Qiao Gen), each with its own taste, indications and habitat. The Codex keeps them in one entry because the translator gives both the same Latin species name, but they are different entries in the source, and the second is flagged by the translator as uncertain.

*The class headings.* Yang's table of contents prints "Herbs: Inferior Class" (line 729). The preface (lines 391 to 395) says of the inferior class: "There are 125 inferior class medicinals which are used as assistants and envoys. They mainly treat disease and correspond to earth. They are usually toxic and cannot be taken for a long time. If one intends to eliminate cold and heat and evil qi, break accumulations and gatherings, and cure disease, one should base [one's efforts] on the inferior class." The count printed there (125) and the count in Yang's commentary on it ("In our source text, there are 119 superior class medicinals, 120 middle class medicinals, and 122 inferior class medicinals", lines 365 to 366) differ. The commentary does not say why, and this entry does not rely on either count.

### Active Components

General composition, stated without numbers: the fruit of *Forsythia suspensa* is known to contain lignans and phenylethanoid glycosides such as phillyrin and forsythosides. The composition of the root, and of whatever the Shennong text meant by Qiao Gen, was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record, with a long continuing use of the fruit in Chinese medicine (Yang's note 224 says that in modern times it is "among the most frequently used medicinals for various sorts of sores"). The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. The Shennong text lists indications in its own terms (scrofulas, welling abscesses, malign sores, goiters and tumors, gu toxins) which are not modern diagnoses, and these conditions need medical care. The root entry's identity is uncertain.

### Preparation & Dosing

The two entries give no preparation, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. The text gives only taste, indications, other names and habitat. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: the inferior class (下品).* Lian Qiao stands under the printed heading "Herbs: Inferior Class" (line 3610, `[[p97]]`), and Qiao Gen stands in the same section by position (heading at line 3749, `[[p101]]`; next heading at line 3809). In the preface (lines 391 to 395) the inferior class "mainly treat disease and correspond to earth", is "usually toxic and cannot be taken for a long time", and is the class on which one bases the effort "to eliminate cold and heat and evil qi, break accumulations and gatherings, and cure disease". Yang's commentary (lines 362 to 363) says the same of this class: "Inferior class medicinals specifically treat disease. They are usually at least slightly toxic." So the grade is the one the spec describes as short-course and disease-attacking, not the nourishing one. The Lian Qiao entry says nothing of long-term use.

The Qiao Gen entry contains a sentence that sits uneasily with this: "Protracted taking may make the body light and slow aging." The phrase "make the body light" is *qing shen* 輕身, which the spec says has no pharmacological equivalent and must not be translated into one. The preface places "to make one's body light, boost the qi, prevent aging, and prolong life" on the superior class (lines 352 to 355 and 383 to 384). The text prints both the inferior class heading and the protracted-taking sentence for the root, and this entry does not resolve the tension. Yang's note 240 says the root's identity is "uncertain" and "provisional". If the root's identity is held to be too uncertain to carry a claim, this paragraph rests on Lian Qiao alone. No jing, qi or shen action is asserted here beyond the words quoted. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** In the Chinese scheme this is a lower-grade drug, taken for a defined complaint and then stopped, and the tradition's own grading is the warning. The root entry's sentence about protracted taking is recorded and not endorsed, because the root's identity is uncertain and it sits in the class that the preface says cannot be taken for long.

### Contraindications

The Shennong entries print no warning. The inferior class is described in the preface as "usually toxic" and not to be taken for a long time, and this is a general statement about the class and not about this plant. The root's identity is uncertain by the translator's own note, so no plant or root should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Scrofulas, abscesses, sores and tumours need medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
