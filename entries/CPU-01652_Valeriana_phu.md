# Valeriana phu — Phou, phu, "sylvestris nardus" (Dioscorides 1-10); "Phu, Cretan Spikenard, Garden Valerian"

## *Valeriana phu [Linnaeus], the fourth name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 1-10 (also printed: Phu magnum, Valeriana maior, Phu verum [Fuchs], Valeriana hortensis [Bauhin], Valeriana dioscorides), glossed "Phu, Cretan Spikenard, Garden Valerian"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM) under the name *phu*.** The name indexes in `Sources/*/extract/names.csv` and the list files in `_sources/` list no other text under *phu* or *Valeriana phu* (`_sources/dioscorides.csv`, line 11, is the only list row). The entry rests on one chapter of one text. Other entries on valerians and spikenards exist, and they are separate rows on separate chapters or from other texts; they are listed in the Overlap paragraph. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *1-10. PHOU* at line 3633, the SUGGESTED line at 3634 ("Phu magnum, Valeriana maior, Phu verum [Fuchs] Valeriana hortensis [Bauhin] Valeriana phu [Linnaeus], Valeriana dioscorides — Phu, Cretan Spikenard, Garden Valerian"), and the text at line 3635. The page number 14 follows at line 3636. A plate caption, "Phu verum after FUCHS — 1545", stands at line 3639 on the next page (15); it shows the Renaissance herbal's plant of that name and is not part of Dioscorides' text. The chapter stands in Book One ("Aromatics"), after *1-9. ASARON* (line 3625) and before *1-11. MALABATHRON* (line 3647). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 11, and the translators' index prints "Valeriana phu 14, 775" (line 17950). The page 775 belongs to *5-67. OINOS DIA NARDOU KAI MALABATHROU* (line 10134), the wine of nard and malabathrum, whose SUGGESTED line also prints this binomial.

The whole chapter reads: "Phu (which some also call sylvestris nardus [garden nard]) grows in Pontus, and it has leaves much like elaphoboscon [2-182] or hipposelinon [3-78], with a stalk of a foot high or more — smooth, soft, inclining to a purple colour, hollow in the middle and distinguished by knots. The lower parts are somewhat like those of narcissus but bigger, more tender and purple in a pale white. The root in its upper part is about the thickness of the little finger, and it has filaments like juncus odoratus [4-52, 1-16] or veratrum nigrum [4-151] that grow within one another — a pale yellow, pleasantly-scented and resembling nardus in its smell, with a certain poisonous kind of heaviness. Dried and given in drinks it is warming and encourages urine, and a decoction of it may do the same. It is good for a painful rectum, encourages the menstrual flow, and is mixed with antidotes. It is adulterated mixed with the roots of ruscus [4-146] but the knowledge of this is easy — for these are hard, not easily broken and without any good smell."

The chapter has three parts. The first is a long description, which is more detailed than most in this batch: provenance (Pontus), leaves compared with two other plants of the text, a stalk "of a foot high or more" that is "smooth, soft, inclining to a purple colour, hollow in the middle and distinguished by knots", the lower parts compared with narcissus, and a root whose upper part is "about the thickness of the little finger" with filaments that "grow within one another", pale yellow, "pleasantly-scented and resembling nardus in its smell, with a certain poisonous kind of heaviness". The second is use: dried and given in drinks it "is warming and encourages urine" (a decoction "may do the same"), it "is good for a painful rectum", it "encourages the menstrual flow", and it "is mixed with antidotes". The third is a test for adulteration: the root is "adulterated mixed with the roots of ruscus", but "the knowledge of this is easy", because the false roots "are hard, not easily broken and without any good smell". The text records one alternative name, *sylvestris nardus*. It prints no foreign names (no "Magi", Egyptian or Roman names), unlike the end of the chapter just before it (1-9, line 3632).

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. It is a general fact that *Valeriana* is a genus of the honeysuckle family (Caprifoliaceae, formerly Valerianaceae) and that the dried roots of valerians are strongly scented. The chapter's "pleasantly-scented" and "a certain poisonous kind of heaviness" are sensory notes and not statements of composition. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the translators' Cretan spikenard. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a warming, diuretic root that eases a painful rectum, brings on the menstrual flow, and goes into antidotes. These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. The chapter records nothing about sleep or calming, and the Codex does not carry the modern uses of the valerian root back into this chapter. The identification is only a suggestion, so there is no known plant on which to build a protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives two preparations: the root "dried and given in drinks", and "a decoction of it". It also says the root "is mixed with antidotes". It gives no quantity, no frequency, no duration and no method for the decoction. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that root preparations of valerians are a long-standing herbal product, and that herbs promoted to bring on the menstrual flow are unsafe in pregnancy. Nothing is stated about the effect of *Valeriana phu* in particular, and nothing from the research on the common valerian is carried into this entry.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 1-10 prints that the dried root given in drinks "is warming". It prints no moist or dry quality and no taste. It prints a smell (pleasant, resembling nardus, with "a certain poisonous kind of heaviness").

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. This chapter is the clearest example in the batch of what the spec calls the discipline of attention that this tradition offers a cultivator, because it contains every element of it: provenance (Pontus), a recognition set (leaf comparison, a foot-high smooth purple-tinged hollow jointed stalk, narcissus-like lower parts, a root of little-finger thickness with interlaced filaments), a sensory assay (pale yellow, pleasant like nardus but with a "poisonous kind of heaviness"), and an adulteration detection, in which the false roots of ruscus are hard, do not break easily and have no good smell. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The identification is the translators', and the cross-references in brackets (2-182, 3-78, 4-52, 1-16, 4-151, 4-146) are theirs. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant as a medicine. The transferable point is the method: know the source, test the root by hand (it breaks easily) and by nose (it smells), and distrust a root that fails the test. That is a method of attention that is useful for any bought root. The abstentive answer for the plant itself is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 1-10 prints no warning, though it notes "a certain poisonous kind of heaviness" in the smell and says the root brings on the menstrual flow, so pregnancy is an obvious question that the text does not answer. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no root should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry, and a root bought as "valerian" is not the plant of this chapter. The chapter warns of adulteration with another root. It gives no dose and no safety information for children or for any medicine taken at the same time. [USER: verify]
