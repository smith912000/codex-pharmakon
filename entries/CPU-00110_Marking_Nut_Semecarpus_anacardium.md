# Marking Nut — bhallataka, dhobi's nut, oriental cashew, the caustic ink-nut

## *Semecarpus anacardium* L.f. — the caustic urushiol-bearing nut, processed rasayana and rubefacient

### Origin & Tradition

The marking nut is the fruit of an Indian tree of the cashew family (Anacardiaceae), and it carries the family's hallmark: a **caustic, blistering phenolic oil** in the nut shell, chemically akin to the urushiol of poison ivy and the irritant oil of the raw cashew shell. Its English name comes from the indelible black mark its juice makes on cloth — washermen (*dhobi*) used it to mark laundry, hence "dhobi's nut." Dropped onto skin the raw oil raises severe blisters.

Despite — indeed because of — this fierce potency, **Bhallataka** is an important and carefully-handled Ayurvedic drug, classed among the *rasayana* (rejuvenative) substances when **properly processed (shodhana)** to remove the caustic oil. After detoxification it is used internally as a powerful **warming, stimulant rejuvenative** for *vata-kapha* conditions: for chronic rheumatic and arthritic disease, for nervous-system and cognitive support (a "medhya"/nervine reputation), for chronic skin diseases, worms, and as a metabolic stimulant. Externally (and in raw form, dangerously) it is a **rubefacient/counter-irritant and caustic** for warts and skin lesions. The Unani tradition (*baladur*) uses it similarly as a hot, nervine, rejuvenative drug.

The entire safe use of the marking nut depends on processing to remove or neutralise the caustic phenolic oil; raw or mis-processed material causes severe blistering dermatitis, mucosal burns and toxicity. It is therefore a specialist substance, recorded here in its processed-rasayana and caustic registers.

### Active Components

- **Phenolic oil — bhilawanol (a mixture of urushiol-type long-chain catechols/phenols)** — the caustic, blistering, allergenic principle of the nut shell oil; chemically related to poison-ivy urushiol; the cause of the severe contact dermatitis and the basis of the rubefacient/caustic action. Removed/reduced by shodhana.
- **Biflavonoids (e.g. semecarpetin, biflavones) and phenolic acids** — constituents of pharmacological/antioxidant and (studied) antitumour interest.
- **Anacardic acids and related** — irritant/active phenolic acids (Anacardiaceae family chemistry).
- **Note:** processing (shodhana, e.g. in brick-dust/cow products) removes the caustic oil — non-negotiable for internal use.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Ayurvedic mapping:** Bhallataka — *tikta-kashaya-katu* (bitter-astringent-pungent), *ushna* (intensely heating), *kapha-vata* reducing, *pitta* increasing; *rasayana* (processed), *medhya* (nervine), anti-rheumatic; requires shodhana.

### Cultivation Register

**Avicennan / Unani medicine (CM).** In the Unani materia medica the marking nut appears as *baladur*, an intensely hot-and-dry drug of nervine and rejuvenative reputation. Unani longevity thinking is conservational: the innate heat (*hararat ghariziyya*) and radical moisture (*rutubat asliyya*) are a finite vital endowment, and ageing is their slow depletion, so the tradition's cultivation concern is with husbanding that endowment rather than adding to it. A fiercely heating, drying stimulant sits uneasily against that logic, and the sources retrieved here record *baladur* as a therapeutic and tonic agent without assigning it a formal place in a regimen of *tadbir* (health-preservation). No named primary text of the corpus was found grading it as a substance-for-long-life. It therefore carries a therapeutic and nervine identity in this tradition but no attested cultivation status. **(b) framed.**

**Sushrutan / Ayurvedic medicine (SS).** Here the substance carries a genuine, named cultivation status. *Bhallātaka* is one of the classical single-drug *rasāyana* (rejuvenation) substances: the Charaka Saṃhitā devotes a section of the Chikitsāsthāna rasāyana chapter to *bhallātaka* preparations — a series of processed confections, oils and ghṛta forms (the *bhallātaka-kṣaudra* and related *kalpa*) taken as a regenerative course after purification. *Rasāyana* is a formal division of the eightfold Ayurvedic corpus, aimed at *dhātu* nourishment, longevity and the qualities the tradition ties to *ojas*; *bhallātaka* is admitted to it only in *śodhana*-processed form, the caustic oil removed. The status is thus attested at the level of the classic itself, not merely a later gloss. Note the discipline the tradition builds in: the rejuvenative claim is bound to the purification step, so the cultivation status and the safety procedure are one and the same teaching. **(a) attested** — Charaka Saṃhitā, Chikitsāsthāna, rasāyana-pāda (the *bhallātaka rasāyana* section).

**Tibetan medicine / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Tibetan medicine inherits a number of Indic drugs, but the marking nut is not developed in this entry's sources as a named Tibetan material, and the body of the profile documents only the Ayurvedic and Unani registers. More fundamentally, Sowa Rigpa has no formal category of "substances taken to advance subtle-body or contemplative practice": in the *rGyud-bzhi* medicine is the third of the four treatment branches and supports practice without reaching the spiritual root, and *bcud len* (essence-extraction) is a distinct discipline that would have to be attested for a given plant, not assumed. No such attestation is present for *Semecarpus anacardium* here. No cultivation framing is attested. **(c) absent.**

**Practitioner's note.** For the cultivator there is one real long-life tradition attached to this nut, and it is Ayurvedic: *bhallātaka* enters the *rasāyana* canon, but only as a purified (*śodhana*) preparation handled by a qualified practitioner, and the raw caustic nut has no self-directed use whatsoever. The honest instruction is procedural before it is aspirational — the rejuvenative reputation exists inside the purification, and outside skilled processing the substance is simply a blistering poison. In the Unani and Tibetan registers there is nothing here a cultivator should act on.

### Contraindications

- **Raw nut/oil — caustic.** Severe blistering dermatitis and mucosal burns; never internal raw; protect skin/eyes.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding — avoid.** Heating, potent; contraindicated.
- **Pitta / heat conditions, inflammation, bleeding.** The intense heat aggravates heat, inflammation and bleeding tendencies.
- **Anacardiaceae allergy.** Cross-reactivity with cashew/mango/poison-ivy urushiol.
- **Under-processing.** Internal use of inadequately processed nut is dangerous.
- **No casual use of any kind.** Specialist framing throughout.
