# Herb Robert / Red Robin / Stinking Cranesbill

## *Geranium robertianum* L. | Family: Geraniaceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM
**Energetic Classification:** Astringent, bitter, cool-neutral; drying; tightening/binding
**Training Phase:** Stabilisation / Sealing (a binding, tissue-tightening corrective — used to stop leakage, not to open or move)

---

### Origin & Tradition

Herb Robert is a small, sprawling annual/biennial of hedgerows, woodland edges and shady walls across Europe, western Asia and North Africa (and now widely naturalised elsewhere). Two features name it. First, the smell: crush the delicate, fern-like foliage and it releases a pungent, slightly acrid odour that earns it "**stinking cranesbill**." Second, the fruit: like all *Geranium* species it forms a long, narrow, beaked seed capsule that looks like a **crane's bill** — the "cranesbill" of the whole genus, from Greek *geranos*, a crane. The reddening stems and autumn foliage give "**Red Robin**." The plant is a classic pungent wild cranesbill: humble, ubiquitous, and — in the traditions that used it — valued precisely for its drying, binding character.

**European folk / domestic medicine.** Across British, Irish and continental folk practice Herb Robert was a household **astringent and vulnerary (wound herb)**. Its uses cluster tightly around the astringent action: **diarrhoea and dysentery, mouth ulcers and sore throats (as a gargle), bleeding, and topical application to wounds, cuts, bruises and skin inflammation.** The bruised herb was laid on wounds; infusions were used as washes and gargles. This is the load-bearing, well-attested folk reputation and it is internally consistent — everything in it follows from "this plant tightens and dries tissue."

**Classical / early herbal tradition (CM — classical/materia-medica herbalism).** In the written herbal tradition (Dioscoridean-derived European herbals through the early-modern herbalists) the *Geranium* cranesbills appear as astringent, wound-healing and "flux-stopping" herbs — the same binding logic, formalised. Herb Robert specifically carried a reputation for staunching bleeding and for complaints of the mouth and gut. The classical framing adds little beyond the folk one except respectability: astringent, drying, for fluxes and wounds.

**The modern folk-cancer reputation — flagged and deflated.** In 20th-century popular herbalism Herb Robert acquired a reputation as an "anti-cancer" plant, promoted in some alternative-health circles as a general tumour remedy. **This is not supported and is deflated here.** According to PubMed, the extract does show measurable in-vitro cytotoxicity against several human tumour cell lines — but it is *also* cytotoxic to non-tumour cells, the effect is modest, dose-dependent and confined to cell culture, and there is no clinical evidence in humans (Graça et al., *Food Funct* 2016, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1039/c6fo01075j)). A separate study measured a cytotoxic concentration (CC50) on human keratinocytes of roughly 958–1771 µg/mL for the extracts (Stanković et al., *J Appl Microbiol* 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/jambio/lxae106)) — i.e. non-selective toxicity at high concentration, which is the opposite of a targeted anticancer drug. "Cytotoxic to cells in a dish" is not "cures cancer." The doctrine is refused.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The traditional doctrine here is simple and unusually honest: **astringency**. An astringent binds, tightens, dries and seals — it stops fluxes (diarrhoea, bleeding, weeping wounds) by contracting tissue and precipitating surface proteins. That doctrine maps directly onto the plant's chemistry: Herb Robert is rich in **hydrolysable tannins**, principally the ellagitannin **geraniin**, along with ellagic acid and flavonoids. Tannins *are* the material basis of astringency — they cross-link and precipitate proteins, which is felt as the puckering "draw" on mucous membranes and which physically tightens a weeping or bleeding surface. The pungent odour comes from the essential-oil fraction. So the folk name (astringent cranesbill), the felt action (drying pucker), and the chemistry (geraniin/ellagitannins) are three views of one thing. The symbolic layer — "Robert," variously tied to St Robert or to Robin Goodfellow/the hedgerow spirit — is folklore ornament, not evidence, and is marked as such.

### Active Components

- **Geraniin** — the signature ellagitannin (a hydrolysable tannin); the dominant phenolic and the chemical basis of the astringent action.
- **Other hydrolysable tannins** — castalagin/vescalagin-type ellagitannins reported in the more active extracts.
- **Ellagic acid** — hydrolysis product of the ellagitannins; antioxidant.
- **Flavonoids** — kaempferol, quercetin, rutin (reported as main components of ethanol/acetone extracts).
- **Essential oil** — the volatile fraction responsible for the pungent "stinking cranesbill" odour.

According to PubMed, chemical characterisation identified **hydrolysable tannins (geraniin and castalagin/vescalagin) as the main phenolic compounds**, concentrated in the acetone extract which also showed the highest antioxidant activity (Graça et al., *Food Funct* 2016, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1039/c6fo01075j)).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Herb Robert is a **sealing / binding** agent — filed in the Stabilisation phase as a tissue-tightener. Its Kenetica use case is the presentation of **"leakage": loose bowels/diarrhoea, weeping or slow-healing minor wounds, bleeding gums, mouth ulcers and sore throat.** Used internally as a mild anti-diarrhoeal astringent tea and topically/orally as a wash or gargle for wounds and mucous-membrane complaints. The intended felt effect is a *drawing-together and drying* — the puckering astringent contraction that firms a loose or weeping surface.

It is explicitly **not** positioned as a systemic disease-treatment, and never as a cancer therapy. Its honest role is a gentle, local, symptomatic astringent — exactly what the folk tradition used it for.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Form | Preparation | Traditional amount | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Infusion (tea) | 1–2 tsp dried aerial parts per cup, steep 10 min | up to ~2–3 cups/day, short-term | For mild diarrhoea; astringent taste |
| Gargle / mouthwash | Cooled infusion | as needed | Mouth ulcers, sore throat, bleeding gums |
| Topical wash / poultice | Strong infusion as wash; bruised fresh herb as poultice | as needed | Minor wounds, cuts, bruises, skin inflammation |
| Fresh herb, external | Crushed leaf applied directly | as needed | Traditional field vulnerary |

**Dosing caveat:** these are traditional folk amounts, marked [USER: verify]. There is **no** established, trial-validated therapeutic dose for Herb Robert; internal use should be short-term and modest because of the tannin load (see Contraindications).

**Cultivator's note.** Herb Robert is almost aggressively easy to grow — a pioneer/ruderal species that self-seeds freely in shade and poor soil. According to PubMed it tolerates **heavy-metal-contaminated and serpentine soils** and even herbicide-sprayed railway ballast, having evolved glyphosate-tolerant biotypes (Hasanović et al., *Environ Sci Pollut Res Int* 2023, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-023-28952-5); Bemowska-Kałabun et al., *Ecotoxicology* 2021, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10646-021-02430-5)). This resilience is a **harvesting warning**: as a metal accumulator it should be gathered from clean ground — never roadsides, railways or industrial/contaminated sites — because it will take up and concentrate soil pollutants.

### Scientific Correlation

The chemistry underwriting astringency is **well-supported**; the broader medicinal claims are **preclinical and preliminary**; the anticancer claim is **rejected**.

- **Astringent chemistry (well-supported).** The presence of geraniin and related hydrolysable tannins as the dominant phenolics gives a solid chemical basis for the traditional astringent/vulnerary use — tannins precipitate proteins and tighten tissue (Graça et al., *Food Funct* 2016, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1039/c6fo01075j)).
- **Antioxidant activity (supported, in-vitro).** Extracts show robust antioxidant capacity tracking their phenolic/tannin content (Graça et al., 2016, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1039/c6fo01075j)).
- **Antibacterial activity (supported, in-vitro).** According to PubMed, optimised ethanol/acetone extracts showed broad antibacterial activity (MIC 1.25–20 mg/mL), disrupted bacterial cell membranes, and **synergised with gentamicin — enhancing the antibiotic up to 32-fold** against resistant *E. coli* and *S. aureus* (Stanković et al., *J Appl Microbiol* 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/jambio/lxae106)). This lends genuine support to the topical wound-wash tradition, though only at the bench so far.
- **Neuroprotective (preliminary, in-vitro).** Aqueous leaf extract protected differentiated SH-SY5Y cells against MPP+ toxicity in a cellular Parkinson's model, via antioxidant/anti-apoptotic action (Arslan & Yılmaz, *Eur Rev Med Pharmacol Sci* 2023, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.26355/eurrev_202301_31058)). Interesting but single-study, cell-culture only — not a clinical claim.
- **Anticancer (NULL / rejected as folk overclaim).** In-vitro cytotoxicity exists but is **non-selective** (kills non-tumour cells too), modest, and has no human evidence (Graça et al., 2016, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1039/c6fo01075j); Stanković et al., 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/jambio/lxae106)). The popular "cancer herb" reputation is not supported.

**Honest grade.** Astringent/wound and antioxidant/antibacterial actions: **mechanistically well-founded, in-vitro-supported, no controlled human trials.** Neuroprotection: **preliminary.** Anticancer: **unsupported — deflated.**

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Taste/nature:** astringent, bitter, cool-to-neutral, drying (DMM/CM).
- **Action:** binds, tightens, seals; stops fluxes (diarrhoea, bleeding, weeping); vulnerary.
- **Kenetica reading:** a *sealing astringent* — matched to leaking, loose, weeping presentations of gut, mouth and skin; local and symptomatic, not systemic.

### Contraindications

- **Tannin load — chronic/high internal use.** Hydrolysable tannins in quantity can irritate the stomach, cause nausea and constipation, and **impair absorption of dietary iron and other minerals** and of some drugs (take separated from medication and iron). Keep internal use short-term and moderate.
- **Distinguish the plant.** Do **not** confuse *Geranium robertianum* (true cranesbill, Geraniaceae) with the unrelated **scented "geraniums" (*Pelargonium* spp.)** sold as houseplants and used for essential oils — different genus, different chemistry, different safety profile. Also distinguish from other medicinal *Geranium* species (e.g. *G. maculatum*, American cranesbill) whose dosing and use differ.
- **Contaminated harvest sites.** Because it accumulates heavy metals and tolerates herbicide-sprayed ground, only gather from clean, unpolluted soil — never roadsides, railways or industrial land.
- **Pregnancy and lactation.** Insufficient safety data; avoid medicinal doses. [USER: verify]
- **Not a cancer treatment.** Must not be used or promoted as a therapy for cancer or any serious disease; the folk anticancer reputation is unsupported.
