# Sesame — *Sesamum indicum* L.

**Family:** Pedaliaceae | **Class:** plant | **Volume:** I · Plant | **Codex id:** CPU-00036
**Attested in:** Dioscorides · rGyud-bzhi · Shennong Bencao Jing · Assyrian tablets
**Parts used:** seed (whole and ground); expressed oil (*til taila*; *hei zhi ma you*; *šamaššammū* oil)

---

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

*Sesamum indicum* L. (Linnaeus 1753) is an erect annual herb of the family Pedaliaceae, reaching 0.5–2 m, bearing tubular white-to-pink flowers and oblong capsules containing the characteristic flattened seeds. The cultivated species is widely held to be the oldest oilseed crop used by humanity [1, 2]. Archaeobotanical evidence from Harappan sites places domestication in the Indian subcontinent by approximately 3500–3050 BCE, with trade diffusion into Mesopotamia established by c. 2000 BCE according to the archaeobotanist Dorian Q. Fuller [2].

Phylogenetic and morphological data confirm that the wild ancestor *Sesamum indicum* subsp. *malabaricum* is native to India; most wild *Sesamum* species are sub-Saharan African, but S. *indicum* is of Indian origin [2]. Classical Chinese sources record the plant as *Hú Má* (胡麻), "foreign hemp," a term used from the Han dynasty onward and attested in the *Shennong Bencao Jing* [3]. Tibetan medicine employs the term *til* (ཏིལ), borrowed from Sanskrit *tila*. Akkadian cuneiform sources render the plant as *šamaššammū* [4].

## 2. Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2a. Assyrian Cuneiform Tablets (c. 2000–600 BCE)

Sesame oil (*šamaššammū*) appears among the approximately 660 medical tablets preserved in the Library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh, now held in the British Museum. The tablets record sesame oil as a dressing in post-operative care, a use consistent with its observed antibacterial properties [4, 5]. Sesame oil is also attested in cuneiform agricultural and commercial records as a trade commodity between Mesopotamia and the Indus Valley [2]. Its presence across multiple cuneiform genres — medical, administrative, and ritual — indicates deep integration into Assyrian material culture.

### 2b. Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica* (c. 50–70 CE)

Pedanius Dioscorides, a Greek physician serving in the Roman army, recorded sesame in Book II of *De Materia Medica*, classifying it alongside cereals and noting that the Egyptians prepared an oil from the seed [6]. He describes the seed as mildly laxative and emollient, and notes the oil's use as a topical preparation — properties consistent with its high oleic and linoleic acid content (see §4). The text remained the authoritative pharmacopoeia of the Western world for over fifteen centuries and explicitly lists sesame among substances with diluent and emollient functions [6].

### 2c. *Shennong Bencao Jing* (神農本草經) (compiled c. 1st–2nd century CE)

The *Shennong Bencao Jing* — attributed by tradition to the mythical Emperor Shennong, compiled in written form during the Han dynasty from earlier oral traditions — places black sesame (*Hú Má*) in the highest "superior" (*shang pin* 上品) category: substances safe for prolonged use and held to nourish life and extend longevity [3, 7]. The text states, in the classical formulation, that long-term ingestion "lightens the body, extends years, and prevents grey hair." This places sesame unambiguously in the category of longevity and essence-nourishing (*jing*-supporting) substances, aligned with later TCM uses for liver and kidney vacuity, hair quality, and intestinal dryness. The *Bencao* tradition classifies sesame as sweet and neutral in nature [7].

### 2d. *rGyud-bzhi* (རྒྱུད་བཞི, Four Medical Tantras) (compiled 9th–12th century CE)

The *rGyud-bzhi*, attributed in Tibetan tradition to Yuthok Yönten Gönpo and rediscovered by Drapa Ngönshé in the 11th century, forms the canonical text of Sowa Rigpa (Tibetan medicine) [8]. The *rGyud-bzhi* drew heavily on Vagbhata's *Ashtanga Hridayam* (7th century Ayurvedic synthesis), and sesame's role in Tibetan medicine reflects this inheritance [9]. Within the Tibetan pharmacological framework, sesame oil (*til mar*, ཏིལ་མར) is employed as a base for medicated compound oils (*snyan sman mar*, "ear medicines in oil") and as a vehicle carrying lipophilic herbal actives into tissue layers. Tibetan medical sources also record sesame oil's sedative reputation, including its use for anxiety and insomnia via nasal application — a route (nasya) directly paralleling Ayurvedic snehana practice [10]. **Vehicle role:** In both Tibetan and Ayurvedic contexts, sesame oil is the primary oil-based drug vehicle. The *Charaka Samhita* explicitly declares sesame oil (*tila taila*) superior to all other oils, citing its penetrating quality (*vyavayi*) and affinity for the seven tissue layers (*dhatu*); every classical *Taila* formulation uses it as base. This doctrine passed into the *rGyud-bzhi* via the Ayurvedic channel [9, 10].

## 3. Convergence Analysis

Four independent civilisations — Mesopotamian, Greek-Roman, Han Chinese, and Indo-Tibetan — converge on a common functional cluster: sesame oil as (1) an emollient and topical wound treatment, (2) a softening/laxative agent for the gut, and (3) a carrier or vehicle for other medicines. This convergence across traditions with no documented mutual contact at the point of initial attestation (Mesopotamia, 2nd millennium BCE; China, 1st–2nd century CE) constitutes an ethnopharmacological consilience supporting biological plausibility. The longevity claim of the *Bencao* tradition has no direct parallel in Dioscorides, but the hair and kidney-essence dimension is unique to Sinitic categorisation. The drug-vehicle role is most fully theorised in Ayurveda and is transmitted explicitly into Tibetan medicine; it is implied but not systematised in Dioscorides.

## 4. Phytochemistry & Composition

Sesame oil consists of approximately 82–85% unsaturated fatty acids [11]. The dominant fatty acids are linoleic acid (omega-6, approximately 38–47%) and oleic acid (omega-9, approximately 37–44%), with minor saturated fractions of palmitic (6–9%) and stearic (5–6%) acids [11, 12]. Vitamin E is present primarily as gamma-tocopherol (approximately 90.5% of total tocopherols), with total tocopherol content ranging from 18.5–49.6 mg/100 g depending on cultivar and origin [11].

The characteristic bioactive constituents are the furofuran lignans sesamin and sesamolin, whose combined concentration in seed may exceed 1.4% [13]. Sesamolin is a precursor that converts partially to sesamol under heating; sesamol is the principal phenolic radical-scavenger [13]. Sesamin exhibits documented anticholesterolaemic, antihypertensive, and hepatoprotective activity in experimental models [13, 14]. A comprehensive PMC review (2022) catalogues additional minor lignans (sesamolinol, pinoresinol), phytosterols, and polyphenols [14].

---

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**Cardiovascular:** Sesamin inhibits delta-5 desaturase, reducing arachidonic acid biosynthesis and thus pro-inflammatory eicosanoid production. Linoleic and oleic acids contribute to endothelial nitric-oxide signalling, supporting vascular tone. Phytosterols compete with cholesterol for intestinal absorption [13, 14].

**Antioxidant:** Sesamol and gamma-tocopherol act as radical-chain terminators in lipid peroxidation cascades. This accounts for the oil's unusual oxidative stability relative to other polyunsaturated oils, a property noted in Ayurvedic preparation protocols (*murchhana* — the maturation heating process) [10].

**Emollient/Vehicle:** The high linoleic acid fraction enhances epidermal barrier repair via ceramide synthesis. The oil's low molecular polarity and penetrating property (*vyavayi*, per Charaka) facilitate transdermal and transmucosal delivery of co-dissolved lipophilic phytochemicals [10].

**Laxative:** Bulk lubrication of intestinal contents, consistent with Dioscorides' mild laxative attribution and *Bencao* classification of sesame as moistening to the intestines.

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**Grade: Moderate (lipid/BP); Low (oil pulling)**

*Lipid profile and blood pressure:* A 2021 meta-analysis of 16 RCTs (n = 908) found sesame intake significantly reduced total cholesterol, triglycerides, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, body weight, BMI, and waist circumference versus controls [15]. A 2024 meta-analysis in *Clinical Hypertension* (GRADE-assessed, focusing on prehypertension and hypertension) corroborated antihypertensive effects [16]. A 2025 Springer *Nutrition & Metabolism* systematic review with dose–response analysis covering lipid, glycaemic, inflammatory, and oxidative stress parameters extended these findings but noted heterogeneity across trials [17]. Limitations: trial durations are short (4–16 weeks), populations heterogeneous, and blinding difficult for food interventions.

*Oil pulling (oral hygiene):* A 2025 randomised controlled trial (Springer *Clinical Oral Investigations*) found sesame oil pulling produced significantly greater plaque reduction than distilled water at 8 weeks (18.98% vs 10.49%; p = 0.023), but remained inferior to chlorhexidine [18]. Multiple systematic reviews (2016–2024) rate the overall evidence quality as very low due to methodological limitations in primary studies [18, 19]. Oil pulling should not be represented as evidence-based clinical treatment on current evidence.

---

## 7. Phenomenological Record

No cross-traditional record of psychoactive, visionary, or pronounced altered-state effects. Sesame is not a psychotropic substance. Subjective reports across Ayurvedic and classical Chinese traditions emphasise a quality of gradual nourishment — warmth, heaviness, stability — consistent with its Ayurvedic characterisation as *guru* (heavy) and *snigdha* (unctuous), properties that ground and stabilise the nervous system over sustained use rather than producing acute phenomenological shifts. Oil massage protocols (abhyanga) are reported in Ayurvedic clinical literature to reduce anxiety and improve sleep quality, though rigorous phenomenological documentation in the Western academic sense is absent.

## 8. Practical Application

**Seed:** Typically consumed whole, ground (*tahini*), or pressed. Classical Chinese dosage for black sesame in nourishing formulas: 9–30 g/day of seed, often with huang jing or he shou wu in liver-kidney vacuity formulas [7]. No standardised modern clinical dosing established; trial doses range from 25–60 g/day of seeds or 15–30 mL/day of oil.

**Oil (internal):** 15–30 mL/day used in cardiovascular and lipid trials [15, 16]. As a cooking or supplement oil.

**Oil (topical/therapeutic):** Unmedicated sesame oil or as base for medicated *Tailas*. For oil pulling: 15–20 mL swished for 5–20 minutes; rinse and expectorate.

**Allergen caution:** Sesame is a major food allergen. In 2023 the United States Food and Drug Administration designated sesame as the ninth major allergen under FASTER Act provisions. Anaphylaxis, contact dermatitis, and urticaria are documented. The allergen is present in oil at low but potentially sensitising levels. Contraindicated in confirmed sesame allergy. Cross-reactivity with other Pedaliaceae reported.

## 9. Spiritual & Esoteric Layer

Sesame holds a prominent position in Hindu mortuary and ancestral ritual. During *Pitru Paksha* (the fortnight of ancestor veneration, typically in the lunar month of Bhadrapada), black sesame seeds (*kala til*) are offered in *tarpan* — the water-and-sesame libation poured to the departed via the *pitru-tirtha* (the area between thumb and index finger of the palm) — accompanied by the Vedic formula *eṣa te tilatoyāñjalis tavopatiṣṭhatām* ("this sesame-and-water offering takes its place before you") [20]. The *tilānjali* rite is performed from the first through tenth day after death, and at Shraddha ceremonies during Pitru Paksha. Black sesame is held, within the ritual schema, to have the property of dispelling malevolent forces (*rakshasas*) and easing the transit of the deceased through the *pitruloka* (ancestral realm) [20].

In Mesopotamia, sesame oil was employed in temple ritual, anointing, and lamp-burning contexts, reflecting its sacred-mundane duality in Sumerian and Akkadian culture. The word *šamaššammū* contains the element *šamaš* (sun), a connection noted in scholarly etymological discussion, though its precise cultic significance remains a subject of ongoing philological analysis [4, 5].

In Taoist longevity practice, black sesame (*hei zhi ma*) features in classical longevity formulas alongside pine pollen and polygonatum, framed within the system of *jing*-essence nourishment — the conservation and cultivation of constitutional vitality [7].

## 10. Functional–Anatomical Correlate

Sesame operates primarily across three anatomical domains as mapped by the attesting traditions, with modern mechanism support:

1. **Vascular system / cardiovascular:** Convergent traditional emphasis (Dioscorides' emollient-softening action; Ayurvedic *snigdha* property on channels) aligns with RCT evidence for lipid and blood pressure modulation via linoleic acid, sesamin, and phytosterols.

2. **Gastro-intestinal tract:** Laxative, lubricating, and intestinal-moistening properties across Dioscorides, Tibetan, and Chinese frameworks correspond to the bulk emollient mechanism of the oil in the gut lumen.

3. **Reproductive and renal essence (Chinese/Tibetan framing):** The *jing*-nourishing, hair-darkening, and longevity attribution in the *Bencao* tradition localises sesame to the kidney-liver axis (hair as an outward expression of kidney essence; moistening of yin fluids). This anatomical framing has no direct Western mechanistic equivalent but finds partial support in the antioxidant protection of lipid-rich cell membranes relevant to reproductive and endocrine tissues.

As drug vehicle, sesame oil's functional correlate is the intercellular lipid matrix of skin, mucosal membranes, and cell membranes generally — the medium through which lipophilic therapeutic agents travel. In this role it is not a substance acting on a system but the medium within which other substances act.
