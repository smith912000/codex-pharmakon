# Euphorbia wallichii — Thamu, Tharnu, thar nu (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary, by column position)

## *Euphorbia wallichii, as the binomial printed with the English "Wallichi's spurge" in the scientific-name run of the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary's "T" block; the Tibetan name "Thamu" (transliteration "thar nu") is set against it by column position only, and Clark 1995 prints "Euphorbia wallichiana", with a different ending, among the names for thar.nu* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under this spelling only by the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk found no *Euphorbia wallichii*; those texts name other *Euphorbia* species, which are not read as this one. Clark 1995 prints no line for the spelling *wallichii*. Its entry for the likely same Tibetan name, *thar.nu*, prints *Euphorbia wallichiana* (see below). This entry rests on one glossary line for the binomial and on running-text lines for the Tibetan name that the glossary does not print beside it. The extraction note reads: "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 375). The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (lines 5858 to 5982).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "T" block (the letter at line 5858). The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. The phonetic names include, in this order, "Thangtrom" (line 5887), "Tharam" (line 5888), "Thamu" (line 5893) and "Tikta" (line 5897); the transliteration run gives "thang khrom" and "tha rams" together (lines 5890 to 5891) and then "thar nu" alone (line 5895). The scientific names are in a separate run from line 5928. There, the lines read in turn "Hyocyamus niger" (line 5956), "Datura thomapple" (line 5958), "Plantago depressa" (line 5960, with "Greater plantain" at line 5962), "Euphorbia wallichii" (line 5964, with "Wallichi’s spurge" at line 5966) and "Swertia chirayta" (line 5968). Counting in order, "Thangtrom" stands against the two names *Hyoscyamus* and *Datura*, "Tharam" against *Plantago depressa* and "Thamu" against *Euphorbia wallichii*. The middle pair can be checked against Clark: *tha.ram* (line 1123) prints "Plantago depressa Willd" first, and the Men-Tsee-Khang group of diarrhoea medicines (line 3644) names "Tharam", while Clark's list of anti-diarrhoeals names "Plantain (Plantago depressa)" (line 1222). The Codex therefore reads the glossary as pairing "Thamu" (*thar nu*) with *Euphorbia wallichii*, by column position only and not as a printed row. Several lines hold more than one name, and the count rests on order. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text on "Tharnu" and "Thamu".* The text gives no Latin name for the drug, and it prints the name in two spellings. In chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), line 3495 reads: "Dhurji and Tharnu purge all forms of hot and cold disorders." In chapter 21, line 3661: "Arura, Dhenda, Dhong ga, Shri khenda, Dhurji, Thamu, Chum tsa, Ngonbu, Tronbu, Tsi takmo, Rechagpa and Chhu tsa are the group of medicines that are purgatives." The index lists "Thamu 211, 223" (line 7510), and "Tharam 211,222" as a separate name beside it. "Thamu" in the glossary, the index and line 3661, and "Tharnu" in line 3495, are the same name in two OCR forms on the Codex's reading, because the transliteration is "thar nu" and the two lines name the drug beside Dhurji; this is a reading of the letters *m* and *rn* and not a printed correction. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, the likely same Tibetan name.* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, line 1134, in the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997): "thar.nu: Euphorbia pallassii: Baliospermum sinulatum: Euphorbia wallichiana: Croton tiglium: [has eight names. It has a thick, heavy stalk and taproot, and red flower, petiole and seed. Euphorbia has hot taste, removes stones and pustules and in calcinated form is anti-emetic.] Jatropha and Euphorbia purge all hot and cold disorders." Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the eight names, the stalk and taproot, the red flower, petiole and seed, and the hot taste, the removal of stones and pustules and the anti-emetic use in calcinated form. Outside the brackets are the Tibetan name, the four Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the sentence "Jatropha and Euphorbia purge all hot and cold disorders", which is the nearest Clark counterpart to the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence at line 3495. Clark prints the epithet as *wallichiana* and not *wallichii*, and his first binomial is *Euphorbia pallassii*. Whether the two epithets name one species the Codex has not checked. Clark's preface (line 83) says: "Therefore more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance". [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Euphorbia* is a very large genus of the spurge family (Euphorbiaceae), that its species carry a milky latex, and that the latex of many species is a skin and eye irritant. Clark's list for the name also includes *Croton tiglium*, a member of the same family whose seed oil is generally known as a violent purgative and skin irritant. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Men-Tsee-Khang text records that "Dhurji and Tharnu purge all forms of hot and cold disorders" (line 3495) and lists Thamu among the purgatives (line 3661); Clark's neighbouring entry says the commentary layer's Euphorbia "removes stones and pustules" and the root text says "Jatropha and Euphorbia purge all hot and cold disorders" (line 1134). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only by a column position, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation any line read gives is a remark in Clark's commentary layer, that the Euphorbia of *thar.nu* is anti-emetic "in calcinated form" (line 1134). It gives no quantity, no frequency and no duration, and no part used, and it is attached to Clark's entry and not to the glossary's "Thamu". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Tibetan terms ("hot and cold disorders", "purgatives") are not modern diagnoses or measured outcomes, so no modern finding can be set beside them here. The identity of the drug rests on a column position and a differently spelled epithet in the other Tibetan text, so a study of *Euphorbia wallichii* would not bear directly on it. It is a general, well-established fact that spurge latex can burn the skin and eyes.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** No taste, power or post-digestive taste is printed for Thamu in the Men-Tsee-Khang lines read, and the name does not appear in its taste-group lists of chapter 19 (which begin at line 3204). Clark's commentary layer for *thar.nu* (line 1134) prints "Euphorbia has hot taste". That sentence belongs to Clark's entry and to its Euphorbia, and the Codex does not carry it over to the glossary's species. The one quality printed in the Men-Tsee-Khang text is the action: purging "hot and cold disorders" (line 3495).

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Men-Tsee-Khang text places Thamu in its list of the efficacy of medicinal substances and in its group of purgatives, and Clark's *thar.nu* line says the same in kind. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the sentences cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and the link from this binomial to that framing rests on a column position and on a spelling.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a purgative in the Tibetan texts, with no dose, and its Latin name rests on a glossary column position and on a differently spelled epithet in Clark. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. The latex of *Euphorbia* species is generally known to be an irritant, a purgative drug can cause dehydration, and the identity is not settled, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Latex that touches the eyes needs urgent medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
