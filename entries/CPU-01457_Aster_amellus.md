# Starwort / Aster amellus — Aster astikos, asteriscos, asterion, bubonium (Dioscorides 4-120)

## *Aster amellus, one of several binomials the Dioscorides translators suggest for chapter 4-120 (also Aster atticus and Aster tripolium)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). **Named only by Dioscorides in this row.** The binomials are the translators' suggestion, not Dioscorides' own identification.

*4-120 ASTER ASTIKOS* (heading at line 9106). The translators' SUGGESTED line (lines 9108 to 9109) reads: "Aster atticus [Fuchs], Aster atticus caeuruleus vulgaris [Bauhin], Aster amellus [Linnaeus], Aster tripolium, Tripolium, Tripolium vulgare — Italian Starwort, Sea Aster, Sea Starwort, Tripoly", with the cross-reference "see 4-135". *Aster amellus* is therefore one name in a list of six, and this row is named for it only because the extraction took that one. The identification is the translators', and it is not settled by the text.

The description (line 9111): "Aster atticus has a woody little stem with a purple flower on the top (or a yellowish one) cut all around like the little head of anthemis [3-154], and it has small leaves similar to a star. The leaves around the stalk are somewhat long and rough." Uses (lines 9111 to 9112): "Smeared on, it helps an inflamed stomach, as well as inflammation of the eyes and the thighs, and prolapse of the perineum. A decoction of the purple part of the flower (taken as a drink with water) helps the synanchic [abscessed throat], and epilepsy in children. It is good (applied fresh and moist) for inflammation of the thighs."

The chapter then records an amuletic use: "Gathered when it is dry (with the left hand of the pained party) and hanged about the thigh, it frees him of the pain." It also records a lore of the plant: "It grows among rocks and in coarse places. The stars [flowers] of this shine in the night, for those who do not know it when they see it think it is a spirit, and it is found by the keepers of sheep." Crateuas the herbalist is cited for a further use: "pounded (green) with old swines' grease it is good for one bitten by a mad dog, or for a swollen throat, and inhaled, it drives away snakes." Names printed: "asteriscos, asterion, bubonium, or sows eye, the Romans call it inguinalis, and the Dacians, rathibis."

*Overlap.* Chapter 4-135 TRIPOLION (heading at line 9217) carries a SUGGESTED line that again lists *Aster tripolium* and *Aster atticus* ("see 4-120"). That chapter describes a sea-shore plant with a flower said to change colour three times a day. It has its own spine row, CPU-01649 *Tripolium vulgare*, and is not read here as the same plant. ### Active Components

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records a smeared-on remedy for inflammation of the stomach, eyes and thighs and for prolapse of the perineum, a drink of the purple flower for the abscessed throat and for epilepsy in children, a pain-charm hung on the thigh, and a snake-repelling inhalation on Crateuas' authority. These are the text's own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for epilepsy, throat abscess or snakebite, which need medical care.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these preparations. The plant "smeared on" (line 9111). A decoction of the purple part of the flower "taken as a drink with water" (line 9111). The fresh and moist plant "applied" to the thighs. The dry plant gathered and hung about the thigh (line 9112). The green plant pounded with old swine's grease on Crateuas' report (line 9112). Inhalation against snakes (line 9112). No quantity or duration is given for any of them. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and the chapter assigns the plant no status. What it offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: where the plant grows (among rocks, in coarse places), how to know it (a woody little stem, purple or yellowish flower, star-like leaves), who finds it (the keepers of sheep), and its many names. It also records, without endorsing, a left-hand amulet for thigh pain and the belief that the flowers shine at night and are mistaken for a spirit. Recording is not endorsing. The identification with *Aster amellus* is the translators' suggestion. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant beyond learning to recognise it. The abstentive answer is the honest one: the text describes external applications and an amulet, and the Codex does not build on them.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Nothing in the text read says the plant is toxic, but the remedies recorded are for conditions (epilepsy in children, an abscessed throat, a bite from a rabid dog, snakebite) that need medical care, and a home remedy must not replace it. The identity of the plant is uncertain, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Avoid in pregnancy as a general precaution, since no safety information was read.
