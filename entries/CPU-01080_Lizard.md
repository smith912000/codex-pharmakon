### Varieties

**Lizard (*Eremias*)** (FMT; Clark line 1202). The Tibetan lizard *rmigs.pa*, identified by Clark as *Eremias argus*, with *Phrynocephalus vlangalii* Strauch given after it. The text gives it nine names, and says it is anthelmintic and neutralizes poisons. The same lizard sits with the burrowing animals in the meats chapter (line 672). The Men-Tsee-Khang text also lists a "mountain-dwelling lizard" (line 2918), which this entry notes without a separate paragraph, since the passages read give it no property.

*Synthesis of the varieties.* One variety is folded, the Tibetan *Eremias* lizard. The texts divide lizards differently. Dioscorides separates them as separate animals, each with its own chapter: the lizard (2-69), the salamander as "a kind of lizard" (2-67), the *seps*, which is poisonous (2-70), and the skink (2-71). He gives each different uses, and names the part (head, liver, whole animal cut apart). Bryan's Ebers does not separate kinds, except for the "Black Lizard", and names the lizard's blood, dung and whole body. The Tibetan text lists the lizard under the whole-body medicines and the burrowing animals, with a binomial and nine names, and gives it two powers. No text ranks lizards. The uses can be placed side by side: Dioscorides applies the cut lizard to a scorpion sting and Bryan's Ebers burns a lizard against a scorpion, while the Tibetan text uses the lizard against worms and poisons. These are different uses and are not equated.

### Active Components

A lizard is a small reptile; its tissues are muscle, fat, organ tissue and bone, and its blood and dung are the usual body products of a reptile. The sources name no active component and give no quantities. Some lizards carry *Salmonella* in their gut, a general fact. The *seps* of Dioscorides is described as poisonous, and no lizard is stated to be poisonous in the passages read for the other texts.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources record the lizard as a poultice for splinters and warts, a liver for toothache, an application against scorpion sting (Dioscorides), as blood and dung in eye and hair remedies (Bryan's Ebers), and as a worm and poison medicine (Tibetan). The Codex has no protocol for any of this and offers none. Reptiles carry *Salmonella*; eye remedies made from animal blood and dung risk serious infection; scorpion stings and poisoning need medical care; and the animal is killed for the use.

### Preparation & Dosing

Greek (2-69): head pounded and applied; liver put into the cavity of a tooth; the animal cut apart and applied to a scorpion sting. Egyptian: blood with myrrh and bat's blood, torn hairs then treated (trichiasis); dung ground with incense (Bryan's text, lines 2843 and 2884); the live lizard burnt on a fire as a means against scorpions (Bryan, line 4224). Tibetan: no preparation given for the *Eremias* lizard in the passages read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Reptiles commonly carry *Salmonella* and other bacteria, and the risk of infection from handling or from putting reptile material near the eye or in a tooth cavity is real. Scorpion venoms differ by species; most stings are managed with local care, and the dangerous species need antivenom. Nothing in the sources ties lizard tissue to a treatment of warts, toothache, trichiasis, worms or poisoning. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The lizard is named as an ingredient for a complaint (blood for trichiasis, dung in eye remedies, the animal in a hair remedy), and in Bryan's account a burnt lizard is used against scorpions. Nothing says what it does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. The Ebers material is indication-organised: the identity of each named substance is a modern hypothesis, and these forms are Bryan's. Bryan reports no recitation with the lizard remedies read; since he summarises, that is not proof that none existed. No deity is attributed. [USER: verify]

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. The chapter gives uses for three parts (head, liver, whole animal), and the neighbouring chapters (2-70, 2-71) show that he distinguishes a harmless lizard from a poisonous one and from the skink; that is a discipline of attention in identification. The skink's aphrodisiac use (2-71) is recorded for a different animal and is not carried to the lizard.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The passages read are therapeutic: the lizard as anthelmintic and against poisons. Medicine in this tradition is the third of four treatment branches and supports practice; it does not reach the root. No *bcud len* use of the lizard was found, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, so none is asserted. Clark's introduction (line 57) notes that animal products are little used today.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do. The lizard is in three traditions for plain reasons: a splinter-puller and tooth remedy in Greece, blood and dung in eye remedies in Egypt, and a worm and poison medicine in Tibet. None of the three assigns it a cultivation role, and none of the uses is safe to repeat.

### Contraindications

- **Infection.** Lizards carry *Salmonella*; do not handle reptile blood or dung near eyes, mouth or wounds. Eye remedies made from animal material can blind.
- **Scorpion stings and poisoning.** Seek medical care; do not apply or burn an animal.
- **Warts, toothache, ingrowing lashes (trichiasis) and worms.** All have safe modern treatments.
- **Poisonous kinds.** Dioscorides names a poisonous lizard (*seps*); some lizards are venomous or toxic, and a lizard cannot be identified from these texts. [USER: verify]
- **Dose unknown.** No dose is given for any use in the sources read.
- **Ethics and law.** Using the animal means killing it; many lizards are protected species, and collection or trade may be regulated. [USER: verify]
