# Abutilon avicennae — srin.shing.na.ma, Cinshing nama (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Abutilon avicennae, the first of three binomials Clark 1995 prints for srin.shing.na.ma (with Daphne odora and Stellaria media); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary does not print this binomial for the name it spells "Cinshingnama"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts in this row.** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the three binomials belong to three different families of plant. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed names (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1047).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads: "srin.shing.na.ma: Abutilon avicennae: Daphne odora: Stellaria media: [has four names. It has silvery bark, scattered green leaves, long, flexible, brownish petioles, deep red flowers and bright red fruits which turn black after ripening. The inferior type has thin petioles, dark-green lobed leaves and white flowers]. The fruit of Abutilon has the same use as Datura [and increases digestive heat]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". So the four names, the whole description of bark, leaf, petiole, flower and fruit, the inferior type and the words "and increases digestive heat" are the commentary layer. What stands outside the brackets is one sentence: "The fruit of Abutilon has the same use as Datura." The root-text layer, as Clark gives it, therefore names the part used (the fruit) and gives no taste, no power and no disease of its own: it refers to another drug.

The drug referred to is the entry two lines above, thang.phrom (line 1045): "Datura has a hot taste, cures infectious tissue degeneration, sores and swellings and is also an aphrodisiac." That sentence is entirely inside Clark's square brackets. The next line (1046) is outside them: "Both Datura and henbane are anthelmintic." The Codex does not decide how much of the bracketed Datura entry the phrase "the same use" carries over. The text does not say.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (Explanatory Tantra, ch.20, and glossary).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. Line 3438 reads, in the chapter on the efficacy of medicinal substances: "Thangtrom and Langthang tsae cures disorders caused by ein. Fruit of Cinshing nama has the same therapeutic effect as Thangtrom and Langthang tsae." (The word "ein" is printed so; the OCR may have altered a letter, and the Codex does not correct it. [USER: verify]) That Cinshing nama is a form of srin.shing.na.ma is a reading from the sound of the name and from the agreement on "the fruit" and "same use as Datura/Thangtrom", and is not stated in the text. The index (line 7296) gives "Cinshing nama 208". Clark's own name index prints the Tibetan name as "sring.shing.na.ma" (line 1768), with an extra g. A different spelling, "Cinshingdru", stands at line 3638 in a list of medicines; the Codex does not equate it with this drug.

The glossary prints its columns in separate blocks. The block at lines 5164 to 5176 prints the phonetic names "Chhu tsa Chum tsa" and "Cinshingnama" (lines 5166 to 5167), the Tibetan "chu rtsa lcum rtsa" and "srin shing sna ma" (lines 5169 to 5170), then the scientific names "Rheum emodi", "Rheum palmatum L." and "Jasminum officinale" and the English "Rhubarb", "East asian rhubarb" and "White jasmine". Three names stand in each column, so by position "Cinshingnama" would take "Jasminum officinale" and not *Abutilon*. The glossary does not print them on one row, and the Codex does not state the pairing. This is an identity doubt between Clark and the glossary. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *srin.shing*, *Cinshing*, *Abutilon* and *Daphne odora* found no existing entry on this drug. I_1175 (*Rheum emodi*) reads the same glossary block (lines 5164 to 5176) for the rhubarb name beside it. I_649 (Chickweed, *Stellaria media*) is the entry on Clark's third binomial; it carries no FMT tag. I_090 (*Datura stramonium*) and I_129 (*Hyoscyamus niger*) are entries on the plants the line compares the fruit to; the Codex has not checked whether they carry an FMT tag for thang.phrom or lang.thang.tse. None of them is an entry on srin.shing.na.ma.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Abutilon* is a genus of the mallow family (Malvaceae), *Daphne* of the family Thymelaeaceae and *Stellaria* of the pink family (Caryophyllaceae), so the three binomials name unrelated plants and no common composition can be stated. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the Tibetan drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record the fruit of a plant that is given the same use as Datura (Clark line 1047) and the same therapeutic effect as thornapple and henbane (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3438), and, in Clark's commentary layer, as increasing digestive heat. These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. Datura and henbane are generally known to be poisonous plants, the botanical identity of the drug is uncertain, and the Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation other than the part used, the fruit (Clark line 1047, outside the brackets; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3438). No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identity of the drug is uncertain between three binomials, and the commentary layer is separate from the root text, so a modern study of one plant would not bear directly on it.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Outside Clark's square brackets the entry prints no taste and no power. It prints only that the fruit has "the same use as Datura". The bracketed commentary adds "increases digestive heat". The Men-Tsee-Khang text prints no taste or power for Cinshing nama (line 3438). No other quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this fruit only a cross-reference to the use of Datura, and the commentary layer adds that it increases digestive heat. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this entry places srin.shing.na.ma in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. In the lines read, the essence-extraction mentions in the herb section are for other plants (for example at lines 971 and 1121), and srin.shing.na.ma is not named there. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The root-text layer refers it to a poisonous genus' use, and the identity is unsettled. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Datura and henbane, to which the texts compare the fruit, are generally known to be poisonous plants. The botanical identity is uncertain (three binomials in Clark, a different one by position in the glossary), so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
