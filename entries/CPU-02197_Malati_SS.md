# Malati — Malati (Sushruta Samhita, Bhishagratna 1907; printed "Malati")

## *A fragrant-flowered plant of the La'ksha'di group whose flowers the Sushruta text calls bitter and sweet-scented; the translation gives no Latin name in the chapters. ### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the *Sushruta Samhita* among the texts on disk, in four places.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder from an extraction of the translation's group lists; the 30 October check pass kept it as a separate row with the identification unresolved.

**Sushruta (SS), Sutrasthana ch.38, the La'ksha'di group.** The chapter classifies drugs "usually made into thirty-seven different groups (Gana)" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch38.txt` line 9). The group is printed at lines 451 to 454 (Vol. I lines 15793 to 15795): "The drugs known as the Laksha, Arevata, Kutaja, Ashvamara, Katphalam, Haridra, Daru-Haridra, Ximva, Saptachchhada, Malati, and Trayamana form the Lakshadi Gana." The metrical text (lines 456 to 460) reads: "This consists of astringent, bitter and sweet taste (Rasa) and acts as a good vermifuge and a purifying (aseptic) agent in cases of bad, malignant or indolent ulcers. Diseases due to the deranged Kapham and Pittam prove amenable to its curative properties, which extend to cases of cutaneous affections (Kushtham) as well." These are group claims, not claims for Malati alone.

**Sushruta (SS), Sutrasthana ch.36, a healing ghee.** In the chapter on miscellaneous remedies, "a medicated Ghritam, prepared with the Prithakparni, Atmagupta, Haridra, Daruharidra, Malati, Sita, and drugs belonging to the Kakolyadi group, is renowned for its healing properties" (`SS_Sutra_ch36.txt` lines 183 to 186; Vol. I line 15096). The context is the treatment of ulcers; the preceding sentence (lines 180 to 182) names a plaster for healing an ulcer. The part of Malati used is not stated.

**Sushruta (SS), Sutrasthana ch.46, the flowers.** In the chapter on the properties of foods and drugs, the flower passage says: "The Mallika and Malati flowers have a bitter taste and subdue the Pittam owing to their sweet scent" (`SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` line 1806 to 1808; Vol. I line 21467). This is the only place the text speaks of Malati as a flower for its own taste and effect. The same passage gives properties for the lotus kinds, Kumuda, Sindhuvara, Vakula, Patala and Nagam flowers, and then, at a later point (line 1822 onward), a general rule that "a flower should be understood as possessed of the same properties which are natural to the tree or plant on which it grows".

**Sushruta (SS), Sutrasthana ch.25, surgical needles.** In the chapter on the eight kinds of surgical operation, the needles for sewing a wound are to be so made "as to be fitted with sharp points capable of being handled with the greatest ease, having a girth equal that of the stem of a Malati flower" (`SS_Sutra_ch25.txt` line 152; Vol. I line 12047). This is a measure of thickness, not a drug use. It does tell the reader that Malati was a well-known flower with a slender stem. [USER: verify the reading of "stem"]

**Appendix No. 3.** The translator's index of drug names with Latin synonyms (`Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`, the M page around lines 32215 to 32345) prints "Malati" (OCR "MjClati") followed by Mallika. By position in the OCR columns the Latin lines read "Aganosam Crayophyllata ; Jasminum Grandiflorum" for Malati and "Jasminum Sambac" for Mallika. The Codex treats such pairings as proposals, since a dropped OCR line shifts a pairing by one. ### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, jasmine flowers (*Jasminum* species) are valued for volatile aromatic compounds, and the genus carries iridoid glycosides and other plant compounds; *Aganosma* belongs to the dogbane family, whose members often carry alkaloids or cardiac-type compounds. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** As a member of the La'ksha'di group: a vermifuge and an aseptic cleanser of bad, malignant or indolent ulcers, and a remedy for Kapham, Pittam and skin diseases. As a flower: bitter, and quieting Pittam through its scent. In ghee: healing.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no preparation of Malati alone, no statement of which part goes into the ghee.

The Codex does not translate the classic's terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend this plant.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources give a medicated ghee (ch.36) built on Malati with other drugs, and the flower as a drug (ch.46). No method, weight or quantity is printed. Chapter 38 ends with a general instruction that the groups be used "two, three or four drugs of the same medicinal group" at a time, or whole, as the physician decides (lines 561 to 568). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any dose from this entry.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Jasmine flowers are used worldwide as perfume and flavouring material, and some jasmine species are used to scent tea. The classic's "Kapham", "Pittam" and "Kushtham" have no modern equivalents and are not translated into any. Because the identification is open, no finding about any named species can be carried over to Malati. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SS (Bhishagratna 1907).** The flowers have "a bitter taste and subdue the Pittam owing to their sweet scent" (ch.46, line 1806 to 1808). The La'ksha'di group is "astringent, bitter and sweet" (ch.38, line 456). No potency (*virya*) or post-digestive effect (*vipaka*) is printed for Malati.

### Cultivation Register

**Sushruta (SS).** *(c) Absent.* Malati appears in a therapeutic group, a healing ghee, a flower-properties passage and a measure for needles. It was not found in a *rasayana* (rejuvenation) or *vajikarana* (generative vitality) chapter, and no *ojas* effect is assigned to it. A "sweet scent" that quiets *Pittam* is a statement about a flower's action, not a cultivation grade.

**Practitioner's note.** A scented flower of uncertain species that Sushruta uses for ulcers, skin disease and Pittam, and cites as a measure of fineness. There is nothing here to take up as a cultivation practice; the entry keeps the name, its four contexts and the open identification on record.

### Contraindications

- **Identity unresolved.** If the Appendix pairing "Aganosma" is correct, the plant belongs to a family in which some members are poisonous; if it is a jasmine, it is generally a food-grade scent plant. Do not substitute either on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
