# Rubus biflorus — Kanta kari, kaN+Da ka ri; kanda.ka.ri (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary by column position)

## *Rubus biflorus, the sole binomial on line 5369 of the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary, which column order sets against "Kanta kari" (English "Rubus, blackberry"); Clark 1995 prints kanda.ka.ri with Solanum xanthocarpum first and Rubus saxatilis second, and no Rubus biflorus* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Shennong (Yang 1998), Sushruta (chapters 45 and 46 and Bhishagratna vol. 3), Ebers and Assyrian texts on disk for *Rubus* found no hit. The Dioscorides text prints *Rubus* in translators' SUGGESTED lines for Greek drugs (lines 4444, 8323 to 8324 and 8328: *Rubus fruticosus* and *Rubus idaeus*), which are not read as this plant. The row was added from a printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 295) the row is keyed by the **scientific name** "Rubus biflorus", with the locus "Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (line 5369)" and the note "Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". Line 5369 prints this one binomial alone, so the key is neither a first nor a later name on a shared line. Clark prints no *Rubus biflorus*. The Tibetan name *kanda.ka.ri* is reached from this row by column position, as set out below.

**Identity doubt.** The binomial is set against the Tibetan name by column position only. Clark's entry for *kanda.ka.ri* (line 972) prints "Indian Salamin: Solanum xanthocarpum: Rubus saxatilis", so the species it names first is a *Solanum*, and its only *Rubus* is a different species from the glossary's. The Codex's Sushruta and Unani entry I_361 (*Kantakari*, *Solanum xanthocarpum*; tags CM, SS) is on a drug of the same sounding name in other traditions and is not merged here. The two Tibetan editions therefore agree on the Tibetan drug and may differ on its genus, and the Codex does not choose. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" and (line 68) that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect". The Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note (line 686) says that its botanical terms rest on the information of its own Materia Medica Department, and it names no earlier flora. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "K" block).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. The block begins with "Kakola" (line 5359, transliteration "ka ko la", scientific name "Amomum sabulatum", line 5363), then "Kanta kari" (line 5365, transliteration "kaN+Da ka ri", line 5367, scientific name "Rubus biflorus", line 5369), then "Kapae" and "Karanza" (lines 5371 to 5372) against "Lagenaria siceraria Caesalpinia bonducella" (line 5376). The block's phonetic names end with "Kyerpa Kyiche Kyurura" (line 5401). Counted in order, "Kanta kari" is the second phonetic name and *Rubus biflorus* the second scientific name, and the English column (lines 5407 to 5418) has "Black Cardamom" first and "Rubus, blackberry" second. The count is checked on five later names: "Khenpa" against *Artemisia vulgaris* (Clark's *mkhan.pa*, line 1078), "Khyungdher" against *Uncaria* (Clark's *hkhyung.sder*, line 963), "Khurmang" against *Taraxacum officinale* ("Dandelion"), "Kyerpa" against *Berberis aristata* (Clark's *skyer.pa*, line 983) and "Kyurura" against *Emblica officinalis* ("Emblic myrobalan"). The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position only. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225; colophon at line 3583). Lines 3422 to 3423 (the break falls in the middle of a sentence): "... Laetae and Kanta kari cure hot disorders associated with loong. Ga dra possesses the same effect as Laetae and Kanta kari and is effective against contagious diseases." In chapter 21 (colophon at line 3689), lines 3612 to 3613 list *Kanta kari* in a group: "Laetae, Kanta kari, Agaru, Go nyoe, Ruta, Gugul and Gokya are the group of medicines that cure hot disorder associated with loong." The index lists "Kantakari 205,221" (line 7405). "Loong" is a Tibetan term that the editors keep untranslated (note at line 686). The sentences give no part used, no preparation and no taste.

*Clark 1995, the same Tibetan name (read through the name, not the binomial).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997), line 972: "kanda.ka.ri: Indian Salamin: Solanum xanthocarpum: Rubus saxatilis: [has twenty names. Its woody stem resembles that of the young wild rose and it has a bright pale yellow flower. Salamin (is a small thorny herb) bearing clusters of red berries having sweet and astringent tastes]. Moonseed and salamin cure wind fevers, [infectious fevers and cough]." Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, and those in round brackets from the oral instructions of Dr. Yeshe Donden and Dr. Pema Dorje. The names, the plant description and the tastes are the commentary layer, and "(is a small thorny herb)" is oral instruction. The root-layer clause "Moonseed and salamin cure wind fevers" is shared with the preceding drug (*sle.tres*, Heart-leaved Moonseed, line 971), so the root layer gives this drug no action of its own. In chapter 21 (heading at line 1205), line 1214 reads: "The class of medicines used in wind fevers includes Heart-leaved Moonseed, Indian Salamin, Eaglewood, Fennel, Costus root, Black Sal resin and Garlic." Clark's name index lists *kanda.ka.ri* (line 1673).

The two editions agree in kind. Clark's drug cures "wind fevers" and the Men-Tsee-Khang *Kanta kari* cures "hot disorders associated with loong", where "wind" in Clark and *loong* in the Men-Tsee-Khang text are the two translators' words for the same term; and the two chapter 21 lists share company: eaglewood (Clark) and *Agaru* (Men-Tsee-Khang), and fennel (Clark's *go.snyod*, line 1122) and *Go nyoe* (Men-Tsee-Khang). The Codex reads *Kanta kari* and *kanda.ka.ri* as one drug from the spelling and that agreement; neither text says so. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Rubus biflorus*, *kanda.ka.ri* and *Kanta kari* found no entry on this Tibetan drug. I_361 (*Solanum xanthocarpum*, *Kantakari*; tags CM, SS) and I_360 (*Brihati*, *Solanum indicum*; tag SS) are entries on drugs of the same family of names in other traditions, with no FMT tag, and are not merged. I_1350 (*Rubus ellipticus*, CPU-01786) is the sibling row from the same batch, written for the neighbouring Men-Tsee-Khang name *Ga dra* and the adjacent Clark entry (line 973). I_837 (*Rubus idaeus*; tags CM, DMM) and I_719 (*Rubus fruticosus*; tags AT, DMM, CM) are entries on other species with no FMT tag. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Rubus* is a genus of the rose family (Rosaceae) with the brambles, and that *Solanum*, Clark's first genus, belongs to the nightshade family (Solanaceae), so the two editions point to plants of different families. The lines read give no composition for the Tibetan drug, and nothing is stated here about what it contains.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that cures "hot disorders associated with loong" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3422) and, for the Clark entry, "wind fevers", with a commentary note on infectious fevers and cough (line 972). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only through column position, and they are not Codex protocols. Fever and cough need medical care, and the Codex gives no protocol and no dose for them.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary mentions the woody stem, the flower and the red berries and does not say which part is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 972) prints "sweet and astringent tastes" for the fruit, in the entry for *kanda.ka.ri*. No power and no post-digestive taste is printed. The Men-Tsee-Khang lines read (3422 to 3423, 3612, 5359 to 5418) print no taste or power; they print the use group (hot disorder associated with loong).

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it cures wind fevers in Clark and hot disorders associated with loong in the Men-Tsee-Khang text. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names (*kanda.ka.ri*, *Kanta kari*, *Kantakari*, *Rubus*) found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, colophon at line 3950), and the herb entries of Clark's chapter 20 that mention essence extraction (lines 971, 1043, 1078, 1103, 1121 and 1144) do not include it; line 971 is the entry of the preceding drug, the moonseed, whose action the root-layer clause shares. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name more securely than to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a wind-fever drug in a shared clause and a list, with no preparation and no dose, and its Latin name is a positional guess between two genera. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Fever needs medical care; the identity is open between plants of different genera, one of which (*Solanum*) belongs to a family containing toxic species, though no toxicity is stated for this drug in the lines read. No preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No conservation information and no safety information for pregnancy were read. [USER: verify]
