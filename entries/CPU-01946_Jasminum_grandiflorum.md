# Jasminum grandiflorum — Jati (Sushruta Samhita, Bhishagratna's Appendix No. 3); the Sanskrit name is a positional pairing

## *Jasminum grandiflorum L., the Latin that Bhishagratna's Appendix No. 3 ("Names of Drugs, with Latin synonyms", Vol. III) prints (OCR "Jasminum Grandiflorum.", line 31847) in the column of Latin names; the Sanskrit name it stands against is Jati, by counting down the OCR columns, not by a printed link. Spanish or royal jasmine, a climbing shrub of the olive family; confidence high for the Latin as the translator's, medium for its pairing with Jati* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Sushruta text (SS); this entry rests on one text, Bhishagratna's English translation of the Sushruta Samhita, and on a Latin name that comes from the translator's appendix and not from the chapters.** The chapters are in `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/` and the appendix is in `Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`. The row was opened on 13 October from the extraction (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/extract/names.csv`), where the Latin line is kept as "not paired to a Sanskrit name (OCR column split)". The Latin is the translator's; the species has not been checked against a current plant list. [USER: verify]

**The pairing, by counting.** Appendix No. 3 prints each page as a column of Sanskrit names followed by a column of Latin names in the same order (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/extract/README.md` warns that a dropped line can shift a pairing by one). On the page that carries this Latin line, the Sanskrit run is "Jambira", "Jambu", "Jata'-ma'nsi", "**Ja'ti**" (line 31797), "Jatila'", "Ja'ti-phala", "Javsi", "Jayapa'la", "Jhinti", "Jiraka", "Jivaka" and "Jayanti": twelve names (lines 31795–31811). The Latin run that follows has twelve entries to the same point: "Citrus medica", "Eugenia gambolana", "Valeriana Jatamansi", "**Jasminum Grandiflorum.**" (line 31847), "See Jatamansi", "Myristica Fragrans", "Hibiscus Rosa sinensis", "Croton tigliura", "Barlarea Cristata", "Cuminum Cyminum. Cumin seed", "Pentaptera tomentosa" and "Sesbania aculeata; S. Aegyptiaca". The fourth Sanskrit name, Jati, is the fourth Latin entry, and the neighbours fit: "Jata'-ma'nsi" with *Valeriana jatamansi*, "Jatila'" with "See Jatamansi", "Ja'ti-phala" with *Myristica fragrans* (nutmeg), "Jiraka" with cumin. The pairing is therefore sound by count and by its neighbours, but it is still positional. [USER: verify against the page image]

**Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *The name in the chapters.* "Jati" occurs in several places, all read from the chapter files. The translator does not give a Latin name in them.

*As a flower in the head-cleansing (errhine) list (Sutra 39, `SS_Sutra_ch39.txt`, lines 52–66).* Jati is a member of a long list of drugs "to be regarded as errhines (Shirovirechanam)": "Pippali, Vidanga, Apamarga, Shigru ... Ingudi, Meshashringi, Matulungi, Murangi, Pilu, Jati, Shala, Tala, Madhuka (Maula), Laksha and Hingu". The text then says which part of each is used: "the flowers of Matulungi, Murungi, Pilu and Jati".

*In ulcer treatments (Sutra 36, `SS_Sutra_ch36.txt`).* Line 128: a medicated ghee "prepared with the expressed juice of Arka roots, Uttama, the milky juice of Snuhi plants, drugs abounding in alkalis, Jati-roots, the two kinds Haridra, Kasisa, Katurohini and the aforesaid plug-drugs (Sodhana-Varti) pasted together" is described (the sentence runs on to the next page); and in lines 120–127 an oil or ghee made with "Kasisa, Katurohini, Jatikanda, and the two kinds of Haridra, and applied to an ulcer or open swelling, purifies its interior". Lines 211–216: a compound for the stage of Avasadana, of "Kasisa, Saindhava (rock salt), Kinvam, Kuruvinda, Manalishila, the shell of a hen's egg, the blossoms of Jati flowers, the seeds of Shirisha, and Karanja, and powders of the abovesaid metals". The compound is printed as used on an ulcer or open swelling.

*As a smell (Sutra 28, `SS_Sutra_ch28.txt`, lines 69–71).* In the chapter on the favourable or unfavourable prognosis of an ulcer, an ulcer "emitting a sweet smell like that of wine, or fragrant aloe wood (Aguru), clarified-butter, Jati flower, Champaka, sandal, lotus or any celestial flower (Divyagandha), should be regarded as the precursor of" death. The flower is named here as a scent.

*In eye-salves and a commentator's note (Uttara 11, `SS_Uttara_ch11.txt`, lines 74–89; Uttara 17, line 89).* In Uttara 11 an eye-salve (Anjana) is made from ashes of blue barley soaked in milk, "mixed with an equal part of burnt ashes of Arjaka, Ashphotaka, Kapittha, Vilva, Nirgundi and Jati flowers" (lines 84–85); the footnote reports the commentator Dallana's reading of the drug list, and a rival reading by "Panjika'ka'ra" that "Murva' and the flowers of Ja'ti only should be taken". In a footnote to Uttara 17 (line 89) "Rasa" is said by others to mean "the juice of the leaves of Jati flower". The footnotes are the translator's notes on commentators' readings, and are weak evidence.

*In the cooling regimen for alcoholism (Uttara 47, `SS_Uttara_ch47.txt`, lines 401–403).* The patient's chamber "should be scented and decorated with Mansi, Tamala, Musta, Kunkuma, Padma-leaves, Jati flower, Utpala (blue-lotus), Priyangu, Kes'ara (Bakula), and Pundarika (red-lotus)" (the OCR prints "Jātir").

*Another Jati.* The chapters also use "Jati" for the nutmeg: in Chikitsa 24 (`SS_Chikitsa_ch24.txt`, line 143) a betel-leaf is prepared with "cloves, camphor, nutmeg (Jati), lime, araca-nut, Kakkola and Katukdhva", where the translator glosses Jati as nutmeg; and Sutra 46 (line 1322) lists "the Jatikosha, the Jati Phalam" with bitter, pungent properties. So the one word covers the jasmine flower (and root) and, in the translator's gloss, the nutmeg. Which meaning each passage carries is the translator's choice. The appendix's Latin, by count, attaches *Jasminum grandiflorum* to the bare "Jati" and *Myristica fragrans* to "Ja'ti-phala". [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, *Jasminum grandiflorum* is a jasmine of the olive family (Oleaceae) whose flowers carry volatile aromatic oils and are used in perfumery; nothing is stated here about the composition of the Sushruta drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Sushruta text records "Jati" as a scented flower (Sutra 28), as a flower in a head-cleansing list (Sutra 39), as an ingredient in ulcer compounds (Sutra 36) and in an eye-salve (Uttara 11). The Latin name is the translator's, and the text does not state which part or preparation did what. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives the preparations above, each with its locus: errhine drugs, part used "the flowers" (Sutra 39, lines 62–66); a medicated ghee with Jati roots (Sutra 36, line 128); a compound with Jati blossoms (Sutra 36, lines 211–216); an eye-salve with burnt Jati flowers (Uttara 11, lines 84–85). The measures in those recipes belong to the compounds, and no dose, part-quantity or duration is printed for Jati itself in the lines read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Jasmine flowers are used for scent and flavour. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SS:** no flavour, potency (*virya*) or after-taste (*vipaka*) is printed for the bare name Jati in the lines read. For the nutmeg (Jati-phala, Jatikosha) Sutra 46 prints "a bitter pungent taste", that they "subdue thirst and Kapham" and are "light in digestion" (line 1322 and following); that sentence is about a different drug under a related name and is not carried over.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. In the Sushruta scheme the name is a scent in a cooling regimen and in the prognosis of an ulcer, a flower in a head-cleansing list, and an ingredient of ulcer compounds. Nothing should be taken on the strength of a Latin name that the text does not itself give.

### Contraindications

The Sushruta passages read print no warning for the drug itself. The errhine use is a physician's procedure and not a household one. Because the Sanskrit word also names the nutmeg in the translator's text, and because the identity rests on a translator's appendix, no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
