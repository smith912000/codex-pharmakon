# Service Tree / Sorb Apple

## *Sorbus domestica* L. (syn. *Cormus domestica* (L.) Spach) | Family: Rosaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Cool / dry / astringent (attributed to the Greek–Arabic humoral stream; species-level energetic is inferred from the fruit's manifest astringency, not from a single named passage — see note below)
**Training Phase:** Foundation — Signature Study

### Origin & Tradition

The true service tree is a long-lived deciduous member of the rose family (Rosaceae), native to southern and central Europe, the Mediterranean basin, North Africa and south-west Asia. It is often confused with its relatives — the rowan or mountain-ash (*Sorbus aucuparia*) and the wild service tree (*Torminaria clusii* / *Sorbus torminalis*) — but *S. domestica* is distinguished by its larger, pear- or apple-shaped pome fruits (the "sorb apples" or "sorbs"), which ripen to a brownish flush and were historically an orchard and hedgerow fruit rather than a purely wild one. The fruit is famously hard, sour and mouth-puckeringly astringent when fresh, and was traditionally eaten only after **bletting** — the controlled over-ripening (softening, partial fermentation) on straw or in storage that breaks down the astringent load and converts starch to sugar, rendering the pulp sweet, soft and palatable. This bletting step is itself a piece of the tradition: the raw fruit is a medicine (or a hardship food); the bletted fruit is a delicacy.

**DMM — Dioscorides / Greek herbal stream.** The Greek and later Mediterranean herbal tradition treated the sorb (Greek *oa* / Latin *sorba*) as a cooling, drying, binding fruit — the archetypal *styptic* (astringent) pome. It was recommended for loose bowels, fluxes and "weakness of the stomach," precisely the profile expected from a tannin-rich unripe pome. The classification of the sorb among the binding/astringent fruits is well attested in the Dioscoridean–Mediterranean stream. **[USER: verify]** the exact Dioscorides *Materia Medica* book/chapter citation before printing a specific locus; the *category* placement (astringent fruit for flux) is secure, the precise chapter number is not confirmed in this run.

**CM — Avicenna (Canon of Medicine, Unani).** The Arabic-Persian medical tradition inherited and systematised the Greek reading: the sorb (Arabic *ghubayrāʾ*) appears in the Unani materia medica as a cold, dry, astringent fruit (*qābiḍ*) used to arrest diarrhoea and strengthen a relaxed stomach. **[USER: verify]** the specific Canon reference; the humoral placement (cold/dry/astringent, antidiarrhoeal) is consistent across the Greek–Arabic stream, but the exact passage is not substantiated in this research run and should be checked against a printed Canon before being cited as a direct quotation.

In short: across the Greek and Arabic streams the service-tree fruit is a **Mediterranean astringent fruit-remedy for looseness of the bowel** — coherent, cross-tradition, and mechanistically plausible. What the tradition does *not* license is any grand systemic-tonic doctrine; the honest reading is "a binding fruit for flux."

### Active Components

The best species-specific chemistry we have for the fruit comes from a 2022 compositional study (Ognyanov et al.), which found:

- **Phenolics / flavonoids:** quercetin-3-*O*-glucoside as a major flavonoid, plus neochlorogenic acid and 3,4-dihydroxybenzoic (protocatechuic) acid as leading phenolic acids.
- **Organic acids:** malic acid dominant.
- **Sugars & polysaccharides:** fructose and glucose as the major soluble sugars, with **pectin** the major polysaccharide fraction.
- **Lipids / sterols:** β-sitosterol as the principal sterol; palmitic acid the main saturated and linoleic acid the main polyunsaturated fatty acid (fruit contains only small amounts of lipid overall).
- **Minerals:** potassium, iron and boron notably abundant.

Two components deserve special comment for the cultivator:

1. **Tannins (condensed/hydrolysable astringents).** The manifest mouth-puckering astringency of the unripe sorb is a tannin/phenolic phenomenon; these are the pharmacologically relevant astringents behind the traditional antidiarrhoeal use. (The 2022 study quantified specific phenolics rather than a bulk "tannin" number, so treat the tannin *quantity* as characterised-by-signature rather than by a single figure.)

2. **Sorbitol — the sugar-alcohol named after this genus.** Sorbitol was first isolated from the berries of the genus *Sorbus* (rowan), which is the origin of its name — a high-confidence etymological/chemical fact. Sorbitol is a characteristic *Sorbus* polyol. **Important honesty caveat:** the 2022 *S. domestica* fruit study reported fructose and glucose as the major soluble sugars and did not headline a large sorbitol figure, so do not assert a specific sorbitol percentage for the sorb apple from this run. The interesting pharmacological *counterpoint* still stands conceptually: a fruit prized as an astringent (binding) remedy also belongs to a genus defined by an osmotic, laxative sugar-alcohol — the plant holds both a "bind" and a "loosen" in tension, and which one dominates depends on ripeness, dose and preparation.

Much of the deeper antioxidant/phytochemical literature in this genus is generated on ***Sorbus aucuparia*** (rowan) rather than *S. domestica*. **Any rowan data must be read as congener data, not as service-tree data.**

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Within Kenetica, *Sorbus domestica* is filed as a **Foundation-phase astringent fruit-signature** — a teaching specimen for the "binding pome" archetype. Its classical, cross-tradition use is narrow and honest:

- **Astringent / antidiarrhoeal:** the tannin-bearing fruit (especially the harder, less-bletted pulp and decoctions of dried fruit) as a mild traditional remedy for looseness of the bowel, dysentery-type flux and a "relaxed" stomach. This is a **traditional-use** grade claim with a plausible tannin mechanism, not a clinically proven one.
- **Mild nutritive tonic (bletted fruit):** the softened, sweetened bletted fruit as an ordinary nutritive food carrying pectin, potassium, iron, organic acids and phenolics — a "food-as-medicine" role, not a specific therapeutic one.

It is a signature-study exemplar precisely because the *raw vs bletted* and *astringent vs osmotic* tensions make it a good lens for teaching how ripeness and preparation reshape a plant's action.

### Scientific Correlation

Grading the claims against real, retrieved evidence:

- **Antioxidant / free-radical scavenging (fruit): PRECLINICAL (in-vitro), species-specific.** Graded *supported at the bench, not the clinic*. The 2022 study directly measured DPPH-type scavenging and protection against peroxyl/hydroxyl radicals in *S. domestica* fruit. No human outcome data.
- **α-Glucosidase inhibition / anti-hyperglycaemic potential (leaf extract): PRECLINICAL (in-vitro), species-specific.** A 2023 formulation study showed a *S. domestica* leaf extract inhibits the α-glucosidase enzyme (a mechanism relevant to blunting post-meal glucose). Enzyme-assay only — this is a mechanistic hint, **not** evidence of a clinical antidiabetic effect.
- **Antidiarrhoeal / astringent use: TRADITIONAL, mechanistically plausible, clinically UNPROVEN.** No species-specific human trial was retrieved. The tannin mechanism is well established in general pharmacology; the specific efficacy of sorb-apple preparations is not.
- **Sorbitol osmotic / laxative effect: ESTABLISHED for sorbitol the molecule (not measured here for the sorb fruit).** Sorbitol is a documented low-glycaemic osmotic polyol with a recognised laxative/colon-hydrating action at higher intake, and is a known cause of osmotic diarrhoea. This is molecule-level pharmacology; do not translate it into a claim about a specific dose of sorb fruit without species data.
- **"Gemmotherapy" for Ménière's disease (venous/lymphatic regulation): NULL / not credible as presented.** A single-author 2024 case series (six patients, no control group, no blinding, subjective outcomes) reported improvement. Graded **anecdotal / uncontrolled — insufficient to support any efficacy claim.** The codex explicitly declines the implied doctrine that *S. domestica* "regulates venous circulation and lymphatic drainage"; the evidence does not support it.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Across the Greek (DMM) and Arabic/Unani (CM) streams the sorb is read as **cold, dry and astringent (*styptic* / *qābiḍ*)** — a binding fruit that "gathers and holds," used to check fluxes and firm a relaxed stomach. In Kenetica's grid this maps to a **cooling, drying, contracting** signature. The energetic is *inferred from the fruit's manifest astringency and its consistent placement among the binding pomes*, not deduced from a single verified passage — hence the caution flags above on the exact Dioscorides/Avicenna loci.

### Contraindications

- **Do not eat raw, un-bletted fruit as food:** intensely astringent and GI-irritating; can cause nausea and gut upset.
- **Tannin load:** strong dried-fruit decoctions may aggravate constipation, and tannins chelate iron and can bind co-administered drugs — space away from iron supplements and critical medications.
- **Osmotic / laxative counter-effect:** the genus's sorbitol content means larger intakes (or sorbitol-rich preparations) can produce the *opposite* of the astringent effect — bloating, cramping and osmotic diarrhoea. Individuals with fructose/polyol malabsorption or IBS are especially sensitive.
- **Not a treatment for vestibular disease:** the codex does not endorse *S. domestica* for Ménière's disease, tinnitus or vertigo; the only supporting report is uncontrolled and anecdotal.
- **Pregnancy/lactation, paediatric, renal:** no species-specific safety data retrieved — treat as **[USER: verify]** and default to culinary (bletted-fruit) amounts only.
- **Identification:** ensure true *S. domestica*; do not substitute rowan (*S. aucuparia*) fruit, whose raw berries carry parasorbic acid and are harsher/emetic until cooked.
