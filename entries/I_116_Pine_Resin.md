### Botanical Identity
The Codex entry covers the *Pinus* genus collectively as a pharmaceutical category — multiple species were used interchangeably by ancient physicians based on geographic availability:
- *P. sylvestris* (Scots pine) — northern/central Europe; the primary European medicinal pine
- *P. halepensis* (Aleppo pine) — Mediterranean; the pine of the DMM and AT traditions
- *P. pinea* (Stone pine/umbrella pine) — Mediterranean; seeds (pine nuts) medicinal; resin less so
- *P. massoniana* and *P. thunbergii* — East Asian pines for SBJ tradition

The resin (oleo-resin exuding from bark wounds) is the primary drug material; additionally: the pitch (heated concentrated resin), the distilled turpentine oil, the rosin (residue after turpentine distillation), pine bark, pine needles, and pine tar (pyrolysed wood resin) are all distinct pharmaceutical preparations with different chemical profiles.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Dioscorides / Greco-Roman (DMM)
Book I, Chapters 68–82: Dioscorides covers pine comprehensively across multiple chapters — resin, pitch, tar, bark, cones, and needles separately. Applications: topical antiseptic and wound treatment (primary); expectorant for chest conditions; diuretic; for skin diseases and parasites; in suppositories; as fumigant; for hair loss.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine / Ibn Sīnā (CM)
Pine resin and tar — warming, drying. Applications: skin antiseptic and parasiticide; for psoriasis and chronic skin disease; expectorant; for joint pain (topical counter-irritant); dental preparations; ear conditions.

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (EP)
Conifer resin (identified as pine and/or cedar) in EP wound preparations and embalming compounds; the Egyptians imported significant quantities of conifer resin from the Levant.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)
Pine resin (*ṭarpu*) is one of the most frequently appearing substances in AT wound treatment and fumigation preparations.

### 2.5 Shennong / Bencao Gangmu (SBJ)
松脂 *Sōng Zhī* — pine resin and the related 松香 *Sōng Xiāng* (rosin) appear in the SBJ tradition for skin conditions, as a styptic (haemostatic), and in compound external preparations. Chinese pine pitch was used as a wound sealing and antiseptic agent.

### 2.6 Four Medical Tantras (FMT)
Himalayan pine species provide resin for Tibetan medicine; used in compound formulas for skin disease, wound treatment, and respiratory conditions.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Antimicrobial / Wound Antiseptic (Primary — Pinenes + Abietic Acid)

**α-Pinene + β-pinene + abietic acid**:
- Membrane disruption of bacterial and fungal cells → bactericidal and fungicidal
- α-Pinene is specifically active against *Staphylococcus aureus* (including MRSA in some studies) at concentrations achievable topically from pine resin applications
- Abietic acid: disrupts bacterial cell membrane through incorporation into the lipid bilayer; particularly active against gram-positive organisms
- The physical properties of the resin (sticky, water-repellent, air-excluding) create a barrier that supplements the chemical antimicrobial action — a combined physical and chemical wound dressing

The universal wound antiseptic application across all 6 traditions reflects the genuinely effective antimicrobial properties of pine resin — the empirical observation was pharmacologically accurate.

### 5.2 Expectorant / Bronchodilator (α-Pinene)

**α-Pinene**: The most important compound for respiratory applications:
- Bronchodilation (α₁-adrenergic stimulation, direct smooth muscle relaxation)
- Mucolytic (reduces viscosity of bronchial mucus → easier expectoration)
- Anti-inflammatory in bronchial mucosa (NF-κB inhibition)

Inhaling fresh pine air (the "forest bathing" / *shinrin-yoku* effect) delivers therapeutic α-pinene concentrations to the lung — the traditional recommendation of mountain pine air for tuberculosis patients in the 19th century had a genuine pharmacological basis.

### 5.3 Vasculoprotective / Anti-Inflammatory (Pine Bark — Pycnogenol)

**Proanthocyanidins + catechin (pine bark)**:
- Inhibit hyaluronidase and elastase → protect vascular matrix
- Antioxidant → reduce oxidative damage to endothelium
- Anti-inflammatory (NF-κB, COX inhibition)
- Nitric oxide synthesis support → vasodilation

**Pycnogenol® (French maritime pine bark extract)**: One of the most studied herbal preparations in modern medicine. Multiple RCTs showing benefit for: venous insufficiency, chronic venous oedema, dysmenorrhoea (Kohama 2004), ADHD (Trebatická 2006), oxidative stress markers, endothelial function. The anti-inflammatory/vasculoprotective mechanism provides pharmacological explanation for the ancient bark-based preparations for skin and vascular conditions.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Pine bark extract (Pycnogenol® — *Pinus maritima* bark)**:
- Chronic venous insufficiency: Multiple RCTs showing reduction in leg oedema and pain
- Dysmenorrhoea: Kohama et al. (2004, *J Reprod Med*, n=116) — significant reduction in dysmenorrhoea pain vs placebo; reduction in analgesic use
- ADHD: Trebatická et al. (2006, *Eur Child Adolesc Psychiatry*) — improvement in attention in children with ADHD
- Oxidative stress: Numerous studies confirming antioxidant effects (ORAC values, reduced 8-OHdG)

**Turpentine (oil of turpentine — distilled from *Pinus* species)**:
- Historical pharmaceutical use as a topical counterirritant and anthelmintic; removed from US pharmacopoeia due to toxicity concern at high doses
- Turpentine liniment used as a rheumatic topical — counter-irritant warming mechanism is valid

**α-Pinene (isolated)**: Research on the bronchodilator and anti-inflammatory effects; in vitro validated; animal model data; entering early clinical investigation for respiratory conditions.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The universal wound dressing**: Pine resin has been used as a wound dressing on every continent where *Pinus* species grow — from ancient Egypt (imported cedar/pine resin), to Assyria, to Greece, to China, to Tibet, to indigenous North America, to medieval Europe. This is the most geographically widespread wound dressing in human history. The mechanism (antimicrobial monoterpenes + physical barrier resin) is pharmacologically valid. The consistent independent discovery of pine resin's wound-healing properties across cultures separated by thousands of miles and thousands of years represents one of the strongest cross-traditional convergence signals in the Codex — the human recognition of pine resin's utility against wound infection was an empirical pharmacological discovery made independently wherever pines grow.

**Shinrin-yoku and the mountain cure**: The 19th-century European practice of sending tuberculosis patients to mountain sanatoria (high altitude, clean pine forest air) — which preceded antibiotic therapy by decades — produced genuine clinical benefit. While the primary mechanism was probably reduced bacterial exposure, isolation, rest, and improved nutrition, the pharmacological contribution of inhaled α-pinene (from the pine forests) to bronchial anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial effects was real. The tuberculosis sanatoria of Davos (Thomas Mann's *The Magic Mountain*), Leysin, and the Colorado Rockies were inadvertently dosing patients with pine essential oil through the environment. The ancient recommendation of mountain air for chest conditions (DMM, CM, FMT) has a direct pharmacological connection to this 19th-century practice.

**Pycnogenol and the democratisation of the bark extract**: The commercial development of Pycnogenol® (standardised French maritime pine bark extract) from the 1960s onward represents the industrial application of an ancient principle (pine bark as medicine) validated by modern RCTs. The bark was not used medicinally by Dioscorides or Ibn Sīnā for the same cardiovascular/anti-inflammatory applications that Pycnogenol targets, but the pharmacological principle (proanthocyanidins from conifer bark) was present in the ancient preparations.

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Bitter, acrid; warm. Channels: Lung (肺), Kidney (腎). Dries Dampness, expels Wind-Cold, promotes healing, antimicrobial. 松脂 is in the category of "substances that transform Phlegm and stop cough."

**Ayurvedic**: Pungent, bitter; heating. Reduces Kapha ↓↓. *Śvāsahara* (anti-asthmatic), *Kandughna* (antipruritic), *Tvagdoṣahara* (skin disease). Not a classical Ayurvedic drug but pine resin is used in Ayurvedic-influenced traditions.

**Tibetan (FMT)**: Hot, rough. Pacifies *Badkan* and *rLung*. Warming; respiratory herb.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Turpentine oil internal use**: Oil of turpentine is hepatotoxic and nephrotoxic at therapeutic doses for anthelmintic use — NOT recommended internally (safer anthelmintics exist)
- **Rosin (colophony) contact allergy**: Colophony/rosin is one of the most common contact allergens in industrial and medical settings (adhesive tapes, surgical resin, violins); patch test before topical use in sensitised individuals
- **Kidney disease**: Pine resin preparations diuretic irritant effect — avoid in nephritis/CKD
- **Pregnancy**: Uterotonic potential from abietic acid at high doses; standard aromatherapy/bark extract doses generally considered low-risk but insufficient data for definitive guidance
