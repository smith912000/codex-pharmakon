# Domestic fowl — ALEKTORIDES, "Parts of Poultry" (Dioscorides 2-52, 2-53) / chicken, hen, rooster (Clark 1995, Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *The domestic hen and cock; Osbaldeston heads 2-52 and 2-53 "Parts of Poultry" and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints "Hen" with* Gallus domesticus | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM, FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

This entry is the bird and its flesh, blood, fat and organs. Hen's eggs are separate Dioscorides chapters (2-54 *OON*, "Eggs and their Yolk", and 2-55, "White of Egg") and are not treated here.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* Book Two, "Living Creatures" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 5142-5151, Osbaldeston and Wood 2000).

- *2-52 ALEKTORIDES*, "Parts of Poultry": "Hens cut apart and applied whilst they are yet warm help the bites of snakes, but they must be changed often."
- *2-53 ENKEPHALOS ALEKTORIDOS*, also "Parts of Poultry". Brains: "given in a drink with wine to those bitten by venomous creatures, and it also stops discharges of blood from the meninx [membranes of the brain and spinal cord]." The hard, clear inner membrane of the cock's stomach, "which has to have the skin taken off when it is boiled", dried, pounded and taken in wine, "is good for the stomach". Broth: "The broth of a chicken dressed simply (without anything else) is particularly given for restraining foul fluids, and for those who have hot burning stomachs. Broth from old cocks is given for purging the body." The method: take out the intestine, put in salt instead, sew up the fowl, "boil it in twenty pints of water until they reduce to one and a half pints, all which must be given after it has stood cooling awhile in the open air." Some boil sea colewort, mercury (4-191), cnicum (4-119, 4-190) or polypody (4-188) with it. Its actions: "It draws out thick, raw, gluey (or sticky) black (fluids). It is good for long-enduring acute fevers, the asthmatic, the arthritic, and stomachs labouring with gas." Finally: "The stomach of fowls salted and dried in the shade is the best help. Three teaspoonfuls are taken as an antidote against excessive evacuations from purging medicines as it presently stops the evacuating, but it must be pounded into powder and mixed with water and given like that."
- *Fat.* In the Fats chapters poultry fat is treated with goose fat: 2-86 (line 5296) "New fat of either geese or poultry (even if it is kept long, but without salt) is good for disorders of the womb. That which is salted or has turned sour through age is an enemy to the womb", with two methods of rendering it (in a jar in a hot sun and strained, or pounded, melted with a little fine salt and strained through linen), and "This fat is good mixed with medicines that take away weariness"; 2-91 (line 5330), how poultry or goose grease is scented with spices and wine; 2-93 (line 5339), how to preserve goose, poultry or calf fat from putrefying (wash, cool, press in linen, hang in shade, wrap in paper, store cold, or keep in honey); and 2-94 (line 5342), "Fats of geese and poultry are good for women's disorders, cracks of the lips, clearing the face, and for sores of the ears."

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The Explanatory Tantra places the chicken in the meat chapter's group of domestic animals: "dzo, yak, camel, horse, donkey, bull, skom.po ... goat, sheep, dog, pig, chicken, cat, etc." (Clark 1995, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 672; Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 2917, "hen"). Its flesh: "Flesh of chicken and sparrow increases semen and is beneficial for wounds" (Clark line 678; Men-Tsee-Khang line 2931: "Chicken meat and meat from a sparrow increase semen production and heals wounds"). Its blood is the only fowl item in the thirteen animal medicines: "bya.(tsha.lu.)yi.ze.khrag: Rooster's comb blood: [has fifteen names]. It stimulates flesh growth, maintains bone resin [and cures anterior transposition of the calf muscle.]" (Clark line 1176, the brackets being commentary interpolations per Clark's note at line 82); Men-Tsee-Khang line 3544: "Blood from a rooster's comb heals muscle tissue and protects spongy bone." Chicken appears in two diet lines: among the foods for retention of sperm ("Sesame oil, milk, chicken and chhang should be taken", Men-Tsee-Khang line 2854), and as an incompatible combination: "it would also be unwholesome to partake of chicken mixed with curd" (Clark line 729). The Men-Tsee-Khang animal glossary prints "Hen" with "*Gallus domesticus*" (lines 6896-6898). The spine's locus for this tag is that glossary line.

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS) is not on this entry's tag line but has fowl flesh.** *Sutrasthana* ch.46 (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` lines 496-503, Bhishagratna): "The flesh of a domesticated cock or fowl is possessed of properties similar to those of its wild prototype with the exception that it is heavy, and proves curative in rheumatism, consumption, vomiting and chronic (Vishama-Jvara) fever." ### Active Components

The sources name none. In general: poultry flesh is muscle protein with fat; the skin and fat carry the bird's fatty acids; broth contains gelatin and amino acids from connective tissue and, if bones are used, minerals; the gizzard lining is keratin-like protein and, in the live bird, the grinding organ of the gizzard; blood is a fluid tissue of cells in plasma. No quantities are given here because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice beyond poultry as ordinary cooked food. The Greek text applies cut hens to snakebite and gives brains in wine for venomous bites, which are emergencies; it gives a salted-fowl broth for fever, asthma and arthritis, which is a food-as-medicine preparation; and it uses poultry fat for women's complaints, chapped lips and ear sores. The Tibetan text records chicken meat as a food that increases semen and helps wounds, and rooster's comb blood for flesh and bone. The Codex has no protocol that depends on these uses. The reasons are plain: a hen applied to a snakebite is no treatment and delays real care; raw poultry blood and brain carry infection; the old-cock broth is ordinary cookery; and the sources give a dose for only one item. The Codex's food lens is unchanged: cooked poultry is food, with the cautions below.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations the texts give are: Dioscorides, hens cut apart and applied warm (and changed often); brains in wine; the stomach lining dried, powdered and taken in wine; plain chicken broth; old-cock broth made with salt in the cavity and boiled down from twenty pints to one and a half; the salted, shade-dried gizzard; and the fat, rendered in the sun or over hot water or coals, strained, and stored cold, or scented (2-91) or preserved in honey (2-93). Tibetan: none in the lines read. The one traditional dose is Dioscorides's, for the salted, dried stomach of fowls: "Three teaspoonfuls are taken as an antidote against excessive evacuations from purging medicines", pounded and mixed with water (2-53). No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Raw and undercooked poultry commonly carries *Campylobacter* and *Salmonella*, and avian influenza viruses can infect people who handle sick birds; cooking to a safe internal temperature removes the main food risks. Chicken is a source of complete protein. Chicken soup is a warm fluid with salt and some protein, and a hot fluid helps hydration in a feverish illness; that is the general case for the Greek broth and not a claim about its effect on fever, asthma or arthritis, none of which is established. Raw brain and blood are infection routes. No clinical studies of the traditional uses were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** No four-quality scheme is printed in the chapters read. The broth is said to restrain foul fluids, to purge (old cocks), and to draw out "thick, raw, gluey (or sticky) black (fluids)"; the fat belongs to the general statement of 2-94 that "All fat is warming, softening and purifying" (line 5344).

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Chicken flesh is described by action only ("increases semen", "beneficial for wounds"); no taste, potency or humour is assigned to chicken specifically. The meats of the domestic animals as a group are called sweet in taste and post-digestive taste (Clark line 673), a group statement not repeated for the chicken.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied. Poultry is a therapeutic and culinary material in 2-52 and 2-53. What the text gives a cultivator is a discipline of attention in making: the broth is made from a fowl dressed "simply (without anything else)" or from an old cock, with a stated salt-in-the-cavity method and a stated reduction; the fat must be new and unsalted for the womb, and the salted or soured fat is called "an enemy to the womb"; and the stomach lining is to be peeled and dried in the shade. These are rules of preparation and freshness, not claims about the person's constitution.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Chicken is a food that "increases semen" and helps wounds in the meat chapter, and rooster's comb blood is a medicine for flesh and bone; both statements are of action. The same chapter calls peacock flesh good "for rejuvenation" (Clark line 678), a claim made for the peacock and not transferred to the chicken. No essence-extraction (*bcud len*) use of the fowl is attested in the lines read, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition; none is asserted. The incompatibility with curd is a food-combining caution, not a cultivation rule.

**Practitioner's note.** Cooked chicken and its broth are food, and both traditions treat the bird as food with a medicinal side. The Greek text's craft rules (dress it plain, use an old cock for purging, use new unsalted fat) are the transferable part; the snake-bite and brain uses are not. Nothing sourced makes the fowl a cultivation substance in either tradition, and the Tibetan "increases semen" is a statement of action, not of a place in practice.

### Contraindications

- **Infection.** Raw or undercooked poultry carries *Campylobacter* and *Salmonella*; handle and cook poultry safely, and do not use raw brain, blood or a freshly cut bird as a dressing. Do not rely on a cut hen for snakebite: a bite needs urgent medical care.
- **Avian influenza.** Avoid contact with sick or dead birds.
- **Dietary rules.** The Tibetan text itself advises against chicken with curd.
- **Ethics and diet.** The use means slaughter; unsuitable for vegetarians and vegans.
- **Dose.** Only the Greek stomach-powder dose is given; follow no other dose from these sources.
