# Camel flesh — "camels" (Bhishagratna), in the Gramya (domestic animal) group of the Sushruta meat chapter; Ushtra (the Sanskrit word in the source list)
## *The meat of the camel. Bhishagratna's English prints "camels" in a list of domestic animals; the source list `_sources/sushruta.csv` (line 234) gives the Sanskrit as "Ushtra (camel)". No binomial is printed in the lines read* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The English is Bhishagratna's translation of the *Sushruta Samhita*. The camel is named in the meat group of *Sutrasthana* ch.46, "the Chapter which treats of food and drink (Annapana-Vidhi-madhyayam)" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` lines 4-6). The chapter lists the kinds of flesh by habitat, "those which are aquatic in their habits (Jaleshaya), or frequent marshy lands (Anupa), or dwell in villages (Gramya), or are carnivorous in their habits (Kravyabhuja), or are possessed of unbifurcated hoofs (Ekashapha), or dwell on high ground (Jangala)", and says that "each succeeding kind is superior to the one immediately preceding it in the order of enumeration" (lines 385-394). The domestic (*Gramya*) kind is named third in that order, so on the chapter's own ranking it stands below the Ekashapha, carnivorous-flesh and high-ground kinds that follow it.

The camel is named in one line, inside the definition of the domestic group (lines 614-619): "Animals such as horses, mules, cows, bullocks, asses, camels, goats, sheep, and Medapuchhas (fat tailed or Turkish sheep) etc., belong to the group of domestic animals (Gramyas)." The group statement follows (lines 621-625): "The flesh of domestic animals is possessed of constructive, tonic and appetising properties, is sweet in taste and digestion. It destroys the deranged Vayu and produces the Kapham and Pittam." The lines after it give separate statements for the goat, the sheep, the Medapuchcha, beef, and the Ekashapha animals (lines 625-650; "the horse and the mule etc." are the examples given for the last). **The English prints no separate statement for the camel.** What the text says of camel flesh is therefore the group statement and nothing more; whether the group statement is meant to hold for each member alike is not said.

Two further statements in the same passage bear on habitat, not on the camel by name: the flesh of beasts or birds "dwelling remote from a village or pool tends to slightly increase the secretions of the internal organs, while the flesh of an animal living in a village or near a reservoir of water, excessively increases that secretion" (lines 653-657). The camel is a domestic animal in this chapter's list, so the village sentence may be meant to include it, but the text does not apply it to the camel in so many words. [USER: verify]

Other places where Sushruta names the camel, which I opened. None of them is camel flesh; they are listed so that the camel's appearances in the book are not lost:

- *Milk.* In the treatment of dropsy of the abdomen (*Udara*), one regimen is that the patient "should live upon the milk of a she-camel alone, foregoing the use of rice and water", combined with a course of *Pippali* (*Chikitsasthana* ch.14, lines 144-146).
- *Bones.* "The bones of the Krauncha, camel and ass", pounded with named drugs and given in wine (*Sura*) or hot water, are said to lead to the disintegration of gravel (*Sarkara*) (*Chikitsasthana* ch.7, the chapter on urinary calculus, *Asmari*, lines 98-100). The bones of "an elephant, horse, hog, ass or camel" are also burnt to ashes to make an alkaline water (*Kshara*) for *Hasti-meha*, a form of urinary disease (*Chikitsasthana* ch.11, lines 177-179).
- *Dung.* "The powdered dung of a camel, a mule, or an ass should be administered to him in food" (*Chikitsasthana* ch.11, the chapter on *Prameha*, lines 222-223).
- *Hide.* A camel or deer hide is one of the materials named for a bag into which a plastered part is put (*Chikitsasthana* ch.4, line 133).
- *As a patient.* In the snake-bite chapter the quantity of collyrium and of blood to be let out is "trebled" in a camel or buffalo, relative to the human dose (*Kalpasthana* ch.5, lines 180-182), and poisoned ground is said to cause swellings in the bodies of "men, bullocks, horses, asses, camels and elephants" (*Kalpasthana* ch.3, lines 100-102). *Ushtra-griva*, "camel's neck", is the name of one type of fistula-in-ano (*Bhagandara*; *Chikitsasthana* ch.8, lines 131-133), a disease name and not a use of the animal.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms camel meat is skeletal muscle: protein, with a variable fat content, iron and B vitamins. The camel's fat is stored mainly in the hump, so the lean meat is relatively low in fat. Camel milk and camel bone, named above, are different materials and are not described here.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records camel flesh as one of the domestic meats: constructive, tonic, appetising, sweet, subduing deranged *Vayu*, and producing *Kapham* and *Pittam*. It gives the camel no use of its own as a drug. Camel is an ordinary food in the regions where camels are kept, and needs no protocol from this Codex. The Codex gives no regimen built on camel flesh, and does not recommend the non-meat uses above (the dung, the bones, or a milk-only regimen for dropsy).

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives no preparation for camel flesh: it is named as a food, and the chapter that names it is a chapter on food and drink. The preparations that involve other camel materials are those listed above (she-camel's milk alone as a regimen, pounded bones with other drugs in wine or hot water, powdered dung in food, bone ashes for alkaline water). ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Camel meat is a source of protein and iron. Raw or undercooked meat of any animal carries bacterial and parasitic risk. Dromedary camels are the known animal reservoir of the Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV), and brucellosis can be acquired from camel products such as raw milk and from contact with infected animals. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Ayurveda (SS):** ch.46 lines 621-625 (domestic group): constructive, tonic, appetising; sweet in taste and in digestion; destroys deranged *Vayu*; produces *Kapham* and *Pittam*. The English prints no separate *virya* (potency) or *vipaka* for the camel, and no line specific to it. The group statement belongs to the group; there is no camel line to set beside it.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Camel flesh is attested in *Sutrasthana* ch.46 only as one member of the domestic group (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` line 616), whose flesh is called "constructive, tonic and appetising". "Tonic" is a property listed for a food group in a chapter on food and drink; it is not a placement in a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* chapter, and it is not read as a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* status. No passage read puts camel flesh in either of those chapters, and the English prints no claim about *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* for it. The popular *sattvic*/*rajasic*/*tamasic* food lists belong to *Samkhya*-era and later material, not to Sushruta, and are not used here.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here makes camel flesh a cultivation substance. Sushruta files it with the domestic meats, ranks that kind below the others, and gives the camel no line of its own. For a cultivator the honest use is ordinary diet where camel is an ordinary food, and nothing more. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** raw, undercooked or unpasteurised camel products carry bacterial and parasitic risk, including brucellosis; dromedary camels are the animal reservoir of MERS-CoV, so contact with sick animals and raw products is a risk.
- **Allergy:** some people react to red meat and to other mammalian meat proteins.
- **Diet:** people with cardiovascular disease or on a lipid-lowering regimen should treat camel meat as any dietary fat and protein source.
- **Non-meat uses:** the dung, bone-ash and milk-only-regimen uses recorded by Sushruta are not recommended; a milk-only regimen for dropsy in particular is a historical record, not advice.
- **Ethics and law:** domestic camels are farmed in several regions; the wild Bactrian camel is a separate, threatened wild species, and its meat and parts should not be taken. Some communities have food rules about camel meat.
- **Pregnancy:** ordinary food hygiene applies.
