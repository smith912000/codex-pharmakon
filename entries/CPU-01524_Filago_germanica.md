# Filago germanica — Gnaphalion (Dioscorides 3-132)

## *Filago germanica [Linnaeus], printed within the translators' SUGGESTED line as the Linnaean form of "Cudweed"; the same line adds, as "other usage", a second plant group, Gnaphalium citrinum, Gnaphalium stoechas and Helichrysum stoechas ("Cassidony, Gold Flower, Golden Tufts")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed under the chapter heading, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line has two parts. The first gives pre-Linnaean forms (*Gnaphalium* [Fuchs], *Gnaphalium vulgare majus* [Bauhin], *Gnaphalium germanicum*), then *Filago germanica* [Linnaeus] and the English name "Cudweed". The second, marked "[other usage]", gives *Gnaphalium citrinum*, *Gnaphalium stoechas* and *Helichrysum stoechas*, with the English names "Cassidony, Gold Flower, Golden Tufts". The second set names a yellow-flowered plant of a different genus, and the translators do not say which of the two Dioscorides meant. The row takes *Filago germanica* from the first part, and the spine carries a separate row for *Gnaphalium vulgare* ("Gnaphalion", Dioscorides) that rests on Bauhin's form from the same line. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *3-132. GNAPHALION* at line 7708, SUGGESTED lines at 7709 to 7711, text at line 7712. The chapter stands in Book Three, "Roots", between 3-131 *GERANION* and 3-133 *TUPHE* (reedmace). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 538.

The whole chapter reads: "Some use gnaphalium leaves instead of flocks [scraps of wool] because they are white and soft. The leaves (given to drink with hard wine) are good for dysentery. It is also called hires, mertryx, anaxeton or anaphalis, the Egyptians call it semeon, the Gauls, gelasonen, the Romans, centunculus, some tucularis, and some, albinus."

So the chapter has no description of the plant at all: no habitat, no stalk, flower or root, no season. It has one craft use, the leaves used in place of wool flocks because they are white and soft, and one medical use, the leaves drunk with hard wine for dysentery, followed by a list of names. The only features of the plant it prints are that the leaves are white and soft. The translators' English name, "Cudweed", and the whole identification therefore rest on the translators' reading of the Greek name and on the later authorities they cite, and not on a description in the chapter. *Other chapters with cudweed names.* The translators' suggestions for two other chapters also use *Gnaphalium* names: *4-57. ELICHRUSON* (line 8495, *Gnaphalium arenarium*, "Helichrysum, Cudweed") and *4-131. LEONTOPODION* (SUGGESTED line 9186, "Gnaphalium leontopodium"). Neither is read into this entry, since each is a separate chapter with its own text. A third chapter prints *Filago lagopus* on a SUGGESTED line (line 8162, a chapter on *Lagopus*, the hare's foot). It is likewise separate. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

No analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. Cudweeds are small plants of the daisy family (Asteraceae) that are covered in fine white down, which is consistent with the chapter's remark that the leaves are "white and soft", but the identity is uncertain and nothing further is stated here about the composition. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records the leaves as a wool substitute and as a drink for dysentery. The Codex gives no protocol. Dysentery is a serious illness that can cause dangerous loss of fluid and needs medical care, and wine is not a treatment for it.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation printed is "The leaves (given to drink with hard wine)". No quantity, frequency or duration is given, and the chapter does not say whether the leaves are fresh or dried, whole or crushed. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality and no taste. The only qualities printed are of the leaves' appearance and feel, "white and soft".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. This chapter gives very little even toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition. The one observation is sensory: the leaves are white and soft, so that people use them in place of wool flocks. The chapter gives no habitat, no season of gathering and no test. It is not a cultivation status, and the identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The text offers nothing more than a texture, a craft use and a drink for dysentery.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Dysentery needs medical care and must not be left to a home preparation. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, and the chapter gives no description to check a plant against, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
