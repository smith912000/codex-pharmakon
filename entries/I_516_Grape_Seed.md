## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary binomial:** *Vitis vinifera* L. (1753) — European grapevine (common grape)

**This entry covers:** *Vitis vinifera* seed and seed extract; leaf extract is covered separately under I-517 (*Vitis vinifera* leaf).

**Synonyms & historical names:**
- *Vitis vinifera* subsp. *sylvestris* (C.C. Gmel.) Hegi (wild ancestor)
- Historical: wine grape, European grape, common grape

**Common names (grape):**
- English: Common grape vine, wine grape, European grape
- German: *Weinrebe*, *Weinstock*
- French: *Vigne*, *Raisin*
- Italian: *Vite*, *Uva*
- Spanish: *Vid*, *Uva*
- Arabic: *Inab* (grape); *Karm* (vine)
- Chinese: 葡萄 (*pú táo*) — grape; 葡萄籽 (*pú táo zǐ*) — grape seed; 葡萄籽提取物 (*grapeseed extract*)
- Hebrew: *Gefen* (vine); *Anav* (grape)
- Sanskrit: *Drākṣā* — grape; major Ayurvedic drug (*Drākṣāvaleha* = grape electuary)

**Etymology:** *Vitis* = Latin for the vine (grapevine specifically); *vinifera* = Latin *vinum* (wine) + *ferre* (to bear) = "wine-bearing." The grape is arguably the most culturally and economically significant fruit plant in the Old World; domesticated ~6,000–8,000 years ago in the Southern Caucasus (modern Georgia, Armenia).

**Botanical authentication — the grapevine:**
- Vigorous climbing deciduous vine (woody liana); tendrils opposite leaves; leaves palmate-lobed (3–7 lobes, dentate); small green flowers in compound raceme (June); berries in clusters (bunches); fruit: spherical to ovoid, green/yellow/red/purple/black (variety-dependent); skin thin; seeds (1–4 per berry) hard, pear-shaped, brown-tan
- **Seed authentication:** pharmaceutical grape seed extract is from *V. vinifera* seeds, typically a byproduct of the wine and juice industry; characterize by HPLC proanthocyanidin fingerprint; standardize to ≥95% total OPC content (oligomeric proanthocyanidins); procyanidin B₁ and B₂ as primary dimer markers
- Wine industry produces >4 million metric tons of grape pomace (skin + seed) annually — seeds separated and extracted for grape seed oil (cosmetic/culinary) and grape seed extract (pharmaceutical); sustainable byproduct utilization

**Convergence note:** With 7/9 confirmed traditions, grape (*Vitis vinifera* in its broadest sense) is among the highest-convergence plants in Volume I. The grape's position as the single most important fruit plant in Old World civilization — from the Fertile Crescent to China, from Egypt to India — makes it pharmacologically familiar across virtually every tradition that had access to it. The specific seed extract pharmacology (OPC-standardized, high-dose proanthocyanidin) is a modern pharmaceutical development; traditional uses documented seeds primarily for their astringent properties.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

Grape is to Old World pharmacology what maize is to New World nutrition — the foundational agricultural product around which entire civilizations organized. The pharmacological use of grape (fruit, leaves, seeds, wine) spans Egyptian, Mesopotamian, Greek, Roman, Indian, Chinese, and Islamic medical traditions with remarkable consistency: the fruit as nutritive tonic, the leaves as astringent and anti-inflammatory, the wine as vehicle and therapeutic agent, the seed as concentrated astringent.

The modern grape seed extract (GSE) pharmacology focuses on the seed's exceptional proanthocyanidin concentration — typically 95%+ OPC in standardized extracts, making GSE the highest OPC-concentrated pharmaceutical botanical product. These type-B proanthocyanidins (primarily procyanidin B₁, B₂ dimers + oligomers) provide:
- Vascular endothelial protection (collagen cross-link stabilization, capillary permeability reduction)
- LDL oxidation prevention
- Anti-inflammatory NF-κB inhibition
- Skin collagen preservation

The "French Paradox" observation (Renaud and de Lorgeril, 1992, *Lancet*) — lower cardiovascular disease rates in populations consuming red wine despite high saturated fat diets — drove the wine polyphenol hypothesis that subsequently positioned grape-derived OPCs as cardiovascular protectants. Resveratrol initially received the most attention (Baur 2006, *Nature*) but subsequent clinical translation has been disappointing; the proanthocyanidin fraction of GSE maintains a more solid mechanistic and clinical evidence base.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Type-B oligomeric proanthocyanidins (primary — seed specific):**
- **Procyanidin B₁** (epicatechin-(4β→8)-catechin): 1–5% of total OPC in seed
- **Procyanidin B₂** (epicatechin-(4β→8)-epicatechin): 2–8% of total OPC; primary dimer
- **Procyanidin B₃, B₄, B₅**: minor dimers
- **Procyanidin C₁** (trimer): 1–3%
- **Higher oligomers** (tetramers to dodecamers): 30–60% of total OPC; primary by mass
- **Total OPC content in standardized GSE:** 95% by OPC assay (Bate-Smith; protein precipitation method) or by HPLC
- GSE contains exclusively type-B linked proanthocyanidins; CONTRAST with cranberry (type-A); distinction pharmacologically important (different receptor binding profiles; different anti-adhesion activity)

**Monomeric flavan-3-ols:**
- **Catechin**: 5–10% DW in fresh seeds; 0.1–0.5% in standardized extract (partial polymerization occurs)
- **Epicatechin**: 3–8% DW in fresh seeds; predominant monomer
- **Gallocatechin, epigallocatechin**: minor; galloyl ester variants present

**Gallic acid:**
- **Gallic acid**: 0.5–2% DW in seeds; free form; antioxidant; antimicrobial

**Stilbenes (skin and seed):**
- **Resveratrol** (trans-3,5,4'-trihydroxystilbene): 0.001–0.01% DW in seeds; primarily in skin; much higher in red wine (fermentation concentrates); SIRT1 activator; anti-aging agent in vitro; extensively studied but clinical translation limited; at concentrations in GSE = pharmacologically modest contribution
- **Pterostilbene** (3,5-dimethoxy-4'-hydroxystilbene): trace; more bioavailable methylated analog of resveratrol

**Phenolic acids:**
- Caffeic acid, caftaric acid, coutaric acid, fertaric acid: 1–3% DW in skin/seed; antioxidant

**Grape seed oil (expressed — separate from extract):**
- Linoleic acid (C18:2 omega-6): 58–78% of oil fatty acids
- Oleic acid (C18:1 omega-9): 12–28%
- Palmitic acid: 5–10%
- Tocopherols (Vitamin E): 100–350 mg/kg oil
- Cosmetic grade oil: moisturizing, non-comedogenic; emollient

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Collagen cross-link stabilization and vascular wall protection:**
Type-B proanthocyanidins (B₁, B₂, oligomers) bind collagen triple-helix structures via hydrogen bonding with proline residues, stabilizing cross-links and inhibiting collagenase, elastase, hyaluronidase (extracellular matrix-degrading enzymes). This mechanism:
- Increases vascular wall tensile strength (relevant to CVI, varicose veins, capillary fragility)
- Reduces transcapillary leakage (reduced edema formation)
- Protects dermal collagen (anti-aging skin application)
- Inhibits platelet-collagen interaction at sub-pharmacological concentrations (anti-thrombotic contribution)

**LDL oxidation prevention:**
OPCs intercalate into LDL particle lipid bilayer → prevent Cu²⁺-induced oxidation → reduce formation of oxidized LDL (the atherogenic form that stimulates foam cell formation). IC50 for LDL oxidation: 0.01–0.1 μg/mL OPC (extremely potent; catechin and procyanidin fractions both active). Grape seed extract is among the most potent dietary LDL antioxidants characterized.

**Endothelial NO enhancement:**
OPCs activate eNOS via Akt phosphorylation → increased NO production → vasodilation; reduced endothelial inflammatory activation (ICAM-1, VCAM-1 downregulation); improved microvascular perfusion.

**NF-κB and COX-2 inhibition:**
Procyanidin B₂ and gallic acid: direct NF-κB pathway inhibition; reduced COX-2 mRNA expression; reduced IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, PGE₂ production. Anti-inflammatory mechanism relevant to CVI inflammation, diabetic microvascular complications, and ocular diseases.

**Platelet aggregation inhibition:**
Procyanidins inhibit TXA₂ production (COX-1 pathway); ADP-mediated aggregation reduction; this mechanism explains the additive anticoagulant interaction with warfarin/aspirin and represents an antiplatelet benefit for cardiovascular prevention at therapeutic doses.

**Antioxidant (broad):**
Total ORAC: extraordinarily high for GSE — among the highest antioxidant capacity density of any pharmaceutical botanical, reflecting 95% OPC concentration. Specific singlet oxygen quenching (relevant to UV skin damage), superoxide anion scavenging, hydroxyl radical quenching.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Chronic venous insufficiency (CVI) — Level A:**
1. **Henriet JP 1993** (*Phlebologie* 46:313-325) — n=92; GSE vs placebo; significant improvement in CVI symptoms (heaviness, pruritus, edema) at 4 weeks; Level A

2. **Dartenuc JY et al. 1980** (*Bordeaux Medical* 13:903-907): GSE significantly improved capillary fragility parameters vs placebo; Level A

3. **Multiple French and Italian RCTs** (1975–2000): consistent positive outcomes for CVI; methodology variable by modern standards but consistent pattern; Level A aggregate

4. **Pycnogenol (maritime pine bark OPC — equivalent OPC class, different source):** Multiple RCTs (Rohdewald 2002, Cesarone 2006); CVI symptom improvement, edema reduction; Pycnogenol is a chemically equivalent OPC preparation to GSE (B-type proanthocyanidins from pine bark vs grape seed); pharmacological equivalence established; Pycnogenol has more modern RCT database.

**Blood pressure — Level A:**
- **Sano A et al. 2007** (*Biological and Pharmaceutical Bulletin* 30:743-747): n=61, prehypertension; GSE (300 mg/day) vs placebo; significant reduction in systolic BP (−12 mmHg vs −7 mmHg placebo); Level A
- **Sivaprakasapillai B et al. 2009** (*Metabolism* 58:1743-1746): n=27, MetS patients; GSE 600 mg/day; diastolic BP significantly reduced; Level A

**Cholesterol/LDL — Level B:**
- Multiple small trials; GSE significantly reduces oxidized LDL; modest reduction in LDL-C (~5%); consistent with in vitro LDL oxidation prevention mechanism

**Diabetic retinopathy — Level B:**
- Fetterman JW et al. 2009 (*Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine*): systematic review; GSE/Pycnogenol associated with improved retinal microvascular parameters in diabetic retinopathy; mechanism: capillary pore sealing + anti-inflammatory + NO enhancement

**Cognitive function (emerging) — Level B:**
- Antioxidant protection of neural tissue; multiple animal studies; emerging human data (Krikorian R et al. 2010); insufficient Level A evidence

**Commission E:** No specific GSE monograph; *Vitis vinifera* leaf has separate Commission E monograph (I-517).

**EMA traditional use:** Red vine leaf extract: CVI (separate monograph). GSE: used under NHP regulations in various countries; no harmonized EMA monograph specifically for GSE.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Hippocrates' grape seed poultice:**
Dioscorides specifically documented grape seed and expressed seed oil for wound healing — the astringent protein-precipitating effect of grape seed tannins (OPCs) provides the same wound-contraction mechanism as other tannin-rich plants. Egyptian papyri include grape (wine + grape paste) in wound preparations — making the grape-wound healing connection the oldest documented use in the Codex convergence table (Ebers Papyrus c. 1550 BCE).

**The "French Paradox" and the OPC hypothesis:**
Serge Renaud and Michel de Lorgeril's 1992 *Lancet* paper "Wine, alcohol, platelets, and the French Paradox" triggered the most-cited dietary epidemiology discussion of the 20th century. The observation: French populations consume more saturated fat than Americans but have lower coronary heart disease rates. Proposed explanation: red wine polyphenols (then attributed largely to resveratrol; subsequently reattributed significantly to OPCs). The paper generated a global red wine consumption increase ("doctors say wine is good for you") and the subsequent pharmaceutical isolation and standardization of OPCs from grape seed as "Pycnogenol" (from maritime pine bark, equivalent chemistry) and standardized GSE.

Whatever the complex epidemiology of the French Paradox (confounded by diet quality, activity, stress patterns, alcohol timing), the OPC hypothesis produced the research program that validated the cardiovascular mechanisms now established for GSE. The paradox was pharmacologically productive even if etiologically oversimplified.

**Jack Masquelier and the OPC discovery:**
French researcher Jack Masquelier (1922–2009) at the University of Bordeaux is credited with isolating the first oligomeric proanthocyanidins from grape seed in 1947 and characterizing their pharmacological activity — beginning the OPC/Pycnogenol research lineage. Masquelier coined "OPC" (oligomers procyanidoliques), developed the first commercial proanthocyanidin preparations from grape seed, and eventually from maritime pine bark (Pycnogenol). His Bordeaux laboratory effectively created the entire pharmaceutical OPC industry from a byproduct of the regional wine industry — a convergence of regional agricultural economy and pharmaceutical science.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Standard doses (evidence-based):**
- **GSE standardized (≥95% OPC):** 100–300 mg/day for general antioxidant/vascular support; 300–600 mg/day for blood pressure reduction and CVI; doses used in RCTs
- **Pycnogenol (pine bark OPC; pharmacologically equivalent):** 100–200 mg/day; equivalent dosing; more modern RCT base
- **Duration:** 8–12 weeks minimum before assessing cardiovascular response; long-term use supported by safety profile

**Preparations:**
- Standardized extract (dry extract, ≥95% OPC by DMAC assay): most common pharmaceutical form; capsule/tablet
- Specify ≥95% OPC content on label; total polyphenol content ≠ OPC content; HPLC procyanidin profile available from quality suppliers

**CONTRAINDICATIONS / PRECAUTIONS:**
- **Anticoagulant therapy (warfarin, heparin, NOACs, aspirin):** additive antiplatelet + anticoagulant effects; **monitor INR** in warfarin patients; case reports of elevated bleeding risk; the most clinically significant interaction for GSE
- Pregnancy: insufficient clinical safety data; avoid pharmacological doses (dietary grape consumption = acceptable)
- Major surgery: discontinue 2 weeks pre-operatively (antiplatelet effect)

**Drug interactions:**
- **Warfarin:** elevated INR risk; monitor; dose-adjust if needed
- **Aspirin/NSAIDs:** additive antiplatelet; monitor for bleeding at combined therapeutic doses
- **Chemotherapy (taxanes, cyclophosphamide):** quercetin fraction may interact with drug transporters; consult oncologist before combining with active chemotherapy
- **Iron supplements:** OPC chelation of iron → separate by 2 hours (reduces iron absorption if taken simultaneously)
- **Metformin:** quercetin may inhibit OCT1/OCT2 transporters; theoretical interaction; monitor blood glucose

**Comparison with horse chestnut (I-507) and witch hazel (I-506) for CVI:**
Three distinct mechanisms converge on CVI treatment within the Codex:
- Horse chestnut (aescin): seals capillary pores directly; Cochrane Level A
- Witch hazel (hamamelitannin): venous endothelial toning; elastase inhibition; Level A
- Grape seed (OPC B-type): collagen cross-link stabilization; endothelial NO; Level A

All three are evidence-supported. Combination (GSE + horse chestnut + compression stockings) is used in European phytomedicine but lacks specific combination RCT evidence; additive mechanism rationale supports empirical combination.
