# Air Plant / Bryophyllum calycinum — Phullon, thelygonon, arrhenogonon (Dioscorides 3-140)

## *Bryophyllum calycinum (the Dioscorides translators' suggestion for chapter 3-140; also Bryophyllum pinnatum — "Air Plant, Floppers, Life Plant")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). **Named only by Dioscorides in this row.** The binomial is the translators' suggestion, not Dioscorides' own identification.

**Identity doubt.** The genus *Bryophyllum* is generally known as a tropical succulent group of Madagascar origin, and the chapter describes a plant that "grows on rocks" with moss-like growth and a slender root, which would be a poor match for such a plant. The suggestion is the translators', and the text does not support it. Treat the identification as unreliable. [USER: verify]

*3-140 PHULLON* (heading at line 7757). The SUGGESTED line (line 7758) reads "Bryophyllum calycinum, Bryophyllum pinnatum — Air Plant, Floppers, Life Plant", with the cross-reference "see 4-192". That chapter, 4-192 KUNOKRAMBE (line 9735), is *Mercurialis perennis* (dog's mercury) in the translators' line and has no evident bearing on this one.

The whole chapter (line 7760): "Phyllum grows on rocks. That which is called thelygonon has (as it were) moss, the leaf greener than the olive, a thin short stalk, a slender root, a white flower, and a bigger seed, rather like poppy. Arrhenogonon is similar in other things to that previously mentioned but differs in having the seed (similar to olives) coming in a cluster out of the flower. It is said that a decoction of arrhenogonon (taken as a drink) produces male offspring, but thelygonum causes females. Crateus relates this concerning these, but he seems to me to relate these things according to the report of them [not experience]. Some call this elaeophyllon, some, bryonia."

The chapter thus describes two forms, *thelygonon* and *arrhenogonon*, and records a belief that a decoction of the one produces male offspring and the other female. Dioscorides places the claim on Crateuas and distances himself: it is told "according to the report of them [not experience]". That is a recording and not an endorsement. The chapter names the plant also *elaeophyllon* and *bryonia*.

The neighbouring chapters repeat the theme, and are not read into this row. 3-139 KRATAIOGONON (heading at line 7749) reports that "drinking the seed causes a woman to bring forth a male child" after a course of "thirty grains with two cups of water for forty days". 3-141 ORCHIS (line 7761) says the bigger root eaten by men "makes their offspring males, and the lesser eaten by women makes them conceive females".

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The one use recorded is the belief that a decoction determines the sex of offspring, reported by Dioscorides on hearsay and not on his own experience. It is not a recommended practice, and no medicine is known to determine the sex of a child. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives one preparation: a decoction "taken as a drink" (line 7760). No quantity or duration is given. The quantities in the neighbouring 3-139 (thirty grains for forty days) belong to a different plant. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that no herbal drink is known to select the sex of offspring.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and the chapter assigns the plant no status. What it offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention, including the discipline of doubt: it describes two forms of a rock plant by leaf, stalk, root, flower and the arrangement of the seed, and Dioscorides himself separates what Crateuas reports from what he has experienced. The sex-determining claim is recorded and not endorsed. The identification with *Bryophyllum* is the translators' suggestion and is doubtful. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in this chapter gives a cultivator a practice. The one lesson is Dioscorides' own: a reported virtue is not a tested one. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. The plant's identity is doubtful and its safety is unknown from the sources retrieved. Many unidentified plants and decoctions are unsafe in pregnancy, and none should be taken on the strength of this chapter. The identification with the modern *Bryophyllum* must not be used to infer safety or toxicity for the plant Dioscorides meant.
