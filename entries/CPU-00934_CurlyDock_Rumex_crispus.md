# Curly Dock — Yellow Dock / Curled Dock / Yang Ti (羊蹄)

## *Rumex crispus* L. | Family: Polygonaceae

**Tradition Tags:** BM, DMM, CM
**Energetic Classification:** Cool and dry; bitter, mildly astringent; the root gently "descending" (purgative) and "blood-moving" (Galenic/humoral reading)
**Training Phase:** Elimination / Depurative

---

### Origin & Tradition

Curly dock is a cosmopolitan Eurasian weed of the buckwheat family, now naturalised worldwide. Unusually for this Codex, one root carries **two distinct and partly contradictory doctrines** — a laxative "blood-cleanser" and an "iron tonic for anaemia" — and honest synthesis requires grading them separately, because the chemistry supports one and undermines the other.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (BM — Babylonian medicine).** The cuneiform medical corpus records numerous plants used as purgatives and for skin eruptions, and a dock-type sorrel-plant plausibly sits among them; however, the identification of any specific Akkadian plant name with *Rumex crispus* is not secure. We therefore record the Mesopotamian attribution as a **tradition of dock-like purgative/skin herbs [USER: verify specific Akkadian binomial]** rather than inventing a native name. The doctrinal slot — a bitter root for the belly and the skin — is genuine even where the botanical identity is not pinned.

**Dioscorides (De Materia Medica, DMM).** The Greek tradition treats the docks (*lapathon*) as pot-herbs and mild remedies: the boiled root and leaves as gently loosening to the belly, the astringent seed as binding for dysentery, and topical applications for itch, scabies and skin eruptions. This is the origin of the enduring Western use of dock root as a laxative and skin remedy — the two indications that modern chemistry actually supports.

**Avicenna (Canon of Medicine).** The Arabic physicians continue the Greek reading: *ḥummāḍ* (sorrels/docks) as cooling, bitter, mildly aperient plants used for the belly and for hot skin conditions, and the astringent parts for fluxes. The humoral logic is cool-dry bitterness clearing "hot, thick, corrupt" humours — the doctrinal ancestor of the later "blood cleanser / alterative" language.

**Shennong / Chinese materia medica (CM).** *Yang Ti* (羊蹄, the root of *Rumex* species including *R. crispus*) is a recognised Chinese herb: bitter and cold, entering Heart, Liver and Large Intestine channels, used to cool the blood, stop bleeding, clear heat, kill parasites/relieve itch, and move the stool — for constipation, skin sores, ringworm and certain bleeding patterns. This maps closely onto the Western profile: a bitter, cold, descending root for the bowel and the skin.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The shared cross-tradition doctrine is "a bitter, cooling, downward-moving root that clears corrupt humours through the bowel and the skin." Map this onto real pharmacology and **one half maps cleanly, the other does not.**

The *laxative / skin-clearing* doctrine is chemically literal. Dock root is rich in **anthraquinones** — emodin, chrysophanol, physcion and their glycosides — the exact stimulant-laxative chemistry shared with rhubarb (*Rheum*, same family) and senna. Anthraquinone glycosides pass undigested to the colon, where bacteria cleave the sugar and liberate the active aglycones, which stimulate colonic motility and secretion. The "descending, purging" doctrine *is* anthraquinone stimulant catharsis. The "blood-cleanser for skin" reputation follows plausibly: gentle catharsis plus anti-inflammatory/antimicrobial polyphenols is a reasonable substrate for the traditional skin use, even if "cleansing the blood" is pre-modern metaphor rather than mechanism.

The *iron-tonic / blood-builder for anaemia* doctrine is where the Codex must **deflate**. The reputation almost certainly arose by the doctrine of signatures — the root's yellow-to-rusty colour and the "blood-moving/blood-cooling" language were read as iron and blood-building — reinforced by the genuine bitter-tonic effect of stimulating appetite and digestion. But curly dock root is **not** a rich dietary iron source, and its high oxalate content would in fact *bind* iron and inhibit its absorption. There is no good evidence that dock root corrects iron-deficiency anaemia by supplying iron. The bitter-tonic and mild-laxative effects are real; the "iron for the blood" claim is a signature-driven overreach and should be presented as folklore, not pharmacology.

The famous **dock-leaf-on-a-nettle-sting** remedy belongs in the same honest frame: relief is most plausibly cooling, moisture, distraction and the ritual of rubbing, plus possibly mild astringency — not a specific chemical antidote to nettle's formic acid/histamine. Effective as folk comfort; essentially placebo/physical as pharmacology.

### Active Components

- **Anthraquinones:** emodin, chrysophanol, physcion, and their 8-*O*-β-D-glucosides — stimulant-laxative fraction (root).
- **Naphthalene derivatives** (e.g., nepodin/musizin) and **stilbenes** — reported antimicrobial/antioxidant constituents.
- **Flavonoids and other polyphenols; tannins** — astringent and antioxidant fractions.
- **Oxalic acid / soluble oxalates** — high, especially in leaves; the principal toxicological concern.
- **Comparatively little bioavailable iron** — contrary to the folk claim.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Curly dock root sits in the Kenetica **Elimination / Depurative** phase as a *gentle stimulant laxative and bitter alterative* — a milder, more food-adjacent alternative to senna or cascara for sluggish bowel and for supporting the traditional "skin through the gut" approach to eruptive skin conditions. It is used as a low-dose bitter tonic to stimulate appetite and digestive secretion, and topically/internally within skin protocols. It is explicitly **not** deployed as an anti-anaemia agent in Kenetica; that traditional indication is retired on evidential grounds. Dose restraint is central: anthraquinone laxatives are for short-term, not habitual, use.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the phytochemistry is well characterised and supports the laxative/antimicrobial/antioxidant profile, while direct human clinical evidence is thin.

- A comprehensive review documents ~224 identified constituents of *R. crispus* — anthraquinones, naphthalenes, flavonoids, stilbenes and others — and traditional use as astringent, laxative, diuretic, mild tonic and "blood cleanser," with demonstrated antimicrobial, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities in preclinical models; it also flags toxicity, including allergenic potential and reported symptomatic thrombocytopenia (Qian et al., 2024, *Int Immunopharmacol*) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intimp.2024.113569).
- Analytical work confirms the laxative anthraquinones in *R. crispus* root as emodin, chrysophanol and physcion (present largely as 8-*O*-glucosides that hydrolyse to the active aglycones), the same stimulant-laxative class as rhubarb and senna (Wianowska, 2014, *Anal Bioanal Chem*) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00216-014-7744-5).
- Comparative work on the related *Rumex nervosus* corroborates the genus's antioxidant, antidiabetic and antimicrobial activity across solvent extracts, consistent with the polyphenol-rich profile of the genus (Islam et al., 2025, *Curr Pharm Des*) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2174/0113816128360594250725110421).

**Evidence grade:** For the **laxative** action — WELL-SUPPORTED mechanistically (anthraquinone chemistry directly analogous to senna/rhubarb), though lacking dedicated *R. crispus* human RCTs. For **antimicrobial/antioxidant/skin** uses — PRELIMINARY (preclinical, in-vitro and animal). For the **iron-tonic/anti-anaemia** claim — UNSUPPORTED / NEGATIVE: no evidence the root supplies usable iron, and its oxalate content would impair iron absorption; graded as folklore. For the **nettle-sting leaf remedy** — no chemical antidote demonstrated; effect is best explained as cooling/physical/placebo.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** *Yang Ti* (羊蹄) — bitter, cold; Heart/Liver/Large Intestine channels; cools blood, stops bleeding, clears heat, kills parasites, relieves itch, moves stool.
- **Ayurveda:** *Rumex crispus* not classically attested in the Sanskrit materia medica; docks broadly read as *tikta/kashaya rasa* (bitter/astringent), *sheeta virya* (cooling) by analogy only. Mark: **not classically attested.**
- **Galenic / Unani:** docks/sorrels (*ḥummāḍ*, *lapathon*) — cool and dry, bitter, mildly aperient and astringent; clears hot corrupt humours.

### Contraindications

- **⚠️ OXALATE — the leaf hazard.** *Rumex crispus* leaves are high in soluble oxalates. Large or repeated ingestion risks nausea, and in extreme cases oxalate nephropathy and hypocalcaemia; livestock poisonings are documented. Avoid in oxalate-type kidney stones, renal impairment, and gout; keep culinary use of leaves small and always cooked.
- **⚠️ Anthraquinone laxative abuse.** As with senna/cascara, chronic stimulant-laxative use of the root risks dependence, electrolyte disturbance (hypokalaemia) and worsened bowel function. Short-term use only; not for habitual constipation management.
- Avoid in pregnancy and lactation (stimulant-laxative/anthraquinone caution) — [USER: verify]; avoid in intestinal obstruction or undiagnosed abdominal pain.
- Reported allergenic reactions and a case-associated symptomatic thrombocytopenia (see Qian et al. review) warrant caution in sensitive individuals.
- **Not a treatment for iron-deficiency anaemia** — do not substitute dock for proper diagnosis and iron repletion.
