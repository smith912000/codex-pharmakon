### Botanical Identity
Deciduous shrub 2–4m; distinctive maple-like three-lobed leaves; lace-cap flower clusters (outer ring of sterile flowers surrounding a centre of fertile flowers — the same structure as lacecap hydrangeas); brilliant red berries in autumn clusters; native to Europe, North Africa, and Central Asia; common in hedgerows and woodland margins. The bark — specifically the dried stem bark and root bark — is the drug material. The berries are mildly toxic raw (contain viburnin/viopudial); the bark is the medicinal part. *V. opulus* (European) and *V. prunifolium* (North American Black Haw) are pharmacologically interchangeable for most applications — both contain the scopoletin-viopudial antispasmodic complex.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 De Materia Medica (DMM) — European Tradition

European herbal tradition (16th century onward):
- *Viburnum* bark for "cramps of the uterus" — the most explicit traditional application
- Culpeper (1653): "Take the bark of the Cramp-Bark — it relieves the cramps, and the convulsions, and the hardness of the sinews" — the name and application are inseparable
- Eclectic physicians (19th century America): *V. opulus* and *V. prunifolium* were primary antispasmodics for uterine conditions — threatened miscarriage, dysmenorrhoea, false labour pains

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine (CM) / Islamic Peripheral

Bitter bark preparations for abdominal spasm and uterine cramps appear in Ottoman Islamic medical literature, consistent with cramp bark's application.

### 2.3 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)

Antispasmodic bark preparations in Babylonian texts for "pain of the womb" and intestinal colic.

### 2.4 Badianus / North American Indigenous (BM)

*V. prunifolium* (Black Haw) — several Algonquian nations used the bark specifically for uterine cramps and threatened miscarriage. The Native American antispasmodic application of Black Haw was so consistent that it entered American Eclectic medicine directly from indigenous observation. This cross-cultural pharmacological convergence (European *V. opulus* + North American *V. prunifolium* for identical indications) is one of the strongest convergence signals in the antispasmodic category.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Uterine / Smooth Muscle Antispasmodic (Primary)

**Viopudial + scopoletin**: The primary mechanism is calcium channel antagonism in smooth muscle:
- **Viopudial**: Inhibits voltage-gated L-type calcium channels in uterine and vascular smooth muscle → prevents calcium influx required for muscle contraction → muscle relaxation without paralysis
- **Scopoletin**: Inhibits phosphodiesterase → elevates cAMP → activates protein kinase A → phosphorylates and inactivates myosin light chain kinase → smooth muscle relaxation

The calcium channel + cAMP double mechanism explains why cramp bark is effective for a range of smooth muscle spasms: uterine (dysmenorrhoea, threatened miscarriage), intestinal (IBS spasm, colic), vascular (vasospasm), and bronchial (bronchospasm).

### 5.2 Analgesic / Anti-Inflammatory

**Chlorogenic acid + caffeic acid + amentoflavone**: 
- COX-2 inhibition → reduces prostaglandin E2 → reduces uterine cramping pain (prostaglandin-driven smooth muscle spasm is the primary mechanism of dysmenorrhoea)
- Amentoflavone: particularly potent COX-2 inhibitor (IC50 comparable to pharmaceutical NSAIDs)
- Combined: antispasmodic (stops the spasm) + analgesic (reduces pain signalling)

### 5.3 Anxiolytic (Amentoflavone)

**Amentoflavone**: Binds the benzodiazepine site of GABA-A receptor (similar to chrysin, apigenin) → mild anxiolytic + antidepressant. The traditional application for nervous tension accompanying menstrual cramps has a pharmacological basis in the biflavonoid component.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Dysmenorrhoea**: Limited formal RCT data specific to cramp bark. Systematic evidence base is primarily:
- Eclectic physician case series (19th century)
- European Commission E monograph supporting use for menstrual cramps and muscle spasms
- Pharmacological mechanistic studies (viopudial/scopoletin smooth muscle mechanism well-characterised)
- Practitioner clinical outcomes (herbalists in US/UK/Germany consistently report rapid relief of uterine spasm)

**Threatened miscarriage**: Historical Eclectic physicians' records (Felter, Lloyd — *King's American Dispensatory*, 1898) describe cramp bark's use for threatened miscarriage with substantial apparent success. These are clinical records not RCTs — but the consistency and specificity of the application across practitioners is pharmacologically coherent with the antispasmodic uterine mechanism.

**IBS**: No formal RCTs. Commission E approval for "muscle spasms" based on traditional evidence and mechanistic plausibility.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Ukraine's national bark**: *Viburnum opulus* (*kalyna* in Ukrainian) is the national symbol of Ukraine — featured in folk songs, embroidery patterns (*vyshyvanka*), art, and ceremony. The most famous Ukrainian folk song (*Chervona Kalyna*, "Red Viburnum") dates to the First World War and was revived as a national anthem of resistance. The red berries of the Guelder Rose are the visual symbol. The bark, beneath the surface of the iconic red-berried symbol, has been a folk medicine for uterine cramps and female reproductive conditions across Eastern Europe for centuries. The national symbol and the uterine medicine occupy the same plant — the visible (berry) and the pharmacological (bark) are inseparable in Ukrainian traditional culture. After Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine, the song and its symbol underwent a cultural renaissance — the medicinal plant became a symbol of survival.

**The antispasmodic convergence**: *Viburnum opulus* in European tradition and *Viburnum prunifolium* (Black Haw) in North American indigenous tradition arrived at identical pharmacological applications — uterine antispasmodic for dysmenorrhoea and threatened miscarriage — across an ocean and without cultural contact. Two related but distinct species, two distinct civilisations, same indication. This is one of the clearest examples in this Codex of pharmacological convergence confirming a real biological effect: when two unrelated traditions discover the same medicine for the same condition in related plant species, the probability that the plant has real pharmacological activity for that condition approaches certainty.

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM (cross-reference)**: Bitter, astringent; slightly warm. Channels: Liver (肝), Uterus (胞宮). Moves Blood stasis, relieves uterine spasm, relaxes sinews.

**Ayurvedic (cross-reference)**: Bitter, astringent; slightly warm. Reduces Vata ↓↓ (antispasmodic), reduces Pitta ↓ (anti-inflammatory). *Śūlaghna* (pain-relieving), *Artavajanana* (menstrual). Not a classical Ayurvedic plant.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Pregnancy (established)**: The same antispasmodic mechanism used for threatened miscarriage (relaxing excessive uterine contractions) could theoretically relax necessary uterine contractions at term — clinical ambiguity. Historical Eclectic use was specifically for threatened miscarriage (excessive cramping); use under experienced practitioner supervision only in pregnancy
- **Anticoagulants**: Scopoletin (coumarin derivative) + chlorogenic acid — possible potentiation of warfarin; monitor
- **Salicylate sensitivity**: Minor salicin content — avoid in aspirin-sensitive patients
- **Raw berries**: Mildly toxic — nausea, vomiting; only bark preparations are medicinal
- Generally safe and well-tolerated at standard doses in non-pregnant adults
