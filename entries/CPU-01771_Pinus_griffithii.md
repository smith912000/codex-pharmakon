# Pinus griffithii — shel.ta, Shelta; "Pine Resin" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Pinus griffithii, the only binomial Clark 1995 prints for shel.ta, after the English name "Pine Resin" (so the drug is a resin and the binomial names the tree it is tied to); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text prints no griffithii and sets the genus-level "Pinus sp." against the name "Shelta" by column position* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and under this binomial on Clark's edition alone.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Ebers, Assyrian, Sushruta and Shennong text on disk for *Pinus* found other species and other drugs: Dioscorides prints *Pinus* names only in translators' lines for the pine chapters (for example lines 4195 to 4263), Bhishagratna's Sushruta volume 3 prints "Pinus Longifolia" in two lists of plant names (lines 32847 and 32991), and a search of the Shennong text (Yang 1998) for "Pinus" found none (the hits for "pine" are *Pinellia* and unrelated words). None is read as *shel.ta*. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. **Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 76) the row is keyed by the Tibetan name *shel.ta*, with the identification "Pine Resin; Pinus griffithii" and the locus "Clark 1995 (line 926)". The binomial is the **only** one Clark prints on the line, so it is neither the first nor a later one of several. What the Tibetan name labels is a resin ("Pine Resin"), and the binomial is Clark's identification of the tree it comes from; the Codex files the entry under the tree because the spine row is a plant row.

**Identity doubt.** Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" and (line 68) that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect". Here one species is given, and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary gives only a genus (below). The two editions agree on a pine and the Codex does not go further. The Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note (line 686) says that the botanical terms in their text "are based on the information procured from the Materia Medica Department of this institute"; it names no flora or earlier work. **Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 926).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), in the Specific Section. The file prints "(4) Tree Medicines" at line 871, whose text says that "Resins [and secretions] are derived from grasses, trees and animals" (line 872), and "(5) Resins" at line 879. The entry stands after the heading at line 879, among entries that include the salts (lines 927 to 935), and before "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The file prints no "(6)" heading. The entry reads:

"shel.ta: Pine Resin: Pinus griffithii: [has three names: thang.chu, etc.] It retains bone resin and bone lymph."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The count of names is inside the brackets, and the action is outside: "It retains bone resin and bone lymph" is the root-text layer as Clark's marks stand. "Bone resin" and "bone lymph" are Clark's terms, left untranslated by the Codex; the same phrase "bone resin" appears in his line for another drug (*rta.lpags*, line 1015: "maintains bone resin and drains lymph accumulations"), so it is Clark's rendering of a recurrent term and not a description of the pine. Clark's name index lists *shel.ta* (line 1763) and *thang.chu* (line 1703), and his Latin index lists *Pinus griffithii* (line 1626).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (read through the name).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, line 3398, in the run of efficacies that opens at line 3380 ("The efficacy of these medicinal substances for healing disorder is as follows"), before the herbal run (line 3428): "Shelta dries up chhuser accumulated between the spongy and compact bone." "Chhuser" is a Tibetan term kept untranslated (the editors' note, line 686). The index lists "Shelta 203" (line 7491). The glossary's "S" block prints the phonetic name "Shelta" (line 5718) and the transliteration "shel ta" (line 5722). Its scientific names (lines 5727 to 5765) are fifteen, as against fifteen phonetic names, and counted in order "Shelta" is the twelfth phonetic name and "Pinus sp." (line 5758) the twelfth scientific name; the English slot there is a dash (line 5761). Two neighbours check the count against Clark: "Seyab" is eleventh against *Chaenomeles speciosa* (Clark's *bse.yab*, line 908, prints *Chaenomeles tibetica* and *Chaenomeles lagenaria*) and "Shingkun" is fifteenth against *Ferula asafetida* (Clark's *shing.kun*, line 918, prints *Ferula narthex*). The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, and the count rests on order. The sentence gives no part used and no preparation. Clark's "retains bone resin and bone lymph" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "dries up chhuser ... between the spongy and compact bone" both concern bone; the Codex records that and does not set "bone lymph" and *chhuser* equal. *Other pine lines in Clark, not this drug.* Clark's line 770 lists a "powdered (pine)root (Padus asiatica)" among astringent-taste medicines, and line 1222 lists "powdered root (of, for example, old pine tree)" among anti-diarrhoeals. These are root drugs, not resin, and the Codex does not read them as *shel.ta*. The conifer drug *sgron.shing* (line 979) is a different Tibetan name and is the subject of I_1336 (*Pinus tabulaeformis*).

II_115 (Amber) names Clark's line 926 as a plant resin, not amber. No entry on *Pinus griffithii* or on *shel.ta* was found. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that the resin of pines is an oleoresin, a mixture of volatile terpenes (turpentine) and non-volatile resin acids (rosin), and that *Pinus* is the pine genus of the conifer family (Pinaceae). Which compounds the Tibetan drug holds, and from which species it was taken, is not shown by the lines read, so no statement about its composition is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a resin that "retains bone resin and bone lymph" (Clark, line 926) and "dries up chhuser accumulated between the spongy and compact bone" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3398). These are the texts' claims in their own terms, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. A bone or joint complaint needs medical care, and pine resin can irritate the skin and cause allergy, so no resin should be taken or applied on the strength of this entry.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no quantity, no frequency, no duration and no route. Neither edition says whether the resin was taken, applied or burned. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that pine resin is a terpene-rich secretion that the tree produces at wounds, and that turpentine and rosin are made from it. The Tibetan terms ("bone resin", "bone lymph", *chhuser*) are not modern diagnoses or measured outcomes, so no modern finding can be set beside them.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The materia medica classes this drug by source (a resin) and by what it does: it concerns the bone. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, lines 3900 to 3950; Clark's chapter 23 is headed "Normal health", lines 1262 to 1276), and Clark's materia medica names other drugs as essence-extraction ingredients (for example lines 853, 971 and 1144), none of them this one. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this resin. It is a bone-related resin drug in one short sentence in each edition, with no preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Pine resin and turpentine can cause skin irritation and allergic reaction, and swallowing turpentine is harmful; no preparation or dose is given, and no resin should be gathered, taken or applied on the strength of this entry. Bone and joint complaints need medical care. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
