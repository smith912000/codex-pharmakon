**Tradition Tags:** CM, SBJ, SS, FMT

**Energetic Classification:** Bitter, pungent, hot/warming (Ayurvedic/Unani reading — *ushna virya*, hot potency); kindling, drying, dispersing cold. **Warming in every Asian tradition that used it, which is the doctrinal fact this entry turns on.**

### ☠️ DEADLY — AND ENDANGERED. TWO SEPARATE REASONS TO LEAVE IT ALONE.

**Reason one: it will stop your heart.** Every part of *Taxus wallichiana* except the fleshy red aril contains **taxine alkaloids** — taxine B principally — which are cardiotoxic sodium- and calcium-channel poisons producing bradyarrhythmia, conduction block, wide-complex arrhythmia, cardiogenic shock and death. There is no domestic preparation that removes them. **Yew ingestion is a medical emergency and yew is a well-documented suicide plant.** No dose is given in this entry.

**Reason two: taking it is theft from a species that is running out.** *T. wallichiana* is **IUCN Endangered** and **CITES-listed**, driven there by the harvest of its own bark for a cancer drug. Wild collection is prohibited or restricted across its range. **This is a plant where a personal harvest is not merely dangerous to you; it is a subtraction from a shrinking global population.**

**This is the second yew in the codex. Do not read it as a repeat.**

### Origin & Tradition

**Sushruta Samhita lineage / Ayurveda (SS) — *Talispatra* (तालीसपत्र) [USER: verify — CONTESTED IDENTIFICATION]**

**This is the entry's live identification problem and it must be handled before anything else.**

***Talispatra*** is a classical Ayurvedic drug — the *patra* (leaf) of *tali* — indicated in the classical texts for **kasa (cough), shwasa (dyspnoea/asthma), jwara (fever), agnimandya (weak digestion), hikka (hiccough)** and cold-natured disorders generally. It is a *kapha–vata* drug: hot, pungent, kindling.

**The plant behind the name is disputed.** The mainstream and probably dominant identification is ***Abies webbiana*** (= *A. spectabilis*, Himalayan silver fir) — a Pinaceae, not a yew, non-toxic, and the source in most commercial Ayurvedic *Talisadi churna*. A competing identification assigns *Talispatra* to ***Taxus wallichiana***. Both trees are Himalayan conifers, both have flat needles, both are used for cough by Himalayan communities, and the vernacular naming is porous.

**The stakes of this ambiguity are not academic.** *Abies webbiana* is a safe household herb. *Taxus wallichiana* is a cardiotoxic poison. **A substitution error in either direction is a clinical event**, and adulteration pressure in the Ayurvedic supply chain makes the error live rather than hypothetical — Semwal et al. (2019) argue exactly this, that the IUCN-listing of heavily used Ayurvedic plants (naming *T. wallichiana* and *T. baccata* explicitly) is driving supply-chain adulteration such that "in coming years most of the Ayurvedic drugs will be adulterated, and will cause only side effects rather than the therapeutic effects" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joim.2019.04.008), PMID 31076374). **[USER: verify the *Talispatra* identification against a primary Ayurvedic nighantu source before this entry's SS tag is treated as attested. The safest reading is that *Talispatra* = *Abies webbiana*, and that *T. wallichiana* is a regional substitute or a separate drug entirely.]**

What *is* securely attested is that *T. wallichiana* itself has a documented South Asian medical tradition, independent of the *Talispatra* question. Juyal et al. (2014) state it plainly: the Himalayan yew "has been used by the native populations for treating **common cold, cough, fever, and pain**. Its uses are described in **Ayurveda and Unani medicine**" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.4103/2225-4110.136544), PMID 25161920). Sharma & Garg's review records traditional use for "disorders of the **digestive, respiratory, nervous and skeletal systems**" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2095-4964(15)60161-3), PMID 25797638).

**Canon of Medicine / Unani (CM) — *Zarnab* (زرنب) [USER: verify]**

The Unani tradition — which in the Indian subcontinent inherits Avicenna's *Qanun* and runs it through local materia medica — knows *T. wallichiana* as ***Zarnab***, classified as **hot and dry**, a cardiac and nervine tonic, aromatic, indicated in cold-natured complaints, and used in cough, catarrh, and as an ingredient of compound cardiac formulations. Juyal et al. confirm the Unani attestation for this species ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.4103/2225-4110.136544), PMID 25161920).

**Note the extraordinary and dangerous item in that list: a *cardiac* tonic.** Of every organ system the Unani physicians could have assigned this tree to, they chose the one it poisons. **[USER: verify *Zarnab*'s species identification and its degree assignment — *zarnab* is a name with multiple candidate referents in the Perso-Arabic literature.]**

**Four Medical Tantras / Tibetan (FMT)**

Tibetan medicine, working the *rGyud bzhi*'s rLung/mKhris/Bad-kan framework across the same Himalayan flora, uses yew within the cold-disorder and Bad-kan (phlegm) category — warming, drying, dispersing. The *T. wallichiana* range overlaps Tibetan practice territory precisely (1800–3300 m). **The specific Tibetan drug name and its indications are not securely recoverable from what was retrieved here. **Chinese Medicine / Shennong lineage (SBJ)**

*T. wallichiana* extends into southwest China, where *T. wallichiana* var. *mairei* is the dominant Chinese taxon and is now the principal cultivated source for taxane extraction. Its traditional Chinese medical footprint is **thinner than the Indian one** — the SBJ tag here is a range/tradition-contact tag rather than a claim to a canonical *Bencao* entry. **[USER: verify whether a *Bencao* attestation exists.]** What is beyond dispute is its **modern** Chinese role: the seasonal-dynamics and UV-B studies below are Chinese cultivation-optimisation research, and China is where the needle-based supply chain now largely runs.

**The Nepali record**

Kunwar et al. (2010), surveying traditional herbal medicine in far-west Nepal, list *Taxus wallichiana* — locally ***Thuner*** — among the 48 assessed medicinal plants, and place its folk use "**for tumor control**" among the nine uses found consistent with "the latest pharmacological findings, common Ayurvedic and earlier uses" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-4269-6-35), PMID 21144003).

**Read that carefully, because it is a trap.** A folk anti-tumour use for the yew, "consistent with the latest pharmacological findings", is an irresistible narrative — the villagers knew about Taxol. **It is almost certainly an artefact.** The survey was conducted 2006–2008, decades after paclitaxel became globally famous and after the bark rush had swept through Nepal. **Ethnobotanical surveys conducted downstream of a pharmaceutical gold rush cannot distinguish indigenous knowledge from feedback.** The authors' methodology explicitly compares folk uses *against* the pharmacological literature, which is a design that finds concordance. The pre-contact Himalayan indications are the ones the record supports: **cough, fever, cold, pain.** Not cancer. **This entry declines the flattering reading.**

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

**The doctrine is unanimous and it is *warming*.** Ayurveda: *ushna virya*, hot potency, kindling agni, dispersing kapha. Unani: hot and dry. Tibetan: a Bad-kan drug. Four traditions, one verdict: **this tree is a fire.** Indicated in cough, catarrh, fever, cold, weak digestion, pain — the cold-and-damp syndrome cluster.

**Now map it onto real chemistry, and watch the doctrine come apart in a very particular way.**

**The warming doctrine has no molecular correlate.** There is no thermogenic taxane. There is no capsaicin here, no gingerol, no volatile pungent driving vasodilation and flushing. The needle's essential oil exists and is aromatic, but "aromatic" is not "hot". **So what were four traditions perceiving?**

Two candidate answers, and they are not flattering:

1. **The bitter-pungent taste was read as heat by systems in which taste** (*rasa*) **predicts potency** (*virya*) **by rule.** In Ayurveda, *katu* (pungent) and *tikta* (bitter) rasa map to specified viryas by doctrine. The physician tasted the needle, applied the rule, and derived "hot" **without observing anything hot.** This is the doctrine generating its own data. The classification is a **theorem, not a measurement.**
2. **The physiological signal that *was* observed was toxicity.** Taxine produces nausea, GI upset, tachycardia and then bradycardia, dizziness, mydriasis, flushing. **A tachycardic, flushed, nauseated patient looks exactly like a patient who has been given a hot drug.** The tradition may have been reading early cardiotoxicity as therapeutic heat.

**If (2) is even partly right, it is the same failure as I_761's hellebore**, and worse: **the Unani tradition classified as a *cardiac tonic* a plant whose only reliable cardiac action is to poison the conduction system.** The tradition saw the heart. It got the sign backwards. **A drug that visibly acts on the heart is not the same as a drug that helps it**, and no tradition in this codex possessed the instrument — an ECG — that would have told them apart.

**The doctrine that was right about the wrong thing.** Every Asian tradition here selected the **leaf/needle** as the drug part. That choice was made for reasons of availability and doctrine (*Talispatra* is literally "*tali* leaf"), and it turns out to be, by pure accident, **the correct part for sustainable taxane chemistry** — needles regenerate; bark does not. The traditions used the renewable organ for two thousand years and did no harm to the species. **Twentieth-century pharmacology went for the bark and nearly finished the tree in twenty.** That is not a story about traditional wisdom being superior; the traditions took needles because needles were what a person could reach, not because they had a forestry policy. **But the outcome is the outcome, and it is the sharpest thing in this entry.**

**The aril.** The single fleshy red edible part of an otherwise deadly tree — seed-coat, sweet, taxine-free, and containing a seed that is not. Birds eat it, disperse the seed, and pass the toxic kernel intact. **This is a real signature and it points the wrong way for a human:** the tree's one attractive, palatable, safe structure is the delivery vehicle for its most concentrated poison. **Do not eat yew berries. The flesh will not hurt you and the pip may kill you**, and a child cannot make that distinction.

### Active Components

**Taxine alkaloids — the poison.** **Taxine B** is the principal cardiotoxin of the genus (Farag et al. 2017, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajem.2017.09.031), PMID 28941873). Present in all parts **except the aril flesh**; concentrated in leaves and seeds. **3,5-dimethoxyphenol** is the taxine metabolite used as a forensic marker (PMID 28941873).

**Taxanes — the drug.**
- **Paclitaxel (Taxol)** — the C-13 side-chain-bearing diterpenoid; microtubule-stabilising antimitotic. **Bark is the richest tissue: 29,162.3 µg/g dry weight** by HPTLC-densitometry (Das et al. 2023, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsomega.3c04309), PMID 37692257). **That number is the extinction driver — see below.**
- **10-Deacetylbaccatin III (10-DAB)** — **the semisynthesis precursor, and the species' redemption.** Extractable from **needles**.
- **Baccatin III**, **cephalomannine**, **10-deacetyltaxol (10-DAT)** — the taxane suite quantified across seasons by Yang et al. (2016, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules21101403), PMID 27775631).

**Biflavonoids** — **ginkgetin**, **amentoflavone**, **quercetin** (PMID 27775631). **Lignans, steroids, sterols, essential oil, polysaccharides** — Sharma & Garg's review catalogues the non-taxane fraction ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2095-4964(15)60161-3), PMID 25797638). **The traditional cough/fever indications, if they have any chemistry at all, live here — and that chemistry is uninvestigated.**

**Seasonal and environmental variation — the practical fact.** Taxoid content in needles peaks in **January (1.77 ± 0.38 mg/g)** and bottoms in **September (0.61 ± 0.08 mg/g)** — a ~3× swing; flavonoids peak in August; polysaccharides peak in September at 28.52 ± 0.57 mg/g, 3× the January low. Temperature negatively correlates with paclitaxel and positively with polysaccharide (PMID 27775631). Supplemental UV-B raises paclitaxel and 10-DAB with dose-dependent gene-expression changes (Zhong et al. 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms25126407), PMID 38928114). **A yew needle is not a fixed substance. Its composition swings threefold with the calendar** — which incidentally destroys any hope of a stable traditional dose.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**None. There is no Kenetica use for this tree.** It is retained for:

1. **The identification hazard.** The *Talispatra* / *Abies webbiana* confusion is a genuine, current, supply-chain-live route by which a person could receive a cardiotoxic conifer while expecting a benign one.
2. **The conservation case.** Endangered, CITES. This is the codex's clearest instance of **use itself being the harm**, independent of toxicity.
3. **The doctrine-inversion case study.** A cardiac tonic that poisons the heart. A warming drug with no warming molecule. A taste-derived potency assignment masquerading as an observation.
4. **The semisynthesis story.** How chemistry, not conservation policy, actually saved a species — and what that says about the "deadly plant → miracle drug" arc that the whole codex has to keep in proportion.
5. **The proportion check on I_761.** Hellebore's entry warns that low-nanomolar in-vitro cytotoxicity is what poisons look like in a dish. **Yew is the counterexample — the one that crossed.** This entry exists partly to show what the crossing actually cost.

**Paclitaxel is a real, essential, life-extending drug.** It is administered intravenously, by oncologists, at dose calculated by body surface area, with premedication against hypersensitivity, under monitoring for neutropenia and neuropathy. **It has nothing whatever to do with drinking yew tea.** The gap between the tree and the drug is not one of concentration. It is chromatography, semisynthesis, formulation, and forty years.

### Scientific Correlation

**Headline grade: STRONG for the toxicology and the taxane chemistry. NULL for every traditional indication.**

**1. The traditional indications — cough, fever, cold, pain: NULL.**
Sharma & Garg's review states the position with unusual candour: *T. wallichiana* is "**pharmacologically underexplored**" and has *been used for* antiepileptic, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, antipyretic, analgesic, immunomodulatory and antimicrobial activities ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2095-4964(15)60161-3), PMID 25797638) — **"has been used for" is an ethnographic statement, not an efficacy one, and the review does not upgrade it.** Juyal et al. likewise document the Ayurvedic/Unani cough-fever-cold-pain tradition without validating it ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.4103/2225-4110.136544), PMID 25161920). **I retrieved zero clinical trials and zero controlled studies of *T. wallichiana* leaf or bark for any traditional indication. Grade: NULL.** Two thousand years of use; no test. **The tradition's entire therapeutic claim is unexamined, and the modern literature on this tree is about something else entirely.**

**2. Taxine cardiotoxicity — STRONG, and the strongest-graded claim in this batch.**
Genus-level (*T. baccata*), and transferred to *T. wallichiana* deliberately and defensibly, because taxine is a **genus-wide** constituent:
- Buetler et al. (2023): 55 ED presentations over 8 years; GI disturbance (56%), tachycardia (27%), hypotension (20%); gastroscopic leaf extraction in 95%; all patients female, all intentional ingestions in patients with pre-existing psychiatric diagnoses; **no fatalities in this series** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/15563650.2022.2158097), PMID 36594830).
- Alarfaj & Goswami (2021): fatal case, 20-year-old, refractory arrhythmia and shock through antiarrhythmics, **digoxin immune Fab**, lipid emulsion and transvenous overdrive pacing; **plus review of 43 published yew poisoning cases**; conclude "the majority of deaths in yew berry poisonings occur due to its cardiotoxic effect" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajem.2021.05.043), PMID 34049761).
- Farag et al. (2017): survival after suicidal ingestion using **ECLS + digoxin-specific Fab**, with mass-spec confirmation that Fab fragments bind taxine — total taxine rose after each Fab dose while the free metabolite 3,5-dimethoxyphenol disappeared ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajem.2017.09.031), PMID 28941873).
- Soumagne et al. (2011): full recovery from ventricular fibrillation after yew leaf ingestion using extracorporeal life support ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajem.2010.03.017), PMID 20825910).

**Grade: STRONG.** Human, clinical, multiple independent series, mechanism (taxine B), forensic marker, and two working rescue strategies. **The honest caveat, stated: every one of these is *T. baccata*.** I retrieved **no** *T. wallichiana*-specific poisoning series. **This is one of the few places in this batch where congener transfer is legitimate** — taxine is defining for the genus and the traditions themselves treat the yews as one drug — but it should be named, not smuggled. **The clinical bottom line is unambiguous: lethal ingestions occur, and survival in the severe cases has required ECMO.**

**3. Paclitaxel content and the extinction mechanism — STRONG, species-specific, and the entry's causal spine.**
Das et al. (2023) quantified tissue-specific paclitaxel in *T. wallichiana* by validated HPTLC-densitometry: **bark yields 29,162.3 µg/g dry weight**, the highest of any tissue, with bark extracts also showing the strongest cytotoxic and mitotic effects in the *Allium cepa* genotoxicity assay. Their framing is explicit — the species "is currently experiencing **endangerment due to excessive harvesting and sluggish growth resulting from the extraction of paclitaxel**", and their stated purpose is to find tissues that permit extraction "while maintaining the species' survival" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsomega.3c04309), PMID 37692257). **Grade: STRONG.**

**This is the whole story in one number.** Bark is the richest source. Bark is also **non-renewable** — you cannot ring a tree and have it live. A slow-growing Himalayan conifer that takes decades to reach harvestable girth, holding the richest known natural concentration of a blockbuster oncology drug in the one tissue whose removal kills it. The market did the obvious thing. **IUCN Endangered and CITES-listed are the receipts.**

**4. The rescue — semisynthesis from needles. Established industrially; the primary chemistry is not in this retrieval.**
The taxoid suite is present in needles at 0.61–1.77 mg/g depending on month (PMID 27775631), and **10-DAB** — the semisynthesis precursor — is among them and is boosted by supplemental UV-B (PMID 38928114). This is the technical basis of the shift from bark-stripping to **needle harvest from cultivated stands → 10-DAB → semisynthetic paclitaxel**, which is why paclitaxel supply no longer requires killing wild trees.

**Grade: MODERATE, and honestly qualified.** The two papers above are **cultivation-optimisation studies** — seasonal dynamics and UV-B agronomy — not semisynthesis chemistry. **I did not retrieve the Holton/Greene/Potier semisynthesis literature in this search and will not cite what I did not retrieve.** [USER: verify and add the primary 10-DAB semisynthesis citations if this entry is to make the claim in full.] What the retrieved evidence *does* support: the precursor is in the needles, the needles are renewable, the content is manipulable by cultivation, and the field's entire research effort has pivoted to making that work. **That pivot is the observable fact.**

**And note what it means.** The tree was not saved by ethics, by CITES, or by anyone deciding cancer patients should go without. **It was saved by a chemist finding a way to start from the renewable organ.** Conservation policy documented the crisis; synthesis ended it. That is an uncomfortable lesson and this entry does not soften it.

**5. Genomics — STRONG, species-specific, and pointed at replacing the tree entirely.**
Li et al. (2025) published the first phased high-quality *T. wallichiana* reference genome — two haplotypes of 9.87 and 9.98 Gb, all 24 chromosomes telomere-to-telomere, a candidate ZW sex-determination system on chr12, and **experimentally validated 2-oxoglutarate/Fe(II)-dependent dioxygenases as candidate C4β-C20 epoxidases in the paclitaxel pathway** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/gigascience/giaf026), PMID 40184434). Qu et al. (2024) assembled the mitogenome ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ygeno.2024.110900), PMID 39067796). **Grade: STRONG.**

**Read the intent.** Nobody sequences a 10 Gb gymnosperm genome to make better tea. This is **pathway mining for heterologous production** — the endgame in which paclitaxel is made in a fermenter and *Taxus wallichiana* becomes, pharmaceutically, unnecessary. **That is the best available outcome for the species.**

**6. The endophyte detour — real, decades old, and commercially stalled. Effectively a NULL.**
Paclitaxel-producing endophytic fungi have been isolated from *T. wallichiana* since the *Taxomyces andreanae* work, and the search continues: Zaiyou et al. screened **435 endophytic strains** from *T. wallichiana* var. *mairei* bark and found **one** *Fusarium* producing paclitaxel — at **0.0153 mg/L** in culture, **0.27 mg/kg** in dry mycelium ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3305/nh.2015.32.6.9781), PMID 26667755). Vélëz et al. (2022) examined why yew endophytes tolerate paclitaxel, finding episodic positive selection at 10 β-tubulin sites, four of which (172, 279, 359, 362) lie in or near the paclitaxel-binding pocket ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2022.956855), PMID 36246258). **Grade: STRONG for the biology, NULL for the supply solution.** Do the arithmetic: **0.0153 mg/L against a bark content of ~29 g/kg.** Thirty years of effort and the fungal route has never displaced a gram of clinical supply. **This is a fascinating null and it belongs in the record precisely because the "fungi will save the yew" story is repeated far more often than its yields justify.**

**7. Conservation status — attested in the literature retrieved.**
Semwal et al. (2019) name *T. wallichiana* among the heavily-used Ayurvedic plants on the IUCN red list, note that collection from natural habitat is prohibited, that cultivation does not meet demand, that "their cultivation in other areas is impractical", and that the predictable consequence is adulteration ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joim.2019.04.008), PMID 31076374). **Grade: GOOD.** [USER: verify the current IUCN category and CITES appendix directly — this entry states "Endangered / CITES-listed" per the task brief and the above corroborates the listing but not the exact category.]

**Honest summary.** The toxicology is strong. The taxane chemistry is strong and species-specific. The genomics is world-class. **The tradition — four systems, two millennia, cough and fever and cold — is entirely untested, and its central doctrinal claim (warming; cardiac tonic) is either unmeasurable or inverted.** The gap between what this tree is famous for and what it was used for has never been bridged, and no one is trying.

### Cultivation Register

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The tradition knows this tree as *zarnab*, hot and dry, aromatic, and — the dangerous item — a cardiac and nervine tonic for cold-natured complaints (Juyal et al. 2014, PMID 25161920). The strongest cultivation idea in the Unani corpus is the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture, a finite vital endowment whose depletion is ageing; but that is a frame for the whole longevity regimen, not a status the tradition assigns to *zarnab*, and no *mufarrih* cardiac-*ruh* constituent role is claimable here beyond the tonic label itself. The tonic classification is therapeutic, not a cultivation status — and the chemistry inverts it, since the only reliable cardiac action of taxine is to poison the conduction system. The species identification of *zarnab* is itself contested in the Perso-Arabic literature. **(b) framed:** attested therapeutically, no cultivation status sourced, and the therapeutic sign is pharmacologically inverted.

**Chinese — Bencao (SBJ).** The Chinese footprint of *T. wallichiana* is thinner than its Indian one: the tag marks range-contact and the tree's modern role as the cultivated taxane source in southwest China (var. *mairei*), not a verified canonical *Bencao* entry. No *san pin* 三品 grade — upper 上品, middle 中品 or lower 下品 — is recorded for this species in the sources retrieved, and within *yang sheng* 養生, the conservational nourishing-life project, it carries no status at all because its classical Chinese attestation is unconfirmed. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status; the classical attestation itself remains unverified.

**Ayurveda (SS).** The SS tag rests on the contested identification of the classical drug *Talispatra* (तालीसपत्र), which the dominant reading assigns to *Abies webbiana* (a non-toxic Pinaceae), not to this yew. What is securely attested for *T. wallichiana* itself is a South Asian tradition of *katu-tikta rasa* and *ushna virya* — hot potency, kapha-vata-hara — worked for kasa (cough), shwasa (dyspnoea), jwara (fever) and agnimandya (Juyal et al. 2014, PMID 25161920; Sharma & Garg 2015, PMID 25797638). No *rasayana* (rejuvenation) or *vajikarana* (generative vitality) assignment is recorded for this species in the sources retrieved; its standing is therapeutic and dietetic, not a formal rejuvenative cultivation status — and the hot-potency reading is itself a rasa-derived theorem rather than an observed thermogenesis. **(b) framed:** therapeutically attested, no rasayana/vajikarana status sourced, identification contested.

**Tibetan (FMT).** Within the *rGyud bzhi*'s rlung/mkhris pa/bad kan framework the yew sits in the cold-disorder, bad kan (phlegm) category — warming, drying, dispersing — but the specific Tibetan drug name and its indications are not securely recoverable from what was retrieved. Tibetan medicine possesses *bcud len* (essence extraction) inside the Four Tantras, yet there is no formal "substances for subtle-body practice" category to assign this tree to, and no *bcud len* attribution to *T. wallichiana* is sourced here; medicine is in any case the third of the four treatment branches, supporting practice rather than reaching the root, which for the three *nyes pa* is at bottom a spiritual condition of the three poisons. No cultivation status is attested. **(c) absent.**

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this tree, and that is the whole instruction. Four traditions classed it warming and worked its renewable needles for cough, fever and cold — untested claims resting, in the Ayurvedic case, on a contested identification that may not be this species at all, and in the Unani case on a cardiac indication the chemistry inverts. There is no longevity use, no rejuvenative or generative status, no *bcud len* or *san pin* standing. The one drug the tree really holds, paclitaxel, is an intravenous, oncologist-administered, body-surface-dosed cytotoxic with no relation to any preparation a person could make — and the tree is IUCN Endangered and CITES-listed, so a personal harvest is both a poisoning risk and a subtraction from a shrinking species. The honest cultivation verdict is abstention on every axis at once.

### Contraindications

☠️ **ABSOLUTE — ALL INTERNAL USE PROHIBITED, ALL PARTS, ALL PERSONS, ALL PREPARATIONS. NO EXCEPTIONS.**

- **Taxine cardiotoxicity — all parts except aril flesh.** Bradyarrhythmia, AV block, wide-complex arrhythmia, cardiogenic shock, asystole. **Lethal ingestions are documented** (PMID 34049761). Severe cases have required **ECMO/ECLS** to survive (PMID 20825910, PMID 28941873).
- **The aril trap.** Red flesh non-toxic; **enclosed seed deadly**. Children cannot make this distinction and neither should anyone else.
- **Pregnancy — absolute.** Taxanes are antimitotic. Yew has a documented history as an abortifacient and the historical record of that use is a record of deaths.
- **Children — absolute.** Attractive red arils on an accessible ornamental shrub.
- **Livestock — a major agricultural hazard.** Yew clippings in hay or thrown over a fence kill cattle and horses. Do not compost or dump yew prunings where stock can reach them.
- **Cardiac disease, conduction disorders, any antiarrhythmic therapy.** And equally: a healthy heart is not protection.
- **Adulteration risk — the live clinical hazard.** *Talisadi* and other *Talispatra*-containing Ayurvedic preparations should contain *Abies webbiana*. Semwal et al. document exactly the supply pressure that produces yew substitution (PMID 31076374). **[USER: verify sourcing on any commercial Talisadi product.]**
- **Paclitaxel therapy is not this plant.** IV, oncologist-administered, BSA-dosed, premedicated for hypersensitivity, monitored for neutropenia and peripheral neuropathy. **No relationship to any preparation of the tree.**
- **⚖️ LEGAL / CONSERVATION — CITES-listed, IUCN Endangered.** Wild harvest is prohibited or restricted across the range. **Collecting this tree is not a grey area.** Trade requires CITES documentation. [USER: verify current appendix and national law before any handling beyond horticulture.]

**If ingestion is suspected:** emergency services immediately. **Say the word "yew" and say the word "taxine".** The literature supports early **activated charcoal**, **gastroscopic extraction of leaf material** (used in 95% of the Bern series, PMID 36594830), continuous cardiac monitoring, and — on the strength of the Fab cross-reactivity demonstrated by mass spectrometry — **early digoxin-specific Fab fragments** (PMID 28941873), with **ECLS/ECMO** as the rescue of last resort in refractory cardiogenic shock (PMID 20825910). Do not induce vomiting. Do not wait for symptoms.
