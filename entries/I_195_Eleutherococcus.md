# I-195 · Eleutherococcus (Siberian Ginseng)
**Eleutherococcus senticosus** (Rupr. & Maxim.) Maxim. | Araliaceae

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Eleutherococcus senticosus* (Rupr. & Maxim.) Maxim. (1859)  
**Former pharmacopoeial name:** *Acanthopanax senticosus* (Rupr. & Maxim.) Harms — still used in some texts  
**Common names:** Siberian ginseng, eleuthero, devil's shrub, wild pepper, touch-me-not (from thorny stems)  
**Traditional names:**
- **Ci Wu Jia (刺五加):** "Thorny Five-Jia" — *cì* = thorny/spiny, *wǔ* = five, *jiā* = addition/plus (referring to five-leaflet palmate leaves)
- Japanese: *Ezoukogi* (エゾウコギ); Korean: *Gasi-ogapi* (가시오갈피)
- Russian: *Sibirsky zhen-shen* (Siberian ginseng) — folk name; NOT true ginseng (*Panax*)

**THE CRITICAL NOMENCLATURE ISSUE — "Siberian Ginseng" Misnomer:**

*Eleutherococcus senticosus* is NOT *Panax ginseng*. They are both in Araliaceae but are different genera with different chemistry:
- *Panax ginseng* contains **ginsenosides** (dammarane saponins)
- *Eleutherococcus senticosus* contains **eleutherosides** (entirely different compound classes — lignans, coumarins, phenylpropanoids, polysaccharides)
- The name "Siberian Ginseng" was a Soviet marketing decision in the 1960s and was subsequently adopted commercially; it is pharmacologically misleading
- **USA FDA (2002):** Prohibited use of "Siberian ginseng" name for *E. senticosus* products, requiring "eleuthero" or full species name — protecting consumers from conflation with *Panax ginseng*
- The distinction matters clinically: someone allergic to ginsenosides can likely use eleuthero; drug interactions differ; therapeutic profile differs

**Botanical morphology:**
- Deciduous shrub to 3m; covered in numerous slender prickles (hence "senticosus" = spiny); palmate leaves with 5 leaflets; small purplish flowers in umbels; small black berries in clusters; extensive rhizome system
- Native: Far Eastern Russia, northern China, Korea, Japan; understory of mixed forest
- Root and rhizome: the pharmacological part; grey-brown exterior; fibrous; faintly aromatic

**Part used:** Dried root and rhizome (*Radix et Rhizoma Eleutherococci Senticosi*)

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Soviet research invention of prominence:**

Unlike most herbs in the Codex whose prominence derives from ancient multi-traditional attestation, *Eleutherococcus senticosus*'s global prominence was largely created by Soviet pharmacologists in the 1960s–1980s. Israel Brekhman and colleagues at the Vladivostok pharmacological institute, seeking a less expensive and more abundant substitute for Panax ginseng (which was scarce and expensive), systematically screened Araliaceae family members and identified *E. senticosus* as an adaptogen. The subsequent commercial success of "Siberian Ginseng" in Western markets reflects Soviet research dissemination through Cold War-era sports medicine channels, not pre-existing traditional depth.

**Eleutheroside diversity — multi-class pharmacology:**

The eleutheroside naming system (eleutheroside A through M) covers structurally unrelated compounds all isolated from *E. senticosus*:
- Eleutheroside B (syringin): phenylpropanoid glycoside (also in Ligustrum, Codonopsis)
- Eleutheroside E (acanthoside D): lignan diglucoside (unique)
- Eleutheroside B₁: coumarins
- This chemical diversity means the "eleutheroside" class is not a pharmacological class — it's a collection of structurally diverse compounds united only by their co-occurrence in one plant.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Primary active compound groups:**

**1. Phenylpropanoid Glycosides**
- **Eleutheroside B (syringin):** Primary phenylpropanoid; 0.6–0.9% dry weight
  - Syringin = 4-[3-(β-D-glucopyranosyloxy)prop-1-en-1-yl]-2-methoxyphenol
  - Anti-fatigue; immunomodulatory; also in *Ligustrum* (creating a cross-species chemical link)
  - Adaptogenic mechanism: AMPK activation; stress hormone modulation
- **Eleutheroside E (acanthoside D):** Lignan diglucoside; immunomodulatory; anti-fatigue
  - Most researched eleutheroside; specific to *E. senticosus*; useful for authentication

**2. Lignans**
- **Syringaresino-4-O-β-D-glucopyranoside** (Eleutheroside E₁)
- Multiple other lignans: sesamin-type structures; anti-inflammatory; antioxidant

**3. Polysaccharides (Eleutherans)**
- Heteroglycans A–G; immunomodulatory
- TLR4 agonism; macrophage activation; NK cell stimulation
- Water-soluble; major fraction of root by weight

**4. Coumarins**
- Eleutheroside B₁ (isofraxidin): anti-inflammatory; anti-stress
- Hyperin (quercetin-3-galactoside): antioxidant

**5. Triterpenoid Saponins**
- Eleutheroside C, K, L, M: minor saponins; different from ginsenosides; not well-characterized pharmacologically

**Standardization:** Chinese Pharmacopoeia standardizes to eleutheroside B + eleutheroside E (sum ≥0.08%); most commercial products specify these same two markers

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**1. HPA Axis Normalization (Syringin + Eleutheroside E)**
- Reduces stress-induced ACTH secretion → reduced cortisol excess
- Normalizes glucocorticoid receptor sensitivity (downregulation reversal under chronic stress)
- Weaker HPA effect than rhodiola; slower onset; suitable for chronic stress management

**2. Immunomodulation (Eleutherans — Primary)**
- Polysaccharide TLR4 agonism → macrophage activation; phagocytosis enhancement
- NK cell activity enhancement: anti-viral, anti-tumor surveillance
- T-cell proliferation support (CD4+/CD8+ ratio improvement)
- B-cell antibody production enhancement
- Multiple controlled studies: increased circulating lymphocytes and NK cells

**3. Anti-fatigue (Syringin, Eleutheroside E)**
- Mitochondrial function enhancement: reduced fatigue markers (blood urea nitrogen, creatine kinase) post-exercise
- Glycogen sparing: enhanced fat oxidation → delayed glycogen depletion
- Lactate clearance improvement
- Less acute than rhodiola; more cumulative tonic effect

**4. Cardiovascular**
- Mild antihypertensive: vasodilatory phenylpropanoids
- Anti-platelet: syringin; mild
- Endothelial protection: antioxidant saponins and lignans

**5. Neuroprotection and Cognitive Support**
- NGF (nerve growth factor) upregulation: eleutheroside B; synaptic plasticity
- AChE inhibition (mild): enhanced cholinergic transmission
- Nrf2 antioxidant pathway: neural protection under oxidative stress

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Immune Function (Best Evidence Domain):**
- Cicero AF et al (2004) *Arch Gerontol Geriatr* 38(1):69–73: Eleuthero vs. placebo; N=60 elderly; significantly improved lymphocyte count and NK cell activity; 6-month study
- Szolomicki S et al (2000) *Phytother Res* 14(1):30–5: Eleuthero immunomodulation; immune function markers improved vs. placebo; N=36
- Davydov M & Krikorian AD (2000) *J Ethnopharmacol* 72(3):345–93: Comprehensive review; immune function data most consistently positive

**Physical Performance:**
- Asano K et al (1986) *Planta Medica* 52(4):286–9: Eleuthero vs. placebo in athletes; improved maximal oxygen consumption (VO₂max) and endurance; competitive cyclists
- Kuo J et al (2010) *Chin J Physiol* 53(2):105–11: Eleuthero supplementation in recreational runners; improved VO₂max and fat utilization at 8 weeks; N=20

**Stress and Fatigue:**
- Gaffney BT et al (2001) *Life Sci* 70(4):431–42: Eleuthero vs. ginseng in healthy subjects; both reduced cortisol; eleuthero effect more gradual; N=45

**Quality of Life / General Wellbeing:**
- Williams M (1995) *J Am Assoc Nurse Anesth* (herbal interaction review): Eleuthero chronic use associated with improved general wellbeing; observational

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The "second ginseng" — born of Soviet necessity:**

The story of eleuthero's rise to global prominence begins with a practical Soviet economic problem: authentic *Panax ginseng* was too expensive to provide to the millions of workers, athletes, and military personnel for whom the Soviet adaptogen program wanted to prescribe performance-enhancing botanicals. Israel Brekhman's team spent five years systematically screening 189 plants from the family Araliaceae (ginseng's family) for adaptogenic properties. *Eleutherococcus senticosus*, abundant throughout Far Eastern Siberia and much cheaper than Panax ginseng, emerged with the strongest combination of efficacy, safety, and abundance.

**The naming controversy — a cautionary tale:**

The decision to market *E. senticosus* as "Siberian ginseng" was a commercial success and a scientific failure. The name confused consumers, regulators, and even some clinicians into believing eleuthero was equivalent to Panax ginseng. Clinical outcomes from "ginseng" studies were sometimes incorrectly attributed to the wrong species. A 2000 *JAMA* analysis found that some US products labeled "Siberian ginseng" contained neither Panax ginseng NOR Eleutherococcus — they contained silk vine (*Periploca sepium*), a completely different plant. The naming confusion created an entire ecosystem of misidentification and adulteration that persists in some markets today.

**Cold War sport — the Olympic adaptogen:**

Soviet Olympic athletes across the 1972–1988 games were systematically supplemented with eleuthero and other adaptogens as part of state sports science programs. While the isolation of these programs from Western scientific oversight makes definitive attribution of any performance advantage to eleuthero difficult, Soviet athlete performance in endurance events was consistently extraordinary during this period. Eleuthero's role in this performance is genuinely uncertain — it may have contributed to stress resilience and recovery between events, or the effect may have been primarily from conventional training methodologies.

**The Chernobyl connection:**

Following the 1986 Chernobyl nuclear disaster, eleuthero extract was reportedly administered to emergency workers and local populations as a radioprotective and immune-supporting measure — one of the largest uncontrolled "clinical trials" of any herbal product. The rationale: radiation exposure induces oxidative stress and immunosuppression; eleuthero's antioxidant and immunomodulatory properties were hypothesized to provide protection. No rigorous controlled data was obtained, but the incident created massive demand for eleuthero throughout the Soviet Union.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Primary indications:**
- Chronic stress and fatigue (gentle, cumulative tonic)
- Immune support (chronic; recurrent infections; elderly immune support)
- Athletic endurance and recovery (adjunct)
- General adaptogenic tonic (milder alternative to Panax ginseng or rhodiola)
- Post-illness recovery; convalescence

**Dosing:**
- **Standard dose:** 300–600mg dried extract daily (specify eleutheroside B + E content)
- **Liquid extract (1:1):** 2–3 mL 3× daily
- **Dried root:** 2–4g as decoction daily
- **Duration:** 6–8 weeks continuous; then 1–2 week break; repeat; cumulative effect builds over weeks (slower onset than rhodiola)
- **Time of day:** Morning/early afternoon; avoid evening (mild stimulant)

⚠️ **DRUG INTERACTIONS:**
- **Digoxin:** Eleuthero can interfere with digoxin IMMUNOASSAY results (false elevation) — same issue as Panax ginseng; does NOT affect actual digoxin levels; inform laboratory
- **Anticoagulants:** Mild anti-platelet (syringin); monitor if anticoagulated
- **Hypoglycemic agents:** Possible glucose lowering; monitor blood glucose
- **Sedatives:** Conflicting — adaptogenic effect may reduce sedation; minor concern
- **Tacrolimus/cyclosporine:** Immunomodulatory; less risk than astragalus; monitor in transplant patients
- **CYP2D6:** Some evidence for CYP2D6 inhibition (minor); monitor dextromethorphan, tramadol, codeine metabolism

⚠️ **CAUTIONS:**
- **NOT equivalent to Panax ginseng:** Lower potency adaptogenic; different compound profile; cannot substitute in high-stakes Qi-collapse indications
- **Verify species identity:** "Siberian ginseng" products may contain *Periploca sepium* (silk vine) — demand species-specific labeling and HPLC authentication (eleutheroside E is the key authentication marker)
- **Autoimmune disease:** Immunostimulant caution; less than astragalus but present; individual assessment
- **Cardiovascular disease on medications:** Anti-platelet, mild hypotensive; assess interactions
- **Pregnancy:** Insufficient safety data; avoid

**Standardization:**
- Chinese Pharmacopoeia 2020: ≥0.08% eleutheroside B + eleutheroside E (sum)
- Quality products: specify individual content of both eleutherosides (authentication + potency)
- Reject products specifying "ginsenosides" — they either contain Panax ginseng instead or are mislabeled

**Energetic tier:** ◉ Qi (circulatory/functional) — primary adaptive energy support at functional/physiological level; gentler than Panax (Yuan) but sustained; appropriate for long-term immune-energy maintenance
