# Elephant Garlic — ampeloprason (Dioscorides 2-180), great-headed garlic, wild leek

## *Allium ampeloprasum* (spine botanical; the translator's gloss for Dioscorides 2-180 reads "Allium ameloprasum var ameloprasum — Wild Leek, Blue Leek, Vine Leek, Great-headed Garlic, Levant Garlic") | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP, AT, DMM, CM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Identity warning.** "Elephant garlic" is a modern vernacular name. The one text that names this plant under a name the Codex can match is Dioscorides. The Egyptian, Assyrian and Avicenna tags reach this row only through the genus: the lists name garlic (*Allium sativum*) and onion (*A. cepa*), which are other species. Those three tags are not supported at species level by anything read. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-180, "AMELOPRASON" (text line 6102), is the whole chapter: "Ampeloprasum is worse for the stomach than leeks but is warmer and more uretic, expelling the menstrual flow. It is good if those bitten by poisonous beasts eat it." The translator's gloss is *Allium ameloprasum* var. *ameloprasum* (Wild Leek, Blue Leek, Vine Leek, Great-headed Garlic, Levant Garlic). The preceding chapter 2-179 PRASON (line 6086) is the leek, glossed *A. ameloprasum* var. *porrum*, and is a separate plant. That chapter calls it "inflative, has bad juice, causes troublesome dreams, is uretic and good for the stomach". Chapter 2-182 (line 6115) is garlic proper, with a wild "ophioscorodon" and an "elaphoscorodon". The comparison "worse for the stomach than leeks but warmer" is the only qualitative statement Dioscorides makes about this plant.

**Egyptian — Ebers (EP).** Bryan's chapter VI list prints "Garlic" (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1294) and "Onions" with the forms Fresh, Green, Onions-from-the-Oasis, Onion-meal, Inner-of-Onions and Sap-of-Fresh-Onions (line 1314). It prints no leek and no second kind of garlic. `_sources/ebers.csv` lines 4 and 5 identify these as *Allium sativum* and *A. cepa*. Nothing read ties either to *A. ampeloprasum*.

**Assyrian (AT).** No locus was found for this plant. `Sources/AT_Assyrian/text/` is cuneiform plates only (`extract/README.md`). `_sources/assyrian.csv` lines 19 and 20 list garlic (*shumu*, *Allium sativum*) and onion (*hazannu* / *samsamu*, *A. cepa*), which are not this species.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The attestation rests on list rows only: `_sources/avicenna.csv` lines 2 and 3 name Onion (*A. cepa*) and Garlic (*A. sativum*), "Canon II". No row names *A. ampeloprasum*, and no Canon chapter text is on disk.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian — Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No Egyptian text read names this species. The garlic and onion lines concern other plants, and the corpus is organised by complaint, not by what a substance does to the person's *ka*, *ib* or *akh*. No *heka* recitation attaches to this plant.

**Assyrian (AT).** *(c) Absent.* No attestation for this species was found, and none is asserted. The blocking warning on Assyrian identifications applies; no tablet number or deity is given.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent* as to cultivation status. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. His practice here is a discipline of attention: tell the leek, the ampeloprasum and the garlic apart, and note that this one is the warmer and the poorer for the stomach. That is sensory discrimination, not a vital-body doctrine.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent.* The tag rests on list rows for other *Allium* species. The Unani frame of innate heat and radical moisture is a frame of the whole regimen, and nothing read assigns this plant a place in it.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this plant on the evidence read. It is a food, and its single sourced medicinal remark is a comparison with the leek. Three of its four tags are inherited from garlic and onion and should be treated as unverified for this species.

### Contraindications

Dioscorides himself calls it "worse for the stomach than leeks" and says it expels the menstrual flow, so it should not be treated as a pregnancy-safe medicinal. General cautions for alliums apply: heartburn in sensitive people, and care with bleeding disorders and blood-thinning medication. Do not use it for snakebite. Seek emergency care.
