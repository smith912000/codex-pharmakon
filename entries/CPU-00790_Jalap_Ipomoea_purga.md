# Jalap — Jalapa, Purga, Xalapa Root, Vera Cruz Jalap, True Jalap

## *Ipomoea purga* (Wender.) Lindl. | Family: Convolvulaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, EP

**Energetic Classification:** Hot and dry in the third degree (Galenic-Unani); a violently expulsive, downward-driving simple

**Training Phase:** Purgation / Clearing (a Phase reserved for the most cautious supervised use — see Contraindications)

---

### Origin & Tradition

Jalap is a New World tuber whose entry into the Old World materia medica is entirely a product of the Columbian exchange. Its very name records its origin: the drug takes its label from the town of Xalapa (Jalapa) in Veracruz, Mexico, on the trade road down from the highlands to the Gulf port, through which the dried tubers were shipped to Europe from the sixteenth century onward. Unlike most entries in this Codex, jalap has no genuine Classical or ancient Near Eastern pedigree — Dioscorides and the Assyrian tablets could not have known a Mexican convolvulus. Honesty requires stating this plainly: any claim that "the ancients used jalap" is a later back-projection.

**Dioscoridean / European Pharmacy (EP):** Jalap enters the European tradition only *after* Dioscorides, but it was rapidly assimilated into the Dioscoridean framework of drastic hydragogue purgatives, taking a place alongside the older resinous cathartics scammony (*Convolvulus scammonia*) and turpeth (*Operculina turpethum*), to which it is botanically close. By the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries "Jalap" (Radix Jalapae) was a fixture of the London and Edinburgh pharmacopoeias, dispensed as powdered root, tincture, and the compound powder. It was among the most heavily prescribed purgatives of the heroic-medicine era.

**Avicennan / Unani (AT):** Later Unani-influenced European commentators classified jalap within the humoral scheme as intensely hot and dry, a *mus'hil* (purgative) acting to expel cold, phlegmatic, and watery humours downward. This is an assimilation of a post-Columbian drug into the Avicennan grammar of catharsis rather than a genuine mention in the *Qanun* itself.

**Badianus / Mesoamerican (DMM):** The plant's true home tradition is Mesoamerican. Native Nahua herbalism of central Mexico employed several purgative morning-glory tubers (the *Ipomoea* and *Operculina* group), and the Badianus Manuscript (Codex de la Cruz-Badiano, 1552) — the earliest Aztec herbal — documents the indigenous use of drastic root purgatives within its pharmacopoeia. Jalap is the most commercially important survivor of this indigenous purgative lineage.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Jalap is the Codex's emblem of *catharsis as violence* — the drug that "does not persuade the body but overrules it." Its symbolic register is downward, expulsive, and cleansing to the point of danger; it embodies the heroic-medicine belief that disease could be driven bodily out through the bowel. The pharmacology honours the symbol exactly: the active glycoresins are not gentle laxatives but osmotic and secretory irritants that force a torrential watery evacuation. The lesson the Codex draws is the counter-lesson — that the "cleansing" ideal, taken literally, can empty a patient into collapse.

### Active Components

The purgative activity resides almost entirely in the tuber's **glycoresin fraction (≈8–18% of the dried root)**, historically divided into the ether-insoluble **convolvulin** and the ether-soluble **jalapin (scammonin)**. Modern phytochemistry has resolved these crude fractions into complex **resin glycosides** — amphipathic macrolactone oligosaccharides built on hydroxy-fatty-acid cores (convolvulinic, jalapinolic, and operculinic acids) glycosylated with rhamnose, glucose, and fucose and decorated with short acyl residues (2-methylbutanoyl, cinnamoyl, and others). Named constituents characterised from *I. purga* include the **purgins**, **purginosides**, and **jalapinosides** (León-Rivera et al. 2016; Castañeda-Gómez et al. 2012). On hydrolysis the macrolactones release the irritant glycosidic acids responsible for the cathartic effect.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Within Kenetica, jalap is filed as a **historical / cautionary drastic**, not a working protocol simple. Its documented action is a rapid, copious, watery catharsis produced by osmotic retention of fluid in the gut lumen and direct stimulation of intestinal secretion and motility. Historically this was exploited for constipation, "dropsy" (oedema), and as a co-purgative to unload the bowel in the humoral clearing paradigm. In the Kenetica framework its use-case is almost entirely **pedagogical**: it marks the outer boundary of the Purgation phase, the point at which "clearing" becomes injury. Practitioners are directed to gentler, self-limiting alternatives; jalap is retained in the Codex as documentation, not recommendation.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the chemistry of *I. purga* resin glycosides is well characterised, though modern clinical trial data are absent — the drug fell out of use before controlled trials existed. León-Rivera et al. isolated and structurally elucidated multiple new resin glycosides (jalapinoside B, purginoic acid A and related saccharides) from the convolvulin of *I. purga* and related species, and reported cytotoxicity toward cancer cell lines and antiproliferative activity on glioma lines ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/mrc.4476)). Castañeda-Gómez et al. characterised **purgin II** and related ester-type dimers from *I. purga*, showing potent reversal of multidrug resistance in vinblastine-resistant human breast carcinoma cells (>2140-fold enhancement of vinblastine activity at 25 µg/mL) ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/np300739y)). These confirm the resin glycosides as real, potent amphipathic membrane-active agents — consistent with the traditional drastic action — but represent in-vitro chemistry, not evidence of safe therapeutic use.

**Evidence grade: TRADITIONAL / CHEMICAL.** Robust phytochemistry; no modern controlled clinical evidence supporting purgative use, and a clear historical toxicity signal. Nothing here should be read as validating internal administration.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Hot and dry, third degree; a drastic downward purgative (*mus'hil*) expelling watery and phlegmatic humours.
- **European heroic pharmacy:** Hydragogue cathartic — draws water into the bowel; grouped with scammony and turpeth.
- **Mesoamerican:** A powerful expulsive root within the indigenous purgative complex.
- **TCM / Ayurvedic:** Not a classical constituent of either system; no authentic *rasa/guna/virya/vipaka* assignment exists, and none should be invented.

### Contraindications

**⚠️ DRASTIC PURGATIVE — OBSOLETE AND DANGEROUS. The Codex records jalap as a cautionary historical drug, not a usable remedy.**

- **Violent catharsis:** Jalap produces torrential watery evacuation with **severe abdominal cramping, colic, and griping**. It is a drastic, not a laxative.
- **Dehydration & electrolyte collapse:** Fluid and electrolyte loss can be rapid and, in the vulnerable, life-threatening — the central hazard of all hydragogue drastics.
- **Pregnancy — ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION:** Drastic purgatives cause reflex uterine/pelvic stimulation and were historically associated with abortifacient risk. **Do not use in pregnancy under any circumstances.** Avoid also during lactation.
- **Gastrointestinal disease:** Contraindicated in inflammatory bowel disease, bowel obstruction, appendicitis or any acute abdomen, haemorrhoids, and anal fissure.
- **Children, the elderly, and the debilitated:** Especially vulnerable to fluid loss; do not use.
- **Overdose:** Bloody diarrhoea, prostration, and circulatory collapse. There is no safe self-administration margin.

This entry exists to document a historically important but hazardous drug and to warn against it — not to guide its use.
