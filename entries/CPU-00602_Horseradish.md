# Horseradish — raphanos agria, "wild radish" (Dioscorides 2-138; armoracia of the Romans)

## *Armoracia rusticana* (spine botanical; the translator's gloss for Dioscorides 2-138 lists "Raphanis sylvestris, Armoracia [Fuchs], Raphanus rusticanus [Bauhin], Cochlearia armoracia [Linnaeus], Nasturtium armoracia, Roripa armoracia, Radicula armoracia Armoracia rusticana — Common Horseradish") | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Identity warning.** The only locus is one short Dioscorides chapter for a plant he calls the *wild radish*. Its identification with horseradish is the translator's, resting on the Roman name *armoracia* and on a list of Renaissance and Linnaean names, and the description is too brief to settle it. The Assyrian and Avicenna tags have no locus at all. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-138, "RAPHANOS AGRIA" (text line 5696), in full: "The wild radish (which the Romans call armoracia) has leaves similar to that which is sown, or rather more similar to those of lampsana. The root is slender, soft, and somewhat sharp; both the leaves and root are boiled instead of vegetables. It is warming, diuretic and burning." The "that which is sown" is the cultivated radish of the preceding chapter 2-137 (RAPHANIS, glossed *Raphanus sativus*). The index lists "Cochlearia armoracia 260". The chapter describes a slender, soft root, whereas horseradish is known today for a thick, pungent one. That difference is noted as a reason for caution, not as a finding.

**Assyrian (AT).** No locus was found. The text on disk is cuneiform plates only (`Sources/AT_Assyrian/extract/README.md`). `_sources/assyrian.csv` has no horseradish or radish row; the nearest is line 21, "Mustard (*kirimbu*)", *Sinapis* or *Brassica* sp., and nothing read links it to this plant.

### Active Components

Cabbage-family roots such as horseradish are known in general for sulfur-containing glucosinolates, which release the pungent, tear-provoking compounds when the root is cut or grated. That fits "somewhat sharp" and "burning". Dioscorides' plant was not tested, and the content of any ancient sample is unknown. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The sourced use is food: leaves and root "boiled instead of vegetables", with a warming, diuretic and burning quality. The Codex gives no protocol. Modern horseradish is a condiment, and the Codex makes no medicinal claim for it.

### Preparation & Dosing

Dioscorides says the leaves and root are "boiled instead of vegetables" (2-138, line 5696). He gives no quantity. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** *(c) Absent.* No attestation was found, and none is asserted. The blocking warning on Assyrian identifications applies; no tablet number or deity is given.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent* as to cultivation status. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. His chapter is three sentences of sensory assay: the leaf compared with the sown radish and with lampsana, the root slender and soft and somewhat sharp, and the effect warming and burning. That is a discipline of attention, and the chapter assigns no vital-body role.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent.* The tag rests on a spine row, not on any list row or text read. No attestation and no cultivation framing was found.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports a cultivation practice. Horseradish may be grown as a hardy kitchen perennial, but the texts read do not say that the plant they describe is the modern one, and two of the three tags are unsupported.

### Contraindications

Horseradish and its relatives are strong mucosal irritants, so raw root and fumes can burn the mouth, eyes and airways. Keep amounts to ordinary culinary use, and avoid it with active stomach ulcers or when pregnant unless a clinician advises otherwise. [USER: verify] Dioscorides' own word for the plant is "burning".
