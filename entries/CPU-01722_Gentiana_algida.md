# Gentiana algida — spang.rgyan.dkar.po, Pang gyen karpo (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Gentiana algida Pall (white type), the first of two binomials Clark 1995 prints for spang.rgyan.dkar.po (then Gentiana triflora); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints Gentiana algida on the same line as "Pang gyen karpo" and its transliteration* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk (including the three Bhishagratna volumes) for the genus name *Gentiana* found Dioscorides 3-3 GENTIANE (line 6541, the translator's glosses *Gentiana lutea* and Yellow Gentian) and Yang 1998's Long Dan (Radix Gentianae Scabrae, line 1936) and Qin Jiao (Radix Gentianae Macrophyllae, line 2494). These are other species and are not attributed to this row. Clark's preface (line 83) says that more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1018).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). It reads:

"spang.rgyan.dkar.po: Gentiana algida Pall (white type): Gentiana triflora: [has a bitter taste, small elongated green leaves and a white, blue or striped flower]. It cures throat disorders, fever caused by poisoning [and infectious fever]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the brackets (commentary layer) are the bitter taste, the leaves, the flower, and the clause "and infectious fever". Outside them (root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the two Latin names, which are Clark's identifications, and the sentence "It cures throat disorders, fever caused by poisoning". A second Clark entry, *kyi.lche* (line 1003), has a commentary that says its white flowers are "similar to Gentiana algida (spang.rgyan)", so Clark's commentary uses this plant as a comparison for another.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* Line 3432 reads: "Pang gyen karpo cures laryngitis and hot disorder associated with poisoning." Line 3605 lists "Pang gyen" among "the group of medicines that cure poisoning". The index lists "Pang gyen karpo 207" (line 7459). The action agrees in kind with Clark's "throat disorders, fever caused by poisoning". The Codex does not treat *laryngitis* and *throat disorders* as one term.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary.* The "P" block prints, on one line (5584), "Pang gyen karpo spang rgyan dkar po Gentiana algida": the phonetic name, the transliteration and the scientific name stand together. The two Tibetan texts agree on the Tibetan name, the genus and the species, which Clark marks "(white type)". [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder found no entry on this drug. I_1172 (*Pterocephalus hookeri*), I_1178 (*Rosa macrophylla*), I_1261 (*Curcuma zedoaria*) and I_1269 (*Drynaria propinqua*) quote the neighbouring "P" block lines or the name "Pang gyen"; I_113 (Gentian, *Gentiana*) is a general entry from other traditions, and I_315 and I_316 are entries on *Gentiana crassicaulis* and *Gentiana straminea*. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Gentiana* is a genus of the gentian family (Gentianaceae), and that bitter secoiridoid glycosides, of which gentiopicroside is an example, are characteristic of the family; Clark's commentary does record a "bitter taste". Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained or which part was used.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "cures throat disorders, fever caused by poisoning" (Clark, line 1018) and "cures laryngitis and hot disorder associated with poisoning" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3432), and that stands in the group that cures poisoning (line 3605). These are the texts' claims and not Codex protocols. Poisoning is an emergency and the Codex offers no advice for it.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] Clark's commentary describes taste, leaves and flower and does not say which part is taken.

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Tibetan terms ("throat disorders", "fever caused by poisoning", "infectious fever") are not modern diagnoses or measured outcomes, so no modern finding can be set beside them. The bitterness of gentians is a general, well-established property of the genus.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 1018) prints "a bitter taste". No power or post-digestive taste is printed. The name "Pang gyen" stands in the Men-Tsee-Khang group of medicines that cure poisoning (line 3605). The one action printed is the curing of throat disorders and of fever caused by poisoning.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it cures throat disorders and fever caused by poisoning, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same in kind. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the sentences cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only. The two Tibetan texts agree on the species, which makes the link from this binomial to that framing firmer than in the neighbouring rows, but it still rests on the texts' own identifications.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a bitter drug for throat disorders and poisoning fever in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Poisoning needs emergency medical care, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Gentians are a large genus and some alpine species are protected in places; no conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
