# Chinese Lantern — Winter Cherry · Jin Deng Long · Bladder Cherry · Strawberry Groundcherry

## *Physalis alkekengi L.* | Family: Solanaceae

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ, AT, DMM, CM
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry — bitter, sour, heat-clearing, detoxifying
**Training Phase:** Phase I — Cooling & Detoxification (clearing throat-and-lung heat, draining toxic heat)

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian tradition (AT):** The bladder-like inflated calyx made *Physalis* a conspicuous and collectable plant across the ancient Near East. It appears among the cooling, urine-promoting "bladder" plants of Mesopotamian and later Semitic herbal practice — its very name *alkekengi* is an Arabised term that carried the plant's reputation as a diuretic and stone-and-bladder remedy into the medieval pharmacopoeia.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM):** In the *Qanun*, *alkekengi* (kaknaj) is a cold, drying, cleansing fruit prized as a diuretic and a remedy for hot disorders of the urinary tract — jaundice, gravel, and "burning" of the bladder. The Arabic-Persian tradition used the ripe berries and their juice to cool inflamed passages and promote urine, a use that persisted into European apothecary practice as "winter cherry" for gravel and gout.

**Dioscorides / De Materia Medica (DMM):** Dioscorides describes *strychnos halikakabos* — recognisable as *Physalis* — noting the fruit enclosed in a bladder-like husk and its diuretic use for the bladder and jaundice, while cautioning about the narcotic character of the wider *strychnos* (nightshade) group to which it belongs.

**Shennong / Chinese Materia Medica (TCM):** The ripe calyx and fruit, **Jin Deng Long** (锦灯笼, "brocade lantern"; the fruit also *Deng Long Cao*), are catalogued among the heat-clearing, detoxifying, throat-benefiting herbs. Classical use centres on **sore, swollen throat and hoarse voice, lung-heat cough, and hot painful urination (heat *lin*)**, with topical folk use for hot skin sores. It has been used across Chinese practice for throat disease, hepatitis, and bacillary dysentery.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The plant is defined by its paper "lantern" — an inflated, papery calyx that swells scarlet around a single berry, a beacon in autumn hedgerows. Traditions read the lantern as a sign of a plant that "opens and cools an inflamed passage," fitting for a throat-and-bladder remedy. The bitter, cold berry belongs to the nightshade family, and the same signature that promised cooling detoxification also warned of toxicity in the unripe fruit and green parts. Modern chemistry vindicates both halves of the doctrine: the characteristic steroidal **physalins** are genuine, potent anti-inflammatory agents, while the plant's Solanaceous alkaloid load underlies the real toxicity of its immature and vegetative tissue.

### Active Components

- **Physalins and neophysalins** — the signature 13,14-seco-16,24-cyclosteroids (withanolide-derived); the major bioactive and anti-inflammatory constituents (physalin A, B, L, O and many analogues).
- **Flavonoids** — luteolin and its glycosides; antioxidant, anti-inflammatory.
- **Withanolides** and other steroids; phenylpropanoids, coumarins, sucrose esters, alkaloids, nucleosides.
- **Carotenoids** (the red calyx pigments), organic acids, and vitamin C in the ripe berry.
- **Solanine-type steroidal glycoalkaloids** — concentrated in unripe fruit and green tissue (see Contraindications).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Jin Deng Long is a **Phase I cooling-and-detoxifying** agent with a specific affinity for the throat and lungs. Its niche in Kenetica is the hot, swollen, painful throat with a barking lung-heat cough — the acute "fire in the upper gate" presentation — extending to hot, burning urination. It is a targeted heat-drainer rather than a fluid-restorer (contrast Lu Gen, CPU-00881, which rehydrates), and belongs in the cooling toolkit alongside other throat-clearing bitters. Because its potent anti-inflammatory physalins sit in a plant with a genuinely toxic unripe stage, Kenetica classes it as a **controlled cooling agent**: ripe calyx/fruit only, defined dose, not for casual or prolonged self-use.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the anti-inflammatory reputation of *Physalis alkekengi* is well supported at the mechanistic level. Zhang et al. isolated ten new physalin steroids from the calyx; the most potent inhibited nitric-oxide production in macrophages (IC50 ≈ 2.26 µM) with low cytotoxicity, suppressed IL-6, IL-1β, iNOS and COX-2, upregulated the HO-1/Nrf2 axis, modulated p38/ERK/JNK MAPK signalling, reduced mouse-ear oedema, and ameliorated DSS-induced ulcerative colitis in vivo ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bioorg.2024.108082)). Ji et al. showed that physalins act as Michael-reaction acceptors that alkylate key cysteine residues on IKKβ — a molecular basis for NF-κB-linked anti-inflammatory activity — with physalins A, O and isophysalin A inhibiting NO production ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.steroids.2011.11.016)). A systematic review by Yang et al. catalogues the calyx and fruit as heat-clearing, detoxifying agents historically used for throat disease, hepatitis and bacillary dysentery, and summarises broad preclinical activity — anti-inflammatory, anti-tumour, immunosuppressive, antibacterial, anti-asthmatic and antioxidant — driven chiefly by physalins and flavonoids ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27030695)).

**Evidence grade:** *Moderate-preclinical.* Strong, mechanistically detailed in vitro and animal evidence for the anti-inflammatory action of physalins, well-characterised chemistry, and documented traditional throat/urinary use; controlled human clinical trials remain lacking. The specific sore-throat and antitussive uses are **traditional-grade** with a coherent physalin-based rationale.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** Bitter, cold; enters Lung. Clears heat, relieves toxicity, benefits the throat, resolves phlegm, promotes urination. For sore swollen throat, lung-heat cough, and hot painful urination.
- **Unani (Avicenna):** Cold and dry; diuretic, deobstruent, cleansing; for hot disorders of bladder, jaundice and gravel.
- **Galenic (Dioscorides):** Cooling, diuretic fruit of the *strychnos* group; used for the bladder and jaundice, with narcotic caution attached to the genus.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** The plant is placed here among the cooling, urine-promoting "bladder" plants of Mesopotamian and later Semitic practice, but the identification rests largely on the Arabised name *alkekengi* carrying a bladder-and-gravel reputation forward, not on a secure cuneiform substance-identification. Under the standing blocking warning that governs every Assyrian substance identification, deity attribution and tablet reference, no cultivation status is asserted; the tradition classifies illness by causal agency, and nothing in the retrieved material assigns this fruit a role in tending a person's vital constitution rather than draining a hot bladder. Cultivation status: **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides records *strychnos halikakabos* — the bladder-husked fruit recognisable as *Physalis* — as a diuretic for the bladder and jaundice, with a narcotic caution attached to the wider nightshade group. He carries no subtle-body doctrine into which such a fruit could be graded, so there is no cultivation status to report. What the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention: the correct placing of the plant within the *strychnos* kin, the warning that the narcotic character of the genus rides alongside the useful cooling fruit, and the judgement to separate the two. That is a practice of provenance and sensory assay, not a vital-body assignment. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** In the *Qanun* tradition *kaknaj* (*alkekengi*) is cold and dry, a cleansing diuretic and deobstruent for hot disorders of the urinary tract — jaundice, gravel, and the burning of the bladder. The strongest cultivation idea in the Unani corpus is the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture across the whole regimen, a longevity frame of temperance rather than a status the tradition pins to any single cooling diuretic; no *mufarrih* or cardiac-*ruh* role is claimed for this fruit, and there are no reliable *Canon* chapter references to cite. Its standing here is therapeutic — a drug to cool and open an inflamed passage — not a cultivation status. **(b) framed:** claimed by the tradition, no cultivation status sourced.

**Chinese — Bencao (SBJ).** *Jin Deng Long* 錦燈籠 (the ripe calyx and fruit, also *Deng Long Cao*) is catalogued among the heat-clearing, toxicity-relieving, throat-benefiting herbs (*qing re jie du* 清熱解毒), worked for hot swollen throat, lung-heat cough, and hot painful urination (*re lin* 熱淋). This is a curative office — draining a defined excess and then stopping — which is the opposite pole from *yang sheng* 養生, the conservational nourishing-of-life that the upper grade of the *san pin* 三品 hierarchy tracks. No *san pin* grade for this species is recorded in the sources retrieved, and a bitter-cold detoxicant is the profile of a disease-attacking drug, not of a substance taken long-term to nourish life. Its Chinese standing is therefore therapeutic, not a cultivation status. **(b) framed:** attested in the Chinese materia medica, no *san pin* grade sourced, and its function is curative rather than conservational.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a substance a cultivator takes to build vitality. Across all four traditions *Physalis alkekengi* is a short-course cooling drug — a diuretic for a hot, gravelly bladder in the western and Unani strata, a throat-and-lung heat-drainer in the Chinese — used against a defined complaint and then set down. The plant's own toxic unripe stage reinforces the discipline: ripe calyx and fruit only, a bounded dose, no casual or prolonged use. The honest cultivation answer is abstention outside the specific hot-excess indication.

### Contraindications

- **⚠️ Unripe fruit and green tissue are TOXIC:** The unripe berry, leaves, stems and green calyx contain **solanine-type steroidal glycoalkaloids** that cause gastrointestinal upset (nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhoea) and, in quantity, systemic toxicity. Only the fully ripe fruit and properly prepared medicinal calyx should be used; never ingest unripe fruit or raw green parts.
- **⚠️ Pregnancy — uterine caution:** *Physalis alkekengi* has a traditional reputation as an emmenagogue/abortifacient and is contraindicated in pregnancy owing to potential uterine-stimulating (oxytocic) activity — avoid entirely — [USER: verify].
- **Cold / deficiency patterns:** As a bitter-cold heat-draining herb, it is inappropriate for spleen-stomach deficiency-cold, cold-type diarrhoea, or throat pain without heat signs.
- **Solanaceae sensitivity:** Members of the nightshade family may not be tolerated by nightshade-sensitive individuals.
- **Dose discipline:** Keep to the traditional 5–9 g range; concentrated extracts are unstudied in humans and its physalins are pharmacologically potent.
