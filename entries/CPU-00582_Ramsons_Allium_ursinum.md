# Ramsons — Wild Garlic, Bear's Garlic, Wood Garlic, Ail des Ours

## *Allium ursinum* L. | Family: Amaryllidaceae (Alliaceae)

**Tradition Tags:** AT (Assyrian), CM (Avicenna), DMM (Dioscorides)
**Energetic Classification:** Hot and Dry (third degree) — a warming, penetrating, dispersing agent in the Galenic-Unani scheme
**Training Phase:** Purification

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian (AT):** The cuneiform medical corpus of Mesopotamia records a family of pungent *šūmū* (alliums) — garlic, onion, and leek among them — deployed in poultices and infusions against fevers, swellings, and "worms of the belly." Wild-gathered woodland garlic of the *Allium ursinum* type falls within this pungent-bulb pharmacy: plants prized for their sharp odour, understood as evidence of a heat capable of driving out corruption and cutting phlegm.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** In *De Materia Medica* (Book II), Dioscorides treats the alliums at length, describing *skorodon* (garlic) as warming, acrid, and diuretic, capable of "expelling tapeworms," clearing the arteries of the lungs, and acting against the bites of venomous creatures. The broad-leaved woodland garlics of temperate Europe were used by later commentators as a milder, spring-gathered analogue of the cultivated bulb — the same acrid virtue in a leafy, seasonal form.

**Avicenna (CM):** In the *Qanun fi'l-Tibb*, Avicenna classifies garlic (*thum*) as hot and dry in the third or fourth degree, a strong resolvent and attenuant that thins gross humours, opens obstructions, and warms a cold stomach — while cautioning that its heat can inflame the blood, irritate the eyes, and provoke headache in hot temperaments. Wild ramsons inherits this humoral portrait: potent, cleansing, and to be moderated in the choleric.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The plant's folk names — bear's garlic, bear leek — tie it to the bear emerging from winter dormancy, said to seek the pungent leaves to purge and restore vigour after the fast. In Kenetica this is the exact symbolic key: ramsons is the plant of the threshold between dormancy and renewed motion. Its pharmacology matches the symbol. The organosulfur compounds that give the leaf its heat are genuinely vasoactive and mildly blood-thinning, so the "spring cleanse" folklore rests on a real, if modest, cardiovascular and antimicrobial action rather than pure metaphor.

### Active Components

Ramsons shares the biosynthetic machinery of the genus *Allium* but is dominated by its leaf chemistry rather than a storage bulb. Key constituents:

- **Cysteine sulfoxides** — chiefly (+)-S-methyl- and S-allyl-cysteine sulfoxides (methiin, alliin analogues), the odourless precursors stored in intact tissue.
- **Alliinase-derived thiosulfinates** — on crushing, alliinase cleaves the sulfoxides to reactive thiosulfinates (allicin-type compounds), the source of the pungent aroma and much of the biological activity.
- **Diallyl and dimethyl polysulfides** — lipophilic breakdown products.
- **γ-glutamyl peptides**, **adenosine**, and vinyldithiins.
- **Flavonoids** (kaempferol glycosides) and **phenolic acids** in the leaf.
- **Steroidal saponins** in smaller quantity.

The lipophilic (alcohol-soluble) fraction appears to carry the antiplatelet activity, whereas the water-soluble fraction is comparatively non-specific.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Ramsons is a **Purification-phase** agent: a seasonal, food-grade cleanser used to mark the turn from a dormant, phlegm-heavy state into renewed circulation and appetite. Its practical value is as a culinary-medicinal green — pesto, soup, infused oil — taken through its short spring window. The intended effects, consistent with both tradition and evidence, are gentle vasodilation, mild inhibition of platelet aggregation, warming of a sluggish digestion, and a broad low-grade antimicrobial action on the gut flora. It is emphatically a *tonic-cleanser*, not an acute remedy, and belongs in the diet rather than the medicine cabinet.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:

- **Hiyasat et al. (2009), *Pharmacology*** — the first report of antiplatelet activity in *A. ursinum*. Alcoholic leaf extracts inhibited ADP-induced human platelet aggregation in vitro in a dose-related manner, with potency comparable to *Allium sativum* and a mechanism the authors likened to clopidogrel; the active fraction was lipophilic. DOI: [10.1159/000196811](https://doi.org/10.1159/000196811).
- **Rousseau et al. (2022), *La Revue de médecine interne*** — a case report of life-threatening *Colchicum autumnale* poisoning caused by confusion with wild garlic, documenting that the two plants share both habitat and spring appearance. DOI: [10.1016/j.revmed.2022.04.022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.revmed.2022.04.022).
- **Amrollahi-Sharifabadi et al. (2013), *American Journal of Forensic Medicine and Pathology*** — a fatal colchicine poisoning following ingestion of a *Colchicum* species mistaken for wild garlic, confirmed by toxicological and botanical analysis. DOI: [10.1097/PAF.0000000000000059](https://doi.org/10.1097/PAF.0000000000000059).

**Evidence grade:** The cardiovascular/antiplatelet claims rest on limited in-vitro data and analogy to *A. sativum* — **preclinical, low-strength** for *A. ursinum* specifically. The traditional warming/digestive uses are **traditional**. The lookalike-toxicity hazard, by contrast, is documented by real clinical case reports and is the single most important fact about this plant.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Hot and Dry, third degree (Avicenna). Resolvent, attenuant, deobstruent; moderated in choleric/hot temperaments.
- **TCM (analogical, from cultivated garlic *da suan*):** warm, acrid/pungent; enters Spleen, Stomach, Lung; warms the middle, moves stagnation, resolves toxicity. Applied to ramsons by analogy only.
- **Ayurvedic (analogical):** *rasa* pungent (katu); *virya* heating (ushna); *vipaka* pungent; kapha-reducing, pitta-aggravating. Attribution is by genus analogy, not classical text.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** The cuneiform corpus records pungent *šūmū* — the cultivated alliums, garlic and onion and leek — worked by the *asû* against fever, swelling and "worms of the belly," within an aetiology that classes illness by the causal agency behind it and a drug partly by the agency it opposes. Wild woodland garlic of the *Allium ursinum* type is not itself identified in the tablets; it is placed here by genus and by pungency, an inference, and the standing blocking warning covers every Assyrian substance identification and tablet reference. No cultivation status can be asserted. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides gives a full therapeutic account of *skorodon*, the cultivated garlic — warming, acrid, diuretic, expelling tapeworms, clearing the vessels of the lung, answering venomous bites — and it is later commentators, not Dioscorides, who read the broad-leaved spring woodland garlics as a milder seasonal analogue of that bulb. He carries no subtle-body system into which any cultivation status could be set. What the tradition offers a cultivator of ramsons is a discipline of attention sharpened to a life-or-death edge here: gathering in the short pre-flowering window, and the sensory assay of odour that separates the true garlic-smelling leaf from the deadly scentless *Colchicum* and *Convallaria* it grows among. That is provenance and assay, not a vital-body doctrine. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** Avicenna classes garlic (*thum*) as hot and dry in the third or fourth degree — a strong resolvent and attenuant that thins gross humours, opens obstruction and warms a cold stomach, while cautioning that its heat inflames the blood and troubles hot temperaments — and ramsons inherits this humoral portrait as a wild, leafy form of the same acrid virtue. The tradition's strongest cultivation idea, conservation of the finite innate heat and radical moisture, is a frame of the whole regimen and not a status assigned to this pungent resolvent; no *mufarrih* / cardiac-*ruh* role is recorded. Note too that the classification described is that of cultivated garlic extended to the wild plant. The attestation is therapeutic. **(b) framed:** claimed by the tradition (via the allium portrait), no cultivation status sourced.

**Practitioner's note.** Ramsons is a seasonal food-cleanser, not a longevity substance. Its honest use is culinary-medicinal through the short spring window — pesto, soup, infused oil, crushed to wake the alliinase — for gentle vasodilation, mild antiplatelet action, and a warming nudge to sluggish digestion, exactly the "bear emerging from dormancy" turn its folk names mark. The bear-and-spring symbolism is an apt Kenetica reading of the threshold from rest into motion, but none of the three source traditions assigns this species a cultivation status, and their allium teachings describe the cultivated bulb. The one instruction that overrides everything: smell each leaf individually, because the plant that will kill you looks identical and simply has no garlic scent.

### Contraindications

**⚠️ LOOKALIKE POISONING — THE PRIMARY HAZARD.** Before flowering, *Allium ursinum* leaves are readily confused with the highly toxic ***Colchicum autumnale* (autumn crocus / meadow saffron)** and with ***Convallaria majalis* (lily of the valley)**, both of which grow in overlapping woodland habitats. Colchicum contains colchicine, for which there is no antidote; ingestion has caused documented fatalities (Rousseau et al. 2022; Amrollahi-Sharifabadi et al. 2013). Convallaria contains cardiac glycosides. **The reliable field check is odour: crushed ramsons smells strongly of garlic; Colchicum and Convallaria do not — but odour transfers to the hands, so smell each leaf individually, not a handful.** When in any doubt, do not harvest. Never gather where identification is uncertain.

Other cautions:
- **Antiplatelet / bleeding risk:** its clopidogrel-like action means it should be avoided in medicinal quantities alongside anticoagulants/antiplatelets and stopped well before surgery or dental extraction.
- **Reflux and hot temperaments:** the pungent heat may aggravate gastro-oesophageal reflux and, per Avicenna, choleric constitutions.
- **Pregnancy/lactation:** safe as an occasional food; medicinal doses [USER: verify] not established.
