# Mouse urine — "their urine" (of mice), a granary device in Bryan's English rendering of the Ebers Papyrus
## *The urine of the animal Bryan calls "Mouse"; Bryan prints no Egyptian word and no binomial, and the one passage found uses mouse excrement and urine against mice, not as a medicine* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian Tradition (EP).** The locus is Bryan's 1930 English *The Papyrus Ebers*. "Mouse" stands in Bryan's list of the animals "whose identity is not in doubt" (Chapter VII; `Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1392, after "Calf" and before "Porcupine"; name-index row `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` line 98, "no Egyptian word or binomial given in this list"). The parts-of-animals list (lines 1405-1415) includes "Excrement" and "Urine" (line 1415) but ties neither to any animal. The spine row reads "Mouse urine, Mus musculus" with the note "Ebers; Wikipedia/Ebers Papyrus" (`_sources/ebers.csv` line 80). Wikipedia was not used. A further list row, "Mouse" (line 136), cites the Ghalioungui 1987 translation, Glossary I; that glossary was not opened.

**The one passage.** The Bryan text on disk was searched for "mouse", "mice", "urine" and "excrement". Mouse urine occurs once, at lines 4236-4240, in Chapter XXIV "Domestic Hints" (heading line 4158): "TO PREVENT THE RODENTS EATING DURRA IN THE GRANARY. Gazelle's dung Put on the fire in the Granary. Cover the wall and the floor with Mice-excrement and with their urine. That will prevent the durra being eaten." Bryan's comment follows ("It would certainly prevent it being eaten by a good many of us"). The passage is a pest measure for stored grain; no person is treated and nothing is taken or applied to a body. The sentence does not say whether the excrement and urine are fresh, how they were gathered, or how much was used. The remaining uses of urine in the text on disk (for instance line 2712, onions cooked in salt and urine for leprous spots, or line 3419, "A Farmer's Urine") print "Urine" without naming an animal, or name a human source ("Human Urine", line 2125; "A Farmer's Urine", line 3419); none is said to be mouse urine.

**Related items, printed separately, not counted as the urine.** The mouse supplies other things in Bryan's text: "Tail-of-a-Mouse" taken in honey as a draught "for cooling the anus" (lines 1381 and 2008); "Mouse Oil" in a salve "to make supple the hardenings" (line 2191); and the mouse itself, whose use in the Predynastic period and as a remedy for a sick child is discussed in the introduction (lines 590-610; the whole animal, not its urine). A separate fat-based device for keeping mice from clothes, "Cat's Fat" (line 4177), is described in the entry on cat fat. None of these is the urine.

**What is not said.** The sources retrieved do not say that mouse urine was ever taken or applied as a medicine. The Ebers material is indication-organised, so the identity of any named substance is a modern hypothesis. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None can be stated for the material as the papyrus names it. In general terms, rodent urine and droppings can carry bacteria, viruses and parasites. Nothing in the sources retrieved says why the device was thought to work. This is a general statement, not a reading of Bryan.

---

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The one passage is a granary pest measure and not a remedy. Mouse urine is a hygiene hazard and has no use here. No modern protocol is supplied and none is implied.

---

### Preparation & Dosing

Only what Bryan reports: the wall and floor of the granary are "covered" with mouse excrement and urine, after gazelle dung has been burnt there (line 4240). No quantity is printed. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] As a general fact, rodent urine and droppings are a recognised route for infections in people, and spreading them on a grain store contaminates it. This point is not drawn from the papyrus.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested, and the passage is not a treatment for a person. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a problem, not what a substance does to the person's vital constitution. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); no recitation is printed with this device in Bryan's summary, which is no proof the papyrus gives none. The person as plural (*ka*, *ba*, *akh*, *ib*) is not engaged. No deity is attributed.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. The record is a household measure against rodents in grain. A cultivator has nothing to do with it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

---

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** rodent urine and droppings can carry disease and must not be put on food stores or on the body.
- **Food safety:** spreading them on a grain store contaminates it.
- **Ethics:** nothing in the sources supports taking mouse urine for any purpose.
