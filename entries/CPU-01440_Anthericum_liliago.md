# Anthericum liliago — Phalaggion (Dioscorides 3-122)

## *Anthericum liliago (the translators' suggestion; "Unbranched Lily Spiderwort"; also *A. ramosum*, branched)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification, and this row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) without being verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The SUGGESTED line also gives *Anthericum ramosum* (*Phalangium ramosam*), "Branched Lily Spiderwort", and the translators add "Phalangium species are now Anthericum."

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading at line 7631.

*3-122. PHALAGGION.* "Phalangium some call phalangite while others call it leucacantha. There are two or three (or more) stems distant from one another. The flowers are white like lilies with many in-cuts. The seed is thick and black similar to half of a lens [lentil] but much more slender. The small little root is thin and green while being pulled out of the earth; afterwards it contracts. It grows in hilly places. A decoction of the leaves, seeds, and flowers (taken as a drink with wine) helps those touched by scorpions or bitten by harvest spiders. It also dissolves griping." The chapter sits under the book's heading "Roots of Akanthoda or prickly plants". The index prints the name at p.507 (and "phalangium 468" is a separate index entry for another use of the word). The name is a Greek word for a venomous spider, and the chapter uses it for a plant.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a wine decoction of leaves, seeds and flowers for scorpion and spider bites and for griping. These are the text's claims. A bite or sting needs medical attention, and the Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

3-122 gives "a decoction of the leaves, seeds, and flowers (taken as a drink with wine)". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant by its parts, where it grows, what it is called, and what is claimed for it. 3-122 gives the form (two or three or more stems apart, white many-cut lily-like flowers, a thick black lentil-half seed, a thin green root that contracts after lifting), the hilly habitat, and the uses. It assigns no status. The identification between the Greek plant and *A. liliago* is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The chapter is a field description with a bite remedy, and the honest practice is to read it for how the plant was recognised.

### Contraindications

The identity of the Greek plant is uncertain, so no safety statement can be made for it. Venomous bites and stings are emergencies for medical care, not for a wine decoction. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
