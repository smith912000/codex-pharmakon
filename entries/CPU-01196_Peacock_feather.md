# Peacock feather — crown feather of the peacock, as printed in Clark's translation of the rGyud-bzhi
## *The feather of the peacock, a large pheasant-family bird; named in the Tibetan materia medica of animal origin and used there in a poison test* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan Tradition (FMT).** The text is Barry Clark's 1995 translation of the rGyud-bzhi, file `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`. The locus is the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (heading at line 792), section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), sub-group "Hair" (line 1192), which in this list includes feathers. The line (1193) reads: "Crown feather of peacock: cures poisoning, pus accumulation in the lungs, [and ear diseases, and subdues nagas]." The bracketed clause is Clark's interpolation (see below). No Tibetan name and no binomial is printed for the peacock in this line; Clark gives only "peacock". The project list has no `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` row for the feather that I found; its row for the flesh (line 346) is a different substance and is treated in the entry for Peacock flesh.

Clark says in his preface (line 80) that he "interpolated liberally from the Blue Aquamarine commentary", and the brackets mark added matter. The root-text sentence is therefore "cures poisoning, pus accumulation in the lungs"; the ear diseases and the *nagas* are commentary-derived. That reading of the bracket convention is an inference. [USER: verify]

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3557) has the parallel sentence: "A peacock's crown feather cures poisoning and lung abscesses." It gives no ear disease and no *nagas*, which agrees with the bracketing.

A second use of the feather is in the peacock-flesh line of the same chapter (Clark line 1168), in a parenthesis: "(Peacock's feather is highly recommended as a test for poisoning. The feather should be swallowed in the evening, removed from the stool next day and washed. If the feather has undergone no discoloration the presence of poison is clearly indicated; however, if discoloration has taken place, then some other type of disease is present)." This is a diagnostic procedure and not an indication. I report it as the text states it, without endorsing it (see Contraindications).

A related but separate peacock statement is in the dietary chapter. Poisoned food "has an unusual color, odor and taste, and when it is burned the smoke takes on the hue of a peacock's neck", and "If [such poisonous food] is seen by a crow it will caw [loudly], whereas a peacock will be pleased" (Clark line 726; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3094 has the same signs). Here the bird is a sign, not a medicine; its feather is not involved.

### Active Components

A feather is built of keratin, a structural protein; peacock plumage colour comes largely from the microscopic structure of the barbs, not from a pigment that is a drug. The text names no active compound. Swallowing a feather is a mechanical event, not a pharmacological one.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The Tibetan text says the crown feather "cures poisoning" and lung pus, and recommends swallowing the feather as a test for poison. The Codex does not recommend either, and gives no protocol. There is no reliable home test for poisoning, and delay in treatment of a real poisoning is dangerous; poisoning is an emergency for a poison-control service or hospital. Swallowing a feather can injure the throat or gut. Lung abscess is a serious infection that needs imaging and antibiotics. The text is retained for the record only.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only procedure the text gives is the swallow-and-recover test above, with its timing (evening swallow, next-day recovery from stool, washing). It is a test, not a treatment, and no number of feathers is given. For the indication line (1193) no preparation and no dose is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Tibetan: the line prints actions only ("cures poisoning, pus accumulation in the lungs"), with no taste, power or post-digestive taste. None is given for the feather in the sources retrieved. No other tradition is tagged.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* The crown feather is a therapeutic and diagnostic item in the animal-origin materia medica (lines 1168 and 1193). Its only non-medical reference is the bracketed, commentary-derived clause "subdues nagas" (line 1193); the Men-Tsee-Khang edition omits it (line 3557). Clark's translation does not say in these lines what *nagas* are to a cultivator, and no cultivation status, no essence-extraction (*bcud len*) role and no rejuvenation role is printed for the feather. The spirit-related clause is recorded and not interpreted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. A cultivator does not swallow peacock feathers. The text records the feather against poisoning and as a poison test, and the Codex keeps the record without carrying it into practice.

### Contraindications

Do not swallow a feather as a test for poison or as a remedy: it can lodge in the throat or gut, and a "negative" result gives false reassurance in a real poisoning. For suspected poisoning, contact emergency services or a poison-control centre. Lung abscess needs medical care. Peacock feathers from protected wild populations may be regulated; the legal status varies by country and species. [USER: verify] This entry gives no medical advice.
