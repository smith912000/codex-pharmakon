# Convallaria majalis — Ephemeron (Dioscorides 4-85)

## *Convallaria majalis (the translators' suggestion, from Linnaeus on the SUGGESTED line; "Lily of The Valley")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The line lists four names: "Ephemerum-non-letale, Lilium convallium [Fuchs], Lilium convallium album [Bauhin], Convallaria majalis [Linnaeus] — Lily of The Valley". Only the last is a Linnaean binomial, and the row takes it. The first name, which appears to mean "non-lethal ephemerum", is the translators' label and was not looked up further. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-85. EPHEMERON* at line 8796, SUGGESTED line at 8797, text at line 8798.

The whole chapter reads: "Ephemerum has leaves and a stalk similar to the lily but whiter, the flowers white, bitter, and the seed soft. The root lies underneath, single, the thickness of a finger, long, astringent and sweet smelling. It grows in woods and shady places. The root of this (used in a mouth rinse) is a remedy for toothache. The leaves (boiled in wine and smeared on) dissolve oedema and tumours without fluid. It is also called agrestis iris."

So Dioscorides describes the plant by comparison with the lily (leaves and stalk similar but whiter), by its white and bitter flowers and soft seed, and by a single finger-thick, long root that is astringent and sweet smelling. It grows in woods and shady places. The root is used in a mouth rinse for toothache, and the leaves, boiled in wine and smeared on, are said to dissolve oedema and "tumours without fluid". One other name is printed, *agrestis iris*. The chapter has no POISONOUS label and gives no internal use.

**Overlap with the preceding chapter.** Chapter 4-84 *KOLCHIKON* (line 8787) comes immediately before and is labelled POISONOUS. Its SUGGESTED line is *Colchicum autumnale* ("Meadow Saffron"), and its last sentence (line 8795) reads "It is also called ephemerum, or agrestis bulbus, and the Romans call it bulbus agrestis." Dioscorides there says colchicum, "eaten, kills by choking, similar to mushrooms", and that he described it "so that it may not lie hidden and be eaten instead of bulbus". So the name *ephemerum* is attached in the text to two different chapters, a deadly bulb in 4-84 and the plant of 4-85, which the translators' line calls the non-lethal ephemerum. The spine's existing colchicum entry I_486 describes *kolchikon* (ephemeron) as poisonous, without giving a chapter number. The two chapters are not merged here. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. The translators' plant, lily of the valley (*Convallaria majalis*, Asparagaceae in current classifications), is generally known to contain cardiac glycosides and to be poisonous if eaten. Whether Dioscorides' plant is that species is the translators' suggestion, not an established fact. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a root mouth rinse for toothache and leaves boiled in wine and smeared on for oedema and fluid-free tumours. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol: the identification is the translators', and the plant they propose is poisonous.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-85 gives two preparations: the root "used in a mouth rinse", and the leaves "boiled in wine and smeared on". No quantity, frequency or duration is given, and the medium of the rinse is not stated. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant (lily-like leaf and stalk but whiter, white bitter flowers, soft seed, one long finger-thick root), where it grows (woods and shady places) and what the root smells and tastes like. The adjoining chapter on a deadly bulb of the same name is a lesson in why that attention matters. It assigns the plant no status, and the identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The plant the translators propose is poisonous, so the toothache rinse and the leaf wash are history and not a practice. The only use for the chapter is as an example of describing a plant closely enough to tell it from a deadly neighbour.

### Contraindications

Lily of the valley is generally known to be poisonous, and a mouth rinse can be swallowed. Do not use any plant on the strength of this entry. The identification is the translators' suggestion, and the neighbouring chapter in the same text describes a deadly bulb under the same name. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
