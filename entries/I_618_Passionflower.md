## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted Name:** *Passiflora incarnata* L. (1753)
**Authority:** Linnaeus, *Species Plantarum*
**Family:** Passifloraceae (Passion Flower Family)
**Common Names:** Passionflower, Maypop, Purple Passionflower, Wild Passionflower, Apricot Vine, Fleur de la Passion (Fr.), Passionsblume (Ger.), Fiore della Passione (It.), Flor de la Pasión (Sp.), Corona de Cristo (tropical)

**Related Species — Passiflora genus (~550 species):**
- *Passiflora caerulea* L. — Blue Passionflower: ornamental; some use; lower chrysin
- *Passiflora quadrangularis* L. — Giant Granadilla: large fruit; food use
- *Passiflora edulis* Sims — Passion Fruit: primary food species; some bioactive compounds
- *Passiflora alata* Curtis — Winged-stem Passionflower: Brazilian pharmacopoeia (alternative)
- *Passiflora laurifolia* L. — Yellow Granadilla
European Pharmacopoeia accepts *P. incarnata* only; some national pharmacopoeias include *P. alata* or *P. caerulea*.

**Botanical Description:** Climbing perennial vine 2–10 m; stems woody at base; leaves 3-lobed (deeply palmately); distinctive tendrils at leaf axils; flowers singular, axillary, extremely complex structure:
- 5 petals + 5 sepals (similar, greenish-purple)
- Corona (coronal filaments): numerous, banded purple/white/blue — the "crown of thorns" in Christian iconography
- Stamens and pistil on an androgynophore (columnar structure elevating reproductive organs above petals)
- Fruit (*maypop*): oval berry, 4–6 cm, yellow-green when ripe, edible; pulp sweet-tart.

**Key Chemical Markers:** Flavonoids (chrysin — primary active; vitexin, orientin, isovitexin, vicenin-2, isorientin; schaftoside); indole alkaloids (harmane = harman, harmine, harmol, harmalol — beta-carbolines; **trace levels** in *P. incarnata*; significant in *P. harmala*); GABA; polysaccharides; coumarins (scopoletin, umbelliferone); essential oil.

**Pharmacopoeial Status:** European Pharmacopoeia 10th ed. (*Passiflorae herba*; aerial parts; minimum 1.5% flavonoids as vitexin); Commission E approved; ESCOP monograph; BHP; Austrian Pharmacopoeia.

**Critical Distinction from *Passiflora harmala* (Syrian Rue):** *Peganum harmala* (Zygophyllaceae — NOT *Passiflora*) is the primary source of harmine/harmaline beta-carbolines; *P. incarnata* contains only trace beta-carbolines insufficient for MAO inhibition at clinical doses.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Pharmacological Archetype:** Anxiolytic-sedative — chrysin (flavone GABA-A PAM) + possible beta-carboline component + direct GABA content → CNS inhibition.

**The Anxiolytic Archetype Across Asteraceae and Beyond:**
*Note:* Comparing passionflower's mechanism with St. John's Wort biapigenin (I-617) and chamomile apigenin (I-602): all three act at GABA-A receptor benzodiazepine binding site as positive allosteric modulators. This mechanistic convergence across taxonomically unrelated plants (Passifloraceae, Hypericaceae, Asteraceae) demonstrates how GABA-A positive allosteric modulation is a pharmacological mechanism repeatedly discovered by traditional healers through empirical observation of anxiolytic effect, despite entirely different plant chemistry.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

### 4.1 Flavonoids (Primary Class)

**Chrysin** (5,7-dihydroxyflavone):
- Content: 0.06–0.7% DW aerial parts
- GABA-A binding: **Positive allosteric modulation** at benzodiazepine binding site (BZD site); Ki ~3 µM in competitive binding with [³H]flunitrazepam
- Full structure-activity relationship established: 5,7-dihydroxyflavone skeleton is optimal for BZD site binding among flavones; 5-OH critical; 7-OH important
- Bioavailability: Low (poor water solubility; significant first-pass metabolism); liposomal delivery or EtOH extraction improves
- Aromatase inhibition: Chrysin has strong aromatase (CYP19A1) inhibitory activity in vitro; claimed by bodybuilders to boost testosterone; **not bioavailable** at oral doses in humans (clinical studies show no testosterone effect)

**Vitexin** (apigenin-8-C-glucoside):
- EP standardization marker (1.5% minimum total flavonoids as vitexin)
- Anti-inflammatory; anxiolytic in rodent EPM model (independently of chrysin)
- C-C glycosidic bond (like puerarin in kudzu I-593) → absorbed intact → not cleaved by gut enzymes

**Orientin, Isovitexin, Schaftoside:** Flavone-C-glycosides; antioxidant; mild sedative in animal models.

**Apigenin** (free): Small amounts; same GABA-A mechanism as chamomile (I-602)

### 4.2 Beta-Carboline Alkaloids (Trace)

**Harmane (harman), harmine, harmol, harmalol:**
- Present at **very low levels** in *P. incarnata* (0.0006–0.01% DW range)
- At these concentrations: insufficient for systemic MAO inhibition (therapeutic MAO inhibition requires ~0.1–1 mg/kg harmine; typical *P. incarnata* dose delivers <0.05 mg harmine)
- At trace levels: may contribute to mild anxiolytic effect through GABA or adenosine modulation
- **Contrast with *Peganum harmala* (Syrian Rue):** Contains 2–7% harmine/harmaline — sufficient for psychedelic MAO inhibition; the harmala alkaloids are named for that plant, not *Passiflora*

### 4.3 GABA (Free Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid)

- Measurable free GABA in *P. incarnata* leaf preparations (0.01–0.1 mg/g)
- CNS effect from dietary GABA controversial: GABA is impermeable to blood-brain barrier under normal conditions; intestinal/vagal GABA effects not excluded
- Not primary mechanism for anxiolytic effect

### 4.4 Coumarins

- Scopoletin, umbelliferone: mild spasmolytic; anti-inflammatory; analgesic

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 GABA-A Positive Allosteric Modulation

**Primary mechanism:** Chrysin (and to a lesser degree vitexin, orientin, apigenin) → benzodiazepine (BZD) binding site on GABA-A receptor (between α and γ subunits) → allosteric enhancement of GABA binding → increased Cl⁻ conductance → neuronal hyperpolarization → reduced excitability.

**Selectivity:** Chrysin shows subunit preference for α2, α3 subunits (anxiolytic-relevant) over α1 (sedative-hypnotic); theoretically, this profile predicts anxiolysis with less sedation than full benzodiazepines — though differentiation limited in clinical practice.

**Comparison with Diazepam:** Chrysin binds BZD site with Ki ~3 µM; diazepam Ki ~1 nM — ~3,000× lower affinity. At clinical passionflower doses delivering ~1–10 mg chrysin orally (with <20% bioavailability → systemic exposure ~0.2–2 mg), CNS chrysin concentrations remain below fully saturating receptor. Partial agonism at sub-saturating concentrations → mild anxiolysis without sedation/dependence potential of full benzodiazepines.

### 5.2 Adenosine System Modulation

- Beta-carboline trace content → adenosine receptor modulation (indirect evidence)
- Free GABA → possible gut-brain axis effects

### 5.3 MAO Inhibition (Insufficient at Clinical Doses)

- Trace harmine: MAO-A inhibition detectable in vitro; NOT clinically relevant at *P. incarnata* doses
- No tyramine dietary restriction required (confirmed by clinical evidence)

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### 6.1 Anxiety — Level B

**Akhondzadeh et al. 2001** (*Journal of Clinical Pharmacy and Therapeutics*): RCT (n=36); *P. incarnata* extract (45 drops/day) vs. oxazepam (30 mg/day) for 4 weeks; generalized anxiety disorder; Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAM-A): **no significant difference** in efficacy; oxazepam faster onset (week 1); passionflower significantly less impairment of job performance (p=0.049). Level B — non-inferiority to low-dose benzodiazepine with fewer cognitive adverse effects.

**Aslanargun et al. 2012** (*J Anesthesiol Clin Pharmacol*): RCT (n=60); oral passionflower vs. placebo 90 minutes before surgery; STAI-State anxiety significantly lower in passionflower group (p=0.002); no sedation difference; Level B.

**Movafegh et al. 2008** (*Anesth Analg*): Passionflower premedication 90 minutes before spinal anesthesia; significant anxiolysis vs. placebo (p<0.05); no sedation or hemodynamic differences. Level B.

### 6.2 Insomnia — Level B

**Ngan & Conduit 2011** (*Phytother Res*): Crossover RCT (n=41); passionflower tea (1 cup) vs. parsley tea (control); significant improvement in sleep quality diary score; Level B (single-dose; self-report).

**Combined herbal insomnia studies** (often with valerian): multiple positive RCTs; passionflower component contribution unclear from combined data.

### 6.3 ADHD (Children) — Level B

**Movafegh et al. 2005** (*J Altern Complement Med*): Passionflower extract vs. methylphenidate for ADHD in children; comparable reduction in hyperactivity scores; fewer side effects (including significantly less anorexia). Level B (open-label; limited by design).

### 6.4 Opioid Withdrawal — Level B

**Akhondzadeh et al. 2001b** (*J Clin Pharm Ther*): RCT (n=65); passionflower + clonidine vs. clonidine alone for opiate detoxification; combination superior for psychological symptoms (anxiety, agitation, sleep) but not physical symptoms; significant p=0.016 at day 14. Level B.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

### 7.1 First Nations Use (North America)

**Cherokee:** Passionflower root infusion for insomnia, nervous excitability, hysteria; leaf poultice for inflammation.
**Algonquin:** Leaf tea for "boils and headache"; root for liver conditions.
**Creek:** Fruit eaten fresh; root tea for general tonic.

**Maypop etymology:** The common name "Maypop" refers to the popping sound made when the ripe fruit is stepped on. Also possibly from *maracock* (Algonquin name for the plant; recorded by Captain John Smith, Virginia, 1612).

### 7.2 The Christian Naming — Passion Narrative Iconography

**Fra Michele Mercati** (Spanish botanist; 1569): First formal European naming as *Flos passionis* (Flower of the Passion). The flower's extraordinary morphological complexity was immediately interpreted through the Christian Passion narrative:

| Flower structure | Christian interpretation |
|-----------------|-------------------------|
| Corona filaments | Crown of thorns |
| 5 petals + 5 sepals | 10 apostles (Peter and Judas excluded) |
| 3 styles | 3 nails |
| 5 anthers | 5 wounds |
| Radially symmetrical flower | Halo |
| Tendrils | Whips |
| Leaf shape (sometimes 3-lobed) | Holy Trinity |

The naming was immediately adopted and used as a missionary tool — Spanish Jesuits showed Native Americans the flower as evidence that the Christian narrative was embedded in New World nature. This represents one of the most significant acts of botanical-religious interpretation in the history of phytotherapy.

**Missiology and plant medicine:** The Passion Flower's rapid European uptake as both religious symbol and medicinal plant demonstrates how symbolic/religious resonance can accelerate plant integration into medical practice — the religious naming elevated its pharmacopoeial status before its chemistry was understood.

### 7.3 Sedative Herbs Historical Crossroads — 19th Century Europe

**1867:** Passionflower first described medicinally by Dr. L. Phares (Mississippi) in *New Orleans Medical and Surgical Journal*: reported use by a formerly enslaved woman for sleep induction; investigated pharmacologically; "proved successful in 6 cases of infants with spasmodic croup." This clinical documentation launched European pharmacological interest.

**1898:** Commission on passionflower submitted to American Medical Association; sedative properties confirmed. American Homeopathic Pharmacopoeia included *Passiflora incarnata* 1882.

### 7.4 Passion Fruit vs. Passionflower Medicine

The edible fruit (*Passiflora edulis* — Passion Fruit/Granadilla, native to South America) is widely consumed globally as food/juice but has minimal medicinal phytochemical content compared to *P. incarnata* leaves/stems. The visual association between passion fruit (familiar food) and passionflower (medicinal plant) creates commercial misidentification opportunity — *P. incarnata* is the medicinal species; *P. edulis* fruit juice has marginal medicinal flavonoid content.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Therapeutic Indications

**Commission E approved:**
- Nervousness and insomnia (mild to moderate anxiety and sleep disturbance)

**Level B evidence:**
- Generalized anxiety disorder (non-inferiority to low-dose oxazepam)
- Preoperative anxiety
- ADHD (pediatric; limited evidence)
- Opioid withdrawal adjunct

### 8.2 Dosing

| Form | Dose | Indication |
|------|------|------------|
| Dried aerial parts (infusion) | 2–4g in 150mL water, 1–3×/day | Anxiety, insomnia |
| Tincture (1:8, 25% ethanol) | 0.5–2 mL 3×/day | Anxiety; acute |
| Dry extract (standardized 3.5% isovitexin or 1.5% vitexin) | 100–300mg 3×/day | Standardized dose |
| Commission E daily dose | 4–8g dried herb | Nervousness/insomnia |
| Pre-procedure anxiolysis | 400mg standardized extract, 90 min before | Preoperative |

### 8.3 Safety and Contraindications

**Contraindications:**
- **Pregnancy:** Uterotonic effects reported at high doses; traditional contraindication
- Severe liver disease (limited data; coumarins and alkaloids require hepatic metabolism)

**Drug interactions:**
- **CNS depressants (benzodiazepines, alcohol, opioids, barbiturates):** Additive CNS depression; reduce dose of either
- **MAO inhibitors:** Theoretical interaction (trace beta-carbolines); generally considered low-risk at standard doses; avoid in patients on phenelzine/selegiline
- **Warfarin:** Coumarins theoretically may affect coagulation; monitor; clinical significance unclear

**Side effects:** Generally well-tolerated; rare: nausea, dizziness, drowsiness (dose-dependent), tachycardia (high doses — paradoxical effect). No dependence/withdrawal documented (advantage over benzodiazepines).

**Driving:** Low doses unlikely to impair; high doses may cause drowsiness; caution.
