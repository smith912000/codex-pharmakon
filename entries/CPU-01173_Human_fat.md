# Human fat — as printed in Clark's translation of the rGyud-bzhi: "Human fat" (listed with the animal fats; no Tibetan-script name is printed for this item)
## *Rendered body fat of a human being, as named in the Tibetan materia medica of animal origin* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan Tradition (FMT).** The only text locus read for this entry is Barry Clark's 1995 translation of the rGyud-bzhi (*The Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine*), file `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`. Under the heading "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147) the text says: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body." In the "Fat" group (line 1180) it prints four items in a row, and the last is: "Human fat: subdues wind disorders and heals psoriasis." The other fats in that group are snake fat, deer fat and pig fat. The project list `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` (line 363) carries the same item as "Mitshil", class animal, source "rGyud-bzhi, Explanatory Tantra ch.20 (Efficacy of Medicinal Substances)". The text I read gives no Tibetan-script name for human fat, so the transliteration "Mitshil" rests on that list row alone. [USER: verify]

The same animal-origin group also lists other human materials (a cemetery skull and bone at line 1160, human flesh at line 1162, human brain at line 1182, human urine at line 1197 and human excreta at line 1199). Human fat is therefore one entry in a general class of body-derived substances, not an isolated item.

Clark himself remarks, in his introduction (line 57), that "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts." This entry records what is documented. It does not describe a current practice.

### Active Components

Human fat is adipose tissue. In general terms adipose tissue is made mostly of triglycerides (fatty-acid esters of glycerol), with smaller amounts of cholesterol, phospholipids and fat-soluble compounds. Its exact composition varies with the individual's diet and body site. Nothing further is stated from the sources, and no figures are given.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The Codex does not recommend using human fat, in any form, as a practice. The Tibetan text records two indications (wind disorders, psoriasis). That is a statement of what the tradition wrote, not an invitation to reproduce it. Obtaining human tissue raises legal, ethical and consent problems in nearly every jurisdiction, and the material can carry blood-borne and prion-type infections (see Contraindications). Nothing in the sources retrieved offers a protocol, and none is invented here. Anyone who wants a topical emollient or a fat-based carrier for a Kenetica application has many ordinary alternatives that do not involve human remains.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Tibetan: Clark's general materia medica sets out the six tastes, eight powers and three post-digestive tastes (introduction, line 57; Specific Section, from line 796). For human fat, the line read prints no taste, power or post-digestive taste. Only the two actions above are given. The "all meats are sweet" statement at line 673 is made of the meat sections listed at line 672, which name animals but not humans, so I do not carry it over to human fat. No other tradition is tagged, so no other classification is given.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* The item sits in the Explanatory Tantra's list of medicines of animal origin (Clark line 1147), with a therapeutic statement only: "subdues wind disorders and heals psoriasis" (line 1180). No cultivation status, no essence-extraction (*bcud len*) role and no rejuvenation role is printed for it in the lines read. (Rejuvenation is mentioned in Clark for other substances, e.g. peacock flesh at line 678, but not here.) Its status in this tradition is therapeutic only. Clark's remark that few animal products are now used (line 57) is a further reason not to assign it any ongoing role.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. A cultivator does not use this substance. The Tibetan text lists it as a medicine for two named complaints, and the Codex keeps it as a record of what was written, not as a practice.

### Contraindications

Not a recommended practice. Human tissue can transmit blood-borne viruses, bacteria and prion disease, and no source retrieved describes any screening or sterilising step. Taking, selling or using human remains is illegal or tightly regulated in most places, and it raises questions of consent and dignity. Where psoriasis or a "wind"-type disorder is the concern, see a qualified clinician; this entry is not clinical advice.
