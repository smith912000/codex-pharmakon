# Phyteuma spicatum — Phuteuma (Dioscorides 4-130)

## *Phyteuma spicatum, the first of two names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-130 ("Spiked Horned Rampion"), with Phyteuma orbiculare ("Round-headed Rampion") printed beside it* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 9183) prints two names with no choice between them: "Phyteuma spicatum — Spiked Horned Rampion" and "Phyteuma orbiculare — Round-headed Rampion". The row takes the first. It was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-130. PHUTEUMA*, heading at line 9182, SUGGESTED line at 9183, text at line 9184. It stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), after the chapter on *cynoglosson* (the paragraph that ends at line 9181) and before *4-131. LEONTOPODION*. The translators' index gives both *Phyteuma orbiculare* and *Phyteuma spicatum* at page 679.

The whole chapter is one sentence: "Phyteuma has leaves similar to radicula only smaller; abundant seed, bored through; and a thin little root close to the earth which some consider good for a love medicine."

That is all the chapter prints. It gives a comparison for the leaf (*radicula*, which the translators gloss as "[radish]" where the word appears elsewhere in the book, for example at lines 5284 and 8614), a note on the seed ("abundant", "bored through", a phrase the chapter does not explain), and a note on the root ("thin", "close to the earth"). The one use is a hearsay use: the root is something "some consider good for a love medicine". Dioscorides does not say that he holds this view, and he gives no preparation, no way of using the root and no measure. The chapter prints no other name for the plant: the usual closing list of Greek, Roman and foreign names is absent.

**Neighbouring chapter.** The next chapter, 4-131 *LEONTOPODION*, also reports a love medicine ("prescribed for love medicines to be hanged on one"). It is the subject of the separate entry I_1118 (*Leontopodium vulgare*). The two plants are different chapters and are not read as one. The name *rampion* also appears on the SUGGESTED line of chapter 2-135 (entry I_1036, *Campanula rapunculus*, "Rampion [Mabberley]"), which is a different chapter, so the shared English word is not read as a shared plant.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read, and nothing is stated here about the composition of the translators' *Phyteuma* species. It is a general fact that *Phyteuma* is a genus of the bellflower family (Campanulaceae). [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides reports, as an opinion held by "some", that the root is good for a love medicine. That is a report of belief, and the Codex cannot assess it as a therapeutic claim. The Codex gives no protocol. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives no preparation and no way of using the root. No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. It prints the leaves as like *radicula* but smaller, the seed as abundant and "bored through", and the root as thin and close to the earth.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter does record a love-medicine use, but in the form "some consider", which reports what others hold and does not endorse it. Recording is not endorsing. What the chapter offers a reader is the small discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: a leaf compared with a known leaf, a seed noted as bored through, a root noted as thin and near the surface. The plant is given no status beyond that. The identification is the translators'.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It offers a short description and a reported love-medicine use, with no preparation, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. A love medicine is not a safety-tested remedy, and nothing here is advice to take any root. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion between two species, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
