# Pig bone — gtig.mug, as printed in Clark's translation of the rGyud-bzhi
## *The bone of the domestic pig; named in the Tibetan materia medica of animal origin* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan Tradition (FMT).** The text is Barry Clark's 1995 translation of the rGyud-bzhi, file `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`. The locus is the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (heading at line 792), section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), sub-group "Bone" (line 1159). The line (1160) is a long run of bones, and the pig entry reads: "Pig bone: [has nine names, for example gtig.mug.] It cures brown phlegm." The neighbours in the run include "Tiger bone: [has fifteen names]. It increases bone resin", "Cowrie shell ... hemostatic", "Sheep bone: cures (all types of) wind disorders" and human bones (skull, scapula, hip). `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 338 ("Pig bone", animal, id_uncertain "yes") points to this chapter.

The brackets in the printed text mark added matter: Clark says in his preface (line 80) that he "interpolated liberally from the Blue Aquamarine commentary". The phrase "[has nine names, for example gtig.mug.]" is therefore commentary-derived, and "It cures brown phlegm" is the part to take as the root-text statement. That reading of the convention is an inference. [USER: verify] The printed *gtig.mug* is given only as an example of the nine names; I do not take it to be the only or the standard Tibetan term.

"Brown phlegm" is a nosological term in the same translation. It appears among the disorders that "pervade the outer and inner" body (line 556) and in the fresh-onset type of phlegm disorder (line 564: "a single phlegm disorder, brown phlegm, a bile disorder and a chronic consumptive disorder caused by poisoning"). I did not read a definition beyond these lists. [USER: verify]

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3523) has the parallel sentence: "Pig bone cures baekan mukpo." That edition renders the disorder by its Tibetan name (*baekan mukpo*), which appears in its disorder lists (for example line 2385, "The baekan mukpo disorders are of ten types"). I treat the two as parallel renderings of the same line and say so as an inference. [USER: verify]

Other pig parts appear in the same chapter and are not carried over to the bone: pig tongue "removes bone warts" (Clark 1170), pig blood "collects [scattered types of] poisoning and brown phlegm" (1176), pig fat "collects poison, heals psoriasis [and cures baldness]" (1180), and pig's stool (1199, see the entry for Pig dung). In the diet chapter "Pork is cool and light, heals wounds and eliminates brown phlegm" (line 677). The same disorder, brown phlegm, is thus named for the bone, the blood and the meat.

### Active Components

Bone is mineral (calcium phosphate in the form of hydroxyapatite) in a collagen matrix, with marrow in the cavities. The text names no active compound. As a general fact, animal bone is a source of calcium and phosphate when ground, but nothing here supports a specific effect.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The Tibetan text says pig bone "cures brown phlegm". The Codex does not recommend using it for that or anything else, and gives no protocol. The text offers no description of a modern disease for "brown phlegm", so the line cannot be matched to a clinical condition without invention. The line is retained for the record.

### Preparation & Dosing

The line gives no preparation (ash, powder, decoction, burnt) and no dose; it does not say whether the bone is raw or treated. In the same run another bone is given in "ash form" (the weather-beaten human scapula, line 1160), which shows that the list can specify form when it wishes to, and that this line does not. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] General fact: powdered bone is a calcium and phosphate source and not a recognised treatment for any disorder.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Tibetan: the line prints an action only ("cures brown phlegm"), with no taste, power or post-digestive taste for the bone. None is given in the sources retrieved. The pork statement (line 677, "cool and light") concerns the meat and is not applied to the bone. No other tradition is tagged.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* The pig bone appears once in the animal-origin materia medica, in the "Bone" run (line 1160), with a therapeutic action only. No cultivation status, no essence-extraction (*bcud len*) role and no rejuvenation role is printed for it in the lines read. The line mentions no spirit and no protective use. Unlike some neighbouring items in the Bone run (for example the human skull from a cemetery and the bones of those killed by thunderbolts) it carries no special provenance either.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. A cultivator does not use pig bone. The text records it against "brown phlegm", a term that cannot be matched to a modern condition on the sources retrieved, and the Codex keeps the record without carrying it into practice.

### Contraindications

Bone from slaughter or scavenged sources can carry pathogens, and swallowing bone fragments risks injury to the throat and gut. Pig products are prohibited in some religious traditions; respect them. Raw or undercooked pork carries risks (including *Trichinella* and tapeworm cysts). Do not use any animal bone as a remedy for a disorder you cannot identify; seek medical care. This entry gives no medical advice.
