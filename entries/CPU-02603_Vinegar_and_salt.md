# Vinegar and salt — Oxalme (ὀξάλμη, Dioscorides 5-23)

## *Acetum salsum, a mixture of vinegar and salt used as an applied medicine; the identification is the chapter's own: the translator glosses "Vinegar and Salt" and the text names the Latin acetum salsum, so confidence in "salted vinegar" is high, and it is a made preparation, filed under plants because vinegar is a wine product* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk under this name.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 8 November 2026 as an unverified placeholder; this entry replaces it. Oxymel (vinegar, salt and honey), the next chapter before it, and thyme vinegar after it, are separate preparations.

**Dioscorides (DMM).** The chapter is 5-23, "OXALME", glossed "Vinegar and Salt" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 9882 to 9883). Full text: "Acetum salsum (applied with hot cloths) is good for gangrenous ulcers, rotten ulcers, dog bites and bites from venomous creatures. It stops discharges of blood from those who are castrated, poured warm into the wound straight after the surgeon has done. It helps a perineum that is fallen down, and it is given as a suppository for dysentery with gangrenous ulceration, but afterwards you must give an enema with milk. Sipped up and gargled it kills leeches, and cleans dandruff and running ulcers on the head."

The chapter sits in the book on wines and their derivatives, between oxymel (5-22, line 9880) and thyme vinegar (5-24, "thumoxalme"), and the translator's page break falls between 5-23 and 5-24. The preceding chapter on oxymel gives the recipe of vinegar, sea salt, honey and water boiled until it has boiled ten times; that is a different preparation and is not folded in here.

### Active Components

The source names no constituent. In general terms, vinegar is a dilute solution of acetic acid (with small amounts of other organic acids and wine residues), and salt is sodium chloride; together they make an acid brine that is irritating to tissue and antimicrobial in strength. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Applied hot on cloths for gangrenous and rotten ulcers, dog bites and venomous bites; poured warm into the wound after surgery to stop blood discharge; for a prolapsed perineum; as a suppository for dysentery with gangrenous ulceration, followed by a milk enema; sipped and gargled to kill leeches; and to clean dandruff and running ulcers on the head.
- **What the text does not say.** No proportion of salt to vinegar, no duration, and no number of applications.

The Codex does not translate these terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend putting salted vinegar into a wound, the rectum or the throat.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives the uses as applications (hot cloths, warm pouring, suppository, enema afterwards, gargle) but no recipe and no quantity (lines 9882 to 9883). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Acetic acid and strong salt solutions are antimicrobial but they are also caustic to damaged tissue and delay healing; neither is a modern wound treatment. Dog and venomous bites need urgent medical care, including rabies assessment and antivenom where relevant. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** The chapter prints no warm or cold, moist or dry quality and no taste. The only printed condition is that it is applied or poured warm. Nothing else is imported.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter assigns no cultivation status. What the chapter shows is a discipline of attention, in the narrow sense that the temperature of the application, the moment of the pouring ("straight after the surgeon has done") and the follow-up (the milk enema) are specified.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing for a cultivator. Salted vinegar is a kitchen item, and the chapter's wound uses are not for the home. The only transferable lesson is attention to timing.

### Contraindications

- **Wounds and bites.** Do not pour acid brine into wounds; clean with water and seek care. Dog and venomous bites are emergencies.
- **Rectal and throat use.** Do not use. Acid brine damages mucous membranes.
- **Eyes.** Avoid.
- **Identity.** The proportions are not stated in the source.
