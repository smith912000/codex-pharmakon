**Jimsonweed — The Jamestown Incident:**
The etymology "Jimsonweed" derives from "Jamestown Weed" — a 1676 colonial American incident of mass tropane alkaloid poisoning with significant historical documentation. Robert Beverley's *The History and Present State of Virginia* (1705) records the episode:

British troops were sent to Jamestown, Virginia, to suppress Bacon's Rebellion. While there, some soldiers gathered young *Datura stramonium* plants from the wild — apparently mistaking them for edible vegetation or deliberately consuming them out of curiosity. The result was a classic anticholinergic toxidrome:

*"The James Town Weed...was gather'd very young for a Boil'd Salad, by some of the Soldiers sent thither to quell the Rebellion of Bacon...and some of them eat plentifully of it, the Effect of which was a very pleasant Comedy; for they turn'd natural Fools upon it for several Days: One would blow up a Feather in the Air; another would dart Straws at it with much Fury; and another stark naked was sitting up in a Corner, like a Monkey, grinning and making Mows at them; a Fourth would fondly kiss, and paw his Companions, and snear in their Faces, with a Countenance more antick than any in a Dutch Droll. In this frantick Condition they were confin'd, lest they should in their Folly destroy themselves...In 11 Days return'd to themselves again, remembering nothing that had pass'd."*

This is a precise clinical description of the scopolamine-dominant anticholinergic delirium: loss of insight, delusional behavior, preserved physical functioning but cognitive disruption, and complete anterograde amnesia ("remembering nothing that had passed"). Eleven days for recovery is consistent with high-dose scopolamine toxicity with slow hepatic clearance.

**Botanical Identification:**
Annual herb 50-150 cm; leaves large (10-20 cm), irregularly deeply lobed, unpleasant odor; flowers white or pale purple, erect (pointing upward — distinct from *Brugmansia* which hangs downward), 5-8 cm long, funnel-shaped; fruit = distinctive spiny capsule 3-5 cm ("thorn apple") with dense sharp spines; seeds numerous, black, kidney-shaped; entire plant glandular and malodorous. The spiny capsule is the most reliable field identification feature — no edible plant has a similar fruiting structure.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Core Cross-Traditional Functions:**
1. **Surgical anesthesia** — SBJ TCM tradition (Hua Tuo); Islamic medicine; Ayurvedic; the New World counterpart of the Old World soporific sponge tradition
2. **Analgesic / antispasmodic** — Ayurvedic (controlled dose); Islamic; TCM for wind-cold convulsions
3. **Respiratory (asthma)** — smoking *Datura* leaves for bronchodilation; 19th-20th century "asthma cigarettes" = dried *Datura* leaves
4. **Ritual / visionary** — Aztec (*Toloache*); North American indigenous (initiation; rites of passage); South American via *Brugmansia*
5. **Criminal poisoning** — India: "datura poisoning" using *D. metel* seeds in food/drink; well-documented in Indian criminal justice records

**Hua Tuo and TCM Surgical Anesthesia:**
The most celebrated TCM reference for *Datura* is the legend of Hua Tuo (华佗, c. 140-208 CE), the most famous physician-surgeon of ancient China. According to Hua Tuo's biography in the *Records of the Three Kingdoms* (三国志), he used a *mafei san* (麻沸散 — "hemp boiling powder") anesthetic formula before conducting open abdominal surgery on patients, allowing operations that would have been impossible without anesthesia. While the precise composition of *mafei san* is lost, historical commentary and pharmacological analysis have suggested it contained *Datura* (曼陀罗) as a primary anticholinergic-narcotic, possibly combined with Cannabis (*dà má*, 大麻 — hence "hemp" in the name), opium (*ya pian*), and other herbs. If authentic, this would place Chinese surgical anesthesia in the 2nd century CE — approximately 1,700 years before William Morton's 1842 ether demonstration.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Tropane Alkaloids:**
| Compound | Typical % DW (leaf) | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| L-Hyoscyamine | 0.2-0.45% | mAChR antagonist; peripheral anticholinergic |
| L-Scopolamine | 0.05-0.15% | CNS-penetrant mAChR; sedation; delirium |
| Atropine | Variable | Racemic hyoscyamine |
| Scopolamine N-oxide | Minor | Less active metabolite |

**Total Alkaloid Content:**
*Datura* species generally contain the highest total tropane alkaloid concentrations of any accessible Solanaceae: seeds (the most concentrated part) can reach 0.4-0.8% total alkaloids DW; leaves and stems: 0.3-0.5% DW. This exceeds typical concentrations in belladonna leaf (0.2-0.6%) and henbane seed (0.1-0.3%). The high concentration combined with extreme variability between individuals, populations, and seasons makes dosing from plant material extraordinarily dangerous.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

Same fundamental mechanism as I-569 Belladonna (atropine mAChR antagonist) and I-570 Henbane (scopolamine dominant): see those entries for the full mAChR mechanism.

**Datura-Specific Notes:**
- **Higher total alkaloid content** than most belladonna or henbane preparations → lower fatal dose relative to dose required for therapeutic effect → narrower therapeutic index
- **Extreme variability** of alkaloid content between plant parts, individual plants, geographic populations, and seasons → "therapeutic dose" from one plant may be five times the "therapeutic dose" from another → consistent with the historical record of unpredictable outcomes with *Datura* preparations
- **Asthma application (historical):** Inhalation of *Datura* smoke → direct bronchial absorption of scopolamine + hyoscyamine → immediate bronchial smooth muscle relaxation (mAChR block) + reduced bronchial secretion → bronchodilation. "Datura asthma cigarettes" (also called "stramonium cigarettes" or "Asthmador") were sold commercially in the USA and UK through the early 20th century; FDA restricted them in the 1970s due to toxicity.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Overall Evidence Level: D** — no current therapeutic botanical use; pharmaceutical derivatives only.

**Level D — Bronchodilation:**
*Datura/stramonium* extract in historical clinical studies (pre-1970) demonstrated bronchodilatory effect in asthmatic patients. Mechanism validated via scopolamine/atropine bronchodilatory pharmacology; replaced by safer beta-2 agonists.

**Historical Record (Level C):**
- Hua Tuo mafei san: TCM surgical anesthesia; 2nd century CE; historical documentation exists; no controlled evidence
- Colonial poisoning records: consistent with scopolamine anticholinergic toxidrome
- Indian Ayurvedic texts: controlled low-dose Dhatūra preparations (Level C traditional)

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

*Covered extensively in Botanical Identification and Cross-Traditional Presence sections above. Additional notes:*

**Datura Poisoning in India:**
Criminal poisoning using *Datura metel* seeds has been documented in Indian criminal records since at least the 18th century and continues to be reported in the 21st century. The modus operandi: seeds or seed powder are added to food, sweets, or drinks offered to travelers or strangers; the anticholinergic delirium incapacitates victims; thieves or criminals can then rob, assault, or traffic victims with no resistance. The victims typically remember nothing (scopolamine anterograde amnesia). "Datura poisoning" or "Dhatura poisoning" appears as a recognized category in Indian forensic medicine texts and court records. This criminal use — exploiting the precise pharmacological properties of amnesia + incapacitation — appears independently across multiple cultures, reflecting a consistent folk understanding of the plant's pharmacological profile.

**The Toloache Tradition — Aztec and Mesoamerican Ritual:**
In the Aztec religious tradition, *Datura* (*toloatzin*, "the one that makes the head nod") was associated with Xochipilli, the god of flowers, beauty, and hallucinogenic plants. *Toloache* (as the plant is called in Mexican Spanish, from Nahuatl) was reportedly used in religious ceremonies and divination rituals. Contemporary indigenous communities in Oaxaca, Puebla, and other Mexican states still use *Datura inoxia* in traditional healing contexts — typically to diagnose the cause of illness through hallucinatory visions, administered by a trained curandero who knows the dosing protocols. The difference between traditional controlled ceremonial use (by trained practitioners with extensive safety knowledge) and recreational recreational use is the critical safety variable.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**CURRENT STATUS: No therapeutic botanical *Datura* preparation in clinical use.**

**Historical pharmaceutical derivatives:**
- Stramonium powder/cigarettes for asthma: historically used; discontinued
- All current therapeutic applications have been replaced by pharmaceutical scopolamine, atropine, or beta-2 bronchodilators

**SAFETY — CRITICAL:**
- *Datura* has caused thousands of documented poisoning deaths and many more serious injuries
- No safe botanical dose protocol exists for self-administration
- Recreational/experimental use carries significant fatality risk due to extreme alkaloid variability
- Criminal poisoning vector — be aware of symptoms in suspicious circumstances
