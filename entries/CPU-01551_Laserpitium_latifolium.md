# Laserpitium latifolium — Seseli aithiopikon (Dioscorides 3-61)

## *Laserpitium latifolium [Linnaeus], the last of four names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-61 (with "Dauci alterum genus", Seseli aethiopicum [Fuchs] and Libanotis latifolia altera [Bauhin]); "Broad-leaved Laserwort" [Loudon]* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 7077) offers four names with no choice among them, and the row takes the Linnaean one. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-61. SESELI AITHIOPIKON* ("Ethiopian seselis"), heading at line 7076, SUGGESTED line at 7077, text at line 7078. It stands in Book Three, in the section headed "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants". The translators' index gives *Laserpitium latifolium* and "Laserwort" at page 436 (line 16281).

The whole chapter reads: "Ethiopian seselis has leaves similar to cissus [2-210] yet smaller and somewhat long, similar to those of periclymenom. It is a large shrub with branches of about two feet, on which are stems eighteen inches long. The little heads are like dill; the seeds black, thick like wheat, yet sharper and more fragrant than the Massaleotican [3-60], and very sweet. It produces similar effects. The Egyptians call it cyonophricen."

So the chapter is a description and a pointer. It gives the leaves (like those of *cissus*, smaller and somewhat long, like those of *periclymenom*; neither comparison chapter was read into this entry), the habit (a large shrub with branches of about two feet and stems eighteen inches long), the heads ("like dill") and the seed ("black, thick like wheat", sharper, more fragrant and "very sweet"). It gives no habitat and no use of its own. It gives one foreign name, the Egyptian *cyonophricen*. The statement "It produces similar effects" depends on the chapter before it.

**The chapter it points to.** The preceding chapter, *3-60. SESELI MASSALEOTIKON* (heading at line 7072), has the SUGGESTED line "Seseli massiliense [Fuchs], Gingidium umbella oblonga [Bauhin], Daucus visagna [Linnaeus], Ammi visagna [in Sprague], Daucus visagna — Pick-tooth, Tooth Pick". It says of its plant: "The seed and root are warming: taken as a drink they cure slow painful urination and orthopnoea [form of asthma]. They are good for urinary constriction and epilepsy, induce the menstrual flow, are abortifacient, and are effective for all disorders within. They cure old coughs, and taken as a drink with wine the seed helps digestion and dissolves griping. It is also good for cooling [sudden] fevers, and is taken as a drink with pepper and wine for chills in childbirth. It is given to goats and other cattle as a drink for hastening delivery. It is also called sphagnon." Chapter 3-61 does not say that it means exactly this passage by "similar effects", though 3-60 is the only chapter it names, and the existing entry I_1024 (*Athamanta cervaria*, 3-62) reads the same connection and keeps the same caution. The uses in the paragraph above are therefore 3-60's claims about the Massaleotican seselis, and are reported here only as what 3-61 points to. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** I_1024 cites 3-61 only in passing, in discussing the neighbouring chapter 3-62. It does not treat 3-61 as its own subject. The name *Laserpitium* also occurs in the existing entry I_587 (*Laserpitium siler*), a different species that does not cite 3-61.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records no use of its own: it says the seed is stronger and sweeter than the Massaleotican seselis and "produces similar effects", which sends the reader to 3-60's list of urinary, respiratory, menstrual and digestive uses. The Codex gives no protocol. Epilepsy, asthma, urinary obstruction and fever need medical care, and the identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

3-61 gives no preparation. The preparations in 3-60 (seed and root "taken as a drink"; the seed "as a drink with wine"; "as a drink with pepper and wine") are not stated for this plant. No quantity, frequency or duration is given in either chapter. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No study of the plant was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identity of the plant is itself the translators' suggestion among four names, so a modern fact about one species would not by itself bear on what Dioscorides says.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** 3-61 prints the seed as "sharper and more fragrant than the Massaleotican" and "very sweet". It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality. (The word "warming" belongs to the seed and root in 3-60, a different chapter.)

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition, in a small form: how to know the plant (leaf shape compared to two other plants, a shrub with branches of two feet, dill-like heads, black wheat-thick seed) and how its seed compares in sharpness, fragrance and sweetness with a named relative. It assigns the plant no status beyond that of a drug whose effects are those of another. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a description and a cross-reference, with no use, preparation or amount of its own, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. The chapter it points to lists abortifacient and menstrual uses, so no safety information for pregnancy can be taken from either chapter, and none beyond that was read. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion among four names, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
