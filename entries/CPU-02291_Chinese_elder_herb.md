# Chinese elder herb — 陆英 *Lu Ying* ("Flos Sambuci Japonici", Yang 1998); with 蒴藋 *shuo diao* (Mingyi layer)

## *Sambucus javanica Reinw. (syn. S. chinensis Lindl.), the herbaceous Asian elder, the identification we give to 陆英; of medium confidence: the Tang commentary quoted by Sun says 陆英 is 蒴藋, but the Guangya gloss Sun also quotes points toward a bramble (莓), and no description of the plant is given. The Mingyi layer calls the companion drug 蒴藋 toxic* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts on disk.** *Lu ying* is a monograph of the Divine Farmer's Classic. It has no chapter in Sushruta, Dioscorides, Ebers or the Assyrian texts. The rGyud-bzhi entry I_1353 (*Sambucus adnata*, Tibetan) and the European elders (I_094, I_637, I_686) are different plants; Entry I_1353 itself notes that Yang's Lu Ying is matched to the genus only and is "not read as this plant". This entry replaces the genus-level placeholder "Sambucus sp." (CPU-02291) with a monograph read from Sun Xingyan's 1799 reconstruction. **Caution: a lower-class drug with a toxic companion drug in the text; historical record only.**

**The Shennong text proper.** The 味 paragraph, in the lower (下) classic, reads 味苦，寒 ("bitter, cold"). It governs 骨间诸痹 ("the various impediments within the bones"), 四肢拘挛、疼酸 ("cramping of the limbs, aching soreness"), 膝寒痛 ("cold pain in the knees"), 阴痿 ("impotence", literally "yin wilting"), 短气不足 ("short breath, insufficiency") and 脚肿 ("swelling of the feet"). The habitat is 生川谷 ("grows in river valleys"). **There is no 久服 clause** and no toxicity word. (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, lower classic, entry 陆英 *lu ying*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 3545)

**Mingyi bielu layer (later).** 《名医》曰 gives the habitat 生熊耳及冤句 ("Xiong'er and Yuanju") and the harvest 立秋采 ("gathered at the beginning of autumn"). It then continues "又曰" ("it also says") with a second drug whose first character is 蒴 and whose second character is lost in the transcription (the standard name is 蒴藋, *shuo diao*; the name is supplied here from the Tang commentary in Sun's note, not from a legible character [USER: verify]): **味酸，温，有毒** ("sour, warm, toxic"); aliases 一名堇 (Sun's bracket says the present text wrongly writes the first alias) and 一名芨; habitat 生田野 ("fields"); "in spring and summer gather the leaves; in autumn and winter gather the stems and roots" (line 3547). This second drug is given by the Mingyi as a separate item with the opposite nature (warm, not cold) and a toxicity statement that the Lu Ying paragraph lacks.

**Sun's note (案).** Sun quotes the *Shuowen* on the aliases, and the *Guangya*, which reads (with a lost character) "…*pen*, Lu Ying, is a *mei*" (广雅云…盆，陆英莓也); a *mei* is a bramble or raspberry-type fruit, and the same *Guangya* line appears in Sun's note on the drug 覆盆 *fu pen* (line 1581). He then quotes the *Tang ben* (Tang dynasty) commentary on 陆英: "This thing is 蒴藋; later people did not recognise it and wrongly put out a separate 蒴藋 item." The "present note" (今注) that Sun also quotes says that 陆英 is bitter, cold, non-toxic, while 蒴藋 is sour, warm, toxic, so "it is hard to call them one species; probably they are of one kind" (难谓一种，盖其类尔) (line 3549).

**Yang 1998 (second witness).** Yang prints "Lu Ying (Flos Sambuci Japonici)", with the same flavour, nature and indications, under "Herbs: Inferior Class" (line 3660; `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`). He names the part "Flos" (flower), which Sun's text does not specify. A footnote on that page (note 226, lines 3665-3667) says that a drug "never prescribed in modern times" has a provisional identification, but it is not clear from the OCR whether the note belongs to Lu Ying or to the preceding item, so the Codex does not rely on it. [USER: verify]

**Identity.** Two lines of evidence conflict. The Tang commentary equates Lu Ying with *shuo diao*, which is the herbaceous elder *Sambucus javanica* (also written *S. chinensis*), a tall perennial herb of the elder genus; this is the standard modern identification and is the one the Codex uses. The *Guangya* gloss links the name to a bramble. That note says the two names denote related but not identical drugs. The Shennong paragraph gives no description. The Codex therefore reads "a herbaceous elder, *Sambucus javanica*, with medium confidence", and does not attribute the Mingyi layer's toxic *shuo diao* paragraph to the Shennong drug.

### Active Components

The elder genus (*Sambucus*) contains species whose raw leaves, stems, roots and unripe fruit carry irritant and cyanogenic compounds, a general fact well known for the European elders. The composition of *S. javanica* is not given in any text read here. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The classic records *lu ying* for bone and joint impediments, cramped and aching limbs, cold knees, impotence, shortness of breath and swollen feet. The Codex records these as the claims of the classic. The same Mingyi layer that supplies the harvest of this drug also warns that the companion *shuo diao* is toxic. A modern reader should not take plant material sold under either name.

### Preparation & Dosing

The paragraph gives no preparation, dose or course. The Mingyi layer gives the harvest, the beginning of autumn for Lu Ying, and, for *shuo diao*, leaves in spring and summer and stems and roots in autumn and winter. The preface of the classic has a general rule for toxic drugs, to begin "as small as a millet grain" and increase only if the illness does not yield (line 4126), but the Codex does not reproduce it as a dose. The later appendix 附：诸药制使 notes that dendrobium "uses *Lu Ying* as its envoy" (石斛 陆英为使, line 4291) and that achyranthes "dislikes" it (牛膝 恶荧火、龟、陆英, line 4299). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Herbaceous elders of East Asia are used in folk medicine for bone and joint complaints and for swelling; this is a general statement about the genus and is not drawn from the texts read. Whether *Sambucus javanica* relieves any of the conditions in the paragraph has not been tested in sources retrieved for this entry. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SBJ (Shennong text proper).** 味苦，寒: bitter; cold. No toxicity word is printed in the Shennong paragraph. The lower-class placement carries the preface's general statement that lower drugs are "mostly toxic" (多毒) and "not for long taking" (不可久服) (line 2935). The companion *shuo diao* is printed as sour, warm and toxic in the Mingyi layer only.
- No organ or channel affinity is printed. No other tradition's classification is applied.

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The paragraph stands in the lower classic. The preface says lower drugs are "assistants" that "treat disease to correspond with the earth", are mostly toxic, may not be taken for long, and are for those who want to remove cold-and-heat evil qi, break accumulations and cure illness (line 2935). The paragraph has a flavour, a nature, a list of complaints and a habitat, and **no 久服 clause**. It therefore carries only the lower class's therapeutic frame and no cultivation status. (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, lower classic, entry 陆英 *lu ying*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` lines 2935 and 3545)

**Practitioner's note.** A lower-grade drug in the Chinese scheme, to be taken for a defined complaint and for a short course, if at all. Nothing here supports long-term use, and the classic's own grading is the warning. The additional lesson of the entry is that the old commentators could not agree whether the plant and its toxic companion were one drug or two. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Contraindications

- **Toxicity.** The Mingyi layer calls the companion *shuo diao* toxic. Do not take any elder plant material, particularly the raw leaf, stem or root, as medicine. Raw parts of elders can cause nausea and vomiting.
- **Identity.** The name 陆英 has been applied to more than one plant over time; plant material sold under it is unreliable. [USER: verify]
- **Complaints named.** Impotence, shortness of breath and bone disease need medical care; the classic's uses are historical claims.
- **Swollen feet.** Swelling of the feet can be a sign of heart, kidney or vein disease and needs diagnosis.
