# Otter liver — sram, as printed in Clark's translation of the rGyud-bzhi (Lutra lutra L.)
## *The liver of the otter, a fish-eating semi-aquatic mammal; named in the Tibetan materia medica of animal origin* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan Tradition (FMT).** The text is Barry Clark's 1995 translation of the rGyud-bzhi, file `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`. The locus is the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (heading at line 792), section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), sub-group "Meat" (line 1161). The line (1170) reads: "sram: Otter: Lutra lutra L.: [has eight names]. Its liver cures urinary retention." The next item on the same line is the marmot ("Its liver unites cracks [and fractures] in bones"), and then goat liver. Directly after the list Clark's text states a general rule for organ meats: "The lungs, heart, liver, spleen, kidneys and flesh (of various animals) cure diseases of those respective organs" (line 1170). That rule is stated for "various animals"; whether it is meant to govern the otter's liver is not said, so it is mentioned and not applied.

The project list row is `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 348 ("Otter liver", animal, source "rGyud-bzhi, Explanatory Tantra ch.20 (Efficacy of Medicinal Substances)", id_uncertain "yes"). That row gives no text beyond the chapter; the line-1170 sentence above is the actual text. The binomial *Lutra lutra* L. is Clark's printed identification; in his preface (line 83) he says that where the identity of an animal product is in doubt the Tibetan name is given with a "probable English identification". The brackets in the printed text mark added matter: Clark says (line 80) that he "interpolated liberally from the Blue Aquamarine commentary". The phrase "[has eight names]" is therefore commentary-derived, and the sentence "Its liver cures urinary retention" is the part to take as the root-text statement. That reading of the bracket convention is an inference. [USER: verify]

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3534) gives the parallel sentence: "Otter liver cures obstruction of urine." Both translations agree on the indication.

The otter appears elsewhere as flesh, not as liver. In the dietary chapter's meat discussion Clark has "Otter flesh is an aphrodisiac and dispels cold of the kidneys and waist" (line 680). Men-Tsee-Khang lists the otter among "the group of aquatic animals" (line 2919). Those statements concern the flesh and are not carried over to the liver.

### Active Components

Liver is a metabolic organ: protein, fat, glycogen, and stores of iron and fat-soluble vitamins (vitamin A in many mammals). In fish-eating animals the liver can also accumulate environmental contaminants such as heavy metals and persistent organic pollutants. No figures are given here and the text names no active compound.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The Tibetan text says otter liver "cures urinary retention". The Codex does not recommend using it for that or anything else, and gives no protocol. Urinary retention is a medical problem that can damage the kidneys if untreated and needs examination; no food or animal part substitutes for it. Otters are protected in many jurisdictions, and a fish-eating wild animal's liver is a poor candidate for human consumption on hygiene and contaminant grounds (see Contraindications). The line is kept as a record of what the text documents. Clark himself notes (line 57) that "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts."

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Tibetan: the line prints an action only ("cures urinary retention"), with no taste, power or post-digestive taste for the liver. Clark says (line 673) that the meats of the eight sections he lists "are sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste", and that aquatic animals' flesh has "oily, heavy and warm powers". That rule concerns meats and flesh, the otter is classed aquatic in the Men-Tsee-Khang text (line 2919), and no source read says the liver shares it; I mention it only as context and do not assign it to the liver. ### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* The otter liver sits in the animal-origin materia medica (line 1170) with a therapeutic action only. No cultivation status, no essence-extraction (*bcud len*) role and no rejuvenation role is printed for it in the lines read. The otter's flesh is called an aphrodisiac (line 680), which is a statement about the flesh in the diet chapter and is not extended to the liver. What the text supplies is an organ remedy against one complaint, and nothing more.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. A cultivator does not use otter liver. The text records it against urinary retention, and the Codex keeps the record without carrying it into practice.

### Contraindications

Wild fish-eating mammals carry parasites and zoonotic infections, and their livers can concentrate pollutants; handling or eating wild otter offal is not advised. Otters are legally protected in many countries and regions, and the Eurasian otter is subject to international trade controls; check the law of the jurisdiction before possessing any otter product.
