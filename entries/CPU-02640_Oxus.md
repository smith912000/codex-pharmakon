### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The row began as an unverified residual on the spine (CPU-02640), class changed from "other" to "plant", from the name index of the English translation (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` row 794, "Acetum — Vinegar"; the list row in `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 750 has class mineral, which is wrong and is not relied on). The chapter was opened and read for this entry. Vinegar is mentioned in a very large number of other chapters as a vehicle and ingredient; a count of the lines mentioning "vinegar" or "acetum" in the English text gave 236, and these were not all read. Only 5-21 and its immediate neighbours are cited here.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-21, *Oxus* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading at line 9873, text from line 9875 to a page break and on to the end of the chapter before 5-22 at line 9879), in Book Five, "Vines & Wines". The chapter is a single long paragraph of actions. In order, it says:

"Acetum cools and is astringent. It is good for the stomach and to encourage an appetite. It staunches excessive bloody discharges, either taken as a drink or sat in as a bath. It is good boiled together with meat for discharges of the bowels. It is fit for bleeding wounds, and applied with unwashed wool or sponges keeps inflammation away. It represses the vulva and perineum that have fallen down, and broken bleeding gums."

It goes on to ulcers and skin disorders: "gangrenous ulceration, erysipela [streptococcal skin infection], shingles [herpes], psoriasis, lichen [skin disease with red pustules], and pterygium [membrane on eye] mixed with some other medicine from those that are suitable"; hot cloths of it stop "eating ulcers and gangrenous ulceration of the cheeks"; with sulphur it "helps gout"; rubbed on it "takes away bruises"; with *rosaceum* [1-53] and wool it is "good for burning fevers of the head".

Then the ears and bites: inhaled as steam it helps "difficult hearing, or noises and hissings of the ears"; dropped in the ears "it kills worms"; it "represses favus" and "soothes itching"; for the bites of venomous creatures that chill, "a warm pack of it is good; but a cold one for those that spit poison that burns". It is "good against all poisons [vomitory] taken warm as a drink and spewed up again, especially against the drinking of meconium [4-65] or hemlock"; given "against bloodclotting, or milk curdling in the stomach, and with salt against mushrooms, ixia [3-103] and smilax"; "Sipped up, it expels leeches that were taken in a drink". Finally the throat and teeth: it "soothes an old cough but aggravates a new one"; "good sipped up warm for asthma"; gargled it "restrains discharges in the throat"; it is "good for a synanchic [abscessed] throat, for the middle ear that has fallen, and is rolled around the teeth (warm) for toothache."

The scatter of uses is part of the evidence. The first sentence gives the quality (cools, astringent), and nearly all the uses that follow are the uses of an astringent cooling liquid applied or drunk: bleeding, discharges, inflammation, ulcers. The dosing words are the manner of use ("taken as a drink", "sat in as a bath", "boiled together with meat", "applied with hot cloths", "rolled around the teeth (warm)"), not amounts.

**Neighbouring chapters.** The vinegar chapter belongs to a short run of related liquids. 5-22 *Oxumeli* (oxymel, line 9879): "Take five half pints of vinegar, two pounds of sea salt, twenty pounds of honey and five pints of water, mix it all, boil it until it boils ten times, cool it, and pour it into another jar", said to expel thick fluids and to help sciatica, arthritis and epilepsy. 5-23 *Oxalme* (vinegar and salt, line 9882) is "Acetum salsum" applied with hot cloths to gangrenous and rotten ulcers and dog bites. 5-24 *Thumoxalme* is a thyme vinegar made with thyme, salt, polenta, rue and pulegium, and 5-25 *Skilletikon Oxus* (squill vinegar, marked POISONOUS in the translation) is vinegar steeped with squill. These have their own chapters, and 5-23 has its own spine row (CPU-02603). They are named here only to show where 5-21 sits.

### Active Components

The text names no constituent and does not say how the vinegar is made. In general terms, vinegar is a dilute solution of acetic acid, produced by the bacterial oxidation of ethanol (as in spoiled wine) and containing small amounts of the organic acids, sugars and polyphenols of whatever it is made from. Acetic acid is a weak acid and an irritant to tissue at strength. This is a general statement about vinegar, not a finding about the ancient liquid, whose strength is unknown. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a recommended practice.**

- **What the text says it does.** It cools and is astringent; it is used for the stomach and appetite, for bleeding and discharges, as a wash or hot cloth for wounds, ulcers, skin disease and inflammation, as a steam or drop for the ears, as a gargle and tooth-rinse, and as an emetic and antidote in poisoning.
- **What the text does not say.** No amounts, no strength, no source of the vinegar, no length of use, and no claim about long life.

The Codex does not translate these uses into modern diagnoses and gives no protocol. In particular, the poison and emetic uses are historical statements: the text recommends a warm drink "spewed up again" for poisons, and nothing here is a treatment for poisoning. Anyone who may have swallowed a poison needs medical help.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives no way of making the vinegar. It gives ways of using it: as a drink (warm), as a bath, boiled with meat, with unwashed wool or sponges, with hot cloths, with rosaceum [1-53], with sulphur, as a steam, dropped in the ears, as a warm or cold pack, gargled, and "rolled around the teeth (warm)". Mixtures are in the next chapters (oxymel 5-22, vinegar and salt 5-23). ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Dilute acetic acid has mild antimicrobial and astringent properties, and vinegar has long been used as a preservative, a cleaner and a condiment. Undiluted vinegar can irritate or burn skin, eyes and the lining of the mouth and throat, and swallowing strong acetic acid can cause burns. Several of Dioscorides's uses (ear drops, ulcer washes, drinking vinegar after poisoning, killing leeches) are not supported by modern practice and some are hazardous. The cooling and astringent character he gives it is in kind with its acidity; the other terms have no modern equivalent and are not translated into one. ### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body or vital system, and nothing in 5-21 ties vinegar to long life, constitution or practice. What the chapter offers is an unusually wide record of how a single common material was used, and that is a discipline of attention to context: the same liquid cooling a fever, staunching a bleed, clearing the ears or lifting bruises depending on how it is applied (warm or cold, with wool, with salt, with sulphur). That is the extent of what the text supports.

**Practitioner's note.** A cooling, astringent household liquid with a long list of external and internal uses, none attested as cultivation. A cultivator can take from it the point that the manner of application changes the use, and nothing else.

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

Per Dioscorides 5-21, vinegar cools and is astringent, and its listed actions reach many body sites. Taken as a drink or sat in as a bath it staunches excessive bloody discharges; it is good for the stomach and for appetite; boiled with meat it treats discharges of the bowels. Applied to bleeding wounds, and with wool or sponges to inflamed areas, it is used for ulcers and skin diseases such as erysipelas, shingles, psoriasis and a membrane on the eye (pterygium). Its uses extend to the gums, teeth, throat and middle ear, to the ears by steam and drops, to the cough and asthma (airway), and to the vulva and perineum when they have fallen. With sulphur it is said to help gout, and rubbed on it takes away bruises. The chapter also gives it as an emetic and antidote in poisoning and against leeches; these are historical statements only.

### Contraindications

- **Strength.** Undiluted vinegar and stronger acetic acid irritate and can burn the skin, eyes, mouth and throat. Do not put it in the ears, eyes or on broken skin on the strength of this entry.
- **Swallowing as an emetic or antidote.** The text's use of a warm vinegar drink to bring up poisons is not a safe or effective measure and is not recommended. Seek medical help for suspected poisoning.
- **Digestive conditions.** Ulcers, reflux or gastritis may be aggravated by acid. [USER: verify as a general medical statement]
