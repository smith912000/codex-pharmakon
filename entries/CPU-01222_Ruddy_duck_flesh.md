# Ruddy duck flesh — *ngur.pa*, "Red Wild Duck" (Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch.20); *Ngurpa*, "Duck" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *The flesh of the wild duck the Tibetan texts call* ngur.pa*; Clark prints "Red Wild Duck" with the Latin names* Anas nyroca *and* Tadorna ferruginea*, and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints* Tadorna ferruginea *Pallas. "Ruddy duck" is the spine's name, not the texts'* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is the Tibetan *Four Tantras*. In Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; "Clark" below) the animal medicines close chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). The duck is filed under the third of these, "Meat" (heading at line 1161), and line 1170 reads: "ngur.pa: Red Wild Duck: Anas nyroca: Tadorna ferruginea (Pallas.) [has fourteen names, for example, bande, etc.] Its flesh cures anterior transposition of the calf muscle." Clark prints the Tibetan name first, as he says he does for every substance (line 83), and gives more than one Latin name where more than one identification is available. The square brackets mark interpolations from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries (line 82), so the sentence on the flesh stands outside them as the root-text claim as Clark renders it. The list row `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 356 ("Duck meat", class animal, identity flagged uncertain) is the spine's other locus and gives only the name.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`; "MTK" below) has the same claim (lines 3536-3537): "Duck meat cures dislocation of the calf muscle." Clark's phrase is "anterior transposition of the calf muscle". The two renderings name the same organ and different disorders, and neither text says what the condition is. Its modern identification is therefore open. [USER: verify] MTK's Glossary on Animals pairs the Tibetan *Ngurpa* with the English "Duck" and the Latin "Tadornaferruginea Pallas" (lines 6856, 6861, 6889; OCR splits the columns, and the pairing follows their order, with *Ngangpa* "Swan" beside it). Clark's dietary chapter also names "red wild duck (sheldrake) (*Tadorna ferruginea*)" among the "animals which live in wet places" (line 672). MTK lists "duck" in its group of aquatic animals (line 2919).

Identity note. Clark's two Latin names do not agree: *Anas nyroca* is a different duck from *Tadorna ferruginea*. The glossary supports the second. In current English usage *Tadorna ferruginea* is the ruddy shelduck, and "ruddy duck" is the usual name of a different, North American duck, *Oxyura jamaicensis*. The spine's "Ruddy duck" is therefore a loose rendering of Clark's "Red Wild Duck" and not the bird in either text. The entry follows the texts. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Duck flesh is mainly muscle protein, with fat concentrated in and under the skin, and the iron-containing pigment myoglobin that makes waterfowl meat dark. The Tibetan texts name no constituent and no mechanism for the claimed action, and this entry supplies none.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The claim is a single one: the flesh cures a disorder of the calf muscle that neither translation defines. It is a food as well as a medicine in the same tradition, and nothing in the passages read gives it a use beyond that disorder. Clark adds that "nowadays only very few animal products are used" (line 57). The Codex's application layer has nothing to add, and no protocol is offered.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither text gives a preparation (roasted, boiled, a broth), a quantity or a dose. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] The compounding chapter (Clark ch.21, from line 1204) lists medicines by disease, and the duck is not named in the lists read.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT).** The dietary chapter gives rules for meats by habitat that cover the duck. In Clark's account all the meats in its groups are "sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste", and the flesh of aquatic animals "has oily, heavy and warm powers and is beneficial in disorders of the stomach, kidneys and waist, and in cold wind disorders" (lines 672-673). Clark places the red wild duck in the wet-places group (line 672), but the next paragraph speaks of "aquatic" animals without saying in so many words which of the listed groups that is. MTK states the equivalent rule: the taste and post-digestive taste of the meats are sweet, and "the meat of animals living in wet places is oily, heavy and warm in quality", relieving "disorders of the stomach, kidneys and waist, cold disorders, and loong disorders" (line 2923), with duck in the aquatic group (line 2919). Those are the general qualities of the group's meat as food. The animal-medicine entry itself prints no quality for the duck.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The duck's flesh is attested in two places in the Explanatory Tantra: as a meat among the animals living in wet places in the dietary chapter, with the group's sweet taste and oily, heavy, warm quality, and as an animal medicine against one disorder of the calf muscle in chapter 20 (Clark lines 672-673, 1170; MTK lines 2919, 2923, 3536-3537). The tradition has a formal division of rejuvenation, "essence-extraction" (Clark line 128, "the division of essence-extraction (rejuvenation, etc.)"; also line 136), and no animal substance in the section read (lines 1147-1202) is assigned to it. Duck is not named in the compounding lists of chapter 21. The dietary chapter does say that peacock flesh is "beneficial ... for rejuvenation" (Clark line 678), so the tradition does link some flesh to rejuvenation, and it says nothing of the sort for the duck. Clark's own statement of what is rejuvenating is about plants (line 69). In this tradition medicine is one branch of treatment and the root of disease lies in the three poisons; no passage read ties the duck to that frame. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in the Tibetan sources, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports a cultivation use. The duck is a food in the Tibetan dietary chapter and a single-purpose medicine in the materia medica, and a cultivator who eats well-sourced, cooked poultry is doing so as food. The text promises nothing beyond that.

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** wild waterfowl are natural carriers of avian influenza viruses and also of *Salmonella* and *Campylobacter*; the flesh must be fresh, handled hygienically and cooked through.
- **Identity:** the Latin names in the sources conflict and the English name in the spine is not the texts' bird; do not substitute one species for another by assumption. [USER: verify]
- **Legal and conservation status:** wild waterfowl are covered by hunting, trade and migratory-bird law in most countries, and the species named in the Tibetan texts are wild birds. Check the law before taking or possessing one. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** the sources do not say how the bird was obtained.
