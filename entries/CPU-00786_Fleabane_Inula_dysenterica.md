# Common Fleabane

## *Pulicaria dysenterica* (L.) Bernh. (syn. *Inula dysenterica* L.) | Family: Asteraceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Cool–Dry; astringent-binding, aromatic-dispersive at the surface

**Training Phase:** Consolidation (gut-binding and field-hygiene work; not a Preparation-phase agent)

---

### Origin & Tradition

Few plants carry their two therapeutic doctrines so plainly in their own name. The Latin binomial is a double inscription: *dysenterica* names the flux of the bowel, and *Pulicaria* — from *pulex*, the flea — names the vermin the burning herb was meant to drive out. One name is internal, the other external. One is a remedy of binding; the other a remedy of banishing. A single yellow-rayed composite of European ditches and damp waysides was asked to do both, and the tradition never regarded these as separate offices. Both are acts of expulsion of the unclean — one from the gut, one from the bed.

**Greco-Roman and Classical European Materia Medica (AT / DMM).** The plant enters the Western record under *Inula*/*Conyza*-adjacent names, and the *dysenterica* epithet is a straight transmission of its classical indication: **dysentery and the bloody flux**. The doctrine is humoral and mechanical at once — a *cold, dry, astringent* herb given to a *hot, wet, loose* bowel. Where the flux is understood as an over-flowing, the remedy is a binder: it draws the tissue together, thickens what has become thin, and stops the escape. The plant is repeatedly reported to have been used as a soldiers' and campaigners' remedy for camp dysentery — a claim that is ethnobotanically plausible for a common wayside astringent but which should be treated as reputation rather than documented history [USER: verify the specific army/campaign attribution before publishing it as fact].

The second doctrine sits alongside the first: the dried herb **burned or strewn as a fumigant against fleas, gnats and flies**, the vernacular "fleabane" recording the practice rather than any theory of it. The classical logic is that of *aromatic fumigation* — the pneuma of the burning plant is hostile to small vermin and to the corrupt air that breeds them. Note that in the pre-germ-theory frame these are *the same doctrine*: bad air breeds both flux and vermin, and an aromatic that purifies air addresses both. The plant is not doing two unrelated jobs. It is doing one job — cleansing corruption — at two scales.

**Assyro-Babylonian (AT — hedged).** The Mesopotamian tablets record fumigation against household vermin and a substantial anti-diarrhoeal materia medica, and Inuleae-type composites grow across the region. But no specific attestation of *P. dysenterica* under a securely identified Akkadian plant name is available here, and the Assyrian botanical vocabulary remains poorly resolved. **No Assyrian claim is made for this species** [USER: verify — if a securely identified Akkadian name exists, add it here with its tablet reference; otherwise this subsection should remain a negative note].

**Vernacular European / Folk Continuum.** Under "fleabane," "harvest flower," and their regional cognates, the plant persisted in two households of use: an infusion or decoction of the aerial parts for **looseness of the bowel, gripe and gut infection**, and the dried herb hung in barns, strewn on floors, or smouldered to keep **fleas and biting flies** out of bedding and byres. Turkish and wider Anatolian folk practice continues the internal branch, using decoctions for **gastrointestinal and inflammatory complaints** — the tradition in which the plant has been most recently tested (see Scientific Correlation).

#### Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer

The doctrines here are not mystical, and that is exactly what makes them worth mapping carefully — the risk with *Pulicaria* is not that the doctrine is too strange to correlate, but that it is so intuitive we forget to check whether it is *true*.

**Doctrine 1 — "the binder."** *Cold-dry astringency stops the flux.* This maps onto a real and specific chemistry: **tannins and polyphenols**, which precipitate proteins on contact with mucosa, producing the literal tissue-tightening the doctrine describes, and **caffeoylquinic acids** (chlorogenic and caffeic acid dominate the decoction), which are anti-inflammatory. The doctrine's mechanical metaphor — drawing together — is not a metaphor at all. Polyphenol–protein crosslinking is a physical drawing-together. This is one of the rare cases where an ancient mechanism-image is a fair, if coarse, description of the molecular event.

But the doctrine is *incomplete*, and honestly so. Astringency alone would explain symptomatic binding, not the resolution of an infective flux. The tradition claimed cure; the chemistry offers **two additional, partly-supported mechanisms**: sesquiterpene/essential-oil antimicrobial action against enteric organisms, and antispasmodic action on gut smooth muscle. Where the tradition said "it binds," the chemistry says "it binds, it may kill some of what is causing the flux, and it quiets the cramping." The doctrine underdescribed its own remedy.

**Doctrine 2 — "the banisher."** *The smoke of the herb is hostile to fleas.* This maps onto the **essential oil**, not the tannins — a completely different chemical fraction from the one doing the anti-dysenteric work. The volatile fraction of *P. dysenterica* is dominated by unusual **3-methoxycuminyl esters** and by caryophyllene-type sesquiterpenes. The mechanistic bridge to the flea doctrine is that the oil shows **acetylcholinesterase-inhibitory activity** — and AChE inhibition is precisely the mechanism of the classical synthetic insecticides. That is a *suggestive* mechanistic correspondence: the plant makes a volatile that interferes with the exact enzyme whose interference kills arthropods, and the tradition burned that volatile at fleas.

It is not proof, and the entry will not pretend otherwise. **No study retrieved here tested *P. dysenterica* oil against fleas, or against any insect.** The doctrine has a plausible mechanism and no direct evidence. That is a specific epistemic state and it deserves to be named rather than smoothed over: the flea doctrine is *chemically coherent and empirically untested*.

The symbolic reading — that a single plant governs both the corrupted gut and the corrupted bed — is therefore **doctrinally elegant and chemically false**. Two different fractions do the two jobs. The unity is in the name, not in the molecule. This is a useful correction to carry: traditions that unify uses under one image frequently obscure that the plant is doing unrelated things with unrelated compounds.

### Active Components

- **Caffeoylquinic acids** — chlorogenic acid and caffeic acid are the dominant phenolics of the decoction; chlorogenic acid measured at 8543.88 ± 38.04 µg/g in the gastric-phase digest and caffeic acid at 6412.00 ± 96.50 µg/g in the intestinal phase (Sinan et al., 2026).
- **Tannins and flavonoid glycosides** — flavonol glucuronides are characteristic vacuolar constituents; the species carries 6-hydroxyflavonol and quercetagetin derivatives in leaf and inflorescence (Williams et al., 2003).
- **Essential oil** — containing **3-methoxycuminyl 2-methylbutanoate** (a compound first described as a natural product from this species) and the rare **3-methoxycuminyl 3-methylbutanoate** (Radulović et al., 2022).
- **Sesquiterpenes (caryophyllene type)** — caryophyllene derivatives are documented in the genus, with an epimer of a *P. dysenterica* compound isolated from *P. vulgaris* (Zardi-Bergaoui et al., 2019).
- **Sesquiterpene lactones** — the Asteraceae-typical class; the allergological reason for the contraindications below.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

*Pulicaria dysenterica* is a **Consolidation-phase agent** and should not be reached for during Preparation or Opening work. Its action is centripetal: it tightens, quiets and closes. Where the field is loose — where the gut is in flux, where the practitioner is losing what should be held — the plant is a binder that is unusually well matched to its indication, because both its symptomatic action (astringency) and its causal action (antimicrobial, antispasmodic) point the same direction.

The **field-hygiene use** — the fumigant branch — is retained in the Kenetica corpus as a *documented tradition*, not as a recommended practice. It is included because the two-doctrine structure of this plant is instructive: it is the corpus's clearest worked example of a name encoding two uses served by two chemistries, one of which the modern literature partially confirms and one of which it has simply never examined.

Practical register: gut-binding for transient looseness, with anti-inflammatory support. The rat hemorrhoid data suggest the anti-inflammatory branch may extend to the lower bowel, which is a modest extension of the classical indication rather than a departure from it.

| Fumigant / strewing herb | Whole dried herb | Burned or strewn | Fleas, gnats, flies | Traditional; no efficacy data retrieved |

**Dosing.** No validated human dose exists for this plant. The animal work used 3.2 mg/kg (low) and 6.4 mg/kg (high) of decoction extract in rats — these are **research doses in a hemorrhoid model, not a human posology**, and must not be scaled by bodyweight into human practice [USER: verify before any dose figure is published]. Acute oral toxicity in rat was not observed up to 2000 mg/kg (OECD 423), which speaks to a wide margin in that model but says nothing about chronic use, human use, or the sesquiterpene lactone sensitisation risk.

### Scientific Correlation

*According to PubMed:*

**The anti-dysenteric / gut doctrine — graded MODERATE (mechanistically supported, clinically unproven).**

- Sinan A, Sinen O, Sinen AG, Dursun İ, Karahan D, Özbek H. "Comprehensive evaluation of *Pulicaria dysenterica* subsp. *dysenterica* decoction..." *J Ethnopharmacol* 2026;368:121700. PMID 42002117. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2026.121700) — 28 of 75 screened phenolics identified; chlorogenic and caffeic acids dominant; the decoction's phenolic content and antioxidant activity **survived simulated gastric and intestinal digestion**, which is a non-trivial finding for an orally taken traditional preparation. High-dose extract significantly reduced rectal-anal coefficient, inflammatory index and TNF-α in a croton-oil rat hemorrhoid model; low dose had limited effect. No acute oral toxicity to 2000 mg/kg. **Grade: this is a real animal-model anti-inflammatory result in the right organ system — but it is a hemorrhoid model, not dysentery, and it is a rat.**

- Nickavar B, Mojab F. "Antibacterial activity of *Pulicaria dysenterica* extracts." *Fitoterapia* 2003;74(4):390-3. PMID 12781813. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0367-326x(03)00060-1) — aqueous, methanolic and chloroformic extracts by disc diffusion; methanolic most effective against three of six bacteria; **all extracts active against *Vibrio cholerae***. **Grade: WEAK-POSITIVE.** Disc diffusion is a screening assay, not a therapeutic demonstration; but activity against a diarrhoeal pathogen is the correct direction for the doctrine.

- Radulović NS, Mladenović MZ, Vukićević DR, Stojanović NM, Randjelović PJ, Stojanović-Radić ZZ, Boylan F. "*[Pulicaria dysenterica]* — Rightfully Earned Name? Identification and Biological Activity of New 3-Methoxycuminyl Esters..." *Plants (Basel)* 2022;11(23):3340. PMID 36501379. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants11233340) — the essential oil tested against 18 microbial strains, plus anti-AChE, antispasmodic (isolated rat distal colon), acute toxicity and immunomodulatory assays. The authors' own conclusion is measured: low acute toxicity and promising antimicrobial potential "might **partially** explain the ethnopharmacological application." **Grade: MODERATE and honestly self-limited by its authors.** This is the study that asked the name-question directly and answered *partially yes*.

- Williams CA, Harborne JB, Greenham JR, Grayer RJ, Kite GC, Eagles J. "Variations in lipophilic and vacuolar flavonoids among European *Pulicaria* species." *Phytochemistry* 2003;64(1):275-83. PMID 12946426. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0031-9422(03)00207-3) — chemotaxonomic; establishes the flavonoid inventory. **Grade: compositional evidence only, no bioactivity claim.**

- de la Luz Cádiz-Gurrea M, Zengin G, Kayacık O, Lobine D, Mahomoodally MF, Leyva-Jiménez FJ, Segura-Carretero A. "Innovative perspectives on *Pulicaria dysenterica* extracts..." *J Sci Food Agric* 2019;99(13):6001-10. PMID 31225640. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/jsfa.9875) — 40 compounds identified; enzyme-inhibition panel (α-amylase, α-glucosidase, tyrosinase, cholinesterases, lipase). **Grade: in vitro enzyme screening. Note the anti-diabetic framing here is a modern add-on with no traditional warrant and should not be imported into the entry as a use.**

**The flea / insecticidal doctrine — graded UNTESTED (null).**

This must be stated plainly. **A targeted search returned zero studies of *Pulicaria dysenterica* essential oil insecticidal activity.** The doctrine that named the genus has, so far as the retrieved literature shows, never been tested against an insect. The only mechanistic thread is indirect: Radulović et al. (2022) report anti-acetylcholinesterase activity for the oil and its constituents, and AChE is the canonical arthropod insecticide target. **That is a hypothesis, not a finding.** Anyone citing "fleabane repels fleas" as science is citing a name, not a result.

**Deflations — what the tradition and the modern literature both overclaim.**

1. **The unity doctrine is wrong.** The astringent/anti-dysenteric action and the volatile/insecticidal action are in different chemical fractions. The plant's name implies a single virtue; the chemistry shows two unrelated ones.
2. **"Weakest in the genus."** A review of *Pulicaria* anticancer activity found *P. vulgaris* strongest and **explicitly identified *P. dysenterica* as having the weakest effect on cancer cells** (Barabadi T, Rahimi H, Mohamadi-Zarch SM, Bagheri SM. *Curr Cancer Drug Targets* 2025;25(10):1210-1221. PMID 39945254. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2174/0115680096358261250115114608)). This is a **negative result worth preserving**: the modern habit of attaching an anticancer claim to any Asteraceae with a phenolic profile fails here, in this genus, for this species, by the reviewers' own ranking. No anticancer claim belongs in this entry.
3. **No human data of any kind.** Every result above is *in vitro* or rodent. The plant has a two-thousand-year indication and zero clinical trials.

**Overall grade: the binder doctrine is partially vindicated; the banisher doctrine is unexamined; the unity doctrine is refuted.**

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** The Mesopotamian tablets record aromatic fumigation against household vermin and hold a substantial anti-diarrhoeal materia medica, and Inuleae-type composites grow across the region — but the entry itself is explicit that no securely identified Akkadian plant name attaches to *Pulicaria dysenterica*, and the Assyrian botanical vocabulary is poorly resolved. Under the standing blocking warning that governs every Assyrian substance identification, no claim of any kind is made for this species here — neither the causal-agency framing by which a drug is classed against a divine hand, ghost or oath, nor any cultivation status. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The plant enters the Western record under *Inula*/*Conyza*-adjacent names as a cold, dry, astringent binder for the flux, and the tradition carries no subtle-body system that could assign it a vital or cultivational status. What Dioscorides offers a cultivator instead is a discipline of attention: correct identification of the yellow-rayed composite against its Inuleae neighbours, the season and part gathered, and a sensory assay of astringency and aromatic pungency — the same doubled sensory reading the plant's two doctrines depend on. The amuletic or fumigatory uses the tradition records are recorded, not thereby endowed with a doctrine of the vital body. The standing is therapeutic and, in the burned-herb branch, hygienic. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** Read through the humoral frame the tag invokes, fleabane is a cold, dry, astringent and drying drug — a binder for a hot, wet, loose bowel — which places it among the contracting remedies, not among the gladdening cardiac drugs that strengthen the *ruh*. The one genuine cultivation idea the Unani corpus carries, the husbanding of innate heat and radical moisture as a finite endowment whose depletion is ageing itself, is a frame of the whole regimen and is not a status any source retrieved assigns to this cooling astringent; no *mufarrih* role is claimed. It should be noted that the entry body documents the Greco-Roman and European-folk strata directly but carries no explicit Avicennan or Unani citation for this species, so the CM attestation rests on the humoral classification rather than on a located *Canon*-tradition passage. **(b) framed:** used as an astringent within the humoral scheme, no cultivation status sourced.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing tonic with this plant. Fleabane is a Consolidation-phase binder for transient looseness and, in its second and untested branch, a strewing and fumigant herb for a verminous bed — both of them acts of expulsion, not of nourishment. There is no register in any of the three traditions in which it is taken over time to build the person; its own directional logic warns against that, since a binder given to an already-bound or dry gut is a mistake by the tradition's own reasoning. The honest cultivational lesson here is the one the entry's whole structure teaches: a single name can fuse two uses served by two unrelated chemistries, and attention — not doctrine — is what keeps them apart.

### Contraindications

- **⚠️ Asteraceae allergy — absolute caution.** Anyone sensitised to Compositae (ragweed, chamomile, arnica, feverfew, chrysanthemum) should not use this plant internally or topically. Cross-reactivity within the family is well established.
- **⚠️ Sesquiterpene lactone contact dermatitis.** SLs are the Asteraceae's characteristic allergenic class and are the standard cause of occupational and handling dermatitis in this family. Handling fresh plant, and especially repeated handling, carries a sensitisation risk. Gloves for any bulk handling.
- **Fumigant use — respiratory risk.** Burning any SL-bearing herb in an enclosed space aerosolises allergen and combustion products. The traditional practice predates any conception of respiratory sensitisation. Not recommended.
- **Essential oil — not for internal use.** The oil is a research isolate; its AChE-inhibitory activity is the very property that makes it interesting and the reason it should not be swallowed.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, paediatric use — no data. Avoid.**
- **No established human dose.** The rat doses in the literature are not convertible to human posology [USER: verify].
- **Constipation / dry-bowel states** — the astringent doctrine is directional. A binder given to an already-bound gut is a mistake by the tradition's own logic.
- **Persistent or bloody diarrhoea is a medical emergency, not a herbal indication.** The classical use for dysentery belongs to a world without oral rehydration or antibiotics. It is a historical fact, not a treatment plan.
