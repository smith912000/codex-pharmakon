# Linaria buriatica — lug.ngal, Lugnyal (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Linaria buriatica, the second of two identifications Clark 1995 prints for lug.ngal, after "Corydalis, meifolia"; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary sets Corydalis meifolia, not Linaria, beside Lugnyal by column position* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and on the second of two names in one line of one of them.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. Only Clark prints *Linaria buriatica* (line 1106 and the Latin-name index, line 1609). A search of Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 for "Linaria" found nothing. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk for *Linaria* found only Dioscorides' translator's glosses, in which the old name *Linaria* stands for other plants (*Linaria elatine* at line 8347, *Linaria cymbalaria* at line 9198, and *Linaria* [Fuchs] and *Linaria vulgaris* at line 9289); none is *buriatica*, and none is attributed to this row. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 193) the Tibetan name *lug.ngal* is recorded at Clark line 1106 with the identification "Corydalis, meifolia; Linaria buriatica", and the row carries no "key uses the first" note, unlike its siblings. The batch name, *Linaria buriatica*, is the **second** of the two names Clark prints, and not the first. The first, which Clark prints as "Corydalis, meifolia" with a stray comma, is the binomial of a separate extraction row (line 313, keyed to the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary line 5448). The files read do not record why the later name was taken for this row. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance".

**Identity doubt.** The two names are of different families as the Codex understands it (a corydalis in the poppy family, a toadflax in the plantain or figwort family; not checked against a flora), and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary agrees only with the first. The commentary in Clark's line speaks of "Corydalis" and not of *Linaria* (see below), so this entry rests on the second of Clark's two names against the commentary's own word. The Codex cannot say which plant the Tibetan texts meant, and the entry stands for the Tibetan drug. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1106).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). It reads:

"lug.ngal: Corydalis, meifolia: Linaria buriatica: [the white type grows on river banks and has a soft leaf and thin petiole. It is adhesive, with a green leaf and pale yellow flower. Corydalis has a sweet taste and cool power,] neutralizes poisons, reduces swellings of the limbs [and is outstanding in relieving pain caused by wind imbalances]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the white type, its habitat, the leaf, the petiole, the flower, the sentence "Corydalis has a sweet taste and cool power" and the clause "and is outstanding in relieving pain caused by wind imbalances". Outside them (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the two Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the clause "neutralizes poisons, reduces swellings of the limbs". The sweet taste and cool power are said of "Corydalis" in the commentary, not of *Linaria*, and are not carried to this entry's binomial. The line gives no part used and no preparation.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "L" block, lines 5433 to 5454).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. The phonetic names (lines 5435 to 5437) are nine: "Lalaphue", "Langthangtse", "Lishi", "Looh dhue dorje", "Lugchhung", "Lugmur", "Lugnyal", "Lugru mukpo", "Lugru serpo". The transliterations (lines 5439 to 5441) include "lug ngal" (line 5441), the same spelling as Clark's *lug.ngal*. The scientific names (lines 5445 to 5448) are nine: "Cnidium monnieri", "Hyoscyamus niger L.", "Syzygium aromaticum", "Codonopsis thalictrifolia", "Aster strachei", "Phlomis younghusbandii", "Corydalis meifolia", "Pedicularis siphonantha", "Pedicularis longiflora". Counting in order, "Lugnyal" is seventh against *Corydalis meifolia*. The count can be checked on neighbours: "Lugchhung" is fifth against *Aster strachei*, as Clark's *lug.chhung* (line 1105) gives, and "Lalaphue" is first against *Cnidium monnieri*, as I_1258 records for Clark's *la.la.phud* (line 896). The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position. [USER: verify] The glossary prints no *Linaria*.

*What the Men-Tsee-Khang running text does not say.* A search of Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 for "Lugnyal", "Lugngal" and "lug ngal" returned only the glossary lines (5437 and 5441). The text therefore gives this drug a name and a binomial, *Corydalis meifolia*, and no taste, action or use. Clark's line 1106 is the only line read that says what the drug does.

**Overlap.** The same Tibetan drug and the same Clark line are treated in I_1258 (*Corydalis meifolia*, tag FMT), which takes Clark's first name, the glossary's binomial and the "Lugnyal" pairing. The two entries are one drug under two binomials, one the first and one the second of Clark's two names; they are not merged, and each says so. I_1258 states that *Linaria* belongs to a different family from *Corydalis*. I_1022 (*Aster strachei*) and I_1256 (*Codonopsis thalictrifolia*) quote the same "L" block for their own names. I_1285 (*Galium spurium*) follows *lug.ngal* in Clark's run (the next entry after line 1106). No other entry on *Linaria buriatica* was found, and the toadflax entries in the folder (for example I_896) are of other species from other traditions and do not cite Clark line 1106. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. Clark's commentary says the plant "is adhesive". It is a general fact that *Linaria* is a genus of the plantain family (Plantaginaceae, formerly placed in the figwort family) and that iridoid glycosides are characteristic of that family. *Corydalis*, Clark's other name, is a poppy-family genus in which isoquinoline alkaloids are characteristic. The sources read do not say which plant the drug is or which part was used, so no general statement about the Tibetan drug's constituents is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text records a drug that "neutralizes poisons, reduces swellings of the limbs" and, in the commentary, "is outstanding in relieving pain caused by wind imbalances" (Clark, line 1106). The Men-Tsee-Khang text records no use. These are the text's claims in its own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and does not translate "wind imbalances" or "poisons" into modern diagnoses. Poisoning needs medical care.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary (line 1106) prints "sweet taste and cool power" for "Corydalis" and none for *Linaria*; the Codex records it as said of the commentary's plant and not of this binomial. No post-digestive taste is printed. Men-Tsee-Khang prints no taste or power. The actions printed are neutralizing poisons and reducing swellings of the limbs, with pain from wind imbalances in the commentary (Clark).

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it neutralizes poisons and reduces swellings of the limbs, and its commentary adds relief of pain caused by wind imbalances. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes, and not in the rejuvenation material; no line read places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name, not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a drug for poisons, limb swellings and wind pain in the Tibetan text, with no preparation and no dose, and the commentary's own word for the plant is *Corydalis*, not *Linaria*. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Poisoning, swelling and pain need medical assessment; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Because the identity is uncertain, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
