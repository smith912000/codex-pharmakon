# Rock lichen (Parmelia) — rdo.dreg ("stone dirt") / Dhodek
## *A type of lichen, glossed Parmelia saxatilis by the translator (Clark 1995); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text names a drug "Dhodek", read here as the same drug on phonetic grounds only* | *(Volume IV — Fungal/Algal, shelved in Volume I)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan Tradition (FMT).** The locus is the Explanatory Tantra's chapter on the efficacy of medicinal substances (ch.20), in Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 869): "rdo.dreg (lit. 'stone dirt'): A type of Lichen: Parmelia saxatilis: [has thirteen names and is found on the shady side of rocks facing north. It is of red, yellow, white, green and black types, among which the red type is superior. It] cures poisoning and chronic fevers."

The placement matters. Clark's "Specific Section" divides individual natural substances into eight: "(1) precious [metal] medicines (2) stone medicines (3) earth medicines (4) tree medicines (5) resins (6) plants (7) herbs (8) animal products" (line 802). Stone medicines are introduced at line 816 and earth medicines at line 857 ("The Exposition of the Power of Earth Medicines"); *rdo.dreg* at line 869 falls in the earth-medicine section, between the vitriols and chalcanthite of the preceding lines and *brag.zhun*, "Mineral Exudate", on line 870, which is also said to be found on the north side of rocks. So the Tantra classes the lichen with minerals and earths, not with plants. That is the tradition's own grouping in this translation; the Codex's list row also carries it as a mineral-class entry (see below).

**Men-Tsee-Khang 2011.** The shorter exposition of the same chapter lists, in a run of short efficacy statements for mineral drugs (alongside *Zae tsha*, *Nag tshur*, *Ser tshur* and *Bigpen*), "Dhodek is an antidote against poison and cures chronic hot disorders" (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 3374; index entry "Dhodek 200", line 7325). The text read prints no Tibetan spelling or Latin name for *Dhodek*. The equation *Dhodek* = *rdo.dreg* is a phonetic one: the two share the pairing "poison" and "chronic" disorders with Clark's line 869, and the agreement is an inference. The Codex's own list row (`_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 167) reads "Dhodek, , mineral, ... id_uncertain: yes". [USER: verify]

**The parent text's other Parmelia hit.** Clark also prints *Parmelia saxatilis* as one of three Latin names for *gser.skud* ("gold thread"): "Cuscuta sinensis: Usnea diffracta: Parmelia saxatilis: [has a slender yellow stalk and bitter taste. The white stalked type is bulkier and is known as dngul.skud ('silver thread'). It has bitter taste and] cures fevers of the lungs, liver and channels, and also fever caused by poisoning" (line 1029). That is a different drug in a different section, with three possible identities. It is cited so that the reader does not take it for *rdo.dreg*; it is not used for the claims of this entry.

### Active Components

Stated generally. A lichen is a partnership of a fungus and an alga or cyanobacterium. Lichens produce characteristic secondary compounds, often called lichen acids (for example depsides, depsidones and usnic acid in some genera), and they contain polysaccharides. Which compounds a particular rock lichen carries was not read in the sources retrieved, and none are asserted for *Parmelia saxatilis*. Lichens also take up metals and pollutants from their surroundings.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts name the lichen as a drug against poisoning and chronic fevers (Clark line 869) or chronic hot disorders (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3374). The sources retrieved give no preparation and no dose, the identity is uncertain, and the red, yellow, white, green and black types Clark mentions cannot be told apart from this text. No modern protocol is supplied.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The Tibetan frame is that the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) arise from the three poisons, and that medicine is a branch of treatment that supports practice and does not reach the root. Within it, *rdo.dreg* appears in the materia medica as a drug against poisoning and chronic fevers, classed among the earth medicines (Clark lines 857, 869). No *bcud len* (essence extraction) or rejuvenation use of the lichen was found in the lines searched; a search of the Clark text for "lichen" and "Parmelia" found only lines 869 and 1029 and the index. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing here to take. The lichen is a minor drug of the Explanatory Tantra with a poison-and-fever indication, an uncertain identity, and no sourced dose. For a cultivator the honest answer is nothing. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Toxicity and identity:** the botanical identity is uncertain (Clark prints *Parmelia saxatilis* here and the same Latin name under another drug at line 1029). Do not substitute another lichen. Some lichens contain compounds that can irritate or sensitise.
- **Contamination:** lichens accumulate heavy metals and pollutants from air and rock, so material collected wild is of unknown purity.
- **Allergy:** lichen compounds are known contact allergens for some people.
- **Conservation:** slow-growing lichens are easily over-collected; legal status where collected was not retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **The tradition's own limit:** the texts give the drug for poisoning and chronic fever only; no other use is claimed.
