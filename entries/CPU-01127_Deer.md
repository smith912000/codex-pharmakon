# Deer (stag) — "Stag" (Bryan's English); Stag's Blood
## *The stag as a source of remedies in the Papyrus Ebers. Bryan's 1930 English prints "Stag" and gives no Egyptian word or binomial; the source list `_sources/ebers.csv` (line 87) gives "Stag's blood, Cervus elaphus" and cites Wikipedia, which is not relied on* | *(Volume III — Animal)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The English text on disk is C. P. Bryan's 1930 *The Papyrus Ebers*, a translation with summaries (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`); the Egyptian words are not printed. In Chapter VII, "Organic Remedies", Bryan says that a complete list of the animal materia medica "would not be possible since (as with many of the plant and mineral substances) the identity of a considerable number of the animals mentioned is still beyond us", and goes on to list "those animals whose identity is not in doubt, and who were called upon to sacrifice themselves in the cause of the Healing Art": "Lion Hippopotamus Crocodile Ox Cow Calf Mouse Porcupine Serpent Tortoise Lizard Mole Vulture Bird-of-Passage Swallow Pigeon Goose Duck Stag Gazelle Ram Goat..." (lines 1386-1398; `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` row "Stag", "Bryan 1930, Ch. VII Organic Remedies (animals) (line 1398)", "Bryan's English identification; no Egyptian word or binomial given in this list"). The stag is therefore one of the animals Bryan treats as securely identified. Bryan then lists the parts the Egyptians took from the animals as a whole (Hair, Feathers, Horns, Hoof, Hide, Eyes, Ears, Teeth, Brain, Blood, Bile, Bones, Marrow, Liver, Spleen, Guts, Uterus, Vulva, Phallus, Testicles, Semen, Fat, Tallow, Flesh, Shell, Wings, Excrement, Urine, Eggs, Milk, Toes, Tail; lines 1408-1417). The list is general, and no part is assigned to the stag. I found no sentence that says which parts of the stag were used.

Where the stag appears in Bryan's text, which I searched and opened:

- **Stag's Blood, in a remedy for hair in the eye** (lines 2882-2894). The heading reads "TO PREVENT THE HAIR GROWING INTO THE EYE AFTER IT HAS BEEN PULLED OUT". The ingredients are listed as "Incense-ground-in-Lizard's-dung, Cow's Blood, Ass's Blood, Pig's Blood, Dog's Blood, Stag's Blood, Collyrium, Incense", and the instruction is "Crush, rub into one in the different kinds of Blood, and place on the part whence the Hair has been pulled out so that it may not grow again." This is the stag's one printed appearance as an ingredient. It forms a set with five other animals' blood, and the text gives no separate action for the stag's blood. The remedy follows a paragraph on trichiasis (ingrowing eyelashes), where Bryan remarks that the sufferers "must have proved a very severe drain on the live-stock of the country" (line 2858).
- **Horn-of-a-Fawn, in a hair-dye** (line 4049). Among remedies "for dyeing grey hairs", Bryan writes: "Rubbing the head with the Horn-of-a-Fawn, warmed in Oil, was recommended." Bryan does not say what animal "a Fawn" is in the papyrus, and I do not equate it with the stag. A fawn is a young deer in ordinary English, but whether the Egyptian word names a deer is not stated in the lines read. [USER: verify]

The source list row also says "Stag's blood", and my reading agrees with the blood item above. The row's source column is Wikipedia, so the row is a pointer and not a locus.

No deity is attributed to the stag in any of these passages, and none is supplied here.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, blood is a fluid tissue of water, proteins (including albumin and haemoglobin), cells, lipids and salts; deer antler is bony tissue of calcium phosphate and collagen that is shed and regrown each year, and Bryan's "horn" may or may not mean antler. Nothing more specific can be said without a source.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Egyptian text, as Bryan summarises it, uses the stag's blood as one of six animal bloods in a preparation to stop an eyelash growing back, and a fawn's horn warmed in oil to dye grey hair. The Codex does not recommend either. The materials are not available from reliable sources, blood is an infection risk, and putting animal blood near the eye is unsafe.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations Bryan reports: the stag's blood crushed and rubbed together with lizard's-dung incense, collyrium and the blood of the cow, ass, pig and dog, "and place[d] on the part whence the Hair has been pulled out" (lines 2884-2894); and the horn of a fawn "warmed in Oil" and rubbed on the head (line 4049). Bryan summarises, and where he gives no measure the Ebers text itself may; the lines read give none. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Animal blood applied to skin is a source of infection (bacteria and bloodborne pathogens), and there is no established evidence that any animal blood prevents hair regrowth or that a deer horn dyes hair. Hair that grows into the eye (trichiasis) is treated today by removing the lash and, where it recurs, by procedures that destroy the follicle, under medical care. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's vital constitution, and Bryan's English renderings are modern identifications. The *ka*, *ba*, *akh* and *ib* are not mentioned in any passage read about the stag. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); the lines read for the stag's blood print no recitation with it, though Bryan summarises, and I cannot say whether the papyrus attaches one. ### Contraindications

- **Eye:** do not apply animal blood, or any animal product, near or in the eye.
- **Infection:** animal blood and untreated horn carry bacteria and can carry bloodborne and zoonotic pathogens.
- **Trichiasis:** ingrowing lashes need an eye clinician; this entry is not a treatment.
- **Law and conservation:** deer are protected or hunting-controlled in many jurisdictions, and the Codex does not recommend sourcing any part.
- **Ethics:** the Codex does not recommend killing or harming wild animals for these uses.
