# Silverweed / Potentilla

## *Potentilla anserina* L. (syn. *Argentina anserina* (L.) Rydb.) | Family: Rosaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Cool, Dry, Astringent (inferred from tannin chemistry and folk antidiarrhoeal use; not a securely codified humoral degree)
**Training Phase:** Foundation — Signature Study

---

### Origin & Tradition

Silverweed is a low, creeping rosette-forming perennial of the Rosaceae, spread across Europe, Asia and North America on damp, disturbed, often saline or trampled ground — roadsides, dune-slacks, riverbanks, pasture edges. Its pinnate leaves are silver-white and silky beneath (hence *argentina*, "silvery," and the English "silverweed"); it runs on long red stolons like a strawberry. The species epithet *anserina* — "of the goose" — records its old use as goose forage on grazed commons.

**DMM (Dioscorides / Greek herbal stream) and CM (Avicenna / Unani):** Silverweed belongs to the broad Western astringent-herb tradition — the tannin-rich Rosaceae used for fluxes, sore mouths and bleeding. It is well-attested in **European folk and early-modern herbal practice** as an astringent: a decoction or gargle for sore throat and mouth ulcers, an antidiarrhoeal, and — its most distinctive folk reputation — an **antispasmodic for cramp**, especially menstrual cramp (dysmenorrhoea) and intestinal/colic cramp, sometimes taken in milk. **Caution on the classical tags:** I could **not this session substantiate a specific chapter attribution in Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* or Avicenna's *Canon* for *P. anserina* under a secure identification.** Much of the transmitted "silverweed" material is Northern-European folk medicine rather than the Greek-Arabic canon proper, and the pre-Linnaean *Potentilla/Argentina/Tormentilla* names are tangled. Treat **CM** and **DMM** as **[USER: verify]** against critical editions before asserting either as a firm canonical citation; the honest current footing is *European folk astringent*, not confirmed Dioscoridean/Avicennan text.

The signature reading Kenetica keeps: a silver-backed, water-margin creeper that "binds and silvers" — read as a tightening, drying, styptic herb that draws slack, weeping tissue closed. That doctrine is coherent with the chemistry; it is not, on its own, evidence of the cramp claim.

**Critical taxonomic caution (the spine of this entry):** "*Potentilla anserina*" names **two practically different medicines.**
1. The **European silverweed** — the aerial, tannin-astringent herb of this entry (folk antidiarrhoeal, gargle, cramp).
2. A **Tibetan / Qinghai food-and-medicine tuber** — the swollen roots eaten and used by Tibetan and Qinghai peoples ("*juema*"/*zang ma*), treated in the Chinese-language literature as a **blood tonic, spleen-invigorating, thirst-quenching, liver-protective** remedy. Much of the modern pharmacology (polysaccharide, triterpene, hepatoprotective, immunomodulatory work) is done on **this tuberous form**, not on the European astringent herb. Every study below is labelled by which form/part it used. Conflating them is the single most common error in the secondary literature.

### Active Components

- **Hydrolysable tannins (ellagitannins)** and **condensed tannins (proanthocyanidins)** — the astringent principal of the aerial herb; agrimoniin-type ellagitannins are characteristic of this Rosaceae group.
- **Flavonoids** (quercetin-type), **phenolic acids**.
- **Triterpenes** and **polysaccharides** — highlighted in the systematic review as the main constituents responsible for the *tuberous* form's tonic/hepatoprotective pharmacology.
- The review catalogued **154 isolated compounds** from *P. anserina* overall, tannins/flavonoids/triterpenes dominating.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Silverweed is positioned as a **companion astringent to Tormentil** (*Potentilla erecta*, CPU-00903) — the milder, more widely-foraged member of the pair. Its honest Kenetica use-case is the classic tannin triad: **antidiarrhoeal, oral/throat astringent gargle, and topical styptic**, all mechanistically consistent with a high-tannin Rosaceae. The distinctive **antispasmodic / menstrual-cramp** reputation is a *traditional* claim carried on folk authority; it is plausible but **not established by human trials**.

Deflation, plainly: human clinical evidence for silverweed is **thin to absent**. The bulk of positive pharmacological data is **preclinical**, and a large share of it is on the **Tibetan tuber**, not the European astringent herb — so it cannot be used to validate the Western gargle-and-cramp use. Present silverweed to students as a sound traditional astringent with a mechanistically credible tannin basis, and be explicit that the cramp indication and the tonic/liver claims rest on different plant material and unproven ground.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Form | Traditional / folk handling | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Aerial herb, infusion/decoction | Handful of dried herb infused; drunk for diarrhoea; **gargle** for sore mouth/throat | Traditional; no validated standardised dose |
| Decoction in milk | Folk preparation for menstrual/intestinal cramp | Milk protein is thought to blunt tannin astringency; traditional, unverified |
| Topical wash / compress | Astringent styptic for minor weeping wounds, skin | External |
| Tibetan tuber (food/medicine) | Boiled/eaten as starchy food; decocted as blood/spleen tonic | **Different plant part and tradition** — do not equate with the astringent herb dose |

**Safety caveat:** no standardised, clinically validated dose exists for any indication — do not fabricate one. Tannin astringency sets the practical ceiling (see contraindications). Mark **[USER: verify]** for any quantified dose.

### Scientific Correlation

**Astringent / antidiarrhoeal / oral use — GRADE: traditional + mechanistically plausible; human clinical NULL.**
- No human RCT located for *P. anserina* in diarrhoea, dysmenorrhoea or sore throat. The rationale is tannin pharmacology (protein precipitation, mucosal tightening), not direct trial evidence.

**Anti-inflammatory (ellagitannin/urolithin pathway) — GRADE: preclinical, ex vivo (aerial herb included).**
- Piwowarski 2014 (J Ethnopharmacol): aqueous extract of ***P. anserina* L. herb** (correctly the European aerial part) was among ellagitannin-rich materials shown to yield **urolithin A/B/C** via human gut microbiota ex vivo; urolithins inhibited TNF-α (and urolithin C, IL-6) in a macrophage model. Supports an oral anti-inflammatory *mechanism* through gut-microbial metabolites — not a clinical outcome.

**Antimicrobial / anti-biofilm (oral) — GRADE: preclinical, in vitro (aerial parts).**
- Tomczyk 2010 (Molecules): aerial-part extracts of several *Potentilla* species incl. *P. anserina* moderately inhibited cariogenic streptococci and, more strongly, inhibited glucan/biofilm (dental-plaque) formation. In vitro; *P. fruticosa* was the strongest, not *P. anserina*.

**Blood-tonic / immunomodulatory / hepatoprotective — GRADE: preclinical; largely the TUBEROUS/Tibetan form (congener-of-use caution).**
- Li 2024 (J Ethnopharmacol systematic review): documents antioxidant, antiviral, blood-tonic, immune-regulatory, cardiovascular, antidiabetic and liver-protective activities, driven mainly by **polysaccharides and triterpenes** — largely the **Tibetan food-tuber** material, and the authors explicitly state traditional applications are only *partially* confirmed and call for further in vitro/in vivo work plus quality-control and safety evaluation. **These activities must NOT be attributed to the European astringent gargle-herb.**

**Extraction/phytochemistry — supporting.**
- Ложienė & Petraitytė 2026 (Plants): confirms hot-water extraction efficiently recovers polyphenols/tannins from Rosaceae leaves incl. this group — relevant to how the traditional infusion actually delivers tannins.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Read as **cool, dry and astringent** — a binding, tightening herb for slack, weeping, over-loose tissue (loose bowels, lax bleeding gums, weeping skin). This is inferred from the tannin chemistry and the consistent folk antidiarrhoeal/styptic use rather than a securely codified Hot/Cold degree in a named humoral text, so it is offered as interpretation.

### Contraindications

- **Tannin astringency:** may cause nausea, gastric upset or constipation on heavy or prolonged use.
- **Iron/mineral absorption:** high tannin intake reduces non-haem iron and other mineral absorption — caution in anaemia; separate from iron-rich meals/supplements.
- **Not for chronic self-treatment of undiagnosed diarrhoea or menstrual pain** — astringent symptom control can mask a condition needing diagnosis; persistent or severe symptoms need medical assessment.
- **Pregnancy/breastfeeding:** insufficient safety data for medicinal (concentrated) doses — avoid beyond culinary amounts without professional advice.
- **Identity/sourcing:** confirm which *P. anserina* material is in hand — the European astringent herb and the Tibetan tuber are used differently and are not interchangeable.
- **Drug binding:** tannins can bind and reduce absorption of some co-administered alkaloidal drugs — separate dosing.
