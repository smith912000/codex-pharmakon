### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The English text on disk is C. P. Bryan's 1930 *The Papyrus Ebers*, a translation with summaries; the Egyptian words are not printed. Bryan's list of the animals drawn on for remedies has the dog between the hog and the cat (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1404, page 35 of the book). Items in the pages read:

- *Toes-of-a-Dog.* The first hair remedy in the chapter on the hair is "Toes-of-a-Dog, Refuse-of-Dates, Hoof-of-an-Ass", which Bryan introduces as "the very first remedy for Baldness recorded in history", prepared for a woman and a queen: "REMEDY FOR HAIR-GROWTH PREPARED FOR SES, MOTHER OF HIS MAJESTY THE KING OF UPPER AND LOWER EGYPT, TETA, DECEASED" (lines 3996-3998; the OCR of this caption is garbled and is normalised here, and the names "Ses" and "Teta" are as the OCR prints them).
- *Vulva-of-a-Bitch.* In a hair-dye of the vulva of a bitch with the hoof of an ass and a tapeworm (lines 1377-1379), and in a second compound, "Hoof-of-an-Ass, roasted, Vulva-of-a-Bitch, a Black Tapeworm, and the *uauit*-worm-which-is-found-in-dung: the whole cooked in Oil and Gum and rubbed well into the scalp" (lines 4056-4058).
- *Dog's dung.* In a poultice for common scurf complicated by "Hardenings in all the Limbs of a Person": "Pieces-of-excrement, Cat's dung, Dog's dung, and Berries-of-the-Xet-plant", with the Scribe's remark that it "drives out all the scurf" (line 2643); and in two further poultices of lead or a hog's tooth with cat's and dog's dung (lines 2682-2688).
- *Dog's blood.* One of five bloods ground with incense and collyrium "to prevent the hair growing into the eye after it has been pulled out": "Cow's Blood, Ass's Blood, Pig's Blood, Dog's Blood, Stag's Blood" (lines 2882-2894). The list row is `_sources/ebers.csv` line 86, "Dog's blood", *Canis lupus familiaris*, source "Ebers; Wikipedia/Ebers Papyrus", identification marked uncertain. The same compound is the Ebers locus for `III_23_Pig_blood.md` and `III_32_Deer_blood.md`.
- *A dog's hide.* A line printed on one page reads "PUT A DOG'S HIDE ON HIM! DO NOT PRESS THE HAND ON HIM!" (line 2178), after a list headed by copper and verdigris at a page break. The OCR does not show whether this is a rubric, a caption or the end of a remedy, and the complaint is not identifiable from the file. [USER: verify]

The spine's list row for the dog itself is not in `_sources/ebers.csv` under "Dog"; the tag rests on the Bryan locus (line 1404). No recitation is reported with any dog compound in the lines read; since Bryan summarises, that is not proof the papyrus gives none. No deity is attributed.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Explanatory Tantra ch.20, the thirteen kinds of animal medicine. Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 1170): "Dog tongue: [has ten names and] heals all types of wounds ... Dog testicle: is ecbolic." Under droppings (line 1199): "Dog stool: [is especially beneficial against spirits]." The brackets are Clark's marker for commentary interpolations (his note, line 82), so the ten names and the spirits use come from the commentaries. Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` lines 3536, 3568): "Dog tongue heals wounds ... dog testicle help deliver placenta" and "The feces of dogs, wolves and the Tibetan snow cock reduce swelling." The two translations differ on the stool action (spirits in Clark's bracket; swelling in Men-Tsee-Khang) and on the testicle ("ecbolic" and "help deliver placenta" may be the same action; I did not check the Tibetan). The dog is also in the meat chapter's domestic-animals group, "goat, sheep, dog, pig, chicken, cat" (Clark line 672; Men-Tsee-Khang line 2917), and the "hill dog" is among the carnivorous animals (Clark line 672); Men-Tsee-Khang names the "dhole (Tibetan wild-dog)" (line 2912). Its animal glossary prints "Dog" and "*Canis familiaris* L." in adjacent columns (lines 6996-7001). The spine's list row is `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 353, "Dog tongue", marked identification uncertain.

### Active Components

An animal tongue, blood, hide or faeces is not a defined compound, and the sources name no active component. In general: the tongue is muscle (protein and some fat), blood is a fluid tissue of cells in plasma, hide is mainly collagen, and faeces carry the gut flora and any parasites of the animal. No quantities are given here because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Egyptian dog items are hair remedies, scurf poultices and an eyelash compound; the Tibetan ones are a wound medicine (the tongue), an ecbolic (the testicle) and a swelling or spirit medicine (the stool). The Codex has no protocol for any of it and offers none. The reasons are specific: dog faeces and blood are infection routes; rabies is a fatal disease carried by dogs and no home remedy replaces vaccination and post-exposure care; the dog is a companion animal and its slaughter for medicine is unacceptable or illegal in many places; and the sources give no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

Bryan gives the Egyptian items as compounds "made into one": the five bloods with collyrium and incense for the eyelash site, the dung poultices, and the dog's-toe, bitch's-vulva and hoof compounds cooked in oil and gum and rubbed on the scalp. The Tibetan texts give the action only, with no method in the lines read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Dogs carry *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter*, *Toxocara canis* (roundworm) and, where it is present, *Echinococcus granulosus* (the hydatid tapeworm), whose eggs pass in faeces; dog bites and saliva can carry *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* and, in endemic regions, rabies virus. Raw blood and offal from any mammal can carry infections. No effect of dog toes, bitch's vulva, dog blood, tongue or faeces on hair growth, scurf, wounds, labour or swelling is established. No clinical studies of the traditional uses were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* Every dog item in Bryan is a remedy for a complaint: hair loss and colour, scurf, an eyelash. None says what the dog does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); Bryan reports no recitation with the dog compounds, and since he summarises, that is not proof the papyrus gives none. No deity is attributed. Because Ebers identifications are modern hypotheses, the dog items are Bryan's English renderings, and the one list row that exists (`_sources/ebers.csv` line 86, dog's blood) rests on a source citing Wikipedia. **Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The dog's tongue, testicle and stool are among the animal medicines of the Explanatory Tantra, given for wounds, labour and swelling or spirits. No essence-extraction (*bcud len*) use is attested in the lines read, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition; none is asserted. The Tibetan texts that name the dog's stool as a remedy "against spirits" do so through Clark's bracketed commentary; that is a framing of disease and not a cultivation role.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do. In Egypt the dog items are hair and skin remedies made from toes, vulva, dung and blood; in Tibet the tongue, testicle and stool are drugs for wounds, labour and swelling. Neither tradition ties them to vitality, and the modern hazards, rabies chief among them, argue against any use.

### Contraindications

- **Rabies and other infections.** Dog bites, saliva, blood and faeces can transmit rabies, *Capnocytophaga*, *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter*, roundworm and hydatid disease; any bite or scratch needs prompt medical assessment. Never apply faeces or raw blood to the eye, scalp or broken skin.
- **Labour.** The testicle is described as ecbolic or placenta-delivering; childbirth needs trained care.
- **Legal and ethical.** The dog is a companion animal; killing or harming it for medicine is an offence in many jurisdictions, and eating or using dog products is prohibited or taboo in many cultures and religious rules. [USER: verify specifics]
- **Dose unknown.** No dose is given in the sources read.
