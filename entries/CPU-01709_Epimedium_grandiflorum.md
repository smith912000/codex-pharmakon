# Epimedium grandiflorum — rta.rmig, Tamig, Tamik (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Epimedium grandiflorum, the first of four binomials Clark 1995 prints for rta.rmig (lit. "horse's hoof"; then Aristolochia contorta, Asarum heterotropoides and Viola biflora); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints the name "Tamig" and, by column position, a different plant* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under this binomial only by Clark.** No other text read for this entry names this species as the Tibetan drug. The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. Two other texts on disk name the genus *Epimedium* at genus level only, and neither is tagged on this row: Yang 1998 prints "Yin Yang Huo (Herba Epimedii)" under "Herbs: Middle Class" (`Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`, lines 2948 and 2956), and Dioscorides 4-19 prints the translators' line "SUGGESTED: Epimedium alpinum — Barrenwort, Bishop's Hat" (`Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 8180 to 8182). Neither names *Epimedium grandiflorum*, and the Codex does not read them as attestations of this drug. They are reported in the notes to this batch as possible tag questions. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says: "Therefore more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the line prints four, of which the spine row takes the first (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 184). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1094).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry follows *brag.lcham* (*Bergenia ciliata*, line 1093) and precedes *gra.ma* (*Caragana franchetia*, line 1095). It reads:

"rta.rmig (lit. ‘horse’s hoof’): Epimedium grandiflorum: Aristolochia contorta: Asarum heterotropoides: Viola biflora: [grows at the base of rocks. It has a lobed leaf and yellow flower, and both leaf and flower are shaped like a horse’s hoof. In the absence of stalk and branches the leaf grows straight from the ground (sessile growth)]. Epimedium constricts the openings of the channels."

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the habitat at the base of rocks, the lobed leaf, the yellow flower, the likeness of both to a horse's hoof and the note on sessile growth. Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the four Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the sentence "Epimedium constricts the openings of the channels." That sentence names *Epimedium* itself, which is the only place in either Tibetan text that does. It gives no taste, no part used and no preparation. Clark's index of Latin names lists *Epimedium grandiflorum* (line 1583).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text on "Tamik" (lines 3456 to 3457).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428). The sentence reads: "Drak cham closes wounds and Tamik seals the aperture of channels." The names stand in the same order as Clark's *brag.lcham* and *rta.rmig* (lines 1093 and 1094), and the action agrees in kind with Clark's "constricts the openings of the channels". The printed index lists "Tamik 210" (line 7507). The text gives no Latin name in the sentence. The Codex reads "Tamik" as the same name as *rta.rmig* from the spelling, the order and the agreement of the action. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary.* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "T" block (the letter at line 5858), prints "Tamig" (line 5865) and, two lines below, the transliteration "rta rmig" (line 5867), which is Clark's *rta.rmig*. The scientific names for the block are in a separate run (from line 5928), where the first four are "Fraxinus rhynchophylla" (with the English "Chinese ash", line 5928), "Oxytropis microphylla" ("Crazyweed", line 5930), "Caltha palustris" ("Marsh marigold", lines 5932 to 5934) and "Angelica sinensis" ("Hog fennel", lines 5936 to 5938). The phonetic names begin "Tab seng", "Tak sha", "Tamig" and "Tangkun" (lines 5860 to 5869). Counting in order, "Tamig" is the third phonetic name and *Caltha palustris* is the third scientific name, and the fourth pair, "Tangkun" with *Angelica sinensis*, agrees with the transliteration "tang kun" (line 5871). The Codex therefore reads the glossary as pairing "Tamig" with *Caltha palustris*, by column position only, and not with *Epimedium*: the word *Epimedium* is not printed anywhere in the Men-Tsee-Khang text read. The two Tibetan texts therefore disagree on the plant, and the Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Epimedium* is a genus of the barberry family (Berberidaceae), and that species of the genus are generally known to carry prenylated flavonoid glycosides such as icariin. It is also a general fact that *Aristolochia* is a genus whose species are generally known to carry aristolochic acids, which are toxic to the kidney and carcinogenic, and that *Asarum* belongs to the same plant family (Aristolochiaceae). *Viola biflora* is a violet. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a plant that "constricts the openings of the channels" (Clark, line 1094) and "seals the aperture of channels" (Men-Tsee-Khang, lines 3456 to 3457). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only by Clark's first choice among four, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary describes the leaf and flower and does not say which part is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** No taste, power or post-digestive taste is printed for this drug in the lines read. Clark's commentary layer (line 1094) gives habitat and shape, not taste. The name does not appear in the Men-Tsee-Khang taste-group lists of chapter 19 (which begin at line 3204) that were read for this entry. The one quality printed is the action on the "channels" in both texts, which is an action and not a taste or power.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it constricts the openings of the channels, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same in kind. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice, and neither text read says that it builds vitality, strength or sexual power: the sentences cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and the link from this binomial to that framing rests on Clark's first choice among four.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. In the Tibetan texts it is a drug that constricts or seals the openings of the channels, with no preparation and no dose, and its Latin name is one of four that Clark offers. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Clark's list includes an *Aristolochia*, and aristolochic acids are generally known to injure the kidney, so the open identity is itself a hazard. No plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
