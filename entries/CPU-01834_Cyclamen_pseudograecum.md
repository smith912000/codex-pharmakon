# Cyclamen pseudograecum — Kuklaminos etera, "the other cyclamen" (Dioscorides 2-195)

## *Cyclamen pseudograecum, printed "Cyclamen psuedo-graecum", the single binomial on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 2-195 (no English name is printed with it)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM); this entry rests on one text, the Osbaldeston and Wood (2000) English translation of *De Materia Medica*, and on one short chapter of it.** The binomial is the translators' SUGGESTED gloss, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. A search of the Sushruta, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Tibetan text folders for *Cyclamen* found no hit in the primary texts, and the row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The row cites chapter 2-195, *KUKLAMINOS ETERA* (heading at line 6240, SUGGESTED line 6241, text at line 6242, printed page 326), in Book Two among the "Herbs with a sharp quality". The whole chapter was read. It is a separate chapter from 2-194, *KUKLAMINOS*, which carries the other cyclamen binomials and is written as I_1397; the two do not share a chapter.

The chapter reads in full: "The other cyclamen (also called cissanthemon or cissophylon) has leaves similar to cissus but smaller; thick, knotty stalks rolling around the trees that grow next to them, similar to the tendrils of vines. The flowers are white and fragrant, and the fruit is bunches of berries similar to cissus, soft, single, sharp to the taste and viscous. The root is useless. It grows in rough places. One teaspoon of the fruit (taken in a drink with two cups of white wine [daily] for forty days) melts the spleen, and brings down the intestines by urine. It is taken in a drink for asthma. Taken in a drink it purges away the residue [placenta] after childbearing."

*What it records.* The plant is described by its leaves (like cissus, smaller), its stalks (thick, knotty, rolling around neighbouring trees like vine tendrils), its flowers (white, fragrant) and its fruit (bunches of soft, single, sharp, viscous berries). The root "is useless", so the part used is the fruit. The place is "rough places". The uses are three: the fruit melts the spleen and brings down "the intestines by urine"; it is taken for asthma; and it purges away the placenta after childbirth. The bracket "[daily]" in the dose is the translators' interpolation, as is "[placenta]". The two names the chapter gives, cissanthemon and cissophylon, are the same two that 2-194 prints in its own list of names.

**Identity doubt.** The translators' SUGGESTED line names one binomial and no English name. The description is of a plant with twining stalks like the tendrils of vines, white fragrant flowers, berries in bunches and a useless root. The chapter before it describes a plant with a short bare stalk and a black root that is the part used, and this chapter calls itself "the other cyclamen" while the details differ; the translators' gloss is the only link to a *Cyclamen* species. The Codex does not decide the identity, and the entry should be read as a record of a Greek chapter with a disputed gloss. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Cyclamen*, *kuklaminos etera* and the chapter number 2-195 found no entry on this chapter. **III_60** (*Cheese*) cites "the second cyclamen" at "2-195, line 6266" for a cheese-keeping use ("wrapped in the leaves of the second cyclamen, cheese 'is kept from putrefying'"). The sentence is "Wrapped in the leaves, cheese is kept from putrefying", and it stands at line 6266 inside chapter 2-196b (*DRAKONTION MIKRON*, heading line 6250), not in 2-195; chapter 2-195 says nothing of cheese. The Codex flags this as a data fix in the report. The row for 2-194 is I_1397. Nothing is merged. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent. As a general fact, *Cyclamen* is a genus whose tubers contain irritant saponins, but the plant described in this chapter has a root the text calls "useless" and a berried fruit, and nothing is stated here about its composition. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a fruit taken in wine for forty days to "melt" the spleen and bring down the intestines by urine, for asthma, and to expel the afterbirth. The Codex gives no protocol for any of these. A forty-day course of an unidentified berry in wine, and an agent to expel the placenta, are not things this entry endorses.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives one measure: "One teaspoon of the fruit (taken in a drink with two cups of white wine [daily] for forty days)" for the spleen and the intestines (line 6242). The asthma use and the use after childbirth are "taken in a drink", with no quantity or vehicle. No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identification is a translators' suggestion, and the description does not clearly fit the species named, so no modern finding about *Cyclamen* can be set beside the chapter.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality. The fruit is "sharp to the taste and viscous", and the translators place the chapter among the "Herbs with a sharp quality".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: the plant is told by its smaller cissus-like leaves, its twining knotty stalks, its white fragrant flowers and its bunched berries, and by its habitat; and the text states which part is worthless (the root) and which is used (the fruit). A forty-day course is given a measure and a vehicle. The chapter assigns the plant no status beyond that of a medicinal fruit. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The useful lesson is Dioscorides' method of fixing a plant by leaf, stalk, flower and fruit, and of saying which part to discard. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter records that the fruit expels the afterbirth and acts on the spleen and the intestines; it prints no warning. The identification is a translators' suggestion that the description does not clearly bear out, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Berries sharp to the taste and taken for forty days in wine are a poisoning risk if the identity is wrong. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy beyond the text's own use after childbirth was read. [USER: verify]
