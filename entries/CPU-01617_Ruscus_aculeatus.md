### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed SUGGESTED line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-132. IPPOGLOSSON*, heading at line 9193, SUGGESTED line at 9194 ("Ruscus aculeatus — Butcher's Broom"), the translators' cross-reference "See 4-147" at line 9195, and the text at line 9196. It stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), after *4-131. LEONTOPODION* and before *4-133. ANTIRRINON*.

The whole chapter reads: "Hippoglosson is a little shrub similar to myrtus agrestis [myrtle] with thin leaves, and prickly filaments; and on the top (as it were) a little tongue by the leaves. The filaments seem to be an effective amulet for headaches. The root and juice are mixed with warm compresses. Some call this antirrhinon, anarrhinon, or lychnis sylvestris."

Three things are in the text. First, a description: a little shrub, like wild myrtle, with thin leaves and "prickly filaments", and at the top, "as it were", a little tongue by the leaves (which is the Greek name's sense, though the chapter does not say so). Second, one amuletic use: "The filaments seem to be an effective amulet for headaches." The wording is "seem", which is a hedged report and not a plain statement, and the chapter says nothing about how the filaments are worn. Third, a plain preparation: "The root and juice are mixed with warm compresses." The chapter does not say what the compresses are for, and does not name a complaint. Three alternative names are printed: *antirrhinon*, *anarrhinon* and *lychnis sylvestris*.

**The cross-reference.** The SUGGESTED line says "See 4-147". Chapter *4-147. DAPHNE ALEXANDRINA* (heading at line 9320) carries the reciprocal "See 4-132" and a SUGGESTED line that also prints *Ruscus hypoglossum* and *Ruscus hippoglossum*; it is a different chapter, with its own description and uses, and is not read into this entry beyond that cross-reference. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides reports that the filaments "seem" to be an effective amulet for headaches, and that the root and juice are mixed with warm compresses. These are the text's reports. The Codex gives no protocol for headache, a charm worn for headache is not a therapeutic claim the Codex can assess, and the compresses are given with no named complaint. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-132 gives two handling instructions: the filaments serve as an amulet, and the root and juice are "mixed with warm compresses". It gives no quantity, no frequency, no duration and no complaint for the compresses. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. It prints only the form of the plant (thin leaves, prickly filaments, a "little tongue" at the top) and the warmth of the compresses ("warm"), which is the preparation and not a quality of the plant.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter does record an amuletic use, filaments that "seem" an effective amulet for headaches, but it records it with a hedge and as something seen, and recording is not endorsing. The part of the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition that is present here is the description of the plant (a little shrub like wild myrtle, thin leaves, prickly filaments, a little tongue at the top) and the naming of the parts used (filaments, root, juice). The plant is given no status in the chapter beyond that of a reported amulet and a root-and-juice compress. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It offers a description, a hedged amulet and an unspecified compress, without amounts, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. A headache that is severe, sudden or persistent needs medical assessment, and an amulet or compress must not replace it. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify] None of the traditional uses above is advice.
