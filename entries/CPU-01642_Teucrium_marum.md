# Teucrium marum — Maron or hysobrium (Dioscorides 3-49); "Marum Germander, Cat Thyme"

## *Teucrium marum, the one name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-49, glossed "Marum Germander, Cat Thyme"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk lists the plant under this name (the name indexes in `Sources/*/extract/names.csv` were searched). The entry rests on one short chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *3-49. MARON* at line 6952, SUGGESTED line at 6954, text at line 6955. The chapter stands in Book Three, "Roots", after *3-48. MELILOTOS* (line 6941) and before *3-50. AKINOS* (line 6956). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 455, and the translators' indexes print "Teucrium marum 420" (line 17852) and "Marum Germander 420" (line 16431).

The whole chapter reads: "Marum or hysobrium is a well-known herb full of sprigs, similar in the flower to origanum, but the leaves of this are much paler, and the flower sweeter. It has abilities similar to sisymbrium [2-155] — somewhat astringent and gently heating. Applied, it stops gangrenous ulceration, and it is mixed with the hot ingredients of compound ointments. It grows in abundance both near Magnesia and near Tralles. It is also called origanis."

The chapter describes the plant by comparison: a "well-known herb full of sprigs", the flower like that of *origanum* but the leaves "much paler" and the flower "sweeter". The qualities it prints are two, "somewhat astringent and gently heating", and it says these are abilities "similar to sisymbrium". Two uses follow, both external: applied, it "stops gangrenous ulceration", and it is "mixed with the hot ingredients of compound ointments". The provenance printed is that it grows "in abundance both near Magnesia and near Tralles". The chapter's one other name is *origanis*, and the heading's Greek *Maron* is paired in the text with *hysobrium*.

**The chapter it points to.** 2-155 *SISUMBRION* (line 5864) is read by the translators as water mint (*Mentha aquatica*) and, as another usage, hedge mustard. It is said there to be "similar to garden mint, yet broader-leaved and with a sweeter scent" and, in its own words, "warming". Only its first lines were read for this entry, and none of its uses is carried over to 3-49.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter number, the Greek heading, *Teucrium marum* and *Marum* found no entry on 3-49. The translators' gloss "Cat Thyme" is also printed on the SUGGESTED line of 3-124 *POLION* (line 7656, *Teucrium polium*), the chapter of I_716, and the two are separate chapters on separate plants. The other Teucrium entries of the volume (I_654 *Teucrium chamaedrys*, I_712 *Teucrium scorodonia*, I_740 *Teucrium fruticans*, I_986 *Teucrium chamaepitys*) belong to their own chapters and were not read for this entry. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. It is a general fact that *Teucrium* (the germanders) belongs to the mint family (Lamiaceae) and that its species are generally aromatic. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the translators' marum germander. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records an external application for "gangrenous ulceration" and a place in warming ointments. These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. Gangrene is a condition for emergency medical care, and the Codex gives no protocol for it.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives two modes of use: the plant "(applied)", and the plant as an ingredient "mixed with the hot ingredients of compound ointments". It prints no part, no quantity, no frequency and no duration, and it does not list the ointments. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that gangrene is a medical emergency in which tissue dies, and that no applied herb is a treatment for it. It is also a general fact that some germander products (taken by mouth, in the species commonly sold as medicine) have been linked to liver injury. Nothing is stated about the translators' marum germander. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 3-49 prints the qualities "somewhat astringent and gently heating", and attributes them by comparison to *sisymbrium*. It prints no moist or dry quality. The sweet smell of the flower is a sensory note and not a quality.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a comparison for recognition (the flower against *origanum*, the pale leaves, the sweeter flower), a short sensory assay (somewhat astringent, gently heating) and two named places where the herb grows in abundance, Magnesia and Tralles. This is the provenance that the spec lists for the tradition. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is the habit of naming where a good supply grew. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 3-49 prints no warning. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. The chapter's own use, on ulceration that is gangrenous, is a condition that needs emergency care. Germanders as a group have been linked to liver harm when taken by mouth, and the chapter gives only external uses, but it also gives no dose and no safety information for pregnancy or children.
