Cosmopolitan weed of disturbed habitats, meadows, roadsides; native to Europe and western Asia; naturalized in North and South America, Australia, South Africa.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Score: 5/9** (DMM · CM · FMT · European folk confirmed; AT tentative)

St. John's Wort achieves 5/9 convergence despite being primarily a European/Mediterranean plant because of its dramatic morphological appearance (the blood-red hypericin oil from crushed flowers creates one of the most visually memorable pharmacological demonstrations in botany) and because its wound-healing tradition is genuine, demonstrable, and consistent across traditions.

**The antidepressant application** is entirely a modern pharmacological discovery — Dioscorides did not treat depression with it, and classical traditions applied it for wounds, diuresis, and nerve pain. The connection between "drives away evil spirits" (Fuga daemonum) and modern antidepressant pharmacology represents one of the most intriguing translations in the Codex: the ancient observation of psychological benefit (the plant that "chases devils" — interpreted as mental darkness, melancholy, evil thoughts in a spiritual framework) has been confirmed by 37 RCTs as antidepressant in mild-to-moderate depression.

**The red oil = wound herb** convergence is the cross-traditional constant: the vivid red hypericin-containing oil was universally associated with wound treatment (the red color was read as blood-treating in Doctrine of Signatures logic; the actual antimicrobial mechanism of hypericin against wound pathogens made this self-fulfilling).

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Naphthodianthrones (signature compounds):**
- **Hypericin** (0.03–0.3% in aerial parts; concentrated in black gland dots and flowering tops) — antiviral (photoactivated); antidepressant (contribution debated); photosensitizer (adverse)
- **Pseudohypericin** — similar structure; co-present with hypericin

**Phloroglucinol derivatives (primary antidepressant class):**
- **Hyperforin** (2–4% in aerial parts; concentrated in flowers; extremely unstable — degrades rapidly; key reason fresh or CO₂-extracted preparations are preferred) — principal antidepressant compound; reuptake inhibitor across multiple neurotransmitter systems (SRI + DARI + NORI + ChRI + GABA reuptake inhibition); CYP3A4 inducer (primary drug interaction compound)
- **Adhyperforin** — structural analog; co-active

**Flavonoids:**
- **Quercetin, kaempferol, rutin** — anti-inflammatory; antioxidant
- **Amentoflavone** (biflavonoid) — antidepressant (MAO-A/B inhibition); anxiolytic (GABA-A)
- **Biapigenin** — anxiolytic; GABA-A modulator; antidepressant
- **Hyperoside** (quercetin-3-galactoside) — antidepressant potential; antiviral

**Xanthones:**
- 1,3,6,7-tetrahydroxyxanthone — MAO inhibition; antidepressant contribution

**Tannins:**
- Catechins, epicatechin, proanthocyanidins — antioxidant; astringent wound healing

**Essential oil (minor):**
- 2-methyl-3-buten-2-ol (minor sedative; GABA-A)

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Antidepressant (multi-target — hyperforin dominant):**
- **Hyperforin:** Inhibits Na⁺-conductive transporter (SCBT — sodium-conductive background transporter) → increases intracellular Na⁺ → vesicular monoamine transporter impairment → reduced reuptake of serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine, GABA, glutamate, acetylcholine simultaneously
- This multi-neurotransmitter reuptake inhibition is mechanistically distinct from SSRIs (which selectively inhibit the serotonin transporter SERT)
- **Amentoflavone:** non-selective MAO-A and MAO-B inhibition → reduced monoamine catabolism → increased serotonin and dopamine availability
- **Biapigenin + 2-methyl-3-buten-2-ol:** GABA-A modulation → anxiolytic component of antidepressant effect
- **Combined mechanism:** SRI + DARI + NORI + MAO inhibition + GABAergic = the broadest-spectrum natural antidepressant in the pharmacopoeia; likely explaining why multi-drug treatment-resistant patients sometimes respond to SJW when SSRIs alone have failed

**Antiviral (hypericin — photoactivated):**
- Hypericin + light (UV or visible): excited state hypericin → generates singlet oxygen (¹O₂) and superoxide (O₂⁻) → oxidative inactivation of enveloped viruses (HIV, HSV, EBV, influenza)
- Without light activation: hypericin is much less virucidal
- Clinical application: photodynamic therapy potential; topical photodynamic virucidal use
- Historical: the red oil applied to wounds under sunlight would have had genuine photodynamic antimicrobial action — the ancient wound treatment was photodynamic therapy millennia before the term existed

**CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein induction (adverse — drug interactions):**
- **Hyperforin:** pregnane X receptor (PXR) agonist → transcriptional upregulation of CYP3A4, CYP2C9, P-glycoprotein, MDR1
- CYP3A4 induction → accelerated metabolism of CYP3A4-dependent drugs → reduced drug plasma levels → therapeutic failure
- P-gp induction → increased drug efflux from cells → reduced intracellular drug concentrations
- **Clinically significant affected drugs:** oral contraceptives (OCP failure → unintended pregnancy), antiretrovirals (HIV treatment failure), cyclosporine (transplant rejection), warfarin (INR reduction → clotting), digoxin, statins, benzodiazepines, irinotecan
- **MHRA (UK) and FDA drug interaction alerts issued; European Pharmacopoeia limits hyperforin content in some preparations to reduce drug interaction risk**

**Photosensitization (adverse):**
- Hypericin: UV absorption → excited state → energy transfer to skin chromophores → phototoxic skin reaction
- Particularly in fair-skinned individuals and with prolonged sun exposure
- Clinical: erythema, burning, blister formation in sun-exposed skin during SJW use
- Relevant at high doses; standard therapeutic doses produce low risk in most individuals

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Regulatory:**
- Commission E (Germany): APPROVED for mild-to-moderate depression; anxiety; nervous restlessness; the original German Commission E approval (1984) predated most of the RCT evidence
- EMA: No positive THMP listing for depression (withdrawn 2007 due to drug interaction concerns); however, remains legally marketed as a traditional herbal in many EU countries under national provisions

**Clinical trials — most extensively trialed antidepressant herb in history (37 RCTs as of 2020):**
- **Linde et al. (1996) BMJ (first major meta-analysis):** 23 RCTs; SJW significantly superior to placebo for mild-moderate depression; comparable to tricyclic antidepressants; fewer side effects
- **Shelton et al. (2001) JAMA (NCCAM-sponsored):** SJW vs. sertraline vs. placebo for major depression; SJW not significantly different from placebo (in SEVERE/major depression context — appropriate limitation)
- **Hypericum Depression Trial Study Group (2002) JAMA:** 340 patients; SJW vs. sertraline vs. placebo; both active treatments showed limited superiority to placebo in this severe depression cohort
- **Kasper et al. (2006) *International Journal of Neuropsychopharmacology*:** SJW extract WS 5570 (900mg/day) vs. paroxetine 20mg for moderate depression; equivalent efficacy; significantly better tolerability (fewer side effects with SJW)
- **Szegedi et al. (2005) BMJ:** SJW extract WS 5570 vs. paroxetine 40mg; SJW non-inferior to paroxetine; superior tolerability
- **Cochrane Review (Linde et al. 2008):** 29 trials, 5,489 patients; SJW superior to placebo; equivalent to standard antidepressants for mild-moderate depression; significantly better tolerability profile

**Consensus conclusion:** SJW is an evidence-based treatment for mild-to-moderate depression with efficacy equivalent to SSRIs/TCAs and significantly superior tolerability. NOT appropriate for severe depression (insufficient evidence; safety risk of delaying effective treatment). Drug interactions are the primary safety concern.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Fuga daemonum — the original name for antidepressant pharmacotherapy:**
The medieval Latin name for St. John's Wort was *Fuga daemonum* — "flight of demons." The plant was hung over icons, doors, and in rooms to drive away evil spirits, darkness, and madness. This spiritual-medical application predates the term "depression" by centuries: the demons being driven away were the interior demons of melancholy, black bile, darkness, and sadness. The 37 RCTs confirming antidepressant efficacy are, in retrospect, validations of a pharmacological observation so ancient that it could only be expressed in the vocabulary of demonic affliction. The pharmacology is exactly what the name claims: hyperforin drives away the neurochemical correlates of melancholy.

**The St. John's Day harvest — pharmacological timing:**
The tradition of harvesting SJW on June 24 (St. John's Day, midsummer) is pharmacologically precise: hyperforin content peaks in the flowers at or just before full flower, which occurs in Northern European latitudes at midsummer. Traditional farmers who harvested "on St. John's Day" were harvesting at peak hyperforin concentration. The religious holiday encoded optimal pharmacological timing.

**The red oil miracle:**
The fresh flower buds of St. John's Wort, when crushed, release a vivid blood-red oil (from hypericin). This visual property created one of the most powerful Doctrine of Signatures identifications: a plant that bleeds red is for treating wounds that bleed. The photodynamic antimicrobial activity of hypericin validates this association: the same red pigment that signals "wound herb" IS the wound antimicrobial compound.

**Dioscorides (DMM III.156):** "Hyperikon drunk in wine for 40 days is good for sciatica." Sciatica (lumbar nerve root pain) is a specific DMM application. Modern herbal use for nerve pain (neuropathy, post-herpetic neuralgia) validates this 2,000-year-old indication through the hyperforin mechanism (multiple neurotransmitter modulation including substance P and glutamate, both involved in neuropathic pain).

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### ⚠️ CRITICAL DRUG INTERACTION WARNING

St. John's Wort significantly reduces plasma concentrations of many important drugs through CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein induction. **ALWAYS review all current medications before recommending SJW.**

**Absolute contraindications (concurrent medications where SJW is NEVER appropriate):**
- **Oral contraceptives** → OCP failure → unintended pregnancy
- **Antiretrovirals (HIV treatment)** → viral load rebound → HIV treatment failure
- **Ciclosporin/tacrolimus** (post-transplant immunosuppression) → transplant rejection
- **Warfarin** → reduced INR → thromboembolism risk
- **Digoxin** → reduced plasma levels → cardiac decompensation
- **Irinotecan** (chemotherapy) → reduced efficacy → cancer treatment failure
- **SSRIs/SNRIs/MAOIs** → serotonin syndrome risk (MAOI component + SSRI = additive serotonergic)
- **Triptans (migraine)** → serotonin syndrome risk

**Primary modern applications:**

1. **Mild-to-moderate depression (evidence-based):** 300mg standardized extract (0.3% hypericin + 3–5% hyperforin) 3× daily (total 900mg/day); full therapeutic effect requires 4–6 weeks; patient must be counseled on drug interactions before commencing; NOT for severe depression, suicidal ideation, or bipolar disorder

2. **Anxiety / Nervous restlessness:** Same dose as depression; combine with valerian and lemon balm for enhanced anxiolytic effect

3. **Topical wound healing / Post-herpetic neuralgia (topical — avoids systemic interactions):** SJW-infused oil (red oil made by cold-infusing fresh flowers in olive oil, 4–6 weeks in sunlight) applied to wounds, burns, nerve pain areas; photodynamic antimicrobial + anti-inflammatory + analgesic without systemic drug interactions

4. **Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD):** SJW 900mg/day + light therapy (10,000 lux) combination; the photosensitization concern (hypericin + UV) becomes the therapeutic mechanism when controlled via structured light therapy — the photodynamic antidepressant activation

**Standardization:** Always use standardized preparations — hyperforin content varies 10-fold between products. CO₂ extract (supercritical extraction) preserves hyperforin better than ethanol extraction. Minimum 3% hyperforin by dry weight in extract.

**Photosensitivity management:** Advise patients to use high-SPF sunscreen and avoid intense sun exposure during SJW use, particularly in fair-skinned individuals. Discontinue 2 weeks before planned intense sun exposure (holiday, outdoor work).
