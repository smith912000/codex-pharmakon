# Bladder stone (urinary calculus) — ḥajar al-mathāna (حجر المثانة)

## *The stone formed in the bladder; the text does not say of which animal, and the Canon declines to endorse the remedy* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** CM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Avicenna's *Canon* among the texts on disk.** Book II, entry *ḥajar al-mathāna*, Arabic text lines 13469 to 13474. It is the last entry of the letter *ḥāʾ*, and the edition ends the letter there with the words "this is the end of the discourse on the letter *ḥāʾ*, and that is fifty-three drugs" (lines 13473 to 13474). The text does not say whether the stone is from a man or from a beast.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** The whole entry reads: "What it is: a group (*qawm*) said that the stone generated in the bladder, when it is drunk by one afflicted with that [complaint], breaks up the gravel of the bladder (*fattata ḥaṣā al-mathāna*), and this is one of the treatments that I do not endorse (*lā aqūlu bihā*)" (قال قوم إن الحجر المتولد في المثانة إذا شرب من ابتلى بذلك فتت حصى المثانة وهذا من المعالجات التي لا أقول بها). So the entry is a report of other people's claim, with Avicenna's refusal. It has no temperament, no dose, no preparation and no authority named. The entry before it, *ḥurār al-ṣakhr* (a growth on rock), also carries an author's refusal: "Dioscorides said: it stops the blood, and I do not say so" (line 13468); that is a different drug. The idea itself appears elsewhere in the Canon: under the ass (*ḥimār*, heading at line 13390) it is said that "the urine of the wild one breaks up the stone in the bladder, as is said" (lines 13396 to 13397). That too is a separate entry.

### Active Components

Urinary calculi are made of crystalline salts and an organic matrix; the commonest are calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate, with uric acid and struvite stones also common. The text gives no composition.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, and the Canon itself rejects it. The entry records a remedy that the author declines to endorse. Drinking the stone of a person or animal is a hygiene and infection hazard and has no recommended use; the Codex gives no protocol.

**Practitioner's note.** The use of the entry is the discipline it shows: the Canon reports a claim, names it, and says it does not hold it. A cultivator can take that as a model for handling inherited remedies.

### Contraindications

- Ingesting human or animal tissue carries infection risk.
- Urinary stone with pain, fever or obstruction needs medical care.
- Animal products may be restricted by law and ethics; the animal is not named.
