# Thalassomeli — Sea Honey (as printed in Dioscorides 5-20)

## *Seawater sweetened with honey: a compound drink of honey, rainwater and seawater, which Dioscorides describes in De Materia Medica 5-20 and the translator glosses "Seawater Sweetened with Honey"; the identification is the text's own description, of high confidence as to what it is, and it is a preparation and not a mineral, shelved in Volume II beside its seawater source* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM) among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 8 November 2026 as an unverified placeholder (key "THALASSOMELI [DMM]"); this entry replaces it. It is a separate row from Seawater (CPU-01304, `II_44_Seawater.md`, from 5-19), which it is made from, and from Water (CPU-01078, from 5-18).

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 5-20, *Thalassomeli*, glossed "SUGGESTED: Seawater Sweetened with Honey" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 9870 to 9872). It stands in Book Five among the wines and honeyed or soured drinks, after 5-19 *Udor thalassion* (seawater) and before 5-21 *Oxus* (vinegar). The whole chapter reads: "Thalassomeli is thought to purge very well. It is made from the same amount each of honey, rainwater and seawater, strained, and placed in the sun in a pitchglazed jar in the burning heat of the hottest days. Some mix two parts of boiled seawater and one of honey and store it in a jar. This is more moderate and milder than seawater alone."

The chapter therefore gives two recipes and one claim. The first recipe is equal parts of honey, rainwater and seawater, strained, and set in the sun in a pitch-glazed jar in the hottest days. The second is two parts of boiled seawater to one of honey, stored in a jar. The claim is that it is "thought to purge very well", and that the second form is "more moderate and milder than seawater alone". Dioscorides attributes the purging to report ("is thought"), and gives no source for it.

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, the drink is a solution of sugars (honey) and mineral salts (seawater, chiefly sodium chloride with magnesium and other salts) in water. Honey is mostly fructose and glucose. Sodium and magnesium salts in strong solution draw water into the gut and act as osmotic laxatives. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Purging ("thought to purge very well"). In its second form, a milder purge than seawater alone.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no number of cups, no duration, no season for taking it, and no condition for which the purge is given. The text only lets the reader place it beside 5-19, where seawater itself is "given as a purge" by itself or with posca, wine or honey.

The Codex gives no protocol, and the drink is not recommended: a salt-laden purge risks dehydration and salt overload, and home-made seawater is of unknown purity.

### Preparation & Dosing

Two preparations, both from 5-20 (lines 9870 to 9872): (1) equal measures of honey, rainwater and seawater, strained, placed in the sun in a pitch-glazed jar in the burning heat of the hottest days; (2) two parts of boiled seawater and one of honey, stored in a jar. No dose of the finished drink is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] A neighbouring chapter, 5-19 (seawater), says that seawater put in jars alone "becomes unpoisonous", and that "some boil it beforehand"; this is a note on 5-19 and is not a dose for 5-20.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Honey and seawater are both well-known materials; strong salt solutions act as osmotic laxatives and can cause dehydration and electrolyte disturbance when swallowed. A pitch-glazed jar in strong sun is also a setting for fermentation of a sugary liquid, which the text does not discuss. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** The text prints one quality for the drink, that it purges, and one comparison, that the second form is more moderate and milder than seawater alone. No hot, cold, dry or moist grade is printed for it in 5-20. For seawater itself, 5-19 prints "hot, sharp, and bad for the stomach", which is a property of the other chapter.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent; framed only by Dioscorides' discipline of attention.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention to making: the proportions, the heat of the hottest days, the vessel, and the choice between the strong form and the milder boiled form. It carries no cultivation status in the text.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports drinking it. The record is a purge of reputation ("is thought"), and the sun-made drink of unknown strength is a hazard of its own. A cultivator may read it as an example of the recorded care taken over a preparation, and leave it there.

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

Little to map: the entry states one site of action, the bowel. Dioscorides says the drink is thought to purge very well, so the claimed target is the intestinal tract and its evacuation. The second form, two parts boiled seawater to one of honey, is said to be more moderate and milder than seawater alone. No body system, organ or humour is named for the drink, and no hot, cold, dry or moist quality is printed for it in 5-20. The neighbouring chapter 5-19 prints seawater as hot, sharp and bad for the stomach; that is a property of seawater in that chapter, so the stomach is not mapped here. The constituents named in the recipe are honey, rainwater and seawater. The purge is reported as thought rather than shown, and anatomical mapping stops at the bowel.

### Contraindications

- **Dehydration and salt load.** A salty purge is unsuitable for anyone with heart, kidney or blood-pressure conditions, for the old, the very young, and in pregnancy. [USER: verify against a clinical source]
- **Purity.** Seawater from coasts may be contaminated; honey must not be given to infants under one year. Fermentation in sun-warmed jars is possible.
- **Purging.** Purging is not a method of cultivation and can do harm.
- **Identity.** Not a mineral; shelved here for its seawater content only.
