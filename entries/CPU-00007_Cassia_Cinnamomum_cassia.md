### Origin & Tradition

Cassia is the bark of an East/Southeast Asian evergreen laurel, and it is among the most ancient and widely-traded aromatics of the Old World — distinct from, but perennially confused with, true Ceylon cinnamon (*Cinnamomum verum*). The classical sources treat "cassia" and "cinnamon" as related but separate goods, and the identity problem runs all the way back: Dioscorides describes several grades of cassia in Book I, the Egyptian Ebers material names cassia-type barks among its aromatics, and the spice's value drove a trans-oceanic trade whose true sources the Mediterranean world deliberately mystified. Where Ceylon cinnamon is the finer, sweeter, more delicate bark, cassia is the **coarser, darker, more pungent and intensely warming** bark — and it is cassia, not the costlier "true" cinnamon, that supplies most of the world's culinary "cinnamon" to this day.

It is in **Chinese Medicine** that cassia reaches its full therapeutic stature, where it splits into two of the great warming herbs: **Rou Gui** (肉桂), the thick inner bark, and **Gui Zhi** (桂枝), the young twig. Rou Gui is one of the premier agents for **warming the interior and supporting the Kidney yang** — it is said to "return fire to its source" (引火歸源), rekindling the **Ming Men**, the "gate of vitality" or life-gate fire, in deep cold and yang-depletion patterns. Gui Zhi, the twig, is milder and more outward-moving: it warms and frees the channels, releases the exterior in wind-cold patterns, and harmonises — the chief herb of the foundational formula Gui Zhi Tang. The single tree thus furnishes both a deep interior-warming tonic and a gentle surface-warming, channel-freeing herb.

Across the Greco-Roman, Egyptian, Tibetan and Unani traditions cassia carries the same essential signature: a hot, acrid, sweet aromatic that warms the cold stomach, stimulates digestion, moves stagnant cold and blood, and serves as a carminative, emmenagogue and circulatory stimulant. Its warmth made it a natural component of fortifying wines, mulled drinks and warming compound medicines from antiquity to the present.

### Active Components

- **Cinnamaldehyde (trans-cinnamaldehyde)** — the principal aromatic constituent of the bark's essential oil and the source of its warm, pungent character; carminative, antimicrobial, circulatory-stimulant and with documented anti-inflammatory and antiplatelet activity. Cassia oil is typically **higher in cinnamaldehyde** than Ceylon cinnamon.
- **Coumarin** — present at **notably higher levels in cassia than in Ceylon cinnamon**; this is the key safety distinction, since high coumarin intake carries hepatotoxic risk (see Contraindications). [USER: verify quantitative coumarin figures before quoting numbers.]
- **Cinnamic acid and cinnamyl acetate** — aromatic acids/esters contributing to flavour and activity.
- **Condensed tannins and procyanidins (type-A polymers)** — astringent, antioxidant constituents; the type-A procyanidins are associated with the much-studied (and much-overstated) glucose-modulating activity of cinnamon. [USER: verify before asserting glycaemic efficacy.]
- **Mucilage and minor diterpenes (cinnzeylanin-type)** — additional bark constituents.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. **Deep interior warming / Kidney-yang support (Rou Gui).** The defining CM use: warming profound interior cold, supporting yang, "returning fire to the life-gate" in cold-from-deficiency patterns — cold limbs, weak digestion, low vitality.
2. **Channel-freeing, exterior-releasing (Gui Zhi).** The twig warms and unblocks the channels and releases wind-cold from the exterior; eases cold-type aches and supports circulation.
3. **Warming carminative / digestive.** Across all traditions — warming the cold stomach, easing cold-type bloating, nausea and poor appetite.
4. **Circulatory stimulant / blood-mover.** Used to move stagnant cold and blood (cold-type menstrual pain, cold extremities); emmenagogue reputation.
5. **Culinary-medicinal aromatic.** The everyday warming spice — in food, mulled drinks and fortifying preparations.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM / Galenic humoral degree:** Hot and Dry in a high degree — a strongly warming, drying, pungent aromatic; a cardiac and stomachic cordial.
- **Chinese Medicine mapping:** **Rou Gui** — acrid, sweet, hot; Kidney, Spleen, Heart, Liver channels; warms interior, supports yang, returns fire to the source, disperses cold, warms channels/blood. **Gui Zhi** — acrid, sweet, warm; Heart, Lung, Bladder; releases exterior wind-cold, warms/frees channels, harmonises.
- **Unani/Tibetan mapping:** hot-dry; warming digestive and circulatory stimulant; component of warming compound formulae.
- **Kenetica Qi/Shen tier:** ◉ **Qi** — at the deep-warming, yang-supporting, channel-freeing end of the axis; one of the volume's archetypal "kindle the inner fire" agents.

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese (SBJ).** The two cinnamon drugs of the *Shennong Bencaojing* — *mu gui* 牡桂 and *jun gui* 菌桂 — are both entered in the **upper grade** (上品), the *san pin* rank Stanley-Baker reads as a genuine cultivation hierarchy (養命, nourishing life over the long term) rather than a potency scale. This is an attested cultivation status. Cassia's warming here is **conservational** — "returning fire to its source" (引火歸源) re-seats and preserves Kidney *yang*, it does not manufacture it. Two cautions down the ladder: the identification of *mu gui* with the thick-barked *rou gui* is Li Shizhen's later gloss (*Bencao Gangmu*, 1596), and **which classical *gui* answers to modern *C. cassia* rather than *C. verum* is a modern botanical step** — the grade attaches to the text's *gui*, not to a Linnaean species. The *neidan* reading of cinnamon as a fire-phase agent is a cultivator's overlay, not a *bencao* category.

**Unani (CM).** Cassia is attested as a hot-and-dry aromatic cordial, and Unani carries the strongest cultivation idea in the corpus — the finite endowment of innate heat and radical moisture, whose conservation *is* longevity practice. But no source retrieved assigns cassia a **formal** innate-heat status; it therefore sits at the therapeutic level, the vital frame noted but its cultivation status **unattested (b)**.

**Greek (DMM).** Dioscorides grades cassia minutely by provenance, colour and aroma in Book I and warns against adulteration. He offers the cultivator not a subtle-body doctrine — he has none — but a **discipline of attention**: telling the true bark from the counterfeit by the senses. That is a real practice, and the whole of what he offers here. **(c)**

**Ayurveda (SS).** Cinnamon-cassia (*tvak*) enters the *dravyaguṇa* as a warming, *dīpana* aromatic. No *rasāyana* or *vājīkaraṇa* status is attested for it in the sources retrieved, so it carries a **therapeutic, not a cultivation, standing (b)**.

**Tibetan (FMT).** Cassia appears as a hot-natured aromatic inside compound warming formulas. The Four Tantras hold no formal category of "substances for subtle-body practice" to place it in; its standing is therapeutic. **(c)**

**Egyptian (EP).** The Ebers corpus is indication-organised, and the identity of its cassia/cinnamon barks is a **modern hypothesis** — actively contested for exactly this pair. Temple-incense and anointing associations are **liturgical** — a use of the aromatic in cult — and must not be read as a doctrine of what the bark does to a person's vital constitution. No cultivation status is attested. **(c)**

**Assyrian (AT).** The Codex's blocking warning is in force for every Assyrian substance identification and tablet reference; nothing about cassia's *asû/āšipu* placement can be asserted. **(c)**

**Practitioner's note.** What a cultivator has here is one solid rung and a set of honest blanks. In the Chinese scheme cassia is an upper-grade, long-life drug used *conservationally* — to warm and re-seat a vitality assumed already present, taken in course against cold-depletion rather than swallowed indefinitely; its own heat is the contraindication in anyone with heat or *yin* deficiency. In every other tradition the register is therapeutic or silent, and Dioscorides' lesson — buy and test the real bark, not the adulterated one — is the most transferable cultivation act cassia offers outside China.

### Contraindications

- **Heat patterns / yin-deficiency with heat (CM).** Contraindicated — a strongly warming herb aggravates heat, dryness and yin deficiency.
- **Pregnancy — caution.** Warming blood-mover and traditional emmenagogue; medicinal doses traditionally avoided in pregnancy (culinary amounts are ordinary food). [USER: verify.]
- **Coumarin / liver.** High habitual cassia intake delivers significant coumarin with hepatotoxic potential; relevant for those with liver disease or on hepatotoxic drugs — prefer Ceylon cinnamon for high medicinal intake. [USER: verify intake thresholds.]
- **Bleeding risk / anticoagulants.** Cinnamaldehyde has antiplatelet activity; caution with anticoagulants and before surgery.
- **Mucosal irritation / allergy.** Concentrated cassia oil is irritant and a contact allergen; not for undiluted use.
