# Melian earth — Ge Melia (GE MELIA), Dioscorides 5-180

## *A rough, ashy-coloured earth like Eretrian earth that creaks when rubbed between the fingers, like grated pumice, and is weaker than alum; the translator's gloss is "Earth from Melia". The place name is ambiguous in the translator's own gazetteer (an Albanian town, Meliani, and the island of Melos are both listed), and the mineral is unidentified, so the identification is of medium confidence as to kind and unresolved as to material* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder. A search of `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` for "Melia", "Melian" and "Melos" found this chapter, the translator's gazetteer entries (below), the sulphur and alum chapters' mentions of Melos, and the index; nothing in another tradition on disk.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-180, *Ge Melia* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 10764, where the heading is printed inline at the end of the previous chapter; name-index row in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` at row 953, with the note "heading printed inline (mid-line)", translator gloss "Earth from Melia"; list row in `_sources/dioscorides.csv` at line 905, "Ge Melia", mineral). The whole chapter was read. It reads (line 10765):

"Melia is coloured like Eretrian earth (which is an ashy colour) but it is rough, and rubbed with the fingers it makes a creaking noise like pumice stone that is being grated. It is like allom [5-123] in quality but somewhat more weak, which is also shown by the taste. It dries the tongue gently. It is able to make the body clean and a good colour, thins hair, and wears off alphos [non-contagious leprosy] and psoriasis. It is useful for painters to make their colours more permanent, and it is helpful in green [herbal] plasters. Earth of any type should be chosen which is without stones, new, soft, brittle, and easily dissolved when it touches moisture."

The chapter thus gives: colour (ashy, like Eretrian earth, 5-171); feel and sound (rough; it creaks when rubbed with the fingers, like pumice being grated); a comparison in quality (like alum, somewhat weaker, "also shown by the taste"); a taste effect (it "dries the tongue gently"); uses on the body (to make the body clean and of good colour, to thin hair, and to wear off *alphos* and psoriasis); two trade uses (painters use it "to make their colours more permanent", and it is "helpful in green [herbal] plasters"); and a general rule for choosing earths: "without stones, new, soft, brittle, and easily dissolved when it touches moisture". The last sentence is stated of "Earth of any type" and so is a rule for the group, not for the Melian earth alone.

**The comparison earths.** Eretrian earth (5-171, line 10718; "Some Eretrian earth is considerably white and some is an ashy colour. The best is that which resembles ash in colour, and is very soft, and when drawn along brass yields a violet line") is the colour reference; alum (5-123, *Stupteria*, line 10468) is the quality reference. Neither is folded into this entry.

**The place name.** The chapter says only "Melia". The translator's gazetteer defines MELIA as "Meliani was an inland Chaonian town in southern Albania" and gives MELOS separately as "an island in the Cyclades group in the Aegean Sea ... now Milos or Milo" (line 3515). The chapter does not say which place it means, and the Codex name "Melian earth" implies the island but the text does not settle it. No choice is made here. (Elsewhere in the same book, the alum chapter and the sulphur chapter name Melos as a source of alum and "abundant" sulphur, lines 10470 and 10479, which makes the island a plausible source, but they do not mention this earth.) **Identity.** The translator offers only "Earth from Melia". The chapter's marks (ashy, rough, creaking, alum-like taste, dries the tongue) do not name a mineral. An ashy, gritty, alum-tasting earth may contain alum or a related sulphate, but the chapter does not say so. The translator gives no suggestion.

### Active Components

None can be stated from the chapter. The chapter compares the earth to alum, "somewhat more weak, which is also shown by the taste", and says it "dries the tongue gently". In general terms, astringent drying of the tongue is the effect of aluminium and some other metal salts; this is a general fact about alum and is not a claim that the Melian earth contained alum.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.** Dioscorides records Melian earth as a cleansing and complexion earth for the body, a remover of hair and of *alphos* and psoriasis, a fixative for painters' colours, and a help in green plasters. The earth is unidentified and the skin conditions named are medical conditions. A hair-thinning earth applied to skin can irritate. The Codex supplies no modern protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation is given in the chapter. The only instruction is the choice of earth: "without stones, new, soft, brittle, and easily dissolved when it touches moisture" (line 10765). It is used "in green [herbal] plasters" by the painters' or healers' trade, and in making body or hair applications, but no method or vehicle is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM).** The chapter prints these qualities: the earth "dries the tongue gently" and is "like allom [5-123] in quality but somewhat more weak". No warming or cooling quality is printed. No degree is given beyond "gently" and "somewhat more weak".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. What the herbal offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, adulteration detection), and this chapter shows the sensory-assay part clearly: colour against a reference earth, roughness, the sound it makes when rubbed, the taste, the dryness on the tongue, and the rule that an earth should be without stones, new, soft, brittle and quick to dissolve in moisture. The chapter is therapeutic and technical and carries no cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to take from it for cultivation beyond the model of sensory assay: look, feel, listen, taste, and test in water. The earth itself is not a recommended practice. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Skin:** a drying, hair-thinning earth can irritate or burn skin; do not apply it to broken skin, the face or the eyes.
- **Skin disease:** psoriasis and other chronic skin conditions need medical assessment.
- **Identity and place:** the earth and its place are unresolved; do not substitute any commercial earth or alum.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children:** no statement in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
