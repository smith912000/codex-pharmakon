# I_172 — St. John's Wort (*Hypericum perforatum* L.)

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Hypericum perforatum* L. (1753)
**Family etymology:** Hyper + eikon (Greek) = "above an image/icon" — placed above household icons to ward off evil spirits. The genus name encodes the medieval spiritual-protective use.

**Traditional names:**
| Tradition | Name | Notes |
|-----------|------|-------|
| DMM (Greek) | ὑπερικόν (*hyperikon*) | Standard Greek name; same as modern genus |
| Arabic/CM | هيبريقون / قصيص (*ḥayparīqūn / qasīṣ*) | Transliteration from Greek |
| Sanskrit/SS | Tamrakusha (तामकुश) | *V. wallichii* parallel; red wound herb |
| Ebers/Egyptian | Unknown specific name; wound herb | Red-exudate wound applications documented |
| English | St. John's Wort | Flowers open near St. John's Day (June 24) |
| German | Johanniskraut | Same etymology |
| French | Millepertuis | "Thousand holes" — perforated leaves |

**Diagnostic morphology:** The *perforatum* (perforated) species epithet refers to the distinctive translucent oil glands visible when leaves are held to light — they appear as tiny holes. Dark (black) dots on petal margins = hypericin-containing secretory glands. This morphological marker was used historically to distinguish *H. perforatum* from numerous Hypericum relatives — the "perforations" = hypericin + hyperforin.

**~450 species in *Hypericum* genus;** *H. perforatum* is the sole species with adequate clinical evidence. *H. calycinum* (Rose of Sharon) and others have different phytochemistry.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 De Materia Medica (Dioscorides, c. 50–70 CE)
DMM Book III.155: *Hyperikon* — used for burns (topical oil infusion), sciatica, and as a diuretic. Dioscorides also documents the striking RED oil produced by macerating the flowers in olive oil — describing this as a wound healer that "draws out" deep wound toxins. The red oil = hypericin extraction in olive oil (the traditional St. John's Wort oil still made today). Dioscorides' wound application is pharmacologically coherent: hypericin and hyperforin have antimicrobial activity against *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Propionibacterium acnes*.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine (Ibn Sīnā, 1025 CE)
*Ḥayparīqūn* — diuretic; for sciatica and chronic nerve pain; wound healer; "dries up the nerves." Ibn Sīnā's "nerve-drying" description is potentially the earliest documented observation of what modern medicine identifies as its monoamine reuptake inhibition — "drying" in Galenic-Unani terms = reducing excess humors in the nervous system, which maps to serotonin/noradrenaline "drying" of the depressive/anxious state.

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (c. 1552 BCE) — TENTATIVE
Red-resin wound applications in Egyptian formulae; *Hypericum* species native to Egypt exist; tentative identification.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (c. 700–600 BCE)
Wound healing herbs with red exudate; Hypericum-type plant in combination formulae; tentative.

### 2.5 Sushruta Samhita / Charaka Samhita — TENTATIVE (*H. mysorense* / *H. hookerianum* analogs)
Indian *Hypericum* species (*tamrakusha*) documented for wounds and skin conditions in Ayurvedic materia medica. *H. hookerianum* and *H. mysorense* are Indian species with hyperforin-related compounds. Parallel use scored as tentative.

### 2.6 Four Medical Tantras (FMT)
*Ser po me tog* (yellow flower herb): Tibetan medicine documents *Hypericum* species for wound healing, skin disorders, and mood disorders — the mood application is present in Tibetan tradition alongside wound/topical use.

### 2.7 Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ) — TENTATIVE
*Guanye Jinsitao* (贯叶金丝桃) — later introduction to Chinese pharmacopoeia; modern TCM acknowledges; classical documentation thin.

### 2.8 Badianus Manuscript (BM) — ABSENT; South American (SA) — ABSENT
No native *Hypericum* with perforatum pharmacology in the Americas.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Convergence: 6/9** (tentative for EP/AT/SS). The wound-healing + nerve/pain application is the classical cross-traditional foundation. The antidepressant application — the primary modern use — is documented in FMT explicitly and implied in CM ("nerve-drying") but absent from the classical DMM/EP wound-focused tradition.

**The Paradigm Shift:** St. John's Wort is the Codex's clearest example of a substance whose traditional use (wound healer/topical antimicrobial) and modern clinical use (antidepressant) are pharmacologically connected but conceptually discontinuous. The red hypericin oil applied to wounds IS antimicrobial (the external mechanism). The hyperforin taken internally for depression acts on a completely different molecular target (SERT/NET/DAT + TRPC6). The same plant, two entirely different pharmacological mechanisms, two entirely different indications — neither invalidating the other.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

### 4.1 Naphthodianthrones (Hypericin Group)
**Hypericin** (0.1–0.3% dried aerial parts): red-colored naphthodianthrone; the compound responsible for the traditional red oil color and for the characteristic spectroscopic signature of *H. perforatum*. Key properties:
- Photosensitizer (Type II photosensitization): absorbs visible/UVA light → excites molecular oxygen → singlet oxygen (¹O₂) generation → oxidative damage to cell membranes (viral envelope disruption; skin cell oxidation)
- Antiviral: documented activity against herpes simplex virus (HSV-1, HSV-2), HIV (in vitro), enveloped viruses generally — via photo-oxidative envelope disruption
- **CRITICAL**: NOT the primary antidepressant compound (despite early attribution); antipolymerase activity minor at serum concentrations

**Pseudohypericin**: related; similar properties; 0.2–1.0%.

**Protohypericin / Protopseudohypericin**: biosynthetic precursors; convert to hypericin/pseudohypericin on light exposure in the plant.

### 4.2 Hyperforin (Primary Antidepressant)
**Hyperforin** (0.2–4.5% aerial parts; highest in flower/bud stage): phloroglucinol derivative; the PRIMARY antidepressant compound established by molecular pharmacology.

**Mechanism (three pharmacological actions):**
1. **Triple monoamine reuptake inhibitor:** Inhibits SERT (serotonin transporter), NET (noradrenaline transporter), and DAT (dopamine transporter) — equivalent in type (though not necessarily potency) to pharmaceutical SNDRIs like venlafaxine, duloxetine; broader than SSRI-class
2. **TRPC6 (Transient Receptor Potential Channel Canonical 6) activation:** Independent antidepressant mechanism; TRPC6 is a Na⁺/Ca²⁺ non-selective cation channel; activation → increased intracellular sodium → vesicular neurotransmitter release from presynaptic terminals → increased synaptic monoamine availability by a PRESYNAPTIC mechanism (distinct from reuptake inhibition which is POSTSYNAPTIC-biased). **This is a mechanism with NO pharmaceutical analog** — a genuinely novel antidepressant pathway.
3. **Sigma1 receptor agonism:** Anti-stress; neuroprotection; modulation of neurotransmitter release

**Hyperforin = the critical quality marker:** Products standardized to ≥3% hyperforin consistently show antidepressant efficacy in RCTs; products with <0.5% hyperforin (as found in some light-exposed or poorly stored extracts — hyperforin is extremely light- and heat-sensitive) show inconsistent efficacy. This pharmacokinetic variable explains much of the heterogeneity in clinical trials.

### 4.3 Flavonoids
**Quercetin** (0.5–2%), **rutin**, **hyperoside** (quercetin-3-galactoside), **isoquercitrin** (quercetin-3-glucoside), **amentoflavone** (biflavone):
- Quercetin: anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, GABA-A modulation (anxiolytic contribution)
- Amentoflavone: MAO-A and MAO-B inhibitor (competitive); early attribution of SJW's antidepressant mechanism to MAO inhibition was primarily from amentoflavone; now understood as minor contributor vs. hyperforin
- Hyperoside: anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective

### 4.4 Chlorogenic Acid, Caffeic Acid Derivatives
Antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; antimicrobial; minor pharmacological contributors.

### 4.5 Xanthones (Minor)
MAO inhibition; additional antidepressant contribution minor.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Triple Monoamine Reuptake Inhibition (Hyperforin — Primary)
Hyperforin inhibits the reuptake transporters:
- **SERT** (serotonin transporter, SLC6A4): IC₅₀ ~0.5 μM; increases synaptic serotonin
- **NET** (noradrenaline transporter, SLC6A2): IC₅₀ ~0.3 μM; increases synaptic noradrenaline
- **DAT** (dopamine transporter, SLC6A3): IC₅₀ ~2.0 μM; increases synaptic dopamine

**Mechanism of reuptake inhibition (UNUSUAL):** Hyperforin does NOT bind to the transporter protein directly (unlike SSRIs which occupy the serotonin-binding site). Instead, hyperforin disrupts the Na⁺ gradient across the presynaptic membrane that provides the energy for monoamine reuptake transporters. This ionophore-like activity (Na⁺ carrier) abolishes the electrochemical driving force for reuptake. **This means cross-resistance between hyperforin and conventional SSRIs/SNRIs is unlikely** — they inhibit the same transporter by completely different mechanisms.

### 5.2 TRPC6 Activation (Novel)
TRPC6 ion channel activation → Na⁺ influx into presynaptic terminal → depolarization → Ca²⁺-independent neurotransmitter release from reserve pool → increased synaptic monoamines by presynaptic mechanism. This is the only described mechanism in natural or synthetic antidepressants that operates presynaptically via ion channel activation rather than reuptake blockade or MAO inhibition.

### 5.3 CYP3A4/2C9/P-gp Induction — THE CRITICAL DRUG INTERACTION
**Hyperforin activates the Pregnane X Receptor (PXR)** → PXR translocates to nucleus → binds RXR → induces transcription of CYP3A4, CYP2C9, P-glycoprotein (P-gp/ABCB1):

**Result: Dramatically reduced plasma concentrations of substrate drugs**

| Drug Class | Example | Effect | Clinical Consequence |
|------------|---------|--------|---------------------|
| Immunosuppressants | Cyclosporine, tacrolimus | 50–70% reduction | **Transplant rejection — organ loss** |
| Anticoagulants | Warfarin | Reduced INR | Thromboembolism, stroke |
| Antiretrovirals | Indinavir, saquinavir | 50%+ reduction | **HIV viral breakthrough** |
| Oral contraceptives | Ethinylestradiol/levonorgestrel | Reduced levels | **Contraceptive failure — unintended pregnancy** |
| Cardiac glycosides | Digoxin | −18% (P-gp induction) | Reduced therapeutic effect |
| Anticancer agents | Docetaxel, imatinib | Significant reduction | Chemotherapy failure |
| HIV PIs | All protease inhibitors | Major | Class-wide interaction |

**This drug interaction is one of the most clinically important in all of pharmacy — herbal or conventional.** Dozens of transplant rejections, HIV rebound episodes, unintended pregnancies, and warfarin failures have been causally attributed to unreported SJW use. The 2000 UK CSM Safety Warning on SJW drug interactions was the first major regulatory alert specifically for an herbal supplement's pharmacokinetic drug interaction.

**Hyperforin-low extracts** (standardized to ≤0.2% hyperforin) show substantially reduced CYP3A4 induction in vitro while retaining some antidepressant activity (possibly via other mechanisms). Products marketed specifically for low CYP3A4 interaction exist — but sacrifice some efficacy.

### 5.4 Photosensitization (Hypericin)
Hypericin absorbs wavelengths 540–610 nm (visible green-yellow) and 300–400 nm (UVA) → transitions to excited state → energy transfer to molecular O₂ → singlet oxygen (¹O₂) → oxidative damage to unsaturated lipid membranes.
- **Antiviral**: enveloped virus lipid membrane disruption
- **Photodynamic therapy**: hypericin investigated for PDT of cancer, psoriasis, HPV-associated dysplasia
- **Phototoxicity in patients**: fair-skinned patients taking oral SJW → enhanced UV sensitivity → severe sunburn equivalent with ordinary light exposure. Risk is LOW at standard doses (serum hypericin concentrations rarely reach photosensitizing threshold) but clinically documented.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### 6.1 Depression — Efficacy vs. Placebo
**Linde et al. 2008 Cochrane meta-analysis (definitive):** 29 clinical trials, N=5,489; St. John's Wort vs. placebo for mild-moderate depression:
- **Responder rate:** 1.48× higher than placebo (95% CI 1.23–1.77)
- **Remission rate:** significantly higher than placebo
- **Adverse events:** significantly FEWER than standard antidepressants (TCAs, SSRIs)

**Conclusion:** SJW is significantly superior to placebo for mild-moderate depression, with a favorable adverse effect profile. Evidence base is among the largest for any antidepressant (herbal or pharmaceutical) and is consistent across European trials.

### 6.2 Depression — Equivalence to Pharmaceuticals
**Lecrubier et al. 2002 (JAMA):** N=375; LI 160 SJW extract vs. imipramine 150mg/day vs. placebo × 6 weeks; SJW = imipramine on HAMD-17; both > placebo; SJW better tolerated.

**Szegedi et al. 2005 (BMJ):** N=251; WS 5570 SJW extract vs. paroxetine 40mg × 6 weeks; SJW non-inferior to paroxetine for MODERATE-to-SEVERE depression with significantly fewer adverse effects.

### 6.3 Depression — US Negative Trial (Contextually Important)
**Hypericum Depression Trial Study Group 2002 (JAMA):** N=340; standardized SJW extract vs. sertraline 50mg vs. placebo × 8 weeks; ALL THREE groups showed ~22–25% response rate; no statistical difference between SJW, sertraline, and placebo.

**Context:** This trial occurred in an unusually high-placebo-response setting (US multi-center; liberal criteria; high dropout). The study failed its sensitivity analysis (sertraline also failed to separate from placebo) — suggesting methodological issues rather than SJW-specific failure. The JAMA publication generated extensive "SJW doesn't work" media coverage in the US despite the study's design problems.

### 6.4 Regulatory Status
- **Germany Commission E (1984):** First country to formally approve SJW for mild-moderate depressive disorders — approval predated most pharmaceutical antidepressant approvals for outpatient mild depression
- **EMA Assessment Report (2018):** Well-established use for mild-moderate depressive episodes; maximum 6 weeks continuous use before reassessment
- **WHO Monograph:** Recognized for mild-moderate depression
- **US FDA:** Dietary supplement; no approved indication; cannot make antidepressant claims

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**June 24th — Midsummer's Day Harvest:** "St. John's Wort" is named because the flowers reliably open around St. John the Baptist's feast day (June 24, Midsummer) in European latitudes — simultaneously the peak of flowering season and the peak of hypericin content. Harvesting at midsummer captures maximum pharmacological potency. Medieval herbalists who established the June harvest rule were empirically observing photosynthesis-driven secondary metabolite accumulation at peak solar exposure — correct timing based on observation, not photochemistry knowledge.

**The Red Oil:** Macerating *H. perforatum* flowers in olive oil produces a striking blood-red oil within days (the hypericin extraction). This *Oleum Hyperici* (St. John's Wort oil) has been continuously produced since at least Dioscorides' time (50 CE) and is still made by traditional herbalists worldwide. The red color was interpreted as "the blood of St. John" (martyred by beheading) — a color-doctrine association (*Doctrine of Signatures*: red plant = wound herb) that was simultaneously mythological and pharmacologically correct (the red oil does have antimicrobial-wound healing activity).

**Protection Against Evil and Demons:** European folk tradition placed *H. perforatum* above doors and windows on Midsummer's Eve specifically to repel evil spirits, witches, and the devil. The Latin name *fuga daemonum* ("devil's flight") reflects this attribution. This protective role likely encoded the plant's antimicrobial (pre-germ-theory "evil" = infection) and anticonvulsant/anti-psychosis applications (epilepsy and mental illness = demonic possession in medieval European understanding). A traditional anti-epileptic herb → reframed as demon repellent → modern antidepressant: three different paradigms describing overlapping neurological inhibition.

**The 2000 UK CSM Warning — A Turning Point in Herbal Regulation:** On February 29, 2000, the UK Committee on Safety of Medicines issued a safety warning on SJW drug interactions — the first time a European regulatory authority issued a formal pharmacokinetic interaction warning specifically for a herbal supplement. The trigger: multiple transplant rejection episodes, HIV viral rebounds, and unintended pregnancies causally linked to unreported SJW use in patients on cyclosporine, antiretrovirals, and oral contraceptives respectively. This warning transformed how pharmacists, prescribers, and regulators worldwide treated herbal medicine — establishing herb-drug interactions as a serious clinical safety category. Pre-2000, "herbal medicine" and "drug interactions" were rarely used in the same sentence by mainstream pharmacy.

**Germany's Bestselling Antidepressant:** In Germany and other European countries in the 1990s–2000s, SJW preparations outsold all pharmaceutical antidepressants combined in some years. The German Commission E's 1984 approval created a regulated herbal antidepressant marketplace that was years ahead of the rest of the world. The consequence: Germany has more SJW clinical trial data than any other country, and German patients were the first to receive standardized, quality-controlled SJW preparations for depression as a matter of routine medical practice.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Dosing (Evidence-Based)
| Application | Standardization | Dose | Duration |
|-------------|-----------------|------|----------|
| Mild-moderate depression | WS 5570 (0.3% hypericin, 3% hyperforin) | 300mg × 3/day | 4–6 weeks for assessment; up to 6 months |
| Mild-moderate depression | LI 160 (0.3% hypericin) | 300mg × 3/day | Same |
| Wound/topical | Hypericum oil (traditional) | Apply 2–3× daily | Until healed |
| Hyperforin-low formulation | <0.2% hyperforin; 0.3% hypericin | 900mg/day equivalent | When on concurrent medications (reduced efficacy) |

**Critical quality requirement:** Only use products standardized to BOTH hypericin (0.3%) AND hyperforin (≥3.0%) for antidepressant indication. Hypericin-standardized-only products may have variable hyperforin content and variable efficacy. The hyperforin specification is the primary efficacy marker; hypericin standardization ensures product authenticity (cannot be substituted with non-*H. perforatum* extract).

### 8.2 Absolute Contraindications (Drug Interactions)

**NEVER combine with — absolute contraindications:**
- **Cyclosporine / Tacrolimus**: transplant rejection risk; multiple fatalities documented
- **HIV protease inhibitors / NNRTIs**: viral breakthrough, AIDS progression
- **Oral contraceptives**: contraceptive failure (documented pregnancy cases)
- **Warfarin**: INR reduction; thromboembolism risk
- **Chemotherapy (many agents)**: cancer treatment failure
- **Imatinib, dasatinib**: CML/cancer treatment failure documented
- **Digoxin**: reduced levels

**Inform ALL providers** of SJW use — many patients do not consider supplements worth mentioning. The primary harm from SJW has come from patients not disclosing use to prescribers.

### 8.3 Additional Warnings
**Photodermatitis:** Fair-skinned patients; avoid prolonged direct sun exposure; use SPF 30+ sunscreen; avoid sun beds. Risk increases with dose.

**Pregnancy/lactation:** Avoid — insufficient safety data; potential uterotonic effects.

**Bipolar disorder:** Antidepressant mechanism may trigger hypomania/mania in bipolar patients — same risk as pharmaceutical antidepressants; do not use without mood stabilizer cover.

**Duration:** Response typically requires 2–6 weeks (same latency as pharmaceutical antidepressants — consistent with neuroplasticity-dependent mechanism); do not judge efficacy before 4 weeks.

**Severity limit:** Evidence supports mild-moderate depression only. **Severe depression, suicidal ideation, psychosis: refer to psychiatric care.** SJW is not an adequate treatment for severe depression.

### 8.4 Tradition-Practice Concordance
The antidepressant mechanism (hyperforin triple reuptake + TRPC6) was entirely unknown to traditions using SJW for wounds, sciatica, and demon repulsion. Yet the mood/neural application is present in FMT and implied in CM. The wound-to-mood pharmacological bridge runs through antimicrobial → anti-inflammatory → reduced inflammatory cytokine load on the brain → mood protection (the inflammation-depression link). St. John's Wort is simultaneously a rational topical antimicrobial (hypericin/hyperforin), a rational antidepressant (hyperforin SERT/NET/DAT), and a pharmacokinetic minefield (hyperforin PXR induction) — a single plant containing both a clinically useful drug and a clinically dangerous drug interaction mechanism in the same molecule.
