### Origin & Tradition

*Asparagus cochinchinensis* is a climbing perennial of the Asparagaceae, native to China, Korea, Japan, and Vietnam (the species epithet preserves the old name Cochinchina for southern Vietnam). The medicinal part is the swollen, spindle-shaped storage tubers, which are harvested, scalded, peeled, and dried into translucent amber pieces. Despite the family relationship, this is emphatically not the culinary garden asparagus (*Asparagus officinalis*, entry I_019): the species, the part used, the preparation, and the therapeutic logic are all distinct. Tian Men Dong is a root tuber prized for its sweet, cooling, intensely moistening character.

The name Tiān Mén Dōng — literally "heavenly gate winter" — reflects both its association with the upper body (Lung, the "heavenly" organ in the chest) and its winter-harvested, fluid-restoring nature. In the classical scheme it is the paradigmatic Yin tonic for dryness of the Lung and Kidney: a remedy for the dry, unproductive cough, the parched throat, the thirst and constipation of fluid depletion, and the wasting heat of chronic Yin deficiency.

**Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ):** Tian Men Dong appears in the earliest canon in the superior class of tonifying drugs, credited with treating "violent wind and damp obstruction," nourishing the flesh, and — with long use — lightening the body and benefiting the Qi. The thread of moistening and nourishing dry tissue runs through the classical record from its earliest appearance.

**Bencao Gangmu (Li Shizhen):** Li Shizhen records Tian Men Dong as sweet, bitter, and very cold, entering the Lung and Kidney, used to clear Lung heat, moisten dryness, generate fluids, and quell the heat of Yin deficiency. He pairs it conceptually with Mai Men Dong (*Ophiopogon*), the two "Men Dong" herbs forming the classical fluid-restoring duo of upper- and middle-burner moistening.

**Four Medical Tantras (Gyushi / FMT, functional analogue):** Tibetan medicine recognises a class of sweet, cooling, fluid-restoring roots used against "heat-dryness" of the lung-channel and against wasting-heat conditions; Tian Men Dong's profile maps onto this functional category of moistening tonics directed at depleted bodily fluids.

### Active Components

- **Steroidal saponins (asparagosides / spirostanol and furostanol glycosides)** — characteristic Asparagaceae saponins; associated with anti-inflammatory, antitussive, and moistening (mucotropic) activity
- **Polysaccharides and fructans** — water-retaining storage carbohydrates contributing to the moistening, fluid-generating quality and to immune-modulating effects
- **Amino acids and asparagine** — abundant free amino acids; asparagine is named for the genus
- **β-sitosterol and phytosterols** — anti-inflammatory membrane sterols
- **Mucilage** — soluble polysaccharide gel underlying the throat-soothing, demulcent action

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. **Respiratory moistening for dry, irritated airways** `[GENERAL]` — The mucilage and saponin fractions soothe a dry, unproductive cough and a parched throat. Useful for athletes training in dry, cold, or air-conditioned environments where the airway mucosa is chronically dried.

2. **Yin-fluid restoration in depletion states** `[GENERAL]` — Classically the first-line moistener for Lung-Kidney Yin deficiency. In training terms this maps onto the dried-out, overheated, low-recovery state of chronic overreaching, night sweats, and persistent thirst.

3. **Constipation of dryness** `[GENERAL]` — By generating fluids and moistening the intestines, Tian Men Dong eases the dry-stool constipation associated with fluid depletion, without harsh purgative action.

4. **Cooling complement to warming Qi tonics** `[GENERAL]` — Paired with warming Qi tonics (Codonopsis, Astragalus), it offsets their drying/warming tendency, balancing a tonic protocol so building Qi does not consume fluids.

### Preparation & Dosing

**Standard Decoction (Traditional TCM):**
Dried tuber 6–15 g simmered in 500 ml water 30–45 minutes; consumed warm, often combined with Mai Men Dong and a Yin-nourishing base.

**Classical Pairing — "Two Dong" base:**
Tian Men Dong + Mai Men Dong (Ophiopogon) in roughly equal parts as the foundational fluid-moistening dyad for dry cough and Yin deficiency.

**Syrup / Paste (gao):**
Long-decocted and reduced with honey into a moistening syrup — a traditional, palatable form for chronic dry throat and cough.

**Powder / Extract (commercial):**
Standardised extract per manufacturer guidance, typically equivalent to several grams of dried herb daily.

**Timing & Duration:** Suited to sustained use in deficiency-dryness patterns; effect is restorative and cumulative. Because it is cold and moistening, it is taken with food in those of weak digestion.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Primary Tier:** ◈ Jing / Yin-fluid — nourishes Lung and Kidney Yin; generates body fluids (Jin Ye)

**TCM Channel:** Lung (Fei), Kidney (Shen)

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese — Shennong / Bencao (SBJ).** Tian Men Dong 天門冬 is placed in the upper grade (上品, *shang pin*) of the *Shennong Bencao Jing*, the grade of substances taken long-term to nourish life rather than to attack disease. The classic credits it, with sustained use, with "lightening the body" (輕身, *qing shen* — an effect-category of the cultivation register, not a pharmacological or weight-loss claim) and benefiting the *qi* 氣, alongside dispelling wind-damp obstruction. This is a genuine *san pin* cultivation status, sourced to the Han primary text itself and consistent across the later record (Li Shizhen's *Bencao Gangmu*, 1596, retains the sweet-bitter-very-cold, Lung-Kidney-moistening account but the grade belongs to the earlier classic). Within *yang sheng* 養生 — conservational, not curative — it is a fluid-restoring tonic of the upper grade. Cultivation status: **(a) attested**, cited to the *Shennong Bencao Jing* upper class (上品).

**Ayurveda (SS).** No *rasayana* or *vajikarana* status can be attested for *Asparagus cochinchinensis* itself in the sources retrieved. The great Ayurvedic *rasayana* of this genus is *shatavari*, *Asparagus racemosus* — a distinct species with its own classical standing in Charaka and Sushruta — and its rejuvenative grade may not be transferred across to this Chinese tuber, which the Sanskrit materia medica does not name. The functional parallel noted in the profile is a resemblance of action, not a shared cultivation assignment. Cultivation status: **(c) absent** for this species — the *rasayana* standing belongs to *A. racemosus*, not to *A. cochinchinensis*.

**Practitioner's note.** In the Chinese register this is a true cultivation substance: an upper-grade Yin-fluid tonic taken over sustained courses to restore the dried, overheated, low-recovery constitution — the parched throat, the dry unproductive cough, the night sweats and thirst of chronic depletion. That standing is Chinese and species-specific; it does not carry into Unani (which knew a different asparagus) or into Ayurveda (whose rejuvenator is shatavari, another species). A cultivator uses Tian Men Dong as the classic intends — a slow, moistening, conservational tonic — and does not borrow the reputations of its cousins.

### Contraindications

- **Spleen/Stomach cold-deficiency with diarrhoea:** as a cold, moistening herb, it can worsen loose stools, poor appetite, and cold-type digestive weakness; combine with warming, drying herbs or avoid in these patterns
- **Phlegm-damp cough (wet, productive):** indicated for dry cough, not for damp/phlegmy congestion, which it may aggravate
- **External wind-cold with chills:** not for acute exterior cold patterns; moistening tonics can retain a pathogen
- **Pregnancy:** insufficient safety data at medicinal doses; use only under qualified supervision
- **Do not confuse with culinary asparagus (I_019):** different species, part, and use — substitution is incorrect
