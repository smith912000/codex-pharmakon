# I-264: Allium ampeloprasum / porrum (Leek)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-264 | Amaryllidaceae (formerly Alliaceae)**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomials:**
- *Allium ampeloprasum* L. — wild leek / great-headed garlic (parent species)
- *Allium ampeloprasum* var. *porrum* — cultivated leek
- *Allium porrum* L. — former designation; syn. still widely used in older literature

**Family:** Amaryllidaceae

**Common Names:**
- English: Leek, Garden Leek
- Welsh: *Cenhinen* — the national emblem of Wales; worn on St. David's Day (March 1st)
- French: *Poireau*
- Arabic: *Kurrāth* (كراث)
- Greek: *Prason* (πράσον)
- Hebrew: *Kereshah* / *Chatzir* — possibly referenced as *chatzir* in Numbers 11:5 alongside onion and garlic

**National Symbol Note:**
Leek is one of four national symbols of Wales (alongside the daffodil, red dragon, and patron saint). The leek-Wales association traces to a legendary 6th century CE battle where Welsh soldiers wore leeks in their helmets to distinguish themselves from Saxon enemies. This cultural depth indicates 1,500+ years of continuous prominence in Welsh cultural-dietary life.

**Taxonomy:**
*A. ampeloprasum* (broad species) includes wild leek, elephant garlic (*A. ampeloprasum* var. *ampeloprasum*), kurrat (Egyptian leek, *A. ampeloprasum* var. *kurrat*), and pearl onion (*A. ampeloprasum* var. *sectivum*). Cultivated leek is a distinct culinary variety selectively bred for long blanched shank.

**Related Egyptian Form — Kurrat:**
*A. ampeloprasum* var. *kurrat* (kurrat) is the leaf-leek of Egypt and the Levant — harvested for its tops rather than its thick base; consumed raw or cooked throughout the Middle East; the "leek" of the Ebers Papyrus is likely this variety.

**Morphology:**
Biennial; 30–90 cm in cultivation; strap-like flat leaves (vs. hollow round leaves of onion and garlic); the edible "stem" is actually a pseudostem of tightly wrapped leaf bases; large globose flower head (10–15 cm) of small white to lavender-pink flowers in second year; does not form underground bulb in same manner as garlic or onion.

**Parts Used:** Pseudostem (blanched white base and green tops); leaves; immature flower buds.

**Growth Region:** Mediterranean origin; SW Asia; now cosmopolitan temperate cultivation. Grows best in cool, moist conditions; major European production in France, UK, Belgium, Netherlands.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domains:**
1. **Diuretic / kidney / urinary tract** — DMM, CM, SS, EP, AT: most consistent single application; promotes urination; kidney stone prevention; urinary tract cleansing
2. **Respiratory / expectorant** — DMM, CM, FMT: URI; mucus; cough; expectorant
3. **Digestive / appetitive** — All traditions: digestive tonic; carminative; appetite stimulating
4. **Cardiovascular / blood** — CM, SS, European folk: circulation; blood purification; anti-hypertensive (Allium class effect)
5. **Wound / topical** — DMM, EP, AT: external poultice; wound antiseptic; hemorrhoid

**Pharmacological Position:**
Leek occupies a middle position in the *Allium* pharmacological spectrum:
- Less concentrated organosulfur than garlic → less potent antimicrobial, less cardiovascular impact per gram
- Higher FOS than garlic → more pronounced prebiotic effect
- Unique kaempferol profile → distinct flavonoid anti-inflammatory signature
- More prominent diuretic activity than garlic or onion in traditional records

**Energetic Classification:**
- ◉ Qi (functional): Primary — diuretic-renal; digestive; respiratory
- ◈ Jing (structural): Secondary — cardiovascular structural; blood
**Primary Tier: ◉ Qi** — functional multi-organ regulation

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Flavonoids

**1. Kaempferol-3-O-glucoside (astragalin)**
- Primary flavonoid of leek; higher kaempferol:quercetin ratio than onion or garlic
- Anti-inflammatory; mild diuretic; anti-proliferative

**2. Kaempferol-3,7-di-O-rhamnoside (kaempferidin)**
- Leek-characteristic kaempferol glycoside

**3. Quercetin-3-O-rutinoside (rutin)**
- Flavonol glycoside; capillary-strengthening; anti-inflammatory

**4. Isorhamnetin glycosides**

### Organosulfur Compounds

**5. Alliin (S-allyl-L-cysteine sulfoxide)**
- Present; lower concentration than garlic (~0.4–0.7% dry weight vs. garlic's ~1.0–1.5%)
- Converts to allicin on cell disruption; characteristic Allium odor

**6. Methyl allyl trisulfide (MATS)**
- Anti-platelet; vasodilatory

**7. S-methyl-L-cysteine sulfoxide (SMCS)**
- Anti-diabetic activity (same compound as in onion); significant in leek

### Polysaccharides

**8. Fructooligosaccharides (FOS) / Inulin**
- 3–10% fresh weight — among highest FOS density in the *Allium* genus
- Prebiotic; bifidogenic; SCFA-producing; greater than garlic or onion per gram

### Other

**9. Vitamin C — 12 mg/100g** (modest; less than ramsons)
**10. Folate — 64 μg/100g** (nutritionally significant)
**11. Provitamin A (β-carotene) — in dark green tops** — green tops far more nutritious than blanched white base (often discarded)
**12. Iron — 2.1 mg/100g** (plant-based iron; Vitamin C content enhances absorption)

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### 1. Diuretic
- **Mechanism:** Not fully elucidated; proposed: kaempferol inhibition of Na+/K+-ATPase → increased renal sodium excretion; increased urine output
- Consistent traditional documentation as diuretic across DMM, CM, SS — stronger diuretic attribution than garlic
- **Urinary tract:** Increased urine flow → flushing effect for lower UTI; urinary stone prevention (dilution)
- **Clinical parallel:** Kaempferol is a known weak diuretic; flavonoid diuresis is well documented for *Apium graveolens* (celery, similar kaempferol-rich species)

### 2. Cardiovascular (Allium Class)
- **H₂S donation (polysulfides):** Same mechanism as garlic; KATP channel opening → vasodilation
- **Anti-platelet (MATS):** TXA₂ inhibition
- **Kaempferol:** eNOS upregulation → NO → vasodilation

### 3. Prebiotic / Gut Microbiome
- **FOS/inulin (3–10%):** Highest in the Allium genus per gram of leek — bifidogenic; SCFA production
- Particularly, inulin-type fructans from leek: robust in vitro and in vivo bifidogenic effect
- Butyrate production → colonic epithelial energy; anti-inflammatory; colon cancer protective

### 4. Anti-inflammatory (Kaempferol)
- **COX-1/COX-2 inhibition** (IC₅₀ ~30 μM for kaempferol)
- **5-LOX inhibition:** anti-leukotriene; anti-allergic; expectorant (reduces inflammatory mucus)
- **NF-κB:** kaempferol inhibits IKKβ → reduces cytokine transcription

### 5. Respiratory / Expectorant (Allium + Kaempferol)
- Allicin volatile components: mildly mucolytic when inhaled or ingested
- 5-LOX inhibition → reduced LTC₄/LTD₄ → bronchodilation
- Warm leek soup / leek broth: traditional cold/URI remedy; mechanism = volatile organosulfur (steam) + kaempferol-leukotriene inhibition + hydration

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Largely traditional use supported by in vitro and animal data; limited human RCT data for leek specifically.**

**Pre-biotic:**
- FOS/inulin class has Level A evidence for bifidogenic effect; leek is confirmed FOS source
- Human microbiome studies confirm leek FOS increases *Bifidobacterium* as predicted
- **Evidence Grade: Level A** (for prebiotic class effect)

**Diuretic:**
- Traditional convergence (4+ traditions) + kaempferol diuresis mechanism; no dedicated leek RCT
- **Evidence Grade: Level C**

**Cardiovascular (Allium class proxy):**
- Garlic (I-261) Level A evidence; leek mechanism overlapping but lower compound concentration
- **Evidence Grade: Level C** (proxy; leek-specific data lacking)

**Anti-inflammatory / Respiratory:**
- In vitro kaempferol data strong; no leek-specific respiratory RCT
- **Evidence Grade: Level D** (mechanistic) to Level C (traditional)

**Cancer Epidemiology:**
- Allium vegetable intake (including leek): inverse association with colorectal, gastric cancer in cohort studies; attributable partly to kaempferol, FOS (butyrate), and organosulfur
- **Evidence Grade: Level C** (observational)

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The Welsh National Emblem Context:**
Leek's status as Wales's national symbol reflects a depth of cultural integration that transcends pharmacology. It is simultaneously the poorest man's food (winter staple; inexpensive; available when other vegetables fail) and a symbol of national pride. Welsh leek soup (*Cawl*) is the quintessential cold-season restorative — thick, warming, deeply savory. The medical and the cultural are inseparable.

**Taste and Texture Profile:**
- White base: Mild, sweet, delicate onion-garlic note; silky when cooked; barely pungent
- Green tops: Stronger; more aromatic; often discarded in commercial cooking but more bioactive
- Cooked leek: Unctuously tender; sweeter than raw; sulfur compounds milder than onion or garlic
- Raw leek: Sharp, fresh, mildly pungent; rings used in salads; less popular raw than garlic or spring onion

**The Long Cook Tradition:**
Traditional leek medicine involves prolonged cooking (stews, braises, soups simmered 1–2 hours). Unlike garlic where "crush and wait" preserves allicin before cooking, leek's primary bioactives (kaempferol glycosides, FOS) are heat-stable — prolonged cooking concentrates and hydrolyzes FOS partially to fructose + fructans of varying lengths, maintaining prebiotic diversity.

**Diuretic Experience:**
Traditional use describes a clear increase in urinary output after consuming substantial quantities of leek (200+ g). This is consistent with kaempferol's known diuretic effect and represents the clinical phenomenology that generated the "kidney cleansing" reputation across Mediterranean and Levantine traditions.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Dietary (primary pathway):**
- 100–200 g leek per serving: 2–4 servings weekly for general prebiotic/anti-inflammatory support
- **Retain green tops:** dark green portions discarded in commercial cooking contain 3–5× the kaempferol of the white base; add to soups/stocks
- Welsh cawl (leek + lamb broth): traditional restorative preparation; medicinal food

**Leek broth / soup:**
- 200 g leek simmered in water 30 minutes → strain → drink broth: traditional diuretic and URI treatment
- Add 2 garlic cloves for synergistic Allium effect

**Supplement (leek extract / kaempferol):**
- Standardized kaempferol supplements (10–50 mg/day): surrogate if dietary leek unavailable
- FOS supplement (3–5 g/day) if leek's prebiotic effect is the target

### Traditional Preparation — Welsh Cawl

Traditional recipe as medicinal food:
- Leek (generous amounts, 300–400 g), lamb bone broth, root vegetables, cooked 2–3 hours
- Eaten in winter and spring; consumed across all social strata in Wales
- Nutritional-medicinal profile: FOS prebiotic + kaempferol anti-inflammatory + bone mineral broth (calcium, magnesium) + iron (leek) + fat-soluble Vitamin A (from dark green tops)

### Safety Profile

**Very safe at dietary doses.** Widely consumed globally without adverse event signals at food quantities.

**Drug Interactions:**
- **Anticoagulants:** Same Allium class additive anti-platelet effect; minor at dietary doses; monitor supplements
- **Diuretic medications:** Additive diuresis; electrolyte monitoring if using large supplemental doses alongside pharmaceutical diuretics
- **Anti-diabetic drugs:** SMCS glucose-lowering; additive with anti-diabetic medications at supplemental doses

**Contraindications:**
- Allium family allergy (rare; cross-reactive with garlic and onion)
- High FODMAP consideration: FOS content may aggravate IBS symptoms in FODMAP-sensitive individuals (paradox: prebiotic for healthy gut; triggers gas/bloating in sensitized gut)

**Hemolytic anemia in animals:**
As with all *Allium* species — thiosulfate compounds toxic to dogs, cats, and horses. Human dietary amounts safe.
