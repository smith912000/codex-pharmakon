# Oriental Plane Tree — Chinar, Buttonwood-of-the-East, Old-World Plane

## *Platanus orientalis* L. | Family: Platanaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry (Galenic); astringent, drying, wound-binding
**Training Phase:** Restoration & Surface-Repair (topical/adjunct)

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian / Babylonian (Cuneiform materia medica — BM):** The plane tree is among the great sacred and shade trees of the ancient Near East. In Mesopotamian tradition the plane was planted along watercourses and temple avenues, prized as much for its cooling shade and civic symbolism as for its bark and leaf preparations. The cuneiform medical tradition drew broadly on astringent barks for binding wounds, checking fluxes, and drying weeping lesions, and the plane — abundant along the Tigris and Euphrates — belonged to this practical repertoire of drying, wound-closing tree-medicines.

**Dioscorides (De Materia Medica — DMM):** Dioscorides describes the plane (πλάτανος) and its uses: the bark boiled in vinegar for toothache and gum complaints, the fresh leaves and bark applied to wounds and inflammations, and the down of the leaves and fruit-balls noted as harmful to the ears and eyes — an early, accurate warning about the plant's irritant hairs. The tender leaves boiled in wine were used against swellings and running sores. This is a coherent astringent/vulnerary profile.

**Avicenna (Canon of Medicine — AT):** The Unani tradition inherited the Dioscoridean plane as a cold, dry, astringent simple. Avicenna's stream used the bark and leaf externally for wounds, burns, and inflammatory swellings, and cautioned — as Dioscorides did — about the fine hairs of the leaves and seed-balls irritating the respiratory passages and eyes.

**The Hippocratic plane:** The most famous plane tree in medicine is the great *Platanus orientalis* of Kos, under which Hippocrates is said to have taught his students. A living descendant still stands in the island's main square. The tree is therefore emblematic of the Western medical lineage itself — a symbol of teaching, shade, and the clinical gaze — even though its own pharmacology is modest.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The plane's doctrine is drying and binding. Its broad, cooling canopy and its astringent, tannin-rich bark gave it a double symbolism: outwardly the sheltering tree of civic and medical life, inwardly a drying agent that "closes and knits" wounds. The astringency that classical authors felt on the tongue is real chemistry — the leaf and bark are tannin- and flavonoid-rich (kaempferol and derivatives), which underwrites the traditional wound-binding and anti-flux uses. The very hairs that make the tree a respiratory irritant are also, symbolically, its warning: a beautiful, sheltering plant that must still be handled with respect.

### Active Components

- **Tannins** (condensed/hydrolysable) — the basis of astringency and wound-binding.
- **Flavonoids — kaempferol and kaempferol glycosides/derivatives** (confirmed by phytochemical analysis of *P. orientalis* leaf).
- **Phenolic acids and volatile constituents** — benzaldehyde, 2,4-di-tert-butylphenol, and fatty acids (palmitic, stearic, linoleic, linolenic) reported in leaf infusions.
- **Leaf and fruit-ball trichomes (fine stellate hairs)** — mechanically irritant, not a therapeutic constituent.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica the Oriental Plane is a **surface-repair and astringent adjunct**, not an internal tonic. Its coherent traditional niche — spanning Assyrian, Greek, and Unani practice — is topical: drying weeping wounds, binding minor lesions, and soothing inflamed gums (bark decoction). It belongs to the Restoration phase as a mild, externally applied astringent, valued more for reliability and gentleness than for any dramatic action. Internally, the leaf infusion has only folk standing (tooth/knee pain, stomach discomfort) and is not a primary Kenetica indication.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the modern evidence for *Platanus orientalis* is real but limited to laboratory and animal work. Ucar et al. (2018) found that water and methanol leaf extracts inhibited α-glucosidase and α-amylase (more than acarbose at matched concentration) and showed antioxidant, antimicrobial and anticancer activity in vitro, alongside acetyl- and butyrylcholinesterase inhibition (PMID 30213283). Dogan & Anuk (2019) characterised the leaf infusion's phytochemistry — kaempferol and derivatives among the actives — and reported a protective, antioxidant effect against ethanol-induced liver and kidney oxidative stress in rats ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11033-019-04741-7) — PMID 30864112). On the irritant side, Sercombe et al. (2011) measured plane-tree bioaerosols in Sydney and confirmed that inhalable *Platanus* trichomes were collected in personal nasal air samples and "may constitute a respiratory irritant," though they did not bind IgE (i.e. mechanical irritation rather than classic allergy) ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anai.2011.08.011) — PMID 22123378).

**Evidence grade: Data-sparse / traditional.** The therapeutic literature is preclinical (in-vitro enzyme and antioxidant assays, one rat study); there are no controlled human trials. The astringent/wound-binding tradition is plausible on tannin chemistry but remains **traditionally graded**. The trichome-irritant claim, by contrast, has direct human aerobiological support.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani (Avicenna):** Cold and dry; astringent (*qabid*); a drying, wound-binding simple for external use, correcting weeping and inflamed lesions.
- **Assyrian / Near-Eastern:** Grouped with drying, flux-checking astringent barks; also a sacred shade-tree of watercourses.
- **(No formal TCM or Ayurvedic rasa/guna classification is attested for this species; it sits outside the classical Chinese and Indian pharmacopoeias.)**

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** The plane belongs to the Near-Eastern repertoire of drying, flux-checking astringent barks and stood as a sacred shade-tree along the watercourses and temple avenues. Under the standing blocking warning that governs every Assyrian substance identification, tablet reference and causal-agency attribution, no cultivation status is claimed here: illness in this corpus is classified partly by the agency it opposes, and nothing in the retrieved material assigns the plane a place in a vital-constitution scheme rather than the practical work of binding a wound or drying a weeping lesion. The civic and sacred standing is the standing of the tree as sacred matter, not a cultivation status worked on the person. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides gives the plane (πλάτανος) a coherent astringent-vulnerary account — bark boiled in vinegar for the gums and toothache, leaves and bark to wounds and inflammations — and even records the accurate warning that the down of the leaves and fruit-balls harms the ears and eyes. He carries no subtle-body doctrine into which a cultivation status could be placed. What the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention: the sensory assay of a genuinely astringent bark, the recognition of the irritant hairs, the judgement of what to gather and what to guard against. Recording the plane's hazards is observation, not a vital-body assignment. **(c) absent.**

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The Unani stream inherited the Dioscoridean plane as a cold, dry, astringent (*qabid*) simple for external wounds, burns and inflammatory swellings, repeating the warning about the irritant hairs. The strongest cultivation idea in the corpus — the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture across a whole regimen — is a longevity frame of the person, not a status the tradition assigns to a cooling drying astringent, and no *mufarrih* or cardiac-*ruh* role is claimed for this tree. The attestation is humoral and therapeutic. **(b) framed** — placed in the humoral scheme, no cultivation status recorded.

**Practitioner's note.** The Oriental Plane is a topical astringent and nothing a cultivator takes to build vitality. Across all three traditions it dries and binds — a wound poultice, a gum rinse, a drying agent for weeping lesions — and its most robust attested property is a hazard, the mechanically irritant leaf and seed-ball hairs to keep away from the face and eyes. The tree's fame as the Hippocratic plane of Kos is emblematic of the teaching lineage, not a cultivation status of the drug. Use the bark externally for the defined job; handle the shedding foliage with respect.

### Contraindications

⚠️ **Trichome (leaf-hair) irritation — the primary documented hazard.**

- **Respiratory and eye/ear irritation from trichomes.** The fine stellate hairs shed by the leaves and seed-balls are inhalable and act as a mechanical respiratory irritant; Dioscorides already warned they harm the ears and eyes. Avoid handling shedding leaves or fruit-balls near the face; those with asthma or reactive airways should take particular care during the spring/summer shedding season. Wear eye protection when crushing dry leaf material.
- **Not for internal therapeutic use without qualified guidance** — internal leaf-infusion use rests only on folk tradition and unquantified doses.
- **Pregnancy/lactation:** insufficient safety data; avoid internal use.
- **Topical use:** discontinue if contact dermatitis or irritation develops; patch-test tannin-rich decoctions first.
- Keep seed-ball "down" away from infants and small children.
