# ANTHEMIS, ANTHEMIS PORPHURANTHES, ANTHEMIS MELANANTHES — Anthemis (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 3-154)

## *Three kinds of "anthemis" that differ only in flower colour. Identification: unresolved. The translator's gloss lists three groups of candidates in turn: wild chamomile (Matricaria recutita / M. chamomilla), dyer's chamomile (Anthemis tinctoria), and forking larkspur (Delphinium consolida); the text gives no way to choose; confidence low* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

> **CAUTION.** The chapter is described as emmenagogue and "abortifacient", and one of the translator's candidates, larkspur, is a poisonous plant. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should take this plant.

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk. This is a single-text entry.** The name was searched in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is 3-154 (line 7845; the heading runs over two lines, with "MELANANTHES" on the second). The name "anthemis" is also given as an alias in the neighbouring chapters 3-153 (ANTHULLIS, line 7837) and 3-155 (parthenion), which are separate entries and are not folded in here.

**Dioscorides (DMM).** "There are three kinds of anthemis (differing only in their flowers) the branches twenty centimetres long, shrubby, with many wings. The smaller branches are little, thin, many, with little round heads, within them flowers of gold colour; but outside there are round about white, yellowish, or purple leaves, the quantities like those of rue. It grows in rough places and byways. It is gathered in the spring." The three kinds are named in the heading: anthemis, anthemis porphuranthes and anthemis melananthes. The chapter then says that "the roots, flowers, and herb have a warming, relieving strength", and that taken as a drink of decoction or by bathing "they expel the menstrual flow, are abortifacient, expel stones [urinary, kidney], and induce urine"; they are taken for gaseousness and intestinal obstruction, "clean away jaundice, and cure liver ailments", and a decoction is used in warm packs for the bladder. "The most effective for those troubled with stones is that of a purple colour, which in all respects is the bigger, properly called eranthemon. That called leucanthemon is more urinary, as well as chrysanthemon." Smeared on, they cure ulcers in the inner angle of the eye; chewed, they cure apta (thrush); some use it as a suppository, beaten finely with oil, for recurrent fevers.

Aliases printed at the end: "leucanthemon, or eranthemon because it flowers in the spring; some call it chamemelum because of the similarity of its smell to apples; some call it melanthemon, chrysocome, or callias; the Romans call it malium, and the Africans, astertiphe." The text's comparison of smell to apples is the only odour given.

**Translator's gloss.** The "SUGGESTED" line lists, as three separate groups: (1) Chamaemelon leucanthemum, Chamaemelum vulgare, Leucanthemum Dioscoridis, Matricaria recucita (as printed), Matricaria chamomilla, "Wild Chamomile"; (2) Chamaemelum chrysanthemum, Anthemis tinctoria, "Dyers Chamomile, Yellow Chamomile"; (3) Chamaemelum eranthemon, Consolida regia, Consolida regalis arvensis, Delphinium consolida, "Forking Larkspur". The three groups are in the order of the translator's lines, and the sources read do not say that they correspond to the three kinds in the heading. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The chapter names no constituent. In general terms, plants of the daisy family (Compositae) such as chamomile carry volatile oils; larkspur (Delphinium) is a different family and contains toxic alkaloids. Which plant the text describes was not decided. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Bringing on the menstrual flow, abortion, urinary stones, promoting urine, gas and intestinal obstruction, jaundice and liver ailments, bladder warm packs, ulcers in the corner of the eye, thrush, and recurrent fevers.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose in measured units.

The Codex does not translate the text's terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend this plant.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these: the herb gathered in spring; "the leaves and flowers must be stored when they have been pounded apart and made into little balls. The root is dried and stored." For use: "sometimes give two parts of the herb, sometimes one part of the flower or root. Sometimes the opposite — give two parts of the flower and one of the herb, doubling it by turn every other day — and you must drink it in diluted honey." These are proportions of parts and not a dose. A bracketed note adds that "Chamomile pounded into small pieces with rosaceum [1-53] cures fevers", that "the shortest is best and grows in sandy places", and that physicians remove them at the beginning of spring. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any dose from this entry.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Chamomile is a familiar aromatic herb of the daisy family, used as a tea. Larkspur is a poisonous garden and field plant. Because the translator's candidates include both, no modern correlation is attempted. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** Printed: "warming, relieving strength" (roots, flowers and herb). No other hot, cold, dry or moist quality is given.

### Cultivation Register

**Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and the chapter claims none. It shows the discipline of attention that he offers: three kinds told apart by flower colour, the habitat (rough places and byways), the season of gathering (spring), the way to store leaves, flowers and root, and which kind is strongest for stones (the purple, "properly called eranthemon").

**Practitioner's note.** An ancient diuretic and menstrual-flow herb whose plant is not settled from the text. Nothing here supports any cultivation use, and the abortifacient claim and the larkspur candidate are reasons for caution. A cultivator should not use it.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy.** The text says the herb is "abortifacient" and expels the menstrual flow. Avoid in pregnancy.
- **Possible poisonous identity.** One of the translator's candidates, larkspur, is poisonous. Do not substitute any plant for it.
- **Urinary stones, jaundice, liver ailments, recurrent fevers.** Medical conditions; use proper care.
- **Daisy-family allergy.** General caution for plants of the daisy family. [USER: verify]
