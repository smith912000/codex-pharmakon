# Crocodile — "Crocodile" (Bryan's English); Crocodile oil, Fat-of-the-Crocodile, Crocodile-earth (Bryan's renderings)
## *The crocodile as a source of remedies in the Papyrus Ebers. Bryan's 1930 English prints "Crocodile" and gives no Egyptian word or binomial; the source list `_sources/ebers.csv` (line 107) records it as "Crocodile", class "animal", from Ghalioungui's glossary* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The English text on disk is C. P. Bryan's 1930 *The Papyrus Ebers*, a translation with summaries (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`); the Egyptian words are not printed. In Chapter VII, "Organic Remedies", Bryan says that a complete list of the animal materia medica "would not be possible since (as with many of the plant and mineral substances) the identity of a considerable number of the animals mentioned is still beyond us", and goes on to list "those animals whose identity is not in doubt, and who were called upon to sacrifice themselves in the cause of the Healing Art": "Lion Hippopotamus Crocodile Ox Cow Calf Mouse Porcupine..." (lines 1386-1392; `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` row "Crocodile", "Bryan 1930, Ch. VII Organic Remedies (animals) (line 1392)", "Bryan's English identification; no Egyptian word or binomial given in this list"). The crocodile is therefore one of the animals Bryan treats as securely identified. I found no sentence in Bryan's list that says which parts of the crocodile were used; the uses below are scattered through his summary of the papyrus.

Where the crocodile appears in Bryan's text, which I opened:

- *Crocodile oil, in a liniment for suppleness* (line 2181): the limbs "could be made supple by smearing them with a liniment composed of Natron, Beans, Honey, Incense, and Sweet Myrrh, beaten up with Hippopotamus Oil, Crocodile Oil, Oil-of-the-Shadfish, Olil-of-the-adu-animal, and Oil-of-Two-Days."
- *Fat-of-the-Crocodile, in a hair-restorer* (line 4027): "ANOTHER FOR THE GROWTH OF THE HAIR ON A HEAD WHICH IS BECOMING BALD: Fat-of-the-Lion, Fat-of-the-Hippopotamus, Fat-of-the-Crocodile, Fat-of-the-Cat, Fat-of-the-Serpent, Fat-of-the-Egyptian-Goat. Make into one and rub the head of the Bald One therewith."
- *Crocodile-earth.* Bryan's rendering "Crocodile-earth" is an ingredient in four prescriptions: a poultice for headache, "Crocodile-earth beaten up with Egg-of-the-Ostrich" (line 2112); a remedy for cataract, with "Lapis lazuli, Milk, Incense, and Crocodile-earth" (line 2824); a poultice for pterygium, "Black Knife-stone and Crocodile-earth, mixed up with Honey and flavoured with Incense" (line 2844); and, for grey eyebrows, "Crocodile-earth-mixed-with-Honey-which-has-been-dissolved-in-Onion-water" (line 4058). Bryan does not say what "Crocodile-earth" is, and whether it is material from the animal or an earth named after it is not stated in the lines read. The source list has a separate row "Crocodile dung" (`_sources/ebers.csv` line 79), citing the Wikipedia article on the Ebers papyrus, which is not relied on here. I do not equate that row with "Crocodile-earth". [USER: verify]
- *The bite of a crocodile.* Bryan says the conditions in the papyrus "range from the Sting of a Wasp to the Bite of a Crocodile" (line 1036), and his contents list names "Bite of a Crocodile" before the burns (line 1080). Bryan places the remedy under Minor Surgery: "WHAT TO DO AGAINST THE BITE OF A CROCODILE. If thou meetest a Crocodile's Bite, and thou findest his flesh equally fallen away on both sides, so thou coverest it with Raw Meat the first day" (lines 2211-2213). That is a treatment of a wound made by the animal. It uses raw meat; it does not use the crocodile.
- *A recitation that names the crocodile* (line 2978). Among the eye remedies, after a compound of "True Collyrium, Red-lead, Wild Honey" with an ingredient taken from a pig, to be crushed, powdered, mixed and injected into the ear, Bryan reports: "When thou hast seen properly to this mixing, repeat this Magic Formula: I HAVE BROUGHT THIS THING AND PUT IT IN ITS PLACE. THE CROCODILE IS WEAK AND POWERLESS. (Twice)." (The OCR prints "[HE" for "THE".) The formula names the crocodile, but in the OCR text the compound before it, which is partly garbled, shows no crocodile ingredient. Why the crocodile is named in that formula is not explained in the lines read.

No deity is attributed to the crocodile in any of these passages, and none is supplied here.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, crocodile fat and oil are animal lipids, mainly triglycerides. Nothing more specific can be said without a source, and the identity of "Crocodile-earth" is unsettled, so no composition is stated for it. No quantities are given here because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Egyptian text, as Bryan summarises it, uses the crocodile as an ingredient in external applications: an oil in a liniment to make the limbs supple, a fat in a hair-restorer, and "Crocodile-earth" in poultices for the head and eyes and in a remedy for grey eyebrows. It also gives a recitation that names the crocodile as weak and powerless, and a first-day dressing of raw meat for a crocodile's bite. The Codex does not recommend any of these. The crocodile is a protected animal in most places, the products are not available from legal sources, a crocodile bite is an emergency, and the identity of one of the main ingredients is not settled.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations Bryan reports: crocodile oil beaten up with natron, beans, honey, incense, sweet myrrh and other oils, as a liniment (line 2181); fat of the crocodile with five other animal fats, "made into one" and rubbed on the head (line 4027); "Crocodile-earth" beaten up with ostrich egg, or with lapis lazuli, milk and incense, or with black knife-stone, honey and incense, or with honey dissolved in onion water (lines 2112, 2824, 2844, 4058); raw meat laid on a crocodile's bite on the first day (line 2213). Bryan summarises, and where he gives no measure the Ebers text itself may; the lines read give none. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Reptile fats and oils are animal lipids; there is no established evidence that crocodile fat or oil restores hair or makes limbs supple beyond the ordinary emollient effect of a fat. Raw meat laid on a wound is an infection risk and does not treat a bite. Crocodilians carry *Salmonella* and other bacteria, and a crocodile bite is contaminated and needs prompt medical care. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Egyptian (EP):** None. Bryan's summary is organised by complaint, and it prints no temperature, moisture, taste or other quality for the crocodile or for "Crocodile-earth". The Ebers material is indication-organised, so no classification of the substance is supplied.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's vital constitution, and Bryan's English renderings are modern identifications. The *ka*, *ba*, *akh* and *ib* are not mentioned in any passage read about the crocodile. One point should be kept in view: Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*), and the crocodile is named in a recitation (line 2978: "The crocodile is weak and powerless"), but the compound that precedes it, as far as the garbled OCR shows, has no crocodile ingredient. So the recitation is a *heka* element that names the animal and is not evidence that the animal was a cultivation substance. Since Bryan summarises, I cannot say whether the papyrus attaches recitations to the crocodile-ingredient remedies above. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The crocodile appears in the papyrus as an ingredient in oils, fats and poultices, as a named enemy in one recitation, and as the cause of a wound. The honest use of the entry is as a record, and as a reminder that the same animal can be remedy, danger and subject of a spell in one text. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Wounds and bites:** a crocodile bite is a medical emergency; do not treat it with raw meat or any animal product.
- **Infection:** crocodilians carry *Salmonella* and other bacteria; raw products and putrid materials are an infection risk.
- **Skin and scalp:** unknown fats and earths applied to the scalp, eyes or limbs can cause irritation and infection; do not apply anything of uncertain identity to the eyes.
- **Identity:** "Crocodile-earth" is not identified in the lines read, so no safe use of it can be stated.
- **Law and conservation:** crocodilians are protected or trade-controlled in most jurisdictions (the Nile crocodile among them); trade in their parts is restricted, and the Codex does not recommend sourcing any.
- **Ethics:** the Codex does not recommend killing or harming wild animals for these uses.
