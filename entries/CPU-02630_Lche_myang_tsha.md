# Lche myang tsha — lche.myang.tsha (Clark 1995, Wood Salt); "Che nyang tsha" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)

## *A salt "derived from the branch of a tree", reddish, said to grow in hot southern valleys, ranked by the Tibetan text with rock salt as one of the two superior salts. The identification is the text's own description only: Clark prints "Wood Salt" and no Latin name, and no botanical or mineral species is given. The class on the spine was changed from mineral to plant on the strength of Clark's phrase "(derived from) the branch of a tree"; the Tibetan lists place it among the salts, so the class is of medium confidence. ### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the rGyud-bzhi tradition among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry, carried by two witnesses to the same Tibetan tradition: Clark's 1995 translation of the Explanatory Tantra and the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation. The row began as an unverified residual on the spine (CPU-02630) from the name index of Clark's text. A search of the other texts on disk for this name found nothing, and the entry records that it is not the same row as *shing tsha* ("tree salt", CPU-02450), which the spine note had earlier folded into it (see below).

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT), Clark 1995.** Clark's materia medica chapter has the entry at line 929 of `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` (name-index row 79 of `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, class guess mineral). It reads in full:

"lche.myang.tsha: Wood Salt: [is (derived from) the branch of a tree, grows in hot areas, for example southern valleys, and has a reddish hue. This and rock salt are the superior types of salt]. Wood salt is also [applied in the] same [way as rock salt and] is beneficial for the eyes."

Clark's introduction (line 82) says interpolations in square brackets are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and the Spreading Tree commentary, so the bracketed words are commentary and the words outside the brackets are the root-text statement. So the root statement is short: wood salt is used in the same way as rock salt and benefits the eyes. The description (a tree branch, hot southern valleys, reddish) and the ranking (it and rock salt are "the superior types of salt") are bracketed commentary. The rock salt entry just above it (line 928) reads "rgyam.tsha: Rock Salt: [has nine names and is a clear, crystalline salt obtained from stone. It resembles male calcite (pho.chong) and has slightly sweet taste.] Rock salt cures phlegm-wind combinations, indigestion and cold disorders." Together they give the use of wood salt by reference: whatever rock salt does (phlegm-wind combinations, indigestion, cold disorders), wood salt is applied in the same way, with the eyes added.

**The salty-taste group, Clark line 767.** "The group of medicines having salty taste includes rock salt, wood salt, horn salt, black 'sanchal' salt, white mineral salt, mirabilite, soda salt (saltpetre), white rock salt, ash salt, aragonite (Tartar salt) and salsoda (sodium bicarbonate)." Wood salt is therefore placed among the salts, not among the tree medicines, even though its source is a tree branch.

**The exception to the salty rule, Clark line 783.** The chapter's taste summary says: "Salty taste cures wind and phlegm (disorders) and, with the exceptions of wood salt and rock salt, increases bile. Excess intake [of these two exceptions] with their heavy power, increases phlegm." Wood salt and rock salt are named as the two salts that do not increase bile, and the text adds that excess of either, through its heavy power, increases phlegm.

**Potency, Clark line 797.** The chapter's discussion of post-digestive taste uses wood salt as its example: "[For example: the normally hot powered wood salt cures rather than harms a bile disorder by virtue of its sweet post-digestive taste]". That is the only potency statement for wood salt in the text: normally hot in power, with a sweet post-digestive taste, and for that reason not harmful in a bile disorder.

**Men-Tsee-Khang 2011.** The same Tibetan name appears in the 2011 translation of the Root and Explanatory Tantra (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`) as "Che nyang tsha". Line 3211 lists it among the salty-taste substances ("Gyam tsha, Gya tsha, Che nyang tsha, Ra tsha, Kharu tsha, Tsabru tsha, Ze tsha, ... Shing tsha ... are the group of medicinal substances having a salty taste"). Line 3251 gives the same exception as Clark: "with the exception of Che nyang tsha and Gyam tsha, all other salty substances aggravate tripa disorders." Line 3400 gives the use: "Che nyang tsha has the same potency as Gyam tsha, and treats eye problems." Gyam tsha is the rock salt (Clark's *rgyam.tsha*), so the two translations agree on rock-salt potency, the eye use, and the tripa (bile) exception.

**Relation to *shing tsha* (tree salt).** The spine note of 2026-10-30 retired an earlier wood-salt row into the tree-salt row as the same substance. The lines read here do not bear that out. Clark's *shing.tsha* (line 916) is cinnamon, a bark medicine, and Men-Tsee-Khang lists "Shing tsha" and "Che nyang tsha" as two separate names in the same salty-taste line (3211). The tree-salt entry II_205 records the same conclusion. This entry therefore stands as its own row. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, a salt "derived from a tree branch" would be a plant-derived salt, most plausibly obtained by burning or leaching plant material, and such salts are mainly mixtures of mineral salts of the plant, chiefly potassium and sodium compounds. That is a general statement about plant-ash salts, not a finding about this substance, and the text does not describe a method. The text's rock-salt comparison and the placement among the salts are all it gives. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify any named constituent before adding it.]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Used in the same way as rock salt, which "cures phlegm-wind combinations, indigestion and cold disorders" (Clark, line 928), and "beneficial for the eyes" (Clark, line 929; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3400: "treats eye problems").
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no preparation, no method of applying it to the eyes, no source plant, and no way to tell the real substance from a substitute.

The Codex gives no protocol for this substance. Because the material cannot be identified from the text, and because a salt applied to the eyes could do harm, the entry is a record of what the Tibetan text says and nothing more.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources retrieved give no preparation and no dose. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Salts of various kinds have been obtained from plant ash for a very long time, and potassium and sodium compounds are the usual major components of such ashes. Concentrated salt solutions irritate the eye, which is a reason to treat the eye use recorded in the text as a historical statement only. [USER: verify] The Tibetan categories (heavy power, post-digestive taste, phlegm-wind) have no modern equivalent and are not translated into one. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT (Clark 1995).** Taste: salty (line 767). Potency: "normally hot powered" (line 797); post-digestive taste: sweet (line 797). Power: "heavy" (line 783). Humoral action: among the salts that do not increase bile (line 783), with excess increasing phlegm (line 783).
- **FMT (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011).** Salty-taste group (line 3211); the exception to salty substances aggravating tripa (line 3251); "same potency as Gyam tsha" (line 3400).

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* The lines read give wood salt a place in the tastes-and-potencies framework and name its uses (the eyes, and by reference to rock salt, phlegm-wind combinations, indigestion and cold disorders), and give no rejuvenative, life-extending or practice-supporting status. Neither the Clark entry nor the Men-Tsee-Khang lines connect it to essence-extraction (*bcud len*) or to any practice. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this text to place it in. Its register is therapeutic only, and the cultivation status is unattested.

**Practitioner's note.** A plant-derived salt, ranked with rock salt as a superior salt, taken for its place in the taste scheme and for the eyes. There is no cultivation use recorded. A cultivator can learn from the entry how the tradition reasons from taste to post-digestive taste (a hot, salty substance with a sweet after-taste that does not harm bile), but should not treat the entry as guidance for using any salt.

### Contraindications

- **Excess.** Clark states that excess of wood salt and rock salt, through their heavy power, increases phlegm (line 783).
- **The eyes.** The text records an eye use but no method. Do not put any salt in the eye on the strength of this entry.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children, those on salt-restricted diets.** No statement in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Identity.** The substance is not identified beyond the text's description. It is easily confused with tree salt (*shing tsha*), which Clark identifies as cinnamon, and the Men-Tsee-Khang list gives as a salt. [USER: verify]
