**Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica* (DMM).** The chapter is 3-99 in the numbering of the English edition on disk (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 7443; name index `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row *SARKOKOLLA*, "3-99 (line 7443)"; the printed index lists "Sarcocolla 484"). The translator's gloss reads "SUGGESTED: Sarcocolla [Bedevian] — Sarcocol". The chapter, which is brief, reads in full: "Sarcocolla is the fluid of a tree growing in Persia (similar to thin frankincense) dark yellow, and somewhat bitter to the taste. It is able to close open cuts and sore wounds, and to stop fluids in the eyes. It is mixed with plasters. It is counterfeited by gum being mixed with it." (lines 7444 to 7445).

Four things follow from the chapter:

1. **Source and place.** It is "the fluid of a tree growing in Persia". The tree is not named. Dioscorides likens the drug itself to "thin frankincense", which is a likeness of appearance, not an identity.
2. **Look and taste.** It is "dark yellow" and "somewhat bitter to the taste". These are the two tests he gives.
3. **Use.** It closes "open cuts and sore wounds", stops "fluids in the eyes", and is "mixed with plasters".
4. **Adulteration.** It "is counterfeited by gum being mixed with it". That is the one adulterant named.

The chapter stands in Book 3 beside other gums and resins: the preceding chapter, 3-98, is *Ammoniakon* (gum ammoniacum, the liquid of a *ferula* near Cyrene), and the next, 3-100, is *Glaukion*. The page headers of the edition carry "ROOTS OF AKANTHODA or PRICKLY PLANTS" in this region; that is the book's section heading, and does not describe sarcocolla.

**Other places in Dioscorides that print the name.** Two lines elsewhere in the same text use "sarcocolla", each for a different purpose, and they are given so that no one mistakes them for this chapter. (i) In the chapter on *Euphorbion* (3-96, line 7419), the resin is said to be "adulterated with sarcocolla and glue mixed together", and a clear, transparent sort is compared to "transparent like sarcocolla [3-99]"; the reader is told to choose the transparent and sharp sort. This confirms that sarcocolla was a known trade material that could be used as a filler for a dearer resin, in Dioscorides' own account. (ii) In the chapter on *Argemone* (2-208, line 6406), "sarcocolla" appears as one of the other names of a herb ("It is also called artemone, arselam, or sarcocolla; the Romans call it artemonia"). That is a plant name, not the Persian gum, and it is not attributed to this row. [USER: verify the two cross-references against the printed edition]

### Active Components

Dioscorides names no constituent. In general terms, plant gum-resins are mixtures of water-soluble gums (polysaccharides) with resinous and sometimes volatile fractions, and a "gum" in the ancient sense is usually a hardened plant exudate. The chapter's own report that it is "counterfeited by gum" shows that the ancient trade distinguished the drug from plain gum. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Closing open cuts and sore wounds; stopping "fluids in the eyes"; as an ingredient of plasters.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no preparation beyond mixing into plasters, no duration, no warning, and no source plant. The phrase "fluids in the eyes" is the translator's wording and is not turned into a modern diagnosis here.

The Codex gives no protocol and recommends no gum-resin for the eye or for a wound.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation: the drug "is mixed with plasters" (line 7445). It gives no quantity, no vehicle, and no method for the eye use. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any recipe from this entry.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. A gum-resin such as this one is a hardened plant exudate; a gum can act as a mucilaginous, wound-covering material, but that is a general physical statement and is not evidence for the ancient uses. Putting any unsterile resin or gum into an eye is not safe. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and this chapter is a drug description with a use and a warning about fraud. What it does offer a cultivator is Dioscorides' discipline of attention: provenance ("growing in Persia"), a sensory assay (dark yellow, somewhat bitter) and adulteration detection ("counterfeited by gum being mixed with it"). That discipline is real, and here it is complete in a single sentence. It is not a spiritual practice, and no amuletic use is recorded for this drug.

**Practitioner's note.** A traded gum-resin for wounds and eyes, with a test of look and taste and a warning about gum. Nothing supports cultivation use, and the eye use is unsafe. A cultivator takes from it the habit of checking provenance and fraud.

### Contraindications

- **Eyes.** Do not put any unsterile resin or gum into the eye.
- **Wounds.** Do not pack a wound with an unsterile resin; open wounds need proper care.
- **Identity and adulteration.** The source plant is not established, and the chapter itself says the drug was adulterated. Do not buy or use anything sold under this name on the strength of this entry.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** No statement in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Allergy.** Plant gums and resins can cause contact allergy in some people. This is a general statement, not drawn from the chapter.
