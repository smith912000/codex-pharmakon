# Geranium dahuricum — zhim.thig.le, Shhimthikle (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Geranium dahuricum, the first of five Latin names Clark 1995 prints for zhim.thig.le (then Plectranthus sp. black type, Geranium pratense, Euphrasia reqellii Wettstein and Morrubium incisum); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text prints the Tibetan name but no Geranium* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); the binomial rests on one printed line of one text.** This entry rests on one tradition's texts, in two modern editions. Only Clark 1995 prints *Geranium dahuricum* (line 1054 and the index of Latin names, line 1590). A search of Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 for "Geranium" found nothing. The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. No other text read for this entry names the plant: a search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk (including the three Bhishagratna volumes) for the genus name *Geranium* found only Dioscorides 3-131 (line 7700, translator's gloss *Geranium robertianum*, among other suggested names), a different species not attributed to this row. Clark's preface (line 83) says that more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance, and that sometimes the same name refers to different plants growing in unrelated places. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (row 156) the Tibetan name *zhim.thig.le* is recorded at Clark line 1054 with the identification "Geranium dahuricum; Plectranthus sp. (black type); Geranium pratense; Euphrasia reqellii Wettstein.; Morrubium incisum" and the note "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The key, *Geranium dahuricum*, is therefore the first name Clark prints. The third name, *Geranium pratense*, is the key of the sibling entry I_1296 in this batch, which rests on the same line and on a second Clark line (977).

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1054).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, after the heading "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). It reads:

"zhim.thig.le: Geranium dahuricum: Plectranthus sp. (black type): Geranium pratense: Euphrasia reqellii Wettstein.: Morrubium incisum: [has fifteen names. It has a square stem with many joints, square petioles and a leaf similar to that of nettle, but without sting. The flower is pale yellow and bitter with black fruit. The black type has a coarse black leaf, square hooded stalk, red flower and three-sided black seeds which resemble cracked buckwheat. It has sweet taste and oily power. The inferior type has a soft, glossy leaf, hard, square stalks, brownish or bluish flowers and oily power. Geranium may also have small, round, lobed leaves and five-petalled flowers. All three main types] dispel cataract [as well as other eye diseases and are anthelmintic]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the brackets (commentary layer) are the number of names, the whole description, the sweet taste and oily power of the black type, the sentence on Geranium, and the words "as well as other eye diseases and are anthelmintic". Outside them (root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the five Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the clause "dispel cataract". The description in the brackets is of a plant with a square, jointed stem and a nettle-like leaf, with a "black type" and an "inferior type"; only one sentence ("Geranium may also have small, round, lobed leaves and five-petalled flowers") speaks of *Geranium*. The Codex does not read the square-stemmed description as describing *Geranium dahuricum*.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011.* Line 3442 (page 208), in the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), reads: "Shhimthikle removes lingthok." The index lists "Shhimthikle 208" and "lingthok 208" (lines 7491 and 7420). The Glossary on Tibetan Terms lists "Lingthok (ling tog)" third in a run of terms at line 4919, and the definitions printed in order at lines 4924 to 4926 give, third, "An eye disorder accompanied with appearance of pimple-like growth in the eyes" (line 4926). That pairing is by position. [USER: verify] The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints "Shhimthikle" (line 5718) and "zhim thig le" (line 5723) in the "S" block, whose scientific names are split from the Tibetan names (lines 5727 to 5765). Counting from the end of the run, the name stands against "Rabdosia ragosa", not against any *Geranium*; the same count is made in I_1251 (*Chaenomeles speciosa*). The Codex does not rely on the count. The two Tibetan texts therefore agree on the Tibetan name and that it is an eye drug (Clark: "dispel cataract"; Men-Tsee-Khang: removes "lingthok"); the Codex does not treat "cataract" and "lingthok" as one term, and Men-Tsee-Khang does not support *Geranium dahuricum*.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder found no entry on *Geranium dahuricum*. I_1296 (*Geranium pratense*, this batch) covers the same Clark line 1054 under its third name. I_814 (*Geranium robertianum*, tagged DMM and EF) is a different species from other traditions. I_1251 (*Chaenomeles speciosa*) and I_1178 (*Rosa macrophylla*) quote the Men-Tsee-Khang "S" glossary block that holds "Shhimthikle". Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Geranium* is a genus of the geranium family (Geraniaceae) and that tannins are common in the genus. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained or which part was used; Clark's commentary records a "bitter" flower for the plant it describes and a "sweet taste and oily power" for the black type.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "dispel[s] cataract", with "other eye diseases", and is "anthelmintic" in the commentary (Clark, line 1054), and that "removes lingthok" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3442). These are the texts' claims and not Codex protocols. Eye disease needs medical assessment and the Codex offers no advice for it.

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 1054) prints, for the black type, "sweet taste and oily power", and the same "oily power" for the inferior type, and calls the flower "bitter". No post-digestive taste is printed. Men-Tsee-Khang prints no taste or power for this name in the lines read. The action printed in both texts is for the eyes.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it dispels cataract and other eye diseases, and is anthelmintic in the commentary. The Men-Tsee-Khang text says it removes lingthok. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the sentences cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and the link from the binomial *Geranium dahuricum* to that framing rests on Clark's first-listed identification of a name for which Clark gives five.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is an eye drug in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and the plant it describes is not securely a *Geranium*. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Eye disease needs medical assessment, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry, least of all for the eyes. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
