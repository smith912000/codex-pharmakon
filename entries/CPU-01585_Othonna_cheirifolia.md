# Othonna cheirifolia — Othonna, a name that Dioscorides gives to a juice and a stone (Dioscorides 2-213)

## *Othonna cheirifolia, "Barbary Ragwort", the second item on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 2-213, which prints first the bare genus name Othonna — "African Ragwort"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 6456) prints "Othonna — African Ragwort" and then "Othonna cheirifolia — Barbary Ragwort". Below it the translators print a caution from Loudon (line 6457): "The plant of the ancients can have had little affinity with that of the moderns [Loudon]." That is, the translators themselves doubt that the plant now called *Othonna* is the plant Dioscorides meant. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *2-213. OTHONNA*, heading at line 6455, text at lines 6458 and 6462 (with a page break between them, the translators' page 355), in the section of Book Two headed "Herbs with a sharp quality". The whole chapter reads: "Some say othonna is the juice of chelidonia major, some of glaucium, some say that it is the juice of the flowers of horned poppy, some that it is a mixture of the juices of anagallis coerulea [2-209], hyoscyamus [4-69] and poppy, and some say that it is the juice of a certain primitive herb called othonna, and that it grows in the part of Arabia that lies towards Egypt. It has only a few leaves (like eruca [2-170]) full of holes as though they were wormeaten, illfavoured or mouldy. It bears a broad-petalled saffroncoloured flower, as a result some think it to be a kind of anemone. It is juiced and put into eye medicines for when there is need of cleansing the eyes; it has a biting nature and removes all things that darken the pupils whatsoever. Some say that there is a certain kind of fluid that flows from the herb, which, washing and removing the stones from it, they make into lozenges for the same use. Some say that othonna is an Egyptian stone found in Thebais — the colour of brass, small in size, biting to the taste, with a certain kind of burning and astringency."

This is a chapter in which Dioscorides does not say what *othonna* is. It lists what "some say": the juice of *chelidonia major*, of *glaucium*, of the flowers of horned poppy, or a mixture of the juices of *anagallis coerulea*, *hyoscyamus* and poppy (the cross-references are to chapters 2-209 *ANAGALLIS, KORKOROS* and 4-69, and the plant names are the text's own, not identified here); the juice of "a certain primitive herb called othonna" growing in the part of Arabia toward Egypt; and, at the end, "an Egyptian stone found in Thebais", brass-coloured, small, biting to the taste. Between these, the chapter describes a herb: only a few leaves, like *eruca* (chapter 2-170 *EUZOMON*, line 6002), "full of holes as though they were wormeaten, illfavoured or mouldy", and a broad-petalled flower of saffron colour, "as a result some think it to be a kind of anemone". One use is given, and it is for the eyes: "It is juiced and put into eye medicines for when there is need of cleansing the eyes; it has a biting nature and removes all things that darken the pupils whatsoever." A variant is added ("Some say"): a fluid that flows from the herb, which is washed, cleared of its stones and made into lozenges for the same use. The chapter prints no other names, no dose and no season.

So the chapter fixes neither the thing nor the plant. The Codex states what the text states: that the use is the clearing of the eyes by a biting preparation, that the substance may be a juice, a mixture, a lozenge or a stone, and that the herb is described by a few leaves with holes and a saffron flower. It does not state that the herb is *Othonna cheirifolia*, and the translators' caution applies.

**The chapters around it.** 2-212 *CHELIDONION MIKRON* (heading at line 6450; its SUGGESTED lines, 6451 and 6452, print *Ranunculus ficaria* — "Celadine") describes a little herb with roots like wheat grains, and its text (line 6453) says "It is sharp like anemone, ulcerating to the outside of the skin". 2-214 *MUOS OTON* (line 6463) is the translators' chickweed and field forget-me-not, whose root "heals ulcers in the inner angle of the eye". The Codex does not carry those statements over to 2-213.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter id 2-213 and the names *othonna* and *Othonna cheirifolia* found no existing entry on this chapter. I_843 (Ragwort, *Senecio jacobaea*) is a different plant with the English name "ragwort" and does not cite 2-213. No entry is duplicated.

### Active Components

The composition of what Dioscorides calls *othonna* was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, the plants called ragworts in the daisy family (Asteraceae) include species that contain pyrrolizidine alkaloids, which are toxic to the liver. Whether this applies to the species called *Othonna cheirifolia* was not checked. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a biting preparation put into eye medicines. The Codex gives no protocol for it. The reasons are plain: the thing itself is not identified in the text, the text records a possible stone, juice or mixture that includes *hyoscyamus* and poppy, and an eye is not a place for an experiment.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives two preparations, with locus (line 6462): the herb "juiced and put into eye medicines", and lozenges made from the fluid that flows from it after "washing and removing the stones". It gives no quantity. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints a "biting nature" for the herb and, for the stone, "biting to the taste, with a certain kind of burning and astringency". It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality for 2-213.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition, and it is notable here as a discipline of doubt: he sets side by side the rival accounts of what *othonna* is ("some say ... some say ...") and describes the herb by its ill-favoured, holed leaves and saffron flower, without choosing. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of an ingredient of eye medicines. The identification is the translators', and they doubt it. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The useful lesson is Dioscorides' habit of reporting disputed identities as disputed, and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning beyond the "biting nature" it ascribes to the substance. Anything biting put into the eye can injure it, and the chapter's own candidates include *hyoscyamus* and poppy. The identification is doubtful on the translators' own caution, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
