# American Wild Yam — Colic Root, Rheumatism Root, Devil's-Bones

## *Dioscorea villosa* L. | Family: Dioscoreaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Cool and dry (Galenic); acrid-bitter, relaxant-antispasmodic
**Training Phase:** Softening / Release (the phase that relaxes cramp, spasm, and colic in smooth muscle)

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Eclectic & North American Herbalism (the living tradition of use).**
*Dioscorea villosa* earned its folk names — Colic Root, Rheumatism Root — from its principal historical application: the relief of spasmodic, gripping pain. Nineteenth-century Eclectic physicians reached for wild yam in bilious colic, intestinal cramp, the cramping of dysmenorrhoea, and the griping of the gut, prizing it as an antispasmodic and mild anti-inflammatory. This is the tradition on which the plant's reputation genuinely rests.

**Dioscorides & the Greek Legacy (DMM).**
The genus carries the name of Pedanius Dioscorides himself, whose *De Materia Medica* is the fountainhead of the Western herbal. Old-World yams (*Dioscorea* spp.) were known to Greek and later Mediterranean pharmacy as starchy, acrid roots; the American species was absorbed into this genus-level heritage by later botanists honouring the ancient authority. The specific attribution to *D. villosa* is New-World and post-Columbian; the Dioscoridean tag marks lineage of the genus, not an ancient monograph on this species.

**Sushruta / Ayurvedic Analogue (SS).**
The *Sushruta Samhita* and the broader Ayurvedic corpus employ Old-World yams (notably *Dioscorea* species of the Indian subcontinent) as nourishing, grounding tubers and as components of rejuvenative and anti-colic formulae. *D. villosa* stands as the North American cousin within this shared *Dioscorea* materia; the ethnobotanical overlap — tubers used against cramp and for vitality — is genuine at the genus level, while the specific American species is not a classical Ayurvedic dravya.

**Avicennian / Unani Reception (CM).**
As with the pinks, the yams entered the Galenic-Unani synthesis as acrid, drying roots; the placement is humoral and general rather than a discrete *Qanun* chapter on *D. villosa*.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.**
The knotted, twisting rhizome — "devil's-bones" — is the plant's own emblem of cramp and its release: a body that coils and must be persuaded to let go. In Kenetica the root belongs to the **Softening / Release** phase because its true, defensible action is the un-knotting of spasm, not the manufacture of hormones.

### Active Components

- **Diosgenin** — the steroidal sapogenin for which the plant is famous, present as its glycoside **dioscin** and related saponins.
- **Steroidal saponins** more broadly, plausibly underlying the antispasmodic and anti-inflammatory activity.
- **Phytosterols, starch, tannins** in the tuber.

**The critical honesty point — diosgenin does NOT become progesterone in the body.** Diosgenin is an *industrial* starting material: in the 20th century, chemist Russell Marker showed that diosgenin could be chemically degraded (the "Marker degradation") in a *laboratory* to yield progesterone and other steroids, which launched the modern steroid and oral-contraceptive industries. That conversion requires multi-step synthetic chemistry with reagents and conditions the human body does not possess. **Eating wild yam, or applying "wild yam cream," does not raise progesterone or DHEA** — the human body has no enzymatic pathway to turn dietary diosgenin into progestogens. Marketing that sells wild-yam cream as "natural progesterone" is selling a category error; any cream that genuinely raises progesterone does so because *synthetic* progesterone has been added to it, not because of the yam.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Wild yam's legitimate Kenetica role is **antispasmodic release**: smooth-muscle cramp of the gut (colic, biliary and intestinal griping) and uterine cramp (dysmenorrhoea), traditionally with an anti-inflammatory adjunct role in joint complaints. It is used as a relaxant, not a hormone. It is explicitly **not** placed in any endocrine/hormone-modulation phase, because the hormonal claim is unsupported.

### Scientific Correlation

Based on articles retrieved from PubMed:

- Komesaroff and colleagues (2001, *Climacteric*) ran a double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial of topical wild yam extract cream in 23 symptomatic menopausal women. After treatment there were **no significant changes** in weight, blood pressure, serum lipids, FSH, estradiol, or serum/salivary **progesterone**, and no meaningful difference from placebo in symptom scores. This is direct human evidence that wild-yam cream does not raise progesterone or relieve menopausal symptoms. (PubMed PMID 11428178; no DOI registered in PubMed.)
- The historical steroid literature (Applezweig, 1969, *Chemical Week*, PMID 12255132) documents the industrial context in which diosgenin-bearing yams became the feedstock for laboratory synthesis of progesterone and related steroids — underscoring that the conversion is a *chemical-industrial* process, not a physiological one. (No DOI registered in PubMed.)

**Evidence grade: Traditional for the antispasmodic use; the hormonal/"natural progesterone" claim is REFUTED by human data.** The cramp-relieving reputation is historically robust but not confirmed by modern trials; the progesterone-precursor marketing claim is contradicted by the Komesaroff RCT.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Eclectic / Western:** Antispasmodic, anti-inflammatory, mild diaphoretic; acrid and slightly bitter.
- **Galenic / Unani:** Cool and dry, relaxant to spasm (attribution general, at genus level).
- **Ayurvedic (analogical, via Old-World *Dioscorea*):** *tikta*/*kashaya* (bitter/astringent) rasa, *shita* (cool) virya; grounding tuber used against *vata*-type cramp. (Analogical — *D. villosa* is not a classical dravya.)

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The tag here is an eponym, not an attestation. *Dioscorea* carries the name of Pedanius Dioscorides because later botanists honoured him when they named the genus; *Dioscorides villosa* is a North American species, post-Columbian and entirely unknown to the *De Materia Medica*, which could not have recorded a plant from a continent the Greek world never reached. Dioscorides in any case holds no subtle-body system into which a root could be given a cultivation status. What survives as genuinely his is a discipline of attention — provenance, part, season, adulteration — and even that attaches to the Old-World roots he actually handled, not to this American cousin. Cultivation status: **(c) absent**, and the species attestation to Dioscorides is nil beyond the genus name.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** As with the pinks, yams enter the Galenic-Unani synthesis only as a general class of acrid, drying roots placed by humoral quality; there is no discrete *Qanun* treatment of *D. villosa*, and could not be, since this is a New-World species reaching Old-World pharmacy after the classical corpus was closed. The Unani conservation idea — innate heat and radical moisture husbanded across a regimen — is nowhere assigned to this root. The placement that exists is genus-level and generic, and it is therapeutic-humoral, not a cultivation status. **(b) framed:** a generic humoral placement at genus level, no cultivation status recorded, and the species attribution is anachronistic.

**Ayurveda (SS).** The *Sushruta Samhita* and later *dravyaguna* do work Old-World *Dioscorea* tubers of the subcontinent as grounding, nourishing roots and as components of *rasayana* (rejuvenative) preparations — but that rejuvenative standing belongs to the Indian species, not to *D. villosa*, which the entry itself states is not a classical Ayurvedic dravya. To read a *rasayana* status onto the American wild yam would be to borrow another species' cultivation status across an ocean; the honest record is that this species carries none. **(c) absent** for *D. villosa* — the genus-level rejuvenative use is real but attaches to Indian yams, not this one.

**Practitioner's note.** The living tradition for this plant is not any of the three tagged: it is nineteenth-century North American Eclectic practice, which used wild yam as an antispasmodic for colic and cramp — and that is a therapeutic use, not a cultivation one. A cultivator takes nothing here for vitality. Above all, the plant builds no hormones: diosgenin becomes progesterone only in a factory (the Marker degradation), never in the body, and the Komesaroff RCT confirms wild-yam cream does not raise progesterone. So the honest instruction is narrow — a short relaxant for smooth-muscle spasm, and emphatically not a "natural progesterone" or a longevity tonic.

### Contraindications

- **Do not use for hormone replacement or as a "natural progesterone."** The claim is false; relying on wild yam for progesterone support risks leaving a genuine deficiency untreated.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** avoid — traditional uterine-relaxant/antispasmodic use and insufficient safety data.
- **High doses** may cause nausea, vomiting, or diarrhoea (saponin irritation).
- Caution with any product marketed as wild-yam cream "with progesterone effect": if it works hormonally, it likely contains undeclared added progesterone — check the label and source.
