# Artemisia herba — Artemisia leptophyllos ARTEMISIA LEPTOPHULLOS, "fine-leaved artemisia" (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 3-128)

## *Artemisia herba-alba Asso, white wormwood, a grey, aromatic shrubby plant of dry Mediterranean and Near Eastern ground; the row's name "Artemisia herba" is the first two words of the English edition's suggestion "Artemisia herba alba — Artemisia", which is the identification offered for chapter 3-128, and it is of medium confidence only, since the chapter describes a plant of rivulets, hedges and sown fields that smells of sampsuchum, a habitat and scent that do not obviously suit a plant of dry steppe, so the edition's suggestion should be treated as a label* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named by Dioscorides among the texts on disk, in chapter 3-128.** This is a single-text entry. The row was added on 13 October as an unverified placeholder from the Dioscorides name list (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 534), and it carries the truncated binomial "Artemisia herba". This entry replaces it. The chapter was read in full on 30 October, together with 3-127 and 3-129, and the Greek and Latin names were then searched in the rest of the text. Nothing else on disk was found for this plant.

**Dioscorides, De Materia Medica (DMM).** The chapter is 3-128, ARTEMISIA LEPTOPHULLOS, in Book Three, `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading at line 7685, text at line 7687. The editor's line reads: "Artemisia herba alba — Artemisia". The entire chapter is:

"Artemisia grows around rivulets and hedges and in sown fields. The flowers and bruised leaves of this give off the smell of sampsuchum [3-47]. Therefore if anyone is suffering in his stomach he should pound this herb well with oil of almonds, and make (as it were) a warm compress and lay it on the stomach, and he shall be healed. If anyone is pained in his strength, having mixed the juice of this with oil of roses let him rub with it, and he shall be whole."

Four things follow from the text alone. (1) The Greek surname *leptophyllos* means "fine-leaved", which the chapter does not itself explain. (2) The only descriptions are the habitat (rivulets, hedges, sown fields) and the smell of "the flowers and bruised leaves", which is compared to sampsuchum. In 3-47 the edition identifies sampsuchum with marjoram. [USER: verify] (3) There are two uses, both external: a warm compress of the pounded herb in almond oil for a pain in the stomach, and a rub of the juice in oil of roses for a man "pained in his strength". (4) There is no dose, no part named other than "flowers and bruised leaves", and no quality.

**Neighbouring chapters.** The chapter before it, 3-127, ARTEMISIA MONOKLONOS, ARTEMISIA MONOKLONOS ETERA, is a longer chapter on other artemisias, and the editor's suggestions there are mugwort, feverfew and tansy. It describes a shrub-like herb "similar to wormwood [3-26] but bigger", states that "they both warm and relieve", and gives uses for the womb, stones and urine, and a travelling charm. It is a different drug in the Greek and a separate chapter, and its content is not carried over to 3-128. The chapter after it, 3-129, AMBROSIA, begins a different plant. Dioscorides does not say in 3-128 that the "fine-leaved" kind shares the properties of the others.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. *Artemisia herba-alba* is a grey-leaved aromatic shrub of dry steppe and semi-desert in North Africa and the Middle East, and has a long record of folk use there. [USER: verify] The wormwoods as a group contain volatile oils, and some contain compounds that can be toxic in quantity. The Dioscoridean uses are not assessed here. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** No quality (warming, cooling, astringent, bitter or other) is printed for this plant in 3-128, and none is imported. The neighbouring chapter prints "they both warm and relieve" for another artemisia; that statement is not carried over.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and 3-128 offers none. The chapter is among his shortest. What it gives a practitioner is a discipline of attention in miniature: where the plant grows (rivulets, hedges, sown fields), which parts are used (the flowers and the bruised leaves), the smell by which the herb is known (like sampsuchum), and the oil each preparation is made in (almond for the compress, rose for the rub). That is provenance and sensory recognition. It is not a cultivation status. The neighbouring chapter 3-127 records a travelling charm for artemisia; Dioscorides records it and the chapter does not endorse it, and it is not part of 3-128.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports any cultivation use. The chapter teaches how to know the plant by its place and its smell, and how a plain external preparation is made.

### Contraindications

- **Identity.** The species is an editorial suggestion of medium confidence, and the wormwoods and mugworts are many and easily confused. Do not gather or use a plant on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
- **Wormwoods in general.** Some species of *Artemisia* are toxic in quantity or cause skin reactions. Do not apply or take any plant of the genus on the strength of this entry.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** No statement in 3-128. The neighbouring chapter lists artemisias as an abortifacient, so avoid in pregnancy. [USER: verify]
