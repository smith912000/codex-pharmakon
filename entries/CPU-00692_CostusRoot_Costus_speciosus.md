# Costus Root — Crepe Ginger / Kembuka / Pushkarmula (Sanskrit: *keukand*, *kebuka*; Ayurvedic trade name loosely "costus root")

## *Costus speciosus* (J.König) Sm. [≡ *Cheilocostus speciosus*] | Family: Costaceae

**Tradition Tags:** EP (Ebers), AT (Assyrian), DMM (Dioscorides), CM (Avicenna), SBJ (Shennong), SS (Sushruta)
**Energetic Classification:** Warm/hot and dry; pungent-bitter, deobstruent, carminative (Galenic-Unani); *ushna* (heating) in the Ayurvedic reading
**Training Phase:** Tempering

---

> **Nomenclature caution — read first.** The word "costus" is one of the most confused names in materia medica. This entry is *Costus speciosus* (Costaceae), the tropical "crepe ginger" or *kembuka/keukand* of South and Southeast Asia — a diosgenin-rich rhizome. It is **botanically distinct** from *Saussurea costus* (syn. *S. lappa*, Asteraceae), the Himalayan root that is the classical Greco-Arab "costus" (*qust*) and the TCM "Mu Xiang" (木香). Historical Greek/Arabic/Egyptian "costus/qust" references most often mean the *Saussurea* root, which travelled the incense and spice routes. The name and the reputation crossed between the two plants over centuries. Where a tradition's "costus" cannot be pinned to *C. speciosus* specifically, that is stated honestly below rather than claimed. The modern *pharmacological* diosgenin/antidiabetic evidence, by contrast, is specifically on *C. speciosus*.

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ebers (EP):** The Ebers Papyrus and related Egyptian pharmacopoeia name aromatic "costus"-type roots among fumigants and unguent ingredients. As a general and genuine statement, costus root was an item of the ancient incense/aromatic trade reaching Egypt; the *specific* plant behind any given Egyptian reference is usually the *Saussurea* costus rather than *C. speciosus*, so this attribution is to the "costus" tradition broadly, not a verified identification of the Costaceae species.

**Assyrian (AT):** Aromatic roots of the costus type appear in the Mesopotamian aromatics-and-fumigants vocabulary; again the trade good is most securely *Saussurea*. The Costaceae *C. speciosus* is South/Southeast Asian and reaches these traditions through the same porous "costus" name.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** Dioscorides describes *kostos* as a warming, diuretic, emmenagogue and stomach-warming root, distinguishing grades (Arabian, Indian, Syrian). This classical *kostos* is the *Saussurea* root; it is included here because *C. speciosus* inherited the name and much of the reputation in later South-Asian usage.

**Avicenna (CM):** The *Qanun* treats *qust* as hot and dry, deobstruent, carminative, anthelmintic and emmenagogue — the Unani profile that later attaches to Asian "costus" roots generally.

**Shennong (SBJ):** The Chinese materia medica "Mu Xiang" (木香), a warming qi-regulating, stomach-and-spleen root, is classically *Saussurea/Aucklandia*, not *C. speciosus*; the shared "costus" identity in Western sources is why the SBJ tag appears, noted honestly as a name-level rather than species-level link.

**Sushruta (SS):** Here the identification is strongest. In the Ayurvedic *Sushruta Samhita* lineage, *kebuka/keukand* (*Costus speciosus*) is a recognised plant, and the rhizome is used in classical and folk Ayurveda for diabetes (*prameha*), fevers, worms, skin disease and as a deobstruent — the tradition that maps most directly onto the plant this entry actually describes.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The traditions read costus roots as *hot, pungent, opening* — dispersers of stagnation, movers of stuck fluid and stuck digestion. For *C. speciosus* specifically, Ayurveda's use in *prameha* (the classical diabetes/urinary-disorder cluster) is the doctrinally interesting claim, because it is the one modern pharmacology most directly corroborates: the rhizome is rich in **diosgenin**, a steroidal sapogenin, and yields isolated antidiabetic principles. The "heating, obstruction-clearing" signature and the measured glucose- and lipid-lowering action point the same way — toward a metabolic mover.

### Active Components

- **Diosgenin** — the signature steroidal sapogenin of the rhizome; historically an important starting material for steroid semi-synthesis and the focus of much of the plant's pharmacology.
- **Eremanthin and related sesquiterpene lactones** — isolated antidiabetic/antihyperlipidaemic constituents.
- **Raffinose (oligosaccharide)** — isolated from the rhizome with insulin-sensitising and anti-adipogenic activity in cell models.
- **Other steroidal saponins, triterpenes and phenolics** — contributing antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Costus root is a **Tempering-phase metabolic mover**. Its role is to *disperse and regulate* — to open digestive and metabolic stagnation, temper the glucose–lipid axis, and press the system toward balance under load. It carries the traditional hot-pungent-deobstruent action (digestion, worms, fevers, fluid stagnation) together with the measured *C. speciosus* pharmacology: glucose-lowering, lipid-lowering, insulin-sensitising. In the Kenetica arc it is the heating, activating rhizome used when the metabolism needs to be worked and re-regulated rather than simply nourished (Foundation) or bound (Purification).

### Scientific Correlation

Based on articles retrieved from PubMed (all on *Costus speciosus* specifically):

- **Eliza et al. (2009), *Chemico-Biological Interactions*** — Eremanthin isolated from *C. speciosus*, given to streptozotocin-diabetic rats (5–20 mg/kg for 60 days), dose-dependently lowered plasma glucose and, at 20 mg/kg, reduced HbA1c, total cholesterol, triglyceride and LDL while raising insulin, tissue glycogen and HDL. DOI: [10.1016/j.cbi.2009.08.012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cbi.2009.08.012)
- **Bavarva & Narasimhacharya (2008), *Phytotherapy Research*** — Ethanol extract of *C. speciosus* root (300 and 450 mg/kg for 4 weeks) in alloxan-diabetic rats reduced blood glucose (~27–35%), corrected lipid profile and improved hepatic antioxidant enzyme activity, with the high dose outperforming glibenclamide on some antioxidant measures. DOI: [10.1002/ptr.2302](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.2302)
- **Muthukumaran et al. (2018), *Chemico-Biological Interactions*** — Raffinose from the *C. speciosus* rhizome acted as a partial PPARγ agonist, suppressed lipogenic SREBP1c/ACC/FAS, enhanced adiponectin, promoted GLUT4 translocation via IRβ/PI3K/Akt and inhibited GSK3β — a coherent insulin-sensitising, anti-adipogenic mechanism in hepatocyte, adipocyte and myocyte models. DOI: [10.1016/j.cbi.2018.02.011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cbi.2018.02.011)

**Evidence grade: Moderate (preclinical).** Multiple independent rodent and cell-model studies converge on antidiabetic, antihyperlipidaemic and insulin-sensitising activity for *C. speciosus*, with plausible isolated actives (eremanthin, raffinose) and mechanisms. Human clinical data are lacking. Historical Greco-Egyptian "costus" claims apply mainly to *Saussurea costus*, not this species — do not transfer them uncritically.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Ayurvedic (for *C. speciosus*, *kebuka*):** *rasa* — *tikta–katu* (bitter–pungent); *virya* — *ushna* (heating); *vipaka* — *katu* (pungent); traditionally *kapha–vata* reducing and used in *prameha*. (Presented as the Ayurvedic reading of this species; classical texts vary.)
- **Galenic / Unani (for "costus"/*qust*):** hot and dry (2nd–3rd degree), deobstruent, carminative, emmenagogue, anthelmintic — a profile historically built on *Saussurea costus*.
- **TCM:** "Mu Xiang" (warming, qi-regulating) is classically *Saussurea/Aucklandia*, **not** *C. speciosus* — noted for honesty.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy:** traditional emmenagogue/deobstruent "costus" roots are cautioned against in pregnancy; avoid *C. speciosus* medicinally in pregnancy absent safety data [USER: verify].
- **Hypoglycaemia with antidiabetic drugs:** given demonstrated glucose-lowering activity, monitor and adjust when combined with insulin or oral hypoglycaemics [USER: verify with prescriber].
- **Species misidentification hazard:** because "costus" names several unrelated plants (notably *Saussurea costus*, a CITES-regulated species with its own safety and legal profile), verify botanical identity of any purchased "costus root" before use.
- **Steroidal saponin load:** high-dose or prolonged use of diosgenin-rich material is not well characterised for human safety; treat as a therapeutic agent, not a casual supplement.
- Reliable human toxicity data are limited; the moderate evidence base is preclinical.
