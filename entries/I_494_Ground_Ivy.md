# I-494 — Ground Ivy
## *Glechoma hederacea* L.

**Family:** Lamiaceae | **Volume:** I — Plant-Based | **Entry:** I-494

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted Name:** *Glechoma hederacea* L. (1753)
**Common Names:** Ground ivy, alehoof, cat's foot, field balm, creeping Charlie, gill-over-the-ground, tunhoof, hedgemaids, catsfoot, robin-run-in-the-hedge
**Synonyms:** *Nepeta glechoma* Benth.; *Nepeta hederacea* (L.) Trevisan; *Glechoma hederacea* var. *micrantha* Moricand

**Etymology:** *Glechoma* from Greek *glēchōn* (a species of pennyroyal-like mint; Dioscorides' term for aromatic Lamiaceae); *hederacea* from Latin *hedera* (ivy) referring to the ivy-like creeping habit and rounded, lobed leaves; "alehoof" or "ale-hoof" from Old English *ealhūfe* (ale + something added to ale) — one of the most important pre-hop beer bittering and clarifying agents in medieval brewing; "creeping Charlie" from the plant's vigorous, low-growing spreading habit

**Botanical Description:** Creeping perennial, 5–25 cm, mat-forming; stems square (Lamiaceae), rooting at nodes, spreading rapidly; leaves kidney-shaped to broadly ovate, 1–3 cm, deeply crenate, petiolate, bright green, glandular-aromatic when crushed; flowers pale violet to blue-lilac, bilabiate, in whorls of 2–4 in leaf axils; bloom March–June; distinctive kidney-shaped leaves + low-creeping habit make this one of the most recognizable Lamiaceae in temperate landscapes

**Distribution:** Native to Europe, western Asia; naturalized throughout North America, Australia; extremely common in gardens, lawns, hedgerows, moist shaded ground; considered an invasive weed in some North American jurisdictions; one of the first spring flowers in temperate gardens

**Plant Part Used:** Aerial parts (leaves, stems, flowering tops); fresh plant preferred; dried herb acceptable

**Adulterants/Substitutions:** Essentially none; the kidney-shaped leaves, creeping habit, and distinctive minty-camphoraceous aroma are not shared by common Lamiaceae; occasional confusion with *Lamium* spp. (dead-nettles) in pre-flowering state

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

### Two Independent Pharmacological Traditions

Ground ivy presents an unusual convergence pattern: significant traditional use in European herbalism (despite relatively modest formal documentation in the canonical texts) and a distinct, well-developed Chinese pharmacological tradition as 连钱草 (*liánqiáncǎo*, "coin grass") — named for the circular leaf shape resembling ancient Chinese coins. The Chinese application focuses almost entirely on urinary stone dissolution, kidney conditions, and jaundice — a completely different therapeutic cluster from the European respiratory/expectorant and brewing tradition. This divergence of therapeutic focus across traditions that independently encountered the same plant is pharmacologically informative: it suggests that *G. hederacea* may have distinguishable biological activities in different preparation and dosage contexts.

### The Pre-Hop Brewing Tradition

Before hops (*Humulus lupulus*) became the universal bittering and preservative agent in European beer production (standardized by law in Germany as late as 1516 via the *Reinheitsgebot*), ground ivy was one of the most important brewing additives in medieval England, Germany, and the Low Countries. The term *gruit* (Old High German) refers to the mixture of herbs used to bitter and preserve ale before hops, and ground ivy was consistently included in gruit preparations alongside sweet gale (*Myrica gale*), yarrow (*Achillea millefolium* I-468), and wild rosemary (*Ledum palustre*).

The pharmacological rationale for ground ivy's brewing role is clear: its combination of antimicrobial volatile oil (thymol, carvacrol trace amounts), bitter-tonic terpenoids, and rosmarinic acid antibacterial activity both bittered the ale and reduced bacterial contamination during fermentation — a genuine dual pharmaceutical-technological application.

### The *Liánqiáncǎo* Urological Application

The TCM application of *liánqiáncǎo* for urinary stones (urolithiasis) and jaundice has received limited but suggestive modern pharmacological attention. The plant's phenolic acids (rosmarinic acid, caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid) demonstrate calcium oxalate crystal growth inhibition in vitro — a mechanism directly relevant to kidney stone prevention and dissolution. This would represent one of the more pharmacologically prescient TCM applications across the Lamiaceae entries in the Codex: the identification of a wild creeping herb as a kidney stone remedy based on empirical observation in a pre-imaging era is remarkable.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

### Essential Oil (Primary Antimicrobial/Aromatic Fraction)
**Pulegone:** in some chemotypes, significant pulegone content (shared with pennyroyal I-489 but at lower concentrations); safety concern at high EO doses; herb-tea concentrations well below threshold

**Menthone:** variable; minty character contributor

**α-Pinene, β-pinene:** monoterpene hydrocarbons; antimicrobial; fresh aromatic note

**Camphene:** bicyclic monoterpene; minor; camphoraceous note

**Linalool:** terpene alcohol; mild sedative; shared with lavender (I-483), marjoram (I-488)

**Thymol, carvacrol (trace):** phenolic monoterpenes; antimicrobial; at concentrations far below thyme/oregano but contributing to the historic brewing antimicrobial role

**β-Caryophyllene:** sesquiterpene; CB2 partial agonist; anti-inflammatory; recurring across Lamiaceae

### Phenolic Fraction (Dominant)
**Rosmarinic acid:** dominant phenolic; primary anti-inflammatory and antiviral agent; GABA-T inhibitor; the 18th consecutive Lamiaceae entry to feature this compound as a major phenolic; in *G. hederacea*, rosmarinic acid is pharmacologically central

**Caffeic acid:** hydroxycinnamic acid; antioxidant; antibacterial; kidney stone crystal inhibition (calcium oxalate growth inhibitor in vitro)

**Chlorogenic acid:** antioxidant; glycemic modulation

**Luteolin, apigenin:** flavone anti-inflammatory/GABA-A modulatory; standard Lamiaceae profile

**Quercetin, hyperoside:** flavonol antioxidants; capillary-protective; anti-inflammatory

**Cynaroside (luteolin-7-glucoside):** luteolin glycoside; anti-inflammatory; also found in artichoke — shared metabolite suggesting conserved phenylpropanoid pathway

### Triterpenes
**Ursolic acid:** anti-inflammatory triterpene; recurring Lamiaceae compound

**Oleanolic acid:** related triterpene; hepatoprotective; relevant to TCM jaundice application

### Tannins
**Condensed tannins:** 2–4% dry weight; astringent; anti-inflammatory; relevant to wound healing and mucosal applications

### Minerals
Significant iron content (historically used as a "tonic" herb for iron-deficiency anemia — traditional rationale for iron supplementation before pharmaceutical iron therapy); also potassium, calcium in nutritionally relevant quantities (plant consumed as food green)

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Expectorant/Respiratory Mechanism

**Volatile oil + rosmarinic acid → respiratory mucosa stimulation:**
The combined EO and phenolic fraction stimulates bronchial secretion through mild mucosal irritation and vagal reflex (similar to horehound I-493 but weaker; lacking marrubiin's potent reflex expectorant action); appropriate for mild upper respiratory congestion and sinusitis rather than productive bronchitis

**Anti-inflammatory (rosmarinic acid → COX/LOX):** reduces mucosal inflammation accompanying respiratory catarrh; synergistic with expectorant effect — reduces inflammation while facilitating mucus clearance

### 5.2 Urolithiasis Prevention Mechanism

**Caffeic acid + rosmarinic acid → calcium oxalate crystal growth inhibition:**
Both hydroxycinnamic acids demonstrate inhibition of calcium oxalate monohydrate crystal growth in vitro — the primary crystal form in the most common kidney stone type (calcium oxalate, ~75% of urolithiasis cases); mechanism involves competitive binding of crystal surface growth sites; relevant to traditional *liánqiáncǎo* use for kidney stone dissolution; in vitro evidence not yet translated to clinical trials

**Diuretic effect:** ground ivy has mild diuretic activity (mechanism not fully characterized; likely related to phenolic organic acid load); increased urine flow dilutes stone-forming mineral concentrations and flushes small calculi — complementary mechanism to crystal growth inhibition

### 5.3 Hepatoprotective / Choleretic Mechanism

**Oleanolic acid + rosmarinic acid → liver protective + bile flow stimulation:**
Oleanolic acid demonstrates hepatoprotective activity in animal models (carbon tetrachloride-induced toxicity model); rosmarinic acid anti-inflammatory activity reduces hepatic inflammatory burden; mild choleretic effect (increased bile flow) relevant to TCM jaundice application — jaundice in the pre-imaging TCM context included obstructive and hepatitic presentations where bile flow enhancement and hepatic anti-inflammatory activity are both relevant

### 5.4 Anti-inflammatory / Antioxidant (Wound Healing)

**Rosmarinic acid → COX/LOX dual inhibition:** primary anti-inflammatory mechanism; relevant to topical wound applications

**Tannins → astringent hemostasis + tissue toning:** complementary wound-healing mechanism

**Ursolic acid → fibroblast stimulation + collagen synthesis:** anti-proliferative in tumors but pro-healing in normal wound response context

### 5.5 Pre-Hop Brewing Mechanism (Historical Pharmacological Interest)

**Thymol + carvacrol (trace) + rosmarinic acid → antimicrobial:**
The combined antimicrobial activity of ground ivy's minor phenolic monoterpene fraction and rosmarinic acid provides measurable inhibition of common beer-spoilage bacteria (*Lactobacillus* spp., *Pediococcus* spp.) and fungi; at the concentrations achieved in gruit preparations, ground ivy contributed genuine preservation alongside flavor; the pre-hop brewing application represents one of the earliest documented uses of a plant's antimicrobial activity in food technology

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### 6.1 Respiratory Conditions

**Evidence Level: C (traditional evidence)**

- Commission E: NOT monographed for *G. hederacea* specifically
- ESCOP: NOT monographed
- British Herbal Pharmacopoeia 1983: Included for sinusitis and upper respiratory catarrh; Level C evidence
- Traditional use documented in multiple European pharmacopoeias; no RCTs

### 6.2 Urolithiasis (TCM Application)

**Evidence Level: B-C (mechanistic in vitro + TCM traditional)**

- Chinese Pharmacopoeia: *Liánqiáncǎo* (*G. hederacea* or closely related species) official monograph for urinary stone conditions
- In vitro calcium oxalate crystal growth inhibition: Grases et al. 1994 (*Phytotherapy Research* 8(8):457-459): caffeic acid and related hydroxycinnamic acids inhibit calcium oxalate crystallization at physiologically relevant concentrations
- No human RCTs for urolithiasis prevention; mechanistic evidence supports the traditional application but clinical validation is incomplete

### 6.3 Sinusitis / Tinnitus (Folk Application)

**Evidence Level: D (anecdotal/folk only)**

Ground ivy was specifically recommended in 19th-century English folk medicine for "painter's colic" (lead poisoning — now explained by the high tannin content binding to lead) and tinnitus. The tinnitus application has no modern pharmacological support. The lead-binding application is historically interesting: tannins do chelate heavy metals, and lead poisoning was common among painters using lead-based pigments; the astringent tannin application may have provided some symptomatic benefit at the GI level.

### 6.4 Regulatory Status

- **Commission E (Germany):** NOT monographed
- **ESCOP:** NOT monographed
- **EMA:** No herbal monograph
- **British Herbal Pharmacopoeia 1983:** Included; traditional herbal remedy status
- **TCM (Chinese Pharmacopoeia):** 连钱草 official monograph; urinary stones, jaundice, liver conditions
- **Food status:** Young leaves consumed as food green in Europe and Asia; salads, soups; GRAS equivalent in food contexts

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

### 7.1 Alehoof and the Pre-Hop Brewing Revolution

The transition from gruit (herb-bittered ale) to hopped beer in medieval and early modern Europe represents one of the most significant shifts in both pharmaceutical history and social history. Gruit herbs — including ground ivy — were typically controlled by ecclesiastical authorities who collected *gruit rights* (the legal monopoly on supplying bittering herbs to brewers), generating substantial income for the church. The adoption of hops, which brewers could grow themselves without ecclesiastical supply, represented a simultaneous economic and pharmacological revolution: hops provided superior bittering (isohumulones), better preservation (humulone antimicrobial), and a distinct soporific effect (lupulin sedative) absent in most gruit herbs.

Ground ivy's displacement as a primary brewing herb by hops is thus not purely a pharmacological event but an economic one: hops freed brewing from ecclesiastical gruit taxation. The pharmacological properties of ground ivy — antimicrobial, bittering, aromatic — were entirely adequate for their brewing role. Politics, not pharmacology, ended ground ivy's 1,000-year run as England's primary ale bittering herb.

### 7.2 "Coin Grass" and Morphological Pharmacognosy

The Chinese name 连钱草 (*liánqiáncǎo*, literally "connecting coin grass") reflects the kidney-shaped leaves resembling ancient Chinese cash coins strung on a cord — a morphological description that also encodes the plant's branching, coin-leaf structure. The name is simultaneously botanical description, aesthetic observation, and pharmacological signifier: in classical Chinese medical epistemology, a plant with kidney-shaped leaves (*shèn xíng*) was considered to have affinity for the kidney organ system (*shèn*). The TCM application of *liánqiáncǎo* for kidney stones and urinary conditions may therefore have been partly guided by morphological signature (kidney-shaped leaf → kidney medicine) that happened to coincide with genuine pharmacological activity (caffeic acid → oxalate crystal inhibition). The doctrine of signatures arrived at a correct therapeutic application for an incorrect reason — an instructive case in the relationship between symbolic reasoning and empirical pharmacology.

### 7.3 The First Spring Herb

In temperate Europe and North America, ground ivy is among the first plants to flower in spring (March–April), providing bright violet-blue flowers in gardens and lawns when most other plants remain dormant. This early availability historically made it the first "tonic herb" of the spring season — consumed as a spring green, a spring beer bittering herb, and a spring respiratory cleanser after the closed-quarters winter months. The seasonal role of ground ivy as a spring detoxification and respiratory tonic herb reflects both the genuine pharmacological activity (rosmarinic acid anti-inflammatory, mild diuretic, respiratory expectorant) and the cyclical nature of traditional medicine synchronized with plant availability.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Indications (Evidence-Based, Within Safety Parameters)

**Upper Respiratory Catarrh / Sinusitis:**
Herb tea; traditional use; Level C evidence; appropriate for mild upper respiratory congestion, sinusitis, post-nasal drip; less potent than horehound (I-493) for productive bronchitis; appropriate for upper respiratory rather than lower respiratory focus

**Urinary Stone Prevention (Adjunctive):**
Herb tea (aqueous extraction for phenolic acid fraction); Level B mechanistic evidence; TCM traditional Level B-C; appropriate as adjunctive to medical management of calcium oxalate urolithiasis; not primary treatment; ensure adequate hydration alongside

**Bitter Digestive Tonic:**
Herb tea before meals; mild bitter-tonic activity; Level C; appropriate for mild digestive atony; fresh plant decoction superior to dried

**Spring Tonic / Nutritional Green:**
Fresh leaves in salad (food use); nutritional iron, minerals; Level C; traditional spring health tonic; safe as food green

### 8.2 Preparations and Dosing

**Herb Tea:**
- 2–4 g fresh herb (or 1–2 g dried) in 250 mL boiling water; steep 10 minutes; 3 cups daily
- Fresh plant preparation preferred where available; fresh plant has higher volatile oil content
- Aqueous extraction preferred over alcohol for phenolic acid retention

**Tincture:**
- 1:5 in 25% ethanol; 1–3 mL three times daily

**Fresh Plant:**
- Young leaves as salad green; 10–30 g fresh daily (food quantities); safe; nutritional and mild medicinal benefit
- Fresh juice: 30–60 mL daily; traditional spring tonic preparation

**Topical:**
- Fresh herb poultice for wounds and skin conditions; standard application
- Strong tea wash for eye conditions (traditionally used; prepare fresh, strain carefully, observe sterility principles — same precautions as eyebright I-474)

### 8.3 Contraindications and Safety

**CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- **Pregnancy:** Traditional emmenagogue/abortifacient mentions in some texts; EO contains pulegone in some chemotypes; avoid during pregnancy
- **Epilepsy:** Pulegone-containing chemotypes; same concern as pennyroyal (I-489) though at lower concentrations; caution

**SAFETY:**
- Generally well-tolerated at herb-tea doses; centuries of food use without toxicity
- EO: topical use only; avoid internal EO use (pulegone content variable by chemotype); GC-MS chemotype determination recommended before EO internal use
- Large quantity intake of fresh plant: possible mild GI irritation from EO volatile components

**DRUG INTERACTIONS:**
- Diuretics: mild additive diuretic effect; monitor electrolytes if using diuretic medications
- Minimal significant pharmacokinetic interactions documented

### 8.4 Energetics

**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi
**Thermal Quality:** Cool-moist (Galenic); suitable for hot-dry presentations
**Directional Action:** Downward-bearing (diuretic, lithiasis-clearing); opening (respiratory, sinuses)
**TCM Classification:** Bitter, pungent; slightly cold; liver, kidney, bladder channels; clears heat, dissolves stones, removes dampness

### 8.5 Quality and Authentication

**Botanical Authentication:** Morphologically distinctive; kidney-shaped crenate leaves on creeping square-stemmed plant; minty-camphoraceous aroma when crushed; violet bilabiate flowers in leaf axils; no significant adulteration risk given abundance and low commercial value

**Seasonal Collection:** Best harvested spring (March–June) when in flower for maximum rosmarinic acid and EO content; summer collection acceptable; avoid heavily trafficked or sprayed lawns; wash thoroughly if collected near roadsides

**EO Chemotype Note:** Commercial *G. hederacea* EO varies significantly in pulegone content by geographic origin; GC-MS analysis recommended for any internal EO application; herb-tea use avoids this concern as EO concentrations in tea are far below threshold
