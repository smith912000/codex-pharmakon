### Botanical Identity
Large canopy tree 6–30m; spectacular purple-pink trumpet-shaped flowers appearing before leaves (makes identification easy even from a distance); extremely hard wood (specific gravity ~1.0 — it sinks in water); native to tropical and subtropical South America (Brazil, Argentina, Paraguay, Bolivia, Peru). The inner bark (between the outer rough bark and the heartwood) is the drug material — the white-to-cream soft inner bark. Commercial products use inner bark only; outer bark contains less active material. Wild harvesting has depleted stands; sustainable harvesting takes strips without girdling the tree.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 South American Indigenous Traditions (SA)

**Multiple nations**: Pau d'Arco's traditional use spans multiple South American indigenous cultures:

**Inca**: Historical records (and Inca oral tradition preserved by Spanish chroniclers) describe lapacho bark tea for chronic infections, cancer-type presentations ("hard growths inside"), and fever. The Inca were reported to use it for scurvy-type conditions — vitamin C content provides nutritional basis.

**Guaraní**: *Tajy* bark decoction for infections, skin diseases, and inflammatory conditions. Guaraní traditional use is particularly well-documented — the herb is embedded in living Guaraní medical tradition.

**Tupí (Brazil)**: *Ipê roxo* decoction for malaria, skin fungal infections, and arthritis. The *Tupí* name *ipê* became the commercial trade name for the genus.

**Widespread tradition across SA**: Anti-infective (bacterial, fungal, parasitic), anti-cancer, anti-inflammatory — consistently recorded across Brazilian, Paraguayan, Argentinian, and Peruvian indigenous traditions.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Anti-Fungal (Primary — Most Clinically Relevant)

**Lapachol + β-lapachone**: Inhibit fungal electron transport chain → disrupts ATP production → fungicidal. Active against *Candida albicans*, *Candida tropicalis*, *Aspergillus fumigatus*, *Cryptococcus neoformans* at concentrations achievable in vivo. Does not induce resistance in the same patterns as azole antifungals — mechanism distinct from fluconazole class.

### 5.2 Anti-Tumour (β-Lapachone — Most Studied)

**β-Lapachone**: Substrate for NQO1 (NAD(P)H:quinone oxidoreductase 1) — an enzyme overexpressed in many tumour cells (pancreatic, prostate, breast, lung cancer). NQO1 converts β-lapachone to a futile redox cycling intermediate → generates massive ROS burst specifically in NQO1-overexpressing cancer cells → selective tumour cell death.

NQO1-positive tumours: pancreatic (80–90%), prostate (90%), lung, breast, colorectal. NQO1-negative normal cells are not affected — selectivity is the basis for ongoing clinical interest. Multiple preclinical models confirm tumour regression. Human clinical trials ongoing.

### 5.3 Anti-Malarial / Anti-Parasitic

**Lapachol**: Active against *Plasmodium falciparum* (IC50 ~2–5µM). Inhibits mitochondrial electron transport in parasites. Active against *Leishmania*, *Trypanosoma* in vitro. The traditional malaria application has mechanistic support.

### 5.4 Anti-Viral

**β-Lapachone**: Inhibits HIV-1 reverse transcriptase; anti-HSV-1; anti-influenza activity in vitro. The traditional broad anti-infective indication extends to viral pathogens.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Anti-fungal (clinical evidence)**: Multiple clinical series (primarily Brazilian and Argentinian) documenting pau d'Arco bark tea efficacy in candidiasis — particularly chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis and vaginal candidiasis. Formal RCT data absent from Western literature; extensive clinical tradition.

**Anti-cancer (preclinical only)**: Multiple in vitro and animal studies confirm β-lapachone anti-tumour activity. Phase I clinical trials of β-lapachone derivatives (ARQ 761) ongoing for NQO1+ solid tumours (pancreatic, breast). The native bark extract is not at clinical trial stage.

**Lapachol — historical warning**: Brazil's National Cancer Institute conducted a human trial of lapachol as a cancer drug in the 1970s (capitalising on traditional use). Lapachol failed to reach anti-cancer plasma concentrations without dose-limiting toxicity (anticoagulation, GI effects). This does not invalidate β-lapachone (the more potent derivative) or the whole bark preparation — it indicates lapachol as isolated agent at anti-cancer doses is toxic.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The hardest wood, the anti-cancer bark**: Pau d'Arco is used for bows because it is among the hardest woods in the world — specific gravity 1.0 means it barely floats. The people who selected this tree for bow-making observed its inner bark's medicinal properties: a soft, yielding inner bark from the hardest of trees. The naming convention encodes this observation: *pau d'arco* (bow wood) names the tree for its physical strength, while the medicine comes from the soft inner layer — another "hard wood, soft medicine" inversion pattern (cf. hawthorn, I_53).

**The NQO1 cancer selectivity**: β-Lapachone's NQO1-dependent cancer cell selectivity is among the most elegant examples of plant secondary metabolites accidentally targeting a cancer-specific metabolic vulnerability. NQO1 is overexpressed in cancer as an adaptive response to oxidative stress — cancer cells upregulate NQO1 to protect themselves. β-Lapachone exploits this protection mechanism, converting it into a death signal. The plant compound and the cancer cell's own defence mechanism conspire toward the cancer cell's destruction. Traditional cancer applications, empirically observed over 2,000 years, point toward a mechanism that pharmacology is still working to exploit clinically.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Pregnancy**: Potential embryotoxic effects (lapachol — animal data) — absolute contraindication
- **Anticoagulants**: Lapachol has anticoagulant properties — significant interaction with warfarin, aspirin; monitor INR
- **Anaemia**: High-dose lapachol may cause haemolysis — avoid in haemolytic anaemia
- **Immunosuppressives**: Immunostimulatory effect — caution with transplant medications
- **Long-term high-dose**: Lapachol accumulates; hepatotoxic at excessive doses
- Short-term tea preparations at traditional doses are generally well tolerated
