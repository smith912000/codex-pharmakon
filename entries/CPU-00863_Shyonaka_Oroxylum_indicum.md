# Shyonaka / Sonapatha / Indian Trumpet Tree / Midnight Horror

## *Oroxylum indicum* (L.) Kurz | Family: Bignoniaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Bitter / Astringent — Cooling then Kindling (Tikta-Kashaya; Ushna vipaka)
**Training Phase:** Working Materia — Anti-Inflammatory Root Bark

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Oroxylum indicum* is a small-to-medium deciduous tree of the monsoon forests of India, Nepal, Sri Lanka, southern China and Southeast Asia. Two features make it unforgettable. First, its enormous flat, sword-shaped seed-pods — up to a metre long — hang down from the bare branches at night like scabbards or blades, which earns it the English trade-name **"midnight horror"** and the Sanskrit sense of a tree standing sentinel in the dark. Second, its papery, winged seeds, which spiral to the ground like small moons — the source of the name *Sonapatha*, "golden leaf/wing."

But the tree's real standing rests underground, in its **root bark**.

**Ayurveda (recorded as SS — Sanskrit/Śāstric source-stream).** Shyonaka is one of the **Dashamula** — the "ten roots," the ten master root-drugs that form the backbone of classical Ayurvedic anti-inflammatory and *vata*-pacifying pharmacy. Dashamula splits into five great roots (*brihat panchamula*, the trees) and five small (*laghu panchamula*); Shyonaka is counted among the great five. The root-bark (and stem-bark) is classed as **tikta-kashaya** (bitter-astringent), and is deployed for inflammatory and rheumatic-arthritic conditions, respiratory disorders (cough, bronchial complaints), and digestive disturbance — including diarrhoea, dysentery, and loss of appetite. It is a constituent of major classical polyherbal formulations, including the Dashamula decoction itself and the celebrated tonic *Chyawanprash*. The classical indication set — jaundice, arthritic/rheumatic problems, gastric ulcers, respiratory disease, diabetes, diarrhoea and dysentery — is well documented in the ethnomedical literature (Dinda et al., 2014).

**Chinese and Southeast Asian folk practice (recorded as CM).** The same tree, and the same *baicalein-bearing* chemistry, is used across its range for inflammatory, respiratory and digestive complaints; the seeds and bark appear in local materia medica of Vietnam, Thailand, Laos and southern China. It is worth naming the chemotaxonomic link: baicalein and oroxylin A, the signature flavonoids of Shyonaka, are the *same* compounds that make *Scutellaria baicalensis* (Huang Qin) a pillar of the Chinese anti-inflammatory pharmacopoeia. The two plants converge on the same chemistry from different families. **[USER: verify]** the precise CM classical attestation if a specific Chinese-canon citation is needed — the strong, citable overlap is chemical and Southeast-Asian-ethnomedical rather than a named entry in the Han classics.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The doctrine here is *the cooling of heat and the opening of obstructed passages* — the bitter root that draws inflammation down and out. Symbolically the tree is a study in contrasts the practitioner is meant to hold together: the fearsome nocturnal blades above, the quiet healing bitterness below. The "horror" is theatre; the medicine is in the root. Mapped onto real chemistry, the doctrine is unusually well-founded. "Clearing heat" is, in modern terms, **suppression of the NF-κB / COX / nitric-oxide inflammatory cascade** — and this is exactly where Shyonaka's flavonoids act. The bitter-astringent taste is not a mystical signature but a fair sensory proxy for a resin of polyphenols. This is a case where the traditional gesture and the molecular pharmacology genuinely rhyme, and Kenetica grades it accordingly — while refusing to overstate a preclinical-heavy evidence base as if it were clinical proof.

### Active Components

Phytochemical work has identified roughly 111 compounds across the tree, dominated by **flavonoids**, with naphthalenoids and cyclohexylethanoids as secondary groups (Dinda et al., 2014). The pharmacologically load-bearing constituents, concentrated in the stem-bark, root-bark and seeds, are:

- **Baicalein** — the flagship flavone; a serious research compound in its own right, a 12/15-lipoxygenase inhibitor and broad anti-inflammatory/antioxidant agent.
- **Baicalin** — the 7-O-glucuronide of baicalein.
- **Chrysin** — 5,7-dihydroxyflavone; anti-inflammatory and anxiolytic-investigated.
- **Oroxylin A** — 6-methoxy-baicalein; now the most clinically advanced of the group (see below).
- **Oroxin A and B**, scutellarein/scutellarin derivatives, and **phenylethanoid glycosides** (acteoside/verbascoside, leucosceptoside A, darendoside A) reported from stem bark (Thi Vien et al., 2020).

Multiple systematic reviews confirm baicalein, baicalin, oroxylin A and chrysin as the consistent marker flavonoids across the plant (Nguyen et al., 2025; Rajendra et al., 2026).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica, Shyonaka root-bark is a **Working-Materia anti-inflammatory** — a genuine, chemistry-backed cooling bitter, used to map the traditional "clears heat, calms the joints and airways, settles the gut" gesture onto a real molecular mechanism.

Primary use-cases:

1. **Inflammatory and arthritic-type heat.** The flavonoid fraction demonstrably suppresses pro-inflammatory signalling — nitric-oxide production in activated microglia and macrophage-type models is inhibited by the plant's phenylethanoid glycosides (Thi Vien et al., 2020). This is the modern correlate of the Dashamula anti-*vata*/anti-inflammatory indication.
2. **Respiratory and digestive support.** Recorded traditionally for cough, bronchial complaint, and for diarrhoea/dysentery and appetite; the bitter-astringent action is consistent with both, though direct clinical respiratory data are lacking (see grading).
3. **Antioxidant / neuroprotective adjunct.** Extracts protect neuronal-type cells against oxidative and amyloid-β injury (Mairuae et al., 2019) and reduce chemotherapy-induced cognitive impairment in mice (Pondugula et al., 2021); a standardised extract (Sabroxy®) has been studied for cognition.

Kenetica treats this as a **near-drug** rather than a folk curiosity: oroxylin A has advanced to **Phase Ib/IIa clinical trials** (as a synthesised first-in-class tablet for hepatocellular carcinoma), which is as strong a validation as any constituent in this Volume carries (Luo et al., 2025). The honest counterweight — see below — is that this is *constituent* and *preclinical* strength, not proof of the whole-bark decoction in humans.

### Preparation & Dosing

Root-bark and stem-bark are the working parts. **Seeds and bark are the concentrated flavonoid sources — treat with the caution below.**

| Preparation | Part | Typical traditional handling | Notes / dose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decoction (kwath) | Dried root-bark or stem-bark | Simmer coarse bark in water, reduce | Classical Dashamula-style; **[USER: verify]** exact gram dose — sources vary (commonly cited ~3–6 g dried bark/day as part of a formula) |
| Powder (churna) | Dried bark | Combined in polyherbal churna | Rarely used single; usually in formula |
| Standardised extract | Bark (baicalein/oroxylin A standardised) | Commercial (e.g. Sabroxy®) | Follow product labelling; do not extrapolate to raw bark |
| Young fruit / pod | Immature pod | Locally eaten as a bitter vegetable | Culinary, not the medicinal dose |

Bitterness is intense — a marker of the active polyphenols, and a practical limit on dose.

### Scientific Correlation

Graded honestly, nulls and weaknesses included:

- **Anti-inflammatory activity:** **Preclinical, well-supported.** Phenylethanoid glycosides from stem bark inhibited LPS-induced NO production in BV2 microglia (IC₅₀ ≈ 57–71 µM; Thi Vien et al., 2020); flavonoids act via NF-κB inhibition and ROS modulation (Rajendra et al., 2026). No RCT of the whole bark for arthritis.
- **Oroxylin A as a drug:** **Clinical (constituent).** In Phase Ib/IIa trials for hepatocellular carcinoma; broad anti-cancer/anti-inflammatory/organ-protective pharmacology (Luo et al., 2025). *This is the isolated, synthesised compound, not the crude herb.*
- **Neuroprotective / antioxidant:** **Preclinical.** Protects SH-SY5Y cells against amyloid-β injury via Akt/CREB/Bcl-2 (Mairuae et al., 2019); prevents chemotherapy-induced cognitive impairment in mice (Pondugula et al., 2021).
- **Anticancer:** **Preclinical.** Stem-bark extract reduced 4NQO-induced oral carcinoma in rats via EGFR/PI3K/AKT (Parvin et al., 2022); in-silico dual EBV/nasopharyngeal targeting (Thrigulla et al., 2024).
- **Antidiabetic / anti-adipogenic:** **Preclinical.** Suppresses 3T3-L1 adipocyte differentiation via PPARγ2/SREBP-1c (Hengpratom et al., 2020).
- **Respiratory (cough/bronchial):** **Traditional — NOT clinically demonstrated.** Widely recorded ethnomedically; no controlled clinical respiratory trial retrieved. Grade as traditional-plausible, mechanistically consistent, unproven.
- **Overall:** **Preclinical-heavy with one constituent in early clinical trials.** Human whole-herb efficacy data remain sparse; a 2025 systematic review flags the lack of clinical trials and standardised dosing as the key gap (Nguyen et al., 2025).

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Ayurveda: **rasa** (taste) tikta-kashaya (bitter-astringent); **virya** (potency) cooling to neutral; **vipaka** (post-digestive) pungent/heating; balances *kapha* and *vata* in the classical Dashamula sense, acting as an anti-inflammatory and *deepana* (appetite-kindling) bitter. In Kenetica's cross-map this places it with the **cooling bitters that nonetheless kindle digestion** — the polyphenol-resin profile that quenches inflammatory heat topically/systemically while stimulating gut tone. The dual "cooling-then-kindling" reading is a genuine reflection of a bitter that both suppresses NF-κB and provokes bile/appetite.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy and lactation: avoid.** Safety in pregnancy is not established; the bark is a traditional emmenagogue-adjacent bitter and several constituents are bioactive at the cellular level. Flag prominently.
- **Seed and bark preparations are concentrated** — the flavonoid load (baicalein/oroxylin A) is highest here. Do not self-escalate concentrated extracts to "more is better"; toxicological safety margins are reported only for aqueous/ethanolic bark and fruit extracts at studied doses (Dinda et al., 2014), not for high-dose isolates.
- **Drug interactions plausible, understudied:** baicalein/oroxylin A modulate CYP and drug-metabolising pathways and undergo phase-II/enterohepatic recycling (Luo et al., 2025) — caution with anticoagulants, chemotherapy, and narrow-therapeutic-index drugs.
- **Endangered status:** *O. indicum* is over-harvested and listed as threatened in parts of its range — a cultivator's-guide flag: propagate and source sustainably, do not wild-strip root bark.
- Correct identification of a mature tree and its Dashamula-grade bark is required before use.
