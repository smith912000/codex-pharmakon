# Ajagara flesh — Ajagara (अजगर), "Ajagara" (Bhishagratna 1907); the translator's glossary gives "A python (rock-snake); a boa"
## *The flesh of the ajagara, a snake that Bhishagratna's notes call a python or a boa-constrictor; the Sanskrit text names no species, and no binomial is printed in the lines read* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *Ajagara* is named twice in the *Sutrasthana* chapter on food and drink, ch.46 (*Annapana-vidhi*), in Bhishagratna's English (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt`). The chapter opens by saying that food is "the chief source of their bodily strength and complexion, as well as of the albuminous (Ojas) principle in their organisms" (lines 8-12), and goes on to list the flesh of animals by group. The first mention is in the list of the *Vileshaya* group, the "hole-dwellers" (page 485, lines 574-577): "Animals such as, the Shvavit, Shalyaka, Godha, Shasha, Vrishadansha, Lopaka, Lomasha-Karna, Kadali, Mriga-Priyaka, Ajagara, Sarpa, Mushika, Nakula and Maha-Vabhru belong to the group of Vileshaya." The second is the one sentence about this animal alone (page 486, line 602): "the flesh of the Mriga-priyaka proves a wholesome diet in diseases due to the action of the deranged Vayu (Vayu-Roga), while that of the Ajagara is beneficial in piles." That is all the chapter says of it by name. The entry's list row is `_sources/sushruta.csv` line 225, "Ajagara (python)", citing "Sutrasthana ch.46 (Annapanavidhi); Bhishagratna trans. vol.1".

*Identity.* The ch.46 text prints no gloss for the name. Bhishagratna's glossary in volume 3 (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`, entry at line 30360, its meaning printed at line 30383; the OCR splits the name column from the meaning column, so the pairing is by list order) reads "A python (rock-snake); a boa." In *Kalpasthana* ch.4 (`SS_Kalpa_ch04.txt` lines 227-229) *Ajagara* is the third name in the list of the *Nirvisha* (non-venomous) snakes, and a later passage (lines 364-367) says the bite of an "Ajagara (Boa-constructor)" kills by swallowing and crushing and not by poison. So the translator reads it as a large constricting snake in both places. The Sanskrit name itself is not analysed in the sources read. [USER: verify]

*The group.* Immediately after the list the chapter gives the properties of the group (lines 579-588): "the general properties of the flesh of animals belonging to this species are to increase the consistency of stool and urine. They are heat-making in potency, sweet in digestion as those of the preceding group. They subdue the Vayu and generate the Pittam and Kapham. They are demulcent and beneficial in cough, dyspnoea and cachexia." The *Sarpa* (snake of a species "other than those specifically described") is listed in the same group and has its own sentence (lines 604-607): its flesh "is curative in piles and derangements of the Vayu. It is a vermifuge and anti-toxic (a neutraliser of chemical or resulting poisons)". That sentence is about *Sarpa*, not *Ajagara*, and is not carried over.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, the flesh of a snake is mostly water and muscle protein with some fat. A large wild snake can carry bacteria such as *Salmonella* and parasites in its flesh and gut, as reptiles generally do. Nothing links any constituent to the one action the text gives, benefit in piles.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The single sourced use is dietary: the flesh of the *ajagara* is "beneficial in piles" (ch.46, line 602), as a food, in a chapter that lists the flesh of scores of animals by their effect on the humours. The Codex does not recommend eating python or boa flesh. Large constrictors are protected by law in many countries, wild-caught snake meat is a hygiene risk, and piles (haemorrhoids) are a common condition that a clinician can assess and that can be confused with more serious bleeding.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives no preparation of this flesh and no amount. The group sentences describe the flesh as food and say nothing of how it is cooked or how often it is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Haemorrhoids are swollen vascular cushions of the anal canal, and management of them is by diet fibre, hygiene and medical or surgical treatment. I have retrieved no evidence that snake flesh helps them. Reptile meat can carry *Salmonella* and parasites such as pentastomids and tapeworm larvae. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Ayurveda (SS):** The qualities are printed for the *Vileshaya* group, not for the *ajagara* by name (ch.46, lines 579-588): increases the consistency of stool and urine; heat-making in potency (*virya*); sweet in digestion (*vipaka*), "as those of the preceding group"; subdues *Vayu* and generates *Pittam* and *Kapham*; demulcent; beneficial in cough, dyspnoea and cachexia. The *ajagara* is a listed member of that group (line 577), so these apply by group membership, which is an inference from the classification and not a sentence about the animal. The only named property of the flesh itself is "beneficial in piles" (line 602). The taste (*rasa*) of the group is not printed in the lines read, apart from "sweet in digestion".

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The ch.46 frame is food as the source of strength, complexion and *ojas* (lines 8-12), but the chapter assigns *ojas* to food in general and not to this flesh. The group sentence calls the flesh of the *Vileshaya* demulcent and heat-making, and elsewhere in the chapter other groups are called "spermatopoietic"; no such word is used of the *Vileshaya* group in the lines read, and in any case a property in a food list is not a placement in a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* chapter. A search of the whole Sushruta text on disk (`text/sutra`, `chikitsa`, `uttara`, `kalpa`, `nidana`, `sharira`) for *ajagara* found only ch.46 (lines 577, 602) and *Kalpasthana* ch.4 (lines 228, 364), the latter concerning snakes and bites. So the substance has only a dietary and therapeutic status. No *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* claim is made for it. The sattvic and rajasic food lists are *Samkhya*-era material, not Sushruta, and are not used.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The sourced use is a dietary one for piles, in a food chapter, and the Codex does not recommend taking the flesh of a protected, wild constrictor. If piles are the concern, the honest advice is to have them assessed. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** flesh of wild reptiles can carry *Salmonella* and parasites; it should not be eaten raw or undercooked, and wild-caught meat is not recommended.
- **Bleeding:** blood from the rectum should be assessed by a clinician; the text's "beneficial in piles" is a historical dietary statement and not a treatment.
- **Conservation and law:** pythons and boas are protected, regulated or listed in trade conventions in many countries; which species the name covers is not settled in the text, so check local law before any handling. [USER: verify]
- **Identity:** the Sanskrit text names no species; the translator reads python or boa.
- **Ethics:** the Codex does not recommend taking an animal for these uses.
