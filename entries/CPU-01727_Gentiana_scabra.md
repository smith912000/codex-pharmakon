# Gentiana scabra — Long Dan 龍膽, Radix Gentianae Scabrae (Shennong Bencao Jing)

## *Gentiana scabra, as the pharmaceutical Latin "Radix Gentianae Scabrae" that Yang 1998 prints for the entry Long Dan* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Shennong text (SBJ); this entry rests on one text.** No other text read for this entry names this species. The entry is in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`). The Latin name is the translator's pharmaceutical Latin and was matched to the binomial by stem in the extraction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv`, row 72: "pharmaceutical Latin 'Radix Gentianae Scabrae' matched by stem to on-disk binomial 'Gentiana scabra'"). The Chinese characters are those of the Codex's own list of Shennong names (`_sources/shennong.csv`, line 50: "Gentiana Root (龍膽 Lóng Dǎn)"), not of Yang's English text. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk, and of the two Tibetan texts (Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011), for the word *scabra* found only Yang's entry and the extraction row. A search for the genus name *Gentiana* found Dioscorides 3-3 GENTIANE (line 6541, translator's gloss *Gentiana lutea*, Yellow Gentian), the Shennong text's own Qin Jiao (Radix Gentianae Macrophyllae, line 2494), and the Tibetan gentians (Clark 1995, ch.20). These are other species and are not attributed to this row. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *Long Dan, PDF pages 50 and 51.* The page marker `[[p50]]` is at line 1928, and the heading "Herbs: Superior Class" is printed at line 1929, at the top of that page. The first entry under it is Mu Xiang (line 1930 onward); Long Dan is the second, at line 1936. It runs on to `[[p51]]` (line 1969), where the running head is printed at line 1970 and no class heading. The text reads, with the page break between "light," and "and slow aging":

"Long Dan (Radix Gentianae Scabrae)112 is bitter and astringent. It mainly treats cold and heat in the bones, fright epilepsy, and evil qi. It mends expiry and damage, settles the five viscera, and kills gu toxins. Protracted taking may sharpen the wits, improve the memory, make the body light, and slow aging. Its other name is Ling You (Mound Amblin~). It grows in mountains and valleys."

(Lines 1936 to 1939 and 1971 to 1974. "Amblin~" is the OCR's form of the English gloss of Ling You and is quoted as printed.) The superscript 112 is Yang's footnote number. The footnote (line 1959 onward) is the translator's commentary and not the classic's text. It begins "Gentiana is bitter in flavor and cold of qi. The word astringent may be a typographical error." and goes on to later clinical uses; it is not used here as the Shennong text's claim. The preface (Book One) says the superior class "mainly nourish life and correspond to heaven", that they are "nontoxic and taking them in large amounts and for a long time will not harm people", and that "if one intends to make one's body light, boost the qi, prevent aging, and prolong life, one should base [one's efforts] on the superior class" (lines 352 to 355 and 380 to 383).

**Overlap.** I_113 (Gentian, *Gentiana*) is a general entry tagged DMM, CM, SBJ, FMT and EF that names *Gentiana scabra* beside *Gentiana lutea* and draws on the same Long Dan passage among other sources. This entry is the single-text Shennong entry for the same plant and passage and is not merged with it. I_1289 (*Gentiana macrophylla*, Qin Jiao) is a sibling Shennong entry on a different gentian, from the middle class. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The entry itself prints no constituent. It is a general fact that *Gentiana* is a genus of the gentian family (Gentianaceae) and that bitter secoiridoid glycosides, of which gentiopicroside is an example, are characteristic of the family; the entry's own words are "bitter and astringent". Which part is meant, and which species a given supply contains, are matters the sources read do not settle.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Shennong entry records a bitter, astringent drug that "mainly treats cold and heat in the bones, fright epilepsy, and evil qi", "mends expiry and damage, settles the five viscera, and kills gu toxins", and whose protracted taking "may sharpen the wits, improve the memory, make the body light, and slow aging". These are the text's claims in its own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and does not translate the entry's terms into modern diagnoses.

### Preparation & Dosing

The entry gives no preparation, no part used beyond the name "Radix" in the translator's Latin, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. "Protracted taking" is the only direction on duration and it carries no number. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SBJ:** "bitter and astringent". The translator's footnote says the word astringent may be a typographical error, so the second quality is uncertain in the edition read. The entry prints no toxicity statement, no temperature and no meridian or other system of later Chinese medicine; the footnote's "cold of qi" is the translator's.

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: the superior class (上品), under the page heading, not directly below it.* The class is assigned by the printed heading "Herbs: Superior Class" (line 1929, the top of `[[p50]]`). Long Dan is the second entry on that page, so the grade rests on the page heading plus the entry's place after Mu Xiang, and not on a heading printed immediately above the entry. The continuation on `[[p51]]` carries no heading of its own. In the preface the superior class is the class of the substances taken for a long time to nourish life, and the only class the preface ties to making the body light, boosting the qi, preventing aging and prolonging life (lines 352 to 355 and 380 to 383).

The entry's own words fit that frame: "Protracted taking may sharpen the wits, improve the memory, make the body light, and slow aging." "Make the body light" is the Shennong effect category that the Codex does not translate into a pharmacological one, and it is not weight loss. The entry asserts no *jing*, *shen* or *neidan* action, and none is added here. The entry is also a bitter drug with a list of disease indications in the same paragraph, and the translator's footnote treats it later as a heat-draining medicinal; that note is a later layer and is not the Shennong text's grade.

**Practitioner's note.** A superior-class entry in the Chinese scheme, and the only class the preface ties to long-term, life-nourishing use. The entry itself is brief and gives no dose, no preparation and no duration, and the root is very bitter, so nothing here supports taking it as an ongoing practice on the strength of the grade. A cultivator can read the entry for what the classic says (a nontoxic superior-class drug whose long use "may" sharpen the wits and slow aging) and leave it there. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The entry prints no warning and no toxicity statement; the preface says the superior class is nontoxic and will not harm people even in large amounts and for a long time, a general statement about the class and not a safety finding for this root. No dose, preparation or safety information is given for it, and the identity is the translator's pharmaceutical Latin. Do not take a plant or root on the strength of this entry.
