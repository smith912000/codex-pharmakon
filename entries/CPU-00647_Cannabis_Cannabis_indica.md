### Origin & Tradition

Few plants sit at the crossroads of so many traditions as cannabis. A single, taxonomically debated species-complex (Linnaeus's *Cannabis sativa*, Lamarck's *Cannabis indica*, and the wild *ruderalis* — now often treated as varieties of one polymorphic species) has served humanity simultaneously as **fibre** (hemp), **oilseed** (the nutritious seed), **medicine** (analgesic, sedative, antispasmodic, appetite-stimulant), and **sacrament** (the visionary intoxicant). It is among the oldest cultivated plants, and it threads through the Indian, Chinese, Persian-Arabic, and European medical records with unusual continuity. Lamarck named *Cannabis indica* in 1785 specifically to distinguish the resinous, intoxicating Indian form from the fibre hemp of Europe — a distinction the traditions themselves had long drawn between the "useful hemp" and the "herb of vision."

**Ayurveda (SS):** Cannabis (*vijayā* — "the victorious," *bhaṅgā*, *gañjā*, *siddhi*) is a recognised drug of the later Ayurvedic materia medica, classified as *tikta* (bitter) and *katu* (pungent), *ushna* (heating), and — critically — as *tridoṣaghna* in correct dose but *madakārī* (intoxicating) and requiring *śodhana* (ritual purification, typically of the leaves) before medicinal use. It is prized as a powerful *deepana-pachana* (digestive kindler), *grāhī* (bowel-binding, antidiarrhoeal), *nidrājanana* (sleep-promoting), analgesic and aphrodisiac, employed for diarrhoea and dysentery, colic, insomnia, pain, and loss of appetite. Ayurvedic pharmacy treats it as a *upaviṣa* (semi-poison) — potent, effective, and to be handled with formal purification and dosing discipline.

**Siddha System:** Tamil Siddha medicine likewise incorporates cannabis (*ganja*) as a processed drug for pain, dysentery, insomnia and appetite, subject to the same logic of purification and cautious dosing that governs the Siddha handling of potent/toxic botanicals.

**Chinese Medicine (TCM):** Cannabis is one of the most anciently recorded Chinese drugs. The **seed** (*huo ma ren*, "fire hemp seed") is a canonical, gentle materia medica item — sweet, neutral and moistening, used to nourish and moisten the intestines and treat constipation in the dry, deficient or elderly; it is entirely non-intoxicating. The **flowering tops** (*ma fen*/*ma bo*) appear in the earliest Chinese pharmacological record as a more powerful, potentially "mind-altering" drug used cautiously for pain and other conditions. Hemp fibre (*ma*) is itself one of the oldest Chinese textile plants, and the character *ma* carries the double sense of hemp and, by extension, numbness — a linguistic fossil of its analgesic-narcotic action.

**Perso-Arabic / Unani (CM):** In Unani-Tibb cannabis (*qinnab*, and the intoxicant *bhang*/*ḥashīsh*) is graded warm and dry and classed among the *mukhaddirāt* (narcotics/anaesthetics). Arab and Persian physicians described the seed and leaf as analgesic, anticonvulsant, sedative, and appetite- and libido-affecting, while clearly recognising and warning of its intoxicating and habit-forming potential. Its integration into the Islamicate medical and (in the *ḥashīsh* tradition) mystical-social world is one of the richest strands of its history.

**Greco-Roman & the Dioscoridean lineage (DMM):** Cannabis (*kannabis*) is described in the classical materia medica descending from Dioscorides, who noted the seed and the plant's use — the juice of the seed for earache and to "quench" sexual function, and the plant's fibre for rope. Galen and later authors registered its heating, drying nature and its heavy, potentially headache-inducing effect when consumed. The European tradition thus received cannabis primarily as a fibre and seed plant with a recognised strong, "heady" action.

**European Physiomedical / Eclectic:** Cannabis re-entered Western clinical medicine forcefully in the nineteenth century — after W. B. O'Shaughnessy's reports from India — and became a widely used Eclectic and orthodox remedy: a tincture of "Indian hemp" prescribed as an analgesic, antispasmodic, sedative-hypnotic, and anticonvulsant for neuralgia, migraine, dysmenorrhoea, insomnia, tetanus and convulsions. It was an official drug of Western pharmacopoeias until it was withdrawn under twentieth-century prohibition — a historically abrupt exit that modern medical cannabis and cannabidiol pharmacology have partly reversed.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Victorious Herb of the Threshold:** Cannabis is, across its traditions, the plant of the *threshold* — the substance that stands at the border between medicine and intoxication, between waking and dream, between the profane and the sacred. Its Sanskrit name *vijayā*, "the victorious," and its inclusion among the sacred plants of Indian religious life mark it as a substance of ritual power, associated with ascetic practice and the loosening of ordinary perception. In the Codex's grammar this places cannabis in the **Gated Visionary** class — a substance whose lower doses sit firmly in the nervine-analgesic register (sedation, pain relief, appetite, sleep, moistening the dry) but whose higher, resinous doses cross into the psychoactive, and therefore demand the disciplines the source-traditions always attached to it: *purification, dosing precision, and a container of set and setting*. Its doctrinal duality — nourishing seed and intoxicating resin from one plant — is the sharpest single lesson of the pharmacopoeia: dose and preparation are the difference between food, medicine, and derangement. Cannabis is the plant that most insists the reader learn that lesson.

### Active Components

- **Phytocannabinoids:** Δ⁹-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) — the principal psychoactive constituent; cannabidiol (CBD) — non-intoxicating, anticonvulsant/anti-inflammatory; plus cannabigerol (CBG), cannabinol (CBN), cannabichromene (CBC), and their acidic precursors (THCA, CBDA).
- **Terpenes:** myrcene, β-caryophyllene, limonene, pinene, linalool — aromatic modulators (the proposed "entourage" contributors).
- **Flavonoids:** including cannflavins.
- **Seed constituents (non-psychoactive):** high-quality protein, and oil rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids (a favourable omega-6:omega-3 ratio); the basis of the seed's nutritive and demulcent-laxative use.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Cannabis occupies a **gated, dual-register** position in Kenetica:

1. **Nervine Sedation & Analgesia (medicinal register):** for pain, muscle spasm, insomnia and appetite loss — the classic Eclectic and Ayurvedic indications, using controlled, sub-intoxicating preparations where lawful.
2. **Visionary / Psychoactive (advanced, gated):** the resinous intoxicant register — treated as an advanced, discipline-requiring category, never a casual one, with the source-traditions' purification and set-and-setting requirements attached. **Legally and clinically gated.**
3. **Moistening Descent (seed):** the non-psychoactive seed (*huo ma ren*) as a gentle moistening laxative and nutritive for the dry, deficient or constipated — the safest and most universally lawful use of the plant.

The seed and the resin are, functionally, two different medicines from one plant.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, Hoch et al. (2024), *European Archives of Psychiatry and Clinical Neuroscience*, review the evidence on cannabis risks and medical benefits, concluding that herbal cannabis and cannabinoid medicines produce **small-to-modest benefits primarily in chronic pain, muscle spasticity, chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting, and (for cannabidiol) refractory epilepsy**, while the evidence for mental disorders and other conditions is inconclusive; they document real risks including cannabis use disorder, acute cognitive/cardiovascular effects, and (with chronic high-THC use, especially in adolescents) associations with psychosis, mood and anxiety disorders [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00406-024-01880-2). Legare, Raup-Konsavage & Vrana (2022), *Pharmacology*, similarly find that data support a role for cannabis/cannabinoids in **pain, seizure disorders, appetite stimulation, muscle spasticity, and nausea/vomiting**, while cautioning that support for CNS, oncologic and other indications is much weaker and that rigorous research on crude cannabis and CBD-oil materials remains limited [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1159/000521683).

**Evidence grade:** Good for a defined set of indications (chronic pain, spasticity, chemotherapy-induced nausea/vomiting, CBD in refractory epilepsy — the latter with regulatory approval); weak/inconclusive for most other claimed uses. Real and well-documented risks temper all use.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** Seed (*huo ma ren*) — Sweet, Neutral; moistens intestines, nourishes; Large Intestine, Spleen, Stomach channels. Flowering tops — strong, cautioned, "acrid"/intoxicating.
- **Ayurveda:** *Rasa* — *tikta*, *katu*; *Virya* — *ushna*; *madakārī* (intoxicating), *grāhī*, *nidrājanana*, analgesic; an *upaviṣa* requiring *śodhana*.
- **Unani (Galenic):** Warm and dry; a *mukhaddir* (narcotic/anaesthetic) — analgesic, anticonvulsant, sedative, with recognised intoxicant and habituating potential.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda (SS).** Cannabis (*vijayā* "the victorious," *bhaṅgā*, *gañjā*) is attested in the later Ayurvedic nighantu literature — not the classical Sushrutan core — as *tikta/katu*, *uṣṇa*, and *madakārī* (intoxicating), handled as an *upaviṣa* requiring *śodhana* before use. Its epithet *vijayā* and its association with Shiva (as *bhang*) place it in a devotional-ritual frame, recorded here historically as attested framing, with no dose or route. But *upaviṣa*-with-*śodhana* is a poison-control status, and the intoxicant is not assigned a *rasāyana* (rejuvenation) status in the classical divisions. (b): claimed and framed, but no cultivation status is attested — and dated as later-nighantu material, not Sushrutan.

**Chinese (TCM).** Cannabis is among the most anciently recorded Chinese drugs. The *Shennong Bencao Jing* records *mafen* 麻蕡 (the fruit/inflorescence) with the famous caution that excess "makes one see ghosts and run about frantically" (Brand and Zhao, *Frontiers in Pharmacology*, 2017) — an attested warning, not a cultivation grade. The sources retrieved do not securely assign *mafen* a *san pin* 三品 grade, so none is asserted here; the non-intoxicating seed (*huo ma ren*, treated at its own entry I_128) is the gentler, canonical item and must not be confused with this intoxicant register. (b), framed — attested in the Han-era classic, grade unattested.

**Tibetan (FMT).** Tibetan tantric use of cannabis is historically attested as a ritual framing and is recorded here as history only, with no dose or route. Within Sowa Rigpa's own structure, however, no formal "substances for subtle-body practice" category exists, and medicine is the third of the four treatment branches — it supports practice, it does not reach the root, which is a spiritual condition. No formal cultivation status can be asserted. (c) absent for status; the tantric association is historical framing only.

**Unani (CM).** *Qinnab*, and the intoxicant *bhang*/*ḥashīsh*, are graded warm and dry and classed among the *mukhaddirāt* (narcotics). Unani cultivation is the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture — a finite endowment preserved, not spent — and a habituating *mukhaddir* is a spender of that endowment; the tradition warns of its intoxicating and habit-forming potential rather than assigning it a conservational role. (c) absent for cultivation status; a therapeutic-narcotic identity only.

**Greek (DMM).** Dioscorides records *kannabis* — the seed juice for earache and to "quench" generative function, the plant fibre for rope — with no subtle-body assignment, which he has none to give. What survives for a cultivator is his discipline of attention and the recorded caution about the plant's heavy, "heady" action. (c) absent for any cultivation framing.

**Assyrian (AT).** Under the standing blocking warning for every Mesopotamian substance identification, no secure identification of cannabis can be asserted — proposed readings such as *qunnabu*/*azallû* are actively debated — and no cultivation status can rest on an unsecured identity. (c) absent.

**Practitioner's note.** Two medicines live in one plant: the non-intoxicating seed (I_128), a gentle moistening food-medicine, and the resinous intoxicant, which every source-tradition hedged with purification, dosing precision, and a ritual container. No tradition assigns the intoxicant a cultivation-of-vitality status; the devotional and tantric framings are real history, not protocols. The psychoactive register is legally controlled and clinically gated — verify the law, do not self-dose, and heed the documented psychiatric and developmental risks in Contraindications.

### Contraindications

- **Legal:** possession/use restricted or prohibited in many jurisdictions — **verify local law**.
- **Psychiatric:** contraindicated / high-risk in personal or family history of psychosis or schizophrenia; can precipitate or worsen psychotic, anxiety and mood disorders; associated with cannabis use disorder.
- **Adolescents / developing brain:** avoid — associations with impaired cognition, reduced educational attainment and psychosis risk.
- **Pregnancy & lactation:** contraindicated — linked to restricted fetal growth and prematurity; THC passes to the fetus and breast milk.
- **Cardiovascular:** acute THC raises heart rate and can trigger cardiovascular events; caution in cardiac disease.
- **Driving/operating machinery:** acute intoxication impairs — do not drive.
- **Drug interactions:** additive CNS depression with sedatives/alcohol/opioids; CBD inhibits CYP enzymes and can raise levels of co-administered drugs (e.g. clobazam, warfarin). `[USER: verify against current interaction data]`
- **Hyperemesis:** chronic heavy use can paradoxically cause cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome.
