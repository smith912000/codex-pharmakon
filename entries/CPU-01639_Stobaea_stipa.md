# Stobaea stipa — Stoibe (Dioscorides 4-12); "Carthmus-like Stobaea", "Feather Grass", "Alfa, Esparto"

## *"Stobaea stipa" is not a binomial printed in the text. The SUGGESTED lines of 4-12 print Stobaea pinnata [Loudon] ("Carthmus-like Stobaea"), then Stipa pennata and Stipa barbata ("Feather Grass") and Stipa tenacissima and Macrochloa tenacissima ("Alfa, Esparto")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk lists the plant under this name (the name indexes in `Sources/*/extract/names.csv` were searched). The entry rests on one short chapter of one text. The label *Stobaea stipa* is the spine's name for the row. The translators print the genus *Stobaea* on one line and the genus *Stipa* on the next, and neither line prints that pair as one binomial. The Codex therefore does not state an identity at all, and neither a composite nor either of the listed plants is asserted. The translators' suggestions are in any case suggestions, not Dioscorides' identification. The row was added from the printed names (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-12. STOIBE* at line 8103, SUGGESTED lines at 8104 and 8105, text at line 8106. The chapter stands in Book Four, "Other Herbs & Roots", after *4-11. OLESTION* (line 8100, the chapter of I_1102, *Holosteum umbellatum*) and before *4-13. KLUMENON* (line 8107). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 593. The translators' index prints "Stipa 555" (line 17727) and "Carthmus-like Stobaea 555" (line 15190).

The whole chapter reads: "Stoebe is well known. The seed and the leaves are astringent, so a decoction of them is given as a suppository for dysentery, and it is dropped into purulent ears. The leaves are applied to help bloodshot eyes caused by a stroke, and they stop excessive bloody discharges. It is also called tobion, while the Romans call it stupa."

The chapter has no description. "Stoebe is well known" is the whole of the recognition aid, so Dioscorides assumes the reader already knows the plant, and nothing in the text lets the Codex say what it looked like. The text prints one quality, that the seed and leaves are "astringent", and gives that as the reason for the uses ("so a decoction of them is given"). Three routes are printed: a decoction as a suppository for dysentery, the same decoction dropped into "purulent ears", and the leaves applied to "bloodshot eyes caused by a stroke". The leaves are also said to "stop excessive bloody discharges", without saying where. The chapter prints two other names: *tobion* (Greek) and, from the Romans, *stupa*. The word "stroke" is as printed, and the Codex does not decide whether it means a blow or something else.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter number, the Greek heading, *Stoebe*, *Stobaea*, *Stipa* and *esparto* found no entry on 4-12. I_1102 (chapter 4-11) mentions it only as the next chapter, and the only other hit, I_453 (cotton), is an author's surname in a reference list. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of any plant listed by the translators was read. It is a general fact that *Stipa* (feather grasses) and *Macrochloa* are grasses (Poaceae). *Stobaea* is a different genus, named on another line, and nothing is stated here about it. A composition for "the" plant of this chapter cannot be given. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a decoction as a suppository for dysentery, the same decoction dropped into purulent ears, and leaves applied to bloodshot eyes. These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. The plant is unidentified, and an infected ear or an injured eye is a condition for medical care. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation: "a decoction of them" (seed and leaves), used as a suppository and as ear drops, and leaves "applied" for the eyes. No quantity, frequency or duration is printed. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that discharge from the ear needs medical examination, that the eye is easily harmed by unprepared plant material, and that dysentery needs rehydration and care.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 4-12 prints one quality, "astringent", for the seed and the leaves. It prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter is nearly empty of the material that the spec lists for this tradition (provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, adulteration): its one sensory note is that seed and leaf are astringent, and it takes the plant to be "well known" instead of describing it. That is itself a fact about the discipline: some entries rest on a recognition that the text does not pass on. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The text does not say what the plant looked like, so there is nothing to look for. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 4-12 prints no warning. The plant is not identified, and the text gives no description, so nothing should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. The routes named (suppository, ear drops, eye application) are routes where an unidentified plant carries a real risk. The text gives no dose and no safety information for pregnancy or children.
