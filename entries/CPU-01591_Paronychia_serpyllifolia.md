# Paronychia serpyllifolia — Paronuchia, the whitlow herb (Dioscorides 4-54)

## *Paronychia serpyllifolia, the single binomial on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-54 ("Thyme-leaved Nailwort"); a small rock shrub* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter, and no other tradition on disk is tagged for it. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-54. PARONUCHIA*, heading at line 8477, the SUGGESTED line at line 8479 and the text at line 8480, in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"). A blank line follows the heading, and a plate caption, "Paronychia serpyllifola after FAGUET — 1888" (line 8482), follows the text. The whole chapter reads:

"Paronychia grows among rocks. It is a small shrub similar to peplus — less in length but larger in the leaves. It is applied (bruised) to all, to heal whitlows and favus [contagious honeycombed skin disease]. It is also called adocetos, neuras, or phrynion, while the Romans call it unguinalis."

This is a very short chapter. The description has three parts: a habitat ("among rocks"), a size and form by comparison ("a small shrub similar to peplus — less in length but larger in the leaves"), and nothing else; no flower, root, seed, colour, taste or season. The comparison is with *peplus*, which is the chapter 4-168 *PEPLOS* (heading at line 9576). That chapter describes *peplos* as "a little shrub full of white juice, with a little leaf like rue, but broader", about twenty centimetres round and "spread on the ground", so the reader of 4-54 is meant to picture a low spreading plant. The one use is also stated in a single sentence: bruised and "applied" to whitlows and to favus. The chapter does not gloss the names; as a general linguistic fact, not read in the chapter, *paronuchia* is the Greek word for a whitlow, a sore at the nail, and the Roman synonym *unguinalis* is built on the Latin word for a nail. [USER: verify] The text gives no deity and no amuletic use, and the other names (*adocetos*, *neuras*, *phrynion*) are printed without comment.

**The chapters around it.** 4-55 *CHRUSOKOME* (line 8484, "goldylocks" in the translators' suggestion) follows and is a separate chapter.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter id 4-54 and for the names *paronychia*, *paronuchia* and "nailwort" found no existing entry on the same chapter. Three entries mention it as a neighbour or comparison: I_1086 (*Euphorbia peplus*) quotes the comparison "a small shrub similar to peplus" and notes that 4-54 describes a different plant; I_1049 (*Chrysocoma linosyris*) says the preceding chapter, 4-54, is a separate plant; I_1127 (*Marchantia polymorpha*) places its own chapter before 4-54 (line 8477). None is a duplicate. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The composition of the shrub Dioscorides describes was not read. As a general fact, *Paronychia* belongs to the pink family (Caryophyllaceae), whose members commonly carry saponins. No constituent is named in the chapter. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records one use: the bruised shrub applied to a whitlow and to favus. The Codex gives no protocol for it. A whitlow is a nail-fold infection that can spread, and favus is a fungal scalp disease; both need medical care.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation, with locus (line 8480): the shrub "applied (bruised) to all", that is, as a bruised poultice. No dose, quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that a nail-fold infection and a scalp fungal infection are treated medically, and that a bruised plant laid on broken skin can introduce infection. The identification is a translators' suggestion, so the chapter does not show that the plant it describes is *Paronychia serpyllifolia*.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The cultivation spec says that what he offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, adulteration detection. This chapter gives only the first, a habitat among rocks, and a size by comparison with another plant. It names no season of gathering, no assay and no adulteration. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a poultice, and the text records no amuletic use. No cultivation framing is attested.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The abstentive answer is the honest one: the chapter is a habitat, a comparison and a single poultice.

### Contraindications

Whitlow and favus are infections for medical care; a bruised plant laid on a nail-fold or scalp sore is a hygiene risk. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, and the chapter's description is too short to identify a plant, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
