# Alcyonium (bastard sponge) — Alkuonion (ALKUONION), "alcyonium"; the translator's heading is "Alcyonium — Zoophyte — Bastard Sponge"
## *A sea or shore growth of which Dioscorides describes five kinds by their shape; the translator suggests "Zoophyte — Bastard Sponge" and the chapter itself compares the kinds to a sponge, a membrane, a little worm, unwashed wool and a mushroom; no species is named in the lines read* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Alcyonium is chapter 136 of Book Five of the *De Materia Medica*, headed ALKUONION with the translator's gloss "SUGGESTED: Alcyonium — Zoophyte — Bastard Sponge" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10539-10545; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` has it at line 909 as 5-136, line 10539, and the book's alphabetical index prints "Alcyonium 813"; the list row is `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 862). The chapter sits in the closing part of the book that the page headers call "METALLIC STONES", between gypsum (5-134) and ash from twigs (5-135) on one side and adarces (5-137) and sponges (5-138) on the other. The names list files it as "mineral", whereas the translator's gloss calls it a zoophyte, an animal-plant growth; the spine has put it in Volume III. [USER: verify]

*The five kinds (line 10541).* The text opens: "There are five kinds of alcyonium." In the order given:

1. "One kind is thick with a sour taste. It looks like a sponge, is poisonous and heavy, and smells of fish. A lot of this is found on the shores."
2. "The next is shaped like pterygium [membrane] of the eyes, and is light and hollow in many places, or like a sponge and with a smell like seaweed."
3. "The third is shaped like a little worm, a deeper purple in colour, and is called milesium."
4. "The fourth is like unwashed wool, hollow in many places, and light."
5. "The fifth is the shape of a mushroom, without smell, and rough, somewhat like a pumice stone within, but smooth and sharp outside." The text adds that "quantities of it are found in Propontis around the island called Besbicum, in which country they call it the foam of the sea" (lines 10541-10545).

*Uses by kind (line 10545).* "Of these, the first and second are included in sebaceous treatments for women, and for freckles, lichen [papular skin disease], leprosy, vitiligines [form of leprosy], black patches, and spots on the face and the rest of the body. The third is suitable to use for dysuria [frequent painful urination], those who gather gravelly stones in the bladder, kidney disorders, dropsy and spleen. Burnt and smeared on with wine it thickens the loss of hair [alopecia]. The last type is able to whiten teeth. It is mixed with salt and included in other sebaceous treatments and psilothra [treatments for loss of hair]." The text names the first, second, third and fifth kinds in its uses; the fourth kind is described but is not assigned a use in the lines read. The "burnt and smeared on with wine" clause follows the third kind in the printed sentence order.

*Burning (line 10545).* "If you want to burn any of these put it with salt into an unfired clay pot, and having sealed around the mouth of it with clay, put it into a furnace. When the clay pot has baked enough take it out, put it in jars, and use it. It is washed like cadmia [5-84]."

*Other places in the text where alcyonium is named.* Five other chapters refer to it; four read as cross-references to the burnt product, and the fifth (adarces) as a comparison of shape. In the chapter on the onion (2-181, line 6106), the juice rubbed on helps the loss of hair, "as it brings out the hair sooner than alcyonium [5-136]" (line 6110). In the chapter on edible bulbs (2-200, line 6301), the bulbs "burnt and mixed with alcyonium [5-136] and rubbed on in the sun" take away sunburn and black scars (line 6308). The wine sediment of 5-132 is "burnt like alcyonium [5-136] but first dried carefully" (line 10520), and sponges (5-138, line 10552) are "burnt the same way as alcyonium". Adarces (5-137, line 10549), a salty hardened fluid that adheres to reeds and grass, is said to be "in its whole shape like alcyonium, soft and hollow in many places", and is called "alcyonium palustre [bastard sponges of the marshes]". Those chapters are separate materials and are not folded in.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, "alcyonium" is a name that is used today for a genus of soft corals, and the old name for a sponge-like growth may have covered several different marine organisms, sponges and others; Dioscorides' five kinds are not a single species. Marine sponges and soft corals contain protein fibres or spicules, salts and a wide range of secondary metabolites, some of them toxic. The text itself calls the first kind "poisonous". Nothing links any constituent to the uses the text gives.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records alcyonium, chiefly burnt with salt in a clay pot, as an ingredient of cosmetic and skin salves for freckles, spots, black patches and leprosy-like conditions, as a hair preparation, a tooth whitener, and, for one kind, a medicine for the bladder, kidney, dropsy and spleen. The Codex does not recommend any of these uses. The material cannot be identified from the text; one kind is called poisonous in the chapter itself; and the burnt product is an irritant. The entry exists to record the text and its method of burning.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation is the burning (line 10545): put the growth "with salt into an unfired clay pot", seal the mouth with clay, put it in a furnace, take it out when the pot is "baked enough", store it in jars, and "wash it like cadmia [5-84]". The text gives no quantity of salt, no length of firing, and no amount for any use or any course. For hair, the third kind is "burnt and smeared on with wine"; for teeth the fifth is used, and for skin spots the first and second are "included in sebaceous treatments". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Marine sponges and soft corals are animals (sponges are Porifera; soft corals are Cnidaria), and the ancient Greek name covered growths whose modern identity is uncertain here. Burnt marine material with salt would leave mainly mineral ash, which can be irritant. Nothing established supports the use of any such ash for hair loss, for the urinary tract, or for leprosy. Leprosy is a bacterial disease treated today with antibiotics. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** The chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality for alcyonium. It does print physical and sensory qualities that served as the assay: "thick with a sour taste", "poisonous and heavy", smell of fish for the first kind; "light and hollow" with a smell like seaweed for the second; deeper purple for the third; "light" for the fourth; "without smell, and rough" within and "smooth and sharp" outside for the fifth. No other energetic classification is given in the lines read.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is asserted here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is the practitioner's discipline of attention: five kinds told apart by shape, texture, smell, colour and place of finding, with the first named as poisonous, and a method of burning with salt in a sealed pot. That is provenance and assay. The uses given are cosmetic and therapeutic, for the skin, hair, teeth, bladder, kidney and spleen, and the chapter makes no claim about the person's constitution, vitality or long life.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator beyond the habit the chapter teaches: tell the kinds of a doubtful material apart by sight and smell, and remember that the first of them is named as poisonous. The Codex does not recommend handling or burning an unidentified marine growth. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Toxicity:** the chapter calls the first kind "poisonous"; marine sponges and corals can contain toxic compounds and can cause skin irritation or allergic reactions on contact.
- **Burnt product:** ash made by burning with salt is an irritant; it should not be put on broken skin, the scalp, the gums or the eyes.
- **Leprosy and skin disease:** persistent patches, spots or numb areas need diagnosis; the text's skin uses are historical statements and not treatments.
- **Urinary and kidney disease, dropsy and spleen:** painful or frequent urination, bladder stones and swelling need a clinician.
- **Identity and conservation:** the text names five kinds and no species; the modern identity is not settled, and many marine sponges and corals are protected, so the Codex does not recommend collecting them.
