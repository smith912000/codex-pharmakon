# Gou Teng (钩藤) / Hook Vine / Chōtōkō (釣藤鈎) / Cat's Claw Vine / Uncariae Ramulus Cum Uncis / Diao Teng Gou (钓藤钩)

## *Uncaria rhynchophylla* (Miq.) Miq. ex Havil. | Family: Rubiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ, FMT

**Energetic Classification:** Sweet, cool; descending and settling. Enters Liver, Pericardium.

**Training Phase:** Advanced — Neurological Settling

---

### ⚠️ NAME-COLLISION WARNING — READ BEFORE ANYTHING ELSE

**This plant is NOT "cat's claw" as that term is used in the Western supplement market.**

Two entirely different medicines share the English name, and confusing them is the most consequential error available in this entry:

| | **THIS ENTRY** | **NOT THIS ENTRY** |
|---|---|---|
| Binomial | *Uncaria rhynchophylla* | *Uncaria tomentosa* |
| Origin | East Asia (China, Japan, Korea) | South America (Amazon, Peru) |
| Traditional name | Gou Teng 钩藤 / Chōtōkō | Uña de gato |
| Traditional use | **Extinguish liver wind** — convulsions, tremor, childhood fright, headache/dizziness of ascendant yang | **Immune modulation**, inflammation, arthritis |
| Key chemistry | Rhynchophylline, isorhynchophylline — **oxindole alkaloids acting on CNS** | Pentacyclic oxindole alkaloids, quinovic acid glycosides — immune-directed |
| Modern framing | Neurological / antihypertensive | Immunostimulant |

They share a genus and a hooked thorn. They share essentially nothing clinically. A practitioner who reads "cat's claw" on a bottle and expects an anticonvulsant CNS drug — or who reads Gou Teng research and buys Peruvian uña de gato — has bought the wrong medicine. Compounding the hazard: *U. tomentosa* is marketed as an **immunostimulant**, which carries its own contraindications (autoimmune disease, immunosuppressant therapy, transplant) entirely absent from Gou Teng's profile. **Verify the binomial. Never the common name.**

Note further that the official pharmacopoeial drug **Uncariae Ramulus Cum Uncis (URCU)** is not single-species: it comprises *U. rhynchophylla*, *U. macrophylla*, *U. sinensis*, *U. hirsuta* and *U. sessilifructus* (Cao et al. 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2024.117848), PMID 38336181). Much of the literature cited below concerns the multi-species drug. This entry is *U. rhynchophylla*-anchored and says so where it matters.

### Origin & Tradition

**Chinese Medicine / Sun Bin Jing lineage (SBJ) — 钩藤 (gōu téng)**

The name is refreshingly free of doctrine. **钩 (gōu)** = hook. **藤 (téng)** = vine. Gou Teng is the vine with hooks — and it is, precisely: *Uncaria rhynchophylla* is a climbing liana bearing recurved, woody, thorn-like hooks at the leaf nodes, modified stems by which it grips and ascends. The genus name *Uncaria* is the same observation in Latin (*uncus*, hook). Two unrelated botanical traditions looked at this plant and named it after the same structure.

This matters as a control case. Most classical drug names carry a claim (see I_759 Feverfew — *febrifugia*, a therapeutic assertion; or I_758 Chen Pi — an insistence on process). **Gou Teng's name carries no claim at all.** It is pure morphology. The hooks are not said to hook anything therapeutically; the vine's climbing habit is not mapped onto any ascent-and-descent doctrine, despite the tradition's evident fondness for such mappings and despite this plant *treating* a disorder of pathological ascent. The name is a botanist's label, not a physician's theory. And the officinal part — the hook-bearing stem, the hooks specifically, 钩藤钩 — was selected because that is where the actives concentrate (Cao et al. 2024 note the hook-bearing stem is "the primary constituent enrichment site"). The tradition named the plant by its most conspicuous feature and then, independently and correctly, harvested that feature. Empiricism without doctrine.

**The doctrine — 平肝熄风 (píng gān xī fēng)**

The therapeutic doctrine lives elsewhere, and it is one of the tradition's most internally rigorous constructs. Gou Teng's classical functions:

- **熄风 (xī fēng)** — *extinguish wind*. Specifically **internal liver wind (肝风内动)**.
- **平肝潜阳 (píng gān qián yáng)** — *calm the liver and subdue ascendant yang*.
- **清热 (qīng rè)** — clear heat.

"Wind" in the internal-medicine sense is a syndromic category defined by **movement that should not be occurring**: convulsions, seizures, tremor, spasm, twitching, tics, rigidity, opisthotonos. The tradition arrived at this category by grouping symptoms that *move erratically* under the metaphor of wind — sudden onset, shifting location, involuntary motion. The category is remarkably clean: it collects, in modern terms, seizure disorders, movement disorders, febrile convulsions, and eclampsia.

Gou Teng's specific classical indications:
- **急惊风 / 小儿惊风 — childhood fright wind / convulsions.** High-fever convulsions in children, and the "fright" presentations of startle, night terror and spasm. Gou Teng is *the* paediatric wind drug, valued for being gentle — sweet and cool rather than harsh.
- **Tremor, spasm, tetany, epilepsy.**
- **头痛眩晕 — headache and dizziness of ascendant liver yang (肝阳上亢).** This is the second great indication and the more common one in adult practice. The syndrome: throbbing headache (often temporal/vertex), dizziness, tinnitus, red face, red eyes, irritability, insomnia, wiry pulse. **In modern terms this is essentially a description of hypertension** — and the tradition's account of it as *yang rising to the head, unanchored below* is a functional description of raised pressure delivered to the cranium. The mapping is not exact and should not be over-claimed, but it is close enough that Gou Teng became a mainstay of Chinese antihypertensive formulas (Tian Ma Gou Teng Yin 天麻钩藤饮).
- **Eclampsia** — the point where the two indications converge: hypertension *becoming* convulsion. The tradition treated this with the same drug it used for both, which is a coherent inference from its own model.

Gou Teng is nearly always compounded, principally with **Gastrodia elata (Tian Ma 天麻)** — the pairing so standard it is effectively a single unit in prescribing.

**Formulaic / Kampo transmission (FMT) — 釣藤鈎 (chōtōkō)**

Gou Teng entered Japanese Kampo as **chōtōkō** and became a component of several standard formulas — **yokukansan (抑肝散)**, **yokukansankachimpihange**, and **chōtōsan (釣藤散)** among them (Kushida et al. 2021, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2021.688670), PMID 34335255). Yokukansan's name means "restrain the liver powder" — the liver-calming doctrine transmitted intact into Japanese practice. Its modern Japanese use is notable: yokukansan is prescribed for the **behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia** — agitation, aggression, hallucination — which is a recognisably direct descendant of "restrain the liver," applied to a condition the classical authors never described.

The formulaic lineage is where Gou Teng is most rigorously studied, because Kampo's standardised manufacturing makes it tractable. The Tsumura laboratory work cited throughout this entry exists because a pharmaceutical company sells these formulas and must characterise them.

The classical record places URCU's first compilation in the **'Mingyi Bielu' (名医别录)**, attributed to Tao Hongjing (Cao et al. 2024, PMID 38336181).

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

Two doctrines here, plus a preparation instruction that is the entry's sharpest chemistry lesson.

**"Extinguish wind" → anticonvulsant chemistry.** The wind category collects involuntary movement. The modern account of involuntary movement's commonest cause is **excitotoxicity and pathological neuronal excitation** — glutamatergic overdrive, NMDA-receptor over-activation, calcium influx, uncontrolled depolarisation. And rhynchophylline's characterised pharmacology is **NMDA-receptor antagonism and calcium-channel modulation**.

Read that as a mapping. The tradition observed a class of disorders defined by *movement occurring where stillness should be*, called it wind, and reached for a drug. That drug turns out to contain oxindole alkaloids that dampen the receptor system responsible for pathological neuronal excitation. Yang et al. (2018) showed rhynchophylline acting specifically by **inhibiting excessive activation of extrasynaptic GluN2B-containing NMDA receptors**, reducing extrasynaptic NMDAR-mediated excitatory postsynaptic currents, downregulating GluN2B expression and preventing Ca²⁺ overload ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropharm.2018.03.007), PMID 29510187). The specificity is worth pausing on: *extrasynaptic* NMDARs are the excitotoxic pool, as opposed to the synaptic pool that carries normal signalling. A drug that suppresses pathological excitation while sparing physiological transmission is, in the tradition's own language, **extinguishing wind without extinguishing function** — which is exactly what "gentle enough for children" was asserting.

This is a good mapping. It is not proof. It is a preclinical mechanism aligning with a syndromic category, and the alignment is real.

**"Subdue ascendant yang" → hypotensive chemistry.** Yang rising unanchored, striking the head, producing throbbing and dizziness and red face. Gou Teng's alkaloids are hypotensive. Ozaki (1989) tested hirsutine, hirsuteine, rhynchophylline, isorhynchophylline and dihydrocorynantheine isolated from *U. rhynchophylla* and found "a mild central depressive effect in mice, a weak non-competitive anti-spasmodic action in the mouse intestine, and **a hypotensive effect in rats**" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1254/fpj.94.17), PMID 2792960). The doctrine described a pressure disorder in hydraulic-hierarchical metaphor; the chemistry lowers pressure. The correspondence holds.

But note where the mapping *fails*, because this is where honest reading earns its keep. The doctrine says Gou Teng *subdues yang from above* while other drugs *anchor from below* — a directional architecture with specific drug assignments. That architecture has **no chemical correlate whatsoever.** There is no "downward" in pharmacology. The tradition's directional logic organised its prescribing usefully and it is not thereby describing anything real about molecules. Half the doctrine maps; half is scaffolding. Say so.

**⚠️ The preparation doctrine — 后下 (hòu xià), "add later."** The classical instruction is unambiguous: Gou Teng must be added to the decoction **late**, in the last five to ten minutes, never boiled from the start. The stated reason is that prolonged decoction destroys its efficacy.

This is a **falsifiable claim about heat and chemistry**, and it is correct. Oxindole alkaloids are thermolabile and epimerise. The rhynchophylline/isorhynchophylline and corynoxeine/isocorynoxeine pairs are C-7 epimers — prolonged heating drives interconversion and degradation, altering the alkaloid profile of the finished decoction. The tradition could not have known about C-7 stereochemistry. It knew that a long-boiled Gou Teng decoction did not work as well as a briefly-boiled one, and it wrote that into the preparation instruction and transmitted it for centuries.

Set alongside I_758 Chen Pi's ageing doctrine, a pattern emerges worth naming: **the Chinese tradition's *processing* instructions have a markedly better hit rate against modern chemistry than its *theoretical* instructions do.** Processing rules were forged against direct feedback — the decoction either worked or it didn't. Theory was forged against nothing. The hòu xià rule is empiricism that happened to be recorded in a doctrinal document, and it should be graded as empiricism.

### Active Components

**Oxindole and indole alkaloids** — the actives, concentrated in the hooks and hook-bearing stem. Per Kushida et al. 2021 (PMID 34335255), the major ones:

- **Rhynchophylline (RIN)** — tetracyclic oxindole; the principal marker. NMDA-receptor effects, calcium-channel effects, neuroprotection, hypotensive activity.
- **Isorhynchophylline** — C-7 epimer of rhynchophylline; overlapping activity. **The epimeric relationship is the chemical basis of the "add later" rule.**
- **Corynoxeine / Isocorynoxeine** — a second epimeric oxindole pair.
- **Hirsutine / Hirsuteine** — indole alkaloids. Hirsutine showed **antiarrhythmic effects** comparable in potency to ajmaline against aconitine- and ouabain-induced arrhythmias, plus hypotensive and gastric-protective effects, without β-adrenoceptor blockade (Ozaki 1989, PMID 2792960).
- **Geissoschizine methyl ether (GM)** — noted by Kushida et al. as exhibiting "comparatively potent pharmacological activity," including **5-HT receptor agonist activity**; uniformly distributed in brain after oral administration in rats. GM is a substantial part of the CNS story and is under-appreciated relative to rhynchophylline.

**Also present:** terpenoids, flavonoids, steroids. Cao et al. 2024 report **107 newly characterised constituents from URCU since 2015** — this is an actively expanding phytochemistry, not a settled one.

**Pharmacokinetics (Kushida et al. 2021, PMID 34335255) — and the caveats matter:**
- Alkaloids are absorbed into blood circulation and **rapidly eliminated** after oral administration.
- Predominantly metabolised by **CYP450** — oxidised and demethylated metabolites.
- **Several alkaloids cross the blood-brain barrier** in rats after oral dosing. This is not assumed; it is measured, and it is the load-bearing fact for any CNS claim.
- GM metabolism by CYP shows a **gender-dependent difference in rats but not in humans** — a direct warning against extrapolating rodent PK.
- The authors explicitly flag unresolved challenges: **pharmacokinetic differences between pure compound and crude drug administration**, food-influenced absorption, and intestinal tissue metabolism. A purified rhynchophylline study does not describe what a decoction does.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Gou Teng sits at **Advanced — Neurological Settling** and is not a foundation-phase agent. Its indication is the settled, over-excited, upward-driven presentation: the practitioner with tension-and-throbbing head symptoms, dizziness, irritability, insomnia of the agitated rather than the depleted kind, and — where present under medical supervision — raised blood pressure.

Three disciplines govern its use:

**1. It is a compounded drug, not a solo agent.** The entire traditional and Kampo record uses Gou Teng in formula, almost always with Gastrodia (Tian Ma) and typically within Tian Ma Gou Teng Yin, yokukansan or chōtōsan. The evidence base — such as it is — is for those formulas. Isolated Gou Teng monotherapy is outside both the tradition and the data.

**2. Prepare it correctly or do not prepare it.** Add in the final 5–10 minutes. A Gou Teng boiled for forty minutes is a different and probably weaker drug. This is not optional; it is the one instruction the chemistry unambiguously endorses.

**3. Convulsive disorders are not a Kenetica indication.** The classical drug treats seizures and paediatric convulsions. **The codex does not.** Epilepsy, febrile convulsion in a child, and eclampsia are medical emergencies with effective conventional treatment, and the preclinical-only status of Gou Teng's anticonvulsant evidence makes substitution indefensible. The doctrine's most striking modern correlate is precisely the indication a practitioner must not act on. That tension is the entry's honest centre: **a good mechanism is not a licence.**

Where Gou Teng is legitimately placeable is as an **adjunct in agitated, hyperexcitable, upward-driven presentations under supervision** — and, following the Japanese lead, in the agitation of neurodegenerative conditions where yokukansan has an established (if imperfectly evidenced) prescribing tradition.

### Scientific Correlation

*All citations below retrieved from PubMed. The pattern here is unusually clean: strong preclinical, near-absent human.*

**1. The alkaloid pharmacology review — the anchor. GRADE: MODERATE-STRONG (as a review of preclinical work).**
Ndagijimana et al., *Fitoterapia* 2013 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fitote.2013.01.018), PMID 23376412). Reviews indole alkaloids from *U. rhynchophylla*, stating the drug is "mainly used for the treatment of **convulsion, hypertension, epilepsy, eclampsia, and cerebral diseases**," with pharmacological activities attributed predominantly to indole alkaloids. Indexed under Anticonvulsants, Antihypertensive Agents, Neuroprotective Agents. **This is the traditional indication list and the modern pharmacological class list being the same list** — which is the entry's central finding and is worth stating plainly.

**2. Rhynchophylline and NMDA receptors — the wind doctrine's mechanism. GRADE: STRONG PRECLINICAL (rat, in vivo + electrophysiology).**
Yang et al., *Neuropharmacology* 2018 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropharm.2018.03.007), PMID 29510187). In rats: rhynchophylline rescued soluble Aβ-induced spatial learning/memory deficits (Morris water maze) and prevented Aβ-induced suppression of LTP in the entorhinal cortex–dentate gyrus circuit. Mechanism: **prevented excessive activation of extrasynaptic GluN2B-NMDARs**, reduced extrasynaptic NMDAR-mediated EPSCs, downregulated GluN2B expression, inhibited Ca²⁺ overload. Behavioural + immunofluorescence + electrophysiology — methodologically strong for its tier. **Honest caveat:** this is an *Alzheimer's* model, not a seizure model. It evidences the receptor mechanism that would underwrite an anticonvulsant action; it does not evidence anticonvulsant efficacy. The mechanism is confirmed; the traditional indication is inferred from it.

**3. Neuroprotection in cerebral ischaemia — including the classical pairing. GRADE: MODERATE PRECLINICAL.**
Zhang et al., *Life Sciences* 2019 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lfs.2019.116935), PMID 31610203). In MCAO mice and OGD-treated BV-2 cells, **gastrodin combined with rhynchophylline** mitigated infarction, neurological deficits and inflammasome activation via upregulation of miR-21-5p and miR-331-5p (targeting TXNIP and TRAF6 respectively); miR inhibitors abolished the protection. **Note what this is:** gastrodin is the marker compound of **Gastrodia elata — Tian Ma** — Gou Teng's inseparable classical partner. Researchers testing the *pair* found synergistic neuroprotection with a defined molecular mechanism. The tradition's most standard drug pairing was tested as a pairing and held. That is a genuine and non-trivial hit for the formulaic tradition, and it is still a mouse.

**4. Hypotensive and antiarrhythmic activity — the yang doctrine's mechanism. GRADE: MODERATE PRECLINICAL, and old.**
Ozaki, *Nihon Yakurigaku Zasshi* 1989 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1254/fpj.94.17), PMID 2792960). Isolated hirsutine, hirsuteine, rhynchophylline, isorhynchophylline and dihydrocorynantheine from *U. rhynchophylla*: **mild central depressive effect in mice, weak non-competitive antispasmodic action in mouse intestine, hypotensive effect in rats.** Hirsutine showed antiarrhythmic potency approximately equal to ajmaline, not via β-blockade, plus gastric-erosion prevention. **Honest caveats:** 1989, Japanese-language, rodent. The hypotensive effect is real and reproduced across the literature; its magnitude, duration and human relevance are not established here. The **"mild central depressive effect"** is the most clinically actionable line in the paper and is the basis of the sedative-interaction warning below.

**5. The comprehensive modern review — scope and toxicology. GRADE: MODERATE (review).**
Cao et al., *Journal of Ethnopharmacology* 2024 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2024.117848), PMID 38336181). URCU comprises five *Uncaria* species; hook-bearing stem is the constituent enrichment site; 107 new compounds characterised since 2015; demonstrated neuroprotective, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, antibacterial and autophagy-enhancing properties. **Critically, the authors' own toxicological summary:** "dosage, frequency, and interactions of the drugs that occur in vivo are of great significance for determining whether the organic bodies can perform efficacy **or produce toxicity**," and they name the field's main obstacles as **"toxicity, low bioavailability, and poor permeability."** This is a review by advocates of the drug naming toxicity as a principal obstacle. That is worth more than a favourable line would be.

**6. Pharmacokinetics — the honest limiter. GRADE: STRONG (for what it measures), and it constrains everything above.**
Kushida, Matsumoto & Ikarashi, *Frontiers in Pharmacology* 2021 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2021.688670), PMID 34335255). Detailed above. The findings that matter: alkaloids **rapidly eliminated** after oral dosing; CYP-metabolised; several **do cross the BBB** in rats; GM binds 5-HT receptors and distributes uniformly in brain. The authors' own list of unresolved challenges is the most valuable content in the paper: **pure-drug vs crude-drug PK differences, food-influenced absorption, metabolite excretion, intestinal tissue metabolism.** Every rhynchophylline mechanism study above used purified compound. The authors are saying, in a review published by the company that manufactures the formulas, that this may not describe the decoction.

**7. Human clinical evidence. GRADE: ABSENT IN THIS RETRIEVAL — the entry's null.**
**No randomised controlled trial of *U. rhynchophylla*, or of Gou Teng-containing formula, for any indication, was retrieved in this run.** The searches conducted returned reviews, mechanism studies, PK work and rodent models — and no human efficacy data. Whatever exists for yokukansan in dementia-related agitation (a substantial Japanese literature is plausible) **was not retrieved here and is therefore not cited here.** [USER: verify the yokukansan clinical trial literature separately before this entry claims anything about human efficacy.]

**Overall honest grade: preclinical STRONG and mechanistically elegant; human evidence ABSENT.** This is the classic profile of a botanical whose doctrine maps beautifully onto receptor pharmacology and whose clinical file is empty. The temptation with Gou Teng is severe precisely *because* the mechanism story is so satisfying — extrasynaptic NMDA antagonism is exactly what "extinguish wind" would need to mean. **A satisfying mechanism is the most seductive form of insufficient evidence.** The correlation earns Gou Teng a place in the codex as a fascinating and probably-active drug. It does not earn it a place in the treatment of seizures.

### Contraindications

- **⚠️ HYPOTENSION AND ANTIHYPERTENSIVE INTERACTION.** The hypotensive effect is Gou Teng's best-attested activity (Ozaki 1989, PMID 2792960; Ndagijimana 2013, PMID 23376412) — which makes it also the drug's clearest interaction hazard. Additive hypotension with ACE inhibitors, ARBs, calcium-channel blockers, beta-blockers, diuretics, alpha-blockers. **Contraindicated in existing hypotension.** In a practitioner already on antihypertensive therapy, adding Gou Teng is adding an unmonitored, unstandardised, variably-dosed antihypertensive. Monitor blood pressure or do not use. Caution with anaesthesia — [USER: verify a pre-surgical discontinuation interval].

- **⚠️ SEDATIVE AND CNS-DEPRESSANT INTERACTION.** Ozaki 1989 documented a **"mild central depressive effect in mice"** for the isolated alkaloids, and Kushida et al. 2021 confirm several alkaloids cross the blood-brain barrier after oral dosing, with geissoschizine methyl ether binding 5-HT receptors and distributing uniformly in brain. This is not a theoretical CNS drug — the alkaloids demonstrably get into the brain and demonstrably act there. Additive effects with benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, opioids, alcohol, antihistamines, antipsychotics, other sedating botanicals. **The 5-HT receptor activity raises a serotonergic-interaction question** with SSRIs, SNRIs, triptans and MAOIs that the retrieved literature does not resolve. [USER: verify GM's specific receptor subtype activity and any serotonergic interaction data before advising a patient on serotonergic medication.]

- **⚠️ PREGNANCY — AVOID.** No adequate human safety data retrieved this run. The plant's classical use in **eclampsia** means it has historical obstetric application, but that use is (a) for a life-threatening emergency now managed with magnesium sulfate and delivery, and (b) not a demonstration of safety in ordinary pregnancy — it is a demonstration that the tradition reached for it when the alternative was death. Additionally, a CNS-active, BBB-crossing, NMDA-modulating alkaloid mixture has an obvious theoretical fetal-neurodevelopmental concern: NMDA receptor signalling is central to neurodevelopment. Avoid in pregnancy and lactation.

- **⚠️ TOXICITY IS NAMED BY THE FIELD'S OWN ADVOCATES.** Cao et al. 2024 (PMID 38336181), a review favourable to the drug, identifies **"toxicity, low bioavailability, and poor permeability"** as the principal obstacles to URCU's use, and states that dosage, frequency and in vivo interactions determine whether it produces efficacy **or toxicity**. Treat the therapeutic window as real and uncharacterised. Do not exceed pharmacopoeial dosing.

- **⚠️ SEIZURE DISORDERS — DO NOT SELF-TREAT OR SUBSTITUTE.** The classical indication and the modern mechanism both point at convulsive disorders. There is **no human evidence**. Epilepsy, febrile convulsions in children, and eclampsia require conventional emergency care. A practitioner or parent who delays treatment because a preclinical mechanism was persuasive may be harmed severely. This contraindication exists precisely *because* the mechanistic story is compelling.

- **⚠️ PAEDIATRIC USE.** The tradition's flagship indication is childhood convulsion. The codex declines this indication entirely. No paediatric safety data retrieved. A BBB-crossing NMDA-modulating alkaloid in a developing brain is not a low-risk exposure regardless of how gentle the classical texts call it.

- **CYP-mediated drug interactions.** The alkaloids are predominantly CYP-metabolised (Kushida et al. 2021). Interaction potential with CYP inhibitors/inducers and with narrow-therapeutic-index drugs is plausible and uncharacterised. [USER: verify specific CYP isoform data.]

- **Species and product identity — the practical hazard.** Two distinct problems. (a) The **cat's claw collision**: *U. tomentosa* is a different medicine with different chemistry, different indications and immunostimulant contraindications (autoimmune disease, immunosuppression, transplant) that do not apply here — and vice versa. (b) The **URCU multi-species issue**: the official drug spans five *Uncaria* species with differing alkaloid profiles, so "Gou Teng" in a study and "Gou Teng" in a bottle may not be the same plant. Demand the binomial.

- **Preparation as a safety and efficacy variable.** Capsules, powders and tinctures bypass the decoction entirely, and with it the hòu xià rule and essentially all of the traditional use record. They are not the studied article.

- **Wind from deficiency.** The tradition's own caution: Gou Teng is for wind arising from *excess* — ascendant yang, liver heat. Wind arising from blood or yin deficiency requires nourishing, not subduing. Applying a cool descending drug to a depleted patient deepens the depletion. Internally coherent and worth respecting.
