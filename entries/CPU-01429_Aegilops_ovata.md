# Goat Grass / Aegilops ovata — Aigilops (Dioscorides 4-139)

## *Aegilops ovata* (the Dioscorides translators' suggested identification for 4-139; not Dioscorides' own) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry: named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** This row was added on 2026-10-09 by the plant-gap run (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP). The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) on the SUGGESTED line of one chapter. The plant is named by no other text read. The chapter is very short, and so is this entry.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`.

*4-139 AIGILOPS* (heading at line 9248; SUGGESTED lines 9250 and 9251: "Aegilops ovata — Goat Grass, Hard Grass Phleum aegylops, Triticum ovatum — Hard Grass, Goat Grass, Oat Grass Aegilops caudata [Loudon]— Cretan Hard Grass"; text at line 9252, in Book Four, Other Herbs and Roots). The whole chapter reads: "Aegilops is a little herb with leaves similar to wheat, but often with two or three red seeds on the top of the head on which beards (or as it were, filaments) grow out. The herb (applied with meal) heals aegilopses [ulcer or fistula in the inner angle of the eye] and dissolves hard lumps. The juice is stored for the same purposes, mixed with meal, and dried. It is also called sitospelos, siphon, or bromos, and the Romans call it avena." A plate caption follows the chapter: "Triticum ovatum after FAGUET — 1894".

**What the translators' line gives.** The SUGGESTED line lists several names, among them *Aegilops ovata*, *Phleum aegylops*, *Triticum ovatum* and, marked "[Loudon]", *Aegilops caudata*. The row's binomial is the first. The translators prefer none of these, and the plate caption (*Triticum ovatum*) is a synonym in their own list. The identification is the translators' and is a grass-level identification only. [USER: verify]

**The word in the text.** The chapter name AIGILOPS is also the name of the disease it treats, which the translators gloss in brackets as "ulcer or fistula in the inner angle of the eye". The same word, in the plural, is printed in other chapters as a complaint treated by other substances (for example by caryae kernels and by chondrus, in lines 4834 and 5501 of the same file). It is a disease name and is not a plant reference in those chapters, and nothing there is attributed to this plant.

**Neighbouring chapter.** 4-140 BROMOS (heading just after 4-139) opens "Bromus is a drying herb similar to aegilops" (line 9265). The chapter 4-140 has its own SUGGESTED line (*Bromus temulentus*, darnel, marked NARCOTIC by the translators) and belongs to another row. It is reported here only because it compares itself to aegilops. Its uses are not attributed to this entry. The bromos named as an alternative name at the end of 4-139 should therefore be treated with care. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Nothing is printed on the plant's constituents. *Aegilops* species are grasses of the wheat tribe, and the plant is a close relative of wheat. Their chemistry was not read for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The sole record is a herb applied with meal to an eye ulcer or fistula and to hard lumps. It is not a recommended practice, the identification is a translators' grass-level suggestion, and the Codex gives no protocol for it.

### Preparation & Dosing

Dioscorides gives two preparations in line 9252: the herb applied "with meal", and the juice "stored for the same purposes, mixed with meal, and dried". No quantity, frequency or duration is printed. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general fact that fistulas and ulcers at the inner corner of the eye can reflect infection of the tear drainage system, and need medical assessment.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** no warming, cooling, drying or astringent quality is printed for 4-139. (The word "drying" in 4-140 is said of bromus, not of this plant.)

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. The chapter gives a description (wheat-like leaves; two or three red seeds on the head, with beards) that serves identification, but no habitat, season of gathering or test of quality. No cultivation status or framing is attested for this plant.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here gives a cultivator a practice. The chapter is a short folk remedy for an eye complaint and for hard lumps. The abstentive answer is the correct one, and an eye ulcer or fistula needs a clinician.

### Contraindications

No safety information is printed. Applying plant material and meal to an eye, or to a fistula next to it, risks infection. Grass seeds with beards (awns) can injure the eye. None of the uses above is advice.
