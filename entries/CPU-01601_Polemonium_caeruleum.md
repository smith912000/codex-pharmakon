### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 8074) prints one binomial with three English names. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-8. POLEMONION*, heading at line 8073, SUGGESTED line at 8074, text beginning at line 8075 and continuing after the page number 551 (line 8076) at line 8079. It stands in Book Four, after the chapter on the second *clematis* and before *4-9. SUMPHUTON PETRAION*. The translators' index gives *Polemonia* and *Polemonium* at page 551.

The whole chapter reads: "Polemonia has thin little winged branches, with leaves a little bigger than rue [3-52, 3-53, 4-98], but longer like those of polygonum [4-4, 4-5] or calamint. On the top of them is what looks like clusters of berries, in which are black seeds. The root is a foot long, whitish, similar to struthium [2-193]. It grows in hilly, rough places. The root of this is taken as a drink in wine against venomous creatures, and with water for dysentery, painful urination, and sciatica. A teaspoonful with vinegar is given for the spleen. The root of this is carried around one to prevent scorpions striking. They say that those who have this shall not be bitten, and though they are touched yet nothing will happen. Chewed, it eases toothaches. It is also called philetaeria, while the Cappadocians call it chiliodynamis."

Dioscorides gives a description built on three comparison plants (rue, *polygonum*, calamint, with the root like *struthium*), a habitat ("hilly, rough places") and a run of root uses. The root is drunk in wine against venomous creatures, drunk with water for dysentery, painful urination and sciatica, and given with vinegar for the spleen; chewed, it eases toothache. The chapter's last use differs in kind: "The root of this is carried around one to prevent scorpions striking." It is followed by a reported belief in the form "They say that those who have this shall not be bitten, and though they are touched yet nothing will happen." The carrying instruction is stated flatly. The claim of immunity is introduced as what "they say". The chapter prints two further names, *philetaeria* and the Cappadocian *chiliodynamis*, with no use attached.

**In the translators' introduction.** The introduction (line 394), in a paragraph that begins "A few superstitious practices are recorded in De Materia Medica", names "Polemonia against the bite of scorpions" among the amulets and mascots. That is the translators' characterisation, and not Dioscorides' own wording.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter number, *Polemonion*, *Polemonium* and *Jacob's ladder* found no existing entry on 4-8. Several entries match only the string "4-8" inside other chapter numbers, and none of them is on this plant.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a root drunk for venomous bites, dysentery, painful urination, sciatica and the spleen, chewed for toothache, and carried against scorpions. These are the text's own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for any of them. A root carried against scorpion strikes is not a protection that the Codex can assess, and nobody should rely on it. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations: the root "taken as a drink in wine" and "with water"; "A teaspoonful with vinegar" for the spleen; the root chewed; and the root "carried around one". The teaspoonful is the only measure printed, and it applies to the spleen use only. No frequency or duration is given. For the other uses no dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that carrying a plant does not prevent a scorpion sting and that venomous bites need urgent medical care.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. It prints the root as "whitish" and "a foot long" and the habitat as hilly and rough.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter does record an amuletic use: the root "is carried around one to prevent scorpions striking", with a reported immunity that "they say" follows. Recording is not endorsing. The immunity is given as hearsay, while the carrying is given as an instruction, and the Codex does not turn either into a practice. The translators' introduction files the plant among the book's "superstitious practices". What the chapter offers is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: comparison with known plants, a measured root, a stated habitat. The plant is given no status beyond that. The identification is the translators'.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It offers a description, a list of root remedies and a reported amulet, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Venomous bites and stings need emergency medical care, and the amulet must not be taken as protection. Dysentery, urinary pain, sciatica and persistent toothache need medical assessment. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
