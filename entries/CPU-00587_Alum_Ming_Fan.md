### Active Components

Potash alum is hydrated potassium aluminium sulphate. Alums are astringent: they precipitate protein and shrink tissue, and they stop minor bleeding. The old texts' "alum" can include other aluminium and iron sulphates, so the material of each passage may differ.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources use alum on the skin, gums, wounds and eyes as an astringent; the Chinese classic adds a long-life line for the sublimated mineral. A cultivator has no sourced reason to take it internally, and Dioscorides' contraceptive and abortifacient use is dangerous. The Codex gives no protocol, dose or preparation.

### Preparation & Dosing

DMM 5-123: burnt and roasted "like chalcitis" (line 10468 ff.); applied with honey, vinegar, salt, pitch or water as listed above. SBJ prints "after being sublimated" without method. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Alum is a well-established astringent and styptic for minor cuts. Aluminium salts are absorbed to a small degree, and it is not a food supplement. Alum applied to the vagina or taken as an abortifacient is dangerous. No clinical studies were retrieved for the traditional uses. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Alum carries an upper-grade listing in the Chinese classic, but the sentence that earns it is a conditional about a sublimated form, and the rest of the entry is about diarrhoea, sores and the eyes. The Greek text treats it as an external astringent whose first lesson is how to choose a good piece. Nothing sourced supports taking alum for cultivation. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Contraindications

Do not take alum internally or place it in the vagina on the strength of this entry. Large doses irritate the gut and can cause vomiting. Alum used as an abortifacient is dangerous. On the skin it can sting and dry. Aluminium compounds are a concern in people with kidney failure.
