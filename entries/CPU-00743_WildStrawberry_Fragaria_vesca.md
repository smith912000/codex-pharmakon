# Wild Strawberry

## *Fragaria vesca* L. | Family: Rosaceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Leaf/root — cool, dry, astringent (binding). Fruit — cool, moist, sweet-acid, nutritive.

**Training Phase:** Foundation / Restoration. A gentle tonic-astringent for the gut wall and mucosa; not a phase-shifting agent.

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Fragaria vesca* — the wild, woodland, or alpine strawberry — is a stoloniferous perennial of the Rosaceae, native across Eurasia and naturalised widely in North America. It is a woodland-edge and calcareous-bank plant: it wants dappled light, a well-drained loam, and a neutral-to-alkaline soil. This is the *original* strawberry of European herbals. It is **not** the strawberry of the modern supermarket, and the distinction matters at every level of this entry.

**Doctrine of Signatures / Mediaeval-Renaissance materia medica (DMM)**
In the northern European herbal tradition the plant is *Fragaria* (from *fragrans*, fragrant) — English **wild strawberry**, **woodland strawberry**; French **fraises des bois** ("strawberries of the woods"); German **Wald-Erdbeere**; Italian **fragolina di bosco**. The tradition splits the plant cleanly in two, and the split is a chemical one, not merely a rhetorical one:

- **Leaf and root (*Herba et Radix Fragariae*)** — the **astringent**. Classical indication: **fluxes**. Diarrhoea and dysentery; immoderate menses; "the whites"; and above all as a **gargle and mouth-wash** for a relaxed uvula, spongy or bleeding gums, and ulcers of the mouth and throat. The root, being woodier and more tannic, was held the stronger binder. The doctrinal logic is straightforward humoral mechanics: a *fluxing* tissue is a tissue that has lost tone, and an astringent restores tone by *drawing together*.
- **Fruit** — the **cooling nutritive**. Indicated for heat: fevers, thirst, "hot livers", hot cholericky stomachs, redness of the face. Explicitly *not* an astringent; the tradition never confused the two.

The most famous doctrinal datum attached to this plant is **Linnaeus's gout claim** — the Swedish naturalist held that eating strawberries in quantity relieved or cured his gout. This is an anecdote, and it should be filed as an anecdote: a well-known claim by a named individual about himself, transmitted as tradition. It is a *doctrinal datum* (it tells us what the tradition believed), not *evidence*. No trial retrieved this run tests strawberry against gout, urate, or xanthine oxidase in humans. **Do not present it as a finding.** [USER: verify the primary source before quoting Linnaeus directly.]

**Contemporary / folk-clinical**
Wild strawberry leaf persists in modern European folk practice as a gentle household tea for diarrhoea, as a mouthwash, and as a mild "spring cleaning" beverage — the last being an inherited depurative habit rather than a defined indication. Ethnopharmacological reviews record its folk use for **gastrointestinal, cardiovascular and urinary disorders**; a 2014 study explicitly set out to test whether an anti-inflammatory mechanism could underwrite those uses (Liberal et al., *J Ethnopharmacol*; PubMed, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2014.09.043)). Folk dermatological use — leaf as an emollient, antiseptic, and skin-protectant wash — is also recorded and has attracted the cosmetic industry (Couto et al., *Antioxidants*; PubMed, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox9020154)).

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**
The astringent doctrine here is unusually well-behaved, and worth taking seriously as an example of a tradition reading chemistry correctly through the wrong theory.

"Binding" is a *tactile* diagnosis. The herbalist put the leaf in the mouth, felt the pucker — the sensation of mucosal proteins precipitating and the surface tightening — and classified the plant by that sensation. That pucker is **tannin**: in *F. vesca* leaf, **ellagitannins** and **proanthocyanidins**, both confirmed in the leaf by HPLC-PDA-ESI/MS analysis alongside quercetin and kaempferol glucuronides (Liberal et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2014.09.043)), and both confirmed as principal constituents in the leaf extract characterised by Couto et al. ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox9020154)). Tannins bind and cross-link proteins. Applied to an inflamed, weeping mucosa, they form a precipitated protein layer at the surface — a physical film. Less exudate crosses it; less irritant reaches the nerve endings beneath it. The tissue *is*, in a real and local sense, drawn together.

So the doctrine maps. But note precisely *how far* it maps. It maps to a **topical, luminal, contact effect** — mouth, throat, gut lumen. It does not map to a systemic one. When the tradition extends the astringent claim inward — to the womb, to the kidneys, to the blood — it is extrapolating a surface phenomenon into a depth it never reaches. Orally ingested ellagitannins are largely not absorbed intact; they are hydrolysed and metabolised by the gut microbiota. The pucker stops at the gut wall. **The astringent doctrine is correct where it is tactile and wrong where it is theoretical.**

The fruit/leaf split is the tradition's own best insight. The fruit does not pucker, and the tradition did not call it astringent. It is chemically a different organ: **anthocyanins** (the red), **ascorbic acid**, and free and conjugated **ellagic acid** — the last quantified across cultivars at 60–341 µg/g frozen weight, and shown to be modifiable by cultivation practice, in *F. × ananassa* (Atkinson et al., *Ann Bot*; PubMed, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcj046) — **that study is garden strawberry, not *F. vesca*; the figure is cited here as congener context, not as a *vesca* value**). "Cooling nutritive" for a watery, acidic, vitamin-C-bearing, sugar-bearing fruit eaten in the heat of summer is not mystical. It is a reasonable description.

### Active Components

**Leaf (the astringent organ):**
- **Ellagitannins** — the defining class. Includes **HeT** (1-O-galloyl-2,3;4,6-bis-hexahydroxydiphenoyl-β-D-glucopyranose), a penta-esterified ellagitannin isolated *from strawberry leaves* and characterised as a plant-defence elicitor (Martos et al., *Plant Physiol Biochem*; PubMed, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.plaphy.2017.12.035)). Agrimoniin is reported in strawberry leaf material (Fecka et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27165293) — *F. × ananassa* cv. Senga Sengana; **congener data, named as such**).
- **Proanthocyanidins** (condensed tannins) — confirmed in *F. vesca* leaf extract.
- **Flavonol glycosides** — quercetin and kaempferol glucuronide derivatives, confirmed in *F. vesca* leaf.
- **Phenolic acids.**

**Root:** woodier, more tannic; specific *F. vesca* root phytochemistry is **data-sparse** in what was retrieved. Treat the traditional "root is stronger" claim as plausible-by-tissue-type and unverified. [USER: verify]

**Fruit:** anthocyanins, ascorbic acid, ellagic acid (free and conjugated), organic acids, sugars, volatile esters (the *fragrans* of the name — dramatically more concentrated in *F. vesca* than in *F. × ananassa*, which is why the woodland berry is worth growing at all).

**Allergen note:** strawberry fruit carries a **gibberellin-regulated protein (GRP)**, designated **Fra a GRP** — see Contraindications.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Wild strawberry is a **housekeeping plant**, not a lever. It belongs in the Foundation phase, where the work is the integrity of the gut wall and the oral mucosa rather than the modulation of state.

**Leaf** is indicated where a surface is weeping, loose, or raw and needs tone: mild non-infective diarrhoea; a sore or ulcerated mouth; spongy gums; a relaxed throat after voice work or illness. It is a *contact* agent. Deploy it where it can touch the tissue and expect nothing where it cannot.

**Fruit** is indicated as what it is: food. A cooling, hydrating, acid-sweet nutritive for hot dry conditions, taken in season. Kenetica does not need to dress this up.

The practitioner should read the fruit/leaf split as a **methodological lesson**, not just a fact about a plant. The same organism yields two agents with different chemistry, different directions, and different scopes. Assuming the properties of one from the other is exactly the error the tradition avoided and modern casual herbalism commits.

**Cultivator's note.** *F. vesca* is one of the easiest medicinal plants to establish and one of the most rewarding: it self-propagates by runners, tolerates part shade, and fruits from a woodland edge or a north-facing bed where little else will. Grow from seed (surface-sow, needs light, cold-stratify for reliability) or lift and divide runners in autumn. Alpine cultivars (e.g. runnerless 'Alexandria'-type strains) are convenient but drift from the wild type. **Harvest leaf after fruiting, in dry weather, from clean plants** — leaf polyphenol content varies with season and cultivar (demonstrated across three seasons in *F. × ananassa*: Fecka et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27165293)), so expect batch variability and do not assume a fixed potency. Dry fast in shade with airflow: slow-dried strawberry leaf is notorious for mustiness and moulds readily. **Wilted-but-not-dry leaf should not be used** — the folk caution against half-wilted Rosaceae leaf is worth respecting on general Rosaceae grounds even though no *vesca*-specific toxicology was retrieved to support it. ### Scientific Correlation

Graded honestly. This is a plant with a **modest, coherent, and largely preclinical** evidence base, and one prominent claim with nothing behind it.

**GRADE: MODERATE — leaf is chemically what the tradition said it was.**
The astringent identification is confirmed. Ellagitannins and proanthocyanidins are documented constituents of *F. vesca* leaf by two independent groups using chromatographic characterisation (Liberal et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2014.09.043); Couto et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox9020154)). Tannin–protein precipitation is settled chemistry. The mouth-wash and gargle use rests on a mechanism that is real, local, and requires no absorption. This is as good as a folk topical claim gets without a trial — and it should be said plainly that **there is no trial**.

**GRADE: WEAK–MODERATE (preclinical only) — anti-inflammatory activity.**
A hydroalcoholic *F. vesca* leaf extract inhibited nitrite production in LPS-stimulated macrophages at non-cytotoxic concentrations (80 and 160 µg/mL), an effect the authors attributed **probably to direct nitric oxide scavenging** rather than to transcriptional suppression. The extract also inhibited proteasome activity, causing accumulation of ubiquitinated proteins, and increased LC3-I→LC3-II conversion (an autophagy marker) (Liberal et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2014.09.043)). The authors themselves state that a role in cancer treatment "requires further studies." **Cell culture. Mouse macrophage line. Direct chemical scavenging is a weak mechanism to extrapolate from — it is the sort of activity almost any polyphenol-rich extract shows in a dish, and it does not survive the pharmacokinetic question of whether these compounds reach any tissue at those concentrations after an oral cup of tea. Treat this as consistent-with, not supportive-of.**

**GRADE: WEAK (preclinical) — membrane/cytoprotection.**
An ellagitannin-rich *F. vesca* leaf extract was non-toxic to erythrocytes and human microvascular endothelial cells (HMEC-1) up to 50 µg/mL and protected both against oxidative damage; biophysical measurements located the extract's action in the **hydrophilic (polar head) region** of the membrane, not the hydrocarbon core (Cyboran-Mikołajczyk et al., *Molecules*; PubMed, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27185865)). Mechanistically elegant and consistent with the tannins' known surface behaviour — which is, note, the same "acts at the surface" story as the astringency. But the paper's framing ("delaying the ageing process of organisms") is unsupported by its own data. **In vitro only.**

**GRADE: VERY WEAK — cosmetic/topical.**
A 2% leaf-extract hydrogel was stable, showed cutaneous compatibility on human repeat insult patch testing, and demonstrated antioxidant efficacy in vivo; the extract also inhibited tyrosinase in vitro (Couto et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox9020154)). This supports **tolerability** and a cosmetic antioxidant claim. It does not support a therapeutic dermatological claim.

**GRADE: NULL — Linnaeus's gout claim. Deflate.**
Nothing retrieved this run tests strawberry, in any part or preparation, against gout, hyperuricaemia, urate handling, or xanthine oxidase, in any model. The claim's entire evidential weight is one 18th-century naturalist's report about his own symptoms — uncontrolled, unblinded, n=1, self-reported, and made by a man with an obvious interest in the plants he loved. **Gout remits spontaneously.** Strawberries are seasonal. A man who eats strawberries every summer and whose gout sometimes improves in summer has established nothing. This is a charming piece of the plant's cultural record and **must not appear in this codex as a health claim.**

**GRADE: NULL — systemic astringency.**
The traditional extension of the binding doctrine to menorrhagia, leucorrhoea, and urinary flux has no retrieved support and no plausible mechanism, for the pharmacokinetic reason given above. **Deflate.**

**A note on evidence hygiene.** Much of the strawberry literature concerns *F. × ananassa*, the garden strawberry — an 18th-century octoploid hybrid of *F. chiloensis* and *F. virginiana*, and a **different plant** from *F. vesca*. Two studies cited above (Atkinson et al.; Fecka et al.) are *ananassa* work and are named as such wherever used. **No *ananassa* figure in this entry should be read as a *vesca* value.** The genus-level habit of treating "strawberry" as one entity is precisely the error this codex exists to resist.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

| System | Leaf / Root | Fruit |
|---|---|---|
| Humoral (Galenic) | Cold and dry, 2nd degree; **binding** | Cold and moist; nutritive, refrigerant |
| Action class | Astringent, mild anti-inflammatory (topical) | Nutritive, refrigerant, mildly diuretic |
| Direction | Inward, contracting; surface-acting | Downward, cooling; ordinary nourishment |
| Tissue affinity | Mucosa — oral, pharyngeal, intestinal | Blood, stomach, "the liver's heat" |

The Galenic reading is internally consistent and, for the leaf, chemically defensible. The fruit's classification is essentially a description of eating cold wet fruit in hot weather, which requires no theory to justify and gains nothing from one.

### Contraindications

- **Strawberry allergy — and here the standard framing needs correcting.** The common assumption is that strawberry reactions are Bet v 1–driven pollen-food cross-reactivity (Fra a 1 / birch). That is only part of the picture, and it is the milder part. A 2022 review states that in Rosaceae fruits **Bet v 1 and profilin homologues mainly cause oral symptoms**, whereas **gibberellin-regulated protein (GRP)** — a small, cationic, disulfide-bridged, **heat- and protease-resistant** allergen — drives *generalized* reactions, and identifies **Fra a GRP** in strawberry (Iizuka et al., *Front Allergy*; PubMed, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/falgy.2022.877553)). A documented case describes a 15-year-old with **generalized, exercise-cofactor-dependent** symptoms on oral food challenge whose serum reacted strongly to strawberry GRP but **not** to peach GRP or peach LTP (Inuo et al., *Allergy Asthma Clin Immunol*; PubMed, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13223-022-00692-0)). Practical consequences: (1) a birch-allergic patient with oral tingling is a different clinical object from a GRP-sensitised patient; (2) GRP is **heat-stable**, so cooking does not reliably detoxify; (3) **exercise and drugs act as cofactors** — a patient may tolerate strawberry at rest and react generalizedly on exertion. Strawberry also appeared among the commonest culprit foods in a challenge-confirmed adult food-allergy cohort (Mohamed et al., *Egypt J Immunol*; PubMed — no DOI indexed; PMID 34185455), with exercise identified as a risk factor for confirmed food allergy in that population. **Anyone with a history of generalized reaction to strawberry requires allergist assessment, not a codex entry.** Note that GRP work to date is fruit-focused; whether leaf preparations carry the allergen is **not established** in retrieved literature. [USER: verify]
- **Tannin load.** Leaf preparations are astringent by design. Sustained high-dose use may impair non-haem iron absorption and irritate an already-inflamed gut. Not for chronic daily use; not alongside iron supplementation at the same sitting.
- **Diarrhoea is a symptom, not a diagnosis.** Astringing an infective or inflammatory diarrhoea can be actively harmful. Fever, blood, mucus, severe pain, or duration beyond 48 hours: **stop and refer**.
- **Pregnancy and lactation.** No retrieved safety data for medicinal-dose leaf or root. Fruit as food is unremarkable; leaf preparations should be avoided for want of data, not because of a known hazard. Say the difference out loud.
- **Not a substitute for gout management.** Restating this because the Linnaeus anecdote is charming enough to be dangerous.
- **Correct identification.** *F. vesca* is straightforward, but **barren strawberry** (*Potentilla sterilis*) is a close mimic in the same habitats and yields no fruit; *Duchesnea indica* (mock/Indian strawberry) has yellow flowers (not white) and insipid, upward-facing fruit. None of these are seriously toxic — but a codex that tolerates misidentification tolerates anything.
