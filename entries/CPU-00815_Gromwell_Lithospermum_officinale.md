# Gromwell — Common Gromwell, Pearl Plant, Stoneseed, Gremil

## *Lithospermum officinale* L. | Family: Boraginaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry — a stony, drying, "descending" herb in the Galenic-Unani frame, classed among the lithontriptics (stone-breakers).
**Training Phase:** Descending / Clearing Phase — traditionally aimed at "stones" and urinary gravel; in Kenetica, a cautionary study-entry rather than a working remedy.

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian (AT).** The Mesopotamian herbal tradition recognised hard, seed-bearing "stone" plants among its urinary and gravel remedies, and the Boraginaceae stoneseeds fit that class of small, flint-hard nutlets used by decoction for difficulty in passing water. As with much of the cuneiform corpus, the attribution is general and traditional rather than fixed to a single verified tablet line.

**Dioscorides & the Greek Materia Medica (DMM).** Dioscorides describes *lithospermon* with unusual admiration, marvelling at seeds "like little pearls" or stones, hard and white — and prescribes them, drunk in white wine, to break the stone (*calculus*) and provoke urine. The very name he coins, *lithos* (stone) + *sperma* (seed), fixes the plant's identity for two millennia. Pliny repeats the account almost verbatim, calling it among the most miraculous-looking of herbs.

**Avicenna & the Unani synthesis (EP as received through the Canon).** The *Qanun* carries the stoneseed forward among the *lithontriptics* — cold, dry, drying agents given for urinary gravel, dysuria and "the stone." The therapeutic logic is inseparable from the plant's appearance.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** Gromwell is the textbook case of the **doctrine of signatures**: a plant whose seed is literally a tiny stone was read as heaven's signature that it treats stones in the body. The white, pearl-hard nutlets — a real botanical feature, silicified and calcified in the pericarp — made the doctrine almost irresistible to ancient and medieval physicians. The symbolism is elegant and the observation genuine; the pharmacology, as it turns out, does not follow the signature. What modern work has actually found in the genus is something quite different — an effect on hormones, not on stones.

### Active Components

- **Pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs):** hepatotoxic alkaloids characteristic of the Boraginaceae — the principal safety concern.
- **Caffeic-acid–derived polyphenols:** including **lithospermic acid** and rosmarinic-acid–type constituents, implicated in the genus's documented antigonadotropic/antithyrotropic activity.
- **Naphthoquinone pigments (shikonin-type):** the red root pigments characteristic of *Lithospermum* and its relatives, carrying anti-inflammatory and antigonadotropic activity in the wider genus (best characterised in *L. erythrorhizon*).
- **Silica / calcium in the pericarp:** the physical "stone" of the stoneseed.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Gromwell enters Kenetica primarily as a **cautionary and historical study-entry**, not an active protocol herb. Its traditional role — a lithontriptic decoction for urinary gravel — rests on the doctrine of signatures rather than on demonstrated stone-dissolving action. Its *real* documented pharmacology (antigonadotropic, antithyroid) makes unsupervised use hazardous. Where it is discussed in the Descending / Clearing Phase, it is as an object lesson in how appearance-based reasoning both generated and misdirected a tradition.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the notable modern finding about *Lithospermum* is not lithotripsy but endocrine activity. Winterhoff, Gumbinger and Sourgens documented **antigonadotropic activity** of *Lithospermum* and *Lycopus* species and their phenolic constituents in rats — the plants and their caffeic-acid polyphenols suppressing gonadotropin activity ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-2006-962360)). This lineage of work is why gromwell was historically investigated as a potential plant "anti-fertility" / anti-thyrotropic agent. In the wider genus, the shikonin naphthoquinones — reviewed by Chen and colleagues, who list **antigonadotropic** among shikonin's pharmacological actions ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.1100)), and by Yadav and co-workers in a full isolation-to-toxicology review ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2022.905755)) — carry anti-inflammatory and endocrine activity but also toxicity concerns.

**Evidence grade: traditional, with a real endocrine pharmacology and a real hazard.** The stone-breaking use is unsupported by modern evidence. The antigonadotropic/antithyroid activity is genuinely documented in the genus but has not been developed into any validated, safe therapeutic. The pyrrolizidine-alkaloid hepatotoxicity of Boraginaceae is a well-established class hazard. Note that several of the strongest pharmacology papers concern related *Lithospermum* species (notably *L. erythrorhizon*) rather than *L. officinale* specifically — the genus-level signal is clear, but species-specific human data are absent.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** cold and dry; a lithontriptic and diuretic, "drying" to the urinary tract.
- **TCM (by analogy):** the genus supplies *zi cao* (gromwell/shikonin root), a bitter, cold, blood-cooling and toxin-clearing drug used topically — but *zi cao* is drawn from *L. erythrorhizon* / *Arnebia*, **not** from *L. officinale*; do not substitute.
- **Ayurvedic (interpretive):** rasa — tikta (bitter), kashaya (astringent); virya — cooling (shita); vipaka — pungent (katu). Interpretive mapping, not a classical dravya.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** The stoneseed reaches the Mesopotamian record only as one of the hard, seed-bearing "stone" plants gathered among urinary and gravel remedies, and the entry itself states that the attribution is "general and traditional rather than fixed to a single verified tablet line." Under the standing blocking warning that governs every Assyrian substance identification and tablet reference, there is no secure line assigning this nutlet a place among the drugs that oppose a named causal agency, and certainly none framing it as a thing worked upon the self; the *asû*'s use, so far as it can be claimed at all, is a therapeutic decoction for difficulty passing water. The identification is too general and the framing too uncertain to reach beyond that. Cultivation status: **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides gives *lithospermon* an admiring but strictly therapeutic account — the pearl-hard seeds drunk in white wine to break the stone and provoke urine — and coins the very name from *lithos* and *sperma*. He carries no subtle-body doctrine that could assign the seed a cultivation status, and the whole use rests on the doctrine of signatures, appearance reasoning rather than any doctrine of the vital person. What the Greek tradition offers a cultivator here is instead a sharp lesson in his own discipline of attention: the marvel of the "little pearls" is a genuine, verifiable botanical mark — the silicified, calcified pericarp — and the correct identification, gathering and assay of that seed is a real practice of provenance, even though the signature it seems to promise (stone treats stone) proves false. That is a discipline of the observing eye, not a cultivation of the body. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The *Qanun* carries the stoneseed forward among the *lithontriptics* — classed cold and dry, drying agents given for gravel, dysuria and "the stone." The strongest cultivation idea in the Unani corpus, the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture as a finite vital endowment, is a longevity frame of the whole regimen and is nowhere assigned to this descending, stone-directed drug; no *mufarrih* or *ruh*-strengthening role is claimed for it, and its documented endocrine activity in the modern genus is, if anything, the opposite of a life-nourishing one. The seed is attested therapeutically within the tradition but carries no recorded cultivation status. **(b) framed:** claimed as a lithontriptic, with no cultivation status sourced.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing here for a cultivator to take, and the honest use of this entry is as a warning. Gromwell's whole traditional standing rests on a resemblance — a seed like a stone — that the pharmacology does not honour; what the genus actually carries is antigonadotropic and antithyroid activity atop a pyrrolizidine-alkaloid liver hazard, so the plant interferes with exactly the hormonal signalling a cultivator would want left intact. It builds no vitality and conserves no innate heat. Keep it on the shelf as an object lesson in how appearance-based reasoning can both generate a tradition and point it in the wrong direction — study it, do not swallow it.

### Contraindications

⚠️ **PYRROLIZIDINE ALKALOIDS — HEPATOTOXIC.** As a member of the Boraginaceae, gromwell carries pyrrolizidine alkaloids, which are cumulatively hepatotoxic and can cause veno-occlusive liver disease; some are genotoxic/carcinogenic. This alone contraindicates habitual internal use.

⚠️ **ANTIGONADOTROPIC / ANTITHYROID ACTIVITY.** According to PubMed, *Lithospermum* and its phenolic constituents suppress gonadotropin activity in animal models ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-2006-962360)); the genus has a documented antithyrotropic action. This means the plant can interfere with reproductive and thyroid hormone signalling — a serious, non-obvious hazard for anyone of reproductive age, anyone trying to conceive, or anyone with thyroid disease.

⚠️ **PREGNANCY AND LACTATION — ABSOLUTELY CONTRAINDICATED.** The combination of PA hepatotoxicity (with documented risk of fetal liver injury across the Boraginaceae) and antigonadotropic/anti-fertility activity makes internal use in pregnancy or lactation categorically unsafe.

- **Reproductive and thyroid conditions:** avoid — the endocrine activity is the opposite of inert.
- **Liver disease:** avoid — PA burden.
- **Do not confuse with TCM *zi cao*** (*L. erythrorhizon*/*Arnebia*), a different species with a different, topical use.
