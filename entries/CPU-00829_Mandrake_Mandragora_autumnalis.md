# Mandrake — Mandragora · Dudaim · Yabruḥ · "Circe's Plant" · Devil's Herb

## *Mandragora autumnalis* Bertol. | Family: Solanaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM, EP, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry in the third degree (Galenic) — a narcotic-refrigerant of the highest, near-lethal potency
**Training Phase:** Phase V — The Deadly Threshold (surgical narcotics and deliriant poisons; masters only)

---

### Origin & Tradition

☠️ **LETHAL PLANT — READ THE CONTRAINDICATIONS FIRST.** Mandrake is not a beginner's herb, a foraging food, or a recreational agent. It is one of the most dangerous plants in this entire Codex: a tropane-alkaloid deliriant with a narrow margin between its sedative dose and anticholinergic poisoning, and it sends people to hospital every year when its rosette is mistaken for borage or spinach. It is included here because no plant more perfectly demonstrates the Codex thesis — that folklore and pharmacology are the *same information* written in two scripts.

Mandrake is the landmark of Western plant-lore. No other species accumulated so dense a mythology, and — crucially — every strand of that mythology is a symptom, a dose, or a use of its real chemistry.

**Assyro-Babylonian (BM).** Mesopotamian medicine knew powerful Solanaceous narcotics; mandrake and henbane sit in the same class of sleep-bringing, pain-dulling, and spirit-affecting drugs administered by the *asû* and *āšipu*. As with other cuneiform plant identifications, the exact Akkadian binomial is philologically uncertain and should be flagged [USER: verify]; the *functional* role — a soporific and analgesic of fearsome strength — is secure across the Near-Eastern stream.

**Hebrew — the Dudaim (SBJ).** Mandrake enters scripture as the *dûdā'îm* of Genesis 30:14–16 — the "mandrakes" Reuben gathers in the wheat harvest, which Rachel bargains from Leah in exchange for a night with Jacob, in an episode saturated with the plant's fertility and aphrodisiac reputation. The Song of Songs (7:13) names the *dûdā'îm* again among the fragrances of love. This is the scriptural seed of the whole Western fertility-and-love doctrine of mandrake.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM) and the Circe complex (EP).** Here mandrake reaches its pharmacological peak. In *De Materia Medica*, Dioscorides gives the single most important pre-modern anaesthesia text: the root, boiled down in wine, is given to those "about to be cut or cauterised" so that they "do not feel the pain" — a *genuine, documented surgical anaesthetic*, with an explicit warning about dose. Mandrake is also *Circe's plant* (*kirkaia*), the sorceress's herb of the *Odyssey* tradition, and Pythagorean and later sources describe its anthropomorphic root. Theophrastus (EP) records the ritual of drawing three circles with a sword and cutting the root facing west — the germ of the "harvesting" mythos.

**Egyptian (EP).** New Kingdom Egyptian art and love-poetry feature the golden mandrake fruit ("love-apple") as an erotic and euphoriant symbol, passed at banquets and depicted being smelled. The fruit's fragrance and mild intoxication feed directly into the aphrodisiac doctrine.

**Ayurveda (SS).** South Asian and Himalayan mandrakes (the *Mandragora* of the region) enter the Sanskrit stream as potent narcotic-sedative and analgesic drugs (sometimes grouped with the *lakṣmaṇā* / powerful-root complex, an identification that is contested and should be treated as [USER: verify]); the functional role again is sleep-bringing and pain-killing, consistent with tropane pharmacology.

**The folklore, itemised.** The forked, often bifurcated taproot resembles a human body; hence the legend that mandrake is half-human and *screams* when uprooted, a scream that kills or maddens the harvester — so a dog is tied to the root to pull it, dying in the human's stead. It is the witch's herb *par excellence*, a core ingredient of the "flying ointment," and a charm for love, fertility, wealth, and protection.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** This is the Codex's central case study, so map it carefully. Mandrake's chemistry is a suite of **tropane alkaloids — (l)-hyoscyamine, its racemate atropine, and scopolamine (hyoscine)** — competitive antagonists at muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, both peripheral and central. *Every* major legend is a face of that pharmacology:

- **The "screaming root that kills the harvester" → the plant is genuinely deadly, and the poison is in the root.** The alkaloids are concentrated in the thick taproot. The lethal reputation and the elaborate dog-uprooting ritual are folk risk-management encoding a true fact: pulling and handling — and above all *eating* — this root can kill. The scream is a mnemonic for lethality.
- **The witch's "flying ointment" and sorcery (Circe) → central anticholinergic delirium.** Muscarinic antagonism in the CNS produces vivid hallucinations, disorientation, and a genuine *sensation of flight* and bodily transformation — the classic deliriant syndrome. Applied in fat to mucous membranes or abraded skin, tropanes are absorbed transdermally. The "flight" of the witch is scopolamine crossing the blood-brain barrier. Circe's power to transform men into beasts is the deliriant's dissolution of the boundary between self and animal.
- **The surgical "wine of mandrake" → real anaesthesia.** Central sedation plus the plant's overall CNS depression, combined (in practice) with the wine's ethanol, produced a workable pre-modern narcosis for cautery and amputation. This is the *pharmacologically soundest* of all mandrake doctrines — scopolamine remained in the modern anaesthetist's kit (as "twilight sleep" and a pre-medication/antisialagogue) into the twentieth century.
- **The fertility / aphrodisiac doctrine (Dudaim, the love-apple) → largely signature and folklore.** This strand has the *weakest* pharmacological footing. Its origins are the anthropomorphic (and frankly suggestive) root shape — pure doctrine-of-signatures — plus the sweet, narcotic, euphoriant fragrance of the ripe fruit and the general disinhibition of a low anticholinergic/sedative dose. There is no credible evidence mandrake enhances fertility or is a true aphrodisiac; anticholinergics, if anything, impair sexual and secretory function. This doctrine should be **deflated**: it is the mythology of a suggestive shape and an intoxicating smell, not a pharmacological aphrodisiac.

So mandrake splits cleanly into a **pharmacologically real axis** (anaesthetic, sedative, deliriant, deadly) and a **signature-folklore axis** (fertility, love-charm, screaming homunculus). The Codex's job is to keep them distinct — and the science does.

### Active Components

- **(l)-Hyoscyamine** — the principal tropane alkaloid; racemises to **atropine**. Competitive muscarinic (M1–M5) antagonist; peripheral antimuscarinic effects (mydriasis, tachycardia, dry secretions, ileus) and CNS effects.
- **Scopolamine (hyoscine)** — the tertiary-amine tropane that most readily crosses into the CNS; the chief agent of sedation, amnesia, antiemesis, and delirium/"flight."
- **Minor tropanes** — apoatropine, cuscohygrine and related alkaloids.
- **Solanaceous glycoalkaloids / "solanum alkaloids"** — contributing the gastrointestinal irritation noted in poisoning case series.
- Concentration is **highly variable** by part, season and specimen — the root richest — which is precisely why the dose margin is so treacherous.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Mandrake belongs to Phase V, "The Deadly Threshold," and is presented as a *studied object*, not a practice. Its historical Kenetica functions are three: (1) the archetypal **surgical narcotic** — the wine of mandrake before cautery/amputation, the ancestor of anaesthesia; (2) a **deliriant/oneirogen** of the witch's-ointment tradition — the pharmacological engine of "flying" and sorcery; and (3) the **teaching plant** for the Codex's central lesson on reading folklore as chemistry. No self-administration protocol is offered. The only responsible modern engagement is intellectual and, at most, botanical (growing/identifying it to *avoid* poisoning).

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the modern clinical literature confirms both the deadliness and the specific anticholinergic mechanism that generated mandrake's entire mythology.

- **The anaesthetic/anticholinergic axis is pharmacologically real.** Mandrake poisoning presents as a textbook central and peripheral **anticholinergic syndrome** — the same muscarinic-blockade physiology that, at controlled sub-toxic doses, produced its sedative and antisecretory usefulness. A documented case describes *Mandragora autumnalis* poisoning in a 72-year-old woman who ate the plant mistaking it for edible *Borago officinalis*, presenting with anticholinergic syndrome from its tropane alkaloids (Piccillo et al., *Int J Clin Pract* 2006; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1368-5031.2006.00864.x)). The scopolamine limb of this same syndrome is why hyoscine remains a clinically validated antiemetic — a systematic review and meta-analysis found scopolamine reduced reported nausea versus placebo (RR 0.35, 95% CI 0.24–0.52) in motion sickness (Zhang et al., *Acta Otolaryngol* 2024; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/00016489.2024.2397556)). The ancient surgical and sleep doctrines rest on genuine CNS-active chemistry.
- **The lethality and dog-uprooting mythos encode true danger.** A case series of *six* Mandragora autumnalis poisonings from the Sicilian countryside documents severe, sometimes "atropine-like" toxicity requiring vital-function support, decontamination, and — in severe cases — antidotal **physostigmine** (Piccillo et al., *Eur J Emerg Med* 2002; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/00063110-200212000-00010)). That physostigmine (an anticholinesterase) is the antidote is the final proof the syndrome is anticholinergic. The "screaming root that kills" is real toxicology.
- **The fertility/aphrodisiac doctrine is unsupported.** No retrieved clinical evidence supports mandrake as a fertility agent or aphrodisiac; the pharmacology (antimuscarinic, secretion-suppressing, sedating) argues against it. Graded: **doctrine of signatures, not pharmacology.**

Honest evidence-grade summary: the **anaesthetic/sedative/deliriant/lethal** axis is strongly corroborated by mechanism and modern poisoning case series; the **fertility/aphrodisiac** axis is not corroborated and is best explained as root-shape signature plus fragrance-euphoria.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Greek/Galenic–Unani:** cold and dry in the third degree; a *narcotic* (*narkōtikon*) — one of the classical "cold" stupefacients, grouped with henbane, opium poppy, and hemlock.
- **Ayurveda (SS):** where the Himalayan *Mandragora* is admitted, a potent *nidrājanana* (sleep-inducing) / *vedanāsthāpana* (analgesic) narcotic; precise rasa/guṇa/vīrya/vipāka grading is not securely attested for this species and is **not invented here** [USER: verify].
- **TCM:** **not attested** — *Mandragora* is not a classical Chinese materia medica plant; no tag assigned.
- **Assyro-Babylonian / Egyptian:** grouped with the strong soporific–narcotic drugs; specific humoral grading not preserved.

### Contraindications

☠️ **SEVERELY TOXIC — POTENTIALLY DEADLY. DO NOT INGEST.**

- **Anticholinergic poisoning / narrow margin.** All parts — the root most of all — contain tropane alkaloids capable of producing life-threatening anticholinergic syndrome: the classic "hot as a hare, blind as a bat, dry as a bone, red as a beet, mad as a hatter" — hyperthermia, mydriasis and blurred vision, dry mucous membranes and skin, flushing, urinary retention, ileus, tachyarrhythmia, agitated delirium, hallucination, seizures, coma and death. The dose separating sedation from poisoning is dangerously small and unpredictable because alkaloid content varies enormously between specimens and seasons.
- **Foraging mistaken-identity is the leading hazard.** The autumn/winter leaf rosette is repeatedly mistaken for **borage (*Borago officinalis*)**, **spinach**, or other edible greens, causing documented hospitalisations (Piccillo et al. 2006, 2002 — DOIs above). Never eat wild rosette greens of uncertain identity.
- **No safe recreational or "entheogenic" use.** The deliriant experience is dysphoric, amnesic, and toxic, not visionary in a controllable sense; deaths and severe poisonings are well documented.
- **Contraindicated absolutely** in children, pregnancy (fertility folklore notwithstanding), the elderly, and anyone with glaucoma, cardiac disease, prostatic obstruction, or on other anticholinergic/QT-affecting drugs.
- **First aid / treatment** is a medical emergency: supportive care, decontamination, and, in severe anticholinergic toxicity, **physostigmine** under clinical supervision (per the cited case series). Seek emergency services immediately for any suspected ingestion.
