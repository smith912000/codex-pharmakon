# I-518 | Dandelion (*Taraxacum officinale* F.H. Wigg.)

**Volume I — Plant-Based Substances**
**Family:** Asteraceae (tribe Cichorieae)
**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (hepatic, diuretic, digestive bitter) primary | ◈ Jing (nutritive mineral-rich) secondary

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary binomial:** *Taraxacum officinale* F.H. Wigg. (sensu lato, 1780) — an apomictic aggregate of >2,000 microspecies (similar taxonomic situation to *Alchemilla vulgaris* I-504)

**Synonyms & historical names:**
- *Leontodon taraxacum* L.
- *Taraxacum dens-leonis* Desf.
- Historical: *Herba Taraxaci* (pharmaceutical); *Dens leonis* (lion's tooth — medieval Latin)

**Common names:**
- English: Dandelion, lion's tooth, priest's crown, blowball, clocks
- German: *Gewöhnlicher Löwenzahn* (common lion's tooth), *Pusteblume* (blowing flower — seedhead)
- French: *Pissenlit* (piss-in-bed — diuretic effect), *Dent de lion* (lion's tooth)
- Italian: *Tarassaco*, *Dente di leone*
- Spanish: *Diente de león*
- Arabic: *Hindibā* (chicory class; *Taraxacum* included)
- Chinese: 蒲公英 (*pú gōng yīng*) — Chinese Pharmacopoeia 2020 official monograph; *T. mongolicum* Hand.-Mazz. and *T. sinicum* Kitag. are the primary Chinese species
- Japanese: *Tanpopo* (タンポポ)
- Hebrew: *Sharbit Hasadeh* (field weed)

**Etymology:** *Taraxacum* = Arabic *ṭarakhshaqūn* or *ṭarakhshagōg* (bitter herb; possibly from Persian *talkh-chakōk*); *officinale* = "of the officina/apothecary" = pharmacopoeial plant. "Dandelion" = French *dent de lion* (lion's tooth) corrupted through English → "dandy lion" → "dandelion." French *pissenlit* = "piss in bed" = direct folk pharmacological name for diuretic effect.

**Botanical authentication:**
- Rosette perennial herb; deeply toothed leaves (the "lion's tooth" = runcinate-pinnatifid; backward-pointing lobes); single hollow scape (flower stalk) bearing single capitulum (composite flower head); flowers: all ray florets, bright yellow; characteristic clock-like white spherical seedhead (achenes with feathery pappus, wind-dispersed); milky white latex throughout (sap in all parts); taproot thick, fleshy, bitter, dark brown externally/white internally
- Apomictic species complex: >2,000 microspecies; pharmacologically treated as aggregate; chemical profiles broadly similar across the complex
- **Harvest:** Root (autumn for highest inulin; spring for highest bitter compounds); leaves (spring — most tender, highest mineral content); flowers (spring, used in culinary/wine preparations)
- Universally naturalized; one of the most widely distributed flowering plants globally; grows in every temperate and subtropical region where the ground has been disturbed by agriculture or settlement

Wider convergence is limited primarily by the plant's geographic distribution and the inadequacy of identification in earlier (Dioscorides-era) texts.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

Dandelion is one of the most pharmacologically underappreciated plants in the Codex — so common as to be overlooked, so widely naturalized as to be considered a weed rather than a medicine. Yet the convergence of Islamic medicine, Chinese pharmacopoeia, European folk medicine, and North American indigenous use (post-naturalization adoption by indigenous peoples) consistently identifies the same pharmacological properties: hepatic/bitter digestive stimulant, diuretic, and mild anti-inflammatory.

The bitter sesquiterpene lactone compounds (taraxacin, taraxacerin, germacranolide class) provide the primary hepatic-stimulating and digestive bitter properties — the same bitterness that makes dandelion leaves a nutritious spring green food also drives the pharmacological actions. The inulin content (25–40% DW in autumn root) provides prebiotic activity that connects dandelion to the modern microbiome-modulating botanical category.

The Arabic naming convergence (*ṭarakhshaqūn* → *Taraxacum*) illustrates the transmission route: Islamic physicians systematized dandelion's hepatic properties, European herbalists received this knowledge through Arabic-Latin translation in the 11th–12th centuries, and the plant name itself carries the Arabic pharmacological tradition in its scientific designation.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Bitter sesquiterpene lactones (root and leaf — primary hepatic/digestive actives):**
- **Taraxacin** (eudesmanolide-type sesquiterpene lactone): 0.1–0.5% DW in root; primary bitter compound; hepatic stimulant
- **Taraxacerin**: related sesquiterpene lactone glycoside
- **Germacranolide derivatives**: 0.1–0.3% DW; include taraxinic acid β-D-glucopyranosyl ester (TetraHydroLactucopicorin = THLP); anti-inflammatory; hepatoprotective in vitro
- **Lactupicrin** (lactucopicrin): bitter sesquiterpene lactone; sedative? (debated; low concentration in dandelion relative to lettuce-type species); anti-inflammatory
- **Total bitter index:** high (dandelion root bitterness threshold ~1:200 dilution; benchmark bitter herb)

**Inulin (root — prebiotic):**
- **Inulin:** 20–40% DW in autumn root (highest at harvest time — September/October); 2–15% in spring root
- Inulin = β-fructooligosaccharide polymer; not digested by human enzymes; fermented by *Bifidobacterium*, *Lactobacillus* in colon → prebiotic activity; butyrate production; bowel health
- **Seasonal variation critical:** spring root = higher bitter compound, less inulin; autumn root = higher inulin, less bitter; harvest timing determines primary pharmacological profile

**Phytosterols:**
- **β-Sitosterol, stigmasterol, campesterol**: 0.2–0.8% DW; anti-inflammatory; mild cholesterol-lowering

**Triterpenes:**
- **Taraxasterol, ψ-taraxasterol**: 0.5–2% DW; primary triterpenes unique to *Taraxacum*; anti-inflammatory (5-LOX and COX-2 inhibition); hepatoprotective
- **β-Amyrin**: 0.2–0.8% DW; anti-inflammatory

**Flavonoids:**
- **Luteolin-7-glucoside**: 0.3–1.5% DW in leaves; anti-inflammatory; COX-2 inhibition
- **Apigenin-7-glucoside**: 0.2–1.0% DW
- **Quercetin derivatives**: minor
- **Chicoric acid** (caffeic acid tartrate ester): 0.5–3% DW in leaves; immunostimulatory (similar to echinacoside in Echinacea); anti-inflammatory; antiviral

**Carotenoids:**
- **Lutein, zeaxanthin**: 0.3–1.5 mg/100g leaves; retinal health; antioxidant
- **β-Carotene (pro-Vitamin A)**: 3–6 mg/100g leaves (higher than most culinary herbs)
- **Xanthophylls**: characteristic yellow-orange pigmentation

**Vitamins and minerals (nutritional significance):**
- **Vitamin A** (as carotenoids): dandelion leaves among highest provitamin A content of any common vegetable
- **Vitamin C**: 35 mg/100g fresh leaves (comparable to citrus)
- **Vitamin K₁**: 778 μg/100g — VERY HIGH (anticoagulant significance — see Section 8)
- **Potassium**: 397 mg/100g leaves; notable diuretic-mineral relationship (loses K in diuresis; dandelion partly replaces lost K)
- **Calcium**: 187 mg/100g; **Iron**: 3.1 mg/100g; **Manganese**: 0.3 mg/100g

**Caffeic acid derivatives (leaves and root):**
- **Chlorogenic acid**: 0.3–1.5% DW
- **Caffeic acid, chicoric acid**: anti-inflammatory; antioxidant

**Latex (milky sap):**
- **Terpenoids**: various sesquiterpenes in latex; bitter
- **Rubber** (natural polyisoprene): trace; *Taraxacum kok-saghyz* = Russian dandelion bred specifically for rubber content (WWII rubber alternative development)

**PhEur standardization:**
- *Taraxaci radix* (root): ≥12% inulin-type fructans (PhEur)
- *Taraxaci folium* (leaf): ≥0.5% caffeic acid derivatives (PhEur)

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Hepatic stimulation (bile secretion — choleretic):**
Bitter sesquiterpene lactones (taraxacin, taraxacerin, germacranolides) stimulate bile secretion from the gallbladder and liver via vagal reflex (bitter taste receptor TAS2R → afferent vagal → efferent cholinergic → hepatocyte/gallbladder bile production). This is the same bitter-reflex mechanism operating in other bitter botanicals (gentian, artichoke, centaury); taraxacin provides the stimulus. Increased bile flow:
- Improves fat digestion (bile emulsification)
- Promotes cholesterol elimination through bile
- Anti-constipating (bile acids stimulate peristalsis)
- Consistent with traditional "liver herb" and digestive use across traditions

**Diuretic (aquaretic):**
Dandelion leaf produces dose-dependent increase in urine production in human volunteers (Clare BA et al. 2009 — see Section 6). Mechanism incompletely characterized; possible contributions:
- Potassium-retaining action (unlike pharmaceutical diuretics that deplete K, dandelion's high K content partially replaces urinary K losses)
- Flavonoid-mediated renal tubular water reabsorption inhibition
- Caffeic acid derivatives' renal osmolarity effects
- NET EFFECT: mild but consistent diuretic with self-balancing potassium profile (dietary K content partially offsets diuretic K loss)

**Anti-inflammatory:**
- Taraxasterol: selective 5-LOX inhibition; COX-2 suppression; NF-κB pathway inhibition
- Luteolin, apigenin: same COX-2 inhibition pattern as Lamiaceae entries; recurrent
- Chicoric acid: TNF-α and IL-6 suppression in LPS-stimulated macrophages
- Combined: mild systemic anti-inflammatory; relevant to hepatic and urogenital inflammation

**Antioxidant:**
β-Carotene + lutein/zeaxanthin + Vitamin C + caffeic acid derivatives = comprehensive antioxidant coverage; DPPH IC50 competitive with pharmaceutical antioxidants; relevant to hepatic oxidative stress reduction

**Hypoglycemic (preliminary):**
- Taraxacin and chicoric acid: α-glucosidase inhibition (reduces postprandial glucose absorption; same mechanism as acarbose)
- Inulin: slows glucose absorption (viscous fiber mechanism)
- *T. mongolicum* aqueous extract: blood glucose reduction in streptozotocin-diabetic mice (animal model; consistent Level D evidence)

**Prebiotic (inulin):**
Autumn root inulin: fermented by colonic flora → butyrate + short-chain fatty acids → colonocyte energy source; increased *Bifidobacterium* and *Lactobacillus* populations; reduced *Clostridium* and potentially pathogenic species. Relevant to bowel health, immune system priming, and potentially systemic inflammation (gut-liver axis).

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Diuretic — Level B:**
- **Clare BA et al. 2009** (*Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine* 15:929-934) — n=17 healthy volunteers; dandelion leaf extract (1 mL tincture 1:0.8, 8 mL/day); significant increase in urine production for 5 hours post first dose; significant increase in urinary frequency; urinary volume increased 13% at hour 5; Level B (small pilot; Level A quality needed)

**Hepatic/digestive — Level C:**
- Commission E: APPROVED for disturbance of bile flow, stimulation of diuresis, loss of appetite, dyspepsia
- Multiple German-language clinical series; positive for digestive complaints; pre-RCT era evidence; consistent with mechanism

**Anti-inflammatory (Chinese *T. mongolicum*) — Level B:**
- Multiple Chinese RCTs on 蒲公英 (*pú gōng yīng*) preparations for acute mastitis (breast inflammation) and breast abscess: significantly reduces pain, swelling, and inflammatory markers vs conventional treatment alone; Level A (Chinese trial quality variable by Western RCT standards)
- Chinese Pharmacopoeia approved for: heat-clearing, detoxicating; mastitis, furunculosis, urinary tract infection, liver inflammation

**Antidiabetic — Level D:**
- Multiple animal studies; consistent reduction in blood glucose in diabetic animal models; insufficient human data; not a clinical recommendation

**Kidney stone prevention — Level D:**
- Preliminary in vitro data: dandelion extract inhibits calcium oxalate crystal adhesion to renal tubular cells; insufficient clinical data; not a recommendation

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**"Pissenlit" and the self-documenting diuretic:**
The French *pissenlit* (piss-in-bed) is among the most direct folk pharmacological names in any language — naming the plant's effect with complete honesty. This folk name is documented from at least the 14th century, indicating that dandelion's diuretic effect was so consistent and notable in traditional use that it defined the plant's primary cultural identity. The modern clinical trial (Clare 2009) confirms what French peasants apparently knew well enough to name a plant after: dandelion leaf produces a measurable diuretic response within hours of consumption.

**The weed that feeds — nutritional economy:**
Dandelion's status as a common springtime food plant across Northern Europe (dandelion salad, dandelion wine, dandelion greens in cooked preparations) reflects its exceptional nutritional density in a historical context where winter stores were exhausted and scurvy was a genuine spring concern. Dandelion leaves at peak spring growth provide Vitamin A (β-carotene), Vitamin C, Vitamin K, calcium, iron, and potassium in concentrations competitive with the best cultivated vegetables. The "weed" status in modern industrial lawn culture masks what was historically recognized as a valuable spring tonic food.

**Chinese mastitis treatment:**
The TCM designation 蒲公英 specifically for mastitis (breast inflammation) is one of the most clinically specific traditional indications in the Codex — not a general "anti-inflammatory" but targeting a specific inflammatory condition in a specific anatomical location. The chicoric acid and taraxasterol anti-inflammatory mechanism, combined with antibacterial activity against *Staphylococcus aureus* (primary mastitis pathogen), provides a plausible mechanism for this specific traditional application. Chinese clinical studies confirm the anti-mastitis indication with Level A-equivalent evidence in the Chinese regulatory context.

**Dandelion rubber (*T. kok-saghyz*):**
During World War II, Germany and the Soviet Union developed *Taraxacum kok-saghyz* (Kazakh dandelion/Russian dandelion) as an emergency natural rubber source when Asian rubber supply was disrupted. The root latex of this *Taraxacum* species contains 8–12% rubber (compared to <1% in common dandelion). European and American research programs during WWII attempted commercial rubber production from dandelion. While never commercially competitive with *Hevea brasiliensis* (rubber tree), the program established that dandelion contains pharmacologically inert (from an antimicrobial/anti-inflammatory standpoint) but industrially significant latex compounds — illustrating that even within a single plant genus, chemical profiles diverge dramatically between species.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Preparations and doses:**
- **Dried root tea:** 3–4 g per 250 mL water, decoct 10 minutes; 2–3× daily; autumn harvest root for inulin-rich preparation; spring root for maximum bitter compound; standard hepatic/digestive use
- **Dried leaf tea:** 3–4 g per 250 mL boiling water, steep 10 minutes; 3× daily; primary diuretic preparation
- **Fresh leaf:** 25–50 g per day as salad; nutritive + diuretic; traditional spring tonic dose
- **Root tincture (1:5, 25% ethanol):** 4–10 mL 3× daily; Commission E approved dose range
- **Standardized dry extract:** Commission E dose = 3–4 g root per day; 1–2 g leaf per day; extract equivalent proportionally

**Commission E approved:**
- Root: bile duct complaints, loss of appetite, indigestion; 3–4 g dried root × 3/day
- Leaf: diuresis; urinary tract inflammatory disorders; kidney gravel; 4–10 g dried leaf × 3/day (or equivalent tincture)

**EMA traditional use:** Root: dyspeptic complaints and mild digestive disturbances; diuretic; Leaf: temporary increase in urine production.

**CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- Bile duct obstruction, empyema of gallbladder, intestinal occlusion: avoid (choleretic activity may worsen these conditions if bile cannot flow normally)
- Allergy to Asteraceae/Compositae family: possible cross-reactivity (shared sesquiterpene lactone class with Asteraceae allergens — mugwort, ragweed)
- **WARFARIN / ANTICOAGULANT THERAPY:** **Very high Vitamin K₁ content (778 μg/100g leaves) — can significantly affect warfarin INR if consumption changes.** Not contraindicated per se (consistent daily intake is manageable); but patients on warfarin should maintain consistent dandelion intake and be warned not to suddenly increase/decrease large quantities of dandelion greens
- Pregnancy: traditional preparation considered safe at culinary doses; high-dose extract — insufficient data; avoid pharmacological doses

**Drug interactions:**
- Warfarin: Vitamin K₁ content → reduced anticoagulant effect at high leaf intake; monitor INR; maintain consistent dietary intake
- Diuretics: additive diuretic effect; monitor potassium (dandelion partially self-compensates via K content)
- Lithium: diuretic-mediated reduced lithium clearance → lithium toxicity risk; monitor lithium levels
- Hypoglycemics/insulin: chicoric acid α-glucosidase inhibition + inulin glucose blunting; additive blood glucose lowering; monitor
