### Botanical Identity
Perennial 30–80cm; pinnate leaves with alternating large and small leaflets (distinctive in the Rosaceae); long spike of small yellow five-petalled flowers (June–August); hooked seed burrs that cling to clothing and fur (like cleavers — another velcro-seed plant); pleasantly aromatic (apricot-like scent from volatile oil); native to Europe, North Africa, western Asia; common in hedgerows, grassland, woodland margins on calcareous soils. The aerial parts (harvested at full flower, dried) are the drug material — distinctive for the pleasant tea (unlike many bitter liver herbs); mild, aromatic, slightly astringent flavour.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 De Materia Medica (DMM)

Dioscorides (*Book IV, Chapter 41*):
> *"Eupatorion of Mithridates: the decoction drunk is good for those bitten by snakes and for those who have drunk poisons. Good for liver conditions and for stopping diarrhoea. Applied as a poultice it helps inflamed wounds, fractures of bones, and cleans old ulcers."*

The attribution to Mithridates VI (the toxicologist-king who immunised himself against all known poisons by taking small daily doses) places agrimony within the antidote-poison tradition of ancient pharmacology. Whether Mithridates actually used it is uncertain; the attribution elevated its status in the canon.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine (CM)

Ibn Sīnā on agrimony:
> *"Warm and dry in the second degree. Strengthens the liver and benefits those with liver weakness. Stops diarrhoea. Helps wounds when applied. The decoction is good for those with 'snake-venom' conditions."* (interpreted as septicaemia/systemic infection in modern terms)

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (EP)

Bitter liver herbs in Egyptian preparations for "weakness of the liver" and "yellow skin" (jaundice) — consistent with the hepatoprotective application.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)

Liver-strengthening bitter plants in Babylonian medical texts for jaundice, liver pain, and digestive failure.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Hepatoprotective (Primary)

**Agrimoniin + ursolic acid + isofraxidin + quercetin**:
- Agrimoniin: upregulates Nrf2 → increases glutathione-S-transferase and SOD in hepatocytes → reduces hepatocellular oxidative damage
- Isofraxidin (coumarin): mild cholagogue → stimulates bile secretion → improves liver detoxification throughput
- Ursolic acid: reduces hepatic steatosis (fat accumulation) in animal models; reduces ALT/AST in toxic hepatitis models
- Quercetin: inhibits hepatic NF-κB → reduces TNF-α-driven liver inflammation

### 5.2 Astringent / Anti-Diarrhoeal

**Agrimoniin + condensed tannins**: The tannin-rich profile (8–15% total) makes agrimony one of the most astringent herbs in the Codex:
- Protein precipitation in gut lumen → reduces mucosal permeability → anti-diarrhoeal
- Antimicrobial against gut pathogens (astringent tannins create inhospitable environment for bacteria)
- Haemostatic in gut haemorrhage (protein precipitation → sealing of haemorrhagic vessels)

### 5.3 Antidiabetic

**Quercetin + luteolin + catechins**:
- Aldose reductase inhibition → reduces conversion of glucose to sorbitol → reduces diabetic complications (neuropathy, retinopathy)
- PPAR-γ agonism (flavonoids) → improves insulin sensitivity
- Alpha-glucosidase inhibition → reduces post-prandial glucose spike

### 5.4 Antitumour (Agrimoniin)

**Agrimoniin**: At concentrations above anti-diarrhoeal threshold → induces apoptosis in cancer cell lines via:
- Caspase-3/7 activation (intrinsic pathway)
- Inhibition of PI3K/Akt survival signalling
- Active in colon, liver, and leukaemia cell lines in vitro; animal model data supportive

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Diabetes (pilot)**: Swanston-Flatt (1989): Agrimony tea significantly reduced blood glucose in streptozotocin-diabetic mice vs control — mechanism consistent with aldose reductase + alpha-glucosidase inhibition. Human RCT data absent but pharmacological mechanism well-supported.

**Mild diarrhoea**: German Commission E approved: *Agrimonia eupatoria* for "mild non-specific diarrhoea and inflammation of the mucous membranes of the mouth and throat" — based on traditional evidence and tannin pharmacology.

**Pharyngitis / tonsillitis (gargle)**: Traditional use for sore throat as a gargle (astringent + antimicrobial tannins) widely reported; clinical documentation in European herbal records; Commission E approval supports.

**Wound healing (topical)**: Commission E approved for external use — consistent with tannin-mediated haemostasis and wound astringency.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Mithridates and the antidote herb**: Mithridates VI Eupator (132–63 BCE), King of Pontus, was the most famous toxicologist of antiquity — driven to pharmaceutical research by his fear of assassination by poison. He allegedly took small daily doses of all known poisons to immunise himself (the practise of *Mithridatism*, which gave the word *mithridate* to antidote preparations). Several herbs bear his name in the ancient pharmacopoeias. The attribution of agrimony to Mithridates reflects two things: first, the plant was recognised as having detoxifying/protective properties (the hepatoprotective mechanism is real); second, the Mithridates attribution was a marketing strategy in ancient pharmacopoeias — if the king of antidotes used it, it must be efficacious. The pharmacological and the political overlap in ancient pharmaceutical naming.

**The apricot tea**: Among the bitter liver herbs (dandelion, milk thistle, agrimony), agrimony is distinctive for being pleasant. The dried herb produces a tea with an apricot-like, slightly sweet aromatic flavour from its volatile oil — making it the liver herb that people actually drink willingly. The German tradition of *Leberkräutertee* (liver herb tea) including agrimony reflects this palatability advantage. Effective medicine that patients comply with is more effective than medicine they refuse. Agrimony's pharmacological case includes the practical advantage of compliance through pleasantness — a pharmacological consideration that rarely appears in clinical trial design.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

| Form | Dose | Application |
|------|------|-------------|
| Tea (aerial parts) | 3–6g per cup | Primary form; pleasant flavour; liver; diarrhoea; sore throat |
| Tincture (1:5, 45% ethanol) | 2–4ml 3× daily | Anti-inflammatory; hepatoprotective; anti-diarrhoeal |
| Gargle (strong tea) | Gargle undiluted 3× daily | Sore throat; tonsillitis; oral inflammation |
| Topical poultice (fresh herb) | Applied to wound | Wound haemostasis; anti-inflammatory |
| Combined with dandelion root (I_57) | — | Liver/biliary formula |
| Combined with chamomile (I_14) | — | GI anti-inflammatory + antispasmodic |

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Bitter, astringent; cool. Channels: Liver (肝), Lung (肺), Spleen (脾). Clears Heat, stops bleeding, astringes and consolidates, tonifies Spleen.

**Ayurvedic (cross-reference)**: Bitter, astringent; cooling. Reduces Pitta ↓↓. *Yakṛt* (hepatic tonic), *Śoṇitasthāpana* (haemostatic), *Grāhī* (astringent, anti-diarrhoeal).

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Constipation**: High tannin content — may worsen constipation; reduce dose; use only when diarrhoea is the presenting condition
- **Iron absorption**: Tannins chelate iron — take separately from iron supplements (2h interval)
- **Pregnancy**: Cholagogue + astringent — avoid therapeutic doses; dietary tea amounts probably safe
- **Photosensitisation**: Coumarin content — potential photosensitisation with very high doses and sun exposure; standard tea amounts safe
- **Generally mild and safe**: Very long tradition of food-grade use (common tea herb in Germany, Russia, Eastern Europe) with no documented serious adverse effects
