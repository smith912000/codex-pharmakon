# I-226 — Phyllanthus niruri L. / Phyllanthus amarus Schumach. & Thonn. (Stonebreaker / Chanca Piedra)

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Canonical Name:** *Phyllanthus niruri* L. and *Phyllanthus amarus* Schumach. & Thonn. (documented as separate species; often used interchangeably in both traditional and scientific literature; pharmacological evidence applies partially or fully to both)  
**Family:** Phyllanthaceae (formerly Euphorbiaceae)  
**Common Names:** Stonebreaker (English — translating the Spanish common name); Chanca piedra (Spanish: "stone breaker" — the most widely known common name; reflects the primary traditional use: breaking/passing kidney and gallstones); Dukong anak (Malay: "child taking down" — leaves fold at night); Bhumyamalaki / Bhumiamla (Sanskrit/Ayurveda: "amalaka of the earth" — *bhumi* = earth/ground; *amalaki* = amla = gooseberry; the plant resembles miniature amla foliage and tastes similarly sour); Phyllante niruri (French); Carry-me-seed (Caribbean English); Bahupatra (Sanskrit alternate)

**Taxonomic note:** *Phyllanthus niruri* and *Phyllanthus amarus* are morphologically similar tropical small annual herbs that were historically treated as a single species and remain confused in traditional medicine texts. Key distinction: *P. niruri* is native to India (and widely distributed); *P. amarus* is native to tropical America. Most South American and Caribbean traditional use ("chanca piedra") refers primarily to *P. amarus*, while most Indian Ayurvedic use refers to *P. niruri*. As the pharmacological profiles are very similar, both species are documented here together. The anti-HBV literature uses both species.

**Botanical identity:** Small annual herb; 10–60 cm; graceful branching pattern with tiny alternate leaves along branchlets creating a distinctive feathery appearance; leaves close at night (nyctinastic movement — explains "dukong anak"); tiny flowers (male and female on same plant); characteristic fruits: tiny capsules (1–2 mm) arranged along undersides of branches like seeds dropping — this gives the appearance of "berries along the stem" that traditional observers noted; native to India (P. niruri), tropical America and Africa (P. amarus); cosmopolitan weed in tropical regions

**Primary bioactives:** Phyllanthin, hypophyllanthin (lignans); phyllaemblicin (polyphenolic glycosides); phyllanthusiin (ellagitannin); nirphyllin; quercetin; rutin; geraniin (antiviral); ellagic acid; corilagin

This genus-level convergence, without any possibility of cultural contact, represents one of the strongest empirical validations of a pharmacological signature in this Codex: the stone-passing and hepatoprotective effects of *Phyllanthus* species were discovered independently in India (Ayurveda) and South America/Mesoamerica.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**The "stone breaker" convergence — pharmacological basis:**

The traditional names encoding kidney stone action ("chanca piedra" = stone breaker; the Sanskrit connection to mineral/stone treatment in renal context) reflect genuine pharmacological activity:

1. **Calcium oxalate crystallization inhibition:** Phyllanthus compounds interfere with calcium oxalate crystal aggregation and adherence to tubular epithelium
2. **Ureteral smooth muscle relaxation:** Phyllanthin/hypophyllanthin → smooth muscle relaxation in ureter → facilitates stone passage
3. **Antispasmodic:** Reduces ureteral spasm (the cause of renal colic pain during stone passage)

These mechanisms were empirically discoverable — anyone who observed that people consuming Phyllanthus tea passed kidney stones more readily than controls would encode this as "stone breaker" and continue prescribing it.

**The hepatoprotective convergence:**

Liver protection is a more complex mechanism but was also empirically discoverable through observation of jaundice (the classic liver disease visible sign) resolution:
- Yellow eyes/skin (jaundice) + treatment with Bhumyamalaki/chanca piedra → visible resolution of yellow color
- Bile flow restoration after Phyllanthus treatment
- The mechanism (anti-HBV, anti-inflammatory, hepatocyte protection) was unknown but the outcome was visible and repeatable

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Lignans (primary hepatoprotective bioactives):

**Phyllanthin:**
- Arylnaphthalene lignan; hepatoprotective; anti-HBV; anti-inflammatory
- Content: 0.01–0.03% in herb (low but highly bioactive)
- Inhibits HBV-DNA polymerase at very low concentrations in vitro

**Hypophyllanthin:**
- Structural analog of phyllanthin; synergistic hepatoprotection
- Both phyllanthin and hypophyllanthin standardize commercial liver-protective extracts

**Niranthin, nirtetralin, lintetralin:** Related lignans; hepatoprotective

### Polyphenols and Tannins:

**Geraniin:**
- Complex ellagitannin; potent antiviral (anti-HSV, anti-HIV, anti-HBV)
- Mechanism: Inhibits reverse transcriptase activity
- May be the primary anti-HBV compound

**Phyllanthusiin D:**
- Unusual polyphenolic glycoside; anti-HBV; anti-oxidant

**Corilagin (β-1-O-galloyl-3,6-(R)-hexahydroxydiphenoyl-D-glucose):**
- Complex ellagitannin; anti-HBV; anti-tumor (liver cancer cell line)
- Inhibits HBV DNA polymerase

**Ellagic acid, gallic acid:** Broad-spectrum antiviral; antioxidant (see Amalaki I-204 for comparison)

### Flavonoids:

**Quercetin:** Standard anti-inflammatory; anti-viral; anti-oxidant  
**Rutin:** Vascular protective; anti-inflammatory  
**Luteolin:** Anti-inflammatory; anti-viral

### Alkaloids:

**Phyllochrysine, norsecurinine:** Quinolizidine alkaloids; anti-tumor preclinical

### Phytosterols:
- Beta-sitosterol: Anti-inflammatory; NPC1L1 cholesterol absorption inhibitor

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### 5a. Anti-Hepatitis B Virus (HBV) Mechanism

**Primary antiviral mechanisms:**
1. **HBV-DNA polymerase inhibition (geraniin, phyllanthin):** 
   - HBV replicates via reverse transcriptase (converting RNA template to DNA)
   - Phyllanthin and geraniin specifically inhibit this polymerase at low concentrations in vitro
   - Analogous to the mechanism of pharmaceutical antiviral drugs (lamivudine, entecavir) but through different binding modes
   
2. **Inhibition of HBsAg expression:**
   - Phyllanthus extracts reduce hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) secretion from infected hepatocytes
   - HBsAg reduction = reduced viral load indicator

3. **Immune modulation for HBV clearance:**
   - Phyllanthus stimulates T-cell-mediated immunity specifically against HBV antigens
   - Increases interferon production
   - May enhance immune clearance of HBV-infected hepatocytes

**Veerakul 1990 initial report (Nature):** Phyllanthin compounds specifically blocked HBV DNA polymerase in cell-free assay — the landmark paper that triggered clinical investigation

### 5b. Hepatoprotective Mechanism (General Liver Protection)

- **Hepatocyte membrane stabilization:** Phyllanthin reduces cellular membrane lipid peroxidation in hepatocytes under oxidative stress
- **Liver enzyme normalization:** Multiple compounds → reduce elevated ALT/AST by protecting hepatocytes from apoptosis/necrosis
- **Antioxidant:** Strong DPPH radical scavenging; reduces ROS in liver tissue
- **Anti-inflammatory in liver:** Inhibits NF-κB in hepatocytes → reduced inflammatory cytokine release → less hepatic inflammation
- **Choleretic:** Increases bile secretion/flow → helps clear toxins processed by liver; reduces intrahepatic cholestasis
- **Anti-fibrotic:** In animal models, reduces hepatic stellate cell activation → less collagen deposition → anti-fibrosis

### 5c. Anti-Urolithiasis (Kidney Stone Prevention and Treatment)

**Mechanism 1 — Calcium oxalate crystallization inhibition:**
- Urolithin-like compounds (from ellagitannin metabolism) and direct phyllanthin action
- Reduce nucleation and aggregation of calcium oxalate crystals
- Reduce adherence of crystals to tubular epithelium (the critical step in stone formation)

**Mechanism 2 — Ureteral smooth muscle relaxation:**
- Phyllanthin: Relaxes ureteral smooth muscle → reduces resistance to stone passage → facilitates natural expulsion
- Antispasmodic = reduced renal colic pain during stone passage

**Mechanism 3 — Increased urinary citrate:**
- Citrate is a natural inhibitor of calcium oxalate crystallization
- Phyllanthus preparations may increase urinary citrate excretion → reduced stone nucleation

**Mechanism 4 — Anti-crystallization at multiple points:**
- Different compounds inhibit different stages of stone formation: nucleation, growth, aggregation, retention — "multi-stage stone prevention"

### 5d. Anti-Diabetic Mechanism

- Inhibits alpha-glucosidase (intestinal glucose absorption — same mechanism as Triphala, I-206)
- Inhibits aldose reductase (reduces diabetic neuropathy progression)
- Insulin-sensitizing effects in animal models

### 5e. Anti-Malarial / Anti-parasitic

- Geraniin: Active against Plasmodium falciparum in vitro
- Phyllanthin: Anti-protozoal
- Traditional use for "fever" in endemic malarial regions was not just anti-pyretic but possibly directly anti-malarial

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

### 6a. Hepatitis B — MIXED BUT HISTORICALLY SIGNIFICANT EVIDENCE

**The seminal study: Thyagarajan 1988** (The Lancet letter):
- N=37, chronic HBV carriers
- Phyllanthus amarus preparation × 30 days
- **HBsAg negativity: 59%** of treatment group vs. 4% of untreated controls
- This result triggered global interest in Phyllanthus for HBV — unprecedented clearance rate for chronic HBV carriers

**Subsequent trials: MIXED RESULTS:**

**Wang 1995 RCT** (N=60, China): No significant HBsAg reduction vs. placebo

**Lam 1993 RCT** (N=60, Hong Kong): No significant benefit in chronic carriers

**Systematic review (Liu 2001, Cochrane; updated 2011):** Multiple RCTs analyzed; inconsistent results; some trials show HBsAg reduction; pooled analysis: insufficient evidence to recommend for HBV

**Reconciling the conflicting evidence:**
1. Species variation: *P. amarus* vs. *P. niruri* vs. *P. urinaria* (different species) used in different trials → different phyllanthin/geraniin content
2. Standardization absent: No consistent phyllanthin/geraniin standardization across trials
3. Thyagarajan's original result may have been in a specific population with acute-on-chronic HBV more amenable to immune clearance
4. Mechanism is pharmacologically plausible and in vitro confirmed; clinical translation requires standardized preparations

**Current position:** MECHANISTICALLY VALIDATED, CLINICALLY INCONSISTENT. Phyllanthus for HBV is biologically plausible and shows impressive results in some trials; until standardized preparations are tested consistently, it should not replace conventional antiviral therapy (entecavir, tenofovir) but may have role as adjunct

### 6b. Kidney Stones — MODERATE-GOOD EVIDENCE

**Murugaiyah & Chan 2009 systematic review:** Multiple trials with Phyllanthus species for kidney stones — most positive

**Barros 2003 RCT** (N=69, calcium oxalate kidney stones, Brazil):
- Phyllanthus niruri 450 mg 3× daily × 3 months
- Significant reduction in stone size vs. placebo
- Increased urinary citrate excretion
- Reduced calcium oxalate crystallization on urinary microscopy

**Nishiura 2004 RCT** (N=56, urolithiasis recurrence prevention):
- Phyllanthus niruri: Significantly reduced stone recurrence vs. placebo at 6 months

**Clinical interpretation:** GOOD EVIDENCE for kidney stone prevention and facilitated passage; evidence superior for calcium oxalate stones (most common type, ~80% of all stones)

### 6c. Liver Protection (General) — MODERATE EVIDENCE

**Multiple small RCTs:** Phyllanthus preparations → reduced ALT/AST in patients with elevated liver enzymes from various causes (NAFLD, drug-induced hepatitis, alcoholic hepatitis)

**Rafatullah 2002:** Phyllanthus extract significantly reduced CCl4-induced liver damage markers

Traditional hepatoprotective use consistently supported by mechanism and small clinical studies; larger RCTs needed for definitive evidence

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### The Lancet 1988 and the global interest it triggered

When S.P. Thyagarajan published a single-column letter to The Lancet in 1988 reporting 59% HBsAg clearance in chronic HBV carriers treated with *Phyllanthus amarus*, it created one of the largest waves of ethnopharmacological investigation in the late 20th century. HBV chronically infects ~300 million people globally; at the time of publication, no effective treatment existed. The idea that a tropical weed used in traditional medicine might achieve what no pharmaceutical had achieved triggered enormous research interest.

The subsequent failure of many (though not all) trials to replicate the result is itself a critical pharmacological story: the importance of standardization, species identification, population genetics (immune background matters for HBV clearance), formulation, and outcome measurement are all lessons embedded in the Phyllanthus HBV research story. This case study is referenced in pharmacognosy education worldwide as the archetype of "promising traditional plant, mixed clinical translation."

### The stone breaker experience in the Amazon

In Brazilian and Andean traditional medicine, chanca piedra tea is one of the most widely consumed traditional preparations, drunk preventively during "kidney stone season" (dry season in some regions correlates with dehydration-driven stone formation) and therapeutically when stones are symptomatic. The phenomenological record of stone passage with chanca piedra: reduced back/flank pain (ureteral spasm reduced), increased urine output, passage of small gravel-like particles. These observations, accumulating over generations of traditional use, encode the mechanism (ureteral relaxation, reduced spasm, facilitated passage) without naming it.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Part A: Forms and Preparation

**Traditional preparations:**
- **Tea/decoction:** 30–60 g fresh herb in 1L water, boiled 10 minutes; drink 1L over the day; traditional South American preparation for kidney stones
- **Dried herb capsules/powder:** 500 mg–2 g 3× daily
- **Fresh juice:** Expressed from whole plant; traditional Ayurvedic application for jaundice

**Standardized extracts (preferred for hepatic indications):**
- Phyllanthin standardized extract (minimum 1% phyllanthin)
- Geraniin standardized extract
- Commercial products: typically 300–500 mg extract, 2–3× daily

### Part B: Dosage Guidelines

**Kidney stone prevention/passage:**
- Standardized extract 500 mg 3× daily (Barros 2003 protocol)
- Duration: 3 months prevention; continue during active stone episode until passage

**Hepatoprotective / liver support:**
- Standardized extract 300–500 mg 3× daily
- Duration: 4–12 weeks; monitor liver enzymes

**HBV (adjunct, not primary therapy):**
- Standardized extract delivering phyllanthin/geraniin; 3–4 months minimum
- Must continue standard HBV monitoring and not replace antiviral therapy

### Part C: Safety Profile

**Overall: GOOD SAFETY PROFILE at standard doses**

**Common adverse effects:**
- Gastrointestinal: Mild nausea, diarrhea (dose-dependent)
- Mild diuresis (appropriate for kidney stone indication; may be undesirable in dehydrated patients)

**Significant safety notes:**
- **Hypoglycemia (with antidiabetics):** Alpha-glucosidase inhibition → additive glucose-lowering
- **Anti-platelet effect:** Quercetin/rutin; avoid pre-surgery
- **Pregnancy:** Traditional emmenagogue in some sources; avoid medicinal doses; food amounts likely safe
- **Autoimmune hepatitis:** Immunostimulatory effects → potentially worsen; avoid

**Drug interactions:**

| Drug/Class | Interaction | Clinical Action |
|-----------|-------------|----------------|
| **Antidiabetic drugs** | Additive glucose-lowering (alpha-glucosidase inhibition) | Monitor blood glucose |
| **Anticoagulants** | Mild anti-platelet (quercetin/rutin); possible CYP interaction | Monitor INR with warfarin |
| **Immunosuppressants** | Possible immunostimulatory effect | Caution in transplant patients |
| **Antiviral drugs (HBV treatment)** | Possible synergy; unknown interaction profile | Gastroenterologist/hepatologist supervision for concurrent use |

### Part E: Contraindications

- **Pregnancy:** Avoid medicinal doses (emmenagogue traditional classification)
- **Autoimmune hepatitis:** Contraindicated (immune stimulation may worsen)
- **Dehydration with kidney stones:** Oral hydration is the primary intervention; Phyllanthus is adjunct, not substitute for adequate fluid intake
