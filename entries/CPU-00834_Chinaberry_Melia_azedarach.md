# Chinaberry — Pride of India · Persian Lilac · Chuan Lian Zi (川楝子) · Bakain · Azad Dirakht

## *Melia azedarach* L. | Family: Meliaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, DMM, SBJ, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry in the third degree (Galenic/Unani) — a bitter, downward-draining, incisive drug
**Training Phase:** Advanced / Custodial only — a poison-tier plant studied for boundary-knowledge, never a beginner's remedy

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Avicenna & the Unani inheritance (CM).** The Persian and Arabic physicians knew *Melia azedarach* as *āzād-dirakht* ("the free/noble tree"), distinguishing it from the true neem (*āzād-dirakht-i-hindī*, *Azadirachta indica*), its close Meliaceae cousin. In the Unani corpus descending from Avicenna's *Qanun*, the bitter bark and fruit were classed as cold-dry, purgative, and vermifuge — pressed into service against intestinal worms, obstinate skin eruptions, and "corrupt humours" of the gut. The tradition treated it with caution and always after correction (compounding with milder aromatics), reflecting an old awareness that the drug bit hard.

**Dioscorides & the Mediterranean materia medica (DMM).** The ornamental "bead tree" spread early around the Mediterranean, its hard stony fruit-kernels strung as rosary beads (hence *paternoster* tree). Greek and later Byzantine practice used decoctions of the bark externally for parasitic skin conditions and scalp infestation, keeping internal use marginal.

**Shennong & the Chinese tradition (TCM).** In Chinese medicine the fruit is **Chuan Lian Zi** (川楝子), one of the classic *qi*-regulating, pain-relieving drugs. It is described as bitter and cold, entering Liver, Stomach, Small Intestine and Bladder channels, used to "course Liver *qi*, drain heat, stop pain, and kill parasites" — indicated for flank and hypogastric pain, hernial pain, and roundworm. The related root-bark drug **Ku Lian Pi** (苦楝皮) is the classic anthelmintic. Both are formally listed as toxic (*you du*) in the pharmacopoeia and dose-capped.

**Sushruta & Ayurveda (SS).** As *mahānimba* ("great neem"), *Melia azedarach* appears in Ayurvedic practice as a bitter (*tikta*) antiparasitic and skin drug, distinguished from true *nimba*. It is regarded as sharper and more hazardous than neem and used sparingly, chiefly externally.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The rosary-bead fruit encodes the plant's paradox: an object of devotion whose kernel is a poison. Traditional "cold, draining, downward" symbolism maps onto real pharmacology — the fruit's tetranortriterpenoid limonoids are genuinely bitter, genuinely purgative, and genuinely capable of shutting down neuromuscular signalling. The "free/noble tree" that heals worms in small measure kills the child who eats its berries. In Kenetica the plant is filed as a *custodial poison*: its lesson is the margin, not the cure.

### Active Components

- **Limonoids (tetranortriterpenoids):** the defining chemistry of the genus. **Toosendanin** is the principal marker and the most studied — a potent, slow, irreversible presynaptic neuromuscular blocker.
- **Meliatoxins (A1, A2, B1, B2):** the tetranortriterpene fraction of the fruit held chiefly responsible for human and livestock poisonings.
- Additional limonoids: salannin, nimbolinin-type compounds, kulactone, volkensin-class and trichilin-class structures reported across the genus.
- Flavonoids, sterols, and resinous constituents in bark and leaf.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Chinaberry is a **teaching poison**, not a working remedy. It is retained in the Codex for three reasons: (1) it anchors the Meliaceae limonoid family and its relationship to true neem; (2) toosendanin is a real neuropharmacological research tool (studied as a botulinum-antagonist and presynaptic probe); and (3) it is a leading cause of accidental plant poisoning in children where the tree grows ornamentally, so recognition is protective knowledge. Any traditional anthelmintic or *qi*-regulating use belongs strictly to trained practitioners working with pharmacopoeial, dose-capped preparations — never to self-directed practice.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed: a systematic review of *Melia* limonoids (Fan et al., *Frontiers in Pharmacology*, 2022) catalogues over 200 isolated limonoids and identifies toosendanin as the pivotal bioactive marker, documenting anticancer, insecticidal and anti-botulism activities alongside **non-negligible toxicity**, and explicitly notes the near-absence of clinical trial data — placing human therapeutic use on a weak evidence footing ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2021.795565)). A companion review of Meliaceae phytochemistry for pest management (Jaoko et al., *Plants*, 2020) confirms the limonoid profile (toosendanin, salannin, volkensin and related structures) and the family's insecticidal, antifeedant and growth-inhibiting activity, consistent with the traditional vermifuge reputation ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants9020143)).

**Evidence grade: TRADITIONAL / PRELIMINARY.** Robust phytochemistry and preclinical/insecticidal data exist; controlled human efficacy data are essentially absent, while toxicity is well documented. Nothing here supports self-directed medicinal use.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** Chuan Lian Zi — bitter, cold; **toxic**. Liver, Stomach, Small Intestine, Bladder channels. Courses Liver *qi*, drains heat, kills parasites, stops pain.
- **Ayurveda (as mahānimba):** *rasa* — bitter (*tikta*); *guṇa* — light, dry, sharp; *vīrya* — cold (*śīta*); *vipāka* — pungent (*kaṭu*). Kapha–pitta reducing; sharp and potentially aggravating in excess.
- **Galenic/Unani:** cold and dry in the third degree; purgative, deobstruent, vermifuge — corrected before internal use.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system into which a substance could be assigned a cultivation status, so even where the bead tree is used the tradition offers only its discipline of attention — provenance, the assay of a genuinely bitter kernel, the guard against mistaking an ornamental poison for a benign bead. In this case even the therapeutic attestation is thin: *Melia azedarach* is an Asian tree whose Mediterranean spread is late, and the external antiparasitic decoctions attached to it belong to Byzantine and later practice rather than to *De Materia Medica* itself. No cultivation framing is attested. **(c) absent.**

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** As *āzād-dirakht* the bitter bark and fruit are classed cold and dry, purgative and vermifuge, and — tellingly — always *corrected* with milder aromatics before internal use because the drug bites hard. The strongest cultivation idea in the Unani corpus is the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture, a longevity frame of the whole regimen; a cold-dry downward-draining poison is the opposite of a substance one takes to conserve that finite endowment, and no such conservational or *mufarrih* role is assigned to it. It is attested in the materia medica with a therapeutic, corrected-purgative standing only. **(b) framed:** attested, no cultivation status recorded.

**Chinese — Shennong / Bencao (SBJ).** Here there is a genuine cultivation status, and it is a warning one. The fruit, under its original name *lian shi* 楝實 (later *Chuan Lian Zi* 川楝子), is recorded in the *Shennong Bencao Jing* (Divine Farmer's Classic) in the **lower grade**, 下品 — the grade of drugs that attack disease and are taken for a defined short course, explicitly *not* the upper-grade substances taken long-term to nourish life (養命). Its toxic listing (*you du* 有毒) in the later pharmacopoeia is consistent with that placement. Within *yang sheng* 養生, which is conservational rather than curative, a 下品 drug sits outside the cultivation project by the tradition's own hand. (Species note: the modern official *Chuan Lian Zi* is *Melia toosendan*, while *M. azedarach* supplies *Ku Lian Pi* 苦楝皮; the classical 楝實 grade covered the *Melia* group and applies to this plant historically.) **(a) attested** — lower grade, *Shennong Bencao Jing*.

**Ayurveda (SS).** As *mahānimba*, the "great neem," the drug is a bitter (*tikta*), cold, sharp antiparasitic and skin remedy, held sharper and more hazardous than true *nimba* and used sparingly, chiefly externally. No *rasayana* (rejuvenation) or *vajikarana* (generative vitality) assignment is recorded for it in the sources retrieved; its standing is therapeutic and cautionary, not a formal rejuvenative cultivation status. **(b) framed.**

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this that resembles building or conserving vitality. Chinaberry is filed as a custodial poison — retained for boundary-knowledge, for recognising a childhood-poisoning hazard, and as an anchor to the Meliaceae limonoid family. The one tradition that assigns it a cultivation status, the Chinese, places it in the lower grade: taken by a trained hand against a defined complaint and then stopped, never long-term, never self-directed. The grading is the lesson.

### Contraindications

⚠️ **SEVERE HAZARD — POTENTIALLY LETHAL PLANT. FLAG IN FULL.**

- **The fruit is acutely poisonous, especially to children.** The ripe berries are the classic hazard: ingestion of a small quantity of fruit has caused human fatalities, with children at highest risk. **Berries must never be eaten and should be regarded as a childhood-poisoning risk wherever the tree is grown ornamentally.**
- **Toxidrome is multi-system:** gastrointestinal (vomiting, bloody diarrhoea, colic), **neurological** (weakness, ataxia, rigidity, seizures, respiratory depression via toosendanin's irreversible neuromuscular blockade), **cardiac** (bradycardia, arrhythmia), and **hepatotoxicity**. Onset can be delayed; deterioration may be rapid.
- **Toosendanin has a narrow margin of safety** — the gap between anthelmintic/analgesic effect and neuro-/hepatotoxicity is slim. There is no reliable home dose.
- **Absolute contraindications:** pregnancy and lactation; infants and children; hepatic impairment; debilitated or elderly patients; concurrent neuromuscular or cardiac disease.
- **No self-administration.** Any legitimate use of Chuan Lian Zi / Ku Lian Pi is confined to trained practitioners using pharmacopoeial, dose-capped material with monitoring.
- **Emergency:** suspected ingestion (especially a child who has eaten berries) is a medical emergency — seek urgent poison-control and clinical care immediately. Do not wait for symptoms.
- **Livestock:** also toxic to pigs, sheep, cattle and poultry; a recognised veterinary poisoning.
