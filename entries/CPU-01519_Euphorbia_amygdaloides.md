# Euphorbia amygdaloides — Euphorbion (Dioscorides 3-96)

## *Euphorbia amygdaloides, the first of two binomials on the translators' SUGGESTED line (the second is "Euphorbia officinarum — Poisonous Gum Thistle"; the first is glossed "Wood Spurge")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed under the chapter heading, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line prints two binomials with two English names ("Wood Spurge" for *E. amygdaloides*, "Poisonous Gum Thistle" for *E. officinarum*) and a cross-reference line, "see tithymal 4-165 a-f, also 4-170". The row takes the first binomial. The description Dioscorides gives is of "a tree-like ferula in Libya" that yields a resin, and it is the translators, not the text, who put a wood spurge first. A reader should not treat the entry's title as a statement about which species yields the resin. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *3-96. EUPHORBION* at line 7412, SUGGESTED line at 7413, cross-reference at 7414, text from 7415 to 7419 (a page number and a section heading are printed inside it). The chapter stands in Book Three, "Roots", in the group "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants", before 3-97 *CHALBANE* (galbanum). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 502.

The chapter describes a resin, and not a leaf-and-root drug. "Euphorbium is a tree-like ferula in Libya that grows on Tmolus, a hill near Mauretania. It is full of very sharp liquid. The men there are afraid of it because of its extraordinary heat, and gather it as follows. Binding around the tree washed sheep stomachs and standing a distance away, they pierce the stalk with long tools; and presently a quantity of liquid flows out (like out of some jar) into the bellies. When pierced like this it also spills on the ground." There are "two kinds of this liquid": one "transparent like sarcocolla [3-99] (similar to ervum [2-129, 2-131])", the other, gathered in the bellies, "has a glassy look and is compact".

Then the assay. "It is adulterated with sarcocolla and glue mixed together. Choose that which is transparent and sharp, but that which is tasted is very hard to test because the tongue having been once bitten the burning remains for a long time, so that whatever is brought seems to be euphorbium. The first discovery of it was when Juba was king of Libya."

Then the uses. "The juice rubbed on has the ability to dissolve liquids. A decoction (taken as a drink) burns for a whole day; as a result it is mixed with honey and collyriums [eye salve or suppository, as the translators gloss] depending on the sharpness. It is mixed with aromatic liquid medicines and is good (taken as a drink) for sore hips. It removes scales from bones the same day, but it is necessary for those who use it to secure the flesh lying around the bones with linen cloths or stiff ointments." The last sentence is reported as hearsay: "Some claim that no hurt will fall on those bitten by snakes if (having cut the skin of the head even to the bone) you pour in this resin (pounded into small pieces) and sew up the wound."

*The chapters it points to.* The cross-reference "see tithymal 4-165 a-f" leads to the chapter on the seven kinds of *tithumalos* (heading line 9515). Its opening list says "the male is called characias, comatus, or amygdaloides, or else it is called gobius", so the word *amygdaloides* is also printed there as a name of the male tithymal, whose own sub-chapter (*4-165a. TITHUMALOS CHARACHIAS*, line 9522) carries the translators' suggestion *Euphorbia characias* [Loudon]. The cross-reference "also 4-170" leads to *CHAMAISUKE* (chamaesyce, line 9583), which carries "see 3-96" in return. Those are different chapters, and the entry does not merge them. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

No analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. It is well established that members of the genus *Euphorbia* have a milky latex, and that spurge latex is irritant to skin and eyes. Dioscorides' own words, "very sharp liquid" and "extraordinary heat", point the same way. Nothing further is stated here about the composition of this resin. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a burning resin gathered at a distance by men who fear it, tested by taste only with great difficulty, and applied to hips, to bones and to bites. The Codex gives no protocol. A resin that burns the tongue for a long time and is called by the text itself a thing to be feared is an irritant, and nothing here supports taking or applying it. The "pour it into a cut to the bone" report is recorded and not endorsed.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations. The juice is "rubbed on". A decoction is "taken as a drink". The resin is "mixed with honey and collyriums depending on the sharpness", and "mixed with aromatic liquid medicines". For the bones it is applied with the surrounding flesh protected "with linen cloths or stiff ointments". The gathering method (sheep stomachs, long tools, a distance) is also given. No quantity, frequency or duration is given for any use. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints "very sharp", "extraordinary heat", and that the resin "burns for a whole day" when drunk as a decoction. These are the qualities printed. No wetness, dryness or numbered degree is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. This chapter is a clear instance of what the spec calls his discipline of attention: provenance (Libya, Mount Tmolus), the method of gathering (sheep stomachs bound to the tree, long tools, a distance kept), a sensory assay (choose the transparent, sharp kind) with an honest admission that the test fails because the burning lingers, and a list of adulterants (sarcocolla and glue). The chapter also reports without vouching the claim about snakebite ("Some claim"). None of this is a cultivation status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this resin, and the text itself shows why. Its most useful lesson is about attention: a drug whose taste test blinds the tester should not be bought on the tester's say-so, and the one who handles it keeps his distance. The text offers nothing more.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no formal warning, but it says the men who gather it are afraid of its heat and keep a distance, and that tasting it leaves a long burning of the tongue. Spurge latex and resins are irritants of skin, eyes and mucous membranes, and swallowing them can be dangerous, so no preparation of this plant should be taken or applied. The traditional snakebite report is not a treatment, and a snakebite needs urgent medical care. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
