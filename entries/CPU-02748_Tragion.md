# Tragion — طراغيون (Avicenna, *Canon* Book II); Tragium (Dioscorides)

## *The Cretan plant called tragion (ṭarāghiyūn), with a gum like gum arabic, which Avicenna takes from Dioscorides; the species is not identified. The Dioscorides translator on disk suggests Chenopodium vulvaria / Tragium germanicum, which does not fit "grows only in Crete" and is not adopted here (confidence low)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Avicenna's *Canon* among the texts on disk as a spine attestation.** This is a single-text entry on the spine, which attests Avicenna alone (Canon Book II, heading *ṭarāghiyūn*, Arabic text line 13733 in `Sources/CM_Avicenna/text/IbnSina_Qanun_OpenITI_Shamela0010706.txt`). The Canon passage is, however, a close paraphrase of Dioscorides, *De materia medica* 4-49 "TRAGION" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 8440), which is on disk but has no spine row of its own under this name; the Canon itself says "Dioscorides said" (13737-13738). The spine row CPU-02768 (*ṭarāghiyūn ākhar*, "another tragion") is a different heading. [USER: verify whether the Dioscorides entry needs a spine row.]

**Avicenna, Canon (CM).** The entry is *ṭarāghiyūn* (*Canon* Book II, Arabic text lines 13733-13742).

*What it is.* هو نبات ينبت بقريطش وله ورق وقضبان وثمر شبيه بورق وقضبان أخينوس إلا أنها أصغر منه وله صمغ شبيه بالصمغ العربي وقوة ورقه وثمره وصمغه جذابة (13734-13736): "a plant that grows in Crete; it has leaves, stems and fruit like the leaves and stems of *akhīnūs*, only smaller, and it has a gum like gum arabic; the power of its leaf, fruit and gum is drawing (*jadhdhāba*)". The text then says another kind exists whose leaf is like *saqūlūqundriyūn* (scolopendrium) with a root like wild radish (13736-13737); that matches Dioscorides' 4-50 "TRAGION ALLO", which the spine treats under its own heading.

*Actions (quoting Dioscorides).* قال ديسقوريدوس (13737-13738): "Dioscorides said: if a wild she-goat is struck by an arrow and grazes among this plant, the arrow falls from her; and if it is applied as a poultice with wine it draws out from the flesh the splinter, the thorn and everything lodged in it".

*Excretory organs.* If drunk, it cures dribbling of urine, breaks up the stone in the bladder, and brings on the menses "when an amount of a dirham is drunk" (مقدار درخمي, 13740-13741). The other kind, eaten raw or cooked, helps ulcer of the intestines (13741-13742). A page marker (`PageV01P508`) and an OCR marker (`ms0504`) fall in the text and are not part of the sense.

**Dioscorides as printed on disk (DMM 4-49).** "Tragium grows only in Crete. It has leaves, stems and seed similar to lentiscus [1-90] but all smaller. It has a liquid similar to gum. The leaves, seed and fluid (applied with wine) draw out arrowheads, splinters, and all things fastened within. A decoction (taken as a drink) cures slow painful urination, breaks stones in the bladder, and induces the menstrual flow. A teaspoonful is taken. They say that wild goats that have been shot feed on this herb and put out the arrows." The two accounts agree. They differ in one comparison: the English Dioscorides says lentiscus, the Canon text says *akhīnūs* (echinos), and Dioscorides' dose is "a teaspoonful" where the Canon text has "a dirham" (*darkhmī*, presumably a copying of *drakhmē*). ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.** The Canon reports (from Dioscorides) a Cretan gum plant used as a drawing poultice for embedded arrows and splinters and as a drink for urinary stone, dribbling urine and the menses. The Codex gives no protocol, cannot identify the plant, and does not recommend it. The goat-and-arrow account is a report from the Greek author, not a claim the Codex endorses.

### Preparation & Dosing

Preparations given: leaf, fruit and gum applied with wine as a poultice; drunk (the form is not stated) in "an amount of a dirham" (13741); the other kind eaten raw or cooked (13741-13742). Dioscorides on disk gives "a teaspoonful" (line 8441). No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] Do not use any dose from this entry for an unidentified plant.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Many gum-bearing plants are applied as drawing poultices in old medicine; there is no modern evidence that a plant draws embedded arrowheads or breaks bladder stones. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]
