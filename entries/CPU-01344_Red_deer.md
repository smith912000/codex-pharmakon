# Red deer — sha.wa (Clark: "Tibetan Deer"; Men-Tsee-Khang: "Shawa", "Deer"), Cervus elaphus, rGyud-bzhi
## *The deer Clark's materia medica prints as "sha.wa: Tibetan Deer: Cervus elaphus"; the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary pairs the Tibetan "Shawa" with "Deer" and a binomial OCR'd "Cervus elapnus"; the English name "red deer" is the spine's* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The deer enters the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica (ch.20) among the "thirteen medicines of animal origin", which "consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (Clark 1995, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` lines 1147-1149). Under *Horn*: "sha.wa: Tibetan Deer: Cervus elaphus: [has three names]. The horns [of these two types of deer have] similar [action] to Rhino horn", the rhino horn being one that "dries [accumulations of] pus, blood and lymph in the stomach [and is beneficial in cases of poisoning]" (lines 1152-1154; `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` row 224). The "two types" are the roe deer entered just before it (see the Roe deer entry). The bracketed words are Clark's commentary on the root verse. The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text says the same without naming Tibetan words: "The therapeutic effect of roe deer horn and common deer horn are similar to that of rhino horn" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` lines 3506-3508).

Other parts are entered as "deer" without the Tibetan name. Blood: "sha.wahi.khrag: Deer blood: is anthelmintic, halts bleeding from the womb [and prevents hangovers.]" (Clark line 1172); Men-Tsee-Khang: "Deer blood cures ein disorders and stops menstrual bleeding" (line 3539; "ein" is printed so and is not explained in the lines read). Fat: "Deer fat: is anthelmintic and protects against poisoning" (Clark line 1180; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3546). Brain: "Brain of game animals (e.g. deer): halts diarrhoea" (Clark line 1182). The tie between "sha.wa" and these parts rests on the shared word "deer"; the lines do not say that the blood and fat are of this species. [USER: verify]

As food the deer is a game animal. Clark's meat classification puts "deer (Cervus elaphus), musk deer, Procapra pictaudata, Ovis ammon, rabbits and saiga tatarica" in group (c), game animals (line 672), and the Men-Tsee-Khang list reads "The deer, musk deer, gazelle, argali, rabbit and antelope are the group of herbivorous wild animals" (line 2907). Game meat is a recommended food for bile disorders: "game meat (e.g., deer)" (Clark line 175); "meat of game animals" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 1101). The glossary of animals in the Men-Tsee-Khang book has the Tibetan "Shawa" and the English "Deer", with a binomial printed "Cervus elapnus" (lines 7118-7130); the table is OCR-scrambled and the pairing is by column order.

**Identity.** Clark prints the Latin binomial *Cervus elaphus*, the red deer of Europe and Asia in current usage, with the English "Tibetan Deer"; whether the Tibetan text means a particular regional form is not stated in the lines read. The English "red deer" of this entry's name is not used by Clark or the Men-Tsee-Khang text in these passages. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Deer meat is lean muscle: protein, with a low fat content compared with domestic meats, and iron and B vitamins as in other red meats. Antlers and horns are bony or horny growths; deer antler is bone that is grown and shed each year. Blood contains haemoglobin and plasma proteins; fat is mostly triglyceride. No analysis specific to this entry was read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources record deer horn as a drying remedy for pus, blood and lymph, deer blood and fat as worm and bleeding remedies, and deer meat as a game food for bile disorders. Nothing in the sources supports using these in modern practice. Antler, blood and fat each require killing or wounding an animal, and trade in deer products is regulated in many places. The Codex does not recommend any deer product for any complaint and gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation of the horn, blood, fat or meat is described for the deer in the lines read; the entries give only the part and its action. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Venison must be cooked through; deer can carry parasites and bacteria, and in some regions deer populations carry chronic wasting disease, a prion disease. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The deer as meat falls in the dry-land groups. Clark: all meats "are sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste", and "the flesh of dry land animals has cool, light and coarse powers which eliminate wind and phlegm fevers" (line 673). Men-Tsee-Khang: the meat of dry-land animals "is cool, light and rough in quality. It brings down hot disorders associated with loong and baekan" (line 2923). For the horn, blood and fat the lines read give actions (above) and no taste or potency. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Deer parts are substances of the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica, with stated actions and no taste group for the horn, blood or fat. Clark's introduction roots the three humors in attachment, hatred and obscuration (line 51), so that disease at root is a spiritual condition; the animal materials here treat the branch conditions (pus, worms, bleeding, diarrhoea). No line read connects deer with *bcud len* (essence extraction) or rejuvenation, and a search of Clark for "bcud" found nothing. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The tradition records deer horn, blood, fat and meat as ordinary remedies and foods, and the honest reading is historical. A person who does not hunt or eat game has nothing to adopt.

### Contraindications

- **Identity:** the Tibetan, Latin and English names are paired through OCR-scrambled and translated tables; do not rely on them for any species-specific use. [USER: verify]
- **Food safety:** venison and deer blood must be cooked through; wild deer carry parasites and bacteria, and chronic wasting disease occurs in some populations.
- **Blood and fat:** the text offers no safe amount or preparation for either; avoid raw blood.
- **Legal and conservation status:** hunting and trade in deer and deer products are regulated in most countries. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics and religion:** the products require killing an animal; this entry takes no position beyond recording the text.
