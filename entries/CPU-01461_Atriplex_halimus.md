# Sea Orach / Atriplex halimus — Alimos, halimus (Dioscorides 1-120)

## *Atriplex halimus (the Dioscorides translators' suggestion for chapter 1-120; also Chenopodium halimus — "Sea Purslane, Sea Orach")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). **Named only by Dioscorides in this row.** The binomial is the translators' suggestion, not Dioscorides' own identification.

*1-120 ALIMOS* (heading at line 4426). The SUGGESTED line reads "Atriplex halimus, Chenopodium halimus — Sea Purslane, Sea Orach", with the cross-reference "see 2-145". The chapter sits in the part of Book One headed "GUMS from TREES" (line 4423), among the shrubs and thorn-bushes.

The whole chapter (line 4429): "Halimus is a hedge-shrub like rhamnus [above] — somewhat white, without filaments. The leaves almost resemble those of the olive tree, yet they are broader and more tender. It grows in maritime places and hedges. The leaves are boiled like vegetables with meat. One teaspoon of the root (taken as a drink with honey and water) alleviates convulsions, hernias and griping, and causes an abundance of milk [in breastfeeding]."

The chapter lists many names: "damassonium, erymon, britannica, rabdion, a sort of little twig, basilion, or a sort of regia. The Magi call it mercurii basis, or sapsis, some, osiridis diadema, others, heliostephanon, a sort of corona of the sun or sacer caulis. Pythagoras calls it anthenoron, the Egyptians, asontiri, some, asphe, asealuri, or asariphen, the Romans, albucus, and some, ampelucia." These are the names as the translators print them. The chapter attaches no rite to them.

The cross-reference "see 2-145" leads to 2-145 ATRAPHAXIS (heading at line 5755), whose SUGGESTED line is *Atriplex hortensis*, "Goosefoot, Fat Hen". That is a garden vegetable of two types, "eaten boiled as a vegetable", and the translators' link to *halimus* is a link of name only. [USER: verify]

*Overlap.* The spine's I_520 *Atriplex hortensis* (tagged AT, DMM, CM) is a different species and a different Dioscorides chapter (2-145 is the one the translators cite for it). It was not read for this batch. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Plants of the genus *Atriplex* are generally known as salt-tolerant shrubs and herbs of the goosefoot family (Amaranthaceae), and they accumulate salts in their tissues. This agrees with the text's maritime habitat. What the root of *A. halimus* contains was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records the leaves as a boiled vegetable eaten with meat, and a root drink for convulsions, hernia, griping and for increasing milk in nursing women. These are the text's own claims and not Codex protocols.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives two preparations. The leaves "boiled like vegetables with meat". And "One teaspoon of the root (taken as a drink with honey and water)" (line 4429). The teaspoon is the only quantity printed, and no duration is given. No further dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is generally known that *Atriplex* leaves have been eaten as a salty green in many places, and this fits the text's vegetable use in a general way. No study of the root drink was opened.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and the chapter assigns the plant no status. What it offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: where the shrub grows (maritime places and hedges), how to tell it from *rhamnus* (whitish, no filaments, olive-like but broader and more tender leaves), and what part is used (leaf as a vegetable, root as a drink). The long list of names, among them names the translators attribute to the Magi and to Pythagoras, is a record of what the plant was called, and recording is not endorsing. The identification with *Atriplex halimus* is the translators' suggestion. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator learns from this chapter to recognise a coastal hedge shrub and that its leaf was a food. It does not support a vitality practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one for the root drink.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. The root drink is recorded to increase milk and for hernia and convulsions, which need medical care, and it should not replace it. Salt-accumulating plants may carry a heavy salt load, which matters for anyone on a salt-restricted diet. This is a general statement and not from the text. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or eaten on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
