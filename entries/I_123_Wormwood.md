### Botanical Identity
Perennial aromatic herb 60–120cm; silvery-grey deeply divided silky leaves; small yellow globular flower heads; intensely bitter and aromatic; native to temperate Eurasia and North Africa, widely naturalised. The aerial parts (leaf and flowering top) are the drug material. One of the most bitter and most culturally significant herbs in the Western tradition — the source of absinthe and a reference bitter/anthelmintic. Related to mugwort (I_94, *A. vulgaris*) and sweet wormwood/qinghao (*A. annua*, the source of artemisinin antimalarial).

**Thujone**: Wormwood's characteristic compound, α- and β-thujone, is a GABA-A receptor antagonist (convulsant/stimulant at high doses) — the compound that gave absinthe its reputation for inducing madness ("absinthism"). Modern analysis suggests historical absinthe's thujone content was lower than feared, and most "absinthism" was simply alcoholism, but thujone is genuinely neurotoxic at high doses.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Dioscorides / Greco-Roman (DMM)
Book III, Chapter 23: "Wormwood — warming, astringent, digestive; expels intestinal worms; promotes urine and menstruation; good for the stomach and liver; against poison and mushroom toxicity." The anthelmintic, digestive, and hepatic applications are emphasised. Absinthites (wormwood wine) was a standard preparation.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine / Ibn Sīnā (CM)
*Afsantīn* — hot and dry. Applications: bitter digestive and hepatic tonic; anthelmintic; for jaundice and liver obstruction; emmenagogue; antidote; topical for inflammation and ear conditions. A major bitter tonic in Ibn Sīnā's system.

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (EP)
Artemisia/wormwood preparations in EP for intestinal parasites and digestive complaints.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)
Bitter Artemisia preparations in AT for digestive and antiparasitic use.

### 2.5 Shennong / Bencao Gangmu (SBJ)
苦艾 *Kǔ ài* (bitter wormwood) — used as a bitter, anthelmintic, and for clearing summer-heat and Damp. The related 青蒿 *Qīng Hāo* (*A. annua*) is separately important (artemisinin).

### 2.6 Four Medical Tantras (FMT)
Himalayan Artemisia species for fever, digestive conditions, and parasites.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Digestive Bitter Tonic (Primary — Absinthin)

**Absinthin**: One of the most bitter natural compounds; the bitter tonic mechanism (same as gentian, I_122):
- T2R bitter receptor activation → vagal stimulation → increased gastric acid, bile, and digestive enzyme secretion
- Cephalic-phase digestive priming → improved appetite and digestion
- Wormwood is a premier digestive bitter; the European amaro/vermouth tradition rests on it (vermouth = *Wermut* = wormwood)

### 5.2 Anthelmintic (Thujone + Sesquiterpene Lactones)

**Thujone + santonin-like sesquiterpene lactones**:
- Direct neurotoxicity to intestinal helminths (thujone disrupts parasite GABAergic neurotransmission → paralysis/death of worms)
- The "wormwood" name encodes the most consistent cross-traditional application (anthelmintic) — confirmed across all 6 traditions
- This is the original anthelmintic of Western medicine, used for roundworm, pinworm, and tapeworm for over 2,000 years

### 5.3 Hepatoprotective / Choleretic

**Chlorogenic acid + caffeic acid + flavonoids**:
- Choleretic (increases bile flow) → the jaundice/liver applications
- Hepatoprotective antioxidant effects
- The Chinese and Tibetan "clears Liver Damp-Heat/jaundice" and the Western "hepatic tonic" applications converge on these mechanisms

### 5.4 GABA-A Antagonism (Thujone — The Absinthe Question)

**α-Thujone**: Blocks the GABA-A receptor chloride channel:
- At low doses: mild stimulant/excitatory effect (possibly contributing to absinthe's reputation for creative stimulation)
- At high doses: convulsant, neurotoxic
- The historical "absinthism" (madness, hallucination, convulsion attributed to absinthe) was likely a combination of chronic alcoholism (absinthe was ~70% alcohol), thujone neurotoxicity, and adulterants. Modern analysis shows vintage absinthe thujone levels were lower than the legend suggested. Modern absinthe is regulated for thujone content.

### 5.6 Antimalarial (Artemisia genus context)

While *A. absinthium* itself has weak antimalarial activity, its relative *A. annua* (sweet wormwood/qinghao) is the source of artemisinin — the most important antimalarial drug of the modern era (Tu Youyou, Nobel Prize 2015). The Artemisia genus's antiparasitic reputation across traditions ultimately yielded, through *A. annua*, one of the most significant drug discoveries of the 20th century. (Artemisinin/qinghao receives its own dedicated Codex entry.)

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Digestive (dyspepsia)**: Wormwood is approved by German Commission E for dyspepsia and loss of appetite. The bitter tonic mechanism is well-validated.

**Crohn's disease**: Omer et al. (2007, *Phytomedicine*, n=40): wormwood extract added to standard therapy in Crohn's disease produced significant steroid-sparing effect and symptom improvement vs placebo. A notable RCT result — the sesquiterpene lactone anti-inflammatory mechanism (NF-κB inhibition) is relevant to IBD. Krebs et al. (2010) replicated benefit in a second small trial.

**Anthelmintic**: Traditional use strongly documented; thujone anthelmintic mechanism confirmed. Largely superseded by modern anthelmintics but mechanistically valid.

**SIBO/digestive**: Wormwood is a component of herbal antimicrobial protocols for small intestinal bacterial overgrowth; some clinical support in combination formulas (Chedid 2014).

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The green fairy and the artists**: Absinthe — the wormwood-flavoured spirit — was the defining drink of late 19th-century Parisian artistic and bohemian culture (the "green fairy," *la fée verte*). Van Gogh, Toulouse-Lautrec, Degas, Manet, Verlaine, Rimbaud, Wilde, and Hemingway all drank it; it features in their art and writing. The drink was banned across much of Europe and America in the early 20th century (France in 1915) amid moral panic about "absinthism" — a syndrome of madness, hallucination, and degeneration attributed to thujone. Modern scholarship has substantially revised this: most "absinthism" was ordinary alcoholism (the drink was ~70% alcohol), compounded by toxic adulterants (copper salts for colour, antimony) added by unscrupulous producers, with thujone playing a smaller role than believed. The ban was as much moral/social panic (the temperance movement, fear of bohemian culture) as toxicology. Absinthe was re-legalised in most countries by the 2000s under thujone content regulations. The wormwood-as-madness narrative is a case study in how genuine pharmacology (thujone IS a GABA antagonist neurotoxin), social anxiety, and commercial adulteration combined to create a pharmacological folk-devil.

**Wormwood the bitter star**: Revelation 8:10–11 — "a great star, blazing like a torch, fell from the sky... the name of the star is Wormwood. A third of the waters turned bitter, and many people died from the waters that had become bitter." The use of wormwood (*apsinthos* in Greek, *laʿănāh* in Hebrew) as the name of the apocalyptic poisoning star encodes the herb's cultural identity as the archetype of bitterness and poison. (Notably, "Chernobyl" — site of the 1986 nuclear disaster — is the Ukrainian/Russian name for *Artemisia* / mugwort-wormwood, leading to widespread association of the disaster with the Wormwood prophecy.) The biblical and apocalyptic resonance of wormwood reflects its extreme bitterness, which made it the natural symbol of sorrow, poison, and divine judgement throughout Western literary tradition.

**The Artemisia genus and the Nobel Prize**: The cross-traditional anthelmintic/antiparasitic reputation of the Artemisia genus — documented across all 6 of this entry's traditions — found its ultimate vindication in Tu Youyou's discovery of artemisinin from *Artemisia annua* (qinghao), guided by the 4th-century Chinese physician Ge Hong's text describing qinghao for fever. Tu Youyou won the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this discovery, which has saved millions of lives from malaria. While *A. absinthium* (this entry) is not the artemisinin source, the genus-wide traditional knowledge of Artemisia's antiparasitic properties — recorded independently across Greek, Arabic, Egyptian, Assyrian, Chinese, and Tibetan medicine — was the empirical foundation that ultimately directed modern science to the most important antimalarial of our age.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

| Form | Dose | Application |
|------|------|-------------|
| Tincture (1:5, 45% ethanol) | 1–2ml (or a few drops) before meals | Digestive bitter; appetite; hepatic |
| Tea/infusion (1–2g dried herb in 250ml) | 50–100ml before meals | Digestive; anthelmintic; bitter tonic |
| Wormwood + gentian (I_122) + ginger (I_38) | Compound bitter | Comprehensive digestive tonic |
| Crohn's protocol (standardised extract) | Per Omer 2007 study (500mg 3× daily) | Adjunct in Crohn's — under medical supervision |
| Vermouth / amaro (culinary) | Aperitif amounts | The diluted traditional bitter |

**Dose caution**: Do not use high doses or prolonged courses — thujone accumulation is neurotoxic. Short courses (2–4 weeks) at standard doses are appropriate. The bitter effect requires only small amounts.

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Bitter, acrid; cool. Channels: Liver (肝), Gallbladder (膽), Stomach (胃), Spleen (脾). Clears Damp-Heat, kills parasites, clears summer-heat, moves Liver Qi.

**Ayurvedic (cross-reference)**: Bitter, pungent; heating (despite bitterness, the aromatic warmth dominates). Reduces Kapha ↓↓, Vata ↓. *Krimighna* (anthelmintic), *Dīpana* (digestive kindler), *Yakṛduttejaka* (hepatic stimulant). Related to Indian Artemisia species (*A. nilagirica*).

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Thujone neurotoxicity**: Do not use high doses or prolonged courses; thujone is a GABA-A antagonist convulsant at high doses — risk of seizures; avoid the essential oil internally (concentrated thujone)
- **Pregnancy**: ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION — emmenagogue and abortifacient (thujone uterotonic + emmenagogue sesquiterpenes); avoid entirely in pregnancy
- **Epilepsy**: Thujone lowers seizure threshold — contraindicated in epilepsy
- **Asteraceae allergy**: Cross-reactivity with ragweed, chamomile, etc.
- **Porphyria**: Wormwood may aggravate porphyria
- **Breastfeeding**: Thujone passes into milk and is bitter — avoid
