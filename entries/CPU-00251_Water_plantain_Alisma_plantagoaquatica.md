### Origin & Tradition

Few herbs declare their function so plainly through their habitat. *Alisma plantago-aquatica* — and its East Asian sibling *A. orientale*, the source of most commercial Zé Xiè — grows with its roots submerged in the slow margins of ponds, ditches, and rice paddies. Chinese physicians, who read the doctrine of signatures through the lens of the Five Phases, drew the obvious conclusion: a plant that lives in standing water and thrives by moving it can teach the body to do the same. The name 泽泻 says it directly — 泽 (zé) means "marsh" or "pool," and 泻 (xiè) means "to drain" or "to purge downward." Zé Xiè is, literally, the marsh-drainer.

The rhizome (corm) appears in the *Shennong Bencao Jing* (SBJ), the foundational first-century CE pharmacopoeia, in the Superior Class — the highest classification, reserved for herbs that could be taken over long periods to nourish life rather than merely treat acute disease. The SBJ credits it with the ability to dispel wind-cold-damp Bi, to promote urination, to consolidate the Essence, and — taken over time — to "lighten the body." That last phrase recurs throughout the Superior Class and reflects the classical observation that draining excess fluid relieves the heavy, waterlogged sensation that classical medicine called dampness.

Zé Xiè's most enduring home is the formula **Liù Wèi Dì Huáng Wán** (六味地黄丸, Six-Ingredient Pill with Rehmannia), assembled by the pediatrician Qian Yi during the Song dynasty and recorded in his *Xiao Er Yao Zheng Zhi Jue* (1119 CE). In that formula, three tonifying herbs (Rehmannia, Cornus, Dioscorea) are deliberately balanced by three draining herbs (Zé Xiè, Poria, Moutan). Zé Xiè's specific role is to drain damp-heat from the Kidney so that the rich, cloying tonics do not stagnate — a piece of architectural elegance that made the formula the template for the entire family of Kidney-Yin tonics that followed.

The other classical anchor is **Wǔ Líng Sǎn** (五苓散, Five-Ingredient Powder with Poria) from Zhang Zhongjing's *Shang Han Lun* (c. 200 CE), where Zé Xiè is the chief herb — the largest dose in the formula — directing the treatment of fluid accumulation that fails to transform, with thirst, difficult urination, and a sensation of fullness below the heart.

### Active Components

- **Alisol A, Alisol B, and their 23-acetates** — protostane-type triterpenes; the signature constituents of the rhizome. Documented hypolipidemic activity (LDL and total cholesterol reduction in animal models); choleretic and hepatoprotective effects; alisol B 23-acetate has documented anti-inflammatory action via NF-κB pathway inhibition in cell studies. These triterpenes are the basis of the herb's modern interest as a lipid-lowering agent.
- **Alisol monoacetates and related protostanes** — a structurally related family contributing to the diuretic and lipid-modulating profile.
- **Triterpene-rich volatile fraction** — contributes the characteristic action on renal water handling.
- **Polysaccharides** — water-soluble fraction with documented immunomodulatory activity in preliminary studies; contributes to the broader tonic-class reputation.
- **Sesquiterpenes (alismol and relatives)** — minor constituents with reported vasorelaxant activity in vitro.
- **Potassium salts** — present in meaningful quantity; relevant to the herb's diuretic behaviour, which is not strongly potassium-wasting in the manner of synthetic loop diuretics. `[USER: verify quantitative potassium content figure before citing a number]`

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Within the Kenetica framework, Zé Xiè addresses a problem that synthetic diuretics handle bluntly and badly: the athlete who is simultaneously dehydrated *and* waterlogged. This is not a contradiction. Intracellular and interstitial fluid can accumulate — puffy, heavy, "soft" tissue that masks muscle definition and produces the sensation of dead, sluggish limbs — even while plasma volume and true hydration are inadequate. TCM names this dampness, and Zé Xiè is the premier herb for moving it without the violent fluid-and-electrolyte crash that follows furosemide.

The mechanism matters here. Zé Xiè's diuretic action is comparatively gentle and is paired, in classical use, with herbs that strengthen the Spleen's transformation of fluids (Bai Zhu, Poria) rather than herbs that merely force water out. The Kenetica reading is that you do not just want to *expel* water — you want to restore the body's capacity to *handle* it. For this reason Zé Xiè is best deployed in `[RESET]` phases to clear accumulated damp-heat after high-volume blocks, and at the tail of `[BUILD]` phases where heavy caloric and fluid intake has produced soft tissue retention.

The triterpene lipid-lowering action is a secondary but real benefit for athletes carrying metabolic load from sustained surplus eating. Alisol-driven cholesterol and triglyceride reduction, documented in animal models, supports the herb's classical use in "phlegm-damp" patterns — the TCM correlate of dyslipidemia and central adiposity.

This is a `[GENERAL]` herb: gentle enough for routine cyclical use, but not a tool for acute "making weight" — using any diuretic to manipulate competition weight is outside the Kenetica protocol and carries the contraindications below.

### Preparation & Dosing

- **Standard decoction:** 6–12g dried rhizome; simmer 15–20 minutes; strain; drink warm, earlier in the day to avoid nocturnal urination.
- **Salt-processed (yán Zé Xiè, 盐泽泻):** stir-fried with brine; classical preference when the target is the Kidney channel and the intent is to drain damp-heat from the lower burner (as in Liù Wèi Dì Huáng Wán).
- **Bran-fried (fǔ chǎo):** milder, more harmonizing on the Spleen; preferred when draining must not deplete.
- **In Wǔ Líng Sǎn (powder):** Zé Xiè is the chief ingredient and takes the largest proportion; the classical powder is taken in small repeated doses with warm water.
- **Timing:** morning and midday; avoid evening dosing because of the diuretic effect on sleep.
- **Course:** short-to-medium cyclical use; not intended as an indefinite daily supplement on its own.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**TCM Organ-Channel Assignment:**
- Sweet, bland, cold; enters the Kidney (肾) and Bladder (膀胱) channels.
- **Primary action:** promotes urination, drains dampness and damp-heat from the lower burner; clears Kidney deficiency-heat (the specific function in Liù Wèi Dì Huáng Wán).
- **Secondary action:** resolves phlegm-fluid (痰饮, tán yǐn) accumulation; addresses dizziness and "fluid-vertigo" arising from fluid that fails to transform.

**Water Element Context:** Zé Xiè is a quintessential Water-phase herb, acting on the Kidney and Bladder — the organs governing fluid metabolism. Its character is *draining* rather than *tonifying*; in Five-Phase formula design it is the counterweight that keeps rich tonics from stagnating, which is precisely its role in the Rehmannia family of pills.

**Cross-tradition parallel:** Western herbal medicine reached for very different plants for the same indication — *Taraxacum* (dandelion leaf) as a potassium-sparing diuretic, *Equisetum* (horsetail) for fluid retention — but the functional logic of "drain standing fluid without depleting the patient" is shared. There is no strong direct Ayurvedic analogue for the specific rhizome; the closest functional category is the *mutrala* (diuretic) group of dravyas.

### Contraindications

- **Yin deficiency without dampness:** Zé Xiè's cold, draining nature is contraindicated where there is dryness, true fluid depletion, or Yin-deficiency heat without an accompanying damp pattern — draining a dry patient worsens the depletion.
- **No damp accumulation:** if there is no fluid excess to drain, the herb is inappropriate and can cause excessive urination and dehydration.
- **Spermatorrhea/seminal loss without damp-heat:** classically cautioned, as draining can aggravate.
- **Pregnancy:** use only under qualified practitioner supervision.
- **Diuretic / electrolyte caution:** because the herb increases urination, combine cautiously with synthetic diuretics; monitor in anyone on antihypertensive or potassium-affecting medication. **Never use as a weight-cutting agent** — acute diuresis for competition weight manipulation is dangerous and is explicitly outside the Kenetica protocol.
