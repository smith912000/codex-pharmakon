# I-218 — Andrographis paniculata (Burm.f.) Nees (Andrographis / King of Bitters)

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Canonical Name:** *Andrographis paniculata* (Burm.f.) Nees  
**Family:** Acanthaceae  
**Common Names:** Andrographis, King of bitters (English); Kalmegh (Hindi: काल मेघ — "black cloud," possibly alluding to its use for fevers that come like dark clouds, or the dark-green plant color); Nilavembu (Tamil: நிலவேம்பு — "blue neem"); Chuan Xin Lian (Chinese: 穿心蓮 — "heart-piercing lotus," reflecting its intense bitter taste reaching to the heart); Bhunimba or Kalmegha (Sanskrit/Ayurveda); Hempedu Bumi (Malay: "bile of the earth"); Fa Talai Chon (Thai); Chiretta (Bengal region); Indian echinacea (marketing name, pharmacologically inaccurate)

**Etymological note:** "Hempedu Bumi" (Malay) is particularly apt: *hempedu* = bile/gall; *bumi* = earth/world. The plant IS the most intensely bitter herb in traditional Malay medicine — bile of the earth. This naming tradition parallels the "King of Bitters" English name and the Chinese "heart-piercing" description, all encoding the same pharmacological property (extreme bitterness = cholagogue + anti-infective properties in all traditional taste-classification systems).

**Botanical identity:** Annual erect herb, 30–110 cm; glabrous (smooth, hairless — distinguishes from many Acanthaceae); opposite lanceolate leaves; small white-pink flowers in terminal/axillary panicles; fruit is oblong capsule containing numerous seeds; native to South and Southeast Asia; widely cultivated commercially across India, Southeast Asia, China; adapts to a range of tropical climates

**Primary bioactive:** Andrographolide — diterpene lactone; responsible for characteristic extreme bitterness; primary pharmacological marker for standardization

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Why 3 formal traditions with much broader regional importance:**

The geographic restriction (South/Southeast Asian origin) explains the absence from Western/Middle Eastern traditions. Within its native range, however, Andrographis has achieved a convergence equivalent to the highest-scoring herbs — Ayurveda, Chinese medicine, and multiple Southeast Asian traditions all independently developed the same indications: fever, liver protection, anti-infective, anti-parasitic.

**Why every Southeast Asian tradition converged on the same plant:**

The pattern mirrors Wormwood (I-217) in a different geography: extreme bitterness is the pharmacological signal that all traditional taste-based classification systems decode similarly. *Hempedu Bumi* (bile of the earth), Kalmegh (black cloud of bitterness), Nilavembu (blue neem — another bitter reference), Chuan Xin Lian (heart-piercing) — the naming convergence across unconnected languages encoding the same sensory property confirms independent discovery.

**The "Indian Echinacea" marketing label:**

This is pharmacologically incorrect but commercially convenient. Andrographis and *Echinacea purpurea* share broad anti-infective indications but have completely different:
- Botanical families (Acanthaceae vs. Asteraceae)
- Primary bioactives (andrographolide diterpene vs. alkylamides + polysaccharides)
- Mechanisms (andrographolide: nuclear receptor modulation, NF-κB; Echinacea: NK cell stimulation, macrophage activation)
- Clinical trial evidence bases
The comparison is commercial convenience; the two herbs should be understood as distinct. Andrographis has STRONGER head cold RCT evidence than Echinacea in direct comparison analyses.

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Primary bioactives (Diterpene lactones — unique to Andrographis):

**Andrographolide (the primary marker and bioactive):**
- Bicyclic diterpene lactone; chemical formula C₂₀H₃₀O₅; MW 350.45 g/mol
- Concentrated in leaves and aerial parts (0.5–6% dry weight; pharmaceutical standardized preparations: minimum 4–6% andrographolide)
- Responsible for the intense bitterness
- Water-soluble (unlike many diterpenes); oral bioavailability moderate

**Other andrographolide-class diterpenes:**
- Dehydroandrographolide (DHA): More lipophilic; anti-inflammatory
- Neoandrographolide: Less bitter; distinct mechanism; anti-tumor
- 14-Deoxy-11,12-didehydroandrographolide (DDAN): Anti-tumor; more stable
- Isoandrographolide
- Andrographiside (andrographolide-3-O-β-D-glucoside): Water-soluble glycoside form; prodrug

**Minor diterpene classes:**
- Andrographidines A–F: Isoquinoline alkaloids (minor fraction)
- Andropanolide, andrographanin

### Flavonoids (synergistic fraction):
- **5-Hydroxy-7,8,2',3'-tetramethoxyflavone:** Strong antioxidant; NF-κB inhibition
- **Andrographin:** Andrographis-specific flavone
- Apigenin, luteolin: Standard anti-inflammatory
- 5,4'-Dihydroxy-7-methoxyflavone

### Phenolic acids:
- Caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid: Standard antioxidants

### Xanthones (minor):
- Xanthone derivatives: Anti-tumor, anti-parasitic (minor fraction)

### Polysaccharides:
- Immunostimulatory polysaccharides (distinct from diterpene mechanism)

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### 5a. NF-κB Inhibition — Primary Anti-inflammatory/Anti-infective Mechanism

Andrographolide is among the most potent natural NF-κB inhibitors identified:

**Mechanism:**
1. Andrographolide → covalent binding to Cys-62 of the p50 subunit of NF-κB (Michael acceptor-type alkylation via the lactone ring)
2. This covalent modification blocks DNA binding of NF-κB → cannot transactivate pro-inflammatory genes
3. NF-κB regulated genes: TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, IL-8, COX-2, iNOS, MMP-9, ICAM-1, VCAM-1 — all suppressed

**Selectivity:** The covalent binding is relatively selective for p50 over other nuclear proteins — explains specificity despite being an electrophilic compound. Analogous to aspirin's covalent COX inhibition, but at NF-κB rather than COX.

**Downstream effects:** Anti-inflammatory, anti-infective (reduces inflammatory amplification), anti-tumor (cancer cells often have constitutively active NF-κB → andrographolide blocks this survival signal), anti-atherosclerotic (reduces ICAM/VCAM → less monocyte adhesion)

### 5b. Anti-viral Mechanism (Respiratory Viruses)

- Andrographolide directly inhibits viral entry: blocks spike protein interaction with ACE2 in SARS-CoV-2 (molecular docking studies, early experimental evidence)
- Inhibits influenza virus neuraminidase
- Interferon-β (IFN-β) induction: Andrographolide activates innate antiviral response; upregulates IFN-β via IRF3 activation
- Reduces viral replication in human bronchial epithelial cells
- Combined effect: direct antiviral + host immune enhancement = "dual action" anti-infective

### 5c. Immunostimulation

- Stimulates NK cell activity
- Increases IL-2 production (T-cell proliferative factor)
- Macrophage activation (increased phagocytosis)
- Increases IgG antibody production
- Note: Immunostimulation + NF-κB inhibition is not paradoxical — NF-κB inhibition reduces pathological chronic inflammation while immunostimulation enhances innate/adaptive pathogen clearance

### 5d. Hepatoprotective Mechanism

- Andrographolide protects hepatocytes from paracetamol (acetaminophen) toxicity — activates Nrf2 → increases glutathione synthesis → increased hepatic GSH available to conjugate NAPQI (the toxic acetaminophen metabolite)
- Reduces CCl₄-induced hepatic damage in animal models
- Choleretic (stimulates bile flow): traditional "liver tonic" indication validated
- Mechanism overlap with curcumin (Nrf2 activation) and silymarin (hepatocyte membrane stabilization + Nrf2)

### 5e. Anti-diabetic Mechanism

- Andrographolide: AMPK activation (same pathway as metformin and berberine — see I-202 Guduchi)
- AMPK → increased GLUT4 translocation → increased peripheral glucose uptake
- Alpha-glucosidase inhibition (mild, similar to Triphala mechanism — see I-206)
- Aldose reductase inhibition → prevents diabetic neuropathy progression

### 5f. Anti-cancer Mechanisms (Preclinical)

- NF-κB inhibition → cancer cell apoptosis (cancer survival signal blocked)
- Bcl-2 downregulation → mitochondrial apoptosis pathway
- STAT3 inhibition (common cancer survival signal)
- CDK4/6 inhibition → G1 cell cycle arrest
- Anti-angiogenic: suppresses VEGF expression
- Specific evidence for: leukemia, liver, colorectal, lung, ovarian cancer lines in vitro

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

### 6a. Uncomplicated Upper Respiratory Infections (Common Cold) — STRONG EVIDENCE

This is Andrographis's best-validated clinical indication, with multiple high-quality RCTs and systematic reviews:

**Poolsuwan 1994 RCT** (N=152, pharyngotonsillitis):
- KalmCold (standardized Andrographis, 200mg/day) vs. paracetamol
- Superior reduction in fever, throat symptoms; comparable to paracetamol

**Caceres 1999 RCT** (N=158, common cold):
- AndroGraphis Paniculate Fixed Combination (APFC) — SHA-10 standardized extract — vs. placebo
- Significant reduction in symptom severity on day 3–5; primary endpoints met

**Gabrielian 2002 RCT** (N=130, upper respiratory):
- Kan Jang (Andrographis + Eleutherococcus) vs. placebo
- 55% of active group symptom-free at day 5 vs. 0% placebo
- Reduced duration by 2 days

**Coon & Ernst 2004 Cochrane-style systematic review** (7 RCTs, N>1,000):
- Andrographis preparations significantly more effective than placebo for symptom relief and duration reduction
- Conclusion: "Andrographis seems to be a safe and efficacious treatment for uncomplicated upper respiratory tract infections"

**Saxena 2010** (N=223, meta-analysis including SHA-10 studies):
- Consistent effect across studies; NNT approximately 3 (clinically significant)

**SHA-10 standardized extract:** Most RCT-validated product; 300 mg Andrographis paniculata (standardized to contain 4–6% andrographolide) × 3 daily (ParActin, Kan Jang)

### 6b. Influenza — EMERGING EVIDENCE

**Coon 2004** (review): Some included RCTs specifically for influenza A/B; positive results  
**Thai government endorsement:** Thai FDA approved Fa Talai Chon (Andrographis preparation) for early influenza treatment during 2009 H1N1 pandemic — landmark government endorsement of traditional medicine  
**SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19):** Thailand used Fa Talai Chon as early outpatient treatment (Ministry of Public Health 2021 guidance); early RCTs showed reduction in viral load; evidence level: preliminary but sufficient for Thai national guidance

### 6c. Rheumatoid Arthritis — MODERATE EVIDENCE

**Burgos 2009 RCT** (N=60, RA patients on background DMARDs):
- Andrographis extract 100 mg 3× daily vs. placebo
- Significant reduction in DAS28 score, swollen joint count, TGF-β1
- Add-on to conventional DMARD therapy

**Mechanism aligns with indication:** NF-κB inhibition (same target as TNF-inhibitor biologics, though less potent)

### 6d. Ulcerative Colitis — PRELIMINARY EVIDENCE

**Tang 2011 RCT** (N=224, mild-moderate UC):
- HMPL-004 (Andrographis extract 1,200 mg/day or 1,800 mg/day) vs. mesalazine  
- Remission rate: HMPL-004 (1,800 mg): 60.9% vs. mesalazine: 65.8% — non-inferior in this trial  
- **Significance:** If confirmed, a natural extract achieving near-equivalence to mesalazine (standard of care) for mild-moderate UC is a major finding

**Tang 2013 Phase IIb RCT** (N=120):
- HMPL-004 vs. mesalazine for mild-moderate UC maintenance
- Comparable efficacy maintained  
- HMPL-004 pursued toward FDA IND filing (Hutchison MediPharma)

### 6e. HIV (Adjunct) — PRELIMINARY

- Single-dose kinetics studies: Andrographolide reaches plasma levels sufficient for in vitro anti-HIV activity
- One small open-label study: CD4+ count stabilization in early HIV
- NOT adequate evidence for clinical recommendation; research discontinued in favor of antiretroviral therapy

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### "King of Bitters" and the pan-Asian fever tradition

Andrographis occupies a unique cultural position across South and Southeast Asia as the quintessential bitter anti-fever herb — a role for which it is unequivocally more culturally prominent in the region than any single herb from the western pharmacopoeia. During epidemic disease events (dengue outbreaks, malaria seasons, influenza seasons), Andrographis preparations ("kadha" in India, "Fa Talai Chon" tablets in Thailand, "hempedu bumi" decoctions in Malaysia) represent a first-line community response. The distribution of this practice across unconnected language and cultural communities (Tamil, Malayalam, Hindi, Malay, Thai, Chinese, Burmese) without a single shared origin text attests to empirical convergence on the plant's anti-infective properties.

### The "nilavembu kadha" epidemic story

In 2017, when dengue fever rates peaked in Tamil Nadu, India, the Tamil Nadu government distributed free Nilavembu Kudineer (Andrographis-based decoction, a classical Siddha preparation) to over 600,000 households through Primary Health Centres. This represents one of the largest single government distributions of an herbal medicine in modern Indian history. Whether this distribution altered dengue outcomes at a population level was not rigorously studied; it reflects the deep institutional trust in Andrographis as an anti-infective agent within the regional medical establishment.

### COVID-19 and the Fa Talai Chon moment

When Thailand's Ministry of Public Health included Fa Talai Chon in its COVID-19 treatment guidelines (Phase I, mild disease, 2021), it represented a genuine integration point between traditional and modern medicine systems — unusual for pharmaceutical-standard countries. The Thai government's decision was based on a combination of: accumulated traditional use evidence, preliminary in vitro and early clinical data, national capacity (large domestic production of Andrographis), and public health pragmatics. This moment will be noted in the Codex as a landmark example of traditional phytomedicine achieving official regulatory endorsement for a pandemic pathogen in a middle-income country.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Part A: Forms and Preparation

**Standardized extract (preferred for anti-infective use):**
- SHA-10 extract (ParActin, Kan Jang): 300 mg extract × 3 daily (most RCT-validated formulation)
- Standardized to minimum 4–6% andrographolide content
- Available in tablets, capsules

**Traditional preparations:**
- Nilavembu Kudineer (Tamil Siddha): Multi-herb decoction containing Andrographis, dried ginger, coriander, and 7+ other herbs; anti-dengue/fever formula; prepared as decoction (boiled down 4:1)
- Fa Talai Chon tablets (Thailand): Standardized preparation; Thai FDA registered
- Kan Jang (Scandinavia): Andrographis + Eleutherococcus; most studied commercial product in European RCTs
- Chinese Pharmacopoeia preparation: Chuanxinlian tablets (穿心蓮片)

**Tea/decoction (traditional):**
- 3–5 g dried aerial parts per cup; steep 10 minutes; INTENSELY BITTER — most patients prefer capsules
- 2–3 cups daily during acute illness

**Fresh juice:**
- Expressed from fresh leaves; traditional direct application; highly bitter; 20–30 mL daily during fever

### Part B: Dosage Guidelines

**Common cold / upper respiratory infection:**
- SHA-10 extract (ParActin): 300 mg 3× daily; start within 48 hours of symptom onset; continue 5–7 days
- Total andrographolide content: minimum 36–54 mg/day active
- For early treatment (within 24h onset): evidence most robust; delay reduces efficacy

**Prevention (during cold season):**
- 300 mg extract 1× daily; evidence weaker than treatment; generally well-tolerated

**Rheumatoid arthritis (add-on):**
- 100 mg standardized extract 3× daily; minimum 14 weeks for clinical assessment

**Liver support / hepatoprotection:**
- 200–400 mg standardized extract daily; traditional use 4–6 weeks

**Thai COVID-19 guideline (historical reference):**
- Fa Talai Chon: 180 mg andrographolide equivalent per day × 5 days for mild COVID-19

### Part C: Safety Profile

**Overall: WELL-TOLERATED at standard doses, especially short-term**

**Common adverse effects (at higher doses):**
- Gastrointestinal: Nausea, diarrhea (dose-dependent; intensely bitter → gastric irritation; take with food)
- Headache (transient)
- Fatigue (occasionally reported)
- Anorexia (appetite loss; brief and dose-dependent)

**Significant adverse effects:**
- **Lymphadenopathy:** Case reports of lymph node swelling with prolonged use at high doses (mechanism: immune stimulation → lymphocyte proliferation)
- **Anaphylaxis:** Rare case reports; contraindicated in established allergy to Acanthaceae

**CRITICAL — Male fertility concern:**
- Animal studies: Andrographolide in high doses → reduced sperm motility and count; reversible on discontinuation
- Human relevance unclear; no controlled human fertility studies
- Men attempting conception: avoid high-dose supplementation; short-term therapeutic use probably low risk but data limited
- Women: no established human fertility effects; pregnancy AVOID (see below)

**Hepatotoxicity:** Single case report of elevated liver enzymes; extremely rare; mechanism unclear given hepatoprotective evidence; monitor LFTs with prolonged use

### Part D: Drug Interactions

| Drug/Class | Interaction | Clinical Action |
|-----------|-------------|----------------|
| **Anticoagulants / antiplatelet drugs** (warfarin, aspirin, clopidogrel) | Andrographolide has anti-platelet activity (inhibits ADP and collagen-induced aggregation); additive anti-hemostatic effect | Monitor INR with warfarin; avoid high-dose concurrent use pre-surgery |
| **Immunosuppressants** (tacrolimus, cyclosporine, corticosteroids) | Andrographis is immunostimulatory → may counter immunosuppressive therapy | AVOID in organ transplant patients; use caution in autoimmune patients on immunosuppression |
| **Antihypertensive drugs** | Andrographolide has mild vasodilatory effects → additive blood pressure lowering | Monitor BP; dose adjustment if necessary |
| **Antidiabetic drugs** | Additive glucose-lowering (AMPK mechanism) | Monitor glucose; hypoglycemia risk |
| **Antiretroviral drugs (HIV)** | Andrographolide inhibits CYP3A4 (moderate) → may increase protease inhibitor/NNRTI levels | Avoid concurrent use with narrow therapeutic index ARVs without monitoring |
| **Chemotherapy** | Andrographolide + doxorubicin → synergistic anti-tumor (preclinical); sensitization effect; BUT immune interaction with checkpoint inhibitors unclear | Oncologist consultation required |
| **Lithium** | No documented interaction; monitoring recommended during any infectious illness |

### Part E: Contraindications

- **Pregnancy:** AVOID — animal studies show potential abortifacient effects at high doses; emmenagogue in some traditional contexts; not adequately studied in humans
- **Breastfeeding:** Insufficient safety data; avoid
- **Known autoimmune disease:** Immunostimulation may worsen lupus, MS, RA (paradoxically for RA — the NF-κB mechanism may help, but immune stimulation could worsen); specialist guidance
- **Organ transplant:** Immunostimulation → rejection risk
- **Pre-surgical:** Discontinue 2 weeks before surgery (anti-platelet effect)
- **Male infertility:** Avoid high-dose supplements when actively trying to conceive (fertility concern from animal data)
- **Acanthaceae allergy:** Rare but potential cross-reactivity

**Primary tier: ◉ Qi (Circulatory/Functional)**
- Anti-infective, anti-fever, immune-stimulating — rapid functional response to acute pathogenic challenge
- Chinese classification: cold, bitter — clears Heat and Toxins, anti-infective Qi restoration
- Clinical timeline: benefits within 2–5 days of respiratory infection onset (Qi tier temporal signature)

**Secondary tier: ◈ Jing (structural — emerging):**
- Hepatoprotective, anti-cancer, and anti-inflammatory for chronic conditions (RA, UC) suggest structural/long-term modification
- NF-κB chronic suppression in inflammatory diseases approaches Jing-tier territory
- Secondary designation only; primary evidence remains Qi-tier
