# Lu ru spurge — 闾茹 *Lu Ru* (printed 兰茹 in Sun's heading; aliases 离楼 *Li Lou*, 屈居 *Qu Ju*, 屈据, 离娄; "Radix Euphorbiae Adenochlorae", Yang 1998)

## *Euphorbia adenochlora Morr. & Decne., a spurge, the identification we give to 闾茹; of medium confidence: the Wu Pu layer describes a yellow-rooted, juicy-rooted plant with yellow flowers and black fruit, which suits a spurge, and Yang names the species, but the Shennong paragraph itself gives no description, and Euphorbia ebracteolata (the wolf-poison plant) is an alternative. A toxic drug of the lower class: historical record only, with a plain toxicity caution* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts on disk.** *Lu ru* is a monograph of the Divine Farmer's Classic. It has no chapter in Sushruta, Dioscorides, Ebers or the Assyrian texts. **Toxic drug: historical record only.** The Wu Pu layer calls it toxic, and spurges carry a caustic latex. Nothing in this entry is a recommendation. The Codex has related spurge entries, for example I_106 (Fischer's spurge, *Lang Du*) and I_221 (*Euphorbia characias*), but the Shennong drug is a separate monograph and is not read as either.

**The name.** Sun's heading prints **兰茹** (*lan ru*) and a bracket says that the *Taiping yulan* writes the first character 闾 (*lü* or *lu*) "and this is right" (《御览》作闾，是); the Wu Pu layer, the Mingyi layer and the *Fan Zi Jiran* quotation below all write 闾茹. The Codex uses 闾茹 *Lu Ru*, with 兰茹 as the printed variant. The later appendix also writes 兰茹, in the line "*danggui* dislikes *lan ru*" (当归 恶兰茹, line 4355). (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` lines 3491-3493)

**The Shennong text proper.** The 味 paragraph, in the lower (下) classic, reads 味辛，寒 ("acrid, cold"). It governs 蚀恶肉、败创、死肌 ("eating away malign flesh, rotten sores, dead flesh"), "kills itch worms" (杀疥虫, scabies-type parasites), 排脓恶血 ("expels pus and malign blood"), 除大风 ("removes great wind") and 善忘不乐 ("forgetfulness, joylessness"). The habitat is 生川谷 ("river valleys"). **There is no 久服 ("taken long") clause.** (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, lower classic, entry 闾茹 *lu ru*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 3495)

**Wu Pu layer (3rd century).** 《吴普》曰 gives the aliases 离楼 and 屈居, and three views of the flavour: "Shennong: acrid; Qibo: sour, salty, **toxic** (有毒); Mr Li: greatly cold." It gives a description of the plant, the only one in the sources: gathered in the second month; "the leaf is round and yellow; the height is four or five *chi*; the leaves stand four and four opposite each other; in the fourth month the flower is yellow; in the fifth month the fruit is black, the root yellow with juice, also yellow like that"; "gather the root in the third and fifth months; those with a black head are good" (line 3497). The three physicians' names are the layer's own device for citing earlier opinions; the Codex attaches no further authority to them.

**Mingyi bielu layer (later).** 《名医》曰 gives the aliases 屈据 and 离娄, the place 生代郡 ("Dai commandery") and the harvest 五月采，阴干 ("gather in the fifth month; dry in the shade") (line 3499).

**Sun's note (案).** The *Guangya* says 屈居，芦茹也 ("*qu ju* is *lu ru*"), and *Fan Zi Jiran* says 闾茹 comes from Wudu and "the yellow-coloured is the good" (line 3501).

**Yang 1998 (second witness).** Yang prints "Lu Ru (Radix Euphorbiae Adenochlorae)" in "Herbs: Inferior Class" and reads the flavour as "sour and salty. It is toxic" (line 3623, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`), which follows the Qibo entry in the Wu Pu layer and not Sun's "acrid, cold". His note 224 adds that the drug "is among the most frequently used medicinals for various sorts of sores" in modern times (lines 3628-3633). The two witnesses thus differ on flavour and on toxicity, and the Codex records both: Sun's Shennong text has no toxicity word, and the Wu Pu layer has one.

**Identity.** The Wu Pu description (yellow root with juice, round yellow leaves in fours, yellow flowers, black fruit) matches no single plant exactly, but a root with a yellow juice and the classic's use for eroding sores suit a spurge; Yang names *Euphorbia adenochlora*, an upright perennial spurge of China, and the Codex follows it with medium confidence. *Euphorbia ebracteolata* (the 狼毒 *lang du* plant of the Chinese pharmacy) is an alternative. The Codex does not claim a species.

### Active Components

Spurges (*Euphorbia*) contain a milky or coloured latex rich in irritant and tumour-promoting diterpene esters, which blister the skin and mucous membranes; this is general toxicology of the genus and does not come from the texts read. The composition of *E. adenochlora* is not stated in any source retrieved. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice, and not to be taken. The classic records *lu ru* as an external-and-internal remedy for rotting flesh and sores, itch worms and pus, and adds "removes great wind" and a mood-and-memory phrase. The Codex records these as the claims of the classic. The classic's own Wu Pu layer calls the drug toxic, and its use "to eat away" dead flesh describes a caustic. A caustic spurge root should be read as a corrosive, not as a medicine to be taken by a cultivator.

### Preparation & Dosing

The paragraph gives no preparation, dose or course. The Wu Pu layer gives the root as the part ("those with a black head are good"), gathered in the third and fifth months, and the Mingyi layer says it is dried in the shade. The preface of the classic gives a general rule for toxic drugs, to begin "as small as a millet grain" and increase only if the illness does not yield (line 4126); the Codex does not reproduce it as a dose for this plant. No dose is given in the sources retrieved, and the Codex does not supply one. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Many spurge roots are used in East Asian medicine and have been studied in the laboratory; the same irritant compounds that give them an effect also make them toxic, so they are used in processed form and small amounts in the tradition, and ingestion of raw spurge can cause severe vomiting, diarrhoea and mucous-membrane burns. This is a general statement about the genus. Whether *E. adenochlora* has any effect on the complaints named has not been tested in sources retrieved for this entry. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SBJ (Shennong text proper).** 味辛，寒: acrid; cold. The Wu Pu layer adds the alternative flavours "sour, salty" (Qibo) and "greatly cold" (Mr Li), and "toxic" (有毒) for Qibo. The Shennong paragraph prints no toxicity word; the lower-class placement carries the preface's general statement that lower drugs are "mostly toxic" (多毒) and "not for long taking" (不可久服) (line 2935).
- No organ or channel affinity is printed. No other tradition's classification is applied.

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The paragraph stands in the lower classic. The preface says lower drugs are "assistants" that "treat disease to correspond with the earth", are mostly toxic, may not be taken for long, and are for those who want to remove cold-and-heat evil qi, break accumulations and cure illness (line 2935). The paragraph has a flavour, a nature, indications and a habitat, and **no 久服 clause**. It therefore carries only the lower class's therapeutic frame and no cultivation status. (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, lower classic, entry 闾茹 *lu ru*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` lines 2935 and 3495)

**Practitioner's note.** A lower-grade, toxic drug in the Chinese scheme: a caustic for a defined complaint, for a short course under skilled hands, and then stopped. Nothing here supports long-term use, and the classic's own grading is the warning. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Toxic and caustic.** Do not take or apply any spurge root. The latex burns skin and eyes and can cause severe vomiting and diarrhoea if swallowed.
- **Skin and eyes.** Wash at once with plenty of water if latex contacts them, and seek medical care if the eye is involved.
- **Pregnancy, children and the frail.** Avoid. This is a precaution drawn from the genus and the stated toxicity, not from the Shennong paragraph.
- **Identity.** Several spurges, including the wolf-poison plant, may be sold under this name; do not substitute one for another.
- **Sores and "forgetfulness".** The classic's uses are historical claims; a spreading sore, or a change in memory or mood, needs medical care.
