# Lungwort — Common Lungwort, Jerusalem Cowslip, Spotted Dog, Lung Moss (of the herbalists), *Pulmonariae Herba*

## *Pulmonaria officinalis* L. | Family: Boraginaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM
**Tags (previous line, retained for reference):** AT, DMM, BM (Avicennian/Unani)
**Energetic Classification:** Cool and moist (Galenic) — a demulcent of the second degree, softening and soothing rather than heating
**Training Phase:** Grounding / Restoration (soothing the respiratory gate)

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian (AT).** The cuneiform materia medica of Nineveh records a large repertoire of "chest" and "cough" remedies — mucilaginous, soothing herbs given for dryness of the throat and hard breathing. Lungwort belongs to this broad Near-Eastern demulcent tradition: a soft-leaved, hairy plant of damp woodland whose slippery leaf infusion was taken to ease a rasping cough. The Assyrian frame is symptomatic and humoral rather than botanical, so attribution here is by therapeutic category (soothing chest herbs), graded *traditional*.

**Dioscorides / Graeco-Roman (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* catalogues a family of woolly, mucilage-rich herbs used against coughs, roughness of the windpipe, and spitting of blood. Lungwort entered later European herbals within this lineage; by the medieval and early-modern period *Pulmonaria* was a fixed entry in the Western apothecary for "diseases of the lungs," prescribed as a syrup, an infusion, or an ingredient in pectoral (chest) teas.

**Avicennian / Unani (CM).** In the Qanun tradition that synthesised Galen with Persian-Arabic practice, cool-moist mucilaginous simples were used to counter hot, dry conditions of the chest — the burning dry cough, hoarseness, and thirst of an over-heated respiratory humour. Lungwort fits this class as a moistening, relaxing pectoral, tempering dryness and irritation.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** Lungwort is the classic textbook case of the medieval Doctrine of Signatures: its leaves are broad, spongy, and marked with pale spots said to resemble diseased or ulcerated lung tissue — hence the name (Latin *pulmo*, lung; German *Lungenkraut*; English "lungwort"). The signature was read as divine instruction that the plant treats the organ it resembles. Modern pharmacology does not vindicate the mystical claim, but it partly rescues the empirical practice: the same leaf that "looks like a lung" is genuinely rich in mucilage that coats and soothes inflamed mucous membranes, and in phenolic antioxidants that damp the oxidative and inflammatory chemistry of airway irritation. The symbol was wrong about *why*; the use was defensible on *what*.

### Active Components

- **Mucilage (polysaccharides):** the demulcent backbone — forms a soothing film over irritated pharyngeal and bronchial mucosa.
- **Phenolic acids and depsides:** rosmarinic acid, lithospermic, salvianolic, monardic, shimobashiric and yunnaneic acids, and danshensu/caffeic-acid conjugates — the dominant fraction of the aerial parts, and the principal antioxidant/anti-inflammatory carriers.
- **Tannins:** mild astringency, tightening and protecting inflamed tissue.
- **Allantoin:** associated with soothing and tissue-supportive action.
- **Flavonoids, silicic acid, saponins:** minor contributors.
- **Pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs):** as a Boraginaceae member, *Pulmonaria* can carry low levels of hepatotoxic PAs — see Contraindications.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Within Kenetica, lungwort is a **soothing respiratory-gate herb** used in the Grounding/Restoration phase: where the breath is dry, scraping, and reactive, the mucilage restores a protective, moist lining and the phenolics calm the underlying irritation. It is a supportive, low-intensity botanical — never a stimulant or a "clearing" purge, but a softener that lets an inflamed airway settle. Practically it pairs with other demulcents (marshmallow, mullein) for dry, unproductive coughs, hoarseness, and throat rawness.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the modern evidence base is small and mechanistic (in vitro / in silico), not clinical — the grade is best stated as **traditional use with early laboratory plausibility**.

- Phenolic-rich fractions from *Pulmonaria* aerial parts reduced peroxynitrite-induced oxidative damage in human blood plasma and inhibited cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) in vitro, with in-silico docking of rosmarinic and salvianolic acids to the enzyme — a plausible molecular basis for an anti-inflammatory, soothing action on irritated airway tissue. Krzyżanowska-Kowalczyk et al., *Molecules* 2021 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules26030631)).
- Detailed LC-MS/MS profiling confirmed that lungwort aerial parts are dominated (>80% of the isolated fraction) by phenolic acids and depsides, identifying nine previously undescribed compounds and documenting seasonal variation. Krzyżanowska-Kowalczyk et al., *Molecules* 2018 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules23092277)).
- Yunnaneic acid B, first reported in *P. officinalis* here, showed DPPH-radical and peroxynitrite-scavenging activity and protected blood-plasma proteins and lipids from oxidative stress without cytotoxicity to mononuclear cells at ≤50 µg/mL. Krzyżanowska-Kowalczyk et al., *J. Agric. Food Chem.* 2017 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jafc.7b00718)).

Together these support antioxidant and COX-2-directed anti-inflammatory activity of the phenolic fraction. There are **no robust human respiratory trials**; the traditional demulcent/expectorant reputation rests on mucilage physiology plus this in-vitro anti-inflammatory signal, not on clinical proof.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani (as attested):** cool and moist; demulcent, relaxing, moistening — indicated for hot, dry, irritated conditions of the chest and throat.
- **TCM / Ayurveda:** *Pulmonaria officinalis* is a European woodland herb outside the classical Chinese and Ayurvedic canons; no authentic TCM channel-entry or Ayurvedic *rasa/guna/virya/vipaka* attribution exists. By analogy only, its actions read as *sweet/bland, cooling, moistening* — but this is a cross-mapping, not a traditional citation, and is marked as such.

### Contraindications

⚠️ **PYRROLIZIDINE-ALKALOID (PA) HEPATOTOXICITY — INTERNAL USE.** *Pulmonaria officinalis* is a member of Boraginaceae, the plant family that includes comfrey and borage and is notorious for hepatotoxic 1,2-unsaturated pyrrolizidine alkaloids. These PAs are cumulative hepatotoxins linked to hepatic veno-occlusive disease (sinusoidal obstruction syndrome), and are genotoxic/potentially carcinogenic. Even where individual lungwort samples test low, the family-level risk means **internal use should be short-term, limited to PA-tested material, and avoided entirely in:**

- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding** (PAs cross the placenta and enter milk).
- **Infants and young children.**
- **Anyone with liver disease or on hepatotoxic medication.**

Do not combine with other Boraginaceae (comfrey, borage) or PA-containing herbs; avoid prolonged or high-dose internal courses. For habitual use, prefer PA-free monographed alternatives. Topical use over broken skin should also use tested material. This is a **hard safety flag**: the traditional "lung remedy" reputation does not override the modern PA hepatotoxicity concern.
