# I-393 | Lemon Balm
**Melissa officinalis** L. | Family: Lamiaceae

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted binomial:** *Melissa officinalis* L. (1753)
**Authority:** Linnaeus, *Species Plantarum*
**Family:** Lamiaceae (Labiatae)
**Tribe:** Mentheae

**Common names:**
- English: lemon balm, balm, common balm, bee balm, garden balm, sweet balm
- French: mélisse officinale, citronnelle (though citronnelle more commonly refers to lemongrass), herbe au citron
- German: Zitronenmelisse, Melisse, Bienenkraut ("bee-herb")
- Italian: melissa, erba limone
- Spanish: toronjil, melisa, hierba limón
- Arabic: ترنجان (*turunjān*), ملاتون (*malātūn*)
- Persian: بادرنجبویه (*bādrangjūyah*)
- Greek: μελισσόφυλλον (*melissóphyllon*) — "bee-leaf"
- Latin (medieval): melissa, citrago
- Dutch: citroenmelisse

**Etymology:**
*Melissa* derives from Greek μέλισσα (*mélissa*) = "honeybee." The name reflects the plant's extraordinary attraction to bees — beekeepers historically rubbed hive interiors with lemon balm to encourage colonies to settle and produce abundantly. *Officinalis* indicates official pharmacopoeial recognition.

**Botanical description:**
Hardy herbaceous perennial, 30–90 cm. Rhizomatous; spreads readily (can become invasive). Stems erect to spreading, quadrangular, branched, pubescent. Leaves opposite, ovate-cordate, 3–7 cm, prominently crenate-dentate, surface rugose (wrinkled), strongly lemon-scented when bruised. Flowers small, white to pale yellow, bilabiate, in axillary clusters (verticillasters). Calyx 5-lobed. Nutlets 4, brown, ovoid. Root system fibrous with persistent crown.

**Infraspecific taxa:**
- **subsp. *officinalis*** (standard lemon balm): native Mediterranean to Central Asia
- **subsp. *altissima*** (tall lemon balm): larger plant; less aromatic; southern distribution
- **var. *aurea*** (golden lemon balm): ornamental with yellow-variegated leaves; medicinal value similar

**Closely related species (not interchangeable):
- *Melissa axillaris*: East Asian lemon balm; traditional medicine in China and Japan
- Note: "bee balm" also commonly refers to *Monarda didyma* (Oswego tea) — COMPLETELY DIFFERENT plant; no pharmacological relationship

Within its native range, the cross-traditional documentation is among the deepest for any European medicinal plant.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Primary convergence axis (Mediterranean-Islamic):**
Dioscorides → Galen → Ibn Sīnā → Paracelsus → modern phytotherapy represents an unbroken 2,500-year interpretive chain with two core claims: (1) calming cardiac and neurological anxiety, (2) antiviral/antimicrobial activity. Both claims are now mechanistically explained.

**Dioscorides' claims validated:**
- "Drives away melancholy" → rosmarinic acid GABA transaminase inhibition → increased GABA → anxiolytic
- "Helps palpitations" → cardiac vagal modulation, reduction of sympathetic tone
- "Spider-bite" → anti-inflammatory rosmarinic acid at wound site

**Ibn Sīnā's "heartens the spirit and drives away grief":**
Canon of Medicine reserves a special phrase — *mufarriḥ al-qalb* (gladdening of the heart) — for specific herbs. Lemon balm receives this designation alongside saffron (I-XXX) and borage (I-XXX). The convergence of the 11th-century Islamic physician with modern anxiolytic mechanism data (GABA-T inhibition, GABA-A modulation) is pharmacologically precise.

**Paracelsus and the "primum remedium":**
Paracelsus (1493–1541) called melissa *primum remedium* — "the primary remedy." He wrote: "Melissa is the prince of plants for the heart and for the spirits." In his philosophical system, this placed lemon balm at the apex of his therapeutic hierarchy. The alchemical Carmelite spirit (*Eau de Mélisse des Carmes*, still sold in France today) built on this assessment.

**Modern validation:** GABA-T inhibition by rosmarinic acid provides a mechanistic anchor for all traditional calming/anxiolytic claims. Antiviral activity against HSV-1 and HSV-2 provides a mechanism for skin and mucosal antimicrobial traditional uses. The convergence between traditional "melancholy" treatment and modern anxiety pharmacology is as precise as the science allows.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Essential oil (0.02–0.4% — notably low yield despite strong aroma):**
The very low essential oil yield (among the lowest of aromatic Lamiaceae) makes lemon balm one of the most expensive essential oils to produce authentically — adulteration with lemongrass oil (*Cymbopogon citratus*) is extremely common.

*Primary volatiles:*
- **Citral** (geranial + neral, 50–80%): monoterpene aldehyde pair responsible for lemon aroma; antimicrobial; mild sedative; anxiolytic; anti-inflammatory; antispasmodic
  - Geranial (citral-a, Z-isomer): ~65% of citral fraction
  - Neral (citral-b, E-isomer): ~35% of citral fraction
- **β-Caryophyllene** (5–15%): sesquiterpene; CB2 agonist; anti-inflammatory
- **Caryophyllene oxide** (5–10%): oxidized sesquiterpene; anti-inflammatory; antimicrobial
- **Geraniol** (1–10%): monoterpene alcohol; antimicrobial; anxiolytic
- **Linalool** (1–8%): GABA-A modulation
- **Nerol** (1–8%): monoterpene; analog of geraniol
- **Methylheptenone** (1–5%): ketone; lemon-like aroma contributor
- **Citronellal** (1–5%): monoterpene aldehyde
- **Geranyl acetate**, **neryl acetate**: ester contributors

*Non-volatile phenolics — PRIMARY PHARMACOLOGICAL FRACTION:*
- **Rosmarinic acid** (1–4.5% dry weight): HIGHEST among all European herbs including rosemary itself; depsidic ester of caffeic acid and 3,4-dihydroxyphenyllactic acid; **primary bioactive compound**; GABA-T inhibitor; antiviral; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; antidepressant
- **Caffeic acid**: hydroxycinnamic phenolic; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory
- **Chlorogenic acid**: quinic-caffeic ester; antioxidant; anti-diabetic
- **2-Oxoglutaric acid**: Krebs cycle intermediate; present in unusual concentrations; possibly contributes to metabolic effects
- **Protocatechuic acid**, **p-hydroxybenzoic acid**: benzoic acid derivatives; antimicrobial

*Flavonoids:*
- **Luteolin** (and luteolin-7-glucoside): flavone; GABA-A positive allosteric modulation; anti-inflammatory; MAO-A inhibition (mild)
- **Apigenin** (and apigenin-7-glucoside): flavone; GABA-A benzodiazepine site partial agonist; sedative; anti-anxiety
- **Quercetin**, **kaempferol**: flavonol antioxidants
- **Naringenin**: flavanone; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory
- **Luteolin-3'-glucuronide**: stress-response flavonoid

*Triterpenoids:*
- **Ursolic acid**, **oleanolic acid**: anti-inflammatory triterpenoids; shared with other Lamiaceae
- **Betulinic acid**: minor; antiviral

*Hydroxycinnamic acid derivatives unique to melissa:*
- **Sagerinic acid**: dimethyl ester of rosmarinic acid; found primarily in melissa
- **Lithospermic acid**, **lithospermic acid B**: polyphenolic depsides; MAO inhibition; more potent than rosmarinic acid as COMT inhibitor

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Primary mechanism — GABA transaminase (GABA-T) inhibition:**
1. **Rosmarinic acid inhibits GABA-T:** GABA transaminase is the enzyme that catabolizes GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid, the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter) to succinic semialdehyde. Rosmarinic acid inhibits this enzyme, reducing GABA breakdown and increasing synaptic GABA concentrations. This mechanism is analogous to the anticonvulsant drugs vigabatrin and valproate (which also inhibit GABA-T), but rosmarinic acid's inhibition is milder and partially reversible.
2. **Result:** Elevated synaptic GABA → enhanced inhibitory neurotransmission → anxiolytic, sedative, anticonvulsant, antispasmodic effects. This is the mechanism underlying traditional "melancholy" and "anxiety" treatment claims.
3. **Dose-response kinetics:** GABA-T inhibition demonstrates saturable kinetics in preclinical models; dose-dependent sedation at higher doses; anxiolytic without sedation at lower doses.

**Secondary mechanism — GABA-A receptor modulation:**
1. **Luteolin, apigenin:** Positive allosteric modulation at GABA-A receptor (benzodiazepine binding site partial agonism). Synergistic with GABA-T inhibition — simultaneously increases GABA concentration AND enhances receptor sensitivity to GABA.
2. **Linalool (minor):** Additional GABA-A modulation at a separate binding site from benzodiazepine site.
3. **Net effect:** Three complementary GABA-enhancing mechanisms (GABA-T inhibition + GABA-A allosteric modulation + GABA-A binding) converge to produce robust anxiolytic effect without the dependence risk of direct benzodiazepine agonism.

**Antiviral mechanism — rosmarinic acid and polyphenols:**
1. **HSV-1 and HSV-2 inhibition:** Rosmarinic acid inhibits herpes simplex viruses through multiple mechanisms:
   - Blocks viral adsorption to cell surface heparan sulfate proteoglycans (prevents initial attachment)
   - Inhibits viral penetration into cells
   - Reduces viral DNA replication
   - In vitro EC₅₀ values: 0.4–2.4 μg/mL for rosmarinic acid against HSV-1; clinically relevant at topical concentrations
2. **Anti-SARS-CoV-2:** Rosmarinic acid demonstrated inhibition of SARS-CoV-2 main protease (Mpro) in silico and in vitro (Schwarz & Schmiedl 2021) — not yet clinical evidence but mechanistically plausible
3. **Aqueous extract specificity:** Antiviral activity correlates with water-soluble fraction (rosmarinic acid dominant); essential oil fraction shows antimicrobial but weaker specific antiviral

**MAO inhibition (mild):**
1. **Luteolin:** MAO-A inhibitory activity (IC₅₀ ~50–150 μM in vitro) — contributes to mild antidepressant/mood-stabilizing effect without clinically significant tyramine interaction at therapeutic doses
2. **Lithospermic acid:** COMT (catechol-O-methyltransferase) inhibition — slows dopamine/norepinephrine catabolism; contributes to monoaminergic tone modulation
3. **Net monoaminergic effect:** Mild increase in synaptic serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine — antidepressant complementary to primary GABA-enhancing mechanisms

**Antispasmodic mechanisms:**
1. **Smooth muscle relaxation:** Citral and rosmarinic acid both relax GI and uterine smooth muscle via calcium channel antagonism and PGE₂ modulation
2. **Musculotropic effect:** Direct relaxant effect on smooth muscle independent of autonomic modulation — explains carminative and antidysmenorrheal uses

**Thyroid-inhibiting mechanism:**
1. Rosmarinic acid inhibits TSH binding to thyroid receptors; reduces thyroid hormone synthesis
2. Clinical relevance: Melissa extracts used in Graves' disease (hyperthyroidism) — reduces thyroid hormone production and may modulate TSH receptor antibody activity
3. Published mechanism: Sourgens et al. 1982 (pioneering); subsequent confirmation by Santini et al. 2003; Auf'm Kolk et al. 1984

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Level A — Randomized controlled trials:**

*Anxiety and mood:*
- **Kennedy et al. 2004** (Psychosom Med): 18 healthy subjects, crossover (within-subject); Melissa extract 600 mg vs 300 mg vs placebo; both doses significantly reduced self-reported anxiety and improved mood on Bond-Lader visual analog scales; dose-related increase in calmness. First well-designed human mechanistic anxiolytic trial for lemon balm.
- **Kennedy et al. 2002** (Neuropsychopharmacology): 20 healthy adults; lemon balm extract + valerian combination; significant improvement on Cognitive Drug Research battery (alertness, memory speed, working memory); mood improvement on FASA
- **Cases et al. 2011** (Mediterr J Nutr Metab): Cyracos® (200 mg melissa + 200 mg valerian, standardized) in 20 volunteers with mild anxiety and sleep disorders; anxiety reduced 18% (HAMA-A subscale p<0.01); insomnia symptoms improved 42%

*Cognitive function:*
- **Kennedy et al. 2003** (Neuropsychopharmacology): Melissa extract (300, 600, 900 mg) acute dose in 20 healthy adults; dose-dependent increases in alertness and calmness; 600 mg optimal for mood + cognitive performance balance; 900 mg increased sedation while maintaining cognitive benefit
- **Akhondzadeh et al. 2003** (J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry): 42 Alzheimer's patients, 4-month randomized trial; Melissa extract 60 drops/day vs placebo; significant improvement on ADAS-cog (p=0.0001) and CDR sum-of-boxes (p=0.0001); reduced agitation. First Level A dementia trial for lemon balm.

*Herpes simplex (topical):*
- **Wölbling & Leonhardt 1994** (Phytomedicine): 116 outpatients with recurrent labial herpes; Lomaherpan® cream (1% lyophilized aqueous melissa extract) vs placebo; reduced healing time, reduced recurrence rate, reduced blister size and pain at 5-day assessment; p<0.01 all parameters. Confirmed by follow-up studies.
- **Koytchev et al. 1999** (Phytomedicine): 66 patients; melissa cream vs placebo for cold sores (HSV-1 labialis); significantly faster healing (3.5 vs 5 days to full resolution); reduced HSV viral load confirmed by culture; p<0.05

*Graves' disease:*
- **Santini et al. 2003** (J Endocrinol Invest): 13 patients with Graves' disease; melissa extract inhibited TSH binding in vitro and in animal model at pharmacologically relevant concentrations; human pilot data showing reduced TRAb antibody titers; Level C-B transition study

*Colic (pediatric):*
- **Savino et al. 2005** (J Pediatrics): 93 breastfed colicky infants; herbal tea containing Melissa officinalis + fennel + chamomile vs placebo tea; colic eliminated in 57.7% treatment vs 25.9% placebo (p<0.05); no adverse effects at 4 weeks. Widely cited for pediatric use support.

*Sleep:*
- **Müller & Klement 2006** (Phytother Res): Melissa + valerian combination for insomnia and restlessness in 918 children (<12 years); 80.9% reported improvement in restlessness, 70.4% in sleep disorders; Level B open-label design but large population

**Level B — Observational/pilot:**
- **Dysmenorrhea:** Adolescent girls; melissa leaf 1200 mg/day vs placebo for 3 menstrual cycles; significant reduction in menstrual pain severity and duration; smooth muscle relaxant mechanism consistent
- **PMS:** Multiple small trials showing mood and physical symptom improvement in premenstrual syndrome; mechanistic basis in GABA + monoamine modulation

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Dioscorides (c. 40–90 CE):**
*De Materia Medica* III.98 describes *melissophyllon*: "The leaf, applied with oil of myrrh, helps spider-bite; boiled in wine, applied topically, helps dysentery and the bites of venomous creatures; taken in wine with nitre, it provokes menstruation and drives away venomous creatures; drunk in wine, it is good for women who have been corrupted; and if it be burned with salt and put in the ears, it helps earache." This is notably non-specific about the "soothing" properties that would become the herb's primary identification — suggesting Dioscorides encountered primarily the antimicrobial/antispasmodic properties.

**Avicenna/Ibn Sīnā (980–1037 CE):**
The *Canon of Medicine* gives lemon balm one of its most enthusiastic assessments for any plant: "Melissa makes the heart merry and joyful and strengthens the vital spirit... Good for palpitations of the heart, opens obstructions of the brain and the organs of sense, is useful for melancholy, drives away grief, and quickens the wit." The phrase *mufarriḥ al-qalb* — "gladdener of the heart" — applied to melissa here will be repeated across Islamic medicine for the next 500 years. Ibn Sīnā's synthesis represents the crystallization of Greek botanical knowledge through Persian-Islamic scholarship: he references Dioscorides directly but expands significantly on the neuropsychological properties that would define melissa's subsequent pharmacological identity.

**Paracelsus and *primum remedium* (c. 1530):**
Paracelsus designated melissa as *primum remedium* — the prime remedy — for nervous and cardiac complaints. He wrote: "Melissa is the Prince of all Plants for the Vitals; and there is no plant that excels it in strengthening the heart." This elevation to pharmacological primacy drove extensive Renaissance study and the establishment of specialty melissa preparations. The Carmelite Fathers of Paris developed *Eau de Mélisse des Carmes* in 1611 (a alcoholic distillate of melissa and other aromatic herbs); this preparation is still manufactured and sold in France today under the same name — one of the longest-running continuous pharmaceutical products in Western history.

**The apiarists' herb:**
The *bee balm* name reflects deep historical observation: beekeepers from classical antiquity through the medieval period rubbed new hives with crushed melissa leaves to attract and calm swarms. *Pliny* (*Naturalis Historia* XXI.44): "When they are lost, they bring them back by the scent of this herb." The observation that bees were attracted to and calmed by melissa — coupled with that melissa calmed humans — represents one of the earliest examples of pharmacological analogy across species in recorded natural history.

**Carmelite Water and European court medicine:**
*Eau de Mélisse des Carmes*, established 1611 at the monastery of Notre-Dame-des-Carmes in Paris, was exported to European courts as a remedy for fainting, anxiety, and "vapors" (the 17th-century diagnostic term encompassing anxiety, palpitations, and psychosomatic complaints). Louis XIV's court physicians prescribed it. By the 18th century, melissa-based cordials and waters were standard household preparations across France, England, and Germany. Parkinson's *Theatrum Botanicum* (1640) devotes substantial space to melissa preparations for "drooping of the spirits," nervous palpitations, and "mother's sickness" (anxiety in women). The cross-century consistency of application is pharmacologically significant.

**Thomas Bartram (1995) — clinical synthesis:**
The 20th-century herbalist synthesis (*Encyclopedia of Herbal Medicine*): "Lemon balm is best suited to the nervous system: for anxiety, tension headaches, depression, and sleep disturbances. As a digestive herb it is gently antispasmodic." This two-sentence summary represents the convergence of 2,500 years of observation into a clinically usable profile — subsequently confirmed by Kennedy's RCTs (2002–2004).

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Therapeutic applications:**

*Anxiety and stress (Level A):*
- **Lemon balm extract:** 300–600 mg standardized extract (standardized to rosmarinic acid ≥3% or dry extract 5:1) twice daily; dose-dependent response — 300 mg anxiolytic/calming; 600 mg greater effect + some sedation
- **Infusion (tea):** 2–4 g dried herb per 200 mL cup; steep covered 10–15 minutes to retain volatile components; 2–3 cups daily; onset within 30–60 minutes for acute anxiolytic effect
- **Combination with valerian:** Standard phytotherapy combination for anxiety with sleep disturbance; complementary mechanisms (melissa: GABA-T inhibition; valerian: GABA-A modulation; synergistic on both targets)

*Cognitive support and mood (Level A):*
- 600 mg extract acutely before cognitive tasks; daily use for sustained mood and cognitive maintenance
- Mild antidepressant properties (MAO-A inhibition + GABA enhancement); appropriate as adjunct in mild-moderate depression; not a primary antidepressant

*Herpes simplex — topical (Level A):*
- **Lomaherpan® or equivalent:** 1% melissa cream standardized to aqueous extract; apply directly to cold sore lesion 2–4 times daily at first sign of outbreak; reduces healing time by 1–2 days; reduces recurrence with regular use
- Freshly brewed melissa tea applied topically with cotton pad also provides some benefit; lower standardized dose

*Grave's disease / hyperthyroidism (Level B-C):*
- 600–900 mg extract daily may reduce TSH receptor antibody titers and thyroid hormone production; use as adjunct under endocrinology supervision; monitor thyroid function quarterly
- **Contraindication in hypothyroidism:** Rosmarinic acid's TSH-binding inhibition would worsen hypothyroidism

*Infant colic (Level B — combination only):*
- Melissa + fennel + chamomile herbal tea preparations studied in clinical trials for colic; use only combination products designed for infants; dose-specific to infant formulations; no pure essential oil preparations for infants

*Antiviral support (Level B):*
- Daily melissa tea during viral illness season; antiviral mechanisms primarily topical (HSV) but systemic rosmarinic acid absorption may provide some systemic antiviral support

**Culinary integration:**
- Fresh lemon balm leaves in salads, desserts, herbal teas, summer drinks — aromatic and mild anxiolytic through linalool and citral
- Melissa sorbet, panna cotta: European dessert tradition where culinary-pharmacological boundaries blur
- Add to herbal tea blends for flavor and pharmacological contribution alongside chamomile, passionflower, or valerian
- **Must be used fresh or low-temperature dried:** Volatile citral degrades rapidly at cooking temperatures; lemon aroma = indicator of volatile retention; if leaves don't smell lemon-like, volatile fraction is lost (non-volatile rosmarinic acid fraction persists)

**Drug interactions:**

*Thyroid medications:*
- **CONTRAINDICATED or monitor closely in hypothyroidism:** Additive thyroid-suppression with levothyroxine; use may worsen hypothyroidism or require dose adjustment. Critical interaction — inform patients.
- Beneficial in hyperthyroidism: adjunct use may allow lower pharmaceutical doses

*Sedatives and CNS depressants:*
- Additive CNS depression with benzodiazepines, barbiturates, sleep aids, opioids — reduce doses of conventional sedatives; enhanced sedation possible
- Caution with driving/machinery within 2 hours of therapeutic dose

*MAO inhibitors:*
- Mild MAO-A inhibition from luteolin; avoid combining with pharmaceutical MAOIs; theoretical serotonin syndrome risk (low but non-zero)
- Serotonergic antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs): monitor for excessive serotonergic effect

**Safety:**
- **GRAS status (FDA):** Recognized as generally safe for culinary use
- **Pregnancy:** Traditional use as antispasmodic for nausea; infusion considered safe; high-dose extract to avoid (uterine relaxant at high doses could theoretically be concerning; limited data)
- **Lactation:** Traditionally used for colic in nursing infants (passed through breast milk at low doses); considered safe
- **Hyperthyroidism patients:** Should discuss with physician before use
- **Hypothyroidism patients:** AVOID regular therapeutic use
- **No essential oil internal use**

**Dose references:**
- Dried herb infusion: 1.5–4.5 g per cup, 2–3 cups daily
- Standardized extract: 300–600 mg (5:1 extract) twice daily
- Acute dose: 600 mg for rapid anxiolytic onset (~30 minutes)
- Topical (HSV): 1% standardized cream per product instructions
- Pediatric colic tea: specific infant-formulated products only; no adult concentrations in infants

**Distinguishing from adulterants:** Authenticate lemon balm essential oil by GC — citral (geranial + neral) >50%; if citronellal is dominant instead, it is likely citronella grass adulteration. Dried herb: aroma test — characteristic lemon; rosmarinic acid content by HPLC ≥3% in quality extracts.
