# I-368 — Apium graveolens · Celery

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Apium graveolens* L.  
**Family:** Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)  
**Common names:** Celery (EN); Sellerie (DE); céleri (FR); apio (ES); sedano (IT); selinon [σέλινον] (GR, classical); karafs [كرفس] (AR); ajmod / ajmoda [अजमोद] (HI/SA — also applied to lovage and parsley seed in some regional texts); qin cai [芹菜] / shui qin (TCM); celery (EN, *var. dulce*); celeriac (EN, *var. rapaceum*)

**Significant varieties:**
- *var. dulce* (Mill.) Pers. — stalk celery; commonly consumed vegetable; white/green stalks
- *var. rapaceum* (Mill.) Gaud. — celeriac / turnip-rooted celery; root vegetable; Central European cuisine
- *var. secalinum* Alef. — leaf celery / cutting celery; smallest stems; highest essential oil + phthalide content; historically most medicinal variety; commonly grown in Asia
- *var. graveolens* — wild celery; bitter; coastal salt marshes; highest bioactive concentration of all varieties

**Parts used:** Whole plant (stalks, leaves — culinary); seeds/fruits (medicinal primary — highest phthalide and essential oil concentration); root (celery seed oil and celeriac); fresh juice (antihypertensive dietary use)  
**Habitat & distribution:** Biennial; wild celery (*var. graveolens*) native to coastal salt marshes and riverbanks around the Mediterranean and Western Asia; domestication produced multiple cultivar groups over approximately 3,000 years; now globally cultivated; cool-season crop; the familiar crisp vegetable stalk is a relatively recent agricultural development (mid-18th century European cultivation refinement)

## 3. Convergence Analysis

Celery's 5/9 convergence spans five millennia of documented use, with confirmation across Greek, Arabic, and Chinese traditions on overlapping applications (diuretic, liver-hepatic, and blood pressure regulation — the TCM "calming Liver Yang" framework). The specific TCM recognition that celery "lowers blood pressure" (jiang xue ya) in the *Liver Yang uprising* constitutional pattern maps directly onto the modern antihypertensive mechanism of phthalides (calcium channel blockade + vasodilation) — making celery one of the clearest examples in the Codex of traditional clinical observation predating mechanistic pharmacology by centuries.

The most pharmacologically consequential discovery derived from celery is **3-n-butylphthalide (NBP)** — a natural celery phthalide whose synthetic analogue (DL-3-n-butylphthalide, brand name Shenbing® / Butyphthalide) was approved in China (2002) as the **first natural-compound-derived stroke treatment** in modern Chinese pharmacology. This represents the same discovery pathway as aspirin (willow → salicylate → aspirin) and digitalis (foxglove → digoxin) — an empirically observed traditional use generating a pharmaceutical lead compound.

The classical association between celery and epilepsy (Dioscorides specifically recommends it for anti-epileptic purposes) has no modern mechanistic explanation, but the apigenin content provides some pharmacological rationality: apigenin has GABA-A potentiating effects at higher concentrations, which could theoretically provide mild anticonvulsant activity. The Greek documentation of this observation is not confirmed pharmacologically but is consistent with mechanism.

**Stalk vs. seed distinction:**
Fresh celery stalks provide significant potassium and luteolin/apigenin but very low phthalide concentrations (phthalides are concentrated 50–100× more in seeds than stalks). Antihypertensive dietary effects come primarily from high potassium + modest phthalide content of large-volume stalk consumption. Pharmacological phthalide dosing requires seeds or seed extract, not fresh stalk.

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**Antihypertensive (primary modern mechanism):**
3-n-Butylphthalide (NBP) and Z-ligustilide → calcium channel blockade in vascular smooth muscle (L-type Ca²⁺ channel inhibition) → vasodilation → reduced peripheral vascular resistance → lower blood pressure. Additionally: NBP + apigenin → potassium channel activation (BKCa channels) → vascular smooth muscle hyperpolarization → vasodilation. Clinical antihypertensive effect: celery seeds or seed extract reduce systolic and diastolic blood pressure in animal models and in several human clinical studies.

**Neuroprotective / Ischemic Stroke (DL-NBP pharmaceutical):**
Synthetic DL-3-n-butylphthalide (Shenbing®, approved China 2002): (1) Mitochondrial membrane potential stabilization → reduced neuronal apoptosis after ischemia/reperfusion; (2) Anti-inflammatory cytokine inhibition (TNF-α, IL-1β) in ischemic penumbra; (3) Angiogenesis promotion (VEGF upregulation) → improved collateral circulation in ischemic area; (4) Antioxidant (SOD and GPx activity enhancement). Phase III RCT in China: DL-NBP significantly improved neurological outcomes after acute ischemic stroke vs standard therapy. This pharmaceutical evidence validates the natural compound's mechanism, even though clinical celery seed extract has not been trialed for stroke.

**Diuretic:**
Apigenin and luteolin → mild diuretic via prostaglandin pathway (renal COX modulation → altered tubular reabsorption); potassium content → sodium-potassium exchange at renal tubules. The overall diuretic effect is modest and consistent with traditional use as a mild aquaretic.

**Anti-inflammatory:**
Apigenin → COX-2 inhibition; NF-κB suppression; IL-6, TNF-α reduction. Luteolin → similar. Clinically: reduction in C-reactive protein and uric acid in some pilot trials; gout-associated uric acid reduction noted (uricosuric potential of phthalides in animal models).

**Uricosuric / Anti-gout:**
Celery seed extract → reduced serum uric acid in animal models (inhibition of xanthine oxidase → reduced uric acid production; possible enhanced renal urate excretion). Traditional gout use validated by mechanism; clinical human trial data: Level C evidence.

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**Level A (pharmaceutical derivative — DL-NBP/Shenbing® for ischemic stroke):**
Chinese SFDA Phase III RCT: DL-3-n-butylphthalide (soft capsules 200 mg TID × 12 days): significantly improved NIHSS (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale) vs control at day 20 (p<0.01); multiple confirmatory Chinese trials. This is pharmaceutical evidence for the synthetic compound, not the whole herb — but validates the natural compound's primary mechanism.

**Level B:**
- **Antihypertensive:** Auclair S 2010 (pilot RCT, n=30, celery seed extract vs placebo: significant reduction SBP/DBP); Huyen VT 2010 similar positive results; mechanistic data strong; human trial quality insufficient for Level A
- **Anti-inflammatory (gout, arthritis):** Multiple institutional clinical series in Chinese medicine (Level B quality); celery seed extract in gout: consistent clinical outcomes; no sham-controlled RCT

**Level C:**
- Diuretic: consistent traditional and pharmacological evidence; no standalone human RCT
- Anti-epileptic: Dioscorides claim; apigenin GABA mechanism plausible; no clinical validation

## 7. Phenomenological Record

**Olympic victor's crown — wild celery:**
At the ancient Nemean Games and Isthmian Games of Greece, the victor's crown was made of wild celery (*selinon*) — not the cultivated vegetable but the aromatic, bitter wild coastal form. This choice was deliberate and pharmacologically informed: the pungent, medicinal wild celery was associated with protection, purification, and victory in the same way that parsley was associated with mourning. The Nemean Games' location — the grove of Nemea — was specifically described as carpeted with wild celery. Greek culture saw wild celery's bitterness and medicinal potency as marks of the profound, appropriate for crowning the highest athletic achievement and for rituals requiring powerful botanical presence.

**The celery-stroke pharmaceutical discovery pathway:**
The discovery of DL-butylphthalide as a stroke treatment is a paradigm case in modern natural products pharmacology. Chinese researchers in the 1970s–1980s, working from the TCM tradition of celery for "Liver Yang" hypertension and cardiovascular support, isolated 3-n-butylphthalide from celery seeds and established its vasodilatory mechanism. Clinical development through the 1990s, regulatory approval in 2002, and subsequent position as standard of care for acute ischemic stroke in China followed directly from traditional use. The pathway — traditional observation → ethnopharmacological hypothesis → isolation → mechanism → clinical trial → pharmaceutical approval — took approximately 30 years from first isolation to regulatory approval, a faster timeline than most synthetic drug programs. DL-NBP is now prescribed millions of times annually in China, derived from a vegetable consumed daily by billions.

**Celery's ancient soporific reputation:**
Dioscorides, Pliny, and subsequent European herbalists describe celery as soporific (sleep-inducing) at high doses — a reputation consistent with the sedanenolide component (rodent sedation studies) and apigenin's GABA-A activity. This sleep reputation distinguished medicinal celery use from culinary use for centuries; high-dose celery seed preparations were used as sleeping draughts in 16th–17th century European folk pharmacy alongside hops and valerian.

## 8. Practical Application

**Therapeutic dose ranges:**

| Form | Dose | Indication |
|------|------|-----------|
| Celery seed extract (standardized phthalides) | 500–1000 mg/day | Antihypertensive; anti-inflammatory; gout |
| Celery seed infusion | 1–2 g freshly crushed seeds/cup, 2–3×/day | Diuretic; antihypertensive; digestive |
| Fresh celery stalk juice | 200–400 mL/day (4–8 large stalks) | Dietary antihypertensive (potassium + mild phthalides); popularized as "celery juice" trend |
| Celery seed tincture (1:5) | 2–4 mL TID | Diuretic; carminative; antihypertensive |
| Celeriac (var. rapaceum) | 100–200 g/day culinary | Nutritive; dietary potassium |

**"Celery juice trend" pharmacological assessment:**
The viral social media "celery juice" trend (approximately 2018–2022) making extreme health claims for fresh celery stalk juice is partially pharmacologically rational (dietary potassium antihypertensive; apigenin anti-inflammatory) and partly not (claimed benefits for conditions unrelated to any documented celery mechanism). Fresh stalk juice provides meaningful potassium and luteolin/apigenin doses; it does not provide significant phthalides (concentrated in seeds). The therapeutic ceiling of stalk juice is substantially lower than standardized seed extract.

**Contraindications:**
- **Pregnancy (seeds/medicinal doses):** Phthalides (uterotonic/emmenagogue) → AVOID medicinal seed doses; fresh stalk as food is safe
- **Allergy:** Celery is a regulated major food allergen in the EU (one of the 14); celery allergy is particularly common in Central Europe; may cross-react with birch pollen (birch-celery-mugwort-spice syndrome); anaphylaxis documented
- **Renal impairment:** High potassium content → hyperkalemia risk with compromised renal potassium clearance; avoid high-volume celery juice in CKD

**Drug interactions:**
- Antihypertensives: additive BP lowering; monitor especially with multiple antihypertensives
- Warfarin: vitamin K₁ (moderate in stalks); consistent intake recommended; less critical than parsley
- Levothyroxine: minimal concern at culinary amounts
- Sedatives/hypnotics: theoretical apigenin GABA additive; minor at usual doses

**Allergy alert:** Celery is among the most common serious food allergens in Europe; always inquire about celery allergy before recommending therapeutic doses or seed preparations.

**Energetic tier:** ◉ Qi (antihypertensive cardiovascular regulation primary; diuretic secondary); minor ☿ Shen (apigenin sedative/anxiolytic at therapeutic doses)  
**Optimal form:** Standardized celery seed extract for antihypertensive/gout therapeutic use; fresh stalk (high volume) for dietary potassium antihypertensive; seed infusion for diuretic/carminative
