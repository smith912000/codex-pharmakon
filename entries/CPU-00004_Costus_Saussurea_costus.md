# Costus — Kushtha · Kustha · Putchuk · Indian costus root

## *Saussurea costus* (Falc.) Lipsch. — the warming root of the high Himalaya

### Origin & Tradition

Dioscorides treats *kostos* (costus) in *De Materia Medica* Book I among the warm, pungent aromatic roots, distinguishing several grades by colour and pungency and ranking it with the most valued imports of the spice routes. The drug he describes — the dried root of a tall Himalayan thistle-like *Saussurea* (synonyms *Saussurea lappa*, and the recently adopted *Dolomiaea costus*) — reached the Mediterranean from the high valleys of Kashmir as "putchuk," one of the great trans-Asian aromatics traded alongside spikenard and cinnamon. Its warm, dry, persistent woody-musky odour fixed its place in incense, perfume, and the warming medicines.

In Ayurveda the same root is **kushtha** (also *kustha*), a major dravya of the warming, *vata*- and *kapha*-reducing class, used for respiratory complaints, digestive sluggishness, and skin conditions, and incorporated into classical formulations. (The Sanskrit *kushtha* also names a class of skin disease, reflecting the root's traditional use in such conditions — but the plant name and the disease name should not be confused.) Chinese tradition knows the closely related trade article in the *mu xiang* group of Qi-moving aromatics; *Saussurea/Aucklandia* costus root has historically been one of the materials supplied to that category, used to move stagnant Qi of the Middle Burner, relieve distension, and harmonise the stomach.

Across these traditions costus is consistently a **warming, aromatic, Qi-moving root** for cold and stagnation of the digestion and chest. Its reputation as a tonic and aromatic carminative is one of the most stable cross-traditional readings in the whole nard/aromatic complex.

Crucially, *Saussurea costus* is now listed on **CITES Appendix I** owing to over-harvesting of wild Himalayan populations. International trade in wild-collected material is prohibited; only cultivated, properly documented sources are legitimate. This conservation status is part of the entry, not a footnote: the root must be sourced responsibly or substituted.

### Active Components

- **Costunolide and dehydrocostus lactone (sesquiterpene lactones)** — the signature constituents of costus root; carry much of the anti-inflammatory, antispasmodic, and bitter-aromatic activity. (Sesquiterpene lactones are also a sensitisation risk on skin contact.)
- **Essential oil ("costus root oil")** — a warm, musky volatile fraction used in perfumery; a recognised skin sensitiser, restricted in fragrance use.
- **Phenolic acids — isochlorogenic acid and chlorogenic acid** — among the dominant phenolics quantified in modern root extracts; antioxidant and implicated in the carbohydrate-metabolism effects seen in animal models.
- **Inulin and carbohydrate fraction** — typical of the Asteraceae storage root; contributes to the demulcent/tonic character.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. **Warming aromatic for cold, stagnant digestion.** The core indication: bloating, distension, poor appetite, and sluggish, cold digestion. Costus moves stagnant Qi of the Middle and warms the centre — the function it shares across DMM, Ayurveda, and the *mu xiang* tradition.

2. **Aromatic support for cold-pattern respiratory complaints.** Traditionally used (notably in the kushtha tradition) for cough and chest congestion of a cold, damp character, as a warming expectorant aromatic.

3. **Anti-inflammatory / antispasmodic application.** The sesquiterpene lactones underlie a traditional and experimentally-supported anti-inflammatory and smooth-muscle-relaxant action, applied to cramping digestive complaints.

4. **Metabolic / glycaemic support (experimental).** Modern animal work points to a carbohydrate-metabolism-modulating effect; in Kenetica this is a forward-looking, not-yet-clinical note rather than a recommendation.

5. **Incense and aromatic atmosphere.** Following its ancient role, the dried root is burned as a warming, grounding incense.

### Scientific Correlation

(2024) "Unveiling the chemical profiling and remarkable modulation of carbohydrate metabolism by costus root, *Dolomiaea costus* (Falc.) in streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic rats." *Journal of Ethnopharmacology* 326: 117911. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM / Galenic:** Hot and dry, on the order of the second-to-third degree; pungent, bitter, aromatic. A warming Qi-mover of the stomach and chest.
- **AV (Ayurveda):** Kushtha — *ushna* (heating); reduces *vata* and *kapha*; used for respiratory, digestive and skin conditions.
- **CM (by relation):** Within the *mu xiang* class of Qi-regulating aromatics — moves stagnant Qi of the Middle Burner, relieves distension, harmonises the stomach; warm and pungent.
- **Kenetica tier:** ◉ Warming Qi-moving aromatic root — a primary, cross-traditionally stable member of the warm-aromatic family.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides treats *kostos* as a warm, pungent aromatic root, grading it by colour and pungency and by trade provenance. This is a therapeutic and commercial reading, not a cultivation status: Dioscorides has no subtle-body system to assign the root to, so no vital-constitutional claim is attested here. What the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention — the assaying of a costly imported root by smell, colour and origin to tell true putchuk from adulterated. That is a real practice, and it is the whole of what DMM contributes on this substance.

**Unani — Avicennan (CM).** In the Greco-Arabic materia medica *qust* is classed hot and dry and used as a deobstruent, carminative and warming corrective. Within the Unani frame of innate heat and radical moisture — where longevity is the conservation of a finite vital endowment — a hot, drying, dispersing drug of this kind is a corrective taken against a cold or obstructed state, not a conservational agent of the vital heat itself. No cultivation status (no longevity or *ruh*-strengthening assignment) is attested for costus in the sources retrieved; the claim here is therapeutic only.

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing tradition (SBJ).** Costus root enters the Chinese materia medica as *mu xiang* 木香, a warming mover of stagnant Qi of the Middle Burner. It is tempting to read an upper-grade (上品) cultivation status onto it, since a "mu xiang" stands in the classic — but textual research on the *Shennong Bencao Jing* finds the classical *mu xiang* cannot be securely identified with *Saussurea costus* (candidates include eaglewood and *Aucklandia*), so no *san pin* grade can honestly be assigned to this species. The attestation is therefore (b): a qi-moving therapeutic role is recorded; a cultivation grade and any *yang sheng* framing are not attested for the plant of this entry.

**Ayurveda — Sushrutan tradition (SS).** As *kushtha* the root is a warming, *vata*- and *kapha*-reducing dravya for cold digestive, respiratory and skin complaints. This is a *dravyaguna* therapeutic placement; no *rasayana* (rejuvenative) or *vajikarana* (generative) status is attested for *kushtha* in the sources retrieved. It sits inside the materia medica as a corrective, not inside the formal cultivation disciplines — an honest (b).

**Tibetan (FMT).** Costus is known in the Tibetan materia medica as *ru rta*, a warming aromatic used against cold and stagnation. No *bcud len* (essence-extraction) or subtle-body-practice assignment is attested for it, and per the tradition's own structure no formal "substances for subtle-body practice" category exists to place it in. Its cultivation status here is (c) — absent; the attestation is medical only.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does the same thing with this root in every tradition that actually names it: takes it, warm and drying, against a defined cold-and-stagnation complaint of the digestion or chest, then stops. Nothing in the retrieved sources supports long-term self-cultivation use, and the plant's CITES Appendix I status makes casual or prolonged use indefensible regardless. The honest cultivation reading is a short, purposeful, well-sourced course — and Dioscorides' assaying discipline as the transferable practice.

### Contraindications

- **CITES Appendix I:** wild-collected material is prohibited in international trade; use only cultivated, documented sources.
- **Skin sensitisation:** sesquiterpene lactones and costus root oil are recognised skin sensitisers; avoid undiluted topical use and use in the sensitised.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** insufficient safety data; avoid internal medicinal use. [USER: verify]
- **Heat-pattern presentations:** as a warming, drying aromatic, inappropriate for clear heat/dryness conditions.
