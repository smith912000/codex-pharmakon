# Entada scandens — gla.gor.zho.sha, Laagor shho sha (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Entada scandens, the first of two binomials Clark 1995 prints for gla.gor.zho.sha (with Entada phaseoloides); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints "Mucuna monosperma" on the same line as the name "Laagor shho sha"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under this binomial only by Clark.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. Both Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`, and both name the drug. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk found no *Entada*. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says: "Therefore more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the line prints two, of which the spine row takes the first (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 89). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 950).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792). The chapter lists eight sections of individual natural substances at line 802. The text file prints the headings "(5) Resins" (line 879) and "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997) and none for section (6), and this line lies between the two, so the Codex does not say under which of Clark's headings the file places it. It is the third of three *zho.sha* lines in a row (lines 948 to 950). It reads:

"gla.gor.zho.sha: Entada scandens: Entada phaseoloides: [is also known as mchher.pa.zho.sha or glang.mig (lit. ‘spleen power or bulls eye’) and is likely to be mistaken for cowhage. It has a small, slender trunk, thick round leaf and white flower. The heart-shaped pods contain black and white striped kidney-shaped seeds which are thick at the center. The spleen-shaped one is of superior quality and] cures spleen fever."

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the second name, the lore about mistaking it for cowhage, the description of trunk, leaf, flower, pods and striped seeds, and the grading of the spleen-shaped seed as superior. Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the two Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the clause "cures spleen fever". The line gives no taste, no power, no part used and no preparation. Clark's index of Latin names lists both binomials (line 1583: "Entada phaseoloides Entada scandens").

*The two lines before it.* Line 948 (*snying.zho.sha*, *Spondias axillaris*) ends "It cures heart fevers", and line 949 (*mkhal.zho.sha*, "Cowhage", *Canavalia gladiata*) ends "It cures kidney fever." Clark's introduction (line 73) names the three together: "the three zho.sha fruits which are shaped like and named after the kidney, spleen and heart. These three are identified respectively as Canavalia gladiata, Entada scandens and Spondias axillans". The passage presents them as an example of a "doctrine of signatures", and says the "tastes, powers, etc. of the other substances covered by this principle often support or even surpass" it. Clark's order there, kidney, spleen, heart, is the order of the identifications, and not the order of the lines (heart, kidney, spleen). The spleen drug is the second of the three in the introduction and the third of the three on the page; both agree that *Entada scandens* is the spleen one.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3417).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225). The sentence reads: "Nying shho sha, Khelma shho sha and Laagor shho sha cure hot disorders of the heart, kidneys and spleen respectively." Taken in order, the third drug is the spleen drug, which agrees with Clark's "spleen fever". The Codex reads "Laagor shho sha" as the same name as *gla.gor.zho.sha* from the spelling and from the agreement of the three-part series with Clark's lines 948 to 950. The sentence lies before the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), and the Codex does not say which of the categories of line 3324 the text means. The printed index lists "Laagor shho sha 205" (line 7415). The text gives no Latin name in the sentence. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (line 5422).* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints, on one line just after the letter "L" (line 5420): "Laagor shho sha gla gor zho sha Mucuna monosperma". The phonetic name, the Tibetan transliteration and the scientific name stand on a single line here, and they are not split into separate columns. The transliteration "gla gor zho sha" is Clark's *gla.gor.zho.sha*. The glossary therefore names *Mucuna monosperma* for this drug, and does not name *Entada*. The extraction took that binomial as a separate row ("Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)") which is not what this line shows. The two Tibetan texts therefore disagree on the plant: Clark prints *Entada scandens* and *Entada phaseoloides*, and the glossary prints *Mucuna monosperma*. The Codex does not choose. ### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Entada* is a genus of large woody climbers of the legume family (Fabaceae), with very long flat pods and hard, glossy, rounded seeds, and that *Mucuna* is another legume genus, so Clark's two binomials and the glossary's one all point to climbing legumes with large seeds. *Entada* seeds are also generally reported to contain saponins. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a seed-bearing climber for the spleen: "cures spleen fever" (Clark, line 950) and cures "hot disorders" of the spleen (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3417). These are the texts' claims, and the plant behind them is uncertain between *Entada* and *Mucuna*. They are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary layer describes the seeds and says the spleen-shaped one is of superior quality; it does not say how it is prepared or how much is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Tibetan terms ("spleen fever", "hot disorders" of the spleen) are not modern diagnoses or measured outcomes, so no modern finding can be set beside them here. The identity of the drug is open between two legume genera, so a study of one species would not bear directly on it. Clark's own framing of the three *zho.sha* drugs is a likeness of shape between the seed and the organ, and a likeness of shape is not a pharmacological finding.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** No taste, power or post-digestive taste is printed for this drug in the lines read. Clark's commentary layer (line 950) gives shape and grade, not taste. The name does not appear in the Men-Tsee-Khang taste-group lists of chapter 19 (which begin at line 3204) that were read for this entry. The only quality printed is the grading of the spleen-shaped seed as "of superior quality", which is a grade of the seed and not a taste or power.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it cures spleen fever, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same in kind. The frame Clark adds in his introduction is the likeness of the seed to the organ it is said to treat. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the lines cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a spleen-fever drug in the Tibetan texts, with a likeness-of-shape rationale in Clark's introduction, no preparation and no dose, and the plant behind the name is uncertain. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. The identity is open between two legume genera and the seeds are large and hard, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Fever of any organ needs medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
