# Carlina acaulis — Chamaileon leukos, "white chamaeleon" (Dioscorides 3-10); also called ixia, chrysisceptrum

## *Carlina acaulis* (printed by the Dioscorides translators as the third binomial on the first SUGGESTED line for 3-10, attributed to Linnaeus, glossed "Stemless Carline Thistle"; the same line gives *Chamaeleon albus* [Fuchs, Brunfels] and *Carlina caulescens* [Bauhin], and a further line gives, as "other usage", *Carlina gummifera* / *Atractylis gummifera*) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry.** This plant is named only by Dioscorides (DMM). It was added 2026-10-09 from the extraction list (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) at the locus 3-10, line 6622.

**Identity note.** The binomial is the translators' (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) suggestion and not Dioscorides' identification. The SUGGESTED lines (6623 to 6625) read: "Chamaeleon albus [Fuchs, Brunfels], Carlina caulescens [Bauhin], Carlina acaulis [Linnaeus]", then "— Stemless Carline Thistle [other usage] Carlina gummifera, Atractylis gummifera", then "— White Chamoeleon". The "other usage" species, *Atractylis gummifera*, is a different plant. The chapter's description does not choose between them. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is in Book Three under the heading "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants", and it is the white half of a pair: 3-11 CHAMAILEON MELAS (line 6630) is the black.

*3-10 CHAMAILEON LEUKOS* (heading at line 6622; text at line 6626). The whole chapter reads: "White chamaeleon is called ixia because in some places viscous matter is found at the roots of it which women use instead of mastic [1-89, 1-90]. It has leaves similar to silybum [4-159] or carduus nutans [musk thistle] but rougher, sharper, and stronger than the black chamaeleon [below]. It does not have a stalk but out of the middle puts out a prickle similar to that of the sea urchin or cinara. The flowers are a purple colour, like hairs, flying away in down, with seed similar to cnicus [4-119, 4-190]. The root is thick in fertile hills but in the mountains it is more slender, white at the bottom, somewhat aromatic, with a strong sweet taste. An acetabulum [vinegar cruet] of this (taken in a drink) expels broadworms. It is taken in hard wine with a decoction of origanum. For dropsy a teaspoonful is given with wine to ease them. A decoction is taken in a drink for frequent painful urination. Taken as a drink with wine it is an antidote to poison. Kneaded with polenta then diluted with water and oil it kills dogs, swine, and mice. It is also called chrysisceptrum, or ixia; the Romans call it carduus nutans varius, the Egyptians, epher, and some, epthosephim."

The chapter gives: the origin of the name *ixia* (a viscous matter found at the roots in some places, which "women use instead of mastic", with a pointer to the mastic chapters 1-89 and 1-90); a description (leaves like *silybum* or musk thistle but rougher; no stalk, a single prickle from the middle like a sea urchin's; hair-like purple flowers that fly away in down; seed like *cnicus*); the root (thick on fertile hills, slender on mountains, white at the bottom, somewhat aromatic, strong sweet taste); and its uses: against broadworms, for dropsy, for frequent painful urination, as an antidote to poison, and as a poison for dogs, swine and mice when mixed with polenta. It gives the other names *chrysisceptrum* and *ixia*, a Roman name (*carduus nutans varius*) and an Egyptian (*epher*, with *epthosephim* for "some").

The black chamaeleon (3-11, line 6630) is the "black chamaeleon [below]" that the leaf comparison refers to and is a separate chapter. It gives uses for skin disease and teeth, and is not attributed to this row. Chapter 3-12 KROKODEILION (line 6635), which says that crocodilium is "similar to black chamaeleon", is the chapter of CPU-01475 (*Carthamus lanatus*).

### Active Components

The stemless carline thistle (*Carlina acaulis*, Asteraceae) is generally known to have a root that holds an essential oil, with polyacetylene compounds among its constituents. *Atractylis gummifera*, the translators' "other usage", is generally known as a seriously toxic plant (its toxins are glycosides called atractylosides). Which of these Dioscorides meant, and what either holds, was not checked against a source. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records the root as a worm remedy, a remedy for dropsy and painful urination, an antidote to poisons, and as a bait poison for dogs, pigs and mice. The same chapter that records it as an antidote also records its use as a poison. If the plant is the *Atractylis* of the translators' note, it is generally a dangerous poison. The Codex gives no protocol for it.

### Preparation & Dosing

Doses are printed in 3-10, as the translators render them: "An acetabulum [vinegar cruet] of this (taken in a drink) expels broadworms. It is taken in hard wine with a decoction of origanum." "For dropsy a teaspoonful is given with wine." For painful urination "A decoction is taken in a drink", and "Taken as a drink with wine it is an antidote to poison". For the animal poison: "Kneaded with polenta then diluted with water and oil". The chapter gives no dose for the decoction. These are historical quotations and not advice.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. The chapter offers a discipline of attention: how to know the plant (leaf, prickle, down-flying purple flowers, seed), how the root varies with place (thick on fertile hills, slender in the mountains), a sensory assay of the root (white at the bottom, somewhat aromatic, strong sweet taste), and the viscous matter that gives it the name *ixia*. It records no status as a long-term substance. The binomial is a translators' suggestion, and one of their alternatives is a toxic plant. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice. Dioscorides records the same root as a medicine and as a poison, and the plant cannot be identified with confidence. The abstentive answer is the correct one.

### Contraindications

The chapter itself says the root, kneaded with polenta, "kills dogs, swine, and mice". The translators' alternative, *Atractylis gummifera*, is generally known as capable of causing fatal poisoning in people (liver and kidney failure). No traditional use above is advice, and no home preparation is safe. Anyone who has swallowed any part of a thistle-like root of uncertain identity needs urgent medical care. Do not use in pregnancy.
