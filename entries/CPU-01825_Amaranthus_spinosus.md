**Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *The names in the chapters.* "Tanduliyaka" (also "Tanduliyakam" and "Tanduliya") is printed on fourteen lines of the chapter files; "Tandula" alone in the chapters means rice or seeds and is not used here. The passages below were read.

*The pot-herbs (Sutra 46, lines 1613 to 1645).* In the chapter on food and drink: "The potherbs, such as the Tanduliyaka, Upodika, Ashvavala, Chilli, Palankya, and Vastuka etc., are laxative and diuretic, and have a sweet and alkaline taste. They slightly generate the Vayu and Kaphani, and cure haemoptysis." The metrical text adds: "Of these, the species known as the Tanduliya is sweet in taste and digestion, and proves curative in cases of haemoptysis and somnolence (due to the effects of poison, wine or vitiated blood). It is antitoxic and extremely cooling in its potency, and produces a state of parchedness in the system." The Palankya is said to be "identical" in its properties to the Tanduliya.

*Foods (Sutra 19, 20; Chikitsa 6; Uttara 17).* Among pot-herbs "which may be safely used by a man to give a greater relish to his food", with Chilli, Vastuka, Sunishannaka, Jivanti and Mandukaparni, the text names Tanduliyaka, and counts clarified butter, Saindhava salt, pomegranate and Amalaka juice with them as "the most wholesome articles of food" (Sutra 20, lines 62 to 72). In the diet of an ulcer-patient, the pot-herbs "Tanduliyakam, Jivanti, Sunishannaka, Vastuka, immature Mulaka, Vartaku, Patola and Karavella", fried with rock salt and ghrita and seasoned with pomegranate or Amalaka juice, are prescribed with boiled rice, or with Mudga soup (Sutra 19, lines 211 to 221). In all types of haemorrhoids the patient takes his meal "with Vastuka, Tanduliyaka, Jivanti, Upodika, Ashvavala, tender Mulaka, Palanka" and other pot-herbs according to the Doshas involved (Chikitsa 6, lines 149 to 160). Among "edibles" thought "invigorating to eye-sight" in a case of Timira, with Jivanti, Sunishannaka, Vastuka, Chilli and Mulaka (Uttara 17, lines 440 to 444).

*Applications and drinks (Sutra 16; Uttara 21 and 45; Kalpa 5, 6 and 7).* In the chapter on piercing and bandaging the ear-lobes, a plaster, or an oil boiled with them, of "Gauri, Sugandha, Shyama, Ananta, Tanduliyakam" is for the Shyava type of the complication treated there (Sutra 16, lines 400 to 403). In the chapter on ear diseases, four kinds of sneha are cooked with the paste of Tanduliyaka, the fruits of Amkota and other drugs (Uttara 21, lines 73 to 77). In haemorrhage, powders "of the roots of Tanduliyaka" are licked with honey (Uttara 45, lines 219 to 221). In the chapter on snake-bite, an *Agada* of "Shleshmataka, Katphala, Matulunga, Sveta, Girihva, Kinihi, sugar and Tanduliya" is "the best remedy in cases of poisoning by Darvi-kara or Rajila-bites" (Kalpa 5, lines 446 to 449). In the chapter on rat-poisoning, for the bite of a rat of the class the OCR prints "L^flana", with copious saliva, hiccough and vomiting, "a lambative made of the roots of Tanduliyaka mixed with honey" (Kalpa 6, lines 51 to 55), and a medicated ghrita cooked with the roots "should be given to the patient for drink" (lines 227 to 230). In the antivenom chapter, the powder of Tanduliyaka is among those added to the alkali prepared in the *Ksharagada* (Kalpa 7, lines 20 to 27).

*What the chapters do not say.* A search of the vajikarana chapter (Chikitsa 26) and the rasayana chapters (Chikitsa 27 to 30) found none of the names. The text does not name the part of the plant used in the food passages. The Latin identification is the appendix's. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. As a general fact, *Amaranthus* is a genus of leafy plants of the amaranth family (Amaranthaceae); the plants are eaten as greens in many countries, and many leafy amaranths contain oxalates. Nothing else is stated here about the composition of the drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Sushruta text records Tanduliyaka as a pot-herb rated sweet, cooling and antitoxic, as a relish for the diet of ulcer-patients, haemorrhoid-patients and eye disease, and as an ingredient in plasters, ghritas and antivenoms. These are the text's statements about a Sanskrit name, and the Latin name the appendix attaches to it is the translator's, by position and adjacency. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and recommends no use of the plant as a medicine.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these preparations, each with its locus. The pot-herb fried with rock salt and ghrita (Sutra 19, lines 216 to 221). The plant in a plaster or boiled in oil (Sutra 16, lines 400 to 403). The root pounded and licked with honey (Uttara 45, lines 219 to 221; Kalpa 6, lines 53 to 55). The root cooked in ghrita as a drink (Kalpa 6, lines 227 to 230). A powder with other drugs and sugar (Kalpa 5, lines 446 to 449). Four sneha cooked with its paste (Uttara 21, lines 73 to 77). No quantity of the plant is given in these passages. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SS:** the passages read print for the pot-herbs together that they are "laxative and diuretic", have "a sweet and alkaline taste", "slightly generate the Vayu and Kaphani" and "cure haemoptysis" (Sutra 46); for the Tanduliya alone that it is "sweet in taste and digestion", "antitoxic and extremely cooling in its potency", and "produces a state of parchedness" (Sutra 46, lines 1623 to 1628). The taste is printed as "sweet" and the potency as "extremely cooling"; no further *rasa*, *virya* or *vipaka* is printed in the lines read.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Sushruta text names Tanduliyaka as a wholesome pot-herb for the diet and as an ingredient in plasters, ghritas and antivenoms. A search of the vajikarana chapter (Chikitsa 26) and the rasayana chapters (Chikitsa 27 to 30) found none of its names, so the text assigns it no rejuvenative or generative status in the lines retrieved. The chapters say nothing of *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* for it. The dietary place it holds is a place among relishes and a cooling, antitoxic green, not a regimen of cultivation. The Latin identification is the appendix's. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** The Sushruta text gives a cultivator a plain thing here: a pot-herb is cooked with salt and ghrita and eaten as a relish, and it appears in the diet of patients. No special status is claimed for it. Nothing should be gathered or taken as medicine on the strength of a Latin name the chapters do not give.

### Contraindications

The Sushruta passages read print no warning for the pot-herb itself, but the text says it "produces a state of parchedness in the system" and rates the group as slightly aggravating Vayu and Kapha (Sutra 46). As a general fact, people prone to oxalate kidney stones are often advised to limit oxalate-rich greens, and the spines of some amaranth species make them unfit for casual picking. The identity is open, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
