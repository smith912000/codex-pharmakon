# Pterocephalus hookeri — spang.rtsi.do.wo, Pang tsi dowo, spang rtsi do po (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Pterocephalus hookeri Hook, the first of two binomials Clark 1995 prints for spang.rtsi.do.wo (with Saussurea nepalensis), and the one the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints on the same line as "Pang tsi dowo" and "spang rtsi do po"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts in this row.** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and the row's binomial is the first of two on Clark's line. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed names (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (lines 999 and 1000).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The first entry in that section is *hong.len* (picrorhiza, line 998), and *spang.rtsi.do.wo* follows it. The entry reads: "spang.rtsi.do.wo: Pterocephalus hookeri Hook: Saussurea nepalensis: [has ten names and includes three types of chig.thub.dkar.po used in infectious fevers of the small intestine.13 It has large, plain leaves, bitter taste, a white flower similar to the head of an old man and long petioles. The subspecies cures disturbed infectious fever.]" The next line (1000) continues: "[The second type,] (sbrang.rtsi.)hbyar.bag.(chan), [is similar and cures urinary disorders, polyuria, chronic diseases and fever of the urinary bladder.]"

Clark's preface (line 82) says "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Everything on line 999 after the two binomials is inside square brackets, and so is everything on line 1000 after the opening bracket. The whole description (ten names, three *chig.thub.dkar.po* types, the leaves, taste, flower and petioles, and what the subspecies cures) is therefore the commentary layer. This entry has no clause outside the brackets that states a taste, a power or an action: the root-text layer, as Clark gives it, holds only the name. The footnote Clark attaches (note 13, line 1543) reads: "chhig.thub.dkar.po: a generic name referring to plants having a unique power to heal disease without being compounded with other substances."

The drug is named again in chapter 21, "The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines" (heading at line 1205), outside the brackets of the plant list. Line 1210 reads: "The class of medicines which cure blood disorders consists of Red Sandalwood, Caraganajubata, Catechu tree, Picrorhiza, Vasaka (Malabar Nut tree), Emblic Myrobalan, Corydalis edulis, Pterocephalus hookeri, Madder and Bastard Teak." Line 1212 reads, in part: "The class of medicines which neutralize poisons consists of Musk, Red, Yellow and White Aconites, ... Gentiana algida, Pterocephalus hookeri, Wild Rose and the cambium (middle bark) of Indian Barberry." Clark's name index prints *Pterocephalus hookeri* (line 1632) and *spang.rtsi.do.wo* (line 1715).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (Explanatory Tantra).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The name is spelt *Pang tsi dhowo* in the text and *Pang tsi dowo* in the glossary. In chapter 20 (the colophon of that chapter is at line 3583), line 3428 reads: "Pang tsi dhowo cures infectious diseases, chronic hot disorder and is an antidote against poisoning." In chapter 21 (colophon at line 3689), line 3598 lists it among the blood-disorder group ("Tsendhen marpo, zhomo, Sengdeng, Honglen, Bhashaka, Kyurura, Rekon, Pang tsi dhowo, Tsod and Tshoe are the group of medicines that cure blood disorders"), and line 3605 lists it among the group that cures poisoning ("... Vombu, Pang gyen, Pang tsi dhowo, Saegoe and Kyerpai barshun are the group of medicines that cure poisoning"). The glossary on medicinal ingredients prints one line (5592) that carries the phonetic name, the Tibetan and the scientific name together: "Pang tsi dowo spang rtsi do po Pterocephalus hookeri". No English name is printed on that line. The index gives "Pang tsi dhowo 206,220". The two texts agree on the blood-disorder group, the poison group and an infectious-disease use, though the Men-Tsee-Khang text does not state the plant's taste.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *spang.rtsi.do.wo*, *Pang tsi dowo* and *Pterocephalus* found no existing entry on this drug. Three entries match the search only on a similar string. (The Men-Tsee-Khang pages also give *Pangpoe*, a separate name, at line 3448 and line 5586.) I_241 (Valerian) prints "Spang rtsi spos" in its title line and describes it as a Tibetan name for valerian root; that is a different name (Clark's index prints *spang.rtsi* alone at line 1715, and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints a separate entry *spang spos* for *Nardostachys jatamansi* at lines 5586 to 5590), and this entry does not read them as the same drug. III_286 (Pearl) quotes Clark's materia medica, where line 813 says the sixth type of pearl "comes from the stomach of a sea creature called spang.rtsi, which resembles an oyster"; that *spang.rtsi* is an animal and is a homonym of the first part of this name. III_289 (Mussel) quotes the same line 813 about the *spang.rtsi* sea creature. None of them is an entry on *spang.rtsi.do.wo*.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Pterocephalus* is a genus of the teasel family (Caprifoliaceae, formerly Dipsacaceae). Nothing is stated here about the composition of the Tibetan drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Clark's commentary layer records a bitter plant that cures infectious fevers and, in its second type, urinary disorders and fever of the bladder. The class lists in chapter 21 place it among the medicines for blood disorders and for neutralizing poisons, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says it cures infectious diseases, chronic hot disorder and is an antidote against poisoning. These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for fever, infection or poisoning, and the botanical identity is uncertain.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation for *spang.rtsi.do.wo* alone. It appears only in lists of single names and in class lists. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's line 999 prints "bitter taste" inside the square brackets, so it belongs to the commentary layer. It prints no power (hot, cool, heavy or light) and no post-digestive taste. The Men-Tsee-Khang text calls the drug a cure for "chronic hot disorder" (line 3428), which is an indication and not a printed power. No other quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes the plant by what it cures: infectious fevers (and, in the second type, urinary disorders), and it stands in the blood-disorder and poison classes of chapter 21. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this entry places *spang.rtsi.do.wo* in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Clark's essence-extraction mentions in this part of the list (lines 1043 and 1078, for example) belong to other plants and do not name this one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and the generic term *chig.thub.dkar.po*, which the footnote defines as plants that heal "without being compounded with other substances", names a healing property and not a cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a fever, blood and poison drug in the Tibetan texts, with no line outside Clark's commentary layer describing the plant, and nothing more can be said from them. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. Infectious fever and suspected poisoning need medical care, and the Codex gives no treatment for either. The botanical identity is uncertain (two binomials in Clark, and the description is commentary), so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
