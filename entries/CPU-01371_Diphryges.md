# Diphryges — Diphruges (δίφρυγες), "twice-roasted" (Dioscorides 5-120)
## *Pyrites slag: three kinds of furnace residue and burnt mineral; the translator's suggestion is "Pyrites — Disilphide of Iron"* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek Tradition (DMM).** The main locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-120 *DIPHRUGES* in the 2000 English translation (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10448-10453; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 893; book index "DIPHRUGES 804", line 12073; list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 846, "Diphruges, , mineral, DMM V, id_uncertain yes"). The translator's heading: "SUGGESTED: Pyrites — Disilphide of Iron."

Dioscorides' account, read in full, is:

**Three kinds.** "It is thought that there are three kinds of diphryges." (1) "One is a mineral and is found only in Cyprus. It is dirty and is taken out of the bottom of places there. After being taken out it is dried in the sun, then small brushy sticks are laid all around it and it is burnt. It is called diphryges because it is dried and burnt and (as it were) roasted in the sun amid the sticks." (2) "Another is (as it were) the sediment and dregs of worked brass, for after pouring around the cold water (as we explained when discussing flower of brass) and after taking away the brass, there is found stuff such as this in the furnace sticking to the bottom of it, with both the astringent quality and taste of brass." (3) "The third sort is made as follows. The supervisors place stones called pyrites into a furnace and they burn it (as they do chalk) for many days. When the colour becomes like red ochre they take it out and put it in jars. Some say that this sort is only made from by-products of purifying brass. This is heated in the furnaces, carried from there into the ditches and burnt there, for it is deposited around the ditches and is found after taking away the stones." (line 10450-10452). The cross-reference "flower of brass" is chapter 5-88 (line 10256), which was not read in full.

**Quality and testing.** "The best of it is brassy to the taste, looks rusty, and is astringent and excessively drying to the tongue. This quality the burnt ochre does not possess, though some burn it and sell it instead of diphryges" (line 10452). So the chapter gives a test by taste and by comparison, and names the adulterant: burnt ochre sold as diphryges.

**Actions.** "It is astringent, cleans thoroughly, wipes away, dries, represses abnormal growths of the flesh, and forms new skins on malignancies and creeping sores. Taken with turpentine or wax ointments it dissolves abscesses" (line 10452).

**Cross-references.** (1) In the cadmia chapter (5-84, line 10223) a hill over Solis is said to hold veins of "chalcitis, misy, sory, melanteria, coeruleus, chrysocolla, calcanthus and diphryges" alongside cadmia, which shows the author grouped it with those mineral drugs; nothing further is said of it there. (2) In the pyrites chapter, 5-143 *Purites Lithos* (line 10582), "Pyrites is a kind of stone from which brass is made... Either burnt or raw it is warming and purging, cleaning away things that darken the sight, and dissolving and ripening hard lumps... Some call burnt pyrites, diphryges [above]." So Dioscorides himself records that diphryges is sometimes the name given to burnt pyrites, which is why the translator suggests the pyrites identification. The qualities "warming and purging" belong to pyrites in 5-143, not to diphryges in 5-120.

**Identity.** The three kinds in 5-120 are not one mineral: one is a natural Cypriot material roasted in the sun, one is the furnace dregs of brass working, and one is roasted pyrites. The translator's "Disulphide of Iron" matches only the third and the 5-143 note. The name, then, is a trade name for roasted residues of several origins. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Stated generally. Pyrites is iron disulphide (FeS2). Roasting it drives off sulphur as sulphur dioxide and leaves iron oxides, which fits Dioscorides' colour change to "red ochre" (line 10452). Brass-furnace residues are mixtures of copper and zinc oxides and slag, and the text says the second kind keeps "the astringent quality and taste of brass", which is consistent with a copper-bearing material. No composition is stated in the text read, and no figures are given here.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides gives diphryges as an external astringent and cleansing application for malignant-looking and creeping sores, for fleshy growths and for abscesses. The material is a furnace residue likely to contain copper and iron compounds, possibly arsenic or lead from the ores, and nothing in the sources retrieved makes it a self-care practice. No modern protocol is supplied.

### Preparation & Dosing

Only the preparations of the mineral itself are given (loci above): sun-drying and burning amid brushwood (Cyprian kind); collecting the sediment from brass working (second kind); roasting pyrites for many days until the colour is that of red ochre, and storing in jars (third kind). For the medicinal use the chapter states only that it is "taken with turpentine or wax ointments" for abscesses (line 10452). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM).** Printed qualities for diphryges (line 10452): astringent; "excessively drying to the tongue" (of the best kind); and, as actions, cleans thoroughly, wipes away, dries, represses growths, forms new skins. No hot or cold degree is printed for it in 5-120. The 5-143 passage calls pyrites "warming and purging" but that is the raw or burnt stone, named separately.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers is a discipline of attention, and this chapter shows it plainly: three origins distinguished, the best kind identified by taste and look ("brassy to the taste, looks rusty, astringent and excessively drying"), and a named counterfeit (burnt ochre) with the test that tells it apart. The chapter is therapeutic and no cultivation status is assigned to the substance. No amuletic use is recorded for it in the lines read.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here for a cultivator to take. What carries over from the chapter is the habit of asking where a material comes from, how it was made, and how it can be told from its imitation. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Toxicity:** furnace residues of brass and roasted sulphide ores can contain copper, lead, arsenic and other toxic metals; the roasting releases sulphur dioxide and, from ores with arsenic, arsenic fumes.
- **Skin and wounds:** astringent metal salts irritate and can cause caustic injury; the text's "forms new skins on malignancies" is not a recommendation to apply it to cancer.
- **Internal use:** the sources do not give a route of internal use and none is endorsed.
- **Identity:** the three kinds are different materials, so the name cannot guarantee composition.
- **Occupational:** roasting pyrites in a closed place is hazardous.
