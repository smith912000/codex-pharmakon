# Hedgehog — Echinos Chersaios (as printed in the Osbaldeston and Wood Dioscorides, "earth hedgehog")
## *The land ("earth") hedgehog; the translators print "Erinaceous genus — Hedgehog" under the heading* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The hedgehog is chapter 2-2 of *De Materia Medica*, headed "ECHINOS CHERSAIOS", in the Osbaldeston and Wood English (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, line 4915; the entry is also in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`). Under the heading the translators print "Erinaceous genus — Hedgehog" (line 4917), which is their gloss; Dioscorides' own name, *echinos chersaios*, means the land (earth) echinos, in contrast to the preceding chapter 2-1, *echinos thalassios*, the sea echinos (line 4911), a separate substance not folded in here. The chapter reads (line 4918):

"The burnt skin of the earth hedgehog is good for alopecia [baldness] rubbed on with moist pitch. The dried flesh (taken in a drink with honey or vinegar and honey) helps inflamed kidneys, water under the skin [dropsy], and those who have convulsions, elephantiasis, or cachexy [defective nutrition]. It dries up discharges from the bowels and liver. Dried in a sun-dried clay jar, and stored, then given, it does as much good for the same things."

So the chapter has two materials. The first is the burnt skin, applied externally with moist pitch for hair loss. The second is the flesh, dried and drunk in honey or in vinegar and honey, for a list of complaints, and a method of drying and storing it: dried "in a sun-dried clay jar" and then "stored, then given", with the same effect as the freshly dried flesh. The bracketed glosses ("[baldness]", "[dropsy]", "[defective nutrition]") are the translators' additions; whether the Greek disease names match those modern terms exactly is not stated. The same book gives other hair-loss recipes of the same type: the "hippocampus" is burnt and its ashes used in goose grease, liquid pitch or ointment to fill "alopecia [baldness] with hair" (line 4925); frogs "burnt ... rubbed on with liquid pitch" (line 5050); and goats' hooves burnt and rubbed on with vinegar (line 5125). They are separate substances and are listed only to show a recurring form in Book Two. An illustration labelled "Hedgehog — Echinus species after DAVIS — 1907" (line 5024) is part of the translation's apparatus and is not Dioscorides' text.

### Active Components

The hedgehog is a small insectivorous mammal. Its back is covered with spines, which are modified hairs made of keratin. The skin and flesh are, as in other mammals, water, protein and fat with minerals. Burning the skin converts the protein largely to char and ash, so the burnt material is not the raw skin; drying the flesh removes water and concentrates the rest. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is a first-century hair-loss application and a drink of dried flesh for kidney, dropsy, convulsive, wasting and bowel complaints. The Codex gives no protocol. No source read supports these claims, several of the named conditions (kidney inflammation, dropsy, convulsions, wasting, chronic discharges) are serious and call for medical care, and hedgehogs are wild animals that are legally protected in many countries and are declining in some. Taking one for a medicine is neither lawful nor justified in those places.

### Preparation & Dosing

Two preparations are given (line 4918). Skin: burnt, "rubbed on with moist pitch". Flesh: dried, "taken in a drink with honey or vinegar and honey"; it may be dried "in a sun-dried clay jar" and stored, "then given". No quantity, frequency, duration or degree of burning or drying is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] Routes: external (skin with pitch); oral (flesh as a drink).

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Keratin applied to the scalp does not supply anything the hair follicle uses; hair loss has many causes. Wild mammals can carry zoonotic infections and parasites; hedgehogs in particular are known carriers of *Salmonella* and ringworm fungi (general fact). Pitch is a tar product and is a skin irritant. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** No quality (warming, cooling, astringent or otherwise) is printed in the hedgehog chapter, beyond the verb "dries up discharges from the bowels and liver" in the action line. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied here. What he can offer a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, season, sensory assay, detection of adulteration). This chapter has a small element of it, in the method for drying and storing the flesh (dried in a clay jar and kept), but no source, season or quality sign for the animal. There is no cultivation status in the chapter and no amuletic use, so there is no recording-versus-endorsing question.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. The chapter is a record of what a first-century physician reported, and a protected wild animal is not a source of medicine. The one transferable point is the attention to preparation and storage: material is dried, kept and given, not used raw.

### Contraindications

- **Conservation and law:** hedgehogs are protected by law in many countries, and some populations are declining; capturing or killing one may be an offence. [USER: verify]
- **Zoonotic infection:** hedgehogs can carry *Salmonella*, ringworm and parasites.
- **Serious conditions:** kidney inflammation, dropsy, convulsions and wasting need medical care; do not rely on this text.
- **Skin irritation:** pitch and tar products can irritate or burn the scalp.
- **Ethics and religion:** killing a wild animal for a medicine with no supported benefit is hard to justify.
