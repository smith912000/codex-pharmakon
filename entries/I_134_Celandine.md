# I_134 — Chelidonium majus | Greater Celandine / Tetterwort / Swallowwort / Felonwort

**Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry:** I_134
**Convergence Score:** 5/9 traditions
**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (hepatobiliary/antispasmodic) | ☿ Shen (ophthalmic — traditional only)
**Safety Class:** ⚠️ HEPATOTOXIC — numerous case reports; Commission E approved but with hepatotoxicity warning; do not exceed dose; avoid prolonged use; monitor liver function

## Section 1 — Nomenclature

| Register | Name |
|----------|------|

| DMM (Greek) | Chelidōnion (Book II.180) — from *chelidōn* (swallow); named for the swallow myth: plant flowers when swallows arrive, withers when they depart; also Hirundinis herba (Latin calque) |
| CM (Arabic/Persian) | Mamīthā (ماميثا) — Persian; also Sarish-hā / Qaq aghaci (various regional names); Ibn Sīnā discusses under eye applications and liver |
| EP (Egyptian) | Possibly identified in eye preparations; tentative |
| AT (Akkadian) | Possibly identified in eye/liver herb tablets; tentative |
| SBJ | Bó luò zǐ / Jì mǐ cǎo — lesser used Chinese identification; *Chelidonium* not native to China but encountered in border trade; Bencao Gangmu notes it as a foreign herb |
| European folk | Tetterwort (for skin conditions — "tetter" = skin eruption); Swallowwort; Felonwort (for felon = whitlow, paronychia); Killwart (for warts); Kill-weed; Garden Celandine (to distinguish from Lesser Celandine = *Ficaria verna*, an unrelated buttercup) |
| Latin | Chelidonium; Herba chelidonii; Herba citrinae (for yellow sap) |
| German folk | Schöllkraut (from Latin "Schelonien" = the Greek name); Schwalbenkraut (Swallow herb) |

**Botanical identity:** Biennial/perennial herb to 1m; brittle stems containing bright orange-yellow latex throughout; pinnate leaves grey-green; flowers yellow 4-petalled; elongated siliqua-like capsules. The bright orange latex is immediately diagnostic — no other common European herb exudes this vivid orange milk from broken stems or leaves. Widely distributed weed of walls, hedges, woodland edges. All parts contain alkaloids; the latex contains the highest concentration.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Score: 5/9** (DMM · CM confirmed; EP · AT · European folk tradition)

The convergence pattern for celandine follows the jaundice-detection principle: the bright yellow-orange latex of *Chelidonium* → yellow herb → yellow jaundice treatment (Doctrine of Signatures operating pharmacologically here, since the underlying mechanism — bile stimulation via chelidonic acid and alkaloids — is genuine). Additionally, the alkaloid berberine (also in goldenseal, barberry — see I_051) contributes hepatobiliary and antimicrobial effects consistent with the traditional applications.

**The eye application** (DMM, CM, EP tentative) is the most controversial element of celandine's traditional use: applying caustic orange latex directly to the eye for cataracts represents an application where the doctrine "this yellow/orange substance cures yellowing of the body and spots before the eyes" was applied with more symbolic conviction than pharmacological caution. Modern ophthalmology regards direct chelidonine application to corneal tissue as potentially harmful. The eye tradition should be understood as historical documentation, not therapeutic recommendation.

**Hepatotoxicity paradox:** A plant used across 5 traditions for liver and jaundice treatment (bile stimulant, liver tonic) has produced numerous case reports of serious hepatotoxic drug reactions. This is the clearest example in the Codex of a plant used FOR the organ it can damage. The hepatotoxicity is alkaloid-mediated; at recommended doses the hepatoprotective/choleretic effects predominate; at high doses or with individual susceptibility, serious idiosyncratic hepatotoxicity occurs.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Isoquinoline alkaloids (primary active class; 0.5–1.5% total in aerial parts):**
- **Chelidonine** (dominant; 0.5–1.0%) — antispasmodic (smooth muscle); sedative; the principal pharmacologically active alkaloid; analgesic
- **Berberine** — the well-known antimicrobial/hepatobiliary alkaloid found also in goldenseal (*Hydrastis*), barberry (*Berberis*), and Oregon grape; anti-inflammatory; antidiarrheal; bile-stimulating
- **Coptisine** — antimicrobial; anti-inflammatory
- **Sanguinarine** — antimicrobial (antiplaque, antifungal); cytotoxic; antitumor potential; found in bloodroot (*Sanguinaria*)
- **Chelerythrine** — antimicrobial; cytotoxic; toxic to cells at higher concentrations
- **Sparteine** (trace) — antiarrhythmic; oxytocic
- Allocryptopine, protopine — additional isoquinoline alkaloids

**Chelidonic acid:**
- Hydroxamic acid; chelates iron; contributes to latex color and biological activity

**Flavonoids:**
- Quercetin, rutin, kaempferol — anti-inflammatory

**Other:**
- Saponins (minor)
- Organic acids: citric, malic, chelidonic

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Antispasmodic / Choleretic (primary validated mechanisms):**
- **Chelidonine:** binds opioid μ and κ receptors → smooth muscle relaxation (antispasmodic); also direct calcium channel blockade → reduced smooth muscle tone
- **Result:** relaxation of biliary tract smooth muscle (sphincter of Oddi) → improved bile flow; antispasmodic for biliary colic; relieves right-sided abdominal spasm in gallbladder disease
- **Berberine:** stimulates hepatic bile acid synthesis; increases bile flow (choleretic); antimicrobial activity against biliary bacteria

**Hepatobiliary (validated at low doses):**
- Chelidonine + berberine: choleretic (increases bile secretion) + cholecystokinetic (stimulates gallbladder contraction)
- Clinical implication: useful for biliary dyskinesia, functional biliary pain, mild cholecystitis, dyspepsia with biliary component

**Hepatotoxic (at high doses / idiosyncratic):**
- **Mechanism:** Multiple alkaloids (especially chelerythrine, sanguinarine) are cytotoxic to hepatocytes; mitochondrial dysfunction; reactive oxygen species generation; covalent protein binding
- **Pattern:** Idiosyncratic hepatitis (resembles drug-induced liver injury, DILI); onset 1–16 weeks after starting celandine; resolves on cessation; rare but documented
- **Case reports:** 30+ published cases of acute hepatitis from celandine preparations (Germany, Belgium, Italy, Netherlands, Australia); severity ranged from mild transaminase elevation to fulminant hepatic failure requiring transplant
- **BfArM (German regulatory) action:** Required hepatotoxicity warning on all celandine preparations; dose limits established

**Antiviral / Antimicrobial:**
- Chelidonine + berberine + sanguinarine: active against herpes simplex virus; antibacterial; antifungal (*Candida*)
- Sanguinarine: antiplaque activity (approved ingredient in dental products in some countries)

**Antitumor (in vitro):**
- Chelidonine: apoptosis induction in multiple cancer cell lines (Bcl-2 inhibition; caspase activation)
- Ukrain (thiophosphoric acid derivative of chelidonine): marketed as cancer drug in Ukraine and some Eastern European countries; limited clinical evidence; controversial

**Wart treatment (topical latex):**
- Caustic alkaloids (sanguinarine, chelerythrine) → keratinocyte apoptosis → wart tissue destruction
- Analogous to salicylic acid wart treatments in mechanism (tissue destruction) but less predictable
- Traditional wart application (fresh orange latex directly on wart) is the safest use of celandine because it avoids systemic alkaloid exposure

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Commission E (Germany): APPROVED** with extensive caution wording:
- Indication: spastic discomfort of the bile ducts and GI tract
- Required label: hepatotoxicity warning; maximum 4 weeks continuous use without medical supervision; liver function monitoring recommended

**BfArM (Federal Institute for Drugs, Germany): 2008 Safety Assessment:**
- Required strengthened hepatotoxicity warnings on all celandine-containing products
- Risk assessment concluded: genuine hepatotoxicity risk, idiosyncratic pattern; benefits outweigh risks at recommended doses for biliary complaints; monitoring essential

**Clinical trials:**
- Madisch et al. (2004) *American Journal of Gastroenterology*: RCT, *Chelidonium* extract vs. placebo in irritable bowel syndrome. **Result:** Significant improvement in abdominal pain, particularly in patients with upper abdominal/biliary-type symptoms. N=60. This is the strongest RCT for celandine's modern indications.
- Multiple European cohort studies confirming choleretic + antispasmodic effects

**Topical wart treatment:**
- Traditional latex application widely practiced; some small clinical evaluations confirming efficacy but no formal RCT
- Compared with salicylic acid: comparable wart clearance in observational studies

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The swallow myth — one of the most enduring botanical legends:**
*Chelidonium* (from Greek *chelidōn* = swallow) carries one of the most persistent explanatory myths in botanical history: ancient sources claim that swallow parents find the plant and use its sap to restore sight to their blind nestlings. This "zoopharmacognosy legend" is recorded in Dioscorides, Pliny, and numerous medieval herbalists. Pliny (*NH* XXV.50): "The plant was first discovered, as they say, by the swallows; for when their young have their eyes put out, they anoint them with the juice of this plant, and so restore their sight."

The myth encodes the eye application (the yellow-orange latex → yellow-tinged eye conditions) in a charming animal-discovery narrative. Subsequent European herbalists repeated this for 1,500 years; the eye application of celandine latex persisted in folk practice long after its safety concerns became evident.

**The timing myth (secondary):**
Aristotle and Dioscorides record that celandine flowers when swallows arrive (spring) and withers when they depart (autumn). This is approximately accurate for European birds; the plant's phenology loosely tracks migratory swallow timing. The naming and myth are thus grounded in accurate calendar observation, not pure fantasy.

**Doctrine of Signatures — the most mechanistically accurate instance in the Codex:**
The orange-yellow latex → yellow herb → jaundice (yellow skin disease) → hepatobiliary treatment chain is one of the most pharmacologically accurate Doctrine of Signatures connections: the substance that looks yellow-bilious genuinely has choleretic and biliary antispasmodic properties. The correlation is not incidental — the alkaloids that color the latex orange (berberine) ARE the hepatobiliary active compounds. In this case, the Doctrine operated as an accidental mechanistic predictor.

**Paracelsus (1493–1541):** Enthusiastically promoted celandine as a primary liver herb; Paracelsian "signature medicine" emphasized the orange/gold color as evidence of its affinity for liver and bile (gold/yellow → solar → liver under Renaissance astrological medicine). The Paracelsian tradition amplified celandine's hepatobiliary reputation and increased its use throughout early modern Europe — potentially contributing to the increased hepatotoxicity exposure that generates case reports in the current literature.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### ⚠️ HEPATOTOXICITY WARNING

**Monitor liver function** during any course of celandine use exceeding 2 weeks. Discontinue immediately if symptoms of liver injury appear: jaundice, dark urine, right upper quadrant pain, fatigue, loss of appetite.

**Maximum safe use:** 4 weeks continuous; then minimum 4 weeks break. Do not use with other potentially hepatotoxic herbs (kava, comfrey, germander, pyrrolizidine alkaloid plants) or concurrent hepatotoxic drugs.

**Modern therapeutic applications:**

1. **Biliary dyskinesia / Biliary spasm:** 2–4g dried herb as infusion, 3× daily; or standardized extract (12–30mg chelidonine equivalent) 3× daily; maximum 4 weeks; liver function monitoring; this is the indication most supported by evidence and most appropriate for the risk profile

2. **Functional dyspepsia with biliary component:** Same protocol; useful when upper abdominal pain is right-sided or post-prandial and associated with biliary tract dysfunction

3. **Wart treatment (topical — safest application):** Apply fresh orange latex from broken stem directly to wart, avoid surrounding skin; cover with plaster; repeat daily for 2–4 weeks; the caustic alkaloids destroy wart tissue; this avoids systemic exposure entirely

**Contraindications:**
- **Pregnancy:** AVOID — chelidonine has uterotonic potential; sparteine (trace) is oxytocic
- **Active liver disease, hepatitis, cirrhosis:** ABSOLUTE contraindication
- **Biliary obstruction (gallstones blocking duct):** increased bile flow can worsen obstruction; use only under specialist supervision
- **Children under 12:** avoid
- **Concurrent hepatotoxic drugs or herbs:** ABSOLUTE contraindication

**Preparation:**
- Standardized dry extract preferred over whole herb infusion for dose control
- Commission E dose: 2–4g daily of dried herb equivalent
- Standardized to 5mg chelidonine per dose (typical German pharmaceutical preparation)
