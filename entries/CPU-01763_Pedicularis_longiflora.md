# Pedicularis longiflora — Lugru serpo, lug ru ser po (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Pedicularis longiflora, the binomial the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints in ninth and last place of its "L" scientific-name run, with the English name "Yellow lousewort", and the first of three binomials Clark 1995 prints for lug.ru.ser.po (as "Pedicularis longiflora Rudolph var. tubiformis (Klotz) Tsoong"); the only entry in this batch for which the two editions name the same species* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Ebers, Assyrian, Badianus, Avicenna and Sushruta material on disk for *Pedicularis* found only Dioscorides lines that print *Pedicularis* inside translators' lists for other drugs (for example line 9431, a SUGGESTED line for *staphisagria*), which are not this drug. The Shennong text (Yang 1998) has *Ma Xian Hao* (*Herba Pedicularis Resupinatae*, line 3063, `names.csv` line 141), a different species that is not read as this drug. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** The spine row (CPU-01763) was added from the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary line 5448 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 315, with the note "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)"). Line 5448 prints **four** binomials on one line ("Phlomis younghusbandii Corydalis meifolia Pedicularis siphonantha Pedicularis longiflora"), and each has its own row: this one is the fourth of the four on the line and the last of the block's nine scientific names, a later one and not the first. Clark's line for *lug.ru.ser.po* (line 1031; names.csv line 143) prints three binomials, *Pedicularis longiflora* var. *tubiformis*, *Pedicularis tenuirostris* and *Pedicularis bicomuta* (as printed), and the names.csv row keys the first, which is the species of this entry. The second binomial has its own entry (I_1329).

**Identity doubt.** For this drug the two editions name the same species, though Clark adds a variety and two other species. The positional pairing in the glossary is checked by the colour word in the name (below). The Codex does not state that the plant is *Pedicularis longiflora*, since Clark's own preface (line 68) says that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect". The Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note (line 686) says that the botanical terms in their text "are based on the information procured from the Materia Medica Department of this institute"; it names no flora or earlier work. **Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "L" block, lines 5433 to 5454).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. The phonetic names are nine (lines 5435 to 5437), ending "Lugru mukpo" and "Lugru serpo" (line 5437). The transliterations end "lug ru smug po lug ru ser po" (line 5441). The scientific names are nine (lines 5445 to 5448), ending "Pedicularis siphonantha Pedicularis longiflora" (line 5448). The English names (lines 5450 to 5454) are seven and end "Lousewort Yellow lousewort" (line 5454). Counted in order, "Lugru serpo" is the ninth and last phonetic name, *Pedicularis longiflora* the ninth and last scientific name, and "Yellow lousewort" the last English name. The count is checked on three names by Clark, as in I_1310 and I_1258: "Lalaphue" is first against *Cnidium monnieri* (Clark's *la.la.phud*, line 896), "Lugchhung" fifth against *Aster strachei* (Clark's *lug.chhung*, line 1105) and "Lugnyal" seventh against *Corydalis meifolia* (Clark's *lug.ngal*, line 1106). The colour word fits as well: "serpo" is Clark's *ser.po* (yellow) and the English is "Yellow lousewort", and Clark's commentary says the yellow type "has a yellow flower". The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is positional. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3433).* Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428): "Lugru mukpo nutrilizes toxin and cures meat poison. Lugru serpo and Chhurug dries up chhuser. Sewai metok cures tripa disorders and suppresses loong." "Chhuser" is a Tibetan term that the translators keep untranslated (the Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note, line 686, lists it among the "key technical terms" kept in phonetic Tibetan), and the Codex keeps it so. The index lists "Lugru serpo 207" (line 7428). The name is not in the taste groups of chapter 19 and not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23). The sentence gives no part used and no preparation.

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1031).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry follows *lug.ru (smug.po)* (line 1030) and reads: "lug.ru.ser.po: Pedicularis longiflora Rudolph var. tubiformis (Klotz) Tsoong: Pedicularis tenuirostris: Pedicularis bicomuta: [grows in shady meadows and has a yellow flower similar to that of the brown type. It collects poisons and dries 'reservoirs' of pus.]" Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. **The whole of the description and both actions are inside the brackets**, so, as Clark's marks stand, the root-text layer gives this drug only the name, and its actions ("collects poisons and dries 'reservoirs' of pus") come from the commentary. The next line, *chhu.rug*, closes: "Both types, as well as] Pedicularis (above) dry fluid accumulations [and Halerpestes sarmentosa cures bone fever]" (line 1032), which is outside the brackets and names "Pedicularis (above)" without saying whether the yellow type is meant; the Codex does not carry it over. Clark's name index lists *lug.ru.ser.po* (line 1758); his Latin index (line 1626) lists *Pedicularis longiflora*, *Pedicularis tenuirostris* and *Pedicularis bicomuta*.

The two editions agree in kind on the action only loosely: Clark's "collects poisons and dries 'reservoirs' of pus" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "dries up chhuser". The Codex reads Clark's *lug.ru.ser.po* and the glossary's "Lugru serpo" as one drug from the spelling and the colour word; neither text says so. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Pedicularis longiflora*, *lug.ru.ser.po* and "Lugru serpo" found no entry on this drug. I_1329 (*Pedicularis tenuirostris*) is a sibling spine row from the same Clark line (1031), and I_1328 (*Pedicularis siphonantha*) from the neighbouring glossary row; the three are one drug and its brown neighbour under different binomials, and are not merged. I_1310, I_1256, I_1258 and I_1022 print the same "L" block and give the name no entry. I_617 (*Pedicularis foliosa*, tags CM, DMM, FMT) says, with no locus, that "lug-ru" louseworts are used as "tonics" and for "heat conditions" and wounds; the lines read here print poison, pus and fluid actions and no tonic, and that claim was not found. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Pedicularis* is a large genus of hemiparasitic herbs (they photosynthesise but draw water and nutrients from the roots of neighbouring plants) and that "lousewort" is its English name. Which compounds this species or the Tibetan drug holds is not shown by the lines read, so no statement about its composition is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "collects poisons and dries 'reservoirs' of pus" (Clark's commentary layer, line 1031) and "dries up chhuser" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3433). These are the texts' claims in their own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. A pus-filled swelling or a poisoning needs medical care, and no herb should be used for either on the strength of this entry.

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary describes the habitat and the flower and does not say which part is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** No taste, power or post-digestive taste is printed for this drug in Clark's line 1031 or in the Men-Tsee-Khang lines read (3433, 5437). The only quality printed is the colour of the flower (yellow, "similar to that of the brown type"), which is a description of the plant and not a taste or power. The brown type's line (1030) prints "bitter taste, fluid power", and the Codex does not carry those over to the yellow type.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug only by name in the root-text layer (Clark's brackets enclose the rest of the line), and the commentary classes it by what it does: it collects poisons and dries pus. The Men-Tsee-Khang text says it dries up *chhuser*. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23 in both editions), and Clark's materia medica names other drugs as essence-extraction ingredients (for example lines 853, 971 and 1144), none of them this one. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a poison-and-pus drug in the Tibetan texts, known from the commentary layer in Clark, with no preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Poisoning and pus-forming infections need medical care; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No conservation information was read.
