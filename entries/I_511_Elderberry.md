# I-511 | Elderberry (*Sambucus nigra* L.)

**Volume I — Plant-Based Substances**
**Family:** Adoxaceae (formerly Caprifoliaceae/Sambucaceae)
**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (antiviral, immunomodulatory, diaphoretic) primary

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary binomial:** *Sambucus nigra* L. (1753, *Species Plantarum*)

**Synonyms & historical names:**
- *Sambucus canadensis* L. (American elder — now recognized as separate species by APG IV; pharmacologically treated as equivalent in most literature)
- *Sambucus nigra* subsp. *canadensis* (L.) R.Bolli (intermediate classification)

**Common names:**
- English: European elderberry, black elder, elder, elderberry, elderflower (flower-specific)
- German: *Schwarzer Holunder*, *Holunder*
- French: *Sureau noir*
- Italian: *Sambuco nero*
- Spanish: *Saúco negro*
- Arabic: *Khamān* (*Sambucus* spp.)
- Chinese: 接骨木 (*jiē gǔ mù*, "bone-joining wood") — *Sambucus* spp. used for fracture healing
- Sanskrit: *Saptaparṇa* (some attributions; disputed; different plant)
- Greek: *Sambucos* — Sambuca is the ancient instrument made from elder wood
- Old English: *Aeld* (from which "elder" derives, not from "old man")

**Etymology:** *Sambucus* = Latin for the elder tree (possibly from Greek *sambuke* = ancient stringed instrument made of elder wood); *nigra* = black (referring to the black ripe berries). "Elder" = Old English *aeld* (fire-kindling wood — hollowed stems used as bellows for fire).

**Botanical authentication:**
- Deciduous shrub or small tree 3–10 m; pinnate leaves (5–7 leaflets, serrate, characteristic foetid smell when crushed); large flat-topped umbel inflorescence (corymb) of creamy-white flowers in June (elderflower); berries turn from green → red → black-purple as they ripen (September–October); fruit = small drupe 5–6 mm, very dark purple-black at maturity
- **CRITICAL TOXICITY NOTE:** All parts of *S. nigra* — unripe berries, leaves, bark, roots, seeds — contain cyanogenic glycosides (sambunigrin; prunasin). Raw unripe berries and seeds cause nausea and vomiting. Fully ripe berries contain lower cyanogenic levels; cooking or pasteurization destroys the HCN-releasing enzyme (β-glucosidase) → commercial elderberry products are heat-processed.
- Authentication: flat umbel inflorescences (not pyramidal as in *S. ebulus* = dwarf elder, which is TOXIC); flowers cream-white (not pink/red); ripe berries deeply black-purple (not red = *S. racemosa*, which is poisonous)
- **Species confusion**: *S. ebulus* (dwarf elder, danewort) = poisonous; grows low to ground; pyramidal flower clusters; DO NOT confuse with *S. nigra*. *S. racemosa* (red elderberry) = red berries at maturity = TOXIC.

**Harvest:**
- Flowers: full bloom, before browning (June); hand-harvested for elderflower preparations
- Ripe berries: deep black-purple (September–October); machine or hand-harvested

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

Elder occupies a unique position in European folk medicine: the "medicine chest of the people" (*Volksapotheke* in German), containing within a single plant species applications for nearly every common acute illness. The Hippocratic and Galenic traditions recognized elder bark as emetic, leaf as vulnerary, flower as diaphoretic/antipyretic, and berry as nutritive/diuretic — a whole-plant use pattern indicating very long-standing and deep observation of the plant's diverse properties.

The TCM use of 接骨木 (bone-joining wood) encodes a fracture-healing indication not present in European tradition, reflecting the distinct TCM diagnostic framework for musculoskeletal conditions. The bioactive basis for this indication may involve the flavonoid anti-inflammatory fraction reducing swelling and promoting healing environment, though this is insufficiently studied.

The modern antiviral evidence for elderberry — particularly the Zakay-Rones 1995 influenza RCT and subsequent Tiralongo 2016 travel cold RCT — represents a pharmacological mechanism (lectin-like viral envelope binding by cyanidin glycosides → viral inhibition) not present in any traditional rationale but validating the traditional diaphoretic/anti-fever application through a newly characterized mechanism. This is the reverse of the typical discovery arc: traditional use → mechanism discovery.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Anthocyanins (primary pharmacologically characterized fraction — berries):**
- **Cyanidin-3-glucoside (C3G)**: 30–50% of total anthocyanins; 50–250 mg/100g fresh berries; primary active constituent in antiviral and antioxidant studies
- **Cyanidin-3-sambubioside (C3S)**: 30–45% of total anthocyanins; co-primary
- Cyanidin-3-rutinoside, cyanidin-3-sambubioside-5-glucoside: minor
- **Total anthocyanins:** 100–600 mg/100g fresh weight (highly variable; variety, ripeness, processing dependent); 3,000–15,000 mg/L in elderberry juice/syrup
- The cyanidin glycoside fraction is the primary characterizable constituent; concentration in standardized preparations expressed as mg cyanidin-3-glucoside equivalent

**Flavonoids:**
- **Quercetin-3-rutinoside (rutin)**: 0.5–5% DW in flowers (highest in flowers)
- **Quercetin, kaempferol, isorhamnetin glycosides**: present in berries and flowers
- **Isoquercitrin, hyperoside**: present
- **Eriocitrin**: in some varieties

**Phenolic acids:**
- **Chlorogenic acid**: 0.1–2% DW; significant antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; inhibits glucose absorption (relevant to metabolic syndrome)
- Caffeic acid, 3-p-coumaroylquinic acid, 3-feruloylquinic acid: present

**Lectins (elderberry hemagglutinin):**
- **Sambucus nigra lectin II (SNA-II/SNAII)**: carbohydrate-binding protein specific for sialic acid-containing glycoproteins; present in berries and bark; the proposed antiviral mechanism involves SNA-II and cyanidin glycosides binding to influenza hemagglutinin sialic acid receptor → blocking viral attachment to host cells

**Cyanogenic glycosides (TOXIC — must be considered):**
- **Sambunigrin** (β-D-glucoside of (R)-mandelonitrile): 0.005–0.01% DW in ripe berries; 0.05–0.1% in unripe berries; higher in leaves, bark, seeds
- β-glucosidase (present in same plant material) hydrolyzes sambunigrin → mandelonitrile → HCN + benzaldehyde on crushing/chewing raw berries; cooking inactivates β-glucosidase → stops HCN release
- HCN yield from one standard elderberry syrup dose: negligible after proper preparation; clinically not significant from commercially prepared products; raw berry juice (uncooked) may cause GI upset; massive consumption of raw berries → cyanide toxicity possible (rare; case reports)
- **Commercial elderberry preparations are heat-processed** → safe

**Volatile compounds (flowers — elderflower):**
- **Hotrienol, linalool, nonanal, α-terpineol**: characteristic elderflower aroma
- **Nerol oxide, lilac alcohols**: floral notes
- Flower EO concentration: 0.03–0.1% DW; complex terpenoid/phenylpropanoid bouquet; responsible for elderflower fragrance used in cordials and cosmetics

**Triterpenes:**
- α-Amyrin, β-amyrin, ursolic acid, oleanolic acid: 0.3–1.5% DW; anti-inflammatory
- Ursolic acid: NF-κB inhibition (recurrent Codex pattern across plant families)

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Primary antiviral mechanism (cyanidin glycosides):**

1. **Viral attachment inhibition:**
Cyanidin-3-glucoside (C3G) and cyanidin-3-sambubioside bind to influenza surface proteins (hemagglutinin — HA and neuraminidase — NA). C3G binding to HA blocks viral attachment to sialic acid receptors on host epithelial cells; C3G binding to NA reduces viral release from infected cells. Mousa Zakay-Rones demonstrated this mechanism in 1995; Krawitz et al. 2011 (*BMC Complementary and Alternative Medicine* 11:16) showed that elderberry standardized extract had antiviral activity against H1N1 (including oseltamivir-sensitive and resistant strains).

2. **Direct virucidal effect:**
Elderberry extract inactivates influenza virions directly via membrane disruption (anthocyanin-mediated) at concentrations achievable in respiratory secretions after oral administration. Roschek B et al. 2009 (*Phytochemistry* 70:1255-1261): flavonoids from elderberry bound H1N1 HA directly; IC50 for HA binding: quercetin 3-glucoside 75 μM, 5,7,3',4'-tetrahydroxyflavone <500 nM.

3. **Immune stimulation (cytokine induction):**
Elderberry preparations stimulate production of cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8) in macrophages and monocytes. This represents an immunostimulatory (not immunosuppressive) mechanism. **IMPORTANT CONCERN:** Some researchers have raised concern that elderberry's cytokine-stimulating effect could theoretically worsen cytokine storm in severe influenza. This concern applies specifically to severe COVID-19/H5N1-type severe disease; evidence is theoretical; clinical evidence does not support this concern for typical mild-to-moderate influenza.

**Antioxidant:**
High cyanidin glycoside and quercetin content → exceptional ORAC and DPPH radical scavenging; TEAC comparable to blueberry extracts; reduces oxidative burst in phagocytes (anti-inflammatory in inflammatory contexts).

**Anti-inflammatory (flavonoid-mediated):**
Quercetin, rutin, ursolic acid: COX-2 inhibition; NF-κB suppression; reduction of IL-6, TNF-α in stimulated macrophages; anti-inflammatory mechanism relevant to respiratory infection symptom reduction.

**Diaphoretic (traditional mechanism):**
Elder flowers (not berries): mild sudorific effect via flavonoid and volatile compound effects on sweat gland stimulation; traditional mechanism for fever management via perspiration; direct antiviral mechanism may now explain this better.

**Antidiabetic (chlorogenic acid):**
Chlorogenic acid inhibits intestinal glucose-6-phosphatase and reduces postprandial glucose absorption; metabolic syndrome relevance; consistent with emerging interest in elderberry for metabolic conditions.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Influenza — Level A:**

1. **Zakay-Rones Z et al. 1995** (*Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine* 1:361-369) — n=40 influenza B outbreak; Sambucol (standardized elderberry extract) vs placebo; average recovery 2.8 days (elderberry) vs 6.0 days (placebo); HI antibody titers higher in elderberry group; Level A (small but landmark)

2. **Zakay-Rones Z et al. 2004** (*Journal of International Medical Research* 32:132-140) — n=60, influenza A and B; Sambucol 4× daily for 5 days; significant reduction in symptom duration (4 days faster recovery vs placebo); Level A

3. **Tiralongo E et al. 2016** (*Nutrients* 8:182) — n=312, international air travelers (double-blind RCT); elderberry 900 mg/day (capsules) from 10 days before travel to 5 days after; significant reduction in cold duration (2 days less) and severity vs placebo; Level A

4. **Hawkins J et al. 2019** (*Complementary Medicine Research* 26:164-169) — elderberry supplementation vs placebo for upper respiratory symptoms in community; reduced symptom severity and duration; Level A

5. **Wieland LS et al. 2021** (Cochrane Review on elderberry for cold/flu): included 5 RCTs; positive trend for symptom reduction; moderate evidence quality; Level A (Cochrane)

6. **Meta-analysis (Hawkins 2019):** 9 RCTs, 994 subjects; elderberry supplementation significantly reduced upper respiratory symptoms (pooled effect)

**COVID-19 — Level D/C:**
- In vitro studies show elderberry inhibits SARS-CoV-2 attachment; no clinical RCTs completed as of 2024; observational data limited; not a COVID-19 treatment recommendation

**Cardiovascular — Level B:**
- Elderberry anthocyanins: LDL oxidation reduction; endothelial function improvement; blood pressure reduction (small studies); consistent with anthocyanin cardiovascular evidence from blueberry literature

**Immunomodulation — Level C:**
- Elderberry increases cytokine production in healthy subjects (modest effect); consistent immunostimulatory mechanism; limited clinical evidence for immunocompromised populations

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**"The medicine chest of the people" — Hippocrates:**
Hippocrates referred to the elder tree (*Sambucus*) as his "medicine chest" — the single tree containing remedies for the most common illnesses: berries for fever, flowers for the cold, bark for edema and rheumatism, leaves for wounds. This designation, preserved in the classical record and transmitted through European folk medicine, captures 2,000 years of integrated whole-plant pharmacological observation. The modern antiviral evidence for elderberry represents a mechanistic validation of what Hippocrates observed empirically — that this plant reliably shortened the duration of febrile respiratory illness.

**Elder mythology across European cultures:**
No European plant carries a denser mythology than elder. Norse tradition: Freyja/Hulda (goddess of fertility and magic) lived in the elder tree (*Hyldemoer* = elder mother in Danish); cutting an elder without asking permission angered her. Celtic tradition: elder marks the boundary between worlds; associated with death and regeneration. English country tradition: elder trees at cottages protect against witchcraft and lightning. Shakespeare: elder = grief, sorrow (Cymbeline). The cross-cultural European association of elder with boundary, protection, and both danger and healing maps onto the plant's pharmacological reality: it contains both toxic cyanogenic compounds (dangerous without processing) and potent antiviral and immunostimulatory compounds (protective with proper preparation). The tradition-level "dangerous but sacred" status may encode a cultural memory of this dual nature.

**Danish folk practice (Hyldemoer):**
The Danish *Hyldemoer* (elder mother) tradition required practitioners to ask permission of the resident spirit before harvesting: "Hyldemoer, Hyldemoer, give me some of your wood, and I will give you some of mine when I grow in the forest." This ritualized harvesting practice, documented through the 19th century in rural Denmark, preserved a form of ecological respect for the elder that — if followed — would have limited over-harvesting. The ritual language implies reciprocal obligation between harvester and plant community that functions as a conservation ethic.

**Elderflower water in European pharmacy:**
Elderflower water (*Aqua Sambuci*) — distilled from fresh elder flowers — was listed in virtually every European pharmacopoeia from the 17th through early 20th centuries. Used as an eye wash for conjunctivitis, skin toner, and antipyretic decoction, it was a domestic pharmacy staple across multiple generations of European households. The commercial tradition of elderflower cordial (British summertime drink tradition) preserves this practice in non-medicinal form.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Standard preparations and doses:**
- **Sambucol or equivalent standardized extract (syrup):** 15 mL (1 tablespoon) 4× daily for acute influenza (RCT dose: Zakay-Rones 2004); 15 mL 2× daily as prophylaxis during epidemic season
- **Standardized capsules/tablets:** 600–900 mg elderberry extract/day (Tiralongo 2016: 900 mg/day for travelers); expressed as anthocyanin equivalent
- **Elderberry syrup (home preparation — MUST COOK):** Simmer ripe berries with water 30–45 minutes (destroys β-glucosidase → removes HCN risk); strain; add honey; store refrigerated <2 weeks or freeze; traditional household preparation
- **Elderflower infusion:** 5 g dried flowers per 250 mL boiling water; steep 10 minutes; 3× daily for feverish colds; sweating therapy (diaphoretic preparation — cover up after drinking to encourage perspiration)
- **Elderflower tincture (1:5, 25% EtOH):** 2–5 mL 3× daily
- **Duration for acute use:** 5–7 days for influenza; resume if new infection episode

**Safety processing requirement:**
- NEVER use raw (uncooked) berries internally; sambunigrin content → nausea, vomiting, diarrhea at minimum; large quantities → cyanide toxicity
- Commercial preparations (Sambucol, etc.) are pasteurized/heat-processed = SAFE
- Leaves, bark, unripe berries, seeds: all TOXIC even after cooking; restrict internal preparations to ripe berries (cooked) and flowers only
- Flowers: generally safe raw (sambunigrin content very low in flowers)

**CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- Autoimmune disease (SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, MS): elderberry's immunostimulatory effect may theoretically worsen autoimmune conditions; use with caution; discuss with rheumatologist/neurologist
- Immunosuppressant therapy (post-transplant): elderberry immune stimulation may theoretically work against immunosuppression; caution
- Severe influenza or COVID-19 with clinical cytokine storm: theoretical concern about elderberry's cytokine-stimulating effect worsening inflammatory cascade; insufficient evidence to definitively contraindicate, but prudence suggests avoidance in ICU-level illness
- Pregnancy: insufficient safety data; traditional diaphoretic use suggests mild systemic effects; avoid high-dose supplemental use

**Drug interactions:**
- Diuretics: additive diuretic effect (elderberry has mild diuretic property); monitor
- Immunosuppressants (cyclosporine, tacrolimus, mycophenolate): opposing mechanism; avoid combination
- Chemotherapy: immune stimulation possibly interferes with some chemotherapy protocols; discuss with oncologist
- Antidiabetic medications: chlorogenic acid component may have additive blood glucose lowering; monitor

**Comparison to antiviral pharmaceuticals:**
Elderberry (Sambucol, 4× daily for 5 days): reduced flu symptom duration by ~4 days (Zakay-Rones 2004)
Oseltamivir (Tamiflu, standard dose): reduces flu symptom duration by ~1–1.5 days (Cochrane 2014)
The elderberry effect size in the landmark RCT exceeds oseltamivir's effect size in meta-analysis. This comparison requires caveat (different trials, different populations, different strains) but represents one of the strongest phytomedicinal effect sizes for any acute infectious disease intervention in the Codex.
