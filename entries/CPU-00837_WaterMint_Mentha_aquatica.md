# Water Mint — Menta Acquatica / Marsh Mint / Wild Water Mint
## *Mentha aquatica* L. — Water Mint, Marsh Mint, Hairy Mint; native names: Latin *mentha aquatica*, Greek *hēdýosmos* (of the *mínthē* group), Arabic *na'na' mā'ī* ("water mint"), Italian *menta acquatica* | Family: Lamiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** EP, AT, DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification (Galenic/Unani):** Warm in the second degree, Dry in the second-to-third degree (*ḥārr yābis*) — a warming, opening carminative whose cool sensation on the tongue belies a heating digestive action

### Origin & Tradition

Water mint is the wild mint of ditches, riverbanks and marsh — one of the botanical parents (with *M. spicata*, spearmint) of cultivated **peppermint** (*M. × piperita*), which is their sterile hybrid. As one of the aromatic *mínthē*, it inherits the whole classical mint doctrine: a warming, opening aromatic that settles the stomach, breaks wind, sweetens the breath, and was strewn on floors to freshen a room and repel vermin. Its distinguishing feature within the genus is chemical, and it matters: *M. aquatica* is not a menthol-dominant mint. Its essential oil is typically rich in **menthofuran** and **menthone/carvone-type** ketones rather than the cooling menthol of peppermint — which both weakens its "cooling" signature and raises a specific safety note the sweeter culinary mints do not carry.

**DMM (Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica*, c. 50–70 CE):** Dioscorides treats the mints (*hēdýosmos* / *mínthē*) as warming, drying aromatics that "strengthen the stomach," stop hiccough and vomiting, dispel flatulence, and stimulate appetite; he notes their use against griping and as a general digestive and carminative. The wild marsh mints of the genus fall squarely within this description. He also records mint's reputation for scenting and for topical/antiseptic-type applications.

**AT (Avicenna, *al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb*, c. 1025 CE):** Avicenna's *na'na'* is a warm, dry aromatic: a *muqawwī al-ma'ida* (strengthener of the stomach), carminative, antiemetic, appetite-stimulating, and breath-sweetening. He notes its power to settle nausea and to "cut" thick humours in the gut — the classical carminative/stomachic profile — and, consistent with the genus's warming classification, cautions on its drying, dispersing effect in excess.

**BM (Assyrian / Mesopotamian tradition):** Aromatic mints of the Lamiaceae were part of the Mesopotamian pharmacological and culinary world, used for stomach complaints and as fragrant/strewing herbs; the cuneiform plant lists include several aromatic-herb entries in this functional space. A secure, species-level identification of *Mentha aquatica* in the tablets is not established, and no Akkadian binomial should be asserted [USER: verify against a current Mesopotamian materia medica concordance]. The defensible claim is the general Near Eastern use of aromatic mints as digestive/stomachic herbs.

**EP (Ebers Papyrus / Egyptian tradition, c. 1550 BCE):** Egyptian medicine used aromatic mints for digestive and carminative purposes and in fragrant compounds; mint appears in the general "settling the belly" and breath-freshening space. Precise species assignment to *M. aquatica* from the papyri is not possible, so the Egyptian tag denotes the genus-level digestive/aromatic tradition rather than a specific water-mint monograph [USER: verify species attribution].

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The signature doctrine of mint is the **warming–cooling paradox**, and it is one of the most elegant doctrine-to-chemistry mappings in the aromatic pharmacopoeia. Every classical authority classes mint as *warming and drying* — a heating stomachic — yet the plant produces an unmistakable sensation of **cold** in the mouth. The resolution is molecular. The cold *sensation* is a receptor illusion: menthol (and, more weakly, related monoterpenes) are agonists at **TRPM8**, the cold-and-menthol receptor on sensory nerve endings; activating TRPM8 makes the brain report "cold" with no drop in tissue temperature. The *action* — warming, opening, carminative — is the smooth-muscle and secretory pharmacology of the essential oil: relaxation of gastrointestinal smooth muscle (in part via calcium-channel modulation), stimulation of bile and gastric secretion, and local counter-irritant hyperaemia. So the tradition's paradox is literally true at two different molecular addresses: a *sensory* cooling (TRPM8) laid over a *functional* warming (secretory/motility and vasodilatory action). **The honest twist specific to water mint** is that *M. aquatica* is comparatively **menthol-poor and menthofuran/ketone-rich**, so its TRPM8 "cool" is weaker than peppermint's — the paradox is present but muted — while the menthofuran that replaces the menthol carries a mild hepatotoxicity liability (shared with its precursor pulegone) that the cooler, menthol-dominant mints largely avoid. The plant thus embodies the doctrine and its caveat simultaneously.

### Active Components

- **Menthofuran** — Furanoid monoterpene; a chemotype-defining constituent of *M. aquatica* essential oil. Contributes aroma but is the **safety-limiting** compound: a bioactivation product / metabolic relative of pulegone implicated in monoterpene hepatotoxicity via reactive-metabolite formation.
- **Menthone / isomenthone and (in some chemotypes) carvone** — Ketone monoterpenes; carminative and antimicrobial; carvone-rich chemotypes shift the oil toward a spearmint-like profile. Chemotype varies widely by population and habitat.
- **Menthol (minor / variable)** — The TRPM8 cold-receptor agonist responsible for the cooling sensation and part of the antispasmodic action; present at **lower** levels than in peppermint, hence weaker cooling.
- **1,8-cineole, limonene, β-caryophyllene** — Supporting terpenes; antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, mild spasmolytic.
- **Rosmarinic acid and hydroxycinnamic acids; flavanones/flavones** — Non-volatile polyphenols of the leaf; antioxidant and anti-inflammatory; the main actives of water-based infusions (as opposed to the essential oil).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. `[RESET]` **Carminative / stomachic after training or heavy meals.** A leaf infusion to relieve bloating, gas, and post-prandial fullness — the plant's oldest and best-supported use, working through smooth-muscle relaxation and secretory stimulation.
2. `[REFINE]` **Antispasmodic support for functional gut discomfort.** On the essential-oil spasmolytic mechanism (shared in principle with peppermint-oil use in IBS), water mint may ease cramp-type gut discomfort — though for characterised, evidence-backed antispasmodic dosing the Codex directs practitioners to standardised **peppermint oil**, whose clinical base is far larger and whose menthofuran load is controlled by pharmacopoeial limits.
3. `[RESET]` **Aromatic freshening / mild antimicrobial rinse.** As a breath-freshening and mildly antimicrobial mouth rinse or gargle, consistent with both tradition and modern antimicrobial screening of the extract.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Preparation | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|

| Culinary use | Ad libitum culinary quantities | GRAS-tier; negligible menthofuran exposure |
| Essential oil (internal) | **Not recommended for water mint** | Menthofuran/ketone content makes the neat oil a poor choice; use pharmacopoeial peppermint oil if oil-based antispasmodic action is the goal [USER: verify any commercial oil's menthofuran %] |

### Scientific Correlation

Evidence grade: **MODERATE for genus-level carminative/antspasmodic and antioxidant rationale; the menthofuran safety signal is well-characterised; note a real NULL result on gut motility.**

According to PubMed:
- de Oliveira Braga LE, da Silva GG, de Oliveira Sousa IM, de Oliveira ECS, Jorge MP, Monteiro KM, Sedano TC, Foglio MA, Ruiz ALTG (2022), "Gastrointestinal effects of *Mentha aquatica* L. essential oil," *Inflammopharmacology* 30(6):2127–2137 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10787-022-00989-x)) — the essential oil (in this sample **carvone-dominant**, 54.8 g/100 g) was potently **gastroprotective** (>90% at 75 mg/kg, via non-protein sulfhydryl and nitric-oxide modulation and reduced gastric secretion) but, importantly, **did not affect gastrointestinal motility** and did **not** protect against DSS-induced colitis. This is an honest, mixed result: it supports a stomach-settling/gastroprotective action but is a **NULL for a simple "antispasmodic slows motility" reading**, and it also shows how much the oil's chemotype (here carvone, not menthofuran) varies.
- Aitfella Lahlou R, Gonçalves AC, Bounechada M, et al. (2024), "Antioxidant, Phytochemical, and Pharmacological Properties of Algerian *Mentha aquatica* Extracts," *Antioxidants* 13(12):1512 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox13121512)) — HPLC-MS identified 57 phenolics (hydroxycinnamic acids, flavanones, flavone, isoflavonoids); extracts showed notable radical-scavenging, α-glucosidase-inhibitory (antidiabetic) and erythrocyte-protective activity, and were relatively non-cytotoxic to normal fibroblasts and dopaminergic neurons — supporting the antioxidant/anti-inflammatory value of the **water** infusion (the polyphenol fraction) as opposed to the volatile oil.
- Zárybnický T, Matoušková P, Lancošová B, Šubrt Z, Skálová L, Boušová I (2018), "Inter-Individual Variability in Acute Toxicity of R-Pulegone and R-Menthofuran in Human Liver Slices…," *International Journal of Molecular Sciences* 19(6):1805 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms19061805)) — in human liver slices, **R-menthofuran was hepatotoxic in 2 of 5 donors** (pulegone in all five, and more potently), confirming a genuine but **variable and generally milder** hepatotoxic potential for menthofuran, with marked inter-individual differences.
- Zárybnický T, Boušová I, Ambrož M, Skálová L (2017), "Hepatotoxicity of monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes," *Archives of Toxicology* 92(1):1–13 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00204-017-2062-2)) — review placing pulegone and **menthofuran** among monoterpenes with demonstrated liver toxicity via reactive-metabolite formation and oxidative stress; the mechanistic basis for restricting concentrated water-mint oil.
- Khojasteh-Bakht SC, Nelson SD, Atkins WM (1999), "Glutathione S-transferase catalyzes the isomerization of (R)-2-hydroxymenthofuran to mintlactones," *Archives of Biochemistry and Biophysics* 370(1):59–65 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1006/abbi.1999.1361)) — identifies **(R)-menthofuran as the proximate toxic metabolite of pulegone** and maps its downstream hepatic metabolism, anchoring the menthofuran caution in defined biochemistry.

Synthesis: the warming–cooling paradox is vindicated at the receptor level (TRPM8 sensory cooling vs secretory/motility warming), the carminative/gastroprotective doctrine has real support, and the antioxidant leaf infusion is well-evidenced. The doctrine that must be **deflated for water mint specifically** is any assumption of strong menthol-type cooling (it is menthol-poor and chemotype-variable) and any casual use of its concentrated essential oil (menthofuran is a defined, if variable and modest, hepatotoxicant). The genus's flagship clinical evidence — for antispasmodic action in IBS — belongs to standardised peppermint oil, not to wild water mint.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Warm (2nd degree), Dry (2nd–3rd degree); carminative (*kāsir li'l-riyāḥ*), stomachic (*muqawwī al-ma'ida*), antiemetic, appetite-stimulating, breath-sweetening. **Attested** (Avicenna, Dioscorides).
- **TCM:** *M. aquatica* is not the Chinese pharmacopoeial mint; that role belongs to *bò he* (*Mentha haplocalyx/canadensis*), classed acrid and cool, entering Lung and Liver, dispersing wind-heat. For *M. aquatica* specifically, **not attested** — do not transfer *bò he*'s classification onto it.
- **Ayurvedic:** *M. aquatica* is not a classical Ayurvedic dravya (the Ayurvedic mint is *pudina*, generally *M. spicata/arvensis*); a *rasa/guṇa/vīrya/vipāka* profile for water mint specifically is **not attested** and is not invented here.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian (EP).** Egyptian medicine used aromatic mints for settling the belly, for carminative purposes and in fragrant compounds, but the corpus is indication-organised and — as the entry states — *M. aquatica* cannot be assigned from the papyri, so the tag denotes a genus-level digestive/aromatic tradition rather than a water-mint monograph. No *heka* recitation, no *ka*/*ib* framing, and no vital-constitution status is recorded for this species. Cultivation status: **(c) absent.**

**Assyrian (AT).** Aromatic mints figured in the Mesopotamian pharmacological and culinary world as stomachic and strewing herbs, and several aromatic-herb entries occupy this functional space in the cuneiform lists. But the standing blocking warning is in force for every Assyrian substance identification, no secure species-level attestation of *Mentha aquatica* exists, and nothing assigns the plant a status touching a person's vital constitution as opposed to a digestive complaint. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides treats the mints (*hēdýosmos* / *mínthē*) as warming, drying aromatics that strengthen the stomach, stop hiccough and vomiting, dispel flatulence and stir the appetite — a clear therapeutic account, and one carrying no subtle-body system into which a cultivation status could be placed. What the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention: recognising the aromatic mint by scent and habit and knowing the warming, drying character behind its deceptively cool taste. That is sensory assay and provenance, not a vital-body doctrine. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** Avicenna's *na'na'* is a warm, dry aromatic — a *muqawwī al-ma'ida* (strengthener of the stomach), carminative, antiemetic, appetite-stimulating and breath-sweetening, with a cautionary drying, dispersing effect in excess. This is a genuine humoral-therapeutic grading, but strengthening the stomach as an organ is not a cultivation status: the strongest cultivation idea in the Unani corpus, conservation of the innate heat and radical moisture that constitute the finite vital endowment, is a frame of the whole regimen and is not a role the tradition assigns to this warming carminative, and no *mufarrih* (cardiac, *ruh*-strengthening) standing is claimed for it. **(b) framed:** attested therapeutically, with no cultivation status sourced.

**Practitioner's note.** Water mint is a digestive herb, not a cultivation substance — a leaf infusion after a heavy meal or hard session to relieve bloating and settle the stomach, and a mildly antimicrobial aromatic rinse. It stores and builds nothing. The one discipline it demands is negative and specific: keep to the leaf and culinary forms, because this menthol-poor, menthofuran-rich mint carries a real (if variable and modest) hepatotoxicity liability in its concentrated essential oil, and where oil-based antispasmodic action is genuinely wanted the Codex sends the practitioner to pharmacopoeial peppermint oil with controlled menthofuran limits.

### Contraindications

- **Menthofuran / essential-oil caution.** Do not use concentrated *M. aquatica* essential oil internally or in prolonged high doses: menthofuran is a defined (if variable and generally mild) hepatotoxicant and a relative of pulegone. Avoid entirely in liver disease. Leaf infusion and culinary use carry a far lower volatile load and are the appropriate forms.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** avoid medicinal/essential-oil doses. Pulegone/menthofuran-type monoterpenes are associated with the abortifacient, hepatotoxic pennyroyal profile; culinary quantities of leaf are generally regarded as acceptable, but concentrated preparations should be avoided [USER: verify].
- **Infants and young children:** avoid mint essential oils near the face/nose of infants (laryngospasm risk with menthol-type monoterpenes) and avoid concentrated internal use.
- **Gastro-oesophageal reflux (GERD):** mint's smooth-muscle relaxation can relax the lower oesophageal sphincter and worsen reflux in susceptible individuals.
- **Chemotype variability:** wild *M. aquatica* oil composition (menthofuran vs carvone vs menthone dominant) varies widely by population; potency and safety of any given batch of oil are unpredictable — another reason to prefer the leaf infusion or, for oil-based antispasmodic use, pharmacopoeial peppermint oil with controlled menthofuran limits.
- **Drug interactions:** high-dose polyphenol/oil intake may theoretically affect CYP-mediated drug metabolism; relevant mainly to concentrated extracts, not culinary use.
