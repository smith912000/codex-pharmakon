# Bamboo — Zhu Li (竹沥) & Zhu Ru (竹茹)

## *Bambusa tuldoides* Munro | Family: Poaceae (Gramineae)

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ, FMT

**Energetic Classification:** Cold / Sweet (Zhu Li); Slightly Cold / Sweet (Zhu Ru) — descending, phlegm-transforming, heat-clearing

**Training Phase:** Clearing & Settling — used where heat and turbid phlegm obstruct clear perception; not a builder, not a stimulant

---

### Origin & Tradition

Bamboo is the rare case of a single plant yielding **two distinct official drugs from two distinct tissues of the same culm**, prepared by two entirely different processes, and treated by the classical materia medica as separate medicines with separate indications. This is not a synonym problem. It is a genuine pharmacognostic fork, and it is the reason this entry exists.

A third bamboo product — **tabasheer / vamshalochana**, the siliceous concretion that forms inside the hollow internodes — is a separate codex entry and is *not* covered here beyond noting that it must not be confused with either drug below.

**Chinese Medicine (SBJ) — 竹沥 Zhu Li, "bamboo dripping"**

Zhu Li is the **sap driven out of the fresh, green culm by roasting**. The cultivator's method is unambiguous and physically specific: a fresh culm section is cut, both ends left open or one end tilted downward, and the mid-section heated over fire. Heat drives a yellow-brown to amber fluid out of the cut end, which is collected drop by drop. It is a fire-extracted exudate, not a decoction and not a pressed juice — and the fluid keeps badly, spoiling within days unless preserved.

Classical indication: **清热化痰 — clear heat, transform phlegm.** Zhu Li is the drug reached for in *acute, hot, obstructive* presentations — wind-stroke with phlegm blocking the orifices (中风痰迷), sudden loss of speech, convulsion in children with high fever, and the thick yellow sputum of a hot cough. Its reputation is for *penetration*: the doctrine holds that where other phlegm drugs work on the chest and stomach, Zhu Li reaches the channels and the orifices of the heart. It is characteristically paired with ginger juice (姜汁), a pairing the tradition itself explains as a corrective — Zhu Li is cold and cloying enough to injure the stomach, and the ginger both offsets that and is said to carry the sap through the channels.

**Chinese Medicine (SBJ) — 竹茹 Zhu Ru, "bamboo shavings"**

Zhu Ru is a different thing entirely: the **green outer skin is scraped away and discarded**, and the pale intermediate layer beneath it is shaved off in thin curling ribbons and dried. No fire, no exudate. Just the middle tissue of the culm wall.

Classical indication: **清热化痰, 除烦止呕 — clear heat, transform phlegm, eliminate vexation, stop vomiting.** Zhu Ru is the milder, more domestic drug. Its signature is the *rebellious stomach qi* picture: nausea, vomiting, hiccup, retching in pregnancy, and — critically — the **restless irritable insomnia (虚烦不眠)** of gallbladder-heat harassing the heart. It is the defining ingredient of *Wen Dan Tang* (温胆汤, "Warm the Gallbladder Decoction"), a formula prescribed for exactly the syndrome a Kenetic practitioner recognises immediately: a person who cannot settle, whose sleep is broken by vivid disturbing dreams, whose chest feels full, and who is nauseated by their own agitation.

Zhu Ru also appears in the composite prescription *Naozhenning granule* alongside ten other herbs, where the source lists the Zhu Ru botanical as *Phyllostachys nigra* rather than *Bambusa* — a reminder that the official Zhu Ru drug is polyspecific ([DOI: 10.1016/j.jep.2019.112247](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2019.112247), PubMed).

**Folk Medicine & Materia Traditions (FMT)**

Across Southeast Asia bamboo culm and leaf infusions appear as cooling drinks and febrifuges, and bamboo leaves are drunk as tea in Malaysia and southern China. This is a genuine, widely distributed folk usage, but it is *leaf* usage — it is adjacent to, not identical with, the Zhu Li / Zhu Ru drugs, and the codex declines to merge them.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

The doctrine here is unusually literal, and worth mapping carefully rather than dismissing.

*"Phlegm" (痰) in the Chinese system is not sputum.* It is a category of **turbid, sticky, obstructive material that blocks movement** — physically in the airway, metaphorically in the channels, and phenomenologically in the mind. "Phlegm blocking the orifices of the heart" is the classical description of a person who has gone opaque: unresponsive, muttering, not reachable. That the same word covers thick sputum and clouded consciousness is not sloppy thinking; it is a claim that both are the same *kind* of failure — a medium that has thickened until signal cannot pass.

The **fire-extraction** of Zhu Li is symbolically exact and should be read as an instruction, not a poem. The tradition takes a hollow green rod full of water and *drives the fluid out with heat*. What is collected is what fire pulls from the living tissue. A cold drug produced by fire, used to clear heat: the tradition is explicit that the process transforms the substance. Chemically this is defensible — roasting a green culm will hydrolyse and mobilise bound phenolics and partially depolymerise cell-wall hemicelluloses into shorter, water-soluble, more absorbable fragments. The "penetration" doctrine maps, at least in principle, onto molecular size.

Where the doctrine **deflates**: the claim that Zhu Li "opens the orifices" in acute stroke has no mechanistic support that survives contact with the evidence below, and should be read as a phenomenological description of the intended target, not a pharmacological claim. There is no credible reading in which a bamboo exudate reverses cerebral infarction. Anyone using this entry as a licence to treat stroke with sap is misreading both the tradition and this book.

The **silica** point deserves a caution rather than a mapping. Bamboo is genuinely a silica accumulator, and it is tempting to explain the whole heat-clearing doctrine by silica content. Resist this. The silica is overwhelmingly concentrated in the epidermis and in the tabasheer concretion — and Zhu Ru's preparation *specifically discards the green outer skin*. The tradition scraped away the most siliceous tissue. Whatever Zhu Ru does, the silica story is the wrong one.

### Active Components

Reported constituents of bamboo culm shavings and bamboo leaf, by tissue:

- **O-acetylated arabinoxylans (hemicelluloses)** — the best-characterised bamboo-shaving constituent. Two fractions isolated: BSH-1, a neutral O-acetylated-arabinoxylan (Mw ≈ 12,800 g/mol), and BSH-2, an acidic O-acetylated-(4-O-methylglucurono)-arabinoxylan (Mw ≈ 11,300 g/mol) ([DOI: 10.1631/jzus.B1500274](https://doi.org/10.1631/jzus.B1500274), PubMed).
- **Flavone C-glycosides** — orientin, homoorientin (isoorientin), vitexin, isovitexin. Concentrated in leaf ([DOI: 10.1089/jmf.2014.3189](https://doi.org/10.1089/jmf.2014.3189), PubMed).
- **Hydroxycinnamic acids** — chlorogenic, caffeic, ferulic, and *p*-coumaric acids, in both leaf and shaving extracts (same source).
- **Triterpenoids** — present in shavings at levels sufficient to support a "triterpenoid-rich extract" as a defined preparation ([DOI: 10.1016/j.fct.2004.07.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fct.2004.07.005), PubMed).
- **Total phenolics / flavonoids in *B. tuldoides* leaf specifically** — quantified alongside five other bamboo species, with TPC across species in the 2.69–12.59 mg GAE/g range and TFC 0.77–2.12 mg QE/g ([DOI: 10.3390/molecules27196458](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27196458), PubMed).
- **Silica** — well established for the genus, epidermally concentrated. Not quantified species-specifically here.

⚠️ **Congener caution.** Almost all the chemistry above is from *bamboo shavings* or *bamboo leaf* as commercial commodities, and the source papers are indexed under *Sasa* or generic *Bambusa*, not *B. tuldoides*. Only the drying-methods paper tests *B. tuldoides* by name, and it tests **leaf**, not shavings or sap. **No composition data for *B. tuldoides* Zhu Li (the sap) was retrieved in this run.** Treat the constituent list as genus-level and tissue-approximate.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Bamboo occupies the **Clearing & Settling** phase, and it is the phase's least glamorous and most reliable member.

**Zhu Ru** is the working drug for the Kenetic practitioner. Its target is the state the tradition calls *gallbladder heat harassing the heart* and which presents in practice as: unable to settle at night, mind churning, chest tight, faintly nauseated, sleep broken by dreams that are vivid and unpleasant rather than useful. This is a common and specific failure mode in intensive practice — the nervous system is up-regulated, the practitioner reads the agitation as progress, and the dream material degrades into noise. Zhu Ru, classically within Wen Dan Tang rather than alone, is the tradition's answer.

**Zhu Li** has essentially no place in routine Kenetic use. It is an acute-crisis drug in the classical system, it is unstable, its chemistry is uncharacterised, and its indications are medical emergencies that belong in a hospital. It is documented here because the codex documents what the tradition did, not because it is recommended.

Bamboo is not a psychoactive, not a dream-potentiator, and not a tonic. Nothing in the retrieved evidence or the classical record suggests otherwise. Its Kenetic value is **subtractive** — removing an obstruction to clear function rather than adding anything.

### Preparation & Dosing

**Cultivator's note.** *B. tuldoides* is a clumping (sympodial) tropical bamboo, non-invasive, hardy to roughly USDA zone 9. It grows vigorously under high nutrient supply: a one-year container trial at two NPK rates found that heavy fertilisation raised culm *number* and above-ground biomass but *reduced culm diameter*, and improved photosynthetic activity ([DOI: 10.1080/15226514.2013.810583](https://doi.org/10.1080/15226514.2013.810583), PubMed). For a medicine drawn from the culm *wall*, this is a direct trade-off: feed heavily and you get more, thinner culms; feed moderately and you get fewer, thicker-walled ones with more usable shaving tissue. Harvest culms in their second or third year — first-year culms are too soft, older culms too lignified.

**Drying matters more than most steps.** Across six bamboo species, **freeze-drying preserved the highest phenolic, flavonoid and antioxidant yield**, with the sole exception of *B. vulgaris*, where microwave-drying performed best; FRAP values differed significantly by drying method across a 6.40–36.65 mg TE/g range ([DOI: 10.3390/molecules27196458](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27196458)). For the home cultivator without a freeze-dryer, **shade-drying is the conservative choice** over sun-drying.

### Scientific Correlation

Graded honestly. **This is a data-sparse entry, and the sparsity is concentrated exactly where the doctrine is strongest.**

**Grade: D (no direct evidence) — Zhu Li (sap) for stroke, convulsion, or phlegm-obstruction.** No pharmacological, chemical, or clinical study of roasted-culm bamboo sap in any species was retrieved in this run. Not a weak study — *none*. The tradition's most dramatic claim is its least examined. State this plainly.

**Grade: C− (mechanistically suggestive, preclinical, wrong tissue) — the immunomodulation / phlegm link.** Bamboo-shaving hemicelluloses BSH, BSH-1 and BSH-2 markedly enhanced phagocytosis and nitric oxide production in RAW264.7 murine macrophages dose-dependently, and BSH/BSH-2 stimulated mouse splenocyte proliferation while BSH-1 did not ([DOI: 10.1631/jzus.B1500274](https://doi.org/10.1631/jzus.B1500274)). This is in-vitro, species-nonspecific, and — note carefully — the *stimulatory* direction sits awkwardly beside a doctrine of "clearing heat," which one would naively expect to look anti-inflammatory. The doctrine does not map cleanly. Do not pretend it does.

**Grade: C (animal evidence, genuine, but for an indication the tradition did not claim) — gut motility.** BSH-1, the O-acetylated xylan from bamboo shavings, significantly shortened defecation time and raised GI transit rate, stool production and caecal short-chain fatty acid concentration in loperamide-constipated mice over 14 days of gavage, while shifting caecal microbiota toward *Lactobacillus* and *Roseburia* and reversing colonic expression changes in motility, water/ion transport and inflammation genes ([DOI: 10.1016/j.carbpol.2021.118761](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.carbpol.2021.118761), PubMed). This is the strongest single result for any bamboo-shaving preparation. It is also **not** what Zhu Ru is classically for. Zhu Ru is an *anti-emetic* — it stops things coming up; this paper shows a prokinetic effect moving things down. A generous reading calls both "descending." A rigorous reading calls it an interesting unrelated finding.

**Grade: B− (robust in vitro, but only for antioxidant assays) — phenolic activity.** Bamboo leaf n-butanol fraction showed IC50 values of 1.04, 4.48, 5.37 and 1.12 µg/mL against DPPH•, O2•−, •OH and H2O2 respectively, outperforming bamboo leaf water extract and bamboo shavings extract ([DOI: 10.1089/jmf.2014.3189](https://doi.org/10.1089/jmf.2014.3189)). Real and reproducible — but radical-scavenging assays are a weak proxy for clinical effect, and the shavings extract was the *worst* of the three tested. The evidence favours the tissue the tradition did not make a drug from.

**Grade: B (good, and directly useful) — safety.** A triterpenoid-rich bamboo shavings extract showed an oral maximum tolerated dose >10 g/kg body weight in both rats and mice, was negative in Ames, mouse bone-marrow micronucleus and mouse sperm abnormality tests, and produced no significant differences in body weight, food consumption, haematology, clinical chemistry or organ/body-weight ratios over a 30-day rat feeding study, with no histopathological abnormality ([DOI: 10.1016/j.fct.2004.07.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fct.2004.07.005)). Separately, bamboo leaf extracts from six species including *B. tuldoides* showed no toxicity in brine shrimp lethality assay (LC50 >1000 µg/mL) ([DOI: 10.3390/molecules27196458](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27196458)).

**No human trial of Zhu Ru, Zhu Li, or *B. tuldoides* in any preparation was retrieved in this run.** The Naozhenning study is an eleven-herb formula in rats and cannot be attributed to its bamboo component.

**Net verdict:** a well-tolerated, chemically real, clinically unstudied drug. The safety data is better than the efficacy data by a wide margin — which is an honest and unusual position for a herb to be in, and worth stating as such.

### Contraindications

- **Cold-deficiency patterns.** Both drugs are cold. In a person with a cold, weak stomach — loose stools, aversion to cold, poor appetite, pale tongue — Zhu Ru will worsen the picture and Zhu Li markedly so. This is the tradition's own stated caution and it is the reason ginger-processed Zhu Ru exists.
- **Zhu Li specifically:** the classical texts warn it injures the stomach; ginger juice pairing is a standard corrective, not an optional flourish.
- **Diarrhoea / loose stool.** The strongest animal finding for bamboo shaving xylan is *prokinetic* ([DOI: 10.1016/j.carbpol.2021.118761](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.carbpol.2021.118761)). Anyone already loose should expect this to be unhelpful.
- **Pregnancy.** Zhu Ru is classically used *in* pregnancy for morning sickness and "calming the fetus." This is a strong traditional attestation with **zero modern safety data in pregnancy**. The codex does not endorse it. [USER: verify before any pregnancy-related use — this is exactly the case where traditional confidence and evidential support diverge most dangerously.]
- **Acute stroke, convulsion, high fever, or loss of consciousness — do not use this entry.** These are the classical indications for Zhu Li and they are medical emergencies. Grade D evidence does not license a home remedy in a stroke. Call emergency services.
- **Do not substitute tabasheer.** The silica concretion is a different drug with different indications and is covered elsewhere.
- **Sap spoilage.** Fresh Zhu Li has no preservative and ferments within days. Do not use a preparation of uncertain age.
- No drug-interaction data for either drug was retrieved in this run. Absence of reported interactions is not evidence of absence.
