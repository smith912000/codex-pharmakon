# Codonopsis convolvulacea — snyi.wa, Nyiwa (rGyud-bzhi); "Bell flower"

## *Codonopsis convolvulacea, the first of two binomials Clark 1995 prints for snyi.wa, and the one the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts in this row.** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark prints two binomials for it, and the row takes the first. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed names (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (lines 1061 to 1065).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads: "snyi.wa: Codonopsis convolvulacea Kurz: Fritillaria thunbergii: [has seven names and is a climber similar to Clematis. It has a thin, green leaf and a bell-shaped flower similar to blue utpala. The root has predominantly sweet taste, improves appetite and sense of smell, and cures fevers of the channels and heart.]" The square brackets are as printed. The entry that follows (*lug.mur*, line 1062, a *Phlomis*, labelled "(Leucas)" in the text) is split by a page break at line 1063 (printed page 106) and ends at line 1065 with a sentence that covers both: "sweet taste and cures lung disorders. Both Codonopsis and Leucas] cure chest fevers and common cold." So the text gives a seven-named climber with a thin green leaf and a bell-shaped flower, a root of mostly sweet taste, and four effects: it improves appetite, improves the sense of smell, cures fevers of the channels and heart, and (jointly with *lug.mur*) cures chest fevers and the common cold. Clark's name list (line 1696) also prints *snyi.wa*, with no further information. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the second name, *Fritillaria thunbergii*, is a different genus. The two are not shown to be one species, and this entry stands for the Tibetan drug *snyi.wa*.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (glossary and Explanatory Tantra).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate blocks. The phonetic names (lines 5539 to 5541) read "Ngonbu / Nheu / Nimpa Nyalo Nyangtse trae Nye shing Nyiga Nying shhosha Nyiwa". The Tibetan names (line 5546) end with "snyi ba". The scientific names (lines 5537 to 5570) end with "Codonopsis convolvulacea Bell flower" (line 5570). "Nyiwa" is the last phonetic name and *Codonopsis convolvulacea* the last scientific name, so the pairing is by position and was not printed in one row. Clark's *snyi.wa* has the same Tibetan name and the same binomial, which supports it. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a sweet-rooted climber said to improve appetite and the sense of smell and to cure fevers of the channels and heart, chest fevers and the common cold. These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for fevers of the heart or chest, which need medical care.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation and no quantity for *snyi.wa*. Clark (line 1061) gives only the plant's description, taste and effects, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text (line 3446) only the uses. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes the plant by its taste and by what it cures: fevers of the channels and heart, chest fevers and the common cold, with appetite and the sense of smell improved. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. Nothing read places *snyi.wa* in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a sweet-rooted medicine for fevers and colds in the Tibetan texts and nothing more can be said from them. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. The Tibetan texts record the drug for fevers of the heart and chest, which need medical care, and a home preparation must not replace it. The botanical identity is uncertain (two genera in Clark), so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
