# Fowl brain — Enkephalos alektoridos (ΕΓΚΕΦΑΛΟΣ ΑΛΕΚΤΟΡΙΔΟΣ), "Parts of Poultry" (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 2-53)
## *The brain of poultry, as the Osbaldeston English renders chapter 2-53, headed "Parts of Poultry"; the edition prints no species and no binomial* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The text on disk is the 2000 English edition of Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). The chapter is 2-53, headed "ENKEPHALOS ALEKTORIDOS", with the English "Parts of Poultry" (line 5145; row 243 of `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, "2-53 (line 5145)"). The Greek heading means the brain of the *alektoris*, the hen or fowl; the English text says "poultry", "cocks" and "fowls", and the edition prints no identification line. The chapter belongs to Book Two, which the author's preface (line 4904) describes as dealing with "living creatures and of honey and of milk and of animal fat". It follows 2-52 "ALEKTORIDES", also headed "Parts of Poultry", which reads in full (lines 5142-5144): "Hens cut apart and applied whilst they are yet warm help the bites of snakes, but they must be changed often." It precedes the eggs, 2-54 (line 5152), and the white of egg, 2-55.

Although the chapter's title names the brain, it covers several parts of the fowl. It reads in full (lines 5146-5151):

"Brains of poultry are given in a drink with wine to those bitten by venomous creatures, and it also stops discharges of blood from the meninx [membranes of the brain and spinal cord]. The membrane of cocks which lies in the inner part under the ventricle, (hard and clear) like a horn, and which has to have the skin taken off when it is boiled, is good for the stomach, dried, pounded to a powder and taken in a drink with wine. The broth of a chicken dressed simply (without anything else) is particularly given for restraining foul fluids, and for those who have hot burning stomachs. Broth from old cocks is given for purging the body. Having taken out the intestine [of the fowl] you must put in salt instead, sew up the fowl, and boil it in twenty pints of water until they reduce to one and a half pints, all which must be given after it has stood cooling awhile in the open air. There are some who boil sea colewort, mercury [4-191], cnicum [4-119, 4-190], or polypody [4-188] together with it. It draws out thick, raw, gluey (or sticky) black (fluids). It is good for long-enduring acute fevers, the asthmatic, the arthritic, and stomachs labouring with gas. The stomach of fowls salted and dried in the shade is the best help. Three teaspoonfuls are taken as an antidote against excessive evacuations from purging medicines as it presently stops the evacuating, but it must be pounded into powder and mixed with water and given like that."

For the brain itself, then, the chapter gives two uses, both in a single sentence: the brains "given in a drink with wine" to those bitten by venomous creatures, and the stopping of "discharges of blood from the meninx", which the edition glosses as the membranes of the brain and spinal cord. The sentence does not say whether the second effect also follows the drink, nor how many brains are used. The rest of the chapter concerns other parts: the horn-like membrane inside the cock (the English does not name the organ, and I do not identify it [USER: verify]); chicken broth; broth of old cocks; a long-boiled salted fowl; and the dried salted stomach of fowls.

The chapter gives no description of the bird, no place or season of gathering, and no assay or sign of adulteration, which Dioscorides gives for many plant and mineral materials. The English uses the plain voice for all of it; it has none of the hedging words ("said to", "they say") that he uses elsewhere in the same book for remedies he does not vouch for.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, bird brain is soft tissue rich in lipids (including cholesterol and phospholipids) with protein and water; a fowl's broth contains dissolved gelatin, salts and small amounts of fat. Nothing in the chapter links any constituent to the uses it gives.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records poultry brains in wine for venomous bites and for bleeding from the brain membranes, and then a set of poultry broths and organ preparations for stomach and bowel complaints. The Codex does not recommend any of them. A venomous bite needs emergency medical care and the right antivenom; bleeding around the brain is an emergency; and brain tissue, from any animal, can carry infection. Chicken broth as a food is ordinary nutrition and is outside the scope of this entry.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations are those quoted. Brain: "given in a drink with wine". Membrane of cocks: skin taken off while boiled, then dried, pounded to a powder and "taken in a drink with wine". Broth of a simply dressed chicken, for foul fluids and burning stomachs. Broth of old cocks, for purging. A fowl with the intestine removed and salt put in its place, sewn up and boiled "in twenty pints of water until they reduce to one and a half pints", given after cooling in the open air; some add sea colewort, mercury, cnicum or polypody. Stomach of fowls, salted and dried in the shade, with "three teaspoonfuls" taken as an antidote to excessive evacuations from purging medicines, pounded to powder and mixed with water. No quantity of brain, number of brains or length of course is given. No dose is given for the brain in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. No home remedy treats a venomous bite; treatment is emergency care and, where one exists, antivenom. Animal brain tissue can carry prions (as in bovine spongiform encephalopathy in cattle) and bacteria, and poultry carry *Salmonella* and *Campylobacter*; none of this was tested for in the ancient preparation. A clear chicken broth is a nourishing fluid with salts and some protein. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** The chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or astringent quality for the brain or for any other part of the fowl. It does print effects: the broth "draws out thick, raw, gluey (or sticky) black (fluids)", the old cock's broth "purges", and the stomach of fowls "presently stops the evacuating". Another chapter of the book prints "All fat is warming, softening and purifying" (line 5344); that is a statement about fat and is not carried to the brain.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied here. The chapter is a set of remedies for bites, bleeding and stomach and bowel complaints. It assigns the brain of the fowl no cultivation status, and no passage read connects it to *pneuma* or to any practice. The discipline of attention it shows is the cook's: how a fowl is dressed, which membrane is skinned, how long the broth is boiled down, how it is cooled; it has no provenance, season, assay or adulteration test, as he gives for many plant and mineral materials.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The chapter is a list of remedies, mostly broths and organs of the fowl, and the Codex does not recommend taking the brain. The honest use of the entry is as a record. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Venomous bites:** an emergency; seek medical care immediately. Do not rely on any drink.
- **Bleeding:** bleeding from the brain membranes (a head injury, severe headache, or confusion) is an emergency.
- **Brain tissue:** avoid eating the brain of any animal; it can carry infectious agents, including prions.
- **Poultry hygiene:** raw or undercooked poultry carries *Salmonella* and *Campylobacter*; cook thoroughly.
- **Wine as the vehicle:** not for children, in pregnancy, or in anyone who avoids alcohol.
- **Ethics:** the Codex does not recommend harming any animal for these uses.
