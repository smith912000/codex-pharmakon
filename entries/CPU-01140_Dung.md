# Dung (various animals) — Apopatos (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 2-98), "Dung — CAUTION"
## *The dung of the herd animals, goat, sheep, wild swine, ass and horse, doves, poultry, stork, vulture, mice, dog, man and the earth crocodile, as Dioscorides' chapter 2-98 gathers them in the Osbaldeston English. The edition prints no binomial for this chapter* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The text on disk is the 2000 English edition of Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). The chapter is 2-98, headed "APOPATOS" with the English "Dung — CAUTION" (line 5366; the row in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, "2-98 (line 5366)"; its text runs from line 5368 to line 5376). It sits in Book Two, which the author's preface (line 4904) describes as dealing with "living creatures and of honey and of milk and of animal fat" and the cereals and pot herbs, in the run of chapters on blood (2-97, "Blood — CAUTION"), dung (2-98), and urine (2-99, "Urine — CAUTION"). The edition prints the word "CAUTION" in the headings of these three chapters; I found no key to it in the edition's text (the other "CAUTION" heading in the file, line 6987, belongs to rue and carries its own words about allergy and overdose), so what the edition means by it here is not stated. The chapter is one long paragraph, in the order below. It is a list of dungs by animal, with a use or uses for each. The English uses "(is) thought to", "reported" and "found among secrets" in some places, and plain statements in others.

- *Cow from the herd* (line 5368): "(applied whilst fresh) lessens the inflammation of wounds. It is wrapped in leaves, warmed in hot ashes, and applied. The application of it in the same way serves as a warm pack for lessening sciatica. Applied with vinegar it dissolves hardness, scrofulous tumours [goitres], and bone inflammation." The smoke of burning dung "of a male beast of the herd restores a uterus that has fallen down, and the fumes also drive gnats away."
- *Goat* (line 5372, "The berries [dung] of goats, especially those that live on the mountains"): in a drink with wine it "cures yellow jaundice"; in a drink with spices it induces the menstrual flow and "is an abortifacient"; dried and pounded, applied in wool with frankincense it stops the flows of women, and with vinegar restrains other discharges of blood; burnt and rubbed on with vinegar, or vinegar and honey, it cures baldness; applied with swine grease it helps the gouty; boiled with vinegar or wine it is applied to the bites of snakes, creeping ulcers, erysipelas and parotitis. A method for sciatica is given in full: wool moistened in oil is laid in the hollow between the thumb and forefinger, hot pills of goat dung are laid on it one by one "until the sense of it comes through the arm to the hip and lessens the pain", and "This type of burning is called Arabic."
- *Sheep* (line 5372): applied with vinegar it heals epinyctis, corns, hanging warts and warty growths; used with a waxy ointment of rosaceum [1-53] it heals burns.
- *Wild swine* (line 5372): the dry dung in a drink with water or wine "prevents throwing up of blood, and lessens a long-enduring pain of the side"; for hernia and convulsions it is taken in vinegar; it cures dislocations used with rosaceum waxy ointment.
- *Asses and horses* (line 5372): "The dung of asses as well as horses staunches bleeding (whether used raw or burnt) mixed with vinegar." The dung of one of the drove that goes at grass, dry and steeped in wine, "considerably help[s] those bitten by scorpions".
- *Doves* (line 5372): "Doves' dung (being more hot and burning) is effective mixed with barley meal, but with vinegar it dissolves tumours [possibly goitre]. It breaks carbuncles ... pounded together with honey and hempseed and oil, and it heals burns."
- *Poultry* (line 5372): "does the same but less effectively, yet in particular it is good (taken in a drink with vinegar or wine) as an antidote against deadly mushrooms and the suffering of coli [colic]."
- *Stork* (line 5372): in a drink with water "is thought to be good for epilepsy".
- *Vulture* (line 5372): "Inhaled, the smoke of the dung of a vulture is reported to be an abortifacient."
- *Mice* (lines 5372-5376): pounded with vinegar and rubbed on it cures baldness; "Taken as a drink with frankincense and honeyed wine it expels urinary stones"; given to children as a suppository it "encourages the bowels to evacuate".
- *Dog* (line 5376): dung taken up "in the heat of the dog days [midsummer], dried, and taken in a drink with water or wine, is an astringent for the bowels."
- *Man* (line 5376): "That of men (new-made, applied as a poultice) keeps wounds from inflaming; and it closes open cuts and joins them together. Dried and rubbed on with honey it is reported to help those troubled with tonsillitis."
- *Earth crocodile* (line 5376): "good for women to colour the face and make it shine". The chapter then gives an assay for it: "The best is smallest and soon crumbled, smooth as amyl [starch] and quickly melted in liquid. Pounded, it is somewhat sour, resembling fermented dough in the smell." And it names the frauds: "They counterfeit it by feeding starlings with rice and selling their dung because it is similar. Others mix amyl [starch] or cymolia [cimolite — soft earth — hydrous silicate of alumina], colour it with anchusa, sift it finely through a thin sieve, dry it until it looks like little worms and sell it instead of this."
- *A closing note* (line 5376): "(It is found among secrets that man's dung as well as dogs' dung mixed with honey and applied to the throat is a remedy for tonsillitis.)"

Dung named as an ingredient in other chapters of Book Two, which I read: goat fat for the gouty is tempered "with the berries of a goat [i.e. with goat's dung] and saffron" (2-94, line 5344); doves' dung is one of the ingredients with pitch and rosin in the barley chapter (2-108, line 5436); and pigeons' dung is boiled in wine with flaxseed in the aira chapter (2-122, line 5539). I found no other chapter of Book Two that treats dung, but I searched the English only for the word "dung" and for "berries of a goat".

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms herbivore dung is undigested plant fibre with bacteria, water and mineral salts; it carries gut bacteria and parasite eggs from the animal that produced it. Dried goat and sheep dung is made of compacted plant fibre. Nothing more specific can be said without a source.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides lists many uses of the dung of many animals, from wounds and burns to epilepsy and stones. The Codex does not recommend any of them. Dung on a wound or burn is a direct source of infection, including tetanus; dung taken by mouth carries bacteria and parasites; and the uses as an abortifacient or to induce the menses are dangerous. The chapter's own heading carries the edition's word "CAUTION".

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations given are those quoted above: fresh dung wrapped in leaves and warmed in hot ashes; dung applied with vinegar, or with honey, or with swine grease, or with a waxy ointment; dung taken in a drink with wine, water, vinegar, spices or frankincense and honeyed wine; dung burnt, dried, pounded or applied in wool; hot pills of goat dung laid in sequence on oiled wool; smoke inhaled; and dung given as a suppository. The chapter gives no weights or measures for any of them, and the one count is "one by one" for the goat-dung pills. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Animal and human faeces carry pathogenic bacteria, viruses and parasite eggs, and tetanus spores are common in manure; applying dung to a wound or burn is dangerous, and eating it spreads disease. None of the uses listed is an established treatment. Some of the named complaints (jaundice, epilepsy, bleeding, stones, a prolapsed uterus) need medical care. Abortifacient use is dangerous. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** Almost none printed. One quality is given: doves' dung is "more hot and burning" (line 5372), and poultry dung "does the same but less effectively". The rest of the chapter prints actions and no qualities. The wider book prints qualities such as "warming" and "astringent" for other materials; those are not carried to the dung.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied here. The chapter assigns no dung a cultivation status, and no passage read connects any of it to *pneuma* or to any practice. What the chapter does offer a reader is a discipline of attention: the assay for earth-crocodile dung by size, crumbling, smoothness, melting and smell, and the list of counterfeits (starling dung from rice-fed birds, starch or cimolite coloured with anchusa). He also records, with distancing words ("thought to", "reported", "found among secrets"), uses he does not vouch for. Recording is not endorsing.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator, and the Codex does not recommend any use in the chapter. The honest use of the entry is as a record, and as an example of an author separating what he reports from what he vouches for. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Infection and tetanus:** dung on a wound, burn or ulcer is a direct source of infection; dung of any animal carries bacteria, viruses and parasites.
- **Eating or drinking dung:** a route for infection and poisoning; do not.
- **Pregnancy:** the chapter names abortifacient and menstrual uses (goat dung, vulture-dung smoke); they are dangerous and are not recommended.
- **Serious conditions:** bleeding, vomiting of blood, jaundice, epilepsy, urinary stones, a prolapsed uterus and tonsillitis need medical assessment, not dung.
- **Smoke:** inhaling smoke from burning dung is an irritant and a respiratory risk.
- **Ethics and law:** the Codex does not recommend harming any animal for these uses. The earth crocodile in the chapter is a reptile, not an identified species, and the dung of any protected species should not be collected.
