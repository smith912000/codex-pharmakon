# Rosa macrophylla — se.rgod, Saegoe (rGyud-bzhi); "Wild Rose (female type)"

## *Rosa macrophylla, the one binomial Clark 1995 prints at line 1042 for the entry "se.rgod: Wild Rose (female type)"; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints "Rosa macrophylla" in its scientific-name column at line 5727, but not against the name Saegoe* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts in this row.** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance". Here the name *se.rgod* has two entries in Clark with different binomials (see below), and the Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed names (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1042).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads: "se.rgod: Wild Rose (female type): Rosa macrophylla: [is of superior and inferior sub-types and grows on very high hills]. Its rosehip cures liver fevers and fever caused by poisoning."

Clark's preface (line 82) says "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". The bracket here holds only the two sub-types and the habitat ("very high hills"). What stands outside the brackets, as the root-text layer, is the clause "Its rosehip cures liver fevers and fever caused by poisoning." The text thus gives the part used (the rosehip) and two indications in the root layer, and no taste or power anywhere in this entry. The words "(female type)" are in round brackets, which the preface assigns to Dr. Yeshe Donden's and Dr. Pema Dorje's oral instructions and not to the commentaries.

*A second Clark entry with the same Tibetan name (line 984).* Between the heading "(5) Resins" (line 879) and the heading "(7)" (line 997), line 984 reads: "se.rgod: Wild Rose: Rosa avicularis: Rosa sericea: Rosa laevigate: [has two names. With respect to the female type, the interior of the stem is red with brown bark and red flower. The roots resemble iron hooks and the hips are reddish-green and furry. The stem of the male type is hollow, brown and without thorns]. Indian barberry and wild rose collect toxins and cure lymph disorders. [Wild rose is also recommended for use in disorders caused by spirits.]" That entry carries three other binomials, and a different action (collecting toxins and curing lymph disorders, with a bracketed commentary note on spirit-caused disorders). Clark's footnote 12 (line 1543) says the section on plants "does not appear as a separate section in the original text". The Codex does not choose between the two entries, and this file is the one for line 1042 as the batch row gives it. A third Clark entry, *Se.wahi.me.tog* (line 1033), also glossed "Wild Rose" (*Rosa roxburghii*, *Rosa sericea*, *Rosa avicularis*), has "sweet and sour tastes" and "oily power" in the commentary and cures bile disorders and wind imbalances; it is a different Tibetan name and is not part of this entry.

In chapter 21, "The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines" (heading at line 1205), line 1212 lists "Wild Rose" in the class of medicines which neutralize poisons ("... Gentiana algida, Pterocephalus hookeri, Wild Rose and the cambium (middle bark) of Indian Barberry"). It does not say which of the wild-rose entries is meant. Clark's name index prints *Rosa macrophylla* (line 1639) and *se.rgod* (line 1765), together with the other rose binomials.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (Explanatory Tantra).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The name is spelt *Saegoe* in the text and the glossary, with the Tibetan "se rgod" printed at line 5692. In chapter 20 (the colophon of that chapter is at line 3583), line 3427 reads, at its middle: "Kyerpa and Saegoe neutralized poison and cure chhuser disorders." Line 3438 reads: "Fruit of Saegoe cures hot disorder associated with poisoning and the liver." In chapter 21 (colophon at line 3689), line 3605 lists it among the group that cures poisoning ("... Pang gyen, Pang tsi dhowo, Saegoe and Kyerpai barshun are the group of medicines that cure poisoning"). The index gives "Saegoe 206,208,220". The Men-Tsee-Khang text agrees with Clark's line 1042 on the substance of the claim, a fruit that cures a hot disorder of the liver and of poisoning, and with Clark's class list on the poison group. That *Saegoe* is *se.rgod* is a reading from the sound and from the glossary, which prints "Saegoe" and "se rgod" together as a pair of entries (lines 5689 and 5692). [USER: verify]

The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate blocks. "Rosa macrophylla" stands at line 5727 beside "Syzygium cummi", with the English "Black plum" and "Rose" at lines 5729 to 5730, and the phonetic names in that block ("Sewai metok", "Seyab", "Shelta", "Shhimthikle", "Shhu khen", "Shingkun", lines 5715 to 5719) do not include *Saegoe*. No row prints *Saegoe* with *Rosa macrophylla*, and the line numbers do not show which phonetic name that binomial belongs to, so the glossary does not confirm the pairing. The Codex does not infer one. [USER: verify]

Which species the Tibetan drug is is only as stated above, and nothing is stated here about the composition of the Tibetan drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a rosehip that cures liver fevers and fever caused by poisoning (Clark line 1042, and Men-Tsee-Khang line 3438 for the fruit of *Saegoe*), a wild rose that is among the medicines which neutralize poisons (Clark line 1212, Men-Tsee-Khang line 3605), and, in another Clark entry that shares the name, a wild rose that collects toxins and cures lymph disorders (line 984). These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for fever or poisoning, and the botanical identity is uncertain.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's line 1042 prints no taste, power or other quality, either inside or outside the brackets. The Men-Tsee-Khang text prints "hot disorder" as the thing the fruit cures (line 3438), which is an indication and not a printed power. ### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes the plant by what it cures: liver fevers and fever caused by poisoning, and it sits in the poison-neutralizing class of chapter 21. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this entry places *se.rgod* in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. The one line about wild rose that touches the unseen, the bracketed note on line 984 that "wild rose is also recommended for use in disorders caused by spirits", belongs to a different entry with the same name and to the commentary layer, and it is a use against spirit-caused disorder and not a cultivation status. Clark's essence-extraction mentions near this entry (for example at lines 1043 and 1078) are for other plants, and *se.rgod* is not named there. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a fever and poison drug in the Tibetan texts, and nothing more can be said from them. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. Fever and suspected poisoning need medical care, and the Codex gives no treatment for either. The botanical identity is uncertain (two Clark entries with the same name and different binomials, and a glossary that does not print the pairing), so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
