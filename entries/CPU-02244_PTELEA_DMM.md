# PTELEA [DMM] — ptelea (Dioscorides, De materia medica 1-111)

## *A tree whose leaves, branches and bark are astringent, with a flexible bark and leaf-sprouts that bear "bladders"; the translators print "UNKNOWN" for the ancient plant and add only a "modern usage" tag, *Ptelea trifoliata* L. (hop tree, wafer ash) marked "[exotic]". Identification: unresolved. The tree is probably an elm (*Ulmus*), which is the usual reading of the Greek name, but this rests on our reading of the description and not on anything the translators print; confidence low. *Ptelea trifoliata* is a North American tree and cannot be the plant Dioscorides described* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder, "PTELEA [DMM]"; this entry replaces it. The text is the Osbaldeston and Wood English, `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The names.csv row (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 113) reads "PTELEA, 1-111 (line 4365), UNKNOWN". The name "Ptelea trifoliata" in the spine's check is the translators' "modern usage" tag, which they bracket as "exotic"; it is not their identification of Dioscorides's plant.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The chapter, in full. Book 1, chapter 111 (line 4365 to 4369): "The leaves, branches and bark of ptelea are all astringent. The leaves, pounded into small pieces with vinegar and so applied, are good for leprosy and heal wounds; but especially the bark, if it is wrapped around like a bandage, for it is flexible like a girdle. An owner [?uncia - ounce] of the thicker bark (taken in a drink with wine or cold water) expels phlegm. A decoction of the leaves or bark of the roots, applied with hot cloths, consolidates by drawing a callum [hard skin] over the fracture of a bone sooner. The moisture which is found in the bladders [undeveloped fruit] at their first sprouting clears the face when rubbed on it. The same moisture, dried, is formed into little creatures like gnats. The newly-emerged leaves are used for sauce like vegetables."

The text gives no description of the tree's shape, leaf or habitat. The details it does give are a flexible bark, a bladder-like growth at the first sprouting that holds a moisture which dries into small gnat-like creatures, and edible young leaves. In our reading, the bladders full of insect-like creatures are most naturally the leaf galls of an elm (aphid galls), which is why we put elm first; this is our inference from the wording, not a statement of either text on disk. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Leprosy and wounds (leaves with vinegar), a bark bandage, expelling phlegm (bark in wine or cold water), setting a bone fracture (decoction, hot cloths), clearing the face (the bladder moisture), and a vegetable sauce (young leaves).
- **What the text does not say.** The identity of the tree. "Leprosy" is Dioscorides's term and is not translated into a modern diagnosis here.

### Preparation & Dosing

Leaves pounded with vinegar; bark wrapped as a bandage; bark in a drink with wine or cold water; decoction of leaves or root bark applied with hot cloths; young leaves as a sauce. The one quantity is "An owner [?uncia - ounce] of the thicker bark" (line 4369); the translators' bracket marks the unit as uncertain, and the dose is quoted as printed and is not a recommendation. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Barks rich in tannins are astringent, and the inner bark of elm is a traditional demulcent in European herbal practice. *Ptelea trifoliata* is a North American shrub or small tree of the citrus family, introduced to gardens in Europe after Dioscorides's time. ### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is attributed to this tree. What the chapter offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: the parts of the tree are named separately (leaves, branches, bark, root bark, first-sprouting bladders), each with its own use, and the use of the bark as a bandage depends on a sensory test of its flexibility.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here for a cultivator beyond the habit of naming the part used. The tree is not identified, and no plant should be gathered on this entry's authority.

### Contraindications

- **Identity.** Unresolved. Do not use *Ptelea trifoliata* on the strength of this entry; it is a modern name attached by the translators and the plant is not Dioscorides's.
- **Fracture and "leprosy".** These are medical conditions; use proper care.
