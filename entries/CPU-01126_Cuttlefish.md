# Cuttlefish — SEPIA, Dioscorides De Materia Medica II.23, "Cuttlefish"
## *The cuttlefish, with its ink and its shell, as a remedy in Dioscorides. The 2000 English edition on disk prints the heading "2-23. SEPIA" and the editor's gloss "SUGGESTED: Sepia officinalis — Cuttlefish"; the binomial is that edition's suggestion and not Dioscorides' own* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The text on disk is the English edition of *De Materia Medica* in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The cuttlefish stands in Book II, in the section headed "LIVING CREATURES" (line 4910), among sea creatures and land animals. The name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` has the row "DMM, SEPIA, 2-23 (line 5015), Sepia officinalis — Cuttlefish, animal", and the source list `_sources/dioscorides.csv` (line 204) has a row "Sepia, Sepia officinalis, plant, DMM II", where the class "plant" looks like an error for an animal; the chapter text is unaffected. The chapter reads in full (lines 5015-5017):

"2-23. SEPIA. SUGGESTED: Sepia officinalis — Cuttlefish. The black (ink) of the boiled sepia is hard to digest when eaten and it softens the bowels. The shell formed into washes is good to rub on rough cheeks. Burnt in its own shell until the crusty matter is gone and afterwards pounded into small pieces it cleans vitiligines [form of leprosy], dandruff, teeth and sunspots. It is washed and mixed with eye medicines. It is good for white spots on the cornea (in the eyes) of cattle [veterinary] blown into them. It removes pterygium [membranes on eyes] pounded into small pieces with salt and applied."

Read closely, the chapter gives these items:

- **The ink ("the black").** Of the boiled sepia, the black is "hard to digest when eaten" and "softens the bowels". This is a statement about the ink as food, with a laxative effect, and no therapeutic use is attached to it.
- **The shell as a wash.** "The shell formed into washes is good to rub on rough cheeks."
- **The shell burnt and pounded.** The shell is "burnt in its own shell until the crusty matter is gone" (the sentence is as printed; the edition does not explain it) and then "pounded into small pieces", and it "cleans vitiligines [form of leprosy], dandruff, teeth and sunspots". The brackets are the editor's.
- **In eye medicines.** The shell material "is washed and mixed with eye medicines", and "blown into" the eyes of cattle it is "good for white spots on the cornea". The bracketed "[veterinary]" is the editor's.
- **For pterygium.** It "removes pterygium [membranes on eyes] pounded into small pieces with salt and applied".

Dioscorides names the cuttlefish once more in the neighbouring chapter on the sea hare (2-20, line 4997-5000), which "is similar to the little cuttlefish" and, pounded and applied, "makes any place without hair". That is a description of another animal by comparison and attaches no use to the cuttlefish. The edition's own index lists "Cuttlefish 190" and "Sepia officinalis 190", and its plate of the animal follows the chapter ("Sepia officinalis after OWEN — 1909", line 5028). I searched the English text for "cuttle" and "sepia" and found no other chapter on the cuttlefish.

No provenance, season of gathering, adulteration or assay is given in this chapter, and no quantity.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, the cuttlefish shell (cuttlebone) is mainly calcium carbonate (aragonite) in a porous structure; the ink is a suspension of melanin pigment with proteins and small molecules. The sepia flesh is protein.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides lists the cuttlefish as an external preparation of its burnt shell for the skin, the teeth and the eyes, and records the ink as a food that loosens the bowels. The Codex does not recommend any of these. Putting burnt animal shell into the eye is unsafe, a skin disease called a form of leprosy in the edition's gloss needs medical diagnosis, and nothing in the chapter supports a modern protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations in the chapter (line 5017): the ink of the boiled animal, eaten; the shell made into washes; the shell burnt in its own shell and then pounded; the pounded material washed and mixed into eye medicines; and the pounded material with salt, applied for pterygium. The text gives no quantity, no duration and no number of applications. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Cuttlebone is a source of calcium carbonate and has long been used as a calcium supplement for caged birds and as a mild abrasive; calcium carbonate is an antacid and a gentle polish. Burning calcium carbonate drives off carbon dioxide and leaves calcium oxide, which is caustic, so a "burnt" shell is not an inert powder. There is no established evidence that burnt cuttlefish shell treats pterygium, corneal opacity or vitiligo. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM):** The chapter prints no temperature, moisture or other quality for the cuttlefish. Its only quality words are about the ink, "hard to digest" and "softens the bowels", and about the action of the burnt shell, which "cleans". Where Dioscorides gives a general character for a class of animal materials, as in the chapter on fats (2-94, "All fat is warming, softening and purifying"), that statement is made of the fats and does not mention the cuttlefish. No classification of the cuttlefish is therefore supplied.

### Contraindications

- **Eyes:** do not put burnt shell or any animal-derived powder into the eye; the tradition's own eye use is not a safe modern practice.
- **Caustic residue:** shell burnt to remove its "crusty matter" can contain calcium oxide, which burns skin and mucous membranes.
- **Skin disease:** the chapter's "vitiligines [form of leprosy]" is the editor's gloss; a persistent skin lesion needs a medical diagnosis.
- **Allergy:** cephalopod flesh and ink are common allergens; shellfish-allergic people should avoid them.
- **Hygiene:** raw or poorly stored marine material can carry bacteria and parasites.
- **Ethics and conservation:** cuttlefish are commercially fished; the Codex does not recommend harvesting them for these uses.
