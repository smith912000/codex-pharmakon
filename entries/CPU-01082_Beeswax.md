# Beeswax — Keros (as printed in Dioscorides 2-105) / Wax, Ibis-of-Wax (Bryan's Ebers)
## *Wax of the honeybee (the translator of Dioscorides glosses "Beeswax"); Bryan gives only "Wax" and "Ibis-of-Wax", and files them under the mineral remedies* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP, DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The English text on disk is C. P. Bryan's 1930 *The Papyrus Ebers*, a translation with summaries (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`); the Egyptian words are not printed. The spine's list row is `_sources/ebers.csv` line 63 ("Beeswax", *Apis mellifera* (product), source "Ebers; sae.saw-leipzig.de", id uncertain: no), a secondary web source not relied on. The names row is `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` line 39: "Wax", "Bryan 1930, Ch. V Mineral Remedies (line 1211)", forms listed "Wax | Ibis-of-Wax", "Bryan's English identification". Bryan files "Wax" among the minerals; the text he prints says only "Wax", and that it was the wax of the bee is a modern identification. **Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-105, *Keros*, "Beeswax" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 5407-5413; the names row is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`). The chapter has three parts. Grade: "The best wax is a pale yellow, somewhat fat, with a sweet taste and having the scent as it were of honey, yet pure. It is usually either Pontic or Cretan. The next best is somewhat white and fat." Whitening: the clean wax is cut small, put in a new jar with sea water "taken out of the deep" and a little saltpetre sprinkled on it, and boiled; after cooling the lime and filth are scraped off and it is boiled again with fresh sea water; cakes are taken up on the bottom of a moistened jar, pierced with thread and hung, set "in the sun" by day, sprinkled with water, and "at night set under the moon until they become perfectly white"; those who want it extraordinarily white "boil it more often"; some boil it in very sharp brine and dry the cakes on thick grass in the sun, and "They advise to set about this work in the spring when the sun both lessens its intensity and yields dew, so that the wax does not melt." Use: "All wax is warming, softening and reasonably filling. It is mixed in broths for dysentery, and is swallowed down in an amount equal to ten grains of millet, as it does not allow the milk to curdle in those who suckle [breastfeeding]." The next chapter, 2-106 *Propolis*, is bee-glue and a separate material. Wax appears elsewhere in the same book as an ingredient of ointments and plasters; those passages were not read in context and are not cited here.

### Active Components

Beeswax is a mixture of esters of long-chain fatty acids and alcohols (mostly myricyl palmitate), with free fatty acids, hydrocarbons and small amounts of pigment and propolis residue; it melts at a little above 60 degrees C; it is not digested by humans and passes through the gut (general facts, not the sources). The yellow colour and honey scent come from pollen pigments and honey residues. The sources name no active component and give no quantities other than the single dose in 2-105.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The texts record wax as the base of plasters, salves and cosmetics (Egypt), as a thing swallowed in broth for dysentery (Greece), and as a modelled figure burned for fumes (Egypt). The Codex has no protocol for any of this and offers none. Wax as an ointment base is an ordinary pharmaceutical use; the dysentery use and the fumes are not safe to repeat, and dysentery needs medical care.

### Preparation & Dosing

Greek (2-105): whitened by repeated boiling in sea water with a little saltpetre, cooled, scraped and bleached in the sun and moon, or boiled in sharp brine and bleached on grass; mixed into broths for dysentery; swallowed "in an amount equal to ten grains of millet" to prevent milk curdling in the breastfeeding woman. Egyptian: boiled with onions, gruel, oil and honey and taken for four days (diarrhoea); mixed with fennel and incense (toes); cooked with oil as a plaster (ear); mixed with cow's fat and palm-fibre as a plaster (burns); mixed with olive oil, incense-cake and cyperus in fresh milk as a face application (wrinkles); burnt as an ibis figure on coals for fumes (womb). The only dose given in any source read is the Greek "ten grains of millet". No dose is given for the Egyptian prescriptions in the passages read. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Beeswax is used in candles, polishes, cosmetics and as an ointment base and a food-grade glaze; it forms a protective film on the skin, and it is mostly not absorbed by the gut. A small amount is generally harmless to swallow; large amounts can cause intestinal blockage. No effect of swallowed wax on breast milk is established. Nothing in the sources read ties wax to a healing effect on dysentery, toes, ears, burns, wrinkles or the womb. Saltpetre (potassium nitrate) in the whitening method is a general chemical agent. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** Bryan prints no quality for wax; the corpus is organised by complaint.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** "All wax is warming, softening and reasonably filling" (2-105). The grade of wax is judged by colour, fat, taste and scent.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Wax is an ingredient for a complaint (diarrhoea, toes, tremblings, burns, wounds, ears, wrinkles). Nothing says what it does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. The Ebers material is indication-organised: the identity of each named substance is a modern hypothesis, and these forms are Bryan's. The "Ibis-of-Wax" is a wax figure burned for fumes. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); whether this figure was used with a recitation is not stated in the lines read, and Bryan reports none; since he summarises, that is not proof that none existed. The reason for the ibis form is not given in the passage and no deity is attributed. [USER: verify]

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. 2-105 is a pure instance of what he does offer a cultivator: a discipline of attention. Know the origin and look of good wax (pale yellow, fat, sweet, honey-scented; Pontic or Cretan), know how it is bleached over days and nights, with sun and moon, in spring when the dew falls, and know the dose, ten grains of millet. The moon and the dew are part of his method of bleaching, and he does not claim they act on the person. The chapter assigns wax no cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do. Wax earns its place as a base for plasters and salves, the carrier of other things, and not as something to take. A cultivator can take from the entry Dioscorides's care in grading and preparing a material, and the plain observation that in the Egyptian prescriptions read wax appears mostly in plasters and salves with oils, fats, honey and incense.

### Contraindications

- **Dysentery.** Bloody diarrhoea needs medical care; swallowing wax is not a treatment.
- **Gut.** Large amounts of swallowed wax can block the intestine, particularly in children.
- **Fumes.** Burning wax figures on coals and inhaling or applying the fumes to the genitals can burn tissue; the Egyptian womb remedy is not advised.
- **Allergy.** Beeswax can carry residues of propolis, pollen and honey, to which some people are allergic.
- **Breastfeeding.** The Greek claim about milk curdling is not supported by anything read; do not use wax for this.
- **Impurity.** Wax of unknown origin can carry pesticides and adulterants; Dioscorides's own instructions are for cleaning it.
- **Dose unknown.** The only dose in the sources is Dioscorides's "ten grains of millet"; the Egyptian prescriptions give none in the passages read.
- **Ethics.** Beeswax is a hive product, and in ordinary beekeeping no animal is killed to make it (a general fact), unlike most entries in this volume. Wax from wild nests may be a different matter. [USER: verify]
