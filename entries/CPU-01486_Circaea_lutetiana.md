# Circaea lutetiana — Kirkaia (Dioscorides 3-134)

## *Circaea lutetiana (the translators' suggestion; "Common Enchanter's Nightshade")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The line lists two species, "Circaea lutetiana — Common Enchanter's Nightshade Circaea alpina — Alpine Enchanter's Nightshade", and the row takes the first.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *3-134. KIRKAIA* at line 7717, SUGGESTED line at 7718, text at lines 7719-7721.

The chapter reads: "Circea (also called diraea) has leaves similar to garden solanum nigrum [4-74], many shoots, many small black flowers, and seed similar to milium [3-158] in certain (as it were) little horns. The three or four roots are twenty centimetres long, white, sweet smelling, warming. It grows chiefly in some rocky, windy and open sunny places. As much as three pounds of the root (bruised and steeped a day and a night in three pints of sweet wine and taken as a drink for three days) cleans the womb. The seed (taken in liquids and sipped) draws down milk."

So Dioscorides describes leaves like garden black nightshade (chapter 4-74, Struchnon manikon, which is marked POISONOUS; heading at line 8675), many shoots, small black flowers and seed in little horns like millet. The three or four roots are white, sweet-smelling and "warming". The plant grows in rocky, windy, open sunny places. The root is used for cleaning the womb and the seed to "draw down milk". One other name is printed, *diraea*. The translators' index lists *Circaea lutetiana* at page 516. A plate captioned "Circaea alpina after FAGUET — 1878" stands at line 7253, away from the chapter, and shows the alpine species the translators list second.

**Overlap.** No existing entry covers this chapter. I_672 Mandrake (*Mandragora autumnalis*) states that mandrake is also "Circe's plant (*kirkaia*)". The only occurrence of *kirkaia* in the Dioscorides text on disk is the heading of 3-134, and the mandrake chapter 4-76 (line 8699) was not seen to carry the name, so I_672's statement is not supported by the Dioscorides text read here. It may come from another source. This is flagged as a possible data fix, not changed here. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. The translators' plant, *Circaea*, belongs to the willowherb family (Onagraceae), and nothing is stated here about its composition. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a warming, sweet-smelling root steeped in wine to cleanse the womb, and the seed sipped in liquids to draw down milk. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol: the identification is the translators', and the quantity printed is very large and for a gynaecological purpose.

### Preparation & Dosing

3-134 gives the dose in full: "As much as three pounds of the root (bruised and steeped a day and a night in three pints of sweet wine and taken as a drink for three days) cleans the womb." The seed is "taken in liquids and sipped" with no quantity. The pound and pint measures are the translators' renderings of ancient measures and were not checked. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes: the comparison to black nightshade and millet for recognition, the root's colour, scent and quality, the habitat, and an exact steeping regime. It assigns the plant no status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The root regimen is gynaecological and the quantity is large, so it is history and not a practice.

### Contraindications

A preparation said to clean the womb must not be used in pregnancy. The plant's identity rests on the translators' suggestion, and the leaf comparison is with a poisonous nightshade, so no wild plant should be gathered or drunk on this basis. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
