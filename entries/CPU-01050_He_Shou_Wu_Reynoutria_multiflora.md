## *Reynoutria multiflora (syn. Polygonum multiflorum) — "Mr. He's Black Hair," Fleeceflower Root, Chinese Knotweed Root*

**Tradition Tags:** CM · SBJ

**Energetic Classification (TCM):** ◈ Jing / ☿ Shen

**Training Phase:** `CYCLE-OFF` `SPECIALIST ONLY`

---

> ### ⚠ HEPATOTOXICITY WARNING — READ BEFORE USE
>
> **He Shou Wu (Polygonum multiflorum / Reynoutria multiflora) carries a well-documented risk of serious, potentially severe hepatotoxicity (liver damage) in both raw and processed forms.** Multiple international regulatory agencies — including the UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA), the Australian Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA), Health Canada, and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) — have issued safety communications, restricted sales, or required labelling warnings for products containing this herb.
>
> **Key facts:**
> - Hepatotoxicity cases range from transient liver enzyme elevation to acute liver failure requiring transplantation; rare fatalities have been reported
> - Both raw (*sheng*) and processed (*zhi*) forms carry risk; raw form is higher risk; processing reduces but does not eliminate hepatotoxic potential
> - Idiosyncratic immune-mediated mechanism suspected in many cases; predictable dose-dependent toxicity via emodin and reactive metabolites in others
> - Individual susceptibility varies widely; CYP2D6 genetic polymorphisms influence metabolic activation of toxic intermediates
> - Interactions with other hepatotoxic agents (alcohol, paracetamol/acetaminophen, statins, azole antifungals) substantially increase risk
>
> **This entry is classified `SPECIALIST ONLY`. He Shou Wu should not be self-prescribed. Use only under the supervision of a qualified practitioner with baseline and periodic liver function monitoring.**

### Origin & Tradition

Reynoutria multiflora (family Polygonaceae) is a twining vine native to central and southern China, producing a distinctive tuberous root with rich chestnut-brown bark that darkens further with processing. The name Hé Shǒu Wū — literally "Mr. He's Black Hair" — derives from a famous Tang dynasty legend in which an elderly man named He, reportedly white-haired and enfeebled, discovered this root growing around a stone and, upon consuming it, regained black hair, virility, and lived to an exceptional age. This narrative has made He Shou Wu the pre-eminent symbol of Jing restoration and anti-aging in Chinese popular and medical culture, a reputation that has driven enormous global demand in the modern supplement market and, consequently, contributed to widespread hepatotoxicity events as non-clinical consumers self-administer without awareness of its risks.

The plant's active chemistry is sophisticated and bifurcated: the processed root (*zhi he shou wu*) — prepared by steaming with black soybean broth through multiple cycles — transforms the free anthraquinone emodin (higher toxicity) into bound forms while concentrating the beneficial stilbene glycoside TSG (2,3,5,4'-tetrahydroxystilbene-2-O-β-D-glucoside), which accounts for many of the neuroprotective, SIRT1-activating, and anti-aging effects. The raw root (*sheng he shou wu*) is used primarily as a cathartic and is rarely indicated as a tonic; its use as a longevity supplement is a traditional and modern misapplication that carries disproportionate hepatotoxic risk.

**Canon of Medicine (Ibn Sina / CM):** He Shou Wu was not part of the Islamic medical tradition's direct pharmacopoeia. However, the conceptual category it represents — a root that "darkens hair, restores the blackness and moisture of youth, and strengthens the Kidney-essence that underlies vitality" — corresponds closely to Ibn Sina's category of *mukattim al-sha'r* (hair-darkening agents) and *maqawwiyat al-bah* (agents strengthening sexual vitality and vital essence). The Silk Road trade routes brought Chinese medicinal knowledge into contact with Persian physicians, and functionally analogous concepts of "essence-restoring" roots appear in both traditions even without taxonomic identification.

**Bencao Gangmu (Li Shizhen / SBJ):** Li Shizhen dedicates a substantial entry to Hé Shǒu Wū in the 1596 Bencao Gangmu, classifying it as a premier tonic for the Liver and Kidney channels. He distinguishes between the vine (*ye he shou wu*, used for insomnia and anxiety — the "Night-Intertwining Vine"), the raw root (used as a cathartic), and the processed root (used as a Jing tonic). Li Shizhen records its classical indications: premature greying, hair loss, weakness of the knees and lower back, seminal emission, leucorrhoea, and general signs of Kidney and Liver Essence depletion. He emphasises the requirement for proper processing and cautions against consumption of raw or improperly prepared root — a caution that modern clinical data has vindicated with compelling force. The processing protocol described by Li Shizhen (steaming with black soybean juice, nine times) was specifically designed to reduce the cathartic and potentially harmful components while concentrating tonic activity.

### Active Components

- **2,3,5,4'-Tetrahydroxystilbene-2-O-β-D-glucoside (TSG)** — primary stilbene glycoside; resveratrol structural analogue; SIRT1 pathway activation; neuroprotective, anti-aging, antioxidant; increased significantly in processed vs raw root; principal therapeutic compound
- **Emodin** — anthraquinone; primary hepatotoxic compound in raw root; CYP450-mediated conversion to reactive quinone intermediates causes hepatocyte DNA damage and oxidative stress; partially detoxified by traditional nine-cycle steaming with black soybean; bioavailability and toxicity both reduced in processed form
- **Resveratrol** — parent stilbene (present at low levels); SIRT1, SIRT3 activator; anti-inflammatory via NF-κB suppression; cardiovascular protective
- **Physcion and other anthraquinone derivatives** — contribute to cathartic and mild hepatotoxic load; largely transformed during processing
- **Stilbene dimers (rhapontigenin, rhaponticin)** — secondary stilbene compounds; additional antioxidant and anti-aging activity
- **Phospholipids (lecithin-type)** — support neuronal membrane integrity; complement neuroprotective TSG activity
- **Polysaccharides** — immunomodulatory fraction; contributes to immune-tonic properties of the processed root

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

> **All use cases below require SPECIALIST ONLY supervision. He Shou Wu is not a general supplement. The following describes the pharmacological potential that has made this herb medically relevant — not a recommendation for unsupervised use.**

1. **Jing restoration and biological aging deceleration** `CYCLE-OFF` `SPECIALIST ONLY` — TSG activates the SIRT1/SIRT3 longevity pathway, upregulating cellular antioxidant defences (SOD, CAT), reducing mitochondrial oxidative stress, and modulating the same deacetylase pathway that mediates caloric restriction's lifespan extension effects. In elite athletes undergoing years of intensive training — a state that accelerates cellular aging through oxidative load — supervised processed He Shou Wu protocols represent one of the deepest Jing-restoration interventions in the Chinese material medica. Relevant during extended off-season CYCLE-OFF phases.

2. **Neuroprotection and cognitive longevity** `CYCLE-OFF` `SPECIALIST ONLY` — TSG demonstrates robust neuroprotective activity: BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) upregulation, β-amyloid clearance support, and cholinergic neurotransmitter preservation in preclinical models. The Liver-nourishing action in TCM (Liver stores Blood, Blood nourishes the Sinews and the Mind) maps onto this neuroprotective and cognitive-maintaining effect with unusual coherence. Relevant for athletes in cognitively demanding sports or those managing career-transition cognitive demands.

3. **Hair loss and premature greying** `CYCLE-OFF` `SPECIALIST ONLY` — The founding clinical claim and among the best-supported traditional indications. Mechanistically attributed to improved scalp microcirculation (Liver Blood nourishment), SIRT1-mediated melanocyte preservation, and reduction of oxidative stress at the hair follicle. TCM clinically combines He Shou Wu with Eclipta prostrata (Han Lian Cao) and other Liver/Kidney tonics for this indication.

4. **Antioxidant support and recovery optimisation** `RESET` `SPECIALIST ONLY` — TSG and stilbene dimers provide substantial antioxidant activity; reduction of systemic oxidative stress markers (MDA, 8-OHdG) observed in animal aging models. The acute post-competition or post-overtraining oxidative load that athletes accumulate may theoretically benefit from supervised short-course processed He Shou Wu intervention, though the hepatotoxicity risk must be weighed against this benefit — safer alternatives (Astragalus, Rhodiola) should typically be preferred.

### Preparation & Dosing

> **The following preparatory information is provided for clinical and scholarly reference. All He Shou Wu use should be under medical supervision with baseline and periodic liver function tests (LFTs).**

**Processed Root (*Zhi He Shou Wu*) — the only form with an established tonic safety record:**
Classical decoction: 9–15 g in 500 ml water; simmer 45–60 minutes; twice daily. The nine-cycle black soybean steaming process (*jiu zheng jiu shai*) must have been completed; commercial Chinese pharmaceutical preparations carrying the *zhi* designation and GMP certification are preferred over raw imports.

**Classical Formula — Shou Wu Wan / Er Zhi Wan combination:**
Zhi He Shou Wu 15 g + Nu Zhen Zi (Ligustrum lucidum) 9 g + Han Lian Cao (Eclipta prostrata) 9 g — standard formula for Liver/Kidney Yin and Essence tonification affecting hair and aging.

**Commercial standardised extract:**
When used, should be standardised for TSG content (minimum 0.5 mg/g); maximum 500 mg/day processed extract equivalent; lower doses preferred initially for LFT monitoring. Duration must not exceed 4–8 weeks without a washout period and LFT review.

**Absolute contraindication:** Raw root (*Sheng He Shou Wu*) in tonic/longevity use. Confirmed cathartic indication only, and even then only under clinical supervision.

**Monitoring protocol (SPECIALIST ONLY):**
- Baseline LFTs before initiation
- LFTs at 2 weeks, 4 weeks
- Immediate cessation and urgent LFT if any symptoms of hepatotoxicity appear: jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, right upper quadrant pain, nausea, fatigue

### Scientific Correlation

- Wang M et al. (2015) *J Ethnopharmacol* 159:160–170 — Systematic review of Polygonum multiflorum hepatotoxicity; collated international case reports of liver injury attributed to He Shou Wu; characterised raw vs prepared form risk differential; identified emodin and related anthraquinones as principal hepatotoxic agents; CYP2D6 genetic polymorphisms identified as susceptibility modifiers; confirmed idiosyncratic immune-mediated mechanism in subset of cases alongside predictable dose-dependent toxicity

- Lin L et al. (2015) *Phytomedicine* 22:269–276 — TSG (2,3,5,4'-tetrahydroxystilbene-2-O-β-D-glucoside) characterised as primary bioactive compound of processed Polygonum multiflorum; demonstrated neuroprotective mechanisms in aging model including SIRT1 pathway activation, mitochondrial protection, and β-amyloid processing support; anti-aging activity confirmed at cellular level; concentration significantly higher in processed vs raw root, validating traditional processing rationale

- Zhang X et al. (2014) *Food Chem Toxicol* 65:363–370 — Emodin hepatotoxicity mechanism characterised: CYP1A2 and CYP3A4-mediated oxidative metabolism produces reactive quinone intermediates causing hepatocyte glutathione depletion, mitochondrial membrane potential collapse, and apoptosis; nine-cycle steaming significantly reduces free emodin content and reactive intermediate generation; provides direct mechanistic validation for the traditional processing requirement and explains why improperly processed or raw root carries substantially greater hepatotoxic risk

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Primary Tier:** ◈ Jing — Liver Blood and Kidney Jing (Essence) replenishment; the deepest level of constitutional restoration; addresses the substrate of structural vitality, reproductive capacity, and the biological basis of aging

**TCM Channel:** Liver (Gan), Kidney (Shen)

**Energetic Action:** Tonifies Liver Blood and Kidney Jing; nourishes the Essence; darkens hair and benefits the sinews and bones; (processed form) mildly astringes Essence to prevent leakage; the vine form (*ye he shou wu*, night-combining vine) additionally calms the Shen and treats insomnia — a different preparation for different indications

**Ayurvedic:** No classical Ayurvedic equivalent. Functionally most similar to Shatavari (Asparagus racemosus) for its deep Ojas-building and Rasayana properties, and to Amalaki (Phyllanthus emblica) for its antioxidant and hair-nourishing qualities. Sweet (*madhura*) and slightly bitter (*tikta*) rasas; primarily *Vata* pacifying; *Pitta* pacifying in its nourishing aspect; the hepatotoxic potential of emodin in hot, *Pitta*-dominant constitutions has an intuitive Ayurvedic parallel in the classical caution against highly acidic or pungent hepatic irritants in *Pitta Prakriti* individuals

### Contraindications

- **Liver disease of any aetiology:** Absolute contraindication — pre-existing hepatic impairment dramatically increases risk of severe liver injury
- **Concurrent hepatotoxic agents:** Do not combine with alcohol, paracetamol/acetaminophen, statins, azole antifungals, methotrexate, or other hepatotoxic pharmaceuticals; additive hepatotoxicity is well-documented
- **CYP2D6 poor metabolisers:** Genetic testing recommended before use; reduced ability to metabolise reactive emodin intermediates increases susceptibility; cannot be determined clinically without pharmacogenetic testing
- **Raw (*sheng*) form for tonic use:** Absolutely contraindicated; the raw root is a cathartic medicine with substantially higher hepatotoxicity; its use as a longevity tonic is a misapplication that accounts for the majority of severe hepatotoxicity cases
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** Contraindicated; insufficient safety data; emodin demonstrated teratogenic potential in animal models
- **Children and adolescents:** Contraindicated; hepatic metabolic capacity and susceptibility patterns not established
- **Excess Heat / Yin deficiency with strong Fire:** The tonifying properties of processed He Shou Wu are directed at Yin, Blood, and Jing deficiency; in vigorous Heat excess states, the herb is not indicated
- **Self-medication without medical supervision:** Contraindicated categorically; the severity and idiosyncratic unpredictability of hepatotoxicity make this herb unsuitable for unsupervised consumer use regardless of constitution
