# Myriophyllum spicatum — Muriophullon (Dioscorides 4-115)

## *Myriophyllum spicatum, the one name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-115; "Water Milfoil"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 9059) reads "Myriophyllum spicatum — Water Milfoil", one name and no alternative. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-115. MURIOPHULLON*, heading at line 9058, in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"). The whole chapter (line 9060) reads: "Myriophyllum has a tender little stalk growing singly from one root around which are many smooth leaves like marathrum [3-81], from which it is named. The stalk is somewhat hollow, with various colours (as it were) on purpose artificially polished. It grows in marshy places. Smeared on green or dry with vinegar this keeps the later sores of ulcers uninflamed. It is also given to drink with water and salt for falls. It is also called myllophullon, stratiotice, or achillea; the Romans call it millefolium, some, supercilium veneris, and the Gauls, beliucandas."

Dioscorides describes the plant by its single tender stalk rising from one root, by its many smooth leaves "like marathrum", and says the name comes from that likeness ("from which it is named"), by the stalk, which is somewhat hollow and coloured as though polished, and by its habitat, marshy places. The uses printed are two: "smeared on green or dry with vinegar", it "keeps the later sores of ulcers uninflamed", and "given to drink with water and salt" it is for "falls". The chapter prints other names as names only: the Greek *myllophullon*, *stratiotice* and *achillea*, the Roman *millefolium* and *supercilium veneris*, and the Gaulish *beliucandas*.

**The chapter it points to.** 3-81 *MARATHRON* (heading at line 7242) is the translators' common fennel, "Foeniculum ... Common Fennel", with the other usage "Marathrum [Bedevian] — Waterweed". Its leaf is the one to which this plant's leaves are compared. That chapter lists many uses of the fennel (milk, kidneys and bladder, snake bites, eye juice) and none of them is carried over to 4-115.

**Chapters with the same names.** Three of the names printed here, *stratiotice*, *millefolium* and *supercilium veneris*, also belong to a different plant in the same book. 4-103 *STRATIOTES CHILIOPHULLOS* (heading at line 8937) is the translators' "Stratiotes-millefolium [Fuchs] ... Achillea millefolium [Linnaeus] — Yarrow, Milfoil, Nosebleed", which is "a small little shrub twenty centimetres long (or more) with leaves similar to the feathers of a young bird" and is "excellent for an excessive discharge of blood, old and new ulcers, and for fistulas [ulcers]" (line 8940). Its SUGGESTED line also lists "Millefolium, Stratiotes, Supercilium veneris". That plant is covered by entry I_770 (yarrow). The two chapters are separate in Dioscorides, with different habitats (4-103 grows "in somewhat rough fields and especially around the ways", 4-115 in marshy places), and the Codex does not merge them. 4-102 *STRATIOTES O EN TOIS UDASIN* (line 8934) is the water soldier, a water plant that swims. A search of the entries folder found no existing entry on the chapter 4-115. The only mention of it is as a neighbouring chapter in I_1042 (*Chaerophyllum sylvestre*), which says it did not read it.

### Active Components

The composition of the plant Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, *Myriophyllum* is a genus of aquatic plants with finely divided feather-like leaves, and the species the translators suggest is a submerged water plant. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a marsh plant applied with vinegar to keep the sores of ulcers from inflaming, and drunk with water and salt for falls. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol for ulcers or for injuries from falls. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives two preparations: the plant "smeared on green or dry with vinegar", and "given to drink with water and salt". It gives no quantity for either. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Greek name has more than one plant in Dioscorides under overlapping names (see above), so a modern fact about a single water milfoil would not by itself bear on the remedies recorded. It is a general, well-established fact that a chronic ulcer or a serious injury needs medical care.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying, moistening or astringent quality and no taste for 4-115. It prints the action only ("keeps the later sores of ulcers uninflamed").

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant (a single tender stalk from one root, many smooth leaves like fennel's, a hollow stalk, marshy ground), and how it stands beside a differently-named plant that shares names with it (4-103). The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of an applied and drunk remedy for ulcers and falls. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a marsh herb applied with vinegar and drunk with salt water, with no stated quantity. The useful lesson is Dioscorides' habit of describing a plant by a leaf-likeness and a habitat, and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Ulcers and falls need medical assessment, and a home remedy must not replace it. Drinking salt water is not harmless, and the chapter gives no amount. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, and the Greek name shares names with another plant (4-103), so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
