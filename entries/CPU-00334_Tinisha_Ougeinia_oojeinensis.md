## *Ougeinia oojeinensis* (Roxb.) Hochr. (≡ *Desmodium oojeinense* (Roxb.) H.Ohashi) | Family: Fabaceae (Leguminosae)

### Origin & Tradition

**Suśruta Saṃhitā (SS) & the Salasārādi gaṇa:** Tiniśa is a classical Āyurvedic tree-dravya included by Suśruta in the **Salasārādi gaṇa** — the group of hard-heartwood, astringent-bitter trees headed by *Sāla-sāra* (*Shorea robusta* heartwood) and *Asana* (*Pterocarpus marsupium*). This *gaṇa* is the canonical grouping for **Prameha / Madhumeha (diabetic-pattern), Meda (obesity/fat), Kuṣṭha (skin disorders), Kṛmi (parasites), Kaṇḍū (itching), and Pāṇḍu (anaemia)** — a suite of Kapha-Medas disorders treated by cooling, astringent, drying heartwoods. Membership in Salasārādi is Tiniśa's principal classical identity: it is one of the "hard woods that dry the excess sweetness and moisture of the body." The heartwood and bark are the medicinal parts, used in decoction and as *kaṣāya*.

**Nomenclature and identity note:** the plant is taxonomically important to fix. Long known as *Ougeinia oojeinensis* (Roxb.) Hochr. (and older *Ougeinia dalbergioides*), it has been transferred to *Desmodium oojeinense* (Roxb.) H.Ohashi in modern legume systematics — the two binomials denote the same species. Vernacular *Sandan* / *Tinsa* refers to this central-Indian deciduous tree, valued also for its hard timber. This entry uses the given binomial *Ougeinia oojeinensis* with the *Desmodium* synonym noted.

**Folk & regional use:** across central India the bark and heartwood are used in decoctions for diabetes-pattern complaints, obesity, skin disease, diarrhoea/dysentery (astringent), and as a general *kaphaghna* astringent tonic; ethnobotanical surveys of the Garhwal Himalaya and central-Indian forests record *O. oojeinensis* among the traditionally harvested medicinal trees.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Hard Wood of the Dry Path:**

Tiniśa belongs, in the Codex, to the fraternity of **hard heartwoods** — the Salasārādi trees whose doctrinal signature is *dryness applied to excess moisture*. Where soft, unctuous, sweet *rasāyanas* build and moisten, the Salasārādi woods do the opposite work: they are dense, bitter, astringent, and cooling, and their symbolic action is **desiccation of the swampy, over-sweet, Kapha-Medas terrain** — the internal marshland that, in Āyurvedic pathology, gives rise to *prameha*. The heartwood is the tree's own most compacted, least watery tissue; the medicine is, symbolically, *the part of the tree that has already conquered its own moisture*, offered to a body that must do the same.

In Kenetica, Tiniśa sits alongside Meṣaśṛṅgī in the **Metabolic Discipline** register but with a different temperament: Gurmar is warm and craving-severing; Tiniśa is cool, hard, and *terrain-drying*. It is the quiet structural corrective — not the dramatic taste-abolisher but the slow astringent that firms and dries what has become loose and wet. Its ram-of-the-forest hardness gives it a grounding, stabilising, boundary-firming character: the medicine of **consolidation** — of tightening slack tissue and slack habits alike.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

- **Metabolic Discipline / Purification & Rebalancing:** as a Salasārādi-class *prameha-medohara* corrective — cool, astringent, drying — for Kapha-Medas excess. Complements the warm Meṣaśṛṅgī with a cooling structural action.
- **Grounding & consolidation:** the hard-heartwood signature translates to a firming, stabilising role — tightening "loose/wet" states, both tissue-level (the astringent/anti-diarrhoeal use) and, symbolically, at the level of scattered discipline.
- **Astringent support:** traditional use in loose stool/dysentery and skin (*kuṣṭha*-pattern) complaints places it in the astringent-corrective cluster.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the modern evidence for *Ougeinia oojeinensis* is **sparse and preclinical** — essentially a single relevant pharmacology study plus ethnobotanical documentation.

- An ethanolic bark extract of *Ougeinia oojeinensis* (200 mg/kg) showed significant antihyperglycaemic and hypolipidaemic activity in alloxan-induced diabetic rats — lowering blood glucose, urine glucose, triglycerides, LDL, VLDL and total cholesterol, and raising HDL — an effect attributed to steroids, tannins, alkaloids and triterpenoids in the extract (Velmurugan et al., 2011, *Medical Journal of Malaysia* [DOI unavailable — PMID 23765138]). This directly corroborates the classical Salasārādi *prameha-medohara* use, but only in a rodent model.
- The species is documented among traditionally harvested ethnomedicinal trees of the Garhwal Himalaya, confirming its living folk use though not its efficacy (Kumar, Sheikh & Bussmann, 2011, *Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine* [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-4269-7-32)).

**Evidence grade:** Preclinical / traditional. One animal antidiabetic study aligns with tradition; no human data, no isolated-compound mechanism. Treat the antidiabetic/hypolipidaemic claim as *traditionally grounded with single-study animal support*.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Rasa (taste):** Tikta (bitter), Kaṣāya (astringent)
- **Guṇa (qualities):** Laghu (light), Rūkṣa (dry), Guru-leaning density of heartwood
- **Vīrya (potency):** Śīta (cooling)
- **Vipāka (post-digestive effect):** Kaṭu (pungent)
- **Doṣa action:** Kapha-Pitta śāmaka; net Kapha-Medas reduction. Vāta may be aggravated by *rūkṣa/śīta* in excess.
- **Gaṇa membership (Suśruta):** **Salasārādi gaṇa** — the defining classical placement, indicating primary use in Prameha, Meda, Kuṣṭha, Kṛmi, Kaṇḍū, Pāṇḍu.
- **Karma (actions):** Pramehaghna (anti-diabetic-pattern), Medohara (fat-reducing), Kaphaghna, Kuṣṭhaghna (skin), Grāhī/Stambhana (astringent, anti-diarrhoeal), Kṛmighna
- **Dhātu/Srotas:** *medovaha* and *mūtravaha* srotas; astringent action on *purīṣavaha* (bowel).

### Contraindications

- **Vāta-predominant / dry / depleted states:** cooling astringent dryness can aggravate Vāta and constipation.
- **Concurrent antidiabetic therapy:** potential additive glucose-lowering (as with other prameha herbs) — supervise if combined.
- **Pregnancy/lactation:** safety data absent; avoid without practitioner guidance.
- **Standardisation gap:** because human dosing is unestablished, internal use only under qualified supervision.
- Not a substitute for medical diabetes management.
