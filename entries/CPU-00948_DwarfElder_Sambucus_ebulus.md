# Dwarf Elder — Danewort · Walwort · Blood Hilder · Χαμαιάκτη (*chamaiaktē*) · Ebulus · Khaman-e Kuchak
## *Sambucus ebulus* L. | Family: Viburnaceae (Adoxaceae; formerly Caprifoliaceae)

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Hot in the first–second degree, Dry in the second degree (Galenic/Unani); *muḥallil* (resolvent), *jālī* (abstergent), strongly *mus·hil* (purgative) — a **caustic-grade** drug, not a gentle one
**Training Phase:** Phase II — Purification & Clearing, **external application only**

---

### ⚠️ IDENTITY WARNING — THIS IS NOT ELDERFLOWER

*Sambucus ebulus* is **not** *Sambucus nigra*. It is a herbaceous, non-woody perennial (0.5–2 m, dying back each year), with erect flat-topped cymes, red-purple anthers, an acrid fetid smell when crushed, and glossy black berries that are **markedly more toxic** than those of true elder. Every year, foragers poison themselves by mistaking danewort for elderberry. The Codex treats *S. nigra* as a food-adjacent medicine and *S. ebulus* as a **hazard-class external drug**. Do not substitute one for the other under any circumstance.

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (AT).** The cuneiform therapeutic corpus prescribes a large class of counter-irritant and "drawing" plant poultices for *maškadu*-type conditions — the stiff, painful, swollen limb complaints that modern readers map loosely onto rheumatic and joint disease — bound onto the affected part with fat, beer-mash or flour. The Mesopotamian doctrinal move is consistent: swelling and stiffness are *gathered* matter, and the drug's job is to *disperse* it through the skin. Species-level identification of Akkadian plant names remains contested; *S. ebulus* is placed here as a member of the attested resolvent-poultice class of the region, not as a securely read cuneiform lexeme. **Dioscorides (DMM).** *De materia medica* treats *chamaiaktē* — the low, herbaceous elder — alongside *aktē* (true elder), and is explicit that both are **purgative of phlegm** and that the herbaceous kind is the stronger. The leaves are applied in poultice for inflammations, burns, dog-bite, gouty and hot swellings; the root is used for dropsy; and internal use of the plant (leaf-decoction, root in wine) is described as violently evacuant. Dioscorides is therefore the earliest clear witness to the two facts that structure this entry: *S. ebulus* is more potent than *S. nigra*, and its potency is **evacuant and irritant**.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM).** In the Arabic-Persian tradition dwarf elder (Persian/Arabic naming under the *khamān*/*ebulus* group) is classed as hot and dry, *muḥallil* (resolving hard swellings), *jālī*, and a powerful *mus·hil li'l-balgham* — a purger of phlegm and of watery humour. It is prescribed externally for joint pain, gout, sciatica and hard tumours (*ṣalābāt*), and internally, with caution, in dropsy. The Unani texts characteristically flag it as a drug requiring *iṣlāḥ* (correction with adjuvants) because of its harshness — the tradition itself is telling you it is dangerous.

**European folk stratum.** "Danewort" and "walwort" (from *wal*, the slain / battlefield) carry a burial-and-blood signature — the plant was said to spring where blood was shed, and its juice stains a deep blood-purple. The Balkan and Iranian folk traditions retain a robust living use: the leaves, crushed or as ointment/juice, laid on inflamed joints, burns, insect stings, eczema, and haemorrhoids.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.**

The tradition assigned this plant three qualities: *it disperses gathered matter*, *it evacuates violently*, and *it stains like blood and belongs to the dead*. Each of these maps onto real chemistry — and the mapping is unusually direct.

- **"Muḥallil / resolvent — it disperses hardness."** The leaf and flower are rich in **phenylpropanoids and hydroxycinnamic acids** (chlorogenic/caffeic-acid class), **flavonol glycosides** (rutin/quercetin-type) and in the ripe fruit **anthocyanins**. These are the classic polyphenol families of topical anti-inflammatory herbalism, and they are the plausible substrate for the antirheumatic poultice — the doctrine's "dispersal of gathered matter" is, in modern terms, the topical suppression of a local inflammatory infiltrate.
- **"Mus·hil — it purges violently."** This is *not* a benign action and the chemistry is now known. Dwarf elder contains **cyanogenic glycosides** (sambunigrin-type) and, critically, **ribosome-inactivating proteins (RIPs)** — the **ebulin** family, particularly **ebulin f** (fruit) and related type-2 lectins. Type-2 RIPs are structural relatives of **ricin**: a catalytic A-chain that depurinates the sarcin-ricin loop of 28S rRNA and irreversibly halts protein synthesis, coupled to a lectin B-chain that binds galactosides on the cell surface. When Dioscorides and the Unani physicians say the plant "purges violently," they are describing an *enterotoxin*. The violent evacuation is the clinical signature of enterocyte protein-synthesis arrest and mucosal damage — not a therapeutic bowel-mover.
- **"Walwort — plant of the slain, blood-staining, of the burial-ground."** The Codex reads this as a genuine folk-toxicological signature rather than superstition: the *name-class of the dead* attaches to a plant whose principal fruit protein is a ricin-relative. The doctrine encoded, in mythic register, exactly what the pharmacology confirms. The plant is not a food. It was never a food. The tradition *told* you that.
- **Why "external only" is doctrinally coherent.** RIPs are large proteins with poor transdermal penetration and are heat-labile; cyanogenic glycosides largely stay put in intact plant tissue applied to unbroken skin. The traditional preference for poultice, ointment and juice — and the traditional insistence that internal use is drastic and hedged — is a pre-modern, empirically-derived route-of-administration rule that modern toxicology ratifies.

### Active Components

- **Ribosome-inactivating proteins (type-2 RIPs / lectins):** **ebulin f** (fruit), ebulin l (leaf), ebulin r/nigrin-related proteins (rhizome). Ricin-family architecture. **This is the principal toxicological hazard.**
- **Cyanogenic glycosides:** sambunigrin-type, releasing HCN on hydrolysis (especially in unripe fruit, leaf, bark, seed).
- **Phenylpropanoids / hydroxycinnamic acids:** chlorogenic and caffeic acid class (leaf and flower). Caffeic acid has been used as the marker compound in *S. ebulus* extract formulation work.
- **Flavonoids:** quercetin/kaempferol glycosides, rutin.
- **Anthocyanins:** in ripe fruit (the blood-purple stain).
- **Iridoids, tannins, sterols, essential-oil traces** (the fetid note).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**External, prepared, topical only.** Dwarf elder is retained in the Codex as a **counter-irritant / resolvent poultice** for local inflammatory, rheumatic and swelling complaints, and as a folk topical for burns, stings and haemorrhoids — the indication set is stable across Assyrian, Greek, Unani and living Balkan/Iranian practice, which is why it earns an entry at all. It is **not** used internally in Kenetica. It has **no** role as a nourishing, tonic, or immune substance. Its purgative reputation is reclassified here as **toxicity**, not therapy.

### Scientific Correlation

- **Rojo et al. (2020), *Toxins* 12(9):542** — sublethal intraperitoneal **ebulin f** (2.5 mg/kg) in mice produced reversible intestinal derangement; strikingly, co-administration with green-tea polyphenols (Polyphenon 60, 30 mg/kg oral) — neither lethal alone — produced **synergistic lethal toxicity**, with ~40% body-weight loss and >50% lethality by day 10. This is a hard, specific warning: dwarf elder RIP toxicity can be *potentiated* by common polyphenol supplements. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/toxins12090542)
- **Arranz-Paraiso et al. (2025), *Toxins* 17(7):333** — ebulin-f at sublethal doses is used deliberately as a **reversible model of intestinal malabsorption** in mice, with intestinal damage peaking at day 3 and regenerating by day 22. The plant's own protein is, in current pharmacology, a *tool for inducing gut injury*. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/toxins17070333)
- **Farina et al. (2024), *Insects* 15(7):482** — chemical characterisation of *S. ebulus* leaf and flower extracts found **twelve phenylpropanoids** (hydroxycinnamic-acid dominant) and fourteen amino acids; extracts were larvicidal and acetylcholinesterase-inhibiting against mosquito vectors. Confirms the phenylpropanoid chemistry underlying the topical tradition — in an entomological, not clinical, model. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/insects15070482)
- **Areses-Huete et al. (2024), *Pharmaceutics* 16(6):705** — a *S. ebulus* ripe-fruit **hydrophilic extract** (caffeic acid as release marker) was formulated as the aqueous phase of a cannabidiol microemulsion **for topical administration** in inflammatory skin disorders. Formulation/characterisation study, no clinical outcome. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/pharmaceutics16060705)
- **Mahmoudi et al. (2014), *Eur Rev Med Pharmacol Sci* 18(22):3350–3** — methanolic *S. ebulus* and *S. nigra* extracts reduced immobility in mouse forced-swim and tail-suspension tests. Rodent behavioural screen only; **cited for completeness, not as an indication**, and it does not license internal human use. PMID 25491608 (no DOI registered in PubMed).

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani (attested):** Hot 1°–2°, Dry 2°. *Muḥallil* (resolvent of hard swellings), *jālī* (abstergent), *mus·hil li'l-balgham wa'l-mā'* (purger of phlegm and watery humour), *muḥarrik* (irritant/stimulant to the part). Flagged in the tradition as requiring correction — a *dawāʾ ḥādd*, a sharp drug.
- **Greek (Dioscoridean):** hot, drying, phlegm-purging; *stronger than true elder*; poultice for hot swellings, burns, gout, bites.
- **TCM:** not attested — *S. ebulus* is not a *Bencao* drug. No Chinese energetic assignment offered.
- **Ayurveda:** not attested for this species. No rasa/guna/virya/vipaka assigned.

### Contraindications

# ⛔ HAZARD CLASS — DWARF ELDER IS A POISONOUS PLANT

- **⛔ NEVER EAT THE RAW BERRIES.** Raw *S. ebulus* fruit causes severe gastrointestinal toxicity — violent vomiting, cramping, profuse diarrhoea, dehydration. Poisonings recur wherever the plant is mistaken for elderberry. It is **more toxic than *S. nigra***.
- **⛔ RIBOSOME-INACTIVATING PROTEINS (ebulin f / ebulin l).** Type-2 RIPs of the **ricin structural family**. They arrest protein synthesis in enterocytes; sublethal doses in mice are used *as a deliberate model of intestinal injury*. There is **no domestic preparation that can be relied on to destroy them safely**, and "it's cooked" is not a licence.
- **⛔ SYNERGISTIC LETHALITY WITH GREEN-TEA POLYPHENOLS.** Rojo et al. (2020) found that ebulin f and green-tea polyphenol concentrate, each non-lethal alone at the doses tested, were **lethal in combination** in mice. Anyone taking polyphenol supplements is at *elevated*, not reduced, risk. Do not assume "natural + natural = safe."
- **⛔ CYANOGENIC GLYCOSIDES.** Sambunigrin-type glycosides release hydrogen cyanide on enzymatic hydrolysis — highest in unripe fruit, leaf, bark, seed.
- **⛔ NO INTERNAL USE. NO EXCEPTIONS.** The historical purgative/dropsy indications are recorded as history. They are **not** endorsed and no dose is supplied.
- **Misidentification risk:** herbaceous habit, fetid crushed smell, red-purple anthers, erect flat cymes, no woody stem. If it has a woody trunk and a sweet smell, it is not this plant. If you are unsure, **do not use it**.
- **External use cautions:** irritant/vesicant potential; patch-test; intact skin only; never on mucosa, eyes, or open wounds (broken skin defeats the barrier that makes topical use survivable). Avoid entirely in pregnancy, lactation, children, and the immunocompromised.
- **Ingestion is a medical emergency.** Seek urgent care; bring plant material for identification.
