### Botanical Identity
Twining vine 2–5m; heart-shaped leaves; small, greenish-yellow flowers; native to eastern North America (Nova Scotia to Florida and Texas), also Central America. The root/rhizome is the drug material — yellowish, knotty, contorted root with starchy-bitter taste. Not the sweet yam of commerce (*D. alata*) — *D. villosa* is distinctly medicinal and not a significant food. The plant gives its name to a genus of ~650 species (*Dioscorea*) with global distribution; several Old World species are major food staples.

**The steroid hormone precursor story**: *D. villosa* contains diosgenin — the steroidal saponin that was the primary industrial starting material for synthesising progesterone, cortisone, and other steroid hormones in the 1940s–1960s. Russell Marker's discovery in 1940 that Mexican *Dioscorea* species (especially *D. mexicana*) contained diosgenin that could be converted to progesterone led to the Mexican steroid hormone industry — the industrial foundation of hormonal contraception. However: **the human body cannot convert diosgenin to progesterone**. The conversion requires a specific industrial chemical process not available in vivo. Products marketed as "natural progesterone cream" made from wild yam that claim to provide progesterone are misleading — the body cannot perform the conversion.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 North American Indigenous / Eclectic Medicine (BM)

Multiple Native American nations used *D. villosa* root:
- **Cherokee**: Root tea for nausea in pregnancy (morning sickness) and "female weakness" — a consistent specific use
- **Algonquian peoples**: Root preparations for colic, stomach cramps, and "rheumatism"
- **Mesoamerican**: Various *Dioscorea* species used for muscle spasm and inflammatory conditions

The American Eclectic physicians (19th century) codified these indigenous observations into formal medical practice. *D. villosa* was listed in the US Pharmacopoeia (1820–1882) for its antispasmodic and anti-inflammatory properties.

### 2.2 South American (SA)

South American indigenous peoples used *Dioscorea* species (multiple — *D. convolvulacea*, *D. mexicana*) for colic, digestive spasm, and "pain in the joints."

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Anti-Inflammatory (Diosgenin)

**Diosgenin**: The most pharmacologically characterised mechanism:
- Inhibits NF-κB activation → reduces TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β
- Inhibits COX-2 expression
- Inhibits the NLRP3 inflammasome (a cytosolic danger-sensing complex) → reduces IL-18 and IL-1β
- Inhibits VEGF-driven angiogenesis (relevant to both inflammation and tumour growth)

The NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition is a mechanistically significant finding — this pathway is implicated in gout, atherosclerosis, Alzheimer's disease, and inflammatory bowel disease. Diosgenin is one of the few natural products shown to inhibit NLRP3 directly.

### 5.2 Antispasmodic

**Dioscorine + saponins**: Smooth muscle relaxation through calcium channel modulation — similar mechanism to viopudial in cramp bark (I_88). The colic application is mechanistically coherent.

### 5.3 Anticancer (Emerging — Diosgenin)

**Diosgenin**:
- Induces mitochondrial apoptosis (cytochrome c release, caspase-9, caspase-3) across multiple cancer cell lines
- Inhibits Akt/mTOR signalling → reduces cancer cell proliferation
- Reduces cholesterol-rich lipid raft formation in cancer cell membranes → disrupts oncogenic signalling
- Phase I trials of diosgenin derivatives (dioscin) for hepatocellular carcinoma and leukaemia — preliminary safety data established

### 5.4 Metabolic / Anti-Diabetic

**Diosgenin + dioscin**: 
- Reduces hepatic glucose output (inhibits gluconeogenesis)
- Improves insulin sensitivity (GLUT4 translocation)
- Reduces HbA1c in animal models; human clinical data limited but mechanistically coherent

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Rheumatoid arthritis**: Limited direct RCTs for *D. villosa* specifically. Animal model evidence for diosgenin anti-inflammatory is strong. Human evidence extrapolated from traditional practice and Eclectic physician case records.

**Nausea in pregnancy**: No formal RCTs. Cherokee traditional use and Eclectic physician records are the evidence base. Physiologically, the antispasmodic mechanism (reducing GI smooth muscle spasm associated with nausea) is coherent.

**Diosgenin (isolated)**: The pharmacological research base for diosgenin is substantial — multiple in vitro and animal studies across inflammation, cancer, metabolism, and neuroprotection. Human RCT data on isolated diosgenin is limited, primarily because industrial interest is in synthetic steroid production from diosgenin rather than direct human supplementation.

**Wild yam cream (progesterone claim)**: Benster (2009, *Climacteric*): Wild yam cream did NOT increase serum progesterone levels vs placebo in postmenopausal women — confirming the in vivo conversion does not occur. The widespread marketing of "natural progesterone" wild yam cream as a progesterone source is pharmacologically false.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The discovery that changed medicine**: In 1940, American chemist Russell Marker drove into the mountains of Veracruz, Mexico, bought two large sacks of *cabeza de negro* (*D. mexicana* root) from a local market, brought them back to an improvised laboratory in Mexico City, and produced 3kg of progesterone from them — more progesterone than had existed in the world's combined pharmaceutical supply. The discovery that *Dioscorea* steroidal saponins could be converted to steroid hormones through a simple 4-step process (now called the Marker degradation) founded the Mexican pharmaceutical steroid industry (Syntex), made cortisone and progesterone affordable and mass-producible, and made the oral contraceptive pill economically viable. Marker himself never benefited financially — he left academia in 1944, moved to Mexico permanently, founded Syntex, then walked away from his stake before the company became one of the most profitable pharmaceutical enterprises of the 20th century. The wild yam root of a North American medicine plant, traded through indigenous Mexican markets, financed the hormonal contraceptive revolution.

**What the plant cannot do for you**: The irony of the wild yam story is that while *Dioscorea* diosgenin industrially produces progesterone, the body cannot perform this conversion. The millions of women who purchased wild yam cream in the 1980s–2000s believing it would provide "natural progesterone" were consuming a product with no in vivo hormonal activity (at the diosgenin level). The medicine is real (anti-inflammatory, antispasmodic, diosgenin anti-cancer) — but it is not the hormonal medicine it was marketed as. The plant that gave the world progesterone cannot give it to an individual human body through its own chemistry.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Hormone-sensitive conditions**: Despite the false progesterone marketing, diosgenin has weak oestrogenic-like activity at high doses in some studies — use with caution in ER+ breast cancer; discuss with oncologist
- **Pregnancy**: Anti-spasmodic (smooth muscle relaxation) — while Cherokee tradition used for morning sickness, avoid therapeutic doses given insufficient safety data for smooth muscle effects on uterus during pregnancy
- **Drug interactions**: Limited data; theoretically possible interactions via CYP3A4 (steroidal saponins) — monitor if on multiple medications
- **Wild yam cream for "natural progesterone"**: Do not use for progesterone replacement — confirmed no in vivo conversion; for actual bioidentical progesterone, pharmaceutical progesterone preparations are required
