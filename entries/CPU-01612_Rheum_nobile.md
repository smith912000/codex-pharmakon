# Rheum nobile — chhu.ma.rtsi, Chhuma tsi, tsi.stag, rtsi.stag.mo (rGyud-bzhi); "a type of Indian Rhubarb"

## *Rheum nobile Hook f. et Thoms, the first of three binomials Clark 1995 prints for chhu.ma.rtsi (with Oxyria digyna and Polygonum polystachyum), glossed "a type of Indian Rhubarb"; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints "Rheum nobile" and "Himalayan rhubarb" in its scientific-name and English columns for the block that holds "Chhuma tsi"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts in this row.** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and the row's binomial is the first of three on Clark's line, the other two being different plants (a mountain sorrel and a knotweed). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed names (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1079).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads: "chhu.ma.rtsi: a type of Indian Rhubarb: Rheum nobile Hook f. et Thoms: Oxyria digyna (mountain sorrel): Polygonum polystachyum: [is also known as rtsi.stag.mo. Some say it resembles a white bird on a perch, whilst others call it mkhah.hgro.la.hphug, and still others identify it as a hot tasting plant similar to lchags.tig, all of which are erroneous. It grows in muddy areas and fields. The superior or black type has a bluish-green, lanceolate leaf, red inflorescence and red petioles. The white type has long, green petioles, a sword-shaped leaf, a pink flower which leaves behind a straight seed pod, and bitter taste. Both types] purge lymph (accumulations) and oedema."

Clark's preface (line 82) says "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". The bracket closes after "Both types". So the other name *rtsi.stag.mo*, the three mistaken identifications, the habitat, the black and white types and the bitter taste are the commentary layer. What stands outside the brackets, as the root-text layer, is the closing clause: the drug "purge[s] lymph (accumulations) and oedema".
The drug is named again in chapter 21, "The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines" (heading at line 1205). Line 1225 reads: "The category of medicines inducing purgation includes (long-tipped) Chebulic myrobalan, Castor Oil seed, Cassia pods, Euphorbia sp., Physic nut (Baliospermum montanum), Euphorbia pallassii, Rheum emodi (a type of rhubarb), Lactuca dissecta (a type of larkspur), Physalis peruviana, Rheum nobile,1 Stellera chamaejasme and chhu.rtsa (Rheum spiciforme)." The footnote for chapter 21 (line 1545) reads: "1. tsi.stag is equivalent to chhu.ma.rtsi." Clark's name index prints *Rheum nobile* (line 1636) and *chhu.ma.rtsi* (line 1692).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (Explanatory Tantra).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. The name is spelt *Chhuma tsi*. In chapter 19, on taste and post-digestive taste (colophon at line 3264), line 3222 reads: "The medicinal substances like Gabur, Chhuma tsi and etc., which are endowed with two or more combined tastes, should be categorized on the basic taste." In chapter 20 (colophon at line 3583), line 3456 reads: "Chhuma tsi drains out chhuser and ascites." The index gives "Chhuma tsi 188, 210, 303". The Men-Tsee-Khang text keeps *chhuser* untranslated as a technical term (line 686 says that key technical terms, including *chhuser*, are not translated), and the Codex does not equate it with Clark's "lymph". The two texts agree that the drug drains or purges a fluid disorder (Clark: lymph accumulations and oedema; Men-Tsee-Khang: *chhuser* and ascites), and the Men-Tsee-Khang text additionally treats it as a drug of combined tastes (line 3222), which Clark's line does not say outside the commentary (where only a bitter taste is given for the white type).

The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate blocks. The block at lines 5130 to 5160 prints eight phonetic names, of which *Chhuma tsi* is the seventh (line 5132), eight Tibetan names, of which the printed line "Ice tsha chu ma rtsi" (line 5139; the print reads "Ice" for *lce*) holds the sixth and the seventh, "chu ma rtsi", eight scientific names, of which "Rheum nobile" (line 5145) is the seventh, and an English column that ends "Buttercup Himalayan rhubarb" (line 5160). The glossary does not print them on one row, so the pairing of *Chhuma tsi* with *Rheum nobile* rests on the order of the columns and not on a printed row. The Codex notes the agreement with Clark's binomial and does not state it as a printed pairing. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Rheum nobile*, *chhu.ma.rtsi*, *Chhuma tsi*, *tsi.stag* and *Himalayan rhubarb* found no existing entry on this drug. Two entries on rhubarb carry Tibetan material. I_203 (Rhubarb, *Rheum palmatum* and *R. officinale*; tags CM, DMM, SBJ) has an FMT paragraph (line 36) on *lcum-rtsa* (*chu-tsa*), and does not name *chhu.ma.rtsi* or *Rheum nobile*. I_305 (Tangut Rhubarb, *Rheum tanguticum*; tag FMT) names *chu rtsa* and *lcum rtsa* but not *chhu.ma.rtsi*. Neither is an entry on this drug. The neighbouring entry I_1175 (*Rheum emodi*, *lchum.rtsa*) is in this batch, and Clark's line 1225 lists the two drugs in the same purgative class; they are separate Tibetan names, and the Codex does not merge them.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Rheum* is a genus of the knotweed family (Polygonaceae) and that rhubarb roots are generally known to contain anthraquinones, which are laxative; Clark's other two binomials (*Oxyria*, *Polygonum*) are in the same family. Which species and which part the Tibetan drug is is only as stated above. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the Tibetan drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that purges lymph accumulations and oedema (Clark line 1079), that drains out *chhuser* and ascites (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3456), and that is placed among the purgatives (Clark line 1225). These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for oedema or any fluid disorder, and the botanical identity is uncertain.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation for *chhu.ma.rtsi* and no quantity. It appears as a single name in the plant list, in a class list of purgatives, and in the Men-Tsee-Khang passages above. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark line 1079 prints "bitter taste" for the white type inside the square brackets, so it belongs to the commentary layer. It prints no power (hot, cool, heavy or light) outside them. The Men-Tsee-Khang text groups *Chhuma tsi* among substances with "two or more combined tastes" (line 3222) and prints no individual taste in the lines read. No other quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes the plant by what it does: it purges lymph accumulations and oedema, and it sits in the purgative class of chapter 21. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this entry places *chhu.ma.rtsi* in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. The essence-extraction mentions near this entry (for example at lines 1043 and 1078) are for other plants, and *chhu.ma.rtsi* is not named there. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a purgative drug for lymph accumulations and oedema in the Tibetan texts, and nothing more can be said from them. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. Purgatives can cause dehydration and cramping, and oedema or swelling that persists needs medical care. The botanical identity is uncertain (three binomials in Clark, two of them other plants, and commentary that lists three identifications Clark's source calls erroneous), so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
