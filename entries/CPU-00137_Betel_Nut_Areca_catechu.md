### Origin & Tradition

Betel nut is among the most widely used psychoactive substances on earth, and one of the most dangerous in everyday use — a combination that makes it one of the Codex's clearest cautionary entries. The seed of the slender areca palm, *Areca catechu*, is not the betel *leaf* (that is *Piper betle*, the pepper-family vine in which the nut is wrapped); the everyday "betel quid" or *pan* combines sliced areca nut, slaked lime and the betel leaf, often with tobacco and spices. The areca nut itself is the source of the stimulant alkaloid and the principal driver of the chew's well-documented harms. Across South Asia, Southeast Asia, Taiwan, southern China and the Pacific, hundreds of millions chew it for its mild stimulant warmth, the flush and alertness it brings, and its deep social and ritual embedding.

In **Ayurveda** the nut is **Puga** (or Kramuka), and the chewing of *tambula* (the betel quid) is woven into both daily life and classical medicine — described as a digestive, breath-sweetener and post-meal custom, and used medicinally as an astringent, an agent for the mouth and teeth, and as a vermifuge. The pharmacological seriousness of the nut sits alongside its everyday domesticity in a way few substances share.

In **Chinese Medicine** the nut is **Bing Lang** (檳榔), a long-established and important drug recorded in the materia medica tradition and the *Bencao Gangmu*. Its principal clinical roles are precise: it **kills parasites** (a classic anthelmintic, especially against tapeworm), it **promotes the movement of qi** and breaks up food stagnation and abdominal distension, and it **drains downward** — moving accumulation and dampness out through the bowel. Its peel/pericarp (Da Fu Pi) is a separate, gentler drug used to move qi and reduce oedema. Bing Lang is a genuine workhorse of the parasite-and-stagnation pharmacy.

In the **Unani** tradition the nut (*Fufal*, *Supari*) is an astringent and stomachic, used for the gums, mouth and digestion. The folk and ritual chewing complex of Asia and the Pacific — the staining red spittle, the offered quid as a gesture of hospitality and ceremony — represents one of the oldest continuous mass uses of any plant drug.

But the modern verdict on habitual chewing is unambiguous and must lead, not trail, the medical reading: **areca nut is classified as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) by IARC**, and betel-quid chewing is causally linked to oral submucous fibrosis and cancers of the mouth and upper digestive tract, independent of any added tobacco. The traditions used a real and active drug; mass habitual chewing of it carries a real and severe disease burden. ### Active Components

- **Arecoline** — the principal psychoactive alkaloid of the seed; a cholinergic (muscarinic) agonist responsible for the stimulant, parasympathomimetic effects (salivation, sweating, warmth, mild euphoria/alertness, raised tone of gut and other smooth muscle). It is also the basis of the anthelmintic action (paralysing the worm's musculature). The dominant pharmacological agent.
- **Arecaidine, guvacoline, guvacine** — related minor areca alkaloids contributing to the overall effect (guvacine, for example, has GABA-uptake activity of research interest). Named compounds are real.
- **Condensed tannins / proanthocyanidins** — abundant astringent polyphenols giving the nut its puckering astringency and red-staining chemistry, and implicated, together with the alkaloids and the lime, in the mucosal damage of chronic chewing.
- **Catechins / flavonoids** — additional polyphenols of the seed.
- **General class:** pyridine-type alkaloids (arecoline and congeners) + condensed tannins. The alkaloids drive the stimulant/anthelmintic action; the tannins and the alkaloid–lime combination drive much of the mucosal toxicity.

### Contraindications

- **Habitual chewing — strongly discouraged.** A Group-1 human carcinogen in chewed form; causes oral submucous fibrosis, oral and upper-digestive cancers, and dependence. This is the headline contraindication.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding — avoid.** Areca-nut use in pregnancy is associated with adverse outcomes; cholinergic alkaloid exposure is inappropriate.
- **Cardiac disease / arrhythmia.** Arecoline's cholinergic action can affect heart rate and rhythm; caution in cardiac disease.
- **Asthma / bronchospasm.** Cholinergic (muscarinic) stimulation can provoke bronchoconstriction.
- **Peptic ulcer / GI conditions.** Cholinergic stimulation increases gut motility and secretion; caution.
- **Drug interactions.** May interact with cholinergic and anticholinergic drugs and with other cardioactive agents. [USER: verify specific interactions before practical statement.]
- **General:** acute overdose is a cholinergic-crisis emergency; chronic use a progressive oral-disease risk. Both require medical care.
