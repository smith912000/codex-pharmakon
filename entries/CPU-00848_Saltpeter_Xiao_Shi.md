# Saltpeter — Xiao Shi (Mirabilitum) (Yang 1998) / nitron, nitrou aphros, "Saltpetre — Potassium Nitrate" (Dioscorides) / Saltpetre (Bryan 1930) / Yavakshara (Bhishagratna)
## *Saltpetre in the traditions' own lists, potassium nitrate in the translators' glosses; Yang's Latin for Xiao Shi is "Mirabilitum", which is not the same salt, and Bhishagratna glosses Yavakshara two ways* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** Bryan's Chapter V list (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` lines 1200-1202; `extract/names.csv` row, line 1200) gives "Saltpetre", "Saltpetre-from-the-South" and "Saltpetre-from-Upper-Egypt". Bryan notes of the mineral class that the source of origin of the drugs "is strictly laid down" (line 1137). In his prose it appears as an adjuvant: "a poultice of Black Knife-stone with Fat, Honey, and Saltpetre" for pains in the bones, which Bryan calls "entirely fallacious" (lines 2119-2120); "Saltpetre-from-Upper-Egypt" with red-lead, wood from Arabia, iron, calamine, ostrich egg, sulphur and honey in a remedy "to drive out pterygium" (lines 2846-2850); "Saltpetre-from-the-South" with bullock's fat, wine yeast, garlic, poppy-berries and myrrh oil as a liniment "to make the met supple" (line 3170); and "Saltpetre" with a teun-plant and wasp's blood in a remedy for a growth in the neck (lines 3803-3807). That is Bryan's paraphrase, not the hieratic. The identification as potassium nitrate is the modern hypothesis; `_sources/ebers.csv` has no saltpetre row, so the Bryan `names.csv` row is the only list entry.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Two chapters in Book 5 (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). 5-130 NITRON, "Saltpetre — Potassium Nitrate" (line 10507): "Nitre is preferred which is smooth and rosy or white in colour, with holes through as though it were some spongy thing, like that which is brought from Buni. It has an ability to fetch fluids from the depths." 5-131 NITROU APHROS, "Potassium Nitrate — Saltpetre — Spuma Nitri" (line 10515): the best is "the lightest — crusty, brittle, and drawing to a purple, frothy, and moreover biting", from Philadelphia in Lydia, then Egypt, and it is "found in Magnesia which is in Caria, and both this and nitre [above] have a similar burning strength, like salt". Its uses include: pounded with cumin and taken with honey water (or sapa, or carminatives like rue or dill) it "soothes griping"; "a fit ointment for those troubled with recurrent fevers"; infused it "heals gaseousness, as well as noises in the ears and their suppurations"; with ass or swine grease it "heals dog bites"; with figs "a poultice for dropsy"; and taken with posca "for the ingestion of mushrooms [antidote]". Chapters 5-92, 5-102 and 5-119 also name saltpetre as an ingredient or as a salt "weak raw, but more effective burnt". The identification of *nitron* with potassium nitrate is the translator's gloss; whether the Greek word means natron or saltpetre is a question for the scholarship, which was not read here. **Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** Xiao Shi (Mirabilitum), "Jades and Stones: Superior Class", line 968 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`; `names.csv` row): "bitter and cold. **Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *Yavakshara* is the candidate. In the saline group of *Sutrasthana* ch.42 the list is "Saindhaba, Sauvarchala, Vida, Fakya, Romaka, Samudraka, Paktrima, Yavakshara (nitrate of potash), Ushara and Suvarchika" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch42.txt` lines 316-321). They are anti-spermatopoietic" (lines 2088-2101). *Yavakshara* is also in the *Shodhana Varti* (ch.36 line 99). `_sources/sushruta.csv` has no saltpetre row. ### Active Components

Saltpetre is potassium nitrate, KNO3, a white, water-soluble crystalline salt and a strong oxidiser, which forms naturally as an efflorescence in some soils, caves and old walls. Its modern uses are fertiliser, food curing, and gunpowder. It differs from natron and soda (sodium carbonate), from Glauber's salt (sodium sulphate; the "mirabilite" of Yang's Latin), and from potash (potassium carbonate), which are all salts that the older lists may have meant by the same names.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. In the sources saltpetre is an adjuvant in poultices and liniments (Egypt), a cleansing, drawing and griping remedy and mushroom antidote (Greek), a cathartic-type drug of the highest Chinese grade (Yang's rendering), and an alkali-group digestant (Sanskrit). None supports taking it for health. The Codex offers no protocol, dose or preparation.

### Preparation & Dosing

Only preparations the texts give. DMM 5-131: pounded with cumin and taken in honey water, sapa or a carminative infusion for griping; infused in warm water or wine for gaseousness; with fat as a dressing; "Some burn these salts, setting them on burning coals (a new clay jar first placed under) until completely burnt." SBJ: Xiao Shi "may be sublimated into a paste". SS: no preparation of *Yavakshara* is given in the lines read. EP: in poultices and liniments, no amounts given in the lines read. ### Scientific Correlation

Potassium nitrate is an oxidising salt; in the body, nitrates are reduced to nitrites, which can cause methaemoglobinaemia in excess, and potassium nitrate was formerly used as a diuretic. No clinical studies were retrieved for the traditional uses. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. The saltpetre in Bryan's lists is a named adjuvant, with a sourcing instruction (from Upper Egypt, from the South) and no status. The identification is a modern hypothesis.

**Assyrian (AT).** *(c) Absent, and the attestation rests on a list row.* Nothing was read. The blocking warning is in force. [USER: verify]

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, thinly.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. What the two chapters offer a cultivator is the discipline of attention: how to choose the stone (smooth, rosy or white, holey like a sponge), which spuma is best (the lightest, crusty, brittle, from Philadelphia in Lydia), that the salt is weak raw and better burnt, and the antidote use.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent, and the attestation rests on a spine row.* No Avicenna text or list row was found. Innate heat and radical moisture cannot be tied to saltpetre without a source and are not tied to it here. [USER: verify]

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: Superior Class.* Xiao Shi stands under the heading "Jades and Stones: Superior Class". The preface (lines 352-355) says of that class that its members "mainly nourish life and correspond to heaven. They are nontoxic and taking them in large amounts and for a long time will not harm people", and (lines 383-384) that "If one intends to make one's body light, boost the qi, prevent aging, and prolong life, one should base [one's efforts] on the superior class". The entry's own wording is "Protracted taking may make the body light". "Make the body light" (*qing shen*) is the text's term; it has no pharmacological equivalent and is not weight loss. The grade is assigned to Yang's Xiao Shi as printed, not to potassium nitrate, and Yang's own note 41 doubts the juxtaposition of this entry and the next; the Codex records the grade without endorsing the claim.

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed, thinly.* *Yavakshara* is claimed by *dravyaguna* (the saline and alkali groups) and by wound practice (the *Shodhana Varti*). It is not named in the *rasayana* or *vajikarana* chapters searched (*Chikitsasthana* ch.26-30), and no *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* claim is printed for it. Its identity as saltpetre rests on one of two conflicting glosses.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here for a cultivator to take. Saltpetre is, in these sources, an adjuvant, a drawing and cleansing salt, and in the Chinese list a superior-class entry whose line the translator himself suspects. The honest cultivator's use is attention: the Greek text's way of choosing and testing the salt is the only discipline on offer. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Potassium nitrate is a strong oxidiser, and a gut and kidney irritant in quantity; large doses are poisonous. The older lists may mean other salts (natron, mirabilite, potash) under the same name, with their own risks. It must not be heated or stored with combustible material. The Chinese superior-class "nontoxic" wording is the text's claim and is not a safety guarantee. Ritual and spoken uses are ritual, not therapy.
