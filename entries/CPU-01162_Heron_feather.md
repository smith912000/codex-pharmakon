# Heron feather — rGyud-bzhi, so.bya (Clark's "Heron"), feathers, under "Hair"
## *Feathers of a water bird; Clark prints the Tibetan name so.bya, the English "Heron", and two Latin names (Larus canus kamtschatschensis, Phalacrocorax carbo sinensis)* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The heron feather is listed in the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, the chapter on the efficacy of medicinal substances, in Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; the entry is also in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, which lists it at line 1194 of Clark and, mistakenly, with class "plant"). It is in section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), whose thirteen headings include "hair" (line 1148). Under "Hair" (line 1192) Clark prints (line 1194): "so.bya: Heron: *Larus canus Kamtschatschensis*: *Phalacrocorax carbo sinensis*: [has fourteen names]. Its feathers relieve urinary retention [and dispel spirits]." The square brackets mark commentary interpolations from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries (Clark, line 82): "has fourteen names" and "and dispel spirits" are commentary material, and "relieve urinary retention" is the root-text wording as Clark gives it.

The Latin names Clark gives are not those of a heron. *Larus canus* is the common gull and *Phalacrocorax carbo* the great cormorant. Clark says in the introduction (line 83) that more than one scientific name is often given because the identification of a Tibetan name is uncertain. So the bird that so.bya denotes is not settled by the text read. The same Latin pair appears in Clark's list of meats (line 672), where "heron (*Larus canus kamtschatschensis* or *Phalacrocorax carbo sinensis*)" is among "(i) Animals which live in wet places", with "crane, swan, red wild duck (sheldrake), ... bittern, otter and fish". The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, lines 3557-3558) has, in the corresponding hair-and-feather passage: "A peacock's crown feather cures poisoning and lung abscesses. A cormorant's feather cures obstruction of urine. Owl feather cures ascites." The file's OCR prints "cormarant"; the sense is cormorant. So that translation renders the bird as "cormorant", where Clark renders "Heron", with the same indication (urinary obstruction). The same file's list of aquatic animals (line 2919) names both "cormorant" and "heron" separately. The Tibetan wording is not in the files read, so which English rendering is right cannot be decided here. [USER: verify]

Clark's introduction notes that "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57).

### Active Components

A feather is made of keratin, a tough structural protein rich in cysteine, arranged as a central shaft with barbs. Burning a feather chars the protein. No composition specific to this entry was read, and no preparation is given that would indicate whether the feather was burnt, powdered or used whole. [USER: verify] Wild birds' feathers can also carry feather mites, bacteria and fungi, and, in waterbirds, avian influenza virus.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is one indication in a materia medica list: the feathers "relieve urinary retention". The Codex gives no protocol. Urinary retention (being unable to pass urine) is a medical emergency or a sign of an underlying problem that needs prompt assessment, and no source read supports the claim. Waterbirds such as gulls, cormorants and herons are wild birds that are legally protected in many places, and collecting their feathers can be unlawful.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation, route or dose is given in the sources retrieved: the Clark and Men-Tsee-Khang lines state the indication only. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Keratin is a structural protein; swallowed or applied feather material does not act on the bladder or urethra. Urinary retention has many causes (for example prostate enlargement, stones, nerve injury, medicines) that need medical assessment. Wild waterbirds can carry avian influenza and other zoonotic pathogens. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The Clark line (1194) gives an indication only. No taste, potency, post-digestive taste or humoral action is printed for the feather in the sources retrieved. Clark's general statements on meats (lines 672-673) concern flesh, and are not applied to feathers. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* Neither translation read assigns the feather any cultivation framing. Clark's commentary bracket attaches the feather to "dispel spirits", which is a protective or exorcistic use rather than a cultivation status, and it comes from a commentary and not from the root-text wording; the Men-Tsee-Khang rendering of the same passage does not carry it. The frame around it is the tradition's own: the opening invocation in Clark (lines 100-102) praises the Master of Medicine who "dispels the three poisons", and the project's cultivation spec records that the *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) are derived from the three poisons and that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root. Those points come from the spec and not from any line about the feather. No source read attaches the feather to *bcud len*, and there is no formal Tibetan category of "substances for subtle-body practice".

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a one-line indication in a list of animal medicines that the translator says is little used now, the identity of the bird is uncertain between two translations, and no practice is attached. The "dispel spirits" gloss is a commentary's addition and is recorded, not endorsed.

### Contraindications

- **Urinary retention:** needs prompt medical assessment; do not rely on this line.
- **Infection:** wild-bird feathers can carry bacteria, fungi, mites and avian influenza virus.
- **Legal and conservation status:** gulls, cormorants and herons are protected wild birds in many jurisdictions, and collecting feathers, or the birds, may be an offence. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** killing a wild bird for a substance with no supported benefit is hard to justify; Clark's omens section (line 431) values saving animals from slaughter, though that passage concerns merit and omens, not medicine.
- **Identity uncertainty:** the bird named is not settled (heron, gull or cormorant, depending on the translation).
