# Hypericum coris — Koris (Dioscorides 3-174); "Heath-leaved St John's Wort, Coris"

## *Hypericum coris, the first of two identifications on the translators' SUGGESTED line for chapter 3-174 (the second is Coris monspeliensis / Symphytum petraeum, "Montpellier Coris"), glossed "Heath-leaved St John's Wort, Coris"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk names this plant under this name. The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The SUGGESTED line carries two different plants and the entry takes the first. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *3-174. KORIS* at line 7972, SUGGESTED lines at 7973 and 7974, the cross-reference "see 3-88, 4-9" at line 7975, text at line 7976. The chapter stands in Book Three under the running head "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants", after 3-173 *ANDROSAIMON* and before 3-175 *CHAMAIPITUS*, both different chapters. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 580.

The whole chapter reads: "Coris has a leaf similar to erica but smaller, fatter and red. It is a shrub twenty centimetres high, pleasanttasting, sharp, and with a good smell. A decoction of the seed of this (taken as a drink with wine) induces the passage of urine and the menstrual flow. A decoction (taken as a drink with pepper) helps those bitten by harvest spiders, or with sciatica, tetanus, or chills. Used with oil it is also an effective ointment for severe spasms. The root of this (boiled with wine and taken as a drink) is thought to help those who faint often. It is necessary for the patient when drinking it to be well covered all over for it causes the whole body to sweat, and from this one recovers agility. Some also call this hypericum." The square brackets and the joined word "pleasanttasting" are as printed.

So the chapter gives a description for recognition (a leaf like *erica* but smaller, fatter and red; a shrub twenty centimetres high; pleasant-tasting, sharp, with a good smell), a set of uses by part and vehicle, and one other name. The seed decoction in wine induces urine and the menstrual flow. A decoction taken with pepper is for harvest-spider bites, sciatica, tetanus and chills. With oil it is an ointment for severe spasms. The root boiled in wine is "thought to help" those who often faint. The chapter adds an instruction that the patient must be well covered when drinking it, because it makes the whole body sweat, "and from this one recovers agility". The sweating instruction is the chapter's own and is the only regimen it gives. The single other name is "hypericum" ("Some also call this hypericum"). The chapter says only that some give it that name.

**Overlap.** The same chapter is already cited by the existing entry I_1056 (*Coris monspeliensis*), whose Overlap paragraph records the translators' second binomial on this SUGGESTED line and says the two are not merged. ### Active Components

The text prints no constituent and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. It is a general fact that the genus *Hypericum* contains many species, and that the best-known medicinal one, *H. perforatum*, contains hypericin and hyperforin. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the translators' *Hypericum coris*. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a seed decoction to induce urine and the menses, a pepper decoction for spider bites, sciatica, tetanus and chills, an oil ointment for spasms, a root drink for fainting, and a sweating regimen. These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for tetanus or for the induction of menstruation, and the identity is a suggestion between two different plants.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations and no quantities: a decoction of the seed with wine; a decoction with pepper; the plant "with oil" as an ointment; the root "boiled with wine". It gives one instruction for the patient, that the body be well covered while drinking. It prints no dose, frequency or duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identity is uncertain between two unrelated plants, so a modern study of one would not bear on the Dioscorides drug. It is a general, well-established fact that tetanus is a dangerous infection that needs urgent medical care.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 3-174 prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality. The taste and smell it prints are "pleasanttasting, sharp, and with a good smell". It records that the drink "causes the whole body to sweat".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a description for recognition (a leaf against *erica*, a height, a taste, a sharpness and a smell), separate uses for seed and root, and a regimen of covering and sweating. The statement that one recovers agility is a physical claim and not a vital one. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is the use of taste, sharpness and smell, together with a stated height, as a check on identity. The text offers nothing more, and the abstentive answer is the right one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 3-174 prints no warning. The text uses the seed to "induce" the menstrual flow, which is a warning in itself for pregnancy, and no safety information for pregnancy was read. The identification is only the translators' suggestion between two unrelated plants, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Tetanus, sciatica and repeated fainting need medical care. The text gives no dose. [USER: verify]
