# Cat fat — "Fat-of-the-Cat", "Cat's Fat" (Bryan's English renderings of the Ebers Papyrus)
## *The fat of the animal Bryan calls "Cat"; Bryan prints no Egyptian word and no binomial, so the spine row's "Felis catus" is the list-maker's gloss and is not read from the text* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian Tradition (EP).** The locus is Bryan's 1930 English *The Papyrus Ebers*, Chapter VII "Organic Remedies (animals)". "Cat" stands in Bryan's list of the animals "whose identity is not in doubt" (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1404, after "Dog" and before "Frog"; name-index `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` has no row for the fat). The list gives no Egyptian name and no use; the parts-of-animals list (lines 1405-1415) includes "Fat" but ties it to no animal. The spine row reads "Cat fat, Felis catus" with the note "Ebers (baldness pomade); hairdoc.com" and a marker that the identification is uncertain (`_sources/ebers.csv` line 74). The website was not opened and is not used as authority.

**Where the fat is prescribed.** The Bryan text on disk was searched for "cat". The fat occurs in two places.

- **The baldness pomade, lines 4023-4029**, in Chapter XXII "The Hair" (heading line 3993): "ANOTHER FOR THE GROWTH OF THE HAIR ON A HEAD WHICH IS BECOMING BALD. Fat-of-the-Lion, Fat-of-the-Hippopotamus, Fat-of-the-Crocodile, Fat-of-the-Cat, Fat-of-the-Serpent, Fat-of-the-Egyptian-Goat. Make into one and rub the head of the Bald One therewith." The cat fat is the fourth of six fats in one compound.
- **A mouse-repellent for clothes, lines 4176-4179**, in Chapter XXIV "Domestic Hints" (heading line 4158): "TO KEEP MICE AWAY FROM CLOTHES. Cat's Fat. Smear on everything possible." Bryan's comment follows: "Nowadays our expansive wardrobes, not to mention other prejudices, prohibit this method, but it is still the cat which we employ to keep the mice away." The comment is Bryan's. This is not a medicine for a person, and Bryan's wording does not say whether the cat's fat itself or the presence of the cat was the point of the device. [USER: verify]

**Related items, printed separately, not counted as the fat.** The cat supplies other things in Bryan's text, each a different item: "Cat's dung" (a plaster for hardening in the abdomen, line 1907; "ANOTHER ON THE FIRST DAY Elderberries uah-corn Cat's dung", line 2250; a poultice for a suppurating burn, line 2273; also lines 2682, 2688 and 3558); "Cat's Oil" (a salve "to make supple the hardenings", line 2191; one of the salves of a further passage, line 3153); "Hair-of-a-Cat" (line 2241); and "Womb-of-a-Cat" warmed in oil with the egg of the *gabgu*-bird as a hair-dye (lines 1377 and 4056). Bryan does not say whether "Cat's Oil" is the fat rendered or something else, and these passages were read only in the lines quoted here.

**What is not said.** The sources retrieved do not say whether the domestic cat or a wild cat is meant, how the fat was obtained or rendered, or in what amount it entered the pomade. The Ebers material is indication-organised, so the identity of any named substance is a modern hypothesis. The Ghalioungui 1987 glossary row for "Cat" (`_sources/ebers.csv` line 104) was not opened. ### Active Components

None can be stated for the material as the papyrus names it. In general terms, animal fat is mainly triglycerides, and rendered fat is a solid or semi-solid ointment base. Nothing in the sources retrieved says that cat fat contains any component that acts on hair or that deters mice. This is a general statement, not a reading of Bryan.

---

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved show cat fat in a six-fat hair pomade and, separately, as a smear against mice on clothes. No quantity is legible in either. No modern protocol is supplied and none is implied. The cat is a companion animal in most places where this would be read, and killing it for fat is not justified by anything in the sources.

---

### Preparation & Dosing

Only what Bryan reports. For the pomade, the six fats are "made into one" and rubbed on the head (line 4029). For the clothes, the fat is "smeared on everything possible" (line 4177). No quantity is printed for either. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] As general facts: animal fats are used as ointment bases; no animal fat is an established treatment for baldness; and cats and their fat can carry zoonotic organisms. These points are not drawn from the papyrus.

**Egyptian (EP).** None is given. Bryan records the name only, and the Ebers material is organised by complaint, not by the qualities of a substance.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's vital constitution, and the two uses found are a hair pomade and a household repellent. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*). The one spoken text in the hair chapter, "TO CHARM AWAY ALOPECIA O Shining One, Thou who hoverest above! [...] To be spoken over Iron Red-lead Onions Alabaster Honey" (lines 4069-4072), is attached to a different mixture and not to the fats. Bryan prints no recitation with either use of the cat's fat, though he summarises, and that is no proof the papyrus gives none. The person as plural (*ka*, *ba*, *akh*, *ib*) is not engaged by this entry. No deity is attributed.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. The record is a fat in a hair pomade and a household smear against mice, with no quantity and no cultivation role. A cultivator has nothing to do with it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** animal fat on the scalp or on broken skin carries infection risk; cats can carry bacteria and parasites that infect people.
- **Legal and ethical:** in most jurisdictions killing a domestic cat for its fat is cruelty, and the animal is a companion animal. The pomade should not be reproduced.
- **Conservation:** if a wild cat were meant, many wild cat species are protected; the animal is not identified in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
