# Wild Thyme / Creeping Thyme / Serpyllum

## *Thymus serpyllum* L. | Family: Lamiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Hot and dry in the third degree; incisive, opening, drying; an aromatic antiseptic and carminative of the "sharp-phenol" class.

**Training Phase:** Purification & Clearing — used where thickened phlegm, stagnation, or unclean flesh must be cut, warmed, and driven out.

---

### Origin & Tradition

Wild thyme is the low, mat-forming, purple-flowered thyme of dry banks, heaths, and mountain turf — the creeping thyme whose very name records its habit. The Latin *serpyllum* and the Greek *herpyllos* (ἕρπυλλος) both derive from the verb "to creep." Unlike the upright garden thyme (*Thymus vulgaris*), *T. serpyllum* sprawls flat, forming fragrant carpets; the two share the same essential-oil chemistry and the same doctrinal role, and are used interchangeably in folk practice, but the wild plant is the older, foraged drug. Across the ancient traditions its use is strikingly consistent: an aromatic remedy **for the chest and for worms** — warming, opening the breath, cutting phlegm, settling the gut, and killing "corruption."

**Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* treats *herpyllos* (wild/creeping thyme) as a hot, sharp herb: taken in drink it moves the menses and urine, eases gripings, and — as a warming aromatic — assists the chest and expels intestinal worms; the bruised herb is applied for bites and inflammations. Classical indication: a heating, incising, emmenagogue-and-carminative aromatic. Doctrinal category: **hot, thinning (*leptynō*), diuretic-emmenagogue.**

**Avicenna (CM).** In the Arabic tradition wild thyme corresponds to *nammām* (نمّام) — distinguished from cultivated *ḥāshā* — classed as hot and dry. The *Canon* lineage uses such thymes to *cut and expel* thick phlegm from the chest, warm a cold stomach, dispel wind (carminative), promote urine and menses, and expel worms — the exact "chest and worms" doctrine. Doctrinal category: **hot-dry muṣaffī/muḥallil** (cleansing, resolving) aromatic. [USER: verify precise *nammām* identification with *T. serpyllum* vs. related Thymus/Origanum]

**Assyrian (AT).** Mesopotamian medical texts prescribe numerous aromatic herbs for cough, "the sick chest," and internal vermin, and a thyme-type aromatic is plausible among them, but a secure cuneiform identification with *T. serpyllum* specifically is not established. **Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** This is one of the codex's cleanest doctrine-to-chemistry convergences. The whole traditional profile — antiseptic, expectorant, carminative, vermifuge, "cutting corruption" — collapses onto a single chemical fact: wild thyme's essential oil is dominated by the **monoterpene phenols thymol and carvacrol**, the same phenol class that powers garden thyme and oregano. Phenols are membrane-active biocides: they partition into and disrupt microbial membranes, which is *literally* the ancient "kills corruption / cleans the flesh" action. The "opens the chest and cuts phlegm" doctrine maps onto two real effects of the same volatiles — direct antimicrobial action against respiratory pathogens plus a **smooth-muscle-relaxant (antispasmodic)** effect that eases bronchial and gut cramping. "Settles the gut and expels wind" is the carminative face of that antispasmodic-plus-antiseptic action on the intestine. The pungency the herbalist tastes *is* the therapeutic principle; the doctrine of a "hot, sharp, cleansing" herb is an accurate sensory readout of phenol chemistry.

### Active Components

- **Monoterpene phenols:** **thymol** and **carvacrol** — the principal antimicrobial/antispasmodic actives (proportions vary widely by chemotype).
- **Monoterpene precursors / aroma:** *p*-cymene, γ-terpinene (biosynthetic precursors of thymol/carvacrol), plus linalool, geraniol, borneol in some chemotypes.
- **Rosmarinic acid and phenolic acids:** antioxidant, anti-inflammatory backbone of the water extract.
- **Flavonoids:** luteolin/apigenin glycosides — antioxidant, mild antispasmodic.
- **Tannins:** astringent, contributing to the "drying" quality.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica, wild thyme is a **Purification & Clearing** workhorse: the herb reached for when there is thick, cold, stuck phlegm in the chest; a wet, unproductive cough; sluggish, gassy, cramping digestion; or a need to disinfect. As a warm infusion or steam it is expectorant and antiseptic for the respiratory tract; as a carminative tea it warms a cold stomach and relieves wind and gripe; as a gargle or wash it is an antiseptic for mouth, throat, and minor wounds. Its historical vermifuge use belongs to the same "cleansing" logic but is not a first-line modern deworming strategy. It pairs naturally with other aromatic expectorants and demulcents (the demulcent softening the sharpness of the phenols). Because it is genuinely *hot and drying*, it is a clearing agent, not a soothing one — deployed to move and disinfect, then handed off to gentler remedies for recovery.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, wild thyme shows one of the strongest doctrine-to-evidence alignments in the plant volume, driven by its phenol chemistry.

- **Antimicrobial / antiseptic (strong, species-specific).** *Thymus serpyllum* essential oil (thymol ~18.8%, carvacrol ~17.4%, *o*-cymene ~15.4%) showed high free-radical-scavenging and pronounced antimicrobial and antibiofilm activity against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria and fungi, supporting food-preservative use (Galovičová et al., *Plants* 2021; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants10071416)). Broader work on thymol-dominant Thymus oils corroborates strong antibacterial action tied to thymol content (Shanaida et al., *Saudi J Biol Sci* 2021; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sjbs.2021.06.068)). **Grade: strong — the "cleans/kills corruption" doctrine is well supported.**
- **Antispasmodic / expectorant (good mechanistic support, related actives).** Carvacrol-rich essential oil relaxed airway (tracheal) smooth muscle, an effect traced to potassium-channel opening and soluble-guanylyl-cyclase activation — a real mechanism for "opening the chest" and easing bronchospasm (Menezes et al., *J Ethnopharmacol* 2018; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2018.03.040)). The active (carvacrol) is the same phenol wild thyme carries, though this study used a carvacrol-rich *Lippia* oil. **Grade: good for the mechanism, indirect for the exact species.**
- **Antioxidant / anti-inflammatory (good).** The rosmarinic-acid and flavonoid fraction gives consistent radical-scavenging activity in the same essential-oil studies above, supporting the general "cleansing/protective" reputation. **Grade: good in vitro.**
- **Carminative and vermifuge (traditional; sparse modern data).** The gut-antispasmodic and antimicrobial actions plausibly underlie the carminative use, but direct clinical carminative trials for *T. serpyllum* were not retrieved this run, and the historical anthelmintic use has little controlled human support. **Grade: mechanistically plausible, clinically under-evidenced — partial null.**

A note on chemotype and dose is essential to reading this evidence honestly. *Thymus serpyllum* is not a single fixed drug: its essential-oil composition swings widely with genetics, altitude, soil, and harvest time, so the same "wild thyme" can be a thymol-rich, strongly antiseptic oil in one stand and a linalool- or geraniol-dominant, far milder oil in another. The strong antimicrobial grades above apply to the phenol-rich chemotypes; a low-phenol gathering will not perform the same way. This variability is exactly why traditional herbalists judged wild thyme by *pungency* — the sharp, hot taste is a crude but real assay of thymol/carvacrol content, and a bland specimen was rightly considered weak medicine. It also means clinical extrapolation from a single essential-oil study to "wild thyme tea" is imperfect: a water infusion extracts the volatile phenols only partially and pulls far more of the rosmarinic-acid/flavonoid fraction, shifting the balance toward antioxidant-and-mild-antispasmodic rather than heavy biocidal action.

Honest summary: the antiseptic and antispasmodic-expectorant doctrines are well grounded in phenol chemistry and preclinical data; carminative and vermifuge uses rest more on tradition and mechanism than on human trials.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Hot and dry in the third degree. Qualities: incisive/thinning (cuts thick phlegm), aromatic, antiseptic, carminative, diaphoretic, emmenagogue, drying. Organ affinities: lungs and chest (expectorant), stomach and intestine (carminative, antispasmodic), and the surface/flesh (antiseptic wash). A quintessential "hot, sharp cleanser."

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** Mesopotamian medical texts prescribe many aromatic herbs for cough, "the sick chest" and internal vermin, and a thyme-type aromatic is plausible among them, but a secure cuneiform identification with *T. serpyllum* specifically is not established, and the entry's own attribution is flagged provisional. Under the standing blocking warning governing Assyrian substance identifications, tablet references and their causal-agency framing — where illness is classed by the agency it opposes (divine hand, ghost, oath) — no cultivation status can be asserted, and the identification itself is too insecure to carry even a firm therapeutic one. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* gives *herpyllos* (creeping thyme) a clear therapeutic account: a hot, sharp herb that in drink moves the menses and urine, eases gripings, assists the chest and expels intestinal worms, with the bruised herb applied for bites and inflammations. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system into which such a herb could receive a cultivation status; what he offers a cultivator instead is his discipline of attention — the sharp, hot taste as a sensory assay of the herb's strength, correct identification of the creeping wild plant against related aromatics, and judgement of a gathering's potency. That is a genuine practice of provenance and assay, not a vital-body doctrine. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** Wild thyme corresponds in the Arabic tradition to *nammām* (نمّام), distinguished from cultivated *ḥāshā* and classed hot and dry, used to cut and expel thick phlegm from the chest, warm a cold stomach, dispel wind, promote urine and menses, and expel worms — a *muṣaffī* / *muḥallil* (cleansing, resolving) aromatic. The tradition's governing cultivation idea, the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture as a finite vital endowment whose depletion is ageing, frames the whole regimen and would place a fiercely hot-dry, drying resolvent among the agents that *spend* rather than conserve — but no formal cultivation status is assigned to this simple in the corpus, and no *mufarrih* or cardiac-*ruh* role and no *Canon* chapter is cited for it. It is claimed as a therapeutic cleanser with no cultivation status recorded. **(b) framed.**

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a substance a cultivator takes to build or preserve vitality; wild thyme is a clearing agent, worked hot and short to move stuck phlegm, disinfect, and settle a cramping gut, then handed off to gentler remedies for recovery. Its cultivator's discipline is the old one Dioscorides implies — judge it by pungency, because a bland, low-phenol gathering is weak medicine — and respect its heat: it is used to spend and expel, not to nourish, and its own hot-dry grading is the warning against habitual long-term use.

### Contraindications

- **Thymol/carvacrol mucosal irritation:** the essential oil and strong preparations irritate skin and mucous membranes; never apply the oil neat, and avoid high-dose internal oil. Overdose of thymol can cause nausea, gastric irritation, and (in extremes) systemic toxicity.
- **Pregnancy:** avoid medicinal/therapeutic doses and the essential oil — wild thyme is a traditional emmenagogue and the phenols are potentially irritant/uterotonic; culinary amounts are considered acceptable. [USER: verify]
- **Infants / young children:** do not use the essential oil (menthol/thymol-type volatiles can cause laryngospasm in the very young); avoid steam-inhalation near the face of small children.
- **Allergy:** Lamiaceae hypersensitivity possible.
- **Anticoagulation / bleeding:** high phenolic intake theoretical caution; culinary use fine.
- Keep away from eyes; heavily dilute for any topical use.
