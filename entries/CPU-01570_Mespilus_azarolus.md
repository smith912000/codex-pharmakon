# Mespilus azarolus — Mespilon eteron, the other mespilus (Dioscorides 1-170)

## *Mespilus azarolus, the first of two names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 1-170 (which also prints Crataegus azarolus); "Azarole Tree, Neapolitan Medlar"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 4770) reads "Mespilus azarolus, Crataegus azarolus — Azarole Tree, Neapolitan Medlar", two scientific names that the translators give without choosing between them. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *1-170. MESPILON ETERON* ("the other mespilus"), heading at line 4768, in Book One among the trees and their fruits. The whole chapter (line 4771) reads: "There is another kind of mespilus growing in Italy also called epimelis or setanium. It is a tree similar to an apple tree even in the leaves for it is not smaller. This also has a round edible fruit with a broad navel, somewhat astringent and slow to ripen."

This is a chapter of four statements: a place (Italy), two other names (*epimelis* and *setanium*), a likeness (an apple tree, even in the leaf, which is "not smaller"), and a fruit that is round, edible, with a broad navel, somewhat astringent and slow to ripen. It prints no use. It does not name a part used, a preparation or a complaint, and it does not say that the fruit is good for the stomach or the bowels. Those are the words of the chapter before it.

**The chapter it is "another" of.** The previous chapter, *1-169. MESPILON* (heading at line 4764; SUGGESTED line "Pyrus chamaemespilus — Bastard Quince; Mespilus germanica, Pyrus germanica — Medlar Tree"), reads (line 4767): "Mespilus (which is called aronia by some) is a prickly tree similar in leaves to the oxyacantha, bearing a sweet fruit similar to a little apple with three stones within, from which it is also called tricoccos (as we should say, threefold seed-endowed). It is long in ripening and if eaten is astringent, good for the stomach, and therapeutic for the bowels." The "also" in 1-170 ("This also has a round edible fruit ... somewhat astringent and slow to ripen") ties the second kind to the first for the fruit's astringency and slow ripening. The Codex does not carry 1-169's "good for the stomach, and therapeutic for the bowels" over to 1-170, because the second chapter does not say it. Entry I_603 (medlar) already carries a Dioscorides tag for the medlar; it does not give the chapter id, and this entry is for the second chapter only.

**Other places in the same text.** The index lists "Mespilus azarolus 170" and "Mespilus germanica 170" (line 16607) and "Crataegus azarolus 170" (line 15496). The tree *pyracantha* is cited with the pair, "pyracantha [1-169, 1-170]", in the chapter on the balsam tree (line 3708), which compares the balsam to it in size; that is a pointer to these two chapters as a pair and says nothing of a use.

### Active Components

The composition of the fruit Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, the fruits of the Rosaceae in this group are pomes, and astringency in fruit is commonly due to tannins. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records an Italian fruit tree whose edible round fruit is somewhat astringent and slow to ripen. He prints no use for it. The Codex gives no protocol, and a fruit that the text only describes is not a remedy.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives no preparation and no quantity. It says only that the fruit is "edible". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identification is a translators' suggestion between two names, and a Roman-period fruit "slow to ripen" cannot be matched to a modern cultivar from the lines read.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one quality, that the fruit is "somewhat astringent". It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste beyond that.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers only the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the tree and tell it from its sister (a tree like an apple tree in the leaf, growing in Italy, with a round fruit having a broad navel, slow to ripen). The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of an edible fruit. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this tree. It is an edible astringent fruit with no use printed, so nothing more can be said from the text. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Because it prints no use, there is nothing in it to apply. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion between two names, so no tree should be identified or its fruit eaten on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional statements above is advice. [USER: verify]
