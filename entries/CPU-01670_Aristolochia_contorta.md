# Aristolochia contorta — ba.le.ka, Bhaleka (rGyud-bzhi); "birthwort"

## *Aristolochia contorta, the second of four binomials Clark 1995 prints for ba.le.ka (after "Aristolochia saccata Wall (a type of birthwort)", and before Akebia quinata and Menispermum dahuricum), and the same binomial that Clark prints second for rta.rmig; the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints a different Aristolochia beside the name "Bhaleka"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts in this row.** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and the row's binomial is the second of four on Clark's line. The extraction note says "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, row 109), while the first name on the line is *Aristolochia saccata*, so the spine key differs from that note. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed names (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 976).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, in chapter 20. Clark's list of the eight sections (line 802) puts "(6) plants" between "(5) resins" and "(7) herbs". The text file prints no heading for section (6); the line stands after the printed heading "(5) Resins" (line 879) and before "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997), so it belongs to section (6) by position only. The entry reads: "ba.le.ka: Aristolochia saccata Wall (a type of birthwort): Aristolochia contorta: Akebia quinata: Menispermum dahuricum: [has six names and is a twining shrub similar to clematis which has no fruit nor flower. It has bitter taste, coarse power and cures phlegm, painful infectious fever and blood disorders]. Birthwort also cures fevers of the lungs, liver and vessel organs."

Clark's preface (line 82) says "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". So the six names, the description (twining shrub, no fruit or flower), the bitter taste, the coarse power and the three indications (phlegm, painful infectious fever, blood disorders) are the commentary layer. Outside the brackets are Clark's own identifications (the four Latin names and the gloss "a type of birthwort", which are editorial and not root text) and the root-text sentence "Birthwort also cures fevers of the lungs, liver and vessel organs." The root layer thus gives one action and no taste or power. The English word "Birthwort" is Clark's, and it stands there for the first binomial.

Two more Clark lines name the second binomial or the sibling genus. The entry for *rta.rmig* (line 1094) reads: "rta.rmig (lit. 'horse's hoof'): Epimedium grandiflorum: Aristolochia contorta: Asarum heterotropoides: Viola biflora: [grows at the base of rocks. It has a lobed leaf and yellow flower, and both leaf and flower are shaped like a horse's hoof. In the absence of stalk and branches the leaf grows straight from the ground (sessile growth)]. Epimedium constricts the openings of the channels." There *Aristolochia contorta* is the second of four, the description is in the commentary layer, and the only root-layer sentence is about *Epimedium*, so no use is assigned to *Aristolochia* there. That is a different Tibetan drug, and the Codex does not treat it as the same plant. The entry for *ma.nu.pa.tra* (line 957) prints "Aristolochia sp." as the second of three names (no species). Clark's name index prints *ba.le.ka* (line 1722) and, in the Latin run, *Aristolochia contorta* and *Aristolochia saccata* (line 1557).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (Explanatory Tantra).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. Chapter 20, in the efficacy of herbal medicines (the section begins at line 3428), line 3423 reads: "Bhaleka cures hot disorders of the lungs, liver and vessel organs." The index (line 7267) gives "Bhaleka 205". That *Bhaleka* is a form of *ba.le.ka* is a reading from the sound of the name, supported by the agreement of the organs (lungs, liver and vessel organs) with Clark's root-layer sentence. The text does not state it. [USER: verify]

The glossary prints a different Aristolochia for this name. In the Men-Tsee-Khang Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "B" run holds one name per column in each small block: "Bhaleka" (line 5085), the Tibetan transliteration "ba le ka" (line 5087) and the scientific name "Aristolochia friffithii" (line 5089), with "Indian birthwort" in the common-name block (line 5153). The glossary does not print them on one row, and the Codex reads them together because each block holds a single name per column. The pairing is by position and not stated. The spine holds a separate row for that binomial (CPU-01862, *Aristolochia griffithii*, not yet written). This is an identity doubt between Clark (*Aristolochia saccata* first, *contorta* second) and the glossary (*friffithii*, as printed). The row stands for the Tibetan drug and not for one species. ### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general, well-established fact that plants of the genus *Aristolochia* contain aristolochic acids, which are nephrotoxic and carcinogenic in humans, and that *Akebia* and *Menispermum* are genera of other families. Clark's four binomials thus name plants of three different families, and no common composition can be stated for the Tibetan drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that cures fevers of the lungs, liver and vessel organs (Clark line 976, outside the brackets; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3423: "hot disorders of the lungs, liver and vessel organs") and, in the commentary layer, phlegm, painful infectious fever and blood disorders. These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. Because the genus *Aristolochia* contains aristolochic acids, and the identity of the drug is uncertain, the Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation and name no part used for *ba.le.ka* (Clark line 976 says the shrub has no fruit or flower, in the commentary layer, and says nothing of a part taken). No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identity of the drug is uncertain between four binomials in Clark and a fifth in the glossary, and the commentary layer is separate from the root text, so a modern study of one plant would not bear directly on it. It is a general, well-established fact that aristolochic acid exposure from *Aristolochia* species is associated with kidney injury and urothelial cancer, and that plant materials sold under such names have been restricted in several countries. No source read for this entry says that of this drug.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes the drug by what it does: in the root layer, it cures fevers of the lungs, liver and vessel organs, and in the commentary layer, phlegm, painful infectious fever and blood disorders. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this entry places *ba.le.ka* or *Bhaleka* in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. In the lines read, the essence-extraction mentions near this entry are for other plants (for example at lines 853, 870, 971 and 1078), and *ba.le.ka* is not named there. The rejuvenation chapters (Clark chapter 23, Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23) were read for names and do not name it. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a fever-curing drug in the Tibetan texts, its species is not settled, and the genus it may belong to is a known kidney and cancer hazard. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. Plants of the genus *Aristolochia* contain aristolochic acids, a general, well-established hazard to the kidneys and a cancer risk, and the Tibetan drug may or may not be such a plant. The botanical identity is uncertain, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Fevers of the lungs, liver and vessel organs need medical care.
