# Leather (old) — Leather-from-the-Sandal-maker (Bryan's Ebers) / KATTUMATA, "Old Leather" (Dioscorides 2-51)
## *Tanned animal hide, used: the leather of old shoe soles in Dioscorides, and "Leather-from-the-Sandal-maker" in Bryan's Ebers; neither text names the animal* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP, DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

This entry is leather, a worked animal material and not a living-animal product, and it is the one entry in this batch in which the animal is not named by either text. Cattle are in `III_17_Cattle.md`; untanned hides (such as the dog's hide of Ebers) are noted only where a source mentions them.

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The English text on disk is C. P. Bryan's 1930 *The Papyrus Ebers*, a translation with summaries; the Egyptian words are not printed. Bryan's chapter on mineral remedies explains why leather appears among the "drugs": most of the substances he groups there are not "prescribed in their crude or native state", and "the source of origin of the drugs is strictly laid down, or various preparations made from the parent drug are prescribed. Thus, the Iron must be Iron-from-Apollonopolis-parva; the Leather must be Leather-from-the-Sandal-maker; the Soot must be Soot-from-the-bet'a-pot; the Soda must be Soda-from-Upper-Egypt" (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1137, Chapter V). Bryan then prints an alphabetical list of the mineral remedies in which "Leather-from-the-Sandal-maker" stands alone between "Lead, Lead-vitriol, Fresh Lead-earth" and "Myrrh" (line 1183, pages 21-22 of the book). The one use found is in the nervous-system chapter: "To strengthen the *met* was likewise accomplished with no great amount of trouble, the choice of a variety of salves being given. One of these salves contained Cat's Oil; another Worm-oil; another had as an ingredient Leather-from-the-Sandal-maker!" (line 3153, the exclamation mark being Bryan's). Bryan notes that *met* "undoubtedly refers to the nerves of the body; but there, it would appear just as undoubtedly to apply to the blood-vessels" (lines 3148-3150). So the papyrus, as Bryan reports it, requires a specific provenance for the leather (the sandal-maker's workshop, which suggests offcuts or worn pieces, an inference and not a statement), and uses it in one salve. The spine's locus is Bryan's list, line 1183. Bryan reports no recitation with the salve in the lines read; since he summarises, that is not proof the papyrus gives none. No deity is attributed.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-51 *KATTUMATA*, "Old Leather" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 5139-5141, Osbaldeston and Wood 2000): "The old leather of old soles of shoes (burnt, pounded to powder and applied) helps burns, skin inflammation from rubbing, chafing, and blisters caused by wearing shoes." The chapter follows the liver of a seagull (2-50) and precedes "Parts of Poultry" (2-52). It gives a source (old soles of shoes), a preparation (burnt, then pounded to a powder), a route (applied) and the complaints (burns, rubbing and chafing, and shoe blisters). The complaints are those that a worn shoe itself causes, which the text does not remark on. The edition indexes it as "Leather 200" and "Old Leather 200".

**Tibetan (FMT) is not on this entry's tag line.** The Explanatory Tantra's animal medicines have no leather, but they name hides in the "Skin" paragraph: "Rhino hide and Ox hide: cure smallpox" (Clark 1995, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 1185; Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 lines 3554-3555, "The hide of the rhinoceros and the ox cure small pox"). That is untanned hide and is not leather; it is noted only so the editor does not conflate them. No FMT paragraph is written because the tag line does not carry it.

### Active Components

The sources name none. In general: leather is tanned animal skin, mainly the protein collagen, cross-linked by a tanning agent (plant tannins, mineral salts or others); the sources do not say how either text's leather was tanned. Used shoe-sole leather also holds sweat, dirt and oils. Burning it chars the protein to carbon and leaves a mineral ash. No quantities are given here because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Greek record is burnt old shoe-sole leather, pounded and applied to burns, chafing and blisters; the Egyptian record is leather from the sandal-maker as an ingredient in a salve for the *met*. The Codex has no protocol for either and offers none. The reasons are specific: ash and char on a burn or an open blister is an infection risk and delays proper care; used leather is dirty and may carry bacteria and fungi; leather tanned in the modern way may carry chromium or other chemicals that are not safe on broken skin; the sources give no dose; and the Egyptian use is a one-line report in Bryan's summary.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparation is Dioscorides's: old leather from old shoe soles, burnt, pounded to powder, applied (2-51). Bryan gives no preparation for the Egyptian leather beyond the provenance rule and the salve in which it is an ingredient. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Burns are treated by cooling with clean running water and clean covering, and ash, char and other dry powders are not part of first aid: they contaminate the wound and make assessment harder. Friction blisters heal best clean, intact or covered. Chromium-tanned leather can contain chromium compounds, some of which cause contact dermatitis; untreated hides can carry anthrax spores in regions where the disease occurs; and burning leather gives off irritating smoke. No effect of burnt leather on burns, chafing or blisters, or of leather in a salve on the *met*, is established. No clinical studies of the traditional uses were retrieved for this entry. ### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* Leather appears only as an ingredient in a salve for the *met* and as a provenance requirement ("the Leather must be Leather-from-the-Sandal-maker"). It records what was done for a complaint and does not say what leather does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); Bryan reports no recitation with this salve, and since he summarises, that is not proof the papyrus gives none. No deity is attributed. Because Ebers identifications are modern hypotheses, "Leather" is Bryan's English rendering, and the spine row rests on Bryan's list, not on a glossary read.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied. Old leather is a plain remedy for burns and shoe injuries in 2-51. The chapter's only discipline of attention is in the source, "old soles of shoes", and the preparation, burnt and powdered. That is a rule about material and method, not a claim about the person's constitution.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do. The Greek text is a hint that people once made medicine from what they had to hand; the Egyptian text, in Bryan's summary, makes the leather's origin part of the prescription. Neither assigns leather any role in vitality, and the dirt, smoke and dressings involved argue against use. A cultivator may take from the Ebers line only the habit of naming the provenance of an ingredient.

### Contraindications

- **Burns.** Cool with clean running water and seek care; do not apply ash or powder.
- **Blisters and open skin.** Dirty, burnt material on broken skin risks infection and tetanus; keep blisters clean and covered.
- **Chemicals.** Modern tanned leather may contain chromium and other agents; burning it releases irritating smoke.
- **Anthrax.** Untanned or poorly tanned hides from endemic regions can carry spores.
- **Ethics and diet.** The material is an animal product; unsuitable for vegans.
- **Dose unknown.** No dose is given in the sources read.
