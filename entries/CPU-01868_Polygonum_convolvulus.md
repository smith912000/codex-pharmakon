# Polygonum convolvulus — hbra.go, Dra go (rGyud-bzhi); "Date"; printed "Polygonum convulvulus"

## *Polygonum convolvulus, as Clark 1995 prints it (OCR and printer's "Polygonum convulvulus", line 954) after the English "Date" in the entry hbra.go; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary, by column position, sets the same Tibetan name against Phoenix dactylifera and "Dates", so the Latin name and the English name in Clark's own entry are not one plant* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi (Four Tantras), read in two modern translations, Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011. The binomial *Polygonum convolvulus* is Clark's alone and sits beside an English name, "Date", that points to a different plant; the identity of the drug is therefore open.** Both texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP, confidence "medium") and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. **Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 93) the row is keyed by the Tibetan name *hbra.go*, with the identification "Date; Polygonum convulvulus" and the locus "Clark 1995 (line 954)". The first item of the identification is the English "Date", not a Latin name; *Polygonum convulvulus* is the only Latin name Clark prints for the drug, and the spine binomial is that name, with the spelling normalised from "convulvulus" to "convolvulus". The key is therefore the first and only binomial, and not the first name in the row. Clark's Latin index prints it at line 1629 and his index lists "Date" at line 1575 and *hbra.go* among the Tibetan names (line 1725).

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, chapter 20, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (heading at line 792), the run between "(5) Resins" (line 879) and "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997); no "(6)" heading is printed in the text on disk.* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, line 954: "hbra.go: Date: Polygonum convulvulus: [has sweet taste. The trunk is red with green leaves and yellow, white or blue flowers. The fruit of the supreme type is known as a.mrar. One type has a red, thorny trunk with green leaves, white flowers and a fruit which resembles a horse's teat. It is also known as ha.re.nu.ka, ma.nu.rta.ska or chhi.pa.khar. It] cures brown and yellow phlegm and stomach disorders [and in addition is beneficial in wind fever, blood fever, insanity caused by spirits and as an aphrodisiac]." Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the first bracket, the commentary layer, are the sweet taste, the description of the tree and the alternative names; inside the second bracket are the further uses. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the English name, the Latin name, which is Clark's identification and not the root text's (his preface, line 83, says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance"), and the sentence "cures brown and yellow phlegm and stomach disorders". The commentary describes a plant with a trunk, a fruit and a "supreme type"; the Codex does not try to match the description to any plant.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents line 225), line 3417, page 205: "Adre, Sa dre and Jamdre cure kidney disorders. Dra go cures baekan mukpo, baekan serpo and stomach disorders. Manu pata cures loong, blood and hot disorder." The index lists "Dra go 205" (line 7345). The text gives no Latin name for the drug. The Codex reads *Dra go* as the same drug as *hbra.go* for three reasons that no text states: the transliteration is "'bra go" (line 5207), the neighbours "Adre, Sa dre and Jamdre" stand in the same order as Clark's *a.hbras*, *sra.hbras* and *hjam.hbras* (lines 951 to 953) just above *hbra.go*, and the action ("baekan mukpo, baekan serpo and stomach disorders") is the sentence that Clark renders "brown and yellow phlegm and stomach disorders". "Baekan" is the editors' kept Tibetan term (note at line 686); whether it is Clark's "phlegm" the Codex does not decide. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "D" block, lines 5179 to 5261).* The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. The phonetic names run from "Dali" (line 5181) to "Drita sazin" (line 5196) and number twenty-two when each name is counted separately: "Dhatig" is third and "Dharyaken" fourth (both on line 5183), "Dhong ga" ninth and "Doen shing" thirteenth, and "Dra go" (on line 5189, with "Drakcham") is fourteenth. The transliterations (lines 5198 to 5213) have "'bra go" fourteenth (line 5207). The scientific names begin at line 5215; counted in order, the fourteenth is "Phoenin daetylifera" (line 5241, so printed), and the fourteenth English name is "Dates" (line 5244, on the line "Dates Cortusa"). The count is checked against Clark in three places: "Dhatig" is third against *Schisandra* (Clark's *da.trig*, line 994), "Dhong ga" ninth against *Cassia fistula* (Clark's *dong.ga*, line 990) and "Dhurji" twelfth against *Euphorbia helioscopia* (a spurge, as Clark's *dur.byid*, line 1133, prints spurges). By position, then, the glossary sets *Dra go* against the date palm, *Phoenix dactylifera*, and prints no *Polygonum*. The pairing is not printed in a row and rests on column order. [USER: verify]

**Identity: the date against the knotweed.** Clark's English name for *hbra.go* is "Date" and the glossary's scientific name for *Dra go* is *Phoenix dactylifera*, the date palm; Clark's Latin name is *Polygonum convulvulus*, which is a knotweed (family Polygonaceae) and not a palm. As a general botanical fact these are different plants. The texts read do not say which one the Tibetan drug is. The row stands for the Tibetan drug and not for either species, and the Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Polygonum convolvulus*, *convulvulus*, *hbra.go*, *Dra go* and "Date" found no entry on this Tibetan drug. **I_089** (Date Palm, *Phoenix dactylifera*, tags EP, AT, DMM, CM, SS, SBJ) is the entry on the date palm; it carries no FMT tag and does not cite *hbra.go* or *Dra go*, so it is not an entry on this drug; whether the FMT tag should be added to it is for the spine owner, and nothing is applied here. **I_1413** (*Phoenix sylvestris*, tag SS) is another palm. The other *Polygonum* entries, **I_729** (*P. hydropiper*), **I_742** (*P. bistorta*), **I_763** (*P. aviculare*), **I_187** (*P. capitatum*) and **I_1337** (*P. alopecuroides*, Clark *na.ram*, line 1124), are different species with different Tibetan names. The Dioscorides names file prints "Convolvulus" only for other plants (for example 4-39, line 8338), and the Sushruta names file prints it against *Karkaru* (a cucumber) in Appendix 3 (line 31926); none is *Polygonum convolvulus*. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. As a general fact, the date is the fruit of a palm and is eaten as a food rich in sugars, and *Polygonum convolvulus* is a twining annual herb of the knotweed family. That is a statement about two possible plants and not a finding of the Tibetan texts, which do not say which is meant. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the drug. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record one action in the root layer: the drug "cures brown and yellow phlegm and stomach disorders" (Clark line 954, outside the brackets) and "cures baekan mukpo, baekan serpo and stomach disorders" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3417). Clark's commentary layer adds wind fever, blood fever, insanity caused by spirits and the word "aphrodisiac". These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. The identity is open, so the Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation and name no part used; Clark's commentary mentions a fruit, which is not an instruction. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Both translations place the drug in the materia medica with a therapeutic action (Clark line 954; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3417). Clark's commentary layer, inside the second bracket, calls it an "aphrodisiac". That is a therapeutic word in the bracketed layer and not a rejuvenation or *bcud len* (essence extraction) assignment, and the Codex does not read it as a cultivation status; it is noted here so that a reader can see that the word was read. The rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23: Clark lines 1264 to 1276; Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3904 to 3945) does not name the drug. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** The Tibetan texts give a cultivator nothing to do with this drug beyond what a physician would use it for: brown and yellow phlegm and the stomach. If "Date" is the true gloss it is an ordinary fruit, and if the bindweed-like Latin name is true the texts say nothing more. Nothing here supports a practice built on the word "aphrodisiac" in a bracketed commentary.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for the drug. The identity is uncertain between a fruit and an herb, so no plant should be gathered or taken on the strength of this entry. No conservation information and no safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
