# Sedum rupestre — Aeizoon to mikron (Dioscorides 4-90)

## *Sedum rupestre [Linnaeus], "Stonecrop", the second of the two names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-90 ("Sedum minus mas [Fuchs], Sedum rupestre [Linnaeus] — Stonecrop")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 8838) prints two names, the first with the namer Fuchs and the second with Linnaeus, and the row takes the second. It was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-90. AEIZOON TO MIXRON* ("the small aizoon"), heading at line 8836, SUGGESTED line at 8838, text beginning at line 8839. The chapter is split by page breaks and two plates: the page number 640 stands at line 8840, a running head at 8842 and a plate caption "Senecio vulgaris after THIEBAULT — 1888" at 8843, page 641 at 8844, a plate caption "Potamogeton after FUCHS — 1545" at 8847 and page 642 at 8848, and the text resumes at line 8852 ("the same strength as the previous one"). The captions belong to the plates and not to the chapter. It stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), after *4-89. AEIZOON TO MEGA* and before *4-91. AEIZOON ETERON* (heading at line 8853). The translators' index gives *Aeizoon to mikron* at page 640 (line 11976).

The whole chapter reads, with the page breaks removed: "Sempervivum parvum [aizoon] grows in walls, rocks, mounds, and somewhat shady ditches. The many little stalks emerge from one root, thin, full of little round, fat, leaves, small, sharp on the top. It sends out a stalk in the middle also, in size about twenty centimetres, with a tuft and flowers, thin and green. The leaves of this have the same strength as the previous one. Some call the thin-leaved sempervivum, sempervivum parvum, petrophthes, or sempervivum sylvestre, and the Romans call it sempervivum minus. Sempervivum is also called petrophues, brotion, theobrotion, crobysson, chimerinen, or ceraunia; the Romans call it vitalis, some, herba semperviva, and the Egyptians, etijcelta."

The description is the fullest part: a small plant of walls, rocks, mounds and shady ditches, with many thin little stalks from one root, full of little, round, fat leaves that are "sharp on the top", and a central flowering stalk about twenty centimetres high with a tuft of thin, green flowers. The chapter gives no use or preparation of its own. Its only statement of power is "The leaves of this have the same strength as the previous one." The text does not say which chapter that is. The previous chapter is 4-89, and what 4-89 says of its plant ("It is cooling and astringent; the leaves ... are good for erysipela, herpes ...") is placed here only as a labelled cross-reference, not as a claim of 4-90. That the "previous one" is 4-89 is the reader's reading of the order of the chapters, and the text does not state it. [USER: verify] The names printed are *sempervivum parvum*, *petrophthes*, *sempervivum sylvestre* and the Roman *sempervivum minus*, and, for the family, *petrophues*, *brotion*, *theobrotion*, *crobysson*, *chimerinen*, *ceraunia*, the Roman *vitalis* and *herba semperviva*, and the Egyptian *etijcelta*.

**The three sempervivum chapters.** 4-89 (heading at line 8827) is the basis of entry I_1188 (*Sempervivum tectorum*) and 4-91 (heading at line 8853) of entry I_1186 (*Sedum acre*), both in this batch. The existing entry I_1058 (*Cotyledon orbiculata*) mentions all three chapters as the sempervivum its plant is "good for the same uses as". No entry is duplicated.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides gives no use for this plant of its own: its leaves have "the same strength as the previous one", which the text leaves unnamed. The Codex gives no protocol, and the identification is the translators'.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste of its own. It prints the leaves as "sharp on the top", which describes their form, and refers the strength to "the previous one".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use; it prints the Egyptian name *etijcelta* and the Roman *vitalis* as names only. The part of the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition that is present here is the description of the plant, in which the chapter separates the small kind from the great one by the size and form of its leaves and stalk and by where it grows. The plant is given no status beyond that of a small sempervivum with the strength of the one before. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It offers a description and a reference to another plant's strength, without a use or an amount, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.
