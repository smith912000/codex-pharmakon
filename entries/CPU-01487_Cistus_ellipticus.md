# Cistus ellipticus — Kustos arren, Kustos thelus (Dioscorides 1-126)

## *Cistus ellipticus (the translators' first suggestion; "Common Rockrose, Sunrose")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The SUGGESTED entry has two lines. The first reads "Cistus ellipticus, Helianthemum chamaecistus, Helianthemum vulgare — Common Rockrose, Sunrose" and the second "Cistus creticus, Cistus vulgaris, Cistus polymorphus — Cretan Rockrose". The row takes the first name on the first line. The translators' index lists *Cistus ellipticus* at page 125, together with *C. ladaniferus*, *C. ledon*, *C. polymorphus* and *C. vulgaris*.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *1-126. KUSTOS ARREN, KUSTOS THELUS* at line 4459, SUGGESTED lines at 4460 to 4461, text at line 4462.

The whole chapter reads: "Cistus (also called cistharon or cissaron) is a shrub which grows in rocky places. It has many low branches full of round, black, rough leaves. The flowers of the male are like the pomegranate, but the female are white. It has an astringent quality; as a result the flowers (pounded into small pieces and taken as a drink twice a day in sharp wine) are good for dysentery. Applied as a poultice by themselves they stop gangrenous ulceration of the cheeks, and with a stiff ointment they heal burns and old ulcers."

So the chapter gives a habitat (rocky places), a description (many low branches, round, black, rough leaves), two kinds distinguished by flower (the male's like the pomegranate's, the female's white), one stated quality (astringent) and three uses, all of the flowers: a drink for dysentery, a poultice for gangrenous ulceration of the cheeks, and an ointment for burns and old ulcers. Two other names are printed, *cistharon* and *cissaron*. The heading carries two names, *kustos arren* and *kustos thelus*, and the text itself speaks of "the male" and "the female", so the heading appears to name the two kinds; the Greek words were not looked up for this entry. [USER: verify] The chapter does not say which kind is meant in the uses.

**Neighbouring chapters.** The next chapter, 1-127 *UPOKUSTIS* (line 4463), describes hypocistis, a plant that "grows around the roots of the cistus [rock roses]" (line 4466). It has its own spine row, CPU-01498 *Cytinus hypocistis*. Chapter 1-128 *LADANON* is the ladanum chapter; the spine's ladanum entry, I_143, cites Dioscorides Book I without giving a chapter number.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records astringent rockrose flowers drunk in wine for dysentery and applied for ulceration of the cheeks, burns and old ulcers. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol: dysentery, gangrenous ulceration and non-healing ulcers need medical care, and the plant's identity is the translators' and is not settled by the text.

### Preparation & Dosing

1-126 gives these preparations of the flowers: "pounded into small pieces and taken as a drink twice a day in sharp wine" for dysentery; "applied as a poultice by themselves" for gangrenous ulceration of the cheeks; and "with a stiff ointment" for burns and old ulcers. The only frequency is twice a day. No quantity or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

An astringent drink and an astringent poultice are claims about tissue-tightening action. No study of any rockrose species was opened, and the identity of the plant is itself uncertain. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: where the shrub grows (rocky places), how to know it (low branches, round black rough leaves), how to tell the two kinds apart by flower colour, and what it is said to do. It assigns the plant no status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a wound and dysentery remedy in a text that records both the male and the female kind without choosing between them. The honest use is to learn the described marks, which is the discipline of attention, and to leave the remedies as history.

### Contraindications

Dysentery, gangrenous ulceration and old ulcers are conditions that need medical care, and a home preparation must not replace it. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion that spans several species, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
