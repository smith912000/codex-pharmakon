# Bergenia ciliata — brag.lcham, Drak cham (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Bergenia ciliata, the first of three binomials Clark 1995 prints for brag.lcham (with Pyrola rotundifolia and Pyrola incarnata, as printed); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text prints the name only as "Drak cham" and "Drakcham" and sets no Bergenia beside it* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts, and under this binomial only by Clark.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. Both Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the line prints three, of which the row takes the first (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, row 183). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1093).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads:

"brag.lcham: Bergenia ciliata: Pyrola rotundifolia: Pyrola incarnata: [grows on rocks. It has a thick round, glossy, green leaf with dewdrops and a red or white flower. This plant] heals wounds."

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the habitat, the leaf and the flower, and the words "This plant". Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the clause "heals wounds", and the three Latin names, which are Clark's own identifications and not the root text's. The line prints no taste, no power and no number of names. The line that follows, *gra.ma* (line 1095), is a separate drug with its own entry, treated in the *Caragana* entries of this batch.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3457).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, in the section "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (it opens at line 3428). The sentence reads: "Drak cham closes wounds and Tamik seals the aperture of channels." The Codex reads "Drak cham" as the same name as *brag.lcham* from the spelling and from the agreement in action (wounds); the text does not say so, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text gives no Latin name in this passage. The printed index (line 7346) lists "Drak cham 210". [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (lines 5189 and 5208).* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate blocks. In the "D" block (the letter at line 5179), the phonetic names (lines 5181 to 5196) include "Drakcham" (line 5189), and the Tibetan transliterations (lines 5198 to 5213) include "brag lcham" (line 5208). The scientific names are printed in a separate run (lines 5215 to 5259). Counting the names in the three columns, "Drakcham" is the fifteenth phonetic name and the fifteenth binomial is "Cortusa brotheri" (printed "Cortusa brother", line 5242), with "Cortusa" in the English column (line 5244). Two lines of the block hold several names each (lines 5217 and 5246), so the count rests on order and not on rows, and several neighbours agree with Clark ("Dhambu kara" with *Hippuris vulgaris*, "Drita sazin" with *Fragaria nubicola*, "Drama" with the English "Pea shrub"). The Codex therefore reads the glossary as pairing "Drakcham" with *Cortusa*, by column position only, and not with *Bergenia ciliata*. The glossary prints no *Bergenia ciliata*. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Bergenia ciliata*, *brag.lcham*, *Drak cham* and *Bragcham* found no entry on this plant. The *Bergenia* entries that exist are on other drugs or other binomials: I_308 (*Bergenia purpurascens*, tagged FMT) and I_884 (Elephant Ears, *Badan*, on the Clark entry *(li.)ga.dur*, line 977, with *Bergenia crassifolia* as the third of four Latin names; I_884's own prose counts five). I_884 mentions Clark's *brag.lcham* line (1093) in its prose as a second *Bergenia* line and calls it genus-level for that row. I_1243 (*Bergenia stracheyi*), written in this batch, is the glossary-only sibling row. I_396 (Varuna) names *Bergenia ligulata* in a cross-reference. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Bergenia* is a genus of the saxifrage family (Saxifragaceae), and that the genus is generally known to contain the compound bergenin along with tannins. *Pyrola* belongs to a different family. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained, because the Latin name is not settled. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a rock-growing drug that "heals wounds" (Clark, line 1093) or "closes wounds" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3457). These are the texts' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation for this drug. The Men-Tsee-Khang sentence gives an action without a quantity, a frequency or a duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identity of the Tibetan drug is uncertain between *Bergenia* and two species of *Pyrola*, so a modern study of one plant would not bear directly on it.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's line 1093 prints no taste, no power and no post-digestive taste for *brag.lcham*; the only descriptive terms in it ("thick round, glossy, green leaf with dewdrops") are inside the brackets and are not qualities in the sense of the eight powers or six tastes. The Men-Tsee-Khang sentence (line 3457) prints none either. No quality is printed for this drug.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes this drug by what it does, which is to heal wounds, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same ("closes wounds"). The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this drug places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: a search of both Tibetan texts for *brag.lcham*, "Drak cham" and "Drakcham" returned only the lines above and the glossary and index entries. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a wound drug in the Tibetan texts, its Latin name is not settled, and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary appears to pair its name with another plant. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. The identity is uncertain, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. A wound that does not close, or that shows signs of infection, needs medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
