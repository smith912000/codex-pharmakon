# Gotu Kola — Mandukaparni, Brahmi (Ayurveda), Ji Xue Cao (積雪草, TCM), Indian Pennywort

## *Centella asiatica (L.) Urb.* | Family: Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM, SBJ, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Cool and moist, bitter-sweet (cross-tradition: cooling, wound-knitting, calming)
**Training Phase:** Integration

---

### Origin & Tradition

Gotu Kola is one of the rare simples honoured across four major traditions as both a **wound-healer** and a **mind-tonic**. A low, creeping marsh plant of the carrot family, it spreads by runners — a habit its Chinese name (*Ji Xue Cao*, "accumulating snow herb") and its reputation for regeneration both echo.

**Sushruta (SS):** In the Ayurvedic surgical corpus *Sushruta Samhita*, and throughout later Ayurveda, *Mandukaparni* ("frog-leaf," for its rounded leaf) is a premier *medhya rasayana* — a rejuvenative for intellect and memory — and a wound-and-skin remedy. It is one of the classical brain tonics prescribed to sharpen *dhi, dhriti, smriti* (comprehension, retention, recollection). (Note: the name "Brahmi" is shared confusingly with *Bacopa monnieri*; in much of India *Centella* is the plant called Mandukaparni, sometimes Brahmi.)

**Shennong / Chinese materia medica (SBJ):** Within the Chinese herbal tradition descending from the *Shennong Bencao Jing* lineage, *Ji Xue Cao* is classed as cooling and bitter, used to clear heat, resolve dampness, cool the blood, and reduce swelling and toxic sores — a wound-and-detoxifying herb.

**Dioscorides (DMM) & Avicenna (CM):** The Greek and Arabic canons record small creeping Apiaceous marsh-herbs among cooling vulnerary and diuretic simples used for wounds, ulcers, and swellings. *Centella* sits within this Mediterranean-to-Unani stream of cooling wound-plants at the level of plant-class; its firmest classical home is South and East Asian rather than Greek, so the DMM/CM tags mark tradition-class inclusion rather than a pinpointed named entry.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Gotu Kola uniquely unites the two poles Kenetica seeks to join in the **Integration** phase — the healing of the *body's* wounds and the strengthening of the *mind*. The doctrine is that true integration knits tissue and cognition by one action: the same regenerative principle that closes a wound also consolidates memory. This is one of the few cases where symbol and pharmacology genuinely converge: the plant's triterpenes demonstrably stimulate collagen synthesis and wound closure, and separate clinical signals point to effects on alertness and mood — two literal correlates of "knitting together."

### Active Components

The active class is a group of **pentacyclic triterpenes**: the glycosides **asiaticoside** and **madecassoside**, and their aglycones **asiatic acid** and **madecassic acid** (collectively the basis of the pharmaceutical fraction sometimes standardised as "ECa 233" or "TTFCA/Centellase"). These drive collagen stimulation and wound repair. Accompanying constituents include **flavonoids** (quercetin, kaempferol derivatives), **caffeoylquinic (chlorogenic-type) acids**, and **polyacetylenes**.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In the **Integration** phase, Gotu Kola is the flagship dual-action simple: applied or taken to support tissue repair, skin and scar quality, and connective-tissue integrity, while simultaneously supporting calm alertness, memory consolidation, and mood — the cognitive side of integration. It is the entry in this cluster with the strongest modern evidence, and Kenetica presents it as a genuine functional agent with clinical support for its topical wound use and preliminary support for its cognitive/mood use.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, Changsan et al. (2023), *Journal of Wound Care*, ran a multicentre randomised controlled trial (n=60, clean-contaminated acute wounds) of a polymeric spray film containing *Centella asiatica* triterpenes (asiatic acid, madecassic acid, asiaticoside, madecassoside), reporting significantly reduced PUSH and exudate scores with no adverse effects — clinical support for triterpene-based wound closure ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.12968/jowc.2023.32.Sup12.S22)). Damkerngsuntorn et al. (2020), *Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine*, conducted a split-face, double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT of the standardised extract ECa 233 gel after Er:YAG laser resurfacing (n=30), finding significantly less erythema and better early wound appearance on the treated side ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1089/acm.2019.0325)). For cognition and mood, Pedregosa et al. (2026), *Complementary Medicine Research*, a systematic scoping review, found two randomised trials in healthy participants (n=58) reporting improvements in alertness and an open-label study in generalised anxiety disorder (n=33) reporting reduced anxiety and depressive symptoms, with no serious adverse events — but graded the overall human evidence as preliminary and heterogeneous ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1159/000553172)).

**Evidence grade: Clinical (topical wound healing) — relatively strong; Preliminary/hypothesis-generating (cognition, mood, anxiety).** The wound and skin-repair action has multiple controlled human trials; the neuro-cognitive claims rest on small trials and open-label data and should be stated cautiously.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM (SBJ lineage):** Bitter, acrid, cold. Clears heat, resolves dampness, cools blood, relieves toxic swelling; channels typically given as Liver, Spleen, Kidney.
- **Ayurveda (SS):** *Rasa* — tikta (bitter), kashaya (astringent), madhura (sweet); *Guna* — laghu (light); *Virya* — sheeta (cooling); *Vipaka* — madhura (sweet). A *medhya rasayana*; balances all three doshas, especially pacifying Pitta and Vata.
- **Galenic / Unani (DMM/CM class):** Cool and moist; cooling vulnerary and diuretic.

### Contraindications

Avoid in pregnancy and lactation (insufficient safety data; traditional caution). Rare hepatotoxicity has been reported with prolonged high-dose oral use — avoid in existing liver disease and limit continuous oral courses (traditional practice cycles the herb). May potentiate sedatives and drugs metabolised hepatically; use caution with hepatotoxic medications. Discontinue two weeks before elective surgery (possible sedative/anaesthetic interaction).
