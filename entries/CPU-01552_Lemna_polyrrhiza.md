# Lemna polyrrhiza — Phakos epi ton telmaton (Dioscorides 4-88)

## *Lemna polyrrhiza (Spirodela polyrrhiza is the translators' second name on the same line); "Greater Duckweed", with Lemna minor, "Water Lentils, Water Lens, Duckweed", printed as a further suggestion* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the SUGGESTED line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The line (lines 8820 and 8821) reads "Lemna polyrrhiza, Spirodela polyrrhiza — Greater Duckweed" and then, on a second line, "Lemna minor — Water Lentils, Water Lens, Duckweed". The second line may be a separate suggestion or a further suggestion for the same chapter; the lines read do not say which. The Codex does not choose between the two plants.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-88. PHAKOS EPI TON TELMATON*, heading at line 8819, SUGGESTED line at 8820, text at line 8822. It stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), after *4-86. ELXINE* and *4-87. ALSINE*. The translators' index gives *Lemna minor*, *Lemna polyrrhiza* and *Spirodela polyrrhiza* at page 639 (lines 16285 and 17729).

The whole chapter reads: "Lens (which grows in marshes) is found in standing waters, being a moss similar to lentils which is cooling. It is good (applied both by itself or with polenta) for all inflammation, erysipela [streptococcal skin infection], and gout of the feet; and it also heals vaginal hernias found in children. It is also called wild lens, or epipteron, the Romans call it viperalis, and some, iceosmigdonos."

So Dioscorides describes the plant in one clause: a "moss" like lentils, growing in standing water, and "cooling". It is applied alone or with polenta to inflammation, to erysipelas and to gout of the feet, and it "heals vaginal hernias found in children". He gives four other names: *wild lens*, *epipteron*, the Roman *viperalis* and *iceosmigdonos*. The chapter gives no description beyond the likeness to lentils, no season and no method of gathering.

**The comparison plant.** The chapter's heading echoes *2-129. PHAKOS* (heading at line 5602), whose SUGGESTED line (line 5604) gives *Lens culinaris* and "Lentils" and whose text opens "Phaca (which the Latins call lens or lentils) is frequently eaten." The name is shared and the plants are different: 4-88 is an aquatic "moss similar to lentils", not the pulse. No text of 2-129 is read into this entry beyond that opening.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. As a general fact, the duckweeds are very small floating aquatic plants of still water. Nothing is stated here about the composition of the translators' *Lemna polyrrhiza* or *Lemna minor*. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a cooling pond plant applied, alone or with polenta, to inflammation, erysipelas and gout of the feet, and used for a vaginal hernia in children. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol. Erysipelas is a spreading skin infection, and a hernia in a child is a medical matter. Plants from standing water can carry microbes. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-88 gives one preparation: the plant "applied both by itself or with polenta". No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. This chapter offers only a very small amount of the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: where the plant is found (standing waters), what it looks like (a lentil-like moss) and its quality (cooling). It assigns the plant no status beyond that of an external drug. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is an external remedy for inflammation and gout with no stated amounts. The sources support nothing more, and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Erysipelas, a hernia in a child and gout need medical care and must not be left to a home preparation. Pond plants are not clean and should not be put on skin that is broken or infected. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion with two different plants, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
