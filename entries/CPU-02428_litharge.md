# Litharge (lead monoxide) — Litharguros (LITHARGUROS), Dioscorides 5-102

## *Litharge, the yellow lead monoxide that forms when lead or silver-bearing lead is fired in a furnace; the Dioscorides translator glosses it "Lithargyrum, Litharge" and marks it TOXIC, and the identification as lead monoxide (PbO) is high-confidence on the name, the furnace origin and the translator's own gloss* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-102, *Litharguros* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 10339; name-index row in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` at row 875, translator gloss "Lithargyrum, Litharge — Monoxide of Lead Monoxide of Silver and Monoxide of Molybdenum TOXIC", class guess mineral; the list row in `_sources/dioscorides.csv` is at line 828, where the entry is mis-labelled as a plant; the book index lists "LITHARGUROS 794"). The whole chapter was read. It runs from line 10339 to line 10353, across two page breaks.

**What it is and where it is made (line 10342).** "Some spuma argenti or froth of silver is made from sand called molibditis [molybdenite — disulphide of molybdenum] blown in a furnace until perfectly fired. Some is made from silver and some from lead. The Attican is best, next to this is the Spanish, after these that from Dicaearchia in Campania and from Sicily, for much of it is made in these various places from lead plates that are fired. The best is a yellow glittering sort called chrysitis; that from Sicily is called argyritis [silver], and that from silver is called calabritis." The chapter thus gives three sources (a sand called *molibditis*, silver, and lead plates), a ranking of places (Attic best, then Spanish, then Dicaearchia in Campania and Sicily), and three named sorts: *chrysitis* (the best, yellow and glittering), *argyritis* (from Sicily) and *calabritis* (from silver).

**Its qualities and uses (same line, 10342).** "It is astringent and softening, filling up hollownesses, repressing abnormal growths of flesh, and forming new skins; as well as being cooling, and closing the pores."

**Burning (same line, 10342).** "Divide it into pieces the size of carya [1-178], place them on burning coals, and blow on them until thoroughly fired. Then wipe away the filth that has formed on it and put it in jars. Some quench it three times with vinegar or wine then burn it again, and having done these things, put it in jars. It is washed like cadmia [5-84]."

**Whitening and washing (lines 10343-10353).** The chapter then gives a long method "to make it white": break an Athenian *choenix* (the translator says "two pints") of *argyritis* into pieces the size of beans, boil them in a new ceramic jar with water and two pints of white wheat, with a handful of barley in a linen cloth, until the barley is broken; pour out, separate, wash with strong hand-rubbing, dry, beat in a Thebaean mortar with warm water, and repeat three times daily for seven days; then "mix to a pound of lithargyrum five teaspoonfuls of mineral salt" and keep beating and washing until it is white and has no saltiness; and dry in the sun. A second method uses a pound of *argyritis* with three times as much mineral salt, stirred each morning and evening for thirty days, then washed free of salt and made into lozenges "kept in a lead box". A third boils the sediment in the raw intestines of a swine; others beat equal weights of salt and litharge in the sun; and a last method wraps litharge in white wool and boils it with beans, repeatedly, "until the wool absorbs no more colour", then beats it with mineral salt (one pound to "eighty Athenian teaspoons") and "forty seven teaspoonfuls of the very whitest saltpetre", washes it until the water is clear and sweet, and dries it in the sun for forty days. The chapter ends: "Lithargyrum that has been washed seems to be good to put in eye medicines, as well as for foul scars, wrinkled faces and those full of spots."

**Cross-references.** The burnt-lead chapter (5-96) warns that lead burned too much "takes on the colour of lithargyrum [5-102]" (lines 10308-10312). The molybdaina chapter (5-100) says that material "has properties like lithargyrum [5-102] and [slag] of lead" (line 10332).

**Identity.** The translator's gloss is "Lithargyrum, Litharge", with the three oxides (lead, silver, molybdenum) listed in the gloss; the chapter's own account, lead plates fired in a furnace, supports lead monoxide. The "silver" and "molybdenum" in the gloss reflect the chapter's three sources; the Codex does not claim that litharge from silver or from *molibditis* is lead monoxide, only the lead-plate sort. ### Active Components

The chapter names no constituent. Litharge is, in general chemical terms, lead(II) oxide (PbO), a yellow to reddish solid formed when molten lead is oxidised in air; the lead was the main metal in "silver-bearing lead" ores and litharge is the by-product of the cupellation by which silver was refined. These are general facts about litharge and are not drawn from Dioscorides.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Toxic.** Dioscorides records litharge as an astringent and softening material that fills hollows, checks fleshy growths, forms new skin, cools and closes the pores, and, washed, a material for eye medicines and for foul scars, wrinkled and spotted faces. Lead monoxide is poisonous: it is absorbed through the gut, lungs and (as a powder or in a plaster) broken skin, accumulates in the body, and damages the nerves, blood and kidney. It is not placed in the eye or on the face in modern care. The Codex gives no protocol and does not recommend litharge in any form.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives the burning method (broken to the size of carya, placed on burning coals, blown on until thoroughly fired, cleaned and put in jars; optionally quenched three times with vinegar or wine and burned again) and several washing and whitening methods, as summarised above. It also gives preparation quantities (a choenix of argyritis; five teaspoonfuls of mineral salt to a pound; eighty Athenian teaspoons of litharge to a pound of salt with forty-seven teaspoonfuls of saltpetre), which are quantities of a refining method and **not doses for use**. The chapter states no dose. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any quantity from this entry.** [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] As general facts: lead monoxide is toxic by every route; lead poisoning causes abdominal pain, anaemia, nerve and kidney damage and, in children and in pregnancy, lasting harm to development; there is no safe level of exposure; and washed or whitened lead compounds remain lead compounds. The historical use of litharge in plasters and eye medicines reflects the astringent action of lead salts on tissue, which is the same property that makes them harmful. ### Contraindications

- **TOXIC.** Lead monoxide is poisonous. Do not ingest it, inhale its dust, or apply it to skin, face or eyes. Do not handle it dry.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children:** no statement in the sources retrieved. Lead is especially harmful in pregnancy and childhood; avoid all exposure. [USER: verify]
- **Eye and face:** the chapter's eye and facial uses are not safe; lead on the eye or broken skin can be absorbed.
- **Washing and furnace work:** firing and washing litharge release fumes and lead-bearing water; the burnt-lead chapter warns "the vapour is harmful" (line 10308).
- **Lead box:** the chapter stores lozenges in a lead box, which is itself a source of exposure.
