# Korean Mint / Huo Xiang (藿香) / Patchouli-mint

## *Agastache rugosa* (Fisch. & C.A.Mey.) Kuntze | Family: Lamiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ, FMT

**Energetic Classification:** Acrid, aromatic, slightly warm; drying. Enters the Spleen, Stomach and Lung channels. Ascending-dispersing at the surface, descending-settling in the middle.

**Training Phase:** Foundation — Stabilisation. A digestive-axis herb for the practitioner whose gut has stopped cooperating with the work: the summer-sick stomach, the nausea of overheating, the practitioner who cannot eat before practice.

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Agastache rugosa* is a tall, square-stemmed, rugose-leaved perennial mint of East Asia — Korea, China, Japan, the Russian Far East — carrying a scent that is neither quite mint nor quite anise but a sharp sweet green in between. It is one of the very few Lamiaceae that is simultaneously a mainstream culinary leaf, a garden ornamental, and a principal drug of a classical pharmacopoeia.

**Chinese Medicine (中醫) — Huo Xiang / Tu Huo Xiang (土藿香)**

In the Chinese materia medica *huo xiang* sits in the category **化濕藥 — herbs that transform dampness**, and its stated actions are remarkably stable across sources: to **transform damp and awaken the Spleen** (化濕醒脾), to **harmonise the middle and stop vomiting** (和中止嘔), and to **release the exterior in summer-damp** (解暑發表). The classical indication is the specific misery of hot wet weather: a heavy head, a "stuffed" chest, no appetite, a greasy tongue coat, loose stools, and above all nausea and vomiting. It is the ruling herb of *Huo Xiang Zheng Qi San* (藿香正氣散), "Agastache Powder to Rectify the Qi" — the formula reached for in summer gastroenteritis, traveller's stomach, and the sick queasiness of eating cold food in hot weather. Because the leaf's action is held to reside in its volatile fragrance, the classical instruction is emphatic and pharmacologically correct: **add late, decoct briefly** (後下), or the medicine boils away.

**⚠️ The Huo Xiang problem — a naming collision you must resolve before you buy.** The name 藿香 is applied in modern commerce to **two different plants in two different genera**. *Guang huo xiang* (廣藿香) is **true patchouli, *Pogostemon cablin*** — the source of the perfumery oil — and it, not *Agastache*, is what the current Chinese Pharmacopoeia lists as the official *huo xiang*. *Agastache rugosa* is distinguished as *tu huo xiang* (土藿香, "native/earth huo xiang") or in Korea as *baechohyang* (배초향). The two are used near-interchangeably in clinical practice and share the damp-transforming reading, but their chemistry is **not the same**: patchouli's oil is sesquiterpene-dominated (patchoulol), *Agastache*'s is phenylpropanoid- or monoterpene-dominated (estragole, pulegone, methyleugenol). **Never transfer a *Pogostemon* study onto *Agastache* or the reverse.** This entry concerns *Agastache rugosa* only; where the classical literature is ambiguous as to which plant was meant, that ambiguity is flagged rather than resolved. **Korean Tradition — Baechohyang (배초향)**

In Korea the plant is not primarily a drug at all; it is **food and incense**. The aerial parts are used fresh as a leaf herb in soups and salads for aroma and taste, and the plant's fragrance has been used in the preparation of incense in Korea reportedly since the Goryeo Dynasty (Hong et al., *Molecules*, 2022 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27196341)). This double life is worth noting: a plant eaten as a vegetable is being asked, in the Chinese reading, to act as a medicine. That is a low-dose, high-frequency, food-adjacent pharmacology — which is exactly what a "harmonise the middle" herb should be.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

"Transform dampness" is not a metaphor floating free of chemistry — it is a description of a specific clinical syndrome mapped onto a specific plant behaviour, and the mapping is unusually good.

*Dampness* in the Chinese reading is stagnant, heavy, turbid, and above all **wet and fermenting**: the greasy coat, the loose stool, the loss of appetite, the sensation of being water-logged. The claim is that an intensely aromatic, volatile, *drying* substance can move it. Read chemically: *huo xiang*'s entire therapeutic identity is its **essential oil** — a lipophilic, fast-evaporating fraction. The doctrine that the drug "must not be over-boiled" is a pre-modern statement that the active principle is volatile. The doctrine that it "awakens the Spleen" — restores appetite — is a statement that an aromatic acts on gastric secretion and motility. The doctrine that it "stops vomiting" is an antiemetic claim. And the doctrine that dampness is a **fermenting, turbid** state that the herb clarifies maps, without much strain, onto an **antimicrobial** action in a gut that has been colonised by something it should not have eaten in hot weather.

The season is the tell. Damp-heat is a **summer** syndrome; summer is when food spoils and enteric infection spikes. A culture without a microbe theory, observing that a particular aromatic reliably settles the summer sick stomach, will encode the observation as "transforms summer damp." The doctrine is a correct clinical generalisation wearing a pre-microbial vocabulary.

Where the doctrine over-reaches: "damp" in later usage swells into a catch-all for anything heavy, sluggish, or overweight, and *huo xiang* is sometimes recruited for chronic conditions it has no demonstrated action on. **Deflate this.** The evidence base — such as it is — points at the acute, aromatic, gastric, antimicrobial end. There is no reason to think this leaf resolves anything chronic and metabolic, and the entry should not pretend otherwise.

### Active Components

The single most important cultivator's fact about this species is that **there is no such thing as "the" *Agastache rugosa* chemistry.** It is a chemotypic species.

Screening 90 Korean native populations grown from wild-collected seed under common-garden conditions, Hong et al. identified **204 volatile components** and resolved **six chemotypes** — estragole, pulegone, methyl eugenol, menthone, isopulegone, and nepetalactone — clustering into three main groups (estragole, methyl eugenol, pulegone); oil yield ranged 0.11–0.86% (*Molecules*, 2022 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27196341)). The variation is genetic, not merely environmental. Two plants from the same seed packet may not be the same drug.

Reported dominant constituents, by source:

- **Estragole (methylchavicol)** — up to **89.5%** of the oil in a Korean estragole-type sample (Hong et al., *Behav Sci*, 2022 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs12070238)); 63.3% in a Romanian *A. rugosa* "Aromat de Buzău" cultivar and 41.7% in another *Agastache* sp. (Nechita et al., *Int J Mol Sci*, 2024 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms25105366)); a main component alongside limonene and β-caryophyllene in the Korean oil examined by Kim et al. (*Molecules*, 2024 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules29204907)).
- **Pulegone / p-menthan-3-one (menthone)** — dominant in other populations: flower oil from Xinjiang, China was pulegone 34.1%, estragole 29.5%, p-menthan-3-one 19.2%; leaf oil was p-menthan-3-one 48.8%, estragole 20.8% (Haiyan et al., *Saudi J Biol Sci*, 2016 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sjbs.2016.02.020)).
- **Methyleugenol** — dominant in yet another population: 50.5%, with estragole 8.6% and eugenol 7.5% (Li et al., *Molecules*, 2013 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules18044170)).
- **Minor volatiles** — D-limonene, β-caryophyllene, thymol, eugenol.
- **Non-volatile fraction** — the species is also reported in the phytochemical literature to carry **rosmarinic acid** and the methylated flavonol **pachypodol**. *No paper characterising either compound in* A. rugosa *was retrieved in this run;* both are listed here as claims requiring a source before use. [USER: verify — do not cite rosmarinic acid or pachypodol content until a primary paper is in hand.]

The biosynthetic split is now partly understood: the pulegone chemotype is monoterpenoid-dominant and the estragole chemotype phenylpropanoid-dominant, with 46 candidate biosynthetic genes and a chavicol methyltransferase and limonene-3-hydroxylase identified across the two (Dang et al., *Front Plant Sci*, 2022 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2022.850130)).

**⚠️ Estragole — the flag.** Estragole is a phenylpropanoid that is genotoxic and hepatocarcinogenic in rodent bioassay and has been the subject of adverse regulatory opinion in Europe as a constituent of herbal medicines. A leaf whose oil is up to 89% estragole is not a leaf to take as concentrated oil, chronically, at dose. **Methyleugenol carries the same class of concern.** This is the central safety fact of the entry and it is *not* softened by the plant's culinary status — culinary use is small-quantity, whole-leaf, intermittent. See Contraindications.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

The use is narrow and unglamorous, which is the point.

**Primary — the summer-sick gut.** A short course for the acute picture the tradition describes: nausea, appetite loss, heaviness, and looseness in hot humid conditions or after questionable food. Aromatic, food-adjacent, taken as a light infusion or as leaf in soup, not as a heroic dose. This is a **stabilisation** herb, not a state-changer.

**Secondary — the pre-practice stomach.** Practitioners who cannot sit because the gut is churning, particularly in heat. The rationale is the same aromatic-antiemetic one; the effect, honestly, is mild.

**Tertiary — atmosphere.** The plant is incense-grade, and there is one small piece of direct human data: EEG exposure work in 38 volunteers found that inhaling the essential oil decreased absolute and relative theta and increased relative alpha, slow alpha, and spectral edge frequency measures relative to no odour — a pattern the authors associate with "freshness and concentration" (Hong et al., *Behav Sci*, 2022 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs12070238)). This is an alerting/attentional signature, not a sedative one. Treat it as a weak, single-study finding — see grading below.

**Not for:** any chronic indication, any concentrated-oil internal protocol, any "clears damp so it clears weight/fog/torpor" reading. That extrapolation is unsupported.

### Preparation & Dosing

⚠️ All internal use of the **concentrated essential oil** is contraindicated (estragole/methyleugenol). The preparations below are aqueous/leaf preparations only.

| Preparation | Part | Method | Working range | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Culinary leaf | Fresh aerial | Chopped into soups, salads | Ad libitum, food quantity | Korean primary use; safest form |
| Infusion (tea) | Dried aerial | Steep covered 5–10 min off the boil | ~3–9 g dried herb/day, divided [USER: verify against your materia medica] | **Covered vessel is not optional** — the drug is the vapour |
| Decoction adjunct (後下) | Dried aerial | Add in final 3–5 min of a longer decoction | Per formula | The classical instruction; preserves volatiles |
| Fresh juice/steam | Fresh aerial | Bruised, brief steam | — | Traditional, poorly documented |
| Essential oil — **topical / diffusion only** | Aerial | Steam-distilled; yield 0.11–0.86% | Diffusion; ≤1% dilution topically | **Never internally.** Chemotype unknown = risk unknown |
| Incense | Dried aerial | Burned | — | Korean/Goryeo-attested use |

**Cultivator's guide.** *A. rugosa* is a forgiving hardy perennial (roughly USDA 4–9): full sun, free-draining soil, self-sows readily, and is a heavy pollinator magnet — bumblebees strip it. Grow from seed with light; it germinates easily. **The hard part is chemotype.** Because a seed-grown population may be estragole-, pulegone-, methyleugenol-, menthone-, isopulegone- or nepetalactone-type (Hong et al., 2022), a grower who intends any oil or medicinal use should **propagate a known plant by cuttings or division, not by seed**, and — if the material is going anywhere near a dose — have the oil GC-MS'd once. Nose alone will separate the anise-scented (estragole/methyleugenol) types from the pennyroyal-scented (pulegone/menthone) types, and that crude test is worth doing on every seedling before you commit. Note that pulegone types carry a *different* toxicity concern (pulegone is the hepatotoxin of pennyroyal), so "avoid estragole" does not mean "choose pulegone."

Harvest aerial parts at flowering; dry fast in shade and store airtight — the medicine is the smell, and a jar that no longer smells sharply is no longer medicine. Leaf and flower oils differ substantially in composition (Haiyan et al., 2016; Gong et al., *BMC Complement Altern Med*, 2017 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-017-1605-7): leaf oil 0.57% w/w, flower 0.29%), so harvest fraction matters.

### Scientific Correlation

Graded honestly. **This is a data-sparse species clinically. There is no retrieved human trial of *Agastache rugosa* for any gastrointestinal indication.**

**Antimicrobial — MODERATE (in vitro only).** This is the best-supported activity. Xinjiang leaf oil showed MICs of 9.4–42 µg/mL across tested organisms, with antibiofilm activity (Haiyan et al., 2016 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sjbs.2016.02.020)); flower oil strongly inhibited *S. aureus* and *E. coli*, leaf oil *E. coli* (Gong et al., 2017 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-017-1605-7)); *Agastache* sp. oils showed antibacterial activity in the Romanian survey (Nechita et al., 2024 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms25105366)). Most directly relevant to the "turbid damp" reading: the oil inhibited growth, acid production, virulence-gene expression and biofilm formation of a cariogenic *Streptococcus* at ≥0.02 mg/mL, comparable at 0.04 mg/mL to 0.1% NaF (Kim et al., 2024 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules29204907)). **All in vitro; none establishes that a drinkable infusion reaches an antimicrobial concentration in a human gut.** That gap is the whole gap.

**Gastroprotection — WEAK-to-MODERATE (animal, single study).** In an EtOH/HCl-induced gastric mucosal gastritis model, treatment with *A. rugosa* extract reduced gastric damage by more than 40% versus the induction group and reduced inflammatory protein expression by about 30%; island- and inland-grown material performed similarly despite differing in trichome density and pulegone content (Song et al., *Planta Med*, 2023 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1055/a-2189-7272)). This is the only retrieved in vivo GI result and it is a chemical-injury model, not a nausea model.

**Antiemetic / gut motility — UNSUPPORTED (NULL RESULT FOR THE SEARCH).** The tradition's headline claim — *stops vomiting* — has **no retrieved supporting study in this run**, in any model, for this species. Not a negative trial; an absence. The most clinically famous action of *huo xiang* is, as of this retrieval, an untested claim. State it as tradition; do not state it as pharmacology.

**Anti-damp / anti-summer-heat as a syndrome — NOT TESTABLE AS STATED.** One adjacent finding: *A. rugosa* extract at 100–200 µg/mL increased ConA/LPS-induced proliferation of heat-stressed mouse splenic lymphocytes in vitro, as part of a four-herb anti-heat-stress prescription (Zhu et al., *Arch Pharm Res*, 2011 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12272-011-0710-2)). This is suggestive of *something* in the heat-stress space and proves nothing about the syndrome.

**Neuro/attentional — WEAK (single small human study).** The 38-volunteer EEG study above (Hong et al., 2022 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs12070238)) is real human data on real inhaled oil, but it is n=38, unreplicated, no clinical endpoint, and EEG band-power shifts are a soft proxy for cognition.

**Cytotoxicity — reported, and to be read as a warning, not a promise.** Oils showed dose- and time-dependent cytotoxicity against SGC-7901 gastric carcinoma cells (Gong et al., 2017) and *Agastache* oils were cytotoxic to MDA-MB-231, HCT116 and HepG2 lines, IC50 203.70 ± 0.24 µg/mL for one cultivar on MDA-MB-231 (Nechita et al., 2024 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms25105366)). **In vitro cytotoxicity in a phenylpropanoid-rich oil is not an anticancer finding.** It belongs in the safety column.

**Nematicidal — established, non-medical.** Oil LC50 47.3 µg/mL against *Meloidogyne incognita*, with eugenol (66.6) and methyleugenol (89.4) as active constituents (Li et al., 2013 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules18044170)). Relevant to the cultivator as a companion-planting note, not to the patient.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

| System | Classification |
|---|---|
| Chinese (as *tu huo xiang*) | Acrid (辛), slightly warm (微溫). Spleen, Stomach, Lung. Transforms damp, harmonises the middle, stops vomiting, releases summer-damp exterior |
| Korean (as *baechohyang*) | Primarily a culinary aromatic and incense material; the medical reading is imported from the Chinese frame |
| Western herbal | No independent tradition; adopted as a hardy ornamental mint and pollinator plant |
| Kenetica | **Drying, ascending-then-settling, middle-warming.** A stabiliser of the digestive axis under heat load. Explicitly *not* a state-changing herb |

### Contraindications

- **⚠️ ESTRAGOLE AND METHYLEUGENOL.** Estragole can constitute the overwhelming majority of the oil (up to ~89.5%; Hong et al., 2022) and methyleugenol up to ~50% in other chemotypes (Li et al., 2013). Both are phenylpropanoids of established genotoxic/carcinogenic concern in rodent models and subject to adverse regulatory opinion. **Do not use the concentrated essential oil internally. Do not use any form chronically at dose. Do not use in pregnancy, lactation, or in children.**
- **⚠️ PULEGONE CHEMOTYPES.** Pulegone/menthone-dominant populations (up to ~34% pulegone in flower oil, ~49% p-menthan-3-one in leaf oil; Haiyan et al., 2016) carry pennyroyal-type hepatotoxicity concern. Avoiding estragole by selecting a pulegone type substitutes one hazard for another.
- **⚠️ CHEMOTYPE UNKNOWN = RISK UNKNOWN.** Because six chemotypes exist in wild Korean populations (Hong et al., 2022), unassayed seed-grown material cannot be dose-assessed. This is a hard limit on responsible use.
- **Identity risk.** Material sold as "huo xiang" may be *Pogostemon cablin*, a different plant with different chemistry. Confirm species before use.
- **Pregnancy and lactation — avoid.** Phenylpropanoid load; no safety data.
- **Children — avoid** beyond incidental culinary quantity.
- **Lamiaceae sensitivity** — cross-reactivity possible.
- **No human clinical safety data exist for medicinal-dose *A. rugosa*.** Everything above is inference from constituent toxicology.
- **Not a substitute** for rehydration or medical assessment in acute vomiting or diarrhoea, which is the exact scenario the tradition points it at. Dehydration kills; a leaf tea does not treat it.
