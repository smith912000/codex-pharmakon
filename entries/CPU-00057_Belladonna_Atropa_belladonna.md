## *Atropa belladonna L. — Deadly Nightshade / Belladonna*
**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM
**Energetic Classification (TCM):** ☿ Shen [SPECIALIST ONLY]

⚠️ **CRITICAL SAFETY CLASSIFICATION: SPECIALIST ONLY — TOXIC AT THERAPEUTIC MARGINS**
*Belladonna is one of the most toxic plants in the European flora. The therapeutic window between medicinal and lethal dose is extremely narrow. This entry is documented for completeness of the tradition record. Modern therapeutic use requires pharmaceutical-grade preparations with precise dosing. Self-treatment is dangerous and potentially fatal. All preparations of the whole plant or uncharacterized extracts must be avoided outside supervised clinical contexts.*

### Origin & Tradition

*Atropa belladonna* takes its genus name from Atropos — the Greek Fate who cuts the thread of life. The species epithet *belladonna* ("beautiful woman" in Italian) reflects its cosmetic use — Renaissance Italian women instilled belladonna extracts into their eyes to dilate pupils, which was considered attractive. This cosmetic application inadvertently revealed the primary pharmacological action: atropine-mediated muscarinic acetylcholine receptor blockade causing mydriasis (pupil dilation). This same mechanism is exploited in modern clinical medicine for bradycardia treatment, preoperative medication, and ophthalmic procedures.

**AT — Assyrian (Banj / documented in Nineveh tablets as a narcotic and witchcraft plant):** Belladonna or related Solanaceae (henbane, mandrake, belladonna) form the core of the Assyrian narcotic-witchcraft-sedative pharmacopoeia. The Nineveh tablets document "banj" preparations (which cover multiple Solanaceae species including belladonna) for pain management, surgical anesthesia in compound preparations, and ritual/magical applications. The anticholinergic syndrome (dry mouth, flushing, hallucination, delirium) was understood as a "demonic" state induced by these preparations.

**DMM — De Materia Medica (Στρύχνος μανικός — Strychnos manikos; various interpretations):** The "mad nightshade" (*Strychnos manikos*) in Dioscorides is interpreted by most historians as belladonna or a closely related Solanaceae. He documents it as a sedative-hypnotic; to be used very cautiously (he explicitly warns of death from excess); the berry juice in wine as an amnestic; topical leaf preparations as analgesic poultices. His dosing cautions are among the most emphatic in De Materia Medica.

**CM — Canon of Medicine (بنج — Banj; related to stramonium/henbane complex):** Ibn Sina covers belladonna and its Solanaceae relatives under the *banj* (henbane/nightshade) category. His account is sophisticated: he identifies the anticholinergic syndrome (hot-dry skin, dilated pupils, delirium), distinguishes therapeutic from toxic dose, and describes the *banj* antidote preparation (hot decoction + vigorous exercise to prevent absorption, followed by vinegar emetic). His pharmacological sophistication about anticholinergic toxidrome is remarkable for the 11th century.

**SS — Ayurveda (मदन / धत्तूर — Madana / Dhattūra — related Solanaceae including *Datura metel*; belladonna proper via trade):** The anticholinergic Solanaceae group in Ayurveda centers on Datura (*Dhattūra*) rather than belladonna, but the pharmacological classification is shared. The *Mahā Yoga* preparations in Tantra and some Ayurvedic texts include controlled micro-doses of Solanaceae alkaloids for specific ritual and therapeutic contexts — always under experienced supervision. Classification: Tikta-Katu, Ushna virya, Katu vipaka, extreme Pitta-increasing.

**BM — Badianus Manuscript (related anticholinergic Solanaceae documented in Aztec medicine):** The Aztec pharmacopoeia documents *Solanaceae* species (jimsonweed, thorn apple relatives — *Datura* species) with similar pharmacological properties to belladonna. The Badianus Manuscript (1552) records anticholinergic plant preparations for pain and ritual use in Aztec medical practice.

### Active Components

- **Atropine (dl-hyoscyamine)** — the primary alkaloid (0.3–0.6% of leaf dry weight); competitive muscarinic acetylcholine receptor antagonist (all five muscarinic subtypes); pharmacological effects: mydriasis (pupil dilation); tachycardia; dry mouth; urinary retention; constipation; hyperthermia; delirium and hallucinations at higher doses; the most studied anticholinergic drug
- **Scopolamine (hyoscine)** — secondary alkaloid; more CNS-penetrant than atropine; sedative; antiemetic (motion sickness); amnestic; used clinically as preoperative sedation and transdermal motion sickness patches
- **Apoatropine, noratropine** — minor alkaloids; similar anticholinergic properties
- **Scopolin, scopoletin** — coumarins; anti-inflammatory; UV-absorbing (separate from alkaloids)

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Documented only for tradition record. Not for practitioner self-application.**

The pharmacological applications of belladonna alkaloids in modern medicine are well-established:
- Atropine: bradycardia treatment; organophosphate poisoning antidote; preoperative glycopyrrolate equivalent; ophthalmic mydriasis
- Scopolamine: motion sickness prevention; preoperative sedation; postoperative nausea
- Historically: surgical anesthesia (soporific sponge — see I_129 Henbane); pain management; ritual consciousness alteration

The anticholinergic toxidrome (hot, dry skin; dilated pupils; fever; tachycardia; urinary retention; delirium) is a medical emergency requiring immediate clinical management. The therapeutic window for belladonna whole-plant preparations is too narrow for any reasonable self-administration context.

### Preparation & Dosing

**⚠️ NO SELF-PREPARATION RECOMMENDED**

Modern pharmaceutical preparations only:
- **Atropine sulfate injection** — standardized pharmaceutical preparation; clinical use
- **Scopolamine transdermal patch** — standardized pharmaceutical preparation; motion sickness
- **Homeopathic belladonna preparations** — the extreme dilution (30C and above) renders these pharmacologically inert regarding alkaloid content; used traditionally for fever with the specific fever-symptom picture

**Historical preparations (not recommended for modern use):**
The classical "soporific sponge" preparation combining belladonna + henbane + opium + mandrake was used for surgical anesthesia in medieval European medicine. This history is documented in Entry I_129 (Henbane).

### Scientific Correlation

The pharmacology of atropine and scopolamine is among the most extensively documented in medicine:
- **Atropine pharmacology:** Marshall BE, Longnecker DE (1990): standard pharmacology reference; muscarinic receptor antagonism mechanism fully characterized; used in clinical medicine continuously since isolation in 1831
- **Anticholinergic toxidrome:** Pentel P, Peterson CD (1980): clinical characterization of anticholinergic poisoning; management principles
- **Scopolamine motion sickness:** Nachum Z et al. (2006) Cochrane review: scopolamine transdermal patch significantly effective for motion sickness prevention vs. placebo; the best-evidenced motion sickness treatment
- **Traditional dosing evidence:** The tradition record documents the lethality of overdose consistently and unambiguously — Dioscorides, Ibn Sina, and every subsequent tradition include explicit fatality warnings

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** Attested only inside the Old-World *banj* narcotic-witchcraft complex of the Nineveh tablets, where a "demonic" state is the classification — illness by causal agency, the plant defined by the agency it induces. Under the standing blocking warning, the identification of belladonna *specifically* (versus henbane or mandrake) is not secure. No cultivation status: it is a substance that opposes or induces an agency, not one that nourishes a constitution. Framed, status absent.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** Ibn Sīnā's *banj* account is pharmacologically sophisticated — it names the anticholinergic toxidrome and gives an antidote protocol — but it treats the plant as a poison to be managed, not a *mufarrih* cardiac-gladdening or longevity drug. Against the Avicennan idea of conserving a finite innate heat, a deadly narcotic sits wholly outside cultivation. Framed; no cultivation status.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *Strykhnos manikos*, the "mad nightshade," is recorded with some of the most emphatic death-warnings in *De Materia Medica*. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system to assign it to; what the tradition offers is his discipline of attention and his refusal to soften the danger. No cultivation status — the register is the warning.

**Non-tradition note (outside the tagged set).** Belladonna's best-known "cultivation" reputation — the early-modern European witchcraft and "flying-ointment" literature, and the Greek naming after Atropos, the Fate who cuts the thread — falls **outside** the six tagged traditions and is recorded here as such rather than forced into a tradition slot. It is early-modern and classical European folklore, not an attested subtle-body assignment from AT, CM, BM, DMM, SA or SS.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. Belladonna is among the most toxic plants in the European flora, with a therapeutic margin too narrow for any self-directed practice. No tradition in the tagged set assigns it a cultivation or nourish-life status; every one that records it records a poison. The register's answer is abstention.

### Contraindications

- **All routine applications** — the primary contraindication is the plant itself at full concentration for non-clinical use
- **Glaucoma** — atropine-induced mydriasis raises intraocular pressure acutely; potentially blinding in angle-closure glaucoma
- **Urinary retention / BPH** — anticholinergic blockade of bladder contraction; acute urinary retention in vulnerable individuals
- **Cardiac arrhythmia** — atropine's tachycardia can precipitate arrhythmia in susceptible hearts
- **Hot climate / fever** — anticholinergic inhibition of sweating in a hot environment = hyperthermia; potentially fatal
- **Elderly patients** — extremely sensitive to anticholinergic effects; delirium, falls risk
