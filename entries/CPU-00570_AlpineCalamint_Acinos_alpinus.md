# Alpine Calamint

## *Clinopodium alpinum* (L.) Kuntze (syn. *Acinos alpinus* (L.) Moench) | Family: Lamiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM (Dioscoridean/Mediterranean Materia Medica), CM (Continental/Central European Mountain folk practice)

**Energetic Classification:** Warm, dry, aromatic-dispersing — but see Scientific Correlation: the degree of warmth conventionally assigned to this plant rests on a chemical assumption that the retrieved data does not support.

**Training Phase:** Preparatory / Digestive — a table-side aromatic, not a therapeutic intervention.

---

### Origin & Tradition

Alpine calamint is a low, mat-forming, intensely aromatic mint of European mountain rock — limestone screes, sunny alpine and subalpine turf, dry crevices from the Pyrenees through the Alps and the Apennines to the Balkans, Sicily's Madonie, and the Anatolian highlands. It is a small plant with a large smell, and that combination is the whole of its traditional standing.

The codex must open this entry with a warning about its own subject matter. **This is one of the most data-sparse species in Volume I.** A PubMed search retrievable this run returns four records for the species and two for the genus's essential oil work — and of those, two are heavy-metal soil surveys that use the plant as a bioindicator, not a medicine. There is no clinical literature. There is no toxicology. There is no pharmacokinetics. There is barely a phytochemistry. The traditional record, likewise, is largely a record of the *group* rather than the species. What follows is therefore graded downward throughout, and the reader should treat the confident tone of most herbal sources on "calamint" as unearned when applied to this particular plant.

**Dioscoridean / Mediterranean Materia Medica (DMM).** The Greek *kalaminthē* of Dioscorides is the root of the whole calamint tradition: a warming, drying aromatic indicated for cold stomach, wind, colic, torpid digestion, and — in the classical sources — as an emmenagogue and abortifacient, a use that also attaches to pennyroyal (*Mentha pulegium*) and is the historical reason calamints carry a reputational danger. The doctrine is *dispersal of cold wind*: an aromatic that breaks up trapped gas, restarts a stalled stomach, and moves stagnation downward and out.

**[USER: verify] — the critical attestation problem.** Dioscoridean *kalaminthē* almost certainly does **not** refer to *Acinos alpinus*. The classical references are best matched to *Clinopodium nepeta* (lesser calamint) and adjacent lowland Mediterranean taxa that were actually available to Greek and Roman practice. *A. alpinus* is a high-mountain rock plant of limited accessibility and limited biomass. The codex's honest position: **this species inherits the calamint doctrine by botanical association, not by attestation.** Any source that cites Dioscorides for *Acinos alpinus* specifically is performing a substitution, and the substitution has never been validated. Flag every classical claim in this entry accordingly.

**Continental / Central European Mountain practice (CM).** The species' own defensible attestation is thinner and more local: as a mountain herb tea — gathered where it grows, in the Alps, Carpathians, Dinarides and Anatolian highlands — used as a *carminative and stomachic*, drunk after heavy food; occasionally as a mild expectorant and throat rinse for a cold; occasionally as a domestic aromatic and strewing herb. This is the genuine, modest, and probably accurate picture: a pleasant regional digestive tea. It is the herbal equivalent of an after-dinner amaro, and Kenetica files it there without embarrassment. **[USER: verify]** any specific historical dose or indication — the folk record is oral, regional, and poorly documented.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The received doctrine is *warmth against cold wind*: the aromatic mint that drives trapped gas out of a chilled belly. In the calamint group generally, that doctrine has a real and well-known chemical anchor — **pulegone**, the ketone that dominates pennyroyal and appears heavily in several *Clinopodium* oils. Pulegone is genuinely hot, genuinely spasmolytic-at-dose, genuinely emmenagogic, and genuinely hepatotoxic. It is the molecule that makes the classical calamint doctrine both true *and* dangerous.

**This entry must break that mapping.** The doctrine-to-chemistry map that every calamint source reaches for does not survive contact with the actual retrieved data for this species. According to PubMed, GC-MS of the aerial-parts essential oil of *Acinos alpinus* subsp. *nebrodensis* from central Sicily was dominated not by monoterpene ketones but by **sesquiterpene hydrocarbons, with germacrene D at 37.9%** as the single most abundant component, followed by (E)-β-caryophyllene at 5.1%; among oxygenated monoterpenes the leader was **thymol at 8.3%**, with hexadecanoic acid at 6.8% ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2021.1973465)). **Pulegone is not the story.** A germacrene D / thymol oil is a fundamentally different material from a pulegone oil — different pharmacology, different toxicology, different energetics.

So the honest doctrinal map is this. To the extent the plant is warming and antiseptic at all, that character belongs to **thymol** — a phenolic monoterpene, a real and well-characterised antimicrobial and mild spasmolytic, the active principle of thyme, and the reason the plant's folk name "rock thyme" is more chemically apt than "calamint." To the extent the oil is *not* hot, that is because germacrene D — a sesquiterpene hydrocarbon, and the actual bulk of the oil — is largely a plant-defence and insect-signalling compound with no meaningful warming or carminative pharmacology in humans. **The plant is chemically closer to a thyme than to a pennyroyal, and its doctrine has been inherited from the wrong relative.**

The codex therefore **deflates** three doctrines here:
1. **That *A. alpinus* is a strong emmenagogue or abortifacient.** This claim travels with the calamint name and the pennyroyal association. The retrieved chemistry gives no pulegone-based reason to believe it for this species. Do not repeat it as fact — and do not, on the strength of its deflation, treat the plant as proven safe in pregnancy either. Absence of a mechanism is not evidence of safety when there is no toxicology at all.
2. **That it is a potent digestive medicine.** It is a pleasant aromatic tea. There is no evidence of therapeutic potency.
3. **That the Dioscoridean pedigree applies.** It does not, absent attestation.

One caveat the codex must state against itself: the cited GC-MS is of **subsp. *nebrodensis* from a single Sicilian population** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2021.1973465)). Lamiaceae essential oils are notoriously chemotype-variable across geography, altitude, and season. A Pyrenean or Anatolian population may differ substantially, and a pulegone-rich chemotype somewhere in the range cannot be excluded. What can be said is that the pulegone assumption is **not supported by the data we actually have**, and an assumption unsupported is not a fact.

### Active Components

Sparse, and to be read strictly.

- **Germacrene D — 37.9%** of the aerial-parts essential oil in the Sicilian subsp. *nebrodensis* sample ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2021.1973465)). The dominant constituent. A sesquiterpene hydrocarbon; ecologically an insect semiochemical and defence compound. It is not a carminative and does not account for the traditional indication.
- **(E)-β-Caryophyllene — 5.1%** (same source). A dietary sesquiterpene with a genuine and interesting pharmacology in the wider literature — but at 5.1% of a minor oil in a tea, its contribution here is negligible. Do not import caryophyllene's literature into this entry as if it were this plant's activity.
- **Thymol — 8.3%**, the leading oxygenated monoterpene (same source). The most plausible carrier of whatever antiseptic and mild spasmolytic character the plant has. The reason "rock thyme" is the better common name.
- **Hexadecanoic acid (palmitic acid) — 6.8%** (same source). A fatty acid; pharmacologically inert here; its presence at this level is a signal about extraction and plant part, not about activity.
- **Polyphenols, flavonoids and tannins.** According to PubMed, a comparative study of methanol extracts of six *Acinos* species from Serbia and Montenegro found that **the highest antioxidant activity of the set was in the *A. alpinus* extract, which had high levels of all polyphenol classes examined** (total flavonoids, polyphenols, tannins; FRAP and DPPH assays); the *A. alpinus* extract, along with *A. suaveolens* and *A. majoranifolius*, was characterised by high monoterpene content, and the authors concluded it could be a possible source of antioxidant and antimicrobial compounds ([PMID 25026735](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25026735/) — no DOI is registered for this record). **This is the strongest positive datum in the entry and it is an in-vitro assay of a methanol extract, not a medicine.**
- **Pulegone — NOT DEMONSTRATED.** Explicitly recorded here as a negative finding. No retrieved analysis of this species shows it as a significant constituent.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Kenetica files alpine calamint as a **minor preparatory aromatic** — the lowest tier of the digestive class. It is a warm, pleasant, faintly thymic infusion taken after food, for the same reason and with roughly the same expectation as any table-side aromatic: it makes the diner feel better and it slightly encourages the machinery. It is not a treatment for any condition, and any practitioner reaching for it as one has misjudged the material.

Its actual value in the training sequence is **pedagogical**, and this is why it earns an entry at all. Alpine calamint is the codex's standing case study in *doctrinal inheritance by name*. A student who learns "calamint = pulegone = hot, dispersing, emmenagogue, hepatotoxic-at-dose" and applies that block to every plant carrying the calamint name will be wrong about this one in every particular. The genus name did the reasoning; the plant was never consulted. Kenetica uses this entry to make the point that **the binomial is not a pharmacology**, and that a nomenclatural neighbour is not a chemical one. The correct response to a data-sparse species is not to borrow the confident answer from its relatives — it is to say that you do not know.

**As a cultivator's material:** a straightforward and genuinely rewarding rock-garden plant, and one of the few in Volume I that is easier to grow than to source.

- **Habitat to replicate:** limestone. Sharp drainage is non-negotiable — gritty, alkaline-to-neutral, low-fertility substrate; a scree bed, trough, or crevice planting. It rots in winter wet faster than it dies of drought or cold.
- **Aspect:** full sun. In shade it stretches, and the oil — already modest — thins further.
- **Propagation:** seed sown in autumn and exposed to winter cold germinates readily; softwood cuttings in early summer root without difficulty. Self-sows gently in suitable grit.
- **Feeding:** do not. Rich soil produces lax growth and weaker aromatics. This is a plant that concentrates under stress.
- **Harvest:** aerial parts at flowering, which is when the cited oil analysis was performed ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2021.1973465)) and therefore the only phenological point for which we have any chemistry at all. Dry in shade, fast, with air movement; the volatiles are the point and heat destroys them.
- **⚠️ Site selection — a real and documented hazard.** This species is used in the literature as a **heavy-metal bioindicator**, and it accumulates. According to PubMed, in a post-wildfire survey on Vidlič Mountain, Serbia, *Acinos alpinus* showed higher phytoaccumulation of **zinc and cadmium** in burnt-over areas ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chemosphere.2011.05.048)); and in a survey around the Uludağ tungsten mine in Bursa, Turkey, *A. alpinus* was among alpine species whose Mg, Mn, Fe and Zn contents were measurably altered by mining activity, with element content shown to be species-specific ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0269-7491(02)00177-x)). **Do not wildcraft this plant from mineralised ground, mine spoil, old smelting districts, burnt sites, or roadsides.** Cadmium is the specific concern. Cultivate on known-clean substrate.

### Scientific Correlation

Graded honestly. The overall grade for this species is **traditional**, with two thin in-vitro anchors and one important negative.

- **Essential-oil composition — SUPPORTED, but single-population.** Germacrene D 37.9%, thymol 8.3%, (E)-β-caryophyllene 5.1%, hexadecanoic acid 6.8%, in subsp. *nebrodensis* from central Sicily, by GC-MS ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2021.1973465)). One study, one subspecies, one locality. The authors themselves framed the work chemotaxonomically and noted that the genus's botanical placement is "quite problematic" given disagreement among botanists and a thicket of synonyms — which is itself a warning about how much of the literature on "*Acinos*" can be safely pooled.
- **Pulegone as the active principle — NOT SUPPORTED. Recorded as a NULL.** This is the entry's most important finding. The assumption is standard in secondary sources and is not borne out by the retrieved chemistry. The oil is sesquiterpene-dominant. Any pharmacology, energetics, or toxicology built on pulegone for this species is built on nothing.
- **Antioxidant activity — WEAK (in vitro only).** *A. alpinus* methanol extract had the highest antioxidant activity among six *Acinos* species tested, with high levels of all polyphenol classes measured, by FRAP and DPPH ([PMID 25026735](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25026735/); no DOI registered). FRAP and DPPH are chemical assays in a tube. They do not demonstrate an effect in a body. This is a reason to look further, not a health claim.
- **Antimicrobial activity — WEAK / PRELIMINARY (in vitro only).** The same study screened methanol extracts by disk diffusion against a panel of bacteria (including *S. aureus*, *E. coli*, *P. aeruginosa*, *K. pneumoniae*) and fungi (*A. niger*, *C. albicans*, *S. cerevisiae*), and the authors' own language was that results were "preliminary" and that the extract "could be a possible source" of such compounds ([PMID 25026735](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25026735/)). Disk diffusion on a crude methanol extract is a screening result, not evidence of clinical antimicrobial activity. The thymol content ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2021.1973465)) makes some activity mechanistically unsurprising.
- **Carminative / stomachic effect — TRADITIONAL ONLY. NO DATA.** Not a single study retrieved addresses the plant's one traditional indication. Grade: traditional.
- **Emmenagogue / abortifacient — TRADITIONAL, INHERITED, AND MECHANISTICALLY UNSUPPORTED.** See the doctrinal deflation above. Neither confirmed nor safely refuted.
- **Toxicology — ABSENT.** No acute toxicity study, no hepatic assessment, no reproductive toxicology. There is simply nothing.
- **Heavy-metal accumulation — SUPPORTED, and the most practically actionable science on this plant.** Zn and Cd phytoaccumulation post-fire ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chemosphere.2011.05.048)); mining-altered element content at Uludağ ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0269-7491(02)00177-x)). The best-evidenced fact about alpine calamint is where *not* to pick it.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Conventionally: **warm and dry, second degree; aromatic, carminative, dispersing** — the standard calamint assignment, descending from Dioscoridean *kalaminthē* through the mediaeval herbals.

Kenetica **revises this downward**. The classical assignment presumes a pulegone-type hot ketonic oil, which this species does not appear to have. Against a germacrene D / thymol profile ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2021.1973465)), the defensible classification is **mildly warm, dry, gently aromatic — first degree at most**, with what warmth exists attributable to thymol rather than to any ketone. It disperses lightly. It does not drive.

Filed energetically alongside thyme rather than alongside pennyroyal. The reclassification is the entry's substantive contribution.

### Contraindications

Stated with the honesty the evidence demands: **there is no toxicological literature for this species. Every item below is precautionary reasoning, not established risk.** The absence of documented harm reflects the absence of study, not the presence of safety.

- **Pregnancy — AVOID.** The calamint group's traditional emmenagogue/abortifacient reputation is unresolved for this species. The pulegone mechanism is not demonstrated here, but no reproductive toxicology exists either. Both the traditional warning and the modern data are unfit to clear it. Do not use.
- **Lactation — AVOID.** No data.
- **Infants and young children — AVOID.** No data; thymol-bearing aromatics warrant caution in this group generally.
- **Essential oil, internal use — DO NOT.** No dose, no toxicology, no characterised chemotype range.
- **Lamiaceae sensitivity** — cross-reactivity with thyme, oregano, mint is plausible.
- **Heavy-metal exposure — a real, documented risk of the wild material.** Zn/Cd accumulation is demonstrated ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chemosphere.2011.05.048)); element uptake responds to local mineralisation ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0269-7491(02)00177-x)). This is the plant's best-evidenced hazard and it is entirely a sourcing problem. Provenance is the contraindication.
- **Misidentification** — the *Acinos*/*Clinopodium* complex is taxonomically unstable, with the genus's placement described in the literature as "quite problematic" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2021.1973465)). Material sold as "calamint" may be any of several species with materially different chemistry. Verify the plant, not the label.
- **Therapeutic substitution** — do not use in place of an evidenced treatment for any condition. It is a tea.
