# Aloe Vera — صبار (Ṣabbār) / Ἀλόη (Aloē) / घृतकुमारी (Ghṛtakumārī) / 芦荟 (Lúhuì)
## *Aloe vera (L.) Burm.f. (syn. A. barbadensis Mill.) — Aloe Vera; A. ferox Mill. — Cape Aloe*
**Tradition Tags:** EP · AT · DMM · CM · SS · SBJ · FMT · BM
**Energetic Classification (TCM):** ◉ Qi · ◈ Jing

### Origin & Tradition

Aloe vera is documented in all eight major ancient medical traditions — the widest tradition coverage of any plant in the Codex. The universality of its use reflects genuinely broad pharmacological activity: the clear inner gel (containing acemannan, polyphenols) provides wound-healing and anti-inflammatory effects; the yellow latex layer (containing barbaloin/aloin anthraquinones) provides the cathartic effect. These two fractions have completely different therapeutic indications and safety profiles — a distinction that all eight traditions partially recognized through practical experience (the "bitter aloe" as the purgative versus the "aloe gel" as the wound remedy). Modern pharmacology has provided the molecular explanation for what ancient practitioners observed empirically across eight independent medical systems.

**EP — Ebers Papyrus (wound preparation):** Aloe appears in the Ebers Papyrus in wound-healing and skin preparations. The gel application for burns and wounds is the primary documented use — consistent with the acemannan wound-healing mechanism now confirmed by clinical RCTs.

**AT — Assyrian (Alû in Nineveh tablets):** Aloe preparations in Assyrian texts for skin conditions, wound healing, and as a cathartic. The Assyrian medical tradition documents both the topical and internal applications.

**DMM — De Materia Medica (Ἀλόη — Aloē):** Dioscorides covers aloe in Book III. He documents: the dried juice (the bitter aloin latex) as a cathartic and bitter digestive tonic; wound healing; anti-hemorrhagic (staunches bleeding — the astringent and coagulation-supporting effect); the eye preparation for conjunctivitis; and the hair-loss treatment. He notes the difference between the dried juice preparation and the fresh gel — an implicit recognition of the two-fraction pharmacology.

**CM — Canon of Medicine (صبر — Ṣabr / Aloe):** Ibn Sina covers aloe extensively. He classifies the dried juice as hot and dry in the second degree. Primary applications: cathartic (digestive purgation); anti-hemorrhagic; liver tonic; wound healing; and the specific headache treatment (aloe latex suppository — rectal administration to avoid gastric irritation, a sophistication acknowledging the GI side effects of oral anthraquinones). He also notes the topical hair application for alopecia.

**SS — Sushruta Samhita (Ghṛtakumārī — "the young woman filled with butter/ghee" — the unctuous gel):** *Ghṛtakumārī* is one of the most important Ayurvedic medicinal plants. Classification: Tikta-Madhura rasa, Sheeta virya, Tridosha shamaka (pacifies all three doshas). Primary applications: Jīvanīya (life-giving rasayana); hepatoprotective; digestive (both laxative and tonic, depending on dose); wound healing; Tvak (skin); gynecological tonic (the rasayana application for female reproductive health). The classical preparation *Kumāryāsava* (aloe vera fermented wine) is one of the most important Ayurvedic hepatic-reproductive tonics.

**SBJ — Bencao Gangmu (芦荟 — Lúhuì):** Li Shizhen documents aloe for: killing intestinal worms (the anthraquinone mechanism); calming Liver Fire; clearing Heat; cooling blood; treating fungal skin conditions; the classic liver-supporting bitter tonic. TCM classification: bitter, cold; Liver-Stomach-Large Intestine channel. The *Dāng Guī Lúhuì Wán* formula uses aloe for Liver-Fire patterns (the combination of bitter cold + blood-nourishing Angelica).

**FMT — Four Medical Tantras (Tibetan — aloe as imported Indian medicine):** Aloe preparations appear in Tibetan medicine as an imported herb from India, used for liver and digestive conditions. The bitter-cold classification parallels the TCM designation.

**BM — Badianus Manuscript (Aztec):** Aloe-like plants (though likely *Agave* species native to the Americas, with convergent pharmacology) appear in Aztec medical preparations for burns, wounds, and skin conditions — a remarkable convergent ethnobotanical parallel to Old World aloe use.

### Active Components

**Gel fraction (inner parenchyma — wound healing, anti-inflammatory):**
- **Acemannan (acetylated mannose polymer)** — the primary gel polysaccharide; stimulates macrophage activity and cytokine production (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α) → wound debridement; direct wound healing via fibroblast proliferation stimulation; immunomodulatory (used in veterinary oncology as adjunct); the mechanism of the traditional wound-healing application
- **Aloesin, aloin-B** (minor gel components) — anti-inflammatory; inhibit thromboxane B2 and prostaglandin E2; contribute to the topical anti-inflammatory activity
- **Polyphenols, flavonoids** — antioxidant; UV-protective; the skin photoprotective mechanism
- **Glycoproteins (aloctin A, B)** — anti-inflammatory; wound healing; lectins that stimulate immune cell activity

**Latex fraction (yellow sap between rind and gel — cathartic):**
- **Barbaloin (aloin A), isobarbaloin (aloin B)** — anthraquinone glycosides; prodrug: converted by gut bacteria to aloe-emodin → stimulates enteric nervous system → increases intestinal motility → catharsis; the mechanism identical to senna (I_216) anthraquinone pathway
- **Aloe-emodin** — the active metabolite; anti-cancer (apoptosis induction in cancer cell lines — in vitro); anti-viral (herpes simplex inhibition); potent cathartic; the compound of concern for chronic use (see contraindications)
- **Chrysophanol** — minor anthraquinone; additional cathartic and antimicrobial activity

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**1. Topical wound healing and burn treatment ([RESET] — skin injury recovery)**
Acemannan's wound-healing activity is among the most robustly clinically validated topical applications in botanical medicine. Prospective RCTs confirm that aloe vera gel significantly accelerates partial-thickness burn healing, post-surgical wound healing, and radiation dermatitis recovery. For practitioners with skin injuries from training (abrasion, friction burns, minor wounds), topical aloe vera gel is the evidence-based first-line botanical treatment.

**2. Hepatoprotective rasayana ([BUILD] — liver support)**
The Ayurvedic *Ghṛtakumārī* rasayana application for liver health is supported by modern pharmacology: aloe vera gel polyphenols protect hepatocytes from oxidative damage; aloesin inhibits COX-mediated hepatic inflammation; the *Kumāryāsava* preparation is specifically formulated for hepatic and gynecological tonic effect. For practitioners recovering from high metabolic-stress training phases or pharmacological supplementation loads, whole-leaf aloe vera (gel fraction only, depurinated) provides food-grade hepatoprotection.

**3. Digestive and bowel regulation (gel fraction — gentle laxative; not the latex)**
The non-anthraquinone gel preparation (decolorized, depurinated aloe — the "high molecular weight fraction") has documented anti-IBS activity through different mechanisms than the anthraquinone latex: reduces intestinal inflammation, normalizes gut motility through enteric nervous system modulation. This is distinct from the cathartic latex application.

**4. Sunburn and UV protection (topical)**
Aloe vera polyphenols and polysaccharides reduce UV-induced skin inflammation and accelerate recovery from UV damage. Confirmed by multiple clinical trials for post-UV exposure application and photodermatitis treatment.

### Preparation & Dosing

**Topical gel (wound healing / burns — standard application):**
Apply 100% aloe vera gel liberally to affected area 3–4x/day; keep moist; do not cover with occlusive dressing except for large wounds. Use stabilized whole-leaf gel or fresh-cut inner leaf gel. The most evidence-based preparation.

**Inner fillet gel drink (digestive/hepatic — Ayurvedic application):**
30–50mL pure inner fillet aloe gel (not whole leaf, not containing anthraquinones) in water or juice; 1–2x/day before meals. The food-grade hepatoprotective and digestive application. Duration: up to 12 weeks; reassess.

**Depurinated standardized extract (IBS application):**
300mg aloe vera extract (standardized, decolorized — anthraquinone <10ppm per EU standard) 2x/day; the dose with clinical evidence for IBS symptom improvement (Hutchings HA et al. study).

**Latex/whole-leaf juice cathartic — SHORT TERM ONLY:**
100–200mg aloin equivalent (whole-leaf juice); maximum 10 days; not for chronic constipation management. Anthraquinone cathartic application — same protocols as senna (I_216).

### Scientific Correlation

- **Wound healing (acemannan):** Maenthaisong R et al. (2007) *Burns* 33:713-718: systematic review of 4 RCTs confirmed aloe vera significantly reduced burn wound healing time vs. conventional dressing (mean 8.79 days faster); acemannan fibroblast-stimulating mechanism confirmed
- **Radiation dermatitis:** Heggie S et al. (2002) *Cancer Nurs* 25:442-451: aloe vera gel significantly reduced acute radiation dermatitis scores in breast cancer patients receiving radiotherapy vs. aqueous cream in RCT (n=225)
- **IBS (depurinated extract):** Hutchings HA et al. (2011) *Int J Clin Pract* 65:560-568: aloe vera extract (standardized) significantly reduced IBS symptom severity vs. placebo in double-blind RCT; intestinal anti-inflammatory mechanism
- **Hepatoprotective:** Chandan BK et al. (2007) *J Ethnopharmacol* 113:175-179: aloe vera gel extract significantly reduced liver enzyme elevation (ALT, AST) and hepatic lipid peroxidation in paracetamol-induced hepatotoxicity model; mechanism via NRF2-mediated antioxidant enzyme upregulation
- **Anthraquinone genotoxicity (safety reference):** EFSA (2013) *EFSA Journal* 11:3409: European Food Safety Authority systematic review of aloe anthraquinones; aloe-emodin demonstrated genotoxic and carcinogenic activity in animal studies; established ADI and maximum aloin content for food-grade preparations (10mg/kg/day maximum)

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese (TCM).** The *san pin* 三品 grading of the *Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing* is a cultivation hierarchy in its own terms: the superior class comprises drugs that "mainly nourish life and correspond to heaven... they are nontoxic," as against the lower class which attacks disease on a defined course. Aloe (蘆薈 *lú huì*) carries **no *san pin* grade in the sources retrieved** — it is an import that entered the Chinese materia medica through the later compilations rather than the Han-period classic, and no grade could be confirmed from the classic itself. It therefore holds a therapeutic standing in this tradition and not a cultivation one. Nothing here supports *yang sheng* 養生 use, and the anthraquinone fraction argues actively against it.

**Ayurveda (SS).** *Ghṛtakumārī* / *Kumārī* is securely attested in the Indian materia medica, and the entry above records its *dravyaguṇa* profile. Whether it carries formal *rasāyana* status in the Sushrutan or Charakan divisions — as distinct from being used restoratively — **could not be confirmed from named scholarship in the sources retrieved**, and it is recorded here as unattested rather than asserted. *Ojas* is not claimed for it by any source consulted; the term is not to be attached to this plant on the strength of the *rasāyana* label alone.

**Tibetan (FMT).** No *bcud len* (essence-extraction) attestation for aloe was found. Per the Four Tantras' own architecture, medicine is the third of four treatment branches — it supports practice without reaching the root, which is the three poisons underlying the *nyes pa*. Aloe sits in the third branch or nowhere; **no formal category of "substances for subtle-body practice" exists in this system** and none is asserted here.

**Unani / Greco-Arabic (CM).** The governing cultivation idea in this tradition is that innate heat (*ḥarārat gharīziyya*) continuously consumes radical moisture (*ruṭūbat gharīziyya*) as a flame consumes the oil of a lamp — a **finite endowment**, so ageing *is* its depletion and longevity practice is **conservation, not acquisition** (see the analysis of innate heat in Avicenna's *Canon*, Springer, 2025). Ṣabir (aloes) is attested as a purgative and bitter within this pharmacology. **No claim on the *ruḥ* or on the conservation of innate heat is attested for it**, and a strongly evacuant drug sits awkwardly with a conservational project — that tension is the honest reading, not a hidden endorsement.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides has **no subtle-body system**, and none is manufactured here. What his text offers a cultivator is real but different: a **discipline of attention** — provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, and the detection of adulteration, which Riddle identifies as central to his method and which the text applies particularly to drugs not grown locally (Riddle, *Dioscorides on Pharmacy and Medicine*, Univ. of Texas Press, 1985; Beck's translation, Olms-Weidmann). Aloes, an imported resin traded over long distances, falls squarely in that class of goods. The specific wording of his aloe chapter was **not verified directly here**, so no test or provenance ranking is quoted.

**Egyptian (EP).** No cultivation framing is attested. The corpus is indication-organised — it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the *ka*, *ba* or *ib* — and substance identifications in the medical papyri remain modern hypotheses. The widely repeated claim that aloe was an **embalming or funerary material is not supported**: molecular characterisation of Egyptian funerary wrappings and animal mummies identifies conifer resin, plant gum, plant oils and animal fats, aromatic extracts, petroleum and beeswax, with no aloe among them (Buckley et al., *PLOS ONE*; Kom Mereh gazelle-mummy analysis, 2022). *Heka* — where spell and drug are one intervention — is a genuine framing in this tradition, but it is not attested for this plant.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (AT).** No cultivation framing is attested. The blocking warning on substance identifications, tablet references and deity attributions is in force, and illness in this corpus is classified by causal agency (divine hand, ghost, oath) rather than by a substance's effect on a vital constitution. Nothing is asserted for aloe here.

**Nahua / Badianus (BM).** No cultivation framing is attested. Aloe is an Old World plant, and the Badianus manuscript is itself **naturalistic — it does not contain the *tonalli* / *teyolia* / *ihiyotl* model**, which is reconstructed from later ethnohistory (López Austin). Importing that model onto this plant would be doubly unfounded.

**Practitioner's note.** For a cultivator the honest answer is mostly *nothing*. Aloe is a wound-healer, a demulcent and — in its latex fraction — a purgative, and none of the eight traditions tagged here grants it a cultivation status that survived checking. Use the gel externally and short-term internally for a stated indication; the anthraquinone fraction is a defined-course drug that the traditions' own logic, and the modern safety data, say to stop. The one durable practice this plant supports is the Dioscoridean one: know where your material came from, know which fraction you have, and test it.

### Contraindications

- **Anthraquinone fraction (aloin/whole leaf) — long-term use contraindicated** — chronic anthraquinone use causes melanosis coli (harmless but indicates long-term exposure); possible association with colorectal cancer risk (EFSA review); the EU food standard requires <10ppm aloin in food-grade products; do not use whole-leaf latex products for chronic constipation
- **Pregnancy** — anthraquinone fraction is uterotonic and potentially teratogenic; avoid whole-leaf preparations and latex during pregnancy; inner fillet gel (depurinated) is generally considered safe but therapeutic doses should be avoided in first trimester
- **Topical allergy** — approximately 1% of individuals develop contact allergy to aloe (sensitization to anthraquinone fraction or gel polyphenols); patch test before large-area application
- **Antidiabetic drug interaction** — aloe vera gel has documented hypoglycemic activity; additive with metformin and insulin; blood glucose monitoring required
- **Electrolyte imbalance (cathartic fraction)** — anthraquinone-induced diarrhea causes potassium depletion; critical interaction with digoxin (hypokalemia potentiates digitalis toxicity) and with diuretics
