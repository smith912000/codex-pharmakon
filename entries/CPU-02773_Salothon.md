# Salothon — salūthūn (سلوثون), "the Syrians' spider plant" (عنكبوت)

## *A wild edible herb with an acrid, bitter taste, whose milky juice is a strong emetic; Avicenna quotes Dioscorides and reports that the Syrians call it 'ankabūt; the species is not identified; confidence low* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** CM

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** The root juice is recorded as producing excessive vomiting. This entry is a historical record only. Nobody should take this plant.

**Named only by Avicenna's *Canon* among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. Book II, entry *salūthūn*, Arabic text from line 16390 of `Sources/CM_Avicenna/text/IbnSina_Qanun_OpenITI_Shamela0010706.txt`. A search of the other texts on disk was not run beyond the spine check. 

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** What it is, "Dioscorides said": "some people claimed that *salūtūn* is a plant which the people of Syria (*al-Shām*) call *al-ʿankabūt*; its leaf is like the white [kind] of *khāmālāʾūn* (chamaeleon); it is eaten when fresh with salt and oil after it has been boiled." Digestive organs: "if a *mithqāl* of its milk (*laban*) or its tear (*dam‘a*) extracted from its root is drunk with honey-water, it makes one vomit excessively in the day." A second description (the file marks it "what it is" again): "a wild herb; its taste inclines to acridity with something of bitterness in it; it is eaten raw and cooked." Excretory organs: "it loosens the belly" (مسهل البطن).

**Name.** *ʿAnkabūt* is the Arabic word for spider; the text gives no further description.

**Identity doubt.** The Canon calls it "a plant", the comparison is with chamaeleon (itself not identified here), and no species can be named. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

None is given. A plant with a milky juice that provokes vomiting contains something irritant; the text names nothing.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Emetic.** The text records a boiled leaf eaten with salt and oil, and a root latex that provokes violent vomiting. The Codex does not recommend either.

### Preparation & Dosing

The leaf is boiled, then eaten with salt and oil. The latex or tear from the root: one *mithqāl* in honey-water. This is the Canon's figure, quoted as it stands, and is a dose of an emetic latex; it is not advice and must not be used. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General fact only: milky latex from wild herbs is commonly irritant. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

Little to map: the entry names two actions and no organ for either. Under digestive organs, a mithqal of the milk or tear from the root, drunk with honey-water, is said to make one vomit excessively during the day. Under excretory organs, the herb is said to loosen the belly (musahhil al-batn). Its taste inclines to acridity with some bitterness, and it is eaten both raw and cooked, or boiled and eaten with salt and oil. No temperament, humour, or degree of heat or dryness is printed. The Canon names the Syrian name 'ankabut and compares the leaf with the white kind of chamaeleon, but gives no body system beyond the emetic and purgative actions. The plant is not identified. The root latex is marked as a toxicity caution, with excessive vomiting and dehydration as risks, and the entry says nobody should take it.

### Contraindications

- Emetic latex: excessive vomiting, dehydration. Do not use.
- The plant is not identified; do not eat wild herbs on the strength of this entry.
