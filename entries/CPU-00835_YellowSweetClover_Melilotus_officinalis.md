# Yellow Sweet Clover — Melilot · King's Clover · Hay-scented Clover · Corona Regia

## *Melilotus officinalis* (L.) Lam. | Family: Fabaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM
**Energetic Classification:** Warm and dry, mollient and discutient (Galenic/Unani) — a softening, resolving drug for congested swellings
**Training Phase:** Intermediate — a venous/lymphatic conditioning herb carrying a real anticoagulant hazard

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian & Mesopotamian record (SBJ).** Sweet-smelling clovers belong to the oldest stratum of Near-Eastern herbal practice. The Assyrian cuneiform medical tablets record aromatic meadow herbs used in poultices and fomentations for swellings and painful joints; *Melilotus*, with its unmistakable new-mown-hay fragrance, sits in this tradition of softening, resolving external applications laid over congested or inflamed tissue.

**Avicenna & the Unani inheritance (AT).** Under the name *iklīl al-malik* ("the king's crown/garland") melilot is a well-defined drug of the *Qanun* lineage. Classed as warm and dry, mollient (softening) and discutient (dispersing), it was applied as a plaster or poultice to disperse hard swellings, soothe inflamed eyes, ease headache, and relax spasm and colic. The garland name reflects both the arching flower-racemes and the drug's esteem.

**Dioscorides & the Greek materia medica (DMM).** Dioscorides describes *melilōtos* and its use in emollient cataplasms for inflammations and gatherings — of the stomach, eyes, and other soft-tissue swellings — typically combined with wine or other bases. This softening-and-resolving indication is the through-line of the Mediterranean tradition and matches the modern venous/lymphatic reputation of the plant.

Across all three lineages the practical signature is consistent: an aromatic legume used externally and internally to **soften, disperse, and drain congested swellings** — the traditional ancestor of today's coumarin-based venotonic and anti-oedema use.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The plant's sweetness *is* its chemistry: the hay scent that named it (*mel* = honey) is coumarin volatilising as the herb dries. That same coumarin is the doctrinal hinge — a fragrance of resolution that, if the drying goes wrong and the herb moulds, transmutes into **dicoumarol**, an anticoagulant that thins blood to the point of haemorrhage. Melilot therefore teaches the Kenetica doctrine of *the remedy that spoils into a poison*: the drug and its danger are the same molecule at different stages.

### Active Components

- **Coumarin** — the dominant fragrant constituent, released on drying/wilting; the basis of the venotonic, anti-oedema and lymphagogue reputation.
- **Melilotoside** (o-coumaric acid glucoside) and related hydroxycinnamic precursors that liberate coumarin.
- **Dicoumarol** — NOT present in the fresh, sound herb; formed by mould/fungal action on spoiled coumarin-rich hay. The classic anticoagulant.
- Flavonoids, melilotin, rutin-type flavonol glycosides, and a small volatile-oil fraction.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Melilot is Kenetica's **venous–lymphatic conditioning herb**: traditionally and pharmacologically oriented toward reducing high-protein oedema, supporting venous return, and dispersing soft-tissue swelling and bruising. It is deployed in the intermediate phase for lymphatic sluggishness and venous heaviness, always paired with the standing caution that its active principle is a bleeding-risk molecule. It is not a first-line herb for anyone on anticoagulants or with bleeding risk, and it must always be sound, well-dried, mould-free material.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed: an experimental study of *Melilotus* extract on high-protein (thermal/burn) oedema in rats (Nishikawa et al., *Nihon Yakurigaku Zasshi*, 1983; PMID **6862319** — no DOI registered in PubMed) found that the coumarin-containing extract markedly reduced swelling and prevented necrosis, apparently by mobilising phagocytic cells rather than by simple lymphatic drainage — a mechanistic correlate of the traditional anti-oedema/discutient use. A more recent in-vivo study of coumarin-rich *Melilotus indicus* extract (Saleem et al., *Inflammopharmacology*, 2020) demonstrated significant, dose-dependent anti-inflammatory activity across carrageenan-, histamine-, serotonin- and formaldehyde-induced oedema models with downregulation of serum TNF-α, plus anti-angiogenic activity and a high LD50 (>8000 mg/kg for the extract) ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10787-020-00703-9)).

**Evidence grade: TRADITIONAL / PRECLINICAL.** Anti-oedema and anti-inflammatory effects are supported by animal pharmacology consistent with the coumarin mechanism; robust human RCT data for *Melilotus officinalis* specifically remain limited. The **anticoagulant hazard of spoiled material is historically well established** (the "sweet clover disease" of cattle that led to the discovery of dicoumarol and, ultimately, warfarin).

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic/Unani (as iklīl al-malik):** warm and dry; mollient (softening), discutient (dispersing/resolving), mildly analgesic and antispasmodic.
- **Dioscoridean/Greek:** emollient, resolving; for inflammations and gatherings via cataplasm.
- (No canonical TCM channel-entry or Ayurvedic rasa attribution is asserted here — the plant's documented tradition in this Codex is Assyrian–Unani–Greek.)

### Contraindications

⚠️ **BLEEDING HAZARD — FLAG IN FULL.**

- **"Sweet clover disease" / dicoumarol risk.** When coumarin-rich melilot spoils or moulds, coumarin is converted to **dicoumarol**, a potent vitamin-K-antagonist anticoagulant. Ingestion of spoiled sweet clover (historically as mouldy hay by cattle) causes internal and external **haemorrhage and can be fatal**. **Only sound, properly dried, mould-free material may ever be used. Discard any melilot that is damp, discoloured, or off-smelling.**
- **Anticoagulant / antiplatelet interaction.** Do not combine with warfarin, heparins, DOACs, aspirin, clopidogrel, or other agents affecting haemostasis — additive/synergistic bleeding risk. This is the single most important interaction.
- **Bleeding disorders and perioperative use.** Contraindicated in haemophilia and other bleeding tendencies; discontinue well before surgery or dental extraction.
- **Coumarin hepatotoxicity.** High-dose or prolonged coumarin exposure carries a risk of reversible hepatotoxicity in susceptible individuals; avoid in hepatic impairment and monitor with standardised products.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** avoid — anticoagulant and safety data are insufficient.
- **Watch for signs of bleeding:** unusual bruising, gum or nose bleeds, blood in urine/stool, prolonged bleeding from cuts — stop immediately and seek care.
