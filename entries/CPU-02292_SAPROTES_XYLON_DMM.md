# SAPROTES XYLON — rotten stuff of old wood (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 1-112)

## *The meal-like rotten matter gathered out of old wood and the stocks of trees; the translator's heading gloss is "Dry Rot". Identification: decayed wood (a wood-mould or powdery rot), as the text itself describes it; no organism, genus or species is named by Dioscorides or by the translator, and "dry rot" should not be read as a fungal species. Basis: the chapter's own description. Confidence: medium for the material, low for anything finer* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder taken from the Dioscorides name list; the first-pass check on 30 October proposed to drop it as a misreading, and the audit disagreed: *Saprotes xylon* is a real chapter heading. The audit is right and the row stands. No other text on disk was found to name it, and none is claimed here.

**Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica* (DMM).** The chapter is 1-112 in the numbering of the English edition on disk (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 4370, with the text on the following lines; name index `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` row for *SAPROTES XYLON*, "1-112 (line 4370)"). The heading is followed by the translator's gloss "SUGGESTED: Dry Rot". The whole chapter reads: "The rotten stuff like meal which is gathered out of old wood and stocks of trees cleans ulcers and brings them to scar when it is laid on them. It also stops serpentia [?snakebite] kneaded together with the same amount of anise [3-65] and wine, as well as pounded into small pieces, put into linen and applied." The bracketed "?snakebite" and the cross-reference to anise (3-65) are the translator's. The word *serpentia* is printed as in the translation; the sense of "serpentia" is the translator's query, and no more is claimed.

What the chapter gives, then, is three things only: the **material** (the "rotten stuff like meal", that is, a powdery decay gathered out of old wood and from the stocks or stumps of trees), the **use on ulcers** (it "cleans ulcers and brings them to scar" when laid on them), and the **use on a condition the translator queries as snakebite** (kneaded with an equal amount of anise and wine, or pounded and applied in linen). The chapter gives no source tree, no habitat, no season, no taste or colour and no test of quality. The sources on disk do not say which woods the decay is to come from.

The chapter stands in Book 1 between *Ptelea* (1-111) and *Aigeiros* (1-113); the neighbours are tree products, and the sequence is the likely reason it is filed with trees. That is an observation about the order of chapters, not an explanation by Dioscorides. [USER: verify the neighbours against the printed edition]

### Active Components

Dioscorides names no constituent. In general terms, wood that has been decayed by fungi and other organisms loses much of its structural carbohydrate and is left as a brown, crumbling, powdery residue rich in the more resistant polymers; it may also carry living fungal spores and bacteria. Which of these the ancient drug contained is not known. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify any named constituent before adding it.]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Do not apply to a wound.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Laid on ulcers, it cleans them and brings them to scar. Mixed with an equal measure of anise and wine, or pounded and applied in linen, it is used for the condition the translator reads as snakebite.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose beyond "the same amount" of anise for the paste, no source wood, no duration, and no warning.

The Codex does not recommend rubbing rotten wood into a wound. A wound dressed with soil-borne or wood-borne decay material is exposed to infection, and the claim of the text is a historical one. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

Two preparations are given in the chapter and nothing else. The first is the dry material laid on an ulcer. The second is the material "kneaded together with the same amount of anise [3-65] and wine", or "pounded into small pieces, put into linen and applied" (line 4371 and following). The only quantity is "the same amount" (equal parts of the rot and of anise). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any recipe from this entry.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Decayed wood is not a sterile material, and applying decay material to an open wound or ulcer is a recognised route for infection, including wound infection by spore-forming organisms such as the agents of tetanus. A powdery absorbent dressing may take up fluid from a wet ulcer, but that is a general physical statement and is not evidence for the ancient use. The anise of the second recipe (3-65) is a separate substance with its own chapter. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and the chapter gives a topical remedy for ulcers and a second use that the translator queries. What the chapter does offer a cultivator is its discipline of attention: it names a material gathered out of old wood and stocks, and it tells the reader which two things to look for, that the stuff be "like meal" and that it be rotten. It gives no test, no season of gathering and no provenance, so even that discipline is thin here. The wording does not record an amuletic use and none is claimed.

**Practitioner's note.** A decay material for ulcers, in an old text that gives it three lines. Nothing supports cultivation use, and the wound use is unsafe. A cultivator reads it as an instance of how little the ancient pharmacy excluded.

### Contraindications

- **Infection.** Do not put rotten wood, soil or mould on an open wound or ulcer. Decay material can carry spores and bacteria.
- **Mould and dust.** Decayed wood can carry mould spores that irritate the airways when dust is raised; people with asthma or allergy should avoid it.
- **Snakebite.** The translator queries the reading "snakebite". A snakebite is a medical emergency; use proper care.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** No statement in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Identity.** The chapter describes a material, not a species. Do not equate it with the building-timber fungus called "dry rot" or with any named fungus.
