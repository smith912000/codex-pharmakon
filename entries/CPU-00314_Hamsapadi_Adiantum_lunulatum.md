### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda (Suśruta & the nighaṇṭu tradition).** Haṃsapadī — "the goose-foot" or "swan-foot," from *haṃsa* (goose/swan) + *pada* (foot) — takes its name from the shape of its delicate, lunate (half-moon) pinnae, which the classical eye read as the webbed footprint of a goose. It is a small maidenhair fern of shaded, damp rock and wall, and belongs to the cooling, bitter, blood-and-liver group of the Ayurvedic materia medica. Suśruta and the later nighaṇṭus place Haṃsapadī among the drugs for **rakta-pitta** (bleeding/heat-in-the-blood disorders), **jvara** (fever), **dāha** (burning sensation), **visphoṭa** and skin heat, and disorders of the liver. It is treated as a *raktaprasādana* (blood-clarifying) and cooling agent, used where the picture is one of heat lodged in the blood and the hepatic tissue.

The synonym-cluster is largely morphological — **Tripādikā** ("three-footed"), **Godhāpadī** ("lizard-foot"), **Raktapādī** ("red-foot") — all reading the plant's distinctive frond as an animal's track. As a fern (a spore-bearing, "flowerless" plant), it sits among the cryptogamic dravyas that the tradition associated with damp, shaded, watery habitats and, by the doctrine of correspondence, with cooling and moistening action on internal heat.

**Siddha and folk practice.** Adiantum maidenhair ferns are used across South and Southeast Asian folk medicine for fever, cough, skin disease, and liver complaints; poultices of the frond are applied to burns and inflammations, and decoctions taken for "heat" and jaundice. The hepatoprotective study below explicitly notes that "*Adiantum lunulatum* Burm. F. leaf (AL) and its related species have been used traditionally for the treatment of various diseases."

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Goose-Foot at the Shaded Water:** Haṃsapadī's doctrine in the codex is *cooling at the source of heat*. It is the plant of the shaded, dripping rock — a creature of dampness and shade, its every habitat the opposite of the dry heat it is used to treat. The goose, in Indic symbolism (the haṃsa), is the bird that separates milk from water, the emblem of discernment and of the moving-yet-serene; the plant that bears its footprint is the codex's image of *cooling discernment* — the settling of overheated, agitated blood, the clarifying of a liver run hot. Where the nightshades (Bṛhatī, Kaṇṭakārī) are the fierce, thorned, heating openers of the breath, Haṃsapadī is their tonal opposite: soft, cool, shade-loving, directed at heat and blood rather than cold and congestion. It teaches that recovery from inflammation is a *lowering of temperature at the tissue*, not a further stimulation.

### Active Components

Maidenhair ferns of the genus *Adiantum* characteristically carry **flavonoids** (the hepatoprotective study attributes activity to a flavonoid-rich ethanolic extract), **triterpenoids** (adiantone and related fern triterpenes), **phenolic acids**, and sterols. GC-MS profiling of *A. lunulatum* ethanol extract (Jerom et al., 2024) identified 18 volatile phytochemicals, including fatty-acid esters (ethyl oleate, octadecanoic acid ethyl ester, linoleic acid propyl ester), **phytol**, and long-chain aldehydes. The flavonoid and phenolic fraction is the presumed carrier of the antioxidant and hepatoprotective activity.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Haṃsapadī is the codex's **cooling hepato-protective and blood-settling** substance — the counterpoint to the heating nightshades. It is deployed where the somatic picture is *heat lodged in the blood and liver*: a hot, inflamed, oxidatively stressed hepatic tissue; burning sensations; heat-driven skin and bleeding tendencies. Functionally it supplies antioxidant and hepatoprotective support with a cooling, blood-clarifying character, so it belongs to the recovery-from-inflammation and hepato-protective maintenance phases. It pairs naturally with Punarnava (*Boerhavia diffusa*) as a companion in hepato-renal maintenance, contributing the cooling, blood-directed side to Punarnava's fluid-draining side.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the modern evidence for *A. lunulatum* is preclinical but directly supports the traditional liver indication. Kakadia et al. (*Journal of Complementary & Integrative Medicine*, 2020) tested an ethanolic leaf extract (250 and 500 mg/kg) against ethanol-induced hepatotoxicity in rats: pretreatment significantly reduced the elevated serum enzymes (AST, ALT, ALP), total bilirubin, reversed histological liver damage, and raised the antioxidant enzymes SOD, catalase and glutathione peroxidase — demonstrating hepatoprotective and antioxidant activity attributed to the flavonoid content. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1515/jcim-2018-0063)

A more recent phytochemical/computational study (Jerom et al., *Heliyon*, 2024) characterised the ethanol extract by GC-MS (18 volatile compounds), measured cytotoxicity on Neuro-2a neuroblastoma cells (IC50 ≈ 0.09 mg/mL), and used molecular docking to predict interactions of several constituents with amyloid-forming proteins and enzymes (tau, huntingtin, prion protein, acetylcholinesterase, BACE-1, γ-secretase), flagging the plant as a source of candidate anti-amyloid agents pending further study. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2024.e38127)

**Evidence grade: LOW–PRELIMINARY — animal, in vitro and in silico only.** The hepatoprotective/antioxidant signal is a single rodent study; the neuro/anti-amyloid work is exploratory docking plus a cytotoxicity assay, not a demonstration of clinical effect. No human data. Claims are attributed to PubMed-indexed sources and must be read as preliminary.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Rasa (taste):** *Tikta* (bitter), *Kaṣāya* (astringent); a trace of *Madhura* (sweet) in some readings.
- **Guṇa (qualities):** *Laghu* (light), *Snigdha–Mṛdu* (unctuous/soft, reflecting the moist habitat); some sources give *Rūkṣa*.
- **Vīrya (potency):** *Śīta* (cooling).
- **Vipāka (post-digestive effect):** *Madhura* (sweet) — consistent with its cooling, blood-settling action.
- **Prabhāva (specific action):** *Raktaprasādana / dāha-praśamana* — clarifying the blood and quelling internal burning.
- **Doṣa action:** *Pitta-Kapha-śāmaka*; broadly Vāta-neutral, mildly Vāta-aggravating only if over-dried.
- **Karma (actions):** *Raktapittahara* (checks bleeding/heat-in-blood), *Jvaraghna* (antipyretic), *Dāhapraśamana* (relieves burning), *Yakṛd-rakṣaka* (hepatoprotective — modern reading), *Kuṣṭhaghna* (skin-disease directed), *Śītala* (cooling tonic).
- **Srotas (channels):** primarily *raktavaha* (blood-carrying) and hepatic tissue; secondarily *mūtravaha* and skin.
- **Gaṇa membership:** grouped with the *raktapittahara* / *jvaraghna* cooling dravyas of the classical corpus (not a Daśamūla member).

### Contraindications

- Cooling and blood-directed — less suited to cold, kapha-stagnant, low-agni states without warming co-adjuvants.
- Human safety and dosing data are absent; the extract doses in the literature are rodent doses, not domestic ones. Keep to traditional preparations and practitioner guidance.
- Avoid in pregnancy without qualified supervision.
- Botanical confusion within the *Adiantum* genus (many near-identical maidenhair ferns) is a real sourcing risk — verify the species.
- Not a substitute for medical management of hepatic or febrile disease; the hepatoprotective evidence is a single animal study.
