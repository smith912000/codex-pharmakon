### Active Components

Pyrite is iron(II) disulphide, a hard, brassy-yellow mineral that gives sparks when struck with steel. Weathering of pyrite in damp air produces iron sulphates and sulphuric acid, and pyrite often carries traces of arsenic, nickel, cobalt and other metals. Galena (named in Clark's gloss) is lead sulphide, and analcite is a sodium aluminium silicate (a zeolite); neither is pyrite. The sources read give no composition.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan entry records the stone for the eyes (white or yellow types), against "rabies poisoning" (black or brown types), and for brain disorders and the draining of lymph accumulations. Because the gloss may mean galena, a lead mineral, as readily as pyrite, nothing in it can be taken as a recommendation to swallow a ground stone. The Codex offers no protocol, dose or preparation.

### Scientific Correlation

Pyrite is a common sulphide mineral and is not a recognised medicine in current practice. Ground sulphide ores can liberate sulphur compounds and the toxic metals they contain, and lead and arsenic minerals are poisonous. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Ground mineral sulphides can contain arsenic, lead and other metals and are not safe to swallow; if the stone was galena it is a lead compound. Powdered mineral medicines are unstandardised, and sale of them is regulated in many places. Because the gloss lists three minerals, the Tibetan stone cannot be treated as pyrite. Rabies is a fatal viral disease that needs immediate medical treatment, and the text's "rabies poisoning" use is recorded as history only.
