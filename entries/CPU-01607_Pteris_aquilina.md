# Pteris aquilina — Theluipteris (Dioscorides 4-187, printed THELUPTERIS); nymphaea pteris, lingua cervina

## *Pteris aquilina, the fourth name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-187 ("Pteris aquilina [Linnaeus]", after "Thelypteris [Pliny]", "Felix, Felix foemina [Fuchs]" and before "Pteridium aquilinum [in Sprague]"), glossed "Buckler Fern, Brake Fern, Bracken, Eagle Fern, Female Fern"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 9708) prints four Latin names and five English names, and the row takes the one attributed to Linnaeus. It was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-187. THELUPTERIS*, heading at line 9707, SUGGESTED line at 9708 to 9709, text at line 9710. It stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), after *4-186. PTERIS* (heading at line 9698) and before *4-188. POLUPODION* (heading at line 9711). The translators' name index gives *Thelupteris* at page 736, and *Pteris aquilina*, *Pteridium aquilinum* and *Bracken* at page 736.

The whole chapter reads: "Thelupteris has leaves like those above, only not singlestemmed, but with many higher abnormal growths. The roots are underneath — long, thwarting, numerous, yellowish-black, and some also red. These are taken with honey as linctus [syrup] to expel broadworms. Three teaspoons of a decoction (taken as a drink with wine) expels roundworms. Given to women they cause barrenness [birth control], and if any conceives she aborts [abortifacient]. Dried, they are applied to ulcers that are moist and hard to cure, and they heal the necks of yoked beasts. The newly put out leaves of it are used as vegetables, and eaten boiled they soften the bowels. It is also called nymphaea pteris, and the Romans call it lingua cervina."

The description is a comparison. "Those above" are the leaves of *4-186. PTERIS*, which is a different chapter (the translators' male fern) and which the chapter's own wording sets against this plant: that one has leaves "without stalks" from "one stem", while this one is "not singlestemmed" and has "many higher abnormal growths". The roots are described as "long, thwarting, numerous, yellowish-black, and some also red". The chapter then gives uses in this order: roots with honey as a linctus for broadworms; a decoction with wine for roundworms; roots given to women, which "cause barrenness" and make a conceiving woman abort (the bracketed glosses "birth control" and "abortifacient" are the translators'); roots, dried, applied to moist ulcers that are hard to cure and to "the necks of yoked beasts"; and the newly put out leaves "used as vegetables", which "eaten boiled ... soften the bowels". The chapter prints two further names: *nymphaea pteris* and the Roman *lingua cervina*.

**Names shared with other chapters.** The name *lingua cervina* is also printed on the translators' SUGGESTED line for *3-152. EMIONITIS* (heading at line 7832), where it is a name the translators attribute to Fuchs and Bauhin for hart's-tongue fern, and not a name Dioscorides prints there. Here it is a Roman name in Dioscorides' own text. The Codex does not read the two chapters as one plant.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter number, *Thelupteris*, *Pteris aquilina*, *Pteridium* and *Bracken* found no existing entry on 4-187 itself. I_982 (*Polypodium filix-mas*, 4-186 and 4-189) names 4-187 only as a neighbouring chapter, "a different fern in the translation", and does not attribute it to that row. I_335 (Common Polypody) is the entry for 4-188, and its only mention of bracken is a general remark that the unrelated genus *Pteridium* carries ptaquiloside. I_1020, I_1019 and I_1160 are entries that cite chapter 3-152 (*Emionitis*), the other chapter on the translators' list that carries the name *lingua cervina*. None of them is an entry on 4-187.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. It is a general, well-established fact that bracken (*Pteridium aquilinum*) contains ptaquiloside, a carcinogenic and toxic compound, and that the plant has been associated with poisoning of grazing animals. The chapter does not mention this, and the identity of the plant is the translators' suggestion. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a root taken against intestinal worms, a root said to cause barrenness and abortion, a dried root applied to stubborn ulcers and to the necks of yoked animals, and young leaves eaten as a vegetable that loosen the bowels. These are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for any of them. The abortifacient and contraceptive claims, and the wormer claim, describe things the Codex does not recommend, and the identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations: the roots "taken with honey as linctus"; "three teaspoons of a decoction (taken as a drink with wine)"; the roots "given to women"; the roots "dried" and applied to ulcers; and the newly put out leaves boiled and eaten as vegetables. The only quantity in the chapter is the three teaspoons of the decoction for roundworms. No quantity, frequency or duration is given for the others. Apart from that one figure, no dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The one well-established general fact is the one stated under Active Components: bracken is a recognised toxic plant, so the vegetable use in the chapter should not be taken as safe on the chapter's authority.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. It prints the roots as "long, thwarting, numerous, yellowish-black, and some also red", and says the boiled young leaves "soften the bowels".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use. What it offers is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: a fern told apart from the one before it by its stems and its growths, and its roots described by length, number and colour, including the note that some are red. The plant is given no status beyond a list of remedies, and a vegetable use. The identification is the translators'.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It offers a description, an anthelmintic, an abortifacient and ulcer remedies, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one, and the plant should not be eaten or drunk.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning on the plant, and says outright that the roots given to women "cause barrenness" and cause a conceiving woman to abort. It is a general, well-established fact that bracken is carcinogenic and toxic, so neither the roots nor the leaves should be eaten, and the abortifacient claim describes a danger to a pregnancy that the Codex does not advise on. Intestinal worms need medical diagnosis and treatment. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no wild fern should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
