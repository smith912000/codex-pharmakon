It is pounded and washed in water by rubbing in a mortar, allowed to settle, strained, and repeated until "pure and unadulterated", then dried in the sun; it may be burnt on light coals. It "is able to wear off scars, and repress fleshy abnormal growths, and it is cleansing, astringent, warming, gently corrosive and a little biting. A leek-green colour and a companion copper stone point more to the translator's second gloss, malachite, than to borax; the identification as borax is not established here. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Borax is hydrated sodium tetraborate, a white crystalline salt of boron found in dried lake beds. It is mildly alkaline in water and mildly antiseptic. The boron in it is the compound's notable feature: boron is not established as an essential nutrient for people, and borates in quantity are toxic.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources use borax as an alkali for the digestive tract and gravel (Sushruta), as a wound and purgative salt (Tibetan), and, if the translator's gloss holds, in a Greek chapter marked toxic that is not clearly about borax. Ingesting borax is hazardous (see Contraindications), so the Codex offers no protocol, dose or preparation.

### Preparation & Dosing

DMM 5-104 (if the identification stands) gives washing by rubbing in water, settling and drying, and burning on light coals. SS and FMT give no preparation. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Borax and boric acid are weak antiseptics and have been used topically and in pest control. Ingested, they cause vomiting, diarrhoea and, in larger amounts, kidney and nervous-system toxicity; infants are especially vulnerable. Boron compounds are regulated in some places because of reproductive toxicity. No clinical studies were retrieved for the traditional uses. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Borax is toxic if swallowed in more than tiny amounts, and especially dangerous to infants and young children. Do not use it in or near the eyes or on broken skin of infants. Avoid in pregnancy and while breastfeeding. Laxative or "blood-thinning" claims from old texts are not a basis for use. The Greek chapter, if it concerns a copper mineral as the translator's alternative suggests, describes a substance "strong enough to kill".
