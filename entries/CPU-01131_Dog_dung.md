# Dog dung — "Dog stool" under "Droppings" (Clark's English); "the feces of dogs" (Men-Tsee-Khang's English)
## *The faeces of the dog as a remedy in the Tibetan Explanatory Tantra. Clark's translation prints "Dog stool" with no Tibetan headword and no binomial; the Men-Tsee-Khang English prints "feces of dogs"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The text on disk is Barry Clark's 1995 *The Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). Chapter 20, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (line 792), says that "The power of medicines is twofold: (a) the power of taste and (b) the natural power [of the substance]" (line 794), and its "specific section" divides the individual substances into eight kinds, the eighth being "(8) animal products" (line 802). The animal products are arranged by body part, and "Droppings" is one heading (line 1198). The run of entries under it (line 1199) begins:

"Vulture's droppings: increase digestive heat, disperse tumors and suppurate swellings. Pig's stool: cures indigestion, septic disorders, infectious fevers and bile tumors. Human excreta: cure gall stones, neutralize poisons, reduce swellings [and subdue spirits]. Horse dung: is anthelmintic, anti-emetic, cures wind-bile combinations [and is beneficial against spirits]. Rabbit droppings: purge oedema [and are beneficial for wounds]. Dog stool: [is especially beneficial against spirits]. Wolf stool and droppings of Gong.mo: Tetragallus himalayensis: [all three] reduce swellings. Bird and Rat droppings: both drain pus [accumulations]."

For the dog, then, Clark prints "Dog stool: [is especially beneficial against spirits]", and the whole clause is in square brackets. Clark's introduction says that "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary" (line 82). So the Tibetan root text, as Clark gives it, names dog stool in the list and the stated action comes from the commentary. What "spirits" translates is not explained in the lines read, and I do not gloss it. [USER: verify] The sentence that follows puts the wolf's stool and the gong.mo bird's droppings together and says "[all three] reduce swellings". The words "all three" are also in brackets, and they count three, which includes the dog stool just named, but the text does not state that. The Men-Tsee-Khang version bears on the question.

The second English text on disk, the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`), gives this passage (line 3568): "Vulture droppings generate heat, dissolve tumors and suppurates inflammed swellings. Pig feces cure indigestion, epidemic and cholelithiasis... Horse dung cures ein disorder, combined disorder of tripa and loong, and stops vomiting. Rabbit dropping drain ascites. The feces of dogs, wolves and the Tibetan snow cock reduce swelling. The droppings of birds and rats drain pus." So that text states that dog faeces "reduce swelling", with the wolf and the snow cock. The two English texts do not give the same action for the dog: Clark's bracketed line says "especially beneficial against spirits" and the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence says "reduce swelling". I report both. Clark's following "[all three] reduce swellings" agrees with the Men-Tsee-Khang reading if the three include the dog. [USER: verify]

The Men-Tsee-Khang text introduces the animal group with "There are thirteen medicinal substances derived from animals. They are the horn, bone, flesh, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, fur, urine, feces and the entire body of an animal" (line 3503). Faeces are one of the thirteen kinds. Other dog materials in the same chapter, which I read and list: "Dog tongue: [has ten names and] heals all types of wounds" and "Dog testicle: is ecbolic" (Clark line 1170; entry III_75 covers the testicle).

Clark's introduction says that "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57). The Men-Tsee-Khang preface says that "the use of animal products was negligible", that "animal products in Tibetan medicine have been completely substituted by equally effective herbal substances", and that their potencies are given "to preserve the understanding of the therapeutic values of animal products" (line 687).

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, dog faeces are a mixture of undigested food residue, bacteria, bile pigments, water and sometimes parasite eggs. Nothing more specific can be said without a source.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text lists dog faeces among the droppings that are used medicinally, with an action against swellings (Men-Tsee-Khang) or against "spirits" (Clark's bracketed commentary). The Codex does not recommend it. Dog faeces carry parasites and bacteria that cause serious disease in people, and the tradition's own present-day practice, as its translators describe it, has moved to herbal substitutes.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives no preparation for dog faeces. It does not say whether the material is dried, burnt, taken in a drink or applied, and the lines read give no quantity. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Dog faeces can carry the eggs of roundworms (*Toxocara*) and other parasites, and bacteria such as *Salmonella* and *Campylobacter*; they are a recognised route of zoonotic infection. There is no established evidence that they reduce swelling or act on any condition the text mentions. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** None printed for this item. Chapter 20 says that medicines act by taste and by natural power, and lists eight powers (heavy, oily, cool, blunt, light, coarse, hot, sharp; Clark line 796). The dog-stool entry (line 1199) prints no taste, power or post-digestive taste, and neither does the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence (line 3568). For other droppings in the same list the text does print an effect on heat, for instance the vulture's droppings "increase digestive heat", and nothing similar is said of the dog's. Clark's introduction says that the later *Crystal Orb* and *Crystal Rosary* give each medicine's tastes, potencies and qualities (line 57), but that text is not on disk, so none is supplied here.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for dog faeces in the lines read. Chapter 20 gives it, through its commentary, an action against "spirits", and in the Men-Tsee-Khang version an action against swellings, and says nothing about essence extraction (*bcud len*) or any other practice. The Men-Tsee-Khang preface records a Buddhist objection to causing harm to any being as the reason animal products have been replaced by herbs (line 687), which is the tradition's own comment on animal products in general and not a cultivation status for this one.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. Dog faeces are one clause in a list of droppings, with the action stated by a commentary, and the tradition describes animal products as largely replaced. The honest use of the entry is as a record. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Infection and parasites:** dog faeces can carry *Toxocara* and other worm eggs, *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter* and other pathogens; do not ingest them or apply them to skin, wounds or the eye.
- **Wounds:** faeces on a wound risk tetanus and serious infection.
- **Swellings:** a persistent or growing swelling needs medical diagnosis; this entry is not a treatment.
- **Ethics and law:** none specific to the dog; the Codex does not recommend the use of any animal excrement as medicine, and the Tibetan translators themselves record a Buddhist objection to harming any being in the use of animal products.
