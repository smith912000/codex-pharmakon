# Crocodile claw — "Crocodile" under "Nails" (Clark's English); "the claw of crocodiles" (Men-Tsee-Khang's English)
## *The claw of the crocodile as a remedy in the Tibetan Explanatory Tantra. Clark's translation prints "Crocodile" and no Tibetan headword or binomial for it; the source list `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` does not carry a separate row for it* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The text on disk is Barry Clark's 1995 *The Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine*, a translation of the Explanatory Tantra and the Oral Instruction Tantra (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). Chapter 20, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (line 792), says that "The power of medicines is twofold: (a) the power of taste and (b) the natural power [of the substance]" (line 794), and its "specific section" divides the individual substances into eight kinds, the eighth being "(8) animal products" (line 802). The animal section is arranged by body part: horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings. Under the heading "Nails" (line 1186) the translation reads, in full (line 1187):

"Crocodile: [has twenty-two names]. Its claw cures bone fevers. If crocodile claw is not obtainable one should use na.gi, which is an ingredient in compounded incense."

Three points follow from the lines read. First, the entry names a single part, the claw, and a single action, that it "cures bone fevers". Second, the text itself supplies a substitute: if the claw "is not obtainable", na.gi is to be used instead, and the translator says only that na.gi is "an ingredient in compounded incense". What na.gi is, is not stated in the lines read. [USER: verify] Third, the bracketed words are interpolations. Clark's introduction says that "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary, whilst those in parentheses ( ) are derived from the oral instructions of Dr. Yeshe Donden and Dr. Pema Dorje" (line 82). So "has twenty-two names" is commentary, not the root-text sentence. The claw and its action, and the substitute instruction, are printed outside brackets.

The second English text on disk, the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`), gives the same item in one sentence (line 3556): "The claw of crocodiles cures hot disorder of the bone." It introduces the animal group with the sentence "There are thirteen medicinal substances derived from animals. They are the horn, bone, flesh, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, fur, urine, feces and the entire body of an animal" (line 3503). Its glossary lists "Crocodile" among animal names (line 7186) and prints no Latin name there. Clark's "bone fevers" and the Men-Tsee-Khang "hot disorder of the bone" are two renderings of the same entry. I do not reconcile them further, and no Tibetan term for the disorder is printed in the lines read. [USER: verify]

Clark's introduction notes that "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57). The Men-Tsee-Khang preface says that "the use of animal products was negligible" and that "animal products in Tibetan medicine have been completely substituted by equally effective herbal substances", and that their potencies are given "to preserve the understanding of the therapeutic values of animal products" (line 687).

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, a claw is a keratinised structure, mainly protein (keratin). Nothing further can be said without a source. The nature of na.gi is not given in the lines read, so no composition is stated for it.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text records the claw of the crocodile as a remedy for a bone fever and offers a substitute where the claw cannot be had. The Codex does not recommend sourcing it. Crocodilians are protected or trade-controlled in most jurisdictions, the material cannot be identified or checked by a buyer, and the Tibetan tradition's own present-day practice, as its translators describe it, has moved to herbal substitutes. The entry stands as a record of what the text lists.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives no preparation for the claw. It does not say whether it is burnt, powdered, taken in a decoction or used in a compound, and the lines read give no quantity. The only instruction is the substitution: use na.gi if the claw is not obtainable (Clark line 1187). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Claws are keratin; there is no established evidence that crocodile claw treats any bone or febrile disorder. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** None printed for this item. Chapter 20 says that medicines act by taste and by natural power, and lists eight powers (heavy, oily, cool, blunt, light, coarse, hot, sharp; Clark lines 794-796). The crocodile entry (line 1187) prints no taste, power or post-digestive taste. Clark's introduction says that the later *Crystal Orb* and *Crystal Rosary* document each medicine "according to which of the six tastes, eight natural potencies, three post-digestive 'tastes,' seventeen secondary qualities and individual uses each possesses" (line 57), but that text is not on disk, so none is supplied here. The Men-Tsee-Khang phrase "hot disorder of the bone" names a disorder, not a quality of the claw.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for the crocodile claw in the lines read. Chapter 20 gives it a therapeutic action against a bone fever and nothing about essence extraction (*bcud len*) or any other practice. The Men-Tsee-Khang preface adds that Buddhism "strictly denounces causing harm to any being" (line 687), which is a reason the tradition itself gives for the decline of animal products, not a cultivation status for this one.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The crocodile claw is one line in a table of animal products, with a substitute named in the same line, and its own tradition describes animal products as largely replaced. The honest use of the entry is as a record. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Infection and hygiene:** an unprepared animal claw is an infection risk, and nothing in the sources gives a safe preparation.
- **Identity:** the Tibetan name of the item and the identity of the substitute na.gi are not given in the lines read.
- **Law and conservation:** crocodilians are protected or trade-controlled in most jurisdictions; trade in their parts is restricted, and the Codex does not recommend sourcing any.
- **Ethics:** the Codex does not recommend killing or harming wild animals for these uses, and the Tibetan translators themselves record a Buddhist objection to harming any being.
- **Bone fever:** a fever with bone pain is a medical matter; this entry is not a treatment.
