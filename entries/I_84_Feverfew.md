### Botanical Identity
Aromatic perennial 30–70cm; strongly aromatic (bitter, camphor-like); distinctive daisy-like flowers with white ray florets and yellow disc florets; deeply lobed leaves with strong characteristic scent; native to southeastern Europe, Balkans, Caucasus; naturalised throughout temperate zones. The entire aerial part (leaf + flower) harvested at full bloom. The flavour is strongly bitter and unpleasant — leaves were historically eaten in sandwiches (with bread to reduce gastric irritation) as a migraine prophylactic. The bitterness is primarily from parthenolide, the sesquiterpene lactone that is the primary bioactive compound.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 De Materia Medica (DMM)

Dioscorides (*Book III, Chapter 53 — as Matricaria/Parthenium*):
> *"Parthenion: warming and drying. Good for female obstructions, for those who are hysterical, for those with difficult menstruation. The decoction drunk or applied in a hip bath draws down the menses. Relieves headache and pain in the ears."*

The Greek application is primarily gynaecological (emmenagogue) and for head pain — the migraine and menstrual applications that persist into modern evidence-based herbal practice.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine (CM)

Ibn Sīnā on *āzārbakht*:
> *"Warm and dry in the third degree. Bitter, aromatic. Resolves obstructions in the head, reduces cold-type headache and migraine. The herb warmed and placed on painful joints relieves cold arthritis and stiffness. Emmenagogue — draws down the menses and expels the afterbirth. Taken in decoction for intermittent fevers."*

Ibn Sīnā's characterisation of feverfew for headache and cold-type joint pain maps directly onto the two principal modern evidence-based applications: migraine prophylaxis and arthritis.

### 2.3 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)

Bitter aromatic herbs for fever and "pain in the head" in Babylonian medical texts — consistent with the bitter febrifuge application.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Anti-Migraine (Primary — Parthenolide)

**Parthenolide**: Multiple converging mechanisms for migraine prophylaxis:

1. **Serotonin release inhibition**: Parthenolide inhibits serotonin (5-HT) release from platelets — the platelet serotonin release theory of migraine (elevated 5-HT during prodrome, depletion during attack) is the proposed mechanistic link
2. **NF-κB inhibition (IKKβ alkylation)**: Parthenolide forms a covalent bond with IKKβ's cysteine residue → irreversible inhibition of NF-κB → reduces trigeminal nucleus inflammation (the central site of migraine pain)
3. **Prostaglandin E2 suppression**: COX-2 and phospholipase A2 inhibition → reduces inflammatory mediators in the meningeal vasculature
4. **Smooth muscle relaxation**: Prevents vascular smooth muscle contraction → reduces vasoconstriction component of migraine

The combination of anti-platelet, NF-κB, and vasomodulatory effects addresses multiple pathophysiological mechanisms simultaneously — explaining why feverfew is effective prophylactically but not acutely (NF-κB inhibition requires chronic dosing to suppress sensitisation; serotonin normalisation requires sustained platelet pool modulation).

### 5.2 Anti-Inflammatory

**Parthenolide + canin + apigenin**: Beyond migraine:
- Parthenolide inhibits NF-κB → reduces TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 broadly
- Specific inhibition of phospholipase A2 → reduces arachidonic acid mobilisation → reduces both COX and LOX pathway inflammation
- This is the mechanistic basis for the arthritis and joint pain application

### 5.3 Emmenagogue / Uterine Stimulant

**Parthenolide + chrysanthemyl acetate**: Stimulate uterine smooth muscle contraction — the traditional emmenagogue application. The same mechanism that makes feverfew contraindicated in pregnancy (risk of uterine contractions) validates the traditional use for delayed menstruation.

### 5.4 Anticancer (Emerging — Parthenolide)

**Parthenolide**: One of the most actively researched natural NF-κB inhibitors in oncology:
- At concentrations above anti-migraine threshold, induces apoptosis in acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) cancer stem cells selectively — this is a specific cell type (cancer stem cells) that most chemotherapy agents fail to kill
- Dimethylaminoparthenolide (DMAPT) — a water-soluble prodrug — has entered Phase I clinical trials for AML

The anticancer mechanism (NF-κB-dependent cancer stem cell apoptosis) uses exactly the same IKKβ alkylation mechanism responsible for the anti-inflammatory/anti-migraine effects — at higher concentrations.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Migraine prophylaxis**: Johnson (1985, *British Medical Journal*, first RCT): Patients who had been using feverfew (whole leaf) for >6 months and stopped (withdrawal group) had 3× more migraines than those who continued — a withdrawal design that strongly supported efficacy. Murphy (1988, *Lancet*): Feverfew leaf 82mg/day significantly reduced migraine frequency and severity vs placebo over 4 months. Palevitch (1997): Similar findings.

**Cochrane review (Wider 2015)**: Feverfew significantly more effective than placebo for migraine prophylaxis in 6 of 6 included trials; effect size moderate; comparable to low-dose beta-blockers; better for frequency than severity. The evidence base is consistent.

**Rheumatoid arthritis (Pilot)**: Small RCT (Pattrick 1989) — feverfew did not significantly improve RA vs placebo over 6 weeks. A negative result that limits the arthritis application despite pharmacological plausibility; larger trials are absent.

**AML (Phase I — DMAPT)**: Safety profile established; preliminary efficacy signals in AML patients who had failed standard therapy (Kantarjian 2013 presentation). Phase II ongoing.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The word that survived**: The English name "feverfew" is a folk corruption of the Latin *febrifuga* — the original pharmacological name in the European tradition. The plant's original recorded indication in Western medicine was fever; the name survived the 2,500-year journey from *febrifuga* through Anglo-Saxon to modern English with the same structure, even though the fever application was eventually supplanted by the migraine application. The naming archaeology: *febrifuga* (Latin) → *feferfuge* (Middle English) → *feverfew* (Early Modern English). The word itself is a pharmacological record.

**The BMJ sandwich trial**: The Johnson 1985 BMJ trial — one of the most creatively designed trials in herbal medicine — recruited patients from a self-prescribing community of migraine sufferers who had been eating fresh feverfew leaves daily (in sandwiches, because of the bitter taste). Half were randomised to continue; half to capsules of dried freeze-dried leaf. The freeze-drying preserved parthenolide; the sandwich group also preserved it. The trial's strength was its use of a pre-existing patient population who had already selected themselves into the treatment — the real-world outcome confirmed what the self-treating community had already observed. Evidence-based medicine and traditional self-use converged on the same conclusion.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

| Form | Dose | Application |
|------|------|-------------|
| Standardised dry leaf (0.2–0.3% parthenolide) | 100–300mg daily | Migraine prophylaxis (prevention, not acute treatment) |
| Fresh leaf (in food) | 2–3 leaves daily (bitter — use in sandwich or with bread) | Traditional migraine prophylaxis |
| Tincture (1:5, 25% ethanol) | 3–5ml 3× daily | Anti-inflammatory; emmenagogue |
| Freeze-dried leaf capsule | 50–125mg 2× daily | Standardised equivalent of fresh leaf |
| Topical (crushed leaf) | Applied to painful joints | Local anti-inflammatory (limited evidence) |

**Parthenolide degradation note**: Parthenolide degrades rapidly when dried at high temperature or extracted in certain solvents. Freeze-drying (lyophilisation) or alcohol tincture (25–35% ethanol) best preserves active content. Standard air-dried capsules may be inactive if parthenolide has degraded during drying — this is the primary quality failure mode in commercial feverfew products.

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Bitter, acrid; slightly warm. Channels: Liver (肝), Stomach (胃). Disperses Wind-Heat, relieves pain, moves Blood, regulates menstruation.

**Ayurvedic (cross-reference)**: Bitter, pungent; slightly warming. Reduces Kapha ↓↓. *Śūlaghna* (pain-relieving), *Artavajanana* (emmenagogue), *Jvaraghna* (febrifuge).

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Pregnancy**: Emmenagogue + uterine stimulant — absolutely contraindicated throughout pregnancy; risk of miscarriage
- **Blood thinners / antiplatelets**: Additive anti-platelet effect with aspirin, clopidogrel, warfarin — monitor, reduce dose or avoid
- **Asteraceae allergy**: Cross-reactivity with ragweed, chamomile, yarrow, chrysanthemum — patch test; allergy screen before use
- **Post-feverfew syndrome (withdrawal)**: Abrupt cessation after prolonged use → rebound headache, muscle stiffness, anxiety — taper slowly rather than abrupt stop
- **Mouth ulcers**: Fresh leaf (not encapsulated) → oral mucosal irritation in ~18% of users; encapsulated preparations avoid this
- **Acute migraine**: Feverfew is prophylactic only; no evidence for acute migraine treatment
