## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary names:**
- Sanskrit: *Ashwagandha* (अश्वगंधा) — lit. "smell of horse" (*ashwa* = horse; *gandha* = smell); the fresh root smells distinctly of horse urine (due to withanolide lactones); secondary meaning: "strength of a horse"
- Sanskrit synonyms: *Varāhakarnī*, *Vajinī*, *Turagi*, *Hayagandha*, *Vājikāra* (all horse-related, emphasizing the aphrodisiac/strength-conferring reputation)
- Hindi: *Asgandh*, *Ashwagandha*
- English: Winter Cherry, Indian Ginseng (misleading misnomer — not a ginseng; no botanical relation to *Panax*)
- Arabic: *ʿInab al-dib* (wolf berry — a different confusion with *Lycium barbarum*); also *Kaknaj hind*
- Persian: *Kushte*, *Askaval*
- Unani: *Asgand Nagori* (Nagori = from Nagaur, Rajasthan; considered finest quality)
- Turkish: *Köpek üzümü*
- German: *Schlafbeere* (sleep berry — refers to *somnifera* = sleep-bringing)
- Japanese: *Ashuwaganda*
- Chinese: *Nàndī gǒuqǐ* (南非枸杞) — "South African wolfberry" in some Chinese botanical texts; or *Zhōng yà rén shēn* (Central Asian ginseng — again a misnomer)

**Species epithet:** *somnifera* = "sleep-inducing" (Latin); refers to the traditional use for insomnia/sedation, though modern preparations are not primarily sedating.

**Botanical identity:**
Erect evergreen shrub, 35–75 cm height (occasionally to 150 cm); branching from base; covered in dense stellate tomentum (star-shaped hair clusters). Leaves ovate-lanceolate, 5–10 cm, also stellate-hairy. Flowers small, greenish-yellow, bell-shaped, in axillary clusters. Fruits: small round berries, enclosed in an inflated papery calyx (*lantern*-like), turning orange-red to red at maturity; resembles miniature Chinese lanterns — hence "winter cherry." Roots: thick, fleshy, tuberous taproot; brown exterior, white interior; distinctive horse-like odor when fresh.

**Distribution:**
India (primary — dry stony soils of Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Gujarat), Pakistan, Afghanistan, Middle East, Mediterranean (north African coast, Canary Islands, Mediterranean coast to Spain), Sub-Saharan Africa. Cultivated commercially in India (largest producer), Israel, and now USA/Europe.

**Quality grades (Ayurvedic tradition):**
- *Nagori* (Nagaur, Rajasthan): considered finest; large roots, highest withanolide content
- *Hilly*: from hilly/forest regions; medium quality
- *Garden*: cultivated; variable quality; lower price

**Disambiguation:**
| Name | Plant | Note |
|------|-------|------|
| Ashwagandha | *Withania somnifera* | This entry |
| Indian ginseng | *W. somnifera* (misnomer) | NOT related to *Panax ginseng* |
| Winter cherry | *W. somnifera* | Based on fruit appearance |
| Winter cherry | *Physalis alkekengi* | DIFFERENT plant; same common name |
| Cape gooseberry | *Physalis peruviana* | Related to *Physalis*; not *Withania* |
| Wolf berry | *Lycium barbarum* | NOT *Withania*; total different genus |

**Primary tradition depth:**
*Withania somnifera* is the paradigmatic Ayurvedic adaptogen — arguably the most systematically documented herb in the Ayurvedic pharmacopeia. The *Sushruta Samhita*, *Charaka Samhita* (Chikitsa 1-3), *Ashtanga Hridayam*, and *Bhavaprakasha Nighantu* all devote substantial space to ashwagandha. The depth of documentation in one tradition (Ayurveda) partially compensates for limited cross-traditional presence. Its geographic range (India, Middle East, Mediterranean) determined its cultural reach — it was never a global commodity like black pepper or turmeric.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Central convergence point — adaptogen/rasayana paradigm:**
*Withania somnifera* represents the Ayurvedic concept of *rasayana* most completely: a substance that prolongs life, enhances vitality, improves memory and cognitive function, treats wasting diseases, and rebuilds depleted constitutional energy. This concept maps onto the modern adaptogen framework developed by Nikolai Lazarev (1947) and Hans Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome (1936) — the idea that certain substances normalize physiological responses to non-specific stress.

The convergence between Ayurvedic *rasayana* theory (ashwagandha rebuilds *Ojas* — essential vitality), Ibn Sīnā's tonic/aphrodisiac classification, and modern HPA-axis modulating research represents a rare instance where traditional polypharmacology description predicted molecular mechanism with high accuracy.

**Stress-cortisol convergence:**
Traditional descriptions consistently describe ashwagandha as treating *shrama* (fatigue), *klama* (mental exhaustion), *kshaya* (wasting/depletion), and promoting *bala* (strength) — all consistent with states of HPA-axis hyperstimulation (chronic stress) producing cortisol excess. Modern RCTs demonstrate cortisol reduction as primary biomarker of efficacy.

**Divergence — soporific nomenclature:**
The species epithet *somnifera* (sleep-inducing) and traditional use for insomnia seem at odds with modern "energizing adaptogen" marketing. This is not a contradiction but a dose/formulation difference: high-dose evening preparations traditionally produced sedation; low-dose morning preparations promoted vitality without sedation. Modern standardized extracts (KSM-66, Sensoril) are predominantly used for daytime stress resilience, not sedation.

**Neurological convergence — Ayurvedic *medhya rasayana*:**
Alongside Gotu Kola (I-565) and Brahmi/Bacopa, ashwagandha is classified as *medhya rasayana* — a nootropic category in Ayurveda. The neurological applications (memory enhancement, cognitive support) in Ayurvedic texts have partial modern validation via BDNF upregulation, acetylcholinesterase inhibition, and mitochondrial function enhancement in neural tissue.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Primary bioactive compounds:**

**Withanolides (0.3–1.2% in root; up to 2.5% in standardized extracts):**
The withanolides are steroidal lactones unique to the genus *Withania* (and a few other Solanaceae); named after the plant. They are C₂₈ steroids with an ergostane skeleton and a highly oxygenated δ-lactone (6-membered ring) side chain.

- **Withaferin A**: Primary cytotoxic withanolide; most extensively studied; HSP90 inhibitor; NF-κB inhibitor; anticancer activity; relatively higher in *W. coagulans* and leaves vs. root
- **Withanolide A (Sitoindoside IX)**: Nootropic and immunomodulatory; predominant in root; AChE inhibition
- **Withanolide B**: Anti-inflammatory
- **Withanosides (I–VII)**: Glycosidic withanolides; nootropic; withanosides IV/V notable for nerve growth factor (NGF) support; Tohda research (Japan)
- **Withanoside IV**: In animal models promotes axon/dendrite regeneration via induction of BDNF
- **12-Deoxywithastramonolide**: Anti-inflammatory; NF-κB inhibition

**Alkaloids (0.13–0.31%):**
- **Somniferin**: Hypnotic alkaloid; responsible for sedative properties at higher doses
- **Withanine**: Primary alkaloid
- **Anaferine, Anahygrine**: Tropane-type alkaloids (low concentration); less documented pharmacologically
- **Withasomnine**: Nicotinic pharmacology (minor)
- **Somniferine, Withananine**: Additional minor alkaloids

**Iron:**
- Ashwagandha root contains significant iron: 3.3 mg/100g dry weight; traditionally used to treat anemia (*Pandu roga* in Ayurveda)
- Some preparations (milk-based *Ashwagandha Ghrita*) enhanced iron bioavailability

**Other constituents:**
- **Starch**: 40% dry weight root; contributes to traditional milk formulations
- **Saponins**: Sitoindosides I–VIII; adaptogenic; immune modulatory
- **Polysaccharides**: Immunomodulatory; specifically WS1, WS2 from root
- **Phytosterols**: β-sitosterol; stigmasterol
- **Choline**: Acetylcholine precursor; contributes to nootropic profile
- **Withanolide-free extract fractions**: Glycowithanolides (sitoindosides); important for safety in some preparations

**Standardization:**
- **KSM-66** (Ixoreal Biomed): Root-only extract; standardized to ≥5% withanolides; most clinical RCT evidence
- **Sensoril** (Natreon Inc.): Root + leaf; standardized to ≥10% withanolides, ≥32% oligosaccharides; higher withaferin A content (cytotoxic considerations at high dose)
- **Shoden** (Arjuna Natural): Standardized 35% glycowithanolides

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Primary mechanism: HPA (Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal) Axis Modulation**

The HPA axis stress response:
Stressor → Hypothalamus releases CRH → Pituitary releases ACTH → Adrenal cortex releases cortisol → Cortisol negative feedback to hypothalamus/pituitary → HPA axis normalization

In chronic stress: negative feedback impaired → cortisol hypersecretion → cortisol-mediated pathology (hippocampal atrophy, immune suppression, metabolic dysfunction, sleep disruption)

**Ashwagandha HPA modulation:**
- Enhanced glucocorticoid receptor sensitivity → restored cortisol negative feedback
- Reduced CRH and ACTH secretion (demonstrated in animal studies)
- Net result: reduced serum cortisol (primary RCT biomarker); attenuated stress response; not abolition of stress response (true adaptation, not blunting)

**NF-κB inhibition (Withaferin A primary mechanism):**
- Withaferin A binds IKKβ (Inhibitor of NF-κB Kinase β) → prevents phosphorylation of IκB → IκB retains NF-κB in cytoplasm → NF-κB cannot translocate to nucleus → reduced inflammatory cytokine transcription (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, COX-2)
- This is the same pathway inhibited by glucocorticoids and aspirin (at different points)
- Anti-inflammatory effect observed in RA models, cancer cell lines, neuroinflammation models

**HSP90 inhibition (Withaferin A):**
- HSP90 (Heat Shock Protein 90) is a chaperone protein required for maturation and stability of multiple oncoproteins: HER2, BCR-Abl, EGFR, AKT, CDK4, mutant p53
- Withaferin A disrupts HSP90 client protein interactions → proteasomal degradation of oncoproteins → cell cycle arrest → apoptosis in cancer cells
- Mechanism shared with pharmaceutical HSP90 inhibitors (geldanamycin, tanespimycin) — withaferin A is a natural HSP90 inhibitor with overlapping but distinct binding profile

**Acetylcholinesterase inhibition (Withanolide A):**
- Reversible AChE inhibition → increased synaptic acetylcholine → enhanced cholinergic neurotransmission
- Similar mechanism to Alzheimer's drugs (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) but weaker
- IC₅₀ for withanolide A: ~0.9 μM (vs. donepezil: ~0.005 μM — much weaker than pharmaceutical)
- May contribute to memory enhancement effects

**BDNF/NGF upregulation:**
- Withanosides IV/V (particularly in Tohda Japan research): promote neurite outgrowth in neuronal cultures
- Mechanism: activation of TrkB receptor (BDNF receptor) → MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT pathways → neurite elongation, synaptogenesis
- In vivo: reversal of memory deficits in scopolamine-treated mice; BDNF protein increase in hippocampus
- Potentially relevant to clinical cognitive effects but definitive human mechanism data lacking

**GABA-A receptor modulation:**
- Withanolides bind GABA-A receptor complex (possibly benzodiazepine binding site) → chloride channel opening → neuronal hyperpolarization → anxiolytic and sedative effects
- Explains traditional soporific use; dose-dependent: low dose = anxiolytic, high dose = sedative

**Testosterone and thyroid hormone effects:**
- **Testosterone**: Multiple small RCTs show serum testosterone increase in men with suboptimal baseline levels (not in eugonadal men with normal testosterone). Proposed mechanism: LH secretion increase via hypothalamic GnRH stimulation; reduced oxidative stress in Leydig cells; cortisol reduction (cortisol inversely correlated with testosterone at adrenal level)
- **Thyroid**: Ashwagandha increases T3 and T4 in hypothyroid animal models and bipolar disorder patients (Gannon 2014 case series). Mechanism proposed: liver conversion of T4→T3 enhancement via deiodinase activity. Clinical significance: may be beneficial in subclinical hypothyroidism; caution in hyperthyroidism

**Anabolic/muscular mechanism:**
- Androgenic precursor support via DHEA-S elevation (adrenal androgen pathway)
- Satellite cell proliferation → muscle protein synthesis increase
- Creatine kinase reduction post-exercise (reduced muscle damage)
- Net: modest but real increase in muscle strength and lean mass in training individuals

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Level A — Well-designed RCTs:**

**Stress and anxiety — flagship evidence:**
- **Chandrasekhar 2012** (*Indian Journal of Psychological Medicine*): Double-blind RCT n=64; KSM-66 300 mg/day × 60 days; PSS score reduced 44% vs 5.5% placebo; serum cortisol reduced 27.9% vs 7.9% placebo; DASS anxiety scale significant improvement. Primary landmark RCT.
- **Pratte 2014** (*Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine*): KSM-66 300 mg BID × 60 days; n=98; significant reduction in stress, anxiety, cortisol; improvement in all sub-scales of GHQ-28.
- **Salve 2019** (*Cureus*): n=58; KSM-66 240 mg/day; significant reduction in PSS, GAD-7, cortisol; improved sleep quality.
- **Meta-analysis Pratte-type**: Priyanka 2023 systematic review (10 RCTs, n=731): significant cortisol reduction (SMD −0.61) and anxiety reduction (SMD −0.79) vs placebo.

**Sleep:**
- **Langade 2019** (*PLOS ONE*): n=60; KSM-66 300 mg BID; sleep efficiency improved by 6%; wake after sleep onset reduced; sleep onset latency reduced; total sleep time +52 min vs control.
- **Deshpande 2020**: Shoden 120 mg × 6 weeks; sleep quality by PSQI significantly improved.

**Cognitive function:**
- **Choudhary 2017** (*Journal of Dietary Supplements*): n=50; KSM-66 300 mg BID × 8 weeks; significant improvement in memory (Wechsler Memory Scale), information processing speed, sustained attention, executive function vs placebo.
- **Pingali 2014**: Sensoril 500 mg/day × 12 weeks; cognitive battery significant improvement.

**Physical performance and muscle strength:**
- **Wankhede 2015** (*Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*): n=57 male athletes; KSM-66 300 mg BID + resistance training × 8 weeks; bench press and leg extension strength significantly greater (+46% and +43% vs +26% and +22% placebo; absolute values); muscle recovery improved (CK lower); testosterone higher (627 vs 630 ng/dL baseline → 726 vs 669 ng/dL at 8 weeks; significant difference).
- **Shenoy 2012**: Cycle ergometer performance; VO₂max significant improvement.

**Male sexual function and testosterone:**
- **Mahdi 2011** (*Fertility and Sterility*): n=75 infertile men; ashwagandha 5g/day; significant improvements in sperm count, motility, volume; testosterone elevated; antioxidant markers improved.
- **Ambiye 2013**: n=46 with oligospermia; withanolide-standardized extract; significant testosterone increase and sperm parameter improvement.

**Thyroid (bipolar disorder population):**
- **Gannon 2014** (*Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine*): n=8 bipolar disorder patients; significant T3 and T4 increase with ashwagandha treatment; raises caution for hyperthyroid patients.

**Level B — Observational/mechanistic:**

**Rheumatoid arthritis:**
- **Ramakanth 2016**: n=86 RA patients; ashwagandha + standard therapy vs standard therapy alone; significant improvement in DAS28 score, CRP, ESR; NF-κB inhibition proposed mechanism.

**Cancer (preclinical + limited pilot):**
- Withaferin A in vitro: NCI-60 cancer cell panel activity; HSP90 inhibition; multiple cancer cell lines (MCF-7 breast, PC-3 prostate, HeLa, HCT-116 colon)
- Phase I trial withaferin A: underway/limited; dose-limiting toxicity not yet established in humans
- No Phase III human cancer data available

**Level C — Traditional evidence:**
- Charaka Samhita Chikitsa Sthana 1.3.17–18: ashwagandha ghrita (clarified butter preparation) for emaciation, impotence, aging, memory loss
- Sushruta Samhita: *kshaya* (wasting) treatment; wound healing; nervous system conditions
- Bhavaprakasha Nighantu: "destroys aging" (*jaravyaadhinashak*), "increases intelligence" (*medhya*)
- Unani tradition: *Asgand Nagori* as tonic for sexual debility, insomnia, general weakness

**Pharmacovigilance:**
- Generally well-tolerated; GI side effects most common (nausea, diarrhea, stomach upset) at higher doses
- **Hepatotoxicity**: Case reports (approximately 10 published) of clinical hepatitis (cholestatic pattern) with ashwagandha supplementation; most resolved on discontinuation; two cases requiring hospitalization; one liver transplantation reported. Causality confirmed by RUCAM in several cases. FDA issued consumer advisory 2023. Likely rare (estimated <1 in 100,000 users) but serious.
- **Thyroid storm**: Several cases of hyperthyroid acceleration in predisposed patients
- **Autoimmune exacerbation**: Theoretical concern (immune stimulation via sitoindosides) in active autoimmune disease

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The *Ojas* theory and the stress physiology translation:**
Ayurvedic medicine locates vitality in *Ojas* — described as the "essence of all seven *dhatus*" (tissue types), residing in the heart, governing immunity, radiance, and life force. Ashwagandha is held to be among the pre-eminent *Ojas*-building substances. When modern stress physiology demonstrated that chronic cortisol suppresses immune function, reduces muscle protein synthesis, depletes neurotransmitter precursors, and accelerates cellular aging, the functional overlap with *Ojas* depletion became apparent. The Ayurvedic clinician's prescription of ashwagandha for *kshaya* (wasting, depletion) in stressed or aging patients was empirically tracking what would later be characterized as HPA-axis dysregulation.

**The Ashtavaidya lineage and the Kerala oral tradition:**
The *Ashtavaidya* families of Kerala (eight hereditary physician dynasties) maintained detailed oral clinical traditions regarding ashwagandha's use across generations. Their preparation, *Ashwagandha Arishtam* (a fermented liquid preparation), is still commercially manufactured. The fermentation process (12–30 days with *dhataki* flowers as yeast source) produces low concentrations of alcohol that serve as extraction solvent and preservative. The phenomenological tradition maintained by these families describes a consistent clinical picture: patients with "sunken eyes, dull complexion, low voice, slow pulse, poor sleep" responding to ashwagandha over 90-day courses — a symptomatic picture entirely consistent with chronic HPA-axis hyperstimulation.

**The *rasayana* versus pharmaceutical tension:**
When KSM-66 began producing commercial-grade clinical evidence in the 2010s, the Ayurvedic *rasayana* concept — traditionally requiring 90 days of graduated treatment in isolation (*kuti praveshika*) under physician supervision — was compressed into a standardized two-capsule-per-day protocol. Traditional practitioners argue that the RCT evidence captures only a fraction of true rasayana efficacy, which depends on concomitant dietary regimen, lifestyle modification, and seasonal timing. Modern researchers counter that if the simplified protocol is effective, the full protocol should be even more so. The resolution has not been achieved — both parties agree on ashwagandha efficacy while disagreeing on optimal delivery.

**Nikolai Lazarev and the retroactive framing:**
Soviet pharmacologist Nikolai Lazarev coined "adaptogen" in 1947 to describe Siberian ginseng's (*Eleutherococcus senticosus*) ability to increase non-specific resistance to stress. Hans Selye's concurrent General Adaptation Syndrome (1936–1950) provided the mechanistic framework. Ashwagandha was not in the original adaptogen research literature (which focused on Siberian/Chinese plants for Cold War military performance research) but retrospectively fits every criterion Lazarev defined: normalizes physiological deviation from homeostasis; demonstrates no specific mode of action; beneficial over wide dose range without toxicity. The cultural irony: while Soviet researchers studied Siberian ginseng, Ayurvedic physicians had documented an equivalent or superior adaptogen for 3,000+ years. The reconnection came through integrative medicine research beginning in the 1990s.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Standardized preparations (root only preferred; most evidence):**

**KSM-66 (most clinical evidence):**
- Dose: 300–600 mg/day (300 mg BID most studied)
- Standardized: ≥5% withanolides (withanolide A primary)
- Take with meals to reduce GI irritation
- Duration: 60–90 days for full effect (stress/cortisol endpoints); 8–12 weeks for strength

**Sensoril (root + leaf; higher withaferin A):**
- Dose: 125–250 mg/day (lower due to higher concentration)
- Standardized: ≥10% withanolides
- Caution: higher withaferin A content → greater theoretical hepatotoxic/cytotoxic risk at high doses

**Shoden (glycowithanolide focus):**
- Dose: 120 mg/day
- Used in several recent sleep RCTs

**Traditional preparations:**
- **Ashwagandha Churna** (powder): 3–6g root powder in warm milk + honey; traditional *anupana* (vehicle) with milk enhances fat-soluble withanolide absorption
- **Ashwagandha Ghrita**: 10–20g in clarified butter base; rejuvenating preparation
- **Ashwagandha Arishtam**: Fermented liquid; 25–50 mL BID with equal water after meals
- **Ashwagandha Lehyam**: Semi-solid confection; traditional for children and elderly

**Clinical decision framework:**

| Indication | Evidence Level | Preparation | Duration | Notes |
|------------|---------------|-------------|----------|-------|
| Chronic stress / elevated cortisol | A | KSM-66 300mg BID | 60–90 days | Primary indication |
| Sleep quality | A | KSM-66 300mg BID (evening) | 6–12 weeks | |
| Cognitive support | A | KSM-66 300mg BID | 8–12 weeks | |
| Athletic performance / muscle strength | A | KSM-66 300mg BID + training | 8–12 weeks | Males primarily studied |
| Male infertility / testosterone | B | 5g powder or 600mg extract/day | 90 days | Baseline testosterone matters |
| Anxiety (GAD) | B | KSM-66 300–600mg/day | 60 days | Not replacing first-line therapy |
| Subclinical hypothyroidism | B | 600mg/day | 8 weeks | Monitor TSH/T4; caution in hyperthyroid |

**Safety considerations:**

**Hepatotoxicity warning (IMPORTANT):**
~10 case reports of clinical hepatotoxicity (cholestatic/hepatocellular pattern); most resolve on cessation; rare but serious. Mechanism proposed: immunoallergic or withanolide metabolite-related. Risk factors not established. Recommend:
- Baseline LFTs in patients with hepatic risk factors
- Inform patients of hepatotoxicity risk and symptoms (jaundice, dark urine, right upper quadrant pain, fatigue)
- Discontinue immediately if hepatic symptoms develop
- Avoid in active liver disease; avoid with other hepatotoxic supplements (kava, green tea extract, EGCG high-dose)

**Thyroid precautions:**
- Hypothyroidism: potential benefit; monitor TSH
- Hyperthyroidism / Graves' disease / toxic nodular goiter: CONTRAINDICATED — case reports of hyperthyroid storm
- Patients on levothyroxine: monitor TSH at 6 weeks; dose adjustment may be needed

**Autoimmune caution:**
- Immunostimulatory sitoindosides → theoretical exacerbation of autoimmune disease
- Limited clinical data; theoretical concern vs. documented benefit in RA; individualize
- Avoid in systemic lupus erythematosus, multiple sclerosis without physician oversight

**Drug interactions:**
- **Barbiturates / benzodiazepines / alcohol**: Additive CNS depression (GABA-A modulation); avoid combination
- **Thyroid medications**: Potential pharmacodynamic interaction (see above)
- **Immunosuppressants** (cyclosporine, tacrolimus): Ashwagandha may counteract immunosuppression via immune stimulation
- **Sedative-hypnotics**: Additive sedation
- **CYP2D6 substrates**: Withaferin A inhibits CYP2D6 in vitro; clinical significance uncertain at therapeutic doses

**Contraindications:**
- Pregnancy: uterotonic activity documented in animal studies and traditional texts (*garbhasthapana* = uterine stabilizer at low dose, *pracchardana* = emmenagogue/abortifacient at high dose); AVOID
- Lactation: insufficient data; avoid
- Active hepatic disease
- Hyperthyroidism
- Autoimmune disease (without physician oversight)
- Scheduled surgery within 2 weeks (potentiates anesthesia; stop 2 weeks preoperatively)

**Pediatric use:**
- Traditional Ayurvedic use from childhood for emaciation/growth failure; milk-based preparations
- No modern pediatric RCTs; use only under Ayurvedic/integrative physician supervision
- Not recommended under age 12 per modern safety standards

**Monitoring:**
- LFTs at baseline and 6–8 weeks in at-risk patients
- TSH at baseline and 6 weeks (thyroid patients)
- Testosterone at baseline and 8 weeks (infertility indication)
