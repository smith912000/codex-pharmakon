# Gopaghonta / Spiny Gourd — गोपघोण्टा (Gopaghoṇṭā), Karkoṭaka, Kartoli, Kakrol, Bhat Karela

## *Momordica dioica* Roxb. | Family: Cucurbitaceae

**Tradition Tags:** Suśruta Saṃhitā (SS) · Bhāvaprakāśa Nighaṇṭu (as Karkoṭaka) · Regional Ayurveda & tribal/Siddha food-medicine
**Energetic Classification (Ayurvedic):** Tikta–Kaṭu rasa · Uṣṇa vīrya · Kaṭu vipāka — *kapha-pitta-śāmaka* (tridoṣa-balancing in the classical reading)

**Training Phase:** Kindling (Dīpana–Pācana) · Purification (light Śodhana) · Metabolic conditioning (dietary medicine)

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Suśruta & the Nighaṇṭu tradition.** The spiny gourd — a dioecious perennial cucurbit whose small, soft-spined green fruits (Karkoṭaka-phala) are both vegetable and medicine — is an old *pathya* (wholesome-diet) plant of the Indian materia medica. Under names Karkoṭaka / Gopaghoṇṭā it appears in Suśruta's and the later nighaṇṭus' rosters of bitter, kindling cucurbits used to correct sluggish, kapha-laden digestion, to clear *āma*, and as a *jvarahara* (antipyretic) and *krimighna* (anthelmintic). The tuberous root is the stronger medicine: classified as *tīkṣṇa* and used in preparations for fever, obstinate skin disease, urinary disorders, snakebite adjuvant use, and, notably, as a *vamaka/virecaka* aid in some regional traditions (a family resemblance to its relative *M. charantia*, bitter gourd). The fruit, milder, is the everyday *dīpana-pācana* food-medicine — eaten to stoke *agni* and lighten the body in kapha season.

**Tribal & Siddha food-medicine.** Across central and peninsular India the fruit (Kartoli/Kakrol) is a prized seasonal vegetable credited with "blood-purifying," anti-diabetic and tonic virtues; tribal healers use the root paste externally on abscesses, snake and scorpion bites, and inflamed swellings. This straddling of *food* and *drug* — nutritive enough to be a vegetable, sharp enough to be a medicine — is the plant's defining traditional character.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Armoured Fruit:** Gopaghoṇṭā's soft green spines make it a botanical emblem of *protected bitterness* — a nourishing flesh sheathed in a defensive coat. In the doctrinal reading this maps to its role as a **guardian of agni**: it feeds while it defends, giving substance (it is food) yet scraping and cleansing (it is bitter medicine). The dioecious habit — separate male and female plants, fruit borne only where the two meet — lends it a quiet fertility/complementarity symbolism in folk practice. Within Kenetica it is filed among the *pathya-pharmaka*: agents mild enough to live in the daily diet, whose medicine is delivered by consistency rather than force — the slow-conditioning tier, opposite pole to a shock-agent like Madana.

### Active Components

- **Bitter cucurbitane-type triterpenoids / cucurbitacins** (family-characteristic) — the bitter, kindling, and putative hypoglycaemic principles.
- **Flavonoids & phenolics** (rind and fruit) — antioxidant fraction correlating with reported antihyperglycaemic and hepatoprotective effects.
- **Saponins, tannins, alkaloids**; lectins in seed.
- **Ascorbic acid, carotenoids, and minerals** in the edible fruit (the "food" side of the profile).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Gopaghoṇṭā is filed as a **metabolic-conditioning food-medicine** for the Kindling/Purification tiers. Its action is to sharpen *agni*, clear *āma*, and gently correct kapha-pitta metabolic sluggishness — the modern shorthand being blood-sugar and lipid conditioning through dietary use. Indicated in the boggy, over-full, insulin-sluggish, kapha-pitta picture: metabolic heaviness, seasonal congestion, sluggish liver. Unlike the shock-purifiers of this volume, its therapeutic logic is *cumulative and dietary* — eaten in season, in courses, as pathya. The tuberous root is the escalation option when a sharper, more medicinal (rather than dietary) action is required.

### Scientific Correlation

Evidence is **preclinical (rodent) for the antidiabetic use; no confirmatory human trials.** Per PubMed:

- Hassan MM et al., 2022, *Heliyon* — phytochemical screening plus antidiabetic evaluation of *Momordica dioica* Roxb. fruit rind in streptozotocin-induced type-2 diabetic rats, reporting significant reduction in blood glucose alongside a defined phytochemical (flavonoid/phenolic) profile. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2022.e08771)

This corroborates the traditional "anti-diabetic / metabolic" reputation at the animal level and identifies the antioxidant phytochemistry likely responsible. **Evidence grade: LOW–MODERATE for the metabolic claim** (consistent traditional + preclinical signal, but no human RCT; other traditional uses — fever, skin, anthelmintic — remain ethnobotanically documented but clinically unverified). Attribution: PubMed.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Rasa (taste):** Tikta (bitter), Kaṭu (pungent)
- **Guṇa (qualities):** Laghu (light), Rūkṣa (dry), Tīkṣṇa (sharp — esp. root)
- **Vīrya (potency):** Uṣṇa (heating)
- **Vipāka (post-digestive):** Kaṭu (pungent)
- **Prabhāva (specific action):** Metabolic-correcting *pramehaghna* tendency (anti-"prameha"/anti-diabetic reputation) exceeding what the taste alone predicts, especially of the root.
- **Doṣa action:** ↓ Kapha, ↓ Pitta (via bitter); balancing Vāta in dietary use — classically read as *kapha-pitta-śāmaka* / broadly tridoṣa-balancing. Root leans more kapha-vāta-clearing (heating, sharp).
- **Karma (actions):** Dīpana–Pācana (kindling–digestive), Āmapācana, Krimighna (anthelmintic), Jvarahara (antipyretic), Kuṣṭhaghna (anti-skin-disease), Pramehaghna (anti-diabetic/urinary), Śothahara (external), Raktaprasādana ("blood-purifying").
- **Suśruta gaṇa membership:** Grouped with the bitter, kindling *śāka/tikta* dietetic-medicinal cucurbits of the nighaṇṭu tradition rather than a single named Suśruta nutritive gaṇa; classed among *pathya* dīpana herbs.

### Contraindications

Pregnancy — avoid the concentrated root/medicinal preparations (tīkṣṇa; family emetic/purgative kinship); fruit-as-food generally tolerated but use caution. Diabetics on hypoglycaemic drugs — additive blood-sugar lowering: monitor to avoid hypoglycaemia. High Vāta / depletion / emaciation — the drying, bitter, heating profile can aggravate; not a building agent. Root in excess may cause GI irritation, nausea, purging. Not for hyperacidity if it aggravates. Introduce dietary use seasonally and moderately.
