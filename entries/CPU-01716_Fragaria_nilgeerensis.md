# Fragaria nilgeerensis — hbri.ta.sa.hdzin, Drita sazin, "a type of Wild Strawberry" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Fragaria nilgeerensis Schlecht, the first of four binomials Clark 1995 prints for hbri.ta.sa.hdzin (then Fragaria indica, Saxifraga flagellaris and Cuscuta europaea), under the English name "a type of Wild Strawberry"; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints "Fragaria nubicola", a different species, in the place that column order gives to "Drita sazin"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under this binomial only by Clark.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk found no *Fragaria*; the only "strawberry" hits in Dioscorides are the strawberry tree (*Arbutus*), the strawberry spinach (*Blitum*) and the strawberry tomato (*Physalis*), which are other plants. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says: "Therefore more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the line prints four, of which the spine row takes the first (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 160). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1058).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry follows *hdam.bu.ka.ra* (*Hippuris vulgaris*, line 1057) and precedes *bya.po.tsi.tsi* (*Celosia cristata*, line 1059). It reads:

"hbri.ta.sa.hdzin: a type of Wild Strawberry: Fragaria nilgeerensis Schlecht: Fragaria indica: Saxifraga flagellaris: Cuscuta europaea: [has two names, for example, glang. It has small blue leaves, a red stalk, small pale yellow flowers, slightly sweet-tasting fruit and long runners]. Fragaria is an emetic in pus, blood and lymph disorders [as well as diseases in the upper body]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the two names, the small blue leaves, the red stalk, the small pale yellow flowers, the slightly sweet fruit and the long runners, and the clause "as well as diseases in the upper body". Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the English name, the four Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the sentence "Fragaria is an emetic in pus, blood and lymph disorders". That sentence names *Fragaria* itself, as a genus. Clark's index of Latin names lists "Fragraria indica Fragraria nilgeerensis" (line 1585, with the OCR spelling "Fragraria"), "Mock Strawberry" (line 1614) and "Wild Strawberry" (line 1661).

*Clark 1995, chapter 21 (line 1224).* The compounding chapter lists categories of medicines for disease. The line reads: "The class of emetics contains pericarp of Soapnut, Hill Dockleaf, Thistle (Cirsium sp.), Sweet Flag Root, Thladiantha harmsii Cogn., Fragaria nilgeerensis (a type of mock strawberry), seed of Sophora moorcroftiana, Pyrola incarnata and mustard seed." Here the species is named *Fragaria nilgeerensis* and is described as "a type of mock strawberry", and it is a member of the class of emetics.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text on "Drita sazin".* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. In chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), line 3446 reads: "Dhambu kara cures hot disorder of the lungs, liver, and channels. Drita sazin drain pus, blood and chhuser. Jhapo tsitsi stops menstrual bleeding." The names stand in the order of Clark's *hdam.bu.ka.ra*, *hbri.ta.sa.hdzin* and *bya.po.tsi.tsi* (lines 1057 to 1059), and the action ("drain pus, blood and chhuser") agrees in kind with Clark's "emetic in pus, blood and lymph disorders". The Codex does not translate *chhuser* into another word. In chapter 21, line 3659: "Soenchha, Ri sho, Chang tsher, Shudag, Serphue, Drita sazin, Kyi dru, Yakyima and white mustard seed are the group of medicines that are emetics." This group stands where Clark's list of emetics stands (line 1224): the sixth name is *Fragaria nilgeerensis* in Clark and "Drita sazin" in the Men-Tsee-Khang text, and the first two names (soapnut and Hill Dockleaf in Clark; "Soenchha" and "Ri sho" in the Men-Tsee-Khang text) and the last (mustard seed in both) fall in the same places. The index lists "Drita sazin 209, 223" (line 7351). The text gives no Latin name in either passage. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary.* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "D" block (the letter at line 5179), prints the phonetic names from "Dali" (line 5181) to "Drita sazin" (line 5196, the last, twenty-two names in all) and the transliterations to "gesar ’bri ta sa ’dzin" (line 5213). The scientific names end with "Fragaria nubicola" (line 5259), with the English "Wild strawberry" (line 5261). Counting in order, "Drita sazin" is the last phonetic name and *Fragaria nubicola* is the last scientific name, and the English fits. The glossary therefore names *Fragaria nubicola* for this drug, by column position, and does not print *Fragaria nilgeerensis*. The two Tibetan texts agree on the Tibetan name and on the genus and differ on the species. The Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Fragaria*, *hbri.ta*, *Drita sazin* and the strawberry names found no entry on this drug. I_1259, I_1248, I_1242, I_1250 and I_1270 quote the same "D" block of the glossary or its neighbouring lines, and are entries on other plants. The spine has a sibling row for *Fragaria nubicola* (CPU-01717, from the glossary line 5259) with no written file at the time of this entry. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Fragaria* is a genus of the rose family (Rosaceae) of low, creeping plants with runners and small fruit, that *Saxifraga* is a genus of another family (Saxifragaceae), and that *Cuscuta* (dodder) is a genus of leafless parasitic climbers, so the four names Clark prints point to plants of three unrelated families. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a plant that "is an emetic in pus, blood and lymph disorders" (Clark, line 1058) and a member of the class of emetics (Clark, line 1224), and a drug that "drain[s] pus, blood and chhuser" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3446) and is in the emetics group (line 3659). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only by Clark's first choice among four, and they are not Codex protocols. Inducing vomiting is a hazard and not a practice here. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary layer describes the leaves, stalk, flowers, fruit and runners and does not say which part is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Tibetan terms ("pus, blood and lymph disorders", "emetic", "upper body") are not modern diagnoses or measured outcomes, so no modern finding can be set beside them here. The identity of the drug is open between *Fragaria* species and plants of other genera, so a study of a strawberry species would not bear directly on it. It is a general, well-established fact that deliberately induced vomiting is not a safe home remedy.

The one action printed is the emetic action in the root-text sentence and in the chapter 21 class.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it is an emetic in pus, blood and lymph disorders, and the chapter on compounding counts it among the emetics. The Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same in kind. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the sentences cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and the link from this binomial to that framing rests on Clark's first choice among four.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is an emetic in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and its Latin name is one of four that Clark offers, with a different species in the glossary. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Emesis can cause dehydration and, in some conditions, harm, the identity is open between plants of different families, and no preparation or dose is given, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Pus, blood and upper-body disorders need medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
