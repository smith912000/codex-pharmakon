# Tibetan ant (water beetle) — chhu.sbur (Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch.20); "Tibetan ant" (Clark); "water beetles" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *An insect that Clark names "Tibetan ant" under the Tibetan name chhu.sbur and gives no Latin name for; Men-Tsee-Khang's parallel sentence reads "water beetles". An ant and a beetle are different insects, so the identity is unresolved* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is the Tibetan *Four Tantras*. In Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; "Clark" below), the materia medica of chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra ends with the medicines of animal origin: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). The insects are printed at the end of the "Droppings" paragraph, with no heading of their own in the OCR text, so the category Clark places the ant in is not stated; presumably it is the last, "whole body". At line 1199, after the droppings of vulture, pig, human, horse, rabbit, dog, wolf, bird and rat, comes:

"byang.pa: Beetle: Mylabris phalerata: Mylabris cichorii Fabricius: [has four names]. It purges all channel disorders. Crab: [has nine names], and chhu.sbur: Tibetan ant: Both dispel urinary retention. (Tibetan ant in particular is a mild purgative of channel disorders). bse.sbur: Tibetan cockroach ..."

Clark's introduction (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, and those in parentheses from oral instructions. So the claim that the crab and the *chhu.sbur* "both dispel urinary retention" is the unbracketed line, and the remark that the Tibetan ant "in particular is a mild purgative of channel disorders" is the oral-instruction layer. Clark gives no number of names and no Latin name for *chhu.sbur*. His preface (line 83) says that where the identity of an animal product is in doubt he has given the Tibetan name with "the probable English identification", and that is all that is printed here. The word "ant" is also listed in his index (line 1652).

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`; "MTK" below) gives the matching passage at lines 3572-3573: "Blister bettle cleanse disorders of the channels. Crabs and water beetles cure obstruction of urine. Dark beetles and wingless cockroaches relieve abdominal cramps." The sequence matches Clark's (blister beetle, then crab with the second insect, then the dark beetle and cockroach), so MTK's "water beetles" stands where Clark's *chhu.sbur* "Tibetan ant" stands. That correspondence is by position, since neither text prints the other's Tibetan word. The project's list name "Tibetan ant (water beetle)" combines the two English renderings.

MTK's glossary on animals has an insect table (lines 7154-7171, with the OCR columns split): phonetic names "Nabun bhumo", "Jangpa", "Jingjing Thulu", "Sebur", common names "Snail", "Blister Beetle", "Wingless Cockroach", "Dark Beetle", and scientific names "Eulota similalis Farussa", "Mylabris phalerata pall", "Eupolypnaga sinensis walker". There is no "water beetle" or "ant" row, so the glossary does not resolve the identity. Clark's names for the Tibetan ant (*chhu.sbur*) and the Tibetan cockroach (*bse.sbur*) both end in *sbur*, and MTK's phonetic "Sebur" is glossed "Dark Beetle"; I draw no inference from the shared syllable. ### Active Components

No text read names a constituent, and the insect is not identified, so no composition can be stated. In general terms, insect bodies are mainly protein and chitin, and some beetles (the blister beetles, which are a different entry in the same sentence) carry the irritant cantharidin. Whether any of that applies to the water beetle or the ant is not known. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text names the insect as a medicine that dispels urinary retention, and as a mild purgative of the "channel" disorders. The insect is not identified, the sources give no preparation, and Clark notes that few animal products are still used (his introduction, line 57). The Codex does not recommend taking an insect on the strength of this entry.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither text gives a preparation or a quantity for the insect. The sentence says only that, together with the crab, it dispels urinary retention. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** No taste, potency or post-digestive taste is printed for this insect in either text. The only qualities stated are its actions: it dispels urinary retention (with the crab), and, in the oral-instruction layer, it is a mild purgative of channel disorders. The animal-meat statements of the Explanatory Tantra (Clark line 673; MTK line 2923) concern meats and are not applied to an insect.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The frame is the thirteen-part animal section of the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica (Clark line 1148), and in it the insect is a drug for urinary retention and a mild purgative. Nothing in the passages read places it in any practice of essence extraction (*bcud len*), and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, so none is asserted. The wider frame is that the three humors are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration (Clark line 51), so disease is at root a spiritual condition, and that food, behaviour, medicine and accessory therapy are the four supports of treatment (line 1299), of which medicine is the third. The chapter on routine behaviour asks the practitioner to refrain from the ten negative actions, the first of which is "taking life" (line 616). The rejuvenation passage (lines 1266-1275) names no animal substance in the lines read.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The text records a small insect as a remedy for urinary retention, and the tradition's own preface notes that few animal products are still used. The Codex does not recommend it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Identity:** the insect is not identified (an ant in Clark, a beetle in MTK), so do not substitute any insect, and especially not a blister beetle, which MTK and Clark list separately and which is irritant and toxic.
- **Hygiene:** wild-caught insects can carry bacteria and parasites.
- **Medical:** urinary retention can be an emergency and should be assessed by a clinician.
- **Ethics:** the Codex does not recommend taking an animal's life for these uses.
