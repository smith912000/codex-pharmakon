# Horse dung — "Horse dung" (rGyud-bzhi, Explanatory Tantra ch.20, "Droppings"; Tibetan name not printed in Clark's line)
## *The droppings of the horse, one of the "Droppings" group of the animal medicines in the Tibetan materia medica* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The locus is Clark's 1995 translation, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, "the powers of medicines" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). The eighth section is "Medicines of Animal Origin", which "consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (lines 1147-1148). Under the sub-heading "Droppings" (line 1198) the text gives, in one run (line 1199):

"Vulture's droppings: increase digestive heat, disperse tumors and suppurate swellings. Pig's stool: cures indigestion, septic disorders, infectious fevers and bile tumors. Human excreta: cure gall stones, neutralize poisons, reduce swellings [and subdue spirits]. Horse dung: is anthelmintic, anti-emetic, cures wind-bile combinations [and is beneficial against spirits]. Rabbit droppings: purge oedema [and are beneficial for wounds]. Dog stool: [is especially beneficial against spirits]. Wolf stool and droppings of Gong.mo ... [all three] reduce swellings. Bird and Rat droppings: both drain pus [accumulations]."

For horse dung the text therefore gives three plain actions (it is anthelmintic, it is anti-emetic, it cures "wind-bile combinations") and a bracketed fourth (it is "beneficial against spirits"). `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 378 lists "Horse dung", animal, with the source "Explanatory Tantra ch.20 (Efficacy of Medicinal Substances)"; it gives no Tibetan name.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 3568) renders the item as: "Horse dung cures ein disorder, combined disorder of tripa and loong, and stops vomiting." Here "combined disorder of tripa and loong" matches Clark's "wind-bile combinations" (Men-Tsee-Khang uses the Tibetan words, Clark the English "wind" and "bile"), and "stops vomiting" matches "anti-emetic". "Ein disorder" has no counterpart in Clark's line; the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary has an entry on a tumour condition spelled with the same letters, but I did not establish that it is the same word, and the lines read do not say. [USER: verify] The Men-Tsee-Khang line lacks the "anthelmintic" and "spirits" clauses that Clark prints.

Clark's preface says that "nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57). He also says he "interpolated liberally from the Blue Aquamarine commentary" (line 80); the square brackets in the lines above are not explained in the lines read, so which words are root text and which are commentary is not established here. [USER: verify] The "spirits" clause is a statement about spirit-caused illness in the Tibetan frame and is recorded only as the text states it.

### Active Components

Herbivore dung is the undigested remainder of plant fibre (cellulose, lignin) with gut bacteria, bile pigments and salts, and water. The horse is a hindgut fermenter, and its droppings are coarse and fibrous. The droppings can also carry parasite eggs. No analysis of the Tibetan material was retrieved, and no composition figures are given. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The text lists the dung among the animal medicines with the actions above. The Codex's application layer adds nothing, and the entry is not a protocol.

It is not a recommended practice. Animal faeces are a source of infectious organisms and parasites; the sources give no dose, no method of preparation and no route (by mouth, as a poultice or as smoke), so no safe use can be described. The entry stands as a record of what the Tibetan text states, in a section the translator says is little used now.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation is given in either translation: the line says only what the dung does. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] Whether it was fresh, dried, burnt or fermented, and whether taken, applied or inhaled, is not stated in the lines read.

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] General facts: faeces are not a recognised treatment for worms, vomiting or digestive disorders; they can carry bacteria such as *Salmonella* and *E. coli*, and parasite eggs, and horse manure in particular can carry tetanus spores. The one medical context where faecal material is used today is carefully screened and processed donor stool for specific gut infections, which is a clinical procedure with no connection to the unprocessed dung in this entry.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT): the chapter 20 line gives actions only (anthelmintic, anti-emetic, cures wind-bile combinations). It prints no taste, potency or post-digestive taste for horse dung, and none is assigned here.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Horse dung is attested in the Explanatory Tantra ch.20 animal section as a therapeutic substance: three plain actions and one bracketed action against spirits. The frame it sits in is read from Clark's preface and root text: the three humors (wind, bile, phlegm) "are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration respectively" (line 51), and treatment relies on "the fourfold food, behavioral pattern, medicine and accessory therapy" (line 1299). Nothing in the lines read gives this substance a place in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or in any practice of the subtle body, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition. The "spirits" clause is a statement about illness, not about cultivation.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. There is no cultivation claim, no dose and no method, and the substance is faecal material. The honest use is to read the line as a record of the breadth of the Tibetan animal materia medica.

### Contraindications

- **Not a recommended practice.** This is a historical record. Nothing here is advice to ingest or apply horse dung.
- **Infection and parasites:** faecal material, including horse manure, carries bacteria, parasite eggs and tetanus spores, and must not be eaten or put on broken skin.
- **Unknown preparation and dose:** neither is given in the sources, so no safe use can be described.
- **Vomiting, worms or digestive illness:** persistent symptoms need medical assessment.
- **Spirit claims:** recorded as the text states them and not a basis for treatment.
