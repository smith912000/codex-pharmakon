# Tibetan gazelle horn (Procapra) — *dgo.wa*, "Procapra pictaudata" (Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch.20)
## *The horn of the animal the Tibetan text calls* dgo.wa*; Clark prints two Latin names for it,* Procapra pictaudata *Hodgson v. Hook and* Prodorcas gutturosa*, and the Men-Tsee-Khang translation renders the corresponding word as "gazelles"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is the Tibetan *Four Tantras*. In Clark's 1995 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; "Clark" below) the materia medica of chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra ends with the medicines of animal origin: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). Horn is the first of the thirteen (lines 1151-1158).

The entry is at line 1156: "*dgo.wa*: *Procapra pictaudata* Hodgson v. Hook: *Prodorcas gutturosa*: [has nine names. It dries pus (accumulations) in the abdomen]. The horn of Hodgson's antelope and Procapra halt diarrhoea." Clark's introduction (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets are taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries, and those in parentheses from the oral instructions of Dr. Yeshe Donden and Dr. Pema Dorje. So "has nine names" and "dries pus in the abdomen" are in the commentary layer, and the unbracketed sentence that follows is the one that attaches an action to the horn: it halts diarrhoea, together with the horn of the Tibetan antelope (*gtsod*, the entry just above it at line 1155; see III_198). The Latin spelling *pictaudata* is as Clark prints it.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 edition of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`; "MTK" below) has at line 3508 "The horns of antelopes and gazelles cure diarrhea." Its animal classification (line 2907) lists the gazelle with the deer, musk deer, argali, rabbit and antelope in "the group of herbivorous wild animals". MTK does not give the Tibetan word or a Latin name for the gazelle in that sentence, so the link between its "gazelles" and Clark's *dgo.wa* rests on position and action, as for the antelope horn above. In Clark's dietary chapter (line 672) "*Procapra pictaudata*" appears among the "game animals" of group (c), which is a second place where the name occurs and gives the animal as a food animal.

On identity: the Tibetan word and its Latin names are Clark's. He prints two Latin names, which the extract row records as "*Procapra pictaudata* Hodgson v. Hook; *Prodorcas gutturosa*" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, row 225, "Clark 1995 (line 1156)", with the note that more than one Latin name is given and the first is used). That row is classed "plant" in the class column, which is wrong for an animal horn; the spine row correctly files it as an animal. The two Latin names are as printed and I do not judge between them. The English "Tibetan gazelle" is the spine's name for the first name, and the identification is a translator's. No row for this horn was found in `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv`. No other tradition's text on disk was found to name it.

### Active Components

Horn of the bovid type is mainly keratin, a structural protein, over a bony core that carries calcium phosphate. These are general facts about horn. The Tibetan texts name no constituent and no mechanism for the actions claimed, and this entry supplies none.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The record is one claim: the horn halts diarrhoea, in company with the horn of the Tibetan antelope, plus a bracketed commentary note that it dries pus in the abdomen. The Codex does not recommend using it. A wild gazelle is a hunted animal, hygiene is a concern for unprocessed horn, and diarrhoea has safer remedies, the first of which is replacing lost fluid. No protocol is offered.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither text gives a preparation (powdered, burnt, in a compound), a quantity or a dose for this horn. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] Clark remarks that "nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57), a remark that applies to the whole animal section.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Diarrhoea can cause dehydration and loss of salts, and the main treatment is oral rehydration; persistent or bloody diarrhoea, or diarrhoea in infants, the elderly or the ill, needs medical assessment. Keratin is a largely indigestible protein, and nothing in the sources links it to an anti-diarrhoeal action. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi.** No taste, potency or post-digestive taste is printed for this horn in the passages read (Clark line 1156; MTK line 3508). The only quality attached to it is the action "halts diarrhoea", with the bracketed "dries pus (accumulations) in the abdomen". Other horns in the same passage carry stated qualities (takin horn "increases [body] heat", Clark line 1158), but these are not extended to this one.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The frame is the thirteen-part animal section of the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica (Clark line 1148), and in it the horn is a drug for a stated action, halting diarrhoea (line 1156). Nothing in the passages read places it in any practice of essence extraction (*bcud len*), and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, so none is asserted here. The tradition's wider frame is that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching the root; this entry adds nothing to that.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports cultivation use. The horn appears as part of one line in a list of horn medicines, with a single action, in a section of the materia medica that Clark says is little used today. A cultivator has nothing to take from this entry; the honest practice is abstention.

### Contraindications

- **Diarrhoea:** needs fluid replacement and, if severe, bloody or prolonged, or in infants and the frail, medical care; do not delay it for an animal remedy.
- **Hygiene:** unprocessed animal material from the wild carries a risk of infection.
- **Law and conservation:** the wild gazelles of the Tibetan plateau and Mongolian steppe are hunted and in places protected; the status of a particular species was not checked in the sources and should be confirmed before any trade or use. [USER: verify]
- **Identification:** the animal is taken from Clark's gloss, with two Latin names; this entry does not say that any product on sale is the *dgo.wa* of the text.
