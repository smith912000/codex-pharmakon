# Dyer knotweed (Chinese indigo) fruit — 蓝实 *Lan Shi* ("Semen Indigonis", Yang 1998)

## *Persicaria tinctoria (Aiton) H.Gross (syn. Polygonum tinctorium), the dyer's knotweed or Chinese indigo, the identification we give to 蓝实 ("indigo fruit"); of medium confidence: modern materia medica takes 蓝实 as the seed of this plant, but the Chinese name 蓝 covered several blue-dye plants, and the classical glosses Sun quotes (马蓝, "large-leaved winter indigo") point at a different one, probably Strobilanthes. The Shennong paragraph gives no description of the plant* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts on disk.** *Lan shi* is a monograph of the Divine Farmer's Classic. It has no chapter in Sushruta, Dioscorides, Ebers or the Assyrian texts. The Codex already has two other blue-dye plants, *Indigofera tinctoria* (I_455) and woad, *Isatis tinctoria* (I_583); neither is the Shennong drug as the texts read here place it. This entry is the separate monograph of 蓝实 from Sun Xingyan's 1799 reconstruction.

**The Shennong text proper.** The 味 paragraph, in the upper (上) classic, reads 味苦，寒 ("bitter, cold"). It "governs" (主) 解诸毒 ("resolving the various poisons"), 杀蛊 (one character lost after 蛊 in the transcription; Yang reads "kills worms"), 注鬼 ("*zhu* ghost", Yang's "infant ghost" and "demonic influx" for the run) and 螫毒 ("stinging poison", Yang's "insect bite toxins"). Then comes the life-nourishing clause: **久服，头不白、轻身** ("taken long: the head does not whiten [the hair does not go grey], lightens the body"). The habitat is 生平泽 ("level marshes"). (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, upper classic, entry 蓝实 *lan shi*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 663)

**Mingyi bielu layer (later).** 《名医》曰 says **其茎叶可以染青**, "its stems and leaves can be used to dye blue", and gives the place 生河内 ("Henei") (line 665). This is the only description of the plant, and it is about the dye, not the fruit. The drug is named "fruit" (实) while its fame is as a dye plant; the text does not explain this.

**Sun's note (案).** Sun collects the glosses on the word *lan* (line 667 and line 669). The *Shuowen* says (one character lost at the head of the clause) "…, the *ma lan*", and "*lan* is the grass that dyes blue". The *Erya* has the same lost-character word "*ma lan*", and Guo Pu glosses it as **今大叶冬蓝也** ("today's large-leaved winter indigo"). The *Zhouli* office "in charge of dye-plants" and Zheng Xuan's note say that dye-plants are "*lan*, madder (*qian*), *xiang tou* and the like" (蓝茜，象头之属). The *Xia xiaozheng* (calendar) has the line 五月启灌蓝 for the fifth month (Sun does not explain the verb phrase, which the Codex leaves untranslated), and the *Mao Shi* (Odes) has "all morning I gathered *lan*", on which the *Jian* commentary says "*lan* is a dye-plant" (终朝采蓝；蓝，染草也).

**Yang 1998 (second witness).** Yang prints "Lan Shi (Semen Indigonis)" in "Herbs: Superior Class" (line 2076; `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`), with the same flavour, nature and indications, and renders the life-nourishing clause as "Protracted taking may prevent the head hair from turning white and make the body light" (lines 2077-2079). His note 122 says that the paragraph is "very terse, yet pregnant with meaning", and that "resolving various toxins" implies, besides insect bites, a wider range of heat-toxin conditions; the note is Yang's clinical gloss from later practice and is not Shennong text. The Codex records them only as Yang's gloss. (His note 124, which lists later clinical uses of an indigo drug, appears to belong to the next entry and is not used.)

**Identity.** The Chinese word *lan* is a group name for blue-dye plants. In Sun's quotations the early glosses name *ma lan* and "large-leaved winter indigo"; these are usually referred to a plant of the genus *Strobilanthes*, not to the knotweed. Modern Chinese materia medica takes 蓝实 as the seed of 蓼蓝, which is *Persicaria tinctoria*, and the Codex follows that for the entry's name. The dyes of woad (*Isatis*), *Strobilanthes* and *Indigofera* are all indigo, and the drug may stand for more than one. Confidence is medium, and the Codex does not claim the Shennong text means the knotweed in particular. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Indigo-yielding plants contain colourless indigo precursors (indican and related glycosides) that give the blue dye indigo on fermentation and oxidation; this is the general basis of all indigo dyes. The composition of the seed is not given in any text read. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The classic records the fruit for "resolving poisons", for the *gu* and ghost afflictions of its period, for poisonous stings, and, on long use, for keeping the hair from going grey and lightening the body. The Codex records these as the claims of the classic. No source read shows that the seed prevents grey hair or resolves poison, and the Shennong text states no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The paragraph gives no preparation, dose or course. The Mingyi layer notes only the dye use of stems and leaves. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Indigo-yielding plants are the source of the dye indigo, which is old and widely used; this is well established. Hair greying is due to loss of pigment-forming cells and is not known to be preventable by any plant seed. Indigo-derived compounds such as indirubin, from the prepared dye (*qing dai*) of indigo plants, have been investigated in modern pharmacology, but that concerns the prepared dye and not this seed, and no study of this seed was retrieved. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SBJ (Shennong text proper).** 味苦，寒: bitter; cold. No organ or channel affinity is printed. The paragraph prints no toxicity word; the upper-class placement carries the preface's general statement that upper drugs are non-toxic (无毒).
- No other tradition's classification is applied.

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: upper class, with a "taken long" clause.* The paragraph stands in the upper classic. The preface says upper drugs are "ruler" drugs that "nourish life (养命) to correspond with heaven", are non-toxic, may be taken in quantity and for long without harming the person, and are for those who wish to "lighten the body and benefit the qi, not age and extend the years" (line 43). The paragraph itself carries **久服，头不白、轻身** ("taken long, the head does not go white, lightens the body"). *Qing shen* (轻身, "lighten the body") is a classical effect term with no pharmacological equivalent; it is not weight loss. The register is (a) as a statement of what the classic assigns to the drug; it is not a statement that the seed does it. (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, upper classic, entry 蓝实 *lan shi*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` lines 43 and 663)

**Practitioner's note.** The Chinese scheme classes this seed with the drugs for long-term life-nourishing use, with the promise of a dark head of hair and a light body. The cultivator's use of the entry is mostly to read the claim for what it is: a grading made by the classic and not a result. The plant is a dye plant first, and the identification of the seed is not secure. If used at all, use it only as the text allows. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Identity.** Several different plants carry the name *lan*; seed sold as 蓝实 may not be *Persicaria tinctoria*.
- **Pregnancy.** Indigo-plant preparations are not known to be safe in pregnancy; this is a precaution drawn from general practice, not from the Shennong text. [USER: verify]
- **Poisoning and stings.** The classic's "resolving poison" uses are historical claims. A serious sting, bite or poisoning needs urgent medical care.
- **"Taken long".** The long-use clause is the classic's claim and is not supported by modern evidence.
