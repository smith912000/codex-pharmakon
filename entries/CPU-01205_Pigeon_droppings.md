# Pigeon droppings — rGyud-bzhi, phug.ron, "Pigeon droppings" (droppings of the pigeon, listed under "Droppings")
## *Droppings of the pigeon; Clark prints the Tibetan name phug.ron and the binomial Columba rupestris rupestris Pallas* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Pigeon droppings are listed in the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, the chapter on the efficacy of medicinal substances, in Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). They fall in section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), whose thirteen headings are "horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). Under "Droppings" (heading line 1198) the long line 1199 reads, for the relevant clause: "phug.ron: Pigeon droppings: Columba rupestris rupestris Pallas: suppurate swellings [and, as a fomentation, are beneficial in phlegm disorders and 'white' rheumatism]." The clause names the Tibetan word *phug.ron* and gives a binomial, which is the translator's identification (the hill pigeon in general usage; the lines read do not say how Clark reached it). The words in square brackets are Clark's interpolation; he says in his introduction (line 80) that he has "interpolated liberally from the Blue Aquamarine commentary" and relied on oral teachings, but the lines read do not state the bracket convention outright. The same line gives the neighbouring droppings: "Vulture's droppings: increase digestive heat, disperse tumors and suppurate swellings. Pig's stool: cures indigestion, septic disorders, infectious fevers and bile tumors. ... Rabbit droppings: purge oedema [and are beneficial for wounds]. Dog stool: [is especially beneficial against spirits]. Wolf stool and droppings of Gong.mo ... [all three] reduce swellings. Bird and Rat droppings: both drain pus [accumulations]." Pigeon droppings thus share the "suppurate swellings" action with vulture droppings in the list.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3568) is shorter and agrees on the main action: "Pigeon droppings suppurate inflammed swelling." It does not carry Clark's added fomentation clause for phlegm disorders and "white" rheumatism. The Tibetan wording is not in the files read, so whether that clause belongs to the root text or only to the commentary Clark draws on cannot be said. ### Active Components

Bird droppings are a mixture of faecal matter and urates (uric acid and its salts, since birds excrete nitrogen mostly as uric acid), with water, undigested food residue and gut bacteria. Pigeon droppings are rich in nitrogen and phosphorus, which is why bird guano has been used as fertiliser. No active principle is claimed by the sources, and no composition specific to this entry was read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is one action in a materia medica list: pigeon droppings "suppurate swellings" and, as a fomentation, help "phlegm disorders and 'white' rheumatism" (Clark; the second part is bracketed commentary). To suppurate a swelling is to bring it to a head, and the Tantra gives it as a treatment, not as a hazard. The Codex offers no protocol. Pigeon droppings are a recognised source of infection (see Contraindications), and a swelling that is hot, painful or spreading needs clinical assessment. The Tantra gives no preparation, dose or route in these lines. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Dried pigeon droppings can carry the fungi *Cryptococcus neoformans* and *Histoplasma capsulatum* and the bacterium *Chlamydia psittaci*, and inhaled dust from them is a known route of infection, notably in people with weakened immunity. An infected swelling is an abscess or a related condition and is managed by clinical assessment and often drainage; a poultice of droppings does not treat it and may add infection. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* Neither translation read assigns pigeon droppings any cultivation framing. They are a short action in a list of animal droppings. The frame around the list is the tradition's own: the project's cultivation spec records that the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) are derived from the three poisons and that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root. That point comes from the spec and not from any line about pigeon droppings. No source read attaches pigeon droppings to *bcud len* (essence extraction), and there is no formal Tibetan category of substances for subtle-body practice. Clark's remark (line 57) that "only very few animal products are used" nowadays is a note on usage, not on status.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a one-line action in a list of animal droppings, with an infection hazard attached and no cultivation claim. Its use today is as a record of what the Tantra says.

### Contraindications

- **Infection:** dried pigeon droppings can carry fungal and bacterial pathogens (histoplasmosis, cryptococcosis, psittacosis); handling or inhaling dust is a hazard, and applying them to skin or a swelling risks wound infection and tetanus.
- **Not a treatment for abscess or arthritis:** a swelling or joint disease needs clinical assessment; do not rely on this line.
- **Immunosuppression, pregnancy, children:** these groups are at higher risk from the pathogens above.
- **Legal and conservation status:** wild pigeons and their roosts may be protected, and collecting droppings from them may need permission. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** the droppings themselves do not require killing the bird, which is the least troubling aspect of the entry; the infection risk remains.
