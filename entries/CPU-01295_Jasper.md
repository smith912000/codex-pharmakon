# Jasper — Iaspis Lithos / "jaspis stone" (as printed in the 2000 English edition of Dioscorides)
## *Jasper, the translator's gloss being "previously name for green Chalcedony"; Dioscorides describes it as a stone of several appearances, and the English edition does not fix it to one mineral* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus for this entry is *De Materia Medica* 5-160 *Iaspis Lithos* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, line 10664; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row "5-160 (line 10664)"; `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 886, class mineral, identification marked uncertain). The translator's gloss under the heading is "Jasper — previously name for green Chalcedony" (line 10665). The chapter stands in the run of Book 5 stones, after 5-159 and before 5-161 *Aetites Lithos*, the eagle stone (line 10667).

The whole chapter reads (line 10666): "Some jaspis stone is like emerald and some like crystal, resembling phlegm; some like the air and some capnia as we should say, smoky; and some called assyrius is glittering, with white middle partitions. Some is called terebinthizusa as it is coloured like calais. All are said to be amulets used as personal ornaments, and to speed delivery [in childbirth] tied to the thigh." The bracket "[in childbirth]" is the translator's. There is no medicinal action, no taste, no place of origin and no dose in the chapter. What it gives is a list of looks (emerald-like, crystal-like, air-coloured, smoky, glittering with white partitions, and the colour of *calais*, a word the chapter does not explain) and one use, which Dioscorides reports as hearsay: "are said to be".

That wording matters. The translator's introduction (line 394) notes that "A few superstitious practices are recorded in De Materia Medica. Amulets and mascots were valued", and gives plant examples (Anchusa against snakes, Polemonia against scorpion bites). Recording a use is not endorsing it: the chapter says jasper stones "are said to be" amulets and does not say that Dioscorides tried it or that it works.

Jasper also appears once as a comparison in another chapter: 5-154 *Thuites Lithos* (line 10634, the chapter text at line 10636) says thyites "is greenish like jasper [previously this was green chalcedony]", and the translator glosses thyites as turquoise. The bracket repeats the translator's note on jasper and tells us nothing more about the stone.

No other tradition is tagged for this entry. The Ebers (Bryan), Assyrian (Thompson) and Shennong (Yang) texts, the Sushruta sutra chapters and Clark's 1995 Tibetan translation were searched for "jasper" and returned nothing; the other Sushruta volumes and the Men-Tsee-Khang text were not searched.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, "jasper" today names opaque, coloured, fine-grained silica (quartz, a form of chalcedony coloured by iron and other impurities), and the translator's older gloss calls it green chalcedony. The chapter describes stones of several colours and appearances under one name, and it does not say whether these were all silica; the ancient use of the word may have covered more than one mineral. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved give no medicinal use for jasper; the only use is that it was "said to be" an amulet and an ornament, tied to the thigh to speed childbirth. The Codex does not recommend any amulet or other measure as a way of hastening labour. Nothing in the sources supports a modern application, and the stone is not taken internally in the text.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources retrieved give no preparation for jasper: nothing is ground, boiled, burned or mixed. The only handling is wearing the stone as an ornament or tying it to the thigh (line 10666). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Jasper and chalcedony are silica, which is insoluble and inert. A stone worn on the skin or tied to the body has no known effect on labour or childbirth. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** no quality is printed for jasper in the chapter. There is no taste, no degree of heat, cold, dryness or moisture, and no action such as astringent or drying. Dioscorides' qualities for neighbouring stones are not transferred to it.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for jasper. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, assay, adulteration), and this chapter gives a small part of it: a catalogue of the looks of the stone (emerald-green, crystal-clear, air-coloured, smoky, glittering with white partitions), which is a description for recognition and not a test of authenticity. The one use recorded is amuletic, introduced by "are said to be". Recording is not endorsing, and nothing in the chapter gives the stone a status as a thing that nourishes or protects the person.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. Jasper is in Dioscorides as a stone to look at and wear, and the chapter's one use is hearsay about childbirth that the Codex does not recommend relying on. Wearing a stone as an ornament is a personal choice, not a treatment. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Childbirth:** labour and delivery need a midwife or clinician; do not rely on a stone, amulet or any tied object to hasten or ease delivery, or delay seeking help.
- **Ingestion:** a hard stone should not be swallowed, and the text does not say it was.
- **Dust:** cutting or grinding jasper produces silica dust, which is a lung hazard.
- **Identification:** "jasper" in the English edition is a translator's gloss ("previously name for green Chalcedony"); the chapter describes several colours and appearances, and the sources read do not say which mineral or minerals they were.
