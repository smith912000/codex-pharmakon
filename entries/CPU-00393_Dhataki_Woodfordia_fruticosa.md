### Origin & Tradition

Dhataki is a scrambling, much-branched shrub of the loosestrife family (Lythraceae), native across the Indian subcontinent and into Southeast Asia and Indonesia, growing on dry rocky hillsides, scrub forest and degraded ground from the plains up into the lower Himalaya. It is unmistakable in flower: dense clusters of tubular scarlet-orange blossoms borne along leafless or nearly leafless branches, giving the vernacular English name *fire-flame bush*. It is the flowers — dried, dark red-brown, and traded in bulk — that constitute the drug. The Indonesian trade name for the dried flower is *Sidowayah*, and it is under that name that the material entered the Japanese phytochemical literature that gave us most of what is chemically known about it (PubMed: Yoshida et al., 1990, *Chem Pharm Bull*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1248/cpb.38.1211)).

**SS** here denotes Sushruta, the surgical-tradition stratum of Ayurveda, and by extension the classical Ayurvedic corpus in which Dhataki is a named drug. The cultivator should understand the code as a claim about lineage, not about a single verse: Dhataki appears in the Ayurvedic materia medica as a *kashaya* (astringent) drug and, far more distinctively, as an operational ingredient in the pharmaceutical process called *Sandhana kalpana*.

That process is the reason this entry exists, and it is the plant's true signature. **Sandhana kalpana** is the Ayurvedic art of self-generating alcoholic medicines: *asava* (made with cold water decoction or juice) and *arishta* (made with a hot decoction). Herbs, water and a sugar source — jaggery, honey, sugar — are sealed in a vessel and left to ferment. No distilled spirit is added. The alcohol makes itself, and that self-generated ethanol both extracts the active constituents of the polyherbal charge and preserves the finished medicine. Dhataki flowers are the classical *initiator* of this fermentation — the standard starter charged into the vessel to begin the process. A review of the Sandhana literature from the Vedic period forward treats the "variety and relevance of initiator of fermentation" as one of the core technical variables of the dosage form (PubMed: Chaudhary et al., 2011, *Ayu*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4103/0974-8520.93925)).

This is a genuinely distinctive traditional role, and one worth stating precisely rather than romantically. What the peer-reviewed record supports is that asava/arishta preparations ferment by means of *intrinsic* microbes carried in on the plant material rather than an added commercial culture, and that this consortium comprises identified yeasts and bacteria (PubMed: Vinothkanna & Sekar, 2018, *Ayu*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4103/ayu.AYU_237_17)). What I could **not** retrieve this run is a paper isolating the fermentative yeast specifically from *Woodfordia fruticosa* flowers and demonstrating that it, rather than another component of the charge, is the source organism. So: the traditional and textual role of Dhataki as the initiator is well attested; the mechanistic claim that the Dhataki flower is the physical carrier of the wild yeast is **[USER: verify]** — plausible, widely repeated in secondary literature, and not confirmed by a primary source in this retrieval. The cultivator should hold the doctrine and the mechanism apart.

Beyond fermentation, the classical use is astringent: diarrhoea and dysentery, excessive menstrual bleeding (*menorrhagia*), and topical application to wounds and ulcers. This is exactly what one would predict from a flower this rich in tannin, and it is one of the rarer cases where traditional indication and known chemistry line up without strain.

### Active Components

The chemistry is dominated by **hydrolysable tannins**, and specifically by a family of macrocyclic ellagitannin oligomers named for the plant itself. From the dried flowers, woodfordins A, B and C were characterised along with seven previously known hydrolysable tannins including oenothein B, a dimer with documented host-mediated antitumour activity; woodfordin C carries a macro-ring structure (PubMed: Yoshida et al., 1990, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1248/cpb.38.1211)). A companion paper described the trimeric macro-ring tannins woodfordin D and oenothein A, joined by a novel constituent unit the authors named the *woodfordinoyl* group (PubMed: Yoshida et al., 1991, *Chem Pharm Bull*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1248/cpb.39.1157)). Woodfordin I, a macrocyclic ellagitannin dimer, is also documented from the genus (PubMed: Liu et al., 2004, *Toxicol Appl Pharmacol*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.taap.2003.08.017)).

Alongside these, the general phytochemical screening literature reports polyphenolics, flavonoids, tannins and phytosterols in this class of material; a study of Ayurvedic fermented medicines attributes their antioxidant activity to phenolics, flavonoids, tannins and phytosterols (PubMed: Vinothkanna & Sekar, 2018, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4103/ayu.AYU_237_17)). Anthocyanins are commonly cited for the red flowers in secondary sources; I did not retrieve a primary characterisation this run — treat as **[USER: verify]**.

The practical point for the cultivator: this is a very high-tannin drug. Everything the tradition does with it — the astringency, the wound work, the bleeding indications, and arguably the polyphenol load carried into the ferment — follows from that single fact.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Dhataki is filed in Kenetica as a **binding and initiating** agent — an unusual pairing, and the reason it is a Foundation signature study. Two apparently opposed functions live in one flower.

*Binding.* Tannins precipitate protein. On a mucosal surface this produces the classic astringent effect: tightening, reduction of secretion, reduction of seepage. The traditional targets — loose bowel, flooding menses, an open wound — are all conditions of a boundary that will not hold. The Kenetica reading is straightforward: Dhataki is used where containment has failed and the tissue needs to be drawn shut.

*Initiating.* The same flower, put in a sealed jar with sugar and water, starts a process. It is a threshold drug in the literal pharmaceutical sense — the thing you add to make the transformation begin. In the training frame this is worth sitting with: the cultivator's usual instinct is to associate initiation with heating, dispersing, opening agents, and Dhataki is none of those. It initiates by being present, not by acting.

Honest limitation, stated plainly: neither function has been demonstrated in a controlled human trial that I could retrieve. The astringent indications are traditional with supportive animal data (see Scientific Correlation). The fermentation role is pharmaceutical-technical rather than therapeutic — nobody is claiming Dhataki treats anything by initiating a ferment.

**No numeric human dose is given here because I could not source one from primary literature this run.** Any figure the cultivator encounters in a secondary compilation should be traced to a pharmacopoeial monograph before use — **[USER: verify against the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India]**.

The finished ferment carries the additional variable of ethanol. Measured ethanol content across 20 marketed asava/arishta products from three manufacturers differed statistically significantly between products, which the authors read as poor GMP compliance and a safety hazard, since the procedure is nominally identical across manufacturers and the ethanol content therefore ought not to vary (PubMed: Maithani et al., 2018, *Am J Drug Alcohol Abuse*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/00952990.2018.1529181)). **Practical consequence: you cannot assume you know the alcohol content of a commercial asava or arishta.**

### Scientific Correlation

Grading each claim:

- **Fermentation-initiator role — TRADITIONAL / PHARMACEUTICAL-TECHNICAL, well documented as practice.** The Sandhana process, its self-generated ethanol and the role of an initiator are documented in the Ayurvedic pharmaceutical literature. The specific claim that Dhataki flowers carry the fermenting yeast is **NOT DEMONSTRATED in retrieved primary sources** — the intrinsic-microbe work identifies yeasts and bacteria in the finished medicines without tracing them to the Dhataki flower.
- **Antidiarrhoeal — PRECLINICAL (animal), positive.** Statistically significant antidiarrhoeal activity in rodents for bark extract at both 200 and 400 mg/kg and for leaf extract at 400 mg/kg. Note this study used **leaf and bark, not flower** — congener-of-organ data, not the classical drug.
- **Antioxidant — PRECLINICAL (in vitro), positive.** DPPH radical scavenging with IC50 6.11–20.79 µg/mL across extracts and fractions (leaf/bark).
- **Antimicrobial — PRECLINICAL (in vitro), positive.** Disc diffusion activity; separately, extract inhibited *E. coli* and *S. aureus*.
- **Analgesic / anti-inflammatory — PRECLINICAL (animal + in vitro), positive.** Central and peripheral analgesic responses in rodents (leaf/bark).
- **Hepatoprotective — PRECLINICAL only, and the review literature overstates it.** A narrative review lists *W. fruticosa* among hepatoprotective plants and quotes "25–35% improvement in liver enzymes," but the same review states that clinical evidence is *not* as strong as preclinical, and gives no trial citation. **Treat those percentages as unsupported for this plant.** In vitro hepatoprotective screening on CCl4-intoxicated hepatocytes is real but is bench work.
- **Antitumour / cytotoxic — PRECLINICAL, mechanistically interesting.** Woodfordin C (woodfruticosin) is a DNA topoisomerase II inhibitor, with inhibitory activity against topo-II reported as much stronger than etoposide or adriamycin, though weaker growth inhibition against most human tumour cell lines tested; in vivo activity against subcutaneous colon38 in mice. Woodfordin I induced apoptosis in K562 leukaemia cells via the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway. **This is not a cancer treatment and must not be presented as one — these are isolated compounds in cells and mice.**
- **Prebiotic-like activity — PRECLINICAL (in vitro), positive, novel.** Extract stimulated growth of *Lacticaseibacillus casei* and *L. rhamnosus* and enhanced their adhesion to Caco-2 cells.
- **Human clinical efficacy for any indication — NULL.** No controlled human trial of *Woodfordia fruticosa* was retrieved in this run for any indication.
- **Menorrhagia — NULL.** No clinical or preclinical evidence retrieved. Traditional only.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Ayurvedic attribution. The drug is classed among the *kashaya* (astringent) group; the dominant *rasa* is astringent, and the classical actions ascribed to it — *stambhana* (arresting, binding), *grahi* (absorbent, checking of flux) and *vrana-ropana* (wound-healing) — are consistent with that reading and with the tannin chemistry. The specific *guna*, *virya* and *vipaka* values circulated in secondary compilations are **inferred here and not sourced from a primary nighantu in this run — [USER: verify]**.

The Kenetica energetic reading places Dhataki at the *lower* pole of the transformative axis: it does not move, heat or disperse. It closes and it begins. In the fermentation vessel it is the silent condition of change rather than the agent of it, and the tradition's willingness to make so central a pharmaceutical role out of so passive an action is itself the doctrinal teaching here.

### Contraindications

- **High tannin load.** Sustained or high-dose use of tannin-rich astringents can irritate the gastric mucosa and impair absorption of dietary iron and of protein-containing or alkaloidal co-administered drugs. Separate from iron supplementation and from other oral medicines.
- **Constipation and dry conditions.** An astringent, binding drug is directionally wrong in constipation, dryness of the mucosa, and states of deficient secretion.
- **Alcohol content of the finished ferment.** Asava and arishta contain self-generated ethanol, and measured content varies significantly and unpredictably between commercial products (Maithani et al., 2018, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/00952990.2018.1529181)). This makes these preparations inappropriate for: pregnancy and breastfeeding; anyone in recovery from or abstaining from alcohol; children; hepatic impairment or active liver disease; and anyone on metronidazole, disulfiram, or other agents with disulfiram-like reactions. The same paper flags the possibility of misuse of these products as an alcohol source.
- **Liver disease.** Despite the "hepatoprotective" framing in review literature, the evidence is preclinical and the delivery vehicle in traditional practice is alcoholic. Do not use asava/arishta as a liver therapy in established liver disease.
- **Pregnancy.** No safety data retrieved for the flower itself; the ethanol vehicle is independently disqualifying. Avoid.
- **Menorrhagia self-treatment.** Abnormal uterine bleeding requires diagnosis. There is no clinical evidence for Dhataki in this indication and the traditional use must not displace investigation.
- **Product quality.** Commercial asava/arishta GMP compliance was found to be poor in the study cited above. Source matters more than usual with this material.
- Acute rodent toxicity of leaf and bark extracts gave LD50 > 5000 mg/kg (Rahman et al., 2023, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/hsr2.1654)), which indicates low acute toxicity in that model but says nothing about chronic human use or about the flower.
