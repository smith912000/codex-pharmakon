# Opopanax — Sweet Myrrh, Bisabol, Opoponax, Hercules' Woundwort

## *Opopanax hispidus* (Friv.) Griseb. / *Opopanax chironium* (L.) W.D.J.Koch | Family: Apiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, EP, CM
**Energetic Classification:** Hot and dry (3rd degree) — a resolving, warming aromatic that disperses cold obstruction
**Training Phase:** Mobilisation / Cleansing (the dispersing, spasm-releasing phase that opens obstruction and moves stagnation)

---

> **Nomenclatural caution — two different "opopanax" plants.** The name *opopanax* (or *opoponax*) is genuinely shared by two unrelated gum-resins, and honest reading of any old text requires holding both in view:
> 1. **The Apiaceae opopanax** — the gum-resin of *Opopanax chironium* / *O. hispidus*, an umbellifer of the eastern Mediterranean and Balkans. This is the classical *opopanax* of Dioscorides and the Greco-Roman pharmacy, tapped from the incised root/stem.
> 2. **"Bisabol myrrh" / sweet myrrh** — a Burseraceae gum-resin of a *Commiphora* species (perfumery's "opoponax"), a relative of true myrrh and frankincense, prized in incense and fragrance.
> Modern perfumery "opoponax" is usually the *Commiphora* material; the classical *materia medica* "opopanax" is the umbellifer. Where a source does not specify, the identity is genuinely uncertain. This entry treats the **Apiaceae** *Opopanax chironium/hispidus* as the primary subject, and flags where the *Commiphora* sense may be intended.

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian tablets (AT):** Aromatic gum-resins tapped from umbelliferous and resinous plants figure among the fumigants and wound-remedies of the cuneiform medical tradition — burned to purify the sickroom and drive off the spirits blamed for disease, and applied to obstinate swellings. Opopanax belongs to this broad Mesopotamian pharmacy of pungent resins used to "open" and "resolve," though precise species identification behind the Akkadian drug-names is not securely recoverable.

**Dioscorides / De Materia Medica (DMM):** Dioscorides describes *opopanax* (the "all-heal juice," from *pan-akes*, "all-healing") as the concreted juice of the root of a Panakes/Opopanax umbellifer — warming, softening, and dispersing. He assigns it to hardened swellings and indurations, to spasms and "convulsions," to chest and side pains, and as an emmenagogue to bring on suppressed menses. This dispersing-and-emmenagogue profile is the through-line of the plant's Western reputation.

**Ebers Papyrus / Egyptian tradition (EP):** The Egyptian pharmacy leaned heavily on aromatic gum-resins for fumigation, wound-dressing, and gynaecological applications; opopanax-type resins sit within this class of *sonter* (incense/aromatic) materials used both ritually and medicinally. As with the Assyrian record, exact botanical correspondence to a modern binomial is uncertain, but the functional class — a warming aromatic resin — is secure.

**Avicenna / Canon of Medicine (CM):** In the Greco-Arabic synthesis, *jāwshīr* (opopanax) is a hot-and-dry resin of the third degree: a resolvent (*muhallil*) and attenuant that thins thick humours, opens obstruction of the liver and spleen, relieves colic and spasm, and provokes the menses. Its heat and pungency place it squarely among the deobstruent aromatics.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The very name — *pan-akes*, "all-heal" — encodes the doctrine: a pungent, hot resin read as a universal *disperser* of cold, stagnation, and hardened matter. The pharmacology gives this teeth. The Apiaceae opopanax is rich in phthalides of the ligustilide/butylidenephthalide family — the same antispasmodic phthalides that make *dong quai* and celery-seed relatives smooth-muscle relaxants — which underwrites the classical "relieves spasm and convulsion" and emmenagogue claims. The resin's warming, penetrating aroma and its coumarin load align with the "opening/resolving" role every tradition assigned it.

### Active Components

- **Phthalides:** Z-butylidenephthalide, butylphthalide, cnidilide, Z-ligustilide, senkyunolide — antispasmodic, smooth-muscle-relaxant volatiles characteristic of the roots.
- **Furano- and dihydrofuranocoumarins:** imperatorin, heraclenin, and related prenylated coumarins — bioactive (pro-apoptotic in vitro) and presumed responsible for photosensitising/irritant potential.
- **Essential oil:** sesquiterpene-rich (e.g. γ-elemene and other sesquiterpenes reported for the genus), plus fatty-acid esters.
- **Gum-resin matrix:** the tapped exudate combining volatile oil, resin, and gum.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Opopanax is filed as a **Mobilisation-phase disperser**: a warming, spasm-releasing aromatic for states of cold obstruction and stagnation — cramping, "stuck" gynaecological patterns (used traditionally as an emmenagogue), and as a fumigant/topical for hardened swellings. In the Kenetica frame it is a *mover*, not a builder: reached for when something is congealed, cramped, or blocked and needs opening, then discontinued. Because of its coumarin/furanocoumarin content it is treated as a potent, short-course agent rather than a daily tonic.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, chemical work on the Apiaceae *Opopanax chironium* supports the traditional "resolving/antispasmodic" profile, while clinical evidence is essentially absent:

- Gijbels *et al.* (1983, *Planta Med*) identified five phthalides in the roots — Z-butylidenephthalide, butylphthalide, cnidilide, Z-ligustilide, and senkyunolide — the antispasmodic phthalide class that provides a direct chemical rationale for the classical anti-spasm and emmenagogue uses [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-2007-969937).
- Appendino *et al.* (2004, *J Nat Prod*) showed the plant is a rich source of furano- and dihydrofuranocoumarins, described two distinct chemotypes, and found the prenylated furanocoumarins heraclenin and imperatorin induced apoptosis in Jurkat leukaemia cells — while noting that coumarin accumulation, especially in the roots, is presumably responsible for the species' *poisonous* properties [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/np0340652).
- Babacan *et al.* (2023, *Molecules*) characterised the essential-oil composition of the *Opopanax* genus in Turkey by GC-MS, reporting sesquiterpene-dominated oils (e.g. γ-elemene) — establishing the volatile chemistry behind the aromatic, fumigant tradition [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules28073055).

**Evidence grade: Traditional / data-sparse.** Phytochemistry is documented, but there are essentially no controlled clinical trials of opopanax for any indication, and one key paper explicitly flags the resin's toxic potential. Therapeutic claims rest on historical use plus plausible mechanism, not on human efficacy data.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Greco-Arabic / Unani (Avicennan frame):** Hot and dry, third degree. Resolvent (*muhallil*), attenuant, deobstruent, emmenagogue; opens liver/spleen obstruction and relieves colic.
- **Greco-Roman (Dioscoridean):** Warming, softening, dispersing; for indurations, spasm, chest/side pain, suppressed menses.
- **Mesopotamian / Egyptian:** Purifying aromatic fumigant and wound-resin (functional class; humoral grade not formalised in the surviving texts).
- *(No formal TCM or Ayurvedic rasa/guṇa classification is attested for this Mediterranean umbellifer; the Commiphora "sweet myrrh" sense would instead map to myrrh's warm, bitter, blood-moving profile.)*

### Contraindications

**Pregnancy — contraindicated.** Traditional emmenagogue use implies uterine/menstrual stimulation; do not use in pregnancy or when attempting to conceive. **Furanocoumarin content** (imperatorin, heraclenin) confers photosensitising and irritant/toxic potential — a cited study explicitly links the plant's coumarins, especially in the root, to poisonous properties — so avoid photosensitising exposure and treat internal use as high-risk. Furanocoumarins are also classic inhibitors of drug-metabolising enzymes, so assume potential interaction with medications metabolised by CYP enzymes. Avoid in children and lactation. Distinguish clearly from *Commiphora* "sweet myrrh" before sourcing, as the two carry different safety profiles. [USER: verify] any internal use with a qualified practitioner.
