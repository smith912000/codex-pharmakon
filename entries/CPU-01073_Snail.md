# Snail — Kochlias (as printed in Dioscorides 2-11) / hbu.skyog (snail shell) / na.bun.bu.mo (Clark: "Slug"; Men-Tsee-Khang: "Snail")
## *Land, sea and river snails: Helix aspersa, Helix hortensis (garden snails) and Helix pomatia (edible snail), the translator's suggestions for Dioscorides 2-11; Cipangopaludina chinensis Gray, Clark's identification of the Tibetan snail shell* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM, FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-11, *Kochlias* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 4956-4963; the names index row is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 201). The translator suggests *Helix aspersa* and *Helix hortensis* (garden snails) and *Helix pomatia* (edible snail). Dioscorides separates snails by where they live and gives each a different standing. "The earth snail is good for the stomach and spoiled with difficulty. The best are in Sardinia, Africa, Astypalaea, Sicily and Chios, as well as those in the Alps near Liguria, surnamed pomatias (because of their covering)." "The sea snail is also good for the stomach and is easily transient, but the river snail is poisonous." "The field snail (called sesilon or seselita) that hangs on bushes and shrubs troubles or disturbs the intestines and stomach causing vomiting." The chapter then gives uses of the shell and the flesh. The burnt shells of all of them "are able to heat and burn, and clean leprosy, vitiligines [form of leprosy] and the teeth." Burnt whole with the flesh, pounded and rubbed on with honey, they "take away scars in the eyes, white spots on the cornea, sunspots, and moisture of the sight." Applied raw with their shells they "dry up dropsy tumours", soothe gouty inflammation and draw out thorns; pounded and applied they "expel the menstrual flow"; the flesh pounded and applied with myrrh and frankincense "heals wounds, especially those around the tendons"; pounded with vinegar it stops nosebleeds. "The live flesh (especially of the African snail), eaten, pacifies pains of the stomach." Taken whole with the shell, in wine and myrrh, it "cures those troubled with colic and pains of the bladder." Last: "The earth-snail heals falling hair if one thrusts a needle through the flesh of the snail and touches the hair with the slimy matter that comes from there." Snail flesh also appears as a vehicle in another chapter (line 4140, "Rubbed on with the flesh of a snail it cures broken ears and exposed bones"); the chapter number was not confirmed, so that line is noted and not relied on. **Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The English texts on disk are Clark 1995, *The Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`) and the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`). The spine's list rows are `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` lines 342 ("Snail shell") and 382 ("Snails"), both citing that chapter. Clark's section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147) opens: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body." Two different Tibetan materials are called snail in the two translations, and they are kept apart here. ### Varieties

**Snail shell** (FMT; Clark line 1160; `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 342). The shell of the Tibetan snail *hbu.skyog*, which Clark identifies as *Cipangopaludina chinensis* Gray and lists in the "Bone" group of animal medicines. The text says it has ten names, and that the shell "is anthelmintic and purges oedema fluid." It is also in the anthelmintic class at line 1221. It is a part of the animal listed under its own heading, not a separate animal.

*Synthesis of the varieties.* Only one variety is folded here, the shell, and it is a part, not a kind. The two texts divide the material differently. Dioscorides separates snails by habitat (earth, sea, river, field) and, within those, by place of origin of the best earth snails; he treats shell and flesh together in one chapter. The Tibetan text splits the animal by body part under its thirteen headings, so that the shell sits under "Bone" and the whole animal (or the slug) sits under the small-animal group. Dioscorides ranks the earth snails by locality (Sardinia, Africa, Astypalaea, Sicily, Chios, the Alps near Liguria) and calls the river snail poisonous; the Tibetan text gives no ranking among snails. Both texts use the shell; Dioscorides burns it, and the Tibetan text states no preparation for it.

### Active Components

A snail is a soft-bodied mollusc in a shell. The flesh is mostly water and protein; the mucus is a hydrated mixture of glycoproteins; the shell is largely calcium carbonate (burning a shell drives off carbon dioxide and leaves calcium oxide, which is caustic, a general chemical fact and not a statement of the sources). Land snails eat plants and can carry what they eat. The sources name no active component and give no quantities.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The texts record snails as stomach food, as burnt shell for the skin and teeth, as a poultice, and as a shell medicine against worms and oedema. The Codex has no protocol for any of this and offers none. The reasons are plain. Dioscorides himself warns that the river snail is poisonous and the field snail causes vomiting. Wild snails carry parasites (see Contraindications). No dose is given in either tradition. And the animal is killed for the use.

### Preparation & Dosing

Greek (2-11): the shells burnt, alone or with the flesh, the flesh pounded into small pieces and rubbed on with honey for the eyes; raw snails pounded and applied with their shells for tumours, gout, thorns and menstrual flow; flesh pounded with myrrh and frankincense on wounds, or with vinegar for nosebleed; live flesh eaten for stomach pain; the whole snail with its shell in wine and myrrh as a drink for colic and bladder pain. Tibetan: the shell "anthelmintic and purges oedema fluid" (Clark line 1160), with no preparation given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Edible snails are a food in several cuisines. Raw or undercooked land and freshwater snails can carry the rat lungworm (*Angiostrongylus cantonensis*), which causes eosinophilic meningitis, and other parasites; thorough cooking is the general safeguard. Wet snail mucus is used in some cosmetic products, but no effect of snail flesh, mucus or ash on tumours, gout, menstrual flow or eye scars is established by anything read here. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides prints one quality for snails: "The burnt shells of all of them are able to heat and burn" (2-11). He also grades the stomach effect by kind: earth and sea snails "good for the stomach", river snail "poisonous", field snail "troubles or disturbs the intestines and stomach". These are the chapter's words; no humoral scheme is stated.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The passages read give uses only ("anthelmintic and purges oedema fluid"; "stops discharge of sinus fluid"). No taste, potency or post-digestive taste is printed for the snail in the passages read. The introduction to the Clark translation (line 57) says that such taste and potency data are recorded in later materia medica such as the *Crystal Orb*; that text is not on disk. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: provenance (the best earth snails from Sardinia, Africa, Astypalaea, Sicily, Chios and the Alps near Liguria), kind (earth, sea, river, field), and a warning that the river snail is poisonous and the field snail upsets the stomach. The chapter is therapeutic throughout, and assigns the snail no cultivation status.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 passages read are therapeutic: the shell as anthelmintic and for oedema, the slug or snail for sinus or cerebrospinal discharge. Medicine in this tradition is the third of four treatment branches and supports practice; it does not reach the root. No *bcud len* use of the snail was found, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, so none is asserted. Clark notes that animal products are little used today.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do. The snail is in two traditions for a few plain reasons: a food and poultice in Greece, a shell for worms and oedema in Tibet. Neither text assigns it a cultivation role. A cultivator can take from the Greek chapter the habit of asking where a food comes from and which kind it is, and from the Tibetan text the reminder that its own translators disagree on whether its "snail" is a snail or a slug.

### Contraindications

- **Parasites and poisoning.** Raw or undercooked snails can carry parasites such as the rat lungworm; Dioscorides warns that the river snail is "poisonous" and the field snail causes vomiting. Do not eat wild snails raw.
- **Burnt shell.** Dioscorides says burnt shells "heat and burn"; burnt shell (calcium oxide) is caustic and must not be put in the eyes or on broken skin.
- **Pregnancy.** The chapter says snails applied pounded "expel the menstrual flow"; pregnancy is a reason for caution. [USER: verify]
- **Shellfish and mollusc allergy.** Snails can provoke allergic reactions in people allergic to molluscs or dust mites.
- **Dose unknown.** No dose is given for any use in the sources read.
- **Ethics and law.** Using the animal means killing it; some snail species are protected or restricted for export or collection in various places.
