# I-404 | Yarrow
**Achillea millefolium** L. | Family: Asteraceae (Compositae)

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted binomial:** *Achillea millefolium* L. (1753)
**Authority:** Linnaeus, *Species Plantarum*
**Family:** Asteraceae (Compositae) — Daisy family
**Tribe:** Anthemideae

**FAMILY TRANSITION NOTE:**
This entry marks the transition from Plantaginaceae (I-403, Plantain) to **Asteraceae** — one of the largest plant families (~24,000 species). Asteraceae is distinguished pharmacologically by sesquiterpene lactones, flavonoids, and polyacetylene compounds. Yarrow begins an extensive Asteraceae section that will include chamomile, calendula, echinacea, milk thistle, dandelion, and many others. The shift from Lamiaceae/Plantaginaceae monoterpene/iridoid chemistry to Asteraceae sesquiterpene lactone chemistry represents a significant phytochemical transition.

**Common names:**
- English: yarrow, common yarrow, milfoil, thousand-leaf, soldier's wound-wort, knight's milfoil, sanguinary, nosebleed plant, old man's pepper, devil's nettle, woundwort
- German: Schafgarbe ("sheep garbe/bundle"), gemeine Schafgarbe
- French: achillée millefeuille, mille-feuille, herbe à la coupure
- Italian: millefoglio, achillea
- Spanish: milenrama, aquilea
- Arabic: حشيشة نفس الكلب (*ḥashīshat nafas al-kalb*), أوقيليا (*awqīliyā*)
- Persian: بومادران (*bōmādaran*)
- Greek: ἀχίλλειον (*achílleion*), μύριοφυλλον (*myrióphyllon*) — "ten-thousand-leaved"
- Chinese: 蓍草 (*shī cǎo*) — "milfoil"; also 一枝黄花 (sometimes applied to related species)
- Sanskrit: (translated): *gandhanākuli*
- Anglo-Saxon: garwe, gearwe

**Etymology — mythological and pharmacological:**
*Achillea* from Achilles — the Greek mythological hero who allegedly used yarrow to treat the wounds of his soldiers at Troy. *Millefolium* = "thousand leaves" (Latin *mille* + *folium*) — describing the highly divided feathery leaves. The multiple wound-related common names (soldier's woundwort, sanguinary, knight's milfoil) encode the herb's primary traditional pharmacological identity.

**The Achilles legend — encoded pharmacology:**
The myth that Achilles discovered yarrow's wound-healing properties and used it on his Trojan War soldiers is the most pharmacologically embedded military medical legend in Western classical tradition. Whether Achilles used yarrow or not is mythologically unresolvable; what the legend preserves is: (1) the plant's wound-healing reputation was old enough by Homeric times (c. 8th century BCE) to require a hero's origin story; (2) the military medical application was considered primary; (3) the hemostatic and anti-inflammatory properties were empirically established centuries before Dioscorides systematized them.

**Botanical description:**
Perennial herb, 20–80 cm. Stems erect, furrowed, hairy, arising from rhizome. Leaves alternate, 2–3 times pinnately divided (feathery, fern-like), 5–20 cm; leaf texture soft, glandular-hairy. Flower heads small, numerous, in dense flat-topped compound corymbs; ray flowers 5, white or pink; disk flowers cream-yellow. Receptacle with chaffy bracts. Achenes (fruits) compressed, oblong, no pappus. Strong distinctive aromatic smell (azulene precursors).

**Intraspecific variation:**
*A. millefolium* sensu lato is a complex aggregate of polyploid populations. Essential oil composition varies dramatically by geographic population:
- European populations: high azulene precursors (pro-chamazulene); anti-inflammatory emphasis
- North American populations: higher camphor content in some
- Cultivated varieties: white, pink, red, golden flower heads; ornamental
For pharmacological use, chemotype specification (similar to oregano, I-389) is important.

**I Ching connection (Chinese tradition):**
The primary Chinese identity of *shī cǎo* is as the divination stalks used in I Ching (*Yijing*, 易經) — the 49 yarrow stalks used to generate hexagrams. This is one of the few plants in this Codex whose primary traditional use is not pharmacological but philosophical/divinatory. The medicinal use of *shī cǎo* is secondary in the Chinese tradition; the divination use is primary. This dual identity (medicine + oracle) represents a unique pharmacological-philosophical integration.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Universal wound herb:**
Yarrow's wound-healing pharmacological signal is strong enough to have been independently identified across classical Mediterranean, Chinese, Middle Eastern, and Native American traditions. The hemostatic (tannin), anti-inflammatory (chamazulene/azulene, sesquiterpene lactones), and antimicrobial (camphor, cineole, achillicin) combination creates an observable wound-healing effect that generates cross-cultural discovery.

**The achillicin mechanism connects myth to pharmacology:**
Yarrow contains achillicin (and related guaianolide sesquiterpene lactones) — anti-inflammatory compounds that were only identified in the 20th century but whose effects were observable empirically. The Achilles myth preserves empirical observation in mythological form: the "divinely discovered" wound remedy is pharmacologically real.

**Fever tradition:**
Secondary convergence on fever management (antipyretic) across European, Chinese, and Native American traditions reflects azulene and sesquiterpene lactone anti-inflammatory activity reducing fever through prostaglandin pathway inhibition — same mechanism as aspirin, but discovered millennia before salicylate chemistry.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**FAMILY-DEFINING COMPOUND CLASS — Sesquiterpene lactones:**
- **Achillicin** (guaianolide): primary sesquiterpene lactone; anti-inflammatory; anti-tumor; cytotoxic at high concentrations; bitter principle
- **Achillin**, **achillifolide**: related guaianolide sesquiterpene lactones; similar activity
- **Desacetylmatricarine**: anti-inflammatory sesquiterpene lactone
- **α-Peroxachifolide**: anti-tumor; anti-inflammatory

**Essential oil (0.2–1.5%):**
- **Azulene precursors → chamazulene:** *A. millefolium* contains matricine (a non-blue precursor); upon steam distillation with heat, matricine converts to chamazulene (giving the oil characteristic deep blue color). Chamazulene is anti-inflammatory (5-LOX inhibition), anti-allergic, and antibacterial. The blue color itself indicates chamazulene content; blue = anti-inflammatory in Asteraceae essential oils.
- **β-Pinene** (5–20%): monoterpene; antimicrobial
- **Sabinene** (3–15%): monoterpene; antimicrobial; anti-inflammatory
- **Camphor** (0–50%): varies by chemotype; monoterpene ketone; some antiseptic value but potentially toxic at high concentrations
- **Borneol** (1–10%): monoterpene alcohol; antimicrobial; analgesic
- **1,8-Cineole** (0–15%): varies by chemotype; bronchodilatory; expectorant
- **β-Caryophyllene** (3–10%): sesquiterpene; CB2 agonist; anti-inflammatory
- **Thujone** (trace–significant): variable by chemotype; GABA-A antagonist; caution

**Flavonoids:**
- **Apigenin** (and 7-glucoside): GABA-A modulation; anti-inflammatory; antispasmodic
- **Luteolin** (and glucosides): COX-2 inhibitor; anti-inflammatory
- **Rutin** (quercetin-3-rutinoside): capillary protective; anti-platelet
- **Quercetin**, **kaempferol**: antioxidant flavonols
- **Artemetin**: anti-inflammatory; antiviral

**Alkaloids (unique to Achillea):**
- **Achilleine** (betaine glycoside): hemostatic; reduces clotting time significantly; primary compound responsible for rapid hemostatic effect; specific to *Achillea*
- **Stachydrine**: shared with motherwort/betony; cardiac/vasodilatory

**Tannins:**
- Condensed proanthocyanidins: hemostatic; antimicrobial; astringent

**Phenolics:**
- **Chlorogenic acid**, **caffeic acid**: antioxidants
- **Salicylic acid** (trace): aspirin-related; anti-pyretic; explains fever-reducing use

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Hemostatic mechanisms:**
1. **Achilleine:** The alkaloid achilleine (betaine glycoside specific to *Achillea*) reduces blood clotting time by activating platelet aggregation and enhancing thrombin activity. Shown to reduce bleeding time in animal models (Belache et al. 1979). This compound provides rapid hemostasis — the primary pharmacological basis for millennia of wound application.
2. **Tannin vasoconstriction:** Condensed tannins constrict capillaries and precipitate proteins on wound surface; complementary hemostatic mechanism
3. **Rutin capillary support:** Reduces capillary permeability; reduces bleeding tendency

**Anti-inflammatory mechanisms (Asteraceae-specific):**
1. **Chamazulene 5-LOX inhibition:** Chamazulene (generated from matricine during distillation) inhibits 5-lipoxygenase — reduces LTB4 and LTC4 (proinflammatory leukotrienes); mechanism shared with chamomile
2. **Sesquiterpene lactone NF-κB inhibition:** Achillicin and related guaianolides inhibit NF-κB through Michael addition to IκB kinase cysteine residues — the same mechanism as parthenolide (feverfew, I-XXX) and artemisinins; characteristic Asteraceae sesquiterpene lactone anti-inflammatory mechanism
3. **Apigenin COX-2 inhibition:** Flavone-mediated COX-2 selective inhibition
4. **β-Caryophyllene CB2 agonism:** Endocannabinoid anti-inflammatory pathway

**Antimicrobial:**
1. **Camphor + borneol:** Monoterpene antimicrobials; broad-spectrum bacterial membrane disruption
2. **Chamazulene:** Anti-fungal (*Candida*); antibacterial (Gram-positive)
3. **Achillicin:** Antibacterial at MIC concentrations; anti-tumor secondary

**Antispasmodic (GI and uterine):**
1. **Apigenin:** Smooth muscle relaxant; calcium channel antagonism; explains traditional use for menstrual cramps and GI spasm
2. **Flavonoid combination:** Luteolin and apigenin synergistic on smooth muscle relaxation

**Antipyretic:**
1. **Salicylic acid traces + chamazulene COX inhibition:** Mechanisms converge on prostaglandin reduction → fever reduction; explains traditional "sweating herb" (induces perspiration) use for fever management

**Uterine modulation (complex):**
- At low doses: antispasmodic (apigenin); reduces cramping
- At high doses: uterotonic stimulation reported in traditional sources
- This dual dose-dependent effect explains both the emmenagogue and anti-cramping traditional uses — different ends of a dose-response curve

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Level A — No standalone yarrow RCTs published for primary indications.**

**Level B — Pilot/observational:**
- **Wound healing:** Numerous in vitro and animal studies confirming achilleine hemostatic activity; wound healing acceleration by chamazulene anti-inflammatory; no controlled human wound trials
- **Menstrual pain:** Small trials (n=20–40) showing yarrow tea (apigenin content) reduces dysmenorrhea; mechanism smooth muscle relaxant; Level B design
- **Liver function:** Iranian research; yarrow extract reduced liver enzymes in NAFLD pilot study (n=60); chamazulene and flavonoid hepatoprotective mechanism
- **Irritable bowel:** German hospital data; yarrow-containing compound formulas reduced abdominal pain scores; flavonoid + chamazulene combination

**Traditional medicine regulatory recognition:**
- **Commission E (Germany):** *Millefolii herba* (yarrow herb) — positive monograph for "loss of appetite and dyspeptic complaints such as mild spastic discomforts of the gastrointestinal tract"
- **HMPC (EMA):** Traditional use monograph — "temporary relief of minor spasmodic pain of the gastrointestinal tract" and "temporary relief of minor inflammatory conditions of the oral cavity and throat"
- **European Pharmacopoeia:** *Millefolii herba* — standardized to total flavonoids expressed as apigenin ≥0.02%

**Level C — Traditional evidence (exceptional depth, 3,000+ years):**
- Wound healing, hemostasis: universal cross-cultural use; most consistent single traditional application
- Fever reduction: consistent European folk use; salicylate + chamazulene mechanism
- Menstrual regulation (both anti-spasmodic for cramping and emmenagogue for delayed cycles): consistent traditional use; dual mechanism at different doses
- Digestive: bitter sesquiterpene lactones stimulate digestive secretion; Commission E approved for dyspepsia

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Achilles and Troy (c. 1200 BCE mythological, recorded c. 800 BCE Homeric):**
The naming of the genus *Achillea* after the Greek hero Achilles encodes the oldest pharmacological attribution in European botanical nomenclature. Whether mythologically or historically based, the story preserves: yarrow's use on military wounds, its efficacy for staunching blood, and its reputation strong enough to require heroic divine origin. The naming also establishes yarrow as the pharmacological representative of a broader tradition of "heroes' wound herbs" — herbs carried by soldiers and associated with martial healing.

Pliny (*Naturalis Historia*): "It is called Achillea, after Achilles, who first made use of it, being taught by Chiron [the centaur who educated Achilles in medicine]." The Chiron attribution connects yarrow to the broader Chiron pharmacological tradition — Chiron being the mythological centaur responsible for teaching medicine to Achilles, Jason, and Asclepius.

**I Ching divination stalks — Chinese philosophical pharmacology:**
The use of *shī cǎo* (yarrow) stalks in I Ching divination (attested from at least the Zhou Dynasty, c. 1046–256 BCE) represents a unique case where a plant's pharmacological identity (medicinal herb) was completely secondary to its philosophical-divination identity. The 49 yarrow stalks used in the classic I Ching hexagram generation method appear in the *Yijing* text itself. The selection of yarrow for this purpose may have reflected its visual properties (uniform, long, straight stalks easily counted and separated) rather than pharmacological ones, but it created a cultural connection between yarrow and cosmic consultation that has no pharmacological parallel in any other tradition.

**Medieval "Achillea" in European military medicine:**
Crusader-era medical texts (c. 12th century) continued the Achilles wound-herb tradition. Yarrow (variously named herba Achilleae, milfoil, yarrow) was carried in soldier's medical kits alongside plantain and shepherd's purse as the primary hemostatic herbs. The continuity from Troy to the Crusades — whether direct transmission or parallel rediscovery — maintained yarrow's military medical identity for 2,000 years.

**Native American I Ching parallel:**
Multiple Native American nations independently discovered *Achillea* species' wound-healing properties. Navajo, Cherokee, Blackfoot, and many other nations used yarrow for cuts, wounds, and fever — the same three primary applications that appear in European tradition, independently arrived at from different cultural starting points, driven by the same pharmacological properties (achilleine hemostasis, chamazulene anti-inflammatory, salicylate antipyretic).

**John Gerard's comprehensive account (1597):**
*The Herball*: "Yarrow stancheth blood... The leaves of Yarrow doe close up wounds, and keep them from inflammation or fiery swelling; it stancheth blood in any part of the body... The decoction drunk easeth the toothache and taketh away the inflammation of the gums... A decoction of the leaves drunk healeth gripes and torments in the guts." Gerard's account covers wound healing, hemostasis, anti-inflammatory, anti-toothache, GI applications — all mechanistically consistent with the known phytochemistry.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Therapeutic applications:**

*Wound care and hemostasis (Level C, strong traditional + mechanistic basis):*
- **Fresh leaf poultice:** Crush fresh yarrow leaves, apply directly to wound, cut, or abrasion; most effective hemostatic; achilleine is most concentrated in fresh herb; replace every 2–4 hours
- **Dried leaf powder:** Applied directly to wound for hemostasis; traditional styptic
- **Infusion compress:** 3–5 g dried herb per 200 mL; cool; apply to wounds, bruises, sprains

*Menstrual support — antispasmodic (Level C):*
- **Infusion:** 3–5 g dried herb per cup, 2–3 cups daily during menstrual period for dysmenorrhea; apigenin smooth muscle relaxation
- **Tincture (1:5, 25–40% ethanol):** 2–4 mL three times daily; better sesquiterpene lactone extraction
- Use for menstrual cramps and irregular periods; antispasmodic for cramping + emmenagogue for delayed cycles

*Fever and sweating (Level C):*
- Hot yarrow infusion (3 g per cup) drunk hot; promotes sweating (diaphoretic); traditional fever management
- Mechanism: salicylate traces + chamazulene prostaglandin inhibition + peripheral vasodilation (apigenin)
- Combine with elderflower and peppermint (traditional British cold and flu tea blend): complementary mechanisms

*Digestive dyspepsia (Commission E approved, Level C-B):*
- Infusion or tincture for loss of appetite, mild GI spasm, dyspepsia; sesquiterpene lactone bitter tonic stimulates gastric secretion; apigenin antispasmodic

*External anti-inflammatory (Level C):*
- Yarrow tea as bath additive for skin inflammation, eczema, rashes; chamazulene anti-inflammatory + antimicrobial; 50–100 g dried herb in bath water (in muslin bag)
- Wound care — same mechanism as topical

**Culinary integration:**
- Young leaves and flowers edible; mildly bitter; traditional salad herb in European folk cuisine
- Young yarrow shoots in spring as potherb (cooked); flavor similar to tansy/tarragon (mildly anise-like)
- Traditional ingredient in Scandinavian *aquavit* and some German digestive bitters
- Yarrow-infused ales: historical European ale herb before hops predominated (14th century); bitter sesquiterpene lactones provided both flavor and preservative function (antimicrobial)

**Safety:**

*Allergic contact dermatitis (CRITICAL):*
- Yarrow is a member of Asteraceae; sesquiterpene lactones (especially achillicin) cause **contact dermatitis** in Asteraceae-sensitive individuals — the same mechanism that causes ragweed and chrysanthemum sensitivity
- **Screen for Asteraceae/Compositae allergy before prescribing**; particularly relevant for topical wound use; people with known ragweed, chamomile, chrysanthemum, or daisy allergies should use with caution
- Patch test before extended topical use

*Pregnancy:*
- **AVOID THERAPEUTIC DOSES IN PREGNANCY:** Uterotonic effects at higher doses (emmenagogue); traditionally used to stimulate delayed periods — same use is an absolute contraindication in confirmed pregnancy
- Culinary amounts (occasional cooking use) generally considered safe

*Photosensitivity:*
- Furanocoumarins present in small amounts; possible photosensitization with extensive topical use; minimize sun exposure after large topical applications of fresh herb

*Thujone (chemotype-dependent):*
- High-thujone chemotypes of *A. millefolium* have proconvulsant potential (similar to hyssop I-396); chemotype specification advisable for therapeutic preparations; most commercial preparations are low-thujone

**Drug interactions:**
- **Anticoagulants:** Rutin + tannin anti-platelet activity; achilleine also affects coagulation parameters; MONITOR with warfarin; additive anti-coagulant effect — may reduce INR therapeutic dose needed; risk of over-anticoagulation
- **Antihypertensives:** Apigenin vasodilation; mild additive effect; not usually clinically significant
- **Sedatives:** Apigenin GABA-A modulation; mild additive sedation

**Dose references:**
- Dried herb (infusion): 3–5 g per cup, 2–3 cups daily (Commission E: 3 g per cup, 3 times daily)
- Tincture (1:5, 25–40% ethanol): 2–4 mL three times daily
- Fresh herb poultice: as needed for wound care
- Bath additive: 50–100 g per bath
- Commission E approved dose: 3 g per cup as infusion for dyspepsia
