# Gnaphalium arenarium — Elichruson (Dioscorides 4-57; also chrysanthemon, amarantum)

## *Gnaphalium arenarium [Linnaeus] (one of several names on the translators' SUGGESTED line; the same line gives "Helichrysum arenarium [in Sprague]" and the English names "Helichrysum, Cudweed, Eternal Flower, Golden Sunflower")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk names this plant. The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line is long: *Amarantus luteus*, *Stichas citrina*, *Helichryson* [Fuchs], *Gnaphalium arenarium* [Linnaeus], *Helichrysum arenarium* [in Sprague], *Helichrysum chinophylum*, then *Helichrysum arenarium* again with the English names. The entry takes the Linnaean form as its title because that is the form the spine row carries. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-57. ELICHRUSON* at line 8495, SUGGESTED lines at 8496-8497, text at line 8498. The chapter stands in Book Four, "Other Herbs & Roots", after 4-56 *CHRUSOGONON* (read in I_1028) and before 4-58 *CHRUSANTHEMON*. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 637. Page numbers and figure captions (596 to 598) follow the text and are not part of it.

*Description.* "Helichrysum (with which they crown their statues) has a little stem — white, green, straight and strong — and narrow leaves (similar to those of abrotanum) set apart at distances, the filaments circular, shining like gold; a round tuft, (as it were) dry bunches of berries, and a thin root. It grows in rough places near running water."

*Uses.* "A decoction of the filaments (taken as a drink with wine) helps painful urination, the bites of snakes, sciatica, and hernia. A decoction (taken as a drink with must [pulp from grapes]) induces the menstrual flow, and dissolves clots of blood in the bladder or bowels. Thirty grains in a dilution of white wine (given to one fasting) stops dripping fluids. It is stored together with clothes, protecting them from moths."

*Names.* "It is also called chrysanthemon, while some call it amarantum."

*Overlaps and neighbours.* The name *chrysanthemon* also heads the next chapter, 4-58, and the Codex's existing entries I_1049 (4-55 *Chrusokome*) and I_1088 (3-132 *Gnaphalion*, *Filago germanica*) mention this chapter only in cross-reference. Neither entry is on this chapter, and nothing from them is read in. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records drinks in wine or must for urinary complaints, bites, sciatica, hernia, the menstrual flow and clots, a small dose against "dripping fluids", and a use as a clothes-store against moths. It also records that the plant was used to crown statues. The Codex gives no protocol for any of these, and the identity is a translators' suggestion among several.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations: a decoction of the filaments drunk with wine; a decoction drunk with must; "thirty grains in a dilution of white wine (given to one fasting)"; the plant stored among clothes. Only the third carries a quantity ("thirty grains", the translators' wording; the unit is not defined in the text read). No other dose, duration or schedule is printed. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 4-57 prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality and no taste. The qualities it prints describe the plant's appearance (white-green stem, filaments "shining like gold").

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a description for recognition (a stem white and green, narrow leaves set apart like those of abrotanum, shining golden filaments, a round tuft), a habitat (rough places near running water) and a storage practice (kept among clothes against moths). It also records that "they crown their statues" with the plant. That is a recorded custom, and it is not a cultivation status: the text does not say what the crowning is for. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable points are the habit of identifying a plant by form and habitat and the household use of keeping a dry herb among stored cloth. The text offers nothing more, and the abstentive answer is the right one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 4-57 prints no warning. The identification is only the translators' suggestion among several, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. A drink said to "induce the menstrual flow" must not be taken in pregnancy. Snake bite, sciatica and hernia call for medical care, not a herbal decoction. [USER: verify]
