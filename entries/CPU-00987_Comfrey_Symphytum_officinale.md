# Comfrey — Knitbone / Boneset / Bruisewort / Blackwort / *Symphyton* (Gk. *symphyō*, "to make grow together")
## *Symphytum officinale* L. | Family: Boraginaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM, EP
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry with a moistening, gluey (mucilaginous) surface quality (Galenic); astringent, agglutinant, consolidating
**Training Phase:** Phase I — Structural Repair (topical only)

---

### Origin & Tradition

Comfrey is the Codex's perfect triangle: a doctrine encoded in the plant's own name, a chemistry that *actually vindicates* that doctrine, and a poison sitting inside the same root. Very few substances let all three vertices be drawn this cleanly. Read carefully — the tradition here was right in one direction and lethally wrong in the other, and the difference between those two directions is the difference between a poultice and a cup of tea.

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The Ebers papyrus preserves a large formulary of wound and inflammation dressings — poultices of ground herb, honey, oil and fat laid on swellings, wounds and "hot" injuries — and comfrey has long been placed in this class by compilers. The Codex flags this as functionally plausible and philologically insecure: the Egyptian plant names in the wound-dressing recipes are not all resolvable to Linnaean species. **[USER: verify]** any specific Ebers prescription number before citing one. What is defensible is that the *technique* — the mucilaginous plant poultice on a wound — is Egyptian in its documented antiquity.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (AT).** Likewise: the Assyrian medical tablets record poultices of pounded root laid on swellings and broken limbs. No secure Akkadian binomial for *Symphytum officinale* is established, and this entry does not invent one. **[USER: verify].**

**Dioscorides / Greco-Roman (DMM).** Here the attestation is exact, and it is the hinge of the entry. Dioscorides names the plant *sýmphyton* — from **συμφύω**, *symphyō*, "to grow together", "to unite" — and the name **is** the indication. He describes the root as glutinous and astringent, and reports that it is applied to fresh wounds to close them, to inflammations, and for ruptures and broken bones. Pliny records the same doctrine, and adds the famous demonstration-claim that pieces of meat boiled with comfrey root will knit back together. The Latin and vernacular names carry the doctrine intact for the next eighteen centuries: *consolida*, *knitbone*, *boneset*, *bruisewort*.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM).** The Arabic-Persian reception classes comfrey among the *mulaḥḥim* / consolidating agents — the "flesh-growing" simples — and among the astringents (*qābiḍ*). The Unani theory is that of a drug which *builds and binds tissue*: applied to wounds it agglutinates the lips of the wound and grows flesh; applied to fractures it consolidates. It is also, and this is the tragedy of the tradition, given internally for ulcer, cough, haemoptysis and dysentery — the same "consolidating" logic pointed inward at bleeding surfaces.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** No substance in the Codex has a tighter name-to-mechanism seal. *Symphyō* is not a poetic flourish; it is a therapeutic hypothesis stated as a verb: **this drug makes separated tissue grow together again.** Every layer of the tradition restates it — *consolida*, *knitbone*, the meat-knitting anecdote. And the astonishing thing is that the hypothesis is, at the level of cell biology, *correct*.

Map it:

- **"Makes flesh grow together" (*symphyō*, *mulaḥḥim*, "knitbone")** → **ALLANTOIN**. Comfrey root is unusually rich in allantoin, a purine-derived small molecule that is a recognised **keratolytic and cell-proliferant/epithelialising agent** — used for that property in modern dermatological and cosmetic preparations. The tradition claimed the plant *causes tissue to proliferate and close*. The plant contains a compound whose accepted pharmacological description is that it promotes epithelial proliferation. That is not coincidence and it is not a stretch. It is the doctrine, in a molecule.
- **"Astringent, agglutinant, binds the wound" (*qābiḍ*)** → **tannins and mucilage**. Tannins precipitate surface protein and tighten; the root's abundant mucilaginous polysaccharide forms a protective, hydrating film that keeps a healing surface moist — which is, in modern wound care, exactly the condition that accelerates re-epithelialisation. Traditional occlusive "gluey" poulticing is moist wound healing, discovered by hand.
- **"Cools the hot swelling, takes down the bruise" (*bruisewort*)** → **rosmarinic acid** and related caffeic-acid derivatives, which are genuinely anti-inflammatory, plus glycopeptides and mucopolysaccharides. This is the vertex where the modern *clinical* evidence is strongest, and it is strong (below).
- **The inward turn** — "if it consolidates a wound outside, it will consolidate an ulcer inside" — is the **symbolic overreach**, and it is where the tradition kills people. The doctrine of consolidation is a *surface* doctrine. Taken through the gut and delivered to the liver, comfrey's chemistry does not consolidate; it **occludes**, and the thing it occludes is the hepatic venules. The symbolic logic that made comfrey a wound herb is the same symbolic logic that made it a stomach tea, and only one half of it survives contact with pharmacology. This is the single most important lesson in the entry: **a doctrine's validity is route-dependent, and traditions do not know about routes.**

### Active Components

**The healing fraction:**
- **Allantoin** — the signature constituent; cell-proliferant, keratolytic, epithelialising. Highest in the root.
- **Rosmarinic acid** and other caffeic-acid derivatives (chlorogenic, α-hydroxycinnamic acids) — anti-inflammatory; inhibit inflammatory mediator cascades.
- **Mucilage / polysaccharides** — demulcent, film-forming, moisture-retaining.
- **Tannins** — astringent, haemostatic on surfaces.
- **Glycopeptides and amino acids** — reported to contribute to the tissue-regenerative profile.

**The poison fraction:**
- **Pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs)** — **echimidine, symphytine, lycopsamine, intermedine, symlandine, lasiocarpine** and their **N-oxides**. Concentrated in the **root** (far higher than the leaf), and higher in *S. × uplandicum* (Russian comfrey) and *S. asperum* than in *S. officinale*. These are **hepatotoxic and genotoxic-carcinogenic in rodents**. They are the reason the internal tradition is abandoned and the reason topical use is regulated. See Contraindications; the flag is not decorative.

Modern licensed comfrey ointments in Europe are made from **PA-depleted** root extract with a specified maximum PA content, and are **not** the same material as a home-made root poultice.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Topical, external, intact skin, time-limited.** That is the whole permitted envelope, and Kenetica does not extend it by one millimetre.

Legitimate: acute ankle sprain and distortion; contusion and bruise; strain and myalgia; painful degenerative joint complaints (knee osteoarthritis); acute upper and lower back pain; abrasion wounds (superficial, clean, closed) under licensed preparations only.

**Categorically prohibited in Kenetica:** all internal use — no tea, no tincture, no capsule, no "comfrey for the ulcer", no "leaf as a vegetable", ever. The historical internal tradition is recorded here as **history**, not as instruction.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed — and this is the rare case where the tradition has actually been *tested*, repeatedly, in humans:

**(i) Topical comfrey works. This is a genuine finding, not a courtesy.**

- **Knee osteoarthritis, RCT (n=220, double-blind, placebo-controlled).** Comfrey root extract ointment (Kytta-Salbe f, 6 g/day for 3 weeks) reduced the VAS total pain score by **51.6 mm (54.7%)** versus **10.1 mm (10.7%)** on placebo — a between-group difference of **41.5 mm (95% CI 34.8–48.2), p < 0.001** — with WOMAC, mobility and quality-of-life scores moving in parallel (Grube, Grünwald, Krug & Staiger, *Phytomedicine* 2007; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2006.11.006)).
- **Acute ankle sprain, RCT (n=164, observer-blind, active comparator).** Comfrey root ointment was **non-inferior to diclofenac gel** on the primary endpoint (AUC of pressure-pain), with the 95% CI (19.01–103.09 h·N/cm²) lying entirely above the non-inferiority margin, and secondary variables suggesting **superiority** to diclofenac (Predel et al., *Phytomedicine* 2005; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2005.06.001)). A confirmatory re-analysis for superiority reached the same conclusion (D'Anchise, Bulitta & Giannetti, *Arzneimittelforschung* 2007; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0031-1296672)).
- **Acute upper/low back pain, RCT (n=379, multicentre, double-blind, three-arm).** A 35% comfrey root extract plus 1.2% methyl nicotinate cream, applied 3× daily for 5 days, produced a markedly smaller AUC of movement-VAS than methyl nicotinate alone or placebo (ANOVA **p < 0.0001**) (Pabst, Schaefer, Staiger, Junker-Samek & Predel, *Phytother Res* 2013; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.4790)).
- **Independent syntheses agree, with caveats.** A critical scoping review of 26 studies (13 RCTs) found evidence of benefit for ankle distortion, back pain, abrasion wounds and osteoarthritis, judged topical use apparently safe, but rated most trials at **unclear risk of bias due to poor reporting** (Frost, MacPherson & O'Meara, *Complement Ther Med* 2013; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctim.2013.09.009)). The Cochrane review of herbal medicine for low-back pain graded the comfrey ointment evidence as **low quality** for short-term pain improvement (Oltean et al., 2014; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD004504.pub4)). And a topical comfrey-plus-tannic-acid product in knee OA reduced pain over 12 weeks (VAS **−9.9 mm, p = 0.034**) but produced **no change in IL-6 or CTX-II** — i.e. it relieved pain without measurably altering inflammation or cartilage breakdown — and caused **local rash in 21% vs 1.6% on placebo** (Laslett et al., *Osteoarthritis Cartilage* 2012; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joca.2012.07.019)).

Note the honest deflation embedded there: comfrey **relieves pain**; it has **not** been shown to knit bone, regrow cartilage, or reverse the underlying pathology. The name promised consolidation; the trials delivered analgesia and reduced swelling. That is a substantial win — and it is not the whole of the claim.

**(ii) The allantoin story is real but must not be over-sold — and here is the NULL result the Codex refuses to hide.** Allantoin's cell-proliferant reputation is widely asserted and thinly evidenced. In a controlled rat model of intra-oral excisional wounds (n=125), **allantoin 0.5% gel "did not positively or negatively affect wound healing"**, while chlorhexidine and hyaluronan gels *did* significantly accelerate wound-area reduction — the authors noting explicitly that allantoin is used to promote epithelial proliferation "with very little scientific evidence to support such uses" (Hammad et al., *Int J Dent Hyg* 2011; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1601-5037.2009.00410.x)). Allantoin remains a standard component of wound-healing biomaterials (e.g. in multifunctional wound-dressing hydrogels; Ahmadian et al., *Adv Healthc Mater* 2020; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/adhm.202001122)), but the clean single-agent demonstration that *allantoin knits tissue* is weaker than the herbal literature pretends. **The doctrine's molecule is right in principle and under-evidenced in isolation.** Say it that way, or don't say it.

**(iii) The poison is not hypothetical.** Pyrrolizidine alkaloids from comfrey — chiefly **lasiocarpine, symphytine and their N-oxides** — are bioactivated by hepatic microsomal enzymes into **highly reactive pyrroles that act as powerful alkylating agents**. The characteristic injury is **hepatic veno-occlusive disease**: a non-thrombotic obliteration of the small hepatic veins, progressing to cirrhosis and liver failure, presenting with portal hypertension, hepatomegaly and abdominal pain. There is **carcinogenic potential in rodents**. Treatment is cessation of intake and, if failure is imminent, **liver transplantation**. Distribution of comfrey for internal use has been banned in Germany and Canada (Stickel & Seitz, *Public Health Nutr* 2000; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/s1368980000000586)).

**Honest evidence grade:**
- **Topical, pain/swelling/sprain/OA/back pain: MODERATE-to-GOOD clinical evidence.** Multiple RCTs, consistent direction, one non-inferiority-to-diclofenac result — tempered by unclear risk of bias across the literature, a Cochrane grade of *low quality*, and heavy industry sponsorship (several of the key trials are authored from Merck Selbstmedikation, the manufacturer of the tested ointment; this is a real limitation and is stated here rather than buried).
- **Topical, bone/fracture healing ("knitbone"): NOT DEMONSTRATED.** No trial shows accelerated fracture union. The name outruns the data.
- **Internal, any indication: CONTRAINDICATED.** Not merely unsupported — actively harmful.

The comfrey verdict, in one line: **the tradition was right about the poultice, right about the molecule in principle, wrong about the mechanism's reach, and catastrophically wrong about the cup.**

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian (EP).** Comfrey's place in the Ebers material is, as the entry states plainly, functionally plausible but philologically insecure — the wound-and-swelling poultice technique is Egyptian in its documented antiquity, but the plant names in those recipes are not all resolvable to *Symphytum officinale*, and this entry invents no prescription number. The Egyptian corpus is in any case indication-organised: it records what was laid on a hot swelling, not what a substance does to the person's *ka*, *ib* or vital constitution, and no *heka* recitation is attested for comfrey in the retrieved sources. There is therefore nothing here that assigns the plant a status in the cultivation of the person's vitality. Cultivation status: **(c) absent** — and doubly so, since even the therapeutic attribution rests on an unresolved identification.

**Assyrian (AT).** The Mesopotamian tablets record pounded-root poultices laid on swellings and broken limbs, but no secure Akkadian binomial for *Symphytum officinale* is established and the entry rightly refuses to mint one. Under the standing blocking warning for Assyrian substance identifications, and given that the tradition classifies illness by causal agency (divine hand, ghost, oath) rather than by any vital-constitution scheme this plant could be slotted into, no cultivation status is claimable. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** This is the tradition's exact attestation — *sýmphyton*, from *symphyō*, "to grow together" — but the claim it encodes is a therapeutic hypothesis about tissue (the root applied to close fresh wounds, ruptures and broken bones), not a doctrine about a subtle body. Dioscorides carries no vital-body system into which comfrey could be graded, so there is no cultivation status to assign. What the tradition offers a cultivator instead is his discipline of attention: the glutinous-and-astringent root correctly identified, its consolidating action assayed by hand, the name itself read as a testable claim rather than a charm. That is a real practice of provenance and judgement, not a vitality doctrine. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The Arabic-Persian reception gives comfrey the most theory-laden framing of the four: it is classed among the *mulaḥḥim* (flesh-consolidating) agents and the *qābiḍ* (astringents), a drug that builds and binds tissue. This is a genuine functional category, but it is a therapeutic one — a surface doctrine of wound-closure and fracture-consolidation — not a status in the tradition's actual cultivation idea, which is the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture across the whole regimen. Comfrey is nowhere given as a *mufarrih* or cardiac-*ruh* drug, and no such role is claimed here. The tradition's inward turn (comfrey given internally for ulcer and haemoptysis on the same consolidating logic) is repudiated by this Codex on toxicological grounds and is in any event a therapeutic extension, not a cultivation one. **(b) framed:** attested within a consolidating-agent frame, with no cultivation status sourced.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing internal with this plant and takes nothing from it toward vitality — the whole permitted envelope is a topical, time-limited application to intact skin for a sprain, bruise or aching joint, using a licensed PA-depleted preparation. None of the four traditions assigns comfrey a longevity or vital-constitution status, and the one tradition that theorises it most (Unani's *mulaḥḥim* framing) points at wound tissue, not at the person's inner economy. The honest reading is that comfrey is a repair-of-surface substance whose most famous internal promise is precisely the thing that kills; the doctrine's reach stops at the skin, and so does the cultivator's use of it.

### Contraindications

> ## ⚠️ PYRROLIZIDINE ALKALOID HEPATOTOXICITY — READ BEFORE ANY USE
> Comfrey contains **hepatotoxic, genotoxic and rodent-carcinogenic pyrrolizidine alkaloids** (echimidine, symphytine, lasiocarpine, lycopsamine, intermedine and their N-oxides), concentrated in the **root**. Hepatic bioactivation yields alkylating pyrroles. The characteristic lesion is **hepatic veno-occlusive disease** — obliteration of the small hepatic veins, progressing to cirrhosis and liver failure. **This has caused human deaths.** Internal comfrey is banned or restricted in multiple countries.

- **NO INTERNAL USE. EVER.** No tea, decoction, tincture, capsule, juice, or leaf-as-food. There is no safe internal dose and this Codex specifies none. Injury is **cumulative and dose-dependent over time** — a "small daily cup" is the classic route to veno-occlusive disease, not a safe compromise.
- **DO NOT APPLY TO BROKEN, OPEN, ULCERATED OR DEEP WOUNDS.** Percutaneous PA absorption is limited through intact skin and is **substantially increased through damaged skin**. The traditional gesture of packing a raw wound with comfrey root is exactly the gesture to abandon. Use only on **intact** skin (licensed preparations for superficial abrasions excepted, per their own labelling).
- **NOT IN PREGNANCY OR LACTATION.** PAs cross the placenta and are excreted in milk; PA hepatotoxicity in neonates is documented for the alkaloid class. Absolute avoidance, topical included.
- **NOT IN CHILDREN** except where a licensed, PA-depleted topical product explicitly permits it (some European ointments are licensed from age 3–4 upward; follow the product label, not the folk tradition).
- **LIMIT DURATION.** Do not use continuously. Regulatory limits on cumulative annual topical exposure exist; commonly cited in the region of **4–6 weeks per year** **[USER: verify against the current national monograph and the specific product]**.
- **Contraindicated in existing liver disease**, in anyone with a history of hepatic vascular disease, and alongside other PA-containing plants (borage, butterbur, coltsfoot, ragwort, heliotrope) — PA exposure is **additive across sources**.
- **Local skin reaction** is common enough to matter: 21% local rash vs 1.6% placebo in one 12-week topical trial (Laslett et al. 2012). Discontinue on rash.
- **Species matters.** *S. × uplandicum* (Russian comfrey) and *S. asperum* carry substantially higher PA loads than *S. officinale* and are frequently sold or grown as "comfrey". Assume unidentified garden comfrey is the worse one.
- **Use licensed, PA-depleted preparations wherever a topical comfrey product is genuinely indicated.** A home-made root salve is not the material the trials tested, and inherits none of their safety record.
