# Lactuca dissecta — sngon.bu, Ngonbu (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Lactuca dissecta, the first of three identifications Clark 1995 prints for sngon.bu (the others are Lactuca dolichophylla and Lactuca lessertiana); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary sets a different binomial, Cyananthus lobatus, beside Ngonbu by column position* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. Only Clark prints *Lactuca dissecta* (lines 1135 and 1225, and the Latin-name index, line 1606). A search of Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 for "Lactuca" found nothing. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk for the genus name found Dioscorides' lettuces (the translator's glosses *Lactuca sativa*, *Lactuca scariola* and *Lactuca virosa* for the lettuce chapters around lines 5966 to 5975, and *Lactuca sylvestris*). They are other species, in another tradition, and are not attributed to this row. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 213) the Tibetan name *sngon.bu* is recorded at Clark line 1135 with the identification "Lactuca dissecta; Lactuca dolichophylla; Lactuca lessertiana" and the note "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The key, *Lactuca dissecta*, is therefore the first of the three names Clark prints, and not a later one. All three are *Lactuca* species, so the doubt is about the species and not the genus, as far as Clark goes.

**Identity doubt.** The two Tibetan texts do not agree on a plant for this name. Clark prints three *Lactuca* species. The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary, by column position, sets *Cyananthus lobatus* beside "Ngonbu" (see below). The Codex cannot say from the sources read which plant the Tibetan texts meant, and the entry stands for the Tibetan drug. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1135).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). It reads:

"sngon.bu: Lactuca dissecta: Lactuca dolichophylla: Lactuca lessertiana: [has four names and is said to be a tree from which paper is produced. (However) it grows in high meadows, has a green, furry leaf which spreads on the ground, a blue bell-shaped flower with a reddish tint, and milky sap]. Lactuca purges lymph disorders."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries; those in parentheses come from the oral teaching of two named doctors. Inside the square brackets (the commentary layer) are the number of names, the remark that the drug "is said to be a tree from which paper is produced", the "(However)" correction in parentheses, the habitat, the leaf, the flower and the milky sap. Outside them (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the three Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the sentence "Lactuca purges lymph disorders". The word "Lactuca" in that sentence is Clark's own word for the drug. The line gives no taste, no power, no part used and no preparation. The remark about paper is printed as quoted, and the Codex does not interpret it.

*Clark 1995, chapter 21.* In "21. The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines" (heading at line 1205), line 1225 reads: "The category of medicines inducing purgation includes (long-tipped) Chebulic myrobalan, Castor Oil seed, Cassia pods, Euphorbia sp., Physic nut (Baliospermum montanum), Euphorbia pallassii, Rheum emodi (a type of rhubarb), Lactuca dissecta (a type of larkspur), Physalis peruviana, Rheum nobile,1 Stellera chamaejasme and chhu.rtsa (Rheum spiciforme)." Here the plant is named by its first binomial, and the gloss "a type of larkspur" sits beside it. A larkspur is a *Delphinium*, not a *Lactuca*, and Clark's own entry for *bya.rkang* (line 1125) prints "Larkspur: Delphinium grandiflorum". The gloss is inconsistent with the line 1135 entry's three *Lactuca* names and is reported for a decision rather than resolved. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* The text prints "Ngonbu", with no Latin name. In chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), line 3495 reads: "Ngonbu cleanses chhuser disorders, while Tronbu cleanses tripa disorders." In chapter 21, "The Group and the Method of Compounding Medicine" (contents, line 229), line 3661 reads: "Arura, Dhenda, Dhong ga, Shri khenda, Dhurji, Thamu, Chum tsa, Ngonbu, Tronbu, Tsi takmo, Rechagpa and Chhu tsa are the group of medicines that are purgatives." The index lists "Ngonbu 211,223" (line 7453). Clark prints "purges lymph disorders" and puts the plant in a list of purgatives; Men-Tsee-Khang says "cleanses chhuser disorders" and puts "Ngonbu" in a purgative group. The two texts agree that it is a purgative, and the Codex does not treat Clark's "lymph disorders" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "chhuser disorders" as one term. That the "Ngonbu" of these lines is Clark's *sngon.bu* rests on the order and the spelling, since the glossary gives the transliteration "sngon bu". [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "N" block).* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate runs. The phonetic names (lines 5539 to 5541) are nine: "Ngonbu", "Nheu", "Nimpa", "Nyalo", "Nyangtse trae", "Nye shing", "Nyiga", "Nying shhosha", "Nyiwa". The transliterations (lines 5543 to 5546) are nine, beginning "sngon bu". The scientific names (lines 5548 to 5570) are nine: "Cyananthus lobatus" (line 5548), "Chenopodium album", "Azadirachta indica", "Aconogonom campanalatum", "Coptis teetoides", "Asparagus adscendens", "Malva botka", "Choerospondias axillaris" and "Codonopsis convolvulacea". Counting in order, "Ngonbu" is first against *Cyananthus lobatus*. The count can be checked at two points: the third name, "Nimpa", stands against *Azadirachta indica* and the English "Neem" (line 5555), and the last, "Nyiwa", against *Codonopsis convolvulacea*, which Clark prints for *snyi.wa* (line 1061). The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position. [USER: verify] The glossary therefore sets beside the Tibetan name a plant of a different genus than any Clark prints for it, and does not print *Lactuca*.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Lactuca dissecta*, *sngon.bu* and *Ngonbu* found no entry on this plant. I_1175 (*Rheum emodi*) and I_1176 (*Rheum nobile*) quote the chapter 21 line 1225 list, which includes this plant, for their own members of it; they are not entries on it. I_1236 (*Asparagus adscendens*) and I_1054 (*Codonopsis convolvulacea*) quote the glossary's "N" block for other names, and I_1236 lists "Ngonbu" among the nine phonetic names. Entries on *Lactuca virosa* (I_1111, I_147), *Lactuca sativa* (I_679) and *Lactuca indica* (I_586) are on other species and other traditions, and do not cite Clark line 1135. Clark's separate entry *yu.mo.srol.gong.wa* (line 1011) names *Lactuca sibirica* for another drug and is not read as this one. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. Clark's commentary records "milky sap". It is a general fact that *Lactuca* is a genus of the daisy family (Asteraceae), that its species carry a milky latex, and that bitter sesquiterpene lactones are characteristic of the genus. The sources read do not say which part was used. Because the glossary points to a plant of another genus, no general statement about the Tibetan drug's constituents is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a purgative: "Lactuca purges lymph disorders" (Clark, line 1135), a member of the "category of medicines inducing purgation" (Clark, line 1225), "cleanses chhuser disorders" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3495), and a member of the purgative group (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3661). These are the texts' claims in their own terms and not Codex protocols. A purgative is not a self-treatment. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and does not translate "lymph disorders" or "chhuser disorders" into modern diagnoses.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** No taste, power or post-digestive taste is printed for this drug in the lines read; Clark's commentary (line 1135) gives only the plant's appearance and its milky sap. The action printed is purging lymph disorders (Clark) and cleansing chhuser disorders (Men-Tsee-Khang), and a place among the purgatives in both texts.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it purges lymph disorders, and chapter 21 lists it in the category of medicines inducing purgation; the Men-Tsee-Khang text puts the Tibetan name in its purgative group. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes, and not in the rejuvenation material; no line read places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name, not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a purgative in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and the plant behind the Tibetan name is uncertain between two texts. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. It is recorded as a purgative, and purgatives can cause harm, particularly dehydration; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Because the two texts point to different plants, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read.
