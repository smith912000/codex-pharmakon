# Common Vetch — Tare / Vesce commune / بيقية

## *Vicia sativa* L. | Family: Fabaceae
**Tradition Tags:** EP, AT, DMM, CM
**Energetic Classification:** Cool and dry; astringent-emollient (as attested for the vetch/ers group in Galenic-Unani use)
**Training Phase:** Restoration (external / poultice use only) — internal use hazardous

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Vicia sativa* is an ancient Old-World forage and famine legume — the "tare" of biblical and agrarian memory, sown as fodder and green manure across the Mediterranean and Near East, and eaten by people only under duress. Its history is a two-faced one: a plant that keeps livestock and, in bad years, humans alive, while carrying a hidden neurotoxic burden that makes it dangerous to the very non-ruminants (including people) who fall back on it.

**Dioscoridean stratum (DMM).** The classical materia medica treats the cultivated vetch/ers group (*Vicia*, and the closely used *Ervum/Lens* relatives) as a drying, binding legume: the ground seed-meal is described as an emollient and cleansing poultice for the skin — used to smooth, soften, and draw — and the flour as a mildly astringent food. The medicinal centre of gravity in the ancient texts is **external** (seed-meal cataplasm), with internal use as a coarse aliment.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian stratum (AT).** Vetch belongs to the Mesopotamian fodder-and-famine legume complex; a specific securely-attested Akkadian binomial for *V. sativa* cannot be reconstructed with confidence, so the identity here is by crop-group and habitat. **[USER: verify the specific Assyrian lexical entry before printing any Akkadian term.]**

**Ebers / Egyptian stratum (EP).** Egyptian agrarian practice used vetch and related pulses as fodder and, in scarcity, as human food; ground legume meals appear in the poultice/emollient register of the Egyptian remedy tradition. As with the Assyrian layer, the exact species behind the generic "pulse" cannot be fixed from the papyri.

**Tibetan / adjacent stratum .:** *Vicia* pulses sit within the broad legume category of the Himalayan food tradition; no distinct Sowa Rigpa monograph is independently cited here — the tag reflects group-level use, not a specific attested entry.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The doctrine to map is the **emollient, softening poultice** — "the meal that smooths and draws." This maps cleanly and honestly onto real chemistry: the seed flour is rich in **starch and mucilaginous polysaccharides** which, hydrated into a cataplasm, form a soft, occlusive, moisture-holding matrix — the physical basis of every "emollient" plaster. There is no mystery here: the "drying yet softening" paradox of the humoral classification is exactly what a starch-mucilage poultice does — it draws and binds surface moisture while keeping the covered skin supple. But the deeper doctrinal truth of *Vicia sativa* is a **warning encoded as a food-hierarchy**: every tradition that ate it ate it *reluctantly*, and ranked it below the "clean" pulses. That folk-ranking is the pre-chemical registration of a real toxin. The seed carries **β-cyano-L-alanine (BCA)**, a neurotoxic non-protein amino acid, together with the pyrimidine glucosides **vicine and convicine** — the same toxin-class chemistry that makes the related grass pea (*Lathyrus*) cause neurolathyrism. The "cool, dry, coarse, last-resort" energetic placement is thus doctrinally *correct as a hazard signal*: the tradition's instinct to distrust the seed is vindicated by toxicology. In Kenetica, *V. sativa* is filed as a **Restoration-phase external emollient only** — its internal register is closed by the toxin.

This monograph therefore performs an unusual doctrinal service: it is a study in how a tradition can be *right for the wrong reason*. The humoral physicians had no concept of a non-protein amino acid or a pyrimidine glucoside; what they had was a taste, a texture, and a folk-memory of harm — the seed was "coarse," "windy," "melancholic," fit for beasts and desperate men. Those adjectives are not chemistry, but they are a faithful record of an outcome: people who relied on the seed sometimes sickened, and the culture encoded that in a low ranking. When the codex maps "melancholic and last-resort" onto β-cyano-L-alanine neurotoxicity, it is not claiming the ancients *knew* the toxin — it is showing that a well-kept folk hazard-signal and a modern toxicological finding converge on the same seed. The symbolic layer here is a caution, not a virtue, and the honest reading is to let the caution dominate: the emollient poultice is a minor, real, external use; the seed's true significance in the materia medica is as a **teaching example of a toxic look-alike food.**

### Active Components

- **β-Cyano-L-alanine (BCA)** — neurotoxic non-protein amino acid; the principal hazard, a glutamate/homoserine-pathway disruptor implicated in lathyrism-type neurotoxicity.
- **Vicine and convicine** — pyrimidine glucosides (the same pair that cause favism from fava beans); potentiate BCA neurotoxicity and drive oxidative red-cell stress.
- **γ-Glutamyl-β-cyanoalanine** — the storage/precursor form of the cyanoalanine toxin.
- **Seed starch and mucilage polysaccharides** — the benign, emollient poultice fraction.
- **Standard legume protein and phenolics** — nutritional bulk (the reason it is eaten at all).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**External only:** hydrated seed-flour as an emollient, softening, drawing poultice on intact skin — the mucilage/starch matrix doing the work. **Internal use is not endorsed by this codex** given the cyanoalanine/vicine load. Its inclusion is as much a cautionary monograph — a famine-food and documented lentil-trade adulterant — as a therapeutic one.

The plant's genuine place in a Kenetica frame is didactic. It sits in the **Restoration** phase only in its narrow external role, and even there it is outclassed by cleaner emollients (linseed, fenugreek, oat) that carry no neurotoxin and no risk of being misidentified. What *V. sativa* uniquely teaches is the discipline of **provenance**: that a legume can look, cook, and even taste close enough to a food staple to enter the supply chain undetected, while carrying a nervous-system poison that no amount of ordinary kitchen preparation reliably removes. In a materia medica that prizes cross-tradition virtue, this entry exists to hold the opposite lesson — that the correct response to some traditional "foods" is refusal, and that the folk hierarchies which ranked such seeds last were doing real toxicological work with the only instruments they had.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:
- **Neurotoxin identity.** The neurotoxin **β-cyano-L-alanine was isolated and identified from common vetch** and proposed as a factor in neurolathyrism (Ressler, *J Biol Chem* 1962; PMID 14491568 — no DOI registered). This is the foundational toxicological identification.
- **Toxin synergy and non-ruminant hazard.** Feeding raw *Vicia sativa* seed to broiler chicks produced dose-dependent neurotoxicity and shortened survival; **vicine and convicine were shown to enhance the neurotoxicity of β-cyanoalanine**, and water-soaking for 48–72 h was needed to reduce BCA and remove vicine/convicine enough for birds to survive (Farran et al., *Int J Toxicol* 2002, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/10915810290096333)). This directly documents the seed's toxicity to monogastrics and the difficulty of detoxifying it.

- **Food-fraud context (analytical detection).** The wider problem of legume proteins being substituted into foods where consumers do not expect them is now met with sensitive analytical methods — e.g. UHPLC-MS/MS marker-peptide assays that detect and quantify nine legume species (including lentil and broad bean) down to sub-percent substitution levels in processed products (Spörl et al., *Foods* 2021, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/foods10050947)). While that study targets meat substitution rather than vetch-in-lentil specifically, it illustrates the modern toxicological/regulatory answer to exactly the kind of legume adulteration that makes *V. sativa* dangerous: species-specific detection is now feasible where visual inspection fails.

**Honest evidence grade.** The pharmacology of *V. sativa* is **overwhelmingly toxicological and agronomic, not therapeutic.** There is no credible clinical evidence for internal medicinal benefit; the emollient poultice use rests on the physical properties of starch-mucilage, not on trials. The robust, reproducible finding is the **hazard**: a characterised neurotoxin, potentiated by co-occurring glucosides, requiring aggressive processing to render even animal feed safe. The doctrine that matters here is the one that survived — *distrust the seed* — and it is scientifically vindicated.

### Contraindications

- **⚠ NEUROTOXIC / CYANOGENIC SEED — do not eat.** The seed contains **β-cyano-L-alanine**, a neurotoxic non-protein amino acid capable of producing lathyrism-like neurological damage, plus **vicine and convicine** which both potentiate that neurotoxicity and can trigger favism-type haemolysis in G6PD-deficient individuals. Human consumption is a famine practice, not a safe one.
- **Especially toxic to non-ruminants** (poultry, pigs, humans); ruminants tolerate it far better. Soaking/cooking reduces but does not reliably eliminate the toxins — documented processing required 48–72 h of repeated water changes even for animal feed.
- **Documented adulterant of red-lentil (*Lens culinaris*) trade** — split, dyed *V. sativa* seed has been passed off as red lentils, introducing the neurotoxin into a food supply where consumers do not expect it. - **G6PD deficiency:** absolute avoidance (vicine/convicine).
- External poultice: keep to intact skin; do not apply the seed-meal to open wounds where systemic absorption is possible.
