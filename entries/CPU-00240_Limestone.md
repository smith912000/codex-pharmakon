# Limestone — Shi Hui (石灰) "Limestone" (Yang 1998); other name Wu Hui "Chalk Lime"
## *Limestone in the Divine Farmer's Classic; Yang 1998 prints "Limestone" for Shi Hui and gives one other name, Wu Hui, but does not say whether the stone was raw or burnt* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** Shi Hui is printed in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`) on PDF page 35, the last pages of the run headed "Jades and Stones: Inferior Class" (heading at line 1311, page 34; the next heading, "Herbs: Superior Class", is at line 1383). The text (lines 1357-1361): "Shi Hui (Limestone) is acrid and warm. It mainly treats flat abscesses, scabs, heat qi, malign sores, lai disease,76 dead muscles, and falling eyebrows. It kills hemorrhoidal worms77 and eliminates black moles and polyps. Its other name is Wu Hui (Chalk Lime). It is produced in mountains and valleys." Yang's footnotes: 76 "This refers to leprosy" (line 1371); 77 "The translator has failed to identify hemorrhoidal worms. This term may refer to pinworms. This sentence may also mean healing hemorrhoids and killing parasites" (lines 1373-1375); and for "dead muscles", footnote 40 on page 24 says they "refer to a sensation of itching in the flesh like wriggling worms, i.e., formication" (lines 975-976). `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv` has the row (line 42) "Shi Hui, Jades and Stones: Inferior Class, PDF p.35 (line 1357), Limestone, mineral". The list `_sources/shennong.csv` carries 石灰 (line 247), in characters only, directly after 粉錫 and before 冬灰; Yang's page runs Fen Xi, Xi Tong Ling Bi, Shi Hui, Dong Hui. The match of the characters to Yang's pinyin is by position and not by a printed character. [USER: verify]

**Identity and naming traps.** The spine's note is "low", and its label for the row is the formula CaO (burnt lime). Yang prints "Limestone", the raw rock, and the sources read do not say whether the Classic's drug was the raw stone or the burnt lime. His "Wu Hui (Chalk Lime)" is the only other name given. Two other terms in the same book use the word lime and are not this entry. On page 24 Yang renders "Po Xiao" as "Slaked Lime" (line 962) and in the next entry "Xiao Shi (Mirabilitum)" is "also named Mang Xiao (Aristate Lime)" (lines 968, 972-973). His footnote 41 says "Mirabilitum is a very good cathartic" and that "the translator suspects that Mirabilitum and Slaked Lime have been mistakenly juxtaposed in terms of their indications" (lines 978-981). Those are the Superior-Class entries, not Shi Hui. A different tradition's quicklime chapter (Dioscorides, entry II_113) is a separate entry and is not read into this one.

### Active Components

Limestone is mainly calcium carbonate, CaCO3 (calcite). Heating it strongly drives off carbon dioxide and leaves quicklime, calcium oxide, CaO; quicklime reacts violently with water to give slaked lime, calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)2, which is strongly alkaline and caustic. The Classic does not say which of these is meant. The sources read give no composition.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Classic records Shi Hui for abscesses, scabs, malign sores, leprosy, the itching "dead muscles", loss of eyebrows, and for removing moles, polyps and "hemorrhoidal worms". Its listed actions are on the surface of the body. The Codex does not endorse any of these uses and offers no protocol, dose or preparation. A caustic lime applied to skin or mucous membrane burns it, and nothing here supports using it.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation is printed in the monograph, and the lines read do not say whether it was used raw, burnt, powdered or in water. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Calcium carbonate is a common antacid and calcium source; quicklime and slaked lime are industrial caustics used in mortar, plaster and water treatment. Quicklime and slaked lime can cause severe chemical burns to skin and eyes. Nothing in general chemistry supports lime as a treatment for leprosy or abscesses. ### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: a lower-grade drug (下品).* Shi Hui stands in the run headed "Jades and Stones: Inferior Class" (Yang line 1311, page 34; the run continues through page 35 to line 1382). Yang's rendering of the Classic's preface says the inferior class "mainly treat disease and correspond to earth. They are usually toxic and cannot be taken for a long time. If one intends to eliminate cold and heat and evil qi, break accumulations and gatherings, and cure disease, one should base [one's efforts] on the inferior class" (lines 391-395), and his note says this class "specifically treat disease" and "cannot be taken in large amounts or for prolonged periods of time without developing negative side effects" (lines 362-366). The monograph prints no phrase about protracted taking, lightness of body or long life. The grade is a statement of the Classic as translated by Yang in 1998 and is not carried forward from later materia medica.

**Practitioner's note.** A lower-grade drug in the Chinese scheme: for a defined complaint and a short course, and the tradition's own grading is the warning. Lime burns tissue, so for a cultivator this entry is a record of how the Classic placed a caustic stone among the drugs that "attack disease". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Contraindications

Quicklime and slaked lime are caustic: they burn skin, eyes, mouth and gut, and swallowing them is dangerous. Raw limestone dust irritates the lungs. Lime should not be applied to moles, polyps or sores; skin growths and persistent sores need medical diagnosis. Leprosy (Hansen's disease) is a curable infection treated with modern antibiotics, not a condition for a mineral remedy. The Classic's grade for this class says it cannot be taken for long.
