# Asian black bear — "bear" (Selenarctos thibetanus, Clark 1995); "black bear" (Ursus thibetanus, Men-Tsee-Khang 2011); the Tibetan name is not paired in the sources on disk
## *The bear that Clark's rendering of the Explanatory Tantra glosses "bear (Selenarctos thibetanus)" and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary lists as "Black Bear — Ursus thibetanus", a carnivorous wild animal in the Tantra's meat classification; its one medicinal product in the lines read is "bear bile"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The Asian black bear appears in the Explanatory Tantra (*bshad rgyud*) in three places. The sources are Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`) and the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`). The entry's list locus is `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` line 411, "Ursus thibetanus", from the Men-Tsee-Khang "Glossary on Animals (line 7021)", with the note that the Tibetan name is not paired because the OCR splits the glossary columns.

*The meat classification (ch.20, Clark line 672).* Meats "are classified into nine sections", and in section (e) "Carnivorous animals include the tiger, leopard, bear (Selenarctos thibetanus), snow bear (Ursus arctos), snow leopard (Panthera pardus), wolf, lynx, fox, hill dog and weasel." The Men-Tsee-Khang text lists the same group at line 2912: "The tiger, leopard, black bear, Tibetan brown bear, snow leopard, wolf, lynx, fox, dhole (Tibetan wild-dog) and monkey are the group of carnivorous wild animals" (the OCR prints "canivorous", line 2913). So Clark's "bear" is the Asian black bear (his Latin name *Selenarctos thibetanus* is the older genus name; the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints *Ursus thibetanus*), and the "snow bear" is the animal of the separate Brown bear entry. The glossary lines are 7019 ("Black Bear") and 7021 ("Ursus thibetanus"), with "Tibetan Brown bear — Ursus arctos" at line 7017. The two translations differ in the members of the group: Clark has the weasel and the hill dog; Men-Tsee-Khang has the dhole and the monkey.

*Bear bile (Clark lines 890-891).* In the list of medicines the Tantra names "dom.mkhris: Bear Bile: [has seven names. The summer bile is golden whilst the winter bile is turquoise-colored. Good quality bear bile forms a hanging thread in water.] It constricts the mouths of the channels, halts necrosis, grows new flesh [and is beneficial in eye disorders]." Clark does not name the species there. The project list row `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 100 reads "Asian Black Bear Bile, Selenarctos thibetanus G.Cuvier", an identification by the list and not by Clark's line; that material has its own spine row, CPU-00182, and the material is not written up here beyond what the line says. In the section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (Clark lines 1147-1178) bile is one of the thirteen kinds of animal product, and the general line reads: "All the various types of bile constrict the mouths of the channels, halt necrosis, stimulate flesh growth, neutralize poison and are beneficial in eye disorders" (line 1178; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3545: "All kinds of bile stops bleeding. They also heal necrosis and muscle tissues, act as an antidote against poisons, and are beneficial to eye problems"). The Men-Tsee-Khang list of animal products as read does not name bear bile.

*What the texts do not say.* Neither text, in the lines read, gives the bear's flesh a medicine of its own, names its fat, skin or paws as a medicine, or says how the bear is killed or the bile collected. The name "dom" for the bear was not found in the Men-Tsee-Khang text, and the Tibetan names of the two bears are not paired to the glossary entries. [USER: verify]

*Other texts on disk, not folded in.* In the Sushruta Samhita (Bhishagratna), the *bear* is named in the group of cave-dwelling mammals (*Guhashaya*): "the lion, tiger, wolf, hyena, arboreal leopard, cat, jackal, bear, and Mriga-ervaruka" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` lines 534-536), whose flesh the text calls "sweet, heavy, demulcent and strength-giving", subduing *Vayu*, heat-making in potency, and beneficial in diseases of the eyes and anus (lines 540-544). The list row is `_sources/sushruta.csv` line 199, "Riksha (bear)". Dioscorides names bears' grease and a bear's lung in other chapters (2-21, line 5006; 2-40, line 5101; 2-87, line 5300), which belong to the Lard and Pig-lung entries. Sushruta and Dioscorides are not on this entry's tag line; these loci are reported to the editor, not applied.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, bile in general contains bile acids, bilirubin, cholesterol and phospholipids, and bear bile is the source from which ursodeoxycholic acid was first named; the flesh is muscle protein and fat. The bile acids are used in modern medicine as a prepared drug, made without the need for bears. Nothing links any constituent to the actions the Tantra gives.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts class the bear as a carnivorous wild animal whose meat shares the group's qualities, and list "bear bile" among the animal medicines that constrict the mouths of the channels, halt necrosis, grow new flesh and help the eyes. The Codex does not recommend the use of any part of a bear. The Asian black bear is a threatened species, protected by law in most of its range, and its trade is restricted internationally; the capture of bears for bile is a major welfare and conservation problem. The entry exists to record what the text says.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources retrieved give no preparation of any part of the bear and no method of collecting bile. The only description of the bile is the assay in the commentary bracket: summer bile golden, winter bile turquoise, good bile "forms a hanging thread in water" (Clark line 891). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. The Asian black bear is a species of bear found in parts of Asia and is listed as threatened. Bile acids are digestive detergents made by the liver; ursodeoxycholic acid is made synthetically for use in some liver and gallstone conditions. That modern use does not support the traditional claims about wounds or the eyes, and bile applied to the eye is not safe. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** The Tantra assigns qualities to the group and to the class of meats, not to the bear by name. All meats in the eight groups are "sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste" (Clark line 673; Men-Tsee-Khang line 2923). The Men-Tsee-Khang text says that "the meat of birds, carnivorous animals and birds of prey that feed on raw meat has rough, light and sharp qualities, and therefore generates digestive heat, destroys tumors, increases weight and eliminates all cold disorders" (line 2931); Clark's parallel sentence names only the birds of prey (line 673). The bear is a listed member of the carnivorous group (Clark line 672; Men-Tsee-Khang line 2912), so the Men-Tsee-Khang qualities apply by group membership, which is an inference from the classification and not a sentence about the bear. For the bile, the line read gives actions (constricts the mouths of the channels, halts necrosis, grows new flesh, helps the eyes) and the colour assay, and no taste or potency of its own.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The bear is a listed member of the carnivorous animals (Clark line 672) and its bile a listed medicine (line 890); both are therapeutic placements. The wider frame does not reach it. The three humors are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration (Clark line 51), and these are "the three causes" of wind, bile and phlegm disorders (line 147), so disease is at root a spiritual condition and medicine is one support. The chapter on routine behaviour calls for refraining from the ten negative actions, the first being "taking life" (line 616). The chapter on normal health (chapter 23, lines 1262-1277) describes rejuvenation with a preliminary purge and medicinal butters, and does not name any part of a bear. The Tibetan tradition has no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice, and none is asserted here.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The sourced use is a listed animal medicine for wounds and the eyes, and the tradition's own first precept is to refrain from taking life. The Codex does not recommend taking any part of a bear. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Conservation and law:** the Asian black bear is threatened and protected in most of its range; international trade in it and its parts is restricted. The Codex does not recommend any use.
- **Welfare and ethics:** bile has been taken from live caged bears; the Tibetan text itself lists "taking life" first among the ten negative actions (Clark line 616).
- **Eyes and wounds:** the text's "beneficial in eye disorders" and "grows new flesh" are historical statements; raw animal bile should not be put into the eyes or on a wound.
- **Hygiene:** wild-animal flesh and bile can carry bacteria and parasites, and bear meat is a known source of trichinosis when undercooked.
- **Identity:** Clark prints "bear" with a Latin name and the Men-Tsee-Khang text prints "black bear"; the bile line itself does not name the species.
