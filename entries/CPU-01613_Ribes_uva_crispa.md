# Ribes uva-crispa — Ramnos trissus (Dioscorides 1-119); persephonion, leucacantha, spina alba

## *Ribes uva-crispa, the fourth Latin name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 1-119 ("Uva-crispa [Fuchs], Grossularia simplici acino, Spinosa sylvestris [Bauhin], Ribes uva-crispa [Linnaeus], Ribes grossularia [Mabberley] — Gooseberry"), which also gives "[other usage] Common Buckthorn, Purging Buckthorn — Rhamnus catharticus"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (lines 4417 to 4419) gives the gooseberry first, in four Latin forms with attributions to Fuchs, Bauhin, Linnaeus and Mabberley, and then, as an "other usage", the common or purging buckthorn, *Rhamnus catharticus*. The chapter's own name is *rhamnus*, which is the name of the buckthorn genus, and the translators' line allows both. The row takes *Ribes uva-crispa*. It was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *1-119. RAMNOS TRISSUS*, heading at line 4416, SUGGESTED line at 4417 to 4419, text at lines 4420 to 4425, with the page number 117 and the running head "GUMS from TREES" falling inside it (lines 4421 to 4423). It stands in the part of Book One whose running head reads "GUMS from TREES", after *1-118. AKAKALIS* (heading at line 4413) and before *1-120. ALIMOS* (heading at line 4426). The translators' indexes give *RAMNOS TRISSUS*, *Gooseberry*, *Uva-crispa* and *Rhamnus catharticus* at page 117.

The whole chapter reads: "Rhamnus is a shrub (growing around hedges) with upright stems and sharp thorns like oxyacantha, and the leaves are small, somewhat long, thick and soft. There is another besides this that is paler, and a third having darker and broader leaves, a little inclined to red; with long stems of five feet and more, thorny, with its hairs less strong and stiff. The fruit of it is broad, white and thin, shaped like a little pouch or whorl. The leaves of all of them are effective rubbed on for erysipelas [inflammatory skin disease] and herpes [viral skin infection]. It is said that the branches laid in gates or windows drive away the enchantments of witches. [If anyone picks up rhamnus while the moon is decreasing and holds it, it is effective against poison and mischief; and it is good for beasts to carry it around them; and for it to be put around ships; and it is good against headaches; and against devils and their assaults.] It is also called persephonion, or leucacantha, the Romans call it spina alba, some, spina cerualis, and the Africans call it atadin."

The chapter's heading prints *trissus* after the name, and the text describes three kinds: a first with upright stems, sharp thorns and small, soft leaves; a second that is paler; and a third with darker, broader, reddish leaves and long thorny stems of five feet and more. The fruit is "broad, white and thin, shaped like a little pouch or whorl". The only medicinal use is the leaves of all three kinds, "effective rubbed on" for erysipelas and herpes (the translators' bracketed glosses are an inflammatory skin disease and a viral skin infection). The rest is amuletic: it is "said" that branches laid in gates or windows drive away the enchantments of witches, and a bracketed passage says that anyone who picks it up while the moon is decreasing and holds it is protected against poison and mischief, that it is good for beasts to carry and to be put around ships, that it is good against headaches, and against "devils and their assaults". The chapter prints four further names: *persephonion* and *leucacantha*, the Roman *spina alba*, "some" *spina cerualis*, and the African *atadin*.

**The bracketed passage.** The passage on the decreasing moon is printed in square brackets in this edition. The lines read do not say why the translators or the edition enclose it, and the Codex does not claim it is a later addition. The chapter thus has two amuletic statements: the unbracketed "it is said" about gates and windows, and the bracketed one. Recording is not endorsing: the Codex neither recommends nor treats either as a cultivation practice.

**Identity.** The name in the chapter, *rhamnus*, is that of the buckthorn (the translators' "other usage"), and the plant described is a hedge shrub with thorns whose leaves are rubbed on skin complaints. The gooseberry is a thorny shrub too, which is the translators' first suggestion; the description of white, thin, pouch-like fruit is the only fruit detail the chapter gives. The Codex does not choose between them. [USER: verify]

**Names shared with other chapters.** The name *leucacantha* is also printed in other chapters. *3-22. LEUKAKANTHA* (heading at line 6717) is headed with the Greek form, and *3-122. PHALAGGION* (heading at line 7631) lists *leucacantha* among its other names at line 7635 ("Phalangium some call phalangite while others call it leucacantha"). The Codex does not read these chapters as one plant. Two chapters in the neighbourhood compare plants to *rhamnus*: *1-120. ALIMOS* (text at line 4429) describes *halimus* as "a hedge-shrub like rhamnus [above]", and *4-113. KUTISOS* (heading at line 9043; the comparison is at line 9050) says cytisus is "a white shrub like rhamnus".

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter number, *Ramnos*, *Ribes uva-crispa*, *Ribes grossularia* and *Gooseberry* found no existing entry on 1-119 itself. I_1048 (*Chrysanthemum leucacanthemum*, 3-22) records the shared name *leucacantha*, citing 1-119 at line 4425 for them; it is an entry on a different chapter. I_261 (Soapnut) and I_569 (Amla, "Indian Gooseberry") mention a gooseberry that is unrelated to this chapter. III_21 matched the search string only. None of them is an entry on 1-119.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. It is a general, well-established fact that gooseberry fruit contains organic acids and vitamin C, and that buckthorn berries are a known laxative source. The chapter mentions neither, the identity of the chapter's plant is not settled, and the fruit is not what the chapter uses medicinally (it uses the leaves). [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records leaves rubbed on for erysipelas and herpes, and a shrub whose branches in doors and windows, or picked under a waning moon, are said to ward off witchcraft, poison, mischief and "devils". These are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for skin infection, and the identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one medicinal preparation: the leaves "rubbed on". The amuletic uses are laying the branches in gates or windows, carrying the plant picked under a decreasing moon, putting it around ships, and (for beasts) carrying it. No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] Erysipelas and herpes are infections, and it is a general, well-established fact that they need medical assessment. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. It prints the leaves as "small, somewhat long, thick and soft" for the first kind and the stems as "upright" with "sharp thorns".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter does record amuletic uses: branches laid in gates and windows against "the enchantments of witches", which the text gives as "said", and, in the bracketed passage, picking the shrub under a decreasing moon against "poison and mischief" and "devils and their assaults". Those are records of practices around the plant and not an endorsement by Dioscorides, and they are not a cultivation status. What the chapter offers as a discipline of attention is a shrub told apart in three kinds by leaf colour, breadth and the length and stiffness of the stems, and its fruit described by shape. The plant is given no status beyond a skin remedy and charms. The identification is the translators'.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant beyond the charms it records, which the Codex neither recommends nor treats as a cultivation practice. It offers a description of three kinds, one skin remedy and a set of amulets, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Erysipelas and herpes need medical assessment, and a charm is not protection against poison. If the plant were the buckthorn, its berries are a general, well-established laxative with a risk of dehydration, and the Codex does not recommend eating any part. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion that allows two different shrubs, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
