# Wild Safflower — Wild Safflower, Spiny Safflower, *Kurtum* (Assyrian lineage), *Usfur* (Unani), *Knēkos agria* (Dioscorides)

## *Carthamus oxyacantha* M.Bieb. | Family: Asteraceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT (Assyrian), CM (Avicenna), DMM (Dioscorides), EP (Ebers)
**Energetic Classification:** Warm and Dry — a Galenic deobstruent, emmenagogue and mild aperient
**Training Phase:** Purification

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Carthamus oxyacantha* is the spiny wild relative and probable progenitor complex of cultivated safflower, *Carthamus tinctorius*. Unlike cayenne and papaya, safflower is a genuine Old World plant of the Near East, Levant and Iranian plateau, so the ancient tags below reflect real, contemporaneous use of *Carthamus* (wild and cultivated forms were not always sharply distinguished in antiquity). The entry treats the wild taxon as the field-growing kin of the safflower whose seed oil and florets the ancients used.

**Ebers (EP):** The Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE) and the wider Egyptian pharmacopoeia record safflower (*Carthamus*) — its florets were used as a dye and colourant (safflower yields both a yellow and a red pigment) and the plant featured among Egyptian oil- and unguent-bearing seeds. Safflower garlands and textiles have been recovered from Egyptian tombs, confirming the plant's antiquity in the Nile cultures.

**Assyrian (AT):** Cuneiform medical and administrative tablets of Mesopotamia reference *kurtum*, identified with safflower, cultivated for its oil-bearing seed and its dye. As a seed-oil and colourant plant it sits within the Assyrian materia of oils and unguents used in both medicine and ritual.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** *De Materia Medica* (1st c. CE) describes *knēkos* (safflower), noting the seed's purgative action on the bowels and the plant's use to move the belly and, in the humoral idiom, to stimulate the menses and evacuate phlegm. Dioscorides' account of the seed as an aperient is the classical root of safflower's deobstruent reputation.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM):** The *Qanun* and later Unani practice know safflower as *usfur* (*qurtum* for the seed), classed warm and dry, used as a resolvent and emmenagogue, the seed as a laxative, and the floret to move stagnant humours. Unani retained its use to promote menstruation — the emmenagogue reputation flagged in Contraindications.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Safflower is the plant of *colour and flow*. Its florets bleed yellow and red into water and cloth — the visible releasing of pigment mirrored the doctrine of a plant that "releases" and "moves": moves the bowels, moves the menses, moves stagnant blood (the last being the central claim of the cultivated species in Chinese medicine, *hong hua*). The symbolism of a substance that draws out colour maps to its traditional role as a deobstruent that draws out stagnation, placing it in the **Purification** phase.

### Active Components

- **Seed fixed oil** — rich in linoleic acid (an omega-6 polyunsaturated fatty acid), plus oleic acid
- **Floret pigments** — carthamin (red) and the yellow safflower pigments (chalcone/quinochalcone glycosides such as hydroxysafflor yellow A in the genus)
- **Flavonoids and phenolics** — with antioxidant activity
- **Carotenoids and chlorophylls** in the cold-pressed oil
- **Serotonin derivatives and lignans** reported in *Carthamus* seed coats

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Filed under the **Purification** phase as a deobstruent and gentle mover. In Kenetica the seed oil is used as a nourishing, linoleic-rich culinary and topical oil (skin-barrier support, wound care) and the florets in traditional deobstruent and emmenagogue preparations — with the pregnancy caution front and centre. It represents the phase's principle of "moving what is stagnant": bowel, menses, and (by the cultivated species' reputation) blood stasis. Modern use leans on the seed oil's antioxidant and skin-supportive profile.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:

- Khémiri, Essghaier, Sadfi-Zouaoui & Bitri (2020), *Oxidative Medicine and Cellular Longevity* — cold-pressed safflower (*Carthamus tinctorius*) seed oil showed notable antioxidant capacity (DPPH, ABTS and FRAP assays) and high total phenolic, flavonoid and carotenoid content, plus antibacterial (bacteriostatic and bactericidal, including lysozyme-associated activity) and antifungal effects against ten skin pathogens, supporting its traditional use in managing skin wounds and their microbial infections. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1155/2020/4103418)
- Michalak (2023), *International Journal of Molecular Sciences* — a review of plant extracts as skin-care and therapeutic agents documenting the moisturising, antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory and wound-healing roles of plant constituents (including seed oils) in dermatological and cosmetic use, contextualising safflower oil's skin applications within a broader evidence base. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms242015444)

**Evidence grade:** Moderate preclinical/in-vitro evidence for safflower seed-oil antioxidant, antimicrobial and skin-supportive activity; traditional deobstruent, aperient and emmenagogue uses are well-attested historically but not confirmed by controlled clinical trials for *C. oxyacantha* specifically (much modern pharmacology is on cultivated *C. tinctorius*).

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM (cultivated *hong hua*, by lineage):** acrid, warm; invigorates blood, dispels blood stasis, unblocks menstruation.
- **Ayurveda (assimilated):** *katu/tikta* (pungent/bitter) rasa; *ushna* (warm) virya; *kapha-vata* reducing; deobstruent and emmenagogue in traditional use.
- **Galenic / Unani:** Warm and dry; deobstruent, resolvent, emmenagogue; seed aperient (per Dioscorides and the *Qanun*).

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian — Ebers (EP).** Safflower is recorded in the Egyptian material chiefly as a dye and colourant plant and among the oil- and unguent-bearing seeds, with florets and textiles recovered from tombs. The Egyptian person is a plurality — *ka*, *ba*, *akh*, and the *ib* as the seat of judgement — but the Ebers corpus is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, and the identity of a given plant behind it remains in part a modern hypothesis. No *heka*-bearing recitation and no operation on the vital constitution is attested for *Carthamus*; its standing is as dyestuff, seed-oil and funerary material. **(c) absent.**

**Assyrian — cuneiform (AT).** *Kurtum*, identified with safflower, appears in the tablets as an oil-bearing and dye plant used in medicine and ritual. Under the standing blocking warning that governs every Assyrian substance identification, deity attribution and tablet reference — and given that illness in this system is classified by causal agency (a divine hand, a ghost, an oath) rather than by any status a substance holds in itself — no cultivation status is claimed. The attestation is of use, not of a vital assignment. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *Knēkos* is given a plain therapeutic account: the seed purges the bowel, the plant moves the belly and, in the humoral idiom, the menses and phlegm. Dioscorides holds no subtle-body doctrine into which a cultivation status could be placed. What the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention — knowing the seed's aperient force, gathering and assaying the floret, guarding a dye-and-oil plant against substitution. That is a practice of provenance and judgement, not a doctrine of the vital body. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** Safflower is known as *usfur* (the seed *qurtum*), classed warm and dry, worked as a resolvent, emmenagogue and mild seed-aperient to move stagnant humours. The tradition's genuine cultivation frame — conservation of the innate heat and radical moisture — is a matter of whole-regimen longevity and is not a status the corpus attaches to a warm-dry deobstruent used to *move* rather than to *husband*; no gladdening role for the *ruh* is claimed for it. Attested therapeutically, no cultivation status sourced. **(b) framed.**

**Practitioner's note.** Safflower is a mover, not a substance to accumulate. The oldest four traditions here use it as dye, seed-oil, aperient and emmenagogue — outward work on bowel, menses and stagnation, or on cloth and skin — and none records a status for building the constitution. The cultivator's uses are the modest ones the sources support: the linoleic-rich seed oil as a culinary and skin-barrier oil, the florets as a traditional deobstruent kept well away from pregnancy. Its blood-moving fame belongs to its cultivated cousin *hong hua*, a different (Chinese) register not attested for this wild taxon.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy — avoid.** Safflower florets and seed preparations are traditional emmenagogues and are reputed to stimulate uterine activity; the cultivated species (*hong hua*) is a classic Chinese herb contraindicated in pregnancy for its blood-moving action. Avoid therapeutic floret/seed preparations in pregnancy and during heavy menstrual bleeding.
- **Bleeding risk / anticoagulants:** the blood-invigorating reputation implies theoretical potentiation of anticoagulant and antiplatelet drugs; use caution, and discontinue before surgery.
- **Asteraceae allergy:** cross-sensitivity possible in those allergic to daisy-family plants (ragweed, chrysanthemum, etc.).
- Culinary safflower *seed oil* is a common edible oil and is not the same hazard profile as concentrated floret preparations; the pregnancy and bleeding cautions apply chiefly to medicinal floret/seed dosing [USER: verify].
