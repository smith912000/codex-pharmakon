# Fagonia cretica — byi.tsher, Ji tsher (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Fagonia cretica Linn, the first of three binomials Clark 1995 prints for byi.tsher (then Xanthium sibiricum Patr. and Xanthium strumarium), and the only one Clark's chapter 21 list of fever medicines prints; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints "Xanthium strumarium" against "Ji tsher"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under this binomial only by Clark.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk found no *Fagonia*. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says: "Therefore more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the entry prints three, of which the spine row takes the first (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 176). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1080).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry follows *chhu.ma.rtsi* (a type of Indian rhubarb, line 1079) and precedes *de.wa* (line 1081). It reads:

"byi.tsher: Fagonia cretica Linn: Xanthium sibiricum Patr.: Xanthium strumarium: [has three names and grows in sandy areas. It has a small, dark, thick leaf, short, thick, hard, dark yellowishgreen stalk and burrs which stick to one’s clothes. Fagonia has bitter taste, coarse power,] cures infectious fever, poisoning, kidney fever [and relieves urinary retention]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the three names, the sandy habitat, the leaf, the stalk, the burrs, the sentence "Fagonia has bitter taste, coarse power," and the clause "and relieves urinary retention". Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the three Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the clause "cures infectious fever, poisoning, kidney fever". The commentary names *Fagonia* itself and gives it a taste and a power, which is the only place in either Tibetan text that does. The description of burrs that stick to clothing fits the *Xanthium* names Clark also prints (the glossary's English for the name is "Cocklebur", line 5352) at least as well as *Fagonia*, and the Codex does not decide which plant the commentary describes. Clark's index of Latin names lists "Fagonia" and "Fagonia cretica" (line 1585).

*Clark 1995, chapter 21 (line 1211).* The compounding chapter ("The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines") lists categories of medicines for disease. The line reads: "The categories of medicines which cure infectious fevers includes Bile of elephant and so forth, Bitter Cucumber, White Aconite, Meadow Cranesbill (Geranium pratense), Fagonia cretica, Populus alba (tree), Primula sibirica (herb), Common Fumitoty and Gold Thread." Here *Fagonia cretica* stands alone, without the *Xanthium* names. Three of the other members are the plants that Clark's chapter 20 gives for other Tibetan names: *Populus alba* and *Primula sibirica* are in the *de.wa* entry (line 1081), Common Fumitory is *par.pa.ta* (line 1055), and Gold Thread is *ser.po.khrag.rkang* (line 1072).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text on "Ji tsher".* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. In chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), line 3456 reads: "Chhuma tsi drains out chhuser and ascites. Ji tsher cures epidemic dis¬ eases, acts as an antidote against poison and eliminates hot disorder of the kidneys. Dhewa cures epidemic and Tsa tri cures tripa disorders." (The break "dis¬ eases" is the OCR form as printed.) The names stand in Clark's order: *chhu.ma.rtsi*, *byi.tsher*, *de.wa*, *rtsa.mkhris* (lines 1079 to 1082). In chapter 21, line 3601: "Giwam, Sergyi metok, Bongnga karpo, Gadhur, Ji tsher, Dhewa, Parpata and Traagkang are the group of medicines that cure contagious" (the sentence continues on line 3602, "disorders"). That group matches Clark's list at line 1211 in kind and in several members (the Codex reads *Parpata* as *par.pa.ta*, *Traagkang* as the gold thread and *Dhewa* as the *de.wa* plants, from the spellings), and Clark's "Fagonia cretica" stands where the Men-Tsee-Khang group has "Ji tsher". The index lists "Ji tsher 210, 220, 307" (line 7403). The text gives no Latin name in these sentences. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary.* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "J" block (the letter at line 5335), prints its columns in separate runs: seven phonetic names (lines 5337 to 5340, "Jagoepoe" to "Jitanga"), seven transliterations (line 5342, including "byi tsher") and the scientific names (lines 5344 to 5348). Counting in order, "Ji tsher" is the fourth phonetic name and the fourth scientific name is "Xanthium strumarium" (line 5345), with the English "Cocklebur" (line 5352). The glossary therefore names *Xanthium strumarium* for this drug, by column position, and does not print *Fagonia*. The word *Fagonia* is not printed anywhere in the Men-Tsee-Khang text read. The two Tibetan texts therefore agree on the Tibetan name and on one of Clark's three binomials, and the binomial the spine row uses, *Fagonia cretica*, is Clark's first choice only. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Fagonia* is a genus of low, spiny shrubs of dry country, of the caltrop family (Zygophyllaceae), and that *Xanthium* (cocklebur) is a genus of the daisy family (Asteraceae) whose burrs and seedlings are generally known to be toxic to livestock. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "cures infectious fever, poisoning, kidney fever" (Clark, line 1080), that Clark's chapter 21 counts among medicines for infectious fevers (line 1211), and that "cures epidemic dis¬ eases, acts as an antidote against poison and eliminates hot disorder of the kidneys" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3456). These are the texts' claims, and the plant behind them is open between *Fagonia* and *Xanthium*. They are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary describes the leaf, the stalk and the burrs and does not say which part is taken, and adds that the drug "relieves urinary retention". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Tibetan terms ("infectious fever", "poisoning", "kidney fever", "epidemic diseases") are not modern diagnoses or measured outcomes, so no modern finding can be set beside them here. The identity of the drug is open between two genera of different families, so a study of *Fagonia cretica* would not bear directly on it, and nor would one of *Xanthium*. It is a general, well-established fact that suspected poisoning and fever need urgent medical care.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer prints, in the same sentence as the name *Fagonia*, "Fagonia has bitter taste, coarse power" (line 1080). *Coarse* is one of the eight powers Clark lists in the general section of chapter 20 (line 796: "The eight powers are heavy, oily, cool, blunt, light, coarse, hot and sharp"). No post-digestive taste is printed. The name does not appear in the Men-Tsee-Khang taste-group lists of chapter 19 (which begin at line 3204) that were read for this entry. The Codex draws no effect from the taste and power, because the sentence is in the commentary layer and attaches to the name *Fagonia*, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text does not repeat it.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it cures infectious fever, poisoning and kidney fever, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same in kind and counts it among the medicines for contagious disorders. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the sentences cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and the link from this binomial to that framing rests on Clark's first choice among three.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a drug for infectious fever, poisoning and kidney fever in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and the plant behind the name is open between a desert shrub and a burr-bearing weed. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. The identity is open between *Fagonia* and *Xanthium*, and *Xanthium* is generally known to be poisonous, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Poisoning and fever need urgent medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
