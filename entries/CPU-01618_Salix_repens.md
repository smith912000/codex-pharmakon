# Salix repens — Itea (Dioscorides 1-136)

## *Salix repens [Linnaeus], "Creeping Willow [Mabberley]", the fifth of the names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 1-136, which also prints Primum salicis genus, Alterum salicis genus [Fuchs], Salix purpurea, Salix vitellina and, as an "other usage", Itea ilicifolia, Itea virginica and Itea riparia* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line prints several names with no choice among them, and the row takes the one that reads *Salix repens*; the same chapter is the basis of the existing entry I_894 (see Overlap below). The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *1-136. ITEA*, heading at line 4528, SUGGESTED line at 4529, text at line 4530, with the page number 138 at line 4531. It stands in Book One, after *1-135. AGNOS* (heading at line 4521) and before *1-137. AGRIELAIA* (heading at line 4534), the wild olive, which is a different plant. The translators' index gives *Itea* and *Salix repens* at page 138 (lines 12169 and 17335).

The whole chapter reads: "Itea is a tree known to all whose fruit, leaves, bark and juice are astringent. The leaves pounded into small pieces and taken in a drink with a little pepper and wine help those troubled with iliaca passio [painful intestinal obstruction]. Taken by themselves with water they cause inconception [birth control]. The fruit (taken in a drink) is good for those who spit blood, and the bark does the same. Burnt and steeped in vinegar it takes away calluses and corns, rubbed on them. The juice from the leaves and bark warmed with rosaceum [1-53] in a cup of malum punicum [pomegranate] helps sores in the ears, and a decoction of them is an excellent warm pack for gout. It also cleans away scurf [eczema]. A juice is taken from it at the time of its flowering, the bark being cut, for it is found coalesced within. It has the ability to clean away things that darken the pupils."

The plant is "a tree known to all", which Dioscorides does not describe, and the SUGGESTED line's "Creeping Willow" is a low shrub's name and not a tree's. The Codex does not reconcile the two. The chapter names four parts: fruit, leaves, bark and juice, all called astringent. Uses given, in the chapter's order: (1) the leaves, pounded and drunk in a drink with a little pepper and wine, for *iliaca passio*; (2) the leaves alone, taken with water, "cause inconception"; (3) the fruit in a drink, and the bark, for those who spit blood; (4) the bark burnt and steeped in vinegar, rubbed on calluses and corns; (5) the juice of leaves and bark, warmed with rosaceum in a pomegranate cup, for sores in the ears; (6) a decoction as a warm pack for gout; (7) cleaning away scurf; (8) a juice taken at flowering time from the cut bark, which "has the ability to clean away things that darken the pupils". The chapter gives no other names.

**Names and cross-references.** The chapter refers to *rosaceum* by the note [1-53] and to *malum punicum* as "pomegranate"; neither is read here. The translators' introduction (line 363) also quotes the chapter: "itea, probably salix species — 'a decoction of them is an excellent fomentation for ye ... gout'". The introduction does not name a species.

**Overlap.** The same chapter is the basis of I_894 (*Salix caprea*, Goat Willow), which carries the tags AT, DMM and CM and quotes the whole of 1-136. That entry takes the spine's botanical *Salix caprea*, which no text read names. The two entries are not merged here.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read, and the chapter prints none. It is a general fact that willows (*Salix*) are known to contain salicylate-type compounds in the bark, and that this is the reason the genus figures in the history of pain-relieving drugs. Whether the plant of 1-136, or the translators' *Salix repens*, was the source of the effects the chapter reports is not stated by the text. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides reports an astringent tree whose parts are used for intestinal obstruction, spitting of blood, calluses, ear sores, gout, scurf and dimness of the pupils, and reports that the leaves taken with water "cause inconception". These are the text's reports. The Codex gives no protocol for any of them, and the identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

1-136 gives these preparations: leaves pounded and drunk with a little pepper and wine; leaves alone drunk with water; fruit and bark drunk; bark burnt, steeped in vinegar and rubbed on; leaf and bark juice warmed with rosaceum in a pomegranate cup; a decoction as a warm pack; and a juice taken from the cut bark at flowering. The chapter gives no quantity, frequency or duration; "a little pepper" is the only measure. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No study of the plant was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one quality: the fruit, leaves, bark and juice are "astringent". It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality. "Warmed" and "warm pack" describe the preparation and not a quality of the plant.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use. The part of the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition that is present here is the naming of the parts used (fruit, leaves, bark, juice), the one sensory quality (astringent) and the timing of one collection (the juice taken "at the time of its flowering", from the cut bark). The plant is given no status in the chapter beyond that of an astringent tree with several remedial uses. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It offers an astringent tree, a list of remedies without amounts, and a contraceptive report, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. It reports that the leaves taken with water "cause inconception", so use in pregnancy or when conception is wanted is a question the text raises and does not answer. [USER: verify] Bark and leaf preparations of willow are generally thought to need care in people who are sensitive to aspirin or who take blood-thinning drugs; this is a general statement and not a finding of the chapter. Blood spitting, intestinal obstruction and sudden loss of sight need urgent medical assessment, and none of these remedies must replace it. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion among several names, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
