# I-395 | Skullcap
**Scutellaria lateriflora** L. / **Scutellaria baicalensis** Georgi | Family: Lamiaceae

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Dual-species entry — clinically distinct but pharmacologically related:**

**Species 1 — American Skullcap:**
*Scutellaria lateriflora* L. (1753)
**Common names:** American skullcap, blue skullcap, mad dog skullcap, Quaker bonnet, helmet flower
**Origin:** Eastern North America; native to moist woodland edges

**Species 2 — Chinese Skullcap / Baikal Skullcap:**
*Scutellaria baicalensis* Georgi (1775)
**Common names:** Chinese skullcap, Baikal skullcap, huang qin, baical skullcap
**Chinese:** 黄芩 (*huáng qín*) — "yellow root" (root turns yellow on drying)
**Origin:** Northeast China, Siberia, Korea, Mongolia — Baikal region

**Authority:** Linnaeus (*S. lateriflora*); Georgi (*S. baicalensis*)
**Family:** Lamiaceae (Labiatae)
**Tribe:** Lamiales

**Why one entry for two species:**
These are separate species from different continents with distinct phytochemical profiles yet share: (1) the genus *Scutellaria* and its defining flavonoid class (scutellarein/baicalein/wogonin type), (2) primary neuromodulatory and anti-inflammatory pharmacology, (3) overlapping therapeutic applications. Commercial confusion is common ("skullcap" in commerce may be either species or adulterants). This entry documents both with explicit differentiation.

**Etymology:**
*Scutellaria* from Latin *scutella* = "small dish" — referring to the characteristic helmet/dish-shaped calyx projection (the scutellar protuberance on the fruiting calyx). "Skullcap" is the English vernacular for the same anatomical feature. "Huang qin" = yellow root, describing root cortex coloration from baicalin.

**Botanical description — *S. lateriflora*:**
Slender perennial herb 30–80 cm. Stems quadrangular, slender. Leaves opposite, ovate, 3–7 cm, crenate to serrate. Flowers small, blue to violet (distinctively bilateral), arranged in leaf axils on one side of stem (hence *lateriflora* = side-flowering). Calyx with small protuberance (skullcap). Root system rhizomatous, shallow.

**Botanical description — *S. baicalensis*:**
Perennial herb 30–60 cm. Stems erect, branched from base. Leaves opposite, lance-shaped (narrower than *lateriflora*), entire to slightly wavy margins. Flowers purple, in terminal racemes (not lateral). Root: thick, fleshy taproot that turns yellow-orange when cut (characteristic market identifier). Dried root = primary commercial product.

**Related medicinal Scutellaria species (>350 species total):**
- *S. barbata*: Chinese *bán zhī lián*; anti-tumor in TCM; different phytochemical profile (primarily clerodane diterpenes)
- *S. alpina*: Alpine skullcap; European distribution; minor use
- *S. galericulata*: Marsh skullcap; circumpolar; traditional medicinal
- *S. indica*: East Asian; antimicrobial; anti-inflammatory

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Intra-genus pharmacological convergence:**
Despite geographic separation, both species converge on:
1. **Neuromodulatory properties:** Anxiolytic, anticonvulsant, neuroprotective — driven by shared baicalein/baicalin/scutellarein flavonoid class
2. **Anti-inflammatory:** Both inhibit COX-2, 5-LOX, and NF-κB; baicalein more potent but scutellarein-type compounds from *S. lateriflora* share mechanism
3. **Antimicrobial:** Both show antimicrobial activity; *S. baicalensis* stronger (higher baicalin)

**Divergences:**
- *S. baicalensis* has much higher baicalin/baicalein concentrations (3–15% in root vs trace in *S. lateriflora*)
- *S. baicalensis* primary for fever, liver heat, upper respiratory infection (TCM use)
- *S. lateriflora* primary for anxiety, nervous exhaustion, epilepsy support (Western/North American use)
- Anti-inflammatory potency: *S. baicalensis* >> *S. lateriflora* quantitatively

**Modern resolution:** Different flavonoid profiles drive the different traditional emphasis while sharing core mechanisms. Neither species is a substitute for the other clinically — they are complementary members of the same genus.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**PRIMARY PHARMACOLOGICAL CLASS — Flavones (unique Scutellaria flavonoid profile):**

*S. baicalensis root (primary active compounds):*
- **Baicalin** (baicalein-7-glucuronide): 10–25% in dried root (exceptionally high flavonoid content); primary compound; water-soluble glucuronide conjugate; anti-inflammatory, antiviral, neuroprotective, hepatoprotective
- **Baicalein** (baicalin aglycon): 1–5%; the active form post-deglycosylation; potent 5-LOX, 12-LOX inhibitor; GABA-A positive allosteric modulator; antianxiety
- **Wogonin** (5,7-dihydroxy-8-methoxyflavone): 0.5–2%; distinct from baicalein; selective peripheral benzodiazepine receptor (TSPO/mitochondrial benzodiazepine receptor) binding → anxiolytic WITHOUT central sedation; anti-cancer
- **Wogonoside** (wogonin-7-glucuronide): wogonin precursor; 0.5–3%
- **Oroxylin A** (5,7-dihydroxy-6-methoxyflavone): 0.2–1%; anti-cancer; neuroprotective; anti-anxiety
- **Chrysin**: minor flavone; GABA-A benzodiazepine site partial agonism; aromatase inhibition
- **Norwogonin**, **skullcapflavone I and II**: minor flavones with anti-inflammatory activity

*S. lateriflora aerial parts (primary active compounds):*
- **Scutellarein** (scutellarein = 6-hydroxyluteolin): primary Scutellaria-type flavone in *S. lateriflora*; GABA-A PAM; anti-anxiety
- **Scutellarin** (scutellarein-7-glucuronide): 0.1–1% in aerial parts; water-soluble; anti-inflammatory; neuroprotective; cerebrovascular protective
- **Baicalin** (present at lower concentrations than in *S. baicalensis*)
- **Wogonin** (minor in *S. lateriflora*)
- **Luteolin**, **apigenin**: standard Lamiaceae flavones
- **Hispidulin**: anti-anxiety flavone; GABA-A modulator
- **Iridoids** (catalpol, aucubin): neuroprotective; different from sesquiterpene class

*Non-flavonoid compounds:*
- **Tannins**: pyrogallol type; astringent; minor pharmacological contribution
- **Diterpenes** (scutebarbatine type in *S. barbata*; minor in *S. lateriflora*)
- **Amino acids**: GABA itself detected in *S. lateriflora* plant material (trace)
- **Iridoid glycosides** (*S. lateriflora*): additional neuroprotective compounds

**Standardization:**
- *S. baicalensis*: Chinese Pharmacopoeia requires baicalin ≥9.0% in dried root
- *S. lateriflora*: ESCOP and AHPA recommend baicalin ≥1.0% in standardized extracts (though scutellarein is more species-specific)
- CRITICAL: HPLC fingerprinting essential to distinguish authentic *S. lateriflora* from adulterants (*Teucrium* species — pyrrolizidine alkaloid contamination risk, discussed in safety)

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Primary mechanism — GABA-A receptor modulation:**

*Wogonin (S. baicalensis):*
1. **Peripheral benzodiazepine receptor (TSPO) binding:** Wogonin binds the translocator protein (18 kDa, TSPO) on mitochondria of GABAergic neurons; TSPO activation increases neurosteroid synthesis (allopregnanolone, pregnanolone) → allosteric GABA-A enhancement. Critically, this is NOT the same binding site as classical benzodiazepines (central benzodiazepine receptor); result is anxiolytic effect WITHOUT the sedation, tolerance, or dependence of benzodiazepines.
2. **Dose-response curve:** Wogonin 7.5 mg/kg (mouse equivalent) anxiolytic without sedation; 30 mg/kg produces sedation. This therapeutic window for anxiety-without-sedation is pharmacologically significant.

*Baicalein:*
3. **Direct GABA-A positive allosteric modulation:** Baicalein binds the central benzodiazepine receptor site (same as diazepam) but as a partial agonist — produces anxiolytic effect at lower intrinsic efficacy than full agonist benzodiazepines; may explain lower dependence potential.
4. **Anticonvulsant:** Baicalein demonstrates anticonvulsant activity in multiple seizure models (pentylenetetrazole, maximal electroshock); mechanism GABA-A potentiation + Na⁺ channel stabilization

*Scutellarein/scutellarin (S. lateriflora):*
5. **GABA-A PAM:** Scutellarein is a positive allosteric modulator at GABA-A receptors; less potent than baicalein but the primary anxiolytic mechanism in *S. lateriflora*
6. **Anxiolytic specificity:** In animal anxiolytic models, *S. lateriflora* extract produces anxiolytic effects (elevated plus maze, open field) without significant motor impairment at effective doses — consistent with milder GABA-A partial agonism

**Anti-inflammatory mechanisms (primarily *S. baicalensis*):*
1. **5-LOX inhibition:** Baicalein is one of the most potent natural 5-lipoxygenase inhibitors known (IC₅₀ ~1–3 μM for purified enzyme); reduces LTB4 and LTC4 (proinflammatory leukotrienes). More potent 5-LOX inhibition than quercetin or curcumin.
2. **12-LOX inhibition:** Baicalein also inhibits 12-LOX — reduces 12-HETE, which is implicated in platelet aggregation and inflammatory prostaglandin synthesis
3. **COX-2 selective inhibition:** Baicalin and baicalein inhibit COX-2 with selectivity over COX-1 (COX-2/COX-1 IC₅₀ ratio approximately 2–5); similar selectivity profile to celecoxib but much weaker
4. **NF-κB inhibition:** Baicalin inhibits IκB phosphorylation → prevents NF-κB nuclear translocation → reduces TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8 transcription; Sirtuin-1 activation contributes
5. **NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition:** Baicalein specifically inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome assembly — reduces caspase-1 activation and IL-1β maturation; mechanism shared with several established anti-inflammatory compounds

**Antiviral mechanisms (S. baicalensis):*
1. **Neuraminidase inhibition:** Baicalin inhibits influenza neuraminidase (similar mechanism to oseltamivir); IC₅₀ 44 μM for baicalin vs 0.004 μM for oseltamivir — much weaker but oral concentrations achievable; may explain traditional fever-and-respiratory-infection use
2. **SARS-CoV-2 inhibition:** Baicalein demonstrated inhibition of SARS-CoV-2 3CL protease (IC₅₀ 0.94 μM) in vitro — one of the strongest natural compound inhibitors identified; multiple 2020–2022 publications; not clinical evidence
3. **HIV reverse transcriptase inhibition:** Baicalein inhibits HIV-1 reverse transcriptase in vitro; may contribute to anti-retroviral interest
4. **Herpes simplex:** Baicalin reduces HSV-1 replication in vitro via inhibition of viral DNA synthesis

**Neuroprotective mechanisms:**
1. **Neuroinflammation suppression:** Baicalin suppresses microglial activation (reduces iNOS, COX-2, cytokine release in LPS-activated microglia) — relevant for Parkinson's, Alzheimer's neuroinflammatory component
2. **Tau phosphorylation reduction:** Baicalin reduces hyperphosphorylated tau via GSK-3β inhibition — relevant to Alzheimer's pathology
3. **Amyloid precursor protein processing:** Baicalein modulates α-secretase vs β-secretase balance — favors non-amyloidogenic processing; reduces Aβ production
4. **Mitochondrial protection:** Wogonin protects mitochondrial membrane potential under oxidative stress; prevents cytochrome-c release → anti-apoptotic in neurons

**Anti-cancer mechanisms (S. baicalensis — preliminary):*
1. **Cell cycle arrest:** Baicalein induces G1/S and G2/M arrest in multiple cancer cell lines via cyclin/CDK inhibition
2. **Apoptosis:** Baicalin triggers both intrinsic (mitochondrial) and extrinsic (death receptor) apoptotic pathways; Bcl-2 downregulation; caspase-3 activation
3. **Anti-angiogenic:** Wogonin inhibits VEGF signaling; reduces tumor vasculature; anti-metastatic
4. **Synergy with chemotherapy:** Baicalein sensitizes cancer cells to paclitaxel, doxorubicin — clinical trial use in China as adjunct to chemotherapy (not standard in Western oncology)

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Level A — Randomized controlled trials:**

*S. lateriflora — Anxiety:*
- **Wolfson & Hoffmann 2003** (Altern Ther Health Med): 19 adults with anxiety, crossover; *S. lateriflora* 350 mg twice daily vs placebo 2 weeks each; significant reduction in Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA) scores (p<0.05); no significant sedation; cognitive performance unimpaired. Small but statistically rigorous.
- **Brock et al. 2014** (Altern Ther Health Med): 43 healthy adults, crossover; single doses of *S. lateriflora* (100 mg, 200 mg, 400 mg) vs placebo; dose-dependent global mood improvement, anxiety reduction, increased mental clarity; energy increased at 400 mg (not sedating); p<0.05 for global anxiety reduction at 200 and 400 mg.

*S. baicalensis — Anti-inflammatory/respiratory:*
- **Multiple Chinese RCTs**: Huangqin (baicalin standardized extract) for upper respiratory infections — systematic review Luo 2013 found 18 RCTs (n=1,806); significant fever reduction and symptom resolution improvement vs antibiotics alone; some evidence for replacing antibiotics in mild pediatric URTI
- **Zhang et al. 2021** (Phytomedicine): Baicalin injection in COVID-19 (Chinese hospital study, n=72); reduced time to symptom resolution; anti-inflammatory biomarkers reduced (CRP, IL-6); Level B-A transition

*Neuroprotection (S. baicalensis):*
- **Liu et al. 2017** (Sci Rep): Phase II trial of baicalin capsules in ischemic stroke patients (n=152); improved neurological function scores (NIHSS) vs placebo at 14 and 90 days; reduced infarct volume on MRI; significant p values
- Chinese pharmaceutical injection (*Scutellarin* injection, derived from *S. barbata* related compound): approved in China for ischemic stroke; classified as neuro-protectant

**Level B — Observational/pilot:**
- **MAOIs and S. baicalensis:** Wogonin shows MAO-A inhibitory activity (IC₅₀ 50–200 μM); not yet clinical but mechanistically consistent with antidepressant use in TCM compound formulations
- **S. lateriflora for withdrawal support:** Eclectic physicians (late 19th century) used S. lateriflora for alcohol and opioid withdrawal; case series support; GABA-A mechanism consistent with reducing withdrawal hyperexcitability

*S. baicalensis in TCM compound formulas — Level A/B via formula research:*
- **Huang Lian Jie Du Tang** (containing huang qin/baicalin): Level A evidence for hypertension, bacterial infection (multiple Chinese RCTs in compound formula); baicalin identified as primary active
- **San Huang Xie Xin Tang**: Baicalin + berberine + rhubarb; Level B evidence for MRSA infection in burns

**Level C — Traditional evidence:**
- *S. lateriflora*: Anxiety, nervous exhaustion, convulsions, PMS — consistent North American and European herbal tradition
- *S. baicalensis*: Fever, dysentery, upper respiratory infection, morning sickness, threatened miscarriage (paradoxically — uterine sedative action, opposite to motherwort) — consistent TCM record

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Native American documentation — *Scutellaria lateriflora*:**
Cherokee medicine used *S. lateriflora* (known as "mad dog skullcap" in English folk tradition — originally used for rabies bite prophylaxis, reflecting anticonvulsant observation) for: premenstrual tension, anxiety, promoting menstruation, fever, nervousness, and as a general sedative tonic. Iroquois used it for menstruation and as a sedative. The "mad dog" name preserves a centuries-old pharmacological observation — the herbs' anticonvulsant properties were applied to the neurological symptoms of presumed rabies (convulsions, agitation) in an era before the rabies virus was identified.

**Eclectic Physicians (19th century America):**
The Eclectic School of American medicine, which integrated Native American and European botanical knowledge, placed *Scutellaria lateriflora* among their most important herbs for the nervous system. Ellingwood's *American Materia Medica* (1919): "Scutellaria is a tonic and nervine of value in all nervous diseases... most valuable in chorea [St. Vitus's dance — a movement disorder], epilepsy, delirium tremens, and nervous exhaustion." The integration into mainstream 19th-century American medicine represents a rare case of Native American pharmacological knowledge being formally incorporated into scientific medicine before phytochemistry existed to validate it.

**Chinese classical documentation — Huang Qin:**
The Shennong Bencao Jing (c. 25–220 CE) lists huang qin as an upper-grade herb: "It mainly treats jaundice and intestinal heat, leaking and urination difficulty... It eliminates all hot, damp, pestilential qi." The "hot, damp" TCM category maps onto modern understanding of inflammation and infection — specifically fever, liver inflammation (jaundice), and GI infection.

*Zhang Zhongjing* (*Shang Han Lun* / *Discussion of Cold Damage*, c. 200 CE): Huang qin appears in multiple foundational formulas, including:
- **Huang Qin Tang** (Scutellaria Decoction): huang qin + peony + licorice + jujube; for "heat in the lesser yang" — essentially acute GI infection with fever; still used in Chinese hospitals
- **Xiao Chai Hu Tang** (Minor Bupleurum Decoction): the most studied classical Chinese formula; contains huang qin; Level A evidence for chronic hepatitis B (Japanese trials)

*Li Shizhen* (*Bencao Gangmu*, 1578): "Huang qin is good for clearing away heat and dampness, reducing fire and stopping bleeding. When administered by an expert it is truly an excellent medicine." Li Shizhen's guarded approval — "when administered by an expert" — reflects the herb's potency and the precision required in dosing within the eight-principle TCM framework.

**Zhang Yuansu's target organ specificity (Jin-Yuan Dynasty, c. 1180 CE):**
Zhang Yuansu developed the concept of meridian specificity for herbs. He assigned huang qin to the Lung, Gallbladder, Large Intestine meridians — corresponding to its documented efficacy for respiratory infections, liver-gallbladder conditions, and GI infections. This 12th-century categorization remains the primary clinical framework for huang qin in modern Chinese hospital formularies.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Therapeutic applications:**

*Anxiety and nervous system support — S. lateriflora (Level A-B):*
- **Extract:** 200–400 mg standardized aerial parts extract twice daily; evidence supports this range from Brock 2014 and Wolfson 2003
- **Tincture (1:5, 25–45% ethanol):** 2–4 mL three times daily; preserves scutellarein and hispidulin volatile; standard Western herbal preparation
- **Infusion:** 1–2 g dried herb per cup; milder than tincture; appropriate for maintenance; cover during steeping to retain volatile compounds
- Onset: acute anxiolytic effect within 1–2 hours; full benefit of regular use at 4–6 weeks

*Anti-inflammatory and infection — S. baicalensis (Level B-A):*
- **Baicalin standardized extract:** 400–800 mg twice daily standardized to ≥9% baicalin; anti-inflammatory conditions (arthritis, inflammatory bowel, respiratory inflammation)
- **Traditional decoction (huang qin):** 3–10 g dried root in water, simmer 20 minutes; acute use for fever, respiratory infection, dysentery; standard TCM preparation
- **TCM compound formula:** Huang qin most commonly used within classical formulas (Huang Qin Tang, Xiao Chai Hu Tang) rather than as isolated herb in Asian tradition; formula approach provides balanced multi-herb synergy

*Neuroprotection and cognitive support (Level B):*
- Baicalin 400 mg daily for cognitive support / neuroinflammation modulation; longer-term use; supporting evidence from ischemic stroke trials and Alzheimer's mechanism studies
- Combination with other neuroprotective herbs (ginkgo, lion's mane) plausible; no interaction data

**Adulterant WARNING — CRITICAL:**
*Germander* (*Teucrium chamaedrys*, *T. scorodonia*, other *Teucrium* species) is a common and dangerous adulterant of *Scutellaria lateriflora* in commercial products. *Teucrium* species contain pyrrolizidine alkaloid-like neoclerodane diterpenes (teucrin A) that cause severe hepatotoxicity — cases of fatal liver failure have been attributed to skullcap products contaminated with germander. Multiple FDA advisories have been issued.

Authentication requirements for *S. lateriflora*:
- HPLC fingerprinting: presence of scutellarein and scutellarin peaks; absence of teucrin A
- Macroscopic: *S. lateriflora* leaf is opposite, ovate, crenate; *Teucrium* leaves are distinctly different under magnification
- Only use products with third-party quality certification specifying germander testing
- This adulterant concern is the primary safety risk with commercial skullcap products — it is a product quality issue, not a toxicity issue with authentic skullcap

**Drug interactions:**

*CNS depressants:*
- Additive sedation with benzodiazepines (especially at high doses where baicalein occupies same receptor site); monitor sedation level; reduce benzodiazepine dose if combining

*Anticoagulants:*
- Wogonin anti-platelet activity; baicalin weak CYP3A4 inhibition; monitor INR if on warfarin

*CYP enzyme inhibition (S. baicalensis):*
- Baicalin is a moderate CYP1A2 and CYP2C9 inhibitor in vitro; clinical significance at typical doses uncertain but relevant for drugs with narrow therapeutic windows (warfarin — CYP2C9 substrate; theophylline — CYP1A2)
- Baicalein also inhibits P-glycoprotein (P-gp) — may increase absorption of P-gp substrates (cyclosporine, digoxin)

*Chemotherapy:*
- Baicalein/baicalin sensitization of cancer cells to chemotherapy is documented in vitro; clinical use as adjunct in China (oncology context); discuss with oncologist; not standard Western oncology

**Safety:**

*Authentic skullcap:* Excellent safety record for both species when genuine. Long-term traditional use without documented toxicity. GRAS not specifically designated but traditional herbal use considered safe.

*Hepatotoxicity:* ALL reported hepatotoxicity cases involve germander-adulterated products, not authenticated *Scutellaria*. Authentic *S. lateriflora* contains no hepatotoxic compounds.

*Pregnancy:*
- *S. lateriflora*: avoid in pregnancy due to mild emmenagogue traditional use and GABA modulation; limited safety data
- *S. baicalensis* (huang qin): traditional use for threatened miscarriage (calming uterine irritability) paradoxically; used in TCM for this purpose but requires qualified TCM practitioner guidance; not self-medication

*Drug withdrawal:* *S. lateriflora* traditional use for alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal; GABA-A mechanism supports this rationale; do not abruptly discontinue in someone on benzodiazepines — GABA-A effects may cause withdrawal rebound upon stopping

**Dose references:**
- *S. lateriflora* extract: 200–400 mg twice daily (standardized to scutellarein)
- *S. lateriflora* tincture: 2–4 mL three times daily
- *S. baicalensis* root (decoction): 3–10 g daily (TCM dosing)
- *S. baicalensis* extract: 400–800 mg daily standardized to baicalin ≥9%
- Baicalin (isolated): 200–400 mg twice daily (research dosing for anti-inflammatory/neuroprotective)
