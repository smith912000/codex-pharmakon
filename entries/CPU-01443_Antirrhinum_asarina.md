# Antirrhinum asarina — Antirrinon [Kunokephalon] (Dioscorides 4-133)

## *Antirrhinum asarina (the translators' first suggestion; "Bastard Asarum"; also *A. cymbalaria* and *A. orontium*)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification, and this row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) without being verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The SUGGESTED line gives three binomials: *Antirrhinum asarina* ("Bastard Asarum"), *Antirrhinum cymbalaria* with *Linaria cymbalaria* and *Cymbalaria muralis* ("Cymbalaria, Kenilworth Ivy, Ivy-leaved Toadflax"), and *Antirrhinum orontium* ("Lesser Snapdragon, Calf's Snout"). It adds "see 4-143". The first-named, *A. asarina*, is this row's title, but the text does not choose among the three. This is an identity doubt. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading at line 9197.

*4-133. ANTIRRINON (KUNOKEPHALON).* "Antirrhinon is a herb similar to anagallis [2-209] in the leaves and stalk, but the flowers are purple, similar to leucoion [3-138] only smaller, so it was also called sylvestris lychnis. It bears a fruit like the nostrils of a calf, carnation-like in appearance. It is said that (used as a personal ornament) this opposes poisons, and that rubbed on with lily oil or cyprine [nutsedge], it makes one beautiful. It is also called anarrhinon, and some have called it lychnis agrestis." The index prints ANTIRRINON at p.680. The cross-reference "see 4-143" points to the chapter *Osuros* (line 9288), whose translators' line lists *Antirrhinum linaria* among other names, and it was not read as part of this entry. The preceding chapter (4-132, Hippoglosson) has a similar name list ("Some call this antirrhinon, anarrhinon, or lychnis sylvestris", line 9196), which shows that the Greek names overlap between chapters. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides reports only hearsay: the plant, worn as a personal ornament, is said to oppose poisons, and rubbed on in lily oil or nutsedge oil to make one beautiful. He gives no internal use. These are the text's claims, and the Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-133 gives "rubbed on with lily oil or cyprine [nutsedge]" as the only preparation, and wearing the plant "as a personal ornament". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant by its parts, where it grows, what it is called, and what is claimed for it. 4-133 gives the look (leaves and stalk like anagallis, small purple flowers like leucoion, a fruit like a calf's nostrils), and reports two things as said ("It is said that") without vouching for them. Dioscorides here records an amuletic use while keeping his distance. Recording is not endorsing. It assigns no status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The honest reading is that Dioscorides passes on a report and does not stand behind it, and the answer is to leave the claim as history.

### Contraindications

The identity of the Greek plant is uncertain, so no safety statement can be made. Wearing the plant as protection from poison is not protection, and anyone poisoned needs medical care. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
