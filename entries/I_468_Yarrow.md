# I-468 | YARROW
## *Achillea millefolium* L.

**Family:** Asteraceae | **Part Used:** Aerial flowering tops; occasionally root

**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi · ◈ Jing

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Etymology:** *Achillea* — directly named for Achilles (Greek: *Akhilleus*), the hero of the Trojan War; legend: Achilles learned from the centaur Chiron to use yarrow to staunch the bleeding wounds of his soldiers on the battlefield at Troy; the hemostatic properties encoded in a mythological name; *millefolium* = "thousand-leaved" (Latin: *mille* = thousand; *folium* = leaf) — the extremely finely divided, feathery leaf gives the appearance of countless tiny leaflets. "Yarrow" from Old English *gearuwe* or *geaerwe* — uncertain etymology

**Common names:** Common yarrow, milfoil (from *millefolium*), soldier's woundwort, nosebleed plant, old man's pepper, devil's nettle, sanguinary (from its blood-stopping use), woundwort, herba militaris

**The Achilles-Chiron legend:** Chiron the centaur — the teacher of medicine who trained Achilles, Asclepius, and Jason — reportedly instructed Achilles in the use of yarrow to stop battlefield bleeding. The naming of the entire genus *Achillea* for this legend represents one of the most direct encodings of pharmacological knowledge in taxonomic nomenclature. The centaur-as-physician archetype throughout Greek mythology consistently involves plant medicine — a mythological system that preserved pharmacological knowledge in narrative form.

**Botanical identity:** Perennial to 1 m; rhizomatous; stems erect, woolly; leaves 2–3-times pinnately dissected into tiny segments, giving the characteristic feathery appearance (*mille folia* — the thousand leaves are the tiny leaflet segments); flower heads 3–4 mm, in dense flat-topped terminal corymb; individual heads: 4–6 white (or pink/red in cultivars) ray florets, white-yellowish disc florets; aromatic, camphor-like; globally distributed in temperate regions; among the most widespread and adaptable plants on Earth; often found in disturbed soils, meadows, roadsides — same habitats as mugwort

**Primary active constituents:**

| Compound | Concentration | Primary activity |
|----------|---------------|-----------------|
| Achilleine (alkaloid) | 0.01–0.1% fresh plant | Primary hemostatic; activates coagulation cascade |
| Chamazulene | 0.02–0.14% EO | Anti-inflammatory (via 5-LOX; same as chamomile chamazulene) |
| α-/β-Pinene | 15–35% EO | Antimicrobial; expectorant; anti-inflammatory |
| Camphor | 5–20% EO | Antimicrobial; stimulant |
| Borneol | 2–8% EO | Antimicrobial; weak GABA-A? |
| 1,8-Cineole | 2–10% EO | AChE inhibition; antimicrobial; bronchospasmolytic |
| Linalool | 2–10% EO | Mild sedative; anti-anxiety |
| Apigenin, luteolin | 0.3–1.0% | Anti-inflammatory; antioxidant |
| Artemetin, casticin (flavonoids) | 0.1–0.5% | Anti-inflammatory |
| Sesquiterpene lactones | 0.2–0.5% | Anti-inflammatory; bitter |
| Salicylic acid | trace | Anti-inflammatory; antiplatelet (paradoxically — see below) |
| Betonicine, stachydrine | trace | Alkaloids; uterotonic (minor) |
| Tannins | 2–5% | Astringent; hemostatic (local) |

**The hemostatic paradox:** Yarrow contains:
- Achilleine: procoagulant (promotes clotting)
- Salicylic acid: antiplatelet (inhibits platelet aggregation)
- Tannins: astringent → vasoconstriction → local hemostasis

Three compounds with opposing effects on hemostasis operating through different mechanisms. The net clinical effect (hemostatic — stops bleeding) reflects the dominance of achilleine + tannin-mediated vasoconstriction over trace salicylate antiplatelet activity. This pharmacological contradiction within a single plant would be considered a drug formulation problem in pharmaceutical science; in whole-plant pharmacology, the balance appears to favor the hemostatic outcome.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Eight-tradition hemostatic consensus:** Five confirmed and two tentative traditions all independently documented yarrow's wound-healing and hemostatic properties — generating one of the highest convergence scores in the Codex. The explanation for this extraordinary cross-cultural consensus is straightforward: yarrow's hemostatic effect is immediately and visibly observable. Apply yarrow to a bleeding wound; the bleeding slows or stops. No laboratory, no delayed effect, no mechanism inference required. The observation was accessible to any culture that encountered the plant and possessed a bleeding wound to treat.

**The I Ching yarrow oracle:** For approximately 3,000 years (Zhou Dynasty through the present), the primary method of I Ching divination used dried yarrow stalks (*shi cao* — 蓍草, *Achillea sinensis* or related species) as the randomizing instrument to generate hexagrams. The operator took 49 or 50 yarrow stalks, performed a complex counting ritual dividing them repeatedly, and derived a hexagram from the remainders. The choice of yarrow stalks — rather than bones (earliest oracle), coins, or other instruments — was not arbitrary: the stalks are long, straight, smooth, and resilient, making them ideal physical tools for the sorting ritual. The same plant that stopped wounds on the battlefield became the instrument of cosmic randomization in Chinese philosophy. These two applications (wound medicine and divination tool) represent the most extreme possible divergence of function for a single plant across cultures.

**Neanderthal burial evidence:** The Shanidar Cave archaeological site (Kurdistan, Iraq; ~50,000 BCE) yielded pollen analysis suggesting flowers including yarrow (*Achillea*) were placed intentionally around a Neanderthal burial. If confirmed (this remains archaeologically debated), it would push the intentional use of *Achillea* back to pre-*Homo sapiens* humanoid populations — making yarrow the oldest documented medicinal plant use in the Codex. Whether the pollen represents intentional use or natural deposition remains debated, but the association is taken seriously in paleoanthropology.

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

**Achilleine — the hemostatic alkaloid:**
- Betaine alkaloid (N-trimethyl derivative of stachydrine class)
- Direct coagulation activator: stimulates platelet aggregation and fibrin formation
- Demonstrated: intravenous achilleine in experimental animals reduces bleeding time significantly within minutes
- Topical: achieves sufficient local tissue concentration for hemostatic effect
- Mechanism: precise coagulation pathway interaction points not fully characterized at molecular level (this is a research gap)

**Chamazulene (from proazulene matricine → distillation, same as chamomile):**
- Same compound as chamomile's chamazulene (I-461); 5-LOX inhibition
- Anti-inflammatory; histamine release inhibition
- Blue EO color indicator of quality (same as chamomile — blue = chamazulene present)

**Tannins (astringent hemostatic fraction):**
- Condensed tannins (proanthocyanidins) 2–5%
- Local vasoconstriction and protein precipitation → mechanical wound sealing
- Rapid topical effect (seconds to minutes) vs. achilleine systemic coagulation pathway activation (minutes)
- Together: immediate topical wound sealing (tannins) + coagulation pathway activation (achilleine) = synergistic hemostasis

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

**Hemostasis (Primary):**
```
Topical application to wound:
1. TANNINS → immediate protein precipitation at wound surface
   → Vasoconstriction of small vessels (astringent effect on vessel walls)
   → Physical "sealing" of wound surface
   → Onset: seconds

2. ACHILLEINE → coagulation pathway activation
   → Platelet aggregation stimulation
   → Fibrin network formation enhancement
   → Bleeding time reduction
   → Onset: minutes

3. THYMOL (if present in chemotype) → antimicrobial
   → Wound infection prevention simultaneously with hemostasis

Net: Multi-mechanism hemostasis at wound site — faster and more complete
     than any single mechanism alone
```

**Anti-inflammatory:**
```
Chamazulene → 5-LOX inhibition → LTB₄ ↓ → neutrophil recruitment ↓
Apigenin, luteolin → NF-κB modulation → pro-inflammatory cytokine ↓
Sesquiterpene lactones → NF-κB IKK-β (mild; less reactive than helenalin)
Combined: broad-spectrum moderate anti-inflammatory — explains wound-healing
          quality beyond hemostasis (prevents wound inflammation escalation)
```

**Antispasmodic (Emmenagogue-adjacent):**
```
Flavonoids + tannins → smooth muscle relaxation (paradoxically, given alkaloid content)
Betonicine, stachydrine → mild uterotonic (trace; context-dependent effect)
Clinical: conflicting traditional use as both hemostatic (stops uterine bleeding)
         AND emmenagogue (stimulates menstrual flow)
Mechanistic explanation: dose-dependent, context-dependent; small doses of whole
plant extract may produce different outcome than isolated compounds
→ Do not use in pregnancy; conflicting uterine effects at any dose
```

**Fever (Diaphoretic):**
```
Volatile compounds (linalool, camphor, borneol) → diaphoretic (sweat-inducing) effect
→ Traditional fever-breaking mechanism: induce perspiration → evaporative cooling
→ Achilleine + flavonoids → anti-inflammatory (reduces febrile cytokines)
```

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Wound Healing:**
- Strong traditional evidence + pharmacological mechanistic data (Level C–D)
- Achilleine hemostatic effect demonstrated in animal models (Level D-B)
- Human clinical trial data for topical wound healing specifically: limited formal RCTs; substantial observational and traditional data

**Hemostasis:**
- Traditional and mechanistic evidence strongly supportive (Level C)
- Historical battlefield application = empirical clinical trial across centuries
- No modern placebo-controlled RCT for wound hemostasis (ethical; wounds are emergencies)

**Irritable Bowel Syndrome:**
- Limited pilot data; antispasmodic + anti-inflammatory mechanism provides rationale (Level C–D)

**Evidence Summary:**

| Indication | Best Evidence | Grade |
|-----------|---------------|-------|
| Wound hemostasis (topical) | Traditional + mechanistic + animal | Level C |
| Wound healing (topical) | Traditional + limited clinical data | Level C |
| Fever (diaphoretic) | Traditional + mechanistic | Level C |
| Dysmenorrhea | Traditional + mechanistic (conflicting) | Level C |
| GI antispasmodic | Traditional + mechanistic | Level C–D |

*Yarrow is one of the most cross-culturally validated plants in the Codex (8/9 convergence) with some of the least modern clinical trial data — reflecting the difficulty of RCT design for wound hemostasis and the plant's "commoditized" status as a widespread weed.*

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

**The Trojan War and pharmacological education:** The Achilles-Chiron legend encodes a medical education narrative: the greatest warrior of antiquity had to learn wound management from a physician (Chiron was the physician-centaur who also taught Asclepius, the god of medicine). That Achilles' lesson included yarrow specifically suggests the plant had sufficient reputation in pre-Classical Greece to be associated with the most important military surgeon in mythology. The legend preserved the pharmacological observation for 3,000 years without requiring literate transmission — every Greek child who knew the Trojan War narrative knew that yarrow stopped bleeding.

**Shanidar Cave and the deep time of plant medicine:** The archaeobotanical evidence from Shanidar Cave (Iraq, ~50,000 BCE) — disputed but not dismissed — places yarrow pollen in a Neanderthal burial context. If intentional, this represents plant medicine before *Homo sapiens* culture as we understand it. The debate itself is pharmacologically irrelevant (yarrow works the same whether or not Neanderthals knew it), but the possibility that hemostatic plant knowledge predates modern human consciousness is remarkable. It would imply that the observation "this plant stops bleeding" is so immediate, repeatable, and valuable that it was discovered independently by a minimum of 50+ cultures across all recorded history and possibly by our evolutionary predecessors.

**The I Ching and structured uncertainty:** The use of yarrow stalks for 3,000 years of I Ching divination is one of the most remarkable repurposings of a medicinal plant in history. The same plant that Achilles used to save lives on the battlefield was used in China to access cosmic order. Both applications involve a kind of boundary-crossings — hemostasis stops the crossing from living to dead; divination attempts to cross the boundary of known to unknown. Whether this philosophical resonance influenced the choice of yarrow stalks for I Ching divination is purely speculative, but the pharmacological plant becomes, in this application, a philosophical tool.

## Section 8: Practical Application

**Primary indications:**
1. Wound bleeding (topical hemostatic): minor cuts, abrasions, epistaxis (nosebleeds)
2. Fever (diaphoretic support)
3. GI antispasmodic / dysmenorrhea (conflicting evidence; use with caution)
4. Anti-inflammatory support (wound healing, post-trauma)

**Dosing:**
- **Topical hemostatic:** Apply fresh or dried crushed leaf directly to wound; hold with gentle pressure; alternatively: tincture on gauze
- **Tea (internal — fever, dysmenorrhea):** 2–4 g dried flowering tops in 200 mL water × 10 min; 3 cups/day during acute fever or dysmenorrhea; NOT for long-term use
- **Tincture (1:5, 25–45% ethanol):** 2–4 mL TID; for internal antispasmodic indication
- **Commission E dose:** 4.5 g herb/day for internal use

**Contraindications:**
- **Pregnancy:** CONTRAINDICATED — betonicine/stachydrine + conflicting uterotonic effects; avoid
- **Allergy to Asteraceae:** Cross-reactivity; achilleine sesquiterpene lactone sensitization possible
- **Anticoagulant therapy (warfarin):** Conflicting — achilleine promotes clotting (potentially reduces efficacy); salicylate (antiplatelet) — additive bleeding risk; net interaction uncertain; monitor INR

**Drug interactions:**
- **Sedatives:** Mild additive CNS depression (linalool, borneol)
- **Antihypertensives:** Mild vasodilatory effects may be additive

**Safety:**
- Well-tolerated at therapeutic doses; one of the least toxic plants in the Codex at normal use
- Dermatitis: contact sensitization possible (sesquiterpene lactone cross-reactivity; thujone class)
- Photosensitivity: rare; furocoumarins in some chemotypes
- The "nosebleed plant" folk name was used BOTH because it could stop nosebleeds AND because fresh leaves inserted into the nostrils were used to INDUCE nosebleeds for therapeutic purposes (bleeding as therapeutic intervention in humoral medicine) — these are opposite applications, demonstrating how folk plant use could be bidirectional
