# Potamogeton natans — Potamogeiton, Potamogeiton eteros (Dioscorides 4-101)

## *Potamogeton natans, the last of three names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-101 ("Potamogeton [Fuchs]", "Potamogeton rotundifolium [Bauhin]", "Potamogeton natans [Linnaeus]"), glossed "Devil's Spoons, Tenchweed, Broad-leaved Pondweed"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (lines 8926 and 8927) prints the three names, the last the Linnaean one. The row was added from the line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. **Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-101. POTAMOGEITON, POTAMOGEITON ETEROS*, heading at line 8925, text at lines 8928 and 8929, continuing after the page number 651 (line 8930) at line 8933. It stands in Book Four, before *4-102. STRATIOTES O EN TOIS UDASIN* (heading at line 8934). The translators' index gives *Potamogeton* and *Potamogeton natans* at page 651.

The chapter has two parts, the second headed in the title by *eteros* ("another"). The first reads: "Potamogeton has a thick leaf (similar to beet) standing a little above the water. It cools and is therapeutic, and is good for itches, feeding ulcers, and old ulcers. It is called this because it grows in marshes and watery places." The second reads: "There is also another potamogeton with leaves the same but longer and thinner, the thin little stalks the same, full of a reddish seed. Eaten, this is binding, and pounded into small pieces with wine and an acetabulum [vinegar cruet] taken as a drink, it helps dysentery and the abdominal cavity, and stops women's excessive bloody discharges [menstrual flow]. It also grows in watery, marshy places. It is also called stachyitis; the Romans call it fatalist, the Egyptians, ethenchis."

The first plant is described by one leaf ("thick", "similar to beet", "standing a little above the water"), and is said to cool and to be "therapeutic" for itches and for "feeding ulcers, and old ulcers". The text gives the reason for its name: it is "called this because it grows in marshes and watery places". The second plant is described by comparison with the first (longer, thinner leaves, "thin little stalks", a reddish seed) and has internal uses: eaten it is "binding", and drunk it helps dysentery, the abdominal cavity and excessive menstrual flow. The chapter does not say which plant the translators' *Potamogeton natans*, a broad-leaved pondweed, corresponds to, and the longer-leaved second kind may not be the same species. The Codex does not decide between them. The closing names (*stachyitis*, and the Roman and Egyptian names) stand at the end of the second part and may belong to it.

**Names shared with another chapter.** The word *potamogeton* is also printed among the other names of *4-16. LEIMONION* (heading at line 8150; the names, at line 8157, are "neuroides, potamogeton, lonchitis, or rapronium"), a seed plant used for dysentery and excessive menstrual flow, with very similar uses to the second kind here. That chapter is a separate chapter on a different plant, and the Codex does not read them as one.
**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter number, *Potamogeiton*, *Potamogeton* and *pondweed* found no existing entry on 4-101.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read, and nothing is stated here about the composition of the translators' *Potamogeton natans*. It is a general fact that *Potamogeton* is the pondweed genus, of aquatic plants. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a marsh plant that cools and is therapeutic for itches and ulcers, and a second plant whose seed is eaten as a binding food and drunk in wine for dysentery, abdominal complaints and excessive menstrual flow. These are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for ulcers, dysentery or bleeding, and wild aquatic plants are not recommended foods. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations: the second plant "eaten"; and "pounded into small pieces with wine and an acetabulum [vinegar cruet] taken as a drink". The sentence is ambiguous about what the acetabulum measures (the translators' bracket glosses it as a vinegar cruet). No frequency or duration is given. For the first plant the chapter gives no preparation at all. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use. What it offers is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: a leaf compared with beet and noted as standing above the water, a second kind separated from the first by its longer, thinner leaves and reddish seed, and a name explained by habitat. The chapter's attention to a distinction between two similar plants is a real practice of identification. The plants are given no status beyond a list of remedies. The identification is the translators'.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with these plants. It offers two descriptions, external and internal remedies and a reason for the name, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Dysentery, abdominal complaints, chronic ulcers and heavy menstrual bleeding need medical assessment. Aquatic plants from marshes may carry contamination, and the plant is not securely identified, so none should be gathered, eaten or drunk on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
