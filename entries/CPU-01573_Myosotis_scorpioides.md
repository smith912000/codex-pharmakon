# Myosotis scorpioides — Skorpioides (Dioscorides 4-195)

## *Myosotis scorpioides, the first of the names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-195 (which also prints Myosotis palustris and, on a second line, Scorpiurus vermiculata — "Common Caterpillar"); "Scorpion Grass, Water Forget-me-not"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (lines 9752 to 9754) offers two different plants, with no choice among them: "Myosotis scorpioides, Myosotis palustris — Scorpion Grass, Water Forget-me-not", and "Scorpiurus vermiculata — Common Caterpillar", with the cross-reference "see 2-214". The chapter describes only a small herb with leaves like scorpions' tails. Of the two suggestions, one is a forget-me-not (Boraginaceae) and one a legume (Fabaceae), and the lines read do not decide between them. This is the main identity doubt of the entry. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-195. SKORPIOIDES*, heading at line 9751, in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), and it is the last chapter of the book; "END OF BOOK FOUR" follows it. The whole chapter (line 9755) reads: "Scorpioides is a little herb with a few leaves like the tails of a scorpion. Smeared on they help those bitten by scorpions considerably."

That is the whole of it: a size ("little"), a leaf (few, "like the tails of a scorpion") and a single use, applied on the skin for scorpion bites. The chapter gives no place, no season, no part other than "leaves", no preparation beyond "smeared on", and no other name.

**The chapters it points to.** The translators' cross-reference "see 2-214" leads to *2-214. MUOS OTON* (heading at line 6463), the translators' "Alsine maior ... Stellara media — Stitchwort, Chickweed, Starwort", with the other usage "Myosotis arvensis — Field Forget-me-not, Mouse Ear" and "Myosotis alpestris", itself carrying the pointer "see 4-87". Its chapter (line 6468) describes "Muris auriculae (also called myosotis)" with reddish hollow stalks, narrow leaves, "little flowers of a skyblue colour", and a root that "applied heals ulcers in the inner angle of the eye". That chapter is a different Dioscorides plant and has a different use. The pointer sends the reader to the chapter in which the Greek name *myosotis* itself occurs. The Codex records the pointer and does not read 2-214 as this plant. The index lists "Myosotis palustris 740" and "Myosotis scorpioides 740" (line 16645), against "Myosotis arvensis 356" and "Myosotis alpestris 356".

**Neighbouring places in the same text.** The two preceding chapters, 4-193 *ELIOTROPION MEGA* (line 9742) and 4-194 *ELIOTROPION MIKRON*, are heliotropes: the first is given as a decoction with wine "and also smeared on" for "those touched by scorpions", and among its names are *scorpiuron* and *scorpioctonon*. Those chapters are treated in I_1097 and I_1098 and are different plants. A search of the entries folder found no existing entry on this chapter. Entry III_58 (caterpillar) mentions the English name "Common Caterpillar" only to say that it is the translators' name for this plant and not an animal.

### Active Components

The composition of the herb Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, the forget-me-nots (*Myosotis*) belong to the Boraginaceae, a family in which some members carry pyrrolizidine alkaloids, and *Scorpiurus* belongs to the Fabaceae. Which plant the chapter means is not settled. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a little herb with leaves like scorpions' tails, smeared on after a scorpion bite. This is the text's claim. The Codex gives no protocol for scorpion stings: a scorpion sting can be dangerous and needs medical care. The identification is the translators', between two unrelated plants.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation: the herb is "smeared on". It gives no quantity. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that a likeness between a plant and an animal, such as the scorpion's tail, is not evidence of an effect. The identity is a translators' suggestion between two unrelated plants, so a modern fact about one would not by itself bear on the remedy recorded.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying, moistening or astringent quality and no taste for 4-195.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter is two sentences. It offers only the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the herb (small, with a few leaves shaped like a scorpion's tail). The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of an applied remedy for scorpion bites. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a small herb smeared on a scorpion bite, with no quantity and no further description. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. A scorpion sting needs medical assessment, and a home application must not replace it. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion between two unrelated plants, and the leaf comparison is too slight to gather a plant from, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
