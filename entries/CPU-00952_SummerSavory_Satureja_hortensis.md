# Summer Savory — Garden Savory, Bean Herb, Greek *thymbra*-group savory, Latin *satureia*, Arabic/Persian *ṣaʿtar* (صعتر) / *marzeh* (مرزه)
## *Satureja hortensis* L. | Family: Lamiaceae
**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM
**Energetic Classification:** Hot and dry in the third degree (Galenic/Unani) — a strong *muḥallil* (resolvent) and *hāḍim* (digestive), grouped with the pungent aromatics
**Training Phase:** Digestive / Antimicrobial support — a culinary-dose kitchen herb in the maintenance phases; a short-course therapeutic agent at oil strength.

---

### Origin & Tradition

An annual Lamiaceae of the eastern Mediterranean and western Asia, grown as a kitchen herb across Europe since antiquity and still the classic seasoning for beans in central and eastern Europe (German *Bohnenkraut*, "bean herb"). It is peppery, thymol-sharp, and drying — a herb you *feel* on the tongue as heat. That immediate sensory fact drives every classical doctrine attached to it, including the one that turns out to be false.

**Dioscorides — Greek materia medica.** The savories appear in the Greek herbal among the sharp, heating pot-herbs (the *thymbra* / *satureia* complex), used to **warm the stomach, drive out wind, and promote digestion**; as an emmenagogue and diuretic; and, applied or taken, as a warming agent that "moves" cold, sluggish, phlegmatic states. The Greek pharmacology of savory is the pharmacology of a **carminative**: it is filed with the drugs that break up *pneuma* trapped in the gut and restore the downward flow. Alongside this, savory carries — through the whole classical corpus and into Roman poetry and Renaissance herbals — a persistent **aphrodisiac reputation**, popularly linked to the resemblance of *satureia* to *satyrus*, the satyr.

**Avicenna — Unani/Arabic tradition (*al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb*).** In the Arabic system savory sits in the *ṣaʿtar* (thyme-savory-oregano) group of hot pungent aromatics, generally classed **hot and dry in the third degree** — a strong grade, meaning a drug used in small quantity and in short courses, capable of overheating a hot temperament. Its indications are the classic ones for that grade: *hāḍim* (digestive, aiding concoction), *ṭārid li'l-riyāḥ* (wind-expelling / carminative), *muḥallil* (resolvent of cold swellings and stagnation), *mudirr* (diuretic and emmenagogue), an anthelmintic, and a warming agent for cold, damp chest and stomach. The Unani reading is coherent and — unusually — almost entirely confirmable, as below.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian.** Cuneiform medical texts are rich in aromatic, pungent kitchen-and-medicine plants used for stomach complaints, wind, and as fumigants and antiseptic dressings; the thyme/savory/oregano aromatics are among the plant groups proposed for several of these entries. **The Codex does not assert a specific Akkadian binomial for *Satureja hortensis*.** [USER: verify any cuneiform plant-name attribution with an Assyriologist before print.] What is defensible: in the Mesopotamian materia medica the class to which savory belongs — the **hot, drying, strongly aromatic herbs** — was consistently deployed for putrefaction, wound-dressing, and gut disorder, i.e. for exactly the indications a modern reader would call *antimicrobial* and *antispasmodic*.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:**

Summer savory is the Codex's cleanest example of a tradition being **half right in a way that is diagnostically informative** — and it is worth dwelling on *which* half.

Two doctrines attach to this plant.

**Doctrine one: the satyr doctrine.** The genus name *Satureja* has been read since antiquity as belonging to the satyrs — the goat-legged, permanently priapic followers of Dionysus — and savory carried an aphrodisiac reputation through Rome and into the Renaissance herbal tradition, where it is repeatedly said to "stir up lust". (The etymology itself is uncertain; the *satyrus* connection may be folk-etymological rather than genuine. Regardless, the *reading* is historically real and is what generated the use.) The doctrinal logic is transparent and it is not stupid: savory is **hot**. Sexual heat is heat. A drug that heats the body and, in particular, is a *mudirr* — one that drives blood and fluids toward the pelvis and the urogenital tract, hence its diuretic and emmenagogue classing — will, by the internal logic of humoral medicine, heat and quicken the generative parts. The doctrine is a valid *deduction* from a false *premise*: that pungent thermogenic sensation and sexual arousal are the same quality of "heat".

**This doctrine is not supported.** No controlled human trial establishing an aphrodisiac or pro-erectile effect of *Satureja hortensis* was retrieved. The Codex records it as **historically attested and pharmacologically unsubstantiated**, and declines to soften that. The honest scientific position is that carvacrol and thymol — the actual actives — have no established mechanism that would produce it. Where a plant's most romantic doctrine is its weakest, saying so is the whole point of the method.

**Doctrine two: the hot-dry carminative doctrine.** Avicenna's *hāḍim* / *ṭārid li'l-riyāḥ* classing says: this herb warms and dries the stomach, expels wind, resolves stagnation, kills worms, prevents putrefaction, and dresses foul wounds. Every element of that maps onto a single, real, well-characterised chemistry.

*Satureja hortensis* essential oil is dominated by **carvacrol** — 48.5% in one GC-MS-characterised Egyptian sample, with γ-terpinene at 36.6% making up nearly all the rest (Abou Baker et al. 2019, *Bioorg Chem*, [DOI 10.1016/j.bioorg.2019.103559](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bioorg.2019.103559)) — together with **thymol** (carvacrol's isomer) and **p-cymene** (their shared biosynthetic precursor). Carvacrol and thymol are **phenolic monoterpenes**: small, lipophilic, and bearing a free, acidic hydroxyl group. That structure does two things. It **partitions into microbial membranes**, disordering the lipid bilayer, increasing permeability, dissipating the proton gradient and collapsing ATP synthesis — which is why these compounds are broadly bactericidal and fungicidal rather than target-specific. And its **free phenolic OH donates hydrogen to radicals** — which is why the same oil is a strong antioxidant.

So: "hot and dry in the third degree" is a sensory report of *carvacrol on the TRPV3/TRPA1-bearing mucosa* and a functional report of *a membrane-disrupting phenol at a strength that demands respect*. "Prevents putrefaction, kills worms, dresses foul wounds" is antimicrobial pharmacology in Galenic vocabulary. "Expels wind, aids concoction" is the carminative/spasmolytic effect of monoterpene phenols on gut smooth muscle. And the *degree-three* grading — the classical warning-label that this is a drug of the strong class, given in small amounts — is vindicated exactly: carvacrol-rich oils are mucosal irritants at undiluted strength and are dosed in *drops*.

The doctrinal verdict, then: **the tradition's boring doctrine is the true one, and the tradition's exciting doctrine is the false one.** Savory is not a satyr's herb. It is a disinfectant and a digestive, and it was right about that for two thousand years.

### Active Components

- **Carvacrol** — principal phenolic monoterpene; 48.51% of the oil in the characterised Egyptian sample (Abou Baker et al. 2019, [DOI 10.1016/j.bioorg.2019.103559](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bioorg.2019.103559)). Membrane-permeabilising; the dominant antimicrobial and antioxidant principle.
- **Thymol** — isomer of carvacrol; same phenolic mechanism; proportion varies strongly by chemotype and provenance.
- **γ-Terpinene** — 36.63% in the same sample; a non-phenolic monoterpene hydrocarbon, biosynthetic precursor to p-cymene and thence to carvacrol/thymol. Low antimicrobial potency itself; contributes to the oil's overall lipophilic membrane effect.
- **p-Cymene** — aromatic hydrocarbon; weak alone but potentiates carvacrol's membrane insertion.
- **Rosmarinic acid and other phenolic acids; flavonoids** — in the aqueous/hydroalcoholic (non-oil) fraction. Total phenolics in the oil measured at 199.1 ± 0.5 µg GAE/mg, total flavonoids 161.5 ± 0.9 µg CE/mg (Abou Baker et al. 2019).
- Chemotype caveat: essential-oil composition in *Satureja* varies substantially with cultivar, harvest stage and climate. A "savory oil" is not a fixed substance; assume the carvacrol fraction is unknown unless a GC-MS certificate says otherwise.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

- **Culinary / maintenance dose (the primary Kenetica use):** the herb with beans, pulses, fatty meat, and rich cold-weather food. This is the traditional use and the pharmacologically sensible one — a carminative and antimicrobial delivered at a dose the gut is designed for.
- **Digestive short-course:** infusion of the dried herb after heavy or badly-tolerated meals; for bloating, wind, and post-prandial sluggishness. Graded **traditional with plausible mechanism**.
- **Topical antimicrobial (diluted oil):** for the antifungal and antibiofilm indications where the in-vitro evidence is strongest (below). Short course, always carrier-diluted.
- **NOT indicated:** as an aphrodisiac or libido agent. The Kenetica protocols do not carry savory in that role, and any product that claims it is making a claim this Codex will not underwrite.

### Scientific Correlation

*Attribution: the following studies were retrieved from PubMed.*

1. **Composition, antioxidant and antibacterial activity of *S. hortensis* oil.** Abou Baker, Mohamed & Rahmy (2019/2020) GC-MS-characterised the oil (carvacrol 48.51%, γ-terpinene 36.63%; 7 compounds = 96.84% of the oil), quantified total phenolics/flavonoids, and reported antioxidant, antibacterial and in-vitro cytotoxic activity. *Bioorg Chem*. [DOI 10.1016/j.bioorg.2019.103559](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bioorg.2019.103559). **Grade: moderate–strong for composition and in-vitro activity.** This is the paper that makes "hot and dry in the third degree" chemically legible.

2. **Antifungal and antibiofilm activity against *Candida albicans*.** Sharifzadeh & Shokri (2016) tested *S. hortensis* essential oil against *C. albicans* isolates taken from buccal lesions of HIV-positive individuals, assessing planktonic growth, biofilm formation and mature biofilms by MTT, broth microdilution and SEM. *Microb Pathog*. [DOI 10.1016/j.micpath.2016.04.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.micpath.2016.04.014). **Grade: in vitro only.** Directly relevant to the traditional antiputrefactive/wound-and-mouth uses — and directly *non-transferable* to a human dose.

3. **Quorum sensing and biofilm inhibition in *Streptococcus pneumoniae*.** Sharifi et al. (2018) showed that essential oils of *Thymus daenensis*, *Satureja hortensis* and *Origanum vulgare* affect planktonic growth, biofilm formation, quorum sensing and the competence system of *S. pneumoniae*, with gene-expression confirmation by qRT-PCR. *Acta Microbiol Immunol Hung*. [DOI 10.1556/030.65.2018.013](https://doi.org/10.1556/030.65.2018.013). **Grade: in vitro, mechanistically informative.** Note the honest reading: this is *sub-inhibitory signalling interference*, not a demonstrated clinical antibacterial effect.

4. **Antioxidant activity is not simply the sum of the main component.** Dawidowicz & Olszowy (2014) compared the antioxidant activity of several essential oils — thyme, basil, peppermint, clove, **summer savory (*S. hortensis*)**, sage, lemon — with the activity of their isolated main components (thymol, carvacrol, etc.) by DPPH, ABTS and β-carotene bleaching. *Nat Prod Res*. [DOI 10.1080/14786419.2014.918121](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786419.2014.918121). **Grade: moderate; important as a corrective.** The whole-oil activity does not reduce cleanly to its dominant molecule — a caution against the reflex of naming one compound and calling the plant explained.

5. **Antispasmodic activity — carvacrol-containing oils on isolated ileum (adjacent evidence, not savory).** Sadraei, Asghari & Alipour (2016) showed that a carvacrol-containing essential oil (*Pycnocycla caespitosa*, carvacrol ~7.1%) inhibited rat isolated ileum contractions evoked by KCl, acetylcholine and electrical field stimulation, with IC50 values of 9.2 ± 1.2, 7.6 ± 0.8 and 6.4 ± 0.8 µg/mL respectively, reversibly. *Res Pharm Sci* 2016;11(1):33–42. [PMID 27051430](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27051430/) (no DOI registered in PubMed). **Grade: weak-to-moderate, and explicitly indirect — this is a different plant.** It is cited to show that the *carminative/antispasmodic doctrine has a real experimental correlate in this chemical class*, and to be transparent that a direct isolated-organ study of *S. hortensis* itself was not retrieved in this search.

**Honest evidence grade, and the null finding.** The antimicrobial, antifungal, antibiofilm and antioxidant claims are supported **in vitro** and only in vitro; no human clinical trial of *S. hortensis* for any indication was retrieved in this search, and the entire PubMed corpus on "*Satureja hortensis* essential oil carvacrol antimicrobial" runs to 17 records. The digestive/carminative doctrine is **plausible-by-mechanism, clinically unproven**. And the **aphrodisiac doctrine is a null: no supporting evidence was found, no mechanism is proposed, and the Codex grades it *unsupported folklore*.** Two millennia of insistence do not constitute a result.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani (attested):** Hot and dry, **third degree**. *Hāḍim* (digestive), *ṭārid li'l-riyāḥ* (carminative/wind-expelling), *muḥallil* (resolvent), *mudirr* (diuretic/emmenagogue), anthelmintic. Contraindicated in hot, bilious temperaments — the classical system's own dose- and constitution-limiting flag, which the carvacrol irritancy data retrospectively justifies.
- **Greek (Dioscoridean, attested):** sharp, heating pot-herb; stomach-warmer, wind-breaker, emmenagogue; aphrodisiac by reputation.
- **TCM:** **Not attested.** *Satureja hortensis* is not a Chinese materia medica item; no channel entry, no TCM temperature is assigned here.
- **Ayurvedic (rasa / guṇa / vīrya / vipāka):** **Not attested.** Not a classical Ayurvedic dravya. Any rasa/vīrya values in circulation are modern extrapolations from the herb's pungency, not textual attestations, and this Codex does not print them.

### Contraindications

- **Essential oil is a mucosal and dermal irritant.** Carvacrol-rich oils burn undiluted. Never apply neat; never take internally unsupervised. Keep away from eyes and mucous membranes.
- **Sensitisation:** possible contact allergy/dermatitis with repeated topical use of the oil. Patch-test before any course.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** avoid at medicinal/oil doses. The herb carries a classical emmenagogue reputation, and phenolic monoterpene oils are conventionally avoided in pregnancy. Culinary quantities in food are not the concern here; therapeutic oil doses are.
- **Infants and small children:** do not apply carvacrol/thymol-rich oils to the face or chest, and do not administer internally. Phenolic monoterpenes carry laryngospasm risk in the very young.
- **GORD, gastritis, peptic ulcer:** pungent phenolic aromatics can aggravate. Reduce or avoid.
- **Anticoagulant/antiplatelet therapy:** exercise caution with concentrated oil and high-dose extracts; monoterpene phenols have reported effects on platelet function. [USER: verify before printing a specific interaction claim.]
- **Chemotype uncertainty:** an unassayed "savory oil" may be far more or far less carvacrol-rich than the sample cited above. Dose to the certificate, not to the label.
- **Do not use for the aphrodisiac indication.** Not because it is dangerous, but because it does not work and the dose escalation that follows a non-working expectation is where the irritancy risk actually materialises.
