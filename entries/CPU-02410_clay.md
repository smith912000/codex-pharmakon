# Clay (from a gate, a statue, etc.) — Clay-from-the-Gate, Clay-from-a-Statue, Clay-from-the-Wall, Mason's Clay (Bryan 1930, Papyrus Ebers)

## *Clay taken from a named source, a gate, a statue, a wall or a mason's supply, used as a poultice base for itch, scabs, eye discharge, bruises and a back complaint; the identification is only Bryan's English rendering of four named clays, and the suggestion that they are mixed clay or mortar is ours, of low confidence, since the Egyptian words are not given in the sources on disk* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** EP

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** This is a single-text entry, and the whole attestation is Bryan's 1930 English version of the Papyrus Ebers (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`). Bryan's Chapter V, "Mineral Remedies", sets out the mineral drugs in alphabetical order and prints (line 1152) four clays together: "Clay-from-the-Gate Clay-from-a-Statue Clay-from-thWea-ll Mason's Clay" (the third is a damaged print of "Clay-from-the-Wall"). `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` carries them as one row, "Clay-from-the-Gate", locus Bryan 1930, Ch. V (line 1152), class mineral, "Bryan's English identification". The row began as an unverified placeholder on 13 October; this entry replaces it. A search of the other texts on disk for these clays found nothing, and no attestation from them is claimed.

Bryan's own remark on the chapter (line 1137) is that few mineral drugs are used in the crude state and that "the source of origin of the drugs is strictly laid down", with examples that are not clays (the iron, the leather, the soot, the soda). The clays printed here follow the same habit: each is named by where it comes from, a gate, a statue, a wall, a mason. What the Egyptian words were, and what material each was, are not given in the sources on disk, and the Egyptian names and the papyrus column numbers are not in them either. [USER: verify]

The recipes that name these clays, as Bryan prints them, are these.

*Clay-from-the-Gate, for itching (line 2613).* In Bryan's account of the skin diseases, when itching "spread to all the limbs and became generalized", one remedy among others was "a Poultice of Corn, Natron, Clay-from-the-Gate, Onions, Incense, and Refuse-of-Dates". Bryan's gloss, in the next lines, is that this was "counter-irritation" and that it "persists to this day, though not in this form". That gloss is Bryan's own modern comment, not the papyrus.

*Clay-from-the-Wall, for scabs (line 2644).* For "Scabs in every Limb", the first attempt was "a poultice of Clay-from-the-Wall, Wheaten Flour, and Fat-of-the-deher-animal, strained into Yeast-of-Sweet-Beer". Other poultices followed if this failed.

*Clay-from-a-Statue, for the eyes (lines 2779 and 2856).* "To him whose two eyes suffer from a flow of matter" Bryan gives "a poultice of Clay-from-a-Statue, Leaves-of-the-Castor-oil-tree, and Honey", and again (line 2856) that "Clay-from-a-Statue was ground up in Honey along with some Leaves-from-the-Castor-Oil-tree, and applied to the eyes".

*Mason's Clay, for wheals from blows (line 2386).* Under the "Remedy to drive away the wheals from blows" the ingredients are "Honey, Cow's Brain, Mason's Clay, Linseed-water, Date-juice", with the instruction "Cook, and apply as a plaster."

*Mason's Clay, in a back remedy (line 3758).* For a person suffering in the abdomen who has "something in his backbone like the trouble in the knife-grinder's disease", the physician is to say that the *uxedu* (Bryan's word) "has spread to his back", and to make "the Back-remedy": a pot-yeast, then "Spring Plant, Peppermint, Resin-of-Acanthus, Mason's Clay", to be crushed, cooked in "Yeast-of-Sweet-Beer" and smeared on for four days "in order to heal him at once". The name of the complaint, the *uxedu* and the "knife-grinder's disease", are Bryan's renderings and are not explained further in the lines read. [USER: verify]

In every case the clay is an ingredient in an external poultice or plaster, with other substances, and never alone, and never a drink.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent, and the Egyptian material is not identified. In general terms, clay is a fine-grained mixture of hydrated aluminium silicates with sand, silt and iron oxides, and clay taken from a wall or a gate would also carry whatever the structure was built or plastered with, including lime, straw, dung and dirt. Clay from a statue could be a modelling clay or the fill of a figure. "Mason's clay" suggests the clay used in building. These are inferences only. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a recommended practice.** The record is of clay as the body of a poultice, a drawing and drying mass that held the active ingredients (onion, incense, honey, natron) against skin and eye. The Codex does not translate "flow of matter", "wheals" or the back complaint into modern diagnoses, and gives no protocol. Clay taken from a wall, a gate or a statue is not clean, and putting it on the eye or on broken skin risks infection.

### Preparation & Dosing

The papyrus, as Bryan gives it, offers these: poultice with other ingredients (line 2613); poultice strained into sweet-beer yeast (line 2644); ground in honey with castor-oil-tree leaves and applied to the eyes (lines 2779, 2856); cooked with honey, cow's brain, linseed-water and date-juice as a plaster (line 2386); crushed and cooked in sweet-beer yeast and smeared on for four days (line 3758). No amounts are given in the passages read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Clay is absorbent and was widely used as a poultice base across ancient and later medicine; it holds moisture and other ingredients against the skin. Soil and wall clay can carry bacteria and spores, including those of tetanus, and building clay and plaster can contain lime or pigments that irritate the eye. Honey has antibacterial properties in its own right, which may account for part of the eye remedy's tolerability, but that is a general fact and not a finding about these recipes. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Egyptian (EP):** None is given. The Ebers material, as Bryan prints it, organises recipes by complaint and does not assign hot, cold, wet or dry qualities to clay.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's vital constitution. The person as plural (*ka*, *ba*, *akh*, *ib*) and *heka*, the operative power in which spell and drug make one intervention, are the frame the tradition itself offers, but no text read here assigns any of them to these clays, and none of the passages read in Bryan's English carries a recitation. The identity of the materials is a modern hypothesis in any case. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** A building-site clay used as the vehicle of external poultices. Nothing here supports ingestion or any cultivation use. What the record shows is the care to name where the clay came from, not what the clay does to the person.

### Contraindications

- **Open skin and eyes.** Do not put clay from a wall, gate or statue on broken skin or in the eye; it risks infection, including tetanus.
- **Ingestion.** None of the recipes is a drink. Do not eat building clay.
- **Heritage and legal.** Taking clay or scrapings from a statue, wall or gate of an ancient site is damage to heritage and illegal in most places.
- **Identity.** Four named clays, not identified. [USER: verify]
