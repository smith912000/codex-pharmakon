# Ginger — *Zingiber officinale* Roscoe

**Family:** Zingiberaceae | **Class:** plant | **Volume:** I · Plant | **Codex id:** CPU-00066
**Attested in:** Dioscorides · rGyud-bzhi · Shennong Bencao Jing · Avicenna

---

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

*Zingiber officinale* Roscoe (1807) is the accepted binomial for cultivated ginger, a perennial herb of the family Zingiberaceae native to tropical Southeast Asia, now pantropically cultivated. The medicinal part is the rhizome — the underground stem that, depending on processing, yields two pharmacologically distinct preparations. Fresh rhizome (*Shēng Jiāng* in TCM) retains higher gingerol concentrations and volatile oil content. Dried rhizome (*Gān Jiāng*) undergoes dehydration-driven chemical conversion, in which gingerols dehydrate to the more potent shogaols; the resulting compound profile and clinical energetics differ substantially from the fresh form. This fresh-versus-dried distinction has been encoded formally in Chinese materia medica since at least the Han dynasty and is now supported by phytochemical analysis.[1]

The genus name *Zingiber* derives from the ancient Greek ζηγγίβερις (*zingiberis*), which is itself a phonetic rendering of a South Asian term. The Roman form *zingiber* gave the botanical epithet; the common English word "ginger" descends through Old French *gingembre* from the same root.[2]

## 2. Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2a. Dioscorides — *De Materia Medica* (c. 65–70 CE)

Pedanius Dioscorides, the Greek military physician whose five-volume *De Materia Medica* described approximately 600 plants and remained a primary reference for fifteen centuries, inscribed ginger as ζηγγίβερις.[2] Within his humoral framework, Dioscorides characterised ginger as warming and digestive — useful for the stomach and as a corrective to cold and moist conditions of the gut. He situated it among spices obtained from trade with Arabia and India, reflecting the commodity routes already active in the first century CE.[3]

### 2b. Shennong Bencao Jing (compiled c. 1st–2nd century CE, attributed to Shennong)

The *Shénnóng Běncǎo Jīng* (Divine Farmer's Classic of Materia Medica), the foundational Chinese pharmacopoeial canon, listed dry ginger (*gān jiāng*) as a **middle-grade** herb (中品), indicating regular therapeutic use with manageable caution.[4] In the Bencao tradition, the critical distinction between *Shēng Jiāng* (fresh, 生姜) and *Gān Jiāng* (dried, 干姜) encodes two medicines from one rhizome:

- **Shēng Jiāng** — slightly warm (微温), acrid, enters the Lung, Spleen, and Stomach channels. Its action is primarily exterior: it releases Wind-Cold from the surface, warms the Stomach to arrest vomiting, and resolves phlegm-cold. Effect is rapid and relatively mild.[5]
- **Gān Jiāng** — hot (热), acrid, enters the Heart, Lung, Spleen, and Kidney channels. Exclusively interior action: it vigorously warms the Spleen and Stomach, rescues devastated Yang, and warms the Lung to transform thin fluid retention. Reserved for Cold-pattern interior conditions with Yang deficiency.[5]

This differentiation appears consistently in the later *Bencao Gangmu* (Li Shizhen, 1596) and in modern TCM pharmacopoeia, and is supported by the chemically distinct compound ratios in the two forms.[1]

### 2c. rGyud-bzhi (Four Medical Tantras, compiled 12th century CE, attributed to Yuthog Yonten Gonpo)

The *rGyud-bzhi* (Gyushi), the foundational text of Tibetan medicine (Sowa Rigpa), comprises four tantras totalling 5,900 verses across 156 chapters.[6] Tibetan pharmacology classifies substances principally by taste (*ro*), post-digestive taste (*zhu ba'i ro*), and potency (*nus pa*). Ginger is classed as acrid in taste, heating in potency, and is employed within the Tibetan system as a digestive stimulant, carminative, and warming agent countering disorders attributed to rLung (wind) and Beken (phlegm-cold) constitutions — conditions corresponding broadly to cold, deficient, and stagnant states. The rGyud-bzhi's pharmacological categories draw on both Indian Ayurvedic and Chinese influences, assimilated into a distinctly Tibetan framework grounded in Buddhist cosmology.[6, 7]

### 2d. Avicenna — *Al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb* (Canon of Medicine, c. 1025 CE)

Ibn Sina (Avicenna, 980–1037 CE), the Persian polymath whose *Canon* became the dominant medical textbook of the Islamic world and medieval Europe, classified substances according to their temperament: the interaction of the primary qualities hot, cold, moist, and dry in four degrees of intensity. Within this system, ginger is characterised as a heating, drying agent used to correct cold and moist conditions of the stomach and to strengthen digestion. The *Canon* identifies ginger as an agent that — administered in conditions arising from cold — warms, fortifies weak digestion, and disperses flatulence.[8] Its temperamental action exemplifies Avicenna's principle that drugs of opposite temperament correct imbalanced states; ginger's heating quality addresses cold-dominated pathologies.

## 3. Convergence Analysis

Across all four traditions, ginger occupies a stable conceptual position: it is a warming, digestive-stimulating substance indicated for cold-type gut disorders and nausea. The convergence is notable: Greek humoral medicine (warm and moist → corrects cold), Galenic-Avicennian Unani (heating temperament, stomachic tonic), TCM (releases exterior cold, warms middle Jiao), and Tibetan Sowa Rigpa (heating potency, counters phlegm-cold) independently arrive at the same primary application. This polycultural coherence across traditions without documented cross-contamination strengthens the prior probability that ginger's gastric and antiemetic effects are pharmacologically real — a hypothesis borne out by modern mechanistic research.

The fresh-versus-dried distinction, formalised most explicitly in TCM, is implicitly present in other traditions: Dioscorides distinguished between freshly gathered and dried specimens of several spice plants; Avicennian pharmacopoeia acknowledged that drying alters a substance's degree of quality. Only the Chinese system, however, encoded this as two separate pharmacopoeial entries with distinct channel tropisms and indications.

## 4. Phytochemistry & Composition

The rhizome contains three principal classes of bioactive compounds:

**Gingerols** (primarily 6-gingerol, with 8- and 10-gingerol homologues): the major pungent principles of fresh ginger, accounting for a substantial fraction of the rhizome's resin. Gingerols are thermolabile vanillyl ketones.[1]

**Shogaols** (primarily 6-shogaol, with 8- and 10-shogaol homologues): formed by dehydration of gingerols during drying or heating. 6-shogaol is quantitatively and qualitatively the dominant bioactive in dried ginger and has greater in vitro potency than its gingerol precursor across multiple assays. This conversion chemically explains why *Gān Jiāng* is classified as hotter and more interior-acting than *Shēng Jiāng*.[1]

**Zingerone**: a degradation product of gingerols formed at high heat (cooking); lower pungency than gingerols or shogaols.

**Volatile oils**: zingiberene, bisabolene, and related sesquiterpenes; responsible for aroma and contribute mild carminative effects.

A 2024 review in *Chinese Medicine* (Springer Nature / PMC) characterised the pharmacological divergence of fresh and dried ginger as arising directly from the shifting ratio of gingerols to shogaols — all types sharing fundamental components but differing in relative strength and clinical inclination.[1]

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**5-HT3 receptor antagonism (antiemetic):** Ginger constituents — particularly 6-gingerol, 6-shogaol, and zingerone — act as non-competitive antagonists of 5-HT3 receptors on enteric neurons, inhibiting serotonin-triggered emetic signalling along vagal afferents to the brainstem. This mechanism is distinct from (and potentially additive to) the competitive 5-HT3 antagonism of ondansetron.[9] The 5-HT3R/Ca²⁺/CaMKII/ERK1/2 signalling cascade has been identified as a key downstream pathway.[10]

**COX-2 inhibition (anti-inflammatory):** 10-shogaol, 8-shogaol, and 10-gingerol inhibit COX-2 in vitro, with 10-shogaol exhibiting the greatest activity (IC₅₀ approximately 7.5 μM). Additional signalling effects include inhibition of NF-κB and MAPK pathways, and activation of the cytoprotective Nrf2/HO-1 pathway.[11]

**Gastric motility:** Ginger modulates cholinergic M3 receptors in addition to 5-HT4 receptors, elevating gastric tone and accelerating gastric emptying — the prokinetic mechanism complementary to its antiemetic action.[9]

**LOX pathway inhibition:** Dual COX/LOX inhibition has been proposed to account for ginger's analgesic effects in musculoskeletal pain, including via thromboxane synthetase inhibition.[12]

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**Pregnancy-associated nausea and vomiting (strongest evidence):** A 2014 systematic review and meta-analysis (Viljoen et al., *Nutrition Journal*, PMC3995184) encompassing 12 RCTs with 1,278 pregnant women found that ginger significantly improved nausea symptoms compared to placebo. Vomiting episodes showed a trend toward reduction but did not reach statistical significance across all trials. A subgroup analysis favoured doses below 1,500 mg/day. Safety: no significant increase in spontaneous abortion risk compared to placebo or vitamin B₆.[13] A 2024 umbrella review of existing meta-analyses (PMC11536023) broadly confirmed this evidence base.[14]

**Chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (mixed evidence):** A 2022 systematic review of 23 RCTs (PMC9739555) found that ginger supplementation ≤1 g/day for >3 days reduced the likelihood of acute vomiting by approximately 60% compared to control. However, a 2019 systematic review of five RCTs (n=872; PMID 31519467) found no statistically significant benefit for control of acute nausea or vomiting incidence. Heterogeneity across trials (dosing, concurrent antiemetic regimen, cancer type) precludes a definitive conclusion. Clinical adoption as adjunct is plausible but evidence remains inconsistent.[15, 16]

**Osteoarthritis pain (modest, heterogeneous evidence):** A 2015 meta-analysis of randomised placebo-controlled trials (*Osteoarthritis and Cartilage*, PMID 25300574) found ginger modestly efficacious in reducing pain and improving physical function in knee and hip osteoarthritis. A subsequent PRISMA systematic review reached more cautious conclusions, citing heterogeneity and poor methodological quality in the underlying trials.[17] Effect size is small to moderate; ginger is not a replacement for standard analgesics.

**Other indications** (dysmenorrhoea, dyspepsia, delayed gastric emptying): evidence exists from individual RCTs but awaits robust meta-analytic confirmation. These are noted but not graded here.

## 7. Phenomenological Record

Across all four traditions, practitioners and patients consistently report a rapid onset of warmth radiating from the epigastrium following ingestion of fresh or dried ginger, particularly in decoction. In TCM clinical texts, this sensation is taken as evidence of the substance "entering the middle Jiao" and mobilising Yang Qi. Dioscorides' correspondents described ginger as producing a distinct warming sensation in the stomach distinct from simple spice-induced irritation. In Tibetan medical teaching, the heating potency (*nus pa drod*) of ginger is considered reliably perceptible to the trained practitioner examining the patient's response.

The phenomenological overlap with the phytochemical finding is notable: TRPV1 (transient receptor potential vanilloid 1) activation by gingerols and shogaols — the same receptor activated by capsaicin — is the likely molecular basis for the warming perception reported uniformly across traditions. This creates a rare correspondence between culturally encoded sensory description and identified receptor pharmacology.

## 8. Practical Application

**Forms:** Fresh rhizome (culinary and medicinal); dried rhizome powder (encapsulated or decoction); standardised extract (typically standardised to gingerol content, 100–1,000 mg per dose); ginger tea (fresh or dried slice infusion); topical preparations (limited evidence).

**Dosing:** Clinical trials for nausea typically used 500–1,500 mg/day of powdered rhizome or equivalent extract, often in divided doses. Meta-analytic data favour doses below 1,500 mg/day for pregnancy nausea; the 2022 CINV review identified benefit at ≤1 g/day.[13, 15] Standardisation note: a 2024 industry survey found that 37% of products labelled as standardised extracts contained less than 50% of claimed gingerol content — third-party verification (USP, NSF, ConsumerLab) is advisable for therapeutic use.

**Drug interaction caution — anticoagulants:** In vitro data consistently show that gingerols inhibit thromboxane synthetase and reduce platelet aggregation. Human clinical evidence is conflicting: one small pharmacokinetic trial found no significant change in INR when ginger was co-administered with warfarin (Jiang et al., *British Journal of Clinical Pharmacology*, 2005); a case report documented INR elevation >10 in a patient stabilised on coumarin who added ginger supplementation.[18] A PLOS ONE systematic review of platelet aggregation studies (2015, journal.pone.0141119) concluded that despite consistent in vitro effects, human evidence is inconsistent. Clinical guidance: patients on warfarin, antiplatelet agents, or pre-operatively (standard advice: discontinue 2 weeks before surgery) should be informed of theoretical potentiation risk. The interaction is plausible but not established at culinary doses.[18, 19]

## 9. Spiritual & Esoteric Layer

In TCM cosmology, *Gān Jiāng*'s capacity to "rescue devastated Yang" (*huí yáng jiù nì*, 回阳救逆) is among the most potent warming actions attributed to any herb in the canon — a Yang-restoring, life-affirming function in a system where Yang represents activity, transformation, and generative force. Its channel tropism to the Heart in this context reflects the view that collapsed Yang in extremis threatens cardiac function. The attribution is framework-internal and not a mechanistic claim.

In the Galenic-Avicennian humoral tradition, ginger's heating quality places it on the side of summer, fire, and choler (yellow bile) — the qualities associated with the choleric temperament. Its application to correct cold-phlegmatic conditions (winter, water, phlegm) situates it within a cosmological polarity in which the physician restores balance between elemental forces, not merely between chemical states.

In Tibetan Buddhist medicine, substances are understood to act within a framework where all illness arises from the "three poisons" — ignorance, attachment, aversion — as they express through the three nyes pa (rLung, mKhris pa, Beken). Ginger's action on Beken (the phlegm-cold humour, associated with attachment and earth/water elements) frames its use within a therapeutic and spiritual economy simultaneously.

These attributions are presented as tradition-internal framings; they are not empirical claims.

## 10. Functional–Anatomical Correlate

Ginger's primary modern functional correlate is the **gastrointestinal tract**, specifically the enteric nervous system and its communication with the brainstem via vagal afferents. Its best-evidenced clinical application — antiemetic and antinauseant activity — operates through 5-HT3 receptor antagonism at the level of the gut-brain axis, with secondary action via enhanced gastric motility (M3 and 5-HT4 modulation). The antiemetic mechanism targets the same receptor class as pharmaceutical antiemetics (ondansetron, granisetron), which partly explains its consistent cross-traditional identification as a remedy for nausea and vomiting regardless of aetiology.

A secondary correlate is **musculoskeletal / inflammatory tissue** via COX-2 and LOX inhibition, though clinical evidence here is less consistent than the antiemetic data.

The warming sensation reported universally across traditions localises precisely to the epigastrium — the gastric bed — consistent with TRPV1 activation in the gastric mucosa and upper GI tract. This makes ginger unusual among traditional botanical medicines in that its subjective phenomenology maps with reasonable precision onto its primary site of molecular action.
