# Rotted Cereals — "Rotted Cereals" (Bryan 1930, English rendering of the Papyrus Ebers)

## *A cereal preparation that Bryan's English calls "Rotted Cereals"; the Egyptian word is not given in the text on disk, no botanical identification is printed, and the grain is not named. The identification is unresolved, at low confidence: the label describes a state of decay or fermentation of cereal grain and nothing more. It is kept as a row of its own because the text names it as an item, not as a plant.* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Ebers papyrus among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry, and the text is a modern English rendering, not the hieratic: Bryan's 1930 translation, `Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` (the running heads read "The Papyrus Ebers"). The name index (`Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv`, line 50) holds the row "Rotted Cereals, Bryan 1930, Ch. VI Plant Remedies (line 1259)", with the note "Bryan's English identification". The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder; a first-pass check called it "Unidentified (EP descriptive label)".

**Egyptian (EP).**

*The list (Ch. VI, "Plant Remedies", line 1259).* Bryan opens the chapter by saying that "of the recognizable Plants, and of their preparations, there is a not inconsiderable showing as the following list will reveal" (line 1233). "Rotted Cereals" is printed as a head word in the alphabetical list, between "Cedar-tree" (with its fat, splinters and tops) and "Chopped-up Chaff" (line 1260), which is printed on its own line. No Egyptian word and no binomial is given for it in the list. Bryan's own caution on the whole list is that many other substances are "completely hidden from us under the guise of meaningless words" (same passage, line 1233).

*The one use in the text (line 2718).* In the discussion of skin disease, Bryan describes the treatment of "Eczema-of-the-Head" as "particularly troublesome": on the first day the head was painted with a "lotion" of durra-meal and Fruit-of-the-Dompalm, warmed in Soft Fat, and bound up; on the second day it was anointed with Fish-oil; on the third with Hippopotamus Oil; on the fourth with abra-oil. Then: "After this course of intensive treatment the offending head, if the Eczema still persisted, was smeared daily with Bread-meal and dressed with 'Rotted Cereals.'" So the material is used as an external dressing, the last step in a graded series. The text does not say how the cereal was rotted, which grain it was, how long it was applied, or whether the passage corresponds to one prescription of the papyrus or is Bryan's summary of several.

*What the text does not say.* No other occurrence of the phrase was found in the Bryan text. The adjacent head "Chopped-up Chaff" is used in a different remedy (line 2142, a poultice "soaked in Water" for a squashed leg, which Bryan calls "the first Bran Poultice") and is not part of this entry.

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent, and the material is not specified, so no composition is stated. In general terms, cereal grain that has rotted or fermented carries the products of microbial growth (organic acids, alcohol, yeasts and moulds). Which of these, if any, mattered to the Egyptian physician is not stated. No analysis was read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** A dressing for Eczema-of-the-Head, applied after other treatments had failed (Bryan, line 2718).
- **What the text does not say.** The grain, the method of rotting, the duration, or any effect.

The Codex does not recommend applying rotted grain to the skin. Mouldy or rotting grain can carry moulds and bacteria and should not be put on broken skin. Persistent skin disease of the scalp should be seen by a clinician.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives the use (a dressing, after Bread-meal had been smeared on the head) and no preparation beyond the name. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Rotting or fermentation of cereal grain involves the growth of microorganisms, and some moulds on stored grain produce toxins. Whether the Egyptian use had any effect on eczema is not known. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]
