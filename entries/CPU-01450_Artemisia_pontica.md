# Southernwood (Pontic wormwood) — Abrotonon, Abrotoninon (Dioscorides 3-29, 1-60, 5-62)

## *Artemisia pontica (the Dioscorides translator's suggestion in the SUGGESTED lines for oil of southernwood, 1-60, and for the herb abrotonon, 3-29, which also lists A. abrotanum)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). The binomials below are the translator's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification. **Named only by Dioscorides in this row.** The queue locus is the oil chapter 1-60; the plant itself is described at 3-29.

*1-60 ABROTONINON* (heading at line 4010). The translator's line: "Abrotonum foemina [Fuchs], Absinthium ponticum [Bauhin], Artemisia pontica [Linnaeus], Abrotanum mas [Linnaeus], Artemisia abrotanum, Abrotoninum — Southernwood — Oil of Southernwood". The text (from line 4012; a page break follows): "Abrotoninum is made as follows. Take eight pounds of the leaves of abrotanum [3-29] and eleven pounds five ounces of the oil aromatized with the ingredients that go into cyprinum [see 1-65], and having steeped them in oil a day and a night, strain it out. If you wish to make it last a long time throw away the first leaves, put in other leaves, steep them too, and strain it out. It is warming and good for closing up or hardness in the vulva, and expelling the menstrual flow and afterbirth."

*3-29 ABROTONON* (heading at line 6776). Translator's line: "Abrotonum foemina [Fuchs], Absinthium ponticum [Bauhin], Artemisia pontica, Abrotanum mas [Linnaeus], Artemisia abrotanum — Southernwood". Text (from line 6778 to the page break at 6791): "The female abrotanum is a tree-like shrub, somewhat white, the leaves with little in-cuts (like those of seriphium) around the branches, with a golden corymbus [flat or slightly convex inflorescence] on the top, full of flowers that display in the summer, with a sweet smell, and some strength, bitter in taste. This seems to be the Sicilian. The other (called male) is full of sprigs, with slender seeds like wormwood. It grows abundantly in Cappadocia, and Galatia in Asia, and Hierapolis in Syria. The seed of these (pounded raw, boiled, and taken in a drink of water) helps difficult breathing, hernia, convulsions, sciatica, difficult painful urination, and the stoppage of the menstrual flow. Taken in a drink of wine it is an antidote for deadly poisons. With oil it is an ointment for those who have chills. It drives away snakes scattered under [foot] or inhaled as smoke. Taken in a drink of wine it helps those who are bitten. It is particularly good for the strikes of the harvest spider and scorpions. It helps inflammation of the eyes applied with boiled quince or with bread. It dissolves pannus [opaque thickening of cornea with veins; eyes] pounded into small pieces and boiled with barley meal. It is also mixed in the composition of oil irinum [1-66]." Names printed: abutonon, absinthium, heraclion, cholopoeon, thelyphthorion, absinthiomenon, procampylon; "The Magi call it nervi palmae, some, cynanchites and the Romans, absinthium ponticum", and also femineus frutex and dulcis cubitus.

*5-62 OINOS KALAMINTHITES, GLECHONITES, ABROTONITES* (heading at line 10105). Wine of southernwood, with glechium and calaminth, "is made like that of thyme. Because they are urinary they are good for stomach disorders, those who lack appetite, and jaundice" (line 10113).

The introduction also names abrotanum among branching plants that "must be harvested when they are full of seed" (line 64), which is Dioscorides' only printed harvest rule for it.

**Identity.** The translator offers two binomials for one abrotanum, *A. pontica* and *A. abrotanum*. Only the first is this row. The text distinguishes a "female" and a "male" abrotanum and says "The other (called male) ..." without a clear description, so the two sexes may be two plants; the translator does not say. The English name "Southernwood" in the gloss is the usual name of *A. abrotanum*. No *A. abrotanum* row exists on the spine at the time of writing. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

*Artemisia* species are generally known to contain volatile oils and bitter sesquiterpene lactones. The composition of the plant of these chapters was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records a warming oil for the vulva and the menstrual flow, a seed drink for breathing, hernia, convulsions, sciatica, urination and the menses, an antidote and snakebite drink, an eye application and a urinary wine. The Codex does not recommend any of these. Several of the uses are emmenagogue uses, and the plant is not safe in pregnancy.

### Preparation & Dosing

Oil (1-60): eight pounds of the leaves with eleven pounds five ounces of the oil prepared as for cyprinum, steeped a day and a night and strained, with a second steeping with fresh leaves if wished to keep long. Seed (3-29): "pounded raw, boiled, and taken in a drink of water", or in wine as antidote. Ointment: with oil. Eyes: with boiled quince or bread; with barley meal. Wine (5-62): made as for the wine of thyme; the thyme method itself was not read here. No dose of the seed is printed. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Southernwood-group plants, like other *Artemisia*, are aromatic bitter herbs with a long history in folk medicine. That is a general statement. No study of *A. pontica* was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** 1-60 prints "warming" for the oil. 3-29 prints "bitter in taste", "sweet smell" and "some strength" for the herb.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers is the discipline of attention the cultivation spec describes: the plant's habitat (Cappadocia, Galatia, Hierapolis in Syria), how to tell female from male abrotanum by leaf, flower and seed, the harvest rule from the introduction (cut when full of seed), the oil's method (steep a day and a night, change the leaves if you wish it to last), and the many names. It records no use for the person's vital constitution and assigns no status. The identification is the translator's, who gives two binomials. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** The chapters give a cultivator a method of making a medicated oil and a rule for when to cut the herb, both exercises in the discipline of attention and neither a practice for the self. The uses printed are for named complaints. Nothing here supports taking it as a tonic.

### Contraindications

Southernwood-group herbs are emmenagogue in the record and are not for use in pregnancy. Dioscorides' uses for snakebite, scorpion sting and deadly poison are not advice, and delay of proper medical care for envenoming can be fatal. No dose is given.
