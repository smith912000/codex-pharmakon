# Blue deer (rishya) flesh — Rishya (Bhishagratna: "blue deer"; variant reading Riksha, "a bear"), venison of the Janghala group
## *The meat of the animal Bhishagratna renders "Rishya (blue deer)"; at one place he notes a variant reading "Riksha — a bear"; no binomial is printed* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The English is Bhishagratna's translation of the *Sushruta Samhita*. The animal is named in the meat group of *Sutrasthana* ch.46, "the Chapter which treats of food and drink" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` lines 4-6; `_sources/sushruta.csv` line 145, "Rishya (blue deer)"). The *Janghala* ("large-kneed") group, which the chapter counts among the most important of the high-ground meats (lines 403-415), is listed as "the Ena (black deer), the Harina (red deer), the Rishya (blue deer), the Kuranga (antelope), the Karala ..." (lines 413-417). The chapter gives no verse on the Rishya alone. What it says is a statement for the whole group (lines 426-429): "These species of venison have a sweet and astringent taste, are light, keen, pleasant (palatable), laxative, and diuretic in their effect. They subdue the Vayu and the Pittam."

The medicinal use that admitted the entry (`_nonplant/meats.csv`, row "blue deer (rishya) flesh", locus `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/uttara/SS_Uttara_ch50.txt` line 184) is the diet for hiccough. *Uttarasthana* ch.50 treats "the (symptoms and) medical treatment of hiccough". Under "Meat as diet" Bhishagratna renders (lines 182-189): "The soup prepared with the meat of S'allaka (porcupine), S'vadamshtra, Godha, Vrisha-damsa (wild cat), Rishya (D. R. Riksha — a bear) and Mriga (different kinds of deer, as well as of Kapota and Parávata (different kinds of pigeon), Lava and other birds should be taken lukewarm with the expressed juice of acid fruits and with Saindhava and a Sneha (clarified butter, etc.)." The "D. R." in the translator's note is, as far as the passage shows, a variant reading of the manuscripts; the chapter therefore carries a doubt about whether the animal is a deer at all. The passage is a mixed list in which the Rishya is one of several meats. [USER: verify]

Other places where the name occurs, which I opened:

- *Asthma.* The burnt skin of "a deer of the Rishya species", "taken with honey", is one of the drugs named in the asthma chapter, listed with the roots of the *Tala* palm and a pith that the OCR text renders unclearly (*Uttarasthana* ch.51, lines 199-202).
- *Poisoned plaster.* Against the effects of a poisoned plaster on the head, black earth treated several times with "the bile of a Rishya (a species of deer)", ghee and plant juices is applied (*Kalpasthana* ch.1, line 313).
- *A skin disease.* The kind of leprosy called *Rishya-jihva* is described as "rough" and as resembling "the tongue of a Rishya (Deer) in shape and colour" (*Nidanasthana* ch.5, lines 88-89). This names the disease after the animal's tongue and is not a use of the animal.

Black deer venison (*Ena*) is in `III_48_Black_deer_flesh.md` and antelope (*Kuranga*) in `III_42_Antelope_flesh.md`; deer blood is in `III_32_Deer_blood.md`.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, venison is lean muscle: mostly water and protein, with little fat, and a source of iron, zinc and B vitamins. If the animal were a bear, as the variant reading allows, the meat would be fattier, and bear meat can carry the parasite *Trichinella*.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records the Rishya's flesh as one of the *Janghala* venisons, light and palatable, and as one meat in a lukewarm sour soup for hiccough. That is food. The animal is not securely identified, and deer (and bears) are wild animals, often protected. The Codex gives no protocol and recommends no one hunt or buy one for this. Persistent hiccough can signal a medical problem and needs ordinary care.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparation the text gives is the soup for hiccough: the meat soup taken lukewarm with the juice of acid fruits, Saindhava (rock salt) and a *Sneha* (fat such as ghee) (Uttara ch.50, lines 182-189). The other uses give: the skin burnt and taken with honey (Uttara ch.51), and the bile ground into black earth for a plaster (Kalpa ch.1). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Lean game meat is a source of protein and iron. Undercooked wild game can transmit bacterial and parasitic infection. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Ayurveda (SS):** by the group statement of ch.46 (lines 426-429) for the *Janghala* list that names the Rishya: sweet and astringent in taste; light, keen, pleasant; laxative and diuretic; subduing *Vayu* and *Pittam*. The English prints no separate *virya* or *vipaka*, and no verse on the Rishya alone. The statement belongs to the group, not specifically to this animal.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed.* The Rishya is attested in *Sutrasthana* ch.46 only as a name in the *Janghala* list, and elsewhere as one meat in the hiccough soup (Uttara ch.50), its burnt skin in an asthma remedy (ch.51) and its bile in an antidote plaster (Kalpa ch.1). Those are food and drug statements. It is not placed in a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* chapter in any passage read, and the English prints no claim about *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* for it. The popular *sattvic*/*rajasic*/*tamasic* food lists belong to *Samkhya*-era and later material, not to Sushruta, and are not used here.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here makes this a cultivation substance. The entry rests on a name in a list and a line in the hiccough chapter whose own translator offers a bear as the alternative reading. A cultivator takes nothing from it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Conservation and law:** wild deer and bears are protected or regulated in many places; killing, possessing or trading in them can be restricted or illegal. The modern identity of the *Rishya* is not settled by the text. [USER: verify]
- **Hygiene and infection:** wild-killed meat carries parasitic and bacterial risk and must be cooked through; handling carcasses carries zoonotic risk.
- **Poison (Kalpa ch.1):** a poisoned plaster needs medical treatment; the bile-and-earth plaster is not a substitute.
- **Ethics and diet:** many people abstain from meat; the Codex recommends no animal food.
- **Pregnancy:** ordinary food hygiene applies; nothing is stated in the sources.
