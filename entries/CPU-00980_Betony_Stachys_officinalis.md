# Wood Betony — Betony / Betonica / Bishopwort / Purple Betony / *vettonica*
## *Stachys officinalis* (L.) Trevis. (syn. *Betonica officinalis* L.) | Family: Lamiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, SBJ
**Energetic Classification:** Warm and dry (Galenic, roughly second degree in the Arabic reception); drying, astringent, "opening" of obstructions
**Training Phase:** Phase II — Nervine Grounding (Kenetica: pre-sleep settling and dream-hygiene practice)

---

### Origin & Tradition

Betony is the great embarrassment of the herbal canon. No plant in the Western tradition was praised more extravagantly for longer, and few have less to show for it under a modern microscope. It is worth reading the tradition on its own terms first — and then reading the pharmacology, honestly, without rescuing anybody.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (AT).** The cuneiform materia medica of the Assyrian tablets contains a substantial roster of aromatic labiate-type herbs used as drying, astringent poultices and as "head" remedies, and betony has been proposed by several herbal compilers as one of them. That proposal should be treated with suspicion. Akkadian plant names cannot be mapped confidently onto Linnaean binomials in most cases, and the honest position is that no secure Akkadian name for *Stachys officinalis* is established. **[USER: verify]** any specific Assyrian attribution before printing it; this entry records the tradition tag as *claimed* rather than *demonstrated*, and treats the Mesopotamian layer as a general context of head-and-wound drying herbs rather than a positive identification. This is precisely the sort of claim the tradition inflates and the Codex must not.

**Dioscorides / Greco-Roman (DMM).** Here the ground is solid. *Kestron* (κέστρον) — also *psychotrophon* — appears in the *De Materia Medica* as a herb of substantial and diverse indication: it is given in wine for the bites of venomous creatures, for complaints of the liver and kidneys, for ruptures and cramps, for coughs, and as a general internal cleanser. What matters is the *shape* of the indication list, not any one item: even in the first century, betony is being described as a broad-spectrum internal remedy rather than a targeted one. Pliny, writing in the same century, is more effusive still — and it is in the Roman reception that betony acquires its runaway reputation. A whole Latin treatise, the *De herba vettonica liber*, was circulated under the name of Antonius Musa (Augustus's physician) and enumerated dozens of complaints betony could treat, from head pain to snakebite to broken bones. The number of indications is the diagnostic feature: this is not a drug profile, it is a panacea profile.

**Avicenna / Unani (SBJ).** Betony enters the Arabic-Persian corpus through the Greek transmission and is classed among the drying, "opening" (*mufattiḥ*) herbs — agents held to clear obstruction (*sudda*) of the liver and spleen, and to strengthen the head. In the Galenic-Unani calculus, this reads as a warm-dry simple applied against cold-moist stagnation: fluxes, catarrh, damp heaviness of the head. Note what the humoral logic actually predicts — a mild drying astringent good for watery discharge — and note how modest that prediction is compared with what the Latin West was claiming for the same plant at the same time.

**Anglo-Saxon and medieval (attested by transmission, not a separate Codex tradition).** This is where betony reaches escape velocity. The Old English *Lacnunga* and *Bald's Leechbook* prescribe *betonice* not merely as a medicine but as an **amulet**: worn on the body, hung in the house, gathered with ritual constraint, it protects against *nihtgengan* (night-walkers), monstrous visitations, "elf-sickness", frightful dreams, and the devil. Betony was planted in churchyards. The medieval Italian proverb — "sell your coat and buy betony" — is the folk summary of a plant that had become a moral object as much as a drug.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The doctrine of betony is a doctrine of *boundary*. Every one of its classical uses is a use against incursion: venom entering the body, flux running out of it, demons entering the sleeper, dreams entering the mind. The Galenic hot-and-dry classification and the amulet function are the same idea in two registers — betony *seals*. And this is where the Codex must do its real work, because there is a genuine, if humble, chemistry underneath the boundary doctrine, and it is not remotely large enough to carry the doctrine's weight.

What betony actually contains is a mild astringent-and-antioxidant profile: hydrolysable and condensed tannins, phenylethanoid glycosides (acteoside/verbascoside and the betony-associated betonicosides), iridoid glycosides (harpagide-type), hydroxycinnamic acids (chlorogenic and rosmarinic acid, the family signature of Lamiaceae), and flavonoids. Tannins precipitate surface proteins. That is a real, felt, immediate effect — a betony gargle *tightens*; a betony wash on a weeping graze *dries*. The doctrinal word "sealing" is an accurate phenomenological description of tannin astringency. The doctrine is not fantasy: it is a correct description of a small effect, subsequently generalised without limit.

The generalisation is the interesting part, and it has a mechanism. Betony is (i) abundant across European grassland, (ii) harmless in ordinary doses, (iii) mildly and reliably *perceptible* — bitter, drying, faintly settling — and (iv) embedded in ritual. A plant that is available, safe, does something small you can feel, and is handled ceremonially is the ideal substrate for reputational inflation: it can be tried for anything (availability), it never disconfirms itself by killing you (safety), it always "does something" (perceptibility), and its failures are absorbed by the ritual frame rather than counted against the drug (amulet logic). An amulet cannot fail a trial, because wearing it and not being visited by a demon is a success. Betony's panacea status is best read not as a pharmacological claim that turned out false, but as a *category error preserved by the structure of the practice*. That is the lesson of this entry, and it is worth more than the plant is.

### Active Components

- **Phenylethanoid (phenylpropanoid) glycosides** — acteoside (verbascoside), and the betony-characteristic betonicosides and betonicolide-type constituents. In vitro these are antioxidant and weakly anti-inflammatory. No human pharmacokinetic or clinical data in betony.
- **Iridoid glycosides** — harpagide and related iridoids; bitter, plausibly the source of the "digestive/hepatic opening" reputation via simple bitter-reflex stimulation of secretion.
- **Tannins** (condensed and hydrolysable) — the pharmacologically load-bearing fraction for the *only* uses that plainly work: astringent gargle, wash, and mild antidiarrhoeal.
- **Hydroxycinnamic acids** — chlorogenic and rosmarinic acid; abundant in the genus, and confirmed as the dominant polyphenols across *Stachys* species by LC-MS/MS (Benedec et al., 2023).
- **Flavonoids** — apigenin/luteolin glycosides typical of the family.
- **Betaine (stachydrine) and choline derivatives** — present in *Stachys*; historically invoked to explain "nervine" action, with no clinical evidence at herbal doses.

Notably **absent**: any potent alkaloid, any confirmed sedative-hypnotic principle, any identified analgesic constituent. There is no molecular candidate in betony equal to the reputation.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Betony is retained in the Kenetica corpus for two reasons, and the practitioner should know both.

1. **As a working herb (minor):** a bitter, mildly astringent, mildly settling infusion taken in the hour before sleep during Phase II. Its plausible contribution is small — a bitter digestive settling and a non-stimulating, ritualised pre-sleep cup. It is *not* a hypnotic and must not be sold as one.
2. **As a teaching case (major):** betony is the Codex's calibration standard for reputational inflation. When a substance's indication list grows without bound, that is evidence about the *transmission*, not about the plant. Any practitioner who cannot say plainly that betony's evidence base is thin is not yet reading the tradition critically, and will make the same error with a substance where the error costs something.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the modern literature on *Stachys officinalis* specifically is extremely sparse, and this is itself the headline finding.

- **Genus-level phytochemistry is real and consistent.** Benedec et al. profiled six Romanian *Stachys* species by LC-MS/MS and found fifteen polyphenols, with **chlorogenic acid the major component in every sample (1131.8–6761.4 µg/g)**, alongside meaningful flavonoid and caffeic-acid-derivative content, and demonstrated antiradical, antimicrobial and antibiofilm activity in vitro (*Antibiotics* 2023; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antibiotics12111644)). This supports a genuine antioxidant/astringent/mild-antimicrobial profile — a plausible basis for the *topical* and *gargle* uses, and nothing more.
- **The Lamiaceae context is confirmed but unremarkable.** A screen of 20 Lamiaceae species (including *Stachys*) found chlorogenic and rosmarinic acids to be the primary phenolics, with *Stachys* not standing out against *Salvia* on antioxidant capacity (Moshari-Nasirkandi et al., *Sci Rep* 2023; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-44337-7)). Betony is chemically an ordinary member of its family.
- **Clinical evidence: effectively none.** There is no body of randomised controlled trial evidence for *Stachys officinalis* in any indication. For contrast, the Cochrane review of herbal medicine for low-back pain assessed 14 RCTs across *Capsicum*, *Harpagophytum*, *Salix* and *Symphytum* — **betony does not appear at all**, despite its centuries as an all-purpose pain remedy (Oltean et al., *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* 2014; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD004504.pub4)). A herb cannot be exonerated by a trial it was never entered into; it is simply absent.

**Honest evidence grade: TRADITIONAL (in vitro support only).** Astringency and antioxidant activity: mechanistically plausible, chemically demonstrated in vitro, never clinically tested. Nervine/sedative action: **unsupported** — no controlled human data of any kind. Panacea claims (snakebite, ruptures, hepatic obstruction, protection against nightmares and "elf-sickness"): **null**, and in the amulet case not even the kind of claim that could be tested. The Codex's verdict is that betony's reputation is a fact about medieval Europe, not a fact about *Stachys officinalis*.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani (attested):** warm and dry; *mufattiḥ* (deobstruent), drying, head-strengthening; deployed against cold-moist flux and catarrh.
- **Greek (Dioscoridean, attested):** *kestron* — broad internal remedy; antidotal, hepatic, renal, antispasmodic.
- **TCM:** not attested. Betony does not enter the Chinese materia medica; no *xing/wei* assignment exists and none is invented here.
- **Ayurvedic (rasa / guṇa / vīrya / vipāka):** not attested. Betony is not a classical Ayurvedic dravya; assigning it a rasa would be fabrication.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy:** traditionally regarded as a uterine stimulant and avoided. On precaution, **do not use in pregnancy.**
- **Tannin load:** as with any tannin-rich herb, prolonged or high-dose internal use may impair iron absorption and irritate the gastric mucosa. Separate from iron supplementation by at least two hours.
- **Hypotension:** older herbals note a mild hypotensive tendency; caution in patients already on antihypertensives **[USER: verify]** — this is a traditional caution without controlled data.
- **No substitute for treatment:** given the complete absence of clinical evidence, betony must never be offered for any serious indication (pain syndromes, hepatic disease, neurological or sleep disorders) in place of an evidenced therapy.
- **Species confusion:** *Stachys* is a large genus, and market material is frequently misidentified (DNA-barcoding work in the Lamiaceae has repeatedly found *Stachys* substituted for other genera in commerce). Source authenticated material only.
