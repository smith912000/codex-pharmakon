# Garlic — *Allium sativum* L.

**Family:** Amaryllidaceae (formerly Alliaceae) | **Class:** plant | **Volume:** I · Plant | **Codex id:** CPU-00069
**Attested in:** Dioscorides · Avicenna · Ebers Papyrus · Assyrian tablets · rGyud-bzhi

---

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted binomial:** *Allium sativum* L. (Linnaeus, 1753)
**Family:** Amaryllidaceae
**Synonyms:** *Allium ophioscorodon* Link; *Allium pekinense* Prokh. (cultivar designations vary)

A perennial bulbous herb, domesticated from the wild Central Asian progenitor *Allium longicuspis* Regel. The compound bulb consists of cloves enclosed in a papery tunic; the plant is propagated vegetatively and has been cultivated for at least five millennia. It does not produce viable seed under standard cultivation.

**Historical names across traditions:**

| Language / Tradition | Term |
|---|---|
| Ancient Greek | *skorodon* (σκόρoδον) |
| Latin | *allium* |
| Arabic / Unani | *thūm* (ثوم) |
| Akkadian (Mesopotamian) | *hazannu* (attested in cuneiform herbals) |
| Ancient Egyptian | *shm* / reconstructed as related to the Coptic *ṡōm* |
| Tibetan (Sowa Rigpa) | *sgog pa* (སྒོག་པ།); white garlic *sgog skya* |

## 2. Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2a. Dioscorides — *De Materia Medica* (c. 50–70 CE)

Pedanius Dioscorides treats garlic in Book II, Chapter 152 under the name *skorodon*, noting the Roman term *allium*. In the Beck translation (2005, Olms-Weidmann), he records that garlic clears the arteries, is good against serpent-bite, expels intestinal worms, clears the voice, provokes urine, and is useful in coughs and asthma when boiled with oregano. He also notes that it disturbs the stomach, produces flatulence, causes thirst, dulls the eyesight, and provokes skin pustules if overused. External applications include rubbing on alopecia areas (combined with nard ointment) and treatment of skin eruptions with salt and oil. Dioscorides frames these effects within a broadly empirical, ingredient-functional register rather than a formal humoral schema.

*Primary source:* Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica*, trans. L.Y. Beck, 3rd rev. ed. (Hildesheim: Olms-Weidmann, 2017). See also Beck's commentary on Book II.

### 2b. Avicenna (Ibn Sīnā) — *Al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb* (c. 1025 CE)

Avicenna's *Canon of Medicine*, Book II (the *Mufradāt*, simple drugs), devotes a monograph to *thūm*. Within the Galenic-Avicennian humoral framework, garlic is classified as **hot and dry in the fourth degree** — the highest and most intense grade — meaning it strongly dissipates cold and moisture. Avicenna documents its indications as: expulsion of phlegm (balgham) obstructing the lungs; treatment of chronic cough and asthma; stimulation of digestion; promotion of menstrual flow (emmenagogue); expulsion of intestinal parasites; and topical use for skin conditions including vitiligo and alopecia. He cautions against use in those with bilious (ṣafrāwī) temperaments and in hot climates, as its extreme heat may exacerbate bilious conditions or produce headache.

*Primary source:* Ibn Sīnā, *Al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb*, Vol. 2 (Beirut: Dār al-Kutub al-ʿIlmiyya, 1999); English translation available via archive.org (see Sources). Secondary: PMC4469963 for Canon pharmacological framework.

### 2c. Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE, Egypt)

The Ebers Papyrus — a 110-column scroll containing approximately 877 prescriptions and among the oldest surviving medical documents — records garlic as an ingredient in compound recipes. According to Britannica and scholarly analyses, it is prescribed in formulas addressing respiratory complaints (given to asthmatics and those with bronchial-pulmonary disorders), digestive conditions (flatulence, constipation), haemorrhoids, and as a general vitality tonic. It also appears in preparations intended to expel pathogenic agents framed in the Egyptian cosmology as malevolent spirits. The papyrus dates to the reign of Amenhotep I and is held at the University of Leipzig.

*Source:* Ebers Papyrus, University of Leipzig MS collection; Britannica, "Ebers Papyrus"; Scanned commentary: *A Small Dose of Toxicology*, asmalldoseoftoxicology.org.

### 2d. Assyrian Cuneiform Tablets (c. 7th century BCE, Library of Ashurbanipal, Nineveh)

The Nineveh Medical Project (ORACC / University of Pennsylvania) catalogues the approximately 660 medical cuneiform tablets from the Library of Ashurbanipal (r. 669–c. 627 BCE) and related earlier Mesopotamian traditions. Garlic (*hazannu* in Akkadian) appears in these therapeutic texts among plant ingredients used by the *āšipu* (incantation priest-healer) and *asû* (physician). It is documented as an ingredient in compound medicinal preparations, administered both internally (in decoctions) and externally (in poultices). The specific indication contexts overlap with purging, respiratory conditions, and anti-parasitic use, consistent with the broader cross-traditional pattern. The Nineveh Medical Project notes that the Ashurbanipal tablets are largely copies of earlier Babylonian originals, extending the attestation back into the second millennium BCE.

*Source:* The Nineveh Medical Project, ORACC (oracc.museum.upenn.edu/asbp/ninmed/); Worthington, M., "Investigating the healing arts of Ancient Mesopotamia," *Research Outreach* (researchoutreach.org); Finkel & Geller, *Disease in Babylonia* (Brill, 2007) — note: specific garlic-to-*hazannu* identification is attested in Assyriology literature but full primary cuneiform text requires specialist access.

### 2e. rGyud-bzhi (Four Medical Tantras, compiled c. 12th century CE, Tibet)

The *rGyud-bzhi* (Tibetan: རྒྱུད་བཞི།), the foundational text of Sowa Rigpa (Tibetan medicine), compiled in written form during the twelfth century CE, addresses garlic (*sgog pa*) in its Oral Instructions Tantra (*Man ngag rgyud*). Within the tridosha-analogous framework of *rlung* (wind), *mkhris pa* (bile), and *bad kan* (phlegm):

- *sgog pa* mixed with bitumen (*brag zhun*) is indicated for blood and *mkhris pa* (bile) disorders.
- Combined with leadwort (*plumbago*), it is prescribed for cold *bad kan* (phlegm) and *chu ser* (lymphatic/fluid) disorders.
- Mixed into certain preparations it addresses *rlung* (wind/nervous system) disorders.
- White garlic (*sgog skya*) is noted as an ingredient rich in *bcud* (essence/nutritive potency).

*Source:* PMC7063332 (dataset of illness classifications from the Oral Instructions Treatise of the *Rgyud bzhi*); medicinetraditions.com Tibetan herb names database; Tibetan Buddhist Encyclopedia, "The Fundamental Treatise rGyud-Bzhi."

## 3. Convergence Analysis

**Independent agreement across traditions:**

- Respiratory conditions (bronchial, asthmatic): Ebers Papyrus, Dioscorides, Avicenna, Assyrian tablets.
- Intestinal parasites / digestive expulsion: Dioscorides, Avicenna, Ebers Papyrus.
- Topical application to skin conditions / alopecia: Dioscorides, Avicenna.
- General vitality / tonic / expulsive function: all five traditions.
- Antifungal/antimicrobial application: implied in wound and skin contexts across traditions.

**Divergences:**

- Avicenna's humoral grading (hot in the fourth degree) has no precise structural equivalent in the Egyptian or Tibetan schemas, though the energetic valence — warming, dispersing, drying — is directionally consistent across all.
- The Tibetan tradition introduces explicit doshic nuance: garlic's action differs depending on combination partner (bitumen vs. leadwort), implying context-dependent polarity.
- Dioscorides is the most explicit about adverse effects (gastric irritation, eye dulling, skin pustules); this caution is absent or understated in the Egyptian record.

## 4. Phytochemistry & Composition

The intact clove contains alliin (S-allyl-L-cysteine sulphoxide, C₆H₁₁NO₃S), a stable odourless precursor held in mesophyll cells. On mechanical disruption (crushing, chopping), the enzyme alliinase — compartmentalised separately in vacuoles — converts alliin to **allicin** (diallyl thiosulphinate, C₆H₁₀OS₂), the principal active compound responsible for the characteristic odour and most antimicrobial activity. Allicin comprises approximately 70–80% of initial thiosulphinate output but is unstable in aqueous solution, degrading rapidly to diallyl disulfide (DADS), diallyl trisulfide (DATS), E- and Z-ajoene, and vinyldithiins.

Other documented constituents: S-allyl cysteine (SAC), S-allyl mercaptocysteine (SAMC), flavonoids (quercetin, kaempferol), selenium-containing compounds, fructooligosaccharides (prebiotic substrates), and saponins.

Total organosulfur content represents up to 82% of the total sulfur fraction.

*Source:* Ansari et al. (2022), *Frontiers in Nutrition* (PMC9650110); Bayan et al. (2014), *Avicenna J Phytomed* (PMC4103721); Gonçalves et al. (2021), *PMC8362743*.

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**Antimicrobial:** Allicin forms disulfide bonds with free sulfhydryl groups of bacterial enzymes, inhibiting essential metabolic activity and compromising membrane integrity. Active in low µM range against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria including MRSA strains, and against fungal pathogens (Gonçalves et al., 2021, PMC8362743).

**Vasodilation / cardiovascular:** Polysulfide compounds derived from allicin (particularly DADS and DATS) act as hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) donors in vascular tissue. H₂S induces smooth muscle relaxation and vasodilation, activates ATP-sensitive potassium channels, and modulates endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS). This mechanism was established by Benavides et al. (2007) in *PNAS* (doi: 10.1073/pnas.0705710104), who demonstrated that garlic-derived polysulfides react with haemoglobin-derived thiols to generate H₂S under physiological conditions.

**Anti-inflammatory:** Organosulfur compounds suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β and inhibit NF-κB pathway activation.

**Antithrombotic:** Ajoene inhibits platelet aggregation; garlic preparations increase fibrinolytic activity and reduce platelet adhesion.

**Antioxidant:** SAC and allicin scavenge reactive oxygen species; preclinical models show protection against oxidative neuronal damage.

*Sources:* Benavides et al. (2007), *PNAS*; Ansari et al. (2022), *Frontiers in Nutrition* (PMC9650110); Gonçalves et al. (2021), PMC8362743.

## 6. Clinical Evidence

Evidence quality varies substantially by indication.

**Blood pressure (hypertension) — Moderate to good evidence:**
A 2025 meta-analysis (PMC12303591) of 12 RCTs in hypertensive populations found garlic supplementation reduced systolic BP by a mean of 8.1 mmHg (95% CI: −10.95 to −5.28; P < 0.0001) and diastolic BP by 4.3 mmHg (95% CI: −5.99 to −2.52; P < 0.0001). Effect appears specific to hypertensive individuals; normotensive subjects show no significant reduction.

**Lipids — Modest, inconsistent evidence:**
A 2024 meta-analysis in *Journal of Health, Population and Nutrition* (PMC11308468) found garlic consumption reduced total cholesterol (WMD −0.64 mmol/L), LDL-C (WMD −0.44 mmol/L), and triglycerides (WMD −0.17 mmol/L), with slight HDL increase. Effect sizes are statistically significant but modest; clinical significance is debated, and heterogeneity across trials is substantial.

**Antimicrobial (clinical) — Weak direct evidence:**
Preclinical data are robust, but controlled human trials for infectious endpoints are limited and inconsistent.

**Cancer prevention — Insufficient evidence:**
Epidemiological associations have been reported; no RCT evidence of sufficient quality supports cancer prevention claims in humans.

**Overall:** The cardiovascular signal (BP reduction in hypertensives) is the most consistently replicated human finding. All other indications remain insufficiently evidenced at clinical trial level as of 2026.

## 7. Phenomenological Record

Fresh crushed garlic produces an immediate, pungent, sulfurous odour attributable to allicin volatilisation. Oral ingestion of raw garlic in effective doses produces warmth and a burning sensation in the mouth, oesophagus, and stomach — consistent with its designation as a hot-natured substance across Avicennian and Dioscoridean accounts. Prolonged or high-dose consumption is associated with halitosis, cutaneous odour (via pulmonary excretion of allyl methyl sulphide), gastrointestinal discomfort, and — as Dioscorides noted — occasional skin eruptions.

Traditional accounts across all five systems emphasise a warming, penetrating, dispersing quality that is experienced physically as well as therapeutically described.

## 8. Practical Application

**Forms:** Fresh raw clove (highest allicin yield); garlic powder (variable allicin-release depending on processing temperature); aged garlic extract (AGE; allicin largely absent, SAC elevated; more stable); garlic oil (primarily DADS/DATS); enteric-coated tablets.

**Documented dosing ranges (supplement context):**
- Dried garlic powder: 600–1,200 mg/day in divided doses (range used across cardiovascular RCTs)
- Aged garlic extract: 600–1,500 mg/day
- Fresh garlic: 2–5 g (approximately one clove) per day

Note: allicin yield depends critically on preparation method. Crushing then allowing 10 minutes before cooking preserves more allicin than immediate heat application.

**Cautions and interactions:**
- Potentiates anticoagulants (warfarin) and antiplatelet agents; classified as a major-severity interaction in herb-drug interaction literature (Ge et al., 2014, *Evidence-Based CAM*, doi: 10.1155/2014/957362). INR elevation and bleeding case reports exist.
- Discontinue supplemental doses before surgery (antiplatelet effect).
- May cause gastrointestinal irritation in sensitive individuals; reduce dose or switch to AGE.
- Avicenna's contra-indication in bilious / hot-natured individuals has no direct pharmacological correlate established, but high doses can exacerbate GERD.

*Sources:* Ge et al. (2014); Drugs.com interactions database (drugs.com/interactions-check); Ansari et al. (2022).

## 9. Spiritual & Esoteric Layer

**Humoral / Avicennian:** Garlic is classified as hot and dry in the fourth (maximum) degree. Therapeutically this makes it a powerful dispersant of cold, moist, and phlegmatic conditions. Its extreme heat, in Avicennian reasoning, also makes it dangerous in hot temperaments and seasons, requiring careful constitutional matching (*mizāj* assessment) before prescription.

**Apotropaic / Egyptian:** The Ebers Papyrus positions garlic among substances that "rid the body of spirits" — pathogenic agency framed as supernatural intrusion. Egyptian medical cosmology did not cleanly separate ritual and pharmacological intervention; garlic served both functions. There are also secondary historical accounts (Herodotus, Histories II.125, though this requires caution as an exact figure) suggesting garlic was provisioned to pyramid workers — though the Herodotean passage is difficult to verify precisely and should not be taken as confirmed record without primary consultation.

**Dioscoridean/Greco-Roman:** No explicit spiritual layer in Dioscorides; his approach is empirical-naturalistic. Garlic does appear in folk-protective contexts in later Roman and Byzantine literature (as an apotropaic against the evil eye), but this is outside the *De Materia Medica* itself.

**Tibetan (Sowa Rigpa):** Within the rGyud-bzhi framework, garlic acts on the subtle energetic-wind (*rlung*) system and the fluid-accumulation channels. Its *bcud* (essence potency) in white garlic is considered nutritively dense. The tradition does not frame garlic as spiritually protective per se, but *rlung* regulation intersects with psychological and nervous-system states.

**Apotropaic (cross-cultural folklore):** Garlic's use as a protective amulet against malevolent forces is documented across Mediterranean, South Asian, and Eastern European folk traditions; this is a consistent ethnographic pattern but is distinct from the medical traditions addressed in the five primary sources above.

## 10. Functional–Anatomical Correlate

**Primary systems:**

- **Cardiovascular:** Antihypertensive (H₂S-mediated vasodilation, eNOS modulation); antithrombotic (ajoene, DADS); lipid-modifying (modest LDL and triglyceride reduction).
- **Immune / Antimicrobial:** Broad-spectrum inhibition of bacterial and fungal pathogens via thiol-reactivity of allicin.
- **Respiratory:** Expectorant and anti-inflammatory action in bronchial mucosa; historical indication for asthma and chronic cough consistent with mucolytic and antimicrobial effects.
- **Gastrointestinal:** Antiparasitic (intestinal helminths and protozoa in preclinical models); prebiotic substrate (fructooligosaccharides support colonic microbiota); carminative at low doses, irritant at high doses.
- **Endocrine/Metabolic:** Modest insulin-sensitising and lipid-modifying effects in diabetic populations (evidence limited).
- **Integumentary (topical):** Antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory activity relevant to skin infections and alopecia (limited modern clinical data; primarily traditional record).
