# Ushara alkali — Ushara-kshara, printed "Usharakshara" (as printed in Bhishagratna's Sushruta Samhita)
## *An alkali (kshara) of the Sushruta group of alkalis, printed "Usharaksliara" in the OCR of Sutrasthana ch.46 and "Ushara" among the salts of ch.42; the translator gives no gloss for it, so its identity is not fixed in the sources retrieved* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** The locus given for this entry is `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt`, line 2107 (Sutrasthana, chapter 46, "the Chapter which treats of food and drink", line 4; the same text is in `Bhishagratna_Vol1_djvu.txt`, line 21767). The chapter opens by linking food to bodily strength, complexion and the "albuminous (Ojas) principle" (lines 10-12), and then runs through groups of substances: the salts (line 2025), the alkalis (line 2087) and the metals (line 2121).

The sentence that names Ushara runs across a page break (lines 2100, 2107-2110): "The alkali known as Usharakshara is heat-making in its potency, and subdues the deranged Vayu. It tends to increase the shmy (mucous) secretions in the organs and is detrimental to the strength of the body." The OCR prints the name as "Usharaksliara" (line 2107), and "shmy" is a garbled reading of the translator's word for the mucous secretions. The sentence follows the account of the Javakshara and Svarjikakshara, and precedes that of Pakima and Tankana. So the text gives Usharakshara, on its own, three statements: it is heat-making in potency; it subdues the deranged *vayu*; it increases the mucous secretions and is detrimental to the strength of the body. As one of the alkalis it also shares in the group's opening statement (lines 2087-2095): the alkalis "cure abdominal glands, piles, mesenteric diarrhoea, and gravel and stone in the bladder", "should be regarded as possessed of digestant or stomachic properties", and "their abuse is known to have brought about cases of haemoptysis". The opening sentence lists four alkalis (Javakshara, Svarjikakshara, Pakima, Tankana) and does not list Ushara, which is introduced afterwards; whether the group statement applies to it is therefore a reading, not something the sentence says. [USER: verify] The statements about being "inflammatory as fire", reducing *kapha* and being "anti-spermatopoietic" are made of Javakshara and Svarjikakshara and are not carried over to it.

A second locus is Sutrasthana ch.42, the chapter on "the specific properties of flavours" (lines 3-5). Its saline group (lines 318-322) reads: "The different kinds of salt such as, Saindhaba, Sauvarchala, Vida, Fakya, Romaka, Samudraka, Paktrima, Yavakshara (nitrate of potash), Ushara and Suvarchika collectively form the Saline group." Here the name is "Ushara", without "kshara", and it stands among the salts. The chapter's account of the saline taste (lines 216-230) is a property of the taste and is not assigned to any one member of the group; it says the saline taste has "corrective (purgative and emetic) virtues", is "heat-engendering in its property", cleanses the internal channels and softens the limbs, and, if abused, may bring on scabies, swellings, "loss of virile potency", haemoptysis and other conditions. The translator's gloss of Yavakshara in ch.42, "nitrate of potash", differs from his gloss of Javakshara in ch.46, "Carbonate of potash" (line 2089); the two may be the same alkali under two spellings, and the sources do not settle it. [USER: verify]

A third passage in ch.46 (line 2082), among the salts, has a salt "quarried from a salt mine situated at the foot of a hill (Shailamulaja), or from a sandy (Valukelam) or alkaline soil (Ushakshara)", pungent in taste and helping the disintegration of *kapha*, "also called pungent (Katu) salt". That spelling, "Ushakshara", is an alkaline soil and the source of a salt, and the sources retrieved do not say whether it is the same as Usharakshara. The group name "Ushakadi" in ch.38 (line 274) also begins with a similar syllable; see the entry on *Ushaka (alkaline earth)*. [USER: verify]

A search of the Sushruta chapter files on disk for the name, in these spellings and in variants with apostrophes or diacritics, returned only these places (ch.46 line 2107, ch.42 line 321, ch.46 line 2082, and the references to the Ushakadi group). No recipe in the Chikitsasthana or Uttaratantra names Ushara or Usharakshara in the OCR. The text gives no place of origin, no method of preparing the alkali, no test of a true one, and no preparation.

No other tradition is tagged for this entry. The Dioscorides, Ebers (Bryan), Assyrian (Thompson), Shennong (Yang) and Tibetan texts were not searched for it.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent of Ushara. In general terms, the alkalis of Ayurveda (*kshara*) are alkaline salts, usually obtained by leaching the ash of burnt plants or from mineral deposits, and "Ushara" in the sense of saline or alkaline soil suggests a salt-bearing earth. The translator glosses two of its companions as carbonate of potash (Javakshara) and borax (Tankana), and offers no gloss here, so what it was chemically cannot be said from the sources. Alkaline salts in general are caustic or irritant to tissue in strong solution. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved say that Sushruta described Usharakshara as heat-making, as subduing deranged *vayu*, as increasing mucous secretions, and as detrimental to the strength of the body, and named Ushara among the salts of the saline group. The substance cannot be identified from the sources, alkalis are caustic and can cause serious harm when swallowed, and the text itself calls this one harmful to bodily strength. No part of the sources supports a modern recommendation.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources retrieved give no preparation for Ushara or Usharakshara: no recipe uses it, and chapters 42 and 46 give only its place and its properties. The lines read do not say whether it was taken by mouth, applied, or used in food. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Alkaline salts such as carbonates are irritants to the gut and to tissue, and strong alkali burns skin and mucous membranes; salts that draw water into the gut act as laxatives. There is no evidence that an alkali treats disorders attributed to deranged *vayu*. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Ayurveda (SS):** for Usharakshara the text prints a potency, *virya*: "heat-making in its potency" (line 2107), together with actions (subdues deranged *vayu*; increases mucous secretions; detrimental to bodily strength; lines 2108-2110). It prints no *rasa* (taste) for it; the saline taste of the chapter-42 group belongs to the group (lines 216-230, 318-322), and no *vipaka* (post-digestive taste) is given. The potency printed for Tankana ("sharp in its potency", lines 2117-2118) is not transferred. No other tradition is tagged.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed; with a caution.* Usharakshara is named in the food-and-drink chapter among the salts, alkalis and metals that Sushruta lists for the qualities they impart to food and body (chapter 46, lines 10-19, 2087-2110). That chapter links food in general to the *ojas* principle (lines 10-12), but it does not link Usharakshara to *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana*; the passage is not a *rasayana* (rejuvenation) or *vajikarana* (generative vitality) passage. The text's statement runs the other way: Usharakshara "is detrimental to the strength of the body" (lines 2109-2110), and the saline taste in the neighbouring chapter may cause "loss of virile potency" if abused (ch.42, lines 227-228), though that sentence is about the taste and not about this alkali. No *shodhana* (purification) of the alkali is described in the lines read. The *sattva*, *rajas* and *tamas* categories are Samkhya and are not in the passage.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator, and the text points the other way. Usharakshara is an alkali the translator describes as heat-making and as detrimental to bodily strength, with no recipe, no gloss and no dose in the sources retrieved. The honest use for a reader is to read the caution, to note that the name is unglossed and appears in three spellings, and to leave it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Caustic and irritant:** alkalis burn the mouth, throat and gut when swallowed in strength; do not swallow an unidentified alkaline salt.
- **Strength and constitution:** the text calls this alkali "detrimental to the strength of the body"; it is not a tonic.
- **Abuse:** the text reports haemoptysis from abuse of the alkalis (lines 2094-2095).
- **Mucous secretions:** the text says it increases them; any chronic cough, vomiting of blood or coughing blood needs medical assessment.
- **Salt restriction:** anyone told to limit sodium (heart, kidney or blood-pressure disease) should not take salts or alkalis from an unidentified source.
- **Identification:** "Ushara" and "Usharakshara" are Bhishagratna's renderings with no gloss; the "Ushakshara" soil of line 2082 and the "Ushaka" of ch.38 may or may not be the same substance.
