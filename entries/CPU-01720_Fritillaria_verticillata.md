# Fritillaria verticillata — shri.khanda, Shri khenda, dpal.gyi.dum.bu (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Fritillaria verticillata, the second of three names Clark 1995 prints for shri.khanda (after "Euphorbia sp." and before "Stewartia sp."); the entry's commentary describes a tree and a leafless plant* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under this binomial only by Clark.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk (including the three Bhishagratna volumes) for the genus name *Fritillaria* found no species name. Yang 1998 prints "Bei Mu (Bulbus Fritillariae)" at line 2666, which gives the genus only, so it is not attributed to this species. Clark's preface (line 83) says that more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance. Here the line prints three. The first, "Euphorbia sp.", is a genus only and so could not key a spine row; the spine took the second, *Fritillaria verticillata* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 119). The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 989).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, in the run that stands after the heading "(5) Resins" (line 879) and before the heading "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997); the file prints no "(6)" heading, so the section is unlabelled in the text read. It reads:

"shri.khanda: Euphorbia sp.: Fritillaria verticillata: Stewartia sp.: [has eight names. ‘shri.khanda’ means dpal.gyi.dum.bu (lit. ‘a fragment of the Lord’). It is a large, blue tree with spreading branches not unlike an old man. It has astringent and slightly bitter tastes, and the branches yield a white, curdlike sap which is melted to make the concentrate. The inferior type grows in Nepal and elsewhere, and is a rounded, leafless plant containing similar sap]. Castor oil seed and shri.khanda are drastic purgatives. [The latter also heals abscesses]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the brackets (commentary layer) are the eight names, the meaning of the name, the description of the tree and its sap, the tastes, the inferior type and the clause "The latter also heals abscesses". Outside them (root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the three Latin names, which are Clark's identifications, and the sentence "Castor oil seed and shri.khanda are drastic purgatives". The entry directly above it (line 988) is *dan.khra*, castor (*Ricinus communis*), and the sentence pairs "Castor oil seed" with *shri.khanda*.

The commentary describes "a large, blue tree" and an inferior type that is "a rounded, leafless plant" yielding a "white, curdlike sap". That is a description of a tree and of a leafless plant, and it names no bulb. Clark's first name is a genus only ("Euphorbia sp."), and the commentary does not say which of the three Latin names it describes. The Codex records the text and does not decide; the identity of this row is in doubt. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* Line 3427 reads, in part: "Dhenrog and Shri khenda strongly purges all disorders." In chapter 21, line 3661: "Arura, Dhenda, Dhong ga, Shri khenda, Dhurji, Thamu, Chum tsa, Ngonbu, Tronbu, Tsi takmo, Rechagpa and Chhu tsa are the group of medicines that are purgatives." The glossary lists the phonetic name "Shri khenda" (line 5775) with the transliteration "shri khan da", and the index lists "Shri khenda 206, 223" (line 7493). The glossary's scientific name for it could not be paired with certainty in this reading, and the running text gives no Latin name.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder found no entry on this drug under a *Fritillaria* name. I_1274 (*Euphorbia helioscopia*), I_1275 (*Euphorbia stracheyi*) and I_1276 (*Euphorbia wallichii*) quote the neighbouring "Shri khenda" lines, and I_1175 (*Rheum emodi*) and I_1259 (*Corydalis melanochlora*) also contain the name. I_1283 (*Fritillaria delavayi*) is a sibling *Fritillaria* row from the same texts. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Fritillaria* is a genus of bulbous plants of the lily family (Liliaceae) known to contain steroidal alkaloids, that *Euphorbia* (spurge) is a genus of plants with a white latex that irritates skin and eyes, and that *Stewartia* is a genus of trees of another family. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained, and the commentary describes a tree and a leafless plant with white sap, without naming a bulb.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that is a "drastic purgative" (Clark, line 989), that "strongly purges all disorders" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3427) and that stands in the group of purgatives (line 3661); Clark's commentary adds that it "also heals abscesses". These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial through Clark's second of three names, and they are not Codex protocols. A drastic purgative is a hazard and not a practice here. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

Clark's commentary says that the white sap of the branches "is melted to make the concentrate" (line 989). That is the only preparation the sources read give, and it is stated for the tree described in the commentary, not for any named *Fritillaria*. No part used, quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Tibetan terms ("drastic purgative", "heals abscesses") are not modern diagnoses or measured outcomes. The identity is open between *Euphorbia*, *Fritillaria* and *Stewartia*, so no study of any one of them would bear directly on the drug. It is a general, well-established fact that strong purgatives can cause dehydration and electrolyte loss.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 989) prints "astringent and slightly bitter tastes". No power or post-digestive taste is printed. The root-text layer prints the action "drastic purgative". Chapter 21 of the Men-Tsee-Khang text (line 3661) places the name among the purgatives.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it is a drastic purgative that also heals abscesses, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says it strongly purges all disorders. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the sentences cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and the link from this binomial to that framing rests on Clark's second choice among three names.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a drastic purgative in the Tibetan texts, with no dose, and the plant the commentary describes does not look like the one the binomial names. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning beyond the words "drastic purgative". Strong purgatives can cause dehydration and harm, the identity is open between plants of different families (some spurges are irritant or poisonous), and no preparation or dose is given. No plant or sap should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
