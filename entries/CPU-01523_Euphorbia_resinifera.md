# Euphorbia resinifera — Tithumalos (Dioscorides 4-165, the seven tithymals)

## *Euphorbia resinifera, the first binomial among the suggestions printed under the heading of the whole tithymal chapter ("Euphorbia resinifera — Euphorbium Gum Plant"); the chapter's own text describes seven kinds of tithymal and no gum* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed under the chapter heading, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The identity is weaker here than for most entries in this batch. The heading of 4-165 carries four lines of suggestions at once. The first begins "Euphorbia resinifera — Euphorbium Gum Plant" and continues "Euphorbia tithymaloides, Pedilanthus tithymaloides — Red Bird Cactus, Slipper Flower, Jew Bush". The others are "Tithymalus diffusis, Euphorbia spinosa — Hippophaiston Euphorbia — Devil's Milk, Spurge", "Tithymalus acutifolius, Euphorbia pithyusa, Tithymalus pithyusa — Pithyusa" and "Tithymalus peplis, Euphorbia peplis — Wild Purslane, Petty Spurge, Purple Spurge". These are listed at the head of the chapter and are not tied to any one of the seven kinds. The seven sub-chapters then carry their own suggestions (*Euphorbia characias*, *myrsinites*, *cyparissias*, *helioscopia*, *paralias*, *dendroides* and *platyphyllos*), and *E. resinifera* is not among them. The entry therefore stands for the chapter as a whole under the translators' first-listed binomial, and it does not claim that Dioscorides' tithymals include *E. resinifera*. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-165. TITHUMALOS* at line 9515, suggestions at 9516 to 9519, the note "Tithumalos — milky white juice" at 9520, the opening list at 9521, and the seven sub-chapters *4-165a* to *4-165g* from line 9522 to line 9555. The chapter stands in Book Four, "Other Herbs & Roots", after 4-164 *KROTON E KIKI* (castor) and before 4-166 *PITUOUSA*. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 743.

The opening reads: "There are seven kinds of tithymal: the male is called characias, comatus, or amygdaloides, or else it is called gobius. Another is called female, myrtites, caryites, or myrsinites; the third, paralius, or tithymalis; the fourth, helioscopius; the fifth, cyparissias; the sixth, dendroides; and the seventh, platyphyllos." The kinds are then described one by one, from the male (characias, line 9522) to the seventh (platyphyllos, line 9553).

The first kind is the fullest. Characias has "red stalks over a foot high full of sharp white juice", olive-like leaves, a thick woody root, and seed in "hollow cases like basins or little hives", growing "in rough hilly places". "Twenty grains of the juice (taken with posca [hot drinks]) purges the bowels below, expelling phlegm and bile. With honey and water it induces vomiting." The juice is collected "about the time of vintage". The collector "must not stand against the wind, nor put his hands to his eyes", and before juicing "must rub his body with grease or oil and wine — especially the face, neck and scrotum". Because "it irritates a sore throat", the pills are wrapped "in wax or boiled honey". "Two or three dried impregnated figs (taken) are sufficient to purge." Externally the new juice "takes off hair", is put into tooth cavities (with the teeth covered in wax to protect the throat and tongue), and "takes away protruding and hanging warts, warty abnormal growths, and lichen", and is "good for pterygium ... carbuncles ... rapidly spreading ulcers, gangrene, and fistulas". The seed is gathered in autumn, dried in the sun and stored, and leaves are dried the same way. "Half an acetabulum [vinegar cruet] of both the seed and the leaves (given to drink) does the same things as the juice." "One teaspoonful of the root (sprinkled upon with honey water and taken as a drink) purges through the belly", and the root boiled with vinegar and used as a wash "helps toothache".

The other kinds are shorter. The female (*myrsinites*) is "similar to daphnoeides", bears a nut-like fruit every second year, and has "similar uses to that above, yet that is stronger for a vomit". The cypress spurge "has the same properties as those above". The helioscopius has "the same properties as those above but are not as forcible", and its seed heads follow the sun's course, from which it is named. *Paralios* grows in maritime places and its "use and storage ... is like those above". *Dendrites* grows on rocks and "works like that mentioned above". The seventh, *platyphyllos*, is "like verbascum [4-104]": "The roots and leaves purge watery matter through the bowels. Pounded and diluted with water it kills fish and those mentioned above do the same."

*The gum.* Nothing in 4-165 describes a gum or a Libyan tree. The resin the translators call "Euphorbium Gum Plant" is described in the separate chapter *3-96. EUPHORBION* (line 7412), which cross-refers to 4-165 ("see tithymal 4-165 a-f, also 4-170"). That chapter is read in I_1083 and is not merged here. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

No analysis of the plants Dioscorides describes was read. It is well established that members of the genus *Euphorbia* have a milky latex that is irritant to skin and eyes, and the chapter's own heading note is "milky white juice". Nothing further is stated here about the composition of these plants or of the resin of *E. resinifera*. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a drastic purge and emetic, a depilatory and a caustic for warts, ulcers and growths, and a toothache remedy. The Codex gives no protocol. The text itself warns the gatherer to stand away from the wind, keep his hands from his eyes and protect his face, neck and scrotum with grease, which shows how irritant the juice is. Nothing here supports taking or applying any spurge juice.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives, for the first kind: "Twenty grains of the juice (taken with posca [hot drinks])"; the juice "with honey and water" as an emetic; the juice dropped into dried figs ("three or four drops"), "Two or three dried impregnated figs (taken)"; pills of the juice pounded alone, or mixed with ervum meal, "the size of ervum [seed]", wrapped in wax or boiled honey; "Half an acetabulum [vinegar cruet] of both the seed and the leaves"; and "One teaspoonful of the root". The seed is gathered in autumn and the leaves are dried the same way. The other kinds use "the same" or "similar" amounts by reference, and none is given. No dose is given for the kinds after the first. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identification of the chapter with *E. resinifera* is only a heading-level suggestion, so a modern fact about that species would not by itself bear on Dioscorides' seven tithymals. It is a general, well-established fact that spurge latex is a skin and eye irritant and that the plants are poisonous if eaten.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints "sharp white juice" for the first kind and "milky white juice" in its heading note. It prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality. The actions printed are purging, vomiting, removing hair and removing warts and growths. The kinds are ranked by force: the female is "stronger for a vomit" than the first, and the helioscopius is "not as forcible".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. This chapter gives the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition in one of its fullest forms: the time of gathering (vintage for the juice, autumn for the seed), a method of collection that protects the collector (not against the wind, hands away from the eyes, the body greased), a way of keeping (jars, figs, wax or honey, the seed dried and stored), and a ranking of the kinds by force. It is attention to season, safety and storage, and it is not a cultivation status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with these plants. The lesson is protective: the one who handles the juice prepares his own body first and works away from the wind. The text offers nothing more, and no spurge juice should be handled on its strength.

### Contraindications

The chapter's own instructions show the hazard: the gatherer must keep out of the wind, keep his hands from his eyes and protect his skin, and the juice "irritates a sore throat". Spurge latex burns skin and eyes and mucous membranes, and swallowing it can cause violent vomiting and purging, so no part of these plants should be taken or applied. The platyphyllos kills fish according to the text. The identification is only the translators' heading-level suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
