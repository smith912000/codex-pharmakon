### Origin & Tradition

**Shennong Ben Cao Jing (SBJ):** Bǎi Hé — literally "hundred [scales] joined together," describing the many overlapping fleshy scales of the bulb — is one of the classical Yin-nourishing bulbs of the Chinese pharmacopoeia. Its earliest recorded medical identity is tied inseparably to a named clinical picture in Zhang Zhongjing's Han-dynasty *Jin Gui Yao Lue* (Essentials of the Golden Cabinet): **"Lily Disease" (百合病, Bǎi Hé Bìng)** — a syndrome of restlessness, mental disquiet, insomnia, a sense that one "wants to eat but cannot eat, wants to lie down but cannot rest," shifting bodily discomfort, and a bitter taste in the mouth. It is one of the earliest and most vivid descriptions in any medical tradition of what a modern reader recognises as an anxious, agitated-depressive, post-febrile or convalescent state — a residual disturbance of the spirit after the body's fluids and calm have been depleted.

**Classical synthesis:** Across later Chinese materia medica, Bǎi Hé settles as a gentle, food-grade Yin tonic: it moistens the Lung to quiet a dry, lingering cough (especially the residue of a heat illness), and it clears a faint heat from the Heart to soothe irritability, palpitation, insomnia, and "vexation." Formulas such as *Bai He Gu Jin Tang* ("Lily Bulb Decoction to Preserve the Metal/Lung") and *Bai He Di Huang Tang* institutionalise its dual Lung-and-Heart role. It is simultaneously a kitchen ingredient — eaten as a mild, cooling, moistening food — and a medicine, a status that reinforces its "gentle and safe" reputation.

**Japanese Kampo:** Retained as *Byakugō*, used along similar Yin-moistening, mind-quieting lines.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Bulb That Gathers Itself:** The name "hundred joined" is doctrinally suggestive: the bulb is an image of scattered pieces reintegrated into one body — precisely the therapeutic task in "Lily Disease," where the patient's spirit is fragmented, unable to settle into any posture or intention. Where Polygala (CPU-00250) *connects* Heart and Kidney along the vertical axis, Bǎi Hé *gathers and moistens* a spirit that has been dried out and scattered by heat — the restorative, fluid-replenishing pole of Shen-care. Modern work honestly supports a calming/antidepressant direction (below), but the human clinical evidence is thin; the strongest claim we can make is the traditional one, framed forward.

### Active Components

- **Steroidal saponins** (spirostanol/furostanol glycosides, including *Lilium*-type saponins) — a principal bioactive class studied for antidepressant-relevant and anti-inflammatory activity.
- **Regalosides** (phenylpropanoid glycerol glycosides, e.g. regaloside A/B/E) — characteristic constituents, altered by processing.
- **Polysaccharides** — associated with immunomodulatory and antioxidant activity.
- **Phenolics and trace alkaloids (colchicine-family alkaloids are a concern in some Liliaceae — see contraindications).**

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Bǎi Hé occupies the **`[SLEEP]`** node as the *moisten-and-soothe* option — distinct from Polygala's mind-connecting action. It fits the profile of restlessness and poor sleep arising from depletion and dryness rather than raw over-stimulation: the wired-but-drained state after illness, over-training, prolonged stress, or a dry irritating cough that itself disturbs rest. Its **`[RESET]`** role is restorative — replenishing fluids and quieting residual "vexation" during convalescence. As a food-grade agent it is one of the gentlest entries in this cluster, suitable where a stronger sedative would be excessive.

### Scientific Correlation

*Attribution: the following findings are from PubMed.*

Human clinical evidence for isolated Bǎi Hé is **effectively absent**; the modern literature is **preclinical / in silico**.

- Tan et al. (2025), *Combinatorial Chemistry & High Throughput Screening*, used UPLC-Q-TOF-MS with network pharmacology and molecular docking to probe the antidepressant mechanism of medicinal and edible lilies (*Lilium* spp., including *L. brownii*). They identified differential constituents (e.g. regalosides B and E, and a *Lilium lancifolium* saponin) predicted to act on core targets such as EGFR, HSP90AA1, STAT3 and TNF, and concluded the constituents act largely by inhibiting the inflammatory response implicated in depression. This is a computational/mechanistic hypothesis, not clinical proof. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2174/0113862073368704250131085339)

**Evidence grade:** Preclinical / computational only. The antidepressant and calming directions are biologically plausible and align with the ancient "Lily Disease" indication, but no robust human trials establish efficacy. Treat all mood/sleep claims as traditional and forward-looking.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM tier:** Tonify-Yin herb with a calm-Shen action. Flavour: sweet; nature: slightly cold. Channels: Heart, Lung.
- **Core actions (classical):** moistens the Lung and stops (dry) cough (潤肺止咳); clears Heart-heat and calms the spirit (清心安神); nourishes Yin and generates fluids.
- **Ayurvedic:** Not attested for this species.
- **Tibetan / Badianus:** Not attested.
- **Dantian correspondence:** Middle–Upper — replenishes the fluid/Yin substrate supporting the Heart-mind; a "moistening and gathering" rather than a moving herb.

### Contraindications

- **Cold/damp patterns:** As a cool, moistening Yin tonic, it is classically contraindicated in cold-phlegm cough, diarrhoea from Spleen–Stomach cold, or damp-heavy states — it may aggravate dampness.
- **Pregnancy & lactation:** Culinary amounts are food; medicinal doses `[USER: verify]` with a clinician — safety data are limited.
- **Species/identity caution — important:** Many *Lilium* and related Liliaceae species contain **colchicine-type alkaloids** or are otherwise toxic (and lilies are notably nephrotoxic to cats). Only correctly identified culinary *Lilium brownii* bulb should be used; misidentified ornamental lily bulbs pose a real poisoning risk. `[USER: verify]` botanical source.
- **Interactions:** No well-characterised herb–drug interactions established; theoretical additive effects with sedatives are minor at food-grade doses.
- **Toxicity note:** Correctly sourced Bǎi Hé is low-toxicity and food-grade; the risk lies almost entirely in misidentification.
