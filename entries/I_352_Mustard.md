# I-352: Brassica nigra / Sinapis alba (Mustard)

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary names:** Black mustard (*Brassica nigra*); White/Yellow mustard (*Sinapis alba*); Brown/Indian mustard (*Brassica juncea*)  
**Botanical names:**  
- *Brassica nigra* (L.) W.D.J. Koch — Black mustard (highest AITC; most pungent; traditional)  
- *Sinapis alba* L. (syn. *Brassica alba*) — White/Yellow mustard (milder; commercial condiment)  
- *Brassica juncea* (L.) Czern. — Brown/Indian mustard (intermediate; primary in Dijon mustard)  
**Family:** Brassicaceae (Cruciferae)  
**Synonyms:** Senape (Italian), Moutarde (French), Senf (German), Sørpe (Norse)  
**Traditional names:** Sarshapa (Sanskrit सर्षप — all mustard species); Khardal (Arabic خردل — the primary Islamic pharmacopoeial name); Jie zi (Chinese 芥子); Rai (Hindi — brown mustard); Rye (Russian горчица — gorchitsa)

**Botanical description (*B. nigra*):** Annual herb 1–2 m; pinnate leaves; yellow flowers; pods appressed to stem (characteristic "mustard" appearance), siliques 1–2 cm with 4–6 seeds. **Seeds:** 1–1.5 mm, dark reddish-brown to black, extremely hard — the traditional commercial product.

**Part used:** Seeds (primary — whole, ground, or pressed to oil); leaves and young shoots (food); pressed seed oil (topical + culinary)

## 3. Convergence Analysis

**Pattern:** Mustard's exceptional convergence (8/9) reflects several factors: Old World distribution (present from Mediterranean through South Asia to East Asia); distinctive pungent character immediately recognizable to any palate; simple preparation (seeds ground with water — immediate AITC generation); and a genuinely consistent mechanism (AITC → TRPV1 → rubefacient/secretomotor/antimicrobial) that produces the same effects regardless of the cultural context observing it.

The Islamic medical tradition's khardal accounts are particularly detailed: Ibn Sīnā distinguishes black mustard (hari sharsha) from white mustard (khardal abyad) by potency, recommends mustard plasters for pleurisy and pneumonia (AITC counterirritant increasing blood flow to chest wall), prescribes mustard seed for hair loss (scalp rubefacient), and recommends emetic doses for poisoning (mustard water as traditional emetic — supraphysiological AITC stimulating vagal afferents). Each of these applications maps to a pharmacologically distinct AITC mechanism.

The Ayurvedic tradition's sarshapa is similarly sophisticated: distinguishing sarshapa (brown mustard) for warming, digestive, and topical applications; the "mustard oil" (*Brassica juncea* seed oil) as a classic Ayurvedic massage medium with rubefacient and antimicrobial properties.

## 4. Phytochemical Composition

### Glucosinolates by Species

| Species | Primary glucosinolate | Isothiocyanate product | Pungency |
|---------|---------------------|----------------------|---------|
| *B. nigra* (Black) | Sinigrin → **AITC** | Allyl isothiocyanate | Very high (sharp, intense) |
| *B. juncea* (Brown/Indian) | Sinigrin → **AITC** | Allyl isothiocyanate | High |
| *S. alba* (White/Yellow) | Sinalbin → **p-OHBITA** | p-Hydroxybenzyl ITC | Mild (thick, warm) |

**Critical distinction:** White mustard's sinalbin produces a milder, less volatile product → less nasal pungency; black/brown mustard's AITC is highly volatile → nasal sting sensation. This explains why American yellow mustard (mostly *S. alba*) is mild while Dijon mustard (brown mustard) is sharp.

**Glucosinolate content:** 1.5–5.0% in seed (highest of Brassicaceae); AITC yield from *B. nigra*: ~0.5–1.0% of seed weight after myrosinase hydrolysis.

### Fixed Oil (seed oil)
- *B. juncea* oil: 35–40% erucic acid (C22:1); 25–30% oleic acid; 15% linolenic acid
- **Erucic acid note:** High erucic acid rapeseed oil was found to cause myocardial lipidosis in rats (1970s); this led to the development of Canola oil (*B. napus* low-erucic acid variety). Traditional mustard oil is NOT canola; high erucic acid content; EU/FDA limit erucic acid in edible oils; *B. juncea* oil labeled "for external use only" in some markets

### Alkaloids
- Sinapine (sinapic acid choline ester): bitter principle; antioxidant; minor pharmacological role

### Vitamins and Minerals (seeds)
- Selenium: exceptionally high (208 μg/100g — among highest food sources)
- Magnesium, calcium, iron, zinc: concentrated in seeds

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

### AITC — Primary (as per I-350 Horseradish; identical mechanism class)

**TRPV1 + TRPA1 Agonism:**
- AITC activates TRPA1 (Transient Receptor Potential Ankyrin 1) — distinct from TRPV1 (capsaicin's receptor) but with overlapping tissue distribution
- TRPA1 mediates: pain → sensory neuron activation → initial sensation → neuropeptide release → substance P + CGRP → neurogenic inflammation → increased local blood flow (rubefacient mechanism)
- TRPA1 also mediates: nasal pungency → sneeze reflex → nasal secretion increase (secretomotor mechanism for respiratory applications)

**Rubefacient mechanism:**
- Local AITC application → TRPA1 → substance P release → histamine release from mast cells → vasodilation + hyperemia → warm, red skin (classic mustard plaster effect)
- Heat sensation + counter-irritant → competing afferent signals → reduced perception of underlying musculoskeletal pain (gate control theory)

**Antimicrobial (as per AITC class mechanism):**
- AITC's electrophilic nature: reacts with bacterial cell wall thiols → membrane disruption
- MIC against common pathogens: 0.2–1.0 mg/mL range

**Emetic mechanism:**
- High-dose AITC (mustard water) → vagal afferent stimulation → chemoreceptor trigger zone activation → emesis
- Historical use as emetic for poisoning; now superseded by activated charcoal and ipecac (itself largely abandoned)

## 6. Clinical Evidence

### Musculoskeletal Pain (Topical Rubefacient) — Level B

- Mustard plaster (sinapism): traditional applied external therapy for pleurisy, pneumonia, arthritis, neuralgia, lumbago
- Cochrane systematic review (Mason 2004; primarily capsaicin but reviewed AITC class): topical rubefacients including AITC preparations — Level B for acute musculoskeletal pain; methodologically limited trials
- Multiple German RCTs of combination mustard/AITC preparations (Ratiopharm preparations): significant vs. placebo for osteoarthritis and back pain; Commission E approved for catarrhal disease of the respiratory tract and topical hyperemia

### Respiratory Conditions — Level C

- Traditional mustard plaster for pneumonia, pleurisy, bronchitis: pharmacologically plausible (AITC vapor inhalation → reflex bronchial secretion; topical → chest wall hyperemia)
- No modern RCTs specifically for respiratory mustard preparations; Commission E approvals based on traditional evidence + mechanism

### Digestive Stimulant — Level C

- Mustard seed (5–10g) with meals: bitter-pungent stimulation of gastric secretion; Commission E: approved for loss of appetite, dyspepsia
- Cholagogue effect: stimulates bile flow (similar to horseradish/other pungent Brassicaceae)

## 7. Phenomenological Record

### The Grain of Mustard Seed — Biblical and Pharmacological Symbolism
Matthew 13:31–32: "The kingdom of heaven is like a mustard seed, which a man took and planted in his field. Though it is the smallest of all seeds, yet when it grows, it is the largest of garden plants." The pharmacological accuracy of this metaphor is remarkable: mustard IS the smallest agriculturally significant seed of its era (1–1.5 mm); mustard plants DO grow to 1–2 m; and the disproportionate output from tiny input (concentrated AITC from minimal seed mass) mirrors the theological point.

Mustard seed is the only plant to appear in all three synoptic gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke); the phrase "faith like a mustard seed" has become one of the most universal religious-botanical metaphors in the Western tradition. The cross-cultural saturation of mustard as a potent-from-small-origin symbol reflects the plant's genuinely distinctive pharmacological property: the most intense pungency from the smallest seed volume.

### Dijon and the Mustard Trade
13th century: Philip the Bold of Burgundy granted Dijon the exclusive right to prepare and sell *moutarde* in 1382. The "Dijon" designation became the European gold standard for mustard; Dijon mustard is made with brown mustard (*B. juncea*) and verjuice (unfermented grape juice) or white wine, giving it its characteristic pungency and slightly fruity acid note. The commercial history of Dijon mustard mirrors the pharmacological story: brown mustard's AITC concentration determines pungency grading; historical Dijon trade was based on recognizing quality gradations that correspond to glucosinolate content.

### Mustard Gas — The Pharmacological Paradox
Sulfur mustard (bis(2-chloroethyl) sulfide — "mustard gas") shares NO pharmacological relationship with botanical mustard despite the name. The naming is coincidental (similar smell claimed by WWI soldiers; disputed). Mustard gas is an alkylating agent targeting DNA; it is classified as a weapon of mass destruction (Chemical Weapons Convention). This naming collision has generated confusion across the literature. The Codex explicitly notes: **mustard gas is not related to botanical mustard pharmacologically.**

### Mustard Oil and South Asian Massage
*Brassica juncea* seed oil (sarshapa taila) is the traditional massage medium of Bengali, North Indian, Punjabi, and Nepali traditions. The practice of sarso tel (mustard oil) massage for newborns is a 3,000-year-old tradition documented in Ayurvedic texts. The oil's AITC content (low — most AITC volatilizes during pressing) and its high erucic acid/oleic acid content makes it warming and penetrating. Research context: a 2006 WHO-funded trial in Bangladesh found newborn mustard oil massage associated with increased mortality (possibly from erucic acid transcutaneous absorption in neonates with immature skin barrier). The traditional practice and the modern safety signal represent an ongoing tension in ethnopharmacological evaluation.

## 8. Practical Application

### External/Topical Applications

**Mustard plaster (sinapism) for musculoskeletal pain:**
- Mix 1 part black mustard seed powder + 4 parts flour + warm water to paste
- Apply between two thin cloths; apply to affected area for 5–15 minutes MAXIMUM
- **CRITICAL:** Direct skin contact with fresh mustard paste → blistering; ALWAYS use cloth barrier
- Remove immediately at sign of significant burning; wash with cool water
- NOT suitable for sensitive skin, broken skin, infants, or areas with impaired sensation

**Ready-made mustard plaster preparations** (commercial): controlled AITC delivery; safer than homemade; follow manufacturer instructions

**Mustard footbath:**
- 1 tbsp black mustard powder in warm water footbath: reflex circulatory stimulation; traditionally used for colds, headache, fatigue
- Safer than topical plaster; less blistering risk

### Internal Applications

**Digestive stimulant:**
- Yellow mustard seed whole: 5–10g with meals; chew before swallowing
- Mustard greens: spring vegetable; significant glucosinolate content before bolting

**Respiratory support (steam inhalation):**
- 1 tsp black mustard powder in bowl of hot water; inhale steam carefully; opens nasal passages via TRPA1

### Food and Condiment Context
Culinary mustard (prepared condiment): significant health food properties in the diet:
- Regular consumption provides isothiocyanate exposure → NRF2 activation, cancer chemopreventive benefits (epidemiological evidence for Brassicaceae family)
- Mustard seed selenium: important micronutrient; 1 tsp mustard = ~10–15 μg selenium

### Safety Notes

**Internal:**
- **Gastric irritation:** high-dose internal mustard → GI irritation, nausea; use with food
- **Thyroid:** glucosinolates + TPO inhibition at high medicinal doses; monitor in hypothyroid patients
- **Pregnancy:** avoid medicinal doses of black mustard internally (emmenagogue risk at high doses); culinary amounts safe

**External:**
- **Skin burns:** most important safety issue; fresh mustard paste directly on skin = chemical burn risk; always use cloth barrier; limit exposure to 5–15 minutes; immediate discontinuation if burning
- **Infants:** NEVER apply mustard plasters to infants (thin skin; extreme sensitivity)
- **Erucic acid oil (internal):** regulatory concern in EU/FDA; limit consumption of high-erucic mustard oil; canola oil is the low-erucic alternative for culinary use

**Drug interactions:**
- Thyroid medications: separate by 2 hours
- Warfarin: vitamin K₁ in leaves (not seeds at culinary amounts)

### Energetic Classification
**Primary:** ◉ Qi (functional) — the dispersing, warming, penetrating, secretomotor, and digestive-stimulant qualities map directly to Qi circulation and dispersal in TCM (xin san = pungent dispersing)  
**Secondary:** None — mustard's pharmacology is primarily functional/surface Qi with limited structural (Jing) or neuromodulatory (Shen) applications
