## ⚠️ CATEGORY: DEADLY — STUDY ONLY

**No dose is given in this entry.** *Digitalis purpurea* is a lethal cardiac poison with a therapeutic window narrower than the variation between two leaves off the same plant. The folk names are not decoration: **dead man's bells**, **witches' gloves**, **bloody fingers**. The tradition that used this plant knew what it was, and still killed people with it — including under Withering's own careful hand. If a man who spent ten years titrating this drug on 163 patients could not make it safe, no reader of this book will make it safe with a kitchen scale. **Grow it. Look at it. Do not drink it.**

### Origin & Tradition

*Digitalis purpurea* is a plant of the Atlantic fringe of Europe — Britain, Ireland, western France, Iberia, Scandinavia — a biennial of acid soils, woodland clearings, hedge banks and burnt ground, throwing a rosette in year one and a metre-and-a-half spire of thimbled purple bells in year two. Bumblebees crawl into the bells. Children have been told for centuries not to.

**Northern European folk medicine — the genuine tradition.**
This is the attested lineage, and it is a real one. In Britain and Ireland, foxglove was a plant of the wise-woman's garden and the hedge-healer's repertoire: applied externally to ulcers and sores, and — critically — given internally for **dropsy**, the swollen, breathless, waterlogged condition we now mostly call congestive heart failure. The vernacular names carry the doctrine: the plant is beautiful, it is associated with the fairy-folk and with the dead, and it will kill you. Folk taxonomy encoded lethality in the name because that was the only labelling technology available. Note what this tradition did *not* have: a standardised dose, a stable preparation, or any way to distinguish a patient who would respond from one who would not. It had a plant, an indication, and a warning.

**Classical Mediterranean — Dioscorides (DMM tag): NOT SUPPORTED [USER: verify].**
*De Materia Medica* is a Mediterranean text. *D. purpurea* is an Atlantic-fringe plant of acid soils, essentially outside the flora Dioscorides catalogued. There is no identification in the DMM corpus that the codex can honestly map onto foxglove. The genus name *Digitalis* is Fuchs's coinage of 1542 — a Renaissance neologism from *digitus*, finger, for the thimble-shaped bell — which is itself evidence that the classical corpus had no name to inherit. **The codex declines to assert this attestation.** The DMM tag on this entry is a filing artefact of the Plantaginaceae grouping and should be corrected or removed.

A necessary qualification, honestly stated: the classical world *did* know cardiac-glycoside plants — squill (*Drimia maritima*) and oleander (*Nerium oleander*) are genuinely Dioscoridean, and squill was a genuinely classical dropsy remedy. According to PubMed, a state-of-the-art review notes that "some knowledge of plants with digitalis-like effects used for congestive heart failure was in evidence as early as Roman times" ([DOI: 10.1007/s40256-018-0292-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40256-018-0292-1)). That is a claim about *the class*, not about *this species*, and the codex will not let the two blur. Rome knew cardenolides. Rome did not know foxglove.

**Arabic-Persian — Avicenna (CM tag): NOT SUPPORTED [USER: verify].**
The *Canon of Medicine* draws on a Mediterranean-and-eastward flora. Foxglove is not in it. There is no reliable Avicennan indication for *Digitalis*, and the codex declines to invent one. **The CM tag should be treated as unverified.**

**The Shropshire folk-healer and the 1785 Account — the real inflection point.**
William Withering, a Birmingham physician, learned in 1775 of a Shropshire woman — a lay healer — who cured dropsy with a family recipe of some twenty herbs. Withering's contribution was not discovering the plant; the folk tradition had it. His contribution was **subtraction and discipline**: he reasoned that in a twenty-herb recipe one herb was doing the work, identified foxglove as the active member, and then did something almost nobody in 18th-century medicine did — he tested it and reported what happened, including when it failed.

*An Account of the Foxglove and Some of its Medical Uses* (1785) reports **163 patients** treated for various forms of dropsy. Withering reported the outcomes of **all** of them, classified them into **responders and non-responders**, and identified the responders as those suffering from heart failure ([DOI: 10.1701/1940.21084](https://doi.org/10.1701/1940.21084)). In an era when a treatment was judged successful if it *conformed a priori to the theory of the four humours*, Withering compared each patient's condition before and after treatment and characterised who actually got better — which the same source argues anticipates, by more than two centuries, the modern turn toward individualised response and personalised medicine. He also documented the toxicity, and appealed explicitly for caution in reporting a new medicine ([DOI: 10.1177/014107680710000316](https://doi.org/10.1177/014107680710000316)).

That is the whole of it: a folk remedy, an active principle isolated by reasoning, a dose established by titration, an outcome reported honestly including the failures, and a toxicity published rather than buried. It is arguably the first systematic clinical evaluation of a herbal drug, and it remains the foundation the field stands on ([DOI: 10.1016/j.cardfail.2009.08.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cardfail.2009.08.001)).

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.**
The doctrine is *dropsy* — water that will not move. The remedy moves it. The doctrine calls foxglove a diuretic.

The doctrine is right about the observation and wrong about the organ. The cardenolide binds and inhibits the myocyte **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase**; intracellular Na⁺ rises; the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger, which runs on that sodium gradient, slows its calcium export; intracellular Ca²⁺ rises; the contraction strengthens. Cardiac output improves, renal perfusion improves, and the kidney — which the drug never touches — performs the diuresis itself. The water moves because the pump was mended. Foxglove is a heart drug that a healer, standing at the bedside watching a chamber-pot fill, could only ever have read as a water drug.

And here is why Withering matters more than the mechanism. He could not have known any of the above — Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase was two centuries away. He had exactly the same wrong doctrine his Shropshire source had. **He got it right anyway**, because he stopped asking whether the treatment fit the theory and started asking who got better. The method rescued the truth from the doctrine. That is not a historical anecdote for this codex; it *is* this codex. Every entry in these nine volumes is an attempt to do to some other tradition what Withering did to a Shropshire recipe: keep the observation, audit the mechanism, report the failures, and refuse to let a beautiful explanation vouch for an unverified fact.

The symbolic layer completes the lesson. **Dead man's bells.** The tradition that could not explain the drug could still name the danger. Folk knowledge's failure mode is *mechanism*; its strength is *pattern* — and toxicity is a pattern. Trust the warning; audit the theory.

### Active Components

- **Digitoxin** — the principal cardenolide of this species; lipophilic, long half-life, hepatically cleared (contrast digoxin's renal clearance — a distinction with real toxicological consequences).
- **Purpurea glycosides A, B and E** — according to PubMed, LC-MS/MS profiling identified these as the major cardenolides of *D. purpurea*, against lanatosides A/C/E in *D. lanata* ([DOI: 10.1016/j.chroma.2020.460903](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chroma.2020.460903)).
- **Verodoxin and digitoxigenin fucoside** — unexpectedly identified as further major cardenolides in this species (same source).
- **Gitoxin, gitaloxin** — minor cardenolides.
- **Aglycone series** — digitoxigenin, gitoxigenin, gitaloxigenin, digoxigenin, diginatigenin (same source).
- **Digoxin** — present, but **not** the commercial source. Industrial digoxin comes from *D. lanata*. This is the single most-confused fact about the two plants.
- **Saponins, flavonoids** — pharmacologically trivial beside the cardenolides.

Every cardenolide above hits the same target: the α-subunit of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. The differences between them are pharmacokinetic — absorption, half-life, route of clearance — not pharmacodynamic. This is why "which glycoside" matters clinically and "does it work" does not vary.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**None. No Kenetica phase employs this plant.**

Its place in the curriculum is as the codex's **founding case study**, and it earns three lessons:

1. **The method lesson.** Withering is the ancestor of everything this book does. Folk knowledge → isolate the active member → titrate → report responders *and* non-responders → publish the toxicity. A practitioner who internalises that sequence has the whole method. One who reveres the plant instead has learned nothing.
2. **The "traditional means safe" fallacy, killed outright.** Foxglove has the deepest, best-attested folk tradition of any plant in this section of Volume I. It is also one of the most lethal. **Depth of tradition predicts nothing about safety.** If any single entry should break that heuristic in a reader permanently, it is this one.
3. **The identification lesson.** A non-flowering foxglove rosette looks like comfrey. People die of this. See Contraindications.

**Cultivator's note.** *D. purpurea* is one of the easiest and most rewarding plants in the British garden — and the codex encourages growing it, on its own terms. It is a biennial: sow surface-scattered onto firmed compost in late spring (the seed is dust and needs **light** — never bury it), prick out into a nursery bed, and let it make a rosette through year one. Year two brings the spire. It self-seeds prodigiously in dappled shade on acid, humus-rich, moisture-retentive ground — a woodland edge, a north-facing bank, the disturbed soil where you took a tree out. It is a first-rate bumblebee plant. Let it colonise; thin the rosettes.

**Handling:** wear gloves for deadheading and for pulling spent plants. **Never dry the leaf. Never bring the leaf into the kitchen. Never store it anywhere near culinary herbs** — the confusion casualties are almost always storage or foraging errors, not deliberate ingestion. If you have children, animals, or anyone who forages your ground: mark it, name it aloud, and make sure they know the rosette as well as the flower. **Do not compost the leaf into a bed you will grow salad in.** Grow foxglove as what it is — a spectacular ornamental with a lethal history — and the relationship is entirely safe.

**The honest summary:** the plant's *method-history* is grade A and priceless. The plant's *pharmacology* is grade A and belongs to a factory and a prescriber. The plant's *folk therapeutics* are a null. All three are true simultaneously, and pretending otherwise is how people get hurt.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Cold and dry in the Northern European folk framing the codex can actually defend: **slowing, contracting, downward-and-outward-moving.** It slows the pulse (real — vagal tone, AV nodal conduction), it strengthens and tightens the beat (real — inotropy), it moves water down and out (real, but secondary to the beat).

This is, unusually, a serviceable *phenomenology* wrapped around a false *mechanism*. Two things the energetic system could never encode are worth naming, because they are what the doctrine cannot warn you about:

- **Xanthopsia** — objects washed yellow-green and haloed. No energetic framework predicts a colour-vision disturbance, because none had a concept of retinal Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase.
- **Hyperkalaemia** — potassium leaving cells because the pump that imports it is blocked. No humoral system has a word for potassium.

**Where a doctrine has no vocabulary for a symptom, it cannot warn you about that symptom.** The gap between "cold and dry" and "yellow vision plus a serum potassium of 6.5" is the exact distance this codex exists to close. **[USER: verify]** any energetic tag before treating it as attested rather than reconstructed.

### Contraindications

**⚠️ ABSOLUTE — no ingestion, by anyone, in any preparation, at any dose, for any reason.**

- **All parts are toxic** — leaf, flower, seed, root, and the water they were soaked in.
- **Toxidrome:** nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, dizziness first — **and this phase looks like gastroenteritis and feels survivable.** Then confusion and **xanthopsia (yellow-green vision, halos)**. Then **hyperkalaemia** and brady- or tachyarrhythmias. Then arrest. In the documented outbreak, cardiotoxicity "developed later" — after the GI phase had already presented ([DOI: 10.1016/S1726-4901(10)70009-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1726-4901(10)70009-5)). **The delay is the trap.**
- **⚠️ COMFREY CONFUSION — the single commonest route to death by this plant.** "Comfrey (*Symphytum officinale*) leaves resemble those of foxglove when the plant is not in bloom." Nine people were poisoned by one batch of alleged comfrey tea (same source). If you forage comfrey: **only in flower, never from a rosette, never from a bed where foxglove self-seeds.** Foxglove rosettes and comfrey rosettes coexist in exactly the same damp, disturbed, half-shaded ground.
- **The antidote may not save you.** Digoxin-Fab at 40–80 mg was given in that outbreak **without effect**; pacing was required (same source). Never let the existence of DigiFab enter a risk calculation.
- **Anyone taking prescribed digoxin or digitoxin** must not go near the plant, its clippings, or its compost.
- **Renal impairment** raises risk severely (digoxin half-life exceeds 100 h in renal failure, as does the antidote's — [DOI: 10.3109/15563650.2014.943907](https://doi.org/10.3109/15563650.2014.943907)). Digitoxin's hepatic clearance shifts but does not remove the hazard.
- **Never combine with any cardenolide/bufadienolide plant:** oleander, lily-of-the-valley, squill, toad venom. Oleander is managed with the same antidote and the same difficulty ([DOI: 10.3390/toxins17030115](https://doi.org/10.3390/toxins17030115)).
- **Children, pregnancy, lactation, livestock, pets:** absolute. Garden clippings have killed animals.
- **If ingestion is suspected:** emergency services immediately. Take the plant with you. Say **"foxglove — possible cardiac glycoside poisoning."** Do not wait for symptoms; do not wait for the GI phase to pass. It is not passing — it is distributing.
