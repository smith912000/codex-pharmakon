# Dog testicle — "Dog testicle" under "Meat" (Clark's English); "dog testicle" (Men-Tsee-Khang's English)
## *The testicle of the dog as a remedy in the Tibetan Explanatory Tantra. Clark's translation prints "Dog testicle" with no Tibetan headword and no binomial; the Men-Tsee-Khang English prints "dog testicle"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The text on disk is Barry Clark's 1995 *The Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). Chapter 20, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (line 792), says that "The power of medicines is twofold: (a) the power of taste and (b) the natural power [of the substance]" (line 794), and its "specific section" divides the individual substances into eight kinds, the eighth being "(8) animal products" (line 802). The animal products are arranged by body part, and "Meat" is one heading (line 1161). The dog's testicle stands in a run of organ entries in that section (line 1170), which reads, in the part that matters here:

"Dog tongue: [has ten names and] heals all types of wounds. Donkey tongue: [has fourteen names]. It halts diarrhoea. thug.hbras: Ram testicle: is aphrodisiac. Dog testicle: is ecbolic."

So for the dog's testicle Clark prints one action, that it "is ecbolic", with no brackets, and places it directly after the ram's testicle, which "is aphrodisiac". The word *ecbolic* is Clark's English; in ordinary medical usage an ecbolic is a substance that promotes the expulsion of the contents of the uterus, especially in childbirth. The text itself does not define the term, and I rely on that ordinary meaning for the English word only. Clark uses the same word of other items in the chapter: the horns of the ram and the saiga antelope "are ecbolic" and monkey bone "is ecbolic [and abortifacient]" (lines 1158 and 1160, bracketed additions as printed). Where he adds "abortifacient" he puts it in brackets, taken from the commentaries (line 82), and he does not add it for the dog's testicle.

The second English text on disk, the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`), gives the same pair (line 3536): "Ram testicles are an aphrodisiac and dog testicle help deliver placenta." So the Men-Tsee-Khang text states the action as helping to deliver the placenta, where Clark has the single word "ecbolic"; the two are consistent with the ordinary meaning of the English word, but neither text equates them, and I record this as an appearance only. [USER: verify] The Men-Tsee-Khang text introduces the animal group with "There are thirteen medicinal substances derived from animals. They are the horn, bone, flesh, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, fur, urine, feces and the entire body of an animal" (line 3503). The testicle is not named among the thirteen kinds; in both texts the sentence that mentions it stands among the flesh and organ entries, and I do not go beyond that.

Clark's introduction says that "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57). The Men-Tsee-Khang preface says that "the use of animal products was negligible", that "animal products in Tibetan medicine have been completely substituted by equally effective herbal substances", and that their potencies are given "to preserve the understanding of the therapeutic values of animal products" (line 687).

Other dog materials in the same chapter, which I read and list: "Dog tongue: ... heals all types of wounds" (Clark line 1170; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3536, "Dog tongue heals wounds") and "Dog stool: [is especially beneficial against spirits]" (line 1199; entry III_74 covers the dung).

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, testicular tissue is glandular tissue made of protein and lipid, and contains sex-steroid hormones (such as testosterone) in small amounts that vary with the animal. Nothing more specific can be said without a source.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text records the dog's testicle as an ecbolic, which the Men-Tsee-Khang version renders as helping to deliver the placenta. The Codex does not recommend it. An action on the uterus in childbirth is a matter for midwifery and obstetric care, an unverified animal tissue is not a safe means to it, and the tradition's own present-day practice, as its translators describe it, has moved to herbal substitutes.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives no preparation for the dog's testicle. It does not say whether it is eaten, dried, burnt, powdered or taken in a drink, and the lines read give no quantity. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. No animal tissue is an established means of aiding the delivery of the placenta; retained placenta is an obstetric emergency, treated by trained attendants and, where needed, by medicines and procedures. Animal glandular tissue taken by mouth carries an infection risk. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** None printed for this item. Chapter 20 says that medicines act by taste and by natural power, and lists eight powers (heavy, oily, cool, blunt, light, coarse, hot, sharp; Clark line 796). The dog-testicle entry (line 1170) prints no taste, power or post-digestive taste, and neither does the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence (line 3536). Clark's introduction says that the later *Crystal Orb* and *Crystal Rosary* give each medicine's tastes, potencies and qualities (line 57), but that text is not on disk, so none is supplied here.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for the dog's testicle in the lines read. Chapter 20 gives it one therapeutic action, and says nothing about essence extraction (*bcud len*) or any other practice. The neighbouring clause records that the ram's testicle "is aphrodisiac", and that is a statement about the ram; the dog's entry does not share it, and the Codex does not carry it across. The Men-Tsee-Khang preface records a Buddhist objection to causing harm to any being as the reason animal products have been replaced by herbs (line 687), which is the tradition's own comment on animal products in general and not a cultivation status for this one.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The dog's testicle is two words in a list of organs, and its tradition describes animal products as largely replaced. The honest use of the entry is as a record. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy and childbirth:** an agent that acts on the uterus is not safe outside obstetric care; do not use any animal tissue for this purpose.
- **Retained placenta or bleeding after birth:** call emergency services.
- **Infection and hygiene:** raw or poorly prepared animal tissue is an infection risk, and dogs can carry zoonotic disease.
- **Hormones:** glandular tissue can contain active hormones; do not take it as a supplement.
- **Ethics and law:** the Codex does not recommend harming any animal for these uses, and the Tibetan translators themselves record a Buddhist objection to harming any being; in many places the slaughter and consumption of dogs is restricted or prohibited.
