# I-247: Thymus vulgaris (Common Thyme / Garden Thyme)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-247 | Lamiaceae (Labiatae)**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Thymus vulgaris* L.
**Family:** Lamiaceae (Labiatae)

**Common Names:**
- English: Common Thyme, Garden Thyme, French Thyme
- German: Thymian, Gartenthymian
- Arabic: *Za'tar* (زعتر) — used broadly for Thymus and related herbs; *Sa'tar*
- Persian: *Avīshan* — Ibn Sīnā's term
- Hebrew: *Ezov* — possibly the hyssop of Exodus (debated; Origanum syriacum also proposed)
- Ancient Greek: *Thýmon* (θύμον) — from *thymós* (courage/spirit) OR *thyein* (to sacrifice/fumigate) — reference to fumigant use in temples
- Latin: *Thymus* — directly from Greek
- Egyptian: *sntr* — possible identification; used as fumigant and preservative

**Taxonomy and Related Species:**
- *Thymus serpyllum* — Wild Thyme / Creeping Thyme (northern European; similar pharmacological profile)
- *Thymus zygis* — Spanish Thyme (carvacrol/thymol chemotypes; used in Spanish-Portuguese tradition)
- *Coridothymus capitatus* / *Thymbra capitata* — Headed Thyme (Eastern Mediterranean; Za'tar Farsi source)
- *Origanum vulgare* — Oregano (different genus but same Lamiaceae subfamily; thymol/carvacrol shared; frequently confused with Za'tar)

**Chemotypes (critical for clinical interpretation):**
Thymus vulgaris has six major chemotypes defined by dominant volatile constituent:
1. **Thymol chemotype** (most common; Mediterranean low-altitude): 40–60% thymol — strongest antimicrobial
2. **Carvacrol chemotype**: 40–75% carvacrol — similar antimicrobial; slightly different spectrum
3. **Linalool chemotype** (higher altitude; cooler climates): linalool-dominant; gentler; less irritating
4. **Geraniol chemotype**: geraniol-dominant; mild; floral
5. **α-Terpineol chemotype**: moderate antimicrobial
6. **Thujanol chemotype**: thujanol + terpinen-4-ol; anti-infectious; less toxic than thymol types

Chemotype identity determines appropriate clinical use and safety margin. Thymol chemotype most studied; most pharmacologically active; but also most irritating at high doses.

**Morphology:** Aromatic perennial subshrub; 15–30 cm; highly branched; small elliptical leaves, densely glandular; pale pink-lilac flowers; intensely aromatic from glandular trichomes on both leaf surfaces.

**Part Used:** Aerial parts (leaves and flowering tops — fresh or dried); essential oil; hydroalcoholic extract.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domain:** Respiratory antimicrobial / expectorant / bronchial antispasmodic — consensus across 7/8 confirming traditions. This represents one of the most strongly convergent indications in the entire Codex.

**Secondary Convergence Domain:** Anti-parasitic / vermifuge — 5/8 traditions (CM, SS, FMT, AT, EP) document thyme for worm/parasite elimination; thymol's strong anti-parasitic activity explains this.

**Tertiary Convergence:** Topical wound care / antimicrobial fumigant — 6/8 traditions.

**Mechanistic Unity:** The convergence is pharmacologically compelling — thymol and carvacrol are among the most potent naturally occurring phenolic antimicrobials known. Any culture encountering thyme would immediately recognize its fumigant/antimicrobial properties from the distinctive aroma and would observe clinical benefit in wound care and respiratory infections. The convergence was inevitable.

**Energetic Classification:**
- ◉ Qi (functional): Primary — respiratory Qi regulation; expectorant; digestive carminative
- ◈ Jing (structural): Secondary — antimicrobial defense; parasitic clearance; wound integrity
- ☿ Shen: Minor (mild nervine via linalool in linalool chemotypes)

**Primary Tier: ◉ Qi + ◈ Jing** (respiratory/immune defense and structural antimicrobial)

**Thematic Archetype:** The Purifier — antimicrobial, expectorant, anti-parasitic, fumigant. The pre-antibiotic era's respiratory and wound infection treatment par excellence, validated across every culture with access to the plant.

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Essential Oil (1.0–3.5% in dried herb)

**1. Thymol (2-isopropyl-5-methylphenol) — Thymol chemotype: 40–60% of oil**
- Monoterpene phenol
- Mechanisms:
  - **Antimicrobial:** Disrupts bacterial cell membrane integrity (phenol ring intercalation); MIC values: *S. aureus* 0.03–0.5 mg/mL; *E. coli* 0.1–1 mg/mL; *Candida* 0.06–0.5 mg/mL; *Streptococcus* 0.003 mg/mL (excellent)
  - **Biofilm disruption:** Reduces S. aureus biofilm at sub-MIC concentrations — clinically relevant for respiratory tract colonization
  - **TRPV3 channel agonism:** Warm sensation receptor activation; anti-pruritic
  - **GABA-A modulation:** Positive allosteric modulation (anxiolytic/muscle-relaxant secondary)
  - **Anti-parasitic:** Direct toxicity to Ancylostoma, Trichuris, Ascaris ova (explains traditional vermifuge use)
- Toxicity: Irritating at high concentrations to mucous membranes; hepatotoxic if ingested neat at high doses (essential oil, not culinary use)

**2. Carvacrol (2-methyl-5-(1-methylethyl)phenol) — 1–25%; dominant in carvacrol chemotype**
- Isomer of thymol
- Nearly identical antimicrobial spectrum; some data suggest slightly broader activity
- Anti-inflammatory: NF-κB inhibition; PPAR-γ activation → reduced cytokines
- Mechanisms nearly parallel to thymol

**3. p-Cymene — 10–25%**
- Monoterpene hydrocarbon; limited direct activity
- Key role as thymol/carvacrol precursor in biosynthesis
- Synergizes with thymol for antibacterial activity (membrane fluidization → enhanced thymol entry)
- Anti-nociceptive: Activates KATP channels in peripheral sensory neurons

**4. γ-Terpinene — 5–20%**
- Antioxidant; synergistic antimicrobial with thymol/carvacrol

**5. Linalool — Dominant in linalool chemotype (20–80%)**
- GABA-A PAM (parallel to lavender, cardamom entries)
- Gentle antimicrobial; anti-anxiety
- Preferred for pediatric formulations (linalool chemotype thyme syrup for children's cough)

**6. 1,8-Cineole (Eucalyptol) — 3–10%**
- Bronchodilator; mucolytic; expectorant

### Non-Volatile Constituents

**7. Rosmarinic Acid — 0.5–3.5%**
- Strong anti-inflammatory; GABA-T inhibition; antiviral (HSV); antioxidant

**8. Luteolin, Apigenin, Eriocitrin — Flavonoids**
- Anti-inflammatory; bronchial antispasmodic; antioxidant

**9. Ursolic Acid, Oleanolic Acid — Triterpene acids**
- Anti-inflammatory; anti-tumor in vitro; antiviral

**10. Thyme-specific glycosides**
- Thymosin; saponins; mild expectorant contribution

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### 1. Broad-Spectrum Antimicrobial (Thymol + Carvacrol)
Thymol and carvacrol work synergistically by:
1. Dissolving in phospholipid bilayer → membrane disruption (primary mechanism)
2. Reducing proton motive force across bacterial membrane
3. Inhibiting ATP synthesis
4. Inhibiting bacterial enzymes (urease, lipase)

Active against: Gram-positive (Streptococcus, Staphylococcus, MRSA); Gram-negative (E. coli, Klebsiella, P. aeruginosa at higher concentrations); fungi (Candida, Aspergillus); parasites (thymol specific).

**Anti-MRSA activity:** Thymol and carvacrol show synergistic activity with certain antibiotics against MRSA; research area for multidrug-resistant infection; in vitro robust; clinical data limited.

### 2. Bronchial Antispasmodic + Mucolytic
- Thymol + 1,8-cineole: bronchial smooth muscle calcium channel antagonism → bronchodilation
- 1,8-Cineole: ciliary beat frequency enhancement → mucociliary clearance improvement
- p-Cymene: reduces mucus viscosity
- Combined: expectorant + bronchospasm reduction = mechanistic basis for chronic bronchitis / productive cough indication

### 3. Antitussive Mechanism
Thymol and carvacrol modulate cough reflex at:
- Peripheral sensory nerve level: TRPV1/TRPA1 desensitization reduces cough-triggering bronchial afferent firing
- Central cough suppression (minor): limited data suggest some central antitussive action
- Combined with bronchodilation: reduces mechanical stimulus for cough from bronchospasm

### 4. Anti-Inflammatory (Multi-Target)
- NF-κB inhibition (carvacrol, rosmarinic acid): reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines
- COX-2 inhibition (rosmarinic acid, ursolic acid): reduces PGE₂
- 5-LOX inhibition (rosmarinic acid, thymol): reduces leukotrienes in respiratory mucosa
- Net: reduces bronchial mucosal inflammation → symptom relief in bronchitis

### 5. Anti-Parasitic (Thymol Specific)
Historical vermifuge use is pharmacologically validated:
- *Ascaris lumbricoides*: Thymol 2 g oral dose sufficient to stun/kill adult worms (historical pharmaceutical hookworm treatment, pre-mebendazole)
- *Ancylostoma* (hookworm): US Pharmacopeia listed Thymol as official treatment until 1950s
- Mechanism: Disrupts parasite mitochondrial electron transport; membrane disruption in non-selective gut parasites

### 6. Oral Antimicrobial (Thymol — Listerine® Mechanism)
Thymol is a primary active ingredient in Listerine® and multiple commercial mouthwashes at 0.064% concentration. The FDA recognized efficacy for plaque reduction and gingivitis. Mechanistic basis: disrupts oral Streptococcus mutans biofilm; reduces Candida overgrowth; reduces volatile sulfur compounds (halitosis).

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

### Respiratory Infections / Bronchitis

**Kemmerich et al. 2006 (Arzneimittelforschung):**
- N=361 patients; acute bronchitis; Bronchipret® (thyme + ivy combination) vs. ambroxol (mucolytic control)
- Significant reduction in cough frequency: Bronchipret non-inferior to ambroxol
- Level B evidence for thyme combination in acute bronchitis

**Kemmerich 2007 (Arzneimittelforschung):**
- N=3,652 patients; acute bronchitis; Bronchipret syrup (thyme-ivy); large open-label study
- 78.4% improvement rate; well-tolerated; Level C (uncontrolled but large N)

**Syrup formulation (thyme leaf standardized, Bronchipret®):**
- European Medicines Agency (EMA): Positive opinion; traditional use authorization for cough associated with cold
- German Commission E: Approved for upper respiratory catarrh and bronchitis

**Thymus serpyllum evidence:**
- Multiple German RCTs: thyme-thyme combinations for productive cough; Level B

### Oral Health

**Listerine RCT meta-analysis (Barnett 2006):**
- Thymol 0.064% + eucalyptol + menthol + methyl salicylate formulation
- Significant plaque reduction vs. placebo/water (standardized Löe-Silness scoring)
- Gingivitis reduction: 26% over 6 months
- Level A evidence for thymol-containing mouthwash for plaque and gingivitis (4 positive RCTs cited by FDA)

### Anti-parasitic (Historical)

**US Pharmacopeia (1946–1956):** Thymol officially listed for hookworm treatment at 3 g/day dose (3 divided doses with castor oil). Replaced by more tolerable synthetic anthelminthics (thiabendazole, mebendazole) not due to lack of efficacy but due to adverse effect profile at high doses. Historical evidence Level A by contemporary standards.

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

Thyme's phenomenological profile is dominated by its immediate sensory impact — the pungent, warm, and penetrating aroma of thymol/carvacrol is unmistakable and cross-culturally recognized as "medicinal" even before pharmacological analysis. The subjective experience of thyme as medicine is primarily somatic — respiratory, digestive, and systemic warmth — without psychoactive qualities at therapeutic doses.

**Oral (tea/syrup):**
- Immediate: Warm, slightly pungent, herbaceous; astringent aftertaste
- 10–20 minutes: Chest warmth; sense of respiratory opening (bronchodilation); loosening of tight chest sensation in URI/bronchitis
- Prolonged: Productive cough facilitation — phlegm "comes up" more easily (mucolytic effect)
- No psychoactive effect; no sedation; mild warmth is systemic

**Aromatherapy / steam inhalation:**
- Intense thymol vapor: Initially sharp/penetrating; then warming
- Nasal passage: mild congestion relief via mucosal antimicrobial + mild decongestant effect
- Traditional midwifery/sick-room fumigation: thyme burned in rooms of the ill — antimicrobial air-fumigation with real basis (thymol vapor reduces airborne S. aureus at experimentally achievable concentrations)

**Traditional Greek "thymos" connection:**
The Greek *thymós* (θυμός) — courage, spirit, vital breath — may etymologically connect to the herb's reputation for enhancing respiratory vitality and bestowing courage before battle (Roman legions). Historically, thyme sprigs were given to soldiers as emblems of courage; worn by knights into medieval tournaments. The "spirit of thyme" phenomenology — a subtle sense of vigor and respiratory vitality — is consistently reported across traditions and likely reflects the genuine bronchodilatory and mild stimulant effects of thymol inhalation.

**Culinary-medicinal continuum:**
Thyme is one of the few herbs where culinary and medicinal use are genuinely continuous — the quantities in Mediterranean cooking (Za'tar spice blend on bread, thyme-heavy stews) provide meaningful thymol exposure. Traditional Mediterranean diet's protective effects on respiratory health may partly reflect this continuous low-dose thymol consumption.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Tea (infusion):**
- 1.5–2 g dried aerial parts per 150 mL boiling water; steep 10 minutes covered
- 3–5 cups daily for acute respiratory infection; 1–2 cups for digestive/general use
- **Cover during steeping** to retain volatile thymol (evaporates readily from hot open vessel)

**Standardized extract (syrup/tablet):**
- Bronchipret® standard: 160 mg dry thyme extract (equivalent to ~1.6 g herb) per dose
- Adult: 160–320 mg extract 3× daily
- Children 1–4 years: Linalool chemotype syrup (not thymol chemotype — less irritating)

**Tincture:**
- 1:5 in 45% ethanol: 2–4 mL 3× daily (acute respiratory)
- 1:1 fluid extract: 0.5–1 mL 3× daily

**Essential oil (topical/inhalation only; never undiluted internal):**
- Chest rub: 1–2% in carrier oil (maximum 2%; higher concentrations irritating)
- Steam inhalation: 2–3 drops in hot water; 10 minutes 2× daily
- **NEVER ingest essential oil neat** — thymol hepatotoxic at high concentrations; mucosal burns

**Mouthwash:**
- 0.063% thymol in base (commercial: Listerine® and equivalents)
- OR: Strong thyme tea (cooled) as mouthwash; less standardized but effective

### Dosing by Indication

| Indication | Form | Dose |
|------------|------|------|
| Acute bronchitis | Standardized syrup | 160 mg extract 3–4× daily |
| URI / productive cough | Strong infusion | 3–5 cups/day × 5–10 days |
| Oral health / gingivitis | 0.063% thymol mouthwash | Rinse 30 sec 2×/day |
| Topical wound (antimicrobial) | 1% thymol in cream | Apply 2–3× daily |
| Steam inhalation (congestion) | EO in hot water | 2 drops; 10 min 2×/day |
| GI infections / parasites | Strong tea or tincture | 3 cups tea or 3 mL tincture 3×/day |

### Safety Profile

**Generally safe** at culinary and therapeutic tea doses. Concerns arise at higher essential oil concentrations.

**Adverse effects:**
- **GI irritation:** Thymol/carvacrol irritating to GI mucosa at high doses; take with food or use linalool chemotype if sensitive
- **Mucous membrane irritation:** Topical essential oil >2% causes contact dermatitis/burns
- **Hepatotoxicity:** Essential oil ingested neat or at high dose; not a concern at tea doses
- **Allergic contact dermatitis:** Lamiaceae family sensitivity; patch test for topical

**Drug interactions:**
- **Anticoagulants (warfarin):** Rosmarinic acid + possible COX inhibition — additive anticoagulant effect; monitor INR with heavy thyme consumption
- **Antithyroid drugs:** Thymol has mild antithyroid activity (iodide oxidation inhibition) — monitor thyroid function in patients on thyroid medications who consume large thyme quantities
- **Antidiabetics:** Mild blood glucose-lowering effect (thymol improves insulin sensitivity); additive; monitor glucose

**Contraindications:**
- Pregnancy (high dose): Uterine stimulant at pharmacological doses (tea at 1–2 cups/day considered safe; excessive amounts avoided)
- Infants under 2 years: Thymol chemotype preparations avoided; linalool chemotype syrup preferred
- Lamiaceae family allergy
