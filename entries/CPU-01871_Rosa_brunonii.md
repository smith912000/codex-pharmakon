# Rosa brunonii — Sewai metok, se ba'i me tog (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary by column position); "Musk rose"; printed "Rosa branonii"

## *Rosa brunonii, as the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints it (OCR "Rosa branonii", line 5753) as the tenth scientific name of its first "S" run, which column order sets against the name Sewai metok (se ba'i me tog) and the English "Musk rose"; Clark 1995 prints no Rosa brunonii, and gives the same Tibetan name, as Se.wahi.me.tog, three other roses* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi (Four Tantras), read in two modern translations, Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011. The binomial comes from the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary's column order alone, and Clark's entry for the same Tibetan name prints other roses, so the botanical identity is open within the genus.** Both texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk found no *Rosa brunonii*; those texts name other roses (for example Yang 1998 prints "Ying Shi (Semen Rosae Multiflorae)" at line 3158), which are not read as this one. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP, confidence "medium") and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. **Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 352) the row is keyed by the scientific name "Rosa branonii", with the locus "Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (line 5753)" and the note "Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". The name stands alone on its line, so the key is the first and only name there. The OCR reads "u" as "a", so the form is read as *brunonii*, and the spine merges the printed "branonii" into it. The Tibetan name comes from the column order below and not from the CSV.

**The glossary, by column position.** `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the first "S" run. The phonetic names (lines 5685 to 5719) are fifteen when each is counted separately: "Sa dre" (first), "Saegoe" (second), "Sae kar", "Sazika", "Sedu", "Sengdeng" (sixth), "Sergyi metok", "Sergyi phuebu" (eighth), "Ser kue", "Sewai metok" (tenth, line 5715), "Seyab", "Shelta", "Shhimthikle", "Shhu khen" and "Shingkun" (fifteenth, line 5719). The transliteration "se ba'i me tog" is printed beside "Sewai metok" on line 5715. The scientific names (lines 5727 to 5765) are fifteen: Syzygium and *Rosa macrophylla* (5727), *Oxytropis kansuensis* (5732), *Ustilago nuda*, *Punica granatum* and *Acacia catechu* (5736 to 5737), *Herpetospermum pedunculosum* (5743), *Luffa aegyptiaca* (5745), *Lethariella* (5749), *Rosa branonii* (5753), *Chaenomeles speciosa* (5757), "Pinus sp." and "Rabdosia ragosa" (both on 5758), *Symplocus paniculata* (5764) and *Ferula asafetida* (5765). The English names are "Black plum", "Rose" (5729 to 5730), "-", "Corn ergot", "Pomegranite", "Catechu tree", "Golden flower", "Sponge gourd", "Golden dodder", "Musk rose" (5755), "Tibetan quince", "-", "Rabdosia", "Sapphire berry" and "Asafoetida". Counted in order, "Sewai metok" is the tenth phonetic name, *Rosa branonii* the tenth scientific name and "Musk rose" the tenth English name. The count is checked at the second, sixth, eighth and twelfth places: "Saegoe" against *Rosa macrophylla* as Clark's *se.rgod* (line 1042) is "Wild Rose (female type): Rosa macrophylla"; "Sengdeng" against *Acacia catechu* as Clark's *seng.ldeng* (line 985); "Sergyi phuebu" against *Luffa aegyptiaca* (I_1311); and "Shelta" against "Pinus sp." as Clark's *shel.ta* (line 926) is "Pine Resin: Pinus griffithii". The same run and the same tenth place are counted in I_1348. The glossary does not print the pairing on one row. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents line 225), page 207.* Line 3433, within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428): "Sewai metok cures tripa disorders and suppresses loong." The index lists "Sewai metok 207" (line 7486). "Tripa" and "loong" are Tibetan terms that the editors keep untranslated (note at line 686). The text gives no Latin name, description or taste for the drug.

*Clark 1995, the same Tibetan name.* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, chapter 20 (heading at line 792), "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997), line 1033: "Se.wahi.me.tog: Wild Rose: Rosa roxburghii: Rosa sericea Lindl: Rosa avicularis: [has five names and is of black and white types. It is a large thorny bush with small, round, slightly coarse leaves, white flower and red hips. It has sweet and sour tastes, and oily power, and] cures bile disorders and alleviates wind imbalances." Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the English name, the three Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's (his preface, line 83, says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance"), and the sentence "cures bile disorders and alleviates wind imbalances"; inside are the five names, the two types, the description and the tastes and power. Clark's name index lists *se.wahi.me.tog* (line 1765). The two translations agree in kind: Clark's "bile" and "wind" and the Men-Tsee-Khang "tripa" and "loong" are the translators' words for the two terms the editors keep in Tibetan. The Codex reads *Sewai metok* and *Se.wahi.me.tog* as one drug from the spelling and that agreement; neither text says so. [USER: verify]

**Identity: the glossary against Clark.** Clark prints *Rosa roxburghii*, *Rosa sericea* and *Rosa avicularis* for the drug; the glossary prints *Rosa brunonii* with the English "Musk rose". All are roses. As a general fact *Rosa brunonii* is a Himalayan rose with white flowers, and Clark's commentary gives the drug a white flower; the Codex does not treat that as an identification, since the commentary says the drug has two types, black and white. The row stands for the Tibetan drug and not for one species, and the Codex does not choose among the roses. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Rosa brunonii*, *branonii*, "Musk rose", *Se.wahi.me.tog* and *Sewai metok* found no entry on this binomial. **I_1348** (*Rosa roxburghii*, tag FMT) is an entry on the same Tibetan drug, keyed to Clark's first Latin name, written from Clark line 1033 and Men-Tsee-Khang line 3433; it counts the same glossary position (the tenth) and prints no *Rosa brunonii* as its key. The two are counted separately because their spine keys differ; they are not merged. **I_1347** (*Rosa avicularis*, tag FMT, *se.rgod*, Clark line 984) and **I_1178** (*Rosa macrophylla*, tag FMT, *se.rgod*, Clark line 1042 and glossary line 5727) are entries on a different Tibetan name for wild rose. **I_349** (*Rosa damascena*, tags EP, DMM, CM, SBJ, SS, FMT) and **I_204** (Rose, tags CM, SS, EP, DMM, SBJ, FMT) carry the FMT tag as general rose entries; I did not find *Se.wahi.me.tog*, *Sewai metok* or *brunonii* in them. **I_681** (*Rosa canina*) and **I_881** (*Rosa gallica*) rest on other texts. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. As a general fact *Rosa* is a genus of the rose family (Rosaceae), and rosehips are generally known to contain vitamin C. The lines read give no composition for the Tibetan drug, and nothing is stated here about what it contains.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record one action: the drug "cures tripa disorders and suppresses loong" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3433) and "cures bile disorders and alleviates wind imbalances" (Clark line 1033, outside the brackets). That is the tradition's own record and not a Codex protocol. The identity is open within the genus, so the Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation and name no part used; Clark's commentary mentions red hips, which is not an instruction. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Both translations place the drug in the materia medica with a therapeutic action on the humours (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3433; Clark line 1033). That is a therapeutic place and not a status in rejuvenation or in *bcud len* (essence extraction). The rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23: Clark lines 1264 to 1276; Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3904 to 3945) does not name the drug. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator has nothing to do with this drug on the strength of the texts beyond what a physician would use it for, which is to settle tripa and loong. It is a rose of uncertain species, and nothing here supports treating a rose as a rejuvenative.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for the drug. The identity is uncertain within the genus, so no plant should be gathered or taken on the strength of this entry. No conservation information and no safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
