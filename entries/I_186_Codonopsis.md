# I-186 · Codonopsis
**Codonopsis pilosula** (Franch.) Nannf. | Campanulaceae

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Codonopsis pilosula* (Franch.) Nannf. (1935)  
**Pharmacopoeial synonyms:** *C. pilosula* var. *modesta*, *C. tangshen* Oliv. (also listed in Chinese Pharmacopoeia as Dangshen source)  
**Common names:** Dang Shen (党参), bonnet bellflower, poor man's ginseng, codonopsis root

**Etymology:**
- **Dang Shen (党参):** Named for Shangdang (上党), a region in Shanxi province famous for producing the finest-quality roots — *shen* (参) = ginseng/root (the same character as in Ren Shen, Panax ginseng); literally "Shangdang root"
- **"Poor man's ginseng":** Price-accessibility distinction from Panax ginseng; Codonopsis serves as a functional substitute in formulas at a fraction of the cost; TCM practitioners began substituting Dang Shen for Ren Shen in many (not all) formulas in the Qing dynasty as ginseng prices rose
- *Codonopsis* = Greek *kodon* (bell) + *opsis* (appearance) = bell-like appearance (corolla shape)
- *pilosula* = Latin = lightly hairy (soft pubescence on stems and leaves)

**Taxonomic note:** Campanulaceae (bellflower family); 40+ *Codonopsis* species exist; primary pharmacopoeial species are *C. pilosula* and *C. tangshen*. Not related to ginseng (*Araliaceae*) despite folk name.

**Identifying morphology:**
- Twining climber to 2m; milky sap (characteristic of Campanulaceae); opposite ovate leaves; solitary bell-shaped pale yellowish-green flowers with purple markings inside; inflated calyx; fleshy taproot
- Root: carrot-like taproot to 30cm; wrinkled, yellowish-tan exterior; white interior with circular growth rings; characteristic sweet, faintly earthy smell; sweet-bitter taste

**Part used:** Dried root (*Radix Codonopsitis*); 3-year minimum, 4-year preferred

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Functional ginseng substitute — convergence by substitution:**

Codonopsis does not have multi-traditional convergence in the usual sense; its cross-traditional presence is essentially mono-traditional (Chinese medicine). However, it occupies an interesting position in the Codex: it became the functional substitute for Panax ginseng in many clinical formulas, meaning any convergence attributed to ginseng-class Qi tonics theoretically transfers to codonopsis in historical context.

**The Spleen-Lung-Blood triad:**

TCM's primary attribution of Dang Shen covers three organ systems: Spleen (digestive qi, transformation, absorption), Lung (wei qi, defensive function, respiration), and Blood (nourishing, moistening — a secondary function). Modern pharmacology found:
- Spleen Qi → polysaccharide immunomodulation + digestive enzyme effects (digestive support confirmed)
- Lung Qi → expectorant saponins + anti-inflammatory bronchial effects (partial)
- Blood nourishing → erythropoietic stimulation + hematinic effects (hematological support with some evidence)

The TCM organ-system attribution maps reasonably well onto measurable physiological targets, even without mechanistic theory connecting them.

**Saponin-polysaccharide shared chemistry:**

Codonopsis shares the saponin + polysaccharide pharmacological platform with Panax ginseng, astragalus, and other major TCM tonics. This chemical convergence within the tonic herb category (a pharmacological class, not a taxonomic one) suggests parallel evolution of similar pharmacological strategies in different plant families: *Campanulaceae* (Codonopsis), *Araliaceae* (Ginseng), *Fabaceae* (Astragalus), *Ranunculaceae* (Actaea), *Apiaceae* (Ligusticum) all deploy saponin-polysaccharide combinations for immunomodulatory tonic effects.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Primary active compounds:**

**1. Polysaccharides (Codonopsis Polysaccharides — CPS)**
- Primary immunomodulatory actives; heterogeneous mixture
- CPS-I, CPS-II: molecular weights 48–180 kDa
- β-(1→4) and β-(1→3) glycosidic linkages; TLR4 agonists
- 20–40% dry weight total polysaccharides
- Distinct from astragalus polysaccharides (APS) but parallel immunological mechanism

**2. Triterpenoid Saponins**
- Taraxerol, taraxeryl acetate: primary triterpene saponins
- Codonopside, codonopsin (codonopsis-specific glycosides)
- Lower saponin content than Panax ginseng (no ginsenosides); explains partial but not equivalent ginseng substitution

**3. Alkaloids**
- Perlolyrine: β-carboline alkaloid; CNS-active; rare in medicinal plants; MAOI activity (weak)
- Codonopsine and codonopsinine: quaternary alkaloids; anticholinesterase activity
- Nicotine: trace (Campanulaceae family characteristic)

**4. Polyacetylenes and Phthalides**
- Lobetyol, lobetyolin, lobetyolinin: acetylenic compounds (characteristic of Campanulaceae)
- Phthalide derivatives: sedative-antispasmodic activity (same class as in Ligusticum chuanxiong and celery)

**5. Phenolic Compounds**
- Syringin (eleutheroside B): also found in Eleutherococcus; adaptogenic correlation
- Chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid: anti-inflammatory
- Ferulic acid: antioxidant, vasodilatory

**6. Sugars and Minerals**
- Inulin-type fructooligosaccharides (FOS): prebiotic; feeds Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium
- Sucrose, fructose, glucose: high sugar content contributes to sweet flavor
- Zinc, copper, manganese, iron: mineral profile supports hematological traditional claims

**Comparison to Panax ginseng:**
- NO ginsenosides (the characteristic biomarkers of Panax ginseng)
- No panaxoside/panaxatriol; no Rg1, Rb1 (the main ginseng pharmacological markers)
- Lower adaptogenic potency per gram than Panax ginseng
- Higher polysaccharide content than Panax ginseng → relatively stronger immunomodulatory effect
- Cost: 5–20× cheaper than equivalent-grade Panax ginseng

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**1. Immunomodulation (CPS polysaccharides — Primary)**
- CPS → TLR4 agonism → NF-κB pathway → macrophage activation, phagocytosis enhancement
- NK cell activation: ADCC (antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity) enhancement
- Dendritic cell maturation: enhanced antigen presentation
- Bidirectional: suppresses excess inflammation via IL-10 induction simultaneously
- Parallel mechanism to APS (astragalus); often combined in TCM formulas for additive effect

**2. Hematopoiesis Support**
- Stimulates erythroid progenitor proliferation in bone marrow
- Increases reticulocyte count → measurable hematological response
- Iron utilization enhancement (mineral cofactor effect)
- Mechanisms: EPO-sensitization pathways; stem cell niche support
- Traditional "nourishes blood" = erythropoietic support, measurable

**3. Cholinesterase Inhibition (Alkaloids)**
- Codonopsinine: AChE inhibitor; BChE inhibitor
- Increases synaptic acetylcholine → enhanced cholinergic transmission
- Cognitive and memory-supporting mechanism
- Parallel to: Alzheimer's drug class (donepezil, rivastigmine); much weaker but structurally analogous

**4. Gastrointestinal and Prebiotic (Polysaccharides + FOS)**
- Inulin-type FOS: selectively fermented by Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium
- Modulates gut microbiome composition toward beneficial species
- Digestive enzyme secretion enhancement (gastric, pancreatic)
- Intestinal barrier function support
- Traditional "tonifies Spleen" = digestive capacity enhancement; microbiome mechanism now partly explains this

**5. Adaptogenic (Stress Response Normalization)**
- HPA axis normalization: reduces excessive cortisol under chronic stress
- Syringin (shared with Eleutherococcus): cellular stress response enhancement
- β-carboline alkaloids (perlolyrine): mild MAO-A inhibition → increased serotonin/NE availability
- Weaker adaptogenic effect than Eleutherococcus, Rhodiola, or Schisandra; primarily a tonic rather than acute adaptogen

**6. Cardiovascular**
- Vasodilation: NO pathway activation (ferulic acid, phthalides)
- Mild antithrombotic (aggregation inhibition)
- Blood viscosity reduction

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Human clinical evidence is more limited than for Panax ginseng or Astragalus; most evidence from Chinese clinical literature.**

**Chemotherapy adjunct:**
- Multiple Chinese studies: codonopsis-based formulas (typically as part of *Si Jun Zi Tang* or similar base formulas) improve quality of life, reduce chemotherapy-induced leukopenia
- Attribution to codonopsis specifically confounded by multi-herb formulas
- Mechanism (hematopoietic + immunomodulatory) supported by animal models

**Anemia:**
- Zhang Y et al (2015) *J Ethnopharmacol* 162:236–44: Codonopsis extract; murine anemia model; significant increase in RBC count, hemoglobin, hematocrit vs. control; P<0.001
- Human data: primarily observational; traditional formula studies in iron-deficiency anemia

**Cognitive function:**
- Li L et al (2012) *Neuropharmacology*: Codonopsin AChE inhibition; in vitro and animal cognitive improvement
- No human RCTs specifically for cognitive endpoints

**Gastric protection:**
- Qin YL et al (2018) *J Ethnopharmacol*: Codonopsis extract; gastroprotective against stress-induced gastric ulcer in animal model; prostaglandin-dependent mechanism

**Regulatory status:**
- Chinese Pharmacopoeia 2020: Included as standard materia medica
- Not regulated or assessed by EMA or Commission E (insufficient Western clinical data)
- Traditional Herbal Medicinal Product status could be sought in EU but not yet established

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Shangdang — the original home:**

The name Dang Shen encodes geography: Shangdang (上党), a high plateau region in Shanxi province (elevation ~1,100m), was historically the primary producing region for the finest codonopsis roots. The terroir concept — that regional growing conditions fundamentally alter the therapeutic quality of plant medicines — is encoded in the traditional Chinese place-name designations. Shangdang codonopsis (上党参 Shangdang Shen) commanded price premiums comparable to regional ginseng designations.

**The ginseng scarcity problem:**

By the Qing dynasty (1644–1912), authentic Panax ginseng (especially Korean and wild Chinese ginseng) had become so scarce and expensive that court physicians began systematically identifying functional substitutes for non-wealthy patients. Codonopsis emerged as the premier Qi-tonic ginseng substitute — capable of substituting in most tonic formulas though not in high-stakes acute qi collapse (ginseng's irreplaceable use). This substitution created a parallel economy: Dang Shen formulas for everyday tonic use, Ren Shen reserved for critical interventions.

**Si Jun Zi Tang — the Four Gentlemen Formula:**

The classical TCM formula *Si Jun Zi Tang* (四君子湯, "Four Gentlemen Decoction") consists of: Ren Shen (ginseng) + Bai Zhu (atractylodes) + Fu Ling (poria) + Zhi Gan Cao (licorice). In modern clinical TCM practice, Ren Shen is almost universally substituted with Dang Shen in this formula for non-acute applications — reducing cost 10–20× with acceptable therapeutic equivalency for most patients. This substitution is now standard and itself documented in TCM pharmacopoeias.

**The polypharmacy platform:**

Codonopsis almost never appears alone in TCM — it is the prototypical "envoy herb" (shi yao, 使药) in tonifying formulas, working synergistically with herbs that target specific organ systems. Its role as foundation tonic (providing Qi and Blood foundation) while other herbs address specific pathologies mirrors its pharmacological profile: non-specific immune/hematopoietic support enabling more targeted therapeutic effects. This use-pattern is distinct from Western herbalism's mono-herb approach.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Primary indications:**
- Qi deficiency: fatigue, shortness of breath, pallor, weak digestion, spontaneous sweating
- Blood deficiency (with astragalus or angelica): pallor, dizziness, heart palpitations
- Chemotherapy support: leukopenia, fatigue, immune reconstitution
- Gastrointestinal weakness: poor appetite, loose stools, digestive insufficiency
- Ginseng substitute (where cost is prohibitive or ginseng is contraindicated): most tonic formulas

**Dosing:**
- **Standard dose:** 9–30g dried root as decoction daily (significantly higher dose than ginseng)
- **In formula:** 12–15g typical in four-herb tonic formulas
- **Extract standardized (10:1):** 1,000–3,000mg daily
- **Duration:** Extended continuous use appropriate (months); designed as tonic herb

**Drug interactions (from alkaloid profile):**
- **MAOIs:** Perlolyrine (β-carboline alkaloid) has weak MAOI activity; concurrent use with pharmaceutical MAOIs → serotonin syndrome risk (theoretical; clinical data absent); caution with selegiline, phenelzine
- **Anticholinergic drugs:** AChE inhibition from codonopsinine may oppose or interfere with anticholinergic medications
- **Antidiabetic drugs:** Mild glucose-lowering effect; monitor blood glucose
- **Immunosuppressants:** Immunomodulatory potential; monitor (less concern than astragalus but relevant for transplant patients)

⚠️ **CAUTIONS:**
- Acute illness (wind-heat, fever): temporarily defer; not for acute conditions
- Pregnancy: traditional caution against strong tonic herbs in first trimester; second/third trimester use traditional but defer to qualified TCM practitioner
- Autoimmune conditions: immunomodulatory activity; assess individually

**Quality and standardization:**
- No European pharmacopoeial standard; Chinese Pharmacopoeia 2020 standard
- Identify source: *C. pilosula* OR *C. tangshen* (both official); not other species
- Authenticity test: sweet-slightly-bitter taste; characteristic wrinkled root morphology
- Avoid: codonopsis powder without species verification; substitution fraud common (inferior species)

**Preparation notes:**
- Traditional decoction: simmered 30–45 minutes; root slices in soup or congee also traditional
- Pairs most commonly in TCM with: Bai Zhu (Atractylodes macrocephala), Fu Ling (Poria cocos), Huang Qi (Astragalus), Dang Gui (Angelica sinensis)
- Can be cooked with chicken or lamb in tonifying meat broths — food-medicine continuum

**Energetic tier:** ◉ Qi (circulatory/functional) — primarily restores and maintains functional energy, digestive capacity, and blood-building; operates at the Qi/functional layer rather than the constitutional/Jing level
