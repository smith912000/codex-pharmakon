# Coris monspeliensis — Sumphuton petraion (Dioscorides 4-9)

## *Coris monspeliensis (the translators' first suggestion; "Montpellier Coris"), with Symphytum petraeum as their second name* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The line reads "Coris monspeliensis, Symphytum petraeum — Montpellier Coris" and is followed by the cross-reference "see 3-174". The second name is the Latin form of the chapter's own Greek name. The translators' index lists *Coris monspeliensis* at pages 539 and 552.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-9. SUMPHUTON PETRAION* at line 8080, SUGGESTED line at 8081, cross-reference at 8082, text at line 8083.

The whole chapter reads: "Symphitum Petraeum grows on rocks. It has little branches similar to origanum, thin leaves, and little heads like thyme. The whole plant is woody and has a sweet smell, is sweet to the taste, and causes spittle. It has a long, faint purple root almost the thickness of a finger. This (boiled with honey and water and taken as a drink) gets up vile stuff from the lungs. It is given with water to those who spit up blood, and for inflammation in the kidneys. Boiled with wine it is taken (as a drink) for dysentery, and women's excessive bloody menstrual discharges. It is boiled with vinegar and honey for convulsions and hernias; and chewed it quenches thirst. Used as a poultice it is good for the roughness of a sore throat, heals new wounds, and represses vaginal hernias. It also joins together broken flesh."

So Dioscorides describes a rock plant with little branches like origanum, thin leaves and little heads like thyme, woody throughout, sweet-smelling and sweet to the taste, which "causes spittle", with a long faint purple root nearly a finger thick. The first preparation follows the sentence on the root and begins "This", so it is of the root; the later ones say "it", and the text does not say which part of the plant is meant. [USER: verify] The uses are: honey and water for "vile stuff from the lungs"; water for those who spit blood and for inflammation in the kidneys; wine for dysentery and women's excessive bloody menstrual discharges; vinegar and honey for convulsions and hernias; chewing to quench thirst; and a poultice for the roughness of a sore throat, new wounds and vaginal hernias, with the added claim that it "joins together broken flesh". No other names are printed.

**Overlap.** Chapter 3-174 *KORIS* (heading line 7972) carries a SUGGESTED line that lists *Hypericum coris* first and then "Coris monspeliensis, Symphytum petraeum [Bedevian]", with "see 3-88, 4-9". That chapter describes a different plant, a shrub twenty centimetres high with a leaf "similar to erica but smaller, fatter and red" (line 7976). It has its own spine row, CPU-01542 *Hypericum coris*, and is not read here as the same plant. The same binomial therefore stands on two chapters of the translators' suggestions. Chapter 4-10 *SUMPHUTON ALLO* (line 8084) follows and describes the plant the translators call comfrey (*Symphytum officinale*); the spine's comfrey entry is I_650. None is merged here. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a sweet, woody rock plant taken in honey water for the lungs, in water for spitting blood and kidney inflammation, in wine for dysentery and heavy menstrual flow, with vinegar and honey for convulsions and hernias, and applied for sore throat and wounds. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol: spitting blood, convulsions, hernias and dysentery need medical care, and the identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-9 gives these preparations: "boiled with honey and water and taken as a drink"; "given with water"; "boiled with wine" and drunk; "boiled with vinegar and honey"; "chewed"; and "used as a poultice". No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: where the plant grows (on rocks), how to know it (little branches like origanum, thin leaves, little heads like thyme, a woody body, a long faint purple root), what it smells and tastes like, and what it does in the mouth (it "causes spittle"). It assigns the plant no status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. Its uses are a catalogue of lung, kidney, bowel and wound remedies with no stated amounts, so it is history and not a practice. The cross-reference to a different chapter under the same suggested name is itself a reminder that the identification is not settled.

### Contraindications

Spitting blood, kidney inflammation, dysentery, convulsions and hernias are conditions that need medical care, and a home preparation must not replace it. The remedy for heavy menstrual bleeding and the poultice for vaginal hernias should not be self-treated, and no safety information for pregnancy was read. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion shared with another chapter, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
