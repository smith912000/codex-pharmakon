# Stachys germanica — Stachus (Dioscorides 3-120)

## *Stachys germanica [Linnaeus], "Hedge Nettle, Woundwort, Betony", the last of the names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-120 ("Stachys [Fuchs], Stachys major germanica [Bauhin], Stachys germanica [Linnaeus] — Hedge Nettle, Woundwort, Betony")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (lines 7625 and 7626) prints three names with their namers, the last being Linnaeus, and the row takes the last. It was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-120. STACHUS*, heading at line 7624, SUGGESTED lines at 7625 and 7626, text at line 7627. It stands in Book Three, after *3-119. PRASION* (line 7607) and before *3-121. PHULLITIS* (line 7628), both different plants. The Codex's own name extraction lists it (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 526); the translators' general index prints "Stachys germanica 507" and "Stachys 507" (line 17697).

The whole chapter reads: "Stachys is a shrub similar to marrubium [3-38] yet somewhat longer; with many thin leaves, somewhat rough, hard, with sweet scent, white; with many small shoots out of the same root, but paler than those of marrubium. It grows in rough hilly places, and it is warming and sharp. As a result a decoction of the leaves (taken as a drink) expels the menstrual flow and afterbirth."

The chapter gives a description by comparison (a shrub like *marrubium*, with the translators' cross-reference 3-38, but "somewhat longer"; many thin leaves, "somewhat rough, hard, with sweet scent, white"; many small shoots from the same root but "paler than those of marrubium"), a place ("rough hilly places"), a quality ("warming and sharp") and one use that the text says follows from that quality ("As a result"): a decoction of the leaves, taken as a drink, "expels the menstrual flow and afterbirth". The chapter prints no other names.

**The cross-reference to marrubium.** The number 3-38 after "marrubium" points, in this edition, to the chapter *3-38. PSEUDODIKTAMNOS* (line 6857; SUGGESTED *Marrubium pseudodictamnus*, "White Horehound, Bastard Dittany"), whereas the text's own horehound chapter is *3-119. PRASION* (line 7607), which is the chapter just above this one. The existing entry I_1129 (*Marrubium pseudodictamnus*) records this as "an odd cross-reference" shared by 3-120 and four other chapters, and states that "The Codex does not resolve it." The present entry takes over that remark and does not resolve it either; the comparison is Dioscorides' own, and the identification of the plant it is compared with is the translators'. ### Active Components

The chapter prints none. It is a general fact that *Stachys* (the woundworts and betonies) is a genus of the mint family (Lamiaceae), whose members are often aromatic. The chapter says "sweet scent" of this plant. The composition of the plant Dioscorides describes was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides reports a warming, sharp shrub whose leaf decoction, drunk, brings on the menstrual flow and expels the afterbirth. This is an obstetric and gynaecological use, the text's report only; the identification is the translators' and the Codex gives no protocol. A drink that expels the afterbirth or provokes the menses is not for self-treatment, and in pregnancy it is a risk.

### Preparation & Dosing

3-120 gives one preparation: a decoction of the leaves "(taken as a drink)". It gives no quantity, frequency or duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints two qualities: "it is warming and sharp". It prints the scent ("sweet scent") as a feature of the leaf, not as a quality of the drug. It prints no moistening or drying quality.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use. What it offers is of the kind the cultivation spec calls a discipline of attention: the plant is described only by difference from a plant the reader is assumed to know (longer, paler, shoots from the same root), with attention to leaf texture (thin, rough, hard), colour (white) and scent (sweet), to the ground it grows on, and to the quality that explains the use ("warming and sharp ... As a result"). The plant is given no status beyond that of a warming, sharp shrub with an obstetric use. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The text describes a plant by comparison with another and then gives one use that follows from its quality. The use is an obstetric one and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

A drink that expels the menstrual flow and afterbirth is dangerous in pregnancy and is not a matter for self-treatment; late or absent periods and retained afterbirth need medical assessment. The chapter states no caution of its own. The identity of the plant rests on a translators' suggestion and a comparison with a plant whose own identity is cross-referenced oddly, so no wild plant should be gathered or taken on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
