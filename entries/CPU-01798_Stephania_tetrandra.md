# Stephania tetrandra — Fang Ji 防己 (Shennong Bencao Jing)

## *Stephania tetrandra, as the pharmaceutical Latin Yang 1998 prints for Fang Ji, "Radix Stephaniae Tetrandrae", matched to the binomial by stem in the extraction; Yang's note 179 says there are two species under the one name, Mu Fang Ji (Cocculus trilobus) and Han Fang Ji (Stephania tetrandra)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Shennong text (SBJ); this entry rests on one text, the Shennong Bencao Jing in Yang Shou-zhong's 1998 translation, and on one entry in it.** The text is `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`. A search of every text folder on disk for *Stephania* found two other hits (the text folders are the .txt files; the Dioscorides, Ebers, Assyrian and Clark texts have none) and neither is read as this plant. The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints "Stephania sp." (line 5428) in the "L" block, where column order sets it against the Tibetan drug "Laetae" (a different drug in another tradition). Bhishagratna's Sushruta appendix prints "Stephania hernandifolia" (Vol. III, line 31229), which `Sources/SS_Sushruta/extract/names.csv` (line 370) pairs by position with the Sanskrit "Ambashtha", a different species in another tradition. Neither is read as Fang Ji. The Latin name is the translator's pharmaceutical Latin and was matched to the binomial by stem in the extraction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv`, line 136: "pharmaceutical Latin 'Radix Stephaniae Tetrandrae' matched by stem to on-disk binomial 'Stephania tetrandra'"). The Chinese characters 防己 are given in the Codex's own list (`_sources/shennong.csv`, line 175: "Stephania Root (防己 Fáng Jǐ)") and in its reconstructed Chinese file (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/SBJ_Original_Chinese.md`, line 475, a reconstruction from Tao Hongjing's recension, cited here for the characters only); Yang's English text prints no characters. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** The row is keyed by the name as Yang prints it, *Fang Ji*, which is the first name of the entry and the only one the extraction records; the entry's other name (below) has no row of its own. The locus in the extraction is "Herbs: Middle Class, PDF p.80 (line 2986)".

**Identity doubt.** Yang's own note 179 (lines 3004 to 3009) opens the question the entry's Latin name closes: "There are two species of Fang Ji-Mu Fang Ji (Cocculus Trilobi) and Han Fang Ji (Stephania Tetrandra). In olden times, Mu Fang Ji and Han Fang Ji were distinguished in different ways. In some old classics, they were regarded even as the same plant, and, according to this approach, the distinction between the two was simply that Mu Fang Ji was the root, while Han Fang Ji was the aerial plant. In modern prescriptions, no differentiation is made between them." So the translator names two plants for one drug, the headword's Latin ("Radix Stephaniae Tetrandrae") chooses one, and the entry itself says nothing that would decide between them. The Codex keeps the spine's binomial and records that the classic's drug may not be a *Stephania*. [USER: verify]

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *Fang Ji, PDF page 80.* The page marker `[[p80]]` is at line 2978 and the page's running head is the book's title (line 2979). The entry follows Kuan Dong (Flos Tussilaginis Farfarae) and precedes Nu Wan (Radix Asteris Fastigiati). It reads, at lines 2986 to 2992:

"Fang Ji (Radix Stephaniae Tetrandrae)179 is acrid and balanced. It mainly treats wind and cold, warm malaria, heat qi, and various [kinds of] epilepsy. It eliminates evils [through] disinhibiting urination and defecation. Its other name is lie Li (Wheel Spoke). It grows in rivers and valleys."

(The printed line breaks, which the OCR splits in the middle of the second sentence, are removed. "lie Li" is the OCR's spelling of the other name, whose first letter is probably a J; the Codex does not correct it. "Wheel Spoke" is Yang's gloss.) The entry gives the taste and nature, four indication groups, one action, one other name and the habitat. It states **no toxicity**: it neither says "nontoxic" nor "toxic".

The reconstructed Chinese file's rendering of the same entry (line 476, "味辛，平。主風寒溫瘧，熱氣諸癰，除邪，利大小便。") has the English gloss "heat qi and all welling-abscesses" where Yang's English reads "heat qi, and various [kinds of] epilepsy". The two differ on one indication. The reconstruction is a later assembly from Tao Hongjing's recension and Yang translates his own source text (his commentary on the classes calls it "our source text", line 365), so the Codex follows Yang for the entry and does not decide which reading the classic had. [USER: verify]

Yang's note 179 continues (lines 3010 to 3014) with the translator's modern commentary and not the classic: "This medicinal is specific for the foot tai yang channel but it is able to penetrate all 12 channels. It frees the interstices, disinhibits the nine orifices, and drains damp heat from the blood division of the lower burner. Therefore, it treats water swelling, generalized swelling (i.e., edema), wind water with panting and coughing, and cold damp feet leading eventually to swollen feet." None of these indications is in the classic's entry, and they are not carried into this register.

*The class headings.* Yang's table of contents prints "Herbs: Middle Class" (line 716). The section's opening heading is printed at line 2263 (`[[p59]]` at line 2262). The running head "Herbs: Middle Class" recurs on later pages; it stands on page 79 (line 2948, `[[p79]]` at line 2947) and on page 81 (line 3020, `[[p81]]` at line 3019), the pages either side of the entry's own. The next section heading, "Herbs: Inferior Class", is at line 3157 (`[[p85]]` at line 3156). The preface (lines 385 to 390) says of the middle class: "There are 120 medium class medicinals which are used as ministers. They mainly nurture personality and correspond to humanity. They mayor may not be toxic, and [therefore,] one should weigh and ponder before putting them to their appropriate use. If one intends to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion, one should base [one's efforts] on the middle class." ("mayor may not" is the OCR's joining of "may or may not".) Yang's commentary (lines 358 to 362) says that middle class medicinals "are able to cultivate personality or modify temperament" and, "since this class of medicinals may be toxic, their prescription requires care". His note on "personality" (lines 404 to 405) reads: "It was believed that some medicinals are effective for treating disease and, at the same time, are good for the cultivation of various virtues in human beings."

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Stephania*, *Fang Ji*, *Han Fang*, *Mu Fang* and *Radix Stephaniae* found no entry on this plant. I_035 (*Aristolochia clematitis*) names *Stephania* in the context of a species substitution (its lines 20, 67 and 69, on *Aristolochia fangchi* and *Stephania tetrandra*) and is not an entry on this plant. I_367 (Patha, *Cissampelos pareira*) is the entry for a different Sushruta drug. The neighbouring Kuan Dong and Nu Wan entries on the same page are separate drugs. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Stephania* belongs to the moonseed family (Menispermaceae), that the root of *Stephania tetrandra* is known in the pharmacological literature to contain bisbenzylisoquinoline alkaloids, among them tetrandrine and fangchinoline, and that *Cocculus*, the other genus Yang's note names, is a member of the same family. These are general statements and not findings of the Shennong text. Which compounds the root named by the classic held is not shown by the lines read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record, with a continuing use of the root in Chinese medicine under the name Fang Ji, if Yang's note 179 is read as a description of the modern practice. The Shennong entry lists indications in its own terms (wind and cold, warm malaria, heat qi, epilepsy) which are not modern diagnoses, and these conditions need medical care. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. The entry's silence on toxicity is not a statement that the root is safe: the preface says the middle class "may or may not be toxic" and requires weighing. A drug sold under the name Fang Ji has been confused with an unrelated, kidney-toxic plant (see Contraindications), and nothing here supports gathering or using it.

### Preparation & Dosing

The entry gives no preparation, no part beyond the root implied by the Latin "Radix", no quantity, no frequency and no duration. The text gives only taste, nature, indications, one action, one other name and the habitat. The note's mention of "root" and "aerial plant" (note 179) concerns the old disagreement over what Fang Ji is, and is not a preparation. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

- **SBJ:** "acrid and balanced". The entry prints no statement of toxicity and no meridian or other system of later Chinese medicine. Yang's note 179 adds modern formulations ("specific for the foot tai yang channel", "drains damp heat"), which are the translator's and are not the classic's.

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: the middle class (中品), by position.* Fang Ji stands in the section headed "Herbs: Middle Class" (heading at line 2263; running heads at lines 2948 and 3020 on the pages either side of its own; next section heading at line 3157). It is **not** directly under the heading: the heading is on page 59 and the entry on page 80, whose own running head is the book's title, so the grade rests on its position within the section and not on standing beneath a heading. In the preface (lines 385 to 390) the middle class "mainly nurture[s] personality", "may or may not be toxic" and calls for weighing, and is the class on which one bases the effort "to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion". The cultivation spec places the middle class between the upper grade (nourishing life, taken long-term) and the lower grade (attacking disease, short course), and the Codex's reconstructed Chinese preface (`SBJ_Original_Chinese.md`, line 17) names the middle class's charge with the characters 養性, the same ground. The entry's own wording is closest to "control disease": it treats wind and cold, warm malaria, heat qi and epilepsy, and "eliminates evils" through freeing urination and defecation. It says nothing of supplementing vacuity, long-term use, "making the body light", nourishing life, personality or temperament, or any change of jing, qi or shen, and none is asserted here. The class position rests on the OCR text of Yang's headings and has not been checked against a page image. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** In the Chinese scheme this is a middle-grade drug: weighed, used for a defined complaint, and with no statement in its entry that it is for long use or that it is safe. The class's own warning ("may or may not be toxic ... weigh and ponder") is the operative one, and the entry gives no dose. The identity rests on a name matched by stem, and the translator's note shows that the name has covered two plants. Nothing here supports long-term use.

### Contraindications

The Shennong entry prints no warning and no statement of toxicity. The middle class is described in the preface as one that "may or may not be toxic" and that calls for weighing before use, and this is a general statement about the class and not about this plant. Malaria, epilepsy and urinary or bowel complaints need medical care. It is a well-established general fact that preparations sold as Fang Ji have in some cases contained species of *Aristolochia*, whose aristolochic acids are kidney-toxic and carcinogenic; the Codex's entry I_035 records this under its heading on species confusion (lines 20, 67 and 69), and the primary reports it cites were not opened for this entry. The identity here is a name matched by stem, so no root should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
