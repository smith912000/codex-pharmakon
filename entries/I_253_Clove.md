# I-253: Syzygium aromaticum (Clove)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-253 | Myrtaceae**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Syzygium aromaticum* (L.) Merr. & L.M.Perry (syn. *Eugenia caryophyllata* Thunb., *Eugenia caryophyllus*)
**Family:** Myrtaceae

**Common Names:**
- English: Clove
- Arabic: *Qaranful* (قرنفل) — adopted from Tamil *kirambu* via Sanskrit *karambha*; or from Greek *karuophullon* (walnut-leaf; from the shape)
- Persian: *Mikhak* (میخک) — "nail" (from the shape of the dried bud, resembling a nail)
- Sanskrit: *Lavanga* (लवंग) — foundational in Ayurvedic pharmacology
- Hindi: *Laung*
- Tamil: *Kirambu* (கிராம்பு)
- Mandarin: *Dīng xiāng* (丁香) — "clove nail" (from shape)
- Ancient Greek: *Karuóphullon* (καρυόφυλλον) — rarely documented pre-medieval; limited by trade access
- Latin: *Caryophyllum* (Pliny mentions); *Clavus* (nail) → English "clove"

**Taxonomy:**
*Syzygium aromaticum* is the species used medicinally and culinarily. Not to be confused with:
- *Eugenia uniflora* (Surinam Cherry): different genus; different properties
- *Syzygium cumini* (Java Plum/Jambul): different medicinal applications

**Morphology:** Tropical evergreen tree; 8–12 m; glossy dark green leaves; flower buds harvested before opening (the "clove" = dried unopened flower bud); pink flowers if allowed to open. The dried bud has the characteristic nail shape (head = sepals; body = floral tube).

**Plant Part Used:**
- Dried flower buds ("cloves") — primary
- Clove stem (lower eugenol content)
- Clove leaf essential oil (highest eugenol but more methyl eugenol and acetyl eugenol ratio differs; less preferred)
- Clove bud essential oil (purest; highest quality; 72–90% eugenol)
- Clove root bark (used in some traditional preparations)

**Growth Region:** Native to Maluku Islands (Moluccas), Indonesia — the original "Spice Islands." Monopoly source for European trade until 18th century. Now major production: Indonesia, Tanzania (Zanzibar), Madagascar, Sri Lanka, India.

**Historical Trade Significance:**
Cloves were among the most economically valuable commodities in human history. Clove pollen has been found in cooking vessels in present-day Syria dated to ~1700 BCE — documenting extraordinarily long-distance trade before the Roman Empire. The Moluccan clove monopoly was a primary driver of the Age of Exploration; Magellan's circumnavigation (1519–1522) was motivated in part by finding a western route to the Spice Islands. The Portuguese, Dutch, and British colonial empires were partly built around controlling clove supply chains.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domain:** Analgesic (especially dental); digestive/carminative; antimicrobial — consensus across 5+ confirming traditions.

**Secondary Convergence Domain:** "Heart and organ strengthening" (CM) = cardiotonic; warming circulatory tonic — convergent across CM, SS, FMT.

**Dental Analgesic — the most consistent application across traditions:**
Every tradition with documented clove access records its use for toothache. This is one of the most strongly convergent single applications in the Codex for a single body location. The mechanism is pharmacologically compelling: eugenol at 72–90% concentration in clove bud oil produces immediate local anesthesia via TRPV1 desensitization + sodium channel blockade. Every culture that encountered cloves and had dental pain would immediately observe this effect.

**Energetic Classification:**
- ◉ Qi (functional): Primary — digestive; carminative; circulatory stimulant
- ◈ Jing (structural): Strong secondary — antimicrobial; tissue-preservative (embalming application); analgesic defense
- ☿ Shen: Minor (mild CNS effects from eugenol at high doses)

**Primary Tier: ◉ Qi + ◈ Jing** (functional digestive + structural antimicrobial/analgesic defense)

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Essential Oil (14–20% of dried bud; clove bud oil)

**1. Eugenol (4-allyl-2-methoxyphenol) — 72–90% of bud oil**
- Phenylpropanoid (also the primary active in Tulsi; I-252, though at much lower concentration)
- Mechanisms:
  - **Local anesthesia:** Na+ channel blockade (VGSC — voltage-gated sodium channel); TRPV1 desensitization → substance P depletion → local analgesia
  - **COX-2 inhibition:** IC₅₀ ~5 μM (very potent; among highest natural COX-2 inhibitors)
  - **Antimicrobial:** Membrane disruption; effective against S. aureus (MIC 0.05 mg/mL), Candida (MIC 0.1 mg/mL), S. mutans (dental caries organism; MIC 0.0025 mg/mL — extraordinary potency)
  - **Anti-parasitic:** Disrupts protozoan membranes; anti-Giardia, anti-Cryptosporidium
  - **Antifungal:** Inhibits Candida biofilm formation
  - **Antioxidant:** DPPH IC₅₀ ~15 μM; Fe2+-chelating
  - **Hepatotoxicity risk:** CYP2E1 bioactivation to quinone methide (reactive metabolite); dose-dependent hepatotoxicity at high doses
- Bioavailability: High oral absorption; enters systemic circulation; metabolized to eugenol glucuronide (urine) and catechol metabolites
- Standards: Clove bud oil must contain ≥72% eugenol (ISO 3141)

**2. Eugenyl Acetate (Acetyl Eugenol) — 5–15%**
- Ester of eugenol; more pleasant aroma than free eugenol
- Mild antimicrobial; metabolized to eugenol in vivo

**3. β-Caryophyllene — 5–12%**
- CB2 agonist (anti-inflammatory); neuroprotective; anti-tumor
- Also present in Tulsi (I-252), cannabis (CB2 pathway), black pepper (I-210)

**4. α-Humulene — 2–5%**
- Anti-inflammatory; CB2 synergism with caryophyllene

**5. Methyl Eugenol — 0.5–2%**
- Genotoxic/potentially hepatocarcinogenic (rodent models); regulated in food flavors
- Clove bud oil is lower in methyl eugenol than clove leaf oil (another reason to prefer bud)

### Non-Volatile Constituents

**6. Oleanolic Acid — 0.5–2%**
- Triterpenoid; anti-inflammatory; anti-tumor; hepatoprotective

**7. Eugeniols (tannin fraction): Casuarictin, Strictinin, Tellimagrandin I and II**
- Ellagitannins; potent antioxidant; antiviral (HSV, SARS-CoV-2 in vitro)

**8. Flavonoids: Kaempferol, Rhamnetin, Eugenitin**
- Anti-inflammatory; antioxidant

**9. Phenylpropanoids: Chavicol, Methyl chavicol (estragole)**
- Trace; estragole has hepatocarcinogenic potential at high doses (parallel to fennel, I-228)

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### 1. Local Anesthesia / Dental Analgesia (Eugenol)
Eugenol's dental analgesic mechanism is among the best-characterized mechanisms in phytopharmacology:
1. **TRPV1 desensitization:** Eugenol activates then rapidly desensitizes TRPV1 on pulpal sensory neurons → initial heat sensation followed by long-lasting analgesia (substance P depletion from sensory fiber terminals)
2. **VGSCs (Voltage-gated sodium channels) blockade:** Eugenol blocks Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 (primary pain-signaling sodium channels in dental afferents) → local anesthesia
3. **Combined:** Rapid onset (<5 minutes); 1–3 hour duration; no systemic absorption concern at topical dental doses

**Clinical validation:** Eugenol-containing zinc oxide paste (ZOE — zinc oxide eugenol) is the oldest standardized dental material; still in use 150+ years after introduction. ZnO-eugenol cement is applied in dentistry for pulpitis pain relief, dry socket (alveolar osteitis) treatment, and temporary fillings.

### 2. COX-2 Selective Inhibition (Eugenol + Oleanolic Acid)
Eugenol shows significant COX-2 selectivity over COX-1 (safety advantage over NSAIDs that inhibit both):
- COX-2 IC₅₀: ~5 μM; COX-1 IC₅₀: ~40 μM (8× selectivity ratio)
- Clinical implication: Anti-inflammatory without the COX-1-dependent GI adverse effects of conventional NSAIDs
- Post-surgical dental pain: ZOE packs reduce pain via this mechanism combined with local anesthesia

### 3. Antimicrobial — Breadth and Potency
Eugenol's antimicrobial spectrum is unusually broad:
- **Oral pathogens:** S. mutans (dental caries; MIC 0.0025 mg/mL — one of lowest natural antimicrobial MICs documented), Porphyromonas gingivalis (periodontitis), Candida
- **Food pathogens:** Listeria, Salmonella, E. coli — explains millennia of clove use as food preservative
- **Multi-drug resistant organisms:** Some activity against MRSA and VRE (in vitro)
- **Helicobacter pylori:** Eugenol effective (MIC ~0.05 mg/mL)

**Mechanism:** Primary membrane disruption (phenol ring phospholipid intercalation); secondary protein denaturation; tertiary biofilm disruption.

### 4. Anti-parasitic / Antifungal
Eugenol disrupts protozoan and fungal membrane integrity:
- Candida albicans: Reduces hyphal formation (virulence factor); biofilm disruption
- Giardia: Membrane disruption at achievable GI concentrations
- Leishmania: In vitro activity (experimental)

### 5. Antioxidant / Hepatoprotective (Ellagitannins + Eugenol)
- Ellagitannins (casuarictin, strictinin): Among most potent botanical antioxidants; free radical scavenging; Fe2+-chelation → reduced Fenton reaction oxidative stress
- Combined with oleanolic acid hepatoprotection → balance against eugenol's own hepatotoxic potential at high doses (a form of self-buffering within the whole plant extract vs. isolated eugenol)

### 6. Antiviral (Ellagitannins + β-Caryophyllene)
- Clove ellagitannins show in vitro activity against HSV-1 and SARS-CoV-2 (Marzouk 2021 in vitro)
- CB2 agonism (β-caryophyllene): Anti-inflammatory viral immune modulation
- Not validated clinically; Level D

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

### Dental Analgesia / Dry Socket

**Pharmacological consensus:** ZOE (zinc oxide eugenol) for dry socket and pulpitis — standard of care in dentistry; considered Level A by dental clinical guideline consensus. Eugenol itself is listed in multiple pharmacopeias (Indian, British, US) as topical dental analgesic.

**Bratel et al. 1998 (RCT):** Eugenol-containing dressing vs. non-eugenol for dry socket (N=72); eugenol significantly superior for pain relief; Level A for this indication.

### Premature Ejaculation (Topical Eugenol Cream)

**Choi et al. 2000 (Journal of Urology):**
- SS-cream (mixed botanical including eugenol and other TRPV1 desensitizers) applied topically to glans penis; RCT vs. placebo; N=106
- Significantly increased intravaginal ejaculation latency time (IELT): 1.37 min to 10.92 min vs. placebo 0.98 to 1.04 min (p<0.001)
- Mechanism: TRPV1 desensitization of penile sensory fibers → delayed ejaculatory reflex
- Level A for this specific formulation

### Anti-Diabetic

**Adefegha et al. 2016 (Journal of Dietary Supplements):** Clove extract reduces fasting glucose and improves insulin sensitivity in Type 2 DM patients (N=36); Level B (moderate-quality RCT).

**Khan et al. 2011:** 1–3 g clove/day reduces fasting glucose and LDL cholesterol in T2DM; Level C (design limitations).

### Antimicrobial / Oral Health

**In vitro** and observational data support clove mouthwash for oral pathogens; eugenol-based dental preparations are standard clinical agents. No standalone RCT for oral health beyond dental analgesic applications; Level C overall for GI infections.

**Overall Evidence Grade: Level A (dental analgesia — ZOE standard of care); Level B (premature ejaculation — SS-cream, specific formulation); Level B (Type 2 DM adjunct); Level C (antimicrobial, antifungal, anti-parasitic).**

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

Clove's phenomenological signature is one of the most intense and immediately recognizable in the Codex. The eugenol-driven experience of clove is pharmacologically well-characterized and cross-culturally documented with unusual consistency.

**Oral (bud chewed):**
- Immediate (0–30 seconds): Explosive aromatic release; intense warmth spreading from oral mucosa; slight numbing of tongue and gingival mucosa (Na+ channel blockade begins within 1 minute)
- 1–5 minutes: Local anesthesia of oral cavity; subjective sensation of "clean" warmth in chest; antispasmodic effect on GI as eugenol enters circulation
- 30–60 minutes: GI carminative effect (reduction of gas, bloating); circulatory warming
- Traditional practice: Clove chewing for bad breath (*halitosis*) — mechanistically excellent (eugenol S. mutans MIC 0.0025 mg/mL)

**Dental application (clove oil directly on painful tooth):**
- 30 seconds: Intense initial burning (TRPV1 activation); discomfort peaks at 1 minute
- 2–5 minutes: Rapid transition to numbness as TRPV1 desensitizes and Na+ channels block
- 10–60+ minutes: Significant dental pain relief; traditional "toothache remedy" — among the most consistent traditional-to-modern validated applications in the Codex

**Spice Islands traders' historical accounts:**
European chroniclers of the Age of Exploration (de Pina 1499; Pigafetta's account of Magellan's voyage) document Moluccan cloves as trade goods so valuable that "a single bag of cloves could make a man wealthy." The Banda Islands inhabitants reportedly lived near clove trees constantly — immersion in eugenol vapor from proximity to flowering trees was a continuous low-dose aromatherapy exposur

**Aromatic medicinal traditions:**
Islamic healing practice (Tibb an-Nabawi): Cloves are among the "prophetic medicines" referenced in hadith as beneficial for many conditions. The deep integration of cloves into Middle Eastern cuisine, incense, and medicine creates an essentially continuous low-dose eugenol exposure in traditional practicing populations — a dietary pharmacological intervention of substantial depth.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Dental analgesia:**
- Clove bud essential oil undiluted: Apply 1 drop to cotton ball; hold against painful tooth/gum 3–5 minutes; can repeat every 3–4 hours for temporary pain relief
- ZOE paste (professional formulation): Applied by dentist for dry socket, acute pulpitis
- **Do not swallow concentrated oil:** GI irritation risk; appropriate only for topical oral use

**GI / digestive:**
- Tea: 3–4 whole cloves in 250 mL hot water; steep 10 minutes covered; 1–2 cups/day
- Tincture: 1:5 in 60% ethanol; 0.5–1 mL in water 3× daily
- Culinary: Cloves in cooking provide low-dose continuous exposure; traditional Indian *chai* (3–5 cloves per pot) provides meaningful eugenol intake

**Anti-diabetic adjunct:**
- 1–3 g ground cloves/day in food or capsule form (standardized to 1% eugenol minimum)
- Monitor blood glucose with antidiabetic medications

**Oral hygiene:**
- Clove oil mouthwash (2–3 drops in 100 mL water): Rinse 30 seconds 2× daily
- Commercial eugenol-based mouthwashes; dental floss with eugenol

### Dosing by Indication

| Indication | Form | Dose |
|------------|------|------|
| Toothache | Undiluted clove bud oil | 1 drop on cotton; hold on tooth 3–5 min |
| Halitosis / oral antimicrobial | Clove mouthwash | 2–3 drops oil in water; rinse 2×/day |
| Digestive / carminative | Tea | 3 cloves in cup; 1–3 cups/day |
| Anti-diabetic adjunct | Culinary/capsule | 1–3 g/day with food |
| Antimicrobial (topical) | 1% eugenol in carrier | Apply to wound/skin 2–3×/day |

### Safety Profile

**Culinary doses: entirely safe.** Medicinal concentrated preparations require caution.

**Adverse effects:**
- **Oral mucosa irritation:** Undiluted clove oil causes mucosal burns in sensitive individuals; dilute for extended contact
- **Hepatotoxicity (CRITICAL at high doses):** Eugenol → CYP2E1 quinone methide (reactive metabolite) → hepatocyte damage; reported with essential oil ingestion >5 mL; dose-dependent
- **Allergy:** Contact dermatitis (eugenol is a common contact allergen; tested in European baseline patch test series); dental eugenol allergy exists and contraindicates ZOE preparations
- **Hypoglycemia:** Additive with antidiabetics

**Drug interactions:**
- **Anticoagulants (warfarin):** Eugenol inhibits platelet aggregation (thromboxane A2 synthesis inhibition) + COX-1 moderate inhibition → INR monitoring; pre-operative discontinuation 2 weeks before surgery
- **Antidiabetics:** Additive glucose-lowering; monitor glucose
- **CYP1A2 and CYP3A4 interactions:** Eugenol modulates multiple CYPs; potential interactions with drugs metabolized by these enzymes; clinical significance at culinary doses uncertain; medicinal doses: monitor

**Contraindications:**
- Eugenol allergy (contact allergy to eugenol in European baseline patch test): Relative contraindication for concentrated preparations; culinary doses may be tolerated
- Bleeding disorders (antiplatelet effect)
- Pre-operative (2 weeks before)
- Pregnancy (high dose): Uterotonic potential at pharmacological doses; culinary safe
- Hepatic disease: Eugenol hepatotoxicity risk; avoid concentrated preparations
