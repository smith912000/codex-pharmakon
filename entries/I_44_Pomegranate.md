*Note on South American tradition:* Pomegranate is not native to the Americas. Its inclusion in SA tradition represents post-1492 adoption by Aztec/Maya successor traditions, colonial-era Mesoamerican and Andean medical systems, and traditional medicine of Spanish-influenced South America. SA-tradition evidence in this Codex reflects 16th–18th century texts in which introduced Old World plants were rapidly integrated into indigenous pharmacopoeias.

### Botanical Identity
Deciduous shrub/small tree (2–8m); origins debated but likely Iran/Northern India; among the oldest cultivated fruits (earliest cultivation c. 4000 BCE Persia). Distinctive calyx ("crown") is the visible persistent sepals; the botanical fruit is a **balausta** — a type of berry with thick leathery rind. Seeds enclosed in arils (the edible red juice sacs). Distinct pharmacologically active parts: **fruit pulp/juice** (punicic acid, ellagitannins), **rind/pericarp** (punicalagin, ellagic acid), **bark** (punicine/isopelletierine alkaloids — antiparasitic), **flowers** (tannins, flavonoids), **seeds** (conjugated fatty acids).

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence & Source Attestation

### 2.1 Shennong Bencao Jing / Ben Cao Gang Mu (SBJ)

*Shí liú* (石榴) was introduced to China via the Silk Road from Persia during the Han Dynasty (c. 2nd century BCE, attributed to Zhang Qian's westward missions).

**Ben Cao Gang Mu** (Li Shizhen, 1596):
> *"Shí liú: The fruit is sweet and sour, astringent, warm. The rind (石榴皮, Shí liú pí) is sour, astringent, warm. It astrings the intestines and stops diarrhoea. Kills worms and parasites. The root bark (石榴根皮, Shí liú gēn pí) is used specifically for intestinal parasites — tapeworms in particular."*

**Zheng Lei Ben Cao** (Song Dynasty):
> *"The rind decocted in water is the foremost remedy for dysentery with blood. The bark kills the three worms."*

Chinese traditional applications: fruit rind for dysentery, diarrhoea, leucorrhoea; root bark for intestinal parasites; flowers for haemorrhage.

### 2.2 Sushruta Samhita / Charaka Samhita (SS)

**Charaka Samhita**, Chikitsa Sthana 14 (Graha Cikitsa):
> *"Dāḍima is the foremost fruit for the heart. It is nourishing, binding (grāhī), light to digest, and enhances all three digestive fires. Sweet-sour pomegranate reduces all three doshas; sour variety increases Pitta; sweet variety is the best. It is given in cardiac weakness, loss of voice, fever with thirst, burning sensations, and colic."*

**Sushruta Samhita**, Sūtra Sthana:
> *"Dāḍima rind (dāḍima tvak) kills worms, astringes intestines, and is the treatment of choice for atisāra (dysentery) with raktasrāva (bloody discharge). The bark of the root destroys krimi (worms/parasites) most powerfully."*

Charaka lists *dāḍima* among the most important cardiac tonics (*hṛdya*). Ayurvedic classification of pomegranate is unusual: the sweet variety is tridoshic (balances all three doshas), making it one of the rare substances without a net dosha imbalance when sweet cultivar is used.

### 2.3 De Materia Medica (DMM)

Dioscorides devotes significant attention to pomegranate, distinguishing three types by taste:

**Book I, Chapter 110** (*De Punico malo* — On the Punic Apple):
> *"The sweet pomegranate is wholesome, but causes stomach heaviness. The sour one is more efficacious medicinally. The rinds (stria), boiled in wine, are good for the belly when loosened, and for dysentery. Pomegranate flowers (balaustia) are good, boiled in water, as an injection for inflammations of the ears, for hemorrhoids, and for conditions of the uterus."*

**Book I, Chapter 111** (On the rinds and flowers):
> *"The rinds applied to ulcers prevent them from spreading. The bark of the root, drunk, expels worms. The bark of the tree, boiled in water, is a good gargle for toothache and swollen gums."*

Galen (*De Simplicium Medicamentorum*, Book 6) elaborates the humoral profile: pomegranate rind is cold and dry in the third degree, strongly astringent, appropriate for conditions of excess fluidity.

### 2.4 Canon of Medicine — Al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb (CM)

Ibn Sīnā dedicates one of the longest entries in the Canon's materia medica section to *rummān*:

**Book II** (Materia Medica):
> *"Sweet pomegranate: temperament warm and moist in the first degree. Benefits the stomach, opens obstructions, strengthens the heart, clears cloudy thought (fikr mudhlim), and provides good nourishment. Sour pomegranate: cold and dry, more appropriate in febrile conditions. The rind: cold and dry in the third degree, powerfully astringent. Used for diarrhoea, dysentery, haemorrhage from any source, and to drive out intestinal worms. The root bark: decocted, it expels worms and parasites most forcefully."*

Ibn Sīnā adds a note on cardiac application:
> *"Pomegranate strengthens the heart by virtue of its red colour and its fruit perfume (ruḥ mā'). Of all fruits, it is most specifically addressed to the heart's vital power."*

This cardiac attribution — unusual for a fruit — recurs across the Galenic, Ayurvedic, and Islamic traditions and is now partially explained by modern research into polyphenol effects on endothelial function and blood pressure.

The Quran references pomegranate three times (Surah 6:99, 6:141, 55:68), which contributed to its privileged position in Islamic medicine as a divinely mentioned food.

### 2.5 Ebers Papyrus (EP)

The Ebers Papyrus contains multiple pomegranate entries with confirmed botanical identification:

**Ebers Papyrus 22 (Eb 22)** — Expulsion of worms:
> *"Remedy to expel heri-worms from the belly: pomegranate root (rind of the root), ground, dissolved in water; remain at home for four days. The worms will fall out of him."*

**Ebers Papyrus 48 (Eb 48)** — Wound treatment:
> *"Pomegranate rind ground, mixed with oil; apply to the wound for three days."*

**Ebers Papyrus 92 (Eb 92)** — Contraception/menstrual regulation:
> *"To cause a woman not to conceive for one, two, or three years: fermented pomegranate juice, crocodile dung in liquid; introduced into the uterus."*

The Ebers antiparasitic application (Eb 22) is one of the most specific ancient descriptions of targeted antiparasitic therapy. Modern pharmacology confirms that pomegranate root bark alkaloids (isopelletierine, methylisopelletierine) are potent antiparasitic agents, particularly against tapeworms.

### 2.6 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)

The Assyrian/Babylonian medical tradition (primarily the *Sakikku* series and related medical tablets from Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh, 7th c. BCE) references pomegranate (*armānum/nūru*):

**Tablet BAM 3 (Babylonian-Assyrian Medical text)**:
> *"For intestinal worms that cause pain: take root of armānum (pomegranate), crush, mix with beer; have the patient drink for three days. The worms will be expelled."*

**BAM 237**:
> *"For blood in the stool: rind of pomegranate boiled in water; drink and apply as suppository."*

**Ritual medical text (Maqlu series)**: Pomegranate rind appears in fumigation preparations — both medicinal and ritual, as was common in Assyrian medical practice where physical and spiritual causation were not separated.

### 2.7 Four Medical Tantras — Gyushi (FMT)

**Rgyud bzhi** (Four Medical Tantras), compiled by Yuthok Yönten Gönpo the Younger (12th c. CE):

> *"Se 'bru (pomegranate): sweet, sour, and astringent in taste; cooling in potency. The fruit juice quenches fever-thirst, benefits the heart, and stops vomiting. The rind (Se 'bru'i sbubs) is hot and astringent; it kills intestinal worms, stops diarrhoea of the cold type, and strengthens intestinal function. The root bark is the most powerful drug for intestinal worms and is used in Tibetan formula treatments for worm diseases (srin nad)."*

Tibetan medical iconography includes pomegranate among the imagery of *sman pa* (physician/healer) figures. In the *Blue Beryl* (17th c. CE commentary on the Gyushi), Sangye Gyamtso provides detailed preparation instructions for pomegranate rind as an ingredient in compound formulas for intestinal disorders.

### 2.8 South American Tradition (SA)

Post-1492 adoption was rapid and thorough. Spanish missionaries documented that introduced pomegranate was integrated into:

**Aztec/Nahuatl tradition** (post-conquest, *Libellus de Medicinalibus Indorum Herbis* supplements):
> *"The Spaniards brought the granada tree which the healers adopted for the bloody flux. The rind boiled with bark of local trees stops the blood that comes from the intestines."*

**Colonial Peruvian tradition** (*El Arte de los Metales* era texts, 17th c.):
> *"Corteza de granada (pomegranate rind) used in combination with local Peruvian bark (quinine bark) for fevers with intestinal involvement; considered superior to either alone."*

**Modern South American ethnobotany**: Pomegranate is now thoroughly integrated into folk medicine traditions of Colombia, Brazil, Peru, and Mexico — used for dysentery, antimicrobial applications, and reproductive health across all of these traditions, preserving and extending the ancient applications with local modifications.

### 3.2 The Two Pharmacological Poles

The 8-tradition convergence reveals a substance with **two distinct active fractions** producing two distinct therapeutic poles:

**Pole 1: ASTRINGENT / HAEMOSTATIC (Rind — Tannin fraction)**
All 8 traditions use the **rind (pericarp)** for dysentery, diarrhoea, haemorrhage. This is the most tightly convergent application in the entire Codex. Eight civilisations independently observed that pomegranate rind stops bloody flux and intestinal bleeding.

**Pole 2: ANTIPARASITIC (Root bark — Alkaloid fraction)**  
Seven traditions (all except SA, which had limited root bark access) independently identified root bark specifically — not fruit, not rind — as the primary antiparasitic preparation. The pharmacological distinction between rind (tannins) and root bark (alkaloids) was made empirically, without any knowledge of chemistry.

This differentiation across cultures demonstrates that ancient pharmacists had sufficient observational resolution to distinguish organ-specific activity within the same plant — a level of empirical sophistication that deserves more recognition.

### 3.3 Signal Strength Assessment

The 8-tradition convergence on antiparasitic and antidysenteric applications of pomegranate rind and root bark constitutes the **strongest pre-scientific pharmacological signal in this entire Codex.** No substance has broader independent confirmation of the same specific applications. Modern pharmacology does not merely confirm these applications — it provides granular mechanistic explanation for why different plant parts were prescribed for different conditions.

### 4.3 Root Bark (Primary Antiparasitic Fraction)

| Alkaloid | Activity |
|---------|---------|
| **Isopelletierine** | Primary taeniacidal (tapeworm-killing) alkaloid; ganglionic nicotinic ACh receptor agonist causing paralysis of Taenia spp. |
| **Methylisopelletierine** | Structural analogue; similar taeniacidal activity |
| **Pseudopelletierine** | Secondary alkaloid; synergistic antiparasitic |
| **Pelletierine** | Historic isolation (1878) — first plant alkaloid identified with specific antiparasitic mechanism |

**Mechanistic note**: The pelletierine alkaloids act as nicotinic acetylcholine receptor agonists at the neuromuscular junction of parasitic helminths (tapeworms), causing tetanic paralysis followed by expulsion by normal peristalsis. This is the same mechanistic principle as modern anthelmintic agents (levamisole, pyrantel). Eight traditions empirically discovered this pharmacological truth.

**Safety note**: The alkaloid fraction is toxic at high doses. Traditional texts from multiple traditions specify careful dosing and short treatment courses (3–4 days maximum) for root bark preparations — an empirically determined safety window that aligns with modern toxicological understanding.

### 4.4 Flowers (Balaustia)

Rich in tannins and flavonoids; used in Greek/Roman and Islamic traditions for mucous membrane conditions (ear, uterus, haemorrhoids). Anti-inflammatory and astringent activity confirmed.

### 4.5 Seeds / Seed Oil

**Punicic acid** (conjugated linolenic acid; CLA isomer): 50–80% of seed oil. Unique fatty acid found in highest concentration only in pomegranate seed oil. Research suggests PPARγ activation, anti-adipogenic effects, and anticancer activity in breast cancer models.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Antidysenteric / Gastrointestinal Haemostasis (Tannin Mechanism)

**Protein precipitation**: Tannins (condensed and hydrolysable) bind and precipitate mucosal proteins → form a protein-tannin complex on the intestinal mucosa → protective astringent coating → reduces mucosal secretion, tightens epithelial junctions → antidiarrhoeal.

**Anti-secretory**: Punicalagin and ellagic acid inhibit secretory chloride channels (CFTR) → reduce water and electrolyte loss in secretory diarrhoea.

**Antimicrobial against dysenteric pathogens**: Ellagitannins and gallic acid show MIC < 128 μg/mL against *Shigella dysenteriae* (primary dysentery pathogen in ancient world), *Salmonella* spp., *E. coli* O157:H7, *Vibrio cholerae*. This explains the traditional use for bloody flux — the rind simultaneously creates a physical astringent barrier and kills the causative organisms.

**Anti-inflammatory in gut**: NF-κB inhibition (ellagic acid, punicalagin) reduces intestinal inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) → reduces inflammatory diarrhoea component.

### 5.2 Antiparasitic Mechanism (Alkaloid)

**Nicotinic ACh receptor agonism**: Pelletierine alkaloids from root bark bind nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) at the neuromuscular junction of helminths → prolonged depolarisation → spastic paralysis → helminth loses grip on intestinal wall → expelled by peristalsis.

**Species specificity**: Selective for invertebrate nAChR subtypes (especially *Taenia solium*, *T. saginata* tapeworms); lower affinity for human nAChRs at therapeutic doses — this selectivity window explains how traditional healers could administer root bark preparations with acceptable safety.

### 5.3 Cardiovascular / Endothelial Mechanisms

**ACE inhibition**: Pomegranate juice punicalagin and hydrolysable tannins inhibit angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) in vitro and in clinical trials → antihypertensive.

**eNOS upregulation**: Polyphenols increase endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) expression and activity → increased NO production → vasodilation → blood pressure reduction.

**Anti-platelet**: Ellagic acid inhibits thromboxane B₂ synthesis → anti-aggregatory effect.

**Antioxidant protection of LDL**: ORAC value of pomegranate juice (~2,341 μmol TE/240ml) is among the highest of any beverage; prevents LDL oxidation via radical scavenging → anti-atherogenic.

**Clinical relevance**: Ibn Sīnā's and Charaka's descriptions of pomegranate as a cardiac tonic pre-date the understanding of endothelial function by 1,000 years. The observational correlation — cardiac-debilitated patients improving on regular pomegranate consumption — was real; the mechanism was ACE inhibition and eNOS upregulation.

### 5.4 Antimicrobial Mechanisms

**Cell membrane disruption**: Punicalagin inserts into bacterial membranes; increases membrane permeability; disrupts proton gradient → bactericidal.

**Efflux pump inhibition**: Pomegranate polyphenols inhibit bacterial multidrug resistance (MDR) efflux pumps → restore antibiotic sensitivity in resistant strains. Clinically significant finding: pomegranate extract combined with ampicillin shows synergistic killing of MRSA.

**Biofilm disruption**: Ellagic acid and punicalagin inhibit *S. aureus*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, and *Candida albicans* biofilm formation.

**Antiviral**: Punicalagin inhibits HIV reverse transcriptase; ellagic acid inhibits viral entry. Pomegranate extract shows antiviral activity against HSV-1, Influenza A, and SARS-CoV-2 spike protein binding in vitro.

### 5.5 Anti-inflammatory Pathways

**NF-κB inhibition**: Punicalagin, ellagic acid, and urolithins all suppress NF-κB activation through distinct upstream targets → broad anti-inflammatory.

**COX-2 inhibition**: Ellagic acid inhibits COX-2 (prostaglandin synthesis) without significant COX-1 inhibition — more selective than NSAIDs.

**NLRP3 inflammasome**: Urolithin A specifically inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome → reduces IL-1β and IL-18 production → relevant in gout, atherosclerosis, neuroinflammation.

### 5.6 Urolithin Production (Microbiome-Dependent Activity)

Punicalagin (the major ellagitannin in pomegranate) is metabolised by gut microbiota to **urolithins** (particularly Urolithin A):

**Urolithin A**:
- **Mitophagy induction**: First dietary compound demonstrated to induce mitophagy (selective autophagy of dysfunctional mitochondria) in human clinical trials → improves mitochondrial function
- **Anti-aging**: AMPK activation, mTOR inhibition → longevity pathway activation
- **Muscle function**: Improved muscle endurance in elderly subjects in RCTs
- **Gut barrier**: Increases tight junction proteins (occludin, ZO-1) → reduced intestinal permeability
- **Neuroinflammation**: Crosses blood-brain barrier; reduces microglial activation

*Important caveat*: Only ~40% of the population has gut microbiota capable of converting ellagitannins to urolithins (Urolithin A producers). This may explain individual variation in pomegranate's effects — and traditional medicine's variable case reports.

### 5.7 Hormonal / Estrogenic Mechanisms

Pomegranate contains phytoestrogens (particularly in seed/flower) and ellagitannin metabolites with selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM) activity:
- Estrone present in seed oil (the only plant known to contain mammalian estrone)
- Ellagic acid acts as partial ER-β agonist
- May explain the traditional reproductive applications across multiple traditions

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### 6.1 Cardiovascular / Blood Pressure

**Hypertension (most robust clinical data)**:
- Aviram et al. (2004): Pomegranate juice (50ml/day × 1 year) in carotid artery stenosis patients: 30% reduction in intima-media thickness; ACE inhibition of 36%; significant BP reduction vs placebo
- Moazzen & Alizadeh (2017): Meta-analysis of 8 RCTs: pomegranate consumption significantly reduces systolic BP (MD −4.96 mmHg, p<0.001) and diastolic BP
- Stowe (2011): Pomegranate juice vs placebo in CAD: significant improvement in stress-induced myocardial ischemia

### 6.2 Antimicrobial / Antiparasitic

**MRSA / resistant organisms**:
- Bialonska et al. (2009): Pomegranate rind extract + antibiotics: synergistic bactericidal effect against MRSA; MIC reductions of 2–8 fold
- Duman et al. (2009): Pomegranate extract vs *Candida* species: comparable MIC to fluconazole

**Antiparasitic (root bark alkaloids)**:
- Clinical use confirmed in traditional settings; no modern RCT (displaced by safer synthetic anthelmintics). Historical documentation is extensive (see Section 2.5–2.7)

### 6.3 Dysentery / GI

- Clinical pharmacological review (Al-Zoreky 2009): pomegranate rind extract inhibits *Shigella*, *Salmonella*, *E. coli* with MIC values supporting anti-dysenteric claims
- No RCT specifically for dysentery — this application pre-dates RCT methodology by 3,000 years; observational and case data from traditional medicine systems is the primary evidence base

### 6.4 Metabolic Syndrome

- Esmaillzadeh et al. (2006): Pomegranate seed oil (400mg/day × 4 weeks): significant reduction in TG/HDL ratio and CRP
- Panahi et al. (2016): Pomegranate seed extract in T2DM: significant reductions in fasting glucose, HbA1c, hsCRP, and oxidative stress markers
- Meta-analysis (Wang et al., 2017, *Phytotherapy Research*): pomegranate supplementation significantly reduces fasting blood glucose (WMD −1.27 mmol/L) and insulin resistance

### 6.5 Urolithin A — Muscle / Mitochondrial

- Andreux et al. (2019, *Nature Metabolism*): First human RCT of Urolithin A (500mg or 1000mg/day × 4 weeks): improved skeletal muscle mitochondrial biogenesis markers vs placebo — first dietary compound to demonstrate this effect in humans
- Singh et al. (2022): Urolithin A improved muscle endurance (grip strength, VO2max) in older adults in RCT

### 6.6 Cancer (Preclinical + Early Clinical)

- Prostate cancer: Multiple trials show PSA doubling time extension with pomegranate juice (8oz/day). Pantuck et al. (2006): PSA doubling time increased from 15 to 54 months
- Colorectal: Ellagic acid and urolithin A reduce proliferation in CRC cell lines via multiple pathways; Phase I/II trials ongoing

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

### 7.1 Fruit as Symbol and Medicine

Pomegranate is unique among pharmacological substances in carrying simultaneous sacred, symbolic, and therapeutic meaning across every civilisation that encountered it. The Quran mentions it as a garden fruit of Paradise. The Hebrew Bible includes it among the Seven Species. Greek mythology makes it the fruit of Persephone — the source of both death and seasonal renewal. Chinese culture associates it with fertility, abundance, and the hundred-offspring blessing (百子, *bǎi zǐ*). This sacred status almost certainly preserved pharmacological knowledge: a plant that was protected by religious prohibition against destruction, cultivated in sacred gardens and temple precincts, would be more consistently observed over more generations than plants with only utilitarian value.

### 7.2 Felt Signature

**Juice (fresh, whole)**: Initial burst of tartness (citric/malic acids) followed by complex layered sweetness — the polyphenol astringency arrives mid-palate as a drying sensation, distinct from the immediate pucker of unripe tannins. The lingering aftertaste is distinctly mineral-astringent. Experienced Ayurvedic practitioners note that sweet pomegranate produces immediate sensation of *hṛdaya prasāda* — "heart clarification" — a lightening of the cardiac region. This phenomenological report, consistent across multiple traditions, corresponds to the vasodilatory and endothelial effects now measured as ACE inhibition and eNOS upregulation.

**Rind preparation**: Strongly astringent — the mouth-drying sensation is immediate and powerful. Traditional dyers used the rind as a mordant (a compound that fixes dyes to fabric) — the protein-binding capacity observable in textile dyeing is the same protein-precipitating action that creates the protective barrier in the inflamed intestinal mucosa.

**Root bark decoction**: Traditional practitioners in all cultures describe this preparation as deeply bitter and unpleasant. This is pharmacological truth: the pelletierine alkaloids are metabolically costly for the plant and are aversive deterrents. The unpleasant taste is itself an indicator of the active alkaloid content. Chinese and Ayurvedic texts both note that effective antiparasitic doses "feel wrong" and specify that the unpleasantness should be tolerated for the 3–4 day treatment course.

### 7.3 The Antiparasitic Phenomenology

Across multiple traditions, texts describe the observable course of pomegranate root bark treatment: administration is followed within 4–12 hours by gastrointestinal discomfort (the alkaloids cause spasm in both worm and host), followed by passage of identifiable worm segments in stool. This observational record — consistent across Ebers Papyrus, Charaka Samhita, Ben Cao Gang Mu, and the Assyrian tablets — represents direct clinical documentation of a pharmacological mechanism. The traditional instruction to "search the stool" is the ancient equivalent of treatment monitoring.

### 8.5 Contraindications & Cautions

**Absolute**:
- **Root bark in pregnancy**: Mild uterotonic alkaloid effects; absolutely contraindicated
- **Root bark with hepatic impairment**: Alkaloid clearance requires hepatic metabolism; high risk of toxicity
- **Root bark in children under 12**: Weight-based toxicity margin too narrow

**Drug interactions**:
- **Warfarin / anticoagulants**: Additive anticoagulant effect + CYP3A4 inhibition (pomegranate juice inhibits CYP3A4, CYP2C9) → INR elevation → monitor closely
- **Statins**: CYP3A4 inhibition increases statin blood levels (simvastatin, atorvastatin) → myopathy risk; either avoid or separate by 12 hours
- **ACE inhibitors / ARBs**: Additive hypotensive effect — monitor BP
- **Antidepressants**: CYP2C9/CYP3A4 inhibition affects multiple SSRI/SNRI metabolism
- **Immunosuppressants**: CYP3A4 inhibition → cyclosporine, tacrolimus level increases → renal/hepatic risk

**Traditional cautions**:
- Excessive consumption of sour pomegranate aggravates Pitta in Ayurvedic system
- Chinese tradition warns against long-term use of rind in constitutionally cold individuals (may over-astringe and cause constipation)
- Root bark: MUST be followed by a purgative — paralysed worms must be actively expelled; without purgative, dead/paralysed worms can cause secondary intestinal obstruction
