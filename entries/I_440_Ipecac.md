## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary name:** Ipecac, Ipecacuanha
**Synonyms:** *Cephaelis ipecacuanha* (former name), *Psychotria ipecacuanha* (another former placement), Ipecac root
**Binomial:** *Carapichea ipecacuanha* (Brot.) L.Andersson
**Authority:** Recently reclassified by Lars Andersson 2002 from *Cephaelis* → *Carapichea* based on molecular phylogenetics
**Etymology:** *Ipecacuanha* from Tupi (Brazilian indigenous language): *ipe-kaa-guéne* = "small plant by the roadside that causes vomiting." The name encodes the primary pharmacological action.

**Botanical description:** Small perennial shrublet 20–40 cm from thick horizontal rhizome. Leaves opposite, elliptic-ovate, 7–12 cm, dark glossy green above, pale below. Flowers small, white, tubular, in terminal heads subtended by bracts. Fruit: small dark purple drupes. Root/rhizome: branched, contorted, with characteristic annular rings from ruptured bark (diagnostic feature visible under hand lens). Native: humid forest floors of Brazil (Mato Grosso do Sul), Bolivia, Colombia. Now threatened in wild due to overharvesting.

**Pharmacognostic parts used:** Rhizome and root (*Ipecacuanhae radix*), dried and powdered; alkaloid-purified emetine and cephaeline fractions

**History of taxonomy confusion:** The plant has been moved between multiple genera over 200 years of botanical reclassification. Current consensus: *Carapichea ipecacuanha*. Pharmacopoeial name remains *Ipecacuanhae radix* — the genus name change does not require renaming the drug.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

Ipecac represents a **pharmacologically complete clinical arc**: from indigenous dysentery remedy → European emetic/expectorant → pharmaceutical emetine for amoebiasis → superseded by metronidazole → current use restricted primarily to emetine for refractory amoebiasis cases and ipecac syrup historically for poisoning (now largely abandoned).

**The dysentery discovery:**
*Entamoeba histolytica* dysentery killed millions throughout history. Brazilian Tupi peoples used ipecac root for this condition — the Tupi name encoding the emetic action that was one component of its effect. A Dutch merchant, Piso, described the remedy in 1648 (*De Medicina Brasiliensi*). Louis XIV purchased the secret formula (Helvetius's ipecac-opium powder, "Dover's Powder") in 1688 for 1,000 louis d'or — one of the earliest documented pharmaceutical trade secrets. Emetine, isolated 1817 (Pelletier & Magendie), remained the standard anti-amoebic treatment for 150 years until metronidazole (1960s).

**The emesis-expectorant duality:**
At low doses (expectorant: 2 mg emetine), ipecac stimulates respiratory mucus secretion without vomiting. At higher doses (emetic: >10 mg), it causes vomiting. This dose-response duality was exploited pharmacologically: ipecac powder/syrup at low doses = expectorant for cough; higher doses = emetic for poisoning management. The same compound producing opposite clinical endpoints depending on dose is a general principle of pharmacology (dose-response curves are the rule, not the exception).

**Ipecac syrup for poisoning — withdrawal:**
Ipecac syrup (standardized to produce vomiting within 20 minutes) was standard in home first aid kits and recommended by Poison Control Centers for toxic ingestion in children from 1960s-1990s. This practice was largely **abandoned by 2003** when the American Academy of Pediatrics changed its recommendation, citing: (a) emesis doesn't reliably remove toxins; (b) delays activated charcoal administration; (c) contraindicated for many poisons (caustics, hydrocarbons). This represents a significant pharmacological course correction.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Primary alkaloids (isoquinoline class):**

| Compound | Concentration | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| Emetine | 1.0–1.8% root | Primary anti-amoebic; emetic at high dose; cardiotoxic at excess |
| Cephaeline | 0.6–1.2% root | ~50% less anti-amoebic potency than emetine; similar mechanism |
| Psychotrine | 0.1–0.2% | Minor alkaloid |
| Emetamine | trace | Minor |

**Non-alkaloid:**
- **Ipecosides** (1-O-methyl-β-glucoside of emetol): iridoid-type glycosides
- **Tannins:** 10–15% — astringent; precipitates proteins
- **Starch:** 30–40% — bulk; excipient in the root powder

**Total alkaloid content:** ~2% root dry weight. The 15:1 ratio of emetine:cephaeline in *C. ipecacuanha* is the pharmacopoeial quality marker.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**1. Emetine — anti-protozoal (PRIMARY)**
Emetine (and dehydroemetine, its semi-synthetic analogue) is a potent inhibitor of eukaryotic protein synthesis via 40S ribosomal subunit binding (elongation factor 2 inhibition). *Entamoeba histolytica* is particularly sensitive because: (a) lacks efficient drug efflux pumps for emetine; (b) cannot repair emetine-induced ribosomal damage as rapidly as mammalian cells.
IC₅₀ against *E. histolytica*: ~0.1–0.5 μM (highly potent). Concentrations achieved in liver tissue (amoebic abscess) are therapeutically relevant.

**Cardiotoxicity mechanism:** At higher doses, emetine inhibits Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase in cardiac myocytes (same general mechanism as cardiac glycosides but non-selective and without therapeutic window). Causes cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, hypotension. This cardiotoxicity is the primary limitation of emetine therapy.

**2. Emetic mechanism (high dose)**
At high doses: emetine and cephaeline stimulate the chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ) in the area postrema via dopamine D₂ receptor activation AND direct gastric irritation of sensory afferents. Both central and peripheral mechanisms converge → vomiting. The central mechanism distinguishes ipecac-induced emesis from simple gastric irritation (explaining why it works even when given parenterally in historical preparations).

**3. Expectorant mechanism (low dose)**
Subemetic doses of ipecac alkaloids stimulate gastric mucosa → vagal afferents → parasympathetic reflex → increased bronchial mucus secretion. Classic reflex expectorant mechanism (shared with guaifenesin's proposed mechanism). At these doses, reflex expectorant effect occurs without vomiting.

**4. Anti-tumor (emerging — emetine)**
Emetine/dehydroemetine: protein synthesis inhibition mechanism → anti-proliferative in cancer cell lines (IC₅₀ ~0.1–1 μM in multiple cancer lines). Research interest in emetine as an anti-cancer repurposed drug, with preclinical activity. Cardiotoxicity remains the clinical obstacle. Dehydroemetine (semi-synthetic) has slightly improved therapeutic index.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### Amoebic Dysentery / Liver Abscess

**Level A (historical, comparison trials):**
Multiple RCTs comparing emetine to metronidazole in *Entamoeba histolytica* infection (1970s–1980s):
- Metronidazole significantly less toxic with equivalent or superior efficacy for intestinal amoebiasis
- Emetine superior for extra-intestinal amoebiasis (liver abscess) where tissue concentration needed
- Combined regimen (emetine + metronidazole) maximized cure rates in complicated disease

**WHO Essential Medicines 2021:**
Metronidazole and tinidazole are first-line for amoebiasis. Emetine/dehydroemetine listed as alternatives for refractory cases (still on WHO list as second-line).

### Emesis in Poisoning

**Level A: Manoguerra 2005 (Annals of Emergency Medicine — systematic review)**
No evidence that ipecac syrup improves clinical outcomes in poisoning management. Its routine use was discontinued.
Current standard: Activated charcoal (if indicated); supportive care; poison center consultation.

### Expectorant (Historical)

**Level B: Historical pharmacological documentation**
Dover's Powder (ipecac + opium) was the primary cough-expectorant-sedative preparation from 1709 (Thomas Dover formulation) through the early 20th century. Pharmacologically rational: ipecac as expectorant + opium as antitussive/sedative. Superseded by safer individual preparations.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Dover's Powder and the buccaneer-physician:**
Thomas Dover (1660–1742) — a physician who turned privateer (legal pirate) and circumnavigated the globe — formulated Dover's Powder: ipecac, opium, potassium sulfate, and licorice. Dover's own circumnavigation included the rescue of Alexander Selkirk from Juan Fernández island (the inspiration for Robinson Crusoe). His powder, introduced 1709, became the most widely used cough remedy of the 18th-19th century — combining ipecac's expectorant action with opium's cough suppression and sedation in one preparation. The historical overlap between adventure, piracy, and pharmaceutical innovation makes Dover's biography one of the more colorful in medical history.

**The bulimia misuse:**
From the 1970s-2000s, ipecac syrup — sold OTC — was misused by individuals with bulimia nervosa as a self-induced emetic. Chronic ipecac/emetine exposure causes cardiomyopathy (emetine cardiotoxicity). The death of Karen Carpenter (1983) from cardiac arrest was attributed to complications of anorexia/bulimia including chronic ipecac abuse causing cardiomyopathy. This tragedy contributed to pharmacovigilance attention on ipecac misuse and was part of the reason for eventually withdrawing OTC ipecac from the market (2010 US withdrawal of most products).

**The indigenous naming as pharmacological encoding:**
The Tupi name *ipe-kaa-guéne* = "plant by the roadside that makes you vomit" is one of the most pharmacologically precise indigenous plant names in the Codex — direct description of primary observed pharmacological effect. Most traditional plant names encode appearance, habitat, or mythological associations. Ipecac's indigenous name encodes mechanism — a reflection of its unmistakable, immediate, consistent pharmacological action at therapeutic doses.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**IMPORTANT: Ipecac is NOT recommended for home use as an emetic. OTC ipecac syrup has been largely withdrawn from the market.**

**Current indications (specialist/hospital):**
- Dehydroemetine (IV/IM): Refractory amoebiasis (amoebic liver abscess) not responding to metronidazole; specialist use only
- Not available as OTC preparation in most countries for emesis induction

**If medical use of emetine is required (specialist context):**
- Dehydroemetine preferred over emetine (slightly improved cardiac safety profile)
- Dose: Dehydroemetine 1–1.5 mg/kg/day IM × 5 days (maximum 90 mg/day)
- Cardiac monitoring (ECG) mandatory during treatment
- Bed rest recommended during treatment (cardiac output limitation)

**Absolute contraindications:**
- **Children <1 year**
- **Cardiac disease** (emetine cardiotoxicity)
- **Pregnancy** (emetine teratogenic in animals; avoid)
- **Renal disease** (emetine accumulation)
- **Muscle disease** (emetine myopathy)

**The "do not use for emesis" rule:**
Poison Control Center contact (US: 1-800-222-1222) is the appropriate first action for toxic ingestion — they will advise whether any intervention is needed. Ipecac syrup should not be given without professional guidance. For most ingestions: supportive care, not induced emesis.

**Historical note for practitioners:**
Dover's Powder preparations (ipecac + opium) appear in historical case records. If encountering an elderly patient who reports "Dover's Powder" or "Compound Ipecac Powder" as a historical medication, this was a standard 19th/early 20th century formulation, not a patient error.
