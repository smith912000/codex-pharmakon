# Eretrian earth — Eretrias (ERETRIAS), "Eretrian earth" (Dioscorides)

## *A soft ashy-white earth that leaves a violet line when drawn along brass, washed like white lead and used as a cooling, astringent dressing for sores and wounds; the translator's gloss reads "Alkaline Earths; Baryta — Barium monoxide; Lime — Calcium oxide; Strontia — Strontium monoxide", which the chapter's description does not support, so the material is left as an unidentified earth, of low confidence at the level of modern mineral* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **IDENTIFICATION CAUTION.** The translator's gloss names barium, calcium and strontium oxides. Barium compounds in particular are toxic. The text describes a soft, ashy earth and not an alkaline oxide. Do not read the gloss as the identity, and do not use any such material.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** This is a single-text entry. The chapter is 5-171 ERETRIAS in Book Five of the *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10718-10721; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` carries it as 5-171, line 10718, class mineral). The name is the Greek *Eretrias*, "Eretrian earth"; the lines read do not say where the earth came from, and the place the name points to is not stated in them. The chapter (line 10721) reads in outline: "Some Eretrian earth is considerably white and some is an ashy colour. The best is that which resembles ash in colour, and is very soft, and when drawn along brass yields a violet line." It then gives the washing: "It is washed like cerussa [white lead ore] or as follows. Pound as much as you want of it into small pieces (first by itself and then with water) and let it settle. Then strain out the moisture gently, dry the earth in the sun, beat it again for a whole day (adding water) and allow it to settle all night. About the break of day strain it, pound it finely in the sun, and form it into tablets, as far as possible." And the roasting: "If there is a need to roast it take (pieces) of earth the size of a cicer [2-126], put it into a clay jar of full of holes, and having corked the mouth carefully, put it among lighted coals and blow on it continuously. When the ashes have turned into sparks (or rather become an airy colour), take it out and put it in jars." The action: "It is astringent, cooling, and gently softening, filling up hollow sores, and closing open cuts and bleeding wounds."

So the text names two colours (considerably white, or ashy); ranks the ashy, very soft kind best; gives a field test (the violet line on brass); gives a washing and a roasting; and names the action as astringent, cooling and gently softening, for hollow sores, open cuts and bleeding wounds. It gives no drink, no dose and no mixture.

*Neighbours in the text that name it.* Two later chapters use Eretrian earth as the standard. 5-172 SAMIA (line 10722) says of the Samian earth called *aster* that it "has the same properties and is burnt and broken like eretrias [5-171]". 5-177 PNIGITIS (line 10750) says Pnigitis earth "in a way resembles Eretrian earth [5-171] in colour" and ends: "Some sell this as Eretrian earth." That last sentence is Dioscorides' own note that Eretrian earth was imitated. The general rule of the run, 5-170 GE (line 10711), is that medicinal earth is "supremely useful for cooling, and for closing the pores".

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent, and the translator's gloss (alkaline earths, barium, calcium and strontium oxides) is not borne out by the description. In general terms, a soft, ashy earth that is washed in water by settling is most likely a fine clay, marl or chalk-like material, and roasting would change it. The mineral cannot be fixed from the text. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a recommended practice.** The text records a washed, cooling, astringent earth laid on hollow sores and fresh cuts. The Codex gives no protocol. Open wounds need clean, proper care, and a roasted earth of unknown mineral is not a safe thing to pack into them.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives two preparations (line 10721). First, washing and settling: pound the earth, let it settle in water, strain off the moisture, dry in the sun, beat it for a whole day with water, let it stand overnight, strain it at daybreak, pound it finely in the sun and form tablets "as far as possible". Second, roasting: pieces "the size of a cicer" in a perforated, corked clay jar among lighted coals, blown on until the ashes "become an airy colour". No quantity, no proportion, no frequency and no dose is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Washing a ground earth by repeated settling separates the fine particles from the coarse, and the method is the same as that used for fine pigments. Heating a clay or carbonate earth changes it; heating a carbonate gives a caustic lime. Fine earths have been used as drying dusts on skin. Barium compounds other than the insoluble sulphate are poisonous, which is why the translator's gloss must not be read as an identification. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** The chapter prints three qualities: "astringent, cooling, and gently softening" (line 10721). No degree and no humour is printed. The general statement of 5-170 (cooling and closing the pores) belongs to earths as a class.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: a sensory and mechanical assay (colour, softness, the violet line on brass), a detailed method of washing by settling over two days, and, in 5-177, a note that a lookalike was sold under the name. The chapter assigns no cultivation status, and the earth has only a therapeutic one.

**Practitioner's note.** A cooling dressing earth of the ancient shop, notable for the care of its assay and its washing. Nothing here supports ingestion or any cultivation use, and the gloss that names barium is a reason for caution. The Codex takes from it the habit of testing a material before use and distrusting a substitute.

### Contraindications

- **Toxic possibility.** The translator's gloss includes barium and strontium oxides. The identity is not established, so treat any "Eretrian earth" of unknown composition as unsafe.
- **Open wounds.** Do not pack earth into a wound; it risks infection, including tetanus.
- **Roasted earth.** Heating may produce a caustic lime or other irritant material.
- **Counterfeits.** The text itself says another earth was sold as this one (5-177).
- **Identity.** Unknown. [USER: verify]
