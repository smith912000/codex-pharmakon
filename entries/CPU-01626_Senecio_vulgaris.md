# Senecio vulgaris — Erigeron (Dioscorides 4-97)

## *Senecio vulgaris [Linnaeus], "Groundsel", the last of the names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-97 ("Erigeron, Senetio [Fuchs, Brunfels], Senecio minor vulgaris [Bauhin], Senecio vulgaris [Linnaeus] — Groundsel")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (lines 8901 and 8902) prints four names with their namers, the last being Linnaeus, and the row takes the last. It was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-97. ERIGERON*, heading at line 8900, SUGGESTED line at 8901 and 8902, text at line 8903. It stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), after *4-96. GALLION* and before *4-98. THALIKTRON* (heading at line 8904).

The whole chapter reads: "Senecio is a reddish little stalk a foot high, with little leaves like eruca [2-170], jagged at the edges only a great deal smaller; the flowers are yellowish, quickly opening, and after blowing turn into down. This is also why it was called erigeron, because in the spring the flowers turn gray like hair; the root is of no use. It grows mostly on unmortared stone walls and about towns. The leaves with the flowers are cooling. The leaves smeared on with a little wine, or else by themselves, cure inflammations from stones [kidney, urinary], and of the perineum. With manna thuris [1-83] it heals other wounds, and nerves. The down smeared on by itself in vinegar does the same. Drunk while fresh they cause strangling. The whole stalk soaked with water and drunk with must [grape pulp] cures pains of the stomach from cholera. Some call it erechthites, the Romans, herbulum, some senecium]"

The chapter gives the reason for the Greek name (*erigeron*, from the flowers turning grey "like hair" in spring), a description (a reddish little stalk a foot high, small jagged leaves like rocket's, quickly opening yellowish flowers that turn to down, a useless root), the place (unmortared stone walls and about towns), and one quality: "The leaves with the flowers are cooling." Uses: the leaves, with a little wine or alone, for "inflammations from stones" (the translators gloss kidney or urinary) and of the perineum; with *manna thuris* (cross-referenced by the text to chapter 1-83) it heals "other wounds, and nerves", and the down in vinegar does the same; the whole stalk soaked in water and drunk with must "cures pains of the stomach from cholera". The text also warns: "Drunk while fresh they cause strangling." The sentence is the only caution in the chapter; the text does not say what "they" refers to beyond the preceding sentence, which is about the down, and a reader may take it to mean the fresh leaves and flowers or the down. The Codex does not decide. [USER: verify] The names printed after the text are *erechthites*, the Roman *herbulum* and *senecium*; the last stands in the printed text as "some senecium]" with a stray closing bracket, which is reproduced as printed.

**Plates and the index.** A plate captioned "Senecio vulgaris after THIEBAULT — 1888" is printed at line 8843, which falls in the pages of chapter 4-90 (page 641), not beside 4-97; the text does not say why. The translators' name index lists *Senecio vulgaris* at page 639 (line 11895), a page number that does not match the plate's position at line 8843 (between the page numbers 640 and 641) or the chapter's own place; the Codex does not resolve it. **Overlap.** The existing entry I_843 (*Senecio jacobaea*, Ragwort) cites this chapter in its Dioscorides paragraph as describing groundsel, "a congener, not *S. jacobaea*", and records "cooling", the smeared use for inflammation and "swallowed fresh they cause strangling". It is an entry on a different species and uses 4-97 as a genus-level note. The other *Senecio* chapters it names (4.75 and 4.123) are different plants and are not read here. The next chapter, 4-98, is *THALIKTRON* (a meadow rue), also a different plant. No entry on 4-97 as its own plant was found.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read, and the chapter prints none. It is a general fact that *Senecio* is a large genus of the daisy family (Asteraceae), and that many species of the genus, groundsel among them, are known to contain pyrrolizidine alkaloids, which are toxic to the liver. Whether this applies to the plant of 4-97 is not stated by the text. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice, and the plant is of the kind generally regarded as hazardous to the liver. Dioscorides reports a cooling herb used on inflammations, on wounds with frankincense dust, and drunk for stomach pain, and he warns that it causes strangling when taken fresh. These are the text's reports. The Codex gives no protocol, and the identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-97 gives these preparations: leaves smeared on with a little wine or alone; leaves with *manna thuris*; the down smeared on in vinegar; and the whole stalk soaked in water and drunk with must. It gives no quantity, frequency or duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter records no amuletic use; the Roman names *herbulum* and *senecium* are names only. The part of the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition that is present here is the description of the plant and its place, the explanation of its name by the flowers turning grey, the statement that the root is of no use, and the caution that the fresh plant causes strangling, which is itself a rule about handling the material. The plant is given no status beyond that of a cooling herb with remedial uses and a warning. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. What the chapter gives is a description, a list of remedies without amounts and one warning, and the warning is the part to keep. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter's own warning is that the plant "drunk while fresh" causes strangling. Groundsel and its relatives are generally regarded as potentially harmful to the liver, and this entry does not recommend taking it by mouth or applying it to wounds. Stomach pain with vomiting and diarrhoea, urinary stones and open wounds need medical assessment. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify] None of the traditional uses above is advice.
