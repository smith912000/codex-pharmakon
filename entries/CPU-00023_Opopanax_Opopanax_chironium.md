### Origin & Tradition

Opopanax — the name is Greek *opos* ("vegetable juice / latex") + *panax* ("all-heal") — is the oleo-gum-resin of *Opopanax chironium*, a tall yellow-flowered umbellifer (Apiaceae) of the eastern Mediterranean and the Levant. Like the Ferula gums, it is harvested by wounding the thick root and stem-base of the mature plant and collecting the latex that exudes and hardens into reddish-brown tears. The epithet *chironium* ties the plant to Chiron, the wise centaur of Greek myth who was the archetypal master of herbal medicine — a folk etymology preserved by the ancient authors, who held that the herb's healing virtue and its discovery belonged to Chiron's lore. The very word *panax* (the same root that names ginseng's genus, *Panax*) marks the ancient esteem in which this resin was held: it was, in name, a universal remedy.

Dioscorides (*De Materia Medica*, Book III) treats *panakes herakleion* — Hercules' all-heal — and its juice *opopanax* as a major article. He describes the gathering of the juice from the incised root, distinguishes good resin (externally yellowish, internally white, bitter, with a strong heavy smell) from adulterated grades, and lays out a broad indication list characteristic of the warm dispersing resins: it warms and softens, provokes urine and menses, helps old coughs and chest complaints, eases spasms and convulsions and sciatic and joint pains, is applied to indurations and swellings to resolve them, and is used against chills and as an emmenagogue. Pliny and the later classical authors echo this, and the resin held a stable place in the Galenic pharmacy as a hot, attenuant, resolvent, antispasmodic gum.

Through the Arabic and medieval Latin transmission, opopanax — under the Arabic *jawshir* / *jushir* and the Latin *opoponax* — remained a standard ingredient of compound electuaries, theriacs, and warming plasters. The Galenic-Unani physicians classed it as hot and dry, a deobstruent and resolvent for cold-damp accumulations, spasmodic disorders, and amenorrhoea. It belongs, in the medieval pharmacy, to the same working family as galbanum, sagapenum, and asafetida: the warming umbelliferous gum-resins used to "open obstruction," move stagnant cold humours, and quiet spasm.

In the modern era opopanax has migrated largely out of the clinic and into perfumery, where "opopanax" is prized as a warm, sweet, balsamic base note. Here a serious nomenclatural caution applies: commercial "opopanax" in the fragrance and incense trade is frequently **not** *Opopanax chironium* at all but a *Commiphora* resin (a Burseraceae "sweet myrrh," sometimes *Commiphora guidottii* / *C. erythraea*), botanically unrelated to the Apiaceae plant. The classical medical opopanax of Dioscorides is the umbellifer described here; the perfumer's opopanax is usually a myrrh relative. Any sourcing or pharmacology must specify which is meant, and the constituent chemistry below pertains to the Apiaceae *Opopanax chironium*, which is less thoroughly studied than asafetida.

Because of that under-study, this entry keeps constituent percentages and dose numbers deliberately absent and flags them for verification rather than inventing values.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Primary domain: a specialist-tier warm, sweet, resolvent gum-resin for cold-damp obstruction, spasm, and stagnation — the "all-heal" of the umbelliferous resin family.**

**1. Resolvent / deobstruent for cold accumulation (classical core).**
The defining classical office of opopanax is to "soften and resolve" indurations, swellings, and cold-damp masses, and to open obstruction. In Kenetica this is the warm-dispersing application: moving congealed cold stagnation in the abdomen and the channels. Its sweet-balsamic character distinguishes it from the harsh fetid asafetida — it is the gentler, more aromatic member of the class.

**2. Antispasmodic for cramp, colic, and convulsive tension.**
The coumarin smooth-muscle-relaxant action grounds the ancient use against spasms, convulsions, and sciatic and joint pains. As a specialist nervine-antispasmodic resin it is used where spasm coincides with cold-damp obstruction rather than with heat.

**3. Old cough and cold-phlegm chest support (classical respiratory use).**
As with sagapenum, the resolvent action extends to thinning and moving thick cold phlegm of the chest in chronic ("old") cough — a complementary, tradition-grounded support, not a treatment for any acute respiratory condition.

### Preparation & Dosing

Prepared, like its kin, **in warm fat, oil, or wine** rather than raw, and historically used as one resin among several in compound preparations.

- **Resin tears in warm vehicle / electuary (classical):** the standard form; opopanax was a compound-formula ingredient, not a bulk single dose.
- **Warming plaster / topical resolvent (classical):** applied over indurations, swellings, and aching cold joints.
- **Tincture / hydroalcoholic extract (modern, rare):** concentrates the coumarin and resin fraction; no standardised clinical product is in common circulation.

### Contraindications

- **ABSOLUTE — Pregnancy:** classical emmenagogue and uterine-stimulant; contraindicated in pregnancy.
- **CAUTION — Coumarin/anticoagulant interaction:** additive risk with warfarin-class anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents; avoid without oversight; discontinue before elective surgery.
- **CAUTION — Acute GI inflammation and heat patterns:** the hot, sharp, resolvent quality aggravates active gastritis/ulcer and Pitta/heat-excess presentations.
- **IDENTITY / SOURCING — CRITICAL:** commercial "opopanax" is frequently a *Commiphora* (sweet-myrrh) resin, not *Opopanax chironium*. The two are botanically unrelated with different chemistry.
