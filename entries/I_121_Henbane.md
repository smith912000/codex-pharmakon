### Botanical Identity
Annual or biennial herb 30–80cm; sticky hairy greyish foliage with a foetid odour; funnel-shaped dull yellow flowers with distinctive purple veining and dark centre; capsular fruits with numerous tiny seeds; native to Eurasia and North Africa, widely naturalised. All parts contain tropane alkaloids; the leaf and seed are the primary drug materials. **The same anticholinergic deliriant alkaloid family as mandrake (I_120) and datura** — hyoscyamine and scopolamine.

**TOXICITY**: *Hyoscyamus niger* is a potent anticholinergic deliriant poison. Historical use as an anaesthetic, analgesic, and soporific operated at the boundary of anticholinergic toxicity. Documented here as historical pharmacology — NOT a recommendation for use. The isolated alkaloids (hyoscyamine, scopolamine) are modern approved drugs; the crude plant is dangerous.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Dioscorides / Greco-Roman (DMM)
Book IV, Chapter 68: Dioscorides distinguishes three varieties of henbane (black, yellow, white) and notes the black is the most dangerous/maddening. Applications: analgesic (for pain, especially with opium); soporific; topical for inflammation, gout, and earache; for cough; to suppress lactation. He warns explicitly about the deliriant danger of the black variety.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine / Ibn Sīnā (CM)
*Banj* — cold and dry (3rd degree). Applications: anaesthetic and analgesic (a key ingredient in the *banj* anaesthetic preparations); soporific; for severe pain; topical for inflammation and toothache; antispasmodic. Ibn Sīnā's *banj* tradition was central to Islamic surgical anaesthesia.

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (EP)
Henbane in EP analgesic and soporific preparations.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)
*Šakirû* (henbane) in AT magico-medical preparations for pain and altered states.

### 2.5 Shennong / Bencao Gangmu (SBJ)
莨菪 *Làngdàng* (henbane seed) — listed in the SBJ tradition. Applications: analgesic for chronic pain; antispasmodic (for spasmodic cough, asthma, GI spasm); for "mania" and mental conditions; topical. The Chinese tradition recognised it as both medicine and dangerous toxin, classifying it carefully in dose.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Anticholinergic Analgesia / Antispasmodic (Primary — Hyoscyamine)

**Hyoscyamine + scopolamine**: Competitive antagonists at muscarinic acetylcholine receptors:
- Peripheral M3 blockade → smooth muscle relaxation → antispasmodic (relieves the colic, spasmodic cough, GI/biliary/renal spasm)
- The analgesic effect is partly the relief of spasmodic pain (visceral) and partly the central sedation
- Reduces glandular secretion (the lactation-suppression and antisialagogue effects)

### 5.2 CNS Sedation / Soporific (Scopolamine)

**Scopolamine**: Crosses the BBB → central muscarinic blockade → sedation, sleep, and amnesia (same mechanism as mandrake, I_120). The soporific/anaesthetic application, particularly in combination with opium (DMM, CM), provided pre-modern surgical sedation.

### 5.3 The Banj Tradition and Inhalational Anaesthesia

The Islamic *banj* tradition (henbane-based anaesthetic preparations) was central to medieval surgical anaesthesia. *Banj* preparations were administered orally, by inhalation (the henbane smoke/vapour), or via the soporific sponge. The anticholinergic + (when combined) opioid effect produced the sedation and amnesia needed for surgery. The word *banj* spread across the Islamic world and into related terms for narcotic preparations.

### 5.4 Antiasthmatic (Anticholinergic Bronchodilation)

**Hyoscyamine + atropine**: Anticholinergic bronchodilation — blocking M3 receptors in bronchial smooth muscle → bronchodilation. This is the SAME mechanism as modern anticholinergic asthma inhalers (ipratropium, tiotropium). Henbane (and datura) cigarettes were a standard asthma treatment in 19th-century Western medicine — patients smoked the dried leaf to deliver bronchodilating anticholinergic alkaloids directly to the airways. The SBJ and CM asthma applications are mechanistically identical to modern anticholinergic bronchodilator therapy.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Isolated alkaloids (modern)**: Hyoscyamine and scopolamine are approved modern drugs (antispasmodic, antiemetic, premedication, motion sickness) — direct pharmaceutical validation of henbane's traditional applications.

**Anticholinergic bronchodilators**: The modern asthma/COPD inhalers ipratropium and tiotropium are synthetic quaternary derivatives of the same tropane anticholinergic mechanism — the direct pharmaceutical descendants of the henbane/datura asthma cigarettes. This is a clear lineage from traditional henbane asthma treatment to modern inhaled anticholinergic therapy.

**Crude henbane**: Not used in modern medicine (unpredictable alkaloid content, toxicity); historically official in pharmacopoeias (Henbane extract, tincture) into the 20th century.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The oracle's herb and the witch's ointment**: Henbane occupied a central place in the European magical-medical tradition. As "Herba Apollinaris" (herb of Apollo), it was associated with the oracular tradition — the deliriant/hallucinogenic effects of the tropane alkaloids were used (with other plants) to induce prophetic/visionary states. The Pythia (oracle of Delphi) may have used henbane fumes among other agents. In the medieval and early modern period, henbane was a primary ingredient in "witch's flying ointment" — the tropane alkaloid-containing salve that, absorbed through the skin, produced the sensation of flight and the vivid hallucinations interpreted as the witches' sabbath. The pharmacology is genuine: scopolamine produces vivid, dream-like deliria with sensations of flying and transformation. The "flight to the sabbath" was a pharmacological experience produced by transdermal tropane alkaloid absorption.

**Beer before hops**: Before hops (*Humulus lupulus*) became the standard beer bittering and preservative agent (from ~the 13th–16th centuries), various herbs were used in the "gruit" beer-flavouring mixture, including henbane. The German town of Pilsen (*Bilsen* = henbane) and the beer style "Pilsner" have a debated etymological connection to *Bilsenkraut* (henbane). Henbane-flavoured beer would have had psychoactive/deliriant effects beyond simple alcohol intoxication — one reason the transition to hops (a mild sedative but not a deliriant) represented a public health improvement as well as a brewing one. The Bavarian Reinheitsgebot (beer purity law, 1516) — restricting beer to water, barley, and hops — partly reflected the desire to eliminate dangerous psychoactive additives like henbane.

**Crippen and the forensic henbane**: The 1910 murder trial of Dr. Hawley Harvey Crippen — who poisoned his wife with hyoscine (scopolamine) extracted from henbane — was a landmark in forensic toxicology. It was one of the first cases where a specific alkaloid (hyoscine/scopolamine) was identified in the victim's remains, and the first use of wireless telegraphy to apprehend a suspect (Crippen was caught fleeing across the Atlantic). The case demonstrated both the lethal potency of henbane's alkaloids and the emergence of modern forensic chemistry capable of detecting them.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

**⚠️ Henbane is a potent anticholinergic deliriant poison. The crude plant is NOT for use. This documents historical pharmacology.**

| Form | Historical use | Status |
|------|------|-------------|
| Henbane extract/tincture (historical pharmacy) | Antispasmodic, analgesic, soporific | HISTORICAL — superseded by isolated alkaloids |
| Asthma cigarettes (19th c.) | Bronchodilation | HISTORICAL — superseded by inhaled ipratropium/tiotropium |
| **Pharmaceutical hyoscyamine / scopolamine** | Antispasmodic, antiemetic, premedication | The modern safe use — isolated, dose-controlled |
| Homeopathic *Hyoscyamus* (30C+) | Ultra-dilute | No material alkaloid |

**No safe self-administration dose exists for crude henbane.**

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Bitter, acrid; warm; very toxic. Channels: Heart (心), Liver (肝), Stomach (胃). 莨菪子 settles spasm, relieves pain, calms mania — used in minute, carefully processed doses in classical Chinese medicine.

**Ayurvedic (cross-reference)**: Not classical Ayurvedic; related Solanaceous deliriants used in processed form only.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **ABSOLUTE: Do not self-administer crude henbane.** Anticholinergic toxidrome (as mandrake, I_120): delirium, hyperthermia, tachycardia, urinary retention, mydriasis, hallucination, seizure, coma, death
- **Anticholinergic drug interactions**: Additive with antihistamines, TCAs, antipsychotics, other tropane plants
- **Glaucoma**: Anticholinergic mydriasis can precipitate acute angle-closure glaucoma
- **Foraging risk**: Henbane root has been mistaken for edible roots; seeds for poppy seeds — causing poisoning
- Included for cataloging completeness and historical/pharmacological documentation — NOT a therapeutic recommendation
