# Woodlouse — Koubarides (KOUBARIDES); "Millipede" [old English], Oniscus asellus — Common Woodlouse (translator's heading)
## *A many-footed small creature that rolls itself into a ball; the translator's heading to Dioscorides 2-37 gives "Oniscus asellus — Common Woodlouse" and the old-English name "Millipede", and the chapter's own description is the only identification the text offers* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The creature is chapter 37 of Book Two, "Living Creatures", of the *De Materia Medica*, headed KOUBARIDES (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 5088; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` has it at line 227 with the translator's identification "Oniscus asellus — Common Woodlouse"). The translator adds a second line: "'Millipede' [old English]" (line 5090). The whole chapter is one paragraph (line 5091): "Millipedes that are found under water vessels are creatures with many feet that roll themselves up round when they are touched with the hand. Taken as a drink with wine these help painful urination and yellow jaundice. Rubbed on with honey they help those with tonsillitis, and warmed in a pomegranate rind with rosaceum [1-53] and dropped in the ears they are good for earaches."

So the text gives four uses and three modes: taken in wine as a drink, for painful urination and for yellow jaundice; rubbed on with honey, for tonsillitis; and warmed in a pomegranate rind with rosaceum, which the translator's heading at 1-53 glosses as oil of roses, and dropped in the ears, for earache. It gives no quantity, no number of creatures, and no instruction on whether they are dried, crushed or taken live. The chapter says where they are found ("under water vessels") and how they behave when touched ("roll themselves up round"), and these two features are the identification the ancient text itself offers. The scientific name in the heading is the translator's, not Dioscorides', and the old-English name "millipede" for a creature that rolls into a ball shows that the modern word has moved: the modern millipede is a different group of animals from the woodlouse, which is a crustacean. Whether Dioscorides' *koubarides* is the woodlouse, the pill-millipede or something else rolled-up cannot be settled from the English text. [USER: verify]

*Neighbouring chapters, to keep them apart.* The chapter before it, 2-36, is *koreis*, the bed bug (*Cimex lectularius*), which is swallowed with beans before a quartan fever or smelled to revive someone who has fainted (lines 5082-5084). The chapter after it, 2-38, is the cockroach, headed in the translation "THESE INSECTS ARE CARRIERS OF MANY DISEASES" (line 5094). Sea centipede is a separate marine creature, *skolopendra*, at 2-16 (lines 4979-4981), which the translator glosses "Myriapoda, Cheilopoda — Sea Centipedes, Millepedes" and of which the text says only that boiled in oil it removes hair and, when touched, breeds itching. None of these is the woodlouse.

*A list row in another tradition, not folded in.* The name list `_sources/ebers.csv` has a row "Millipede" (line 132; marked as an uncertain identification; source "Ebers Papyrus; Ghalioungui 1987 trans., Glossary I (Medicaments)"). The Ebers text on disk (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`) does not contain the word, so the row could not be opened to a passage. It cannot be assumed to be the same creature as *koubarides*, and EP is not on this entry's tag line. [USER: verify]

The dioscorides list (`_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 218) has the name "Koubarides" classed as a plant with an uncertain identification; the text shows it is a creature, in the Book Two "Living Creatures" section, so the class on that row is wrong.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, the woodlouse is a terrestrial crustacean with a hard outer skeleton (exoskeleton) of chitin and calcium carbonate, which it sheds and renews in stages; it feeds on decaying plant matter and lives in damp places. Nothing links any constituent to the actions the *De Materia Medica* gives, which are in painful urination, jaundice, tonsillitis and earache.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records four uses for the creature. The Codex does not recommend any of them. Painful urination can be an infection or a stone, jaundice can be a sign of liver or bile-duct disease, tonsillitis and earache can need antibiotics or drainage, and none of these is a matter for swallowing a wild invertebrate in wine or dropping it into the ear. The entry exists to record what the text says, and to keep the identification question open.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives three modes and no quantity. Taken as a drink with wine; rubbed on with honey; warmed in a pomegranate rind with rosaceum (oil of roses, per the translator's heading at 1-53) and dropped in the ears (line 5091). It does not say how many creatures, whether alive or dead, or for how long. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Woodlice are not known to be a source of any established medicine. Small invertebrates collected from damp places can carry bacteria, moulds and parasites, and putting one into the ear can injure the canal or the eardrum or introduce infection. Painful urination, jaundice, tonsillitis and earache each have causes that are diagnosed and treated clinically. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** No quality, warming, cooling, drying or other property is printed for *koubarides* in 2-37. The chapter gives only its description and its four uses. The qualities printed in neighbouring chapters are not carried over.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is asserted here. The chapter has no cultivation framing. What the chapter does offer a practitioner is a discipline of attention of a limited kind: it names the habitat of the creature (under water vessels) and a behaviour by which to know it (it rolls itself up round when touched by the hand). It gives no season, source, assay or test for adulteration, which other chapters in the same book do give for other materials. A text that records a use is not thereby endorsing it, and the chapter makes no claim about the person's constitution, vitality or long life.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The woodlouse is a four-use entry in the animal section of a Greek materia medica, with no dose and an open identification. The only usable lesson is the one the chapter embodies: know the creature by where it lives and what it does before naming it. The Codex does not recommend taking it. ### Contraindications

- **Infection and hygiene:** wild invertebrates can carry bacteria, moulds and parasites; do not swallow them or apply them to the skin or tonsils.
- **The ear:** do not put any animal or animal material into the ear; an injured canal or perforated drum is a risk.
- **Symptoms:** painful urination, jaundice, a sore throat with fever and earache all need assessment, and jaundice in particular needs prompt medical attention.
- **Identity:** the identification as the common woodlouse is the translator's; the modern millipede is a different animal, and the Ebers list row cannot be assumed to name the same creature.
- **Allergy:** shellfish-allergic people may react to crustacean material; this is a general caution and the text does not mention it.
