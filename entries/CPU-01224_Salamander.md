# Salamander — *Salamandra* (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 2-67)
## *"Salamandridae — Salamander", the translator's gloss; the text calls it "a kind of lizard, lazy, variously spotted"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica*, in the 2000 English edition (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`; "text" below). The chapter is 2-67, headed *SALAMANDRA*, at line 5206 (the spine's locus, `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`: "2-67 (line 5206)", translator's identification "Salamandridae — Salamander", class animal). It sits in Book 2, the book of living creatures, among the insects and small animals that follow the cantharides (2-65) and buprestis (2-66), and it is followed by the spiders (2-68).

The whole chapter, line 5208, reads: "The salamander is a kind of lizard, lazy, variously spotted, in vain thought fireproof. It is antiseptic, ulcerating, and heating. They are mixed in antiseptic and leprosy medicines to the same benefit as cantharides [2-65] and kept in store in a similar way. Moistened with oil they remove hair [depilatory]. They are disembowelled, the head and the feet taken away, and preserved in honey for the same uses." The sentence "in vain thought fireproof" records the popular belief and rejects it; Dioscorides is describing the animal, not endorsing the belief. The chapter gives no place of origin, no season and no assay.

The chapter points back to 2-65 for two things: the benefit (in antiseptic and leprosy medicines) and the manner of storage. Chapter 2-65 (lines 5194-5197) treats the cantharides as POISONOUS and describes gathering the dried beetles, sealing them in an unglazed jar under linen, and stifling them in the fumes of warm vinegar before they are threaded and stored; chapter 2-66 says the related buprestis is "antiseptic, anti-ulcerous and heating" and is mixed in medicines for the cornea, leprosy and wild impetigo. The salamander chapter itself bears no such POISONOUS label in the edition read. The cross-reference is the only guide to how the salamander was kept, and it is a reference to the cantharides' storage, not a procedure given for the salamander. [USER: verify]

No other tradition on the tag line, and no text outside Dioscorides on disk, was found naming the salamander. A search of the Sushruta, Ebers, Tibetan and Assyrian texts on disk for the word returned nothing.

### Active Components

Salamanders are amphibians, and the skin of many amphibians carries glands that secrete irritant or toxic substances. This is a general fact about the group. Dioscorides' own word for the effect, "ulcerating", fits an irritant; the chapter names no constituent, and this entry names none.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The uses recorded are external: an ingredient in "antiseptic and leprosy medicines" and a depilatory when moistened with oil. By Dioscorides' own account the remedy is "ulcerating, and heating" ("ulcerating, and heating"). The Codex's application layer has nothing to add, and no protocol is offered.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives one preparation: the animals "are disembowelled, the head and the feet taken away, and preserved in honey for the same uses" (line 5208). For the depilatory use they are "moistened with oil" (line 5208). No quantity, number of animals or dose is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The only general fact offered is that many amphibians have irritant skin secretions (above). Nothing is claimed for the leprosy use; modern leprosy is an infection treated with antibiotics, and the Dioscoridean term does not map onto it here.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides.** The one printed quality triplet is "antiseptic, ulcerating, and heating" (line 5208). No humoral degree (hot, dry, and so on in the Galenic manner) is given, and none is supplied here.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. Chapter 2-67 gives a description, an action, an external use and a way of keeping the animals, and it states and rejects a popular belief about fire. It records no season, provenance or assay of the kind that makes the discipline-of-attention frame useful for other substances, and it assigns no status to the person taking the remedy.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing in the source for a cultivator to use. The remedy is an irritant and a depilatory; the one thing the chapter models is a refusal to repeat the fireproof story, which is a small piece of the Dioscoridean discipline of attention and not a practice.

### Contraindications

Dioscorides himself calls the material ulcerating and heating, which is reason enough to keep it off the skin. Salamanders handled or prepared whole can carry skin toxins, and the animals are protected in various places. Taking it internally is not recorded in the chapter and is not suggested. Hygiene (wild animal material), toxicity and conservation are reasons enough for abstention.
