# Sow Thistle — Common Sow-Thistle · Hare's Lettuce · Milk Thistle *of the ancients* · *sonchos* (σόγχος) · Ku Ju Cai (苦苣菜)

## *Sonchus oleraceus* L. | Family: Asteraceae

**Tradition Tags:** EP, AT, DMM, CM
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry in the second degree (Galenic) — a cooling bitter; *mubarrid* and *muṣaffī* in the Unani register
**Training Phase:** Cooling / Bitter Correction (Phase III) — the quenching, appetite-restoring, heat-clearing move

---

### ⚠️ NOMENCLATURAL WARNING — READ FIRST

**This plant is not *Silybum marianum*.** In older herbals, and still in some vernacular usage, *Sonchus oleraceus* is called **"milk thistle"** — because it bleeds white latex. The plant modern readers call milk thistle — the silymarin-bearing liver herb — is ***Silybum marianum***, a different genus, a different chemistry (flavonolignans), and a different clinical literature. **Nothing in the silymarin/hepatoprotection evidence base applies to *Sonchus*.** Any entry, database record, or supplement label that transfers evidence between them is in error. The Kenetica index must keep these separate, and the cross-reference below exists precisely to enforce that separation.

### Origin & Tradition

A soft, hollow-stemmed, glaucous annual of disturbed ground and old gardens — one of the most successful weeds on earth, now cosmopolitan. Break any part of it and it bleeds: a copious **white, milky latex**, sticky and bitter. That latex is the entire doctrinal history of this plant compressed into one observation. Its Latin epithet *oleraceus* — "of the vegetable garden" — records the other half of its identity: it is, and always was, **food**. A famine green, a spring pot-herb, a salad bitter, eaten from the Mediterranean to China to New Zealand (where it is *pūhā*, a staple Māori green).

**Dioscorides — *sonchos* (σόγχος), the cooling emollient**
Dioscorides places *sonchos* squarely among the **cooling, emollient, refrigerant** herbs — the same doctrinal neighbourhood as lettuce (*thridax*, *Lactuca*), which it strongly resembles both botanically (Asteraceae, Cichorieae, latex-bearing) and doctrinally. Its uses in the Greek stream: as a cooling poultice for inflamed and hot swellings, for heat in the stomach, and — following directly from the lettuce analogy — as a mild quietener. The Greek doctrine is *psyktikon* (cooling) and *styptikon/emollient* by turns. Note the taxonomy the ancients built by inspection: **latex-bearing Cichorieae = cooling bitters.** That is a real chemotaxonomic group, and they got it right by eye and tongue.

**The Latin/Roman stream — "hare's lettuce" and the sow**
The vernacular names are the doctrine. **Sow thistle** — the plant fed to nursing sows, on the express belief that it increases their milk. **Hare's lettuce** — the plant hares eat, again with a milk-and-vigour association. The folk-veterinary practice of feeding *Sonchus* to lactating sows is genuinely and widely attested across European agricultural usage, and it is the direct ancestor of the human galactagogue claim. This is where the entry earns its keep, and we will deflate it below.

**Avicenna and the Unani stream**
*Sonchus* is received into the Perso-Arabic corpus with the Greek doctrine intact and sharpened: it is classed as **cold and dry**, a *mubarrid* (refrigerant), *muṣaffī* (cleanser/purifier), and a bitter *muqawwī-yi meʿda* (stomach-strengthener) — a cooling bitter given for heat of the liver and stomach, for burning thirst, for hot swellings applied externally, and for the sluggish, heat-oppressed digestion that the humoral system reads as bile-dominant. The Unani logic is a two-move argument: *bitter tonifies and drains the liver; cold quenches the heat; latex softens and soothes.* All three moves are made from sensory properties of the plant.

**Chinese Materia Medica — 苦苣菜 *kǔ jù cài* / 苦菜 *kǔ cài*, "bitter vegetable"**
*S. oleraceus* is a recognised Chinese folk food-medicine — the name says what it does: **bitter**. Its doctrinal placement is the one every bitter cooling green gets: **clear heat and relieve toxicity** (清热解毒 *qīng rè jiě dú*), **cool the blood**, and drain damp-heat; used for sores, boils, dysenteric disorders, and hot swellings. The modern Chinese pharmacological literature explicitly frames it this way — Li et al. (2017) describe *S. oleraceus* as "a dietary and traditional medicinal plant in China" and go on to test the anti-inflammatory action directly ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/13880209.2017.1280514)).

**Assyrian and Ebers attributions — hedge hard**
Cooling, bitter, latex-bearing leafy greens of the lettuce/chicory type are broadly present in both the Mesopotamian and Egyptian medical corpora, and Egypt in particular had a well-known cult and culinary relationship with *Lactuca*. But **no secure species-level identification of *Sonchus oleraceus* in Akkadian medical texts or in the Ebers papyrus can be asserted.** Cuneiform plant identifications are unstable; Egyptian ones are contested. **[USER: verify the Assyrian and Ebers tags. **Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

**The whole doctrine of this plant is its latex.** Everything else follows.

*The galactagogue signature — and why it is a signature and not a mechanism.* A plant that bleeds white when cut, in a pre-chemical medicine, is a plant that says *milk*. This is the **doctrine of signatures** operating in its purest, least metaphorical form — not a symbolic resemblance requiring interpretation, but a direct visual identity: the fluid *looks like* the fluid. From that single observation the tradition built a chain: the plant is milky → the plant makes milk → feed it to the nursing sow → the sow thrives → the name "sow thistle" seals the doctrine into the language, where it has sat for two thousand years, self-reinforcing.

Now map it onto the chemistry, and the chain breaks at the first link. **The white latex of the Cichorieae is not a milk analogue. It is a wound-defence secretion**, and its characteristic constituents are **sesquiterpene lactones** — bitter, reactive, α-methylene-γ-lactone-bearing molecules whose biological function is to deter herbivores and seal injury. (The same tribe gives us lactucarium, the bitter latex of wild lettuce, and the guaianolide bitters of chicory and dandelion.) These compounds are **electrophilic Michael acceptors**: their pharmacology is dominated by covalent adduction to nucleophilic cysteines — including, canonically for this compound class, the p65 subunit of NF-κB. That is an **anti-inflammatory** mechanism. It has **nothing whatever to do with prolactin, oxytocin, or mammary secretion.** The latex is not milk-like in any respect except colour and opacity, which are functions of a rubber-particle emulsion, not of casein and fat.

So: the signature reads *milk*, and the chemistry says *bitter defence lactone with NF-κB activity*. **The galactagogue doctrine of sow thistle is signature-driven and is not supported by evidence.** State it plainly. And note the epistemic trap that kept it alive: a nursing sow given a large volume of a palatable, water-rich, nutrient-dense green will very often *appear* to milk better — because she has been fed, hydrated, and made to eat. Nutrition and hydration are genuine determinants of lactation. The plant "works," in the barnyard, for reasons that have nothing to do with the whiteness of its sap. **The observation was real; the inference was wrong.** This is the same error structure as the China root and syphilis, in a gentler register.

*The bitter-cooling doctrine — the half that survives.* The second doctrinal reading of *Sonchus* is the one that holds up. Bitter + cooling + cleansing, given for hot swellings, sores, inflamed stomach, and heat of the liver, converges — in the Greek, Unani, and Chinese registers independently — on a single physiological target: **inflammation**. And here the chemistry cooperates. The sesquiterpene lactones and the substantial phenolic/flavonoid load (caffeoylquinic acids, quercetin glycosides, luteolin and apigenin glycosides) give exactly the pharmacological profile that doctrine predicts: TLR-4/NF-κB suppression, COX-2 downregulation, reduced NO and pro-inflammatory cytokine output, and free-radical scavenging. **The "clears heat and resolves toxicity" claim is, in modern terms, a claim about innate-immune tone — and it is preclinically supported.** The bitterness that the tongue reports is, quite literally, the molecule doing the work reporting itself.

*The bitter as digestive stimulant.* The Unani *muqawwī-yi meʿda* reading — bitter strengthens the stomach — has a straightforward modern gloss: bitter compounds on lingual and gastric bitter-taste receptors provoke cephalic-phase secretion and appetite. This is a general property of bitters, well described as a class. **It has not been demonstrated for *Sonchus oleraceus* specifically in any study retrieved here** — grade it *plausible by class, unverified by species*.

### Active Components

- **Sesquiterpene lactones** (of the Cichorieae type) — the bitter principles of the latex; the class-defining constituent group of the tribe. **[USER: verify the specific lactone profile characterised for *S. oleraceus*; no species-specific isolation paper was retrieved in this run, and the class attribution here is made on tribal chemotaxonomy, not on a retrieved assay.]**
- **Caffeoylquinic acids** — chlorogenic acid, neochlorogenic acid, chicoric acid, caftaric acid, 1,5- and 3,5-dicaffeoylquinic acids. Sergio et al. (2020, *Foods*) identified this caffeic-acid-derivative suite across a panel of Mediterranean wild edible greens including *Sonchus* ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/foods9091320)).
- **Flavonoid glycosides** — glycosides of **apigenin, luteolin, quercetin, isorhamnetin, kaempferol**; quercetin-3-rutinoside (rutin) among them (Sergio et al., 2020 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/foods9091320)).
- **Triterpenes and phytosterols** — β-sitosterol and ursolic acid were identified by HPLC-MS among the anti-inflammatory compounds of an aqueous extract, together with ferulic acid, rutin, and villosol (Li et al., 2017 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/13880209.2017.1280514)).
- **Total phenolic and flavonoid load** — quantified in an Egyptian leaf extract at **127.66 ± 0.56 mg GAE/g** total phenolics and **74.80 ± 0.55 mg QE/g** total flavonoids, with 43.46% DPPH scavenging (Salim et al., 2023, *Molecules* — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules28176389)).
- **Latex rubber particles, mucilage, water, minerals** — the bulk of the fresh plant; nutritionally significant, pharmacologically inert. This is a **vegetable** first.
- **Notably absent: silymarin.** There are no flavonolignans here. See the nomenclatural warning.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Sow thistle is a **Phase III Cooling/Bitter-Correction agent, and a food-grade one** — the gentlest class of intervention in the system. Its role is corrective and nutritive, not pharmacologically forceful.

Indicated for: (1) **hot, irritable, inflamed presentations** — the traditional "damp-heat"/"bilious" pattern; sores, boils, hot swellings, inflamed skin, applied topically or eaten; (2) **bitter restoration of appetite and digestive secretion** in the heat-oppressed, appetite-suppressed subject; (3) as a **daily nutritive green** carrying a meaningful phenolic/antioxidant load — arguably its single best-supported use, and the one requiring no doctrine at all.

**It is NOT indicated as a galactagogue in Kenetica.** The historical claim is recorded, explained, and declined. Nursing mothers seeking to support supply should be directed to the evidence-based determinants — frequency of milk removal, hydration, and adequate energy intake — not to a signature.

### Scientific Correlation

*According to PubMed*, the modern evidence for *Sonchus oleraceus* is entirely preclinical, moderately consistent, and — importantly — it supports the *cooling-bitter* half of the doctrine and is **silent or negative on the galactagogue half**.

**Anti-inflammatory (evidence grade: MODERATE preclinical; no human trials).**
Li et al. (2017, *Pharmaceutical Biology*) tested an aqueous extract in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages and in mice. The extract significantly inhibited pro-inflammatory cytokines and mediators at gene and protein level from **31.3 µg/ml**, and suppressed expression of **TLR-4, COX-2, NF-κB and pSTAT**; in vivo it produced significant anti-inflammatory effects in the xylene-induced mouse ear oedema model at **125 and 250 mg/kg**. Active compounds identified by HPLC-MS: villosol, ferulic acid, β-sitosterol, ursolic acid and rutin ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/13880209.2017.1280514)). This is a direct molecular correlate of *qīng rè jiě dú* / "cools heat, resolves toxicity" and of the Greek use on hot swellings. Note the mechanistic convergence with China root (CPU-00967): two unrelated plants, two unrelated doctrines, the **same TLR-4/NF-κB brake**. That convergence is itself evidence that the doctrines were tracking a real physiological axis.

**Antinociceptive (evidence grade: PRELIMINARY animal).**
Vilela et al. (2009, *J Ethnopharmacol*) tested hydroethanolic and dichloromethane extracts of the aerial parts in mice across formalin, hot-plate, tail-immersion and acetic-acid writhing models at 30–300 mg/kg p.o. Both extracts reduced writhing and licking and increased reaction time in thermal tests; the authors report that at 300 mg/kg the extracts had "a stronger antinociceptive effect than indomethacin (5 mg/kg, p.o.) and morphine (10 mg/kg, p.o.)" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2009.04.037)). **Treat that comparison with real caution** — a crude extract outperforming morphine in a mouse thermal assay is a result that demands independent replication before it means anything, and no such replication was retrieved. Recorded, not endorsed.

**Antioxidant and antihyperglycaemic (evidence grade: PRELIMINARY animal).**
Salim et al. (2023, *Molecules*) gave alloxan-diabetic Wistar rats *S. oleraceus* leaf extract at 100/200/300 mg/kg for 56 days against glibenclamide 10 mg/kg. At 200 and 300 mg/kg, blood glucose, total lipid, total cholesterol and LDL-C fell and HDL-C rose, with histological improvement in pancreatic tissue ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules28176389)). Rodent, alloxan model, single group — **not a human claim, and not a traditional indication either.** No tradition claimed this plant for diabetes.

**Antioxidant chemistry, and a practical cooking finding.** Sergio et al. (2020, *Foods*) — boiling reduces the phenolics and antioxidant capacity; steaming and microwaving preserve or increase them ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/foods9091320)). Qiao et al. (2021, *Food Chemistry*) confirmed the extract's antioxidant/enzyme-inhibiting power in a food-preservation context, identifying **more than 50 phenolics and flavonoids** by LC-MS/MS ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2021.129132)).

**⛔ GALACTAGOGUE (evidence grade: NONE — DOCTRINE DEFLATED).**
**No study of *Sonchus oleraceus* and lactation was retrieved in this search.** Not a human trial, not an animal study, not a mammary-cell assay. The claim rests on nothing but the signature and the barnyard.

For calibration on what a galactagogue claim *would* need to look like: the field can produce properly controlled work — Saejueng et al. (2022, *PLoS ONE*) ran a three-arm randomised controlled trial of a Thai herbal tea vs domperidone vs placebo in 120 post-caesarean women, with 72-hour milk volume as the primary outcome, and found a significant difference favouring the herbal tea over placebo ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0247637)). That is the evidentiary bar. *Sonchus* has not been anywhere near it. And the underlying mechanistic literature is candid about how thin the ground is generally: Kelleher et al. (2024, *Mol Nutr Food Res*) reviewed 80 studies on polyphenols and lactation and reported "a profound lack of mechanistic information" on how botanical supplements affect milk production ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/mnfr.202300703)).

**Verdict: the galactagogue reputation of sow thistle is a doctrine-of-signatures artefact.** Its persistence is explained by nutrition, hydration, palatability, and two millennia of linguistic reinforcement from the name itself. **Do not carry this claim forward.**

**Overall grade: TRADITIONAL, with MODERATE preclinical support for the anti-inflammatory/cooling-bitter indication and NO support for the galactagogue indication.** No human trial of this species was retrieved for any indication.

### Contraindications

- **Not a galactagogue.** Nursing mothers should not rely on this plant to increase milk supply. Eaten as a vegetable in ordinary food quantities it is not known to be harmful in lactation, but **medicinal doses in pregnancy and lactation are not supported by safety data and should be avoided.**
- **Asteraceae cross-sensitivity.** Persons sensitised to ragweed, chrysanthemum, chamomile, feverfew, arnica, or other Compositae may react. **Sesquiterpene lactones are the classic contact allergens of this family** — Compositae dermatitis is a well-recognised occupational and horticultural entity. Handle the fresh plant and its latex with care; latex on skin, repeated, is a plausible sensitiser.
- **Bitter-lactone GI irritation.** Large or prolonged doses of any bitter Cichorieae preparation may cause nausea, cramping, or diarrhoea.
- **Nitrate accumulation.** Like many fast-growing leafy weeds of disturbed and fertilised ground, *Sonchus* can accumulate nitrate. **Do not harvest from field margins, roadsides, heavily fertilised ground, or contaminated land.** [USER: verify species-specific nitrate data if this is to be a dietary staple; the concern is generic to leafy Asteraceae/Chenopodiaceae weeds.]
- **Heavy metal accumulation.** As a ubiquitous ruderal weed of waste ground, harvest site matters more than harvest technique. Urban and industrial sites: do not.
- **Cold/deficient constitutions (TCM):** a cold, bitter, draining herb is contraindicated in spleen-yang deficiency with cold diarrhoea and poor appetite.
- **Do not substitute for *Silybum marianum*.** If a patient or reader expects liver-protective silymarin, this plant does not contain it, and it will not do that job.
- **Drug interaction data: none retrieved.** Grade unknown.
