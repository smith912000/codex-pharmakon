### Origin & Tradition

*Schizonepeta tenuifolia* is an annual aromatic herb of the mint family (Lamiaceae), reaching roughly 0.5–1 m, with slender, deeply divided (pinnatisect) leaves — the epithet *tenuifolia*, "slender-leaved" — and dense terminal spikes of small pale flowers. It is native to and widely cultivated across China, Japan and Korea, favouring open, sunny, well-drained ground; the volatile-oil content of the aerial parts is strongly light-dependent, with high-light conditions favouring limonene and pulegone accumulation and darkness favouring menthone (per the biosynthesis study cited below). The drug is the dried aerial part or flowering spike, harvested in late summer to autumn.

**SBJ — Shennong / Chinese materia medica stream.** In the Chinese tradition the herb is **Jing Jie (荆芥)**, one of the classic *jiě biǎo* ("release-the-exterior") wind-dispersing herbs. It is grouped with the acrid-warm exterior-releasers and directed at early-stage wind-cold or wind-heat patterns: aversion to cold, fever, headache, and — the feature that most distinguishes Jing Jie from its neighbours — **skin eruptions**. Classical practice uses it to "vent" incompletely expressed rashes (measles, urticaria, wind-rash) and to relieve itch, and it appears in formulae for early sores and carbuncles. A charred preparation, **Jing Jie Tan (荆芥炭)**, is traditionally used to stop bleeding (epistaxis, uterine bleeding, blood in stool). These are traditional-doctrine indications; see Scientific Correlation for how much the laboratory record actually supports.

### Active Components

The pharmacologically dominant fraction is the **essential (volatile) oil** of the aerial parts, a monoterpene-rich mixture. Constituents repeatedly identified by GC-MS include:

- **Menthone** and **isomenthone** — often major.
- **Pulegone** — a p-menthane ketone and the key **toxicological flag** (see Contraindications); the same molecule that makes pennyroyal oil hepatotoxic.
- **Piperitone**, **limonene**, and the sesquiterpene **β-caryophyllene**.
- Menthol-type and "schizonepetoside" menthane derivatives are described in the wider literature for this species (traditional/phytochemical claim; not independently re-verified against a primary paper this run — **[USER: verify specific schizonepetoside identity]**).

A spectrum–effect study singled out **menthone, isomenthone, pulegone, piperitone and β-caryophyllene** as the constituents most correlated with the oil's measured antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity. Beyond the oil, the plant contains flavonoids (the flavone **luteolin** is reported for the genus/species in the phytochemical literature — **[USER: verify exact source]**) and phenolic acids, which are the usual carriers of anti-inflammatory activity in Lamiaceae.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Within Kenetica, Jing Jie is filed as a **surface-opening, wind-dispersing signature** with a specific affinity for the **skin as an organ of expression**. Its Foundation-phase teaching value is the pairing of two ideas the tradition keeps together and the laboratory partly confirms: (1) an acrid-warm, diffusive quality aimed at the body's exterior in the earliest, most superficial phase of an illness, and (2) a directed action on eruptive, itching skin. The cultivator/practitioner uses it where a process is trying to move *outward* and is stuck — an incompletely surfaced rash, an early cold, an itch without a fixed lesion.

The honest correlate is **anti-inflammatory and anti-pruritic (anti-itch) activity demonstrated preclinically** (mouse and cell models), including in atopic-dermatitis and UVB-photoaging skin models. This maps unusually well onto the traditional "vent the rash / stop the itch" indication — but the evidence is animal/in-vitro, not human clinical, and the tradition's "release the exterior for colds" claim has no retrieved clinical trial behind it. Use the concordance as a teaching case in how a doctrinal signature can point at a real pharmacology without proving the whole doctrine.

### Scientific Correlation

Grading each claim:

- **Anti-inflammatory (essential oil, constituent-linked): PRECLINICAL, moderate.** Nitric-oxide inhibition in macrophage (RAW 264.7) assay, with menthone/isomenthone/pulegone/piperitone/β-caryophyllene identified as drivers.
- **Anti-pruritic / anti-atopic-dermatitis: PRECLINICAL, moderate.** Reduced scratching, erythema, IgE, mast-cell/eosinophil infiltration and Th2 cytokines (TNF-α, IL-4, IL-13) in a DNCB-induced atopic-dermatitis mouse model (as one component of a two-herb mixture). Concordant with the traditional skin/itch indication; not human data.
- **Skin photo-protection / anti-photoaging: PRECLINICAL.** Ethanolic extract reduced wrinkling, MMP expression and collagen loss in UVB-irradiated hairless mice.
- **"Release the exterior" for colds/fever: TRADITIONAL only.** No clinical trial retrieved this run.
- **Haemostatic (Jing Jie Tan): TRADITIONAL only.** No controlled evidence retrieved.
- **Pulegone hepatotoxicity: WELL-ESTABLISHED (as a molecule / congener caution).** From the pennyroyal literature — a real, mechanistically characterised CYP-mediated hepatotoxicity of pulegone. This is a *constituent* caution for the oil, not a demonstrated harm of ordinary decoction doses.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Chinese materia medica: **acrid (pungent), slightly warm; Lung and Liver channels.** Category: exterior-releasing, acrid-warm, with a wind-dispersing and rash-venting emphasis; the charred form is re-categorised toward the blood/haemostatic function. Energetics stated per that tradition's own framework; not an independent physical measurement.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy — caution/avoid.** The oil contains **pulegone**, an established abortifacient-associated and hepatotoxic monoterpene in the pennyroyal literature; concentrated oil is contraindicated in pregnancy and the herb should be used in pregnancy only under qualified supervision.
- **Do not ingest the concentrated essential oil.** Pulegone undergoes CYP-mediated bioactivation to hepatotoxic metabolites (menthofuran and downstream reactive species). Ordinary decoction is far lower-dose, but the neat oil is a genuine poisoning risk.
- **Hepatic impairment** — extra caution given the pulegone metabolism pathway.
- Traditional caution against use in patterns of profuse sweating / exterior-deficiency (per Chinese source doctrine).
- Allergy: as a Lamiaceae aromatic, possible sensitisation in atopic individuals.
- The **FMT/Tibetan attestation is unverified** — do not represent this herb as a documented rGyud-bzhi drug until confirmed.
