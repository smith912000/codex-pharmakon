# Aconite root — 乌头 *wu tou*, "Crow's Head" (also 奚毒 *xi du*, 即子 *ji zi*, 乌喙 *wu hui*)

> **TOXICITY CAUTION. This is a lethal poison.** Aconite roots contain alkaloids that are potentially fatal by mouth in small amounts and can be absorbed through the skin. This entry is a historical record of what one early Chinese text says. It is not a recommendation, not a preparation guide, and not a basis for any use. Nothing here should be gathered, prepared, handled raw or taken.

## *Aconitum kusnezoffii* Rchb., the northern monkshood (Chinese 草乌 *cao wu*) is the identification adopted here; the identification is medium-confidence, because the early Chinese texts treat 乌头, 附子 and 天雄 as growth-stages or parts of one plant and the species is not fixed by the text* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts on disk, as a Chinese drug.** The Codex's aconite entry I_004 (CPU-00048, *Aconitum napellus*) is a multi-tradition entry that already carries an SBJ paragraph; other monkshoods (I_231, I_407, I_903, I_1222, I_1223) rest on the Tibetan, Ayurvedic, Greek and Arabic texts and are other species. A search of the non-Chinese texts in `Sources/` for *A. kusnezoffii* found nothing. This entry treats the Shennong monograph 乌头 by itself. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder from Yang under the name *Radix Aconiti*; this entry replaces it. Overlaps: I_004 and CPU-00264 (Fu Zi, *A. carmichaelii*), which I_004 also covers; CPU-02257 (a placeholder, *Radix Lateralis Aconiti Carmichaeli*) and CPU-02005 (*Aconitum* sp.), both unwritten; the Shennong monographs 附子 (line 3039) and 天雄 (line 3063) are separate entries in Sun's file.

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ), the Shennong text proper.** The entry sits in the lower classic (下经). Sun's 味 paragraph reads: "味辛，温。主中内、恶风洗洗，出汗，除寒湿痹，咳逆上气，破积聚、寒热。其汁，煎之，名射罔，杀禽兽。一名奚毒，一名即子，一名乌喙。生山谷。" ("Acrid, warm. It governs [中内], aversion to wind with shivering [洗洗]; it brings out sweat; it removes cold-damp impediment, cough with counterflow and rising qi; it breaks accumulations and gatherings, cold and heat. Its juice, decocted, is called *she wang* [射罔, 'Shooting Net']; it kills birds and beasts. Other names: *xi du* [奚毒, 'Extraordinary Toxin'], *ji zi*, *wu hui* [乌喙, 'Crow's Beak']. It grows in mountains and valleys.") (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, lower classic, entry 乌头 *wutou*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 3051). The Shennong paragraph states the poison in a single clause: the decocted juice kills birds and beasts. It prints no 有毒 (toxic) in the paragraph; the toxicity is stated by the Wu Pu layer and by the class (below).

**Second witness, Yang 1998 (attributed to Yang).** Yang (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`, lines 3348-3355) reads: "acrid and warm. It is toxic, treating mainly wind stroke and aversion to wind as after a soaking. It promotes sweating, eliminates cold damp impediment and cough and counterflow qi ascent, breaks accumulations and gatherings, and [relieves] cold and heat. The decoction of its juice is called She Wang (Shooting Net), and it can kill birds and beasts." Yang writes "toxic" and "wind stroke" where Sun's text shows neither word (Sun has 中内 in that place); the difference is reported, not resolved. Yang's note 207 (line 3358 onward) says the three drugs Wu Tou, Tian Xiong and Fu Zi "are the same substance", distinguished by season of harvest or by size and root position, and is commentary and not the Shennong text.

**Wu Pu layer (3rd century, quoted by Sun).** Line 3053 gives further aliases (莨, 千狄, 毒公, 卑负, 耿子) and records "神农、雷公、桐君、黄帝：甘，有毒" ("Shennong, Lei Gong, Tong Jun, Huangdi: sweet, toxic"). It describes the plant: it "begins to grow in the first month; the leaves are thick, the stem square and hollow, the leaves "four and four" facing each other [叶四四相当; the Codex's rendering is tentative], resembling 蒿". It then describes 乌喙 as a separate form, toxic, "picked in the tenth month, shaped like the wu tou, with two branches joined like a crow's beak", and a further form with "神农、岐伯：有大毒；李氏：大寒" ("Shennong and Qi Bo: greatly toxic; Mr Li: greatly cold"), picked in the eighth month, said to be "the large horn of the fuzi". The Wu Pu layer therefore calls the drug toxic in the name of Shennong, which the Shennong paragraph itself does not.

**Mingyi bielu layer (later additions, quoted by Sun).** Line 3055: "生郎陵。正月、二月采，阴干。长三寸以上，为天雄" ("It grows at Langling. Gathered in the first and second months, dried in the shade. Those three *cun* or more in length are *tianxiong*.").

**Sun's own note (按).** Lines 3057-3059 cite the *Shuowen* (乌喙), the *Erya* ("芨, 堇草" with Guo Pu: "that is wu tou; Jiangdong calls it 堇"), the *Fan Zi Jiran* ("wu tou comes out of the Sanfu; the white is good"), the *Guoyu* (Li Ji put 堇 in the meat; Wei Zhao: "堇 is wu tou"), and the *Huainanzi* ("none is more fierce than 鸡毒"; Gao You: "鸡毒 is wu tou"). Sun adds that 鸡毒 is 奚毒; the line then breaks off ("即子，即…"). The ancient commentators therefore knew the plant as a famous poison.

**Identity.** The Chinese text does not fix the species. Wu tou, fuzi and tianxiong are treated by the text as parts or stages of one plant (Wu Pu and Mingyi above, and Yang's note 207). In modern Chinese pharmacy the name 乌头 is applied to the mother root of *A. carmichaelii* (川乌) and 草乌 to *A. kusnezoffii*; these are general facts and not from the sources read. The job proposes *A. kusnezoffii*; the Codex records that *A. carmichaelii* is the other leading candidate. Confidence: medium. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. As a general fact, *Aconitum* roots contain diterpenoid alkaloids, among them aconitine, mesaconitine and hypaconitine; they act on the voltage-gated sodium channels of nerve and heart muscle and can cause fatal arrhythmias and paralysis. In Chinese pharmacy the roots are processed by prolonged soaking and heating, which is understood to hydrolyse the most toxic alkaloids to less toxic ones; processing is not a guarantee of safety. This is a general statement and not drawn from the Codex's sources. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The Codex has no use case for this drug. The Shennong text lists an acrid, warm drug for cold-damp impediment, cough and counterflow, accumulations and gatherings, with the juice recorded as an animal killer. The Codex does not translate these into modern diagnoses. There is no protocol, and the plant must not be used.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation in the Shennong paragraph is the statement that the decocted juice is called *she wang* and kills birds and beasts (line 3051). It is recorded here as a statement about the poison, not as a method. The Mingyi layer gives the harvest as the first and second months, dried in the shade (line 3055); Wu Pu gives the tenth and the eighth months for the two beak-shaped forms (line 3053). No dose is given in the sources retrieved, and none is supplied. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Aconite poisoning is a well-documented clinical emergency, with palpitations, numbness and paralysis, cardiac arrhythmia and death in severe cases; this is a general fact. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**SBJ.** The Shennong text prints "味辛，温" (acrid, warm). Wu Pu attributes "sweet, toxic" to Shennong and others (line 3053), and Mr Li "slightly cold" for the 乌喙 form and "greatly cold" for a further beak-shaped form; the Shennong paragraph itself and the Wu Pu quotation disagree on the flavour. Sun's appendix of drug relations (附：诸药制使, from line 4191; a later compilation, with Tang Shenwei's remark at line 4193) lists "乌头、乌喙 莽草为使，反半夏、栝蒌、贝母、白蔹、白芨；恶藜芦" (line 4439): "the 莽草 as its envoy; opposed to 半夏, 栝蒌, 贝母, 白蔹, 白芨; averse to 藜芦". It is not the Shennong text proper.

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(b) Framed.* The entry stands in the lower classic (下经), whose preface (line 2935) says lower drugs "treat disease to correspond with the earth; many are toxic and cannot be taken long; for those who wish to remove cold and heat evil qi, break accumulations and gatherings and cure illness, take the lower classic". The entry has no 久服 clause. It is the opposite of a life-nourishing drug: the class is the one the preface bars from long use, and the paragraph itself records a juice that kills animals. The grade is a warning and not a status. (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, lower classic, entry 乌头 *wutou*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 3051)

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with raw aconite. The text's own grade places it in the disease-attacking class, not to be used long, and the paragraph names its poison in one clause. There is no life-nourishing use here, and the honest practice is to read the entry as a record and not to gather, handle or take the plant.

### Contraindications

**Potentially lethal. Do not use.** The Shennong text proper records that the juice kills birds and beasts; Wu Pu records "toxic" and, for the beak-shaped form, "greatly toxic". Poisoning can occur by mouth and by skin absorption of fresh plant material. The plant must not be used in pregnancy, by children, or with other medicines; the Codex gives no safe dose. Poisoning is a medical emergency: contact emergency services at once. The early writers' own confusion of names for this plant (above) is a reason not to rely on any identification from the text. Legal restrictions apply to aconite in many countries.
