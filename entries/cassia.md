# Cassia / Chinese Cinnamon — *Cinnamomum cassia* (L.) J.Presl

**Family:** Lauraceae | **Class:** plant | **Volume:** I · Plant | **Codex id:** CPU-00007
**Attested in:** Dioscorides · rGyud-bzhi · Shennong Bencao Jing · Ebers Papyrus

---

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

*Cinnamomum cassia* (L.) J.Presl — commonly rendered Chinese cinnamon, cassia cinnamon, or bastard cinnamon — is a medium-sized evergreen tree of the family Lauraceae native to southern China and widely cultivated across East and South-East Asia. It must be distinguished from *Cinnamomum verum* J.Presl (syn. *C. zeylanicum* Blume), the Sri Lankan "true" or Ceylon cinnamon. The two are botanically separate species with distinct morphology and markedly different chemical profiles. The bark of *C. cassia* is thick, hard, and reddish-brown; Ceylon cinnamon bark is thin, pale, papery, and multi-layered. In commerce, *C. cassia* accounts for the great majority of global cinnamon sales, particularly in North America and continental Europe. [1]

Within the Chinese materia medica, the plant yields two pharmacologically distinct drugs: **Rou Gui** (肉桂), the dried inner bark from mature trunk and large branches, used as a potent warming and yang-supplementing agent; and **Gui Zhi** (桂枝), the dried young twigs, which act more superficially to release the exterior and warm the channels. The distinction is both botanical and therapeutic and has been maintained in Chinese pharmacy for over two millennia. [2] Synonyms in the historical record include the Greek *kasia* and the Latin *cassia*, which Dioscorides applied broadly to an inferior grade of cinnamon-like bark in contrast to the prized *kinnamomon*. [3]

## 2. Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Shennong Bencao Jing (c. 1st–2nd century CE, compiled from earlier oral traditions)

The *Shennong Bencao Jing* (神農本草經), the earliest systematic Chinese materia medica, lists Rou Gui among the superior (*shang*) class of 120 drugs — those held to nourish life, be non-toxic, and be suitable for long-term use. Within that tradition's framework, the drug is characterised as acrid and sweet in flavour, hot in nature (*re*), and entering the Heart, Kidney, Liver, and Spleen channels. Its attributed actions are reinforcing Fire and strengthening Yang (*bu huo zhu yang*), warming Yang and dispersing cold, and warming the channels to promote blood circulation. [4] This framing is explicitly the system's own theoretical vocabulary, not a biochemical claim.

### 2.2 Dioscorides — *De Materia Medica* (c. 50–70 CE)

Pedanius Dioscorides, a Greek physician serving in the Roman army, described both *kinnamomon* and *kasia* in Book I of *De Materia Medica*, a text covering approximately 600 plant substances. He characterised *kasia* as a heating and drying aromatic, inferior in fragrance and quality to true cinnamon, and employed it in compound remedies for digestive complaints, menstrual disorders, and as an ingredient in aromatic ointments and theriac preparations. Dioscorides' recognition of *kasia* as a distinct, lower-grade cinnamon-type bark represents the first systematic European pharmacological record of the species. [3] His framework is the Galenic humoral model; all claims here are attributed to that system.

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE, Leipzig University Library)

The Ebers Papyrus, a 110-page medical scroll preserved at Leipzig University and dated to approximately 1550 BCE, references aromatic compound preparations including substances rendered in translation as cinnamon or cassia. The incense compound *kyphi*, documented in the Harris Papyrus and referenced in Ebers-adjacent texts, lists cinnamon among its ingredients and was used in temple rites and as a medicinal preparation for lung and liver conditions. [5] Precise botanical identification of the Egyptian term from this period cannot be established with certainty; the attribution to *C. cassia* specifically, as opposed to other Cinnamomum species imported via Red Sea and East African trade routes, remains the scholarly consensus but not a resolved question. [6]

### 2.4 rGyud-bzhi (Four Tantras — compiled 8th–12th century CE)

The *rGyud bzhi* (Four Tantras), the foundational text of Tibetan medicine synthesised under the physician Yuthok Yönten Gönpo during the reign of Trisong Deutsen (755–795 CE) and later revised in the 12th century, incorporates cinnamon bark among warming spice substances (*tsha ba'i sman*) used in compound formulas for cold-natured disorders. Within the Tibetan medical framework, which classifies substances according to taste (*ro*), post-digestive taste (*zhu rjes*), and potency (*nus pa*), cinnamon falls in the pungent-sweet category with heating potency and is applied in treatments corresponding to conditions of excess cold and Wind-Phlegm (*rlung-bad kan*) imbalance. [7] As with all traditional-framework claims, this description attributes the framing explicitly to that tradition.

## 3. Convergence Analysis

Four geographically and epistemologically distinct traditions converged independently on a consistent functional profile: *C. cassia* is warming, aromatic, and beneficial in cold-type or stagnant conditions affecting digestion, circulation, and reproductive organs. The consensus across Dioscorides, Shennong, the Tibetan rGyud-bzhi, and Egyptian medical practice on the aromatic and heating character of the drug represents one of the more robust cross-traditional agreements in the Codex. The convergence is especially notable given the trade routes along which cassia actually moved — from southern China westward through India, Arabia, and into the Egyptian and Mediterranean markets — suggesting that practical commercial knowledge of the drug's properties may have preceded its formal pharmacological codification in any single tradition.

The one major divergence is degree of emphasis: Chinese medicine developed the most granular functional taxonomy (distinguishing bark from twig, and using each for distinct clinical presentations), whereas Mediterranean and Egyptian uses prioritised the aromatic and compound-preparation aspects.

## 4. Phytochemistry & Composition

The principal bioactive constituent of *C. cassia* bark is **trans-cinnamaldehyde**, accounting for approximately 55–90% of the essential oil. Additional constituents include eugenol, cinnamic acid, cinnamyl acetate, and polyphenolic polymers (Type-A proanthocyanidins). [8]

Critically, *C. cassia* contains substantial quantities of **coumarin** (1,2-benzopyrone), measured at approximately 2.23 mg/g dry weight in Chinese cinnamon bark. [9] *C. verum* contains only trace coumarin by comparison — this chemical distinction is the principal safety-relevant difference between the two species and is directly implicated in regulatory dosing guidance (see Section 8). Coumarin is hepatotoxic in susceptible individuals; the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) established a Tolerable Daily Intake (TDI) of **0.1 mg coumarin per kg body weight per day**, based on a NOAEL of 10 mg/kg/day from a two-year dog hepatotoxicity study. [9]

---

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

Modern pharmacological investigation has identified several mechanisms relevant to the drug's traditional indications:

**Glycaemic modulation:** cinnamaldehyde and Type-A procyanidins appear to enhance insulin receptor autophosphorylation, increase GLUT4 transporter synthesis and translocation, activate PI3K/AKT and AMPK signalling pathways, and inhibit intestinal glucosidases, thereby reducing postprandial glucose absorption. [10]

**Anti-inflammatory:** cinnamaldehyde inhibits NF-κB signalling and downregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β) and MAPK pathways. [8]

**Antimicrobial:** trans-cinnamaldehyde disrupts bacterial cell membrane permeability, impairs biofilm formation, and generates reactive oxygen species, with documented in vitro activity against *E. coli*, *Salmonella enterica*, *Listeria monocytogenes*, and *Campylobacter jejuni*. [11]

**Vasodilatory / circulatory:** consistent with the TCM indication of warming the channels, cinnamaldehyde has demonstrated vasodilatory effects in animal models, plausibly mediated via calcium channel antagonism and nitric oxide pathways. [8]

## 6. Clinical Evidence

The clinical evidence for *C. cassia* is most developed in the domain of glycaemic control, and the picture is mixed.

A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis focused specifically on cassia bark in type 2 diabetes found **no statistically significant reduction** in fasting plasma glucose or HbA1c in the pooled analysis, despite trends in individual trials. [12] A 2022 umbrella meta-analysis aggregating eleven prior meta-analyses of RCTs found cinnamon (species often unspecified or cassia-dominant) **did significantly reduce** fasting plasma glucose, insulin, HOMA-IR, and HbA1c. [13] A 2022 *Frontiers in Physiology* meta-analysis reported improvements in glycolipid metabolism. [14]

The heterogeneity across trials reflects inconsistencies in species identification, dose, preparation form (aqueous extract vs. whole powder), treatment duration (40 days to 4 months in reviewed trials), and baseline HbA1c. The clinical verdict remains **promising but unresolved**: neither a strong positive nor a clear null result can be stated without qualification, and no regulatory authority has approved cassia bark as a treatment for diabetes.

**Important safety note:** most clinical trials used doses of 500 mg to 6 g of cassia bark powder daily. At 2.23 mg coumarin/g, a 6 g daily dose delivers approximately 13.4 mg coumarin — equivalent to the EFSA TDI for an adult weighing approximately 134 kg. For a 70 kg adult, the EFSA TDI is 7 mg/day; a 3 g dose of cassia powder may approach or exceed this limit. [9, 12]

## 7. Phenomenological Record

Traditional users across Chinese, Tibetan, Ayurvedic, and Mediterranean contexts consistently report subjective heat — warming of the extremities, thorax, and abdomen — following ingestion. This is consistent with the circulatory mechanisms identified in preclinical pharmacology. The aromatic quality of the drug (principally from cinnamaldehyde volatiles) is reported uniformly across all four attesting traditions as a medicinally significant property in its own right, not merely an incidental characteristic.

No systematic phenomenological study in the Western clinical sense has been conducted for cassia specifically. The traditional phenomenological record is assigned to Section 9 and Section 2 where it overlaps with framework-attributed properties.

## 8. Practical Application

**Forms:** dried bark (whole or ground), aqueous decoction, standardised aqueous-ethanolic extract, essential oil (for topical/aromatic use only — not for internal consumption at therapeutic doses).

**Typical doses (TCM):** Rou Gui 1.5–4.5 g/day decocted, or 1–2 g/day as powder; Gui Zhi 3–9 g/day decocted. [2]

**Coumarin safety ceiling:** for *C. cassia* specifically, EFSA TDI of 0.1 mg coumarin/kg body weight/day constrains supplemental use. At ~2.23 mg coumarin/g of cassia bark, a 70 kg adult should not exceed approximately 3.1 g cassia bark powder per day as a sustainable supplemental ceiling. Individuals with hepatic impairment, those taking anticoagulants (coumarin potentiates warfarin), or those using cassia long-term should be advised accordingly. [9] *C. verum* (Ceylon cinnamon) is the preferred substitute where coumarin avoidance is indicated.

**Drug interactions:** coumarin-derived anticoagulant potentiation; theoretical additive hypoglycaemic effect with insulin or oral hypoglycaemics requires monitoring.

**Contraindications (TCM framework):** avoided in Yin deficiency with heat signs, pregnancy (in large doses), or conditions characterised by excess Fire.

## 9. Spiritual & Esoteric Layer

Within Chinese medical cosmology, Rou Gui is regarded as one of the quintessential Yang-supplementing drugs — warming the Mingmen (Gate of Vitality), the deep Fire source seated between the Kidneys that sustains all physiological transformation. This doctrinal placement is the tradition's own framing, not a modern physiological claim. The drug is used in classical formulas such as *Gui Fu Di Huang Wan* to "guide Fire back to its source" in conditions of floating, rootless Yang.

In the Galenic humoral system, cinnamon and cassia were classed as hot and dry in the third degree — among the most intensely warming of all aromatics, used to counteract cold-phlegm conditions and to fortify the vital spirits (*pneuma*).

In ancient Egyptian ritual and temple contexts, cassia formed part of the *kyphi* incense preparation burned in temple sanctuaries and at sunset rites, believed to facilitate communion with the divine and — according to Plutarch's later account of Egyptian religion — to relieve anxiety, pacify dreams, and brighten the spirit. The aromatic smoke was understood as a medium between the material and numinous realms; this claim belongs to the Egyptian religious framework as reported in the historical record, not to modern pharmacology. [5]

Tibetan medicine situates warming substances with heating potency (*drod nus*) as agents that restore the primordial warmth (*drod*) essential to all metabolic and cognitive function, linking the drug's physiological and spiritual registers in a non-dualist framework.

---

## 10. Functional–Anatomical Correlate

The primary anatomical correlate, across traditions, is the **cardiovascular and peripheral vascular system** — specifically the capacity to deliver warmth and metabolic substrates to cold, stagnant, or deficient tissues. Modern pharmacology partially validates this through cinnamaldehyde's vasodilatory activity and the drug's influence on glucose metabolism and insulin signalling, which affect every metabolically active tissue. Secondary correlates include the **gastrointestinal tract** (digestive warming, glucosidase inhibition) and **hepatic tissue** — relevant not only to the glycaemic evidence base but also, with an important inversion, as the primary site of coumarin hepatotoxicity. The liver is simultaneously the organ most responsive to cassia's metabolic benefits and most at risk from sustained high-dose exposure — a functional paradox that warrants careful dosing in any extended protocol.
