# Seps lizard — *Seps* (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 2-70), "also called the Calchidicen lizard"
## *"Lizard of scincoid genus Seps — POISONOUS", the translator's identification; the text itself names only "Seps (also called the Calchidicen lizard)"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The only tradition attesting this material in the sources retrieved is Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica*, in the 2000 English edition (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`; "text" below). The chapter is 2-70, headed *SEPS*, at line 5218 (the spine's locus, `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`: "2-70 (line 5218)", translator's identification "Lizard of scincoid genus Seps — POISONOUS", class animal). The word POISONOUS is the edition's own label, printed under the heading as on the cantharides (2-65) and buprestis (2-66) chapters, and it is a warning that the animal is harmful and not a recommendation. The index to the edition carries "SEPS 206" and, as a variant name, "Calchidicen lizard 206" (lines 12582, 10873).

The whole chapter, line 5220, reads: "Seps (also called the Calchidicen lizard) taken as a drink in wine heals those bitten by him." In the printed text this sentence is followed on the same line by the heading of chapter 2-71. The chapter does not describe the animal; it says only that the seps is the creature whose bite is treated and that the remedy is the animal itself, taken in wine. It is a like-for-like remedy: the poisoner is the antidote. How the animal is prepared (fresh, dried, burnt, pounded) is not stated. The Latin name is the translator's, and the species meant is open: Dioscorides' own words place the animal among the lizards and give it a bite that harms ("those bitten by him"). [USER: verify]

The seps then turns up as the creature that bites, and there are two more places where the remedies for its bite are given. Chapter 5-22 (*Oxumeli*, oxymel, line 9879) says of the oxymel (a drink of vinegar, sea salt, honey and water) that "It is available for those bitten by the viper seps [poisonous lizard]" (line 9881); the text there writes "viper seps", and the bracket is the translator's. The text at line 5812 reads "dysentery, haemorrhoids, as well as eruptions of blood, and bites of the seps [poisonous lizard]". In the OCR order it follows chapter 2-150 (*Andrachne*, purslane, line 5797) after a page break and a plate caption, and the start of its sentence is not on the lines read, so which substance it describes is not established here. The index lists "bites of the seps 275" (line 12839). So the seps in Dioscorides is the subject of one chapter whose remedy is the animal itself, and a bite for which at least one other chapter names a remedy (5-22). [USER: verify]

The chapter that follows, 2-71 (*Skinkos*, "Scincus officinalis — Skink", heading at the end of line 5220, gloss at line 5221), is the skink, a different animal. It is preserved in salt and used as an aphrodisiac, and has its own treatment. It is not treated here.

No other tradition on the tag line, and no text outside Dioscorides on disk, was found naming the seps. A whole-word search of the Sushruta, Ebers, Tibetan and Assyrian texts on disk for "seps" returned nothing.

### Active Components

Lizards are reptiles whose composition is muscle protein, fat and the other tissues of a vertebrate. Not all lizards are venomous; a few species are. The chapter does not say why the animal harms, and names no constituent, and this entry names none.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The recorded use is an antidote to the animal's own bite, a drink of the animal in wine. The remedy is not a treatment for any bite: a bite from a poisonous animal needs prompt medical care, and the chapter does not report that the remedy worked. The Codex's application layer has nothing to add, and no protocol is offered.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives the animal "taken as a drink in wine" (line 5220) and nothing else. No quantity, no preparation of the animal and no number of doses are given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] Nothing in general knowledge supports the idea that drinking an animal in wine neutralises its bite; envenomation is treated by modern methods that are not related to this passage.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides.** No quality (warming, astringent, or the like) is printed for the seps in chapter 2-70, and none is supplied here. The only characterisation is the edition's label POISONOUS.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. Chapter 2-70 is one sentence naming an antidote that is the harmful animal itself. It records no provenance, season or assay such as makes the discipline-of-attention frame useful elsewhere in the work, and it assigns no status to the person taking the remedy.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing in the source for a cultivator to use, and a poisonous animal in wine is a remedy to leave in the book. The chapter is useful only as a record of a bite and the folk remedy for it.

### Contraindications

The material is labelled POISONOUS in the edition itself. Do not eat or drink the animal. A bite or sting from any venomous or unknown animal needs prompt medical care. Wild reptiles may carry salmonella and other pathogens, and some are protected. These are general cautions and not claims from the text.
