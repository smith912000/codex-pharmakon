# Leontopodium vulgare — Leontopodion (Dioscorides 4-131)

## *Leontopodium vulgare, the first of the names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-131 ("Common Lion's Foot"), with Gnaphalium leontopodium ("Everlasting, Lion's Paw Cudweed") and Leontopodium alpinum ("Edelweiss, Live Ever, Lion's Foot") printed beside it* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (lines 9186 and 9187) prints three names with no choice among them: "Leontopodium vulgare — Common Lion's Foot", "Gnaphalium leontopodium — Everlasting, Lion's Paw Cudweed" and, on a second line, "Leontopodium alpinum — Edelweiss, Live Ever, Lion's Foot". The row takes the first. It was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-131. LEONTOPODION*, heading at line 9185, SUGGESTED line at 9186, text at line 9188, with the page number 679 at line 9189 and the list of names resuming at line 9192. It stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), after *4-130. PHUTEUMA* and before *4-132. IPPOGLOSSON*. The translators' index gives *Leontopodion*, *Leontopodium alpinum* and *Leontopodium vulgare* at page 679 (lines 16287 to 16289).

The whole chapter reads: "Leontopodion is a two-finger long little herb with small, slender, strong leaves the length of three or four fingers — rough, woolier towards the root, and whitish. On the tops of the stalks are little heads (as it were) bored through, which have black flowers. The seed is hardly seen because of the down that wraps it. The root underneath is small. They also say that this is prescribed for love medicines to be hanged on one, and that it dissolves small swellings. It is also called zoonychon, aetonychon, cemus, damnamene, idiophyton, phytobasila, crossion, or crossophthoon. The Magi call it sanguis crocodili, some, crocomerion, the Egyptians, daphnoenes, the Romans, minercium, and some, neumatus, palladium, or flammula."

Dioscorides gives a description and only two reported uses. The plant is a small herb whose leaves are rough, "woolier towards the root" and whitish. The stalk heads are "bored through" and carry black flowers, the seed is hidden by down, and the root is small. The measures are as printed: the herb is "two-finger long" and the leaves "the length of three or four fingers", which the chapter does not reconcile. Of use he writes only "They also say": that it is prescribed in "love medicines to be hanged on one" (carried or worn), and that it "dissolves small swellings". The "They also say" form reports what others say and does not give Dioscorides' own endorsement. The chapter prints eight further Greek names, the Magian name *sanguis crocodili*, *crocomerion* ("some"), the Egyptian *daphnoenes*, the Roman *minercium*, and, from "some", *neumatus*, *palladium* and *flammula*. They are names only, with no use or belief attached to them.

**Names shared with other chapters.** *Leontopodium* is also printed among the other names of *3-110. LEONTOPETALON* (heading at line 7527), a field plant with red flowers and a black knotty root. That chapter is the subject of the separate entry I_1117 (*Leontice leontopetalum*), and the two chapters describe different plants. The translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-131 also uses *Gnaphalium*, the genus of the existing entries I_1088 (*Filago germanica*, which names 4-131 only in a note that it is a separate chapter and is not read there) and I_1094 (*Gnaphalium arenarium*, a different chapter).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides reports, as hearsay, that the herb is prescribed for "love medicines to be hanged on one" and that it dissolves small swellings. These are the text's reports. The Codex gives no protocol, and a charm worn for love is not a therapeutic claim the Codex can assess. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-131 gives one handling instruction: the plant is "hanged on one" for love medicines. For the swellings it gives no preparation. No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. It prints the leaves as "rough, woolier towards the root, and whitish" and the seed as hidden by down.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter does record an amuletic use, a herb "hanged on one" for love medicines, but it records it as something "they also say", and recording is not endorsing. The chapter also records the Magian name *sanguis crocodili* and the Egyptian *daphnoenes* as names only, with no belief attached. The part of the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition that is present here is the description of the plant (small, whitish, woolly towards the root, black-flowered heads, seed hidden in down). The plant is given no status in the chapter beyond that of a reported charm and a reported remedy for small swellings. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It offers a description, a reported charm and a reported remedy for small swellings, without amounts, so it is history and not a practice. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. A swelling that persists or grows needs medical assessment, and a home remedy must not replace it. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion among three names, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry, and an alpine plant that is protected in many places should not be picked on that account.
