# Wolf stomach — sbyang.kihi.pho.wa, "Wolf's stomach" (Clark 1995); "Wolf stomach" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *The stomach of the wolf; Clark prints the Tibetan name and says it "has twelve names" but gives no binomial, and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary pairs "Wolf" with "Canis Lupus" in an OCR-split list* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The Explanatory Tantra (*bshad rgyud*), chapter 20, on the powers of medicines, has a section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin", which opens: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (Clark 1995, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` lines 1147-1148). The wolf's stomach is listed under the sub-heading "Meat" (line 1161), in a run of organ and flesh entries. Clark's line 1170 reads: "The lungs, heart, liver, spleen, kidneys and flesh (of various animals) cure diseases of those respective organs. sbyang.kihi.pho.wa: Wolf's stomach: [has twelve names] It increases digestive heat and digests undigested food, [especially meat]. Its tongue removes swellings of the tongue. Pig tongue: removes bone warts." The stomach is the item that carries the Tibetan name; the tongue that follows it is a separate material with its own entry in this Codex. The bracket "[has twelve names]" and the bracket "[especially meat]" are interpolations from the commentaries: Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets are taken from the *Blue Aquamarine* and *Spreading Tree* commentaries. The root-text sentence without them is "It increases digestive heat and digests undigested food."

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 3535) says: "Wolf stomach generates digestive heat and helps the digestion of all undigested food particles." The two translations agree on the substance and on the two actions, which are one action stated twice: more digestive heat, and digestion of food that has not been digested. Clark's gloss adds that the food is "especially meat"; the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence says "all undigested food particles", and does not say meat.

The name list `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` has a row for "Wolf stomach" (line 350; no binomial; marked as an identification that is uncertain; source "Explanatory Tantra ch.20 (Efficacy of Medicinal Substances)"). That row rests on this chapter and gives no further locus.

The wolf is placed in the Tantra's classification of meats among the carnivorous animals: "Carnivorous animals include the tiger, leopard, bear (*Selenarctos thibetanus*), snow bear (*Ursus arctos*), snow leopard (*Panthera pardus*), wolf, lynx, fox, hill dog and weasel" (Clark line 672, group (e)). The Men-Tsee-Khang text lists "the tiger, leopard, black bear, Tibetan brown bear, snow leopard, wolf, lynx, fox, dhole (Tibetan wild-dog) and monkey" as "the group of carnivorous wild animals" (line 2912). Its glossary of animal names pairs "Wolf" with "Canis Lupus" (lines 7023 and 7027), but the glossary is a split two-column OCR list in which the pairing is by order and not certain. [USER: verify] Clark's preface (line 83) warns that Tibetan materia medica is often "either totally unidentified or only tentatively identified", and that where doubt remains over an animal product he has set the Tibetan name beside the probable English identification.

*Other wolf materials, not folded in.* The wolf's tongue is a separate material (the same line, 1170). The wolf's stool is a third (line 1199; it appears in the "Droppings" paragraph). Outside the Tibetan texts, Sushruta (Sutrasthana ch.46, `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` lines 534-544) places the wolf among the *Guhashayas*, the cave-dwelling mammals, and says of the flesh of that group that it "is sweet, heavy, demulcent and strength-giving" and "subdues the deranged Vayu". That is a statement about flesh. The text read does not mention the stomach, and Sushruta is not on this entry's tag line.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, the stomach wall of a mammal is muscle and mucous membrane, and the glands of the lining make hydrochloric acid, the enzyme pepsin and mucus. The organ's contents vary with what the animal last ate. Nothing links any constituent to the actions the Tantra gives, which are increasing digestive heat and digesting undigested food.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts say that the wolf's stomach increases digestive heat and digests undigested food. The Codex does not recommend it. It is an organ of a wild carnivore that can carry parasites and pathogens, the wolf is a protected animal in many countries, and poor digestion has causes that need assessment. The entry exists to record what the Explanatory Tantra says. In the Tibetan account digestive heat is a central physiological notion: it "refers to the digestive bile", it supplies heat to the humors and the bodily constituents, and "if the digestive heat is strong the digested material moves downwards after digestion, (but if it is) weak the food is passed undigested" (Clark line 319). That is the frame in which an action on "undigested food" is stated, and it is the Tantra's own account, not a modern one.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither translation gives a preparation, vehicle, quantity or course for the wolf's stomach. The text does not say whether it was eaten fresh, dried, burnt or powdered, or whether it was part of a compound. The "medicinal horses" at the end of the chapter (Clark line 1202), raw cane sugar, crystal sugar and honey, are named as vehicles for compounded medicines in general; the text does not say that the wolf's stomach was taken with any of them. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. The gastric juice of a mammal contains acid and the enzyme pepsin, which begins the digestion of protein; an animal organ eaten by a person does not supply a working digestive secretion, because cooking or drying denatures the enzymes and the human stomach makes its own. Raw or poorly cooked organs of wild carnivores can carry parasites and bacteria that infect people. Indigestion and failure to digest food have many causes, some of them serious. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** No taste, post-digestive taste or potency is printed for the wolf's stomach; the printed actions are that it increases digestive heat and digests undigested food. The Tantra's rule for meats, that all types are sweet in taste and post-digestive taste, with cool, light and coarse powers in dry-land animals (Clark line 673), is about flesh and is not carried over to this organ.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The wolf's stomach is named in the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica of animal products with a therapeutic action on digestive heat (Clark line 1170). The wider frame is real but does not reach it. In Clark's account the three humors are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration (line 51), and these three are "the three causes" of wind, bile and phlegm disorders (line 147), so disease is at root a spiritual condition and medicine is one support. The chapter on routine behaviour asks the practitioner to refrain from the ten negative actions, the first of which is "taking life" (line 616). The rejuvenation passage in chapter 23, "Normal health" (lines 1266-1275), does not name the wolf's stomach, and the lines read give it no *bcud len* use. The tradition has no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The wolf's stomach is one line in a list of organ and flesh remedies, and the tradition's own first precept is against taking life. Where digestion is the concern, the Tantra's own advice on protecting the digestive heat is to take "warm (-powered) and light food, drink and behavioral pattern" (line 319), by which "the strength and life of the body will be preserved"; that is the Tantra's general counsel and is not a use of this substance. The Codex does not recommend the organ. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Infection and hygiene:** organs of wild carnivores can carry parasites and pathogens; do not eat, handle or apply them.
- **Digestive complaints:** persistent indigestion, vomiting, weight loss, black stools or pain need medical assessment.
- **Identity:** the Tibetan name is printed but no binomial is, and the identification of the animal rests on the English "wolf" and a glossary pairing that is itself uncertain.
- **Conservation and law:** wolves are protected in many countries and regions, and taking wild animals or their products is regulated or banned; do not collect or buy them.
- **Ethics:** the Tibetan source itself lists "taking life" first among the ten negative actions (Clark line 616).
