# I-589 | Senna

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**

**Botanical name:** *Senna alexandrina* Mill. (syn. *Cassia senna* L.; *Cassia acutifolia* Delile — Alexandrian senna); *Senna obtusifolia* (L.) H.S.Irwin & Barneby (Tinnevelly senna)  
**Family:** Fabaceae (subfamily Caesalpinioideae)  
**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (purgative — clears accumulation)  
**Convergence Score:** 8/9 traditions  
**Entry type:** Medicinal Herb — Anthraquinone Stimulant Laxative

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary names:**
- English: Senna, Alexandrian Senna, Tinnevelly Senna, Wild Senna
- Arabic: *Sanā* (سنا) — the source of all European names; the original Arabic name; from the ancient trade route names (*Sanā Makki* = Meccan senna; *Sanā Mūsa* = Moses's senna — referred to varieties from different origins)
- Latin: *Senna* (direct borrowing from Arabic); *Cassia* (classical Latin name for cassia-type plants; senna was in the *Cassia* genus before molecular reclassification)
- Greek: *Senna* (post-classical; Arabic loan)
- German: *Sennespflanze*, *Sennesblätter* (senna leaves)
- French: *Séné*, *Cassia séné*
- Chinese: *Fān Xiè Yè* (番泻叶) — *fān* = foreign/Western; *xiè* = purgation; *yè* = leaf: "foreign purgative leaf" — explicitly acknowledging the non-Chinese origin when it arrived via the Silk Road
- Japanese: *Senna* (センナ); *Kinetsusōka*
- Sanskrit: *Svarnpatri* (gold-leaf senna); Ayurvedic name reflecting the golden-yellow leaflets
- Hindi: *Sōna mukhi* (golden-faced), *Sanā*
- Tibetan: *Kha-ru-rta* — uncertain identification; *Senna* was adopted into Tibetan medicine via Arabic-Unani influence

**Taxonomy note:**
The genus *Cassia* was extensively reclassified in the 1990s based on molecular systematics:
- *Senna* genus split from *Cassia* for species with distinct floral characteristics
- True *Cassia* retained for a smaller group of trees (*C. fistula* — golden shower tree; golden pods → *Cassia* pods as mild laxative)
- *Chamaecrista* split off for sensitive plants/partridge peas
- All three were previously called *Cassia*; current nomenclature: *Senna alexandrina* (not *Cassia senna*)

**Botanical identity (*S. alexandrina*):**
Erect subshrub or herbaceous perennial, 30–90 cm; stems much-branched. Leaves: evenly pinnate, 3–8 pairs of leaflets; leaflets lanceolate, 1.5–3 cm, asymmetric at base; pale green; slightly leathery. Flowers: bright yellow, 5 petals, 1 cm; racemes. Pods: flat, oblong, 3–6 cm × 1.5–2 cm, distinctive brown papery pod containing 6–8 brown seeds — the "senna pods" of commerce; alternatively, the dried leaves are the primary commercial product.

**Distribution and cultivation:**
*S. alexandrina* native to Upper Egypt and Sudan; now cultivated throughout North Africa, India (Tamil Nadu — Tinnevelly/Tirunelveli district = source of Tinnevelly senna), Pakistan, China. India is the world's largest producer (>50,000 tons/year leaves and pods).

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Cross-traditional agreement on properties:**
Every tradition that documented senna agreed on three properties: (1) laxative action; (2) relatively gentle action compared to harsh purgatives (scammony, aloe in strong form, castor oil); (3) dose-dependent effects — small doses laxative, larger doses cathartic/purgative with cramping. This consistent characterization across Arabic, Ayurvedic, Chinese, Egyptian, and Tibetan medicine reflects accurate empirical observation of the anthraquinone dose-response curve.

**Ibn Sīnā's pharmacological insight:**
Ibn Sīnā's characterization of senna as "opening the bowels without damage to the stomach" — distinguishing it from scammony (resinous; acrid; more irritating) — represents sophisticated clinical discrimination between stimulant laxatives of different mechanisms. The modern distinction between anthraquinone stimulant laxatives (senna, cascara) and osmotic laxatives (lactulose, PEG) and bulk laxatives (psyllium) was intuitively grasped by Ibn Sīnā through clinical observation.

**The trade route dependency:**
Senna's cross-traditional presence is partly driven by active trade: Egypt → Arabia → India via Indian Ocean routes; Egypt → Assyria via Levantine land routes; Arabia → China via Silk Road. This commodity trade pattern means senna's presence in multiple traditions partially reflects trade rather than independent discovery. However, the consistent therapeutic characterization across these traditions is pharmacological, not commercial.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Primary bioactive compounds:**

**Anthraquinone glycosides (primary active fraction; 1.5–3% in leaves; 2.5–5% in pods):**

**Sennosides (the specific senna anthraquinones):**
- **Sennoside A**: dianthrone glycoside; β-1,8-dianthrone (emodin anthrone dimers); PRIMARY sennoside; converted to rhein anthrone by colonic bacteria
- **Sennoside B**: diastereomer of sennoside A; approximately equal amounts to A in commercial preparations
- **Sennoside C**: mixed glycoside of aloe-emodin and rhein anthrones
- **Sennoside D**: related dianthrone

**Mechanism of activation:**
Sennosides are prodrugs. They are:
1. Not absorbed in small intestine (large polar molecules; resist intestinal enzymes)
2. Reach colon INTACT
3. Colonic bacteria hydrolyze glycoside → free anthraquinone aglycones (sennosidins)
4. Sennidins reduced by bacterial enzymes → **rhein anthrone** (primary active metabolite)
5. Rhein anthrone → stimulates colonic motility and secretion

**Other anthraquinones:**
- **Rhein**: oxidized form; present free and as glycosides; less active than rhein anthrone
- **Aloe-emodin**: structurally related; minor in senna; more prominent in aloe vera anthraquinone fraction
- **Chrysophanol**: minor yellow anthraquinone
- **Emodin**: present in very small amounts (primarily found in rhubarb and cascara)

**Non-anthraquinone compounds:**
- **Mucilage**: galactose-containing polysaccharide; contributes to mild demulcent effect
- **Flavonoids**: kaempferol, isorhamnetin; anti-inflammatory
- **Naphthalene glycosides**: tinnevellin; specific to Tinnevelly senna (*S. obtusifolia*)

**Sennoside A/B ratio:**
Standardized senna preparations specify total hydroxyanthracene content (calculated as sennoside B equivalent) per European and US Pharmacopoeias.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Primary mechanism: Colonic secretory and motility stimulation via rhein anthrone**

**Step-by-step anthraquinone laxative mechanism:**

1. **Oral ingestion → small intestinal transit** (minimal absorption; most reaches colon intact)
2. **Colonic bacterial metabolism**: *Bifidobacterium* spp., *Clostridium* spp., and other bacteria:
   - Hydrolyze sennoside glucoside bonds → free sennosidins
   - Reduce sennosidins → rhein anthrone (key active metabolite)
3. **Rhein anthrone → colonic epithelial targets:**

**Mechanism A — Cl⁻ secretion:**
- Rhein anthrone → PGE₂-mediated activation of adenylyl cyclase in colonocytes → increased cAMP → cAMP-dependent activation of CFTR (Cystic Fibrosis Transmembrane Conductance Regulator) Cl⁻ channel → increased luminal Cl⁻ secretion → passive water secretion → increased luminal fluid → softened stool

**Mechanism B — Inhibition of Na⁺ absorption:**
- Rhein anthrone → inhibits active Na⁺ absorption in colonocyte → increased luminal Na⁺ → osmotic water retention → additional fluid in lumen

**Mechanism C — Enhanced colonic motility:**
- Rhein anthrone → ENS (enteric nervous system) stimulation → increased high-amplitude propagating contractions (HAPCs) → accelerated colonic transit
- May involve 5-HT₄ receptor pathway contribution

**Net result:**
Increased luminal fluid + increased motility → bowel movement 6–12 hours after ingestion (the characteristic delayed onset reflects time for small intestinal transit + bacterial metabolism in colon)

**Melanosis coli:**
Long-term senna use → anthraquinone metabolites deposited in colonic mucosal macrophages → brownish-black discoloration of colonic mucosa (melanosis coli, a misnomer — not melanin but lipofuscin-like anthraquinone derivatives). Completely benign and reversible on discontinuation. No functional significance. Can be seen on colonoscopy as distinctive brown-black colonic mucosa; used as a marker for anthraquinone laxative use.

**Cathartic colon (historical controversy):**
Historical concern that long-term senna use causes "cathartic colon" — atonic colon with impaired motility requiring ever-increasing doses. Modern evidence: this condition does not appear to be caused by senna per se; the appearance of cathartic colon on radiograph may have reflected underlying disease rather than drug effect. Melanosis coli does not cause motility impairment. Current consensus: senna is safe for long-term daily use at appropriate doses (supported by Cochrane review data for constipation in elderly patients and in palliative care).

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Level A — Well-designed RCTs and meta-analyses:**

**Constipation (general and palliative care):**
- **Systematic review Candy 2011** (*Journal of Pain and Symptom Management*): senna for constipation in cancer/palliative patients; equivalent to polyethylene glycol for opioid-induced constipation (OIC); Level A evidence for palliative constipation management
- **Cochrane review Ford 2014**: Stimulant laxatives (senna, bisacodyl) for constipation; significant benefit vs. placebo; NNT ~3 for defecation at 24h; sennosides 12–36 mg most studied effective dose

**Bowel preparation for colonoscopy:**
- Senna + polyethylene glycol combinations: Level A evidence for colonoscopic bowel preparation; non-inferior to PEG alone in multiple RCTs; sennosides reduce PEG volume needed

**Postoperative constipation:**
- Multiple surgical RCTs: senna reduces time to first postoperative bowel movement vs. placebo; Level A

**Opioid-induced constipation (OIC):**
- Standard of care: senna (docusate often added; evidence for docusate alone weak; combination common but senna provides most laxative benefit)
- Subcutaneous methylnaltrexone and oral naloxegol (peripheral opioid antagonists) now also available for OIC; senna less effective for OIC than for non-opioid constipation, but standard first-line in palliative care

**Pregnancy (third trimester):**
- Multiple controlled studies: no teratogenic risk; sennosides not absorbed from small intestine; systemic exposure minimal; rhein anthrone metabolites stay in colon
- FDA Category C (historical); currently considered relatively safe for short-term use in pregnancy constipation
- First trimester: caution (limited data); avoid high doses

**Level B — Children:**
- Pediatric constipation: senna liquid preparations; standard dosing by weight; effective; safety profile established

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The Alexandria Trade Hub:**
Alexandrian Senna (*Senna alexandrina*) takes its name from Alexandria, Egypt — not because the plant grows primarily there, but because Alexandria was the primary EXPORT HUB for Upper Egyptian and Sudanese senna along the Mediterranean trade network. The same naming pattern applies to "Alexandrian balm" and other Egyptian-traded commodities: the goods were associated with their port of departure, not origin. Upper Egypt and Sudan grew the plant; Alexandria shipped it to Rome, Arabia, and India. This mercantile naming artifact has persisted in pharmacopoeial use for 2,000 years.

**The Universal Laxative — Pre-Modern Surgery:**
Before enemas and modern bowel preparation, senna preparations were standard pre-surgical bowel preparation in Islamic medicine (10th–15th centuries). The Ibn Sīnā tradition of pre-surgical preparation included bowel cleansing with senna decoctions — a practice that parallels modern colonoscopic preparation protocols. The underlying rationale — reducing fecal contamination risk during abdominal surgery — was empirically rational even before bacterial infection was understood.

**Senna Pods vs. Senna Leaves:**
The pods of senna contain 2–5% sennosides (higher concentration than the 1.5–3% in leaves) but are larger, bulkier to transport, and require more processing. Historically, the LEAVES were the primary trade commodity. Modern pharmaceutical production primarily uses the PODS because of higher standardized sennoside content. This shift from leaves to pods represents one of the clearest examples of industrial pharmaceutical production optimizing the raw material selection of traditional medicine based on pharmacochemical analysis.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Standardized preparations:**

**Senna leaf preparations:**
- Standardized extract: 12–36 mg sennosides daily (typical laxative dose)
- Equivalent crude drug: approximately 1–2g dried senna leaves
- Onset: 6–12 hours (take at bedtime for morning bowel movement)

**Senna pod preparations:**
- Senokot (senna glycoside tablets): 7.5 mg sennosides per tablet; 2 tablets (15 mg) up to BID
- Ex-Lax Chocolated Senna: 15 mg sennosides per piece
- Pericolace (docusate + senna): combination; common in palliative care (docusate stool softener; senna stimulant)

**Senna tea:**
- Traditional preparation: 1–2 pods or 1–2g dried leaves in 150 mL boiling water, steep 10 min; drink at bedtime
- Variable sennoside content in non-standardized tea; generally lower than tablet preparations

**OIC (opioid-induced constipation) protocol:**
- Start prophylactically when opioid therapy begins: senna 15–30 mg at bedtime
- Titrate upward as needed: maximum 70 mg/day (4 tablets × 2 doses or equivalent)
- Add docusate 100–200 mg BID if stools remain hard
- If inadequate response: add lactulose 30 mL/day; or switch to methylnaltrexone (Relistor) SC if senna + lactulose insufficient

**Bowel prep for colonoscopy:**
- Low-volume split prep: senna 12–24 tablets (sennosides 90–180 mg) the evening before + PEG 1L; versus full PEG 4L alone; equivalent cleaning; better tolerance
- Follow gastroenterologist's specific prep protocol

**Safety parameters:**

**Short-term use:** Very safe; well-established profile
**Long-term daily use:** Generally safe; monitor electrolytes in vulnerable populations

**Hypokalemia risk:**
- Significant diarrhea → K⁺ loss → hypokalemia
- Risk groups: elderly, cardiac patients on digoxin (hypokalemia + digoxin = arrhythmia risk), patients on loop diuretics
- Monitor serum K⁺ if using ≥30 mg/day chronically; supplement potassium if low

**Abdominal cramping:**
- Common at higher doses (colonic smooth muscle hyperperistalsis)
- Reduce dose; consider switching to osmotic laxative if cramping limits use

**Melanosis coli:**
- Reversible; no clinical consequence; explain to patients if seen on colonoscopy

**Contraindications:**
- Intestinal obstruction (stimulant laxative in obstruction → perforation risk)
- Ileus
- Inflammatory bowel disease (active flare): avoid stimulant laxatives; increase intestinal inflammation
- Appendicitis risk: abdominal pain of uncertain cause — rule out surgical emergency before using laxative
- Severe dehydration or electrolyte imbalance
- Children <2 years: insufficient data

**Drug interactions:**
- Digoxin: hypokalemia from senna potentiates digoxin toxicity; monitor
- Diuretics (thiazide/loop): additive hypokalemia; monitor
- Estrogens (oral contraceptives): accelerated GI transit may reduce absorption; use additional contraception if significant diarrhea
- Anthraquinones in human milk: small amounts detected; short-term use considered safe during lactation; loose stools possible in infant
