# Stachys sylvatica — Sideritis trite, "the third sideritis" (Dioscorides 4-35)

## *Stachys sylvatica, the one name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-35, glossed "Hedge Woundwort"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk lists the plant under this name (the name indexes in `Sources/*/extract/names.csv` were searched). The entry rests on one short chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-35. SIDERITIS TRITE* at line 8301, SUGGESTED line ("Stachys sylvatica — Hedge Woundwort") at line 8302, text at line 8303. It stands in Book Four, "Other Herbs & Roots", after *4-34. SIDERITIS ETERA* and before *4-36. ACHILLEIOS* (line 8304). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 615, and the translators' own index prints "Stachys sylvatica 576" (line 17725).

The whole chapter reads: "There is said to be yet another sideritis (which Crateuas calls heraclea) growing in walls and vineyards, with many leaves from one root, similar to coriander, smooth tender little stalks about twenty centimetres long, somewhat white and ruddy; the little flowers of a Phoenician [red] colour, bitter to the taste, clammy — and the strength of this (applied) is that it congeals bleeding and new wounds."

The chapter opens with a hedge, "There is said to be", so Dioscorides reports a third *sideritis* without vouching for it in his own voice. It names an authority for one name only: Crateuas is said to call it *heraclea*. The description is for recognition: many leaves from a single root, leaves like coriander, smooth tender little stalks of about twenty centimetres, "somewhat white and ruddy", flowers of a Phoenician (red) colour. The habitat is walls and vineyards. The taste is "bitter" and the plant is "clammy". The one strength printed is that it, applied, "congeals bleeding and new wounds". The chapter prints no other names, no preparation and no quantity.

**The three sideritis chapters.** The text numbers its *sideritis* by position: 4-33 *SIDERITIS*, 4-34 *SIDERITIS ETERA* ("the other") and this chapter, "the third". The first of them (4-33, line 8291) also lists *Heraclea* among its own names, so the name *heraclea* is attached to two chapters in the text. Nothing in either chapter says they are one plant, and the Codex keeps them apart.

**Overlap.** A chapter-level search of the entries folder for 4-35 and for the Greek heading found no entry on this chapter. I_1198 (*Stachys arvensis*, chapter 4-34) and I_1200 (*Stachys recta*, chapter 4-33) are the two sister chapters and are not merged. I_977 (*Achillea ageratum*, chapter 4-36) mentions 4-35 only as a neighbour. I_1198 states that 4-35 "has no entry of its own that was found"; this entry fills that gap.

### Active Components

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records one claim, that the applied plant "congeals bleeding and new wounds", in a chapter that opens with a report ("There is said to be"). It is Dioscorides' claim and not a Codex protocol. The Codex gives no protocol for bleeding or wounds. Serious bleeding is a condition for emergency care, and a plant gathered from a wall or vineyard is not a dressing.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one mode of use: the plant "(applied)". It prints no part, no preparation, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that bleeding from a wound is controlled by direct pressure and clinical care, and that unwashed plant material put on an open wound can introduce infection.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 4-35 prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality. The only taste printed is "bitter", and the only texture is "clammy". Whether either is a quality in the Galenic sense is not said, and the Codex does not assign one.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a description for recognition (leaf compared with coriander, stalk length, colour of stalk and flower), a habitat (walls and vineyards), a taste (bitter) and a feel (clammy), together with a plain statement that the information is second-hand ("There is said to be") and carries the name given by a named authority. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is how the text marks its own evidence: it says "said to be" where it has not seen, and names who gave the name. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 4-35 prints no warning. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. The text offers the plant on hearsay. Bleeding and wounds need proper care and must not be left to an applied herb. The text gives no dose, and no safety information for pregnancy, children or any medicine taken at the same time was read. [USER: verify]
