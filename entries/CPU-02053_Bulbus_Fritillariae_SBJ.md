# Bulbus Fritillariae — Bei Mu 貝母 / 贝母 (Shennong bencao jing); alias Kong Cao 空草

## *A fritillary bulb, genus Fritillaria (Liliaceae); the identification is Yang 1998's "Bulbus Fritillariae" and is secure at genus level only: the classic describes no plant beyond the commentary quoted below, and the species is not settled in the sources read (F. cirrhosa, the species of the existing entry CPU-00156, is one candidate and is not asserted here). Confidence: high for genus, low for species* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

### Origin & Tradition

**Named by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts read here, under this Chinese name.** This is a single-text entry. The principal text is Sun Xingyan's 1799 reconstruction, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt`. Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`) is a second witness and is attributed to Yang. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder "Bulbus Fritillariae [SBJ]", taken from Yang's English. The first-pass check called it a duplicate of CPU-00156 (Fritillary Bulb, *Fritillaria cirrhosa*, tagged SBJ and FMT); an audit disagreed, because the classic's drug is the genus-level 貝母, not that species. This entry is therefore kept as the genus-level classic entry, and the overlap with CPU-00156 (and with CPU-00747, Zhe Bei Mu, *F. thunbergii*) is flagged for the maintainer. [USER: verify]

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** 貝母 is in the middle classic (中经, the middle classic, grade per Sun's monograph list; Sun ed., entry 贝母, line 2003, paragraph at line 2005). Sun's text reads: 味辛，平 ("acrid, balanced"); 主伤寒烦热 ("governs cold damage with vexing heat"), 淋沥 ("dribbling urination"), 邪气 ("evil qi"), 疝瘕 ("mounting and conglomeration"), 喉痹 ("throat impediment"), 乳难 ("difficult lactation"), 金创 ("incised wounds"), 风痉 ("wind tetany"). 一名空草: "one name Kong cao". There is no 久服 clause in the paragraph.

The Mingyi bielu layer (line 2007) adds the aliases 药实, 苦花, 苦菜, 商草 (one character is lost in the e-text) and 勤母, the habitat 生晋地 ("grows in the land of Jin"), and the harvest 十月采根，曝干 ("the root is gathered in the tenth month and sun-dried"). This is the only place that names the part used, the root.

Sun's commentary (案, lines 2011 to 2017) quotes earlier lexicons: the Shuowen defines the (lost) character as 貝母; the Guangya says 贝父，药实也; and the Erya has the same word. Guo Pu is quoted for the plant: 根如小贝，圆而白花，叶似韭 ("the root is like a small cowrie shell, round, with white flowers, and the leaf resembles chives"). Lu Ji is quoted: the leaf is like that of *gualou* but small, and the seeds lie under the root like taro seeds, quite white, four-sided and joined together so that they can be separated. These are the only plant descriptions in the sources read.

**Yang 1998 as second witness.** Yang prints (line 2666): "Bei Mu (Bulbus Fritillariae) is acrid and balanced. It mainly treats cold damage, vexatious fever, dribbling, evil qi, mounting conglomeration, throat impediment, difficult lactation, incised wounds, and wind tetany. Its other name is Kong Cao (Hollow Weed)." Yang's footnote (numbered 157 in the text; the OCR prints "151", line 2670) is modern commentary and not part of the classic: Fritillaria "opens depression and resolves binding, downbears heart fire and moistens the lungs, clears phlegm and stops cough", and its "main indications are the treatment of lung troubles". Yang then quotes an annotation by Zou Shu on the passage, which explains the listed complaints by phlegm drool gathering in the heart and chest (from line 2674); this is a later gloss quoted by Yang and not the classic. The "lung troubles" use does not appear in the classic's own paragraph.

**Drug relations.** The drug-relations table printed in Sun's e-text has a line for it (line 4373): 浓朴、白薇为使 ("houpo and baiwei serve as envoys"), 恶桃花 ("dislikes peach blossom"), 畏秦艽、矾石、莽草 ("fears qinjiao, alum stone, mangcao"), 反乌头 ("opposes wutou", aconite). The table is a later layer and is not part of the classic's paragraph. Aconite's own line (4439) lists 贝母 among the drugs it opposes.

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, fritillary bulbs are known for steroidal alkaloids, and the genus is the standard source of the bulbs used in Chinese cough remedies. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Cold damage with vexing heat, dribbling urination, evil qi, mounting conglomeration, throat impediment, difficult lactation, incised wounds and wind tetany. Yang's footnote adds a lung and phlegm reading that the classic does not state.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no preparation beyond sun-drying the root, no duration.

The Codex does not translate the classic's terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend taking fritillary bulbs. Several fritillaries are toxic, and the species here is not settled.

### Preparation & Dosing

The Mingyi layer gives the harvest only: the root, gathered in the tenth month and dried in the sun (line 2009). The classic gives no preparation and no dose. The preface (Sun ed. line 1677) has a general rule for the middle classic, which "may be toxic or non-toxic, to be weighed as fits the case". That is a rule for the class and not a dose for this plant. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any dose from this entry.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. *Fritillaria* is a genus of bulbous lilies; bulbs of several species are used in Chinese medicine for cough and phlegm, and the genus contains steroidal alkaloids, some of which are toxic at high doses. The classic's "mounting conglomeration" and "wind tetany" have no modern equivalent and are not translated into one. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SBJ (Sun 1799).** Flavour and nature 味辛，平, "acrid, balanced" (line 2005).
- **SBJ (Yang 1998).** "Acrid and balanced" (line 2666).
- No organ or channel assignment is given in the classic. Yang's "lung" reading is in his footnote and is not imported as a classification.

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(b) Framed.* The entry stands in the middle classic, whose preface (Sun ed. line 1677) reads 中药一百二十种为臣，主养性以应人。无毒、有毒，斟酌其宜。欲遏病补羸者，本中经: the middle drugs are the "ministers", mainly nourishing one's nature to correspond with humans, "toxic or non-toxic, to be weighed as fits", and for those who wish "to restrain illness and make up for emaciation". The drug's own paragraph carries no 久服 clause and no promise of lightening the body, not ageing, or lengthening years. It is therefore (b): framed by the middle class, with no life-nourishing status attested for the drug itself.

**Practitioner's note.** A middle-class root taken for a defined complaint, by the classic's own account, and no more. Nothing in the record supports long-term use. The later reading of it as a lung remedy is Yang's modern gloss. A cultivator has nothing to do with it beyond reading it as a record.

### Contraindications

- **Toxicity.** Several fritillaries are toxic, and the classic itself says the middle class may be toxic or not. Do not ingest bulbs gathered from the wild or from gardens. Many ornamental fritillaries are poisonous.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** No statement in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Opposed drugs.** The drug-relations table lists aconite as opposing it (line 4373 and line 4439). That is a later layer and is recorded here only as a historical caution.
- **Identity.** The species is not settled; the genus-level identification is Yang's. [USER: verify]
