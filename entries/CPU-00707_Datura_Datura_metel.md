# Downy Thorn Apple / Devil's Trumpet — Dhattura · Sacred Datura · Yang-jin-hua · Jimsonweed-kin

## *Datura metel* L. | Family: Solanaceae

> ⚠️ **CLASS: DELIRIANT POISON — NOT FOR SELF-ADMINISTRATION.** *Datura metel* contains potent tropane alkaloids (scopolamine, atropine, hyoscyamine). All parts are toxic. Ingestion causes anticholinergic delirium, hyperthermia, seizures, and death. **This entry is documentary and doctrinal only. The Codex prescribes NO internal-use protocol. Do not prepare, dose, or ingest.**

**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, DMM, SA, SBJ, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Galenic — hot and dry in the third-to-fourth degree (a poison-grade drug); Unani *har-yabis* (extreme); TCM warm, acrid, **toxic** (*du*), lung/liver channels
**Training Phase:** Restricted / Doctrinal — no practitioner-use phase assigned

### Origin & Tradition

*Datura metel* — the downy thorn-apple, devil's trumpet, the sacred and poisonous **dhattura** of India — is one of the most consequential poison-plants of the Old World, a Solanaceous herb bearing trumpet flowers and spiny fruit, every tissue of which is loaded with deliriant tropane alkaloids. It sits in the Codex as the archetype of the **sacred poison**: a plant simultaneously worshipped, feared, medicinally deployed at knife-edge doses, and lethally abused. Its traditions are unusually rich precisely because its danger demanded ritual containment; cultures did not merely use dhattura, they *bounded* it with priesthood, prohibition and ceremony.

**Ayurvedic (Sushruta & the classical tradition):** Dhattura holds a substantial, explicit place in classical Indian materia medica. It is classed among the *upavisha* (sub-poisons) — plants too toxic for casual use but rendered usable through careful *shodhana* (purification/detoxification) processing. Sushruta and the later nighantus describe dhattura for pain, respiratory conditions (asthmatic breathing, cough), skin disease, and as an external analgesic, always under the poison-plant discipline. Its association with Shiva made it a temple offering, folding its danger into a devotional frame. This is genuine, documented tradition — dhattura is a named *upavisha* with defined purification requirements, not an invented usage.

**Chinese (materia medica tradition):** As *yang-jin-hua* (洋金花, "foreign golden flower"), *Datura metel* flower is a recognised — and strictly toxic — item of Chinese materia medica. It is classed warm, acrid and toxic (*du*), used historically in minute controlled amounts for wheezing and cough, for pain, and famously as a component of surgical anaesthetic-analgesic preparations in the historical Chinese record. Its toxicity is explicitly flagged in the tradition; it is a poison-grade drug hedged with dosage warnings.

**Unani (Avicenna & the Arabic physicians):** The daturas and their tropane-bearing Solanaceous kin (with *Hyoscyamus*, henbane) entered the Arabic materia medica as narcotic, stupefying, extremely hot-and-dry poison-drugs used topically and, at grave risk, internally for pain and spasm. The Unani frame treats such plants as *summum* (poisons) demanding extreme caution, employed mainly externally as anodynes.

**Greco-Roman & the henbane analogues (Dioscorides horizon):** While *Datura metel* itself is an Old-World-tropical/Asian plant whose classical-Mediterranean identity is debated, the Greco-Roman tradition knew and feared the closely-analogous deliriant Solanaceae — henbane (*Hyoscyamus*), mandrake (*Mandragora*), and the "strychnos" nightshades — for the identical tropane-alkaloid syndrome of stupor, delirium and dilated pupils, and Dioscorides described their narcotic and dangerous properties in *De Materia Medica*. The Codex records dhattura within this Old-World deliriant-Solanaceae complex rather than asserting invented specific Greek dhattura recipes.

**Mesoamerican parallel (Badianus horizon):** In the Americas the sister daturas (*Datura stramonium*, *D. wrightii*, *D. innoxia*) held profound sacred-visionary and medicinal roles, recorded in the ethnobotanical and early colonial record, and the 16th-century Badianus manuscript (Aztec herbal) documents Solanaceous deliriants in the indigenous pharmacopoeia. This entry notes the New-World datura complex as a genuine parallel tradition without transferring its specifics to *D. metel*.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Guarded Gate:** *Datura metel* is the Codex's supreme figure of the **threshold that must not be crossed lightly**. Its trumpet flower opens like a gate; its spiny fruit warns the hand that reaches for it. Across cultures dhattura is bound to deities of destruction-and-transformation (Shiva), to temple ritual, and to strict prohibition — a plant whose *sacredness is inseparable from its lethality*. The doctrinal teaching the Codex draws from it is **containment**: some powers are real, are traditionally attested, and are precisely for that reason walled off from the untrained. Dhattura marks the boundary of the plant kingdom's benevolence — the point at which medicine and poison become a single substance separated only by a dose no lay hand should attempt. In the training arc it is the *forbidden door*: studied, respected, never opened.

### Active Components

- **Tropane alkaloids** — chiefly **scopolamine (hyoscine)**, **atropine**, and **hyoscyamine**; competitive antagonists at muscarinic acetylcholine receptors. Biosynthesis runs through putrescine → **PMT** → tropinone → **tropinone reductases (TRI/TRII)** → hyoscyamine → **hyoscyamine-6β-hydroxylase (H6H)** → scopolamine.
- **Withanolides** and other Solanaceous steroidal lactones.
- **Terpenoids** governed by terpene-synthase (TPS) gene families.
- Alkaloid content varies widely by plant part, population and season — a core reason casual dosing is lethally unpredictable.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**None assigned for practitioner use.** In Kenetica *Datura metel* is filed as a **Restricted / Doctrinal** substance: it is studied for what it teaches about the poison-medicine boundary, sacred containment, and the anticholinergic tropane syndrome, but the Codex assigns it **no internal-use protocol and no training phase of active use.** Its only historically-legitimate applications (surgical-era anaesthesia, poison-purified *upavisha* preparation, temple ritual) require specialist toxicological control entirely outside lay or self-directed practice. It is included for completeness and warning, not for use.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:

- Velázquez-Márquez et al. (2021), *PeerJ*, characterised the **tropane-alkaloid** and terpene-synthase gene machinery of *Datura*, mapping the biosynthetic enzymes (**PMT, tropinone reductases TRI/TRII, H6H**) responsible for producing **atropine and scopolamine**, and showing gene-copy expansion under positive selection as a chemical anti-herbivore defence — the molecular basis of the plant's toxicity. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.11466)
- Jaggernauth et al. (2022), *Cureus*, a clinical case report of **Datura poisoning**, documents the severe **anticholinergic toxidrome** (delirium, and the classic peripheral anticholinergic signs) resulting from misuse and its emergency management, underscoring the real-world lethality of casual ingestion. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.29829)

**Evidence grade:** High for toxicology and the anticholinergic mechanism (well-established pharmacology; documented poisoning cases). The plant's toxicity is not in doubt — which is exactly why the Codex assigns no use protocol.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** Acrid, warm, **toxic (*du*)**; lung, liver; historically for wheezing/pain and surgical anaesthesia, always dosage-restricted.
- **Ayurveda:** *Upavisha* (sub-poison), hot, requiring *shodhana* purification; external analgesic and respiratory use under poison discipline.
- **Galenic/Unani:** Hot and dry to the extreme (3rd–4th degree); narcotic poison-drug, mainly external anodyne.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurveda (SS).** Dhattura (*dhattura*) is attested in classical Indian materia medica as an *upavisha* (sub-poison) / *sthavara visha* (stationary poison) — a plant admitted to use only after *shodhana*, the purification discipline (classically soaking in *gomutra* and boiling in *godugdha*), which measurably strips its tropane load: the *Journal of Drug Research in Ayurvedic Sciences* (2022) records scopolamine reduced almost to zero and hyoscyamine down 70–90% after *shodhana* of *D. metel* seed. This is a genuine attested handling — but it is a poison-control discipline, not a *rasayana* (rejuvenation) or *vajikarana* (generative) status. Its Shaivite association — dhattura as an offering to Shiva, the danger folded into a devotional container — is likewise an attested historical framing, recorded here as history, not as a cultivation status. So: (a) for the *shodhana* requirement, but no nourish-life status is attested — what the tradition assigns is the discipline of bounding a poison.

**Chinese (TCM).** As *yang-jin-hua* (洋金花, "foreign golden flower") the flower is an attested item of the Chinese materia medica — warm, acrid, explicitly toxic (*du*), historically dose-restricted for wheezing/pain and surgical anaesthesia. But the name marks it as a late, imported addition, not a Shennong-era classic drug, and no *san pin* 三品 grade is recorded for it in the sources retrieved. It therefore carries a therapeutic, dose-restricted identity only; its cultivation status is unattested. (b), framed — and dated as a later import, dropping to (c) for any cultivation claim.

**Unani (CM).** The deliriant Solanaceae entered the Arabic corpus as *summum* (poisons), extreme in heat and dryness, used mainly as external anodynes. Avicennian cultivation is the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture — the husbanding of a finite vital endowment — and a violent hot-dry narcotic poison is the antithesis of that conservation, not an ally to it. No conservational status is attested. (c) absent.

**Greek (DMM).** Dioscorides has no subtle-body system to assign, and *Datura metel* is not securely his plant — the Mediterranean deliriant Solanaceae he described (henbane, mandrake, *strychnos*) are analogues, not this species. What his tradition offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: provenance, sensory assay, the recording of a poison's action while distancing from its use. For dhattura that discipline resolves into caution alone. (c) absent for any cultivation framing.

**Assyrian (AT).** Under the standing blocking warning in force for every Mesopotamian substance identification, deity attribution and tablet reference, no secure cuneiform identification of *Datura metel* can be asserted — and no cultivation status can be built on an unsecured identity. (c) absent.

**South American (SA).** ⚠️ This tag appears to be a mis-tag. *Datura metel* is an Old-World species; the New-World *dieta* and teacher-plant tradition attaches to the American daturas (*D. stramonium*, *D. innoxia*, *D. wrightii*) and to *Brugmansia*, not to *D. metel*. No South American cultivation framing is attested for this species, and the SA tag should be removed or reassigned to the New-World datura entries. (c) absent — flagged mis-tag.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is cultivated with. Across every tradition that genuinely holds it, *Datura metel* is a bounded poison: the Ayurvedic answer is *shodhana* under specialist control, the Chinese and Unani answer is minimal, dose-restricted or external therapeutic use, and the cultivator's answer is abstention. The plant teaches containment — the sacred gate that is not opened. Do not prepare, dose, or ingest by any route.

### Contraindications

- **ABSOLUTE:** Not for self-administration by any route. All parts toxic.
- **Lethal in overdose:** anticholinergic delirium, hyperthermia, tachyarrhythmia, seizures, respiratory failure, death.
- **Especially dangerous** in children, the elderly, pregnancy, cardiac disease, glaucoma, and with any other anticholinergic or CNS-active agent.
- **Recreational use is a leading cause of datura fatality** — the "high" and the fatal dose are not separable by a lay user.
- If ingestion is suspected → **emergency medical care immediately** (anticholinergic toxidrome; physostigmine and supportive care are hospital interventions).
