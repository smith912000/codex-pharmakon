# Tripolium vulgare — Tripolion, "Tripoly" (Dioscorides 4-135); "Sea Aster, Sea Starwort"

## *Tripolium vulgare, the first name on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-135, which also prints Aster tripolium and Aster atticus and glosses them "Tripoly, Sea Aster, Sea Starwort"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk lists the plant under this name (the name indexes in `Sources/*/extract/names.csv` and the list files in `_sources/` were searched). The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The same line prints *Aster tripolium* and *Aster atticus* as well, and sends the reader to another chapter ("see 4-120"). The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-135. TRIPOLION* at line 9217, SUGGESTED line at 9218 ("Tripolium vulgare, Aster tripolium, Aster atticus — Tripoly, Sea Aster, Sea Starwort"), the cross-reference "see 4-120" at line 9219, and the text at line 9220. The page number 683 follows at line 9221. The chapter stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), after *4-134. KATANANKE* (line 9213) and before *4-136. ADIANTON* (line 9224). The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 715, and the translators' index prints "Tripolium 671, 683" and "Tripolium vulgare 671, 683" (line 17938). The page 671 in that entry is the page of 4-120, the chapter to which the cross-reference sends the reader.

The whole chapter reads: "Tripolium grows in places near the sea where the sea overflows and departs, neither is it in the dry, nor in the sea. It has leaves similar to woad yet thicker, and a stalk twenty centimetres long, jagged towards the top. It is said that the flower of this changes its colour three times a day — in the morning it is white, around noon, purple, and in the evening, Phoenician [red]. The root is white, sweet smelling, and hot to the taste. Two teaspoonfuls of a decoction (taken as a drink in wine) expel water and urine through the bowels. It is cut for antidotes against poisons. It is also called psyche, meris, potamogeton, or stachyites, and the Romans call it calamaris."

The chapter is mostly description, and the description is of an unusual kind. It begins with the habitat, which it sets with care: "near the sea where the sea overflows and departs", neither in the dry nor in the sea. It then gives the leaves ("similar to woad yet thicker"), a stalk "twenty centimetres long, jagged towards the top", and the root ("white, sweet smelling, and hot to the taste"). In the middle stands a report that Dioscorides marks as hearsay ("It is said"): the flower changes its colour three times a day, white in the morning, purple around noon, and "Phoenician" (the translators' bracket says red) in the evening. Use is one sentence: "Two teaspoonfuls of a decoction (taken as a drink in wine) expel water and urine through the bowels." The root "is cut for antidotes against poisons", which the chapter states without saying how. The names printed are *psyche*, *meris*, *potamogeton* and *stachyites*, and the Roman *calamaris*.

**Overlap.** A chapter-level search of the entries folder for 4-135, the Greek heading, *Tripolium*, *Tripoly* and *Sea Aster* found no entry on this chapter. I_1021 (*Aster amellus*) is the entry on the chapter that this one points to (*4-120. ASTER ASTIKOS*, heading at line 9106). It prints *Aster tripolium* and *Tripolium vulgare* on its SUGGESTED line, as this chapter does, and says in its line 18 that this chapter has its own spine row (CPU-01649) and is not read as the same plant. The two chapters are different texts: 4-120 describes a woody little stem with a purple or yellowish flower, found among rocks, and 4-135 describes a plant at the tide line. The translators' cross-references link them in both directions, and the Codex leaves the question of whether they are one plant open. I_1040 (*Catananche graeca*, chapter 4-134) is the chapter directly before this one, and its line 14 notes the adjacency. The alternative name *potamogeton* printed here is also the name of 4-101 (I_1168) and is printed in 4-16 (I_1202), which are other chapters. Nothing is merged.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a decoction in wine that makes water and urine pass "through the bowels", and a root cut for antidotes against poisons. These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. A plant cannot be taken to move fluid out of the body, or as an antidote to poison, on the strength of a single chapter and an identification that is only a suggestion. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation with a quantity: "Two teaspoonfuls of a decoction (taken as a drink in wine)" (line 9220). It gives no frequency, no duration and no method for making the decoction. The antidote use ("It is cut for antidotes against poisons") gives no preparation and no dose. No dose is given in the sources retrieved for the antidote use. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a precise habitat (the margin where the sea comes and goes), a comparison for recognition (leaves like woad, but thicker), a stalk measure, a root check by colour, smell and taste, and a report of the flower's three colours in a day that the author marks as hearsay ("It is said"). That last point is a small lesson in keeping what is seen apart from what is told. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. The identification is the translators', and their cross-reference to 4-120 leaves open whether the two chapters describe one plant. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is the habitat check, the root check by colour, smell and taste, and the habit of marking a report as a report. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Chapter 4-135 prints no warning, though its one use is to drive fluid out through the bowels. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, and the translators offer three names and a cross-reference to a second chapter, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. A plant that forces water out through the bowels carries a risk of dehydration, particularly for children, the elderly and anyone who is already ill. The use for poisons is not a treatment the Codex endorses, and poisoning should never wait for a herb. The text gives one quantity and no safety information for pregnancy, children or any medicine taken at the same time. [USER: verify]
