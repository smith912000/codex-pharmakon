# Sargassum — Hai Zao 海藻 (printed "Rai Zao" in the Yang 1998 OCR) / Luo Shou, "Fallen Head"
## *Herba Sargassii, as Yang 1998 prints the identification; a brown seaweed of the genus* Sargassum | *(Volume IV — Fungal/Algal, shelved in Volume I)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Chinese / Shennong Ben Cao Jing (SBJ).** The text on disk is Yang Shou-zhong's 1998 English translation, *The Divine Farmer's Materia Medica Classic* (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`). The monograph is at line 3099, on PDF page 83 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv` line 146, "Herbs: Middle Class, PDF p.83 (line 3099)", translator's identification "Herba Sargassii"). The OCR prints the name "Rai Zao"; the list row `_sources/shennong.csv` line 186 gives it as "Sargassum Seaweed (海藻 Hǎi Zǎo)", which is the reading used here. The whole monograph, in Yang's English: "Rai Zao (Herba Sargassii) is bitter and cold. It mainly treats goiters and tumors and nodes in the neck. It breaks and disperses bound qi, welling abscesses and swellings, and concretions and accumulation hardness qi. [It stops] abdominal rumbling going up and down and precipitates the 12 [kinds of] water swelling.188 Its other name is Luo Shou (Fallen Head). It grows in pools and swamps." Yang's note 188 says: "Water swelling can be classified in a number of different ways. The translator has not been able to identify a specific set of 12." The monograph prints no "nontoxic" or "protracted taking" clause, unlike the monograph just above it (Shui Ping, line 3094ff, "Protracted taking may make the body light"). The page heading above the monograph, line 3093, reads "Herbs: Middle Class". Yang prints no botanical binomial for it in the passage read, and no footnote on the identification. No other text on disk was found to name sargassum under this or another name; the entry is tagged SBJ only.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, brown seaweeds such as the genus *Sargassum* contain alginates (the gelling polysaccharide of the cell wall), fucoidans (sulphated polysaccharides), mannitol, polyphenols (phlorotannins), and iodine, in amounts that vary greatly with species, season and place of collection. Seaweeds also concentrate arsenic (largely as organic forms) and other metals from the water.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The classic uses the weed for neck swellings ("goiters and tumors and nodes in the neck"), hard masses, abscesses, abdominal rumbling and water swelling. The Codex gives no protocol: a modern neck swelling needs diagnosis, not a seaweed, and a weed that can carry a large and unpredictable iodine load is a poor choice for self-treatment (see Contraindications).

### Preparation & Dosing

None is given in the monograph read. The preface gives one general rule for toxic medicinals: "When treating disease with toxic medicinals, one should first use a sorghum grain-sized amount. Once the disease is gone, one should stop using it. If the disease is yet to leave, double the amount. If it is still there, increase the amount 10 times" (preface, lines 487-488 and 503-504, across the p.16 page break). This monograph prints no statement of toxicity either way, so the text does not say whether the rule applies to it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Goitre (enlargement of the thyroid) is classically caused by iodine deficiency, and brown seaweeds are among the richest natural sources of iodine; that is a well-known reason that seaweeds have a long record in the treatment of neck swellings in several traditions, but the link is a modern interpretation and is not stated by the classic. Iodine in excess can itself cause thyroid disease (hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism). Seaweeds accumulate arsenic and heavy metals. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Chinese (SBJ):** Yang prints "bitter and cold" (the English of the monograph, line 3099). Under the preface of the same text the middle class is the grade of medicinals that "may or may not be toxic" (preface, line 387), and this monograph prints no statement either way. No organ-channel assignment is printed. Yang's own note 13 to the preface says that "Nowhere subsequently in the body of this classic are the four qi or natures mentioned. Only flavor is attributed ... under each medicinal's individual discussion", yet the monograph as he prints it says "bitter and cold"; the discrepancy lies between his note and his text, and "cold" is therefore reported as printed and not relied on. ### Cultivation Register

**Chinese / Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: middle grade.* In Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`) the monograph stands under the page heading "Herbs: Middle Class" (line 3093, PDF p.83), the middle grade (中品) of the three-grade scheme. The preface (line 385) defines it: "There are 120 medium class medicinals which are used as ministers. They mainly nurture personality and correspond to humanity. They may or may not be toxic, and [therefore,] one should weigh and ponder before putting them to their appropriate use. If one intends to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion, one should base [one's efforts] on the middle class." Yang's note on "personality" (line 405) says: "It was believed that some medicinals are effective for treating disease and, at the same time, are good for the cultivation of various virtues in human beings." So sargassum is graded as a drug for the middle path: neither the upper class taken long term to nourish life nor the lower class that attacks disease for a short course, and the monograph itself assigns it a therapeutic job only (neck swellings, hard masses, abscesses, water swelling). The monograph carries no *qing shen* ("make the body light") or "protracted taking" clause, so no long-term cultivation use is attested for it. The grade is that of the page heading in this translation; the other versions of the classic were not read. No *jing* action is asserted, and no later *Bencao* statement is used.

**Practitioner's note.** A middle-grade drug in the Chinese scheme, listed for neck swellings and hard masses, and nothing in the classic makes it a food or a long-term supplement. The tradition's own grading says to weigh before use, and the modern concern with iodine and arsenic in seaweed points the same way. A cultivator who wants seaweed eats ordinary edible seaweed as food and takes no practice from this entry. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Thyroid disease:** seaweed iodine can precipitate or worsen hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism, particularly in people with existing thyroid disease or on thyroid medication; any neck swelling needs medical diagnosis.
- **Heavy metals:** brown seaweeds can carry inorganic arsenic, cadmium and lead; unregulated dried weed is not a safe product to take regularly.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** excess iodine affects the fetal and infant thyroid; avoid medicinal quantities.
- **Anticoagulants and surgery:** seaweed polysaccharides (fucoidans) have anticoagulant-like properties in laboratory work; caution with anticoagulant drugs is general advice, not a text claim. [USER: verify]
- **Identity:** the genus has many species with different composition; the classic's "Herba Sargassii" is the translator's identification.
