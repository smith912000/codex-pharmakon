# Lake salt — rgya.tsha (Gya tsha), Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 (Clark 1995)

## *A salt that Clark's translation of the Explanatory Tantra calls "Lake Salt" (Tibetan *rgya.tsha*), said to resemble calcite and to have a hot taste like alunite; the identification as a salt of lake origin is Clark's name for it, and no Latin or chemical name is given in the line, so its identity is of medium confidence at best* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named by the rGyud-bzhi (Four Tantras) only among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder; this entry replaces it. Note that Dioscorides (5-126, *Ales*) also mentions salt "from the lakes" as the best salt (line 10488), but that is Dioscorides on salt in general, a different chapter and a different substance record, and it is not used here.

**Tibetan (FMT).** The locus is Clark 1995, *The Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine*, translation of the Explanatory Tantra (*bshad rgyud*) chapter 20, the materia medica (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 927; name-index row "Gya tsha" in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, class mineral, source "rGyud-bzhi, Explanatory Tantra ch.20"; list row in `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` at line 209). The line was read, together with its neighbours. It reads:

"rgya.tsha: Lake Salt: [has seven names. It resembles calcite, and has a hot taste similar to mtsbur (alunite).] It is a vermifuge, neutralizes [all types of] poisons, purges channel disorders, removes quinsy and diphtheria, removes waste-growths of the flesh, and cures urinary retention."

The entry thus gives: the Tibetan name, *rgya.tsha*; "seven names" (not listed in the line); a look-alike (calcite); a taste (hot, like *mtsbur*, which Clark glosses "alunite"); and six uses: it expels worms ("vermifuge"), "neutralizes [all types of] poisons", "purges channel disorders", "removes quinsy and diphtheria", "removes waste-growths of the flesh" and "cures urinary retention". The words in square brackets are printed in brackets by Clark; this entry does not decide which words belong to the root verse and which to the commentary tradition, and does not attribute the bracketed material to a particular author. **The neighbouring salts.** The next lines give the other salts in the list: *rgyam.tsha*, "Rock Salt" (line 928: clear crystalline, resembling male calcite, slightly sweet; "cures phlegm-wind combinations, indigestion and cold disorders") and *lche.myang.tsha*, "Wood Salt" (line 929, said with rock salt to be "the superior types of salt"). Each has its own record; they are not folded in. The line just before, *ze.tsha* (line 860, "Saltpetre: Mirabilite"), says that substance "tastes like lake salt", which shows that lake salt served as a reference taste.

**Lake salt in the lists of classes.** Outside chapter 20's materia medica, the same translation mentions lake salt as the example of a salty medicine for wind disorders: "Medicines for wind disorders should be sweet [e.g., cane sugar]; sour [e.g., wine, vinegar, pomegranate]; salty [e.g., lake salt]; and with oily [e.g., eaglewood tree], heavy [e.g., black salt] and smooth [e.g., Indian Salamin] powers" (line 180). It is also listed in the class "which cure cold phlegm conditions", with white mineral salt, rock salt, horn salt and ash salt among others (line 1217), and in the class of "oedema medicines", with rock salt, golden sand, crab, Smaller Cardamon and a mallow leaf (line 1223). Those lists give only the grouping, not a separate dose or method.

**Identity.** The line gives no Latin name. Clark's English name "Lake Salt" implies a salt obtained from salt lakes, but the line does not itself say where it comes from or how it is obtained. The calcite look-alike and the "hot" taste are the only identifying marks in the line. [USER: verify a modern identification for *rgya.tsha*.]

### Active Components

The line names no constituent. In general terms, a salt gathered from a lake is mainly sodium chloride, with the other salts of the water it comes from (sulphates, carbonates, magnesium and calcium salts) in proportions that differ from lake to lake; this is a general fact and is not drawn from the Tantra. Clark compares the taste to alunite, which is a potassium aluminium sulphate mineral, and says the salt resembles calcite, but he does not say the lake salt is either.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.** The Explanatory Tantra lists lake salt as a vermifuge, a neutraliser of poisons, a purgative of "channel disorders", a remover of quinsy and diphtheria and of flesh growths, and a cure for urinary retention. It also uses it as the example of a salty medicine for wind disorders. The conditions named (diphtheria, urinary retention, poisoning) are serious and need medical care. The material is not identified with certainty, and a salt of unknown composition should not be taken as a poison antidote. The Codex supplies no modern protocol.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT).** The line prints one taste and no potency: "a hot taste similar to mtsbur (alunite)" (line 927). In the classes of medicine, lake salt is given as an example of the salty taste (line 180: "salty [e.g., lake salt]"), and it appears among medicines for cold phlegm (line 1217) and oedema (line 1223). The line assigns no humoral (*nyes pa*) pacification of its own beyond those class lists. No other quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for lake salt in the lines read. In the Tibetan scheme, illness is rooted in the three poisons (attachment, aversion, delusion), and medicine is the third of the four branches of treatment, supporting practice and not reaching the root; this is the frame in which any medicine sits and it is not a claim about lake salt. The line is an indication list (worms, poison, quinsy, retention). The Codex asserts no category of substances for subtle-body practice in this tradition, and none is asserted here.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to take from it for cultivation. Lake salt appears as a practical salty, hot-tasting medicine in the Tibetan list, with uses against specific complaints, and the record carries no cultivation status. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Poisoning, diphtheria, quinsy, urinary retention:** these are medical emergencies or conditions needing assessment; no salt is an antidote to "all types of poisons".
- **Salt load:** large amounts of salt are dangerous, especially for children, pregnant women and people with heart, kidney or blood-pressure disease.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children:** no statement in the lines retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Identity:** the line gives no Latin or chemical name and the material cannot be assumed to match any modern salt.
- **Source reliability:** this is an English translation with bracketed material of uncertain status; do not rely on it for identification.
