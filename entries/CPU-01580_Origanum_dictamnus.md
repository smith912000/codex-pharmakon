# Origanum dictamnus — Diktamnos allo, another dictamnus from Crete (Dioscorides 3-39)

## *Origanum dictamnus, the first of the names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-39 (which also prints Dictamnus creticus, Dictamnus albus, Dictamnus fraxinella and Amaracus dictamnus, with the English names "White Dittany, Gas Plant, Candle Plant, Fraxinella", and then a second group, Origanum hirtum and Origanum creticum — "Hairy Marjoram")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED block (lines 6865 and 6866) is itself mixed. It begins with *Origanum dictamnus* and then lists *Dictamnus albus* and *Dictamnus fraxinella*, which are plants of another genus, and the English names printed after them ("White Dittany, Gas Plant, Candle Plant, Fraxinella") are the names of that other plant; a second group names *Origanum hirtum* and *Origanum creticum* as "Hairy Marjoram". The Codex does not choose among them. The same string "Origanum dictamnus, Dictamnus creticus, Amaracus dictamnus — Dittany of Crete" also stands on the SUGGESTED lines of the *amaracinum* chapter 1-68 (lines 4072 and 4073) and of 3-155 *PARTHENION (AMARAKON)* (lines 7858 and 7859), and in the names list on the wine chapter 5-57 *OINOS DIKTAMNITES* (line 10074), so the binomial is not unique to this chapter. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-39. DIKTAMNOS ALLO* ("another dictamnus"), heading at line 6864, text at line 6867, in Book Three among the roots of the *akanthoda*. The whole chapter reads: "Another kind of dictamnus is brought from Crete that has leaves similar to sisymbrium [2-155], but with bigger branches, and a flower similar to wild origanum — black and soft. The smell of the leaves is most pleasant, between sisymbrium and sage. It is effective for all things (as that above) but somewhat less biting. It is mixed with plasters and antidotal medicines."

So the chapter is brief. It says where the plant comes from (it "is brought from Crete"), compares the leaves with *sisymbrium* (chapter 2-155, *SISUMBRION*, heading at line 5864) and the flower with "wild origanum", and describes the flower as "black and soft". It gives a smell, "most pleasant, between sisymbrium and sage". It names no use of its own. It refers to "all things (as that above)", and the text does not number the earlier chapter: the two chapters immediately before it are 3-37 *DIKTAMNON* (line 6840) and 3-38 *PSEUDODIKTAMNOS* (line 6857). 3-37 is the chapter that lists the uses, among them expelling dead embryos, healing wounds from iron or poisonous beasts, and easing a painful spleen. The Codex does not state that 3-39 does these things; it states only that the text says it is effective for "all things" of the one above, "somewhat less biting". The last sentence gives its place in medicines: "mixed with plasters and antidotal medicines". The chapter prints no other names and no season of gathering.

**The chapters around it.** 3-37 *DIKTAMNON* reads of the "Cretian herb" that "even the smell drives away poisonous beasts". 3-38 says that the "bastard" form "does the same things as dictamnus, but is not similarly effective". 3-40 *ELELISPHAKON* (line 6868), after it, is the translators' sage (*Salvia*). None of these is read into this row.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter id 3-39 and the names *diktamnos allo* and *another kind of dictamnus* found no entry on this chapter. I_1068 (*Dictamnus albus*, chapter 3-37) names 3-39 in a paragraph headed "Other chapters on the same name (not merged)", and I_1129 (*Marrubium pseudodictamnus*, chapter 3-38) is the neighbouring entry. Two generic oregano entries, I_433 and I_177 (*Origanum vulgare*), and I_464 (*Origanum majorana*), speak of Dioscorides in general terms and cite no chapter. No entry is duplicated.

### Active Components

The composition of the plant Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, plants of the mint family (Lamiaceae) that are strongly aromatic, among them the marjorams and oreganos, owe their smell to volatile terpenoid oils. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a Cretan herb used as a milder equivalent of another herb and mixed into plasters and antidotal medicines. The Codex gives no protocol for it. The identification is the translators', and their line mixes two genera.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one preparation: the herb "is mixed with plasters and antidotal medicines". It gives no quantity and no recipe. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one comparison of quality, that the plant is "somewhat less biting" than "that above". It prints no warming, cooling, drying, moistening or astringent quality for 3-39.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers is the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition, and here it is a lesson in provenance and sensory assay: the plant is known as "brought from Crete", by leaves like *sisymbrium*, a black soft flower like wild origanum, and a smell placed between *sisymbrium* and sage. It is set beside the dictamnus before it as the weaker of two. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond a place in plasters and antidotes. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The useful lesson is the way Dioscorides fixes a plant by origin and smell and rates it against its neighbour, and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Its sister chapter 3-37 describes the herb as able to expel dead embryos, and the Codex gives it no use. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion that includes a plant of another genus, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
