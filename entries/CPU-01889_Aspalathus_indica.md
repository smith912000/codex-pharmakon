# Aspalathus indica — Aspalathos ASPALATHOS, "Indian aspalathus" (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 1-19); also called sphagnon, phasganon, diaxylon (Syrians), erysisceptron

## *The woody, thorny, fragrant shrub-wood that Dioscorides calls aspalathos, for which the English edition prints "Aspalathus indica — Indian Aspalathus"; the identification is an old label only: today the genus Aspalathus is a group of South African shrubs, which cannot be the plant of Istrus, Nisyrus, Syria and Rhodes, so the binomial is of low confidence and the plant behind the Greek name is not established here* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named by Dioscorides among the texts on disk, in chapter 1-19.** This is a single-text entry. The row was added on 13 October as an unverified placeholder from the Dioscorides name list (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 20). A first pass proposed merging it with CPU-00010; the audit disagreed, because the target was ambiguous and was not the same species, and the row is kept apart. The chapter was read in full on 30 October, and the name "aspalathus" was then searched in the whole text.

**Dioscorides, De Materia Medica (DMM).** The chapter is 1-19, ASPALATHOS, in Book One (aromatics), `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading at line 3716, text at lines 3718 and 3727-3729. The editor's line reads "Aspalathus indica — Indian Aspalathus". The text:

"Aspalathus is a woody kind of shrub with many prickly thorns — growing in Istrus, Nisyrus, Syria and Rhodes — which the ointment makers use for thickening their ointments. The best is heavy, and after it has been harvested inclining to a red or a purple colour, thick, fragrant, and bitter to the taste. There is also another kind of it with scattered bristles or thorns — white, woody, without any smell — which is considered the worst. It has a heating and astringent quality. As a result, boiled in wine and gargled, it is good for an ulcerated mouth and gangrenous ulceration in the genitals. It is infused for unclean discharges and fetid nasal discharges; and put into a pessary it is an abortifacient. A decoction stops bowels, and taken as a drink it stops bloody vomiting, and dissolves painful urination and gaseousness. It is also called sphagnon, or phasganon, the Syrians call it diaxylon, and some, erysisceptron."

Four things follow from the text alone. (1) The drug is a wood, from a thorny shrub, and it is the ointment makers' thickener. (2) Dioscorides separates two kinds: the best, heavy, reddish to purple, thick, fragrant and bitter, and a worse one, white, bristly and scentless. (3) The properties are "heating and astringent", and the uses are mouth and genital ulcers, discharges, bleeding from the stomach, urination and gas. (4) The names Sphagnon, Phasganon, Diaxylon and Erysisceptron are given as alternatives. The chapter on the sedge cyperus, 1-4 (line 3575), says that "Some call cyperus, as well as aspalathus [1-19], by the name of erysisceptrum", so the name was shared with another plant in the book.

**Other places in the text.** Aspalathus is an ingredient or thickener in several preparations: in the compound incense *kuphi* (1-24, heading line 3758, text line 3764: "a half kilo each of calamus aromaticus [1-2, 1-17, 1-114], aspalathus [1-19] and juncus odoratus"), in the thickened narcissus oil (1-63, NARCISSINON, line 4038: "six pounds of aspalathum [1-19] (pounded and steeped in water)" mixed with a third of the oil, boiled, and then taken out), in the sweet oil *gleucinum* (1-67, line 4066) and in the aromatic wine *vinum aromatites* (5-64, line 10119: "Pound two pints each of dates, aspalathus [1-19], calamus [1-17, 1-114] and Celtic nard [1-7] into small pieces, mix them with passum [raisin wine]... put them into twelve pints of fermented must... leave it alone for forty days"). Those are perfumers' and winemakers' uses of the wood, and they confirm its role as a fragrant thickener. They are cited here for the plant, not as separate drugs.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** A gargle (boiled in wine) for ulcerated mouth and genital ulcers; an infusion for discharges; a pessary as an abortifacient; a decoction for stopping the bowels and bloody vomiting and for urination and gas. As a craft material: the thickener of ointments, oils and perfumed wine.
- **What the text does not say.** The chapter gives no dose, no length of use, and no quantity for the medicinal preparations.

The Codex does not translate the classic's terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend this plant. The pessary use is an abortifacient use. It is recorded and is not to be followed.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations in the text are: boiled in wine and gargled; infused; a pessary; a decoction drunk (all 1-19, lines 3718 and 3727-3729). The craft recipes give amounts for the wood in perfumes, six pounds in the narcissus oil (1-63) and "two pints" in the wine (5-64). Those are the maker's quantities for a batch and are not doses. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. The genus *Aspalathus* today is a group of shrubs native to the Cape region of South Africa (it includes the plant behind rooibos tea), so the label "indica" in the edition cannot describe a Mediterranean or Syrian plant of Dioscorides' list, and the Greek drug was something else. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** In 1-19 Dioscorides prints "a heating and astringent quality", and for the best kind "bitter" with a "fragrant" smell. No other quality is printed, and none is imported.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and 1-19 offers none. The chapter shows the discipline of attention he does teach, in a plain form: it names the places of growth, it separates a best kind from a worst by weight, colour, thickness, scent and taste, and it tells the buyer how to tell them apart. That is provenance and sensory assay. It is not a cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports any cultivation use. What a cultivator can take from the chapter is the test: heavy, reddish or purple, thick, fragrant, bitter is the good wood, and the pale, bristly, scentless kind is the poor one.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy.** The text lists a pessary as an abortifacient. Do not use any preparation of this wood in pregnancy.
- **Ulcers, bleeding, discharges.** These are medical conditions that need proper care; this entry is no guide to them.
- **Identity.** The plant behind the Greek name is not established, and the modern genus of the label is unrelated to it in place. Do not gather or use any plant on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
