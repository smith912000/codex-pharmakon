### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry.** This plant is named only by Dioscorides (DMM). It was added 2026-10-09 from the extraction list (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) at the locus 4-18, line 8176.

**Identity note.** The binomial is the translators' (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) suggestion and not Dioscorides' identification. The SUGGESTED lines (8177 and 8178) read: "Campanula medium — Canterbury Bells, Mercury's Violet" and "Campanula cichoracea — Headed Bell Flower Medium alpinum, Campanula laciniata — Bell Flower, Harebell". The translators offer more than one bellflower for the one chapter, and the chapter's description (below) names no feature that separates them. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"), immediately before 4-19 EPIMEDION.

*4-18 MEDION* (heading at line 8176; text at line 8179). The whole chapter reads: "Medium grows in shady rocky places. It has leaves similar to seris [2-160], a great stalk of three feet, round purple-coloured flowers, and small seed similar to cnicus [4-119, 4-190]. The root is twenty centimetres long, the thickness of a staff, bitter to the taste. Pounded into small pieces when it is dry and licked in with honey that has been boiled for several days, it stops excessive bloody discharges. A decoction of the seed (taken as a drink with wine) draws out the menstrual flow. It is also called medica, trifolium, clemation, osmos, trigonos, cybellium, or polyphyllon; the Romans call it trifolium odoratum, and the Egyptians, epaphou."

The chapter gives, in order: a habitat (shady, rocky places); leaf (like *seris*, 2-160), stalk (three feet), flowers (round, purple) and seed (small, like *cnicus*); the root (twenty centimetres long, staff-thick, bitter); two uses (the dried root with long-boiled honey for "excessive bloody discharges", and a decoction of the seed in wine to draw out the menstrual flow); and seven further names, then a Roman and an Egyptian name. The chapter does not say which discharges are meant, and does not say how much to use.

The cross-references (*seris* 2-160, *cnicus* 4-119 and 4-190) were not opened beyond their citation; they are printed in the chapter as likenesses.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records the dried root in honey as a remedy for "excessive bloody discharges" and the seed decoction in wine as an emmenagogue (it "draws out the menstrual flow"). The second is a use that can end a pregnancy, and the Codex gives no protocol for either. The identity with a garden bellflower rests on a translators' list.

### Preparation & Dosing

Two preparations are printed (4-18). The first is the dry root "pounded into small pieces" and "licked in with honey that has been boiled for several days". The second is "a decoction of the seed (taken as a drink with wine)". No quantity, duration or number of doses is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. The chapter offers the discipline of attention: where the plant grows, how to recognise it (leaf, stalk, flower, seed, the size, shape and bitter taste of the root), and a long list of its names. It records two remedies and no status as a long-term or life-nourishing substance. The match to *Campanula medium* is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice. It supplies a plant description, a honey remedy for bleeding and a drink to bring on the menses. The abstentive answer is the correct one, particularly as the seed decoction is recorded as bringing on the menses.

### Contraindications

No contraindication is printed in the chapter. As a general caution, a preparation said to "draw out the menstrual flow" must not be used in pregnancy, and "excessive bloody discharges" are a symptom that calls for medical assessment and not a home remedy. The identification is uncertain, so no plant should be taken on the basis of this chapter.
