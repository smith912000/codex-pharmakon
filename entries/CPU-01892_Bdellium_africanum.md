# Bdellium africanum — Bdellion (Dioscorides, De materia medica 1-80); alias madelcum, bolchum

## *A gum-resin, the "oozing" of a tree that Dioscorides places in a "Sarandenian" country; the 2000 English edition suggests Bdellium africanum (Balsamodendrum africanum, Heudelotia africana) and "Balsamodendron kua". Our identification: Commiphora africana (A.Rich.) Engl. or a near relative, the African bdellium; basis is the translator's suggestion and the standard modern link between bdellium and Commiphora; confidence medium, because the chapter itself gives only the origin and the tests of quality and a second kind from India* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

> **CAUTION.** Dioscorides records this gum-resin as an abortifacient and as a drug taken by mouth. It is a historical record, not a recommendation.

**Named by Dioscorides only among the texts on disk, as chapter 1-80, BDELLION.** This is a single-text entry. **Dioscorides (DMM).** Chapter 1-80 stands in Book One, in the run of tree gums (the running head reads GUMS from TREES), between styrax (1-79) and frankincense (1-81): `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 4154-4161. The English heading is "1-80. BDELLION", and the translator's line reads "SUGGESTED: Bdellium africanum, Balsamodendrum africanum, Heudelotia africanum — Bdellium Tree, Balsamodendron kua". The text says: "Bdellium (which some call madelcum or bolchum) is the oozing of a Sarandenian tree." The best kind is "bitter in taste, transparent, fat like bull's glue, fat in the inside of it and easily growing soft, without wood or other filth, with a very sweet smell when burnt, like juncus odoratus". A second sort is "filthy and black, in bigger pieces, rolled up into lumps", "brought out of India", and a third is brought "from a town called Petra", "dry, resinous, black and blue underneath, and next in strength to the first". It is counterfeited "by mixing gum with it", which is "not as bitter to the taste" and does not smell as sweet when burnt and smoked.

The uses printed are these. It "is warming and softening, and diluted with the spittle of one fasting it dissolves hardness and swellings of the throat and watery ruptures"; applied, with the smoke inhaled, "it opens the entrance to the vulva"; "it is an abortifacient and draws out moisture"; "taken as a drink it breaks up stones [kidney, urinary] and it expels urine"; it is "effective given for coughs and poisonous creatures' bites" and "good for hernia, convulsions and pain of the side, and for the accumulated wind in those who have run"; it is "put into warm compresses which are good for hardness and the knots in the nerves"; and "bruised, it is worked together with wine or hot water is poured on it". The bracketed words in these quotations are the translator's. The myrrh chapter (1-77, line 4135) also uses bdellium as a comparison: one kind of myrrh is "soft like bdellium", which shows the gum was a familiar reference material.

**What is not in the sources.** The Dioscorides chapter names no constituent, no dose and no duration.

### Active Components

The chapter names no constituent. In general terms, the gum-resins of *Commiphora* trees, which include myrrh and the bdellium group, are mixtures of water-soluble gum, an alcohol-soluble resin and a volatile oil, and they are aromatic and bitter. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the source says it is for.** Dioscorides gives hardness and swelling of the throat, urinary stones and the passing of urine, coughs, bites of poisonous creatures, hernia, convulsions, side pain, wind after running, hardness and knots in the sinews (as compresses), and, as a pessary or fume, opening the neck of the womb. He also records it as an abortifacient.
- **What the source does not say.** No modern indication, no dose and no course of treatment. Whether the drug worked as he says is not tested here.

The Codex gives no protocol for this material and does not recommend it.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter's only preparation notes are these: the gum is "bruised" and "worked together with wine", or hot water is poured on it; it is "diluted with the spittle of one fasting" for the throat; it is burnt and smoked (a quality test and a fume for the womb). ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. *Commiphora* gum-resins are aromatic, bitter and mildly antimicrobial in kind, as is true of myrrh, and they are used today in incense and perfumery. Dioscorides' claims for stones, hernia and convulsions have no modern equivalent and are not translated into one. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** "Warming and softening" (line 4161, the first sentence of uses). The best gum is bitter in taste (line 4156). No other quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter does offer a cultivator is a discipline of attention: it gives sensory tests for the best bdellium (bitter taste, transparency, softness, a sweet smell when burnt), names two inferior sorts by origin and appearance, and says how it is faked (gum mixed in, less bitter, less sweet when burnt). No cultivation status is attested.

**Practitioner's note.** A drug of the physician's chest in Dioscorides, to be known by taste, smell and look, and to be tested against the gum that is passed off for it. Nothing here supports taking it as a cultivation aid; his own listing of it as an abortifacient is the warning.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy.** Dioscorides records it as an abortifacient (line 4161). Avoid in every sense. [USER: verify]
- **Hygiene and adulteration.** The text itself says the gum is faked with other gums.
- **Identity.** Several resins have been sold as bdellium; the species named here is a suggestion, not a certainty. [USER: verify]
