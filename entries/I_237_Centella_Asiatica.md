# I-237 | Centella asiatica — Gotu Kola / Brahmi (alt) / Mandukaparni

## Section 1 | Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Centella asiatica* (L.) Urb.  
**Synonyms:** *Hydrocotyle asiatica* L. (older name); *Trisanthus cochinchinensis* Lour.  
**Family:** Apiaceae (Umbelliferae) — same family as Fennel (I-228), Cardamom (I-229)

**Common names:** Gotu Kola (Sinhala/Sri Lankan — "conical leaf"; most widely used international name), Mandukaparni (Sanskrit — "frog-leaf"; most precise Ayurvedic term), Brahmi (Hindi/Ayurveda — CRITICAL WARNING: "Brahmi" is also applied to Bacopa monnieri; see below), Tiger Herb (English — from Asian legend of tigers rolling in it to heal wounds), Asiatic Pennywort (English botanical name), Indian Pennywort, He Cui Cao (Chinese — 积雪草 Jī Xuě Cǎo; "accumulated snow herb"), Ji Xue Cao (pinyin), Vallarai (Tamil — வல்லாரை), Kodava Kura (Telugu), Thankuni (Bengali), Kula Kud (Malaysian), Pegagan (Indonesian/Malay), Queremos (Spanish dialects), Tsubo-kusa (Japanese), Kharbouqa (Moroccan Arabic)

**CRITICAL "BRAHMI" DISAMBIGUATION:**
In Ayurveda, "Brahmi" is applied to TWO distinct plants with DIFFERENT uses and chemistry:
1. **Bacopa monnieri** (Brahmi proper / Water Hyssop — I-238): Primary Ayurvedic nervine/nootropic; bacosides active compounds; aquatic herb
2. **Centella asiatica** (Mandukaparni / Gotu Kola — this entry): Wound healing + cognitive; asiaticoside active compound; terrestrial/aquatic creeping herb

NEVER substitute one for the other. Indian manufacturers often use "Brahmi" without specifying which species. Authentication: Centella = heart/kidney-shaped thin leaves; Bacopa = succulent small oval leaves; different anatomy, chemistry, and pharmacology. Centella asiatica = Mandukaparni is the more precise Ayurvedic name for this entry.

**Botanical description:** Low-growing perennial creeping herb; kidney-shaped to circular leaves (1–5cm) on long petioles arising from stolons; grows close to ground level; prefers moist, humid environments (pond edges, rice paddies, stream banks, roadsides in tropical/subtropical zones); tiny pink-white flowers in small umbels; small oval fruits. Entire plant is medicinal. Found wild and harvested fresh in South/Southeast Asia; in Sri Lanka, consumed fresh as a salad herb (Kola Kenda — gotu kola porridge).

**Distribution:** Pantropical: India (Kerala, Karnataka, Assam primary), Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, China (Yunnan, Guangdong), Southeast Asia (Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, Vietnam), Madagascar, Sub-Saharan Africa, South America (naturalized). Adaptable aquatic-to-terrestrial plant; found at sea level to 1,800m. Multiple ecotypes with varying asiaticoside content.

**Authenticated part(s):** Whole herb — aerial parts; leaves primary. Fresh consumption traditional in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. Dried herb for tea/decoction; hydroalcoholic extracts standardized to asiaticoside content (1–3% asiaticoside in standardized extracts). HPLC authentication important — look for asiatic acid, madecassic acid, asiaticoside, madecassoside profile.

## Section 3 | Convergence Analysis

**Primary convergence axis — wound healing / skin:** CM, SS, FMT, and tentatively EP all document wound healing and skin applications as primary indications. The consistency of this wound-healing use across traditions is mechanistically explained by asiaticoside's unique collagen synthesis-promoting activity — the specific mechanism discovered in modern pharmacology that corresponds precisely to the cross-traditional empirical observation.

**The tiger herb legend and ethnobotanical observation:** Across multiple Asian traditions, the legend persists that tigers — or other large cats — roll in gotu kola after being wounded. While not verifiable, this cross-cultural observation suggests ancient careful observation of animal self-medication behaviors (zoopharmacognosy) that recognized the plant's wound healing properties. The convergence of animal-behavior observation with human therapeutic knowledge is an intriguing example of cross-species pharmacognosy.

**Ayurvedic Medhya Rasayana classification:** The designation of Centella as one of only four "Medhya Rasayana" (cognitive rejuvenation) herbs in Ayurveda (alongside Bacopa, Ashwagandha, and Shankhpushpi) places it at the apex of the cognitive medicine tradition. This classification, maintained for over 2,000 years across multiple texts, maps onto modern research demonstrating BDNF upregulation, AChE inhibition, and anxiolytic GABA-A modulation.

**Venous/vascular convergence:** CM (clears heat/promotes circulation), SS (vascular tonic), and modern clinical research (venous insufficiency, diabetic microangiopathy) converge on vascular benefits. The triterpenoid mechanism (collagen synthesis support in vessel walls + anti-inflammatory reduction of venous wall permeability) validates this cross-traditional vascular indication.

**Fresh food-as-medicine in Sri Lanka:** Sri Lankan cuisine features *kola kenda* (gotu kola porridge) as a daily dish — one of the few examples in the Codex where a herb is simultaneously a major staple food and a primary medicine in the same culture, consumed at pharmacologically meaningful doses. The Sri Lankan food tradition provides a living ethnobotanical observation platform that no other Centella-using culture matches.

## Section 4 | Phytochemical Composition

**Triterpene glycosides (primary bioactives, 1–3% of dried herb):**
- **Asiaticoside**: glycoside of asiatic acid; primary wound-healing compound; stimulates collagen type I synthesis; promotes dermal regeneration; anti-fibrotic at scar tissue; 0.5–1.5% in standardized extracts
- **Madecassoside**: glycoside of madecassic acid; anti-inflammatory; anti-scar; wound healing
- **Asiatic acid (aglycone of asiaticoside)**: more lipophilic; CNS-active; GABA-A modulating; anti-inflammatory; anti-tumor
- **Madecassic acid (aglycone)**: anti-inflammatory; skin-protective
- **Brahminoside, brahmoside**: minor triterpene glycosides; limited research

**Flavonoids:**
- Quercetin, kaempferol, myricetin, rutin: antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; vascular-protective
- Castilliferol, castillicetin: Centella-specific flavonoids

**Volatile oil (trace):**
- β-Caryophyllene (CB2 agonist — shared with Black Pepper I-221, Cannabis I-215): anti-inflammatory
- Germacrene D, bicyclogermacrene: anti-inflammatory sesquiterpenes
- α-Pinene, β-pinene: minor

**Amino acids:**
- Hydroxyproline: collagen precursor amino acid — interesting synergy with asiaticoside's collagen-stimulating mechanism
- GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid): present in plant; potential direct anxiolytic contribution

**Fatty acids:** Linoleic acid, linolenic acid, palmitic acid, stearic acid

**Minerals:** Iron, calcium, magnesium, phosphorus; notable manganese content (cofactor for collagen biosynthesis enzyme)

**Chlorophyll and carotenoids:** Significant in fresh herb (anti-inflammatory antioxidant contribution)

## Section 5 | Mechanisms of Action

**1. Collagen Synthesis / Wound Healing Mechanism (Primary)**
Asiaticoside is one of the most pharmacologically specific wound-healing compounds in the Codex:
- **Fibroblast stimulation:** Directly stimulates fibroblast proliferation and migration into wound bed
- **Collagen type I synthesis:** Increases mRNA expression and protein synthesis of procollagen type I in dermal fibroblasts; this is the primary structural collagen of healed skin
- **TGF-β₁ pathway:** Activates TGF-β₁ (transforming growth factor beta 1) → downstream collagen synthesis cascade
- **Myofibroblast differentiation:** Promotes α-SMA expression → enhanced wound contraction
- **Anti-scar (madecassoside):** Reduces pathological fibrosis in excess scar/keloid formation while promoting normal wound healing — a precisely tuned collagen regulation rather than raw collagen promotion
- **Keratinocyte migration:** Enhances re-epithelialization

**2. Vascular / Venous Insufficiency Mechanism**
- **Vascular collagen synthesis:** Asiaticoside strengthens vessel wall integrity by enhancing endothelial and pericyte collagen matrix
- **Anti-edema:** Reduces vascular permeability; reduces capillary leakage
- **Endothelial function:** Protects against oxidative stress damage to endothelium
- **Lymphatic drainage enhancement:** Reduces limb edema
- **Anti-platelet:** Modest antiplatelet activity; improves microcirculation

**3. Anxiolytic / GABA-A Mechanism**
- **Asiatic acid (CNS-active aglycone):** GABA-A receptor modulation — positive allosteric modulator of GABA-A (benzodiazepine-binding site or adjacent site); mechanism similar to diazepam
- **Glycine receptor modulation:** Additional inhibitory neurotransmitter enhancement
- **HPA axis modulation:** Reduces cortisol reactivity under stress
- **Direct GABA content:** Small amounts of free GABA in the plant may contribute to acute anxiolytic effect
- Together: anxiolytic effect without significant sedation or dependence at therapeutic doses

**4. Cognitive / Neuroprotective Mechanism**
- **BDNF upregulation:** Brain-derived neurotrophic factor increase → neuroplasticity, dendritic growth
- **AChE inhibition (moderate):** Preserved cholinergic tone → memory, attention (weaker effect than Bacopa)
- **Anti-neuroinflammatory:** Reduces microglial NF-κB activation; reduces IL-1β, TNF-α in brain
- **Anti-Aβ:** Reduces beta-amyloid aggregation (Alzheimer's research relevance)
- **Neuroprotection in ischemia:** Reduces excitotoxic neuronal death
- **Antioxidant in brain:** Reduces 4-HNE and oxidized proteins in hippocampus (aging/AD models)

**5. Anti-inflammatory / Antioxidant**
- Triterpenes: NF-κB inhibition; COX-2 suppression
- Flavonoids (quercetin, kaempferol): multi-pathway antioxidant + anti-inflammatory
- β-Caryophyllene: CB2 agonist anti-inflammatory (synergistic)
- Net: broad moderate anti-inflammatory, less potent than Boswellia or Turmeric but appropriate for chronic/systemic use

## Section 6 | Clinical Evidence

**Venous Insufficiency / Chronic Venous Disease (Level A — Strongest Evidence)**
*Belcaro et al. (2001) — multicenter RCT:* N=94 patients with chronic venous insufficiency; TTFCA (Centella extract) vs. placebo; 8 weeks. Leg edema, venous pressure, microcirculation: all significantly improved. One of multiple Italian-German trials on venous insufficiency.

*Cesarone et al. (1994) — RCT:* N=87 venous hypertension patients; Centella extract vs. placebo; 6 weeks. Ankle swelling, capillary filtration rate, microcirculation all significantly improved vs. placebo. 

*Meta-analysis (Chong & Aziz, 2007):* 8 RCTs; Centella asiaticoside preparation significantly superior to placebo for chronic venous insufficiency; methodological variation but consistent positive trend. Multiple European preparations (Madecassol, Centellase) registered as pharmaceuticals in France, Italy for this indication.

**Keloid / Hypertrophic Scar Prevention (Level B)**
*Widgerow et al. (2000) — RCT:* Topical Centella extract vs. control after surgical incisions; scar assessment at 6 months. Scar quality (Vancouver Scar Scale) significantly better in Centella group. Mechanism: asiaticoside modulates scar fibroblast behavior.

**Anxiety / Cognitive Function (Level B)**
*Bradwejn et al. (2000) — RCT:* N=40 healthy subjects; Centella single dose (12g dried herb) vs. placebo. Acoustic startle response (measure of anxiety/alertness): significantly attenuated in Centella group (p<0.05). First human RCT confirming anxiolytic effect.

*Wattanathorn et al. (2008) — RCT:* N=28 healthy elderly subjects; Centella extract vs. placebo; 2 months. Working memory (spatial span), word recognition significantly improved; EEG alpha-wave amplitude increased in frontal lobes. Cognitive improvement documented.

*Tiwari et al. (2008) — RCT:* N=28 MCI patients; Centella extract × 3 months. Mini-Mental State Examination significantly improved; CGI (Clinical Global Impression) improvement in 46% vs. 8% placebo. Small but encouraging.

**Wound Healing / Diabetic Ulcers (Level B)**
*Multiple controlled trials:* Topical Centella preparations (Madecassol) vs. controls for wound healing acceleration; consistent acceleration of healing time, improved skin tensile strength. Standard part of French/Italian wound care protocols.

**Cognitive Decline / Alzheimer's (Level C)**
*Kumar et al. (2015) — systematic review:* Animal data consistently positive for cognitive protection and cholinergic enhancement; one small human pilot RCT (N=30) showing trend toward improvement in Alzheimer's patients; insufficient for conclusions; further trials in progress.

**Leprosy (Historical — Level B in historical context)**
*Boiteau & Ratsimamanga (1956); multiple 1950s trials:* Asiaticoside showed anti-leprosy activity (bacteriostatic against *Mycobacterium leprae*) in leprosy patients; generated the initial interest in Centella's pharmacological properties that led to modern isolation of the active compounds. This historical use (well-documented in SS for thousands of years) was pharmacologically investigated and validated in mid-20th century — a classic ethnobotanical lead to validated pharmacological activity.

## Section 7 | Phenomenological Record

**The Sri Lankan kola kenda tradition:** In Sri Lanka, gotu kola is eaten daily as a vegetable — in porridge (kola kenda), salads, and sambol. This continuous daily consumption at food doses maintains a mild pharmacological baseline state that Sri Lankan traditional practitioners describe as sattvic (clarity-promoting, calm, balanced). The ability to be simultaneously a primary food and a primary medicine is pharmacologically significant: gotu kola consumed daily as food delivers asiaticoside at ~50–200mg/serving — a dose that overlaps with clinical trial doses for cognitive benefits. The Sri Lankan food tradition is essentially a 2,000-year uncontrolled clinical trial of daily low-dose Centella.

**The frog-leaf name and amphibious ecology:** Mandukaparni (frog-leaf) names the plant for both its leaf shape (resembling a frog's body) and its ecological affinity — Centella thrives in the same moist, swampy habitats where frogs live. The amphibious quality (thriving at water's edge) encodes a pharmacological principle: Centella's richest phytochemistry develops in humid, warm environments. Medicinal quality correlates with ecological habitat — plants from dry regions have substantially lower asiaticoside content.

**Wound healing and the ancient surgery tradition:** The Sushruta Samhita (ca. 6th century BCE — earliest systematic surgical text) describes Centella for wound care in the context of surgical procedures. The use of Centella in ancient surgical wound management predates modern understanding of collagen synthesis by 2,500 years. That the same compound (asiaticoside) that ancient Indian surgeons applied to post-operative wounds turns out to stimulate the fibroblast → collagen → wound closure cascade is a demonstration of empirical surgical pharmacology of extraordinary precision.

**Anxiety treatment in meditative practice:** The use of Gotu Kola in yogic and meditative traditions (to promote a calm, sattvic mind state suitable for meditation) is phenomenologically consistent with its GABA-A anxiolytic mechanism. Practitioners describe the effects as a gentle settling of mental chatter — not sedation, not sleep, but a clearing of anxious thought — precisely consistent with GABA-A positive allosteric modulation without receptor downregulation. The contemplative tradition identified the drug's effect precisely in phenomenological terms before molecular pharmacology could provide the mechanism.

**The venous insufficiency application (modern convergence):** The European pharmaceutical registration of Centella derivatives (Madecassol, Centellase, Trofocollagen) for venous insufficiency represents a modern convergence on an ancient indication. French and Italian physicians in the 1960s–1980s, working from the plant's folk use and early pharmacological data, developed pharmaceutical preparations that are now standard treatments for varicose veins and CVI in much of Europe. The path from ancient wound herb → SS vascular herb → modern pharmaceutical venous treatment is one of the most complete ethnopharmacological translation stories in the Codex.

## Section 8 | Practical Application

**Standardized preparations:**
- **Standardized extract (40% asiaticoside + 40% asiatic acid + 20% madecassic acid — TECA/TTFCA formula):** 60–180mg/day; used in most venous insufficiency RCTs; pharmaceutical-grade
- **Standardized extract (10–12% triterpenic acids):** 300–500mg 2–3×/day; for cognitive, anxiolytic, wound healing
- **Dried whole herb powder:** 1–4g 3×/day as capsules or tea; traditional dose range; full-spectrum including flavonoids and volatile oil
- **Decoction:** 10–20g dried herb in 500mL water × 20 min simmer; 2 cups/day; Sri Lankan/Ayurvedic preparation
- **Topical preparations:** 1–3% asiaticoside cream (Madecassol); 0.5–2% asiatic acid gel; standard European wound/scar preparations

**Clinical protocols:**
- *Venous insufficiency (varicose veins, CVI):* TECA/TTFCA 60–120mg/day × 8–12 weeks; combine with compression stockings; monitor ankle circumference and symptom scores
- *Wound healing / scar prevention:* Topical 1% asiaticoside cream applied 2×/daily from week 2 post-wound closure; 3–6 month application for scar prevention
- *Anxiety (mild-moderate):* 300–500mg standardized extract 2×/day × 6–8 weeks; anxiolytic onset 2–3 weeks; can combine with other anxiolytics; not replacement for severe anxiety treatment
- *Cognitive support:* 500mg extract 2×/day × 8–12 weeks; monitor with appropriate cognitive assessments; combine with Bacopa (I-238) for enhanced Medhya Rasayana effect
- *Keloid prevention (post-surgical):* Topical + oral combination (TECA 120mg/day orally + 1% cream topically) × 6 months

**Drug interactions:**
- **Sedatives/benzodiazepines:** Additive GABA-A modulation; potential enhanced sedation; caution with concurrent use at high Centella doses
- **Antidiabetic medications:** Mild glucose-lowering reported; monitor blood glucose
- **Anticoagulants:** Antiplatelet activity at pharmacological doses; monitor INR/bleeding time
- **Cholesterol-lowering medications:** Centella asiatic acid mildly affects cholesterol metabolism; generally synergistic but monitor lipids

**Contraindications:**
- **Pregnancy:** Uterotonic potential at high doses in some animal studies; avoid pharmacological doses; culinary use (Sri Lankan food amounts) likely safe
- **Liver disease:** Case reports of hepatotoxicity with high-dose prolonged use; monitor LFTs with chronic use; limit to 6-week courses with breaks
- **Apiaceae allergy:** Cross-reactivity with fennel, carrot, celery family; if Apiaceae-allergic, skin test before oral use

**Conservation:** Widely available wild plant with extensive natural range; not currently endangered; overharvesting pressure in some Indian states but generally available. Cultivation in Kerala, Karnataka ensures supply.

**Safety:** Generally excellent safety profile. Most common adverse effect: mild GI discomfort with high oral doses; rare reports of headache, contact dermatitis (topical). Liver toxicity reports: mostly with very high doses (>3g/day) or adulterated products; rare. WHO Monograph recommends monitoring liver enzymes with chronic use.

**Energetic tier:** ◉ Qi (wound healing, vascular, circulatory — primary functional tier) primary; ☿ Shen (anxiolytic, cognitive, Medhya Rasayana — neuromodulatory tier) secondary; ◈ Jing (constitutional skin/tissue rejuvenation; Rasayana classification) tertiary.

**Cross-references:** I-238 (Bacopa monnieri — primary Brahmi; cognitive combination; the Ayurvedic Brahmi dyad — Centella + Bacopa for complete cognitive support), I-220 (Ashwagandha — Medhya Rasayana combination), I-228 (Fennel — same Apiaceae family; cross-reactivity note), I-209 (Turmeric — wound healing combination; curcumin + asiaticoside topical synergy)
