# Celosia cristata — bya.po.tsi.tsi, Jhapo tsitsi (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Celosia cristata L., the first of four Latin names Clark 1995 prints for bya.po.tsi.tsi (with Goniolimon speciosum, Limonium (statice) flexuosum and Ceratostigma minus, as printed); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary sets "Pisum sativum" against the name by column position, and prints no Celosia* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts, and under this binomial only by Clark.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. Both Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the line prints four, of which the row takes the first (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, row 161, which lists three of the four). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1059).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads:

"bya.po.tsi.tsi: Celosia cristata L.: Goniolimon speciosum L. Boiss: Limonium (statice) flexuosum L. (Rtze.): Ceratostigma minus: [has two names and resembles ma.ma.sgo.lchags and lug.ru.smug.po. It has a blue or yellowish-blue flower, red fruit and is also known as bdud.rtse.aahu.rtsi or skyu.rug.ma.] It controls excess menstrual flow, [is hemostatic, supremely beneficial for skull fractures and injuries, and unites bone fractures]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the first pair of brackets (the commentary layer) are the number of names, the resemblance to two other drugs, the description (blue or yellowish-blue flower, red fruit) and the two other names. Inside the second pair are "is hemostatic, supremely beneficial for skull fractures and injuries, and unites bone fractures". Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the four Latin names, which are Clark's own identifications and not the root text's, and the single clause "It controls excess menstrual flow". The line prints no taste and no power. The Codex has not checked the commentary's description (a blue or yellowish-blue flower) against any of the four Latin names, and a description of that kind may not suit a *Celosia*. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3446).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, chapter 20, in the section "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (it opens at line 3428). The sentence reads: "Jhapo tsitsi stops menstrual bleeding." It stands in a run of one-line drug entries ("Dhambu kara cures hot disorder of the lungs, liver, and channels. Drita sazin drain pus, blood and chhuser. Jhapo tsitsi stops menstrual bleeding. Shumosa dries up lung abscesses and stops diarrhea."), and the Codex's reading of "Jhapo tsitsi" as *bya.po.tsi.tsi* rests on the spelling and on the agreement in action (menstrual flow) with the root-text clause in Clark. The text gives no Latin name in the passage. That the order of the run agrees with Clark's (*hdam.bu.ka.ra* at line 1057, *hbri.ta.sa.hdzin* at line 1058, *bya.po.tsi.tsi* at line 1059, *shu.mo.za* at line 1060) is consistent with the reading. *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (lines 5340 to 5355).* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints, under J, seven phonetic names ("Jagoepoe", "Jakang", "Jeruk", "Ji tsher", "Jhapo Tsi tsi", "Jhew laphuk", "Jitanga"; line 5340), seven transliterations including "bya pho rtsi rtsi" (line 5342, the fifth; spelled with "pho", where Clark has *po*), and a scientific run of "Delphinium brunonianum", "Delphinium himalayi", "Mentha longifolia" (line 5344), "Xanthium strumarium", "Pisum sativum" (line 5346), "Torularia humilis" and "Embelia ribes" (seven). The English names are "Larkspur", "Larkspur", "Horsemint", "Cocklebur", "Garden pea" (line 5353), "Alpencress" and "Marsh nut". The fifth phonetic name, the fifth transliteration, the fifth binomial and the fifth English name are "Jhapo Tsi tsi", "bya pho rtsi rtsi", "Pisum sativum" and "Garden pea". The glossary does not print them on one row, and the pairing is by column position only. The neighbouring positions agree with Clark ("Ji tsher", *byi tsher*, against *Xanthium strumarium* and "Cocklebur"), as the existing entry I_974 notes. The glossary therefore sets *Pisum sativum*, and not *Celosia cristata*, against this drug, and the two Tibetan texts differ on the plant. The Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Celosia cristata* (cockscomb) is a plant of the amaranth family (Amaranthaceae), grown for its crested flower heads, and that *Limonium*, *Goniolimon* and *Ceratostigma* belong to the leadwort family (Plumbaginaceae). Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained, which may be any of the four or the glossary's pea. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "controls excess menstrual flow" (Clark, line 1059) or "stops menstrual bleeding" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3446), and, in Clark's commentary layer, one that is hemostatic and unites bone fractures. These are the texts' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes this drug by what it does, which is to control menstrual flow, and in the commentary layer to stop bleeding and unite fractures. The Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same of menstrual bleeding. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this drug places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: a search of both Tibetan texts for *bya.po.tsi.tsi*, "Jhapo" and *Celosia* returned only the lines above and the index and glossary entries. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a drug for menstrual flow and bleeding in the Tibetan texts, and the two texts print different plants for the name. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. The identity is uncertain, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Heavy menstrual bleeding, other bleeding and fractures need medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read, and a drug said to stop or control menstrual flow is not for use in pregnancy without medical advice. [USER: verify]
