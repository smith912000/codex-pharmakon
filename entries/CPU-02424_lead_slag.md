# Lead slag — Skoria Molubdou (SKORIA MOLUBDOU), Dioscorides 5-97

## *The slag (scoria) left from lead, which the translator glosses "Lead Slag" and marks TOXIC; the identification is the translator's and ours together and is of medium confidence, because the chapter describes the slag by appearance (yellowish, glassy, thick, hard to break) but names no mineral* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** The translator marks this chapter TOXIC. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should take or apply lead slag.

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder; this entry replaces it. A search of `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` for "lead slag" and "scoria" found this chapter, the reference to it in the antimony chapter (5-99), and the sister chapters on silver slag (5-101) and lead stone (5-98); nothing in another tradition on disk.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-97, *Skoria Molubdou* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 10313; name-index row in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` at row 870, translator gloss "Lead Slag TOXIC", class guess mineral; list row in `_sources/dioscorides.csv` at line 823, "Skoria Molubdou", mineral; the book index lists "Lead Slag 792", and "Lead Slag 790" in a second index, text line 18240). The whole chapter was read. It reads (line 10316):

"The best lead slag [scoria] looks like cerussa [white lead ore], thick and hard to break with no trace of lead in it, yellowish in colour and glistening like glass. It is good for the same things as burnt lead but is more astringent. It is washed in a mortar, with water poured on and that which remains on top is a yellowish colour when poured out. This is done often until the scoria [slag] is used up. Then after allowing it to settle we pour out the water and make it into tablets."

The chapter thus gives: a rule for the best (it resembles cerussa, is thick and hard to break, has "no trace of lead" in it, is yellowish and glassy); a comparison (the same uses as burnt lead, with more astringency); and a washing method ending in tablets. It gives no place and no season.

**Burnt lead, the comparison.** The chapter before, 5-96 (*Molubdos kekaumenos*, "Burnt Lead", marked TOXIC, line 10305), is the one the slag is compared to. It gives a burning method (lead plates layered with sulphur and fired, stirred with an iron rod until reduced to ash; "having closed your nostrils, for the vapour is harmful") and says burnt lead is washed like *cadmia* [5-84] and "has the same uses that the washed lead has but is far stronger" (lines 10305-10312). Washed lead is in the chapter before that (5-95, *Molubdos peplumenos*, line 10296), whose uses include cooling, closing the pores, filling sores and wounds, and checking fluids in the eyes. These are cited here only as the sources of the comparison; the slag chapter does not repeat them, and its own uses are only "the same things as burnt lead". **References elsewhere.** The antimony chapter (5-99) says some wash stibium "like lead scoria [slag]" (line 10326), which shows that the washing in 5-97 was the standard method for slags. Silver slag (5-101, *Skoria arguroy*) is a separate chapter that Dioscorides says has the same strength as plumbago (5-100); it is not part of this entry.

**Identity.** The translator's gloss is "Lead Slag" and no mineral name is given. "Scoria" here is the glassy residue left from smelting; the chapter's "no trace of lead in it" makes clear that this is a by-product with the metal removed, but what it was made of beyond that is not stated. The Codex check proposes the same plain name. Distinct from the lead stone of 5-98 (CPU-02425) and from the lead ore of 5-100 (CPU-02430): each has its own chapter.

### Active Components

None can be stated from the chapter beyond the name. A slag from lead smelting is a glassy residue of oxides and silicates that may retain lead compounds; this is a general fact about smelting residues and is not drawn from Dioscorides, who says only that the best has "no trace of lead" in it. It is not asserted that this material was free of lead.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Toxic.** Dioscorides records lead slag as an astringent, washed to a powder and made into tablets, for the same ends as burnt lead. The chapter does not list those ends itself. Lead compounds are poisonous when swallowed, inhaled or put on broken skin, and the Codex gives no protocol and does not recommend lead slag in any form.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives a washing method only: wash the slag in a mortar with water poured on; pour off the yellowish water that remains on top; repeat "often until the scoria [slag] is used up"; allow it to settle; pour out the water; and "make it into tablets" (line 10316). The chapter does not say what the tablets are used with, how they are applied or how much. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any quantity from this entry.** [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] As general facts: lead and its compounds are toxic to the nervous system, the blood and the kidney, and no safe level of exposure is established; a washed slag powder can still carry lead, and a powder handled dry can be inhaled. The classical idea that lead compounds are "astringent" refers to their action of precipitating protein on tissue, which is the same property that makes them harmful. These points are not drawn from the chapter.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM).** The chapter prints one quality, relative to burnt lead: the slag is "more astringent". No warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality is printed in 5-97 itself. Burnt lead (5-96) prints none of those either in the lines read; washed lead (5-95) is described as "able to cool, and ... an astringent to close the pores" (line 10304). That quality is the lead chapter's and is not carried to the slag.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. What the herbal offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention (provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, adulteration detection), and this chapter shows part of it: a sensory rule for the best slag (like cerussa, thick, hard to break, yellowish, glassy) and a washing procedure. Its status is therapeutic only, and the translator's TOXIC mark is the tradition's own warning.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to take from it for cultivation beyond noting how the herbal grades a smelting by-product by sight. Lead slag is a poison, not a tonic, and nothing here supports using it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **TOXIC.** Lead and its compounds are poisonous. Do not ingest, inhale or apply lead slag. Do not grind, wash or handle it dry.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children:** no statement in the sources retrieved. Lead is especially harmful in pregnancy and childhood; avoid all exposure. [USER: verify]
- **Environment:** washing waters carry lead; do not pour them into drains or water sources.
- **Identity:** the translator's gloss is a plain name; do not assume any given slag matches Dioscorides' description.
