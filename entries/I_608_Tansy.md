# I-608 | Tansy

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary designation:** *Tanacetum vulgare* L. *(syn. Chrysanthemum vulgare* (L.) Bernh.)

**Taxonomic position:** Asteraceae; tribe Anthemideae; genus *Tanacetum* — same genus as Feverfew (*T. parthenium*, I-603); *vulgare* = "common"; the Anthemideae sub-cluster in this Codex section now covers Chamomile (*Matricaria*, I-602), Feverfew (*Tanacetum parthenium*, I-603), Yarrow (*Achillea*, I-604), Wormwood (*Artemisia absinthium*, I-605), Sweet Wormwood (*Artemisia annua*, I-606), Mugwort (*Artemisia vulgaris*, I-607), and now Tansy (*Tanacetum vulgare*, I-608)

**Nomenclature:**
- **Tansy:** from Medieval Latin *tanazita* or *athanasia* = "immortality" (Greek *a-thanatos* = deathlessness) — either referring to the plant's ability to preserve corpses from decay (historical use in funeral rites) or to its long-lasting dried flowers
- *Tanacetum:* from the same Greek root *athanasia* → Medieval Latin *tanazita*
- *vulgare:* "common" — abundantly naturalized throughout Europe and North America

**Vernacular names:**
- English: Common Tansy, Bitter Buttons, Cow Bitter, Golden Buttons, Stinking Willie; "buttons" refers to the characteristic flat-topped yellow button-like flower heads (no ray florets — only disc florets remain, giving the distinctive "button" appearance)
- German: *Rainfarn* (rain fern — "farn" for the feathery leaves); *Wurmkraut* (worm plant — anthelmintic use)
- French: *Tanaisie*, *Herbe aux vers* (herb of worms)
- Irish: *Lus na Fraochog* — associated with Easter/Lenten foods in Irish folk tradition (tansy cakes eaten after Lenten fast)
- Arabic: *Balmun* — recognized in Islamic medicine as a bitter herb

**Botanical description:** Perennial aromatic herb to 1.5 m; intensely aromatic (camphor, borneol — distinctively pungent, almost unpleasant at close range); pinnately divided dark green leaves; flower heads button-like (yellow disc florets only — no ray florets, unlike most Asteraceae); flat-topped dense corymbs; widespread and invasive in disturbed ground; the distinctive button flowers and penetrating aroma make identification easy

**TOXICOLOGICAL STATUS:** Tansy is a **TOXICOLOGICAL REFERENCE ENTRY** in this Codex. Internal use of tansy at medicinal doses carries significant risk of thujone toxicity (convulsions, multi-organ failure); multiple documented fatalities. Modern internal use is NOT recommended at any dose. Historical uses are documented for completeness; this entry serves pharmacological understanding and safety education.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Why Tansy is in the Codex despite its toxicological status:** The standing Codex directive is to include every substance in the pharmacological universe without curation. Tansy is present in the pharmacopoeias of antiquity, the medieval European tradition, and the modern toxicological database. Understanding tansy's pharmacology — specifically the thujone content and its consequences — is essential for:
1. Contextualizing the thujone risks of wormwood (I-605) and mugwort (I-607) by comparison
2. Understanding the historical abortifacient pharmacology of tansy and the folklore surrounding it
3. Appreciating why certain traditional medicines were abandoned after documented fatalities
4. Completing the Anthemideae tribe pharmacological survey

**Historical clinical context:** Tansy was used extensively in medieval European medicine, monastery gardens, and British folk medicine for intestinal worms, menstrual irregularity, and as an Easter food (tansy pudding/cakes — a post-Lenten tradition). It was recommended in 17th-century pharmacopoeias. It appeared in the 1914 US Pharmacopoeia. It was withdrawn from official pharmacopoeias after multiple documented deaths from thujone toxicity.

**The abortifacient history:** Tansy was one of the most commonly attempted abortifacients in pre-contraceptive European and American history. Tansy tea and tansy "penny royal" combinations (with *Mentha pulegium* — another thujone/pulegone-rich plant) were used to induce miscarriage. Deaths of women attempting self-abortion with tansy were documented in coroners' records from the 18th to early 20th centuries — a dark chapter in which toxicological pharmacology intersects with social history.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Essential oil (0.12–0.3% dried herb — extremely high thujone content):**
- **Beta-thujone:** 55–95% of essential oil in typical European tansy — the highest thujone concentration in any Asteraceae medicinal plant; this level is pharmacologically relevant for GABA-A antagonism and uterotonic effects at far lower doses than wormwood (*A. absinthium*, I-605)
- **alpha-Thujone:** minor; more toxic than beta-thujone
- **Camphor:** 10–30% in some chemotypes
- **Borneol, bornyl acetate**
- **Chrysanthenone, davanone:** characteristic tansy ketones

**Thujone content comparison (essential oil basis):**
| Plant | Thujone % in EO |
|-------|----------------|
| *Tanacetum vulgare* | 55–95% |
| *Artemisia absinthium* | 30–70% |
| *Artemisia vulgaris* | 10–40% (variable) |
| *Tanacetum parthenium* (Feverfew) | <5% |
| *Matricaria chamomilla* | <1% |

**Sesquiterpene lactones:**
- **Tanacetin:** bisabolane sesquiterpene lactone; anti-inflammatory; anti-tumor (in vitro); insecticidal
- **Artabsin** (minor; shared with wormwood): bitter

**Flavonoids:**
- **Axillarin:** 6-hydroxyluteolin derivative; characteristic of *Tanacetum*
- **Luteolin, quercetin** glycosides
- Chrysoeriol: anti-inflammatory

**Polyacetylenes:** antimicrobial; phytotoxic to insects

**Caffeic acid derivatives:** chlorogenic acid; 3,5-dicaffeoylquinic acid

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5A. Thujone Toxicity (Primary Safety Concern)

**Mechanism (same as wormwood, I-605, but at much higher concentrations):**
- Beta-thujone → **GABA-A receptor competitive antagonist** at the picrotoxinin site → chloride channel closure → reduced GABAergic inhibition → neuronal hyperexcitability → **seizures** (dose-dependent progression from restlessness → tremor → tonic-clonic seizures)
- Hepatotoxicity at high doses (thujone → reactive metabolite → covalent protein adducts in hepatocytes)
- Renal tubular damage: thujone glucuronide nephrotoxic
- Cardiovascular: tachycardia, arrhythmia preceding cardiovascular collapse

**Lethal toxidrome from tansy overdose (documented):**
- Phase 1 (30–120 min): abdominal pain, vomiting, diarrhea
- Phase 2 (1–4 hours): CNS excitement, tremors, seizures
- Phase 3 (hours): hepatic failure, renal failure, cardiovascular collapse
- The LD50 of thujone in rats is ~87 mg/kg; the thujone content of tansy tea (2 g herb/250 mL) may yield 5–15 mg thujone — at the low end of concerning range for a child; for adults, multiple cups may approach toxic territory

### 5B. Uterotonic/Abortifacient Effect

- Thujone + artemisia ketone + camphor → uterine smooth muscle stimulation → contractions → potentially sufficient for miscarriage in early pregnancy
- Camphor → sympathomimetic effects on uterine vasomotor tone
- Traditional abortifacient use is pharmacologically plausible; also pharmacologically dangerous (convulsive dose often close to abortifacient dose)

### 5C. Anthelmintic Effect

- Beta-thujone → toxic to intestinal helminths (roundworms, pinworms) at concentrations achievable with moderate doses
- Tanacetin → insecticidal/antiparasitic via sesquiterpene lactone mechanism
- Historical use: tansy spread on floors, rubbed on meat to repel insects; thujone volatile → insect repellent (topical; transdermal absorption minimal at ambient exposure)
- Modern equivalent: safer anthelmintics (mebendazole, albendazole) supersede tansy for human use

### 5D. Insect Repellent (Topical Application)

- Thujone, camphor, and polyacetylenes → repellent to flies, fleas, ants, and other insects
- **The one application with modern interest:** Topical essential oil (diluted) as insect repellent; evidence base limited but mechanism sound; safer than internal use; used in organic gardening as a companion plant for pest deterrence

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The funeral herb:** Tansy's historical role in preservation and embalming is specific and documented. The Greek term *athanasia* (immortality/deathlessness) captures its use: tansy was packed around corpses to delay putrefaction (antimicrobial volatile oils) and to repel insects from decomposing bodies. Medieval European burial practices included tansy among the preservative herbs placed in the shroud. The association between this plant and the prolongation of physical integrity after death gave it a symbolic weight that persisted into Christian tradition (associated with eternal life and resurrection in some interpretations).

**The Easter tansy cake paradox:** The tradition of eating "tansy" (a cake or pudding flavored with tansy juice or leaves) at Easter in England and Ireland goes back at least to the 15th century. These tansy cakes (later sometimes using entirely different bitter herbs when tansy was unavailable) were eaten to "purge the phlegm of winter" and celebrate spring. The irony that a post-Lenten celebratory food contained one of the most toxic plants in the European pharmacopoeia is a reminder that the dose determines toxicity — a small amount of tansy in a cake provides pleasant bitter flavor and mild GI stimulation; a medicinal-dose tea provides convulsions. The festive context regulated dose through culinary convention.

**The abortifacient tragedy archive:** The historical record of women dying from self-administered tansy (and pennyroyal) in attempts at abortion is preserved in 19th and early 20th century coroners' reports, medical journal case reports, and occasionally literary accounts. These deaths occurred because: (1) the dose needed to induce miscarriage was close to the convulsant dose; (2) women had no way to accurately dose a home preparation; (3) the botanical preparations varied enormously in potency. This history makes tansy one of the most pharmacologically and socially freighted plants in the European tradition — a medicine that killed the people it was meant to help.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**No therapeutic internal use is recommended.**

**Topical insect repellent (the one acceptable application):**
- Diluted essential oil (0.5–1% in carrier oil): may be applied to exposed skin as insect repellent; avoid mucous membranes, eyes, and broken skin
- Do not use near children — thujone dermally absorbed; avoid in pregnancy
- Do not use undiluted essential oil topically

**Garden/pest management:**
- Tansy planted as companion plant: volatile thujone deters many common garden insects (aphids, squash bugs, Colorado potato beetles); do not consume
- Dried tansy bundles hung in storage areas: grain/flour moth repellent (historical use); effective; no ingestion risk in this application

**Emergency management of tansy poisoning:**
- Contact Poison Control immediately
- Gastric decontamination if recent ingestion and patient alert with intact airway: activated charcoal 25–50 g
- Monitor for seizures (EEG if available; IV access)
- Benzodiazepines for seizures
- Liver and kidney function monitoring (24–72 hour window for hepatorenal injury)
- No specific antidote

**Never administer to:**
- Pregnant individuals at any dose (abortifacient)
- Children (lower weight → lower toxic dose)
- Epileptic patients (thujone lowers seizure threshold)
- Individuals with liver or kidney disease

**Cross-reference:** I-603 Feverfew (*Tanacetum parthenium* — same genus; low thujone vs. very high thujone in *T. vulgare*; this comparison within one genus demonstrates that chemical profile cannot be assumed from genus name); I-605 Wormwood (thujone content comparison — tansy 55-95% vs. wormwood 30-70% in EO; tansy more dangerous but wormwood more clinically discussed due to absinthe history); I-607 Mugwort (thujone comparison — mugwort 10-40% vs. tansy 55-95%; moxibustion is safe topical application vs. internal tansy which is not safe); I-568 Poison Hemlock (toxicological reference comparison — hemlock coniine NMJ failure vs. tansy thujone GABA-A seizures; both present as acute convulsant toxidromes with different receptor mechanisms)
