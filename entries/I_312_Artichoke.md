# I-312: Cynara scolymus (Globe Artichoke)

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**
**Entry I-312 | Asteraceae (Compositae)**

---

## Section 1: Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Binomial:** *Cynara scolymus* L.
**Synonyms:** *Cynara cardunculus* var. *scolymus* (L.) Fiori — per modern taxonomy (artichoke is a cultivated form of *C. cardunculus*, the cardoon)
**Family:** Asteraceae

**Common Names:**
- English: Globe Artichoke, Artichoke
- German: *Artischocke*; *Kardon* (for cardoon ancestor)
- French: *Artichaut*; *Cardon* (cardoon)
- Italian: *Carciofo*; *Cardone*
- Arabic: *Khurshūf* (خرشوف) / *Ardi-shawkī* (أرضي شوكي) — "earth thorn" — the most etymologically clear Arabic plant name; gave rise to Italian *carciofo* → French *artichaut* → English *artichoke*
- Spanish: *Alcachofa* — from Arabic *al-khurshūfa* (with article)
- Ancient Greek: *Kinara* (κύναρα) — Dioscorides' name; gave rise to genus name *Cynara*

**Etymology:**
*Cynara* — from Greek *kinara* (*kynara*), the Dioscoridean name; possibly from *kyon* (dog) — referring to the spiny bracts resembling dog's teeth. *scolymus* — from Greek *skolymos* (thorn thistle). The Arabic-to-European etymological chain is among the most traceable in pharmacobotany: *ardi-shawkī* (earth thorn) → Andalusian Arabic → Spanish *alcachofa* → Italian *articiocco* → French *artichaut* → English *artichoke*. The vegetable's name carries the Arabic influence on European cuisine directly in its phonetics.

**Cardoon vs. Artichoke:**
*Cynara cardunculus* (cardoon) is the wild ancestor; *C. scolymus* is the cultivated form selected for large, fleshy, edible immature flower heads. Both have similar phytochemical profiles. Therapeutically, **leaf extracts** (not the edible flower head) provide pharmacological activity — the immature bracts eaten as food contain far lower concentrations of active compounds than mature leaf preparations.

**Morphology:**
Perennial; 1–2 m; large thistle-like plant; large basal leaves with prominent white midrib; silver-green; deeply pinnately lobed; spectacular mauve-purple flowers (if allowed to bloom beyond edible stage); edible stage = immature capitulum ("globe") with fleshy receptacle and bracts.

**Parts Used:** Leaves (dried or fresh — for pharmaceutical preparations); Flower heads (culinary — food, not primary pharmacological source).

**Mediterranean Culinary-Medical Integration:**
Artichoke occupies the most medically significant position in Mediterranean cuisine — a food that is simultaneously a Commission E-approved medicine. The leaf extract with high cynarin content is a distinct pharmaceutical product from the cooked vegetable, but the cultural integration of artichoke as both food and liver tonic in Mediterranean countries created one of the most medically engaged culinary traditions in the Codex.

## Section 3: Convergence Analysis

**Primary Convergence Domains:**

1. **Choleretic / hepatic (liver protection + bile stimulation)** — DMM, CM, AT; cynarin + luteolin mechanism; **Commission E primary indication**
2. **Hypolipidemic (cholesterol-lowering)** — modern; cynarin HMG-CoA reductase + bile acid sequestration; Level B evidence
3. **Anti-nausea / digestive bitter** — DMM, CM; bitter receptor; bile stimulation → fat digestion
4. **Diuretic (secondary)** — DMM, CM; mild mechanism

**The Cynarin Discovery:**
*Cynarin* (1,3-dicaffeoylquinic acid) was isolated from artichoke leaf in the 1950s and identified as the primary active compound. Its discovery was a pivotal moment in European phytopharmacology — cynarin demonstrated dose-dependent choleretic activity and cholesterol-lowering effects in animal models, providing the first mechanistic explanation for artichoke's traditional hepatic reputation. Commission E approved artichoke leaf extract based substantially on cynarin research. The discovery also explained why artichoke has the famous effect of making water taste sweet after eating it — cynarin temporarily blocks taste receptors for sweetness, so water tasted after artichoke consumption seems artificially sweet.

## Section 4: Phytochemical Composition

### Caffeoylquinic Acids

**1. Cynarin (1,3-Dicaffeoylquinic acid) — PRIMARY compound**
- Hydrolyzes partially to chlorogenic acid; forms during drying of leaves
- **Choleretic:** Stimulates bile production + secretion from liver and gallbladder
- **Hypolipidemic:** HMG-CoA reductase inhibition (partial; weak relative to statins); bile acid synthesis upregulation → cholesterol clearance
- **Antioxidant:** Radical scavenging
- **Sweet taste inhibition:** Competitively inhibits sweet taste receptors → the "water tastes sweet after artichoke" phenomenon

**2. Chlorogenic Acid** — major phenolic; antioxidant; anti-viral; anti-diabetic

**3. 1,5-Dicaffeoylquinic acid** — related choleretic compound

### Flavonoids

**4. Luteolin-7-glucoside (Cynaroside) — MAJOR compound**
- Primary flavonoid; anti-inflammatory (COX inhibition); hepatoprotective
- NF-κB inhibition at higher concentrations

**5. Luteolin-4-glucoside (Scolymoside)** — related flavonoid; anti-inflammatory

**6. Apigenin** — anti-inflammatory; mild anxiolytic (GABA-A)

### Sesquiterpene Lactones

**7. Cynaropicrin** — primary bitter; TAS2R bitter receptor; choleretic reflex stimulation
- Asteraceae sesquiterpene lactone class; allergenicity risk (α-methylene-γ-lactone)

## Section 5: Mechanisms of Action

### Choleretic
- **Cynarin → hepatocyte bile acid transport upregulation + gallbladder contraction** → increased bile volume and flow into duodenum
- **Cynaropicrin → TAS2R bitter receptors** → cholecystokinin reflex → bile secretion
- Combined cynarin + bitter → dual choleretic mechanism (direct hepatic + reflex CCK-mediated)

### Hypolipidemic
- **Cynarin → partial HMG-CoA reductase inhibition** → reduced hepatic cholesterol synthesis
- **Increased bile acid synthesis from cholesterol** → net cholesterol clearance
- **Luteolin → anti-inflammatory** → reduces LDL oxidation and atherosclerotic plaque formation
- Cochrane review (2016) confirmed modest but consistent LDL reduction in human trials

### Anti-Nausea / Digestive
- **Cynaropicrin bitter → CCK → bile + pancreatic enzyme secretion** → fat digestion
- **Cynarin → anti-nausea** (mechanism: reduced GI motility disturbance; direct or secondary to bile normalization)

### Hepatoprotective
- **Luteolin + chlorogenic acid → hepatocyte antioxidant defense** → protect against oxidative hepatic injury
- Animal models: protection against CCl₄ hepatotoxicity

## Section 6: Clinical Evidence

**Dyspepsia / Liver / Choleretic:**
- German Commission E approved: artichoke leaf extract for dyspeptic complaints
- ESCOP monograph: symptomatic treatment of digestive disorders; liver conditions
- Multiple open-label studies showing symptom improvement in dyspepsia
- **Evidence Grade: Level C** (Commission E/ESCOP approved; mechanism-supported)

**Cholesterol Reduction:**
- Cochrane systematic review (Wider et al. 2009; updated): artichoke leaf extract significantly reduced total cholesterol in patients with hyperlipidemia vs. placebo
- Multiple RCTs: Englisch et al. 2000 (n=143): significant LDL reduction; Pittler et al. 2003 (n=75): comparable findings
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (multiple positive RCTs; Cochrane review)

**IBS:**
- RCT (Walker et al. 2001, *Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine*; n=208): artichoke leaf extract significantly improved IBS symptoms vs. placebo
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (RCT)

**NAFLD (Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease):**
- Small RCTs showing hepatic enzyme improvement; promising but preliminary
- **Evidence Grade: Level B** (small RCTs; promising)

## Section 7: Phenomenological Record

### Qualitative Experience

**The Artichoke Effect:**
The sweet-taste-inhibition phenomenon from cynarin is one of the most pharmacologically curious culinary experiences — drinking water after eating artichoke produces a brief sweet taste perception, because cynarin has displaced the threshold for sweetness. This effect has been studied by flavor chemists and is reproducible. Wine drunk after artichoke tastes altered. Artichoke is notoriously difficult to pair with wine for this reason — it makes everything taste slightly sweet and flat. The "artichoke problem" in restaurant wine pairing is a genuine cynarin-mediated pharmacological phenomenon.

**Versailles and the Globe:**
Artichoke cultivation in France was promoted by Catherine de' Medici (1519–1589) who reportedly consumed them obsessively and introduced artichoke culture from Italy to France when she married Henry II. Whether or not the specific historical claims about her consumption are accurate, artichoke became integrated into French haute cuisine partly through Italian Renaissance influence — and the medicinal liver tonic tradition (artichoke after rich meals; artichoke liqueur as digestif) persists in French culinary-medical culture. The aperitif tradition (Cynar liqueur — from *Cynara*) survives as a pharmacological tradition in beverage form.

## Section 8: Practical Application

### Formulation and Dosing

**Commission E Standard (Dyspepsia):**
- Artichoke leaf extract standardized to cynarin: 320–640 mg dry extract 3× daily with meals
- Total dried leaf: 6 g/day

**Cholesterol (Clinical Trial Doses):**
- Standardized extract: 1,800–3,600 mg dried extract daily (3 divided doses)
- Duration: minimum 8–12 weeks for lipid effects; continue while monitoring lipids

**Tea (Traditional):**
- 1–2 g dried leaf per 250 mL water; steep 15 minutes; 3× daily before meals

**Cynar Liqueur (Cultural-Pharmacological):**
- Artichoke-based bitter liqueur; low pharmacological dose; cultural continuation of choleretic bitter aperitif tradition

### Safety Profile

**Generally safe. Excellent safety profile for leaf extract.**

**Asteraceae Allergy:**
- Cynaropicrin (sesquiterpene lactone): contact sensitizer; cross-reactivity with artichoke-related thistles
- Lower sensitization rate than arnica/feverfew but possible
- Oral intake at standard doses: minimal sensitization risk

**Drug Interactions:**
- **Bile acid sequestrants (cholestyramine):** Additive cholesterol-lowering; monitor
- **Statins:** Additive; theoretically complementary at different mechanisms; monitor for enhanced LDL-lowering
- **Warfarin:** Limited evidence; CYP2C9 involvement possible; monitor INR
- **Diuretics:** Mild additive diuretic effect

**Contraindications:**
- **Bile duct obstruction:** CONTRAINDICATED — choleretic action contraindicated if bile cannot flow
- **Gallstones:** Use with caution; choleretic action may mobilize stones into bile duct
- Asteraceae allergy
- Pregnancy: insufficient data; AVOID at medicinal doses
