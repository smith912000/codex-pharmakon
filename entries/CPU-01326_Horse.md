# Horse — rta (Tibetan, as in Clark's *rta.lpags* "horse skin"); *Equus caballus orientalis* Maack (Men-Tsee-Khang glossary)
## *The domestic horse, as a meat, milk and animal medicine in the Tibetan materia medica; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints "Horse" with* Equus caballus orientalis *Maack, and Clark's* Quintessence Tantras *prints "horse" without a binomial* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The horse is named in several places in the Four Tantras as translated by Clark (1995, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`) and by the Men-Tsee-Khang (2011, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`). The spine row rests on the glossary of animals in the Men-Tsee-Khang volume (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, "Equus caballus", line 7011), where "Horse" (line 7009) is followed by *Equus caballus orientalis* Maack and, in the transliteration column, "Tah" (line 6980); the pairing is by column order, and names.csv records that the Tibetan name is not paired in the OCR. The element *rta* is read as "horse" from Clark's own glosses of the plant names *rta.lpags* "horse skin" (line 1015) and *rta.rmig* "horse's hoof" (line 1094).

*As food and diet.* In the Root Tantra's chapter on methods of treatment, the first of four remedies is diet: "The remedies for healing disease are fourfold: diet, behavioral pattern, medicine and accessory therapy", and "the recommended diet for wind disorders (includes) meat of horse, donkey and marmot, one-year-old (dried) meat, human flesh, seed oil, one-year-old butter, raw cane sugar, garlic, onion, milk, grain beer with angelica root and Polygonatum cirrifolium, wine of cane sugar and bone wine" (Clark lines 172-174). Men-Tsee-Khang has the same list for "loong" (wind) disorder: "the meat from horse, donkey, marmot, one-year-old meat ..." (line 1100). The chapter on direct therapeutics repeats it: wind disorders are treated with "heavy, oily, smooth, warm-powered dietary factors such as raw cane sugar, grain beer, old butter, mutton stored [for one year], the flesh of marmot, horse, donkey and human, garlic and onion" (Clark line 1411; Men-Tsee-Khang line 4426).

*In the meat classification.* Explanatory Tantra ch.16 (principles of diet) sorts meats into nine sections (Clark lines 659-672). Section (g) is "Domestic animals include the dzo, yak, camel, horse, donkey, bull ... goat, sheep, dog, pig, chicken, cat, etc." (Clark line 672; Men-Tsee-Khang line 2917). Of the flesh of particular animals: "The rump meats of horse, wild ass and donkey dry up pus and eliminate disorders of the kidneys and waist, as well as cold disorders and lymphatic ailments" (Clark line 674). Men-Tsee-Khang: "The meat of animals having rounded hoof like the horse, wild ass, donkey etc., stops pus formation, relieves cold disorders of the kidneys and waist, and helps against chhuser disorders" (line 2931). Meats in general are "sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste" (Clark line 673).

*Milk.* "The milk of horse and donkey heals lung diseases but increases (mental) dullness" (Clark line 708); "Horse and donkey milk strengthen the lungs, but also cause mental dullness" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 3059).

*Parts in chapter 20.* Under nails: "Horse's hoof: removes tumors [and is beneficial against nagas]. Horse's ankle bone: cures anterior transposition of the calf muscle [and subdues nagas]" (Clark line 1189); under droppings, "Horse dung: is anthelmintic, anti-emetic, cures wind-bile combinations [and is beneficial against spirits]" (line 1199). Men-Tsee-Khang: "horse hooves cure tumors and the fetlock cures cramps of the calf muscle" (line 3556) and "Horse dung cures ein disorder, combined disorder of tripa and loong, and stops vomiting" (line 3568). Clark's square brackets mark interpolations from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries (introduction, line 82), so the *naga* and spirit actions come from commentary, not from the root text as Clark gives it. The hoof, ankle bone and dung each have their own entry (III_112, III_108, III_110); horse flesh in the Sushruta Samhita is III_111.

*As a sign.* The horse also appears in the omens and dreams of the chapters on death and prognosis, not as a substance: seeing "horses and sheep and cows, etc., with their young" on the way to a patient's house is a good omen (Clark line 397); dreaming that one is naked and riding south on a buffalo, horse, pig, donkey or camel is a sign of death (line 402); dreaming of riding "a lion, elephant, horse or ox" is among the fortunate dreams (line 404).

### Active Components

Horse meat is lean red meat: mostly water and muscle protein with little fat, and a source of iron, zinc and B vitamins. Mare's milk is a milk with a composition that differs from cow's milk; the lactose content is high. No analysis was read for the Tibetan materia medica items. Horse hoof is mainly keratin, and dung is largely undigested plant fibre with gut bacteria. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts place horse meat in a diet for wind disorders, and hoof, ankle bone and dung among the animal medicines. The Codex gives no protocol. The Tibetan texts as translated give no doses; horse is not eaten in many cultures, and its slaughter or sale is restricted or prohibited in some places; hoof and dung preparations carry infection risks. Clark himself notes that "nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (introduction, line 57).

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations given are the diet itself (horse meat among the warm, heavy, oily foods for wind disorders), the rump meat, and the milk. No cooking method, quantity or course is printed for the horse in the passages read, and none is given for hoof, ankle bone or dung. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Horse meat and offal must be cooked and handled hygienically; horses can carry *Trichinella* and other parasites that are controlled by inspection and cooking. Dung can carry pathogens including *Clostridium tetani*. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Printed qualities: all meats have a sweet taste and a sweet post-digestive taste (Clark line 673; Men-Tsee-Khang line 2923). For the rump meat of horse, wild ass and donkey: dries pus, removes kidney and waist disorders, cold disorders and lymphatic disorders (Clark line 674). Milk: heals lung disease, increases mental dullness. For the rounded-hoof animals, the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence adds that the meat stops pus formation and helps against *chhuser* disorders. The Tantra's wider sorting of meats into dry-land, wet-place and both depends on group order, and one group line is unreadable in the OCR (Men-Tsee-Khang line 2915), so the horse's place in that scheme is not asserted. ### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The horse sits inside the Tantra's four remedies, where diet is the first, and inside its materia medica of animal products. Clark's introduction says the three humours are "rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration respectively" (line 51), so disease is not only bodily in this tradition; but nothing says the horse treats that root. The essence-extraction (*bcud len*) compounds Clark's introduction mentions are made from plants, flowers, calcite and mineral exudate (line 69), not horse flesh. There is no category of substances for subtle-body practice in this tradition, and none is claimed.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice. Horse meat is one of the warm, heavy, oily foods the Tantra lists for a wind disorder, and the other horse items are ordinary animal medicines recorded in a list. A cultivator has no regimen to take from them, and the tradition's own comment that few animal products are used today argues for leaving them as history.

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** horse flesh and offal must be cooked through; dung, hoof and bone preparations carry infection risks and are not suitable for internal use.
- **The texts' own caution:** the milk "increases (mental) dullness" (Clark line 708).
- **Legal and cultural status:** horse slaughter and horse meat are restricted or prohibited in many places and taboo in many cultures; status was not checked. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics and religion:** killing an animal for food or medicine is a moral question for many traditions; this entry takes no position beyond recording the text.
- **Not interchangeable:** the horse's parts have their own entries; do not combine claims between them.
