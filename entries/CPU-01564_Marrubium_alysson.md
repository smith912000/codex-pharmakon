# Marrubium alysson — Alusson (Dioscorides 3-105, "Alysson"); "Plaited-leaved Horehound, Moonwort" on the translators' line

## *Marrubium alysson, the first binomial on the translators' SUGGESTED line for chapter 3-105 (a variant spelling, Marrubium alyssum, follows; glossed "Plaited-leaved Horehound, Moonwort"); a second line records that "Sprengel says this is Alyssum alpestre [Loudon]"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text on disk names this plant under this name. The entry rests on one chapter of one text. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed under the chapter heading, and not Dioscorides' own identification. The translators add a note on the next line: "Sprengel says this is Alyssum alpestre [Loudon]". That is a different genus (in general botany a member of the cabbage family, not the mint family), so the translators' own page carries two incompatible suggestions, and the entry follows the first line only because the spine row does. The Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The heading *3-105. ALUSSON* is at line 7478, the SUGGESTED line at line 7479, the note on Sprengel at line 7480 and the chapter text at line 7481. The name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, row 511. The chapter stands in Book Three ("Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants" in the running head), after 3-104 *APARINE* (cleavers, line 7475) and before 3-106 *ASKLEPIAS* (line 7493). The printed page number 488 follows the chapter, and the translators' indexes list *Alysson*, *Alyssum alpestre*, *Marrubium alysson* and *Marrubium alyssum* all at page 488 (lines 14580, 14584, 16480 and 16484).

The whole chapter reads: "Alysson is a somewhat rough little shrub with round leaves. The fruit is similar to little double shields, in which is the somewhat broad seed. It grows in hilly and rough places. A decoction of this (taken as a drink) dissolves afflictions in those without fever. When held or smelled it has a similar effect. Pounded into small pieces with honey it cleans freckles. Pounded together in meat and given, it is thought to cure madness in a dog. Hanged in a house it is said to be wholesome and an amulet for men and beasts. Hanged on them with a purple cloth, it drives away sores on cattle. It is also called aspidium, haplophyllon, accuseton, or adeseton."

So the chapter describes the plant (a "somewhat rough little shrub", round leaves, a fruit "similar to little double shields" holding a "somewhat broad" seed, growing in "hilly and rough places"), and then gives seven uses or reports. A decoction drunk "dissolves afflictions in those without fever", and held or smelled "it has a similar effect". The translators do not say what "afflictions" renders. With honey it "cleans freckles". Pounded in meat it "is thought to cure madness in a dog". It is "said to be wholesome and an amulet for men and beasts" when hung in a house, and hung on cattle "with a purple cloth" it "drives away sores". The names are *aspidium*, *haplophyllon*, *accuseton* and *adeseton*.

**Recorded, not endorsed.** The chapter marks two of its claims as report or opinion: the dog cure is "thought" to work, and the house amulet is "said to be" wholesome. The decoction, the smelling, the honey use and the cattle charm are stated flatly. Dioscorides sometimes records an amuletic use of this kind in the same text, for instance in the asparagus chapter (line 5854, where "Some also use the root as an amulet for scrofulous tumours") and the hippoglosson chapter (line 9196, whose filaments "seem to be an effective amulet for headaches"). In those places and here, recording is not endorsing, and the Codex does not treat the amulet as a practice.

**Overlap.** The chapter 3-105 is not the subject of any existing entry (chapter-level search for 3-105, *Alusson*, *Alysson* and *Marrubium alysson*). Other horehound chapters have their own entries by other chapters: I_597 (White Horehound, *Marrubium vulgare*) and I_524 (Black Horehound, *Ballota nigra*), and the sister chapter of this batch, 3-38 *PSEUDODIKTAMNOS* (I_1129, *Marrubium pseudodictamnus*). None cites 3-105. The three are listed by the same genus and are different chapters, and none is merged. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The text prints no constituent, and no analysis of the plant Dioscorides describes was read. As a general fact, the horehound genus *Marrubium* belongs to the mint family and is known for bitter diterpenoid lactones (marrubiin is the best known, from white horehound). That is a statement about the genus and not about *Marrubium alysson*, and the translators' second suggestion, *Alyssum*, is a different family. Nothing is stated here about its composition. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a decoction for "afflictions" in the afebrile, a honeyed paste for freckles, a remedy for a mad dog, and a house and cattle amulet. These are Dioscorides' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol. A dog that shows signs of madness (rabies) is an emergency for a veterinarian and for anyone it has bitten. The Codex treats the amulet as a record and not as a practice.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives four preparations: a decoction "taken as a drink"; the plant "held or smelled"; "pounded into small pieces with honey" and applied; and "pounded together in meat and given" to a dog. The amulet uses are to hang the plant in a house, or on cattle "with a purple cloth". The text gives no quantity for any of them. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** chapter 3-105 prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality and no taste. The qualities printed are of texture and form: "somewhat rough", "round leaves", a fruit like "little double shields".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter assigns the plant no cultivation status. What it gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a field description (rough little shrub, round leaves, a double-shield fruit with a broad seed, hilly and rough ground) and a sensory use ("held or smelled"). It also records an amuletic use, "wholesome and an amulet for men and beasts", with the "it is said" of report. That is a recorded belief and the text does not endorse it, and the Codex does not turn it into a practice. The identification is the translators', and their page offers two. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant, apart from the observation habit of the description. The amulet is a recorded belief, and the abstentive answer is the right one: nothing here to take up.

### Contraindications

Chapter 3-105 prints no warning. A mad dog, and any person bitten by one, needs urgent professional care. Freckles and "afflictions" need no home remedy from an unidentified shrub. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, with a second suggestion from a different genus on the same page, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. The chapter gives no dose, and no safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
