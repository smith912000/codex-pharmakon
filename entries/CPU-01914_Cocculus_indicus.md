# Cocculus indicus — "Cocculus Indicus" (Bhishagratna, Appendix 3); Kokkos baphike (Dioscorides 4-48, translator's suggestion only)

## *The fruit of a climbing shrub, the fishberry or Indian berry; our identification: Anamirta cocculus (L.) Wight & Arn., from the Latin name as printed in the Sushruta appendix, where the gloss reads "(Fruit of) Cocculus Indicus"; confidence medium for the Sushruta gloss. For Dioscorides the identification does not hold: chapter 4-48 describes dye grains (kermes), and the translator lists Anamirta cocculus only among several suggestions; confidence low* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, SS

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **CAUTION.** TOXICITY. The fruit of Anamirta cocculus is a known poison and has been used as a fish poison. This entry is a historical record only. Nobody should take it.

**Named by the Sushruta translation (SS), and by the Dioscorides translator's suggestion list (DMM).** This is not a single-text entry, but only the SS line is a plant gloss.

**Dioscorides (DMM).** Chapter 4-48, "KOKKOS BAPHIKE" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 8421-8439). The translator's line reads "SUGGESTED: Cocculus officinale, Cocculus plukenetii [Loudon] — Cocculus — twining shrub / Anamirta cocculus, Anamirta paniculata — Cocculus Indicus Plant / Quercus coccifera — Kermes Oak — little coccus insect is found on it", with the note "Coccus means berries as well as being the name of the dyer's insect." The chapter's text describes something else: "Coccum tinctile is a little shrub full of sprigs, to which cling grains like lentils which are taken out and stored. The best is from Galatia and Armenia, then that from Asia and that from Cilicia, and last of all that from Spain." It is "astringent, and good for wounds and lost strength" when "pounded into small pieces and applied with vinegar"; and "that in Cilicia grows on oaks [with grains] similar in shape to a little snail, which the women there gather by mouth, and call them coccum". That is the dyer's kermes insect on the kermes oak, not the Indian fruit. The Cocculus suggestions are therefore the translator's guess at the word *kokkos* and are not supported by the chapter. The Dioscorides attestation of *Cocculus indicus* is thus doubtful, and the tag is kept only because the spine lists it. [USER: verify whether the DMM tag should stay.]

**Sushruta (SS).** One line of Appendix No. 3 of Bhishagratna's translation, headed "Names of Drugs (with Latin synonyms)" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`, heading at lines 31119-31121). The translator's note at the end of the appendices (lines 33351-33354) says he drew on *Vaidyaka-Sabdasindhu*, Wilson's *Sanskrit and English Dictionary*, *Indian Plants and Drugs* and *Kavirsiji S'iksha*. The Latin names are therefore the translator's glosses from those reference works. They are not words of the Sushruta Samhita itself. ### Active Components

The sources name no constituent. In general terms, the fruit of *Anamirta cocculus* is the source of picrotoxin, a convulsant poison (a mixture of picrotoxinin and picrotin). That is a general fact about the plant and is not a claim from either text.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the source says it is for.** Nothing for the fruit in Appendix 3, which states no use. The Dioscorides chapter's uses (astringent, for wounds and lost strength) belong to the dye grains, not to this fruit.
- **What the source does not say.** Any indication, preparation, dose or duration for Cocculus indicus.

The Codex gives no protocol for this material and does not recommend it.

### Preparation & Dosing

None for the fruit. The Dioscorides chapter says the grains are "pounded into small pieces and applied with vinegar" (line 8439); it speaks of kermes. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] **Do not use any dose from this entry.**

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Picrotoxin, the principal poison of the fruit, blocks inhibitory GABA receptors in the nervous system and causes convulsions; the fruit is not a medicine in modern practice. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter concerns dye grains, so it offers nothing for this fruit. What it does show a reader is how the translator's name list can mislead: the Greek word *kokkos* means grain or berry, and the English suggestion list follows the word, not the text.

**Sushruta (SS).** **Sushruta (SS).** *(c) Absent.* The only locus is a name list. Appendix 3 gives a Latin gloss and nothing else: no rasayana or vajikarana chapter is cited for the substance, no ojas action is stated, and no purification (*shodhana*) is described. No cultivation status is attested in the sources retrieved.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to practise. A poisonous fruit named once on a list, and a Greek chapter that is about something else. Do not take it.

### Contraindications

- **TOXIC.** Picrotoxin causes convulsions. Do not ingest this fruit or any preparation of it. Do not gather it.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** Avoid; no safe-use statement exists. [USER: verify]
- **Identity.** The DMM chapter is not this plant; the SS line is a Latin gloss only. [USER: verify]
- **Legal.** Sale of the fruit is restricted in some places for its use as a fish poison; status not checked. [USER: verify]
