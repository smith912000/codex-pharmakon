**Training Phase:** Phase III — Nervine Stabilisation (with a wound-vulnerary sub-note)

### Origin & Tradition

*Hypericum tetrapterum* is a wild, damp-meadow St John's-wort of Europe and western Asia, named for its conspicuously **four-winged, square stem** — the diagnostic feature that separates it from its famous cousin *H. perforatum*. It carries the whole doctrinal weight of the genus **by association**, and an honest entry must keep that distinction sharp: the tradition attaches to "St John's-wort" as a folk-category, while almost all the modern pharmacology belongs specifically to *H. perforatum*.

**Classical Greek herbalism (Dioscoridean *hyperikon*, ὑπέρικον).** The genus enters the written record through Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica*, where *hyperikon* is described as a bitter, warming herb whose seed, taken in wine, was used for sciatic and "burning" complaints and whose topical application drew together wounds and burns. This is the taproot of the enduring **vulnerary** (wound-healing) doctrine: St John's-wort as a herb that "closes and cleans" injured flesh.

**Anglo-European Christian folk tradition (the "blood of St John").** By the medieval period the genus had been absorbed into solar-Christian symbolism. Gathered at the summer solstice around the feast of St John the Baptist (24 June), the plant's flowers, when the glands are crushed, bleed a **blood-red oil** — read as the martyr's blood, and by the doctrine of signatures as proof of the herb's power over wounds, bleeding, and the darkening of the spirit. The macerated red oil (*oleum hyperici*) was the standard European field-dressing for cuts, burns, and nerve-pain, and the herb doubled as a **nervine "for melancholy"** and a charm to drive off darkness — an early somatic-and-spiritual antidepressant framing.

**Chinese Materia Medica (*Hypericum* group, e.g. 贯叶连翘 *guàn yè lián qiáo*).** Chinese medicine uses *Hypericum* species as bitter, cool, heat-clearing, blood-cooling, damp-draining herbs for sores, bleeding, and (in modern integrative practice) low mood. The valence differs from the European "warming solar" reading — here the herb is *cooling and detoxifying* — but the shared applied core is wounds, bleeding, and disorders of mood.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The genus doctrine is a single sustained metaphor: *a plant that captures the sun and bleeds red light heals both open wounds and the wound of melancholy.* This maps onto real chemistry with satisfying directness. The "blood of St John" is literally **hypericin** — a red, photodynamic naphthodianthrone concentrated in the dark glandular dots of the flowers and leaves. The signature is not superstition dressed up: the plant genuinely bleeds red, and the red pigment is genuinely bioactive. The mood-lifting reputation, meanwhile, tracks a different set of molecules — chiefly **hyperforin** — whose broad reuptake-inhibiting action is a plausible material basis for the "against melancholy" doctrine. **But the doctrine over-promises for *this* species.** The red-oil vulnerary signature is a genus-wide, chemically-grounded claim; the antidepressant claim is species-specific to *H. perforatum* and cannot be transferred to *H. tetrapterum* on faith. The symbolism is coherent; the evidence is not evenly distributed across the plants that share it.

### Active Components

- **Naphthodianthrones** — **hypericin** and pseudohypericin (the red, photosensitising glandular pigments; the "blood").
- **Phloroglucinols** — **hyperforin** and adhyperforin (lipophilic, unstable; the principal driver of monoamine-reuptake inhibition and of enzyme induction in *H. perforatum*).
- **Flavonoids** — quercetin, quercitrin, hyperoside, rutin, and the biflavone amentoflavone.
- **Tannins** (astringent; part of the wound-closing action).
- Essential oil and phenolic acids.

Constituent *levels* vary enormously between *Hypericum* species and even between chemotypes; *H. tetrapterum* is documented far less thoroughly than *H. perforatum*, and its hyperforin/hypericin content should be treated as **uncharacterised** rather than assumed.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica, *H. tetrapterum* is filed as a **Phase III Nervine Stabiliser with a vulnerary sub-note** — but deliberately as the *lesser, wild sibling*. It carries the genus's dual signature (topical wound-oil; nervine "against melancholy") while being flagged as **evidence-poor**: the practitioner is directed to reserve mood-support expectations for the pharmacologically validated *H. perforatum* (its own codex entry) and to treat *H. tetrapterum* primarily as a **topical red-oil vulnerary** and secondarily, and only tentatively, as a folk nervine. Its role here is as much cautionary as therapeutic: it teaches that a shared name and a shared red oil do **not** guarantee shared clinical action.

### Scientific Correlation

Attribution: the following is based on articles retrieved from PubMed.

- **The antidepressant evidence belongs to *H. perforatum*, not this species.** A meta-analysis of 14 RCTs (2,270 patients) found standardised *H. perforatum* extract comparable to SSRIs for depression with fewer adverse effects (Zhao et al., 2023, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.17219/acem/152942)). *Hypericum* also shows benefit as adjunct in post-stroke depression network meta-analysis (Zhang et al., 2023, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2023.1180071)). **Grade for *H. perforatum*: MODERATE–STRONG human evidence. Grade for *H. tetrapterum*: NULL — no species-specific antidepressant trial exists.** This is the central honest caveat of the entry.
- **Mechanism of the "against melancholy" doctrine.** Hyperforin is identified as the constituent behind the antidepressant efficacy and behined the plant's cytochrome-induction liability; hypericin contributes and its bioavailability is improved by co-effectors (pharmacokinetic synergy) (Nahrstedt & Butterweck, 2010, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/np1000329)). This gives the folk nervine doctrine a real molecular anchor — **but only where hyperforin is actually present in quantity, which is undocumented for *H. tetrapterum*.**
- **The major drug-interaction class (genus caution).** Hyperforin (and, in cell models, hypericin and quercitrin) induce intestinal **MDR1/P-glycoprotein and CYP3A4** expression (Gutmann et al., 2006, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-2006-931585); Nahrstedt & Butterweck, 2010, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/np1000329)). This is the mechanistic basis of St John's-wort's notorious ability to accelerate the clearance of co-administered drugs. **Grade: STRONG (well-replicated, clinically consequential).** It must be applied as a *genus-level* precaution to *H. tetrapterum* on a precautionary basis, even though the species' hyperforin content is unmeasured.
- **Vulnerary/photodynamic pigment (doctrine-to-molecule).** Hypericin is the red, light-activated naphthodianthrone that literally is the "blood of St John"; its photosensitising nature underlies both the topical-healing reputation and the phototoxicity warning below. **Grade: chemistry SOLID; species-specific wound-healing RCT: absent.**

**Honest bottom line and nulls.** The genus doctrine splits cleanly: (1) the **red-oil vulnerary** signature is chemically real (hypericin) and traditionally deep, but lacks species-specific clinical trials for *H. tetrapterum*; (2) the **antidepressant** reputation is genuinely evidence-based **but for the wrong plant** — it is *H. perforatum*'s, and importing it to *H. tetrapterum* is fabrication by association, which this entry refuses. Everything mood-related here is graded **DATA-SPARSE / UNSUPPORTED for the species.**

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** No Mesopotamian or cuneiform attestation for *Hypericum tetrapterum* appears in the retrieved profile — the sources are Greek, European-folk and Chinese, none Assyrian. Under the standing blocking warning for Assyrian substance identifications, tablet references and causal-agency attributions, no status is claimed; illness in that system is classified by the agency a substance is set against, and nothing here assigns this wild St John's-wort such a role. **(c) absent** — and the AT tag is unsupported by any attestation in the entry.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *Hyperikon* (ὑπέρικον) enters the written record in *De Materia Medica* as a bitter, warming herb — the seed taken in wine for sciatic and burning complaints, the herb applied to draw together wounds and burns. Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system, so there is no cultivation status to assign; and the attestation attaches to the genus-category rather than specifically to the four-winged *H. tetrapterum*. What the tradition genuinely offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention — recognition of the true plant, the season and manner of gathering, the sensory assay of the bitter herb and its red-glanded tops. The later "against melancholy" nervine reading is medieval European folk material, not Dioscorides, and must not be read back into him. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The profile contains no Avicennan or Unani material for this plant; the content sitting under the CM tag is in fact Chinese (贯叶连翘 *guàn yè lián qiáo*, a *Hypericum*-group heat-clearing, blood-cooling herb) and is properly TCM, not Perso-Arabic. On Unani's own terms — the *ruh*, the natural/vital/animal faculties, and the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture — no source assigns this species any status, and no *mufarrih* or cardiac role is attested. **(c) absent** — with the CM tag flagged as a probable mislabel for the Chinese (TCM) stratum present.

**Practitioner's note.** Across the traditions actually on the tag line there is nothing to cultivate with. The honest cultivation content of this plant sits elsewhere entirely: the European folk *oleum hyperici* — the sun-macerated red wound-oil gathered around the summer solstice — is a real practice, but it is European-folk (EF) material, topical and vulnerary, not a longevity or vital-constitution regimen; and the mood-lifting reputation belongs to the validated sibling *H. perforatum*, not to this wild congener. A cultivator uses *H. tetrapterum* as a topical red-oil for cuts and burns and treats any internal "nervine" expectation as unproven for the species, mindful too of the genus-level photosensitising and CYP/P-gp interaction cautions. Nothing here is taken long-term to build the person.

### Contraindications

- **Photosensitisation (hypericin).** The naphthodianthrones are phototoxic; internal use with UV/strong-sun exposure risks phototoxic skin reactions, especially in fair-skinned users. Counsel sun-avoidance during internal use.
- **Major CYP/P-gp drug-interaction class (genus caution).** By genus precaution, treat as a potent inducer of CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein: it can lower plasma levels and cause failure of — among others — **hormonal contraceptives, ciclosporin and tacrolimus, warfarin, many antiretrovirals, certain anticancer agents, digoxin, and numerous others.** Do not combine with prescription medication without pharmacist/physician review.
- **Serotonergic combinations.** Genus caution: risk of serotonin syndrome if combined with SSRIs/SNRIs/MAOIs/triptans.
- **Pregnancy/lactation — avoid** (data-absent for this species).
- **Species-substitution error.** Do not treat *H. tetrapterum* as clinically equivalent to standardised *H. perforatum* antidepressant products.
