### Origin & Tradition

Clove (the dried flower bud of *Syzygium aromaticum*, native to the Maluku Islands of Indonesia) was among the most valuable spices in ancient trade, reaching China by the Han Dynasty (3rd century BCE), the Middle East via the Arabian spice trade, and Rome via Egypt. Han dynasty Chinese courtiers were required to hold a clove in their mouth when addressing the emperor — the first recorded use of a mouthwash/breath freshener. The Chinese *Dīngxiāng* (丁香 — "nail-shaped fragrance") reflects its characteristic shape. The eugenol content — among the highest of any spice — is the reason clove has been the world's most used dental analgesic for millennia and remains in clinical use today as the primary component of zinc oxide-eugenol dental cement.

**AT — Assyrian (Qaranfulu — imported via Arabian trade routes; documented in late Assyrian texts):** Clove appears in Assyrian medical records as a highly valued imported aromatic from the "east." The distinctive powerful aromatic character was used in compound preparations for dental pain and digestive conditions.

**DMM — De Materia Medica (documented via later Byzantine copies as Eastern aromatic; Dioscorides may not have encountered clove directly):** Byzantine additions to the De Materia Medica tradition document clove (*karofyllon*) as a warming digestive and aromatic medicine reaching the Mediterranean via trade.

**CM — Canon of Medicine (قرنفل — Qaranful):** Ibn Sina provides detailed coverage. He classifies clove as hot in the third degree and dry — intensely warming. Primary indications: "strengthens the stomach, liver, and heart"; digestive stimulant; cardiac tonic; aromatic tonic for the nervous system; dental analgesic (applied to toothache — the eugenol mechanism); and as a warming compound formula ingredient for cold-type conditions. His cardiac-tonic characterization reflects the eugenol stimulation of cardiac β1-adrenergic receptors.

**SS — Ayurveda (लवण्ग — Lavaṅga):** Clove is a fundamental spice in Ayurvedic medicine. Classification:
- **Rasa:** Katu (pungent) + Tikta (bitter)
- **Virya:** Ushna (hot)
- **Vipaka:** Katu
- **Dosha action:** Vata-Kapha shamaka; Pitta-increasing in excess
- **Primary actions:** Deepana-Pachana (supreme digestive fire igniter); Krimighna (antimicrobial — especially oral); Vedanasthapana (analgesic); Kasahara (antitussive); Hridya (cardiac tonic)
- *Lavaṅga* is one of the 10 aromatic roots and spices (*Dashanga*) in classical Ayurvedic compound preparations
- Classical preparation: *Lavaṅgadi Vati* — clove compound tablet for cough, bronchitis, and digestive conditions

**SBJ/TCM (丁香 — Dīngxiāng — both the flower bud and a separate species *Syringa oblata* share this name; clove = 公丁香 Gōng Dīngxiāng):** Clove in TCM is classified as pungent, warm; Spleen-Stomach-Kidney-Lung channel. Primary actions: warms the Middle Burner; descends Stomach Qi (stops hiccup and vomiting from Cold); warms Kidney Yang; tonifies the Lower Burner. Classical indication: "stomach cold hiccup" — clove as the primary TCM hiccup remedy reflects the Stomach Qi downbearing mechanism. Formula: *Ding Xiang Shi Di Tang* (Clove and Persimmon Calyx Decoction) — the classic anti-hiccup formula.

**FMT — Tibetan Medicine (བྱི་མ་ར་ — Byi-ma-ra — clove in Tibetan pharmacopoeia):** Clove is documented in the *Gyushi* as a warming, Yang-supporting spice used in Kidney Yang-deficiency formulas and as an aromatic digestive medicine for Cold-type digestive complaints. It is one of the "precious pills" (*Rinchen Rilbu*) ingredient group.

### Active Components

- **Eugenol** — 70–90% of clove bud essential oil; the primary bioactive; **COX-1 and COX-2 inhibitor** (ibuprofen-level anti-inflammatory at equivalent doses); **TRPV1 agonist** (creates warming sensation + analgesia via desensitization); **TRPA1 agonist** (pungent sensation); local anesthetic (Na+ channel blockade — the dental analgesic mechanism); antimicrobial (bactericidal + antifungal); antiplatelet; vasodilatory; β1-adrenergic partial agonist (mild cardiac stimulation)
- **Eugenol acetate** — 5–10%; similar activity to eugenol; the fragrance modification compound
- **β-Caryophyllene** — sesquiterpene; selective CB2 cannabinoid receptor agonist (anti-inflammatory via endocannabinoid system); the anti-inflammatory complement to eugenol; found in black pepper, cannabis, and many Lamiaceae herbs
- **α-Humulene** — sesquiterpene; anti-inflammatory; the hop-like aroma component
- **Kaempferol, rhamnetin** — flavonoids; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**1. Dental analgesic and oral antimicrobial (eugenol — direct application)**
Eugenol is the active in clove oil dental preparations — applied directly to an exposed nerve, pulp, or socket, it provides local anesthetic + anti-inflammatory relief for dental pain. This is one of the most reliable herbal first-aid applications. Beyond dental analgesia, clove oil's bactericidal activity against oral pathogens (*Streptococcus mutans*, *Candida*, *Porphyromonas gingivalis*) makes it a complete oral antimicrobial.

**2. Digestive fire ignition and hiccup/nausea control ([PRIME])**
Clove's role as a Stomach Qi downbearer (TCM) — stopping hiccup, nausea, and reflux from Cold patterns — is validated by eugenol's direct smooth muscle relaxation and prokinetic (speeds gastric emptying) effects. For practitioners experiencing cold-type nausea, post-training gastric emptying delay, or hiccup from rapid breathing, 2–3 whole cloves chewed provides rapid relief.

**3. Anti-inflammatory (COX inhibition + β-Caryophyllene CB2)**
The dual COX inhibition (eugenol) and CB2 agonism (β-caryophyllene) provides a multi-mechanism anti-inflammatory action. The CB2 pathway is particularly relevant for neuroinflammation and gut inflammation — pathways not addressed by COX inhibition alone. For [RESET] phase anti-inflammatory stacking, clove in cooking or as clove water provides meaningful daily anti-inflammatory contribution.

**4. Kidney Yang warming and male reproductive support (TCM Lower Dantian)**
TCM use of clove as a Kidney Yang tonic for cold-pattern reproductive conditions (cold extremities, low libido, poor sperm motility) reflects the eugenol β1-adrenergic stimulation and vasodilatory effect on peripheral circulation. Combined with black pepper and ginger in warming compound preparations, clove forms the core warming Jing support triad.

### Preparation & Dosing

**Clove in cooking (daily culinary-therapeutic dose):**
2–5 whole cloves in cooking (rice, curries, stews); provides meaningful eugenol + β-caryophyllene daily dose. The standard food-medicine application.

**Clove tea:**
3–5 whole cloves (lightly crushed) simmered in 300mL water 5 minutes; strain; drink for digestive warming, cold-type nausea, or hiccup. 1 cup as needed.

**Clove oil (dental analgesic — topical only):**
Apply 1 drop clove essential oil on a cotton pellet directly to affected tooth or socket; provides 30–60 minutes analgesia. **Never ingest undiluted clove essential oil** — eugenol causes hepatotoxicity at concentrated doses internally.

**Clove + ginger + black pepper warming formula (Trikatu equivalent with clove):**
Clove 1g + ginger 1g + black pepper 0.5g decoction; drink for cold-type conditions; Kidney Yang and digestive warming.

### Scientific Correlation

- **Eugenol dental analgesia:** Markowitz K et al. (1992): eugenol directly inhibited C-fiber (pain) nerve firing; Na+ channel blockade mechanism; Grossman LA et al.: clinical dental analgesia confirmed; remains primary component of zinc oxide-eugenol cement (worldwide clinical use)
- **COX inhibition:** Raghavenra H et al. (2006): eugenol demonstrated significant COX-2 inhibitory activity comparable to NSAIDs at equivalent concentrations; anti-inflammatory mechanism in multiple cell models
- **β-Caryophyllene CB2:** Gertsch J et al. (2008): β-caryophyllene identified as a selective CB2 receptor agonist in food; anti-inflammatory via CB2 mechanism confirmed; Klauke AL et al. (2014): β-caryophyllene reduced chronic neuropathic pain in rodent model via CB2
- **Antimicrobial:** Cai L, Wu CD (1996): clove oil and eugenol showed strongest antimicrobial activity against oral pathogens in comparison study; Groppo FC et al. (2002): clove oil mouthwash comparably effective to chlorhexidine

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** Clove is a late imported aromatic (*Qaranfulu*), and its identification in the cuneiform record is uncertain and under the standing blocking warning for Assyrian substance identifications. Assyrian therapeutics classify a drug partly by the causal agency it opposes, not by any doctrine of building the person's vitality. No cultivation status is attested — (c) absent.

**De Materia Medica (DMM).** Dioscorides very likely never handled clove; it enters the Materia Medica tradition only through later Byzantine additions, and Dioscorides has no subtle-body system in any case. What the tradition offers a cultivator here is a discipline of attention — provenance, potency, and adulteration of an expensive traded aromatic — not a status for the person's constitution. (c) absent.

**Unani / Canon (CM).** Ibn Sina classes clove hot in the third degree and dry, and among the drugs that strengthen the heart. But an intensely heating, drying drug is the opposite of what conserves innate heat and radical moisture — the tradition's actual longevity idea, which is conservation of that finite endowment, not its stoking. Its cardiac/gladdening character belongs to the *mufarrih* literature (the *Kitāb al-Adwiya al-Qalbiyya*, not the *Canon*); whether that rises to a cultivation status rather than a therapeutic one is not attested in the sources retrieved. Framed; no cultivation status — (b).

**Ayurveda (SS).** *Lavaṅga* is a firmly attested *dravya* — Katu-Tikta in rasa, Ushna in virya, a supreme Deepana-Pachana (digestive-fire) spice and Hridya (cardiac). These are therapeutic actions; no *rasayana* or *vajikarana* division membership is attested for clove in the sources retrieved, so it carries no cultivation status in the tradition, only a therapeutic one — (b).

**Chinese (SBJ).** Clove (丁香) is a warming Middle-Burner and Kidney-Yang drug that descends Stomach qi — the classic remedy for cold-type hiccup. This is *zhi bing* 治病 (treating disease), not *yang sheng* 養生 (nourishing life); no *san pin* upper-grade status is recorded for it in the sources retrieved, and as a late trade arrival it does not sit among the classic long-term nourishing-life drugs. Framed; therapeutic status only — (b).

**Tibetan (FMT).** Clove (*Byi-ma-ra*) appears in the *Gyushi* as a warming ingredient and figures in the *Rinchen Rilbu* precious-pill formulas — but that is formula membership, not a cultivation status, and Tibetan medicine has no formal "substances for subtle-body practice" category in which to place it. Medicine is the third of four treatment branches; it supports practice, it does not reach the root. (c) absent.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator uses clove as a warming digestive and dental analgesic — taken for a defined cold-type complaint and then stopped, not as a longevity substance. Across all six traditions its status is therapeutic or absent; nothing attested supports taking it to build the person's vitality, and Ibn Sina's "hot in the third degree" is itself the caution against sustained use.

### Contraindications

- **Clove essential oil internal ingestion** — CRITICAL: undiluted clove oil ingested internally causes severe hepatotoxicity (eugenol liver toxicity at high doses); eugenol is safe at food/spice doses but concentrated oil is dangerous
- **Anticoagulant interaction** — eugenol has documented antiplatelet activity; caution with warfarin; discontinue clove supplements before surgery
- **Pitta-excess constitution** — intensely hot; aggravates gastritis, reflux, skin inflammation; use in moderation in Pitta types
- **Drug metabolism (eugenol inhibits CYP1A2)** — theoretical drug interaction with CYP1A2-metabolized drugs at high-dose supplementation (not relevant at culinary doses)
- **Pregnancy (concentrated preparations)** — uterotonic potential at high doses; culinary amounts safe
