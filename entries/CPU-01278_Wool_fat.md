# Wool fat (oesypum, lanolin) — Oisupon (OISUPON), "oesypum"
## *The grease of unwashed wool, scoured out of the fleece and washed; the translator's heading to Dioscorides 2-84 is "Wool Fat, Lanolin", and the chapter's own name for it is "oesypum"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Oesypum is chapter 84 of Book Two, in the section headed "FATS", of the *De Materia Medica*, headed OISUPON with the translator's gloss "SUGGESTED: Wool Fat, Lanolin" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 5280-5283, with the text on lines 5284 and 5288; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` carries it at line 274 as 2-84, line 5282, and the book's page index prints "Lanolin 212"). The chapter opens with the definition: "The greasiness of unwashed wool is called oesypum [lanolin]." The rest is a long account of how to make it, a statement of how to know the best, and its uses.

*How it is made (line 5284).* The text gives a main method and three variants. In the main method one takes "soft unwashed wool scoured with the herb soapweed [soapwort]", washes it in hot water, squeezes out the filth, throws it into a broad-mouthed jar, pours water on it from a height with a great spoon and tumbles it "forcibly until it foams", or stirs it with a stick "until a quantity of foul foam gathers together". The foam is sprinkled with seawater. When the fat that floats has settled it is moved to another ceramic jar, water is poured in, and the stirring and sprinkling are repeated "until there is no more foam on it, the greasy matter having been used up". The oesypum "gathered by hand" is cleared of any filth, the first water is let go "drop by drop", and fresh water is stirred in until the product, "if applied to the tongue", "does not bite, is somewhat astringent and it looks fat, clean and white". It is stored in a ceramic jar, and the work is to be done "while the sun is warm". The variants are three: some press out the grease and wash it in cold water, rubbing it by hand "as women do stiff ointments", and this is whiter; others wash the wool, press out the filth, boil it in a kettle over a gentle fire, take off the grease that floats, wash it as above, strain it into a ceramic platter holding warm water, cover it with linen and set it in the sun "until it becomes sufficiently thick and white"; and some, after two days, pour out the first water and pour in fresh.

*How to know the best (line 5284).* "The best is not cleaned with radicula [radish], is smooth, smells of unwashed wool, and when rubbed with cold water in a shell, grows white. It has nothing in it hard or compacted such as that counterfeited with wax ointment or animal fat." So the text gives four sensory tests (smoothness, smell, whitening when rubbed with cold water in a shell, absence of hard lumps), one rule on method (not cleaned with radish), and names two adulterants, wax ointment and animal fat.

*Uses (lines 5284 and 5288).* "It is able to warm and soften and fill ulcers, especially those around the perineum and vulva. Applied in wool with melilot [3-48] and butter it is an abortifacient and draws out the menstrual flow, (and is good) with goose grease for sores in the ears and genitals. It is also good around the corners of eyes that have been eaten into and are scabbed, and for eyelids that have grown hard and shed their hair." The translator's gloss at 3-48 is the melilot herb; goose grease has its own chapter, 2-86 (line 5296).

*Burnt oesypum (line 5288).* "Oesypum [lanolin] is burnt in a new ceramic jar until it is turned into ashes and loses all its fat. Soot is gathered from it (in the same way as we have previously described), good for eye medicines." The soot method referred to is the one given for butter at 2-81 (lines 5267-5271): the fat is burnt in a new lamp covered by a ceramic jar "narrow above but with holes underneath", more being poured in as the first is used up, and the soot is scraped off with a feather or wing. The text says there that this soot is useful in eye medicines, "to dry, and as an astringent".

*The preceding chapter.* Wool itself, with its unwashed grease still in it, is chapter 2-82 (a separate entry of this Codex), where the text says wool is "softening" "by reason of the oesypum [lanolin] that it contains" (line 5274).

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, lanolin is the wax-like secretion of the sheep's skin glands. It is not a true fat built on glycerol: it is a mixture of waxes, esters of long-chain fatty acids with sterols (including cholesterol) and other alcohols, with free alcohols and fatty acids, and it holds a great deal of water without separating. Raw wool grease also carries sweat salts and dirt, which the washing in the text is meant to remove. Nothing links any constituent to the actions the text gives.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records oesypum as a warming and softening ointment base and as a salve for ulcers around the genitals, ears and eyelids, and, mixed in wool with melilot and butter, as an abortifacient. The Codex does not recommend any use of it as a means of ending a pregnancy, which is dangerous and needs medical care, and does not recommend home-made preparations for the eyes or ears. Purified lanolin is today an ordinary ingredient of skin-care products (see Scientific Correlation), but this entry is about the ancient preparation, and the text's own account of making it describes a crude product washed in a jar.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparation is given in full in the Origin section (line 5284): scour unwashed wool with soapwort, wash in hot water, tumble in water until foam rises, collect the floating fat, and repeat until no foam forms and the product is smooth and white and, on the tongue, does not bite but is "somewhat astringent"; or one of the three variants. The burnt form is made by burning it in a new ceramic jar until it is ash, and soot for eye medicines is collected as for butter (line 5288, with 2-81, lines 5267-5271). The text gives no quantity for any use, no course, and no instruction on how long the ointment may be kept. For the abortifacient use the text gives only that it is "applied in wool with melilot [3-48] and butter"; it does not say how it is applied or in what amount. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Lanolin is a wax obtained from sheep's wool and is used in modern pharmacy and cosmetics as an emollient and as an ointment base, because it absorbs water and softens skin. It is a known cause of contact allergy in some people, and purified (medical-grade) lanolin is distinguished from crude wool grease. The traditional claim that it is an abortifacient is not supported by any established pharmacology of lanolin; attempts to end a pregnancy with substances inserted into the vagina are dangerous. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** The text prints that oesypum is "able to warm and soften and fill ulcers", and in the assay that the right product is "somewhat astringent" on the tongue and "fat, clean and white" to the eye. For the burnt product, the soot is not given any quality in 2-84; the parallel passage on butter soot calls it a dryer and an astringent (2-81, line 5271), which is not carried over to the oesypum soot.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is asserted here. The chapter is one of the fullest examples in this book of the practitioner's discipline of attention that his work does offer: it describes the making in detail, fixes the best by smell, feel and colour-change, and warns of two adulterants by name. That is a practice of provenance and assay, and it is attested for this substance. It is not a cultivation status. The chapter makes no claim about the person's constitution, vitality or long life, and the substance has only a therapeutic status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator beyond the habit the chapter teaches: know how a material is made, test it by taste, smell and look before using it, and suspect it of adulteration with hard fats and wax. The Codex does not recommend the abortifacient use. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy:** the text records oesypum as an abortifacient; the Codex does not recommend it, and any attempt to end or to affect a pregnancy needs medical care.
- **Allergy:** lanolin can cause contact allergy and skin reactions, in some people severe.
- **Eyes and ears:** do not put a home-made wool-grease preparation, ash or soot into the eyes or ears; the products of burning fat are irritant and not sterile.
- **Ulcers and genital sores:** persistent ulcers, especially of the genitals, need diagnosis.
- **Identity:** the chapter says "unwashed wool" and names no species; the translator's gloss is "Lanolin".
