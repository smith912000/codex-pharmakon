(1) Bryan's Chapter VII lists the Swallow among the animals "whose identity is not in doubt, and who were called upon to sacrifice themselves in the cause of the Healing Art" (the list at about lines 1390-1400; the introduction to it at about line 1384). (2) A remedy "against the uases abscess", which Bryan says the Scribe did not describe, so that it cannot be identified. He calls it "a stubborn eruption", and gives the second remedy: "Blood-of-a-Dove, Blood-of-a-Goose, Blood-of-a-Swallow, Blood-of-a-Vulture. Anoint therewith." (lines 2355-2357). Bryan adds his own judgement: "Just as picturesque, and assuredly as useless". (3) In his summary of the diseases of women, Bryan writes: "To protect a virgin from Leucorrheea it was necessary to anoint her breast, body, and all the limbs with a lotion prepared by crushing the dried liver of a Swallow in Sour Milk" (line 2514). This is Bryan's paraphrase, not the papyrus line itself, and the Egyptian text and column were not read. [USER: verify] (4) Bryan also uses a swallow as a figure: in the section on grey hair the papyrus (as he reads it) has "He stretches up his hand to the back of a living Falcon, placing him on his head against a living Swallow", and he explains that the Scribe "merely wishes to convey that the medicament overcomes the disease like the Falcon the Swallow" (lines 4046-4047). That is an image in a spell-like phrase, and does not make the swallow a medicine.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-60, *Chelidon*, "Hirundo rustica — Swallows" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 5173-5179; the names index row is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 250; the page number in the index is 202). The whole chapter: "Cutting apart [at the increase of the moon] young swallows of the first hatching, you shall find stones in their bellies, of which take two, one of various colours and the other clear [and of one colour]. Place these in an heifer's or hart's skin before they touch the ground and tie them to the arm or neck. You shall with this ease and often wholly recover from epilepsy." Then: "Swallows are eaten with their ficedulae [intestines] as a medicine for causing sharp sight, and the ashes of them and of their female parents burnt in a ceramic pot and rubbed on with honey cause sharpness of sight. It is also good rubbed on for those with tonsillitis, and for inflammation of the uvula and tonsils. Swallows and their young ones (dried, and one teaspoon taken in a drink with water) help those who have tonsillitis." The stones tied to the arm or neck are an amulet, and the timing "at the increase of the moon" is printed in square brackets in this translation, whose brackets mark the translator's insertions. ### Active Components

A swallow is a small insect-eating bird, and the parts named are the blood, the liver, the young whole, the ashes, and the "stones" found in the belly of the nestlings (Dioscorides says only "stones" and names no mineral). The sources name no active component and give no quantities. In general terms, bird blood and liver are protein, fat and iron-bearing tissue; burnt remains are mineral ash.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The texts record the swallow as an ingredient (blood in an ointment, liver in a lotion), as a food and ash for the sight, and as a dried powder for the throat; and as the source of two belly-stones tied on as an amulet against epilepsy. The Codex has no protocol for any of this and offers none. Wild swallows are protected birds across much of their range. The animal is killed for the use. Tonsillitis and epilepsy are medical conditions that need medical care, and neither the Egyptian nor the Greek text gives anything that can be verified or dosed today.

### Preparation & Dosing

Egyptian: the blood anointed on; the dried liver crushed in sour milk and used to anoint the body (Bryan's summary, line 2514). Greek: the stones from the nestlings of the first hatching tied in a heifer's or hart's skin to arm or neck; the birds eaten "with their ficedulae"; the ashes of the adult and the young burnt in a ceramic pot and rubbed on with honey; and the dried birds, "one teaspoon taken in a drink with water" (2-60). The teaspoon is the only dose given, and it is for the tonsillitis use. No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Wild birds can carry *Salmonella* and other infections, and ectoparasites, and handling and eating them without cooking is a risk. Dried or burnt remains are not known to treat sore throat, epilepsy or poor sight. Vitamin A deficiency is a known cause of night blindness, and animal liver is a source of vitamin A; but the sources read make no such claim, and nothing here links swallow liver to sight. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The swallow is named as an ingredient for a complaint (the blood for the "uases" abscess, the liver in sour milk in Bryan's account of a lotion against leucorrhoea), and as a figure in an image about a falcon. Nothing says what it does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's vital constitution. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); the falcon-and-swallow phrase looks like a spoken or written image, but Bryan summarises, and it is not clear that it was a recitation. No deity is attributed; none is claimed. [USER: verify]

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. The chapter records an amuletic use (the belly-stones tied to the arm or neck against epilepsy) and the lunar timing. Recording is not endorsing, and the Codex does not adopt the cure, although this chapter states it flatly. What the chapter gives a cultivator is a discipline of attention in preparation: young birds "of the first hatching", the adult and young burnt together in a ceramic pot, ashes with honey, and the teaspoon dose with water. Nothing in 2-60 assigns the swallow a cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do. The swallow is a sourced entry because two ancient texts used the bird, and both are therapeutic or amuletic, not cultivation texts. A cultivator can take from it the observation that one text uses the bird's blood and the other its belly-stones and ashes, and leave the bird alone.

### Contraindications

- **Disease.** Wild birds carry *Salmonella*, avian infections and parasites; do not handle or eat them raw.
- **Epilepsy.** Wearing stones from a bird is no treatment; do not stop or delay medical care for seizures.
- **Throat infection.** Tonsillitis with difficulty breathing or swallowing needs medical care.
- **Dose unknown.** No dose is given for most uses in the sources read.
- **Ethics and law.** Using the animal means killing it; swallows are protected in the law of many countries, and taking the birds, their nests or eggs may be an offence. [USER: verify]
