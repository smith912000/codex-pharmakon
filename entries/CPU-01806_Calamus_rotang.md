# Calamus rotang — Nichula, Vetasa, Vanjula (Sushruta Samhita, Bhishagratna's Appendix No. 3, which prints "Calamus rotang" and "Calamus Rotong")

## *Calamus rotang, the Latin that Bhishagratna's Appendix No. 3 ("Names of Drugs, with Latin synonyms", Vol. III) prints against the Sanskrit names Nichula (line 32440, "Calamus rotang") and Vetasa (line 33301, "Calamus Rotong"), and, by the Codex's own count, against Vanjula (line 33252, "Calamus Rotong"). The pairing of each Sanskrit name with its Latin line is positional, proposed by an agent from two OCR columns and re-checked here by counting; it is not printed on one line, and three Sanskrit names stand against one Latin name* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Sushruta text (SS); this entry rests on one text, Bhishagratna's English translation of the Sushruta Samhita, and on a Latin name that comes from the translator's appendix and not from the chapters.** The chapters are in `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/` and the appendix is in `Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`. The chapters name the drug only by Sanskrit names. A search of the Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Tibetan text folders on disk for *Calamus rotang* and *rotang* found no hit. The Dioscorides text prints "Calamus rotang — Rattan Cane, Chair-bottom Cane" once, in the translator's SUGGESTED lines for chapter 1-114 (*Kalamos nastos, suriggios, donax, phragmites*; line 4385), a chapter on reeds that is not read as this plant and has no entry in the Codex. The Dioscorides text also prints "Calamus ciliaris — Indian Palm" (line 3696) in the translator's SUGGESTED lines for chapter 1-17, and "Calamus aromaticus" (line 3698) in that chapter's text, which the translator glosses as the sweet flag; neither is read as this plant. The row was added from the printed Latin (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP, confidence "medium") and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. **Identity doubt.** Three Sanskrit names stand against one Latin name, and the chapters do not clearly treat them as one drug. In the antivenom compound of Kalpa 7 (`SS_Kalpa_ch07.txt`, lines 24 to 26) the list reads "... Varuna, Lavana, Plaksha, Nichula, Vardhamana, Vanjula, Putra-shreni, ...", with *Nichula* and *Vanjula* listed as separate ingredients. On the other side, Bhishagratna glosses *Nichula* in Chikitsa 37 (line 223) as "Nichula ( Jala-Vetasa )", a link to *Vetasa* that is the translator's. In Sutra 46 (line 1245) *Nichula* is named in a list of fruits ("Vatama, Akshoda, Abhishuka, Nichula, Pichu, Nikochaka, and Urumana") that does not read like the fruit of a cane. The Sushruta chapters give no Latin name, and the appendix's Latin is a translator's identification, from 1907 to 1916, that the Codex has not checked. The entry therefore stands for the three Sanskrit names under the appendix's Latin, and does not say that Sushruta meant one plant by all of them. [USER: verify]

**Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *Vetasa in the chapters.* Sutra 8, on the surgical instruments (line 67 to 68): "The Vetasapatram (knife) resembles the leaf of a Vetasa plant." Chikitsa 1, in a recipe to restore the natural skin colour of a cicatrix (lines 797 to 800): "the powder of a new earthen pot, Vetasa roots, Shala roots, Sulphate of iron, and Madhuka (Yashti-madhu) pasted together with honey may be used." Chikitsa 18, on glandular swellings (*Granthi*), for the Pittaja type (lines 80 to 99): "Hot plasters, prepared by pasting together the bark of the Madhuka (flower) tree, and of the Jambu tree, Arjuna tree, and Vetasa creeper" (the word "creeper" is Bhishagratna's). Uttara 10 (lines 22 to 32), for Pittaja ophthalmia: clarified butter or goat's milk "duly cooked with" a long list that includes "Tala, Vetasa", to be used "as a Tarpana measure or as a wash or snuff". Uttara 26 (lines 62 to 70), for the Pittaja and Raktaja head diseases: a plaster "prepared with Nala, Vetasa, Kahlara (red lotus), Chandana, Utpala, ...". Uttara 30 (lines 8 to 12): the body of a child "possessed by Sakuni" is sprinkled with "the decoction (duly prepared) of Vetasa, Amra and Kapittha". Uttara 39 (lines 1156 to 1161), in a fever of the three Doshas: "A potion of the decoction duly prepared with the roots of Nala and of Vetasa (cane) and Murva and Devadaru would prove remedial". Uttara 40 (lines 472 to 479), for diarrhoea: "Barks of Badari, Arjuna, Jambu, Amra, Shallaki and Vetasa taken with honey and sugar would cure a case of Atisara." Uttara 47 (lines 188 to 192), for the Kaphaja type of *Pandu*: wine "mixed with the expressed juice or decoction of Vimibi and Vidula (Vetasa)". ("Amla-vetasa" is a different, sour drug, and "Vetasapatram" is the knife.)

*Vanjula in the chapters.* Sutra 38, the Nyagrodhadi group (lines 344 to 351): "Nyagrodha, Audumvara, Ashvattha, Plaksha, Madhuka, Kapitana, Kakubha, Amra, Koshamra, Chorakapatra, the two sorts of Jamvu, Piyala, Madhuka (Maula), Rohini, Vanjula, Kadamva, Vadari, Tinduki, Sallaki, Rodhra, Savara-Rodhra, Bhallataka, Palasha, and Nandi-Vriksha, combinedly form the group". The group "proves beneficial in cases of ulcer, cures all disorders of the uterus and vagina, favours the adhesion of fractured bones and all sorts of secretions of the body in addition to its astringent properties (Sangrahi) and proves curative in a case of haemoptysis. It is an anti-fat and assuages the burning sensation of the body" (lines 352 to 360). Chikitsa 5, the chapter on the great Vata disorders (lines 203 to 208): a plaster of "Shali, Shashtika, Nala, Vanjula, Talisha, Shrigataka, Galodya, Haridra, Gairika, Shaivala, ..." pasted with *Dhanyamla* and clarified butter, which "may be applied lukewarm even in cases of Vata-Rakta". Kalpa 8, on insect bites (lines 630 to 634): an *Agada* of "Chandana, Rasna, Ela, Harenu, Nala, Vanjula, Kushtha, Lamajjaka, Chakra and Nalada" for the bite of a white spider.

*Nichula in the chapters.* Sutra 46, on fruits (lines 1245 to 1249, page 505): "Fruits such as the Vatama, Akshoda, Abhishuka, Nichula, Pichu, Nikochaka, and Urumana, etc. are demulcent, heat-making in their potency, heavy of digestion, constructive, tonic, and sweet in taste. They subdue the Vayu, Pittam and Kapham." ("heat-making" is the Codex's reading of the OCR's "heat-ifiaking".) Chikitsa 11, a Prameha chapter (lines 194 to 202): "similar preparations" of a long list that includes "Nichula". Chikitsa 12, on the abscesses that follow a Prameha (lines 60 to 73): the *Dhanvantara-Ghrita*, cooked with a Prastha of clarified butter and "half a Pala weight of each" of "Vacha, Trivrit, Kampilla, Bhargi, Nichula, Shunthi, Gaja-Pippali, Vidanga and Shirisha as Kalka". Chikitsa 16, on abscesses and tumours (lines 94 to 106): the *Karanjadya Ghrita*, "A Karsha measure of each" of a list that includes "Nichula-bark" (so printed), said to "cure malignant ulcers (Dushta-Vrana) and act as a purifier in sinus and recent ulcers". Chikitsa 19, on hernia and hydrocele (lines 220 to 226): "plasters composed of Nichula, castor-seeds, and pulverised wheat and barley grains pasted with Sneha" applied lukewarm to the seat of the disease. Chikitsa 22, on mouth diseases (lines 122 to 126): "Tepid watery solutions of Pippali, (white) Sarshapa, Nagara, and Nichula fruits should also be used as gurgles". Chikitsa 37, on the *Anuvasana* and *Uttara* enemas (lines 223 to 229): "Nichula ( Jala-Vetasa ), Nagara, Shatahva, Nilini, Rasna, Kadali, Vasaka, Renu, Vilva, Ajamoda, Pippali, Danti, Chavya, Naradhipa (Aragvadha) with the decoction of the drugs of the Mushkakadi group, should be cooked with an adequate quantity of sesamum or castor oil." Kalpa 7 (lines 24 to 26, quoted above) and Kalpa 8 (line 305) also name it in antidote lists, which the Codex has not read further.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Calamus* is a genus of climbing, spiny palms of the palm family (Arecaceae) whose long stems are the rattan canes of commerce, and that it is a different plant from the sweet flag, *Acorus calamus* (Acoraceae), which the Codex's I_047 treats. Which compounds the Sushruta drugs held is not shown by the lines read, and nothing is stated here about them. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Sushruta text records *Vetasa*, *Vanjula* and *Nichula* as ingredients in plasters, ghritas, decoctions, enemas and gargles for ulcers, swellings, fever, diarrhoea and other complaints, and in one case (*Nichula*) as a fruit listed with sweet, heavy, "tonic" fruits. *Vanjula* belongs to the astringent Nyagrodhadi group. These are the text's claims about three Sanskrit names, and the Latin name that the appendix attaches to them is the translator's. They are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and no cane should be gathered or used on the strength of a Latin name that the text does not itself give.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these preparations, each with its locus: *Vetasa* roots with other substances in a honey paste (Chikitsa 1, lines 797 to 800); bark in a hot plaster (Chikitsa 18, line 96); barks "taken with honey and sugar" (Uttara 40, lines 476 to 478); a root decoction as a potion (Uttara 39, lines 1156 to 1161); a decoction for sprinkling (Uttara 30); a plaster for the head (Uttara 26); a wine with the expressed juice or decoction (Uttara 47); *Vanjula* in a lukewarm plaster (Chikitsa 5, lines 203 to 208); *Nichula* in the *Dhanvantara-Ghrita*, "half a Pala weight of each" of the Kalka drugs to a Prastha of clarified butter (Chikitsa 12, lines 70 to 73); in the *Karanjadya Ghrita*, "A Karsha measure of each" of the drugs to a Prastha of clarified butter (Chikitsa 16, lines 94 to 102); in a lukewarm plaster (Chikitsa 19); as a gargle of its fruits (Chikitsa 22); and in an enema oil (Chikitsa 37). These measures are quantities in compound ghritas, not doses of the drug. No dose of any of the three drugs by itself is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SS:** Sutra 38 places *Vanjula* in the Nyagrodhadi group, described with astringent (*Sangrahi*), ulcer-healing, bone-uniting, haemoptysis-curing, "anti-fat" and burn-soothing properties (lines 344 to 360). Sutra 46 (lines 1245 to 1249) prints for the fruits that include *Nichula*: demulcent, heat-making in potency, heavy of digestion, constructive, tonic, sweet in taste, subduing Vayu, Pitta and Kapha. The chapters read print no individual *virya* or *vipaka* for *Vetasa*. The group-level statements are the text's, and the Codex does not attach them to a particular Latin plant.

### Cultivation Register

**Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* *Vetasa*, *Vanjula* and *Nichula* are named in the chapters read as ingredients of plasters, ghritas, decoctions, enemas, gargles and antidotes (Uttara, Chikitsa and Kalpa), as a member of the astringent Nyagrodhadi group (*Vanjula*, Sutra 38), and, for *Nichula*, in a list of fruits called "constructive, tonic" in the food chapter (Sutra 46, line 1246). The word "tonic" there is Bhishagratna's rendering in a dietetic chapter and is not an assignment to the rasayana or vajikarana disciplines. A search of the chapter files for the three names found no hit in the rasayana or vajikarana chapters (Chikitsa 26 to 30), and none of the lines read says anything of *ojas*, *tejas* or *prana* for any of them. The frame is therapeutic and dietetic. The sattvic-rajasic-tamasic food lists are Samkhya and later material and are not Sushruta's, so none is imported.

**Practitioner's note.** In the Sushruta scheme these are working drugs of the physician's recipes, used externally and internally for defined complaints, with no rejuvenation or vitality status in the chapters read. A cultivator does nothing special with them. The Latin identification is the translator's appendix pairing across three Sanskrit names, and the chapters do not confirm it.

### Contraindications

The Sushruta passages read print no warning for these drugs. Ulcers, fever, diarrhoea, jaundiced or pallid conditions (*Pandu*), hernia and mouth disease need medical care. The identity is a translator's pairing across three names, so no cane should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Rattan palms are spiny, and handling the canes can wound. The sweet flag, which the appendix prints as "Acorus Calamus" against *Vacha*, is a different plant with its own safety concerns (see I_047). No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
