# Dog tongue — "Dog tongue" under "Meat" (Clark's English); "dog tongue" (Men-Tsee-Khang's English)
## *The tongue of the dog as a remedy in the Tibetan Explanatory Tantra. Clark's translation prints "Dog tongue" with no Tibetan headword and no binomial; the Men-Tsee-Khang English prints "Dog tongue"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The text on disk is Barry Clark's 1995 *The Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). Chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (line 792), opens by saying that "The power of medicines is twofold: (a) the power of taste and (b) the natural power [of the substance]" (line 794). Its Specific Section (line 801) sets out the individual substances in eight kinds, the eighth being animal products. Inside the animal section Clark's layout has sub-headings (Bone, Meat, Blood, Bile, Fat, Brain, Skin, Nails, Hair, Urine, Droppings). The tongue is in the run of organs and meats under "Meat" (line 1170):

"Wolf's stomach: [has twelve names] It increases digestive heat and digests undigested food, [especially meat]. Its tongue removes swellings of the tongue. Pig tongue: removes bone warts. Dog tongue: [has ten names and] heals all types of wounds. Donkey tongue: [has fourteen names]. It halts diarrhoea."

So for the dog's tongue Clark prints two things: that it "has ten names" and that it "heals all types of wounds". Clark says in his introduction that interpolations in square brackets are taken from two later commentaries, the Blue Aquamarine and the Spreading Tree (line 82), so the count of ten names is an interpolation from commentary and not part of the root line; the wound action is outside brackets. The English prints no Tibetan headword for the dog's tongue and no binomial, and the names themselves are not given in the lines read.

The second English text on disk, the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation of the Root and Explanatory Tantras (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`), gives the same line (line 3536): "Wolf tongue reduces swelling of the tongue. Pig tongue removes fragmented bones from comminuted fractures. Dog tongue heals wounds. Donkey tongue cures diarrhea." The two translations agree on the action. Clark's "all types of wounds" has "all types of", which the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence does not; and the Men-Tsee-Khang rendering of the pig's tongue differs from Clark's "bone warts". I note the difference and do not resolve it.

Clark's introduction says that "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57). The Men-Tsee-Khang preface says that Tibetan medicine "is deeply rooted in Buddhist philosophy and Buddhism strictly denounces causing harm to any being", that "the use of animal products was negligible", and that animal products "have been completely substituted by equally effective herbal substances", their potencies being given in the translation "to preserve the understanding of the therapeutic values of animal products" (line 687).

Other dog materials in the same chapter, which I read: dog testicle, "is ecbolic" (line 1170; see III_75), and dog stool, "[is especially beneficial against spirits]" (line 1199; see III_74).

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms the tongue is skeletal muscle covered by a mucous membrane, made mostly of protein with some fat, and carrying the bacteria normal to a dog's mouth. Nothing more specific can be said without a source.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan text records the dog's tongue as healing wounds. The Codex does not recommend applying or eating a dog's tongue. Wound care is a matter of cleaning, closing and dressing the wound and, where needed, medical treatment; an animal tissue is an infection risk, not a dressing. The tradition's own translators say that animal products have largely been replaced by herbal substances.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives no preparation for the dog's tongue. It does not say whether the tongue is eaten, dried, burnt, powdered or applied to the wound, and the lines read give no quantity. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. No animal tissue is an established wound treatment. Open wounds are cleaned, closed or dressed, and treated for tetanus and infection where needed. Dogs' mouths carry bacteria that can cause serious infection, and dogs can carry zoonotic disease, so raw or poorly prepared dog tissue is an infection risk. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** None printed for this item. Chapter 20 says that medicines act by taste and by natural power, and lists eight powers (heavy, oily, cool, blunt, light, coarse, hot, sharp; Clark line 796). The dog-tongue entry (line 1170) prints no taste, power or post-digestive taste, and neither does the Men-Tsee-Khang sentence (line 3536). Clark's introduction says that the later *Crystal Orb* and *Crystal Rosary* give each medicine's tastes, potencies and qualities, but that text is not on disk, so none is supplied here.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for the dog's tongue in the lines read. Chapter 20 gives it one therapeutic action, and says nothing about essence extraction (*bcud len*) or any other practice. The Men-Tsee-Khang preface records a Buddhist objection to causing harm to any being as the reason animal products have been replaced by herbs (line 687); that is the tradition's own comment on animal products in general, and not a cultivation status for this one. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The dog's tongue is a short clause in a list of organs and meats, and its tradition describes animal products as largely replaced. The honest use of the entry is as a record. ### Contraindications

- **Wounds:** do not apply animal tissue to a wound; seek ordinary wound care and, for bites or deep or dirty wounds, medical review.
- **Infection and hygiene:** raw or poorly prepared animal tissue is an infection risk, and dogs can carry zoonotic disease, including rabies in areas where it occurs.
- **Ethics and law:** the Codex does not recommend harming any animal for these uses, and the Tibetan translators themselves record a Buddhist objection to harming any being; in many places the slaughter and consumption of dogs is restricted or prohibited.
