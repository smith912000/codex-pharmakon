# Elm — dardār (دردار), "the tree of bugs" (shajarat al-baqq)

## *Elm,* Ulmus *(the spine's note: Ulmus campestris is a synonym); the identification is the modern one, made from Avicenna's quotation of Dioscorides (a tree like the willow whose swollen pods hold a moisture that turns into bug-like insects) and the Arabic name* dardār*, and is of fairly high confidence at genus level* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** CM, SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

The elm is named only by Avicenna's *Canon* among the texts on disk, in two places: the main entry *dardār* (Book II, Arabic text line 12046) and a short cross-reference under the letter *rāʾ*, *shajarat al-baqq* (line 18398), which says only: "it was said of it in the chapter of the letter *dāl*, at our mention of *dardār*, and it is the tree of the bug" (قيل فيه في فصل الدال عند ذكرنا دردار وهي شجرة البق). It is a single-text entry.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** The description is a quotation of Dioscorides, named at the start of the entry (line 12047): "Dioscorides said: it is a tree like the willow (*khilāf*), and the people of Syria call it *dardār*, and the people of Iraq call it the tree of the bug (*shajarat al-baqq*); there come out of it swollen pods like the pomegranate, in which there is a moisture that becomes bugs (*baqq*), and when they burst the bugs come out; likewise the moisture found in the husks of the tree, when it dries, produces an animal like the bug; and what is of the leaf of this tree is eaten green when cooked" (lines 12047-12051). The "pods" with insect-producing moisture are insect galls on the leaves of elm trees, as a modern botanist would read the account. This reading is the modern one. ### Active Components

Elm bark and leaves contain tannins and mucilage, which accounts generally for the astringency recorded by Avicenna and for the later European use of the inner bark of the slippery elm (a different species, *Ulmus rubra*, of North America). General botany. The *Canon* names no constituent.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The *Canon*'s uses (lines 12051-12060):

- **Actions.** "In it is binding and cleansing; the bark is binding, and the root is near it" (فيه قبض وجلاء والقشر قابض والأصل قريب منه).
- **Cosmetic.** The moisture of its pods cleans the face; its bark, with vinegar, while still fresh, cleans the sight (lines 12052-12053).
- **Wounds.** The bark wound round as a bandage on blows and wounds heals them; leaf, bark and flower-buds are good for wounds, and so is the powder that scatters from the bark, "which prevent the spread of the malignant (ulcer)", especially mixed with anise and made into a paste with the *maṭbūkh* (a boiled drink, probably boiled wine) (lines 12053-12056).
- **Broken bones.** A decoction of the root and leaf is poured over broken bones (lines 12056-12057).
- **Excretion.** "A mithqāl of the thick bark drunk with the boiled (wine) or cold water expels phlegm" (lines 12059-12060).

Not offered as a recommended practice.

### Preparation & Dosing

The one quantity: "a mithqāl of the thick bark, drunk with the *maṭbūkh* (boiled wine) or cold water" (line 12059-12060). Forms: bark as a bandage; decoction of root and leaf as a wash for broken bones; powder of the bark with anise made into a paste with the *maṭbūkh* (lines 12053-12057). No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Tannin-rich barks are astringent, and this is the general basis of their use on minor wounds. Elm trees are also the host of the Dutch elm disease fungus, which is of no medical relevance. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent.* The entry is a Dioscoridean description followed by astringent and wound uses. It says nothing of the innate heat, the radical moisture or the *ruh*, and gives no cultivation status.

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested.* The Chinese drug is 榆皮 (*yu pi*, "elm bark"), usually identified as the bark of *Ulmus pumila*, a congener of this entry's *U. minor*; the Shennong text does not speak of *U. minor*. Its 味 paragraph reads 味甘，平, "sweet in flavour, neutral", and "governs blockage of urine and stool, frees the water passages, and removes evil qi" (主大小便不通，利水道，除邪气). The life-nourishing clause is 久服，轻身、不饥, "taken for a long time: the body is lightened, and one is not hungry". It adds 其实尤良, "its fruit is especially good", and the alias 一名零榆. It stands in the upper classic; the preface says the upper grade is for nourishing life, corresponds to heaven, is non-toxic and may be taken for a long time. (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, upper classic, entry 榆皮 *yu pi*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 1199).

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing is asked of a cultivator. The practice in the entry is plain: a tree is known by its galls, its bark is used for dressing wounds, and the physician records the Syrian and Iraqi names side by side. The entry shows that a single tree was taken by Avicenna from Dioscorides with the regional names attached.

### Contraindications

- **Identification.** Elms are variable and hybridise; the species of Avicenna's drug is not stated.
- **Wine.** Two of the preparations use *maṭbūkh* (probably boiled wine); avoid if alcohol is to be avoided.
- **Open wounds.** The bark bandage is historical record only; unclean plant material on a wound risks infection and tetanus. Use clean dressings and seek care for deep wounds.
- **Conservation.** Many elms have been lost to Dutch elm disease; take no bark from living trees.
