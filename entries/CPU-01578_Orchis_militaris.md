# Orchis militaris — Orchis, also called cynosorchis, the dog's stone (Dioscorides 3-141)

## *Orchis militaris [Linnaeus], the last of the names on the first group of the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-141 (which also prints Orchis mas latifolia [Fuchs, Brunfels] and Cynosorchis latifolia [Bauhin] — "Military Orchid, Soldier Orchid"; with the other usage Orchis undulata — "Wavy-leaved Orchis", Orchis longibracteta — "Sicilian Orchis", and Herminum monorchis, Ophrys monorchis — "Musk Orchis")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED block (lines 7762 to 7764) gives one group of three names for the "Military Orchid, Soldier Orchid" and then an "other usage" of three more plants of other species and genera: *Orchis undulata*, *Orchis longibracteta*, and *Herminum monorchis* (also *Ophrys monorchis*). The Codex does not choose among them. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-141. ORCHIS*, heading at line 7761, text at line 7765, continuing after a page break (the translators' page 520) at line 7769, in Book Three among the roots of the *akanthoda*. The whole chapter reads: "Orchis (also called cynosorchis) has leaves scattered on the earth around the stalk, and the bottom of it is similar to an olive — tender but narrower, smooth and longer; a stalk the height of twenty centimetres on which are flowers of a purple hue. The root is bulbous, somewhat long, narrow like the olive, double, one part above, the other beneath, one full but the other soft and full of wrinkles. The root is eaten (boiled) like bulbus [2-200, 2-201]. It is said that if the bigger root is eaten by men, it makes their offspring males, and the lesser eaten by women makes them conceive females. It is further related that women in Thessalia give it to drink with goat's milk. The tenderer root is given to encourage venereal diseases, and the dry root to suppress and dissolve venereal diseases. In a decoction (taken as a drink) the one dissolves the other. It grows in stony, sandy places."

The chapter therefore gives a description for recognition (leaves scattered on the ground, olive-like at the base and "tender but narrower, smooth and longer"; a stalk of twenty centimetres with purple flowers; a double, narrow, olive-like root, one part full and one soft and wrinkled), a habitat ("stony, sandy places"), and four statements of use. The root is a food, "eaten (boiled) like bulbus", the cross-reference being to the two *bulbus* chapters 2-200 and 2-201. The sex-of-offspring statement is introduced by "It is said", the Thessalian milk drink by "It is further related", and both are the text's reports, recorded here and not endorsed. The last two statements set the "tenderer" root against the "dry" one, the first "to encourage venereal diseases" and the second "to suppress and dissolve" them, so that "in a decoction ... the one dissolves the other". "Venereal diseases" is the translators' English and is quoted as printed. The chapter gives the Greek alternative name *cynosorchis*, and the next chapter, 3-142, refers back to it as "the dog's stone [cyanosorchis 3-141]".

**The chapters around it.** 3-140 and the others before it are not read into this row. The chapters after it are separate plants: 3-142 *ORCHIS ETEROS* (line 7770), the translators' green-winged orchid, whose root applied "is able to dissipate oedema, clean ulcers, and repress herpes" and which the text says carries "a similar story ... as there is of the dog's stone"; 3-143 *SATURION* (line 7775), whose root is drunk in wine for spasms; and 3-144 *SATURION ERUTHRONION* (line 7784), whose root is "said ... an aphrodisiac, like scincus". The Codex does not carry any statement of those chapters over to 3-141.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter id 3-141 and the names *orchis* and *cynosorchis* found no existing entry on this chapter. I_1140 (*Orchis bifolia*, chapter 3-143) summarises 3-141 as a neighbour, and I_1029 (*Bryophyllum calycinum*) quotes 3-141's sex-of-offspring sentence as a comparison in its paragraph on neighbouring chapters (line 20 of that file). Neither treats 3-141 as its subject. No entry is duplicated.

### Active Components

The composition of the root Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, the terrestrial orchids (Orchidaceae) of Europe bear underground tubers, and orchid tubers are known to be rich in a mucilaginous starch-like carbohydrate. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a boiled root food, a root said to determine the sex of a child, a milk drink given by Thessalian women, and a pair of roots that act against each other. The Codex gives no protocol for any of them. The sex-of-offspring statement is a report the text passes on with "it is said", and no plant is known to determine the sex of a child. The identification is the translators', among several plants. Many wild orchids are protected, and their tubers are not to be dug on the strength of this chapter.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives three preparations: the root "eaten (boiled) like bulbus"; the root "given to drink with goat's milk" (by the women of Thessalia, as related); and "a decoction (taken as a drink)". It gives no quantity for any of them. Only the size of the root is said to matter, the "bigger" root for men and the "lesser" for women. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that the sex of a child is not determined by anything the parents eat. The identification is a translators' suggestion among several plants, so the chapter does not show that the plant it describes is *Orchis militaris*.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no taste, and no warming, cooling, drying, moistening or astringent quality for 3-141. It sets a "tenderer" root against a "dry" one by their texture and by their action as described above.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant (leaves scattered on the earth, a twenty-centimetre stalk, purple flowers, a double olive-like root of which one half is full and the other wrinkled) and where it grows (stony, sandy places). The reports on offspring and on the Thessalian drink are given with "it is said" and "it is further related", and recording is not endorsing. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a food root and a root of report. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a boiled root with a reputation that Dioscorides passes on at a distance. The useful lesson is his habit of telling a root by its two halves, one full and one wrinkled, and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Many wild orchids are protected by law in many places and their tubers must not be dug; the particular status was not researched. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion that includes other species and genera, so no wild plant should be gathered, eaten or used on the strength of this entry. Treating the sex of a child as something a root can decide has no basis. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
