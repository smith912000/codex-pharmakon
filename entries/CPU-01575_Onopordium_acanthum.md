# Onopordium acanthum — Akantha leuke, the white thistle (Dioscorides 3-14)

## *Onopordium acanthum [Linnaeus], the second of the two names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-14 (which also prints Spina alba sylvestris [Fuchs]); "Cotton Thistle, Scotch Thistle"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (lines 6650 and 6651) reads "Spina alba sylvestris [Fuchs], Onopordium acanthum [Linnaeus] — Cotton Thistle, Scotch Thistle". The first name is a Renaissance herbal name (Fuchs) and the second a Linnaean binomial. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-14. AKANTHA LEUKE*, heading at line 6649, in Book Three among the roots of the *akanthoda*, the prickly plants. The whole chapter (line 6652) reads: "Acantha leuke grows on mountains and in woody places. It has leaves similar to white chamaeleon [3-10] but narrower, whiter, somewhat rough and prickly. The stalk is over two feet (high), the thickness of the great finger or rather more, a pale white, empty within. On the top of it there is a prickly head similar to a sea urchin, but smaller and somewhat long. The flowers are purple, in which is the seed like that of cnicus [4-119, 4-190] but rounder. The root (taken in a drink) is good for bloodspitters, gastritis, and the abdominal cavity, and it encourages urine. It is laid on oedema, and a decoction of this as a mouth rinse is good for toothache. The seed (taken in a drink) helps convulsed children, and those bitten by snakes. They say that worn as an amulet (by itself) it drives away poisonous creatures. It is also called wild cinara, donacitis, or erysisceptron, and the Romans call it spina regia, or carduus."

Dioscorides describes the plant by its habitat (mountains and woody places), its leaf, its stalk (over two feet, pale white, hollow), its prickly head "similar to a sea urchin", its purple flowers and its seed. The uses are given by part. The root, in a drink, is for those who spit blood, for "gastritis" and "the abdominal cavity", and it "encourages urine"; laid on, it is for oedema; and a decoction as a mouth rinse is for toothache. The seed, in a drink, is for convulsed children and for people bitten by snakes. The last statement is an amulet: "They say that worn as an amulet (by itself) it drives away poisonous creatures." Dioscorides reports this with "They say" and does not put it in his own voice. The Codex records it as the text's report. The chapter prints four other names as names only: *wild cinara*, *donacitis*, *erysisceptron*, and the Roman *spina regia* or *carduus*.

**The chapters it points to.** 3-10 *CHAMAILEON LEUKOS* (heading at line 6622), the white chameleon thistle ("Carlina acaulis ... Stemless Carline Thistle"), is the plant whose leaves are compared with this one's; the chapter 3-10 also says its seed is "similar to cnicus [4-119, 4-190]", the same comparison as here. 4-190 *KNIKOS* (line 9723) is the translators' safflower, "Carthamus tinctorius", whose "seed is white, reddish, somewhat long, and angular" and which is "bad for the stomach". 4-119 *KIRSION* (line 9089) is the translators' bugloss. So the seed comparison leads to two different plants, and the Codex does not read either as this one.

**The next chapter.** 3-15 *AKANTHA ARABIKE* (line 6653), "Acanthus spinosus — Oyster Plant", "seems similar in nature to the white thistle — astringent, good for excessive [menstrual] discharges of women, the throwing-up of blood and other discharges — the root being similarly effective". The word "astringent" is printed there for the Arabian thistle, not for 3-14. Of the two, only the "throwing-up of blood" use is shared.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder found entry I_159 (*Silybum marianum*, milk thistle), which cites "*akantha leuke*" as Dioscorides' name for milk thistle and attributes to the chapter a seed decoction "good for those afflicted with jaundice". The translators of the edition on disk do not identify 3-14 with milk thistle (their milk thistle is 4-159 *SILUBON*, line 9472). The chapter 3-14 as printed has no jaundice use, and 4-159 has none either (it gives the newly sprung-up plant as a boiled vegetable and the root juice as an emetic). The words quoted in I_159 were not found in the text on disk. This is flagged for the user and nothing in I_159 has been changed. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The composition of the plant Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, *Onopordum* is a genus of tall, spiny, thistle-like plants in the daisy family (Asteraceae). [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a tall white-leaved thistle whose root in a drink serves those who spit blood and the abdomen and encourages urine, whose root is laid on swellings and used as a mouth rinse for toothache, whose seed in a drink helps convulsed children and snakebite, and which people said drove away poisonous creatures when worn as an amulet. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol for any of them. The identification is the translators'.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives these preparations: the root "taken in a drink"; the root "laid on" oedema; "a decoction of this as a mouth rinse"; the seed "taken in a drink"; and the plant "worn as an amulet (by itself)". It gives no quantity for any of them. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The identification is a translators' suggestion, and other chapters of the same book (3-10, 3-15, 4-159) treat related prickly plants under related names, so a modern fact about a single thistle would not by itself bear on the remedies recorded. It is a general, well-established fact that blood in the sputum, convulsions in a child and snakebite need urgent medical care.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no warming, cooling, drying, moistening or astringent quality and no taste for 3-14. The comparison in 3-15 ("astringent") belongs to the Arabian thistle.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant (mountain and woody ground, leaves like white chameleon but narrower, whiter and rougher, a hollow pale stalk of two feet, a head like a small sea urchin, purple flowers, seed like cnicus but rounder) and which part is used for which purpose. He records an amulet use with "They say", and recording is not endorsing. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a drug and an amulet. The identification is the translators'. **Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a prickly thistle with drug uses by part and a reported amulet, with no stated quantity. The useful lesson is Dioscorides' method of naming the part used for each purpose, and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Spitting blood, convulsions in children and snakebite are medical emergencies, and a home preparation must not replace care. A prickly thistle must be handled with care. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion that the text itself does not confirm, and other chapters treat related thistles, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
