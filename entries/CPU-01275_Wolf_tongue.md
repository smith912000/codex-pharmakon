# Wolf tongue — "Its tongue" after sbyang.kihi.pho.wa, "Wolf's stomach" (Clark 1995); "Wolf tongue" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *The tongue of the wolf; Clark prints no separate Tibetan name for it (the Tibetan name in the line belongs to the stomach, and the tongue is introduced as "Its tongue") and gives no binomial, and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary pairs "Wolf" with "Canis Lupus" in an OCR-split list* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The Explanatory Tantra (*bshad rgyud*), chapter 20, on the powers of medicines, has a section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin", which opens: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (Clark 1995, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` lines 1147-1148). The wolf's tongue stands under the sub-heading "Meat" (line 1161), in the run of organ entries. Clark's line 1170 reads: "sbyang.kihi.pho.wa: Wolf's stomach: [has twelve names] It increases digestive heat and digests undigested food, [especially meat]. Its tongue removes swellings of the tongue. Pig tongue: removes bone warts. Dog tongue: [has ten names and] heals all types of wounds. Donkey tongue: [has fourteen names]. It halts diarrhoea." The tongue is therefore named as a second part of the same animal, introduced by the possessive "Its", and the Tibetan name printed in the line is that of the wolf's stomach. The sentence reads, with the bracketed interpolations from the commentaries removed (Clark's preface, line 82, explains the brackets), "Its tongue removes swellings of the tongue." The action printed is one: it removes swellings of the tongue.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` lines 3535-3536) reads: "Wolf stomach generates digestive heat and helps the digestion of all undigested food particles. Wolf tongue reduces swelling of the tongue." The two translations agree on the substance and on the single action, the reduction of swelling of the tongue. The neighbouring tongue entries show that the two translators do not always agree: Clark's pig tongue "removes bone warts", while the Men-Tsee-Khang text says "Pig tongue removes fragmented bones from comminuted fractures" (line 3536), so the sentence for the pig tongue is read two ways in English. For the wolf tongue they agree.

The name list `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` has a row for "Wolf tongue" (line 351; no binomial; marked as an identification that is uncertain; source "Explanatory Tantra ch.20 (Efficacy of Medicinal Substances)"). That row rests on this chapter and gives no further locus.

The wolf is placed in the Tantra's classification of meats among the carnivorous animals: "Carnivorous animals include the tiger, leopard, bear (*Selenarctos thibetanus*), snow bear (*Ursus arctos*), snow leopard (*Panthera pardus*), wolf, lynx, fox, hill dog and weasel" (Clark line 672, group (e)). The Men-Tsee-Khang text lists "the tiger, leopard, black bear, Tibetan brown bear, snow leopard, wolf, lynx, fox, dhole (Tibetan wild-dog) and monkey" as "the group of carnivorous wild animals" (line 2912). Its glossary of animal names pairs "Wolf" with "Canis Lupus" (lines 7023 and 7027), but the glossary is a split two-column OCR list in which the pairing is by order and not certain. [USER: verify]

*Other wolf materials, not folded in.* The wolf's stomach is a separate material in the same line (1170), and the wolf's stool a third (line 1199). Outside the Tibetan texts, Sushruta (Sutrasthana ch.46, `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` lines 534-544) places the wolf among the *Guhashayas*, the cave-dwelling mammals, and says of the flesh of that group that it "is sweet, heavy, demulcent and strength-giving" and "subdues the deranged Vayu". That is a statement about flesh. The text read does not mention the tongue, and Sushruta is not on this entry's tag line.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, the tongue of a mammal is a muscular organ covered by a mucous membrane, with taste buds and mucous and salivary glands, and it is rich in connective tissue and fat. Nothing links any constituent to the action the Tantra gives, which is to remove swelling of the tongue.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts say that the wolf's tongue removes swelling of the tongue. The Codex does not recommend it. Swelling of the human tongue can obstruct the airway and may be an allergic reaction, an infection or a side effect of medicine, and it needs urgent medical help, not an animal organ. The wolf is a wild and in many countries protected animal, and its organs can carry parasites and pathogens. The entry exists to record what the Explanatory Tantra says. The text does not say how the tongue was applied or whether it was taken inside, and the Codex adds no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither translation gives a preparation, vehicle, quantity or course for the wolf's tongue. The text does not say whether it was eaten, applied, dried or burnt, or whether it was part of a compound. The "medicinal horses" at the end of the chapter (Clark line 1202), raw cane sugar, crystal sugar and honey, are named as vehicles for compounded medicines in general; the text does not say that the wolf's tongue was taken with any of them. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Swelling of the tongue (glossitis or angioedema) can follow infection, injury, allergy or certain drugs, and when it is rapid or accompanied by difficulty breathing or swallowing it is an emergency. Raw or poorly cooked organs of wild carnivores can carry parasites and bacteria that infect people. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** No taste, post-digestive taste or potency is printed for the wolf's tongue; its only printed action is to remove swelling of the tongue. The Tantra's rule for meats, that all types are sweet in taste and post-digestive taste, with cool, light and coarse powers in dry-land animals (Clark line 673), is about flesh and is not carried over to this organ.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The wolf's tongue is named in the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica of animal products with a single therapeutic action (Clark line 1170). The wider frame is real but does not reach it. In Clark's account the three humors are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration (line 51), and these three are "the three causes" of wind, bile and phlegm disorders (line 147), so disease is at root a spiritual condition and medicine is one support. The chapter on routine behaviour asks the practitioner to refrain from the ten negative actions, the first of which is "taking life" (line 616). The rejuvenation passage in chapter 23, "Normal health" (lines 1266-1275), does not name the wolf's tongue, and the lines read give it no *bcud len* use. The tradition has no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The wolf's tongue is a one-line remedy for swelling of the tongue in a run of organ entries, and the tradition's own first precept is against taking life. The Codex does not recommend it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Infection and hygiene:** organs of wild carnivores can carry parasites and pathogens; do not eat, handle or apply them.
- **Swelling of the tongue:** rapid swelling, or swelling with difficulty breathing or swallowing, is an emergency; seek medical help at once.
- **Identity:** no binomial is printed, and the identification of the animal rests on the English "wolf" and a glossary pairing that is itself uncertain.
- **Conservation and law:** wolves are protected in many countries and regions, and taking wild animals or their products is regulated or banned; do not collect or buy them.
- **Ethics:** the Tibetan source itself lists "taking life" first among the ten negative actions (Clark line 616).
