# Five-leaved Canary Clover — Badassi, Prostrate Canary-Clover, Herbe à cinq feuilles

## *Dorycnium pentaphyllum* Scop. | Family: Fabaceae (Leguminosae)

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Cool & Dry — a drying, binding astringent (Galenic-Unani frame)

**Training Phase:** Purification

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Dorycnium pentaphyllum* (badassi, five-leaved canary-clover) is a small, wiry, silvery-leaved shrub of the pea family, native to the dry hills, garrigue, and rocky slopes of the Mediterranean basin. Botanically it has drifted between genera — modern molecular work often folds it toward *Lotus* — but in the herbal record it stands with the astringent Mediterranean legumes: plants whose tannin-rich leaves and tops were reached for to bind, dry, and close.

**Assyrian (AT):** The Mesopotamian medical tradition preserved on the cuneiform tablets made heavy use of astringent, wound-drying plant preparations — poultices and washes to staunch bleeding, tighten flesh, and dry weeping sores. A tannin-bearing Mediterranean shrub of this kind belongs to that broad Near-Eastern class of binding wound-herbs, applied topically as a drying agent rather than taken as a strong internal drug.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* records a *dorycnion* — a name later attached to this genus — among the plants of the Greek world. In the classical materia medica the astringent legumes and their tops were used, chiefly externally, to draw together wounds and check discharges; the doctrine of the *stryptic* (binding) herb is continuous here. (Note: the classical *dorycnion* was also discussed among suspect/poisonous plants, so classical usage was cautious — see Contraindications.)

**Avicenna (CM):** In the Galenic-Unani system carried through Avicenna's *Qanun*, cool-and-dry astringents (*qabid*) are the tools for excess flux — diarrhoea, weeping wounds, lax tissue, bleeding. A tannin-rich shrub of this character reads as a classic *qabid* topical: drying, tightening, and closing, matched to conditions of dampness and over-flow rather than dryness or cold.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The doctrine follows the tannin. Proanthocyanidins and hydrolysable tannins precipitate proteins on contact — literally tanning and tightening the surface they touch — which is the physical fact behind the whole "binding, drying, wound-closing" tradition. A silvery, drought-adapted shrub that survives the dry garrigue by loading its tissues with astringent phenolics is, symbolically and chemically, a plant *of drying and holding-in*: the Purification-phase logic of closing what leaks and clearing what weeps.

### Active Components

- **Condensed tannins / proanthocyanidins** — the dominant astringent principle of the tops and leaves; the basis of the binding, wound-drying action.
- **Flavonoids / flavonol glycosides** — notably **myricitrin** (myricetin rhamnoside), reported as a major constituent, plus other flavonoids; and **quercetin** as a key phenolic.
- **Phenylbutanone glucosides** — including *dorycnioside*, a compound first isolated from this plant.
- **Cyanogenic glucoside** — a trace constituent typical of some *Lotus*/*Dorycnium* legumes (a toxicological caution, not a therapeutic asset).
- **Cyclitols and hydroquinone glucosides** — minor reported constituents.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Canary clover is a **Purification-phase** astringent: a drying, binding topical for closing and clearing rather than building. Its traditional register is external — washes, compresses, and poultices for weeping wounds, lax or bleeding tissue, and damp sores — and its Kenetica role mirrors this exactly: a tightening, surface-clearing agent used where the task is to *dry and close* a leaking or over-moist condition. It is deliberately not an internal tonic; the cyanogenic trace and the classical association with suspect plants keep it in the topical, short-course, corrective niche.

### Scientific Correlation

The modern literature on *Dorycnium pentaphyllum* is **genuinely sparse** and mostly phytochemical or in-vitro; there is essentially no clinical trial evidence.

- According to PubMed, Koygun et al. (2021), *Biomolecules* — a crude extract of *Dorycnium pentaphyllum* showed time- and concentration-dependent cytotoxicity against MCF-7 breast cancer cells while sparing immortalised normal MCF-12A breast cells, with bioinformatics pointing to **quercetin** as the key active component (affinity for PIM-1 and HCK kinases). DOI: [10.3390/biom11050671](https://doi.org/10.3390/biom11050671)
- According to PubMed, Kazantzoglou et al. (2004), *Zeitschrift für Naturforschung C* — isolated **dorycnioside**, a new phenylbutanone glucoside, from *D. pentaphyllum* subsp. *herbaceum*, alongside myricitrin (the major constituent), further flavonoids, a cyanogenic glucoside, a cyclitol and a hydroquinone glucoside — the first report of phenylbutanones in the Leguminosae. DOI: [10.1515/znc-2004-1-205](https://doi.org/10.1515/znc-2004-1-205)
- According to PubMed, Demir et al. (2019), *Nutrition and Cancer* — a Fabaceae extract (TPC ~141 mg GAE/g, quercetin as major phenolic) showed selective cytotoxicity toward HeLa and WiDr cancer cells via S-phase arrest and apoptosis, consistent with the quercetin-driven activity reported for the genus. DOI: [10.1080/01635581.2019.1636100](https://doi.org/10.1080/01635581.2019.1636100)

**Evidence grade: TRADITIONAL (data-sparse).** The astringent/wound-drying use rests on textual tradition and on the well-understood chemistry of its tannins and flavonoids; modern data are limited to phytochemical isolation and preliminary in-vitro cytotoxicity, with no clinical evidence and a genuine toxicological caution. Treat all internal-use claims as unproven.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani (as attested):** Cool and dry; *qabid* (astringent/binding) — a drying, tightening, flux-checking agent, chiefly topical.
- **Dioscoridean (classical):** a *styptic*/binding herb of the *dorycnion* group, used to draw wounds together and check discharge (with classical caution as a suspect plant).
- No attested TCM (Shennong) or Ayurvedic (Sushruta) classification for this Mediterranean species; by cross-tradition analogy only, its tannin profile would read as *kashaya* (astringent) rasa — offered as analogy, not as a Sushruta citation.

### Contraindications

- **Cyanogenic-glucoside trace:** the plant contains a cyanogenic glucoside; internal use is not advised without expert supervision, and raw/large quantities are potentially unsafe.
- **Classical "suspect plant" status:** the *dorycnion* of the classical authors was discussed among poisonous/dubious plants — historical caution is warranted; keep to external use.
- Avoid in pregnancy and lactation (astringent, cyanogenic trace, no safety data).
- Tannin-rich preparations can irritate the gut and impair iron/mineral absorption if taken internally; may interact with alkaloid drugs (precipitation).
- Data on human safety are absent — do not present as an established internal medicine.
