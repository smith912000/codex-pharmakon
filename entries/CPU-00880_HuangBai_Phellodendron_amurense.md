# Amur Cork Tree / Huang Bai / Phellodendron

## *Phellodendron amurense* Rupr. | Family: Rutaceae

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ, FMT
**Energetic Classification:** Cold, Dry, Bitter — heat-clearing / damp-draining (well-attested in the Chinese source stream)
**Training Phase:** Foundation — Signature Study

---

### Origin & Tradition

The Amur cork tree is a deciduous tree of northeast Asia — the Amur river basin, northern China, Korea, Japan, the Russian Far East — named for its thick, corky, deeply fissured outer bark. The medicine is the **inner bark**, bright chrome-yellow when cut. That colour is the whole signature: *huáng bǎi* means "yellow fir/cypress-bark," and the yellowness is not incidental — it is the visible berberine, the quaternary isoquinoline alkaloid that stains everything it touches. In the doctrine of signatures the plant announces its own class: it is one of the **"Three Yellows"** of Chinese heat-clearing medicine, alongside Huang Lian (*Coptis*, "yellow-thread") and Huang Qin (*Scutellaria*, "yellow root"). All three are bitter, cold, and yellow, and all three are read as draining fire and damp-heat.

**SBJ (Shennong / Chinese materia medica):** Huang Bai is a long-standing bitter-cold heat-clearing drug. Its codified classical action is to **"clear heat, dry dampness, drain fire, and relieve toxicity,"** with a specific territory: **damp-heat of the lower burner** — dysenteric/damp-heat diarrhoea, turbid or painful urination, vaginal discharge (*dai xia*), damp-heat jaundice, and hot sores/ulcers topically. It is classically salt-processed to direct it downward and to temper its coldness for kidney-related "deficiency fire." This attestation is secure.

**FMT (Tibetan rGyud-bzhi / Four Tantras):** Tibetan medicine does use a *Phellodendron/skyer-pa*-type bitter yellow bark within its cold, bile-clearing (*mkhris-pa*) materia medica, but I **could not substantiate *P. amurense* specifically named in the rGyud-bzhi this session.** Tibetan sources often use *Berberis* ("skyer-pa") bark — another berberine-bearing yellow bitter — and the two are easily conflated in secondary literature. **[USER: verify]** whether the Four Tantras attestation is genuinely *Phellodendron* or is actually *Berberis*; do not print an FMT claim for *P. amurense* until the primary text is checked.

**Species caution (built into this entry):** the Chinese Pharmacopoeia distinguishes **two** medicinal Phellodendron barks — ***P. amurense*** = **Guan Huang Bai** (this entry) and ***P. chinense*** = **Chuan Huang Bai**. They are related but not identical in constituent profile, and much of the "Huang Bai" literature does not state which species (or which voucher specimen) was used. Where a study did not verify the botanical source, that gap is noted below.

### Active Components

- **Berberine** — the signature quaternary isoquinoline alkaloid; the yellow pigment and the principal studied molecule.
- **Palmatine, jatrorrhizine, phellodendrine, magnoflorine** — related protoberberine/aporphine alkaloids.
- **Limonoids — obacunone, obaculactone (limonin)** — highlighted in the Phellodendri Cortex review as candidate anti-inflammatory / anti-atopic-dermatitis constituents.
- Phenolic acids, quinic acid derivatives, lignans, flavonoids. The Sun 2019 review catalogued ~140 compounds across the *Phellodendron* species, with a markedly different count between *P. amurense* bark (~84 compounds) and *P. chinense* bark — reinforcing that the two are not interchangeable.

**Critical honesty note on evidence attribution:** the large clinical literature you will meet under "berberine" is **molecule-level or *Coptis*-level**, not *P. amurense*-bark-specific. Berberine used in trials is typically isolated berberine (often sourced from *Coptis chinensis* or produced/standardised as the pure salt), not a whole Huang Bai bark extract. So the metabolic evidence below grades *the molecule berberine*, and is imported here only because berberine is Huang Bai's signature constituent — it is **not** proof that *P. amurense* bark at traditional doses reproduces those trial outcomes.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Huang Bai sits in the practitioner's path as the **archetype of the bitter-cold "drain damp-heat from below" agent** and as a second lesson (after Butterbur) in *molecule-vs-plant* attribution — here the direction is reversed: the plant is old and well-attested, but the shiny clinical data belong to an isolated molecule that may or may not travel back to the bark.

Positioned honestly:
- **Traditional use (bark):** damp-heat of the lower burner — hot, turbid urinary and genital complaints, damp-heat diarrhoea, jaundice, and topical hot sores. This is a coherent, long-transmitted indication, but it rests on traditional-clinical, not modern-RCT, footing for the *whole bark*.
- **Molecule spillover (berberine):** glycaemic and lipid improvement, antimicrobial action, AMPK activation. Real and reasonably robust — for berberine the molecule.

Deflation, plainly: do not tell a student that "Huang Bai bark tea lowers HbA1c by 0.7%." The meta-analyses used dosed berberine, mostly as an adjunct, in trials of generally low methodological quality. The bark's own traditional indications are the honest use-case; the berberine metabolic story is adjacent evidence, clearly labelled as such.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Form | Traditional / historical handling | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Dried inner bark, decoction | Classical range roughly **3–12 g/day** in decoction; often **salt-processed** (*yán zhì*) to guide downward and to blunt coldness for "deficiency fire" | Traditional figure; not a validated pharmaceutical dose |
| Dry-fried / charred bark | Charred for bleeding damp-heat patterns | Traditional processing |
| Topical | Powdered bark or wash for damp-heat sores, eczema | External |
| Isolated berberine (NOT the bark) | n/a — modern | Trials commonly used **~0.5 g berberine 2–3×/day (~1–1.5 g/day)**; this is molecule dosing and must not be relabelled as a Huang Bai dose |

**Safety caveat:** the traditional gram-range is for supervised decoction use; the berberine trial doses are a different substance and route. No standardised, validated whole-bark dose for the metabolic indications exists — do not fabricate one.

### Scientific Correlation

**Clear/dry damp-heat, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory (bark) — GRADE: traditional + preclinical.**
- Sun 2019 (Phellodendri Cortex review) documents anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antiviral, antitumour, antigout and anti-ulcer activity across PC species — but this is a phytochemical/pharmacological review, largely preclinical, and explicitly distinguishes *P. amurense* (PAR/Guan Huang Bai) from *P. chinense* (PCS/Chuan Huang Bai). Note it also flags berberine as the constituent responsible for **toxicity in susceptible users (haemolytic disease, peripartum, neonatal)**.
- Balážová 2022: bark extract of *P. amurense* (berberine as main HPLC alkaloid) showed antiproliferative/antiangiogenic activity in ovo/in vitro — **preclinical only**, an actual *P. amurense* study.

**Glycaemic / metabolic (berberine the molecule) — GRADE: moderate positive, molecule-level, low trial quality.**
- Guo 2021 meta-analysis (46 RCTs): berberine — alone or added to standard therapy — significantly reduced **HbA1c (MD −0.73%)**, fasting plasma glucose (MD −0.86 mmol/L) and 2-h postprandial glucose, and improved insulin resistance and lipids. Concludes berberine is efficacious "especially as an adjunctive therapy."
- Dong 2012 meta-analysis (14 RCTs, 1,068 participants): berberine hypoglycaemic and antidyslipidaemic vs lifestyle; **not superior to metformin/glipizide/rosiglitazone alone**, but additive when combined. The authors caution: **low methodological quality, small samples, possible bias.**
- Both are **berberine-the-molecule** meta-analyses, **not** *P. amurense* bark trials. Congener/molecule label applies.

**Bilirubin displacement / neonatal risk — GRADE: established mechanism (in vitro + animal).**
- Chan 1993: berberine displaces bilirubin from albumin in vitro ~10× more potently than phenylbutazone, and in rats raised unbound and total serum bilirubin; concludes berberine-rich herbs "best avoided in jaundiced neonates and pregnant women." This is the mechanistic basis of the kernicterus contraindication. (Chan's berberine reference source is *Coptis/*huanglian — again molecule-level, but directly relevant to Huang Bai as a berberine-bearing bark.)

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Cold, Dry, intensely Bitter.** Enters (classically) the Kidney, Bladder and Large Intestine channels; drains fire and dries damp-heat, with a downward, lower-burner tropism sharpened by salt-processing. This is a securely codified reading in the Chinese source stream, not an inference — one of the three canonical bitter-cold "yellow" drainers.

### Contraindications

- **Neonates / jaundiced infants: contraindicated.** Berberine displaces bilirubin from albumin — kernicterus risk (Chan 1993).
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding: avoid** — berberine crosses to the fetus/neonate; bilirubin displacement and uterine concerns.
- **G6PD deficiency / haemolytic disease: caution/avoid** — berberine flagged as toxic in susceptible haemolytic users (Sun 2019).
- **Spleen/Stomach cold-deficiency (classical):** contraindicated — a bitter-cold drug will worsen cold, weak digestion; can cause loss of appetite, loose stools.
- **Drug interactions:** berberine inhibits CYP enzymes and P-glycoprotein and can raise levels of co-administered drugs (e.g. cyclosporine); caution with narrow-therapeutic-index medications and with other hypoglycaemics (additive glucose lowering).
- **Species/source note:** ensure the botanical source is verified — do not assume *P. amurense* data apply to *P. chinense* or vice versa.
