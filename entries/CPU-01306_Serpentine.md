# Serpentine (ophites) — Ophites Lithos (as printed in the 2000 English edition of Dioscorides)
## *"Serpentine — green metamorphic rock or marble", the translator's gloss under the heading 5-162 OPHITES LITHOS; the text itself describes three kinds by colour and marking (heavy and black, ashy and pricked, white-lined) and none is called green* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The loci given for this entry are `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` ("5-162 (line 10671)", gloss "Serpentine green metamorphic rock or marble", class mineral) and `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 888 ("Ophites Lithos", DMM V, identification marked yes). The chapter is *De Materia Medica* 5-162 *Ophites Lithos* in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`: heading at line 10671, the translator's gloss at lines 10672-10673, the text at line 10674. It sits among the stones of Book 5, after 5-161 *Aetites Lithos* (eagle stone, line 10667) and before 5-163 *Lithoi Spoggon* (stones found in sponges, line 10679). The page marker 823 and the sub-heading "METALLIC STONES" fall between the end of this chapter and 5-163.

The chapter reads in full (line 10674): "One kind of ophites stone is heavy and black, another an ashy colour distinguished with pricks, and another with white lines. Any of them (tied about the body) is good for snakebites and headaches. That with lines is reported to help lethargy and headaches in particular." The parentheses are the translator's. So the text separates three kinds by colour and marking: a heavy black one, an ash-coloured one marked with pricks (dots), and one with white lines. It then gives a use for "any of them", tied about the body: snakebites and headaches. And it gives one more for the lined kind alone, which "is reported to help lethargy and headaches in particular". The words "is reported" mark the second statement as hearsay, not as Dioscorides' own observation; the first is stated plainly.

The treatment is therefore a worn stone, not a swallowed or applied one: the only act described is tying the stone about the body. The chapter gives no place of origin, no size, no way of testing a true stone, no price, no preparation, no way of tying (to the arm or the thigh, as other stone chapters name), and no duration. Compare the neighbouring chapters, where the manner of wearing is sometimes specified: eagle stone is "tied around the left arm" in pregnancy and "around the thigh" at delivery (5-161, line 10670), and jasper is "tied to the thigh" to speed delivery (5-160, line 10666). The ophites chapter says only "tied about the body".

The chapter's use is amuletic in form, but it is stated as a medicinal one: "good for snakebites and headaches". Dioscorides sometimes records amuletic uses while distancing himself, and the editor's introduction says generally that "a few superstitious practices are recorded in De Materia Medica" (line 394). Here the distancing is the phrase "is reported" on the lined kind; the first statement carries none. Whether Dioscorides endorsed the first use is not something the chapter says, and this entry does not claim it.

**Identity.** The translator's gloss, "Serpentine — green metamorphic rock or marble", names the mineral group in modern usage; the chapter's three kinds are black, ashy-pricked and white-lined, and no green is mentioned. The word *ophites* in Greek derives from the word for snake, which accords with the snakebite use and the name serpentine, but the chapter itself does not explain the name; that is a general fact about the language, not a statement of the source. The sources retrieved do not say whether the Greek stone was serpentine in the modern mineralogical sense, a banded or speckled marble, or another rock. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, serpentine is a group of magnesium silicate minerals (hydrated magnesium silicates), usually green, sometimes black, grey or banded, and some serpentine rock contains fibrous chrysotile, a form of asbestos. The Greek *ophites* may or may not have been serpentine, and the sources do not say. A worn stone is not absorbed into the body whatever it is made of. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved say that Dioscorides recorded a stone, in three kinds, tied about the body for snakebites and headaches, and reported that the white-lined kind helped lethargy. The Codex does not recommend using a stone for a snakebite: a bite from a venomous snake is an emergency that needs antivenom and hospital care, and delay for a stone is dangerous. The stone has no known action on headache. A piece of polished serpentine worn as an ornament is harmless, and the text claims nothing further for it.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only act the chapter describes is tying the stone about the body. The chapter gives no preparation, no grinding, no drink, no application to a wound, no frequency and no duration. For the lined kind, "is reported to help lethargy and headaches" gives no manner of use beyond the first sentence's "tied about the body". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Serpentine is a rock-forming group of magnesium silicate minerals; the fibrous variety chrysotile is asbestos, whose dust, if inhaled, causes lung disease and cancer, so cutting, grinding or filing an unidentified serpentine stone is a hazard. A stone tied to the body has no pharmacological action, and a snakebite is treated with antivenom and supportive care, not with an amulet. Headaches of lasting or sudden severity need medical assessment. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for the ophites stone. Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention, and this chapter offers a small, real instance of it: it separates three kinds of one stone by colour and marking, and does not blur them. It records a worn-stone use for snakebite and headache, and recording is not endorsing; the second use is "reported". The chapter is therapeutic and assigns no cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The entry records that a Greek physician described three kinds of a stone by their markings and said, with some reserve, that it was tied about the body against snakebite and headache. The Codex does not recommend relying on any stone for a bite. The one thing a reader can take from it is the habit of distinguishing kinds by what the eye can see before saying what a thing is for. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Snakebite:** an emergency; seek antivenom and hospital care immediately; a stone is not a treatment.
- **Headache and lethargy:** sudden, severe or persistent headache, or unusual drowsiness, needs medical assessment.
- **Asbestos hazard:** some serpentine rock contains chrysotile asbestos; do not grind, cut or file unidentified serpentine, and do not swallow it.
- **Identification:** "serpentine" is the translator's gloss; the chapter's three kinds are black, ashy and white-lined, and the sources retrieved do not say what rock the Greek stone was.
- **Conservation and trade:** serpentine is a common rock; collection rules vary by place and this entry does not cover them. [USER: verify]
