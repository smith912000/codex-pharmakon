# Pig's-head fossil — phag.mgo (Clark 1995) / Phag go (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *An orange fossil stone shaped like a pig's head, one of the stone medicines of the Explanatory Tantra; Clark gives no scientific name for it, and the sources retrieved do not say what organism the fossil was* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The loci given for this entry are `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` ("Clark 1995 (line 826)") and `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 136, where the name is spelled "Phag go", class mineral, identification marked uncertain. Line 826 of `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` reads: "phag.mgo: Pig's Head Fossil: [bears the shape of a pig's head and is orange in color]. It heals bone disorders and drains lymph [accumulations]." The entry is in chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra, "The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines" (heading at line 792), under "(2) The Powers of Stone Medicines" (line 816). The square-bracketed description is, by Clark's own statement (preface, line 82), commentary taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries; the statement that it heals bone disorders and drains lymph is outside the brackets. The words "drains lymph [accumulations]" carry a bracket of Clark's: the Tibetan term behind "lymph" is not on disk, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text, which does not translate it, prints it as *chhuser*.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`) has the same material in its list of stone medicines: "Phag go restores weak bone and drains out chhuser. Jego gives the same therapeutic effect as Phag go and regenerates damaged tissues." (line 3356). Its name index cites page 199 (line 7467). The two renderings differ: Clark's "heals bone disorders" is the Men-Tsee-Khang's "restores weak bone", and Clark's "drains lymph [accumulations]" corresponds to "drains out chhuser". Both are English renderings of a Tibetan statement that is not on disk. The Men-Tsee-Khang text adds a second stone, Jego, said to act like Phag go. Clark's next entry (line 827) is "bye.mgo: Sparrow's Head Fossil: Spiriferoides: Cyrtiospirifer sinensis", which "promote[s] flesh growth", and the name Jego may correspond to it, but the sources read do not say so. [USER: verify]

So the text gives the pig's-head fossil a place among the stone medicines, a look (pig's-head shape, orange) and two actions: on the bone, and on a fluid that the translations render as lymph or *chhuser*. It gives no place of origin, no way of testing the stone, and no preparation. The neighbouring entries use the same device of naming an animal's body part for the shape of a stone: the hematite "frog's back" stones (lines 817-818) and the sparrow's-head fossil (line 827). That naming is by resemblance in shape, and the text does not say these are the remains of those animals.

No other tradition is tagged for this entry.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, a fossil is mineralised remains of an organism or its shell, and the common matrix is calcium carbonate or silica with iron oxides, which can give an orange colour. The pig's-head fossil is not identified in the sources to a particular organism (the gloss for the next entry, the sparrow's-head fossil, is a genus name, which suggests a shelled marine animal there, but nothing equivalent is printed for this one), so its composition is not known from them. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources retrieved say that the Tibetan Explanatory Tantra lists a pig's-head-shaped fossil for bone disorders and for draining lymph or *chhuser*. The stone cannot be identified, the complaints are broad, and the Codex does not recommend ingesting or applying fossil stone for bone disease. A bone disorder, including a fracture, needs diagnosis and care.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources retrieved give no preparation for the pig's-head fossil: the lines read do not say how it was crushed, cleaned, compounded or taken, or in what vehicle. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Fossil stones are mineral, largely calcium carbonate or silica, and they are not a source of bone-building nutrients in a form the body uses in the way food does; there is no evidence that swallowing a powdered fossil heals bone disease. Fossil powders from unknown sources can carry contaminants such as lead or arsenic. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

The only qualities are physical: the shape and the orange colour. The Men-Tsee-Khang text adds none. No other tradition is tagged, so no other classification is offered.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested for the pig's-head fossil in the lines read. The entry is a statement of use for the bone and for a body fluid. ### Contraindications

- **Bone disorders and fractures:** these need diagnosis, and often imaging and immobilisation; a stone powder does not substitute.
- **Unidentified material:** the composition of the "pig's-head fossil" is not established in the sources read, and an unrefined mineral may contain heavy metals.
- **Ingestion of stone powder:** abrasive and indigestible; avoid.
- **Pregnancy and children:** unsuitable.
- **Conservation and trade:** fossils are often protected or regulated in the country of origin; collecting and export are not covered here. [USER: verify]
