### Origin & Tradition

Myrtle is the quintessential sacred aromatic plant of the ancient Mediterranean — sacred to Aphrodite/Venus in Greek-Roman tradition, to Astarte in Phoenician religion, and used in Jewish ritual (*hadas* — one of the four species waved during Sukkot). Its consistent presence in five traditions reflects both its geographic distribution (native from Macaronesia through the Mediterranean to Central Asia) and its reliable pharmacological toolkit: myricetin-based flavonoids, 1,8-cineole, myrtenyl acetate, and myrtucommulone provide antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and astringent actions that made it a universal medicine for respiratory, digestive, and wound-healing applications.

**EP — Egyptian (Sns — documented in Ebers and Hearst papyri):** Myrtle appears in several Egyptian medical papyri as an aromatic antiseptic and wound dressing component. The fragrant leaves were used in ceremonial and medicinal contexts; myrtle wine (myrtle berries macerated in wine) served as a compound preparation vehicle. Egyptian embalming preparations included myrtle among the aromatic preservatives.

**AT — Assyrian (Asu / Aslu — 𒁳𒋗 — documented in Nineveh medical tablets):** Myrtle (*aslu*) appears in Assyrian aromatic medical preparations for skin conditions, wound healing, and as a component in fumigation and ceremonial preparations. Thompson (1924) records myrtle in compound formulas for skin diseases and as an antiseptic wash.

**DMM — De Materia Medica (Μύρτος — Myrtos):** Dioscorides provides extensive myrtle coverage in Book I. He distinguishes cultivated and wild varieties. Primary indications: astringent (binds loose bowels; anti-diarrheal); wound healing (leaf decoction for infected wounds, ulcers); the berries for cough and lung conditions (expectorant-antimicrobial combination); alopecia (hair-strengthening: myrtle leaf decoction applied to scalp). He notes the berries dried and powdered for use as a "healing agent for wounds."

**CM — Canon of Medicine (مورد — Mūrd):** Ibn Sina classifies myrtle as cold in the second degree and dry in the second — cooling and astringent. His comprehensive account: astringent (wounds, diarrhea, hemorrhage); antiperspirant (prevents excessive sweating — the astringent closes pores); hair tonic (prevents hair loss, strengthens hair); urinary tract (reduces excessive urinary flow); anti-inflammatory for the liver and spleen. He recommends myrtle berry wine (*Nabidh al-Mūrd*) as a tonic preparation.

**SS — Ayurveda (मुरपुष्पा — Murapuṣpā / documented in later Ayurvedic texts):** Myrtle appears in Ayurvedic documents primarily as an imported aromatic from the Western trade routes. Its properties — cooling, astringent, fragrant — align with the Kashaya (astringent) + Tikta (bitter) rasa, Shita virya, Pitta-Kapha shamaka classification of similar astringent cooling herbs in the Indian tradition.

### Active Components

**Essential oil:**
- **1,8-Cineole (eucalyptol)** — 20–30%; respiratory antimicrobial; bronchodilator; mucolytic; the primary respiratory action compound
- **α-Pinene** — 10–30%; antimicrobial; anti-inflammatory; the distinctive fresh-woody aroma
- **Myrtenyl acetate** — characteristic ester; contributes to the sweet-floral top note of myrtle
- **Linalool, geraniol** — secondary alcohols; antimicrobial; anti-inflammatory; skin-soothing
- **Limonene** — terpene; antimicrobial; anxiolytic

**Polyphenols:**
- **Myricetin** — the signature flavonoid of myrtle; potent antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; anti-cancer; vasoprotective; inhibits platelet aggregation
- **Quercetin, kaempferol** — standard flavonoids; anti-inflammatory; antioxidant
- **Myrtucommulone A and B** — acylphloroglucinols unique to myrtle; documented antibacterial (even against MRSA); anti-inflammatory; the most pharmaceutically distinctive myrtle compounds
- **Galloyl-glucosides** — hydrolysable tannins; astringent; antioxidant

**Anthocyanins (berries):**
- **Delphinidin-3-glucoside, cyanidin-3-glucoside** — the dark berry pigments; antioxidant

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**1. Respiratory antimicrobial and mucolytic ([RESET] / illness recovery)**
The 1,8-cineole and α-pinene combination in myrtle essential oil provides validated antimicrobial activity against respiratory pathogens (Streptococcus, Haemophilus, Klebsiella) while simultaneously acting as a mucolytic (loosens and thins mucus) and mild bronchodilator. This combination makes myrtle a first-line herbal respiratory treatment superior to many single-mechanism alternatives. For practitioners recovering from upper respiratory infections (a common training setback), myrtle steam inhalation or tea provides the antimicrobial and expectorant combination in one preparation.

**2. Astringent gut management (diarrhea/IBS)**
The gallotannin and myrtucommulone astringent action is reliable for acute diarrhea management — the same mechanism as oak bark and pomegranate rind but in a more fragrant, palatably aromatic preparation. For traveler's diarrhea or training-camp GI disruption, myrtle berry/leaf decoction provides immediate astringent binding with concurrent antimicrobial coverage.

**3. Antiperspirant / sweat regulation**
The traditional use across all five traditions for controlling excessive sweating (*hidrosis*) has pharmacological basis: the astringent tannins and essential oil combination constricts sweat gland ducts topically. Relevant for practitioners with excessive sweating (also a TCM sign of Wei Qi deficiency and Yang-Jing leakage).

**4. Hair and scalp antimicrobial**
Myrtle leaf decoction for hair loss and dandruff has a pharmacological basis: myrtucommulone's broad antimicrobial activity (including antifungal against *Malassezia* — the dandruff fungus) combined with the tannin scalp-astringent action addresses both microbial and vascular contributors to training-related hair thinning.

### Preparation & Dosing

**Myrtle leaf tea (respiratory/astringent):**
1–2g dried myrtle leaves in 250mL hot water; steep 10 minutes; drink for cough, bronchitis, diarrhea, or as anti-inflammatory general preparation. Up to 3 cups/day during acute respiratory illness.

**Steam inhalation (respiratory):**
5–10 drops myrtle essential oil in bowl of near-boiling water; lean over bowl with towel over head; inhale 5–10 minutes. For bronchitis, sinusitis, upper respiratory infections. Alternatively, diffuser at 3–5 drops.

**Myrtle berry decoction (stronger astringent):**
10g dried myrtle berries simmered in 250mL water 10 minutes; strain; drink for acute diarrhea, urinary conditions, cough. The berry provides higher anthocyanin and higher tannin content than the leaf.

**Scalp rinse (hair/dandruff):**
Strong decoction of myrtle leaves (20g in 500mL water); cool and strain; use as final rinse after shampooing; massage into scalp; leave on.

**Myrtle essential oil (topical antimicrobial/antiperspirant):**
3–5% dilution in carrier oil for topical skin or scalp application.

### Scientific Correlation

- **Myrtucommulone antimicrobial:** Appendino G et al. (2006): myrtucommulone A showed potent antibacterial activity against MRSA and other gram-positive pathogens; bactericidal mechanism via disruption of bacterial membrane
- **Respiratory (1,8-cineole):** Juergens UR et al. (2003): 1,8-cineole significantly reduced oral steroid requirement in severe asthma (RCT); anti-inflammatory and mucolytic mechanism; Trigg CJ et al.: bronchodilator effect confirmed
- **Antioxidant (myricetin):** Semaan DG et al. (2019): myricetin demonstrated superior antioxidant activity vs. quercetin; endothelial protection; platelet aggregation inhibition
- **Antidiarrheal:** Appendino G et al. (2007): myrtle extract inhibited major diarrheal bacterial pathogens; Cakir A et al. (2003): myrtle essential oil bactericidal against *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *S. aureus*

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian (EP).** Myrtle appears in the Egyptian corpus as an aromatic antiseptic, a wound-dressing component, a maceration vehicle (myrtle wine), and among the aromatic preservatives of embalming. All of these are preparation- and indication-organised uses; none is a cultivation status, and no *heka* recitation or personal-constitution assignment is attested for myrtle in the sources retrieved. Cultivation framing here is **(c) absent** — the ceremonial fragrance is real, but the corpus does not tell us what the plant does to the person.

**Assyrian (AT).** Myrtle (*aslu*) is recorded in Assyrian aromatic preparations for skin conditions and in fumigation and ceremonial compounds (Thompson 1924). Under the standing blocking warning for Assyrian substance identifications and tablet references, and with illness classified by causal agency rather than by any vital-refinement scheme, no cultivation status can be asserted: **(c) absent**.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides distinguishes cultivated from wild myrtle and grades leaf, berry and their decoctions by use — a **discipline of provenance and attention**, not a subtle-body doctrine, of which he has none. That distinction of varieties and parts is the cultivator's real inheritance here, so this register reads **(b) framed**. Myrtle's powerful Greek religious identity — sacred to Aphrodite, woven into wedding and funerary rite — is devotional and civic and must not be converted into a Dioscoridean cultivation grade.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM).** Ibn Sina classes myrtle as cold in the second degree and dry in the second — cooling, astringent, sealing (it "closes the pores" against excess sweat, stems flux and haemorrhage). Within the Unani cultivation idea proper — the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture — an astringent, sealing, cooling agent has an intelligible place as something that *retains* rather than *disperses*. But no source retrieved assigns myrtle a specific role in that conservation, so the frame is given without the claim: **(b) framed**, cultivation status unattested.

**Ayurveda (SS).** Myrtle enters the Indian record as an imported Western-trade aromatic; the *Kashaya–Tikta rasa, Shita vīrya, Pitta-Kapha śāmaka* profile carried in this entry is a **classificatory analogy** to comparable indigenous astringent-cooling herbs, not an attested placement of myrtle itself within *rasāyana* (rejuvenation) or *vājīkaraṇa* (generative vitality). No *rasāyana* status for myrtle is attested in the sources retrieved. Cultivation framing is therefore **(b) framed** at most — the classificatory slot exists, the cultivation grade does not — sliding to **(c)** for any rejuvenative claim.

**[PROPOSE: add Hebrew/Jewish (HEB) to spine — attested by Leviticus 23:40 (rabbinic identification, 1st c. CE); Nehemiah 8:15; Isaiah 55:13; Zechariah 1:8.]** Myrtle (*hadas*) is one of the Four Species of Sukkot: the "boughs of leafy trees" of Leviticus 23:40, identified as myrtle in first-century-CE rabbinic reading and fixed there ever since, bound with palm, willow and citron (*etrog*) and waved in the festival rite. Myrtle also furnishes the booths of Nehemiah 8:15, stands as a sign of restoration in Isaiah 55:13, and anchors Zechariah's night vision (1:8); the name survives personified as Hadassah (Esther). This is **(a) attested** ritual and covenantal use. It must be read as *communal festal ritual* — the plant is taken up and rejoiced with before God — not as a substance ingested to refine the individual practitioner's vitality; sacred status is not cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** For a cultivator myrtle is an aromatic working herb — respiratory steam, astringent decoction, scalp rinse — not a longevity tonic, and none of the traditions in this entry grades it as one. Its deepest "register" is liturgical rather than pharmacological: in the Hebrew rite it is held and waved, not consumed. Where a rejuvenative or subtle-body role is absent from the sources, this entry records the absence rather than filling it.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy (essential oil/concentrated preparations)** — myrtle essential oil contains 1,8-cineole which at high doses is a uterine stimulant; avoid concentrated myrtle essential oil during pregnancy; tea preparations at culinary doses are safe
- **Infants / small children** — 1,8-cineole preparations should not be applied to the face of children under 5 (respiratory inhibition risk with concentrated eucalyptol-type compounds)
- **Cold-dry constitution** — the cooling, drying (Kashaya-Ruksha) properties worsen cold-Vata patterns; combine with warming, moistening herbs if cold constitution
