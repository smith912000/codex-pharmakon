# Gueldenstaedtia multiflora — zangs.tig, Zangtik, "red gentian" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Gueldenstaedtia multiflora, the second name Clark 1995 prints for zangs.tig, after the genus-level "Gentiana sp.", and glossed "a type of red gentian"; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints Gentianella moorcroftiana, "Copper coloured gentian", for Zangtik* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); the binomial rests on one printed line of one text.** This entry rests on one tradition's texts, in two modern editions. Only Clark 1995 prints *Gueldenstaedtia multiflora* (line 1008 and the index of Latin names, line 1593). A search of Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 for "Gueldenstaedtia" found nothing. The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. No other text read for this entry names the plant: a search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk (including the three Bhishagratna volumes) for the genus names *Gueldenstaedtia* and *Gentiana* found no *Gueldenstaedtia*, and found Dioscorides 3-3 GENTIANE (line 6541, glosses *Gentiana lutea* and Yellow Gentian) and Yang 1998's Long Dan (line 1936) and Qin Jiao (line 2494). These are other genera or species and are not attributed to this row. Clark's preface (line 83) says that more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance, and that sometimes the same name refers to different plants. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Identity doubt.** Clark glosses the entry "a type of red gentian" and gives as her first name the genus-level "Gentiana sp."; *Gueldenstaedtia* is her second. As the Codex understands it, *Gueldenstaedtia* is a genus of the pea family (Fabaceae) and not a gentian; the Codex has not checked this against a flora. The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary gives for the same Tibetan name a gentian, *Gentianella moorcroftiana* (see below). The Codex cannot say from the sources read which plant the Tibetan texts meant. **Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (row 131) the Tibetan name *zangs.tig* is recorded at Clark line 1008 with the identification "Gentiana sp.; Gueldenstaedtia multiflora (a type of red gentian)". The key, *Gueldenstaedtia multiflora*, is therefore the second name Clark prints, the first being a genus-only entry that cannot serve as a binomial.

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1008).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). It reads:

"zangs.tig: Gentiana sp.: Gueldenstaedtia multiflora (a type of red gentian): [grows in dry places, has a green leaf and petals a little like those of the mustard flower. It has red petioles, many branching stems, a pale red flower and bitter taste.] Red gentian cures infectious fevers."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the brackets (commentary layer) are the habitat, the leaf, petals, petioles, stems, flower and the bitter taste. Outside them (root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the two Latin names and the parenthesis "(a type of red gentian)", which are Clark's identifications, and the sentence "Red gentian cures infectious fevers". The next line (1009, *lchags.tig*) ends: "Both red gentian and Gentianopsis cure bile fevers", which gives this drug a second action together with that one. The entry for that line is I_1294 (*Gentianopsis paludosa*, this batch).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* Line 3428 (page 206), in the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines", ends: "Zangtik and Chaagtik cure hot disorder associated with tripa." The index lists "Zangtik 206" (line 7558). Clark's "bile fevers" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "hot disorder associated with tripa" are the two translations' wordings, and the Codex does not treat them as one term.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary.* The "Z" block (lines 6054 to 6075) prints six phonetic names at lines 6055 to 6058 ("Zangtik Zangtsi", "Zema Zhati", "Zira karpo", "Zira nagpo"), their transliterations at lines 6060 to 6062 ("zangs tig zangs rtsi", "gze ma dzA ti", "zi ra dkar po zi ra nag po"), and the scientific names at lines 6064 to 6067: "Gentianella moorcroftiana", "Galium aparine", "Tribulus terrestris", then "Myristica fragrans Cuminum cyminum Nigella sativa" (six in all). The English column (lines 6069 to 6073) begins "Copper coloured gentian", "Bedstraw", "Small calthreps Nutmeg", "White Cumin", "Black cumin". Counted in order, the first phonetic name, "Zangtik" (transliteration "zangs tig"), stands against *Gentianella moorcroftiana* and "Copper coloured gentian", and the sixth, "Zira nagpo", against *Nigella sativa* and "Black cumin". The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position. [USER: verify] The two Tibetan texts agree on the Tibetan name, that it is a gentian, and that it is a bitter, fever drug; they differ on the genus and species, and the Codex does not choose.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder found no entry on *Gueldenstaedtia*. I_1285 (*Galium spurium*) and I_906 (Cleavers / Goosegrass) describe the neighbouring "Z" glossary block, which holds "Zangtik" beside their own "Zangtsi". I_315 (*Gentiana crassicaulis*) names *zangs.tig* as a bare *Gentiana* sp. in its cultivation text. I_1294 (*Gentianopsis paludosa*, this batch) covers the next Clark line. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. Clark's commentary records a "bitter taste" for the plant it describes. If the plant is a gentian, bitter secoiridoid glycosides are characteristic of that family; if it is *Gueldenstaedtia*, no general statement is made here, and the Codex does not choose. Which part was used is not said.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "cures infectious fevers" and, with *lchags.tig*, "bile fevers" (Clark, lines 1008 and 1009), and that with *Chaagtik* "cure[s] hot disorder associated with tripa" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3428). These are the texts' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it cures infectious fevers and, with its neighbour, bile fevers, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text pairs the Tibetan name with *Chaagtik* for hot disorder associated with tripa. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the sentences cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name, not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a bitter fever drug in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and the plant behind the Tibetan name is uncertain. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Fevers need medical assessment, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Because the identity is uncertain, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
