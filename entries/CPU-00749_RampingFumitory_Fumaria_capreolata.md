# Ramping Fumitory

## *Fumaria capreolata* L. | Family: Papaveraceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Cool, dry, bitter. Depurative-cholagogue; relaxant to smooth muscle.

**Training Phase:** Restoration / Clearance. A bitter for the hepatobiliary–intestinal axis. Short courses only.

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Fumaria capreolata* L. — **white ramping fumitory**, **ramping fumitory** — is a scrambling glaucous annual of the Papaveraceae, native to western and southern Europe, Macaronesia and North Africa, spreading through hedge banks, walls, waste ground and cultivated margins. *Capreolata* means "with tendrils": the plant climbs by twisting its leaf-stalks, which is what separates it in the field from its upright congeners. The flowers are creamy-white with a blackish-red tip — not the pink of *F. officinalis*.

**Mediaeval–Renaissance materia medica (DMM)**
The tradition does not treat fumitories as separate drugs. It treats **fumitory** as one plant, and the name it gives it is the doctrine: **fumus terrae**, *earth-smoke*. English **fumitory**; French **fumeterre**; Spanish **palomilla**, **conejitos**; Italian **fumaria**; German **Erdrauch**; Arabic **shâhtaraj** / **shâhtarraj** — the last carrying the same drug through the Arabic medical corpus into the Latin West.

Two competing etymological stories attach to the name, and both are worth recording as *doctrine* rather than fact: that the grey-glaucous foliage massed on a bank looks like smoke rising from the ground; and the older, stranger claim that the plant is generated *from* the earth's vapour rather than from seed. **Both are traditional readings; neither is a settled etymology and neither should be asserted as one.** [USER: verify]

The classical indication is remarkably stable across a millennium: fumitory is the **depurative bitter**. Its territory is:

- **Skin disease** — "scabbiness", itch, tetters, "foulness of the blood breaking out at the surface". This is the *headline* traditional use.
- **The liver and the bile** — obstructions of the liver and spleen, jaundice, "the yellow choler".
- **Melancholy and adust humours** — the burnt, thickened black bile of scholastic pathology.

These are one indication, not three. In humoral logic the skin is an *outlet*: impure humour that the liver has failed to concoct is driven to the surface, where it erupts. Treat the liver, open the bile, purge the choler, and the skin clears **because the cause was never in the skin**. Fumitory is the exemplary drug of that logic — a bitter that is claimed to work on the skin by refusing to touch it.

**Contemporary / folk-clinical**
Modern folk use has narrowed towards the gastrointestinal and hepatobiliary. Romanian traditional medicine uses *Fumaria* aerial parts for **hepatobiliary disease and as diuretics** (Păltinean et al., *Molecules*; PubMed, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules22040639)). Crucially for this entry, *F. capreolata* **specifically** is documented as a botanical drug used in **North Africa for gastrointestinal and anti-inflammatory** complaints — a use stated as the explicit rationale for the pharmacological work cited below (Bribi et al., *Phytomedicine*; PubMed, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2016.05.003)). A genus-wide ethnomedicinal review names *F. capreolata* among the six *Fumaria* species with substantial folk-medical currency (Zhang et al., *Fitoterapia*; PubMed, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fitote.2020.104697)).

**Distinguish from the congeners already in this codex.** *F. officinalis* (common fumitory, pink flowers, the European pharmacopoeial species — the one behind the German biliary phytomedicines) and *F. parviflora* (fine-leaved fumitory, the species carrying most of the South Asian *shahtara* literature) hold the same doctrinal position. **They are not interchangeable as drugs.** GC-MS profiling across ten *Fumaria* species including *F. capreolata*, *F. officinalis* and *F. parviflora* found the isoquinoline alkaloid complement to vary between species to a degree the authors treat as **chemotaxonomically significant** — i.e. the species differ *diagnostically* in their alkaloids (Suau et al., *Phytochem Anal*; PubMed, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/pca.669)). A shared doctrine is not a shared pharmacology.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**
The depurative doctrine has a real chemical spine and a fictional one, and they must be separated.

*F. capreolata* is an isoquinoline-alkaloid plant. Twenty-six isoquinoline alkaloids were characterised in it by UHPLC-QTOF-MS across seven structural subclasses — protoberberine, protopine, aporphine, benzophenanthridine, spirobenzylisoquinoline, morphinandienone and benzylisoquinoline — with **protopine quantified at 9.6 ± 0.7 mg/g** of extract (Contreras et al., *Int J Anal Chem*; PubMed, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1155/2017/5178729)). A separate characterisation of the total alkaloid fraction found 23 alkaloids, most abundantly **stylopine, protopine and coptisine**, with the assayed fraction containing **1.3% stylopine and 0.9% protopine** (Bribi et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2016.05.003)).

That matters because **protopine is the plausible mechanistic core of the entire fumitory doctrine.** The tradition's "opens obstructions of the liver and gall" is, stripped of humoral vocabulary, a claim about **biliary flow and biliary tone** — and protopine-type alkaloids are the constituents around which the modern amphicholeretic/antispasmodic account of fumitory is built. The tradition felt the bitter, observed something happen downstream of the gallbladder, and named it "opening an obstruction". Bitterness on the tongue is not a metaphor here: it is the tasting of alkaloid, and the alkaloid is the active.

**But the doctrine's headline claim does not survive.** The skin indication — the reason fumitory earned its reputation — rests on a humoral theory of cutaneous elimination that **is simply false**. The skin is not an excretory outlet for "adust humour". There is no such humour. Eczema, scabies and acne are not manifestations of hepatic failure to concoct bile. The mechanism that made the doctrine *coherent* is the part of it that is *wrong*.

What survives is narrower and, notably, **not the part the tradition emphasised**: an alkaloid-bearing bitter with real activity on the inflamed gut. The tradition pointed at the liver-to-skin axis and was mistaken; it happened to be holding a plant that acts on the gut. **The doctrine should be read as a near-miss, not a confirmation** — and the temptation to retro-fit "gut–liver–skin axis" language onto it, so that the mediaeval physicians turn out to have been right all along, should be resisted. That is not scholarship. It is flattery.

### Active Components

All figures below are **species-specific to *F. capreolata*** unless stated. This entry does **not** import *F. officinalis* or *F. parviflora* data.

**Isoquinoline alkaloids** — the defining and active class. In *F. capreolata* specifically:
- **Protopine** — quantified at **9.6 ± 0.7 mg/g** by UHPLC-DAD-QTOF-MS ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1155/2017/5178729)); **0.9%** of the total alkaloid fraction ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2016.05.003)).
- **Stylopine** — **1.3%** of the total alkaloid fraction; the most abundant alkaloid in that fraction.
- **Coptisine** — protoberberine; third of the abundant three.
- 26 alkaloids in total characterised, spanning protoberberine, protopine, aporphine, benzophenanthridine, spirobenzylisoquinoline, morphinandienone and benzylisoquinoline subclasses ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1155/2017/5178729)).
- GC-MS across the genus additionally identifies, in *F. capreolata* among other species: cryptopine, sinactine, bicuculline, adlumine, parfumine, fumariline, fumarophycine, fumaritine, dihydrofumariline, parfumidine, dihydrosanguinarine ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/pca.669)).

**Flavonoids and phenolic acids** — the genus carries high flavonoid levels with **rutin and isoquercitrin** as main compounds ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules22040639) — *genus-level finding across six species; the study reports species-level antioxidant differences and this entry does not assign its strongest results to* capreolata *without checking the source*). [USER: verify which species scored highest in Păltinean et al.]

Saponins and terpenoids are reported genus-wide (Zhang et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fitote.2020.104697)).

**Note the benzophenanthridines.** Dihydrosanguinarine's presence flags this as a plant whose alkaloid family includes genuinely cytotoxic members. It is one reason the dosing section below is conservative.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

*F. capreolata* is a **short-course clearance bitter**, and the honest indication is **not the one the tradition advertises**.

Deploy it where the picture is **gut-inflammatory with a hepatobiliary flavour**: sluggish digestion of fats, biliary-type dyspepsia, a gut that is irritable and low-grade inflamed. This is where the retrieved evidence actually points, and it points there in this species rather than a congener — an unusual luxury in this volume.

Do **not** deploy it as a skin drug. The Kenetica practitioner reaching for fumitory to clear eczema is executing a mediaeval theory of humoral elimination that has no support and no mechanism. If the skin clears while a patient is on a bitter, the practitioner has learned nothing about the bitter.

This is a **phase-limited** agent. Bitters that carry benzophenanthridine and protoberberine alkaloids are not tonics and should not be run continuously. Two to four weeks, then stop and reassess.

**Cultivator's note.** *F. capreolata* is an annual and a scrambler; it wants disturbed, fertile, well-drained ground and something to climb — a hedge base, a wall foot, a fence line. It will not tolerate being crowded at the seedling stage or sitting wet. Sow *in situ* in autumn in a mild climate or early spring elsewhere; fumitories transplant badly and resent root disturbance, so do not raise in modules if it can be avoided. It self-seeds freely once established and will behave as a weed — which is precisely how the tradition encountered it.

**Harvest the aerial parts in flower.** Every pharmacological study cited here used aerial parts, and this is the only plant part with retrieved data. Dry rapidly in shade with airflow.

**The cultivator's real problem with this plant is identification, not culture.** *Fumaria* is a genus of near-identical scrambling weeds separated on fruit shape, sepal dimensions and flower-tip colour — characters that require a key, a lens, and mature fruit. Given that the species differ chemotaxonomically in their alkaloids ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/pca.669)), **a misidentified fumitory is a different drug, not a slightly different one.** Wild-collected fumitory of unverified species should be treated as unidentified plant material. If the species cannot be keyed out with fruit present, do not use it.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Preparation | Part | Method | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Infusion | Dried aerial parts, in flower | 1–2 g in 150 mL boiling water, 10 min | 1 cup before meals, up to 3×/day | Traditional. **No retrieved study establishes a human dose for this species.** [USER: verify] |
| Tincture | Dried aerial parts | 1:5 in 45% ethanol | 2–4 mL, 3×/day | Traditional/consensus figure only. [USER: verify] |
| Total alkaloid fraction (AFC) | Aerial parts | Standardised extract, 1.3% stylopine / 0.9% protopine | **25, 50, 100 mg/kg — mouse, oral** | The doses that produced the effects below. **These are animal doses and must not be converted to a human dose in this codex.** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2016.05.003)) |

**Duration:** maximum 2–4 weeks; do not run continuously.

**A dosing warning that is not decorative.** The entire quantitative evidence base for this plant is a **standardised alkaloid fraction of known composition given to mice**. A home infusion is not that preparation, its alkaloid content is unknown, and it varies with species, season, provenance and drying. The retrieved genus review explicitly names the **lack of studies on pharmacokinetics, clinical efficacy, quality control and toxicology** as an open gap (Zhang et al., [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fitote.2020.104697)). **There is no established safe human dose for *F. capreolata*.** The table above records tradition; it does not license practice.

### Scientific Correlation

Graded honestly. **The hook this entry was written against predicted *F. capreolata* would be data-sparse. It is not** — this species has a focused, species-specific research programme behind it, and that correction is recorded here rather than buried. What *is* sparse is evidence for the use the tradition cares most about.

**GRADE: MODERATE (animal, two models, one group) — intestinal anti-inflammatory activity.**
This is the strongest claim in the entry and it is genuinely species-specific.
- **DNBS colitis, mice.** The total alkaloid fraction (AFC), orally at 25–100 mg/kg, significantly inhibited colonic release and expression of IL-6 and TNF-α, suppressed transcription of IL-1β, iNOS, IL-12 and IL-17, and **normalised expression of MUC-2 and ZO-1** — mucin and tight-junction markers, i.e. barrier integrity rather than immune suppression alone. In vitro, AFC inhibited IL-6, TNF-α and ICAM-1 mRNA in CMT93 intestinal epithelial cells (Bribi et al., *Phytomedicine*; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2016.05.003)).
- **DSS colitis, mice.** AFC dose-dependently inhibited the LPS-stimulated gene expression program in bone-marrow-derived macrophages; in vivo it reduced macroscopic and microscopic signs of intestinal inflammation, restored colonic pro- and anti-inflammatory mediator expression, and enhanced intestinal barrier marker expression (Bribi et al., *Bioorg Med Chem Lett*; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bmcl.2020.127414)).

Two chemically distinct colitis models, consistent direction, dose-dependence, a barrier-function mechanism as well as a cytokine one, and a chemically characterised extract. That is a well-built preclinical case. **The limits are equally real: both studies come from the same collaboration, there is no independent replication, and there is no human data of any kind.** Two papers from one group are not a literature.

**GRADE: WEAK (animal) — antinociceptive / anti-inflammatory.**
AFC reduced NO and IL-6 production dose-dependently in RAW264.7 macrophages without affecting viability, downregulated IL-6, IL-1β, iNOS, TNF-α and COX-2 mRNA, and orally inhibited acetic-acid writhing and formalin paw-licking in mice (Bribi et al., *Evid Based Complement Alternat Med*; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1155/2015/736895)). Standard screening models; same group again.

**GRADE: VERY WEAK / genus-level — diuretic.**
Extracts of *Fumaria* species produced strong increases in urinary volumetric excretion in saline-loaded rats at a single oral 250 mg/kg dose, with increased Na⁺ and K⁺ excretion ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules22040639)). **This is a six-species comparison and the strongest results are attributed to specific species; this entry does not claim them for *capreolata* without verifying which.** [USER: verify] Supports the Romanian folk diuretic use at genus level only.

**GRADE: WEAK / assay-artefact territory — cholinesterase inhibition.**
All *Fumaria* extracts screened — *capreolata* included — showed potent AChE and BChE inhibition at **1 mg/ml**, and **no noticeable inhibition at 10 µg/ml** (Orhan et al., *J Ethnopharmacol*; PubMed, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2003.11.016)). **Read the second half of that sentence.** A hundredfold gap between an active and an inactive concentration, with activity only at the top of the range, is what a crude alkaloid extract does in an Ellman assay. This is not a cognitive indication and must not be written up as one.

**GRADE: NULL — the skin indication. Deflate.**
Nothing retrieved this run tests *F. capreolata*, or any *Fumaria*, against eczema, psoriasis, acne, scabies or any dermatological endpoint, in any model, at any dose. The plant's most famous traditional use — the one that carried it through the Arabic and Latin corpora for a thousand years — has **zero retrieved evidential support**. The doctrine that justified it (cutaneous elimination of adust humour) is false. **This must be stated plainly and not softened.** A well-supported gut action does not launder an unsupported skin claim, and the fashionable move of invoking a "gut–skin axis" to rescue the tradition would be reasoning from the conclusion backwards.

**GRADE: NULL — hepatoprotective/cholagogue in this species.**
"Hepatoprotective" activity is listed among genus-wide bioactivities in the review ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fitote.2020.104697)), but no *F. capreolata*-specific hepatobiliary study was retrieved this run. The protopine→biliary-tone mechanism described in the Doctrinal layer is **a plausible chemical bridge, not a demonstrated one for this plant**. The German pharmacopoeial biliary use belongs to *F. officinalis* and **stays there**.

**Overall.** One species-specific, mechanistically coherent, unreplicated preclinical claim (colitis). One deflated headline doctrine (skin). One borrowed-from-the-congener claim that must not be borrowed (bile). No human data at all.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

| System | Classification |
|---|---|
| Humoral (Galenic) | Cold and dry, 2nd degree; bitter |
| Action class | Depurative ("blood-cleanser"), cholagogue, mild aperient, diuretic |
| Humour targeted | Choler; **adust melancholy** — burnt black bile |
| Direction | Downward and outward; "opens obstruction", drives impurity from the deep to the surface and out |
| Tissue affinity | Liver, gall, spleen, blood, skin |
| Arabic (*shâhtaraj*) | Cold, dry; cleanses the blood, opens the liver |

The Galenic reading is internally consistent and externally false. Its coherence is worth studying precisely because coherence is not evidence — this is a doctrine that held for a millennium, explained its own successes, specified a mechanism, named an organ, and was wrong about all of it. **Fumitory is the entry to reach for when a student mistakes the elegance of a traditional system for its validity.**

### Contraindications

- **This is an alkaloid plant. Treat it as one.** *F. capreolata* carries protoberberine, benzophenanthridine (including dihydrosanguinarine) and protopine-type isoquinoline alkaloids. These are not gentle constituents, and the plant's status as a common weed should not be mistaken for a safety profile. **Overdose caution is the primary warning of this entry.**
- **No toxicology exists.** The genus review explicitly identifies the absence of toxicology, pharmacokinetics, clinical efficacy and quality-control studies as a gap in the *Fumaria* literature ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fitote.2020.104697)). There is no LD50, no NOAEL, no human safety series for this species retrieved this run. **Absence of reported harm here is absence of investigation, not evidence of safety.**
- **Biliary obstruction, gallstones, bile-duct disease — do not use.** A drug claimed to move bile is contraindicated where bile cannot move. This follows from the traditional claim itself and requires no trial to justify.
- **Hepatic impairment.** An uncharacterised alkaloid load in a compromised liver. Avoid.
- **Pregnancy and lactation — avoid absolutely.** Isoquinoline alkaloids, no reproductive toxicology, no data. This is not a precaution; it is a prohibition on grounds of ignorance.
- **Children — avoid.** Same reasoning.
- **Drug interactions — unknown and plausibly real.** Protoberberine alkaloids as a class have CYP-interaction and transporter liabilities. **No interaction study for *F. capreolata* was retrieved.** Avoid alongside narrow-therapeutic-index drugs.
- **Duration.** Do not exceed short courses. There is no evidence base for chronic use and a chemically strong reason not to attempt one.
- **Species misidentification is the practical hazard.** See the cultivator's note. *Fumaria* species differ chemotaxonomically in their alkaloid profiles ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/pca.669)); the codex's *officinalis* and *parviflora* entries are **not** substitutable, in either direction.
- **Not a dermatological agent.** Restated as a contraindication rather than a mere absence, because a patient with treatable skin disease taking an unproven alkaloid bitter instead of treatment has been actively harmed by the omission.
