# Cachrys libanotis — Kagchru, "cachry" (Dioscorides 3-88)

## *Cachrys libanotis* (printed by the Dioscorides translators as the first suggested binomial for 3-88, glossed "Rosemary Frankincense"; the same line also gives *Cachrys panacifolia*, and a further line gives *Crithmum maritimum* / *Cachrys maritimum*) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry.** This plant is named only by Dioscorides (DMM). It was added 2026-10-09 from the extraction list (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) at the locus 3-88, line 7325.

**Identity note.** The binomial is the translators' (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) suggestion and not Dioscorides' identification. The SUGGESTED lines of 3-88 (lines 7326 to 7328) read: "Cachrys libanotis — Rosemary Frankincense Cachrys panacifolia — Parsnip-leaved Cachrys", then "Crithmum maritimum, Cachrys maritimum — Samphire, Sea Fennel, Peter's Cress", then "see 3-87". These are different plants, so the identity is not settled. The same binomial *Cachrys libanotis* is also printed in the SUGGESTED block of chapter 3-87 LIBANOTIS (line 7305; "Hippomaratrum libanotis, Cachrys libanotis, Cachola — Rosemary Frankincense / see 3-88, 3-89"). **Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is in Book Three, in the section "Roots of Akanthoda or Prickly Plants", between 3-87 LIBANOTIS and 3-89 LIBANOTIS.

*3-88 KAGCHRU* (heading at line 7325; text at line 7329). The whole chapter reads: "Cachry is warming and extraordinarily drying, as a result it is good mixed with sebaceous treatments, and it is sprinkled on the head and wiped off after three days for rheumatic eyes." The chapter gives no description of the plant and no other name. It says what *cachry* does and not what the plant looks like. The previous chapter gives the likely reason: it calls the seed of libanotis *cachris*, so 3-88 may concern that seed. This is an inference from 3-87 and the chapter does not say so itself. [USER: verify]

*3-87 LIBANOTIS* (heading at line 7305), read as the context of 3-88. It says that libanotis "has two types — one of which bears fruit called zea by some (or campsanema), the seed of which is called cachris [see cachry below]". The first type has leaves "similar to marathrum [3-81] but thicker and broader, lying like a wheel on the ground, smelling sweet", a stalk "a foot and more [in length] with many wings", "a lot of white seed shaped like a vertebra, round, with corners, sharp smelling, similar to rosin, and chewed is burning to the taste", and a white, very large root that "smells of frankincense". The text goes on (line 7324) to say that for abscesses "we ought to use the kind that bears no cachrys [seed], for that is sharp and harsh to the throat", and it ends with a libanotis described from Theophrastus whose decoction "purges upward and downward". These uses are for the plant of 3-87 and are not attributed to this row.

*3-89 LIBANOTIS* (heading at line 7330) is a different chapter, glossed by the translators as rosemary (*Rosmarinus officinalis*). It is already treated in the rosemary entries I_205 and I_634 and is not part of this row. This row concerns only the one-sentence chapter 3-88 and what 3-87 says of *cachrys*.

### Active Components

Plants of the umbellifer family (Apiaceae), to which the genus *Cachrys* belongs, are generally known to hold essential oils and, in many genera, furanocoumarins that can make the skin sensitive to sunlight. Which compounds are in the plant named by the translators was not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records a seed-or-resin material, warming and drying, mixed into greasy treatments and sprinkled on the head against "rheumatic eyes". The Codex gives no protocol for it. The chapter is one sentence long and the plant is not identified by it.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparation printed (3-88) is that *cachry* is "mixed with sebaceous treatments", and "sprinkled on the head and wiped off after three days". No quantity is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. For this chapter the discipline of attention is slight: it tells the reader what the substance does to the body (warming, drying) and one external use, and the preceding chapter tells how to tell the seed apart from the herb. It assigns the substance no status. The binomial is a translator's suggestion. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice. It is an external, drying application for the scalp, and the plant is not securely identified. The abstentive answer is the correct one.

### Contraindications

No contraindication is printed in 3-88. In 3-87 the seed is called "sharp and harsh to the throat". As a general caution, a "warming and extraordinarily drying" material applied to the scalp near the eyes may irritate, and a plant identified only from a list of suggested binomials should not be handled or used as a remedy. Do not use in pregnancy.
