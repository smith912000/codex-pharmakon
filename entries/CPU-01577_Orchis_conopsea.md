# Orchis conopsea — Saturion eruthronion, the erythronian satyrion (Dioscorides 3-144)

## *Orchis conopsea [Linnaeus], the last of the names on the first group of the translators' SUGGESTED line for 3-144 (which also prints Satyrium-basilicum mas [Fuchs, Brunfels], Orchis palmata angustifolia minor [Bauhin] and Gymnadenia conopsea [Brunfels] — "Gnat-like Orchid"; a second group with Orchis maculata [Linnaeus] and Orchis Fuchsii [in Sprague] — "Early Purple Orchid, Dead Man's Finger"; and the other usage Erythronium dens-canis — "Dog's-tooth Violet")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The chapter's SUGGESTED block (lines 7785 to 7788) offers three different plants: a "Gnat-like Orchid" group of four names, an "Early Purple Orchid" group of four names, and an "other usage" that is the dog's-tooth violet, a plant of another genus altogether. The spine row takes the *Orchis conopsea* name from the first group. The translators add a line of their own at 7789: "Modern satyrium species are only found at the Cape of Good Hope." The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *3-144. SATURION ERUTHRONION*, heading at line 7784, chapter text at line 7790, in Book Three among the roots of the *akanthoda* (the prickly plants). The whole chapter reads: "Satyrium erythronium has a seed similar to flax seed but big, firm, glittering and smooth. It is said that it is an aphrodisiac, like scincus. The bark of the root is somewhat thin and red, but the inside is white, pleasant in the mouth to one who tastes it, and sweet. It grows in sunny, hilly places. It is related that the root (taken into the hand) encourages venereal diseases, but even more so when a decoction is taken as a drink with wine. It is also called satyrium erythraicum, melium aquaticum, entaticon, priapiscus, morion, satyriscus, or testiculum satyri; the Romans call it molorticulum veneris."

What the chapter gives is therefore short. It describes a seed (like flax seed but bigger, "firm, glittering and smooth") and a root with a thin red bark and a white inside, sweet and pleasant to the taste, and it names a habitat, "sunny, hilly places". It prints no leaf, no stalk and no flower. The two uses are both uses of sexual desire or sexual acts, in the translators' English ("venereal diseases" is their rendering and is quoted as printed): the plant "is said" to be an aphrodisiac "like scincus" (the skink, which has its own chapter, 2-71, heading at line 5220 according to the names list), and it "is related" that the root held in the hand has this effect, more so as a decoction drunk with wine. Both are reports, introduced by "it is said" and "it is related", and the Codex records them as the text's reports. It adds seven Greek names and one Roman name, among them *priapiscus* and *testiculum satyri*.

**The chapters around it.** The three chapters before this one are also orchid chapters, each describing a root, and each is a separate plant on the translators' lines. 3-141 *ORCHIS* (heading at line 7761), the translators' military orchid, is eaten boiled "like bulbus"; it is said that the bigger root eaten by men "makes their offspring males". 3-142 *ORCHIS ETEROS* (line 7770) is applied to dissipate oedema and clean ulcers. 3-143 *SATURION* (line 7775) is drunk "in black hard wine for severe spasms" and is said to be an aphrodisiac. The Codex carries no statement of one chapter over to another. The chapter after this, 3-145 *ORMINON EMERON* (line 7791), is the translators' clary, and its text says that "it is also thought that a decoction (taken as a drink with wine) is an aphrodisiac".

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter id 3-144 and for the Greek names *saturion eruthronion*, *erythronium*, *priapiscus* and *satyrium* found no existing entry on this chapter. I_1140 (*Orchis bifolia*, chapter 3-143) names 3-144 in a paragraph on the neighbouring chapters and summarises it, without treating it as its own subject. No entry is duplicated.

### Active Components

The composition of the root and seed Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, the terrestrial orchids (Orchidaceae) of Europe bear underground tubers, and orchid tubers are known to be rich in a mucilaginous starch-like carbohydrate. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a sweet root held in the hand or drunk in wine in the reputation of an aphrodisiac, and the Codex gives no protocol for it. The reasons are plain. The report is one the text itself passes on with "it is said" and "it is related". The identification is the translators', among three different plants. Many wild orchids are protected, and their tubers are not to be dug on the strength of this chapter.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives two ways to use the root: "taken into the hand", and "a decoction ... taken as a drink with wine". It gives no quantity for either, no time of gathering and no way of making the decoction. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general, well-established fact that a plant's reputation as an aphrodisiac is not evidence of an effect. Holding a root in the hand is not a route by which a plant constituent is known to act. The identification is a translators' suggestion among three plants of two genera, so the chapter does not show that the plant it describes is *Orchis conopsea*.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints one taste, that the root is "pleasant in the mouth to one who tastes it, and sweet". It prints no warming, cooling, drying, moistening or astringent quality for 3-144.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to tell the plant (a big, firm, glittering seed; a root with a thin red bark, white within, sweet; a sunny, hilly habitat) and how many names it carries. He reports the aphrodisiac use with "it is said" and "it is related", and recording is not endorsing. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a root of report. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a sweet orchid root with a reputation that Dioscorides passes on at a distance. The useful lesson is his method of telling a root by colour, bark and taste, and the abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Wine with a root of unknown dose is not harmless. Many wild orchids are protected by law in many places and their tubers must not be dug; the particular status was not researched. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion that includes a plant of another genus, so no wild plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
