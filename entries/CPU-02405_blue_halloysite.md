# Blue halloysite — yug (Clark 1995, "a blue type of Halloysite"); Yug (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)

## *A blue stone that Clark places with the red stone btsag as the two kinds of "Halloysite" that cure eye diseases, bone fever and dry lymph accumulations; the Men-Tsee-Khang text says "Ligbu mik, Tsag and Yug cure eye disorders and inflammation of the bones and dries up chhuser". Identification: a blue clay-like stone that the translator glosses as a blue type of halloysite (a clay mineral), from his English gloss only; the mineralogy is not established by the texts; confidence low* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry** in the sense that only the Four Tantras tradition, in two English renderings, names the substance. The two texts read are Barry Clark's 1995 *The Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20) and the Men-Tsee-Khang edition of 2011 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`). The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder, "blue halloysite", from `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` line 155 ("Yug") and Clark line 852.

**rGyud-bzhi, Clark 1995 (FMT).** Chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", Clark line 792 onward) gives the powers of stone medicines at line 816 onward. The entry is at line 852: "btsag: Red Ochre: Halloysite: [has two names and is found in the form of a red stone]. yug [is a blue type of Halloysite; red and blue Halloysite] cure eye diseases and bone fever and dry lymph [accumulations]." In Clark's translation, material in square brackets is taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries (translator's note, line 82), so the description of *btsag* as a red stone and the statement that *yug* is the blue type are commentary, and the root text's statement is that the stones cure eye diseases, bone fever and (dry) lymph. Clark's heading for *btsag* gives two English glosses, "Red Ochre" and "Halloysite", so the translator himself is not settled between an iron-oxide earth and a clay. The word "Halloysite" is applied by Clark to four stones in this list. *be.snabs* (line 824) is "Yellow Halloysite", which cures fractures and glandular growths and stimulates flesh growth; *rdo.klad* (line 837) is "Halloysite", "a white 'pimply' stone that looks like a brain" that maintains the brain and stimulates flesh growth; *btsag* is the red; *yug* is the blue. Yellow halloysite has its own row on the spine, and the others are not claimed here.

**rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (FMT).** The efficacy of stone medicines (line 3356): "Ligbu mik, Tsag and Yug cure eye disorders and inflammation of the bones and dries up chhuser." Here *Tsag* corresponds by sound to Clark's *btsag* and *Yug* to *yug*, and the sentence agrees with Clark's (eye disorders, bone inflammation, drying of lymph or *chhuser*). The text puts the stone with *Ligbu mik*, which Clark has as *lig.bu.mig*, malachite (line 849). That pairing of names is by sound and sentence, not stated in either text. A search of the Sushruta, Dioscorides, Shennong and Ebers texts on disk found no passage for this stone under this name. Shennong's "five-colour halloysite" (*Wu Se Shi Zhi*, Yang's "Halloysitum", filed at II_124) belongs to another tradition and is not claimed here.

### Active Components

The sources name no constituent. In general terms, halloysite is a kaolin-group clay mineral, a hydrated aluminium silicate, usually white to pale, and a blue colour in a clay-like stone would come from an impurity such as copper or iron. The translator's gloss may not describe the Tibetan stone's real nature, since the same heading also glosses its red companion as "Red Ochre". ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Halloysite is a clay mineral in the kaolin group; clays are generally low in toxicity but a stone of unknown colour source may carry metals such as copper. The conditions the texts name (eye diseases, bone fever, lymph accumulations) are not translated into modern diagnoses. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan medicine, rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* This stone has no cultivation status in the texts read; it is a stone medicine for the eyes, bones and lymph. The frame it sits inside is the tantra's own account of disease: Clark's text says that "the sole cause of all disease is said to be ignorance due to lack of understanding of the meaning of selflessness" (line 440), that "from ignorance arise the three poisons of attachment, hatred and closedmindedness whence are produced in turn the humors wind, bile and phlegm" (line 441), and that the humors are "rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration respectively" (line 51). A stone medicine acts on a disorder that the text places downstream of that root cause. No source retrieved assigns the stone a place in essence-extraction (*bcud len*) or any practice, and the Codex asserts none.

**Practitioner's note.** A blue stone for the eyes, bones and lymph, in a list of stones whose identification is the translator's guess. Nothing in the record supports a cultivation use, and a cultivator should not take or apply it.

### Contraindications

- **Eye.** Do not apply any mineral of unknown composition to the eye.
- **Metal content.** A blue colour can come from copper. Do not swallow it. [USER: verify]
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** No statement in the sources retrieved. Avoid. [USER: verify]
- **Identity.** The mineral is the translator's gloss, and the heading for the red companion gives two different glosses. [USER: verify]
