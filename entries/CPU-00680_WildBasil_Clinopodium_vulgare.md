# Wild Basil — Basil Thyme, Wild Basil, Dog Mint

## *Clinopodium vulgare* L. | Family: Lamiaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, CM

**Energetic Classification:** Warm and dry (second degree) — aromatic, astringent, mildly resolving in the Galenic-Unani reckoning

**Training Phase:** Purification

---

### Origin & Tradition

A modest perennial of the mint family, *Clinopodium vulgare* fringes hedgerows, dry grassland, and open woodland margins across Europe, the Mediterranean basin, western Asia, and North Africa. Its whorled pink-lilac flowers and faintly thyme-scented foliage made it a recognisable, if unglamorous, member of the aromatic Lamiaceae — the same family that gave the ancient pharmacy its mints, thymes, savouries, and marjorams. Because so many of these plants share the volatile-oil signature, wild basil was often folded into the broad class of "hot herbs" used to warm a cold stomach, move stagnant digestion, and dry weeping tissue.

**Assyrian (AT):** The cuneiform materia medica of Mesopotamia catalogued dozens of aromatic garden and field herbs pressed into service for gastric complaints, wound washes, and fumigations. Wild basil belongs to this stratum of humble Lamiaceae astringents — small, resinous, strongly scented plants gathered for poultices and infusions rather than reserved as rarities. The tradition prized such herbs for their capacity to "bind" loose tissue and quiet a griping belly, an early recognition of the astringent-carminative pairing that later systems formalised.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** The *De Materia Medica* treats a cluster of wild aromatic mints and calaminths (the *kalaminthe* group, to which *Clinopodium* is botanically close) as warming, drying agents. Dioscorides directs them for flatulence, sluggish digestion, and as external applications to draw and dry. The doctrine is consistent: an aromatic bitter that warms the interior, breaks wind, and tightens relaxed flesh.

**Avicenna (CM):** In the Galenic-Unani synthesis carried forward by the *Qanun*, aromatic mints of this type are classed hot and dry, deployed against cold phlegmatic stomachs, colic, and to encourage the resolution of stagnant humours. The astringency is read as a tightening (*qabid*) virtue; the aroma as a dispersing (*muhallil*) one — the two together making the herb a carminative that also stanches.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The plant's symbolic gesture is *containment through warmth* — it does not purge or flood, it warms, dries, and draws the edges of a wound or a slackened gut back together. That folk-medical intuition maps cleanly onto its real chemistry: rosmarinic and caffeic acids are astringent, protein-binding phenolics, while the volatile terpenes are genuinely carminative and antimicrobial. The "binding" the ancients described is, quite literally, phenolic tannage of exposed tissue.

### Active Components

- **Rosmarinic acid** — the signature Lamiaceae phenolic; astringent, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory.
- **Caffeic and chlorogenic acids** — confirmed in aerial parts by NMR profiling; COX-2-modulating phenolics.
- **Catechin and other flavonoids** — flavan-3-ols and flavone glycosides contributing antioxidant and vascular-tightening astringency.
- **Volatile oil** — mono- and sesquiterpenes underlying the thyme-like aroma and carminative, antimicrobial action.
- **Triterpenoid saponins and ursane-type acids** — reported from the genus, contributing to anti-inflammatory activity.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Wild basil sits in the **Purification** phase as a gentle astringent-carminative — a "tightening and settling" herb rather than a forceful cleanser. It is indicated where digestion is loose, cold, and gassy: the bloated, gurgling stomach that wants warming and drawing-together rather than stimulation. Externally, the phenolic-rich infusion serves as a wash for weeping skin, minor ulceration, and inflamed mucosa, consistent with its long folk use for wounds and skin ulcers. In Kenetica terms it *closes and dries a leaking system* — reducing oozing, damping low-grade inflammation, and restoring tone to slack tissue before deeper tempering work begins.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the pharmacology of wild basil is now moderately well characterised for a folk herb. Amirova et al. (2018), *Food and Chemical Toxicology*, used 1H- and 2D-NMR to profile *C. vulgare* aerial parts, identifying caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, and catechin, and showed that the whole extract together with caffeic and chlorogenic acids inhibited zymosan-induced **COX-2 expression** in murine neutrophils in vitro and in vivo — a mechanistic correlate of the traditional anti-inflammatory use. DOI: [10.1016/j.fct.2018.11.054](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fct.2018.11.054). A 2024 review by Qureshi et al., *Heliyon*, synthesised the phytochemical and pharmacological literature, documenting antioxidant, antibacterial, antiviral, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and enzyme-inhibitory activities and noting the plant's historical use against gastric ulcers, diabetes, and inflammation. DOI: [10.1016/j.heliyon.2024.e24781](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2024.e24781).

**Evidence grade: Preclinical / traditional.** Mechanistic (COX-2, antioxidant) and phytochemical data are real and reproducible in cell and animal models, but there are no controlled human clinical trials. Claims of therapeutic efficacy in humans remain traditional and are not established.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani (as attested):** Hot and dry (≈ second degree); astringent (*qabid*) and dispersing (*muhallil*); carminative, wound-drying.
- **TCM (by analogy, not classical attribution):** would read as warm, aromatic, and mildly astringent — resolving damp-cold in the middle burner. Not a canonical TCM herb; noted here only as a cross-system parallel.
- **Ayurvedic (by analogy):** *rasa* pungent/astringent (katu/kashaya); *virya* heating (ushna); *vipaka* pungent (katu); kapha-reducing. Comparative only — not a classical dravya.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** In the cuneiform materia medica wild basil belongs to the broad stratum of humble, strongly scented Lamiaceae astringents gathered for gastric complaints, wound washes, and fumigations — herbs valued for their capacity to "bind" loose tissue and quiet a griping belly. Mesopotamian illness was classified by causal agency (a divine hand, a ghost, an oath), so a substance was partly known by what agency its application opposed; but the standing blocking warning is in force for every Assyrian substance identification and tablet reference, and the *asû*/*āšipu* record here is one of use, not of any status assigned to the plant for tending a person's vitality. No cultivation status is claimed. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system into which a cultivation status for this plant could be set; the *calaminth* group to which *Clinopodium* is botanically close is given a straightforwardly therapeutic account — a warming, drying aromatic for flatulence, sluggish digestion, and external drawing applications. What the tradition genuinely offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention: knowing the true aromatic from the mimic, judging the plant by its scent and season of gathering, guarding against adulteration in a genus where many near-identical mints and savouries were substituted for one another. That is a real practice of provenance and sensory assay, not a doctrine of the vital body. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The Galenic-Unani synthesis reads aromatic mints of this type as hot and dry, at once astringent (*qabid*) and dispersing (*muhallil*) — a carminative that also stanches, deployed against cold phlegmatic stomachs, colic, and the resolution of stagnant humours. The strongest cultivation idea in the Unani corpus is the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture, the finite vital endowment whose depletion *is* ageing; but that is a longevity frame of the whole regimen, not a status the tradition assigns to a warming digestive astringent, and no *mufarrih* (gladdening, *ruh*-strengthening) role is claimed for this plant — nor would one be published from a source outside the *Kitāb al-Adwiya al-Qalbiyya*. The herb is attested therapeutically with no cultivation status recorded. **(b) framed.**

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing special with wild basil — it is not a substance one takes to build or conserve vitality. It is a modest warming astringent-carminative worked for a defined job and then set down: an infusion to warm a cold, gassy, loose stomach and draw slack tissue together, a stronger cooled wash for weeping skin and inflamed gums. Across all three traditions the record is of use, not of standing. Take it for the leaking, griping system it settles, and stop when the system is settled.

### Contraindications

Generally regarded as a low-risk food-grade aromatic when used in culinary and traditional infusion amounts. Aromatic Lamiaceae with volatile oils are conventionally avoided in concentrated or essential-oil form during pregnancy; use only culinary/infusion amounts if pregnant or breastfeeding [USER: verify]. Astringent phenolics may reduce absorption of iron and some minerals if taken heavily with meals. No robust human safety data exist for high-dose or extract-concentrated use — do not assume the safety of a tea extends to concentrated extracts. Discontinue if allergic reaction (Lamiaceae cross-sensitivity) occurs.
