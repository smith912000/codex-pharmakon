# Donkey liver — Hepar Oneiron, "Ass's Liver" (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 2-42)
## *The liver of the ass, as Dioscorides' chapter 2-42 is headed in the Osbaldeston English. The edition prints no binomial for this chapter* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The text on disk is the 2000 English edition of Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`). The chapter is 2-42, headed "HEPAR ONEIRON" with the English "Ass's Liver" (line 5109; the row in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, "2-42 (line 5109)"). It sits in Book Two, which the author's preface (line 4904) describes as dealing with "living creatures and of honey and of milk and of animal fat" and the cereals and pot herbs. The whole chapter, in the English, is one sentence (line 5111):

"An ass's liver eaten roasted is good for epilepsy but it should be taken while fasting."

That is all the chapter says. It names one preparation (roasted, then eaten), one complaint (epilepsy), and one condition on taking it (while fasting). It gives no quantity, no source animal beyond "ass", no season, no sex or age of the animal, and no account of how it works. The edition prints no binomial for the chapter.

The neighbouring liver chapters, which I read, show how the author treats the organ as a class: the goat's liver (2-47, line 5126) is roasted and its dripping fluid is rubbed on for night blindness, and "They say that epilepsy may be discerned by eating the liver (especially) of the buck goat"; the boar's liver (2-48, line 5129) is taken dried in wine against the bites of snakes and beasts; the liver of a mad dog (2-49, line 5133) is eaten roasted by those bitten, "thought to keep them safe from the fear of water"; and a seagull's liver (2-50, line 5139) expels the afterbirth. The author uses "thought to" and "they say" for the last two and for the goat, and states the ass's liver in the plain voice.

Other ass materials in the same book, which I read:

- *Hooves* (2-44, line 5115): "Two spoonfuls of an ass's hoof that has been burnt (taken in a drink daily for many days) are said to cure epilepsy. Steeped in oil they dissolve scrofulous tumours [glandular swelling, goitres]; sprinkled on they heal chilblains." The same complaint as the liver, in the hedged voice "are said to".
- *Milk* (2-75, line 5236): "Cow's, asses' and mare's milk make the intestines more laxative and trouble it."
- *Dung* (2-98, line 5372): "The dung of asses as well as horses staunches bleeding (whether used raw or burnt) mixed with vinegar." See III_83.
- *Urine* (2-99, line 5383): "Urine of an ass is said to cure inflamed kidneys."

The chapter on the spavins of horses (2-45, line 5119) also says they are "said to cure epilepsy", so the author names epilepsy for three equine remedies in a row (2-42, 2-44, 2-45).

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms liver is an organ rich in protein, iron, vitamin A and B vitamins, and it stores vitamin A and copper, which can reach high concentration in the liver of some animals. Cooking does not remove stored vitamin A. Nothing more specific can be said without a source.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records the roasted liver of the ass as good for epilepsy, to be taken fasting. The Codex does not recommend it. Epilepsy is a medical condition with effective treatment, and an unproven animal remedy must never replace that treatment or delay it. Liver of any animal is a food in many places, and needs no protocol from this Codex.

### Preparation & Dosing

The one preparation given is that the liver is "eaten roasted", and the one rule is that "it should be taken while fasting" (line 5111). No quantity, frequency, or length of course is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. No animal liver is an established treatment for epilepsy; epilepsy is treated with anti-seizure medicines and, in some cases, other medical or surgical treatment, under a doctor. The idea that a diseased organ can be cured by eating the matching organ of an animal is a historical doctrine, not an established mechanism. Raw or undercooked liver can carry bacteria and parasites, including hepatitis E virus, and any animal liver can accumulate heavy metals and fat-soluble vitamins. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is supplied here. The chapter is one sentence about a remedy for epilepsy. It assigns the ass's liver no cultivation status, and no passage read connects it to *pneuma* or to any practice. What the chapter does offer a reader is a small discipline of attention, the instruction that the liver be roasted and taken fasting; that is a condition of the remedy and not a cultivation status.

### Contraindications

- **Epilepsy:** never replace or delay prescribed treatment with an animal remedy; seizure control matters, and a seizure that lasts more than five minutes is an emergency.
- **Infection and hygiene:** raw or undercooked liver of any animal is an infection risk (bacteria, parasites, hepatitis E virus); donkeys can carry zoonotic disease.
- **Vitamin A and metals:** liver can contain very high levels of vitamin A and of metals such as copper; this matters in pregnancy and in liver disease.
- **Ethics and law:** donkeys are working animals in many places and the slaughter of donkeys for parts is restricted or illegal in some regions. The Codex does not recommend harming any animal for these uses.
