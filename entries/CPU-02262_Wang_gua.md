# Wang gua — 王瓜 *wang gua*, "Wang melon" (also 土瓜 *tu gua*, "Earth Melon")

## *Trichosanthes cucumeroides* (Ser.) Maxim., the red-fruited, bitter-rooted climbing gourd that modern Chinese botany calls 王瓜; the identification is the modern standard one and is of low confidence for what the early text meant, because the commentary that would pin it down is damaged in the file* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts on disk.** A search of the other traditions' texts in `Sources/` for *Trichosanthes cucumeroides* found nothing; the Sushruta *patola* (*T. dioica*, entry I_372) is a different species. The entry rests on one tradition, in Sun Xingyan's 1799 reconstruction and Yang Shou-zhong's 1998 translation. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder from Yang under the name *Radix Trichosanthis Cucumeroidis*; this entry replaces it. A sibling entry, I_1536, treats 栝蒌根 (*T. kirilowii* root), a different Shennong monograph from the same genus.

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ), the Shennong text proper.** The entry sits in the middle classic (中经). Sun's 味 paragraph reads: "味苦，寒。主消渴内痹瘀血，月闭，寒热，酸疼，益气，俞聋。一名土瓜。生平泽。" ("Bitter, cold. It governs wasting-thirst, internal impediment and static blood, closure of the monthly [flow], cold and heat, aching pain; it boosts the qi; it [俞→愈] heals deafness. Another name: Earth Melon. It grows in level marshes.") The character 俞 is read here as 愈 "to heal", which is how Yang's translation takes it ("cures deafness"); the Codex notes that this is a reading and not what the character printed says. (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, middle classic, entry 王瓜 *wanggua*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 2195)

**Second witness, Yang 1998 (attributed to Yang).** Yang (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`, lines 3204-3207) gives: "bitter and cold. It mainly treats wasting thirst, internal impediment, blood stasis, menstrual block, cold and heat, and aching pain. It boosts the qi and cures deafness. Its other name is Tu Gua (Earth Melon). It grows in plains and swamps." This agrees with Sun and is a translation of the same text. Yang's file places the entry among the lower-class herbs ("PDF p.86"), where Sun places it in the middle classic; the Codex follows Sun and notes the difference. [USER: verify]

**Mingyi bielu layer (later additions, quoted by Sun).** Line 2197 reads: "生鲁地田野及人家坦墙间。三月采根，阴干。" ("It grows in the fields of the land of Lu and among people's low walls; the root is gathered in the third month and dried in the shade.") This is the only statement about where it grows or which part is used; the Shennong text proper names neither.

**Sun's own note (案), damaged.** Line 2199 is a long chain of citations about the name, with characters missing from the transcription at several points, so it can only be read in pieces. It cites the *Shuowen* ("…，王…也"), the *Guangya* ("葵茹、瓜…，王瓜也"), the *Xia xiaozheng* ("in the fourth month, 王…"), Guo Pu ("钩，…也，一名王瓜，实如…瓜，正赤，味苦" "…also called wang gua; the fruit is like a … melon, properly red, bitter in taste"), the *Yueling* ("the wang gua is born" 王瓜生), Zheng Xuan and Kong Yingda ("疑王…，则王瓜也" "if one suspects 王…, then it is wang gua"), a line of the *Guanzi* chapter on land ("五沙 … 白茎青秀以蔓", "white stem, green and flourishing, trailing"), and the *Bencao tujing* ("大…，即王…也"), and breaks off at "芴，亦谓之土瓜，自" ("…is also called Earth Melon, from…"). Of this, only Guo Pu's description is a plant description: a red, bitter fruit. The Codex reports it as printed and does not complete the lost characters.

**Identity.** Guo Pu's red bitter fruit, the trailing habit and the alias "Earth Melon" (土瓜) agree with a climbing gourd. The sibling 栝蒌 entry (I_1536) shares the melon-family frame, and the discussion of "王" names in its note (*Lüshi chunqiu*, "王善生", line 1865) touches the same name, but characters are lost there too and the Codex does not resolve it. *T. cucumeroides* is the modern assignment; the early text does not separate it from other trailing gourds. Confidence: low. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. As a general fact, *Trichosanthes* is a genus of the gourd family (Cucurbitaceae) and the roots of several species contain proteins and bitter triterpenoid compounds. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry, and nothing about its chemistry is asserted. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Shennong text lists a bitter, cold drug for wasting-thirst, blocked monthly flow, aches and deafness. These are the text's terms; the Codex does not translate "wasting-thirst", "internal impediment" or "static blood" into modern diagnoses and gives no protocol. Thirst, menstrual disorders and deafness need medical assessment. Yang's note 197 (line 3215) says only that the drug "is often used to drain heat, move the blood, and expel stasis", adding "precipitate water and promote lactation", which is a later commentary and not the Shennong text.

### Preparation & Dosing

The Shennong text proper gives no preparation. The Mingyi layer says the root is gathered in the third month and dried in the shade (line 2197). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Several *Trichosanthes* roots are used in modern Chinese pharmacy; *T. cucumeroides* is one of the species so named, which is a general fact and not a finding. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**SBJ.** The Shennong text prints "味苦，寒" (bitter, cold). The Wu Pu layer has no entry here and the Mingyi layer adds no flavour or temperature. The text proper prints no toxicity word. The middle classic's preface (line 1677) says its drugs are "non-toxic or toxic, to be weighed as is fit", so the grade does not settle the question.

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(b) Framed.* The entry stands in the middle classic (中经), whose preface (line 1677) says middle drugs "nourish the nature to correspond with man; non-toxic or toxic, weigh what is fit; for those who wish to check disease and supplement the thin [羸], take the middle classic". The entry has no 久服 (taken long) clause and no promise of lightening the body or lengthening life. Its one tonic-sounding phrase is 益气 ("boosts the qi"), a single action among the others and not a status. The grade of the class is a frame, not a cultivation claim for this drug. (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, middle classic, entry 王瓜 *wanggua*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 2195)

**Practitioner's note.** A middle-grade drug taken, in the text's frame, for a defined complaint and then set aside. Nothing in the entry supports a long-term practice, the plant behind the name is not firmly identified, and no dose is given. The honest practice is to read the entry and leave the plant alone.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. Gourd-family roots can be strongly active (some *Trichosanthes* roots are known to be unsafe in pregnancy), but that is a general statement about the genus and not something the sources say about this species, and the text itself lists "closure of the monthly [flow]" as an indication. Pregnancy should be treated as a contraindication unless a source says otherwise. [USER: verify] Nothing should be gathered or taken on the strength of this entry; a plant could not be matched to the name from the text read.
