# Epimedium alpinum — Epimedion (Dioscorides 4-19)

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this binomial. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed under the chapter heading, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line reads "Epimedium alpinum — Barrenwort, Bishop's Hat", and the row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The existing entry I_100 (*Epimedium brevicornu*, tags SBJ, FMT) is a different species with a different record, and it does not carry the DMM tag. The two are not merged here.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-19. EPIMEDION* at line 8180, SUGGESTED line at 8181, text at line 8182. The chapter stands in Book Four, "Other Herbs & Roots". The translators' name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 599. The chapter before it, 4-18 *MEDION* (line 8176), is a different plant. The next, 4-20 *XIPHION* (line 8183), is also different.

The whole chapter reads: "Epimedium has a stalk that is not great, with about ten or twelve leaves similar to cissus, (but it bears neither seed nor flowers); the roots are thin, black, stronglyscented and unsavoury to the taste. It grows in watery places. The leaves (pounded into small pieces with oil) make a poultice for the breasts so that they do not swell. The root causes barrenness. Three teaspoonfuls of the leaves pounded into small pieces, and taken as a drink in wine for three days after the menstrual flow purgation, keeps women from conception [birth control]. It is also called erineos, thrias, or polyrrhizon, while the Romans call it vindicta."

The description gives a stalk "not great", about ten or twelve leaves like *cissus*, no seed and no flowers, and thin black roots that are strongly scented and unsavoury. It grows in watery places. The printed uses are four. The leaves pounded with oil make a poultice to keep the breasts from swelling. The root "causes barrenness". Three teaspoonfuls of the leaves in wine, for three days after the menstrual purgation, keep women from conception, where the bracketed "[birth control]" is the translators' gloss. Three other names are printed: *erineos*, *thrias* and *polyrrhizon*, and the Roman name *vindicta*. The comparison plant *cissus* was not read for this entry.

The text records an anti-fertility use only. Nothing in it speaks of a tonic or of any other use, and nothing from other traditions' records of other species is read back into this chapter.

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. The genus *Epimedium* belongs to the barberry family (Berberidaceae), and some Asian species are known to contain prenylated flavonoids such as icariin. Whether the European species the translators suggest contains the same compounds was not checked, and nothing is stated here about the composition of Dioscorides' plant. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The uses Dioscorides records are a breast poultice and two anti-fertility uses, one a drink taken for three days after menstruation. The Codex gives no protocol and does not present an unidentified plant as a contraceptive. The record is of a belief in an ancient text, not of an effect.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives two preparations: the leaves "pounded into small pieces with oil" as a poultice, and "three teaspoonfuls of the leaves pounded into small pieces, and taken as a drink in wine for three days after the menstrual flow purgation". This is a dose printed in the source (line 8182) and is quoted as history, not as advice. No dose is given for the root. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints no hot, cold, moist or dry quality. The one taste it prints is of the roots: "unsavoury to the taste", and they are "stronglyscented" (the translation's printed spelling).

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter gives the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition: a plant told by its habit (watery places), its few large leaves, the absence of flower and seed, and the thin black, strong-smelling roots. It assigns the plant no status, and its record is of preventing swelling and conception. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The use Dioscorides records is one of limiting fertility, which is outside the aims of the Codex's cultivation register. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning, though it describes a root that "causes barrenness". Any plant said to affect conception must not be taken in pregnancy, or by anyone seeking to conceive, and this record must not be used as contraception. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so nothing should be gathered or taken on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
