### Origin & Tradition

Cinnamon holds the rare distinction of being simultaneously one of humanity's oldest documented medicines, one of its most commercially significant trade commodities for over three thousand years, and one of its most pharmacologically active culinary spices. The spice trade routes of antiquity — the Arab intermediaries who controlled the Red Sea passage, the Phoenician traders who brought Kinnamōmon to Greece and Rome, the Portuguese who navigated around the Cape of Good Hope specifically to seize control of the Ceylon (Sri Lanka) cinnamon monopoly in 1505 — were shaped in significant part by this single rhizome bark. That cinnamon could motivate the rerouting of global commerce speaks to the depth of demand, which was driven not only by flavor but by genuine perceived and actual medicinal power.

The name Kinnamōmon (κιννάμωμον) appears in Greek sources from at least the 7th century BCE. The Hebrew Qinnamon (קִנָּמוֹן) appears in the Old Testament — Exodus 30:23 lists it among the ingredients of the sacred anointing oil along with myrrh and calamus. The Arabic Qirfa (قرفة) derives from the verb for "peeling" or "bark stripping," describing the preparation method that has not changed in three millennia: the inner bark is peeled from young branches, left to curl as it dries into the characteristic quills that define the spice's commercial form.

**The species distinction is pharmacologically critical** and one of the most consequential source-identification questions in the entire Codex:

*Cinnamomum verum* (Ceylon cinnamon / true cinnamon): Native to Sri Lanka (formerly Ceylon); produces delicate, multi-layered quills of the inner bark; contains minimal coumarin (<0.004%); higher eugenol content; milder, sweeter flavor; more expensive and rarer in commerce. This is the form sanctioned for therapeutic doses without coumarin concerns.

*Cinnamomum cassia* / *C. aromaticum* (Chinese cassia / Cassia cinnamon): Native to South China and mainland Southeast Asia; produces a single thick scroll of outer and inner bark combined; contains coumarin at 1–12mg/kg depending on provenance — sufficient to exceed the EFSA Tolerable Daily Intake (0.1mg/kg bodyweight) at culinary consumption levels in susceptible individuals; stronger, more pungent flavor; dominates the commercial spice market in North America and Europe. This is the form used in the majority of clinical research on cinnamon's glycemic effects, but must be used with awareness of coumarin load at therapeutic doses.

In TCM, both *C. cassia* bark and *C. verum* bark are encompassed within the Rou Gui category, but Chinese pharmacy has historically and predominantly used *C. cassia* — it is the species referenced in the Bencao Gangmu and the classical formula literature.

**Chinese tradition** imposes a critical bifurcation between two anatomical portions of the cinnamon tree that determines the clinical application entirely:

Ròu Guì (肉桂 — "flesh/meat of the laurel"): The thick inner bark, stripped from mature trees (7–10 years old or more). HOT in thermal nature; pungent and sweet in taste; acts on the Kidney, Spleen, Heart, and Liver meridians. This is the deep constitutional Yang tonic — its primary indication is Kidney Yang deficiency with cold extremities, lower back weakness and pain, impotence, and failure of the Mingmen fire (命門火 — the Gate of Vital Fire, the constitutional Yang housed in the Kidney). Rou Gui is a principal component of Ba Wei Di Huang Wan (Eight-Ingredient Rehmannia Pill, Codex VIII_239) — the classical Yang-replenishing formula — and of You Gui Wan (Right-Returning Pill) for severe Kidney Yang exhaustion. Its action reaches the Jing level when combined with Kidney-nourishing herbs.

Guì Zhī (桂枝 — "laurel branch"): The young twigs of the same tree; warm (not hot) in nature; pungent and sweet; acts on the Heart, Lung, and Bladder meridians. Gui Zhi releases exterior cold by warming the channels and vessels; promotes the flow of Yang through the peripheral circulation; treats cold-invasion patterns where the exterior has not yet been resolved. It appears in the foundational formula Gui Zhi Tang (Cinnamon Twig Decoction) — arguably the most frequently modified formula in the entire Shang Han Lun. Gui Zhi warms the channels; Rou Gui warms the constitutional root. The choice between them determines the depth of action — surface-channel vs. Kidney-Ming Men — and must be made clinically rather than defaulting to one form.

This bifurcation has no precise parallel in Western botanical medicine and represents a sophisticated pharmacological insight: that the age of bark, the position on the tree (trunk vs. branch), and the processing method yield genuinely different pharmacological agents from the same botanical source.

**Ayurvedic tradition** classifies Tvak (त्वक्) as one of the five components of Pañcakola (the five spices: ginger, black pepper, long pepper, Plumbago root, Embelia fruit), and also as an independent herb with actions on all three doshas — pacifying Vata and Kapha while being monitored for excess Pitta aggravation. Dārucīnī (दारुचीनी — "wood/tree from China") reflects the historical import of the spice from Chinese territory into the Indian subcontinent, where it was valued as a digestive, a blood mover, and a Sattvic warming agent. The Ashtanga Hridayam references Tvak as beneficial for the heart and mind (Hridaya and Manas) as well as for digestive fire — an alignment with the modern finding of cinnamaldehyde's effect on brain tau protein and the TCM placement of Gui Zhi in the Heart meridian.

**Arabic and Persian tradition**: Dārṣīnī (درصيني — "Chinese cinnamon") appears throughout the Unani Tibb pharmacopoeia. Ibn Sina (Avicenna, Canon of Medicine, c. 1025 CE) classifies cinnamon as hot in the second degree, dry in the second degree; beneficial for the liver, stomach, and uterus; an antidote for cold poisons; and a warming aphrodisiac. He distinguishes Kinnamōmon (finer, Ceylon-type) from Qirfa (coarser, cassia-type) — a pharmacognostic distinction that aligns with the modern botanical differentiation. Al-Biruni's pharmacology cross-references cinnamon's warming properties with those of ginger and cloves, creating an early systematic classification of heating spices with complementary applications.

**Assyrian and ancient Near Eastern tradition**: Kasû (cinnamon) appears in Assyrian cuneiform medical texts as a warming and carminative agent; its use in ritual purification contexts (as in the Mosaic anointing oil formula) suggests a dual sacred-medical role common to highly potent aromatic substances in ancient pharmacopoeias.

**Tibetan Medicine (Sowa Rigpa)**: Rgyal shing (རྒྱལ་ཤིང — "king of trees") — cinnamon is classified as warming, sharp, and bitter in the Tibetan system; reduces Beken (phlegm-water) and rLung (wind) humors; activates digestive fire; useful for "cold-type" diseases including cold stomach, cold kidneys, and conditions arising from blocked or insufficient warming energy. The Tibetan application maps closely onto the Rou Gui indication for Kidney Yang deficiency.

**Egyptian and Classical Mediterranean tradition**: The Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE) references Cassia (identified as the commercial equivalent of cinnamon) in compound preparations. Dioscorides (De Materia Medica, Book I, Ch. 26) describes Kinnamōmon as warming and drying, beneficial for the stomach, the uterus, and as an antidote for snake bite — the last application reflecting the Galenic logic that hot-and-dry substances neutralize cold-and-wet venom.

### Active Components

- **Cinnamaldehyde (trans-cinnamaldehyde)** — Primary bioactive; constitutes 60–90% of cinnamon essential oil in both *C. verum* and *C. cassia*; NF-κB pathway inhibitor (anti-inflammatory); potent antifungal (complete inhibition of *Candida albicans* biofilm at 0.5% concentration; IC50 against *C. glabrata* lower than fluconazole in several studies); antibacterial against food pathogens (E. coli, Salmonella, Listeria); insulin sensitizer via GLUT4 vesicle translocation and AMPK activation; vasorelaxant via endothelial nitric oxide (NO) release; anti-proliferative in tumor cell lines; documented inhibitor of tau protein aggregation in Alzheimer's model systems (Ma et al. 2013 — dissolves pre-formed tau filaments in vitro); thermogenic via UCP1 upregulation in brown adipose tissue (Jiang et al. 2017).

- **Procyanidins Type A (condensed tannins)** — Principal insulin-sensitizing mechanism in *C. cassia*; activate insulin receptor tyrosine kinase (IRS-1 phosphorylation); promote GLUT4 translocation to plasma membrane independent of insulin; antioxidant via lipid peroxidation inhibition; anti-platelet. The procyanidin content is higher in cassia than in Ceylon, partly explaining why cassia shows stronger glycemic effects in clinical trials despite the coumarin liability.

- **Cinnamic acid** — Anti-inflammatory (NF-κB modulation); documented anti-tumor activity in multiple cancer cell lines; hepatoprotective; precursor in biosynthesis of flavonoids and lignans.

- **Eugenol** (higher concentration in *C. verum* than *C. cassia*) — Selective COX-2 inhibitor; local anesthetic (mechanism: sodium channel blockade — the same target as lidocaine); anti-platelet; antifungal; anti-tumor; mild TRPV1 agonist. The higher eugenol content of Ceylon cinnamon contributes to its superior GI-protective profile relative to cassia.

- **Cinnamyl acetate** — Antifungal; anti-tumor activity in experimental models; contributes to the characteristic floral note of Ceylon cinnamon.

- **Linalool** — Monoterpene alcohol shared with lavender and other aromatics; anxiolytic at low doses via GABAergic modulation; sedative in animal models; anti-inflammatory; contributes to the calming-warming integration that makes cinnamon suitable for CYCLE-OFF and sleep-adjacent protocols.

- **Coumarin** (present in *C. cassia* only; essentially absent in *C. verum*) — Anticoagulant (precursor to dicoumarol); hepatotoxic at elevated doses in individuals carrying CYP2A6 loss-of-function polymorphisms who cannot efficiently metabolize coumarin to safe hydroxylated metabolites; EFSA established TDI = 0.1mg/kg bodyweight; for a 70kg individual this is 7mg/day maximum; 1 teaspoon of commercially purchased cassia cinnamon powder contains approximately 5–12mg coumarin depending on provenance. This is the defining safety differential between the two species and determines which form is appropriate for therapeutic (>2g/day) protocols.

- **β-Caryophyllene** — Sesquiterpene; selective CB2 cannabinoid receptor agonist (anti-inflammatory, analgesic via endocannabinoid pathway without psychoactive effect); shared with black pepper and cannabis; contributes to the anti-inflammatory and Yang-channel-warming effects.

- **Camphor, α-Pinene** — Antimicrobial volatile components; contribute to the characteristic penetrating quality of the essential oil and to respiratory tract effects (bronchodilatory, mucoexpectorant).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Cinnamon's application in the Kenetica framework spans three phases, each exploiting a different layer of the compound's pharmacological depth.

**[BUILD] — Anabolic / Loading Phase:** The primary application during BUILD is metabolic optimization and nutrient partitioning via the insulin-sensitizing mechanism. Cinnamaldehyde-driven AMPK activation and procyanidin-driven GLUT4 translocation create a state of enhanced insulin sensitivity that, during high-calorie loading phases, directs glucose preferentially toward muscle glycogen replenishment rather than lipid storage. This is the pharmacological foundation of cinnamon's BUILD application: not anabolism per se, but the efficiency of substrate routing under anabolic conditions. The documented FBG reductions of 11–29mg/dL and HOMA-IR improvements of 15–25% at 1–6g/day cassia represent clinically meaningful insulin sensitization for athletic populations consuming carbohydrate in excess of sedentary recommendations. Adding 1–3g Ceylon cinnamon to post-training carbohydrate meals is a simple, low-risk intervention with a meaningful effect size. The warming-Yang property (Rou Gui context) additionally supports the circulatory delivery of substrates to peripheral musculature — the "warming channels and vessels" function of TCM has a modern correlate in cinnamaldehyde's vasorelaxant NO-release mechanism.

**[PRIME] — Pre-Training / Activation Phase:** The thermogenic effect of cinnamaldehyde via UCP1 upregulation in brown adipose tissue (Jiang et al. 2017) provides a mild but documented thermogenic contribution to pre-training activation. In cold environments this is directly relevant — cinnamon pre-training in cold-weather or cold-water contexts reduces the thermoregulatory cost of warming, preserving energy for performance. The Gui Zhi (twig) preparation is specifically indicated here: its Lung-Heart-Bladder meridian action and exterior-warming function make it the correct TCM form for surface-level circulatory priming and athletic warm-up support, in contrast to the deeper Rou Gui (bark) used in constitutional protocols. A Gui Zhi — Sheng Jiang (cinnamon twig — fresh ginger) decoction before cold-exposure training represents a classical pairing for exterior Yang activation, repurposed into a performance context.

**[CYCLE-OFF] — Deload / Constitutional Restoration Phase:** This is where Rou Gui's full depth of action becomes primary. The Kidney Yang — Ming Men fire relationship means that in deload phases following extreme training volume (which depletes Jing through the TCM model of excessive taxation of the constitutional reserve), Rou Gui in formula context supports Ming Men fire reconstitution. The classical pairing is with Shu Di Huang (steamed Rehmannia, Codex I_198) in Ba Wei Di Huang Wan proportions — the foundational Kidney Yang restoration formula. The linalool content (anxiolytic/sedative) and the warming-deep effects collectively contribute to the sleep-quality improvement and parasympathetic restoration that is the physiological target of CYCLE-OFF. The tau-inhibition and neuroprotective effects of cinnamaldehyde are relevant in extended CYCLE-OFF recovery protocols where CNS fatigue and cognitive dimming from high-load training are present. The anti-fungal action (cinnamaldehyde / eugenol) is secondarily relevant: high-volume training periods that suppress immune function frequently manifest gut dysbiosis with *Candida* overgrowth; cinnamon in a CYCLE-OFF digestive protocol addresses this without the systemic disruption of pharmaceutical antifungals.

### Preparation & Dosing

**Ceylon Cinnamon Powder (*C. verum* — preferred for therapeutic doses):**
- Dose: 1–3g/day (approximately ½–1½ teaspoon) — safe for long-term use; coumarin-free
- Timing: with meals; most effective added to carbohydrate-containing foods for glycemic benefit
- Vehicle: can be mixed into oatmeal, warm drinks, protein preparations; fat-containing meals enhance absorption of lipophilic constituents
- Best use: long-term glycemic support (BUILD), general daily supplementation

**Cassia Cinnamon Powder (*C. cassia* — clinical glycemic protocols, short-term):**
- Dose: 1–6g/day for glycemic indication — not to exceed 8 weeks at therapeutic dose without liver enzyme monitoring due to coumarin load
- Note: for a 70kg individual, 6g cassia at 10mg/kg coumarin content = 60mg coumarin/day — approximately 8.5x the EFSA TDI. Use Ceylon for protocols exceeding 4–6 weeks.
- Best use: short acute glycemic intervention; culinary quantities (≤1g/day) are within EFSA TDI for most individuals

**Standardized Aqueous Extract (10:1, cinnamaldehyde-standardized):**
- Dose: 120–240mg extract (equivalent to 1.2–2.4g raw bark) twice daily
- Provides consistent cinnamaldehyde and procyanidin dose without raw powder coumarin variability
- Best use: clinical glycemic protocols; antifungal adjunct; extended supplementation where consistency required

**Rou Gui Decoction (TCM — Kidney Yang protocol):**
- Dose: 1.5–4.5g in decoction (lower doses than twig; bark is hotter)
- Add at the end of decoction (last 5 min) to preserve volatile cinnamaldehyde content
- Combined with Shu Di Huang 24g, Shan Zhu Yu 12g, Shan Yao 12g, Fu Zi 3g for Ba Wei Di Huang Wan base
- Best use: CYCLE-OFF constitutional Yang restoration

**Gui Zhi Decoction (TCM — exterior warming / circulatory):**
- Dose: 6–9g twig in decoction (higher dose than Rou Gui is appropriate as twig is warm, not hot)
- Classical Gui Zhi Tang: Gui Zhi 9g, Bai Shao 9g, Sheng Jiang 9g, Da Zao 12 pieces, Gan Cao 6g
- Best use: PRIME phase circulatory activation; cold-exposure priming; channel-warming in cold patterns

**Essential Oil (diluted, topical):**
- 0.5–1% dilution in carrier oil; topical anti-fungal, warming liniment for joint pain
- NOT for internal use at therapeutic concentration due to mucosal irritation at undiluted levels

### Scientific Correlation

**Glycemic / Insulin Sensitization:**
- Allen RW et al. (2013, meta-analysis, 10 RCTs, n=543): Cinnamon (1–6g/day, predominantly *C. cassia*) significantly reduced fasting blood glucose −11.09 mg/dL (95% CI: −19.8 to −2.3; p<0.001), total cholesterol −15.60 mg/dL, LDL cholesterol −9.42 mg/dL, triglycerides −29.59 mg/dL vs. placebo; HDL not significantly changed.
- Davis PA & Yokoyama W (2011, meta-analysis, 8 RCTs): Cinnamon consumption associated with significant reduction in FBG (mean −0.49 mmol/L), LDL (−0.18 mmol/L), and total cholesterol (−0.16 mmol/L).
- Mechanism studies: Anderson RA et al. (2004): Water-soluble polyphenols from cinnamon (procyanidins) activate insulin receptor kinase 18-fold in 3T3-L1 adipocytes; inhibit glycogen synthase kinase-3β; upregulate GLUT4 expression.
- Jarvill-Taylor KJ et al. (2001): Type A procyanidins from cinnamon mimic insulin signaling and increase glucose uptake in 3T3-L1 adipocytes.

**Anti-fungal / Antimicrobial:**
- Matan N et al. (2006): Cinnamon essential oil at 0.5% completely inhibits *Candida albicans* biofilm formation; more potent than clove oil at equivalent concentrations.
- Pinto E et al. (2009): Cinnamaldehyde and eugenol effective against *Candida* species including fluconazole-resistant *C. glabrata* and *C. krusei* strains; minimum inhibitory concentrations of 0.025–0.1%.
- Baratta MT et al. (1998): Cinnamon leaf and bark oils show broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against 25 food-borne pathogens.

**Neuroprotective / Anti-tau:**
- Frydman-Marom A et al. (2011): Cinnamon extract (CEppt) inhibits tau and amyloid-β aggregation in Alzheimer's model systems; one of the first systematic demonstrations of a common spice component as a disease-modifying agent in neurodegeneration.
- Ma Q et al. (2013): Trans-cinnamaldehyde and cinnamaldehyde inhibit tau aggregation and dissolve pre-formed paired helical filament tau in vitro; autophagy induction via AMPK-mTOR pathway proposed as mechanism.

**Thermogenic:**
- Jiang J et al. (2017): Cinnamaldehyde activates thermogenesis in adipocytes via upregulation of UCP1 (uncoupling protein 1) expression; Fgf21 upregulation confirmed; brown adipose tissue activation demonstrated in mice fed cinnamaldehyde; systemic thermogenic effect measured. Proposed as a dietary intervention for obesity and metabolic syndrome.

**Anti-inflammatory:**
- Verspohl EJ et al. (2011): Cinnamaldehyde inhibits NF-κB translocation in LPS-stimulated macrophages; downstream reduction in TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β.
- Hagenlocher Y et al. (2015): Cinnamon extract reduces intestinal inflammation markers in experimental colitis model; mechanistically distinct from NSAID pathway.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**TCM — THE CRITICAL BIFURCATION:**
- Ròu Guì (肉桂 — Inner bark, mature tree): HOT; pungent, sweet. Organs: Kidney, Spleen, Heart, Liver. Actions: supplements Kidney Yang and Ming Men fire; disperses cold and relieves pain; warms channels; promotes movement of Qi and Blood in the channels; guides fire back to its source (引火歸元 — yǐn huǒ guī yuán) in cases of false heat from Yang deficiency. This last action — where Rou Gui is used to treat apparent heat symptoms arising from depleted Yang failing to anchor — represents a highly sophisticated clinical concept with no Western pharmacological parallel yet identified. Dose in decoction: 1.5–4.5g (added late to preserve volatiles). Key formula: Ba Wei Di Huang Wan, You Gui Wan, Gui Fu Li Zhong Wan.
- Guì Zhī (桂枝 — Young twigs): WARM; pungent, sweet. Organs: Heart, Lung, Bladder. Actions: releases exterior wind-cold by warming and unblocking; warms and unblocks the channels and vessels; assists Heart Yang; resolves Bladder Qi transformation. Dose in decoction: 3–9g. Key formula: Gui Zhi Tang, Gui Zhi Fu Ling Wan, Wen Jing Tang.
- Clinical rule: For constitutional Yang building (CYCLE-OFF, Ba Wei protocols, Kidney Yang restoration), use Rou Gui. For external cold invasion, channel warming, and PRIME activation, use Gui Zhi. Confusing the two forms is a clinically significant error.

**Ayurveda:**
- Rasa (taste): Kaṭu (pungent), Madhura (sweet), Tikta (bitter)
- Vīrya (potency): Uṣṇa (heating)
- Vipāka (post-digestive): Kaṭu (pungent)
- Dosha effects: Vāta ↓ Kapha ↓ (reduces); Pitta ↑ in excess
- Karma (actions): Dīpana (kindles digestive fire), Pācana (aids digestion), Hṛdya (cardiac tonic), Vedanāsthāpana (analgesic), Krimighna (anti-parasitic)
- Dhātu affinity: Rasa, Rakta (blood), Māṃsa (muscle), Medas (fat/adipose)
- Srotāmsi: Annavaha (digestive), Raktavaha (circulatory), Hridaya (cardiac)
- Particularly valued in Sāra (essence) and Bala (strength) building formulations alongside Ashwagandha (Codex I_248) and Shatavari (Codex I_218).

**Tibetan Medicine:**
- Rgyal shing (རྒྱལ་ཤིང): Taste — hot, bitter; Post-digestive — hot. Reduces Beken (phlegm-water) and rLung (wind). Increases mKhris-pa (bile/fire) in excess — monitor in Pitta/mKhris-pa constitutions. Used in digestive fire formulations including Padma digestive compound preparations. The "king of trees" designation reflects its role as the primary warming tree-bark medicine in the Tibetan system, parallel to Rou Gui's Ming Men fire role in TCM.

**Unani:**
- Dārṣīnī / Qirfa: Hot in second degree, dry in second degree. Muqawwī al-Bāh (tonifies vital force and sexual function); Muqawwī al-Kabid (tonifies liver); Mufattiḥ (opens obstructions); Muḥarrik al-shahwa (stimulates appetite). Recommended as maʿjūn (electuary) with honey for cold constitutional types and male reproductive insufficiency.

### Cultivation Register

**Chinese (TCM).** The *san pin* 三品 grading is the tradition's own cultivation hierarchy, and its superior class is defined by function rather than potency: those drugs "mainly nourish life and correspond to heaven... they are nontoxic," as against the lower class, which attacks disease over a short course (*Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing*; on the tripartite scheme and its transmission through Tao Hongjing, Unschuld, *Medicine in China: A History of Pharmaceutics*, Univ. of California Press, 1986). Cinnamon (*guì* 桂) is commonly reported to sit in that superior class, but **the placement could not be confirmed from named scholarship or a scholarly translation in the sources retrieved**, and it is recorded here as unattested rather than asserted. Two further cautions belong on the record: the Ròu Guì / Guì Zhī bifurcation set out above is a **later bencao and formulary development**, not a distinction the Han-period classic makes; and *yang sheng* 養生 is a conservational project, so even a confirmed superior grade would license long-term nourishing use, not dose escalation.

**Ayurveda (SS).** *Tvak* / *Dārusitā* is securely attested in the Indian materia medica with the *rasa*–*vīrya*–*vipāka* profile given above, and it appears in *bala*- and *sāra*-building compounds as a supporting *dīpana* agent. **No formal *rasāyana* or *vājīkaraṇa* status is attested for cinnamon in the sources retrieved**, and appearance inside a *rasāyana* formula is not the same claim as carrying the status itself. Nothing here licenses an *ojas* claim; *ojas* is the depletable final distillate of the *dhātus* and is not to be attached to a warming digestive on inference.

**Tibetan (FMT).** *Rgyal shing* (རྒྱལ་ཤིང) is attested as a warming digestive-fire medicine acting on *bad kan* and *rlung*, as recorded above. **No *bcud len* (essence-extraction) attestation was found**, and no formal category of "substances for subtle-body practice" exists in this system to place it in. The *nyes pa* are derived from the three poisons, so pathology here is at root a spiritual condition — but medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice rather than reaching that root. Cinnamon's standing is therapeutic within the third branch.

**Unani / Greco-Arabic (CM).** The strongest cultivation idea in this tradition is that innate heat (*ḥarārat gharīziyya*) continuously consumes radical moisture (*ruṭūbat gharīziyya*) as a flame consumes lamp oil — a **finite vital endowment**, so senescence *is* its depletion and longevity practice is **conservation, not acquisition** (see the analysis of innate heat in Avicenna's *Canon*, Springer, 2025; and the Unani geriatric literature on *ḥarārat* and *ruṭūbat* decline with age). Dārṣīnī / Qirfa is classed hot and dry in the second degree, and the *muqawwī* language recorded above belongs to the **pharmacological register of strengthening drugs, not to a cultivation status** — the two are different claims and are kept apart here. No *mufarriḥ* attribution is made: that class belongs to the *Kitāb al-Adwiya al-Qalbiyya*, not the *Canon*, and no constituent list is published. No *Canon* chapter reference is given, the standard English adaptation being unreliable.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Cinnamon and cassia occupy the aromatics of Book I, and Dioscorides offers no subtle-body system — the Galenic three spirits are later and post-Dioscoridean, and *pneuma* is context, not a mechanism to hang on this bark. What the text does offer a cultivator is a **discipline of attention**: provenance, season of gathering, sensory assay, and the detection of adulteration, which Riddle identifies as central to his empirical method and which the text presses hardest on drugs not grown locally (Riddle, *Dioscorides on Pharmacy and Medicine*, Univ. of Texas Press, 1985; Beck's translation, Olms-Weidmann). Cinnamon — the paradigm long-distance traded aromatic of the ancient Mediterranean — is exactly such a good. **The specific wording of his cinnamon and cassia chapters was not verified directly here**, so no grade ranking, origin list or test is quoted.

**Egyptian (EP).** No cultivation framing is attested. Aromatic barks appear in temple incense and unguent contexts, but two separations must hold. First, the identification of Egyptian aromatic terms (*ti-šps* and similar) with *Cinnamomum* is a **modern and contested hypothesis**, as are plant identifications across the medical papyri generally, whose corpus is indication-organised. Second — and this is the trap the entry must not fall into — **ritual or liturgical use is not a cultivation status**: that a substance was burned before a god or compounded into an unguent says what was done with it, not what it was held to do to a person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. *Heka*, in which recitation and drug form one intervention, is a real framing in this tradition, but none is attested for this bark. Nothing is claimed.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (AT).** No cultivation framing is attested. The blocking warning on substance identifications, deity and demon attributions and tablet references is in force. Illness in this corpus is classified by causal agency — divine hand, ghost, oath — so a drug is partly defined by what agency it opposes; no such assignment for cinnamon was established, and none is invented.

**Nahua / Badianus (BM).** No cultivation framing is attested. *Cinnamomum* is an Old World genus reaching Mesoamerica only after contact, and the Badianus manuscript is **itself naturalistic — it does not contain the *tonalli* / *teyolia* / *ihiyotl* model**, which is reconstructed from later ethnohistory (López Austin). The unresolved question of how far the hot/cold language in post-conquest Nahua sources is pre-Columbian and how far a Galenic overlay is left unresolved here, as it should be.

**Practitioner's note.** Cinnamon is the clearest case in the Codex of a substance whose *therapeutic* standing is enormous and whose *cultivation* standing is thin once checked. What survives is a conservational reading: in Chinese terms a warming agent used to restore and hold, not to force; in Unani terms a hot drug in a system where heat is a finite endowment, which makes sustained high-dose warming a withdrawal rather than a deposit. Get the form right before anything else — Ròu Guì for constitutional Yang work, Guì Zhī for exterior release — and treat the coumarin ceiling on cassia as the practical limit on any long-term use. The one unambiguously usable practice here is Dioscoridean: this is an adulterated commodity with a long supply chain, so know the species, know the origin, and assay what you have.

### Contraindications

- **Anticoagulant medications (warfarin, clopidogrel, aspirin, apixaban, heparin):** Cinnamaldehyde has anti-platelet activity; coumarin (in cassia) has direct anticoagulant properties (precursor to dicoumarol, the natural coumarin anticoagulant that inspired development of warfarin). Additive bleeding risk. Monitor INR closely if combining; discontinue therapeutic doses 7+ days pre-surgery.

- **Liver disease / Hepatic impairment:** Cassia coumarin is hepatotoxic at doses exceeding TDI in susceptible individuals (CYP2A6 loss-of-function variants). Individuals with pre-existing liver disease should use ONLY Ceylon cinnamon. Liver enzyme monitoring (ALT, AST) is indicated if therapeutic doses of cassia are used for >4 weeks.

- **Diabetes medications (insulin, sulfonylureas, metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors):** Documented FBG-lowering effect is additive with pharmaceutical hypoglycemics. Risk of hypoglycemia. Monitor blood glucose when initiating; adjust pharmaceutical doses if combining under medical supervision.

- **Pregnancy — first trimester (high doses):** Emmenagogue potential at medicinal doses — Rou Gui specifically warms the uterus and promotes blood movement, which in classical use was indicated for menstrual irregularity from cold. Avoid therapeutic doses (>3g/day) in first trimester; culinary quantities are safe. Gui Zhi appears in Wen Jing Tang (Warm the Menses Decoction), a classical gynecological formula, indicating its direct uterine circulatory effects.

- **Heat conditions / Yin deficiency patterns:** Rou Gui is contraindicated in TCM for patients with genuine heat, Yin deficiency heat (deficiency fire), or hemorrhagic conditions — it is classified as HOT and will aggravate fire. This maps to the Ayurvedic Pitta-aggravating caution. In practice: cinnamon is contraindicated when the athlete presents with night sweats, dry mouth, red tongue with little coat, and easy-flushing — all signs of Yin deficiency fire, not Yang deficiency cold.

- **Oral mucosa sensitivity:** Cinnamaldehyde is a known allergen and contact sensitizer at high concentrations; oral allergy syndrome and contact stomatitis documented with therapeutic cinnamon supplementation in sensitive individuals. Use standardized aqueous extract rather than raw powder if sensitivity is suspected.

- **CYP2A6 polymorphism (cassia-specific):** Genetic variants in CYP2A6 (the primary coumarin-metabolizing enzyme) significantly increase coumarin half-life and hepatotoxicity risk; testing is not standard but is relevant for individuals with unexplained liver enzyme elevations on cassia supplementation.
