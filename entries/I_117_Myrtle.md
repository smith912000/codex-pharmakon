### Botanical Identity
Evergreen shrub 1–3m; opposite lance-shaped glossy aromatic leaves; white fragrant five-petalled flowers; bluish-black berries (used as spice); intensely aromatic throughout (leaves, flowers, berries, bark); native to the Mediterranean basin and Middle East; widely cultivated as an ornamental and culinary/medicinal plant. All parts are aromatic and pharmacologically active; the leaf is the primary drug material.

**The physician's plant**: The Akkadian word *āsu* (myrtle) is etymologically related to, or the origin of, the Akkadian word for "physician" (*asû*) — suggesting that the myrtle was so central to Mesopotamian medicine that the plant name became the healer's title. Whether this etymology is certain is debated, but the cultural connection between myrtle and medicine in the Akkadian world is well-documented.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Dioscorides / Greco-Roman (DMM)
Book I, Chapter 155: "Myrtle leaves — astringent; for diarrhoea; for haemorrhage; for ulcers; the leaf decoction for skin conditions; the berry for wounds." Applications: astringent for GI (diarrhoea, haemorrhage); topical for skin ulcers and wounds; aromatic; the berry as a wound dressing.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine / Ibn Sīnā (CM)
*Ās* — cooling and drying; astringent. Applications: primary astringent for diarrhoea and haemorrhage; for urinary conditions (reduces polyuria); for night sweats (hyperhidrosis); topical for skin wounds; oral antiseptic; aromatic. Ibn Sīnā makes myrtle one of his premier drying/astringent herbs.

### 2.3 Ebers Papyrus (EP)
Myrtle appears in EP compound preparations for skin conditions and aromatic formulas.

### 2.4 Assyrian Medical Tablets (AT)
*Āsu* (myrtle) is one of the most frequently appearing plants in AT medical texts — used for wound treatment, skin conditions, gastrointestinal, and in fumigation.

### 2.5 Sushruta Samhita (SS)
Related Myrtaceae species (*Syzygium*, *Rhodomyrtus*) appear in SS for astringent applications; *Myrtus communis* itself does not grow in India, but the pharmacological class (Myrtaceae astringent) is well-represented.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Antimicrobial / Astringent (Primary — Tannins + 1,8-Cineole)

**Ellagitannins + gallic acid + punicalagin**:
- Tannin-protein precipitation → astringent constriction of mucous membranes → reduces secretion and bleeding
- Antimicrobial against enteric pathogens (*E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *Staphylococcus*) through membrane disruption
- Biofilm inhibition → prevents bacterial colonisation of wound surfaces

**1,8-Cineole**: The same compound as eucalyptol (the primary compound in *Eucalyptus* essential oil):
- Mucolytic → thins bronchial secretions → expectorant
- Bronchodilator (cineole acts as a secretolytic and bronchodilator — clinical RCTs for cineole in bronchitis exist)
- Antimicrobial in respiratory tract

### 5.2 Antidiabetic (Myricitrin + Myricetin)

**Myricitrin**: α-Glucosidase inhibitor → reduces post-meal blood glucose spike → antidiabetic.
**Myricetin**: Insulin sensitisation (GLUT4 upregulation); AMPK activation (similar to berberine, I_73).

This antidiabetic mechanism is not documented in the classical literature (blood glucose was not a measured parameter in ancient medicine) but emerges from modern pharmacological investigation of the plant's flavonoid profile. It represents a pharmacological property discovered by modern science in a plant used for 3,000 years for entirely different indications.

### 5.3 Urinary Antiseptic (Arbutin)

**Arbutin** (0.5–2% in leaf): The same mechanism as bearberry (I_98) and pipsissewa (I_98) — converted to hydroquinone in alkaline urine → urinary antiseptic. Myrtle was used by Ibn Sīnā for urinary conditions; the arbutin content provides direct pharmacological validation of the urinary application.

### 5.4 Antiperspirant (Tannins + 1,8-Cineole)

**Tannins + astringent compounds**: Reduce sweat gland secretion through protein precipitation at the secretory duct; antimicrobial against odour-producing bacteria. The CM application for hyperhidrosis (night sweats, excessive sweating) is pharmacologically grounded.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**1,8-Cineole (bronchitis)**: Multiple RCTs for isolated 1,8-cineole (cineole capsules — Soledum®) in acute bronchitis — significant reduction in cough frequency and severity vs placebo (Gillissen 2013, *Arzneimittelforschung*). This validates the bronchial application; myrtle delivers cineole in complex form.

**Myrtle EO (respiratory)**: Standardised myrtle EO preparations (MyrtolTM) are approved in Germany (Commission E) for bronchitis. This is a pharmaceutical validation of Dioscorides' and Ibn Sīnā's myrtle respiratory applications.

**Antidiabetic**: Multiple animal studies for myricetin/myricitrin; small human pilot studies showing blood glucose reduction with myrtle leaf preparations. No large RCTs.

**Antimicrobial**: In vitro data widely validates antimicrobial activity; no clinical trials for wound infection specifically.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Āsu — the physician's plant**: The possible etymological link between *āsu* (myrtle) and *asû* (physician) in Akkadian suggests that in ancient Mesopotamia, the healer was named after the plant — or the plant was named for the healer's characteristic tool. Either way, the cultural identification of myrtle with medicine in Mesopotamia was deep enough to potentially shape the language itself. The AT tradition has myrtle as the most commonly appearing plant in medical texts; if the physician was literally "the myrtle-person," then medicine in ancient Mesopotamia was in some sense myrtle-medicine.

**Venus and the wedding wreath**: Myrtle was sacred to Aphrodite/Venus across the Greco-Roman world — used in wedding garlands, funerary preparations, and victory crowns. The medical and ritual uses were not separate: the same astringent, antiseptic, and aromatic properties that made myrtle useful for wound treatment and oral hygiene also made it appropriate for the ritualistic preparation of bodies (living and dead), purification, and marking of transitions (marriage, death, triumph). The aromatic quality — 1,8-cineole — made it pharmacologically active as a respiratory and antimicrobial agent while simultaneously making it a powerful ritual aromachological agent.

**Myrtol and German pharmaceutical validation**: Germany's Commission E approved standardised myrtle preparations (*Myrtol*) for bronchitis in the 1990s — one of the clearest examples in European phytomedicine of a classical DMM plant moving from ancient text to modern approved pharmaceutical within the same country's pharmacological regulatory system. The path from Dioscorides (1st century CE) to Commission E approval (1990s) is ~1,900 years of continuous clinical application ultimately validated by regulatory evidence assessment.

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM (cross-reference)**: Bitter, astringent; cool. Channels: Lung (肺), Bladder (膀胱), Large Intestine (大腸). Astringes, clears Lung heat, promotes expectoration, stops diarrhoea.

**Ayurvedic**: Bitter, astringent; cooling. Reduces Pitta ↓↓. *Grāhī* (astringent; stops diarrhoea), *Śvāsahara* (anti-asthmatic), *Mūtralā* (diuretic). Related Myrtaceae species (*Syzygium aromaticum* = clove, I_17) are classical Ayurvedic drugs.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Bronchospasm (severe asthma)**: 1,8-Cineole in very sensitive individuals can paradoxically trigger bronchospasm (rare) — use inhaled preparations cautiously in severe asthma
- **Pregnancy**: Emmenagogue applications noted in historical texts — internal use at high doses should be avoided; standard culinary/aromatic use generally considered safe
- **Children (EO internally)**: 1,8-Cineole can cause CNS and respiratory depression in children under 6 via direct application to nose/mouth — never apply undiluted EO to the face of young children
