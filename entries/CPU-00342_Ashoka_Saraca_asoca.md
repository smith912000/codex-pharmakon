# Ashoka — Aśoka, Aśoka-vṛkṣa, Hema-puṣpa, Vañjula, Kaṅkeli (Sanskrit); Sita Ashok, Anganapriya

## *Saraca asoca* (Roxb.) Willd. | Family: Fabaceae (Caesalpinioideae)
**Tradition Tags:** CM, SS
**Tags (previous line, retained for reference):** SS (Suśruta Saṃhitā), CS (Caraka Saṃhitā), Aṣṭāṅga Hṛdaya; Nighaṇṭu literature (Bhāvaprakāśa)
**Energetic Classification (Ayurvedic):** Rasa — Kaṣāya (astringent), Tikta (bitter); Vīrya — Śīta (cooling); Vipāka — Kaṭu (pungent); Guṇa — Laghu (light), Rūkṣa (dry). Pacifies Pitta and Kapha; the astringency mildly aggravates Vāta if used dry and unattended.
**Training Phase:** Stabilisation / Restorative — a Stri-roga (gynaecology) tonic used to steady cyclical instability rather than to force acute change.

### Origin & Tradition

**Suśruta Saṃhitā (SS):** Ashoka is the archetypal *Stri-roga* dravya of the classical corpus. The bark (tvak) is the principal officinal part, decocted (kvātha) and, more famously, fermented into the self-generated wine **Aśokāriṣṭa** — a preparation that survives essentially intact into modern Ayurvedic pharmacopoeia. Suśruta and the later Nighaṇṭus place it squarely in the management of **asṛgdara / raktapradara** (excessive or dysfunctional uterine bleeding) and general *garbhāśaya* (uterine) weakness. The astringent-cooling profile is the doctrinal logic: bleeding disorders of the Pitta-rakta type are checked by a kaṣāya-śīta agent that "holds" the tissue and cools the heat driving the flow.

**Caraka Saṃhitā (CS) & later tradition:** While Ashoka's fame is concentrated in gynaecology, the broader tradition also deploys the bark for *dāha* (burning sensation), *tṛṣṇā* (thirst), and as a general *raktastambhaka* (haemostatic) and *śothahara* (anti-oedema) agent. Bhāvaprakāśa codifies the "sorrow-removing" reading of the name and fixes the bark-decoction/āsava lineage.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Sorrow-less Tree:** The Sanskrit *a-śoka* means literally "without sorrow / grief-removing" (*śoka* = grief). No entry on this plant is complete without the layer the name carries. In the Rāmāyaṇa, Sītā is held captive in the **Aśoka-vana**, the grove of sorrow-less trees — an irony the tradition reads deliberately: the tree that dispels grief frames the heroine's deepest grief. In classical poetics and śilpa (temple sculpture), the Ashoka is bound to the **dohada** motif: the tree is said to burst into flower when struck by the touch or kick of a beautiful young woman's foot — the *aśoka-dohada*, the tree's "pregnancy-longing." This is not idle ornament; it encodes the plant's doctrinal identity. The Ashoka is a *feminine, fertility-aligned, sorrow-dissolving* agent, and its therapeutic domain — the *garbhāśaya*, the womb — is the somatic seat of exactly the grief-and-cyclical-instability the myth dramatises. The tree is also sacred in Buddhist and Jain contexts (associated with the birth of the Buddha under a tree, and as the *kalpavṛkṣa*-like grove). For Kenetica, Ashoka is filed as a **grief-and-cycle stabiliser**: a substance whose symbolic and physiological registers converge on holding, cooling, and settling what bleeds or grieves without containment.

⚠️ **Adulteration note (critical for sourcing):** Market "Ashoka bark" is very commonly adulterated or wholly substituted with the bark of ***Polyalthia longifolia*** (the false ashoka / mast tree, Annonaceae) — a completely unrelated species sharing only the vernacular "ashoka." Authentic *Saraca asoca* is a threatened/vulnerable species with restricted wild populations, which drives the substitution. Any clinical claim below applies **only** to verified *S. asoca* material; *Polyalthia* has a different (and largely unrelated) pharmacology.

### Active Components
Reported constituents of authenticated *S. asoca* include flavonoids and phytoestrogenic markers — **quercetin, kaempferol, luteolin**, and the phytosterol **β-sitosterol** have been quantified in standardised flower extracts (see Scientific Correlation). The bark additionally carries condensed tannins and catechins (accounting for the strong kaṣāya/astringent character), leucocyanidin/leucopelargonidin-type flavonoid glycosides, and saponins. The tannin-rich astringency is the plausible material basis of the haemostatic ("bleeding-holding") action; the flavonoid/sterol fraction is the candidate basis of the estrogen-modulating action.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica
Ashoka is positioned as a **cyclical-stabilising uterine tonic** — the reference agent for menorrhagia-type patterns (heavy, hot, Pitta-rakta bleeding), dysmenorrhoea with a heat/burning quality, and the diffuse "grief-in-the-womb" cluster the tradition treats as a unity. It is a *hold-and-cool* agent, not a stimulant: deployed in the stabilisation phase to reduce excess flow, damp associated inflammation, and settle the tissue. It pairs conventionally with Śatāvarī (*Asparagus racemosus*) as a nourishing counterweight when depletion accompanies the bleeding.

### Scientific Correlation
According to PubMed:

- A standardised **ethanolic extract of *S. asoca* flowers**, HPTLC-quantified for quercetin, kaempferol, luteolin and β-sitosterol, showed **estrogenic activity in ovariectomised Wistar rats** (uterotrophic effect, altered marker enzymes/hormones, vaginal cornification), supporting a phytoestrogenic mechanism for the traditional uterine use. Swar, Shailajan & Menon, 2016, *Journal of Ethnopharmacology* [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2016.11.038).
- A **methanolic bark extract** reduced **estradiol-induced endometrial keratinising metaplasia** in rat uterus, lowered serum estrogen, and showed anti-inflammatory activity with reduced uterine COX-2 — the authors interpret an *anti-proliferative, anti-estrogenic* action relevant to dysfunctional uterine bleeding. Shahid et al., 2015, *J. Basic Clin. Physiol. Pharmacol.* [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1515/jbcpp-2014-0124).

**Evidence grade — Low to Moderate (preclinical).** Both studies are animal/mechanistic; there is no high-quality human RCT evidence cited here for the uterine-tonic claim. Note the two studies point in apparently opposite directions (pro-estrogenic flower extract vs. anti-estrogenic bark extract) — likely reflecting different plant parts, solvents and models, and a genuinely biphasic/modulatory profile rather than a single fixed effect. Claims of clinical efficacy in menorrhagia remain traditionally grounded and not established by the evidence retrieved here.

### Traditional Energetic Classification
- **Rasa (taste):** Kaṣāya (astringent), Tikta (bitter)
- **Vīrya (potency):** Śīta (cooling)
- **Vipāka (post-digestive):** Kaṭu (pungent)
- **Guṇa (qualities):** Laghu (light), Rūkṣa (dry)
- **Doṣa action:** Kapha-Pitta-śāmaka (pacifies Kapha and Pitta); may mildly aggravate Vāta through dryness/astringency
- **Dhātu (tissue) focus:** Rakta (blood) and Rasa; specific affinity to *garbhāśaya* (uterus / reproductive tissue, śukra-ārtava)
- **Karma (actions):** Raktastambhaka / raktapradara-hara (haemostatic in uterine bleeding), Śothahara (anti-oedema), Dāha-praśamana (relieves burning), Stri-roga-hara (gynaecological corrective), Vraṇa-śodhana (wound-cleansing, external)
- **Srotas:** primarily Ārtavavaha (menstrual channel); Raktavaha

### Contraindications
- Given the phytoestrogenic signal, use caution in **hormone-sensitive conditions** (estrogen-dependent tumours, etc.) and avoid casual self-medication in such contexts — `[USER: verify with clinician]`.
- **Pregnancy:** not for self-directed use; classical use is peri-menstrual/uterine, and safety in pregnancy is not established from the evidence here.
- Strong astringency can aggravate Vāta and constipation-type dryness; combine with anupāna appropriately.
- **Sourcing risk:** because market adulteration with *Polyalthia longifolia* is rife, unverified bark should be treated as pharmacologically unknown.
- Not a substitute for evaluation of abnormal uterine bleeding, which requires medical diagnosis.
