# Ivory (elephant tooth) — Elephantos odontos rinisma, "Elephant's Tooth" (the scraping of elephant's tooth)
## *Dentine of the elephant's tusk or tooth, used as a scraping (rasped powder)* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek Tradition (DMM).** The chapter is Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica*, Book 2, chapter 61, headed "2-61. ELEPHANTOS ODONTOS RINISMA" and titled "Elephant's Tooth" in the English edition on disk (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, line 5180; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 251, row for 2-61). The whole chapter is two sentences: "The scraping of elephant's tooth when applied cures whitlows of the finger or toenails. It is astringent." (line 5182).

That is all this chapter says. It gives no source of the tusk, no colour or grade, no adulterant, no method of scraping and no dose. The heading carries the transliterated word *rinisma*; the body text says "scraping", and nothing further is said about the form. No Greek script is printed in the English edition, so none is given here.

Elsewhere in the same book Dioscorides lists "Fat from elephants and deer (rubbed on) drives away snakes" (chapter 2-94, "Medicinal uses of Fats", line 5344). That is elephant fat, a different material, and it is cited only to mark the distinction. In a later chapter the "Arabicus stone" is described as "like a piece of ivory" (line 10613). That is a comparison of appearance for a stone, not a use of ivory, and it is not part of this entry.

### Active Components

Ivory is the dentine of the tusk, a mineralised tissue. In general terms it is made of a collagen matrix with a mineral phase of calcium phosphate (hydroxyapatite). No figures are stated from the sources.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The Greek text records one external use, scraped ivory applied to a whitlow. The Codex does not recommend it, and it provides no protocol. Elephants are protected animals, and trade in new ivory is prohibited or strictly restricted in most countries (see Contraindications). Whitlow (infection beside a nail) is a condition for which an ordinary clinician or pharmacist can offer treatment; no one should treat it with ivory dust.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is invented here. This two-sentence chapter gives no provenance, gathering season, assay or adulteration test for the material. The one thing a reader could fairly call a discipline of attention in this chapter is that it states a single, narrow use and a single quality and does not extend them. No cultivation status is attested.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing. There is no cultivation role for ivory in this text, and the practice it records is one a modern cultivator should not follow.

### Contraindications

Elephants are endangered or threatened across their range. International trade in elephant ivory is banned or heavily restricted, and possession can be a criminal offence. Dusts of any bone-like material can be inhaled; applying an unsterilised powder to an infected nail fold risks making the infection worse. A whitlow can spread to the finger and deeper tissue, so it needs proper care. This entry gives no medical advice.
