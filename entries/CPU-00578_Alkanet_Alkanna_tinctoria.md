# Alkanet — Dyer's Bugloss, Spanish Bugloss, Orchanet, Anchusa Root

## *Alkanna tinctoria* (L.) Tausch | Family: Boraginaceae

**Tradition Tags:** EP (Ebers), AT (Assyrian), DMM (Dioscorides), CM (Avicenna)
**Energetic Classification:** Warm and dry — a drying, astringent, "cleansing-and-closing" red root in the Galenic-Unani scheme.
**Training Phase:** Tempering

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ebers Papyrus (EP):** The Egyptian pharmacopoeia of the Ebers Papyrus draws heavily on red and coloured plant materials for salves and cosmetic-medicinal preparations. Root-derived red dyes of the *Alkanna / Anchusa* type belong to the ancient Mediterranean and Nile-Valley palette used to colour ointments, oils, and unguents — the same red-root class prized both as a stain for the body and as a base for wound and skin salves.

**Assyrian (AT):** Mesopotamian medical and craft texts record red-dyeing roots worked into oils and fats. A root that bleeds a deep crimson into oil but stays colourless in water was a distinctive and valued article of the Assyrian apothecary-dyer, folded into ointments where the colour itself signalled potency.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** Dioscorides describes *ankhousa* (alkanet) in *De Materia Medica*: its root, steeped in oil or wax, yields a red colour and is applied for ulcers, burns, inflammations, erysipelas, and skin eruptions, and taken in wine for certain internal complaints. This is the earliest systematic Western account of alkanet as a wound-and-skin remedy, not merely a colourant.

**Avicenna (CM):** The Unani tradition transmitted through Avicenna's *Qanun* classed alkanet (*shanjar / abu khalsa* in the Arabic materia medica lineage) among the warming, drying, resolving roots — used externally on ulcers, indurations, and skin lesions, its heat "ripening" and "cleansing" corrupt matter while the astringency closed the tissue.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Alkanet is the archetypal *blood-signature* root: colourless in water, it stains oil and fat a deep arterial red, and tradition read this as the plant declaring its affinity for blood, flesh, and the healing of wounds. The doctrine of signatures here is unusually well-earned by pharmacology — the red is not incidental but *is* the active principle. The crimson comes from alkannin and shikonin, naphthoquinone pigments that carry the documented wound-healing, anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial actions. The colour and the cure are the same molecule; symbol and mechanism coincide.

### Active Components

- **Alkannin and shikonin (A/S)** — enantiomeric naphthoquinone (naphthazarin) pigments concentrated in the root; *A. tinctoria* yields the (S)-enantiomer alkannin. These are the dye and the principal bioactives.
- **A/S ester derivatives** — acetyl-, isovaleryl-, and other acyl esters of alkannin/shikonin, modulating stability and activity.
- **Naphthazarin redox core** — the pigment's quinone scaffold underlies its radical-scavenging (antioxidant) and topoisomerase-inhibiting behaviour.
- **Pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs)** — as a Boraginaceae, *Alkanna* can contain hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids; a critical safety consideration for any internal use (see Contraindications).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In the Tempering phase, Alkanet is a *topical resolving-and-sealing* agent: a red salve root for the skin surface — burns, slow-healing ulcers, cracked and inflamed lesions, and pressure sores. Its evidenced naphthoquinone actions (wound healing, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antioxidant) make it a classic base for a medicated oil or ointment, where the pigment both colours and treats. Within Kenetica it is handled as an *external* material — a finishing salve for compromised skin — and expressly **not** as an internal tonic, because the Boraginaceae pyrrolizidine-alkaloid risk removes internal use from the safe repertoire. It tempers and closes the outer surface; it is not swallowed.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:

- Tsermentseli et al. (2013), *Journal of Chromatography A* — describes shikonin as "a potent antioxidant and wound healing agent" and works with hexane extracts of *Alkanna tinctoria* roots and commercial shikonin ointment, confirming the root as a practical source of the bioactive naphthoquinone. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chroma.2013.09.044
- Rat et al. (2023), *Frontiers in Pharmacology* — characterises alkannin, shikonin and derivatives as Boraginaceae root secondary metabolites expressing anticancer, wound-healing and antimicrobial activities, and studies their interaction with endophytic bacteria. DOI: https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2023.1244270
- Beretta et al. (2016), *Archiv der Pharmazie* — reviews alkannin and shikonin from *A. tinctoria* and *Lithospermum erythrorhizon* as long-known dyestuff, wound-healing, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial and antitumour substances whose potency is linked to topoisomerase I inhibition and the redox properties of the naphthazarin scaffold. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1002/ardp.201600286

**Evidence grade:** Preclinical / traditional, with strong mechanistic support. The wound-healing, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial and antitumour actions of alkannin/shikonin are well documented in vitro and in animal models, and shikonin-class compounds have reached topical formulation; controlled human wound-healing trials of *A. tinctoria* itself remain limited. Traditional wound-and-dye uses are of high historical confidence.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Warm and dry; resolving, astringent, cleansing; applied externally to ripen and close ulcers and skin lesions.
- **TCM (related species):** the sister red-root *Lithospermum erythrorhizon* (zǐ cǎo, "purple grass") is the classical Chinese analogue — cools blood, resolves toxin, used topically for skin eruptions, burns and macular rashes. *A. tinctoria* is not itself a classical Chinese herb but shares the naphthoquinone chemistry.
- **Ayurvedic (by analogy):** would read as a *tikta / kashaya* (bitter/astringent), cooling-to-neutral external agent for pitta-type skin inflammation; not a classical Ayurvedic dravya.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian (EP).** The Ebers attestation here is at the level of the red-root *class* — the *Alkanna / Anchusa* dye-roots folded into salves, oils, and unguents — rather than a secure species identification, and the Egyptian corpus is indication-organised: it records what a coloured salve was worked into for the skin, not what the root does to a person's *ka*, *ib*, or vital constitution. No *heka* recitation is attested for it in the retrieved sources, and no deity attribution can be made without one. Cultivation status: **(c) absent**.

**Assyrian (AT).** The Mesopotamian record likewise reaches alkanet only as one of the red-dyeing roots worked into oils and fats, where the crimson itself signalled potency. Under the standing blocking warning in force for every Assyrian substance identification, tablet reference, and causal-agency framing, no cultivation status is claimed; the standing is that of a valued apothecary-dyer's article. Cultivation status: **(c) absent**.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides gives the earliest systematic account of *ankhousa* in *De Materia Medica*: the root steeped in oil or wax yields a red colour and is applied to ulcers, burns, inflammations, erysipelas, and skin eruptions. Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system to assign the root a cultivation status. What the tradition offers a cultivator is instead his discipline of attention — the diagnostic test that the root bleeds crimson into oil or wax yet stays colourless in water, the assay of the colour as the sign of the working principle, and the guard against a mis-gathered or adulterated root. That is a real practice of provenance and sensory judgement, not a vital-body doctrine. Cultivation status: **(c) absent**.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM).** The *Qānūn* tradition classes alkanet (*shanjar / abū khalsa* in the Arabic lineage) among the warming, drying, resolving roots, applied externally to ripen and cleanse corrupt matter while the astringency closes the tissue — a genuine therapeutic use. But the tradition's cultivation idea — the conservation of the *innate heat* and *radical moisture*, the finite vital endowment whose depletion *is* ageing — is a longevity frame of the whole regimen, not a status assigned to an external drying salve-root, and none is sourced for alkanet; no *mufarrih* cardiac-*ruh* role is claimed. Cultivation status: **(b) framed** — attested therapeutically, no cultivation status recorded.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does exactly one thing with alkanet: steeps the root in a warm oil until it bleeds deep red, then works that oil into a salve for the skin surface — burns, slow-healing ulcers, cracked and inflamed lesions. It is never swallowed: the Boraginaceae pyrrolizidine-alkaloid load removes internal use from the safe repertoire, so there is no ingestive cultivation practice to be had here in any of the four traditions. The colour is the medicine, the surface is the target, and the honest cultivation answer is a topical one.

### Contraindications

- **Pyrrolizidine-alkaloid hepatotoxicity — critical.** As a member of the Boraginaceae, *Alkanna tinctoria* can contain hepatotoxic (and potentially genotoxic/carcinogenic) pyrrolizidine alkaloids. **Do not take internally.** Chronic or repeated internal exposure to PA-containing Boraginaceae is associated with hepatic veno-occlusive disease (sinusoidal obstruction syndrome) and liver injury.
- Avoid internal use entirely in pregnancy and lactation — PAs cross the placenta and into milk and are documented developmental hepatotoxicants.

- Not for infants or children internally under any circumstance.
