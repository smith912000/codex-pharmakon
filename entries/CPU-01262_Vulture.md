# Vulture — "Vulture", "Blood-of-a-Vulture", "Egg-of-a-Vulture", "a Vulture's feather" (Bryan 1930, Ebers Papyrus; no Egyptian word printed in the list)
## *The vulture, as Bryan's English identification names it; the list gives no Egyptian word and no binomial* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The only text on disk for this tradition is C. P. Bryan's 1930 English *The Papyrus Ebers* (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`). In his chapter VII, "Organic Remedies", Bryan says a full list of the animal materia medica "would not be possible", because "the identity of a considerable number of the animals mentioned is still beyond us", and then lists those "whose identity is not in doubt, and who were called upon to sacrifice themselves in the cause of the Healing Art" (about lines 1385-1392). The vulture is in that list: "Tortoise Lizard Mole Vulture" (line 1394). The extract row (`Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv`, row 104, "Bryan 1930, Ch. VII Organic Remedies (animals) (line 1394)") records the same and adds that Bryan's list gives "no Egyptian word or binomial". `_sources/ebers.csv` line 160 reads "Vulture,,animal,"Ebers Papyrus; Ghalioungui 1987 trans., Glossary I (Medicaments)",yes", with the last column, identification-uncertain, marked "yes". Ghalioungui's translation is not on disk, so I could not check its glossary. In this corpus the identity of any named substance is a modern hypothesis, since the papyrus is organised by complaint, not by substance; the Egyptian word and the species of vulture are not stated in anything on disk.

Bryan then lists the parts the Egyptians took from their animals, among them "Feathers" and "Eggs" and "Blood" (about lines 1406-1421). He does not say which parts came from which animal, except in a few remarks. The three recipes in his text that name the vulture each use a different part.

1. *The blood, for an abscess.* Following a remedy for the "uases" abscess, Bryan prints: "ANOTHER REMEDY AGAINST THE UASES ABSCESS: Blood-of-a-Dove, Blood-of-a-Goose, Blood-of-a-Swallow, Blood-of-a-Vulture. Anoint therewith" (line 2357). Bryan introduces it with "Just as picturesque, and assuredly as useless, is the second remedy" (line 2356), which is his own judgement; the papyrus gives no reason.
2. *The feather, as a brush.* For bleary eyes Bryan reports a mixture of myrrh, onions, verdigris and *Cyperus*, with antelope dung, clear oil and the entrails of the *qadit*-animal, and adds "This could be painted on with a Vulture's feather" (line 2735). The feather is the applicator, not an ingredient, and "qadit-animal" is Bryan's untranslated Egyptian word, which I do not identify.
3. *The egg, in an eye salve.* In his discussion of pterygium (a growth on the eye) he writes that the scribe "made an attempt at a golden-eye ointment, too, when he crushed some Collyrium in the Egg-of-a-Vulture" (line 2844).

I searched Bryan's text for "vulture" (any case). It occurs in the list (line 1394) and in these three places (lines 2357, 2735, 2844), and line 2735 repeats the word in Bryan's own comment ("Antilopes and Vultures"). Bryan's text is a summary with selected quotations, not a full translation, so the absence of other vulture recipes in it proves little about the papyrus. [USER: verify]

*Other texts on disk, not folded in.* The vulture is in two other texts in the Codex, and each is a separate material. In Sushruta, *Gridhra* (vulture) is named among the *Prasaha* group of birds that "suddenly dart on their prey" (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` lines 546-548), whose flesh is "specially beneficial in cases of consumption and kindred wasting diseases" (line 556); the *Uttaratantra* chapter on diseases of vision, *Timira*, directs "the flesh of a vulture" with deer flesh, rock salt, honey and clarified butter to be applied to the eye, and "the lard of vultures, snakes and cocks" with liquorice as a salve (`SS_Uttara_ch17.txt` lines 280-285; the translator's footnote at line 297 reports Dallana on the lards). The list row is `_sources/sushruta.csv` line 210. In the Tibetan texts, "Vulture meat: [has fourteen names]. It increases [digestive] heat, removes goiters [and dispels spirits]" (Clark 1995, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 1167) and "Vulture's droppings: increase digestive heat, disperse tumors and suppurate swellings" (line 1199), with the Men-Tsee-Khang text at its lines 3529 and 3568. Those traditions are not on this entry's tag line, and the Cultivation Register below covers only the Egyptian one. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, a vulture feather is keratin, a structural protein; an egg is protein, fat and water in a shell of calcium carbonate; blood is a fluid tissue of water, plasma proteins, cells and salts. Nothing in the entry links any constituent to the uses given.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Ebers material, as Bryan prints it, uses the vulture's blood in an ointment for an abscess, its feather as the brush for an eye application, and its egg to grind collyrium for an eye ointment. The Codex does not recommend any of them: an abscess needs examination, applying wild-bird blood to a sore carries infection, and an eye ointment of any kind, let alone an animal one, risks the eye. Vultures are among the most threatened birds in the world and are protected in law in most countries. The entry is a record of what Bryan reports.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations are those quoted: four bloods to "Anoint therewith"; the feather as applicator; collyrium crushed in the egg. Bryan gives no quantity for any ingredient and no length of course. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. A wild bird's blood and eggs can carry bacteria and viruses; none of these materials is safe on an open sore or the eye. Eye growths, such as pterygium, are assessed by an eye specialist. Vulture populations have collapsed in several regions, in part through poisoning, and many species are listed as endangered. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Egyptian (EP):** None. Bryan's list and the recipes quoted give no quality (hot, cold, moist, dry) for the vulture or its parts. The Ebers corpus is organised by complaint and records what was done for a condition, not a classification of the substance.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* No cultivation framing is attested. The Ebers material is indication-organised: it records what was done for a complaint, not what a substance does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. For the vulture, the three recipes read are for an abscess, a bleary eye and a growth on the eye, and in two of the three the bird supplies a part (blood, egg) to an ointment while in the third the feather is a tool. The Egyptian practice of treating spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*) is relevant in general; Bryan reports no recitation with any of the three recipes, and since he summarises, that is no proof that the papyrus gives none. No deity is attributed to the vulture in the text I read, and none is supplied here.

The vulture appears in the Ebers material as a source of blood and egg in ordinary remedies and as the donor of a feather for painting an eye salve on, and the Codex does not recommend the medical uses. ### Contraindications

- **Eyes:** do not put animal blood, eggs or any unsterile material on the eye, and do not paint anything on it with a feather; eye growths need an eye examination.
- **Abscess and open sores:** need examination; do not apply animal blood to an open sore.
- **Infection and hygiene:** wild-bird blood, eggs and feathers carry bacteria and parasites.
- **Conservation and law:** vultures are threatened and protected in law in most countries; the Codex does not recommend taking any animal for these uses.
