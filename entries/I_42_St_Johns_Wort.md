### Botanical Identity
Perennial herb 30–90cm; bright yellow 5-petalled flowers; leaves with translucent glandular dots (the "perforations" = oil glands visible when held to light) and black dots on leaf edges and flower petals (hypericin-containing glands). Native to Europe, western Asia, North Africa; widely naturalised globally. Flowers collected in late June–July at full bloom, dried rapidly to preserve hypericin and hyperforin. **Photosensitisation risk**: hypericin in skin + UV light → phototoxic reaction (relevant for fair-skinned individuals at high doses).

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 De Materia Medica (DMM)

Dioscorides (*Book III, Chapter 155*):
> *"Hyperikon: the seed taken in drink, forty days, drives away sciatica. The juice with honey benefits old wounds applied externally. The seed placed in cloth and inhaled drives away fever phantoms."*

Galen and later medieval European herbalists expanded this to include melancholy and spirit-possession (depression in pre-scientific language), establishing the primary neurological application.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine (CM)

Ibn Sīnā:
> *"Hot and dry in the first degree. Promotes urine, relieves sciatica, clears the skin, benefits wounds applied externally. Taken internally in wine it relieves blockage and melancholy."*

The "relieves melancholy" observation in Islamic medicine for *Hypericum* is pharmacologically significant — this is a specific observation of antidepressant activity using the classical humoral term for depressive states.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

| Compound | Content | Activity |
|---------|---------|---------|
| **Hypericin** | 0.06–0.4% | Signature compound; MAO inhibition (weak); photosensitiser; antiviral (HIV, HSV); mild SERT inhibitor |
| **Pseudohypericin** | Naphthodianthrone | Similar to hypericin; antiviral |
| **Hyperforin** | 2–4.5% | Primary antidepressant compound; SERT, DAT, NET, GABA-T, and glutamate reuptake inhibition simultaneously |
| **Adhyperforin** | Phloroglucinol | Similar to hyperforin |
| **Amentoflavone** | Biflavonoid | MAO-A inhibition; antidepressant; BBB-penetrating |
| **Hyperoside, rutin, quercetin** | Flavonoids | Anti-inflammatory; antioxidant |
| **Xanthones** | Minor | MAO inhibition; antidepressant |
| **Tannins** | 10–12% | Astringent; wound healing |

**Hyperforin** is the primary antidepressant compound (displacing the initial hypericin hypothesis) — it has a unique mechanism inhibiting reuptake of serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine, GABA, and glutamate via non-selective reuptake inhibition (different from SSRIs which are highly selective).

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Antidepressant (Primary Mechanism)

**Hyperforin**: Activates TRPC6 (transient receptor potential channel C6) → sodium influx → reuptake transporter inhibition across multiple neurotransmitter systems (SERT, NET, DAT, GABA-T, glutamate) simultaneously. This is mechanistically distinct from SSRIs: rather than directly blocking a single transporter, hyperforin activates an ion channel that broadly inhibits reuptake — explaining its broader spectrum than any single-target antidepressant.

**Clinical implication**: St. John's Wort affects serotonin, dopamine, AND norepinephrine — effectively an SNRI + mild NDRI simultaneously, without selective transporter blockade.

**Amentoflavone**: MAO-A inhibition → preserved serotonin and norepinephrine → additive antidepressant.

### 5.2 Anti-neuralgic / Analgesic

**Hyperforin**: Inhibits substance P (SP) and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) in sensory neurons → reduced pain signalling → analgesic. This explains the cross-traditional sciatica/neuralgia application.

### 5.3 Antiviral

**Hypericin**: Light-activated photosensitiser → generates reactive oxygen species in viral envelopes → antiviral against enveloped viruses (HSV, HIV, EBV, CMV). In vitro studies strong; clinical translation limited by photosensitisation risks at systemic doses.

### 5.4 Wound Healing (External)

Tannins → astringent/haemostatic. Hypericin/hyperforin → antimicrobial. Anti-inflammatory flavonoids → reduced wound inflammation → accelerated healing. The combined antimicrobial + astringent + anti-inflammatory effect explains the consistent cross-traditional external wound application.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Depression**: The most robustly evidenced plant antidepressant in the Codex.
- Linde et al. Cochrane Review (2008): 29 RCTs (5,489 participants) — St. John's Wort significantly superior to placebo for mild-moderate depression; as effective as standard antidepressants with fewer side effects
- Kasper et al. (1997): SJW vs imipramine — equivalent efficacy in mild-moderate MDD with significantly better tolerability
- European data: SJW is licensed as an antidepressant in Germany and several EU countries for mild-moderate depression

**Limitation**: Evidence limited to mild-moderate depression; insufficient evidence for severe depression.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Flight of demons**: The medieval European name *Fuga Daemonum* (flight of demons) is the most literal traditional description of antidepressant pharmacology in any herbal tradition — depression was understood as possession by demonic/dark forces, and this herb was observed to drive those forces away. From a pharmacological perspective, the observation that this plant relieves melancholy/depression was so consistent and dramatic that it was attributed supernatural agency. The herb makes devils flee because it treats depression.

**The drug interaction problem**: St. John's Wort is the most pharmacologically important herb in the Western tradition from a drug interaction standpoint — hyperforin is a potent CYP3A4, CYP2C9, and P-glycoprotein inducer. It reduces blood levels of: cyclosporine (transplant rejection risk), antiretrovirals (HIV treatment failure), warfarin (thrombosis risk), oral contraceptives (pregnancy), digoxin (cardiac), some chemotherapy agents. These are not theoretical interactions — documented cases of transplant rejection, HIV treatment failure, and unwanted pregnancy have been attributed to concurrent SJW use. This is the most significant herb-drug interaction warning in the Codex.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

| Form | Dose | Application |
|------|------|-------------|
| Standardised extract (0.3% hypericin, 3–5% hyperforin) | 300mg 3× daily (900mg total) | Clinical antidepressant dose; RCT protocol |
| Tincture | 2–4ml 1:5 tincture | Mild depression; anti-neuralgic |
| Tea | 2g dried herb per cup | Mild anxiolytic; less antidepressant activity than extract |
| Infused oil (topical) | — | Wound healing; neuralgia applied externally |

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Bitter, slightly acrid; cool. Clears Liver Heat, calms Shen, moves Liver Qi stagnation. Pattern: Liver Qi stagnation with Shen depression (classical mild depression pattern).

**Ayurvedic**: Bitter; cooling. Reduces Pitta. *Pittahara, Medhya* (mild). Less used in Ayurveda than in European/Islamic tradition.

### 8.3 Contraindications

**MAJOR DRUG INTERACTIONS** — the most important contraindication table in Volume I:
- **Cyclosporine**: CYP3A4 induction → transplant rejection (documented cases)
- **Antiretroviral drugs (HIV/AIDS)**: Treatment failure documented
- **Warfarin**: INR reduction → thrombosis risk
- **Oral contraceptives**: Contraceptive failure → unwanted pregnancy
- **Digoxin**: Reduced levels → cardiac risk
- **Chemotherapy**: Reduced efficacy
- **SSRIs**: Serotonin syndrome risk (additive serotonergic)
- **Photosensitisation**: Avoid excessive sun exposure at high doses (especially fair-skinned)
- **Severe depression/psychosis**: Not appropriate — requires pharmaceutical treatment
