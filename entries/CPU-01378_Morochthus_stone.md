# Morochthus stone — Morochthos Lithos (μορόχθος λίθος), also called galaxia and leucographis (Dioscorides 5-152)
## *A soft, melting stone found in Egypt and used by linen-makers to whiten cloth; the translator's suggestion is "Moroxis — crystallised form of Apatite — Phosphate of Lime — Calcium Oxide"* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek Tradition (DMM).** The main locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-152 *MOROCHTHOS LITHOS* in the 2000 English translation (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10622-10628; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 925; book index "Morochthus stone 820" and "Moroxis 820", line 16616, "galaxia 820", line 11093; list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 878). The translator's heading is "SUGGESTED: Moroxis — crystallised form of Apatite — Phosphate of Lime — Calcium Oxide."

Dioscorides' account, read in full (the chapter runs over a page break, lines 10625-10628), is:

"Morochthus stone (which some call galaxia or leucographis) is found in Egypt. Those who make linen use it for whitening cloth as it is soft and melting. It is thought to close the pores, and is good taken as a drink with water for bloodspitters, the abdominal cavity, and disorders of the bladder. The same is given for the menstrual flows of women, and also in a pessary. It is mixed with softening eye medicines as it fills up hollow sores and stops discharges. Used with wax ointment it forms a scar on the tenderness of ulcers."

**Reading the chapter.** (1) Source and names: Egypt; also called *galaxia* and *leucographis*. (2) Craft use first: linen-makers whiten cloth with it "as it is soft and melting". (3) Stated action: "thought to close the pores", the only quality printed. (4) Internal use: "taken as a drink with water" for those spitting blood, for "the abdominal cavity" and for bladder disorders; the same given for women's menstrual flows, and in a pessary. (5) External use: in "softening eye medicines", because it "fills up hollow sores and stops discharges"; with wax ointment it "forms a scar on the tenderness of ulcers". No test of quality, no place within Egypt and no preparation of the stone itself is given.

**Names.** The index lists *galaxia* at page 820 (line 11093), which is the page of this chapter. The similar-sounding galactites (5-150, line 10617) is a different chapter with a different description (an ash-coloured stone that exudes a milky, sweet stuff); the text does not equate them, and neither is equated here.

**Identity.** The translator's gloss strings several things together: "Moroxis", a crystallised form of apatite, "Phosphate of Lime" and "Calcium Oxide". Apatite and lime are different materials (a calcium phosphate mineral and a calcium oxide), and the chapter describes none of the chemistry; it gives softness, melting, whitening of cloth and Egypt. The Codex's list row records the item under the name "Phosphate of Lime — Calcium Oxide", which is part of the translator's gloss and not a name in the text. The list marks identity uncertain. ### Active Components

Stated generally. If the stone is a calcium phosphate mineral it is a hard, poorly soluble solid; if it is a lime-type material it is alkaline and caustic when wet. The two readings differ greatly in how they would act, and the text gives no composition, so none is asserted for the Morochthus stone. Its whitening of cloth in the text could fit a soft, chalk-like or fuller's-earth-like material, but the chapter does not say so.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The chapter records a soft Egyptian stone drunk for blood-spitting, abdominal and bladder complaints and menstrual flows, used as a pessary and in eye salves and ulcer ointments. Drinking or inserting an unidentified powdered stone is not supported by anything retrieved. No modern protocol is supplied.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers is a discipline of attention; in this chapter that shows as attention to craft use (the stone is first of all what linen-makers use) and to names (it has two other names). **Practitioner's note.** Nothing here for a cultivator to take. What carries over is that the chapter starts from how a material is used in a craft before it speaks of medicine, and that it records alternative names. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Unidentified mineral:** the identity is unsettled between a phosphate mineral and a lime material; an unidentified stone should not be swallowed.
- **Caustic risk:** if the material is lime-type it burns mucous membranes; the pessary and eye uses are the highest risk.
- **Pregnancy:** the chapter gives it for menstrual flows and as a pessary; nothing here supports using a mineral to affect menstruation or pregnancy, and an inserted mineral can cause infection and injury.
- **Bleeding:** blood-spitting or heavy bleeding needs medical assessment, not a mineral drink.
