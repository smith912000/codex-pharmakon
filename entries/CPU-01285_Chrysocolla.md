# Chrysocolla — Chrusokolla (CHRUSOKOLLA), "Gold Solder"
## *A green mineral washed and burnt for use against scars and growths and counted among the medicines that cause vomiting and can kill; the translator glosses it "Chrysocolla. This was a name for Borax or Malachite and means Gold Solder"* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** Chrysocolla is chapter 104 of Book Five of the *De Materia Medica*, in the section "METALLIC STONES", headed CHRUSOKOLLA (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10363-10367; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` carries it as 5-104, line 10363, with the translator's identification "Chrysocolla", class mineral; the book's page index prints "CHRUSOKOLLA 797"). The translator's heading (lines 10364-10366) reads "SUGGESTED: Chrysocolla", then "This was a name for Borax or Malachite and means Gold Solder", then the single word "TOXIC". The whole chapter is a single paragraph (line 10367).

*How to choose it.* "Chrysocolla from Armenia is the best — considerably resembling leeks in colour; that from Macedonia is the second, then the Cyprian. The pure should be chosen, but that which is full of earth and stones must be refused."

*How it is washed.* "Pound it, put it into a mortar, pour on water, and rub it strongly with the palm of your hand against the mortar. Allow it to settle, strain it, pour on other water, rub it again, and do this repeatedly until it is pure and unadulterated. Then dry it in the sun and put it in jars and use it."

*How it is burnt.* "If you want to burn it, do it as follows. Pound as much of it as you need, put it in a platter, set it over light coals, and repeat the procedure previously explained about burning." The text does not say where the burning "previously explained" is given. The chapter just above (5-103, PSIMUTHIOS, the cerussa [white lead] chapter, line 10362) has a burning in which the pounded pieces are put "into a hollow platter set it over live coals, and stir it with a stick of ferula until it is similar in colour to sandarac [5-122]"; that this is the procedure meant is an inference only. [USER: verify]

*What it does.* "Chrysocolla is able to wear off scars, and repress fleshy abnormal growths, and it is cleansing, astringent, warming, gently corrosive and a little biting. It is one of the medicines that cause vomiting and are strong enough to kill."

*Where else it is named.* In the cadmia chapter (5-84, line 10223) chrysocolla is one of the veins found in the same mound as pyrites, "chalcitis, misy, sory, melanteria, coeruleus, chrysocolla, calcanthus and diphryges". In the next chapter (5-105, *Armenion*, "Armenian Stone — blue Copper Carbonate", line 10375) the Armenian stone "does the same things as chrysocolla [above], only it is not as strong"; that stone has its own entry (II_22). Dioscorides uses the Armenian chrysocolla of this chapter as the best chrysocolla and the Armenian stone as the weaker relative; the text does not say they come from the same place beyond the shared name.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent beyond the translator's gloss, which offers two different minerals ("Borax or Malachite") and so leaves the identity open. In general terms, the mineral chrysocolla is a hydrated copper silicate with a blue-green colour; malachite is a basic copper carbonate and is green; borax is a hydrated sodium borate and is white. "Gold solder" fits a flux: borax has long been used to solder and join gold. Copper compounds are poisonous when swallowed in quantity and irritate skin and eye. The identification of the Greek drug is the translator's, not the text's. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The text records a green mineral washed and burnt for use on scars and growths, ranked by origin, and openly called "strong enough to kill" as an emetic. The translator marks the chapter "TOXIC". The Codex does not recommend it. Copper-bearing minerals are poisonous when swallowed and caustic on tissue; no part of the text supports a safe modern use.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives two preparations (line 10367): washing (pound in a mortar, rub with the palm against the mortar under water, let settle, strain, repeat until pure, dry in the sun, store in jars); and burning (pound as much as needed, set on a platter over light coals, "repeat the procedure previously explained about burning"). No dose is given in the sources retrieved, and the text gives no instruction on how an emetic dose might be limited. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Copper silicate and carbonate minerals were ground for blue and green pigments from antiquity. Copper salts taken by mouth cause nausea and vomiting and, in larger amounts, damage the liver, kidneys and blood and can be fatal; they are corrosive to skin and mucous membranes. Borax (sodium borate) is a flux in soldering and a mild antiseptic salt; swallowed in quantity it is toxic, especially to infants. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** The text prints these qualities for chrysocolla (line 10367): "cleansing, astringent, warming, gently corrosive and a little biting". It adds that it wears off scars, represses fleshy growths, and "is one of the medicines that cause vomiting and are strong enough to kill". No degree is printed for the warming.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is asserted here. What the chapter offers a practitioner is the discipline of attention: rank by provenance (Armenian, Macedonian, Cyprian in that order), refuse stone and earth, wash until "pure and unadulterated", and respect that the same drug can kill. That is a practice of assay and caution, attested for this substance in the chapter. It is not a cultivation status; the chapter makes no claim about the person's constitution, vitality or long life.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The chapter is a lesson in two things: how to separate a good drug from a poor one by origin and washing, and that a drug listed among the emetics can also kill. The Codex does not recommend taking or applying it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Toxicity:** the text itself says it can kill; copper compounds are poisonous when swallowed and corrosive to skin, wounds and the eye. Do not swallow, burn, inhale the dust of, or apply it.
- **Fumes and dust:** grinding and burning copper minerals, as the recipe directs, releases irritant dust and fumes.
- **Identification:** the translator offers borax or malachite; the toxicity of the two differs, and the text identifies the drug only by colour (like leeks), origin and purity.
- **Pregnancy and children:** unsuitable.
- **Related material:** the Armenian stone of the next chapter is said to act as chrysocolla does; treat it as dangerous too.
