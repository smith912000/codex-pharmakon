# Elephant Ears / Badan — ga.dur (Tibetan, Clark 1995 ch.20; Men-Tsee-Khang glossary "Gadur")

## *Bergenia crassifolia* (spine botanical; Clark 1995 prints it as the fourth of five Latin names for ga.dur: "Meadow Cranesbill: Geranium pratense: Erodium stephanium: Bergenia crassifolia: Rhodiola wallichiana") | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Identity warning.** Only the Tibetan tag has a locus, and there the Tibetan name ga.dur is given five Latin identifications, of which *Bergenia crassifolia* is neither the first nor singled out. The Avicenna and Shennong tags have no locus. **Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`), Explanatory Tantra ch.20, line 977: "(li.)ga.dur: Meadow Cranesbill: Geranium pratense: Erodium stephanium: Bergenia crassifolia: Rhodiola wallichiana: [The male type has seven names and the female type has six. It has balanced hot and cold power, hot and sweet tastes, cures lung disorders and reduces swellings. It has thick leaves similar to rice shoots, and the root resembles a withered gland. The flower has a pink interior and resembles a bird's beak. The common variety grows in Tibet and has pink petioles. All types] cure infectious fevers, lung fevers and channel fevers." The entry sits in the materia medica of ch.20 (the substance sections are listed at line 802; the herbs section opens at line 997), and the square brackets mark Clark's insertions from the commentary. The description of the flower as "a bird's beak" and the "rice shoots" leaf read like the first-named plant, the cranesbill; that is this entry's inference from the text, which does not say so. A second Clark entry, "brag.lcham" (line 1093), glosses *Bergenia ciliata* with two *Pyrola* species, "grows on rocks" and "heals wounds"; it is genus-level only for this row. The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 5282) sets "Gadur" (ga dur) beside "Bergenia stracheyi", but the OCR splits the Latin column from the names, so the pairing is by order. ### Active Components

Bergenia rhizomes are known in general for tannins and for the phenolic compound bergenin, which fits the astringent character often ascribed to them. The proportions in *B. crassifolia* were not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The sourced Tibetan use is for fevers of the lung and channels and for swellings, from an entry whose Latin identity is mixed. The Codex gives no protocol. The plant is commonly grown as a hardy ornamental groundcover.

### Cultivation Register

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent.* The tag rests on a spine row, not on any list row or text read. No attestation and no cultivation framing was found.

**Chinese — Shennong (SBJ).** *(c) Absent.* The plant is not in Yang's classic as read, so no *san pin* class applies and no cultivation status is claimed. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* Clark's ga.dur entry attests the name, taste, potency and uses, but assigns no cultivation status, and its Latin identity is mixed. The tradition's frame is that all disease arises from ignorance and the three poisons of attachment, hatred and closed-mindedness, which produce wind, bile and phlegm (Clark, lines 440 to 441). A medicinal plant works within that frame as a remedy for the humoral result, not as a root-level cure. No *bcud len* (essence-extraction) role is recorded for this plant in the entry read, although Clark names other substances (for example calcite, line 853, and heart-leaved moonseed, line 971) as ingredients in essence-extraction compounds.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports a cultivation practice. A gardener may grow Bergenia as an easy evergreen perennial, but the texts read do not establish that the Tibetan drug is this species, and two of the three tags have no locus.

### Contraindications

No safety data were read. Do not treat the traditional lung-fever and swelling uses as advice. Seek ordinary medical care for fever and respiratory illness. Tannin-rich plant material can upset the stomach, and the Tibetan entry shares its name with several unrelated plants, so do not substitute one for another.
