# Oinomeli — ΟΙΝΟΜΕΛΙ (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 5-16); Latin "Mulsum"; honeyed wine

## *Mulsum, a drink of old hard wine and good honey, in Dioscorides's account made with nine gallons of honey to eighteen gallons of wine. The identification is the translator's own heading ("Mulsum — Honeyed Wine, Mead") and is of high confidence as a preparation; it is a compounded drink, not a plant, and the spine class "plant" reflects that its two ingredients, grape wine and honey, are plant and bee products, with the class itself of medium confidence. ### Origin & Tradition

The chapter was opened and read for this entry. The book index prints "OINOMELI 752" (line 12345) and "Mulsum 752" (line 16618).

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-16, *Oinomeli* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading at line 9852, text at line 9854), in Book Five, "Vines & Wines", in the run of wines. The whole chapter reads:

"Mulsum is preferred which is made from old hard wine and good honey. Such as this creates less gas and is quickly fit to be used. Old mulsum is nutritive and the middle-aged is good for the bowels and diuretic. Taken after meat it hurts; taken as a drink beforehand it fills, but after that it encourages the appetite. It is usually made with nine gallons of honey mixed with eighteen gallons of wine. So that they may make it fit for use quickly, some boil the honey together with the wine and pour it into jars. For the sake of profit, some mix six pints of boiling must to one pint jar of honey, and after it has done boiling, pour it into other jars. It remains sweet."

Read in order, the chapter gives: (1) the choice, mulsum made from old hard wine and good honey, because it makes less gas and is soon fit for use; (2) the age distinction, old mulsum nutritive, middle-aged good for the bowels and urine; (3) the timing, harmful after meat, filling when taken beforehand and then arousing the appetite; (4) the usual proportion, nine gallons of honey to eighteen of wine; (5) a quick method, boiling the honey with the wine, and (6) a profit method, which the text ascribes to sellers and not to the physician, mixing boiling must with honey. The words "for the sake of profit" mark that last as a cheaper imitation, and the translation says only that it "remains sweet".

**Neighbouring chapters.** The chapters on either side are the wines with honey: 5-15 *Oinos Meletites* ("Old Wine with Honey", line 9845), which Dioscorides distinguishes from mead because "that is made from hard old wine and a little honey", while the meletites takes "five gallons of hard wine ... to four and a half litres of honey and one winecup of salt" (so it is a different drink with more honey and a little salt), and 5-17 *Melikraton* (honey water, heading line 9855, "the same strength as mead"). The latter has its own spine row (CPU-02597) and is not covered here. The wine-and-honey run is the context for reading 5-16. In 5-7 (heading line 9810, text line 9811) Dioscorides says of wines in general that old wines "hurt the nerves and the rest of the senses, yet they are pleasant to the taste", that new wine "is inflative, hard to digest, a cause of nightmares, and is urinary", and that one of middle age "is to be chosen for the uses of both health and sickness". That general statement about age frames the age distinction in 5-16.

### Active Components

The text names no constituent. In general terms, mulsum is a mixture of fermented grape wine and honey: it contains ethanol from the wine, sugars (chiefly fructose and glucose) from the honey and any unfermented must, water, and the organic acids and polyphenols of grape wine. The proportions of the recipe, two parts wine to one of honey by volume, make it a sweet drink. This is general chemistry of wine and honey, not a finding about the ancient drink. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a recommended practice.**

- **What the text says it does.** Old mulsum is "nutritive"; middle-aged is "good for the bowels and diuretic". Taken after meat it "hurts"; taken beforehand it "fills", and then encourages the appetite.
- **What the text does not say.** No illness is named, no course, no dose beyond the proportions for making it, and no statement about strength or long life.

The Codex does not translate the text's terms into modern diagnoses. The drink is an alcoholic sweetened wine, and the record here is of how it was made and sorted by age. It is not offered as a tonic or a protocol, and the entry gives none.

### Preparation & Dosing

Dioscorides gives the making and not the drinking: "nine gallons of honey mixed with eighteen gallons of wine"; for speed, "some boil the honey together with the wine and pour it into jars"; and, as a cheaper practice, "six pints of boiling must to one pint jar of honey", boiled and poured into other jars (line 9854). The units are the translator's ("gallons", "pints", "jar") and no conversion is attempted here. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Honey-sweetened wine is a type of alcoholic beverage with a long history in the Mediterranean world. Alcohol is a diuretic and a central nervous system depressant, and sweet drinks are high in sugar, so the text's "diuretic" is in kind with the usual effect of wine; the other terms (nutritive, fills, encourages the appetite) have no modern equivalent and are not translated into one. ### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body or vital system, and nothing in 5-16 links mulsum to long life, constitution or practice. What the chapter offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention to materials: the choice of old hard wine and good honey, the age of the drink, the time of taking it in relation to a meal, and the warning about the cheaper, boiled-must imitation sold for profit. That is provenance and adulteration detection, and it is as far as the text goes.

**Practitioner's note.** A sweetened wine described by how it is made and aged, with a sharp note about profit-driven imitation. There is no cultivation status attested. If a cultivator takes anything from it, it is the habit of asking what a preparation is really made of and who made it.

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

Per Dioscorides 5-16, mulsum (honeyed wine) acts on the digestive tract, the bowels, the urinary tract and the appetite, and its effect is sorted by age. Old mulsum is called nutritive; middle-aged mulsum is good for the bowels and diuretic. Taken after meat it hurts, and taken as a drink beforehand it fills and then encourages the appetite. The made drink is said to create less gas and to be fit for use quickly. The text gives no organ-specific quality or humoral category for it and names no illness. The systems the entry can map are therefore the gut (bowels and gas), the urinary system (diuresis) and appetite. The cheaper imitation with boiled must is noted as a commercial fault and not as a use. The chapter gives no dose and no energetic classification, and the Codex does not translate nutritive or fills into a modern category.

### Contraindications

- **Alcohol.** Mulsum is an alcoholic drink. It is unsuitable for children, in pregnancy and lactation, for anyone avoiding alcohol, and with driving or machinery. The text itself says taken after meat it "hurts". [USER: verify the last as a general medical statement]
- **Sugar.** The drink is sweet; it is not for those who must restrict sugar.
- **Medicines and conditions.** No statement in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Honey.** Honey should not be given to infants under one year, a general safety point and not from the text.
