# Primula sibirica — de.wa, Dhewa (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Primula sibirica, printed by Clark 1995 as "Primula sibirica Jaqueum (herb)", the first of three binomials for de.wa and the only herb of the three (the other two, "Populus alba" and "Populus bonatii", are marked "(tree)"); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text prints no Primula and its glossary sets Corydalis melanochlora against "Dhewa" by column position* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and under this binomial on Clark's edition alone.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Ebers, Assyrian, Sushruta and Shennong text on disk for *Primula* found nothing. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 177) the row is keyed by the Tibetan name *de.wa*, with the identification "Primula sibirica Jaqueum (herb); Populus alba; Populus bonatii (tree)", the locus "Clark 1995 (line 1081)" and the note "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The spine row (CPU-01775) took the **first** of the three binomials, the herb. The spine has no row for *Populus alba* or *Populus bonatii* as far as the files read show.

**Identity doubt.** Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" and (line 68) that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect". This is the clearest case in the batch: the one Tibetan name covers a small herb and a tree, and Clark's commentary says so in terms ("The de.wa herb ... The de.wa tree ..."). The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary gives a fourth plant by position (below). So *Primula sibirica* names one half of a two-plant Tibetan drug in Clark, and neither half is certain. The Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1081).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997), which runs to "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147). The entry follows *byi.tsher* (line 1080) and precedes *rtsa.mkhris* (line 1082). It reads:

"de.wa: Primula sibirica Jaqueum (herb): Populus alba: Populus bonatii (tree): [has four names. The de.wa herb has a dark blue flower, grows to a height of four finger-breadths, and the inferior type has a thick leaf. The de.wa tree has a whitish bark and many hard knots in the trunk]. It cures infectious fevers [and shooting pains]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The count of names and both descriptions are inside the brackets, as is the second action, "shooting pains"; "It cures infectious fevers" is outside them and is the root-text layer as Clark's marks stand. It is one clause for the herb and the tree together. Clark's name index lists *de.wa* (line 1705) and his Latin index lists *Primula sibirica* (line 1629).

*Clark 1995, chapter 21 (line 1211).* In Clark's chapter 21 ("The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines", heading at lines 1205 and 1206), the class of medicines for infectious fevers reads: "The categories of medicines which cure infectious fevers includes Bile of elephant and so forth, Bitter Cucumber, White Aconite, Meadow Cranesbill (Geranium pratense), Fagonia cretica, Populus alba (tree), Primula sibirica (herb), Common Fumitoty and Gold Thread." Here the tree and the herb stand as two separate members of the class, in that order, each with its marker. That agrees with the materia medica line, which gives one action for both.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (read through the name, not the binomial).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. Chapter 20, within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), line 3456: "Ji tsher cures epidemic diseases, acts as an antidote against poison and eliminates hot disorder of the kidneys. Dhewa cures epidemic and Tsa tri cures tripa disorders." The sentence stops at "epidemic" as printed, and the Codex does not complete it; it reads it as the same word that the sentence before it prints. Chapter 21 ("The Group and the Method of Compounding Medicine", contents, line 229), line 3601: "Giwam, Sergyi metok, Bongnga karpo, Gadhur, Ji tsher, Dhewa, Parpata and Traagkang are the group of medicines that cure contagious" (the sentence continues on line 3602 with "disorders"). The index lists "Dhewa 210,220" (line 7323). The glossary's "D" block prints "Dhewa" as the eighth of its phonetic names (line 5187); its scientific-name run puts *Corydalis melanochlora* eighth, against "Dhewa", by position, with a dash in the English column, as I_1259 sets out. The glossary prints no *Primula* and no *Populus*. The Codex reads the Men-Tsee-Khang "Dhewa" and Clark's *de.wa* as one drug from the spelling and from the agreement in kind of the actions: Clark's "infectious fevers" and the Men-Tsee-Khang "epidemic" and "contagious [disorders]" are the same family of terms as the two translators render them. Neither text says so. The name is not in the taste groups of chapter 19 and not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23).

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Primula sibirica*, *de.wa* and *Dhewa* found these. I_1259 (*Corydalis melanochlora*, FMT) is the entry for the same Tibetan name through the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary; it states that Clark prints no line for "Dhewa", but Clark's *de.wa* (line 1081) is a line for the same spelling in Clark's romanisation, so the two entries bear on one Tibetan name (see the data note in the report). They are not merged, because the binomials differ and the spine holds both. I_1277 (*Fagonia cretica*, which is *byi.tsher*, Clark line 1080) and I_1296 (*Geranium pratense*) quote Clark's line 1211 for their own drugs, and I_1303 (*Ixeris gracilis*) covers the next Clark line (1082). I_625 (*Populus nigra*) and I_728 (*Populus tremula*) are other species of the poplar genus and carry no FMT tag. No entry on *Primula sibirica* was found. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Primula* is a genus of the primrose family (Primulaceae), that some species are generally known to cause contact dermatitis through a substance on their hairs, and that *Populus* is the poplar genus (Salicaceae), whose buds and bark are generally known to contain salicylate-type compounds. Which compounds the Tibetan drug holds, and which of its candidate plants it is, is not shown by the lines read, so no statement about its composition is made.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "cures infectious fevers" (Clark, lines 1081 and 1211) and "cures epidemic" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3456), and place it in the group that cures "contagious disorders" (line 3601); Clark's commentary adds "shooting pains". These are the texts' claims in their own terms, attached to this binomial only through the name, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. An infectious fever needs medical care, and no herb should be used for one on the strength of this entry.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary describes the herb's flower and height and the tree's bark and trunk, and does not say which part of which plant is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The materia medica classes this drug by what it does: it cures infectious fevers, and it stands in the fever and contagion groups of both editions. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, lines 3900 to 3950; Clark's chapter 23 is headed "Normal health", lines 1262 to 1276), and Clark's materia medica names other drugs as essence-extraction ingredients (for example lines 853, 971 and 1144), none of them this one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name and not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a fever drug that covers a herb and a tree under one name, with no preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Some *Primula* species can irritate the skin; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. An infectious fever needs medical care. Because the Tibetan name covers more than one plant, a plant could not be matched to it from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read.
