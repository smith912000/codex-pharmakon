# Black salt (sanchal) — kha.ru.tsha (Clark 1995, "Black 'Sanchal' Salt"); Kharu tsha (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)

## *A red or black salt, natural or artificial, of the Tibetan Explanatory Tantra's list of salts; the translator's gloss gives the English "Black 'Sanchal' Salt" and no composition. Identification: black salt, the dark salt of the Tibetan salts list, by Clark's English name only; its chemistry is not stated; "sanchal" is the translator's quoted word, which is likely an Indian trade name for black salt, but no text on disk says so; confidence medium* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry** in the sense that only the Four Tantras tradition, in two English renderings, names the substance. The two texts read are Barry Clark's 1995 *The Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`; the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20) and the Men-Tsee-Khang edition of 2011 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`). The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder, "black salt (sanchal)", from `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (row "kha.ru.tsha", "Clark 1995 (line 930)", "Black 'Sanchal' Salt").

**rGyud-bzhi, Clark 1995 (FMT).** Chapter 20 of the Explanatory Tantra ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", Clark line 792 onward) lists the salts in a run at lines 927 to 935, after the resins (section 5, line 879) and before the myrobalans; the heading of the section they fall in is not legible in the e-text, so the Codex does not name it. The entry is at line 930: "kha.ru.tsha: Black 'Sanchal' Salt: [has nine names and is of red and black types. That of superior quality is very clear, whilst artificial black salt is common.]" In Clark's translation, material in square brackets is taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries (translator's note, line 82), so everything in this entry as printed is commentary and there is no root-text action in the entry itself. The next entry, at line 931, reads: "tsabs.ru.tsha: White Mineral Salt: Crag halite: [has five names and is of natural and artificial types. Good quality white mineral salt is white and resembles mdung.rtse, whilst the artificial type is a white salt]. Both black and white mineral salt increase [digestive] heat, treat distention [of the abdomen], belching, flatulence and phlegm-wind combinations." The sentence that follows the bracket names both salts and is outside the brackets, so it is the root text's statement for black and white mineral salt together. It is the only action attributed to black salt in Clark.

**rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (FMT).** Three places. (1) The salty taste group (line 3211) lists "Kharu tsha" among the substances with a salty taste. (2) The efficacy of medicinal substances (line 3400): "Kharu tsha and Tsabru tsha generate heat; reduce flatulence, eructation, abdominal distension and the combined disorders of baekan and loong." (3) The group of medicines that cure combined disorders of *baekan* and *loong* (line 3615) names "Men ga, Gakya, Shingkun, Kharu tsha, onion and garlic". The three places agree with Clark's one sentence: heat, flatulence, belching, distention, and the combination of phlegm and wind disorders.

**The name "sanchal".** Clark prints "Sanchal" in quotation marks as an English name in the heading and gives no source for it. No text on disk glosses it. The Ayurvedic texts on disk (Sushruta) name a salt *Sauvarchala* (for example in the lists at Bhishagratna Vol. 1, lines 17015 and 21687), but Clark does not connect his "Sanchal" to it and the Codex does not assert that link. **What the entry is not.** It is not Sushruta's salt and is not tagged to the Ayurvedic tradition. White mineral salt (*tsabs.ru.tsha*) is a separate row.

### Active Components

The sources name no constituent. In general terms, dark salts of this kind are mostly sodium chloride with impurities that give the colour, and "artificial black salt" would be a product of heating or processing with other materials. Neither text says how the artificial type was made. [USER: verify with a source on Tibetan salts before adding any detail.]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the texts say it is for.** Increasing digestive heat, treating abdominal distention, belching, flatulence and combined phlegm-wind disorders (Clark 931, with white mineral salt; Men-Tsee-Khang 3400, 3615).
- **What the texts do not say.** No dose, no preparation, no frequency.

The Codex gives no protocol. Salt is not a remedy to be added to a diet on the strength of this entry.

### Preparation & Dosing

The texts give no preparation. Clark's bracketed commentary says only that the best is "very clear" and that the artificial type is common (line 930). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any dose from this entry.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Sodium chloride is the main compound of table and rock salts; excess salt raises blood pressure and strains the kidneys. The effects the texts name (digestive heat, flatulence, belching) are not translated into modern findings. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011).** Kharu tsha is listed in the salty-taste group (line 3211). The salty taste is described (line 3231) as one that "relaxes stiffness of the body, clears bowel obstructions and blocked energy channels", with heated-salt compresses that "induce sweating, generates body heat and improves appetite", and an excess that causes baldness, grey hair, wrinkles, loss of strength, thirst, leprosy and blood and *tripa* disorders. The salty taste pacifies *loong* and *baekan*, but "with the exception of Che nyang tsha and Gyam tsha, all other salty substances aggravate tripa disorders" (line 3251); black salt is not one of the two exceptions. The post-digestive taste of the salty taste is sweet (line 3262). The entry itself prints no taste or potency. "Generates heat" at line 3400 is the substance's own action.
- **FMT (Clark 1995).** "Increase [digestive] heat" (line 931). No taste or potency is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan medicine, rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed.* Black salt has no cultivation status in the texts read; it is a therapeutic substance for digestive and phlegm-wind disorders. The frame it sits inside is the tantra's own account of disease: Clark's text says that "the sole cause of all disease is said to be ignorance due to lack of understanding of the meaning of selflessness" (line 440), that "from ignorance arise the three poisons of attachment, hatred and closedmindedness whence are produced in turn the humors wind, bile and phlegm" (line 441), and that the humors are "rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration respectively" (line 51). A medicine like this one acts on a humoral disorder, which the text places downstream of that root cause. No source retrieved assigns this salt a place in essence-extraction (*bcud len*) or any practice, and the Codex asserts none; there is no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice to place it in.

**Practitioner's note.** A heating salt for digestive and phlegm-wind complaints and nothing more in the record. A cultivator is not asked to take it; the text itself places the root of disease in the mind, not in a substance.

### Contraindications

- **Salt load.** People with high blood pressure, kidney, heart or liver disease should not add salt on this basis. No statement in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Tripa disorders.** The text says salty substances aggravate *tripa* disorders, and that excess salt does harm (Men-Tsee-Khang 3231, 3251).
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** No statement in the sources retrieved. Avoid. [USER: verify]
- **Identity.** "Black salt" is Clark's English; the chemistry and the word "sanchal" are not settled by the texts. [USER: verify]
