# Oleander / Nerium oleander — Nerion (Dioscorides 4-82); Asva-maraka, Haya-maraka, Karavira (Sushruta, printed "Nerium odorum")

## *Nerium oleander* (the Dioscorides translators' suggested identification, "Nerium, Oleander [Fuchs], Nerium oleander — Rose Bay, Oleander"; Bhishagratna's Appendix 3 prints "Nerium odorum" against three Sanskrit names) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM, SS

### Origin & Tradition

**Identity note.** This row was added 2026-10-09 (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) because two texts on disk each name a plant that their translators call by a *Nerium* binomial. The two spellings *Nerium oleander* (Dioscorides translators) and *Nerium odorum* (Bhishagratna) are merged here as printed; the row has not been checked against a modern botanical authority. [USER: verify] In both texts the binomial is the translator's, not the ancient author's.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000).

*4-82 NERION* (heading at line 8767; text from line 8770). The SUGGESTED line reads "Nerium, Oleander [Fuchs], Nerium oleander — Rose Bay, Oleander", and the chapter is marked POISONOUS. It says: "Nerium is a well-known shrub, with longer, thicker leaves than the almond; a flower similar to a rose, and fruit similar to that of the almond, but with a horn which (opened) is full of a downy stuff similar to thistledown. The root is sharp, pointed, long, woody, and brackish to the taste. It grows in enclosed greens, seabordering places and in places near rivers. The flower and the leaves are able to kill dogs, asses, mules and most four footed living creatures, but they preserve men [dead bodies, perhaps]." The bracket is the translators'. Then: "A decoction is taken as a drink with wine against the bites of venomous creatures, and more so if you mix it with rue [3-52, 3-53, 4-98]; but more weak sorts of living creatures such as goats and sheep die if they drink the steepings of them." The synonyms printed are "rododaphne, rhododendron, spongos, or haemostaris", with Roman "rhododendron, oleander, and laurorosa", Lucanian "icmane", Egyptian "scinphe" and African "rhodedaphane". The text of 4-82 therefore states plainly that the plant kills animals and then, in the same chapter, records a wine decoction against venomous bites. It names no dose.

**Sanskrit — Sushruta (SS).** The translation is Bhishagratna (1907-1916). Three Sanskrit names appear in Appendix 3 ("Names of Drugs, with Latin synonyms", `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`) against "Nerium odorum", and the pairing was checked in column order, as the OCR prints the Sanskrit and Latin columns separately.

- *As'va-maraka*: Sanskrit list line 31297 (between As'va-karna and Asvattha). The Latin column, counted from the aligned entries before it (Asvagandha with *Withania somnifera*, As'va-karna with "See S'iila") and after it (Asvattha with *Ficus religiosa*, Atasi with *Linum usitatissimum*), gives "Nerium Odorum." at line 31344. The pairing holds.
- *Haya-maraka*, printed by the OCR as "Haya-msiraka" (line 31687): in the same list the run Hasti-Pippali, Hasti-sundi, Haya-maraka, Hijjala, Hila-mochika matches the Latin run *Scindapsus officinalis*, *Heliotropium indicum*, "Nerium odorum" (line 31744), *Barringtonia acutangula*, *Enhydra*. This holds as well.
- *Karavira* (line 31922): the Latin column carries "Netium Odorum" (an OCR form of *Nerium odorum*) at line 32007, in a run that pairs Karavella with *Momordica* and Kari-pippali with a cross-reference. This holds too. Karavira was not named in the batch note; it is cited because it was read.

The text uses these names in the chapters. *Hayamaraka* is named in Sutrasthana ch.36 (`sutra/SS_Sutra_ch36.txt`, line 76): "A plaster composed of Chiravilva, Agnika, Danti, Chitraka, Hayamaraka and the dung of pigeons, vultures and storks (Kanka) pasted together, would lead to the spontaneous bursting of a swelling" (the *darana* plaster). *Ashvamaraka* is named in Sutrasthana ch.11 (line 128) in the list of woods, leaves, roots and fruits to be burnt to ashes for the preparation of alkali (*kshara*). *Karavira* is named in several places: in the *shodhana* decoction for washing an ulcer (ch.36, line 90), in the list of errhines (ch.39, lines 51 and 61), and in chikitsa ch.19 (line 279; a leaf decoction to wash an incidental ulcer), ch.20 (line 167; an oil with Malati, Chitraka and Naktamala "highly efficacious" for alopecia) and uttara ch.47 (line 221; a paste for the body in *madatyaya*). All of these are surgical or local uses. The Sushruta chapters read give no rasa, virya or vipaka for these plants by name, and none of these uses is a *rasayana* or *vajikarana* use.

### Active Components

*Nerium oleander* is generally known to contain cardiac glycosides, with oleandrin the best known, and these account for the plant's toxicity to animals and people. No analysis of the plants named in either text was read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records a deadly plant for animals and a wine decoction against venomous bites; Sushruta records plaster, alkali, errhine and ulcer-wash uses under the Sanskrit names. The plant is dangerously poisonous. The Codex gives no protocol for it and does not recommend any internal use.

### Preparation & Dosing

Dioscorides 4-82 (text from line 8770, continuing past a page break): a decoction taken as a drink with wine against venomous bites, better with rue. Sushruta: the plant (as *Hayamaraka*) pasted in a bursting plaster (ch.36, line 76); burnt for alkali (*Ashvamaraka*, ch.11, line 128); a leaf decoction (as *Karavira*) to wash an ulcer (chikitsa ch.19, line 279); an oil with other plants for alopecia (ch.20, line 167). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Oleander is a recognised poisonous plant whose cardiac glycosides can cause serious arrhythmias, and poisoning is a medical emergency. This is a general statement and no study was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** no warming, cooling or other quality is printed for 4-82; the chapter is marked POISONOUS by the translators and the taste of the root is given as "brackish".
- **SS:** no rasa, virya or vipaka is printed for any of the three names in the chapters read.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. Chapter 4-82 offers a discipline of attention: how to know the plant (leaves longer and thicker than the almond, a rose-like flower, a horned fruit full of down, a pointed woody root), where it grows (enclosed greens, the seaside, riverbanks), and what it does to animals. It assigns no status to the person who takes it, and the identification with *N. oleander* is the translators'.

**Sanskrit — Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The plant is claimed under three Sanskrit names for surgical and local uses (a bursting plaster, an alkali, errhines, ulcer washes, a hair oil). It is not named in any *rasayana* or *vajikarana* chapter among the passages read, and no *ojas* or other vital-substrate action is attested for it. The text read does not explain the names *Haya-maraka* and *Asva-maraka*.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing here for a cultivator to take. The plant is a poison in both texts' terms and by general knowledge. The honest practice is to learn it so as not to be harmed by it, and to leave it alone.

### Contraindications

Every part of the oleander shrub is dangerously poisonous, and ingestion can be fatal and needs urgent medical care. Do not use in pregnancy. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
