### Origin & Tradition

Bael is a spiny tree of the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia bearing a hard, woody-shelled fruit with aromatic, mucilaginous pulp. It is among the most sacred plants of Hinduism — the trifoliate **bilva** leaf is offered to Shiva, and the tree is planted at temples — and one of the most important fruits of the Ayurvedic materia medica. The fruit holds a striking dual identity by ripeness: the **unripe (or half-ripe) fruit** is intensely astringent and is the classic remedy for **chronic diarrhoea and dysentery**, while the **ripe fruit** is sweet, mucilaginous, cooling and gently laxative-nutritive, taken as a refreshing sherbet and a mild digestive/demulcent.

In Ayurveda the unripe bael (**Bilva**, often as a dried-slice decoction or the preparation *Bilva avaleha*) is a premier *grahi* (absorbent, bowel-binding) drug for the chronic, sub-acute and dysenteric bowel — for *atisara* and *pravahika* (diarrhoea and dysentery), irritable and over-loose bowels, and as an intestinal restorative after infection, valued for being astringent and binding without being merely constipating. The ripe fruit, by contrast, is used for constipation, as a cooling demulcent for the gut, and as a nourishing convalescent food. The Unani tradition concurs in this ripeness-dependent dual use.

The fruit's astringency derives from **tannins**, while a characteristic coumarin, **marmelosin (imperatorin-type)**, and mucilage and pectin contribute to its activity, giving the traditional anti-diarrhoeal and demulcent uses a phytochemical footing.

### Contraindications

- **Ripeness confusion.** Use the unripe fruit for diarrhoea and the ripe for constipation — the reverse aggravates.
- **Constipation (unripe).** The astringent unripe fruit can over-bind.
- **Furanocoumarin photosensitivity.** Possible with the coumarin content.
- **Pregnancy.** Food use of ripe fruit is ordinary; medicinal unripe-fruit use [USER: verify].
- **Diabetes (ripe sherbet).** The sweet ripe sherbet is often sugared.
