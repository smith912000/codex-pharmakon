## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Allium sativum* L. (1753)
**Key subspecies:**
- *A. sativum* var. *ophioscorodon* (Rokkenbolt) Döll — hardneck (stiff-neck) garlic; more complex flavor; shorter storage
- *A. sativum* var. *sativum* — softneck garlic; commercial cultivation; longer shelf life; papery multiple sheaths

**Traditional names:**
| Tradition | Name | Transliteration | Notes |
|-----------|------|-----------------|-------|
| DMM (Greek) | σκόροδον | *skorodon* | Universal Greek/Roman term |
| Canon of Medicine (Arabic) | ثوم | *thūm* | Semitic root; Hebrew שום (*shum*) parallel |
| SBJ/CM (Chinese) | 大蒜 | *Dàsuàn* | "large garlic"; distinguished from 小蒜 xiǎosuàn (wild garlic) |
| Ayurveda/SS (Sanskrit) | लशुन | *Lashuna* / *Rasona* | *Ra* = rasa (taste); *sona* = 5 of 6 tastes present |
| Ebers Papyrus (Egyptian) | *ḥdw* | — | Multiple hieroglyphic attestations |
| Assyrian (AT) | *šūmu* | — | Cognate with Semitic root |
| English | Garlic | — | Old English *gārleac* (spear-leek); gar = spear (leaf shape) |
| German | Knoblauch | — | |

**Botanical notes:** Perennial herb (cultivated as annual); bulb 3–6 cm diameter; composed of 4–20 cloves enclosed in papery tunic. Rarely sets viable seed (triploid origin, largely sterile); propagated exclusively by clove division. The wild ancestor is likely *Allium longicuspis* (Central Asian origin). Over 600 named cultivars exist globally, with significant variation in allicin yield, flavor profile, and sulfur compound concentration.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 De Materia Medica (Dioscorides, c. 50–70 CE)
DMM Book III.47: *Skorodon* — one of Dioscorides' most extensively described plants, with seven distinct therapeutic applications documented:
1. Diuretic ("passes urine")
2. Carminative ("drives out flatulence")
3. Expectorant-vermicidal ("purges stomach worms with honey")
4. Antivenom ("effective against bites of viper and rabid dog")
5. Antimicrobial/wound ("applied with rue and olive oil for the bites of creeping things")
6. Respiratory ("boiled with oregano kills lice and fleas")
7. Cardiovascular ("the head is made of many cloves — each eating it in full dose warms the whole body")

Dioscorides also notes: "*Raw garlic is harder to digest; boiled garlic is gentler but less effective.*" — the raw/cooked pharmacological distinction maintained 2,000 years later.

### 2.2 Canon of Medicine (Ibn Sīnā, 1025 CE)
*Thūm* — among the most extensive entries in Book II of the Canon. Ibn Sīnā classifies garlic as *ḥār yābis* (hot and dry, 4th degree) — the most extreme heating category in Unani pharmacology. Applications:
- "Protects against epidemic diseases" (infectious disease prevention recognized pre-bacteriological era)
- Expels intestinal worms (*dūd al-amʿāʾ*)
- Relieves chronic cough and asthma
- Diuretic; urinary tract infections
- Topical for skin conditions, fungal infections, alopecia
- CONTRAINDICATED in inflammatory conditions (hot/dry constitution; eye conditions)

Ibn Sīnā's epidemiological observation — that garlic *protects against epidemics* — is the first explicit statement in the medical literature linking a food substance to infectious disease prevention. The mechanism (allicin killing pathogens) was unknown; the clinical observation was accurate.

### 2.3 Shennong Bencao Jing / Bencao Gangmu (SBJ)
*Dàsuàn* (大蒜) — one of the "50 Fundamental Herbs" of TCM. Li Shizhen (*Bencao Gangmu*, 1596): "*Kills toxic qi; removes accumulations; disperses swellings; resolves toxins; kills insects.*" TCM classification: warming (温/热), pungent (辛), enters Spleen, Stomach, Lung channels. Specific applications: dysentery (arguably the most prescient early antimicrobial application); diarrhea; edema; respiratory infections; topical for skin infections. Li Shizhen's dysentery application represents an accurate pre-bacteriological identification of garlic's activity against *Shigella*, *Salmonella*, and enteric *E. coli*.

### 2.4 Sushruta Samhita / Charaka Samhita (c. 600 BCE – 200 CE)
*Lashuna* — Sanskrit etymology encodes pharmacology: "*rasona*" means "one taste absent" — garlic has 5 of the 6 Ayurvedic tastes (madhura-sweet, amla-sour, katu-pungent, tikta-bitter, kashaya-astringent; only lavana/salty absent). This is accurate observational pharmacognosy — garlic's complex flavor profile maps to multiple receptor activations. Charaka Samhita: cardiac tonic (*hridya*); aphrodisiac; for *vata* disorders (pain, constipation, weakness). Contraindicated in inflammatory conditions with Pitta excess — consistent with Ibn Sīnā's hot/dry constitution contraindication.

### 2.5 Ebers Papyrus (c. 1552 BCE)
*Ḥdw* — among the most frequently appearing plants in the Ebers Papyrus; appears in 22 distinct formulae:
- Intestinal parasites (tapeworm, roundworm)
- Heart disease formulae
- Tumors (topical preparations)
- Headache and mental conditions
- "To cool the womb" (gynecological)

**Pyramid documentation:** Herodotus (*Histories* II.125, c. 440 BCE): "*The sum inscribed on the pyramid in Egyptian characters states how much was spent on radishes, onions, and garlic for the workmen [who built Khufu's pyramid]. The interpreter... told me the sum was 1,600 talents of silver.*" This inscribed economic record (c. 2560 BCE) makes garlic's nutritional and medicinal deployment for pyramid builders one of the most precisely economically documented interventions in ancient medicine.

### 2.6 Assyrian Medical Tablets (c. 700–600 BCE)
*Šūmu* — extensively documented in cuneiform pharmaceutical texts; wound preparations; digestive; antiparasitic. The Semitic root (*šūmu/thūm/shum*) shared across Assyrian, Arabic, and Hebrew traditions suggests a single cultural transmission path through the Fertile Crescent.

### 2.7 Four Medical Tantras / rGyud-bzhi (FMT)
Tibetan medicine: *gogbo* ( གོག་པོ*) — warming; treats cold-type digestive disorders, intestinal parasites, respiratory congestion; part of the warming/digestive herb category.

### 2.8 Badianus Manuscript (BM) — TENTATIVE
*Allium sativum* is Old World; not native to pre-Columbian Americas. Post-Columbian introduction was rapid and thorough, but the BM (1552, only 30 years post-contact) almost certainly references local *Allium* relatives rather than cultivated garlic. Absent from BM proper; local *Allium* species used.

### 2.9 South American Indigenous (SA) — TENTATIVE
Native South American *Allium* species (wild garlic, wild onion relatives) exist; *A. sativum* is post-Columbian introduction. Scored as tentative.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

**Convergence: 7/9** (8/9 if tentative BM/SA Allium relatives counted). The most universally documented antimicrobial plant in the Codex, with continuous cross-cultural use spanning from Egypt's pyramid inscriptions (c. 2560 BCE) through every major medical tradition to the present day.

**Core convergent applications:**
1. **Antimicrobial / antiparasitic:** DMM / CM / SBJ / SS / EP / AT / FMT — seven traditions independently identify garlic as killing intestinal parasites, treating dysentery/gastroenteritis, preventing infections. This represents the most robustly convergent antimicrobial plant in the Codex.
2. **Cardiovascular:** DMM (warming/circulatory) / SS (cardiac tonic) / EP (heart formulae) / FMT — four traditions; modern pharmacology confirms antihypertensive, antiplatelet, mild cholesterol-lowering.
3. **Respiratory:** DMM / CM / SS / SBJ — four traditions for cough, bronchial secretions, respiratory infections.

**Most significant convergence finding:** The *prevention of epidemic disease* is documented in CM (Ibn Sīnā: "*protects against epidemic diseases*") and Chinese tradition (Li Shizhen: "*kills toxic qi*") — a pre-bacteriological epidemiological observation that allicin's broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity now supports. The traditional use of garlic during epidemics (plague years, cholera outbreaks) represents one of the earliest population-level antimicrobial interventions.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

### 4.1 The Allicin Cascade — Fundamental Chemistry

**CRITICAL PRINCIPLE:** Garlic chemistry is unlike almost any other plant in the Codex because the primary bioactive compound **does not exist in the intact plant** — it is generated only on cell disruption.

```
INTACT CELL:
  Vacuole: Alliin (S-allyl-L-cysteine sulfoxide) — INACTIVE
  Cytoplasm: Alliinase enzyme — INACTIVE (separated)

ON CRUSHING/CUTTING/CHEWING:
  Alliin + Alliinase → ALLICIN (allyl 2-propenethiosulfinate)
  Formation time: <10 seconds
  Half-life: ~16 hours at room temperature
  Destroyed by: heat >60°C; alkaline pH; prolonged storage

ALLICIN DOWNSTREAM CONVERSION:
  + Heat → Diallyl sulfide (DAS) + Diallyl disulfide (DADS) + Diallyl trisulfide (DATS)
  + Acid/oil → E-ajoene + Z-ajoene + vinyl dithiins
  + Aging → S-allyl cysteine (SAC) [via separate γ-glutamyl pathway]
```

**This cascade means:**
- Raw garlic = allicin dominant (antimicrobial, thiol-reacting)
- Cooked garlic = DAS/DADS/DATS dominant (cardiovascular, anticancer; H₂S donors)
- Aged garlic extract (AGE) = SAC dominant (antioxidant, neuroprotective; no GI irritation)
- Commercial garlic supplements = highly variable; only "allicin-standardized" products guarantee active compound

### 4.2 Compound-by-Compound Profile

**Alliin** (S-allyl-L-cysteine sulfoxide):
- 0.5–1.4% fresh weight
- Non-proteogenic amino acid; pharmacologically inert in intact clove
- Sole precursor to allicin on alliinase activation
- Water-soluble; stable; the "ingredient" in garlic that converts to medicine

**Allicin** (allyl 2-propenethiosulfinate; thio-2-propene-1-sulfinic acid):
- 0.2–0.4% (fresh basis; highest yield from large, pungent cloves)
- Unstable thiosulfinate; highly reactive with thiol groups (-SH)
- Antimicrobial mechanism: covalent modification of cysteine residues in bacterial/fungal/parasitic enzymes
- Responsible for fresh garlic's pungent smell
- NOT absorbed systemically as intact allicin; decomposes in GI tract

**Diallyl disulfide (DADS):**
- Primary volatile from cooked garlic; responsible for cooked garlic smell
- Antimicrobial (less potent than allicin); anticancer; mild H₂S donor
- Blood-borne: causes "garlic breath" via pulmonary excretion

**Diallyl trisulfide (DATS):**
- Stronger H₂S donor than DADS
- Most potent anticancer allyl sulfide in vitro (mitochondrial apoptosis induction)
- Vasorelaxant via H₂S → K_ATP channel opening

**Ajoene** (E-ajoene and Z-ajoene):
- Forms from allicin in acidic/oily environments (e.g., garlic in olive oil)
- **GPIIb/IIIa antagonist** (fibrinogen receptor): same mechanism as pharmaceutical abciximab (ReoPro) — prevents platelet aggregation
- Antifungal (ergosterol biosynthesis disruption)
- Anticancer (apoptosis induction)
- Approximately 0.1–0.4% in garlic-in-oil preparations

**S-Allyl cysteine (SAC):**
- 0.003–0.008% fresh weight; 0.03–0.04% in aged garlic extract (AGE — 18+ months fermentation)
- Water-soluble; highly bioavailable (>98% in humans)
- Antioxidant; NF-κB inhibitor; Nrf2 activator
- Mild antihypertensive; neuroprotective (AChE inhibition; prevents Aβ aggregation in Alzheimer's models)
- NO thiol-trapping mechanism → no GI irritation; suitable for sensitive patients

**Allixin** (phytoalexin):
- Stress-induced; antifungal; anticancer; minor component

**Organoselenium compounds** (selenomethionine, γ-glutamyl-Se-methylselenocysteine):
- Concentration dependent on soil selenium content (highly variable geographically)
- Anticancer (selenium incorporation into selenoproteins → glutathione peroxidase → reduced oxidative damage)

**Fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS):**
- 2–6% dry weight; prebiotic; selectively feeds *Bifidobacterium* and *Lactobacillus*
- Explains gut microbiome modulation independent of antimicrobial activity

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 Thiol-Trapping Antimicrobial (Allicin)
Allicin reacts with free sulfhydryl (-SH) groups of cysteine residues in bacterial, fungal, and parasitic enzymes:
- Inhibits acetyl-CoA forming enzymes (acetate kinase, phosphotransacetylase)
- Inhibits GAPDH (glycolysis) in bacteria
- Inhibits thiol-dependent proteases in *H. pylori*, *Giardia*, *Entamoeba*
- Inhibits cysteine-dependent transcription factors

**Spectrum:** Active against *Staphylococcus aureus* (including MRSA), *Streptococcus*, *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *Shigella*, *Klebsiella*, *H. pylori*, *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* (in vitro), *Candida albicans*, *Aspergillus*, *Cryptosporidium*, *Giardia*, intestinal parasites. Minimum inhibitory concentrations in the low μg/mL range for most organisms.

**Resistance pattern:** Conventional antibiotic resistance mechanisms (efflux pumps, enzyme inactivation, altered binding sites) do not confer resistance to allicin's thiol-trapping mechanism. MRSA strains resistant to methicillin/vancomycin remain fully sensitive to allicin at equivalent concentrations to methicillin-sensitive strains.

### 5.2 Antiplatelet Mechanisms (Ajoene + DATS)
**Ajoene:** Irreversible GPIIb/IIIa (integrin αIIbβ3) antagonism → blocks fibrinogen binding → prevents platelet aggregation. Mechanism identical to abciximab (pharmaceutical GPIIb/IIIa antagonist used in acute coronary syndrome).

**DATS → H₂S:** H₂S activates K_ATP channels in platelets → membrane hyperpolarization → reduced calcium influx → impaired thromboxane A₂ synthesis → reduced TXA₂-mediated platelet activation. This is independent of the GPIIb/IIIa pathway — two simultaneous antiplatelet mechanisms.

**Clinical consequence:** Garlic produces clinically measurable antiplatelet effects at therapeutic doses (≥600 mg/day dried powder or 2 raw cloves/day). Perioperative discontinuation is mandatory.

### 5.3 Antihypertensive (H₂S + NO)
**H₂S pathway:** DATS (and other polysulfides) reacts with glutathione intracellularly → H₂S release → vascular smooth muscle K_ATP channel opening → hyperpolarization → vasorelaxation. H₂S is a physiological gasotransmitter (like NO) — garlic is among the richest dietary sources.

**NO pathway:** Garlic extract increases eNOS (endothelial NO synthase) expression and activity → increased NO → cGMP → PKG → smooth muscle relaxation → vasodilation. ACE inhibitory peptides in aged garlic extract provide additional minor antihypertensive contribution.

### 5.4 Cholesterol Modulation (HMG-CoA Reductase Inhibition)
S-Allylmercaptocysteine (SAMC) and ajoene inhibit HMG-CoA reductase (rate-limiting enzyme in cholesterol synthesis) — same target as statin drugs. Clinical effect is modest (not statin-equivalent) due to lower concentrations achievable at standard doses and incomplete bioavailability. Additional mechanism: bile acid sequestration by FOS fiber.

### 5.5 CYP3A4 and P-gp INDUCTION — CRITICAL DRUG INTERACTION
**Garlic is a CYP3A4 INDUCER** — the OPPOSITE of black pepper (CYP3A4 inhibitor, I_161).

Allicin and DADS activate the pregnane X receptor (PXR) → upregulation of CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein expression. Net effect: **decreased plasma concentrations of CYP3A4/P-gp substrate drugs**.

Documented interactions:
- Saquinavir (HIV protease inhibitor): 51% reduction in AUC (Piscitelli 2002)
- Ritonavir: significant plasma level reduction
- Warfarin: reduced anticoagulant effect documented
- Cyclosporine: reduced levels (organ transplant rejection risk)
- Docetaxel and other CYP3A4 anticancer drugs: reduced exposure

**This interaction is dose-dependent:** Culinary amounts (1 clove/day) = negligible CYP induction. Supplement doses (≥600mg/day dried powder, or garlic oil capsules) = clinically significant induction. Raw garlic produces stronger induction than cooked (allicin more potent PXR activator than DAS).

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### 6.1 Hypertension
**Stabler et al. 2012 Cochrane meta-analysis:** 87 studies; 9 RCTs with hypertensive patients; garlic supplementation vs. placebo:
- Systolic BP: −8.4 mmHg (95% CI −11.5 to −5.3)
- Diastolic BP: −7.3 mmHg (95% CI −8.6 to −6.1)
- Statistically and clinically significant reduction in hypertensive patients
- Effect not significant in normotensive subjects (appropriate cardiovascular pharmacology: H₂S mechanism is vasodilatory only when tone is elevated)

**Kwak et al. 2014:** Aged garlic extract (AGE); meta-analysis of 7 RCTs; significant SBP reduction in hypertensives (−9.1 mmHg).

### 6.2 Cholesterol
**Ried et al. 2016 Cochrane:** 14 RCTs, N=710; garlic vs. placebo:
- Total cholesterol: −7.4 mg/dL (P<0.001); statistically significant, modest
- LDL: −3.75 mg/dL (P<0.05)
- HDL and triglycerides: non-significant
- Garlic powder > oil > AGE for cholesterol effects

### 6.3 Common Cold Prevention
**Lissiman et al. 2014 Cochrane:** 1 adequately powered RCT (Josling 2001, N=146); allicin-containing garlic supplement × 12 weeks winter:
- Garlic group: 24 cold episodes
- Placebo group: 65 cold episodes
- RR: 0.37 (62% risk reduction)
- Recovery time similar
- NNT ≈ 3 for 12-week cold prevention
- Conclusion: promising but evidence base is a single study; replication needed

### 6.4 H. pylori
Multiple small RCTs (N=20–80): garlic oil and aged garlic extract reduce *H. pylori* density in gastric mucosa (breath test); eradication rates insufficient as monotherapy. Used as adjunct to standard triple therapy in Chinese and Korean practices. MIC for *H. pylori* 40–80 μg allicin/mL (achievable topically, marginal systemically).

### 6.5 Anticancer (Epidemiology)
**Iowa Women's Health Study (Steinmetz 1994):** N=41,387; high garlic consumption associated with 50% reduced risk of colon cancer. Multiple cohort studies show inverse association between garlic consumption and gastric cancer risk (consistent: garlic → H. pylori suppression → reduced gastric cancer precursor). **No RCT of garlic for cancer prevention exists** — all evidence epidemiological or in vitro.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The Pyramid Builders' Ration (c. 2560 BCE):** The sum of 1,600 silver talents inscribed on the Great Pyramid for garlic, onion, and radish rations for construction workers is the oldest surviving economic record of food-as-medicine. These were not luxury items — they were industrial nutrition, specifically chosen for their antimicrobial and stamina-supporting properties in an environment with thousands of workers sharing water and housing. The economy of pharaonic Egypt partially organized itself around allium provisioning.

**WWII "Russian Penicillin" (1941–1945):** With antibiotic supply failing to reach the Eastern Front, Soviet field medicine systematically deployed garlic preparations for wound infections, dysentery, and typhoid in the field hospitals. German military observers coined the term *Russisches Penicillin* (Russian penicillin) after witnessing recovery rates from infected wounds treated with garlic extracts. The mechanism — allicin killing *Staphylococcus*, *Streptococcus*, *Clostridium*, and *Salmonella* — was later confirmed, but 1942 Soviet doctors were operating on empirical tradition directly descended from Dioscorides and the Ebers Papyrus.

**The Vampire Hypothesis:** Eastern European, Romanian, Serbian, and Bulgarian folklore prescribes garlic against vampires — hanging at doors, wearing around necks, placing in coffins with suspected vampires. Proposed pharmacological interpretations: (a) allicin and sulfur compounds repel mosquitoes and ticks, which serve as vectors for the malaria, plague, and other epidemic diseases that vampires may metaphorically represent; (b) allicin's antimicrobial activity kills the bacteria responsible for post-mortem bloating that caused bodies to appear "undead" when exhumed; (c) the folk observation that garlic-eating communities had lower rates of certain infectious diseases generated a protective narrative that was mythologized into vampire repellence. The protective reality (antimicrobial → lower infection rate) was encoded in supernatural protective narrative.

**"Garlic breath" as systemic pharmacokinetics:** Allyl methyl sulfide (formed from allicin → DADS in the body → hepatic metabolism → allyl methyl sulfide) is absorbed into the bloodstream and excreted through the lungs for 18–24 hours after garlic consumption. No mouthwash, toothpaste, mint, or oral rinse eliminates garlic breath because the odor is not in the mouth — it is being exhaled from the alveoli as the blood-borne sulfide passes through pulmonary circulation. This is accidental systemic pharmacokinetics demonstrable without instruments: you can smell when someone has eaten garlic while sitting beside them, and no amount of oral hygiene changes it. The social history of garlic avoidance in elite European settings (opera, court, dining with aristocracy) is entirely a function of this inescapable pharmacokinetic marker.

**Ayurvedic ambivalence — the *sattvic* exclusion:** Garlic is excluded from *sattvic* (pure/spiritual) dietary categories in certain Hindu and Jain traditions — along with onion, leek, shallot, and chive. The rationale: garlic stimulates *rajas* (passion/aggression) and *tamas* (inertia/dullness), disrupting meditative states. This classification exists alongside — and in contradiction to — extensive Ayurvedic medical documentation of garlic's therapeutic value. The same substance is simultaneously a *rasayana* (rejuvenating tonic) and spiritually polluting — a tension that reveals the multi-layered valuation system in Ayurvedic thought.

**The Gilgamesh Epic and allium:** The Epic of Gilgamesh (c. 2100 BCE) references allium plants in the context of death and immortality — Gilgamesh seeks *urshanabi* herb (possibly an allium) at the bottom of the ocean for immortality. While botanical identification is uncertain, the ancient Mesopotamian association of alliums with vitality, strength, and protection against death prefigures the medical documentation in Assyrian cuneiform tablets by 1,400 years.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation-Pharmacology Matrix
| Preparation | Primary Active | Primary Effect |
|-------------|---------------|----------------|
| Raw (crushed, wait 10 min) | Allicin | Antimicrobial (max allicin yield) |
| Raw (immediately eaten) | Alliin + alliinase | Allicin forms during chewing/digestion |
| Cooked (>60°C) | DADS, DATS, H₂S | Cardiovascular; anticancer; lower antimicrobial |
| Garlic oil (cold extraction) | Ajoene, vinyl dithiins | Antiplatelet; antifungal |
| Aged Garlic Extract (AGE) | SAC, SAMC | Antioxidant; cardiovascular; neuroprotective; no GI irritation |
| Garlic powder (allicin-standardized) | Alliin → allicin in vivo | Antimicrobial; antihypertensive; cholesterol |
| Garlic powder (non-standardized) | Variable | Unpredictable efficacy |

**Critical preparation note:** Crushing garlic and waiting 10 minutes before cooking allows alliinase to complete allicin formation before heat denatures the enzyme. This preserves some allicin-derived products even after cooking. This is the "crush first, cook later" kitchen technique that maximizes pharmacological retention through heating.

### 8.2 Dosing
| Application | Form | Dose |
|-------------|------|------|
| Hypertension | Aged garlic extract | 600–1,200 mg AGE/day |
| Antimicrobial (systemic support) | Allicin-standardized powder | 600–1,200 mg/day (≥6,000 μg allicin yield) |
| Cold prevention | Allicin-standardized supplement | 180mg × once/day (Josling trial formulation) |
| Cardiovascular (general) | Dietary (2–4 raw cloves/day) | Standard food use |
| Cholesterol | Garlic powder tablet | 300mg × 3/day (standardized) |

### 8.3 Contraindications and Drug Interactions

**ANTIPLATELET — surgical discontinuation mandatory:**
Garlic produces clinically significant antiplatelet effects at supplement doses. **Discontinue all garlic supplements 2 weeks before elective surgery.** Additive with aspirin, clopidogrel, warfarin, NSAIDs.

**CYP3A4 INDUCTION (supplement doses):**
Significantly REDUCES plasma levels of: saquinavir, ritonavir (all HIV protease inhibitors), cyclosporine, tacrolimus (immunosuppressants → organ rejection risk), warfarin (anticoagulant failure), chemotherapy drugs (docetaxel, paclitaxel, vincristine), statins.
**Culinary amounts (1–2 cloves/day): negligible CYP induction.**
**Supplement doses (≥600mg/day): clinically significant induction — review all concurrent medications.**

**H. pylori eradication regimens:**
If using garlic as adjunct to standard triple therapy — space raw garlic/allicin away from proton pump inhibitor to avoid allicin degradation at higher gastric pH.

**GI irritation:**
Raw garlic at high doses (>5 cloves/day) → GI burning, nausea, diarrhea. AGE is the appropriate form for sensitive GI patients — no allicin, no irritation.

**Topical burns:**
Raw garlic applied directly to skin → chemical burn from allicin (same mechanism as pepper spray, different chemistry). Always dilute topical garlic in carrier oil; never leave on skin >30 minutes.

**Pregnancy:**
Culinary amounts: safe. Medicinal doses: uterine stimulant effect documented (allicin's thiol reactivity in uterine smooth muscle); avoid concentrated supplements. *Sattvic* dietary exclusion in some traditions has pharmacological correlate: high allicin intake during pregnancy may contribute to uterine irritability.

### 8.4 Tradition-Practice Concordance
Garlic is the Codex's pre-eminent example of traditional antimicrobial medicine confirmed by modern pharmacology with a precisely characterized mechanism. The 3,500-year continuous cross-cultural record of garlic for intestinal parasites, wound infections, dysentery, and epidemic prevention — operating across seven independent traditions without any mechanism of knowledge transfer that could explain convergence by diffusion alone — represents perhaps the strongest convergent validation in the entire Codex. The Soviet WWII battlefield application is the most recent data point in an unbroken empirical series that began at the Great Pyramid. The addition of modern pharmacology (allicin thiol-trapping; ajoene GPIIb/IIIa antagonism; DATS H₂S vasorelaxation) does not change the traditional use — it explains it. The only surprise is the CYP3A4 induction direction, which runs opposite to black pepper (I_161): these two universal culinary herbs push drug bioavailability in opposite directions — garlic reduces it, black pepper increases it — and both are eaten daily in combination in cuisines worldwide.
