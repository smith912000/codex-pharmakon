# Bitter vetch — karsanna (كرسنة)

## *Vicia ervilia (L.) Willd. (ervil, bitter vetch), small lentil-like seed grown for fodder; the identification is the modern one, matched from the Canon's description and its quotation of Dioscorides' orobos* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** CM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Avicenna's *Canon* among the texts on disk.** The Dioscorides tradition has the same plant under *orobos* (2-131, called *ervum* by the Latins, English "Bitter Vetch", line 5631 of the English text); the entry here is Avicenna's, who quotes Dioscorides inside it. A search of the Volume I entries for "OROBOS" found no entry on 2-131. Book II, entry *karsanna*, Arabic text lines 14156 to 14174.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** What it is: "some said: a grain smaller than *al-malik* (a word the edition does not explain), of the size of the lentil, not flattened but ribbed, its colour between dusty-grey and yellow, its taste between the taste of the mung bean and the lentil, which cattle feed on (*yaʿtalifuhu al-baqar*)" (lines 14157 to 14159). The physician al-Khūzī "claimed that its grain resembles the grain of the quince"; Avicenna says "in my view that is *al-malik* or the wild kind in particular, and it may be white to yellow, as was said, or red" (lines 14159 to 14161). Then he quotes Dioscorides: "a small, fine herb, with dusty leaves, and its seed in ..." (line 14161); the quotation breaks off at a page boundary in the edition (line 14162).
Temperament: "hot in the first to the second, dry in the second" (line 14163). Properties: "opening and polishing (*mufattiḥa jāliya*); it has a bad humour (*khilṭ radīʾ*), and its correction is like the correction of lupin; the whitish ones are less medicinal than the red; when cooked twice, its polishing is reduced and its earthy part remains, so it nourishes with a dry nourishment" (lines 14163 to 14165). Cosmetic: "a good paint on vitiligo, freckles, leucoderma and scars, it improves the colour; a meal (*sawīq*) is made from it and given to the emaciated, as much as a nut, and removes emaciation; its decoction poured on chilblains and their itch cures them; and it benefits *al-labaniyya*" (lines 14165 to 14168). Swellings: "it softens hardnesses and especially hardness of the breast" (lines 14168 to 14169). Wounds: "it cleans ulcers with honey and benefits scalp scab, and softens the hardness of ulcers that kill flesh and limb, and benefits Persian fire (*al-nār al-fārisiyya*) and *al-shahdiyya*" (lines 14169 to 14171). Chest: "it benefits hardness of the breast and makes the expectoration of thick matter easy" (line 14171). Excretory organs: "much of it makes one pass blood in the urine by the strength of its diuresis, and it relaxes the bowels; ground with vinegar and drunk it benefits difficult urination and calms tenesmus and colic" (lines 14172 to 14173). Poisons: "dressed with wine on the bite of the viper and of a rabid dog" and on a fasting person's bite (lines 14173 to 14174). No dose is given beyond "as much as a nut" for the meal.
The flour is also named elsewhere in the Canon in poultices (lines 9015, 10817, 15490 to 15493). Those lines were found by search and not studied.

**Dioscorides cross-reference.** The English Dioscorides on disk (2-131, *orobos*) says the seed meal "ervina" is "good for the bowels, diuretic, and causes a good colour," that eaten in excess it brings blood through the urine and intestines, that with honey it cleans ulcers, freckles and sunburn, and that it softens hard lumps of the breast. Avicenna's entry agrees with this closely, as it should if he follows him.

### Active Components

The texts name none. As a general fact, the seed of *Vicia ervilia* is known as livestock feed and as a source of toxins; the seeds are generally processed (soaked, cooked) before food use, and the Canon's own remark on "a bad humour" and the lupin-like correction fits that. [USER: verify.]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The text records a seed as a skin paint, a softening poultice for hard swellings, a diuretic and a wound cleaner. It also records the drug as one that makes the urine bloody in excess. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

Preparations in the entry: seed meal (*sawīq*), "as much as a nut" for emaciation; decoction poured on; poultice with wine; ground with vinegar to drink; with honey on ulcers. Dose: only "as much as a nut" for the meal (lines 14166 to 14167). No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify.]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify.] The seed's toxicity is a known matter in animal feed; the Canon's "bad humour" and Dioscorides' warning about blood in the urine point the same way.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Avicenna: hot in the first to the second degree, dry in the second (line 14163).

### Cultivation Register

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** The entry gives a meal to fatten the emaciated: "it removes emaciation" (فيزيل الهزال). That is a claim about the body's wasting, and the entry does not tie it to innate heat or radical moisture. No cultivation status is assigned to the seed. **(b) Framed: a restorative claim for the emaciated is attested, but it is a therapeutic claim in the Canon, not a cultivation status.**

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing here to practise. A cultivator may read the entry for its own caution: the same seed that fattens the thin is said to carry a bad humour and to need correction, as lupin does.

### Contraindications

- The Canon itself says the seed has a bad humour and needs correcting, and in excess it passes blood in the urine (line 14172).
- Seeds known to be toxic to livestock are not food for people without processing. [USER: verify.]
- Pregnancy and kidney disease: not addressed in the text.
