# Sting ray — Dioscorides, TRUGON THALASSIA (trugon thalassia; "pastinaca marina")
## *The tail spine ("radius") of the sea sting ray; the translator's gloss prints "Trigonidae pastinaca — Sting Ray"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The sting ray is chapter 2-22 of the *De Materia Medica*, in Book Two, in the section "Living Creatures", in the Osbaldeston and Wood English version (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, lines 5012-5014; printed page 190 in the index). The chapter is headed "2-22. TRUGON THALASSIA", the translator's gloss reads "SUGGESTED: Trigonidae pastinaca — Sting Ray", and the whole text of the chapter is one sentence: "The radius of the pastinaca marina that grows out of its tail (with scales turned backward) lessens a pained tooth for it breaks and expels it."

So the part used is not the flesh of the fish but the spine from its tail, which the translator renders "radius" and describes in the text's own bracket as having "scales turned backward". The action is on a painful tooth: the spine "lessens" the pain, and the text gives the reason in the same sentence, that it "breaks and expels it", meaning the tooth. No route is stated (whether the spine is pressed against the tooth, used to pick at it, or powdered), no preparation is given and no dose. The chapter does not say what is done with the spine before use.

The name "pastinaca marina", the sea parsnip, is the Latin name by which the chapter itself refers to the fish in the English text. The root vegetable parsnip, *Pastinaca sativa*, is a different thing and has its own chapters. The project's source list carries this row (`_sources/dioscorides.csv`, line 203, "Trugon Thalassia, Trigonidae pastinaca") with the class "plant", which is a list label and not a statement of Dioscorides; the chapter is plainly about a sea animal. A different fish, the electric ray (2-17, "Narke"), is a separate chapter and is not this animal. The sting ray is not described in any other chapter read: a search of the English text for "sting ray" finds this chapter and the index only.

### Active Components

The tail spine of a sting ray is a serrated, mineralised barb. In living rays it is covered with a sheath of skin that carries venom glands; the dried spine alone is mostly bone-like tissue (calcium phosphate and collagen). The text says nothing of venom. No active principle is named in the chapter.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The one sourced use is on a painful tooth. The Codex gives no protocol: a toothache needs a dentist, a sharp animal spine in the mouth risks injury and infection, and the spine of a living sting ray is a source of dangerous wounds.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation, route or dose is given in the sources retrieved: the chapter names the part (the spine from the tail) and the effect, and nothing more. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. A tooth that hurts has a cause (decay, infection, a cracked tooth) that a dentist can find. An animal spine used in the mouth can wound the gum and introduce bacteria. Sting ray spines cause painful, easily infected wounds. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and this one-sentence chapter attaches none to the sting ray. What the chapter does offer, as the discipline of attention that the Codex finds in Dioscorides, is specificity of part: it is the spine "that grows out of its tail", with its scales turned backward, and not the fish as a whole. The pneuma and the Galenic spirits are later and are not read in this chapter.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a single remedy for toothache using a dangerous animal part, with no preparation and no dose. The honest use of the entry is as a record of what Dioscorides says.

### Contraindications

- **Injury and infection:** a sharp, serrated barb used in the mouth can wound and infect; do not use it.
- **Handling live rays:** the tail spine of a living ray can cause severe wounds (puncture, venom, infection); the barb should not be handled.
- **Toothache:** tooth pain needs dental examination; an abscess can spread.
- **Legal and conservation status:** many ray species are protected or threatened and their trade is controlled; the species is not given in the chapter. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** taking a ray for a spine with no supported benefit is hard to justify.
