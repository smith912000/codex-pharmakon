# Roe deer — kha.sha (Clark: "Spotted Deer", Capreolus capreolus L.; Men-Tsee-Khang: "Kha sha", "roe deer"), rGyud-bzhi
## *The deer Clark's materia medica prints as "kha.sha: Spotted Deer: Capreolus capreolus L."; the Men-Tsee-Khang translation calls its horn "roe deer horn" in the text and "Spotted Deer" in its glossary; the English name "roe deer" is the spine's* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The deer is entered in the *Horn* section of the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica (ch.20) in Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 1153; `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` row 223): "kha.sha: Spotted Deer: Capreolus capreolus L.: [has two names. It has bulky horns approximately fifteen centimeters long]." The next entry, sha.wa, closes: "The horns [of these two types of deer have] similar [action] to Rhino horn" (line 1154), and rhino horn "dries [accumulations of] pus, blood and lymph in the stomach [and is beneficial in cases of poisoning]" (line 1152). The bracketed words are Clark's commentary on the root verse. The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation says: "Rhino horn dries up pus, blood and chhuser of the torso. The therapeutic effect of roe deer horn and common deer horn are similar to that of rhino horn" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` lines 3506-3508). The horn is the only part of this species the lines read attribute to it by name; the deer blood, fat and brain entries that follow in both texts say "deer" and are placed with the red deer entry.

As food the roe deer is a larger game animal. Clark's meat classification has group (d), the larger hill and game animals: "spotted deer (Capreolus capreolus L. or Muntiacus reevesi)", with wild boar, buffalo, rhinoceros and wild yak (line 672). The Men-Tsee-Khang list reads "The goral, spotted deer, Himalayan tahr, boar, buffalo, black rhinoceros, wild ass, wild yak and wild Zho are the group of larger herbivorous wild animals" (lines 2907-2911); its index has "spotted deer 161" (line 7498). The glossary of animals lists the Tibetan "Kha sha" (line 6976), the English "Spotted Deer" (line 6996) and the binomial "Capreolus capreolus L." (line 7001) in separate OCR-scrambled columns; the three are matched by their order in the table, and the same Latin name sits beside "Spotted Deer" in Clark (line 1153).

**Identity.** The Latin name is *Capreolus capreolus*, the Eurasian roe deer in current usage, but both Tibetan-to-English books print the English name "Spotted Deer" for it, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text also says "roe deer". Clark gives an alternative, *Muntiacus reevesi* (a muntjac), for the meat group only (line 672). The same English words "spotted deer" are Bhishagratna's gloss for Sushruta's Prishata and Chitrila (see the Prishata flesh entry), which is a separate animal in a separate text; nothing here equates them. ### Active Components

Roe deer flesh is lean muscle meat: protein, iron and B vitamins, with little fat. The "horns" of a deer are antlers, which are bone that is grown and shed each year. No analysis specific to this entry was read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources record the horn as an action equal to rhino horn (drying pus, blood and lymph), and the animal as a larger game animal. Nothing supports using deer horn in modern practice, and deer products are regulated in many places. The Codex does not recommend roe deer horn or meat for any complaint and gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation of the horn is described; the entries give the part, a size ("bulky horns approximately fifteen centimeters long", Clark line 1153) and an action. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Antler is bone with a velvet covering while it grows. Game meat must be cooked through. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** For the horn the lines read give an action by comparison with rhino horn and no taste or potency (Clark lines 1152-1154; Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3506-3508). As meat the deer is a dry-land game animal: all meats "are sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste", and dry-land flesh "has cool, light and coarse powers which eliminate wind and phlegm fevers" (Clark line 673); "cool, light and rough in quality. It brings down hot disorders associated with loong and baekan" (Men-Tsee-Khang line 2923). No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The horn is a substance of the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica with a stated action and no taste group. Clark's introduction roots the three humors in attachment, hatred and obscuration (line 51); the horn treats a branch condition (accumulated pus, blood and lymph). No line read connects the roe deer with *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. No formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The roe deer is in the tradition as a horn that stands in for rhino horn and as a game meat, and the honest reading is historical.

### Contraindications

- **Identity:** the animal is named "Spotted Deer" and "roe deer" in the two books, with a muntjac alternative in the meat list; do not rely on the English names. [USER: verify]
- **Food safety:** wild game can carry parasites and bacteria; cook through.
- **Legal and conservation status:** hunting and trade in deer and deer products are regulated in most countries. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** the products require killing an animal; this entry takes no position beyond recording the text.
