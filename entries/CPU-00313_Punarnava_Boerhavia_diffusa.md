# Punarnava (Hogweed) — पुनर्नवा (Punarnavā), Raktapunarnava, Shophaghni, Varshabhu, Shothaghni

## *Boerhavia diffusa* L. | Family: Nyctaginaceae

**Tradition Tags:** Suśruta Saṃhitā (Sūtra & Cikitsā sthāna); Caraka Saṃhitā; Ayurvedic (dravyaguṇa); Siddha; folk/ethnomedicine (Indian subcontinent, Africa, Pacific)

**Energetic Classification (Ayurvedic):** Kapha-Vāta-śāmaka (primarily reduces Kapha and Vāta); mild Pitta-śāmaka via the red-flowered variety; *anulomana* on the fluid channels (mūtravaha and udakavaha srotas).

**Training Phase:** Renewal / Depletion-recovery · Fluid-regulation (anti-oedema) · Hepato-renal maintenance

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Ayurveda (Suśruta & the classical corpus).** Punarnava is one of the oldest and most doctrinally loaded diuretic-restorative dravyas of the Indian materia medica. Its name is a compound — *punar* ("again") + *nava* ("new") — literally "that which renews," or "the one that becomes new again." Two intertwined etymological readings run through the commentarial tradition. The botanical reading observes that the trailing prostrate herb appears to die back in the dry season and springs back into fresh growth with the first monsoon rains — hence the synonym **Varṣābhū**, "born of the rains," and **Śothaghnī**, "destroyer of swelling." The physiological reading, favoured by the vaidyas, is that the plant *renews the body itself*: it clears the accumulated fluid and metabolic sludge that ages tissue, so the patient is made "new again." Suśruta places it among the drugs that reduce śotha (oedema/swelling) and treats it as a principal agent for *anaha* and *udara* (abdominal distension, ascites), for pāṇḍu (anaemia/jaundice states), and for disorders of the mūtravaha srotas (urinary channels). Caraka lists it among the śothahara (anti-oedematous) and kāsahara groups and prizes the root.

Two colour-varieties are canonically distinguished and are **not** interchangeable in refined practice: **Rakta-punarnava** (red-flowered, generally identified with *Boerhavia diffusa*) is the true medicinal renewer, favoured for hepato-renal and anti-oedema work; **Śveta-punarnava** (white-flowered, often *Trianthema portulacastrum* or a related taxon) is regarded as more purgative and less specifically restorative. Substitution of one for the other is a recognised source of therapeutic failure in the classical literature.

**Siddha and folk practice.** In Tamil Siddha medicine (*mukkuṟaṭṭai*) the whole herb is used for jaundice, dropsy, and "heat of the liver." Ethnomedicinal survey work across the subcontinent and beyond records the root used for jaundice, anaemia and oedema — a use documented, for example, in the Samahni valley of Azad Kashmir, where *B. diffusa* root is applied "as anti-jaundice, anemia, edema" (Ishtiaq et al., 2007). The genus is used pan-tropically for hepatitis, urinary disorders and asthma.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — The Renewer at the Threshold of the Rains:** Punarnava is the plant of *return*. In the Ayurvedic imagination it sits precisely on the hinge between depletion and renewal — the dry, cracked, kapha-stagnant body swollen with retained fluid, and the same body cleared, lightened, and made young again by the monsoon that the plant itself embodies. Its lesson in Kenetica is that renewal is not creation *ex nihilo* but the *draining of what has stagnated*: the swelling recedes, the channels open, and the tissue that was waterlogged and heavy is restored to its own proper form. The plant that dies with the drought and resurrects with the rain is a somatic emblem of the cycle the practitioner is trying to induce internally — clear the flood, and the ground becomes new.

### Active Components

According to a genus-level review retrieved from PubMed (Patil & Bhalsing, 2016), some 180 compounds have been isolated from *Boerhavia*, of which *B. diffusa* alone accounts for around 131 and is the exclusive source of most. The characteristic constituents are the **rotenoid pigments — the boeravinones (A, B, D, E, G, H) and coccineones** — together with the alkaloid **punarnavine**, the pigment **betanin**, the flavonoid **eupalitin**, and **quercetin**; roughly 97 of the compounds are phenolic glycosides and flavonoids. Punarnavine and the boeravinones are the constituents most associated with the plant's anti-inflammatory, diuretic, immunomodulatory and reported anticancer activities.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Punarnava is the archetypal **fluid-clearing renewer** of the codex. It is deployed where the somatic picture is one of *stagnant retained water plus a taxed hepato-renal axis*: puffiness, heaviness, sluggish elimination, and the sense of a body that no longer "resets." Functionally it combines a mild diuretic push with hepato-protective and anti-inflammatory support, so it is used less as an acute agent than as a **maintenance / recovery** substance — a way of restoring the drainage the body relies on to renew itself. In the training arc it belongs to the depletion-recovery and fluid-regulation phases, not to the stimulation phases.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the ethnopharmacological and preclinical literature is moderately developed but largely preclinical. A comprehensive genus review (Patil & Bhalsing, *Journal of Ethnopharmacology*, 2016) catalogues the phytochemistry (boeravinones, punarnavine) and the reported anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, immunomodulatory and anticancer activities, while explicitly cautioning that "most of the available scientific literatures have lacked relevant doses, duration and positive controls," and that taxonomic errors are common — a candid statement of the evidence's limits. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2016.01.042)

For the classical diuretic/anti-urolithic claim, an *in vitro* study (Yasir & Waqar, *Urological Research*, 2011) found *B. diffusa* extract significantly inhibited calcium oxalate crystallisation, reduced crystal size, and shifted crystal type from the injurious monohydrate (COM) toward the less harmful dihydrate (COD) form — the authors linking this, together with the plant's diuretic action, to control of urolithiasis. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00240-011-0374-x) The ethnomedicinal use of the root for jaundice, anaemia and oedema is documented by Ishtiaq et al. (*Pakistan Journal of Biological Sciences*, 2007). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3923/pjbs.2007.2241.2256)

**Evidence grade: MODERATE for phytochemistry and traditional use; LOW–PRELIMINARY for clinical efficacy.** The diuretic, anti-urolithic, hepatoprotective and anti-inflammatory signals are consistent across preclinical work, but controlled human trials are lacking. Claims here are attributed to PubMed-indexed sources and should not be read as established clinical outcomes.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Rasa (taste):** *Tikta* (bitter), *Kaṣāya* (astringent); some sources add a trace of *Madhura* (sweet) in the root.
- **Guṇa (qualities):** *Laghu* (light), *Rūkṣa* (dry).
- **Vīrya (potency):** *Uṣṇa* (heating) — per most nighaṇṭus; a minority reading treats the red variety as mildly cooling.
- **Vipāka (post-digestive effect):** *Kaṭu* (pungent).
- **Prabhāva (specific action):** *Śothaghna* — the near-specific anti-oedema / fluid-draining effect for which the plant is named; and *rasāyana*-like renewal of depleted tissue.
- **Doṣa action:** *Kapha-Vāta-śāmaka*, tridoṣa-balancing in the red variety; drains stagnant fluid without excessively aggravating Vāta because of the sweet-astringent root.
- **Karma (actions):** *Mūtrala* (diuretic), *Śothahara* (anti-oedematous), *Yakṛd-uttejaka* (hepatic stimulant/protective), *Pāṇḍughna* (anti-anaemic), *Dīpana* (mild kindling of agni), *Kāsahara* (anti-tussive).
- **Srotas (channels):** primarily *mūtravaha* (urinary) and *udakavaha* (water-carrying) srotas; secondarily *raktavaha* and *annavaha*.
- **Gaṇa membership:** classically grouped among the *śothahara* and *kāsahara* dravyas of the Caraka/Suśruta corpus.

### Contraindications

- Use the correct variety: red-flowered (*B. diffusa*) for renewal; the white-flowered substitute is more sharply purgative.
- Its diuretic action warrants caution in dehydration, electrolyte depletion, and in those on prescription diuretics or in renal impairment — practitioner supervision required.
- Human safety and dosing data are limited; avoid in pregnancy without qualified guidance.
- Taxonomic confusion in commerce is documented (Patil & Bhalsing, 2016) — source-verify the material.
- Not a substitute for medical management of hepatic, renal, cardiac or ascitic disease; the evidence base is preclinical.
