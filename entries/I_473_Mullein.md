# I-473 | Mullein (*Verbascum thapsus* L.)

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Accepted name:** *Verbascum thapsus* L.  
**Family:** Scrophulariaceae (Figwort family) — **NOTE: Family transition from Asteraceae**  
**Common names:** Mullein, great mullein, common mullein, Aaron's rod, flannel leaf, candlewick plant, torch plant, shepherd's club, hag's taper, our Lady's candle, lungwort (folk), velvet plant  
**Synonyms:** *Verbascum thapsiforme* Schrad. — closely related; sometimes considered same species

**Etymology:** *Verbascum* from Latin *barbascum* (bearded plant) — the densely hairy leaves. *Thapsus* — from Thapsus, an ancient city in North Africa (modern Tunisia), though the plant is not native there; possibly named for a similarly woolly-leaved plant found in that region by early botanists. "Mullein" origin debated: possibly from Latin *mollis* ("soft") referring to the extraordinarily soft leaves; or from Old French *moleine*, itself from Latin. "Flannel leaf" — the leaves feel like thick flannel. "Candlewick" — early flower stalks were dipped in tallow for torches ("hag's taper," "torchwood"). "Aaron's rod" — the tall, rod-like first-year stem, like the biblical staff.

**Botanical description:** Biennial. First year: basal rosette of enormous (to 50 cm), thick, densely wooly-felted, grey-green leaves — remarkable for their extreme softness from the interlocking multicellular stellate (star-shaped) trichomes. Second year: erect stem to 2.5 m, unbranched, crowned with dense spike of yellow 5-petaled flowers with orange anthers. Capsule fruits with numerous seeds. Widespread in disturbed ground, roadsides, waste places throughout temperate world.

**Family context:** This entry marks the transition from Asteraceae (the most botanically complex family in Volume I) to Scrophulariaceae. Volume I now moves through several smaller family groupings before the next major family concentrations.

**Part used:** Leaves, flowers; rarely root  
**Preparation:** Leaf infusion (aqueous — preserves saponins and mucilage); flower oil maceration (ear drops); tincture; traditionally leaves smoked

## 3. Convergence Analysis

**Mediterranean-Middle Eastern respiratory consensus:** DMM and CM — the two most geographically proximate canonical traditions — both strongly endorse mullein for respiratory conditions. This reflects mullein's native range (Eurasia, Mediterranean) and proximity to the cultural centers of these traditions. The convergence is not surprising geographically but is pharmacologically coherent: the saponin expectorant and mucilage demulcent mechanisms support the respiratory indications.

**The "soft leaf = lung medicine" doctrine of signatures:** Mullein's extraordinarily soft, spongy leaves — from the dense stellate trichomes — were interpreted via doctrine of signatures as indicating lung affinity (the airy, spongy texture resembling lung parenchyma). While doctrine of signatures is not mechanistically valid as a general rule, in this case the phenomenological association appears to encode a valid pharmacological relationship — mullein leaves do contain respiratory-active compounds.

**Tobacco substitute paradox:** Mullein leaves were historically smoked — both as an expectorant delivery system (similar to coltsfoot) and as a tobacco substitute. Smoking anything introduces combustion products that directly harm respiratory mucosa. This paradox (treating lung conditions by introducing smoke) makes more sense when the primary active delivery mechanism is volatile saponin and terpenoid aerosol reaching bronchial tissue directly, with the beneficial effect potentially exceeding the harmful combustion effect in short-term acute use.

**Ear oil tradition:** The flower oil maceration for earache represents a specific traditional application — olive oil infused with fresh mullein flowers — that has survived into contemporary folk medicine. The mechanism is demulcent anti-inflammatory rather than antimicrobial; it does not treat bacterial otitis media but may reduce pain from mild inflammation.

## 4. Phytochemical Composition

### Saponins (primary expectorant active)
- **Verbascosaponin** (saponin glycoside; oleanolic acid core; expectorant via irritant-secretory mechanism)
- **Aucubin** (iridoid glycoside; anti-inflammatory; hepatoprotective)
- **Catalpol** (iridoid glycoside; related to aucubin; anti-inflammatory; neuroprotective — studied in other Scrophulariaceae species)
- Saponins: soap-like amphipathic molecules that lower surface tension of bronchial mucus → expectoration facilitated

### Mucilage
- **Mucopolysaccharides** (arabinogalactan fraction; leaf and flower; demulcent; anti-inflammatory on mucosal surface)
- Aqueous infusion: rich mucilage extraction — soothes irritated throat and bronchi
- Complements saponin action: saponins loosen mucus; mucilage soothes irritated membrane

### Flavonoids
- **Luteolin** (free aglycone; anti-inflammatory; antiviral; phosphodiesterase inhibition)
- **Apigenin** (GABA-A BZD site — same as chamomile; mild anxiolytic in some contexts)
- **Rutin** (quercetin-3-rutinoside; antioxidant; capillary protective)
- **Hesperidin** (anti-inflammatory; capillary protective)

### Iridoid Glycosides
- **Aucubin** (primary iridoid; anti-inflammatory via NF-κB pathway suppression; hepatoprotective)
- **Harpagide** (iridoid; anti-inflammatory — shared with Devil's Claw *Harpagophytum procumbens*)
- **Catalpol** (neuroprotective; mitochondrial protective; anti-inflammatory)

### Other
- **Phenylethanoid glycosides:** Verbascosides (acteoside/verbascoside) — potent antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; antiviral; inhibits melanin synthesis
- **Verbascum oil** (trace volatile oil in flowers; terpenoids)
- **Tannins** (astringent; antibacterial)

**Verbascoside (acteoside) — notable compound:**
Phenylethanoid glycoside with potent antioxidant activity (ORAC value exceeding most plant antioxidants), anti-inflammatory NF-κB inhibition, and documented antiviral activity against influenza, HSV, HIV. Found in multiple plant families beyond Scrophulariaceae. Mullein is a dietary source.

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

### Expectorant (Saponins)
- Saponin irritant mechanism: low-level irritation of gastric mucosa → vagal reflex → bronchial gland secretion increases → mucus volume increases → viscosity decreases → cough produces effective expectoration
- Classic "secretomotor expectorant" mechanism (shared with senega, guaifenesin)
- Saponins also directly reduce surface tension of mucus → facilitates clearance
- Luteolin inhibition of phosphodiesterase → bronchodilation (secondary mechanism)

### Demulcent / Antitussive (Mucilage)
- Arabinogalactan mucilage → gel coating of pharyngeal and bronchial mucosa
- Reduces sensory nerve receptor activation → decreases cough reflex
- Works synergistically with saponins: loosens mucus while soothing membrane

### Anti-inflammatory (Aucubin, Verbascoside, Luteolin)
- Aucubin: suppresses NF-κB, COX-2 → reduces bronchial mucosal inflammation
- Verbascoside (acteoside): NF-κB inhibition; 5-LOX inhibition; reduces cytokine cascade
- Luteolin: COX-1/2 inhibition; 5-LOX inhibition; reduces LTB₄ (bronchoconstrictor)

### Antiviral (Verbascoside, Aucubin)
- Verbascoside: inhibits replication of influenza A, HSV-1, HSV-2, HIV-1 — *in vitro*
- Aucubin: antiviral activity vs hepatitis B — *in vitro* (rat hepatocyte model)
- Mechanism: may involve viral polymerase inhibition or cell-surface receptor blocking

### Antimicrobial
- Leaf extracts show antibacterial activity vs *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *Bacillus subtilis*
- Saponins disrupt bacterial membrane integrity
- Tannins: protein-precipitation antibacterial mechanism

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**Respiratory (traditional and regulatory):**
- Commission E (Germany): mullein flower approved for mild inflammation of throat mucous membranes; leaf not separately approved
- American Herbal Pharmacopoeia: monograph recognizes traditional evidence Level C
- No RCTs for cough, bronchitis, or respiratory tract infection

**Otitis media (ear drops):**
- Sarrell et al. 2001 (*Archives of Pediatric and Adolescent Medicine*): naturopathic ear drops containing mullein flower (plus garlic, calendula, St. John's Wort) vs anesthetic (amethocaine/phenazone) ear drops in children with ear pain from acute otitis media — equivalent pain reduction over 3 days — Level A
- Note: this is a combination product; mullein-specific contribution not isolable. No controlled data for mullein flower oil alone.

**Antiviral:**
- Turker & Camper 2002: mullein extracts antiviral vs HSV-1, HSV-2, influenza A — Level D (in vitro only)

**Summary by evidence level:**
- Level A (RCT): Combination ear drops (1 RCT; mixed formula)
- Level B: None
- Level C: Commission E approval; pharmacopoeial recognition; traditional evidence
- Level D: Antiviral; antibacterial; anti-inflammatory mechanistic data

## 7. Phenomenological Record

**The great lamp and the witch:** Mullein's dramatic second-year appearance — a single massive rod of densely packed yellow flowers rising to 2 m — made it distinctive and symbolically loaded throughout European history. Romans reportedly dipped the flower stalks in tallow for funeral torches ("verbascum faces"). In Christian contexts, the same form became "our Lady's candle" or "Aaron's rod" (after the biblical flowering staff). Witches in folk belief used the stalks; hence "hag's taper." The plant accumulated religious and magical meaning precisely because its visual presence was unusual and imposing.

**Soft leaf as filter:** The extraordinary soft, grey-felted leaves of mullein were used historically as insulating footwear — placed inside boots in winter. Quaker women reportedly used them as makeshift toilet paper (hence another folk name). The extreme softness made mullein a comfort material as well as a medicine, a dual function that perhaps intensified attention to its pharmacological properties.

**The naturalized American immigrant:** *Verbascum thapsus* is aggressively invasive throughout North America — roadsides, disturbed ground, alpine meadows. Native American tribes — who did not have mullein before European contact — rapidly adopted it into their pharmacopoeias after colonization. The Catawba, Cherokee, Mohegan, Potawatomi, and others all documented mullein use within a few generations of European introduction. This rapid indigenous adoption of an introduced species demonstrates both the plant's pharmacological usefulness and the adaptive capacity of indigenous botanical knowledge systems.

**Ear oil — folk persistence:** The mullein flower oil ear drop preparation (fresh flowers infused in olive oil) has survived unbroken from European traditional medicine through American folk practice into contemporary naturopathic medicine — a remarkable 2,000+ year persistence of a single preparation form, suggesting consistent efficacy against the very common complaint of ear pain.

## 8. Practical Application

**Energetic tier:** ◉ Qi (respiratory functional; expectorant/demulcent)

### Preparation Methods and Dosing

**Leaf infusion (for respiratory conditions):**
- 1.5–2 g dried leaf per 250 mL boiling water; steep 10–15 min; strain carefully through fine cloth
- **Critical:** Strain through fine muslin or coffee filter — stellate trichomes (leaf hairs) can irritate throat if not removed
- 3 cups daily during acute respiratory illness

**Flower infusion:**
- 2–4 g fresh or dried flowers; steep 10 min; 3× daily
- Higher flavonoid and verbascoside content relative to leaf

**Flower oil (ear drops):**
- Fresh mullein flowers macerated in warm olive oil 2–3 weeks (or gentle heat 4–6 hours)
- 3–4 drops warmed oil into external ear canal, 3–4× daily
- Only for external ear pain; do not use if eardrum rupture is possible

**Tincture (1:5, 40% ethanol):**
- 2–6 mL, 3× daily; leaves or combined leaf/flower

**Traditional smoke inhalation:**
- Historical; not recommended — substitute steam inhalation with infusion instead

### Drug Interactions

- **Lithium:** Iridoid glycosides may affect renal clearance — monitor lithium levels with chronic mullein use (theoretical; not documented)
- **Antidiabetic medications:** Aucubin shows some glucose-lowering activity in animal models; additive effect possible

### Contraindications

- **Fine-leaf/pollen allergy** — airborne trichome dust can cause respiratory irritation
- Unfiltered preparations — trichome irritation of mucosa
- No major absolute contraindications documented; avoid in pregnancy due to insufficient safety data

### Quality Markers

- Leaves: dense grey-white velvet covering (both surfaces); characteristic soft texture
- Flowers: bright yellow; fresh-smelling; dried flowers retain aroma
- Aucubin content ≥0.3% (HPLC analysis of dry herb)
- Verbascoside content ≥0.5% (indicative of quality)
