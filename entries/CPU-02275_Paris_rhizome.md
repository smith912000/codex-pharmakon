# Paris rhizome — Zao Xiu 蚤休 (Shennong bencao jing); alias Chi Xiu 蚩休, later called Chong Lou 重楼 and Zi He Che 紫河车

## *Paris polyphylla Sm., the many-leaved paris, a woodland herb with a whorl of leaves and a creeping rhizome that is the drug zao xiu or chong lou; the identification is ours, from the Chinese name and the standard modern identification, and is of high confidence at genus level and fairly high at species level, since Yang 1998 prints "Rhizoma Paridis Polyphyllae Seu Tetraphyllae" (two species) and other Paris species are also used under the name* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** This is a lower-class drug. The classic's paragraph does not itself call it toxic, but the lower class, to which Sun's edition assigns it, is defined as "mostly toxic, not to be taken long," and the rhizome of *Paris* is generally regarded as mildly poisonous. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should take this plant.

**Named only by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts on disk, under this Chinese name.** This is a single-text entry. The principal text is Sun Xingyan's 1799 reconstruction, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt`; the other edition on disk, Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`), is read as a second witness and attributed to Yang. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder under a garbled pharmaceutical-Latin name, "Rhizoma Paridis Polyphyllae Seu Tetraphyllae", taken from Yang's English; this entry replaces it. A search of the other texts on disk for *Paris polyphylla*, *Herba Paridis* and the Chinese and pinyin names found nothing.

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** The entry is 蚤休 in the lower classic (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, lower classic, entry 蚤休 *zao xiu*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 3525, 味 paragraph; name line 3523). The Shennong paragraph reads: 味苦，微寒 ("bitter, slightly cold"); 主惊痫 ("governs fright epilepsy"), 摇头弄舌 ("shaking the head and working the tongue"), 热气在腹中 ("heat qi in the abdomen"), [one glyph lost in this e-text, before] 疾 ("...disease"), 痈创 ("abscesses and sores"), 阴蚀 ("genital erosion"), 下三虫 ("brings down the three worms"), 去蛇毒 ("removes snake poison"); the alias 一名蚩休; and 生川谷 ("grows in rivers and valleys"). There is no 久服 clause. No Wu Pu layer is printed for this entry.

The Mingyi bielu layer (《名医》曰, line 3527) gives only the habitat: 生山及冤句 ("grows in the mountains and at Yuanju"). It gives no harvest and does not name the part used.

Sun's note (案, line 3529) cites Zheng Qiao, a later author the text on disk does not date: 蚤休，曰螫休，曰重楼金绵，曰重台，曰草甘遂，今人谓之紫河车。服食家所用，而茎叶亦可爱。多植庭院间 ("*zao xiu* is also called *shi xiu*, *chong lou jin mian*, *chong tai* and *cao gan sui*; people now call it *zi he che*. It is used by the *fushi* practitioners [those who take drugs as a regimen], and its stem and leaf are also lovely; it is often planted in courtyards"). This is the only description of the plant's use and appearance in the sources read. It is a later author's remark, quoted by Sun, and is not Shennong text; it is not used to assign this plant a status (see Cultivation Register).

**Yang 1998 as second witness.** Yang prints (line 3689): "Zao Xiu (Rhizoma Paridis Polyphyllae Seu Tetraphyllae) is bitter and slightly cold. It mainly treats fright epilepsy, shaking of the head and worrying tongue, heat qi in the abdomen, madness, welling abscesses, sores, and genital erosion. It precipitates the three [kinds of] worms and removes snake toxins. Its other name is Zhe Xiu (End of Stinging). It grows in rivers and valleys." This agrees with Sun's paragraph and fills the lost glyph with "madness". The alias differs: Sun's text has 蚩休 and Yang's "Zhe Xiu (End of Stinging)" seems to match the 螫休 that Sun quotes only through Zheng Qiao. The Codex records the difference without resolving it. ### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, the rhizome of *Paris polyphylla* is a source of steroidal saponins, the polyphyllins, which are irritant to the gut and are the basis of the plant's reputation as mildly poisonous. These are well-established facts about the genus. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify any further constituent before adding it.]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Treat as poisonous.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Fright epilepsy, shaking of the head and working of the tongue, heat qi in the abdomen, abscesses, sores, genital erosion, expelling the three worms, and removing snake poison.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no preparation, no duration, and no part named.

The Codex does not translate the classic's terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend this plant. Snake bite and epilepsy need emergency or medical care.

### Preparation & Dosing

The Shennong text gives no preparation and no dose, and the Mingyi layer gives no harvest. The preface contains a general rule of method for poisonous drugs (line 4125) that begins with a very small amount and stops when the illness goes. It is the classic's rule of method for toxic drugs in general and is not a dose for this plant. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any dose from this entry.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. *Paris polyphylla* is a woodland perennial of Asia whose rhizome has been used in Chinese practice, and its steroidal saponins are known to irritate the digestive tract; wild populations of the genus are under pressure from collecting. [USER: verify] The classic's fright epilepsy, tongue movements and snake poison have no modern equivalent in this entry and are not translated into one. ### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(b) Framed.* The entry stands in the lower classic, whose preface (line 2935) reads 下药，一百二十五种，为左使。主治病以应地。多毒，不可久服。欲除寒热邪气，破积聚，愈疾者，本下经: the lower drugs are "the left envoys, mainly treating disease to correspond with earth; mostly toxic, not to be taken long; for those who wish to remove cold and heat evil qi, break accumulations and gatherings, and heal illness". The paragraph has no 久服 clause and no promise of lightening the body or of not ageing. Sun's note, quoting Zheng Qiao, says the plant is "used by the *fushi* practitioners" (服食家所用). That is a later author's remark, not the classic, and it names a group of users without giving the plant a cultivation status, a purpose or a preparation; the Codex does not raise the register on it. The register is therefore (b): framed by the lower class, whose own preface is the warning, and with no cultivation status attested.

**Practitioner's note.** A lower-grade drug for fright epilepsy, abscesses and snake poison, with a later remark that *fushi* practitioners used it. The tradition's own grading is the warning, and nothing in the record supports any cultivation use. A cultivator should not use it.

### Contraindications

- **POISONOUS.** The rhizome of *Paris* is generally regarded as mildly poisonous and irritant to the gut. Do not ingest it. Do not gather it.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children, the weak.** No statement in the sources retrieved. Given the toxicity, avoid. [USER: verify]
- **Epilepsy, snake bite, abscesses.** These are medical conditions or emergencies; use proper care. The snake-poison use is historical record only.
- **Identity.** Several *Paris* species are sold under the name, and the early plant is our identification. [USER: verify]
- **Damaged text.** One glyph of the Shennong paragraph is lost in Sun's e-text; the missing word is taken from Yang.
