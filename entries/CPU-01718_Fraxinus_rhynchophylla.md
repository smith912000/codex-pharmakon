# Fraxinus rhynchophylla — stab.seng, Tab seng, "Chinese ash" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Fraxinus rhynchophylla, the second of two binomials Clark 1995 prints for stab.seng (after Eucommia ulmoides); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints Fraxinus rhynchophylla with the English "Chinese ash" in the position that column order gives to "Tab seng"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk (including the three Bhishagratna volumes) for the genus name *Fraxinus* found no ash tree. The only hits for "fraxinella" in Dioscorides are the translator's glosses *Dictamnus fraxinella* (white dittany, a different plant) under 3-37, 3-39 and 5-57 (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, lines 443, 445 and 830). The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance, and here the line prints two, of which the spine row takes the second (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 111, notes that the key uses the first name; the spine took *Fraxinus*). The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 978).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines"), in the run that stands after the heading "(5) Resins" (line 879) and before the heading "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997); the file prints no "(6)" heading, so the section this entry sits in is unlabelled in the text read. Neighbouring entries in the run include *ba.sha.ka* (Malabar Nut Tree) and *ba.le.ka* (line 975 onward). It reads:

"stab.seng: Eucommia ulmoides: Fraxinus rhynchophylla: [has five names and is said to be a white or blue type of the spyi.zhur tree (Terminalia tomentosa). It grows in forested, south-facing valleys and has bark similar to that of sbyar.pa (poplar). The leaf is pale green, and a fragment of the middle bark placed in water leaves a pillar-like trace]. It unites fractures and cures bone fevers."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets are taken from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the five names, the relation to the *spyi.zhur* tree, the habitat, the bark, the leaf and the water test. Outside them (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the two Latin names, which are Clark's identifications, and the sentence "It unites fractures and cures bone fevers." The Latin names are not a text's own. The commentary compares the bark with poplar and the tree with *Terminalia tomentosa* and does not mention an ash.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* Line 3423 reads: "Tab seng joins fractured bone and cures hot disorders of the bone." The subject is the same in kind as Clark's "unites fractures and cures bone fevers". The Codex does not equate *bone fevers* with *hot disorders of the bone*. The index lists "Tab seng 205" (line 7506).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary.* In the "T" block the phonetic names begin "Tab seng Tak sha" (line 5860) with the transliteration "rtab seng" (line 5862). The scientific-name run that follows the last phonetic name ("Tukar", line 5924) begins at line 5928: "Fraxinus rhynchophylla Chinese ash". The phonetic names in the block number twenty-four, and the first scientific name stands against the first phonetic name, "Tab seng". The next scientific name, *Oxytropis microphylla* (line 5930), then stands against "Tak sha". The pairing is by position and was not printed in one row; it agrees with Clark's second Latin name for *stab.seng*. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder found no entry on this drug. I_571 (European Ash, *Fraxinus excelsior*) and I_748 (Manna Ash, *Fraxinus ornus*) are other species from other traditions. I_1254 (*Cnidium dahuricum*) and I_1273 (*Epimedium grandiflorum*) quote neighbouring "T" block lines. The spine has no sibling row for *Eucommia ulmoides*. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Fraxinus* is a genus of trees of the olive family (Oleaceae) and that *Eucommia*, the first name Clark prints, is a genus of another family (Eucommiaceae), so the two names point to unrelated trees. Ash bark is known in general to contain coumarin compounds, but nothing in the sources read says what the Tibetan drug contained or that this species is the one meant.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "unites fractures and cures bone fevers" (Clark, line 978) and "joins fractured bone and cures hot disorders of the bone" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3423). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial through Clark's second Latin name and the glossary's column position, and they are not Codex protocols. A fracture is a medical matter and the Codex offers no bone-setting advice.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used (Clark's commentary describes bark, leaf and tree, and the water test of a fragment of the middle bark, and does not say how the drug is taken), no quantity, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Tibetan terms ("unites fractures", "bone fevers", "hot disorders of the bone") are not modern diagnoses or measured outcomes, so no modern finding can be set beside them. The identity of the drug is open between *Eucommia* and *Fraxinus*, which are different genera, so a study of one would not bear directly on the other.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 978) gives no taste, power or post-digestive taste for this drug; it describes the tree, bark and leaf. The name does not appear in the Men-Tsee-Khang taste-group lists read for this entry (the lists begin at line 3204). The one action printed is the uniting of fractures and the curing of bone fever (Clark) or hot disorders of the bone (Men-Tsee-Khang).

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it unites fractures and cures bone fevers, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same in kind. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the sentences cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and the link from this binomial to that framing rests on Clark's second choice between two genera.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this tree. It is a drug for fractures and bone fever in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and its Latin name is one of two that Clark offers. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. The identity is open between two unrelated trees, no preparation or dose is given, and a fracture needs medical care; no plant or bark should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
