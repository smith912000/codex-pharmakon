# Scopolia / Carniolan Belladonna

## *Scopolia carniolica* Jacq. | Family: Solanaceae

### Origin & Tradition

*Scopolia carniolica* is a low, hairless perennial of the nightshade family (Solanaceae), growing 20–60 cm from a stout creeping rhizome, with oval entire leaves and solitary nodding, bell-shaped flowers that are dull brownish-violet outside and yellowish within. It is a plant of shaded, damp deciduous woodland in **Central and south-eastern Europe** — the Carpathians, the eastern Alps and the Balkans; the epithet *carniolica* refers to Carniola (historic Slovenia), the type region. The rhizome and roots carry the highest alkaloid load.

⚠️ **This is a deadly tropane-alkaloid plant.** Like its relatives *Atropa belladonna*, *Hyoscyamus niger* and *Datura stramonium*, it contains the anticholinergic tropane alkaloids **hyoscyamine** (whose racemate is **atropine**) and **scopolamine (hyoscine)**. In fact the genus is the eponym of scopolamine: Linnaeus named *Scopolia* for the naturalist J. A. Scopoli, and the alkaloid was named after the genus. Alkaloid analysis of Slovenian *S. carniolica* found hyoscyamine roughly twice as abundant as scopolamine across plant organs, with large organ-to-organ and site-to-site variation.

**Historical use.** Scopolia was used in parts of Central/Eastern Europe as a cheaper local source of tropane alkaloids and, in folk practice, as an anticholinergic sedative, analgesic and "witch's herb" of the same broad class as belladonna, henbane and mandrake. Its medicinal use today is essentially **obsolete**; its modern relevance is as a botanical **source material for the pharmaceutical alkaloids** extracted from it, not as a therapeutic herb in its own right.

The classical Mediterranean and Arabic authorities wrote in a world where *Scopolia carniolica* — a Balkan/Carpathian woodland plant — was not the obvious member of this drug group. Dioscorides (*De Materia Medica*) and Avicenna (*Canon*) far more plausibly described **mandrake (*Mandragora*), belladonna and henbane (*Hyoscyamus*)**, the Mediterranean/Near-Eastern narcotic Solanaceae. The retrieved historical review notes that depictions attributed to Scopoli's plant trace back through Mattioli's herbal — i.e. Renaissance-era European botany, not classical antiquity. **The CM/DMM tags here are best read as a *tropane-cluster / genus-group inheritance* — the entry sits inside the "narcotic nightshade" family the ancients did describe — rather than a species-level attestation of *S. carniolica* itself.** Do not represent Dioscorides or Avicenna as having described this species until a primary text is checked. The SBJ/FMT/SS streams are not claimed and none was found.

### Active Components

- **(–)-Hyoscyamine** — the principal tropane; racemises to **atropine** on extraction/storage.
- **Scopolamine (hyoscine)** — the tropane the genus is named for; more CNS-penetrant, sedative, antiemetic and amnestic than atropine.
- Minor related tropane alkaloids and the usual Solanaceae accessory constituents.

All are **competitive antagonists at muscarinic acetylcholine receptors** (non-selective). This single mechanism explains the whole toxidrome and the whole (former) therapeutic profile.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica, Scopolia is filed as a **hard-boundary "poison teacher"** of the narcotic-nightshade group: its Foundation-phase value is precisely as a *cautionary signature*, not a usable remedy. It illustrates three lessons the tradition and pharmacology agree on:

1. **One receptor, many faces.** Muscarinic blockade simultaneously produces the historical uses (antispasmodic, drying of secretions, sedation, antiemesis, mydriasis) and the poisoning (see toxidrome). Benefit and harm are the *same* action at different doses.
2. **The alkaloid is not the plant.** The honest distinction is between the whole toxic herb (unusable, unpredictable dose) and the **purified alkaloids** (atropine, scopolamine) that modern medicine extracts and controls. Scopolamine as a controlled drug is genuinely useful (motion sickness, premedication, antiemesis); Scopolia the herb is not.
3. **Rarity of poisoning is not safety.** The literature records *very few* human intoxications from *S. carniolica* specifically — but that reflects its obscurity and woodland habitat, not any margin of safety.

There is **no endorsed home or cultivator use.** The entry exists to be recognised and avoided.

### Preparation & Dosing

| Form | Status | Notes |
|------|--------|-------|
| Whole herb / rhizome | **No safe home dose** | Deadly, unpredictable alkaloid content; do not prepare or ingest |
| Extracted tropane alkaloids (atropine, scopolamine) | Prescription pharmaceuticals only | Dosed in **micrograms–milligrams** by clinicians; e.g. scopolamine transdermal releases ~0.5 mg over 3 days |
| Folk "sedative/analgesic" preparations | Obsolete / dangerous | Historical only — not reproduced here |

**No dose is provided for the herb.** Any therapeutic use is confined to the isolated, standardised pharmaceutical alkaloids under medical control. Scopolamine's oral bioavailability is low and variable (first-pass CYP3A metabolism), which is exactly why crude-plant dosing is uncontrollable.

### Scientific Correlation

Grading each claim:

- **Contains hyoscyamine/atropine + scopolamine: CONFIRMED** by direct HPLC alkaloid analysis of *S. carniolica*.
- **Genus is the eponym of scopolamine: CONFIRMED** (historical/etymological record).
- **Anticholinergic (antimuscarinic) mechanism and clinical pharmacology of scopolamine: WELL-ESTABLISHED** — competitive muscarinic antagonism; central sedative/antiemetic/amnestic and peripheral drying/antispasmodic effects; used for motion sickness and anaesthetic premedication.
- **Anticholinergic toxidrome from this plant group: ESTABLISHED** (review of European Solanaceae intoxications; belladonna case reports). *S. carniolica* itself caused **very few** documented poisonings.
- **Physostigmine as antidote to central anticholinergic syndrome: SUPPORTED** (a cholinesterase inhibitor reversing antimuscarinic toxicity; used in severe cases). Note the antidote literature is partly congener/mechanistic (belladonna case, animal carbamate-antidote work) rather than trials in Scopolia poisoning specifically — flagged as such.
- **Dioscorides/Avicenna described *this species*: NOT SUPPORTED / traditional-attribution only** — see attestation caution.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

No reliable classical energetic assignment exists for *S. carniolica* specifically. By analogy within the narcotic-nightshade cluster the humoral streams class such plants as **cold and dry, strongly narcotic/soporific and dangerous** — but this is an **inferred** cluster reading, not an attested classification of this species. Presented as inference only.

### Contraindications

- ⚠️ **DEADLY POISON — no home, folk, or self-administered use of any part of the plant.** Contains atropine/hyoscyamine and scopolamine at variable, uncontrollable concentrations.
- **Anticholinergic toxidrome** (classic mnemonic): "hot as a hare, blind as a bat, dry as a bone, red as a beet, mad as a hatter" — hyperthermia, mydriasis/blurred vision, dry skin and mucosae, flushing, urinary retention, tachycardia, agitation, hallucinations, delirium; severe cases progress to seizures, coma and death.
- **Children** are at especially high risk (attractive plant parts; low lethal dose).
- **Do not combine** with other anticholinergics (antihistamines, tricyclics, other Solanaceae) — additive toxicity.
- **Antidote is a medical act:** severe central anticholinergic syndrome may be treated with **physostigmine** plus supportive care in a clinical setting — not something to attempt outside hospital.
- **Pregnancy/lactation:** scopolamine crosses the placenta; the whole plant is absolutely contraindicated.
