### Origin & Tradition

The plant's English apothecary name *is* its indication: **Cramp Bark**. Few substances in the Codex wear their doctrine so plainly. The bark of the guelder rose was the classic European antispasmodic for the cramping, gripping, colicky pains of the hollow organs — above all the menstruating and pregnant uterus — and this single therapeutic idea, "the medicine that unclenches," organises every tradition that touches it.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian.** The cuneiform *materia medica* of the Nineveh tablets lists a large repertoire of astringent barks and "womb" (*šassūru*) remedies given for gynaecological gripping and retained menses. A *Viburnum* identity for any single Akkadian plant-name cannot be fixed with confidence — the herbals are notoriously hard to map onto modern binomials — so the Assyrian attribution here is a tradition-category claim (astringent bark for uterine cramp), not a verified species identification. **Dioscorides (*De Materia Medica*).** The Greek tradition knew several *Viburnum* species; the astringent bark and fruit were placed among the *styptika* — the binding, drawing remedies used to check fluxes and haemorrhage. This is the astringent half of the plant's split personality: a tissue-tightening drug for excessive bleeding and lax tissue, given by decoction.

**Avicenna (*Canon of Medicine*).** In the Galenic-Unani frame that Avicenna systematised, a cold, dry, astringent bark is the natural corrective for a *hot, turbulent, spasmodic* motion in the lower organs. The remedy's coldness quiets the "boiling," its dryness binds the flux, and its relaxant action loosens the *qabḍ* (griping constriction). Cramp bark sits exactly on the Unani fault-line between *qābiḍ* (astringent, binding) and *mufattiḥ/murakhī* (opening, relaxing) — it does both, which is why the classical texts prize it for pain that is simultaneously congestive and spasmodic.

**Eclectic coda (post-classical, for lineage).** The name "Cramp Bark" itself is an artefact of the 19th-century North American Eclectic physicians, who made *Viburnum opulus* a signature uterine antispasmodic for dysmenorrhoea, threatened miscarriage, and after-pains. The Codex records this because it is the bridge by which the ancient astringent-relaxant doctrine reached modern herbalism — and because it is the source of the confident name whose evidence base we must now weigh honestly.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The doctrinal signature is the *unclenching of the hollow organ*, and it maps onto a real, if modest, pharmacology. Three constituent-classes carry the doctrine. First, the **coumarin scopoletin** and its glycoside — coumarins of this type are documented smooth-muscle relaxants and spasmolytics, and scopoletin is the constituent most often invoked to rationalise the antispasmodic name. Second, the **hydroxycinnamic acids** (chlorogenic acid dominant) and **flavonol glycosides**, which give the bark and fruit their vasoactive, antioxidant, membrane-stabilising character — the "cooling" of Avicenna's turbulent motion. Third, the **astringent proanthocyanidins and tannins**, the literal *styptika* of Dioscorides: polyphenols that precipitate surface proteins, tighten lax mucosa, and check oozing flux. The plant is thus a physical enactment of its humoral paradox — a drug that binds (tannin, cold, dry) and simultaneously relaxes (coumarin spasmolysis). The doctrine did not invent this tension; it read it off a bark that genuinely contains both an astringent and a relaxant chemistry.

### Active Components

- **Scopoletin** (7-hydroxy-6-methoxycoumarin) and scopolin — the coumarin most cited for the smooth-muscle-relaxant / spasmolytic claim.
- **Viburnin** — the traditional name for a poorly-characterised bitter/"antispasmodic principle" of the bark; note it is a historical umbrella term, not a single defined molecule. [USER: verify — "viburnin" is imprecisely defined in the modern literature.]
- **Chlorogenic acid** and related hydroxycinnamic acids — the dominant phenolics; drastically transformed by digestion (see below).
- **Flavonol glycosides** (quercetin, kaempferol derivatives) and **anthocyanins** (in the red fruit).
- **Proanthocyanidins / condensed tannins** — the astringent fraction.
- **Iridoids and triterpenes** — minor bitter constituents.
- **Note on the berry:** raw fruit contains emetic/irritant principles and is mildly toxic uncooked — see Contraindications.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Cramp bark is deployed in Phase III (Release & Regulation) as the archetypal **spasmolytic of the lower hollow organs**: dysmenorrhoeic cramping, uterine after-pains, and — by extension in the folk-Eclectic tradition — general skeletal and visceral muscle spasm, colic, and "bearing-down" congestive pain. Its Kenetica role is corrective, not tonic: it is given *into* an acute cramping event or cyclically in the days of expected pain, then withdrawn. The astringent fraction gives it a secondary role in checking passive menorrhagic flux where laxity and congestion coexist. It should be understood by the practitioner as a **traditionally strong, clinically under-proven** remedy — the confidence of the name outruns the trial evidence.

### Scientific Correlation

The honest verdict: **the antispasmodic doctrine is chemically plausible and clinically almost unproven in humans.** According to PubMed, the directly on-point pharmacology is a single old report of *Viburnum opulus* antispasmodic activity on smooth muscle (Nicholson et al., *Nature*, 1972; PMID 5037580 — no DOI registered), and coumarins of the scopoletin type have documented smooth-muscle relaxant behaviour generally. There is, at the time of writing, no robust randomised human trial demonstrating that *V. opulus* bark relieves dysmenorrhoea — the flagship indication. The name is a traditional-empirical claim carried by centuries of use, not a validated clinical result.

What the modern literature *does* support is a different, well-evidenced pharmacology of the **fruit**: bioactive phenolics with antioxidant and metabolic effects. Digested *V. opulus* juice fractions stimulated glucose uptake and reduced lipid accumulation in myoblast and hepatocyte models and lowered IL-6 and TNF-α secretion from adipocytes, while chlorogenic acid content fell sharply during simulated digestion — a caution about bioavailability (Pietrzyk et al., *Molecules*, 2021; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules26134086)). Bark and fruit extracts also showed antibacterial/anti-virulence action against *Staphylococcus aureus* (inhibiting sortase A and biofilm) without cytotoxicity to human fibroblasts (Wójcik-Bojek et al., *Molecules*, 2021; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules26061758)), and ethanolic fruit extracts were antifungal against *Fusarium* strains (Zongur et al., *J Microbiol Methods*, 2021; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mimet.2021.106379)).

There is a coherent, if indirect, chain from doctrine to molecule: coumarins and hydroxycinnamates of the type *V. opulus* carries are established, if modest, modulators of vascular and visceral smooth-muscle tone, and the astringent polyphenols provide the tissue-binding action the *styptika* tradition described. What is missing is the crucial middle link — a controlled demonstration that the whole bark preparation, at a realistic dose, relaxes the human uterus or relieves menstrual pain. Until that link exists, the practitioner should treat "Cramp Bark" as a well-reasoned traditional hypothesis, not a validated drug, and should be especially wary of marketing copy that presents the antispasmodic claim as settled science. The gap between the confident name and the thin trial record is exactly the kind of place where fabricated efficacy claims tend to appear; the Codex marks it explicitly.

**Evidence grade.** Antispasmodic/uterine use: **Low — preclinical and historical only; deflate the confident name.** Antioxidant/anti-inflammatory/antimicrobial activity of the fruit: **Low–Moderate, in vitro.** No human efficacy or safety RCTs retrieved. Based on articles retrieved from PubMed.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Cold and Dry (≈2nd degree); *qābiḍ* (astringent) with *murakhī* (relaxant) action — the defining paradox. Attested via Avicenna and the Greek *styptika* tradition.
- **TCM:** Not classically attested (*V. opulus* is not a canonical Chinese materia medica species). Do not assign a category.
- **Ayurveda (rasa/guṇa/vīrya/vipāka):** Not attested for this species; no authentic Sushruta identification. Do not invent one.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (AT).** The Nineveh tablets list many astringent barks and *šassūru* ("womb") remedies given by the *asû* for gynaecological gripping and retained menses, but no single Akkadian plant-name can be fixed to *Viburnum opulus* with confidence, and Mesopotamian illness is classified by causal agency (divine hand, ghost, oath) rather than by any doctrine of what a substance does to a person's vital constitution. Under the standing blocking warning for Assyrian identifications, no cultivation status is claimed; the association is a tradition-category claim, therapeutic at most. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The astringent bark and fruit of *Viburnum* were placed among the *styptika*, the binding, drawing remedies used to check fluxes and haemorrhage, and Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system that could assign the plant a cultivation status. What the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention — the bark and fruit as the medicinal organs, the astringency read directly from the drawing, tissue-tightening sensory effect, the drug located by its binding action rather than by any vital-constitutional role. That is a practice of assay, not a vital-body doctrine. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The Galenic-Unani frame places a cold, dry, astringent bark as the corrective for a hot, turbulent, spasmodic motion of the lower organs, cramp bark sitting on the fault-line between *qābiḍ* (astringent, binding) and *murakhī* (relaxant, opening) — a genuine humoral placement. The strongest cultivation idea in the Unani corpus, the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture as a finite vital endowment, is a longevity frame of the whole regimen and not a status the tradition assigns to a symptomatic spasmolytic; no *mufarrih* or cardiac-*ruh* role is claimed. Its standing here is therapeutic, not cultivational. **(b) framed:** attested therapeutically, no cultivation status sourced.

**Practitioner's note.** None of the three traditions treats cramp bark as a substance taken to build or conserve vitality; it is a corrective, given *into* an acute cramping event or cyclically in the days of expected pain and then withdrawn — the opposite of a long-term cultivation drug. What a cultivator does with *Viburnum opulus* is use the bark decoction or tincture for dysmenorrhoeic cramping and after-pains, understanding that the confident name outruns the trial evidence, and cook the fruit before any culinary use because raw berries are emetic. The astringent-relaxant paradox Dioscorides and Avicenna read off the bark is real chemistry, but it is a therapy for spasm, not a nourishing of the person.

### Contraindications

- **Raw berries are mildly toxic and emetic** — they contain irritant principles and must be cooked before any culinary use; raw ingestion can cause nausea, vomiting, and cramping (the ironic opposite of the intended effect).
- **Pregnancy:** despite the traditional "threatened miscarriage" use, uterine-active herbs must not be self-administered in pregnancy; use only under qualified supervision. The historical indication is *not* a safety endorsement.
- **Coumarin content:** theoretical caution with anticoagulant therapy; scopoletin-type coumarins are not the anticoagulant dicoumarol, but prudence applies with warfarin co-use. [USER: verify]
- **Not a proven treatment** for dysmenorrhoea or any condition — traditional confidence exceeds clinical evidence. Present to patients as traditional, not established.
