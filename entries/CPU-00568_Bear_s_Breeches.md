# Bear's Breeches — akantha erpekantha (Dioscorides 3-19), "Acanthus-tree" (Bryan's Ebers list)

## *Acanthus mollis* (spine botanical; the translator's gloss for Dioscorides 3-19 reads "Acanthus sativus, Acanthus mollis [Bauhin, Linnaeus], Acanthus spinosus — Bears Breeches") | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP, AT, DMM, CM

### Origin & Tradition

**Identity warning.** This row was promoted from MASTER_INDEX on a name match, not on a reading of the texts. Only one of the four tagged traditions, Dioscorides, describes a plant that can be Bear's Breeches. The Egyptian name is a tree, and no Assyrian or Avicenna locus was found at all. The identity of the Egyptian entry with *Acanthus mollis* is doubtful. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 3-19, "AKANTHA ERPEKANTHA" (text line 6689), is glossed by the translator as "Bears Breeches". Dioscorides says it "grows in gardens and moist rocky places" and has "far broader, longer leaves than lettuce, divided like those of eruca, somewhat dark, thick and smooth", with a smooth stalk two feet high bearing "longish little leaves (similar to little hives) of a hyacinth colour", a white, somewhat long, yellowish seed, and roots that are "viscous, mouldy, reddish and long". Of the root he says: "Applied, they are good for burns and dislocations. Taken in a drink they encourage urine and stop discharges of the bowels. They are good for tuberculosis of the lungs, hernia, and convulsions." Other names given are melamphyllon, pasderota, acanthus topiaria, mamolaria and craepula. Two neighbouring chapters are separate plants: 3-15 AKANTHA ARABIKE (line 6653, glossed "Acanthus spinosus — Oyster Plant", astringent) and 3-20 AKANTHA AGRIA (line 6698, "Acanthus spinosissimus"), a wild kind "similar to carduus nutans" whose root "affects as many things as the previous one". Chapter 3-90 (line 7337) carries the label "Acanthus germanica [Fuchs]" in the translator's list, but it is *Heracleum sphondylium*, hogweed, and is not this plant.

**Egyptian — Ebers (EP).** Bryan's chapter VI, "Plant Remedies", lists "Acanthus-tree" with the forms Dough-of-Acanthus, Fruit-of-Acanthus, Nuts-of-Acanthus, Resin-of-Acanthus and Thorns-of-Acanthus (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1234; `extract/names.csv` marks it "Bryan's English identification"). Three uses are visible in the text. Resin-of-Acanthus stands as the first ingredient of a poultice headed "TO DRIVE OUT THE DISEASES CAUSED BY THE TAPEWORM" (line 1986). Resin-of-Acanthus and Dough-of-Acanthus occur in a mixed poultice closing "Make into one and Poultice for four days" (line 2152). In a graded series "AGAINST BURNS", the third day's application is "Thorns-of-Acanthus, dried" (line 2257). A tree with resin, nuts and a fruit is not the herb Dioscorides describes, so the spine's attachment of this Egyptian row to *Acanthus mollis* rests on the English word alone. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides' uses (burns, dislocations, a diuretic and bowel-stopping root drink, lung and hernia complaints) are recorded as his, and the Codex does not recommend them. The plant is commonly grown as a garden ornamental, and the Codex gives no protocol for it.

### Preparation & Dosing

Dioscorides gives the root "applied" and "taken in a drink" (3-19, line 6689) and states no quantity. Bryan's Ebers lists resin, dough, nuts and thorns as ingredients without a quantity in the lines read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** no hot, cold, moist or dry quality is printed in 3-19. The chapter describes the root as "viscous, mouldy, reddish". Chapter 3-15, for the Arabian kind, calls it "astringent".

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian — Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* The Ebers material is organised by complaint (a tapeworm poultice, a burns series), not by what a substance does to the person's *ka*, *ib* or *akh*. No *heka* recitation attaches to the acanthus lines read, and the row's identity with this plant is doubtful.

**Assyrian (AT).** *(c) Absent.* No attestation was found and none is asserted. The blocking warning on Assyrian identifications applies, and no tablet number or deity is given.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent* as to cultivation status. Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system. What he offers a cultivator is attention: he records where the plant grows ("in gardens and moist rocky places"), its leaf, stalk, seed and root, and separates it from the Arabian and wild acanthas. Recording is not endorsing.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent.* The tag rests on a spine row, not on any list row or text read. No cultivation framing, and no attestation of any kind, was found.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing to cultivate here in the Codex's sense. The one real Greek account is a therapeutic and descriptive one, and the Egyptian and Unani tags are unsupported by anything read. A gardener may grow the plant for its leaf, and a practitioner should treat the four-tradition tag line as an unverified inheritance from an index.

### Contraindications

No safety data were read. Do not treat the traditional burn, hernia or lung uses as advice. Seek ordinary medical care for those conditions.
