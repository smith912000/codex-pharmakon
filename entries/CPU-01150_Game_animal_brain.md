# Game-animal brain — "Brain of game animals (e.g. deer)" (Clark 1995); "the brain of herbivorous wild animals" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)
## *The brain of wild game animals, deer given as the example, as the rGyud-bzhi's Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 lists it; neither translation names a single species* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The text is Clark's 1995 translation of *The Quintessence Tantras of Tibetan Medicine* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`), Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, "the powers of medicines". Under "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147) it lists "thirteen medicines of animal origin", the seventh of which is the brain (line 1148). The brain section reads (lines 1181-1182):

"Goat brain: unites severed ligaments and tendons. Sheep brain: cures dizziness and vertigo. Brain of game animals (e.g. deer): halts diarrhoea. Rabbit brain: cures colic. Human brain: reduces swellings and cures lymph disorders."

So five brains are listed, and the one for this entry is the third: "Brain of game animals (e.g. deer)", for which the use is "halts diarrhoea". The parenthesis "(e.g. deer)" is Clark's. No Tibetan name is printed for it in the line. The same words appear in Clark's index of the materia medica as "Brain of game animals" (line 1565). Rabbits have their own brain entry, so they are not in the "game animals" of this line, although the meat classification discussed below counts rabbits among game animals.

The second translation on disk is the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 *Root and Explanatory Tantra* (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`). Its list of the thirteen substances includes "brain" (line 3503). Its brain passage reads (line 3548): "Goat brain heals torn ligaments and tendons. Sheep brain cures dizziness and reeling. The brain of herbivorous wild animals stops diarrhea. Rabbit brain cures dysentery." (the OCR text prints "tom" for "torn"), and a further sentence for human brain, which in this text begins with a name, "Mileh", that I do not interpret. The two translations agree that the brain of this class of animal stops diarrhoea. They differ in the class: Clark says "game animals (e.g. deer)", Men-Tsee-Khang says "herbivorous wild animals". Men-Tsee-Khang also differs on the rabbit's brain: "dysentery", where Clark says "colic".

What the class contains is given elsewhere in the same texts. In the Men-Tsee-Khang text, in a classification of animals by habitat (line 2907): "The deer, musk deer, gazelle, argali, rabbit and antelope are the group of herbivorous wild animals. The goral, spotted deer, Himalayan tahr, boar, buffalo, black rhinoceros, wild ass, wild yak and wild Zho are the group of larger herbivorous wild animals." In Clark, the classification of meats puts "Game animals" as group (c): "deer (Cervus elaphus), musk deer, Procapra pictaudata, Ovis ammon, rabbits and saiga tatarica (Pantholops hodgsoni)" (line 672). I read these as the nearest definitions of the class named in the brain line, but neither passage says that the brain line refers to them, and the species lists differ between the two texts. Both translators' prefaces say that animal products have largely fallen out of use. Clark: "Nowadays only very few animal products are used, although many are documented in the traditional texts" (line 57). The Men-Tsee-Khang translators say that "the use of animal products was negligible" and that they "have been completely substituted by equally effective herbal substances", but that the potencies are recorded "in order to reveal the richness of Tibetan medical knowledge" (line 687).

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, brain tissue is rich in lipids (phospholipids, cholesterol, sphingolipids) with protein and water. Nothing in the entry links a constituent to the stopping of diarrhoea.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The rGyud-bzhi records the brain of game animals as a remedy that halts diarrhoea. The Codex does not recommend it. Diarrhoea is usually managed by fluid and salt replacement, and persistent or bloody diarrhoea needs medical assessment; brain tissue from wild animals can carry infectious agents, including those of chronic wasting disease in deer. Many of the animals in the class are protected or threatened. The entry is a record of what the two translations say.

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Acute diarrhoea is treated with oral rehydration; stopping it is not the first aim, and a bloody or prolonged case needs investigation. The brain of deer and related animals can carry prions (chronic wasting disease in deer, elk and moose), and wild-animal tissue can carry bacteria and parasites. ### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The brain of game animals is named in the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica, as one of five brains, for diarrhoea. The project's cultivation spec places Tibetan medicine as the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice and not reaching the root of illness (the three poisons); that is a statement about the tradition's frame, not about this substance. No passage read connects this brain to *bcud len* (essence extraction) or to any practice, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition. None is asserted. Both translators say animal products have largely been given up in practice, and the 2011 text gives Buddhism's rejection of harm to any being as part of the reason (line 687). The attestation rests on single lines of two translations.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. The line is a record of a remedy for diarrhoea, the tradition itself has largely replaced animal products with herbs, and the Codex does not recommend it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Prion disease:** do not eat the brain of deer, elk, moose or any other animal; it can carry infectious prions.
- **Diarrhoea:** persistent diarrhoea, blood in the stool, fever or signs of dehydration need medical assessment; replace fluids and salts.
- **Zoonosis and hygiene:** wild-animal tissue carries bacteria, parasites and viruses.
- **Conservation and law:** musk deer, saiga, Tibetan antelope and other animals in the class are threatened or protected in law; do not take any animal for these uses.
- **Ethics:** the Tibetan tradition itself, in the 2011 translators' words, denounces causing harm to any being; the Codex does not recommend harming any animal for these uses.
