# Ornithogalum luteum — Bolbos edodimos, the edible bulbus (Dioscorides 2-200)

## *Ornithogalum luteum [Bauhin, Linnaeus], the second of the names on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 2-200 (which also prints Bulbus sylvestris [Fuchs] and Gagea lutea [in Sprague] — "Yellow Star of Bethlehem")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (line 6302) gives three names and the English "Yellow Star of Bethlehem". The chapter itself, however, does not describe a yellow star of Bethlehem. It describes "the edible red bulbus that is brought from Africa", and a bitter bulbus "similar to the squill", and then gives properties common to "all" the bulbs. The chapter is therefore a general chapter on edible bulbs, and the translators' suggestion names one possible plant for the whole heading. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *2-200. BOLBOS EDODIMOS* ("edible bulb"), heading at line 6301, text at lines 6303 and 6308 (with a page break between them, the translators' page 335, in the section headed "Herbs with a sharp quality"). The whole chapter reads: "The edible red bulbus that is brought from Africa is well known to all; good for the stomach and bowels. That which is bitter and similar to the squill is better for the stomach and helps digestion. All are sharp and warming, provoke sexual intercourse [aphrodisiac], and are harsh to the tongue and tonsils. They are very nourishing and replenish flesh but they breed wind. Rubbed on they are good for dislocations, bruises, splinters, sore joints, gangrene, and gout (applied either with honey or by themselves). They are good for oedema from dropsy, and the bites of dogs (applied similarly with honey, and pepper pounded into small pieces). They repress sweats and alleviate pains in the stomach. Roasted with saltpetre [potassium nitrate] they clean away dandruff and running sores on the head. They clean bruises below the eyes applied either alone or with the yolk of an egg, and with honey or vinegar [they also remove] varos [smallpox pustules], as well as freckles. With polenta they are good for broken places of the ears, and for bruise ointments. They take away piles [haemorrhoids] roasted in hot embers and applied with the burnt heads of fish called maenae. Burnt and mixed with alcyonium [5-136] and rubbed on in the sun they take away sunburn and black scars. Boiled and eaten with vinegar they are good for hernias. Care must be taken of feeding too much on them because they hurt the sinews. Boiled with polenta and applied with swines' grease it quickly brings oedema and tubercles [growths] to suppuration and breaks them."

The chapter reads as an account of a food-medicine. It names two bulbs, the red one "brought from Africa", good for the stomach and bowels, and the bitter one like the squill, better for the stomach and for digestion. It then speaks of "all" of them: sharp and warming, an aphrodisiac (the translators' gloss), harsh to the tongue and tonsils, very nourishing and flesh-replenishing but wind-breeding. The remaining sentences are a long list of external uses (for dislocations, bruises, splinters, sore joints, gangrene, gout, dropsical swelling, dog bites, dandruff, running sores of the head, bruises under the eyes, smallpox pustules, freckles, ear injuries, piles, sunburn and scars, and for bringing swellings to a head) and one internal use (boiled, with vinegar, for hernias). It ends with the only warning: "Care must be taken of feeding too much on them because they hurt the sinews." The chapter prints no other names and no habitat, apart from Africa for the red bulb.

**The chapters around it.** 2-199 *ASPHODELOS* (line 6281) is the chapter before it. 2-201 *BOLBOS EMETIKOS* (line 6309), after it, is the "vomitory" bulbus, whose SUGGESTED line prints *Scilla amoena* and *Ornithogalum arabicum*; it says its root "eaten alone ... cures disorders of the bladder and encourages vomiting". The Codex does not carry the statements of 2-201 over to 2-200. A separate chapter, 2-174 *ORNITHOGALON* (line 6044), is treated in I_1148.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter id 2-200 and the names *bolbos edodimos* and *bulbus* found no existing entry on this chapter. Four entries cite 2-200 as a cross-reference and none treats it as its subject: I_1104 (*Hyacinthus comosus*), which compares its leaves and root with the bulbus of 2-200; I_1099 (*Hemerocallis fulva*); III_223 (*Alcyonium*), which cites the sunburn recipe; and I_901 (*Small-flowered Catchfly*), which lists the neighbouring 2-201 in its paragraph on nearby chapters. No entry is duplicated.

### Active Components

The composition of the bulbs Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about the particular species. As a general fact, many bulbs of the lily family (Liliaceae in the older sense) contain starch and other storage carbohydrates, and some bulbs of related genera are known to contain toxic compounds. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records a sharp, warming, nourishing and wind-breeding bulb, eaten and applied. The Codex gives no protocol for any of its uses. The identification is the translators', and the chapter describes the bulbs of Africa and a squill-like bulb rather than any one plant. A wild plant should not be dug and eaten on the strength of this chapter, since many bulbs are poisonous.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives many preparations, with locus (lines 6303 to 6308): rubbed on, alone or with honey; applied with honey and pounded pepper; roasted with saltpetre; with the yolk of an egg, or honey or vinegar; with polenta; roasted in hot embers with burnt fish heads; burnt and mixed with *alcyonium* (chapter 5-136); boiled and eaten with vinegar; and boiled with polenta and swines' grease. It gives no quantity for any of them. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints "All are sharp and warming" and calls the bulbs "harsh to the tongue and tonsils", and one bulb "bitter". It places the chapter in the section on herbs with a sharp quality. It prints no cooling, drying, moistening or astringent quality for 2-200.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: sensory assay (a bulb sharp, bitter, harsh on the tongue and tonsils), the distinction of a red bulb from Africa from a bitter squill-like one, and a plain warning about eating too much of it. The aphrodisiac note is given as one of the properties of "all" the bulbs, and the chapter has no ritual. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a nourishing food-medicine. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The useful lesson is Dioscorides' method of testing a bulb by taste and tongue, and his one warning that too much of it "hurts the sinews". The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The chapter's own warning is that too much of the bulbs "hurt the sinews". It also says that they breed wind and are harsh to the tongue and tonsils. Gangrene, dropsy, hernia, gout and dog bites need medical assessment. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no wild bulb should be eaten or applied on the strength of this entry; many wild bulbs are poisonous.
