# Indrajao — Sweet Indrajao / Pala Indigo / Dudhi

## *Wrightia tinctoria* R.Br. | Family: Apocynaceae

**Tradition Tags:** CM, SS

**Energetic Classification:** Bitter–astringent; cooling (*śīta vīrya*); pungent post-digestive (*kaṭu vipāka*); light and dry (*laghu, rūkṣa*)

**Training Phase:** Restoration — Integumentary

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Wrightia tinctoria* is a small deciduous tree of the dry deciduous forests of peninsular India, Sri Lanka, Myanmar and northern Australia. It carries two threads that rarely sit in the same plant: it is a **dye tree** and a **skin tree**. The species epithet *tinctoria* — "of the dyers" — records the first. The leaves, when fermented in water, yield a blue pigment of the indigo type, and the tree is known in the trade as Pala Indigo, a substitute for *Indigofera* in regions where the true indigo did not grow. The second thread is medicinal: the leaf oil is a classical remedy for scaly skin disease, and it survives today as a marketed Ayurvedic and Siddha psoriasis oil in India — one of the few traditional dermatological preparations that never left commerce.

**A NAME PROBLEM, FLAGGED FIRST.** "Indrajao" is one of the most treacherous vernacular names in the Indian materia medica. It is applied to *Wrightia tinctoria* (Sweet Indrajao) **and** to *Holarrhena pubescens* / *H. antidysenterica* (Kutaj, Bitter Indrajao / Kurchi). Both are Apocynaceae; both have long, paired, pod-like follicles; both yield seeds sold as "Indrayava." They are **not interchangeable drugs.** *Holarrhena* carries the steroidal alkaloid conessine and is the classical amoebic-dysentery drug — that is where the serious antiprotozoal reputation belongs. *Wrightia tinctoria* is the sweet, non-conessine-bearing tree whose centre of gravity is the skin. Market samples and even published papers confuse them. Any dysentery claim attached to "Indrajao" should be assumed to belong to *Holarrhena* until the binomial is confirmed.

**Ayurveda (CM).** Sanskrit *Śvetakuṭaja* ("white Kutaj") and *Strīkuṭaja*; the seed is **Indrayava**, the bark *Kuṭaja tvak*. Ayurveda classifies it as *tikta-kaṣāya* (bitter–astringent) with cooling potency, and assigns it to *kuṣṭha* — the vast classical category of skin disease that spans everything from psoriasis and eczema to vitiligo and leprosy. The doctrine is one of **drying and cooling a wet, hot, spreading lesion**: *kuṣṭha* in the classical reading is a disorder of aggravated *pitta* and *kapha* lodged in skin, blood, muscle and lymph, producing heat (inflammation), moisture (exudate) and proliferation (scale). A bitter, astringent, cooling, drying drug is the doctrinally correct answer. The leaf oil (*Wrightia taila*) is the delivery vehicle. The seed, Indrayava, is separately indicated for *atisāra* and *grahaṇī* — diarrhoea and dysentery — on the astringent principle of binding a loose bowel. Note the collision: this is precisely the indication that also belongs to *Holarrhena*, and the two seeds are traded under the same name.

**Siddha (SS).** Tamil *Veṭpālai* (வெட்பாலை) — "the heat-milk tree," naming both the copious white latex and the thermal indication. Siddha is where the psoriasis use is most explicit and most alive. *Veṭpālai* leaf oil is a standing prescription for *kalanjaga padai* — the Siddha rubric closest to psoriasis — and for other scaling, plaque-forming *padai* conditions. Siddha healers also apply the fresh latex directly to fresh wounds, ulcers and mouth sores. The doctrine is *thoda* correction: a cooling, drying agent against a hot, scaling lesion, with the latex acting as a wound-sealing principle in its own right.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer.** The doctrine and the chemistry converge here more tightly than the folklore usually allows, and the convergence is worth stating precisely.

*The blue.* The dye is not decorative trivia — it is a chemical fingerprint. Indigo-type blue arises from **indole precursors**: colourless glycosides in the leaf are hydrolysed and oxidised to indigoid pigments. That the leaf can make indigo blue is direct evidence that the leaf runs an **indole pathway**. And the indole pathway in *W. tinctoria* also yields **tryptanthrin**, an indoloquinazoline alkaloid — the same compound class that gives *Isatis* (woad, another indigo plant) its anti-inflammatory reputation. So the dyer's tree and the skin tree are the same tree *for a chemical reason*: the pigment and the anti-inflammatory alkaloid are cousins on one biosynthetic branch. The classical eye saw a leaf that turns blue and a leaf that heals skin and did not know they were connected. They were.

*The cooling.* "Cooling" (*śīta*) in a plaque disease is not metaphor if one reads it as **anti-inflammatory**. A psoriatic plaque is erythematous, warm, and driven by the IL-23/IL-17 axis. A drug that reduces the erythema and the heat is doing, in modern terms, cytokine suppression. The doctrine names the observable (heat) and prescribes against it; the chemistry supplies a mechanism the doctrine could not see.

*The drying.* *Rūkṣa* (dryness) maps onto astringency, which maps onto **tannins and triterpenoids** precipitating surface protein and reducing exudate. This is a real, local, physical action.

*The milk.* The white latex is doctrinally read as the tree's own "healing milk" — sealing what is opened. This one is also chemically legible: the latex carries **serine proteases**, and proteolytic wound débridement and matrix remodelling are genuine mechanisms. The symbol (milk that closes a wound) and the enzymology (proteases that remodel a wound) are the same fact described twice.

*Where the doctrine overreaches.* *Kuṣṭha* is a single classical category covering psoriasis, eczema, vitiligo, fungal infection and leprosy — diseases with entirely unrelated pathophysiology. A drug "for *kuṣṭha*" is therefore not a drug for psoriasis in any modern sense; it is a drug for a category that modern medicine has dissolved. The tradition's confidence is a category artefact, not evidence of breadth.

### Active Components

- **Tryptanthrin** — indoloquinazoline alkaloid; isolated from *W. tinctoria*; the best-characterised single molecule in the plant.
- **Indigoid precursors** — indole glycosides yielding indigo/indirubin-type pigments on fermentation and oxidation of the leaf.
- **Triterpenoids** — methyl commate A and B, 24-norursa-3,12-diene (reported as a major GC-MS constituent of methanolic leaf extract), squalene, stigmast-5-en-3-ol.
- **Flavonoids** — flavone glucosides (a flavone 4′-OH,5-OH,7-di-O-glucoside reported as a dominant GC-MS peak in leaf).
- **Latex serine proteases** — thermostable, PMSF-inhibited; caseinolytic, gelatinolytic and collagenolytic.
- **Stem milk-clotting proteases** — three caseinolytic serine proteases (~95.6, 91.1, 83.2 kDa) capable of κ-casein hydrolysis.
- **Fatty acids / phytosterols** — hexadecanoic, octadecatrienoic acids and esters (seedpod); phytol, neophytadiene, γ-tocopherol, β-caryophyllene (leaf).

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

*Wrightia tinctoria* is filed as an **integumentary restoration agent**, and specifically as a topical one. Its case rests on a leaf oil applied to scaling plaque and a latex applied to open wound — both local, both external, both with mechanistically legible chemistry.

Its honest position in the Codex is that of a **traditionally continuous, mechanistically plausible, clinically unproven** topical. The psoriasis use is not a folk memory — it is a live commercial Ayurvedic/Siddha product line — but commercial continuity is a fact about markets, not about efficacy. The wound-healing thread is better supported preclinically than the psoriasis thread.

Filed with a deliberate warning: the *Holarrhena* name-collision means Kenetica treats "Indrajao" as an **unusable identifier**. Only the binomial governs.

### Scientific Correlation

*Evidence below was retrieved from PubMed for this entry; DOIs link to the original reports.*

**Psoriasis — the headline claim. Grade: WEAK–MODERATE preclinical; NO human trial evidence retrieved.**

Chloroform:methanol fractions of *W. tinctoria* (and *Alstonia scholaris*) were tested in HaCaT keratinocytes and in the imiquimod (IMQ)-induced psoriasis mouse model. The fractions reduced HaCaT viability, induced apoptosis, dose-dependently downregulated IL-8 and RANTES, and in vivo reduced skin inflammation, oedema, psoriasis score, epidermal thickness and dermal inflammation, with significant reductions in **IL-17 and IL-23** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ph18030304)). This is the strongest single piece of support in the file, and it is genuinely on-target — IL-17/IL-23 is the axis that modern biologics hit. **But:** it is a mouse model, IMQ psoriasis is an imperfect proxy for human plaque psoriasis, the tested material is a solvent fraction rather than the marketed oil, and the study is small and unreplicated.

A systems-pharmacology paper modelled 67 compounds of *W. tinctoria* against 238 protein targets and proposed synergistic action ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1089/acm.2016.0248)). **This is in silico network modelling and carries essentially no evidential weight for efficacy.** It generates hypotheses; it does not test them. It is cited here to be discounted, because it is frequently cited in commercial literature as though it demonstrated something.

**The honest null: no randomised controlled trial of *W. tinctoria* leaf oil in human psoriasis was retrieved in this search.** A product has been sold for decades on an indication that has not been tested against placebo in a published trial retrievable here. That is the single most important sentence in this entry.

**Wound healing. Grade: MODERATE preclinical, mechanistically strong.**

The latex proteases (WTLP) are the best-argued mechanism in the plant. WTLP contains thermostable serine proteases (fully inhibited by PMSF) with caseinolytic, gelatinolytic and collagenolytic activity. Topical WTLP (10 mg/kg, twice daily) in a mouse excision model produced a healing rate >2-fold over control at day 3 (49% vs 18%, p<0.01), 1.5-fold higher hydroxyproline (2444±100 vs 1579±121 µg/100 mg tissue), 5.6-fold higher catalase, and complete epithelialisation by day 9 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2013.06.056)). **Critically, PMSF-pretreated and heat-denatured latex failed to heal.** That is a proper mechanistic control: it establishes the proteases as the active principle rather than a co-travelling constituent. This is the cleanest doctrine-to-chemistry mapping in the entry — "healing milk" is enzymatically real.

A methanolic *fresh* leaf extract showed antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and antibacterial activity, protected PBMCs from intracellular ROS, induced angiogenesis in the CAM model, and as an ointment healed excision wounds 100% by day 13, faster than betadine ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00114-025-02066-z)). Supportive, single-lab, animal-only.

**Tryptanthrin and melanoma. Grade: MODERATE preclinical — but note the indication drift.**

Tryptanthrin, isolated from *W. tinctoria*, attenuates melanoma by suppressing MITF-M via the MEK/ERK axis, inhibits mutated BRAF and CK2α, and reduced migration and metastasis in murine models while remaining pharmacologically safe ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-022-04476-y)). An earlier semi-purified fraction, DW-F5, showed anti-melanoma and anti-angiogenic activity in NOD-SCID mice, with tryptanthrin as one of two cytotoxic components ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep11107)). This is real and interesting work — **but it is a different indication from the traditional one.** No tradition used this tree for melanoma. This is bioprospecting downstream of a folk lead, and it should not be back-read as validation of the psoriasis doctrine.

Bark extracts showed cytotoxicity against KB oral cancer cells (ethanol IC₅₀ 75.08 µg/mL) with caspase-3/7 induction ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.31557/APJCP.2025.26.5.1753)). **IC₅₀ values in the tens of µg/mL for a crude extract are unremarkable** — this is a weak signal presented as a finding, and it is graded accordingly: NEGLIGIBLE.

**Phytochemistry. Grade: ADEQUATE descriptive.**

GC-MS of methanolic leaf extract identified 24-norursa-3,12-diene (20.36%), a flavone di-O-glucoside (29.34%), methyl commate A (5.20%), squalene, γ-tocopherol and phytol, with DPPH free-radical scavenging attributed to the terpene/flavonoid/vitamin-E complement ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/jaoacint/qsab054)). **DPPH scavenging is a test-tube chemistry result and predicts nothing clinical** — it is reported for completeness, not as support.

**Systemic claims. Grade: WEAK, and off-doctrine.**

Seed extract lowered arterial pressure in anaesthetised rats (1–10 mg/kg) via combined K⁺-channel opening and Ca²⁺-channel blockade, and reduced cholesterol/LDL in dyslipidaemic models ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/FJC.0000000000000100)). Leaf and seed extracts reduced high-fat-diet-induced rises in AST, ALT and GGT in rats ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.6411)). Real animal pharmacology; no human data; not the traditional indication.

**A chemical curiosity worth recording.** *W. tinctoria* stem proteases were concentrated by three-phase partitioning and shown to hydrolyse κ-casein first, then α and β caseins — a pattern matching the commercial vegetable coagulant Enzeco® — making the tree a candidate cheese coagulant ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2018.06.042)). This has no medicinal bearing, but it cross-links directly to *Withania coagulans* (CPU-01035), where milk-clotting is the *defining* property rather than an incidental one. Two unrelated families, two proteolytic answers to the same problem.

**Summary grade: The wound-healing doctrine is preclinically supported with a proper mechanistic control. The psoriasis doctrine is mechanistically plausible, supported by one on-target mouse study, and clinically untested in humans. The dye/skin connection is chemically real. The dysentery indication most likely belongs to a different tree.**

### Contraindications

- **Apocynaceae latex caution — primary.** *W. tinctoria* is a copiously lactiferous member of a family notorious for cardiotoxic glycosides (*Nerium*, *Thevetia*, *Cerbera*). *W. tinctoria* is not among the family's classical cardiotoxins, and no cardiac glycoside toxicity was retrieved for it in this search — but **absence of retrieved evidence is not evidence of safety**, and the family-level prior is adverse. Neat latex should not be regarded as benign.
- **Latex on mucosa and open wounds.** The traditional Siddha application of fresh latex to mouth ulcers is recorded here as ethnography, **not endorsed**. The latex's active principle is a set of collagenolytic and gelatinolytic proteases — enzymes that digest extracellular matrix. On intact granulating tissue under controlled dosing this appears beneficial; on mucosa, on inflamed tissue, or in uncontrolled amounts, a collagen-digesting enzyme is a plausible irritant and injurant. Do not apply neat latex to mucous membranes.
- **Identity — the *Holarrhena* collision.** Material sold as "Indrajao" or "Indrayava" may be *Holarrhena pubescens*, which carries conessine and has a distinct toxicity and pharmacology. **Never accept the vernacular name.** Confirm the binomial.
- **Seed (Indrayava) — unresolved.** Because the dysentery indication and the seed name are shared with *Holarrhena*, and because the *Wrightia* seed's own toxicology was not established in evidence retrieved here, internal seed use is not supported by this entry.
- **Pregnancy and lactation.** No safety data retrieved. Avoid.
- **Antihypertensives / lipid-lowering therapy.** Seed extract lowers blood pressure via K⁺-channel opening and Ca²⁺-channel blockade in animals. Theoretical additive hypotension; no human data.
- **Topical sensitisation.** Not characterised. Patch-test any oil preparation before extended application.
- **Not a substitute for psoriasis care.** Psoriasis is a systemic immune-mediated disease with cardiometabolic comorbidity. Nothing in this entry supports replacing evidence-based therapy with a leaf oil.
