# Sweet Cherry / Gean / Mazzard / Wild Cherry / Kerasos / Kiraz / Cerise / Vogelkirsche

## *Prunus avium* (L.) L. | Family: Rosaceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, CM, SBJ

**Energetic Classification:** Fruit — sweet, cool, moist, nutritive and gently descending. Stalk (peduncle) — bland-astringent, cool, dry, downward and draining through the water passages.

**Training Phase:** Foundation — the fruit is food; the stalk tea is a mild adjunct of the water-course, safe at Foundation but modest in claim

---

### Origin & Tradition

*Prunus avium* is the ancestral dessert cherry — the wild gean or mazzard of European woodland, from which every sweet cultivar descends. The specific epithet *avium*, "of the birds", records the plant's dispersal agent and its wild status. Its Greek name **kerasos** (κέρασος) is the root of Latin *cerasus* and thence of nearly every European word for the fruit; the classical tradition associated the tree's introduction to Rome with Lucullus and the Pontic city of Kerasous, though the tree's actual range in Europe long predates that account.

This entry has an unusual structure, because *P. avium* has **two distinct medicinal careers occupying two different organs of the same plant**: the fruit, which is essentially a food of the cooling-nutritive register; and the **stalk** — the slender peduncle discarded when the cherry is eaten — which is one of the most persistent folk diuretic teas in Europe and Anatolia. The two must be kept apart, because their chemistry is genuinely different.

**Dioscoridean / Mediterranean Materia Medica (DMM)**
The Greek and Roman materia medica placed *kerasia* firmly among the cooling, moistening fruits. The classical reading: sweet cherries eaten fresh are *loosening* to the belly and cooling to the blood — appropriate in heat, in summer, in fever-thirst, in dryness — while dried cherries turn astringent and *binding*. This is a coherent and observationally honest doctrine: the fresh fruit's sugar-and-water bulk is mildly laxative; the dried fruit, concentrated in its skin polyphenols, is not. Fruit for heat and thirst; the tree's gum reputedly for cough and for the voice. The classical record does not make grand claims for the cherry, and Kenetica should not either.

**European & Anatolian Common / Folk Medicine**
Here the stalk takes over. **Cherry stalk tea** — French *queues de cerises*, Turkish *kiraz sapı*, Portuguese *pés de cereja*, Spanish *rabos de cereza* — is a genuine, widely-distributed folk uroflow remedy, sold dried in herb shops and markets across France, Iberia, Italy, the Balkans and Turkey to this day. The indication is consistent and specific: **to increase urine flow, to flush the kidneys and bladder, for oedema and "water retention", for gravel and cystitis, and in the modern Turkish and French domestic register, as a slimming tea.** The doctrine is one of *drainage through the water gate* — a bland, faintly astringent, unmistakably drying tea that is understood to open the urinary passage without heating it. The universality of this use across unconnected Mediterranean regions is the strongest ethnobotanical fact in this entry, and it is corroborated in the scientific literature, which describes *P. avium* stems as "widely recognized by traditional medicine" while noting that concrete data on them remained scarce (Afonso et al., *Antioxidants* 2020; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox9121295)).

**Signature, Bath & Domestic Register (SBJ)**
The cherry belongs to the domestic and seasonal register as much as the pharmacy. Cherry-stalk infusion is a household drink, not a prescription — brewed by the handful from what would otherwise be thrown away, taken in summer, associated with lightness and the shedding of water-weight. Cherry-tree gum was chewed. The bark and leaf entered domestic washes. The fruit is a seasonal marker: the cherry is the first true fruit of the European summer, and its cooling doctrine is inseparable from its arrival in the heat. Kenetica records this as the plant's real cultural location — a **kitchen-adjacent remedy**, whose chief virtue is that it is nearly free, nearly harmless, and habitual.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

Two doctrines, two chemistries, and they map with satisfying precision onto different organs.

**The cooling-nutritive fruit doctrine → anthocyanin chemistry.** The tradition read the cherry as *cooling the blood*, and it read this from the colour. The dark red of a ripe gean is not decorative — it is a specific and now fully mapped chemistry. Metabolomic and transcriptomic work shows that **anthocyanins, not carotenoids, determine sweet cherry colour**, with cyanidin-3-*O*-glucoside as the dominant pigment alongside cyanidin-3-*O*-rutinoside, cyanidin-3-*O*-galactoside, cyanidin-3-*O*-arabinoside, peonidin-3-*O*-glucoside and pelargonidin glycosides (Chu et al., *Gene* 2023; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gene.2023.147602)); the transcription factor PavMYB.C2 has been identified as the module driving Cy3G accumulation via UFGT (Pei et al., *PLoS Genet* 2025; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1011761)). So: the doctrine of blood-cooling was read off a pigment that *is* an antioxidant-anti-inflammatory class. **The signature was a real chemical marker. But the doctrine's therapeutic reach far exceeds what the pigment has been shown to do — see the honest grading below.**

**The drainage doctrine → stalk polyphenol chemistry.** The stalk is bland, dry, and faintly astringent — nothing like the fruit. The tradition read that dryness as an opening of the water passage. Chemically, the stalk is *not* an anthocyanin organ at all: **anthocyanins were found in the fruits and not in the stems** (Bastos et al., *Food Chem* 2014; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2014.10.145)). Instead the stem is dominated by hydroxycinnamic acids and flavonoids — fifty-two phenolics were tentatively identified across *P. avium* by-products, hydroxycinnamic acids most abundant (Nunes et al., *Foods* 2021; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/foods10061185)) — with sakuranetin the major stem phenolic in one cultivar and ellagic acid in another (Afonso et al. 2020). **The tradition split the plant at exactly the line the chemistry splits it: pigment-rich fruit for cooling, phenolic-astringent stalk for draining. That is a genuine hit for observational pharmacognosy.** What the tradition could not know — and what modern data still has not established — is whether the stalk's phenolics actually cause diuresis. The doctrine locates the right organ; it does not prove the mechanism.

**The gout thread — and an honest amputation.** Modern enthusiasm for "cherry and gout" is real, but it **belongs mostly to the sour or tart cherry, *Prunus cerasus*** (see that entry), not to *P. avium*. The anthocyanin-urate literature has been built on tart cherry juice and tart cherry concentrate. Kenetica does not permit *P. avium* to inherit *P. cerasus*'s evidence by fruit-name association. The two species differ in anthocyanin profile and concentration, and the trials were not run on this plant.

### Active Components

**Fruit:**
- **Anthocyanins** — cyanidin-3-*O*-glucoside (dominant), cyanidin-3-*O*-rutinoside, -galactoside, -arabinoside, -3-xyloside; peonidin-3-*O*-glucoside and -rutinoside; pelargonidin-3-*O*-glucoside and -rutinoside (Chu et al. 2023)
- **Flavonols** — a large and cultivar-variable set (85 flavonols differed between red and red-yellow cultivars, Chu et al. 2023)
- **Sugars, organic acids, fatty acids, tocopherols** — characterised chromatographically (Bastos et al. 2014); the nutritive matrix
- **Phenolic acids** — hydroxycinnamates

**Stalk (peduncle) — the medicinal organ:**
- **Hydroxycinnamic acids** — the most abundant phenolic class in by-products (Nunes et al. 2021)
- **Sakuranetin** — major stem phenolic in several cultivars; **ellagic acid** dominant in others (Afonso et al. 2020)
- **Flavonoids, *o*-diphenols, saponins** — elevated in stem extracts (Afonso et al. 2020)
- **No anthocyanins** (Bastos et al. 2014) — the stalk is chemically not a small cherry

**Kernel (seed within the pit) — ⚠️ not a medicinal organ:**
- **Amygdalin** — cyanogenic diglucoside
- Catechin and protocatechuic acid have been characterised as major kernel phenolics (Afonso et al. 2020)

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**The fruit** enters Kenetica as **food, classified as food.** It is a cooling, hydrating, pigment-dense seasonal fruit whose place is nutritional and whose claims are modest. Kenetica's use case: summer heat, thirst, dryness, and as an anthocyanin-bearing component of a whole diet. It is not a therapy and is not dosed.

**The stalk** enters as a **Foundation-phase mild uroflow adjunct** — the one place where *P. avium* acts as a herb rather than a food. Its use case is the folk one: a bland drying tea taken through a course of days where increased urine flow is wanted and no urgent renal indication exists. Kenetica's honest framing: **the stalk tea's strongest credentials are ethnobotanical breadth and a very low harm profile, not demonstrated efficacy.** It is admitted at Foundation *because* its claim is small — a herb that promises little can be given early.

Kenetica's structural rule for this plant: **eat the fruit, brew the stalk, discard the kernel.** Three organs, three verdicts.

### Scientific Correlation

**Stalk as an antioxidant-phenolic organ → CONFIRMED.** The tradition's selection of the stalk over the fruit as the *medicinal* part is chemically vindicated. **All stem preparations — extracts, infusions, and decoctions — showed higher antioxidant potential than the fruit extract**, attributable to higher phenolic acid and flavonoid concentration (Bastos et al., *Food Chem* 2014; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2014.10.145)). Independently, both aqueous and hydroethanolic stem extracts showed the highest DPPH activity among *P. avium* by-products (Nunes et al., *Foods* 2021; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/foods10061185)), and stem extracts showed antioxidant activity positively correlated with phenolic content across four cultivars (Afonso et al., *Antioxidants* 2020; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox9121295)). **Grade: STRONG for the chemistry. The tradition picked the right organ.**

**⛔ THE DIURETIC CLAIM ITSELF → NOT DEMONSTRATED. This is the central null of the entry.** No clinical or animal study establishing a diuretic effect of *P. avium* stalk was retrieved in this run. The literature that exists characterises the stems' phenolics and antioxidant capacity and explicitly notes that despite wide traditional recognition, "detailed and feasible information about their bioactive composition or biological value is still scarce" (Afonso et al. 2020). **A rich phenolic profile is not a diuretic mechanism.** Kenetica states this plainly: the most famous use of this plant is supported by ethnobotanical ubiquity and by nothing else retrieved here. **Grade: UNSUPPORTED — traditional use only. [USER: verify whether uroflow trials exist outside PubMed indexing.]**

**Stalk antimicrobial → PARTIAL, NARROW.** Only stem extracts — not kernel extracts — inhibited the growth of Gram-positive bacterial isolates; they did not inhibit Gram-negative isolates (Afonso et al. 2020). This gives the folk topical/wash use a thin but real footing. **Grade: WEAK-POSITIVE, in vitro only.**

**Fruit anthocyanins → cardiovascular/anti-inflammatory: PLAUSIBLE, OVERSOLD.** A review of cherry and berry phenolics notes antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and vasodilatory properties with signals for reduced blood pressure, improved lipid profile and endothelial function — while stating candidly that **variability in fruit composition, processing and study design makes findings hard to interpret and optimal dosing impossible to establish** (Carvalho, Lahlou & Silva, *Nutrients* 2024; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu16111597)). Kenetica adopts the reviewers' own caution rather than their abstract's optimism. **Grade: SUGGESTIVE, not established; largely aggregated across species.**

**⛔ THE SPECIES-CONFUSION DEFLATION — *P. avium* is not *P. cerasus*.** The exercise and cardiovascular anthocyanin literature runs on **tart cherry**. A randomised trial of tart cherry supplementation (200 mg anthocyanin/day) around hypoxic exercise found **no difference in post-exercise hypotension between placebo and tart cherry conditions** (Horiuchi & Oliver, *High Alt Med Biol* 2024/2025; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1089/ham.2024.0076)) — a null result, and one obtained with the *other* species. Kenetica records this to make the point twice over: the gout/recovery/anthocyanin thread belongs to *P. cerasus*, and even there the results are not uniformly positive. **Grade: the transfer of tart-cherry claims to sweet cherry is REFUSED.**

**Fruit antitumour → weak and organ-specific.** Only the fruit extract showed antitumour potential, selectively against HCT-15 colon carcinoma (GI₅₀ ≈ 74 μg/mL), likely attributable to anthocyanins, which are absent from stems (Bastos et al. 2014). Stem and flower hydroethanolic extracts inhibited Caco-2 growth only **above 200 μg/mL** (Nunes et al. 2021) — a high concentration of little clinical meaning. **Grade: WEAK; in vitro only; no therapeutic inference permitted.**

**Safety signal — a genuine positive.** **None of the fruit or stem preparations showed hepatotoxicity against normal primary cells** (Bastos et al. 2014). This supports the entry's low-harm framing for the aerial organs.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *Kerasia* (κέρασος) sits among the cooling, moistening fruits: fresh sweet cherries read as loosening to the belly and cooling to the blood, appropriate in heat and fever-thirst, while the dried fruit turns astringent and binding. This is a dietetic and therapeutic account, and Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system that could assign the cherry a cultivation status — *pneuma* is context only, and the Galenic three spirits are later and post-Dioscoridean. What the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention: the seasonal reading of the fruit, the distinction of fresh from dried, the honest refusal to make grand claims for a food. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** In the Greco-Arabic humoral continuum the sweet cherry is a cold-and-moist nutritive fruit of the summer table, a cooling dietetic item rather than a drug of the *Canon*; the transmission that actually reaches this entry is Galenic and European-folk, and no distinctly Avicennan cultivation account of *P. avium* was retrieved. The strongest cultivation idea in the Unani corpus — the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture as a finite vital endowment — is a longevity frame of the whole regimen, not a status assigned to a cooling seasonal fruit, and no *mufarrih* or cardiac-*ruh* role is claimed. Used dietetically, no cultivation status recorded. **(b) framed.** (See attestation flag: the CM tag on this species may be better coded EF, European folk.)

**Chinese — Shennong / Bencao (SBJ).** *Prunus avium* is the European wild gean; the cherries of the classical Chinese materia medica are other *Prunus* species (the Chinese cherry, *P. pseudocerasus*, and relatives), and no *Bencao* attestation of this European species was retrieved. It therefore has no *san pin* 三品 grade — upper 上品, middle 中品 or lower 下品 — recorded in the Chinese sources, and nothing within *yang sheng* 養生 attaches to it. **(c) absent.** (See attestation flag: the "SBJ" heading in this entry is used in the local sense "Signature, Bath & Domestic Register" — a European domestic-register frame, likely EF — not the canonical Shennong/Bencao code, and that European folk material carries no cultivation status either.)

**Practitioner's note.** The honest cultivation answer is behavioural and dietetic, not vital. Across the traditions tagged, a cultivator eats the fresh fruit as a cooling summer food and, in the European and Anatolian domestic register, brews the discarded stalk as a bland drying uroflow tea through a short course of days — neither of which is a longevity or vital-building practice, and the diuretic claim itself is ethnobotanically ubiquitous but pharmacologically undemonstrated. Eat the fruit, brew the stalk, discard the kernel; expect a food and a mild habitual tea, not a cultivation substance.

### Contraindications

⚠️ **CYANOGENIC KERNELS — the principal hazard of this plant.**

- **The seed kernel inside the cherry pit contains amygdalin, a cyanogenic glycoside.** Crushed, chewed, ground, or deliberately extracted kernels release hydrogen cyanide on hydrolysis. **Never prepare, crush, grind, blend, or consume cherry kernels, and never accept "kernel" preparations, kernel oils for internal use, or "vitamin B17" products.**
- The Rosaceae kernel hazard is not theoretical. In *Prunus armeniaca* (apricot), **a 3-year-old child ingesting three kernels developed coma, convulsions and severe metabolic acidosis (pH 6.91) with a blood cyanide of 3.15 mg/L**, requiring haemodialysis and hydroxocobalamin (Dalkıran et al., *Pediatr Emerg Care* 2020; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/PEC.0000000000001644)). A 4-year-old given amygdalin ("vitamin B17") and apricot kernel as alternative-medicine treatment presented unresponsive with GCS 3 and markedly elevated serum cyanide, recovering only after sodium thiosulfate (Sauer et al., *Wien Med Wochenschr* 2015; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10354-014-0340-7)). **The genus-level lesson transfers directly: keep pits intact, keep them away from children, and never process the fruit in a way that fractures the stone.** [USER: verify *P. avium*-specific kernel amygdalin content before any quantitative statement — species-specific figures were not retrieved this run.]
- Swallowing an **intact, unchewed** pit is not the hazard described above; the glycoside is not liberated from an unbroken stone. The hazard is mechanical (obstruction, in children) rather than toxicological.

**Other cautions:**
- **Do not use stalk tea as a diuretic where a diuretic is medically indicated.** In cardiac, renal or hepatic oedema, this tea is not a treatment and must never displace prescribed diuretic therapy. Seek medical management.
- **Do not combine with pharmaceutical diuretics without supervision.** Even an unproven diuretic-intent tea should not be layered onto loop or thiazide diuretics; the risk is electrolyte depletion and dehydration, not efficacy.
- **Do not use for slimming.** The modern folk use as a slimming tea is a water-weight illusion at best, and is the use-pattern most associated with excessive, unsupervised consumption.
- **Discontinue and seek assessment** on any haematuria, flank pain, fever, or dysuria — cherry stalk tea is not a treatment for urinary infection or renal stone.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** the fruit is food and unrestricted. **The stalk tea should be avoided** — no safety data; diuretic-intent botanicals are excluded by default. [USER: verify]
- **Rosaceae allergy / oral allergy syndrome:** cross-reactivity with birch-pollen-associated fruit allergy is a known Rosaceae pattern; sweet cherry is a recognised participant. Those reacting to apple, peach or plum should approach with caution.
- **Fructose malabsorption / IBS:** the fresh fruit's loosening effect in quantity is the classical observation and a real digestive limit.
