# Daphne mezereum — Daphnoides (Dioscorides 4-148)

## *Daphne mezereum (one of many names on the translators' SUGGESTED line, where "Mezereon [Pliny]" comes first and "Daphne mezereum [Linnaeus]" stands among them; "Spurge, Laurel, Dwarf Bay, Mezereon, Spurge Olive, Dwarf Laurel")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line (lines 9332 to 9334) is unusually crowded: "Mezereon [Pliny], Thymelaea [Medieval], Daphnoides, Laureola [Fuchs], Clematis aegyptica, Daphne laureola, Daphne mezereum [Linnaeus], Thymelaea praecox, Thymelaea mezereum, Mezereum officinale — Spurge, Laurel, Dwarf Bay, Mezereon, Spurge Olive, Dwarf Laurel". It lists *Daphne laureola* as well as *Daphne mezereum*, and the bracketed names show that *Daphne mezereum* is the Linnaean name, from long after Dioscorides. Which of these the Greek *daphnoides* was is not settled by the translation. The row was added from the chapter id (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. **Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-148. DAPHNOIDES*, heading at line 9330, SUGGESTED line at 9332, text from line 9335, continuing after the page break to the heading of 4-149 *CHAMAIDAPHNE*. It stands in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots").

The whole chapter reads: "Daphnoeides is similar to alypon [4-180] with a flower like nymphaea [3-148], and in the midst of this, something like a cone, in which is the seed. It is a little shrub, a foot high, with many branches (flexible like a thong), bearing leaves from the middle to the top. The bark around the stems is very gluey; the leaves are similar to the bay, but softer, more slender, and not easily broken, biting and burning the mouth and the jaws. The flowers are white and the fruit black when it is grown ripe; the root is useless. It grows in mountainous places. The leaves (taken in a drink either dried or fresh) expel phlegm through the bowels. It encourages vomiting and the menstrual flow. Chewed, it draws mucus from the head, and it also encourages sneezing, and a decoction of as much as eleven grains of the fruit (taken as a drink) purges. It is also called daphnitis, or hydragogon; the Romans call it laureola, some, lactago, and the Gauls, ousubim."

So the description is of a little shrub a foot high, with many flexible branches, leaves from the middle to the top, a very gluey bark, leaves like bay but softer, more slender and tough, "biting and burning the mouth and the jaws"; the flower is white, with something cone-like in its middle that holds the seed; the fruit turns black when ripe; the root is "useless"; it grows in mountains. The uses are all strong evacuations: leaves, dried or fresh, in a drink "expel phlegm through the bowels", induce vomiting and the menstrual flow; chewing draws mucus from the head; and it provokes sneezing; and a decoction of "as much as eleven grains of the fruit" purges. Other names printed are *daphnitis* and *hydragogon* (Greek), *laureola* and *lactago* (Roman), and *ousubim* (Gaulish). The translators' illustration beside the text is captioned "Daphne mezereum after FAGUET — 1878", a modern drawing placed by the translators.

**Other places in the same text.** Chapter 4-165b *TITHUMALOS MURSINITES* (line 9535) says the female spurge "is similar to daphnoeides" and has leaves like *myrsine*, which is a comparison for recognition only. Chapter 4-7a *KLEMATIS* carries "Clematis daphnoides" in its SUGGESTED line (line 8055), but it concerns a vinelike plant with a little leaf like laurel, whose translator-identification is periwinkle (*Vinca minor*); the name overlaps with this entry's line only in the Latin. Neither chapter is read into this entry. The translators' index lists *Daphne mezereum* at pages 675, 693 and 695; 695 is the page of this chapter. Pages 675 and 693 were not opened. ### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. In general terms, *Daphne* species are widely known as poisonous plants, and the berries and bark of the species now called *Daphne mezereum* are well known as irritant to skin and mouth. This is stated generally and is not a finding about the plant of this chapter. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice, and for this chapter the reason is toxicity. Dioscorides himself describes the leaves as "biting and burning the mouth and the jaws", and every use he gives is a forced evacuation: purging, vomiting, bringing on the menses, sneezing. The Codex gives no protocol and recommends none. The fruit decoction has a stated quantity ("eleven grains"), but the unit and the identity are not settled, so it is not a usable figure.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-148 gives these preparations: the leaves "taken in a drink either dried or fresh"; the leaves "chewed"; and "a decoction of as much as eleven grains of the fruit (taken as a drink)". The only quantity is "as much as eleven grains" of fruit. The word "grains" is the translators' rendering and the weight is not defined in the chapter. No other dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: habit (a foot high, flexible branches), bark (very gluey), leaf (like bay but softer and more slender), flower and fruit (white; black when ripe), a sensory test (the leaf burns the mouth and jaws), and a statement of what is not used (the root is "useless"). It assigns the plant no status. The identification is the translators' and is a long list of candidates. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. Its uses are emetic, purgative and sneezing remedies of a plant that burns the mouth, so it is a record to read and not a practice to follow. The sensory test it describes is itself the warning.

### Contraindications

The plant described is a burning, purgative and emetic one, and the Daphne genus is generally known to be poisonous; no part of it should be chewed, drunk or applied on the strength of this entry. Dioscorides' uses to bring on the menstrual flow and to purge are not safe self-treatment, and no safety information for pregnancy was read. The identification rests on a long list of translators' candidates, so no wild plant should be gathered or used. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
