# I-378 | Dong Quai / Dang Gui (*Angelica sinensis*)

**Convergence Score: 5/9 | Energetic Tier: ◈ Jing (Blood/Xue)**

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Binomial:** *Angelica sinensis* (Oliv.) Diels, 1900
**Family:** Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)
**Subfamily:** Apioideae
**Tribe:** Selineae

**Synonymy:** *Ostericum sinense* (Oliv.) Huang & Zhang (taxonomically contested reassignment; *A. sinensis* remains the standard pharmaceutical name)

**Common names:**
- Chinese: 当归 (*Dāng Guī*) — a name of rich semantic layers: *dāng* (当) = "appropriate, ought to, return/restore"; *guī* (归) = "to return, to belong, to normalize." The classical interpretation: "that which makes the blood return/restore to its proper course"; also punning with "homesickness" — the name legendarily evokes the return of husband's blood to wife (used primarily for women's health), or a soldier's return home. One of the richest compound names in the Chinese materia medica.
- English: Dong Quai (Cantonese pronunciation); Dang Gui (Mandarin pinyin); Chinese Angelica; Female Ginseng (modern marketing name — not a classical term)
- Japanese: 当帰 (*Tōki*) — Japanese reading of the same characters; used in Kampo medicine as a distinct drug entity
- Korean: 당귀 (*Danggwi*)
- Vietnamese: Đương quy
- German: Chinesische Engelwurz (Chinese Angelica)
- French: Angélique chinoise

**CRITICAL DISAMBIGUATION — Three "Angelicas":**
1. *Angelica sinensis* (Dong Quai) — **Chinese species; this entry** — blood-nourishing, TCM gynecological herb; Sichuan/Gansu/Yunnan origin
2. *Angelica archangelica* (Garden Angelica) — **European species; entry I-366** — phototoxic furanocoumarin; Scandinavian/Northern European; different pharmacological profile entirely
3. *Angelica dahurica* (Bai Zhi) — **next entry I-379** — Chinese species; sinusitis/headache/phototoxic; different profile from Dong Quai despite shared genus

These three plants share a genus but present substantially different pharmacological profiles. Conflating them in clinical practice is a significant error.

**Botanical description:** Perennial (biennial in cultivation) herb; strongly aromatic; stems 0.4–1m, purple-tinged; leaves 2–3× pinnate, large; flowers white, umbels; root system large, aromatic, multi-branched — the root is the pharmaceutical part. Cultivation: primarily Minxian County, Gansu Province ("Min County Dang Gui" — highest-grade commercial production); also Sichuan (Wenshan), Yunnan, Shaanxi.

**Parts used:** Dried root — specifically the pharmaceutical product is stratified by root section:
- **全当归** (*Quán Dāng Guī*) — whole root: combined blood-nourishing + blood-moving activity
- **当归头** (*Dāng Guī Tóu*) — root head (crown): primarily blood-nourishing (补血)
- **当归身** (*Dāng Guī Shēn*) — root body: primarily blood-nourishing (补血)
- **当归尾** (*Dāng Guī Wěi*) — root tails/fibers: primarily blood-moving (活血)
This anatomical differentiation of pharmacological activity within a single root — head vs. tail having different clinical emphasis — is a unique feature of TCM pharmaceutical tradition rarely encountered in Western herbalism.

## 3. Convergence Analysis

Dong Quai occupies a position in East Asian medicine roughly analogous to the combination of iron supplements + aspirin + estrogen in modern Western pharmacology — a single herb simultaneously addressed blood deficiency (anemia analog), blood stagnation (circulatory obstruction), and menstrual dysregulation (hormonal analog). That a single root achieves mechanistic coverage of these three domains through distinct compound classes (phthalides for smooth muscle, ferulic acid for anti-platelet/antioxidant, polysaccharides for hematopoiesis, and polyphenols for estrogenic activity) is pharmacologically remarkable.

Its 5/9 convergence score, when supplemented by Korean, Japanese, and Vietnamese traditions, effectively represents the entire East Asian pharmacological tradition — a geographical region encompassing approximately 2 billion people across 2,000 years of continuous medical practice. No other single herb in the Codex represents more convergence within a defined cultural pharmacological zone.

The comparison to Western gynecological pharmacology is illuminating: the parallel functions of Dong Quai (blood nourishment, circulation promotion, menstrual regulation, postpartum recovery) correspond remarkably closely to the functions addressed by iron supplementation + hormonal therapy + non-steroidal analgesics in modern Western medicine. The convergence is functional rather than chemical.

## 4. Phytochemical Composition

**Phthalides (shared with Chuanxiong — the phthalide bridge):**
- **Z-Ligustilide**: 45–65% of volatile oil (highest concentration in *A. sinensis* versus other phthalide-bearing Apiaceae); smooth muscle spasmolytic (uterine, intestinal, vascular); vasodilatory; anti-platelet; neuroprotective
- **Senkyunolide A, I, J, M, N, O**: shared with *L. chuanxiong*; spasmolytic; anti-inflammatory; sedative
- **3-n-Butylphthalide** (NBP): Ca²⁺ channel blockade; shared with celery/lovage/Chuanxiong
- **Butylidenephthalide**: anti-tumor in glioblastoma lines in vitro (preliminary; significant research interest)
- Total volatile oil: 0.4–1.0% DW; phthalides dominant fraction

**Ferulic acid (major phenolic — shared with Chuanxiong):**
- **Ferulic acid**: 0.05–0.1% DW; anti-platelet (TXA₂ inhibition); antioxidant; anti-inflammatory; estrogenic modulation (ferulic acid binds ERα at low affinity); hematopoietic support (erythropoietin expression modulation in animal models)

**Polysaccharides (angelica polysaccharides, AP):**
- **APS-1, APS-2, APS-3** (high-molecular-weight polysaccharides): immunomodulatory; hematopoietic stimulation (stimulates bone marrow erythropoiesis in animal models — mechanistic basis for blood-nourishing traditional claim); structurally distinct from phthalides/ferulic acid — separate compound class with separate mechanisms

**Coumarins (phytoestrogenic fraction):**
- **Osthole**: furocoumarin derivative; estrogenic (ERα/ERβ binding); anti-inflammatory; bone density support in vitro
- **Imperatorin, bergapten** (trace): photosensitizing furanocoumarins; minor at typical doses; phototoxicity risk much lower than parsley/parsnip but relevant at high concentrated extract doses
- **Scopoletin**: anti-inflammatory; antispasmodic

**Flavonoids:**
- Quercetin, kaempferol glycosides; anti-inflammatory, antioxidant

**Amino acids:**
- L-Methionine (significant quantity): contributes to tissue-building and blood-nourishing properties from a nutritional perspective

**Vitamins and minerals (nutritional context):**
- Folate, niacin (relevant to blood formation in nutritionally marginal populations)
- Iron (moderate bioavailability; nutritional contribution to blood-nourishing action in iron-deficient women)

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**Blood-nourishing mechanism (hematopoietic, 补血):**
Angelica polysaccharides (APS) → stimulation of hematopoietic stem cells and progenitor cells in bone marrow → increased erythropoiesis → elevated RBC and hemoglobin; confirmed in multiple animal models (cyclophosphamide-induced anemia recovery model; radiation-induced marrow suppression recovery). Ferulic acid modulates erythropoietin expression. Nutritional contribution: iron + folate content provides substrate for hemoglobin synthesis. Combined: plausible multi-level mechanism for blood-building traditional action.

**Blood-moving mechanism (anti-platelet/vasodilatory, 活血):**
Z-Ligustilide + ferulic acid → TXA₂ inhibition → reduced platelet aggregation + vasodilation. Mechanism identical to Chuanxiong (shared compounds); Dong Quai's phthalide profile skewed more toward blood-nourishing activities by virtue of additional polysaccharide/amino acid content — Chuanxiong more purely blood-moving.

**Uterine smooth muscle modulation (dual action):**
Z-Ligustilide exhibits dose-dependent bidirectional uterine effects:
- **Low dose:** uterine smooth muscle relaxation (spasmolytic; Ca²⁺ channel blockade; reduces uterine cramping/dysmenorrhea)
- **High dose:** uterine contraction stimulation (different channel pathway activation)
This dual action explains both the use in dysmenorrhea (low dose, spasmolytic — reduces painful cramping) and the pregnancy contraindication (high dose, oxytocic — can stimulate contractions). It also explains the classical TCM distinction: blood-nourishing dose (root body/head; lower volatile content) vs. blood-moving dose (root tails; higher volatile content).

**Phytoestrogenic mechanism (weak):**
Multiple compounds contribute: osthole (ERα/ERβ binding); ferulic acid (weak ERα affinity); coumestans and isoflavone trace amounts. Overall phytoestrogenic activity measured at weak-moderate (compared to red clover or soy isoflavones). Clinical significance: possible relief of menopausal vasomotor symptoms (hot flashes); possible bone density support; possible interaction with hormone-sensitive cancers and medications. This is the most contested aspect of Dong Quai pharmacology — Western practitioners have sometimes marketed it as a "natural estrogen" while pharmacological data show activity far below therapeutic estrogen levels.

**Anti-inflammatory mechanism:**
NF-κB inhibition (Z-ligustilide, ferulic acid, osthole); COX inhibition (ferulic acid, coumarins); antioxidant (ferulic acid NRF2 activation). Multi-compound anti-inflammatory convergence.

**Neuroprotective mechanism:**
Z-Ligustilide + 3-n-butylphthalide → BBB penetration; mitochondrial protection (as for celery); BDNF expression; anti-apoptotic signaling. Animal model data for ischemic stroke protection parallel to those of DL-3-n-butylphthalide pharmaceutical.

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**Dysmenorrhea:**
- **Level B** — Multiple small RCTs and systematic reviews in Chinese literature; Dong Quai alone and in Si Wu Tang combination reduces primary dysmenorrhea severity; effect comparable to ibuprofen in some trials (Yang 2003, n=120); evidence quality variable but consistent direction

**Menopausal symptoms:**
- **Level B** — Hirata 1997 (JAMA — rare Western RCT for Dong Quai): n=71, 24-week crossover, Dong Quai 4.5g/day vs. placebo; **NO significant effect** on hot flash frequency or endometrial thickness. This well-designed trial challenged Western marketing of Dong Quai as menopausal estrogen substitute. However: trial used Dong Quai alone (not in traditional formula); traditional TCM never uses Dong Quai as a solo menopausal herb.
- Combination formula trials (Dong Quai + other herbs, TCM pattern-matched): positive results in several Chinese RCTs — suggesting formula context matters and isolated-herb trials may not capture traditional use pharmacology

**Anemia/blood support:**
- **Level B/C** — Chinese clinical series and small trials in iron-deficiency anemia and post-chemotherapy anemia support; polysaccharide fraction animal data strong; human trial evidence modest

**Blood viscosity/cardiovascular:**
- **Level B** — Chinese hospital trials for cerebrovascular disease, coronary artery disease: Dong Quai preparations (injection form) reduce blood viscosity, improve microcirculation; evidence base parallel to Chuanxiong evidence structure

**Anti-tumor (butylidenephthalide):**
- **Level D** — Butylidenephthalide shows cytotoxicity against glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) cell lines; Tsai 2006 and subsequent studies; crosses BBB in animal models; early clinical interest but no completed human trials

**Overall:** Strong traditional use evidence base; significant clinical use in East Asian hospital medicine; limited high-quality Western RCT data; Hirata 1997 cautions against simplistic "natural estrogen" framing; formula-context trials more positive than single-herb isolation trials.

## 7. Phenomenological Record

**The Female Ginseng narrative — and its limitations:**
Modern Western marketing frequently labels Dong Quai "female ginseng" — positioning it as the feminine counterpart to panax ginseng's masculine tonic properties. This framing, while commercially effective, distorts traditional usage in two ways: (1) Dong Quai is used in both male and female patients in TCM for blood stasis conditions; (2) the mechanism is nothing like ginseng's adaptogenic/adrenal axis effects. The "female ginseng" label is a marketing construct, not a traditional category. Within TCM, Dong Quai's primary identity is as the premier blood-nourishing herb (补血药), specifically addressing blood deficiency (血虚) and blood stasis (血瘀) — a distinct classification from ginseng's Qi-tonifying role.

**当归 — the name and its legends:**
Classical Chinese medical literature records several origin legends for the name 当归. The most frequently cited: a young man leaves his wife to study medicine or serve in the military. Before departing, he hands her the root of this plant, saying "当归" — "you should return [to health], I should return [to you]." When he returns years later, she has maintained health using the root. The legend encodes medical instruction in romantic narrative — a mnemonic for the herb's role in maintaining women's health during men's absence, when access to physicians might be limited.

A second interpretation: 当归 as "restore what belongs" — the herb returns blood to its proper course, organs to proper function, vital forces to their natural state. This interpretation aligns with the pharmacological reality of the dual blood-nourishing/blood-moving action: normalizing both deficiency and excess, both deficient blood and stagnant blood.

**Shennong Bencao Jing — upper grade (上品) classification:**
Unlike Chuanxiong (middle-grade, reflecting its moving/activating and potentially excessive nature), Dong Quai is classified in some editions as upper-grade — reflecting its dual blood-nourishing action being considered more tonifying than dangerous. The *Bencao* text: 当归，味甘，辛，温。主咳逆上气，温疟寒热洗洗在皮肤中，妇人漏下绝子，诸恶疮疡，金疮。煮汁饮之。("Dang Gui: flavor sweet, pungent, warm; governs counterflow cough and rising qi; warm malaria with alternating chills and heat in the skin; women's prolonged vaginal discharge and childlessness; all malign sores and ulcers; metal-wound injuries. Drink the boiled juice.")

The listing of metal-wound injuries and sores alongside gynecological applications reflects the wound-healing and blood-nourishing properties operating in the same mechanistic space.

**Minxian County (岷县) provenance — the terroir of blood:**
Minxian County (Gansu Province), at 2,600–3,000m elevation in the Min River valley, produces what Chinese pharmaceutical tradition considers the highest-grade Dong Quai. The combination of high altitude, cold winter diurnal temperature variation, mineral-rich loess soil, and specific cultivation tradition (planting in spring after mountain snow melt; harvesting after three to four years) produces roots with the highest Z-ligustilide and ferulic acid content. Modern HPLC analysis confirms the quality gradient correlates with geographic origin — terroir is pharmacologically real for this herb.

**Japanese Kampo integration:**
In Japanese Kampo practice (which derived from Tang Dynasty Chinese medicine and evolved independently thereafter), Dong Quai (当帰, *Tōki*) appears in approximately 70 of the 148 most commonly prescribed Kampo formulas. It is particularly central to gynecological formulas (*Toki-shakuyaku-san*, *Keishi-bukuryo-gan*, *Tokito-Shimotsuto-Tou*) prescribed by Japanese physicians for dysmenorrhea, menstrual irregularity, and menopausal complaints in the mainstream Japanese healthcare system. This represents one of the most substantial examples of traditional herbal pharmacology operating within a modern regulated medical system.

## 8. Practical Application

**Clinical prescribing principles (TCM):**
- Standard dose: 6–15g dried root in decoction daily; pharmaceutical extracts dosed equivalently
- **Root section differentiation:** For blood deficiency (pale complexion, fatigue, dizziness, irregular menstruation with light flow): use root head/body (全/身); for blood stasis (fixed pain, dark menstruation with clots, varicose veins): emphasize root tails; for balanced action: whole root
- **Pattern requirements:** Blood deficiency (血虚) or blood stasis (血瘀) confirmed; not indicated for yang deficiency, spleen dampness, or diarrhea (moistening nature can worsen loose stools)
- Formula use: Almost invariably in combination; Si Wu Tang as base formula for gynecological applications

**Critical contraindications:**
- **Pregnancy:** ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION at high or prolonged doses — Z-ligustilide oxytocic effect at high dose can stimulate uterine contractions; risk of preterm labor or miscarriage. Minimal culinary exposure (trace amounts in Chinese cooking) probably not risk; medicinal dose CONTRAINDICATED.
- **Active bleeding/hemorrhage:** Anti-platelet effect; avoid in acute heavy menstrual bleeding (menorrhagia), hemoptysis, GI bleeding. Paradoxically, low-dose use in mild menstrual irregularity may be appropriate — clinical judgment required.
- **Hormone-sensitive cancers:** Weak estrogenic activity → caution in ER+ breast cancer, endometrial cancer, ovarian cancer. Not absolute contraindication (phytoestrogenic activity very weak) but oncology team consultation required.
- **Diarrhea/loose stools:** Moistening nature worsens; add astringent herbs or discontinue

**Drug interactions (significant):**
- **Warfarin:** Well-documented interaction — Page 1999 case report (INR elevation); multiple subsequent pharmacokinetic studies; Dong Quai + warfarin → INR increase → bleeding risk. **CLINICALLY IMPORTANT.** Avoid concurrent use or monitor INR very closely.
- **Antiplatelet drugs:** Additive (ferulic acid + Z-ligustilide anti-platelet + aspirin/clopidogrel); monitor bleeding
- **Tamoxifen and aromatase inhibitors:** Theoretical phytoestrogenic interaction — weak but real; discuss with oncologist
- **CYP3A4 substrates:** Furanocoumarin content (minor) may inhibit CYP3A4; interaction potential similar to but less than grapefruit at food-level doses

**Preparation:**
- Standard decoction: 6–15g whole root in 300–500ml water, simmer 30 minutes covered (preserve volatile phthalides); strain and drink 2× daily
- The distinctive sweet-anise-musty aroma of well-prepared Dong Quai decoction is a quality marker; insipid preparations have lost volatile fraction
- Granule extracts: standardized to ferulic acid and/or Z-ligustilide content; convenient; less fragrant
- Wine preparation (当归酒, *Dānggūi jiǔ*): historical preparation; alcohol extraction emphasizes volatile phthalides; dose carefully — alcohol concentrates both pharmacological effects and teratogenic risk in pregnancy
- **Avoid essential oil isolates** — concentration of Z-ligustilide increases uterotonic risk and is not how traditional use operated

**Energetic classification:**
- Tier: **◈ Jing (Blood/Xue)** — one of the deepest blood-nourishing herbs in the East Asian pharmacopoeia; operates at the Blood (Xue) level which in Codex notation parallels the Jing/constitutional tier; unlike superficially similar warming herbs, Dong Quai replenishes the constitutional blood substrate rather than merely moving or warming surface Qi
- Thermal nature: warm (温); flavor: sweet-pungent (甘辛); organ meridians: Liver (藏血), Heart (主血脈), Spleen (生血)
- Classical characterization: 补血活血，调经止痛，润肠通便 — nourishes and moves blood, regulates menstruation and stops pain, moistens intestines
