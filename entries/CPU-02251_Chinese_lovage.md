# Chinese lovage — Gao Ben 藁本 (Shennong bencao jing); aliases Gui Qing 鬼卿 and Di Xin 地新

## *Ligusticum sinense Oliv., Chinese lovage, an umbellifer of mountain slopes whose rhizome is the drug (a close relative of the chuanxiong plant); the identification is ours, from the Chinese name and the standard modern identification, and is of medium confidence at species level, since the classic describes no plant, a second species (L. jeholense) is also used under the name, and Yang 1998 prints "Radix Et Rhizoma Ligustici Chinensis"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts on disk, under this Chinese name.** This is a single-text entry. The principal text is Sun Xingyan's 1799 reconstruction, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt`; the other edition on disk, Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`), is read as a second witness and attributed to Yang. The e-text of Sun's edition has lost the first glyph of the name: the heading reads only 本, and the drug is 藁本, as the job and Yang's pinyin (*Gao Ben*) show. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder, "Radix Et Rhizoma Ligustici Chinensis [SBJ]", taken from Yang's English; this entry replaces it. A search of the other texts on disk found only other *Ligusticum* plants: Dioscorides' *ligusticum* (lovage, I_153, *Levisticum officinale*, tags AT, DMM, CM) and the chuanxiong plant (I_067, *Ligusticum chuanxiong*, tags CM, SBJ, SS). Neither is this drug and neither cites this paragraph.

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** The entry is 藁本 in the middle classic (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, middle classic, entry 藁本 *gao ben*, `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 2147, 味 paragraph; name line 2145). The Shennong paragraph reads: 味辛，温 ("acrid, warm"); 主妇人疝瘕 ("governs mounting-conglomeration in women"), 阴中寒、肿痛 ("cold in the genital region, swelling and pain"), 腹中急 ("urgency in the abdomen"), 除风头痛 ("removes wind headache"), 长肌肤 ("grows the flesh and skin"), 悦颜色 ("makes the complexion pleasing"); the aliases 一名鬼卿 ("Ghost Minister") and 一名地新 ("New Earth"); and 生山谷 ("grows in mountains and valleys"). There is no 久服 clause.

No Wu Pu layer is printed for this entry. The Mingyi bielu layer (《名医》曰, line 2149) adds the alias 微茎 ("slight stem") and the habitat 生崇山 ("grows on Mount Chong"), and the harvest 正月、二月采根，曝干，三十日成 ("the root is gathered in the first and second months and dried in the sun; it is done in thirty days").

Sun's note (案, line 2151) is a string of philological quotations, with the name's first glyph lost in each. It cites the *Guangya*: "*shan zhi* (山芷), *wei xiang* (蔚香), [*gao*] *ben*"; the *Guanzi*, chapter "Diyuan", which names [*gao*] *ben* in a passage on the five scents (五臭畴生 [glyph]本); the *Xunzi*, chapter "Dalüe": "*lan* and *zhi* and [*gao*] *ben*, steeped (渐) in honey-wine, one belt-ornament (佩) exchanges for it" (兰芷[glyph]本，渐于蜜醴，一佩易之); and Fan Guang's commentary to the *Erya*: "[*gao*] *ben* is also called *miwu* (蘼芜) and its root *jin zhi* (靳芷)". Sun ends 旧作 [glyph], 非: "the old text wrote [a different glyph]; that is wrong." The quotations show a fragrant plant, grouped with *lan* and *zhi*, and a name shared with *miwu* (an upper-class entry named in the contents list at line 43, which this entry does not cover). They are Sun's citations and are not Shennong text.

**Yang 1998 as second witness.** Yang prints (line 2593): "Gao Ben (Radix Et Rhizoma Ligustici Chinensis) is acrid and slightly warm. It mainly treats mounting conglomeration and genital cold in females, swelling and pain, and abdominal urgency. It eliminates wind headache, promotes the growth of muscles and skin, and renders the facial complexion attractive. Its other name is Gui Qing (Ghost Minister). Another name is Di Xin (New Earth). It grows in mountains and valleys." This agrees with Sun's paragraph, except that Yang prints "slightly warm" (微温) where Sun's text has only "warm". Yang's footnote 153 (line 2603) is modern commentary: "The main action of Ligusticum Chinensis is to eliminate wind, cold, and dampness."

**Appendix.** The drug-relations table (line 4365) has a damaged line, 本 畏 茹 ("[*gao*] *ben*: fears [glyph lost] *ru*"), in which the name of the feared drug is lost in this e-text. The Codex draws no conclusion from it.

### Active Components

In general terms, the rhizomes of *Ligusticum* species (Apiaceae) are aromatic and carry volatile oils and phthalide compounds, the family's characteristic scented constituents. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The classic records an acrid, warm drug for women's mounting-conglomeration and genital cold with swelling and pain, for abdominal urgency, for wind headache, and for growing the flesh and skin and pleasing the complexion. These are the classic's categories and the Codex does not translate them into modern diagnoses. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. The skin-and-complexion clause is a cosmetic benefit in the classic's terms and is not a life-nourishing promise.

### Preparation & Dosing

The Shennong text gives no preparation and no dose. The Mingyi layer gives only the harvest: the root, gathered in the first and second months and dried in the sun for thirty days (line 2149). The preface (line 4121) says that drugs differ in suitability for pills, powders, decoction, wine or paste, with no rule for this one. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. *Ligusticum* is a genus of the carrot family, several of whose rhizomes are aromatic drugs in East Asian medicine, and the family as a whole is known for scented volatile oils. Whether the rhizome relieves any of the conditions named in the classic has not been tested in sources retrieved for this entry. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(b) Framed.* The entry stands in the middle classic, whose preface (line 1677) reads 中药一百二十种为臣，主养性以应人。无毒、有毒，斟酌其宜。欲遏病补羸者，本中经: the middle drugs "nourish the nature, correspond to man; non-toxic or toxic, weigh what is fitting; for those who wish to check disease and supplement the lean". The paragraph has no 久服 clause and no promise of lightening the body or of not ageing. Its last two clauses, growing the flesh and skin and pleasing the complexion, are benefits of the body's surface and are not a status for the person who takes it long. The register is therefore (b): framed by the middle class, with the cultivation status unattested.

**Practitioner's note.** A middle-class, acrid, warm root for women's cold and pain, abdominal urgency and wind headache, with a clause on skin and complexion. No part beyond "root", and no preparation, dose or course, is given. A cultivator has no practice to take from this entry.

### Contraindications

- **Identity.** Several umbellifers carry the Chinese name; do not use any plant on the strength of this entry. Do not confuse the *Ligusticum* roots with water hemlock and other poisonous umbellifers, which resemble them. [USER: verify]
- **Pregnancy, nursing, children.** No safety information was read, and the classic's indications are for women's complaints. [USER: verify]
- **Women's complaints and headache.** The uses named are historical record only; pelvic pain, swelling and persistent headache need medical care.
- **Quantities.** No dose or duration is given.
