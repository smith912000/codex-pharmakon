## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary name:** Scotch broom, Common broom
**Synonyms:** *Sarothamnus scoparius* Wimm. (former classification), Genista (misapplied common name), *Scoparius*
**Binomial:** *Cytisus scoparius* (L.) Link
**Authority:** Johann Heinrich Friedrich Link 1822 (reclassified from Linnaeus's *Spartium scoparium*)
**Related pharmacological species:** *Cytisus laburnum* (laburnum/golden chain — toxic; cytisine source), *Cytisus nigricans* (black broom)

**Botanical description:** Erect multi-stemmed deciduous shrub 1–3 m. Stems green, strongly ridged (photosynthetically active; leaves reduced and often absent in summer), with 5 ridges per stem cross-section (diagnostic). Leaves usually simple (on young growth, trifoliate). Flowers bright golden-yellow, large papilionaceous (15–25 mm), explosive-triggered pollination (bees weight-triggered: explosive anther release deposits pollen on bee). Pod black, hairy at margins, explosive dehiscence in hot weather (audible popping sounds on warm summer days). Characteristic spring yellow-gold color transforms Atlantic European upland landscapes.

**Pharmacognostic parts used:** Aerial parts (young shoots, tops, flowers) — *Scoparii cacumina*, *Herba scoparii* (historical pharmacopoeial designations)
**Active alkaloid harvesting timing:** Alkaloid content highest at full bloom; decreases significantly post-flowering.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

Broom's pharmacological significance lies primarily in its **high sparteine content** — making it the most historically important botanical source of this alkaloid (higher and more consistent concentration than gorse). Medieval and Renaissance European medicine used broom extensively as a diuretic, cardiac tonic, and emmenagogue. The 19th century cardiac pharmacology of sparteine (antiarrhythmic; see I-434) was primarily investigated using broom-derived sparteine, not gorse.

**Royal botanical symbolism:**
The Plantagenet dynasty (England, 1154–1485) took its name from *Planta genista* — a Latinization of broom (*genista* = broom plant; the heraldic plant of the Plantagenets, particularly Henry II). Eleanor of Aquitaine reportedly carried broom as a symbol. This elevated broom from folk medicine to royal heraldry — documenting its cultural prominence in medieval Atlantic Europe.

**The sweeping function:**
Broom's common name reflects its primary everyday utility — branches bundled to make brooms/besoms for floor sweeping. This dual household/medicinal use parallels fennel (culinary/medicinal), dandelion (food/medicine), and other multi-purpose plants where domestic familiarity drives medicinal exploration.

**Sparteine content variation:** Geographical and seasonal variation significant — Alpine specimens lower, Atlantic maritime specimens higher. Alkaloid content 2× higher in spring/early summer than autumn.

**Scoparin — diuretic flavone:**
Scoparin (vitexin 4'-glucoside) is the primary non-alkaloid medicinal constituent. Inhibits tubular sodium reabsorption in kidney → diuresis. Mechanism independent of sparteine — provides the traditional "diuretic" activity while sparteine provides cardiac/oxytocic effects. Clinical use of broom as a diuretic likely reflects scoparin's activity.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**1. Sparteine — see I-434 Section 5 for full mechanism**
Sodium channel blockade (antiarrhythmic); uterine muscle stimulation (oxytocic); CYP2D6 substrate.
Broom contains higher sparteine than gorse (0.3–1.5% vs. 0.08–0.15%) — more pharmacologically potent source.

**2. Scoparin — renal diuretic mechanism**
Scoparin (and related C-glycosides): inhibits Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase in renal tubular cells → reduced sodium reabsorption → osmotic diuresis + natriuresis. Mechanism similar to cardiac glycoside effect on kidney (without cardiac effect). Confirms traditional use as diuretic for edema and urinary tract support.

**3. Tyramine + hydroxytyramine — vasopressor activity**
Biogenic amines present (tyramine, hydroxytyramine, dopamine traces): sympathomimetic → vasoconstriction, elevated blood pressure. These amines are normally metabolized by MAO-A/B. In patients taking MAOI antidepressants: **DANGEROUS tyramine potentiation → hypertensive crisis** (same mechanism as dietary tyramine interaction with MAOIs). Broom is specifically contraindicated with MAOIs.

**4. Lupanine — secondary alkaloid effects**
Lupanine has weaker antiarrhythmic activity than sparteine, similar selectivity. Inhibits nicotinic acetylcholine receptors. Contributes to overall alkaloid profile of broom preparations.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

### Cardiac (Historical)

**Level C (traditional):**
Flückiger & Hanbury (Pharmacographia, 1874): Extensive documentation of sparteine sulfate (isolated from broom) use in cardiac cases — atrial flutter, palpitations, heart failure edema. Pharmacological basis established by 19th century pharmacologists including Schmiedeberg.

**Level B — Sparteine sulfate (pharmaceutical):**
19th-20th century European hospital records document IV sparteine sulfate for obstetric labor induction and cardiac arrhythmia management. No modern RCTs for broom preparations specifically.

### Diuretic

**Level C (traditional):**
Scoparin diuretic activity confirmed in animal studies (rat kidney perfusion, 1970s German pharmacological research). No human RCTs specifically for broom diuretic effect.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Plantagenet heritage:**
The naming of one of England's most significant royal dynasties after this plant (Planta genista = "planted broom") represents the highest level of botanical cultural symbolism. Geoffrey of Anjou reportedly wore broom as his badge; his son Henry II and all subsequent Plantagenet kings maintained this emblem. Broom thus appears in heraldry, manuscript illuminations, and royal iconography across 300 years of English medieval history — an unusual elevation of a medicinal/agricultural plant to dynastic symbol.

**The audible medicine:**
Broom seed pod dehiscence in hot weather produces audible cracking — pods literally explode to scatter seeds. This dramatic botanical behavior, combined with the brilliant golden flowers and complete stem photosynthesis, made broom a constantly noticed plant. In traditional medicine, conspicuous plants (bright color, strong smell, unusual characteristic) attract medicinal attention. Broom's explosive pods, royal heraldry, and landscape dominance ensured sustained human engagement.

**Obstetric history:**
Sparteine from broom (as sparteine sulfate injection) was a standard obstetric drug in British and European hospitals 1900s–1950s for labor augmentation. "Sparteine injection" appears in British obstetric texts. Replaced by synthetic oxytocin (pitocin, 1950s) which is more predictable and controllable. Broom's contribution to obstetric pharmacology — via sparteine — represents a genuine botanical drug that was incorporated into mainstream medical practice before being superseded.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Contemporary status:** Broom is NOT widely recommended for self-treatment due to sparteine toxicity risk. Primary contemporary interest:
1. Historical pharmacological documentation
2. Scoparin's diuretic mechanism as research reference
3. Sparteine as CYP2D6 pharmacogenomics tool

**Traditional preparation (historical reference only):**
Young tops infusion: 1–2 g in 150 mL water; daily dose in historical practice.
NOT recommended for self-treatment due to sparteine content.

**Absolute contraindications (serious):**
- **Pregnancy:** Sparteine oxytocic → risk of miscarriage or premature labor
- **MAO inhibitors:** Tyramine content → hypertensive crisis risk (potentially life-threatening)
- **Cardiac disease:** Antiarrhythmic activity requires medical management; sparteine can cause arrhythmia at excess doses
- **Hypertension:** Tyramine + hydroxytyramine vasopressor activity

**MAOI interaction — CRITICAL:**
Broom must be **completely avoided** by patients on:
- Phenelzine, tranylcypromine, selegiline, rasagiline (antidepressant/anti-Parkinson MAOIs)
- Linezolid (antibiotic with MAOI activity)
- Moclobemide (reversible MAOI antidepressant)
The tyramine in broom + MAOI = potentially fatal hypertensive crisis. This is one of the most significant herb-drug interactions in the Fabaceae chapter.
