# Fox lung — Pneumon Alopekos (Lungs of Fox) / wa (fox), the lungs of the fox and of the khug.rta bird
## *The lungs of the fox, dried; the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary gives the fox as* Vulpes vulpes | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM, FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-41, *Pneumon Alopekos*, "Lungs of Fox" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 5106). The whole chapter: "Lungs of a fox (dried and taken in a drink) help the asthmatic; and the grease of the same melted and poured in the ears lessens earache." It is the first of the run of animal-organ chapters in book 2 (2-42 ass's liver, 2-43 testes of deer, 2-44 ass's hooves, entry III_19, 2-47 goat's liver, entry III_21, 2-48 boar's liver). The "grease" in the second sentence is the fox's fat or grease, not the lung; the translation says "the same", and the Greek may mean the fox generally. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** In Clark's translation of the Explanatory Tantra ch.20, in the paragraph of "Meat" (the third of the thirteen animal medicines), after the testicles of ram and dog: "Lungs of fox (wa) and of khug.rta: Cuculus melanoleucus: Riparia riparia: Hirundo rustica: (a type of swallow): heal punctured lungs." (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 1170). The line before, in the same paragraph, states a general rule: "The lungs, heart, liver, spleen, kidneys and flesh (of various animals) cure diseases of those respective organs." Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 gives: "The lungs of foxes and red-rumped swallows stop haemoptysis" (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 3536), and in the offal sentence before it "Offal meat like lungs, heart, liver, spleen and kidneys cures disorders of the respective organs" (lines 3534-3535). The two translations differ on the result ("heal punctured lungs" in Clark, "stop haemoptysis", coughing of blood, in Men-Tsee-Khang) and on the bird (Clark gives three Latin names for the *khug.rta* with "a type of swallow", Men-Tsee-Khang "red-rumped swallows"). The glossary on animals in the Men-Tsee-Khang text prints the fox as *Vulpes vulpes* (lines 7074-7076). "Punctured lung" is the name of a disorder in Clark's text: for instance, cow's milk is "beneficial for punctured lung and consumption" (Clark line 708). The spine's rGyud-bzhi locus is the Clark line 1170 given above.

### Active Components

Lung is a soft, spongy organ of connective tissue, blood vessels and alveolar tissue. In general it contains protein (including collagen and elastin), fat and, as an organ meat, vitamins and iron in smaller amounts than liver. The fox's fat or grease, in the Greek second sentence, is a lipid. The texts name no active component. No quantities are given here because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The two traditions record the lung of a fox as a drug for the lungs, in the Greek text for asthma and in the Tibetan text for punctured lungs or coughing blood. The Tibetan rule that an animal's organ treats the matching organ in the patient is stated in the text itself. The Codex has no protocol for it and none is offered. Asthma and coughing blood need medical care; foxes carry zoonotic diseases (see Contraindications); and no dose is given for the Tibetan use.

### Preparation & Dosing

Dioscorides: the lungs are "dried and taken in a drink"; the grease is "melted and poured in the ears" (2-41). No quantity is given. The Tibetan text names the organ and the action and no method in the lines read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Wild foxes can carry rabies, the fox tapeworm *Echinococcus multilocularis* (a cause of alveolar echinococcosis in people who handle foxes or contaminated vegetation) and other zoonoses. No effect of fox lung on asthma or on lung injury is established. The Tibetan idea that an organ treats the same organ is a doctrine of the text and not a finding of pharmacology. No clinical studies of the traditional uses were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** None is printed for the fox lung in 2-41; the chapter gives actions only (asthma, earache).

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The translated lines print an action only ("heal punctured lungs", Clark; "stop haemoptysis", Men-Tsee-Khang), with no taste, potency or humour assignment, plus the organ-to-organ rule quoted above.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied. The chapter is two sentences. Its discipline of attention is in the form: dried, drunk, for the asthmatic; the grease separately, in the ear. It assigns no status beyond the therapeutic.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The fox lung stands in the Explanatory Tantra's list of meats among the organs that "cure diseases of those respective organs", with the swallow's lung. This is a rule of correspondence between the organ eaten and the organ treated, and it is stated in the text; it is a therapeutic rule and not a claim about the vital substance of the person. The lines read name no essence-extraction (*bcud len*) use, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition; none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do. A cultivator might note the Tibetan rule of organ to organ as an example of how a tradition reasons about animal medicines, and then set it aside, since no source read ties it to cultivation and the animal carries disease.

### Contraindications

- **Zoonoses.** Wild foxes carry rabies and the fox tapeworm (*Echinococcus multilocularis*) among other pathogens; handling or eating fox tissue is unsafe. [USER: verify specifics]
- **Asthma and haemoptysis.** Both need medical care; do not replace treatment with an animal organ.
- **Ear.** Pouring animal grease into the ear risks infection, and is unsafe with a perforated eardrum.
- **Dose unknown.** No dose is given in the sources read.
- **Law and ethics.** Fox hunting and trapping are regulated or banned in many places; using the lung means killing the animal.
