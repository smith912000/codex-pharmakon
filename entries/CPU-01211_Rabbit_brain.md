# Rabbit brain — rGyud-bzhi, "Rabbit brain" (brain, one of the thirteen animal-derived medicines)
## *Brain of the rabbit; the translator prints only "Rabbit brain", with no Tibetan name and no binomial in the line read* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** Rabbit brain is listed in the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, the chapter on the efficacy of medicinal substances, in Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). It falls in section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), whose thirteen headings are "horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). Under "Brain" (heading line 1181) the whole list, in line 1182, reads: "Goat brain: unites severed ligaments and tendons. Sheep brain: cures dizziness and vertigo. Brain of game animals (e.g. deer): halts diarrhoea. Rabbit brain: cures colic. Human brain: reduces swellings and cures lymph disorders." Rabbit brain stands fourth of five and carries no bracketed comment, no name count and no Tibetan name. Clark says in his introduction (line 80) that he has "interpolated liberally from the Blue Aquamarine commentary"; the bracketed passages elsewhere in the chapter look like such interpolations, but the lines read do not state the bracket convention outright. The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3548) has the same list with a different complaint for the rabbit: "Goat brain heals tom [torn] ligaments and tendons. Sheep brain cures dizziness and reeling. The brain of herbivorous wild animals stops diarrhea. Rabbit brain cures dysentery." Clark therefore reads "colic" where Men-Tsee-Khang reads "dysentery". The Tibetan wording is not in the files read, so which rendering is closer cannot be said. [USER: verify] (The file prints "tom", an OCR error for "torn".)

The rabbit is also named in the Tantra's meat classification, among the "Game animals" (Clark, line 672: "deer, musk deer, Procapra pictaudata, Ovis ammon, rabbits and saiga tatarica"; Men-Tsee-Khang, line 2907: "The deer, musk deer, gazelle, argali, rabbit and antelope are the group of herbivorous wild animals"). Clark's discussion of meats says "Rabbit meat is coarse, increases digestive heat and stops diarrhoea" (line 679). That sentence concerns the flesh and is not applied to the brain. The Men-Tsee-Khang "Glossary on Animals" (running heads at lines 6808-7114) prints "Rabbit" with a binomial that the OCR has garbled ("Lepus iostclus Hodgson", lines 7104-7108); it may stand for a Hodgson hare species, which the lines read do not settle. ### Active Components

Brain is soft nervous tissue. It is high in water and in lipids, including cholesterol and phospholipids (the lipids of cell membranes and myelin), with proteins and smaller amounts of vitamins and minerals. No composition specific to this entry was read, and no active principle is claimed by the sources. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is one indication in a materia medica list: rabbit brain "cures colic" (Clark) or "cures dysentery" (Men-Tsee-Khang). The Codex offers no protocol. Colic and dysentery can both be signs of serious illness and call for assessment and treatment by a clinician; eating raw animal brain carries real risk (see Contraindications). The Tantra gives no preparation, dose or route in these lines. The list pairs the brain of different animals with different complaints; that pairing is the tradition's own organising principle and is recorded as such, with no reason given in the lines read. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Colic (spasmodic abdominal pain) and dysentery (bloody, infective diarrhoea) have different causes and are managed differently; dysentery in particular needs fluids and sometimes antimicrobial treatment. Eating the brain of an animal does not treat either. Wild rabbits and hares can carry tularemia (*Francisella tularensis*), which people catch through handling and eating infected animals, and raw or undercooked game carries other bacterial and parasitic risks. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* Neither translation read assigns rabbit brain any cultivation framing. It is a one-line indication in a list of animal brains, and the complaint is a bowel one. The frame around it is the tradition's own: the project's cultivation spec records that the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) are derived from the three poisons and that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root. That point comes from the spec and not from any line about rabbit brain. No source read attaches rabbit brain to *bcud len* (essence extraction), and there is no formal Tibetan category of substances for subtle-body practice. Clark's remark (line 57) that "only very few animal products are used" nowadays is a note on usage, not on status.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a single indication in a list of animal brains, the two translations disagree about what the indication is, and eating raw brain carries risk with no supported benefit. The honest use of the entry is as a record of what the Tantra says.

### Contraindications

- **Infection:** raw or undercooked game and offal can carry bacteria and parasites, including tularemia from wild rabbits and hares; do not handle sick wild animals.
- **Not a treatment for colic or dysentery:** abdominal pain, bloody diarrhoea, fever or dehydration need clinical assessment; do not rely on this line.
- **Neural tissue:** brain and spinal tissue from some mammals can carry prion disease, and many countries restrict human consumption of such tissue; whether rabbits carry it is not addressed in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Legal and animal-welfare status:** obtaining brain requires slaughter, and hunting wild rabbits and hares is regulated in many places. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** killing an animal for a substance with no supported benefit is hard to justify, and Clark's omens section (line 431) values saving animals from slaughter; that passage is about merit and omens, not about medicine.
