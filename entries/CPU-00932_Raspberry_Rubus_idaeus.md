### Origin & Tradition

The red raspberry, *Rubus idaeus*, is a thorny, suckering perennial cane of the rose family (Rosaceae), native across Europe and temperate Asia and naturalised widely. The plant carries two quite different materials, and Kenetica keeps them strictly separate:

- the **fruit** — a sweet aggregate drupe, treated as food and dietary antioxidant, and
- the **leaf** — the *medicinal* part and the whole subject of this entry.

The leaf is the classic **"partus preparator"** — the herb of the birth-room. Across the European folk tradition and modern midwifery it is drunk as **raspberry leaf tea** in later pregnancy in the belief that it "tones the uterus," eases and shortens labour, and prepares the body for birth. This is the spine of the entry, and the tradition itself is real and well-attested in practice: according to PubMed, more than a third of surveyed pregnant women in Australian cohorts report using raspberry leaf, most often specifically "to make labour easier," and describe it in the language of *ritual, comfort, necessity and self-determination* (Bowman et al., 2026, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-025-05229-7); Farnaghi & Braniff, 2022, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajo.13496)).

The classical tradition tags need honest handling:

- **DMM** (the Dioscoridean/Greek herbal stream, *De Materia Medica*) and **CM** (Avicenna's *Canon of Medicine*, Unani) both know *Rubus* — brambles and their astringent leaves were used for wounds, fluxes and sore mouths, the standard uses of a tannin-rich Rosaceae leaf. That general astringent employment is credible. But the *specific* "prepares/eases labour" doctrine that dominates the modern use is **not clearly the classical claim**; the partus-preparator role is most strongly a later Western/midwifery tradition. So: astringent Rosaceae-leaf use in DMM/CM is reasonable; the labour-preparation doctrine as a *classical* attestation is **[USER: verify]**.

### Active Components

For raspberry *leaf* the credible constituent classes are:

- **Tannins** (hydrolysable and condensed) — the dominant, astringent principle; the basis of the leaf's traditional use for fluxes and sore throats and the source of its mouth-puckering character.
- **Flavonoids** and other **polyphenols** — antioxidant constituents shared broadly with the Rosaceae.
- **"Fragarine"** — a constituent named in the older herbal/pharmacognosy literature and traditionally credited with the uterine effect. It is named here as the *traditionally cited* active principle; a modern, isolated, mechanism-defining assay for it was not retrieved this run, so it should not be presented as pharmacologically nailed-down. **[USER: verify]** the modern chemistry of fragarine before making any mechanistic claim.

Constituent detail beyond these classes is not asserted, to avoid over-claiming a leaf whose "active" for the signature use remains genuinely uncertain in the literature.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

In Kenetica the raspberry **leaf** is the **Foundation-phase emblem of the astringent, gathering, "toning" gesture** — the herb that draws tissue together and prepares a body for an ordeal (birth). Its signature is *preparation*: not a stimulant that forces an event, but a conditioning agent taken over weeks.

The honest Kenetica reading, however, deflates the certainty:
- The **tradition is strong; the proof is weak.** The leaf's cultural role as birth-preparer is real and meaningful. Its measurable efficacy is *not* robustly established.
- Kenetica therefore files it as a **signature study of the astringent/preparatory gesture**, and explicitly does **not** issue it as a reliable clinical labour-shortening agent.

**This entry gives no pregnancy dosing or timing advice.** First-trimester use is *traditionally avoided*, and any decision about whether, when and how much to take in pregnancy belongs to a **midwife or obstetrician**, not to this book.

### Scientific Correlation

Claims graded by evidence:

- **Widely used in pregnancy to ease/shorten labour (as a practice)** — **Established (as a behaviour/tradition).** Documented by surveys and cohorts.
- **Actually shortens labour / eases birth (efficacy)** — **NOT robustly proven / weak.** The best synthesis (systematic integrative review) concludes the evidence base is weak; one human study showed a clinically meaningful but **non-statistically-significant** reduction in second-stage length and augmentation (Bowman et al., 2021, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-021-03230-4)). A prospective observational study found associations with less augmentation/instrumental birth but explicitly warns the results "cannot be relied on or generalised" (Bowman et al., 2024, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-024-04465-7)).
- **In-vitro action on smooth muscle** — **Preclinical & CONFLICTING.** Both directions are reported: a methanolic leaf extract produced dose-dependent **relaxant** activity in guinea-pig ileum (Rojas-Vera et al., 2002, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.1040)), while the review literature notes biophysical effects on uterine/smooth muscle that can include **stimulation** — i.e. the "tone" may be either contraction or relaxation depending on preparation and tissue. The mechanism is not settled.
- **Safety** — **Reassuring but not established.** Human studies to date show no clear harm, but authors stress this is *not* proof of safety; animal studies showed **toxicity at high intravenous/intraperitoneal doses** (Bowman et al., 2021, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-021-03230-4); Holst et al., 2009, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctcp.2009.05.003)).
- **Effect on cervical ripening** — **Uncertain / possibly negative.** A narrative review flagged a *possible negative* impact on cervical ripening alongside a weak labour-induction signal (Socha et al., 2023, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu15143206)).

### Traditional Energetic Classification

In the Western herbal tradition the leaf reads as **cool, dry and astringent** — the temperament of a tannin-bearing Rosaceae leaf that tightens and gathers relaxed tissue. A leaf-specific temperament assigned to a named classical authority (Dioscorides/Avicenna) for the *labour-preparation* use is **[USER: verify]**; the general astringent character is well supported by the plant's chemistry.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy is a clinician-supervised decision.** Do not self-prescribe timing or dose. First-trimester use is traditionally avoided. Because in-vitro data show the leaf can affect uterine/smooth-muscle activity in *either* direction, and a possible negative effect on cervical ripening has been raised, use in pregnancy must be discussed with a midwife/obstetrician.
- **Tannin astringency:** may cause nausea/GI upset; strong or excessive infusions can irritate the stomach.
- **Interaction caution:** tannins can bind and reduce absorption of iron and some minerals and drugs if taken at the same time — separate dosing; **[USER: verify]** specific interactions before combining with medication.
- **Do not conflate leaf with fruit.** The medicinal claims and cautions here apply to the *leaf*, not the food fruit.
- **High parenteral doses were toxic in animals** — an argument against concentrated/non-oral preparations.
