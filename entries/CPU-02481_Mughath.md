# Mughath — Egyptian ginseng (مغاث)

## *Glossostemon bruguieri Desf., the root sold in Egypt as moghat; the identification is the modern one, and the Canon's own first line gives a different identification, so it is a doubt* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** CM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Avicenna's *Canon* among the texts on disk.** Book II, entry *mughāth*, Arabic text lines 15083 to 15089.

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** What it is: "some said: it is the root of the wild pomegranate (*ʿirq al-rummān al-barrī*); and that does not agree with what is mentioned, that its seed suits venery (*al-bāh*) and stirs it strongly" (قال بعضهم إنه عرق الرمان البري وليس يوافق هذا ما يذكر من أن بزره يوافق الباه ويحركها بقوة). So Avicenna reports one identification and says it does not fit the sexual action reported for the seed. Temperament: "hot to the second, moist in the third" (حار إلى الثانية رطب في الثالثة). Property: "it is a strengthener of the organs" (مقو للأعضاء). Cosmetic: "it is fattening" (مسمن). Joints and nerves: "it is beneficial as a poultice for sprain (*wathy*), fracture and weakness of the muscle, and benefits gout (*niqris*) and spasm (*tashannuj*), and it is good for *al-dashbadh* and hardness of the joints." The word *dashbadh* is not explained in the lines read. [USER: verify.] Chest: "a softener of hardnesses of the throat and lung." Excretory organs: "it stirs venery, especially its seed" (يحرك الباه خصوصا بزره). No dose is given in the entry. The entry quotes no other author.
Other uses in the Canon: *mughāth* is named in a list of poultices for hard swellings (line 33151, a list read in its few lines only); it is among the "wonderful poultices" in a passage whose neighbouring lines mention *khanāzīr* (scrofula) (line 51149); and its seed, cleaned, is counted "thirty grains" in one compound recipe (line 41491). The chapters those lines belong to were not studied.

**Identity doubt.** The spine's *Glossostemon bruguieri* (a Malvaceae root used in Egypt, called *mughāth*) agrees with the sexual and strengthening claims. It does not agree with Avicenna's first line, in which some say the drug is a pomegranate root. The Canon says it does not fit, and does not choose. The modern identification is therefore an inference. [USER: verify.]

### Active Components

The Canon gives none. As a general fact the root of *Glossostemon bruguieri* is mucilaginous and starchy, and is sold in Egypt as a hot milk drink. [USER: verify.]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The text records a warming, moistening root with a strengthening and fattening reputation, a poultice for sprains and joint stiffness, and a seed that stirs sexual desire. No protocol is offered, and nothing here supports a modern supplement claim.

### Preparation & Dosing

Poultice (joints). The seed "especially" for venery. In a recipe the Canon counts thirty cleaned seeds (line 41491); the rest of that recipe was not studied and it is not a dose of the drug on its own. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify.]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

Avicenna: hot to the second degree, moist in the third (lines 15085 to 15086).

### Cultivation Register

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** The entry says the drug "strengthens the organs" and is "fattening" and stirs venery, in Avicenna's own terms. The Canon's frame of innate heat and radical moisture, as a finite endowment conserved rather than gained, is the honest frame for a warm, moist drug of this kind; but no line read applies it to *mughāth*, and the word for a heart-gladdening drug, *mufarriḥ*, belongs to a different book of Avicenna's and is not used here. **(b) Framed: a general strengthening claim is attested, but no cultivation status is.**

**Practitioner's note.** What a cultivator can take from the entry is a caution about names. The text itself doubts what the drug is, and the claim that excites readers, the sexual action, is attributed to the seed in the same sentence that doubts the identification. Nothing here is a practice.

### Contraindications

- Identity is uncertain in the source; do not assume the root sold today is the Canon's drug.
- Sexual-vigour claims are traditional and untested here. [USER: verify.]
- Hot, moist drugs in Avicenna's scheme are not stated by him to be harmful in this entry; no contraindication is given.
