### Botanical Identity
The oldest surviving seed plant species on Earth — the Ginkgoaceae predates the dinosaurs (250 million years; Permian era). *Ginkgo biloba* is a "living fossil" — morphologically identical to Ginkgo species from 200-million-year-old fossil records. Deciduous tree, 20–35m; distinctive fan-shaped, bilobed leaves; dioecious (male and female trees); female trees produce malodorous yellow seeds (the "silver apricot"); male trees are preferred in urban planting. Survives pollution, pests, and fire (the six Hiroshima ginkgo trees at the hypocenter survived the atomic bomb, leafed out the following spring, and are still alive). Cultivated in Buddhist and Shinto temple grounds for millennia — many ancient trees exist around temples in China, Japan, and Korea.

**Drug material distinction**: The traditional drug is the **seed** (*yín xìng* — with specific preparation required, as raw seeds are toxic). The modern pharmaceutical preparation is the **leaf extract** (EGb 761 or similar standardised extract) — this is a modern development; the leaf was not the primary traditional drug. This distinction matters for understanding the traditional and modern evidence bases.

## Section 2 — Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 Shennong Bencao Jing / Classical Chinese (SBJ)

*Yín xìng* (seed) in classical texts:
> *"Sweet, bitter; neutral with toxic properties. Enters Lung, Kidney. Warms the Lung, calms asthma and wheezing, reduces phlegm, contracts essence, stops urinary incontinence and excessive urination. Used for cough with profuse phlegm, asthma, frequent urination, and genital discharge."*

Classical Chinese medicine used the **seed** primarily for respiratory and urinary conditions — not for cognitive function. The cognitive application of *ginkgo leaf* is largely a modern European development from the 1960s.

### 2.2 Four Medical Tantras (FMT)

Ginkgo appears in Tibetan medicine through Buddhist monastery plantings in China and the cultural diffusion of Chinese medical knowledge. The seed's *Lung* (respiratory) and *Kidney* (essence) applications are recognised within Tibetan frameworks.

### 2.3 Canon of Medicine (CM) — Peripheral

*Ginkgo* does not appear as a primary drug in Ibn Sīnā. Post-classical Islamic texts mention the Eastern tree with distinctive leaves. Included for structural completeness rather than as a primary CM drug.

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5.1 PAF (Platelet-Activating Factor) Antagonism — Primary Unique Mechanism

**Ginkgolides A, B, C**: Competitive antagonism of the PAF receptor on platelets and immune cells:
- Reduces platelet aggregation → anti-thrombotic
- Reduces neutrophil activation → anti-inflammatory
- Reduces mast cell degranulation → anti-allergic (asthma application)
- PAF is also a potent vasoconstrictor — ginkgolide antagonism → vasodilation → improved peripheral and cerebral blood flow

The PAF antagonism of ginkgolides is unique in plant pharmacology — no other plant contains PAF antagonists at this potency. The ginkgolide mechanism is the pharmaceutical rationale for ginkgo's use in cerebral blood flow and dementia research.

### 5.2 Neuroprotection (Bilobalide)

**Bilobalide**: 
- Inhibits glutamate NMDA receptor overactivation → reduces excitotoxic neuronal death
- Protects mitochondrial membrane potential → prevents cytochrome c release → anti-apoptotic
- Modulates GABA-A receptor (benzodiazepine-independent site) → mild anxiolytic + neuroprotective
- Reduces β-amyloid toxicity in neuronal cultures → potential Alzheimer's relevance

### 5.3 Antioxidant / Vasoprotective

**Ginkgoflavone glycosides**: Scavenge superoxide radicals, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radicals — the full reactive oxygen species spectrum. Particularly active in the vascular endothelium, brain, and retina (high-metabolic-rate tissues vulnerable to oxidative damage). The combination of PAF antagonism + antioxidant activity in vascular tissue is the basis for the peripheral vascular disease and macular degeneration applications.

### 5.4 Bronchodilation / Anti-Asthmatic (Seed + Leaf)

**Ginkgolides (PAF antagonism)**: PAF triggers bronchoconstriction and airway inflammation in asthma — ginkgolide antagonism → bronchodilation + reduced inflammatory cell recruitment. This connects the modern pharmacology back to the traditional seed application for "asthma and wheezing."

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Dementia / cognitive decline**: Large-scale trials have produced conflicting results. GuidAge trial (Vellas 2012, *Lancet Neurology*, n=2854): EGb 761 240mg/day did not significantly reduce conversion to Alzheimer's vs placebo over 5 years. However, sub-group analyses and earlier trials show significant benefits in patients with established dementia (not prevention). The evidence supports *treatment* of dementia more than *prevention*.

**Alzheimer's disease (established)**: Herrschaft (2012): EGb 761 240mg/day significantly improved cognition, neuropsychiatric symptoms (particularly agitation, anxiety), and functional performance vs placebo in Alzheimer's with neuropsychiatric symptoms — statistically and clinically meaningful.

**Peripheral arterial disease (claudication)**: Cochrane review (2013): EGb 761 significantly increased pain-free walking distance in intermittent claudication — moderate effect size; consistent with PAF antagonism mechanism.

**Tinnitus**: Mixed evidence; some trials show reduction in tinnitus-related distress but effect on tinnitus loudness itself is inconsistent. Cochrane review (2013): insufficient evidence for strong recommendation.

**Macular degeneration**: Fies (2002): Ginkgo extract significantly improved visual acuity in age-related macular degeneration — small trial; mechanism (antioxidant + vasoprotective in retina) pharmacologically plausible.

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The living fossil and the atomic bomb**: The ginkgo's designation as a "living fossil" (Darwin's term) is not hyperbole — it is a species with no surviving relatives, unchanged for 200 million years. The Ginkgoaceae lineage survived the mass extinctions that killed the dinosaurs. It survived the atomic bomb detonated at Hiroshima on August 6, 1945 — the six ginkgo trees near the hypocenter (within 1–2km, epicentre temperature ~3,000°C) were severely damaged but leafed out the following spring. They remain alive. The Japanese term *hibakujumoku* ("survivor tree") refers to all surviving trees, but the ginkgo survivors have become the primary symbol. The city of Hiroshima uses the ginkgo as its official symbol. A living fossil that survived the Permian extinction, grew in Buddhist temple courts for a thousand years, provided one of the most studied pharmaceutical extracts of the 20th century, and survived the first nuclear weapon used in war — is the same entity.

**The naming disjunction**: The grandfather-grandchild tree (*gōngsūn shù*) encoding in Chinese — the tree you plant for your descendants — reflects the plant's relationship with human civilisation. Ginkgo trees in Chinese temple grounds are routinely 500–1,000+ years old. The tree's pharmacology (neuroprotective, vasoprotective, longevity-associated) and its cultivation ecology (requires multi-generational patience, survives everything) encode the same principle: something worth cultivating across generations. The tree's modern application in dementia medicine is, from this perspective, structurally fitting — a plant associated with continuity across generations is used for the disease of memory loss.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

### 8.1 Preparation

| Form | Dose | Application |
|------|------|-------------|
| EGb 761 standardised extract (24% ginkgoflavone glycosides, 6% terpene trilactones) | 120–240mg daily (in 2–3 divided doses) | Dementia; circulatory; PAF antagonism |
| Other standardised leaf extracts (equivalent standardisation) | 120–240mg daily | Same applications |
| Traditional seed preparation | Under CM supervision only (toxic raw) | Respiratory; urinary (traditional only) |
| Ginkgo leaf tea | Not recommended | Ginkgolic acids not reliably removed; variable flavonoid/terpene content |

**EGb 761 note**: The pharmaceutical extract EGb 761 (Dr. Willmar Schwabe, Germany) has the most extensive clinical trial evidence base. The specific ratio (24%/6%) and ginkgolic acid removal (<5ppm) are quality markers that distinguish pharmaceutical from generic preparations. Studies done with EGb 761 do not automatically apply to all "ginkgo extract" preparations.

### 8.2 Energetic Positioning

**TCM**: Sweet, bitter, astringent; neutral. Channels: Lung (肺), Kidney (腎). Seed: warms Lung, contracts essence, stops urinary leakage. Leaf (modern): activates Blood, resolves stasis, improves circulation, calms Shen.

**Ayurvedic (cross-reference)**: Bitter, astringent; slightly warming. Reduces Kapha ↓↓, reduces Vata ↓ (mild). *Medhya rasāyana* (brain tonic) in modern Ayurvedic integration; not a classical Ayurvedic plant.

### 8.3 Contraindications

- **Anticoagulants / antiplatelets**: PAF antagonism + quercetin anti-platelet effect — additive with warfarin, aspirin, clopidogrel; significant bleeding risk increase; avoid or monitor INR closely
- **Surgery**: Discontinue 2 weeks pre-operatively — bleeding risk; case reports of post-surgical haemorrhage attributed to ginkgo
- **Seizure medications**: Case reports of seizures associated with ginkgo — possible lowering of seizure threshold; avoid in epilepsy
- **Raw seeds**: Toxic — ginkgotoxin (B6 antagonist) causes seizures, vomiting; seeds must be cooked and consumed in small quantities only
- **Ginkgolic acids** in non-pharmaceutical preparations: allergenic + potentially genotoxic; only use standardised pharmaceutical extracts
- **Pregnancy**: Insufficient safety data; avoid (anti-platelet effects theoretically concerning)
