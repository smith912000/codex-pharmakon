# Copper scales — Lepis (LEPIS), "Brass Scales"; Squama, also called helitis (Dioscorides)

## *The thick, shiny flakes that scale off worked brass (copper alloy), the text's "Brass Scales", used washed in eye medicines and taken in small amounts as a drink or pill; the identification as copper (or copper-alloy) scale rests on the translator's gloss "Brass Scales" and on the text's statement that it is made from Cyprian brass nails, so it is of medium confidence* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** Copper and brass scale are poisonous if swallowed and irritant to eyes and wounds. The chapter's drink and pill, and its eye use, are given here as history. This entry is not a recommendation, and nobody should take or apply this material.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** This is a single-text entry. The chapter is 5-89 LEPIS in Book Five of the *De Materia Medica* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10259-10266; the name list `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` carries it as 5-89, line 10259, translator's identification "Brass Scales", class mineral; `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 816 has "Lepis", mineral). The Greek word *chalkos*, which the translation renders as "brass", is treated in the copper entry (Copper / Tong, CPU-00700, `II_96_Copper_Tong.md`), where the equation of the text's "brass" with copper is noted as the spine's and not the translator's. That entry names this chapter in a list but does not set out its text; this entry does. A search of Sushruta, Shennong, rGyud-bzhi and Ebers on disk found no separate attestation under this name, and none is claimed.

*Naming and choice.* The chapter (line 10261) opens: "Squama is also called helitis and is made from Cyprian brass nails. It is thick and good; but that made from white brass is thin and weak and we refuse it, preferring that which is thick and shiny and turns rusty when vinegar is sprinkled on it." So the text gives a second name, says what it is made from, rejects a thin kind made from "white brass", and gives a test: the good kind goes rusty when vinegar is sprinkled on it.

*Action and use.* "It is contractive and reduces the intensity of symptoms of noma [grazer disease, eats away muscle, tissue and bones], repressing putrification and forming new skins. A decoction (taken as a drink with honey and water) expels fluids. Some work it into a paste with meal and give it as a catapotium [pill]. It is also mixed with medicines for the eyes as it dries eyelids that are rough, removing rheum [excessive fluids]." Uses given: noma (a spreading, destructive ulcer), a decoction as a drink with honey and water to "expel fluids", a pill made with meal, and an ingredient in eye medicines for rough eyelids and rheum.

*Washing.* The text then gives a long washing: "clean half a pound of dry brass scales [flakes], put them into a mortar with clear water, stir them up and down carefully by hand until the scum sinks down, and throw away the remainder." The water is poured off, one wine cup of rain water is added and the scales are rubbed hard with the palm; "when it begins to send out viscousness" up to six cups of water are added and beaten strongly. What runs out into "a red brass box" is "the flower of squama. It is very potent and very effective for eye medicines. The rest has no strength." The remainder is washed again "until it shows no glueyness", covered with a linen cloth and left standing two days, the standing water poured off, the residue dried and boxed. "Some wash it like cadmia [5-84] and put it in jars."

*The next chapter.* 5-90 LEPIS STOMOMATOS (line 10267), translator's note "UNKNOWN: stoma is a little mouth", is a separate chapter and is not described here.

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, scale from worked copper or brass is a mixture of copper oxides (and, from an alloy, zinc or tin compounds) formed by heating, together with metal particles. Copper compounds are astringent and caustic in the amounts that matter. The text's test (the good scale goes rusty when vinegar is sprinkled on it) agrees in kind with copper reacting to form green or red-brown salts in vinegar. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a recommended practice. Toxic.** The text records a copper-alloy scale, washed fine, used as a drying, contracting agent on spreading ulcers and in eye medicine, with a drink and a pill beside them. The Codex gives no protocol. Copper salts are emetic and corrosive when swallowed and irritate the eye.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only quantity in the chapter is for washing, not for dosing: half a pound of dry scales, one wine cup of rain water, up to six cups on the second beating, two days' standing (line 10261 onward). The use as a drink is "a decoction ... with honey and water", and as a pill "a paste with meal" given "as a catapotium". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Copper is an essential trace element, but copper salts taken in quantity cause vomiting, abdominal pain and liver and kidney injury, and copper compounds are irritant to the eye. Copper has antimicrobial properties on surfaces, which fits in kind the text's use against putrefying ulcers, but that does not make scale safe to apply or swallow. Washing a powder by repeated suspension and settling, as the chapter does, separates fine particles from coarse. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** The chapter prints one quality: "contractive" (line 10261 onward). It prints no heat or moisture grade and no degree. The text calls the fine washed fraction, the "flower of squama", "very potent".

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What the chapter offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: how to choose the scale (thick, shiny, going rusty in vinegar), how to refuse the thin one made from white brass, and a long manual washing that separates the potent fraction from the weak. The chapter assigns no cultivation status; the material has only a therapeutic one.

**Practitioner's note.** A toxic metal scale of the ancient eye-salve maker's shop. Nothing here supports taking it by mouth or any cultivation use. The Codex takes from the chapter the care of selection and washing, and nothing else.

### Contraindications

- **TOXIC.** Do not swallow copper or brass scale or its washings. The chapter's drink and pill are history.
- **Eyes and wounds.** Do not apply to the eyes or to open wounds.
- **Occupational dust and filings.** Brass dust and fume can cause metal-fume illness and eye and skin injury.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** No statement in the sources retrieved; given the toxicity, avoid. [USER: verify]
- **Identity.** The copper-alloy reading rests on the translator's "brass". [USER: verify]
