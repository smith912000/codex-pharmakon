# Aster barbellatus — me.tog.lug.mig, Metok lugmig, "sheep's eye flower" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Aster barbellatus, the first of three binomials Clark 1995 prints for me.tog.lug.mig (with Aster heolini and Aster flaccidus Bge.), where the name is glossed "lit. 'sheep's eye flower'"; the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints a different Aster by position beside "Metok lugmig"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** **Named only by the Tibetan texts in this row.** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. Both loci are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and the row's binomial is the first of three on Clark's line. Clark's own name index prints it as "Aster arbellatus" (line 1557), a spelling that differs from the line it indexes (line 1077). The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed names (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1077).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads: "me.tog.lug.mig (lit. 'sheep's eye flower'): Aster barbellatus: Aster heolini: Aster flaccidus Bge.: [grows in rocky meadows and has small, rounded, pale blue leaves, tall, brown stalk and blue flowers with yellow pistils. It has slightly bitter taste, neutralizes] poison and cures contagious fevers."

Clark's preface (line 82) says "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". So the habitat, the description of leaves, stalk and flowers, the slightly bitter taste and the verb "neutralizes" are the commentary layer. Outside the brackets are Clark's own identifications (the three Latin names, editorial and not root text), the gloss in parentheses "(lit. 'sheep's eye flower')" (the preface assigns parentheses to the oral instructions of Dr. Yeshe Donden and Dr. Pema Dorje, and the Codex cannot tell whether a gloss of the name is of that kind), and the root-text words "poison and cures contagious fevers". The sentence is split by the bracket: its first verb comes from the commentary, and "cures contagious fevers" is outside it. The text thus gives two actions in the root layer, one with its verb supplied from the commentary, and no power.

Clark's name index prints *me.tog.lug.mig* (line 1727). One more Clark line compares another drug with it. The entry for *lug.chhung* (line 1105), a different Tibetan drug, says in the commentary layer that its "leaf and stalk resemble Aster flaccidus (lug.mig)". That is a comparison, and it assigns no use to *me.tog.lug.mig*. The Codex does not treat *lug.mig* there as the same name as *me.tog.lug.mig*, though the sound suggests it. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 (Explanatory Tantra).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. Chapter 20, in the efficacy of herbal medicines (the section begins at line 3428), line 3448 reads: "Metok lugmig is an antidote against poison and cures epidemic diseases." The index (line 7441) gives "Metok lugmig 209". That *Metok lugmig* is a form of *me.tog.lug.mig* is a reading from the sound of the name, supported by the agreement on poison and fevers or epidemic disease. The text does not state it. [USER: verify]

The glossary prints another Aster for this name. The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate blocks. The block at lines 5471 to 5484 prints the phonetic names "Maru tse", "Men ga", "Metok lugmig" and "Metok serchen", the transliterations "ma ru rtse", "sman sga", "me tok lug mig" and "me tok ser can", and the scientific names "Butea monosperma", "Hedychium spicatum", "Aster himalaicus" and "Calendula officinalis", with the English "Bastard teak", "Kind of ginger" and "Calendula, marigold" (lines 5495 to 5497). By column position the glossary's binomial for "Metok lugmig" is *Aster himalaicus*, not *Aster barbellatus*, and no English name is matched to it. The glossary does not print the pairs on one row, and the Codex does not state the pairing. This is an identity doubt between Clark and the glossary for one Tibetan drug. The row stands for the Tibetan drug and not for one species. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Aster* is a genus of the daisy family (Asteraceae), and nothing is stated here about the composition of the Tibetan drug, which may not be this species. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a flower that is an antidote against poison and cures contagious or epidemic fevers (Clark line 1077, Men-Tsee-Khang line 3448). These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for poisoning or fever, and the botanical identity is uncertain.

### Preparation & Dosing

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes the flower by what it does: it neutralizes poison and cures contagious fevers. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this entry places *me.tog.lug.mig* or *Metok lugmig* in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. In the lines read, the essence-extraction mentions near this entry are for other plants (for example at lines 853, 870 and 971 and, in the next entry, *mkhan.pa*, line 1078), and *me.tog.lug.mig* is not named there. The rejuvenation chapters (Clark chapter 23, Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23) were read for names and do not name it. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is an antidote-and-fever drug in the Tibetan texts, and its species is not settled. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning. The botanical identity is uncertain, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Poisoning is a medical emergency, and a plant should not be taken as an antidote on the strength of a materia medica. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
