# Buprestis beetle — BOUPRESTEIS (Dioscorides 2-66), "Buprestis — Buprestidae — Bupressedes"
## *A beetle Dioscorides calls a type of cantharides and of pine-tree caterpillar; the translator prints the family name Buprestidae and the edition's label "POISONOUS"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

This entry is one short chapter of one text, with two places where the same beetle is named as a poison to be countered. The preceding chapter, the cantharides or Spanish-fly beetle (2-65), has its own spine row (CPU-00294) and is not yet written; the blister beetle of the Tibetan text has another (CPU-00185). Neither is treated here beyond what 2-66 says of the buprestis.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-66 *BOUPRESTEIS* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading line 5198, text lines 5201-5205; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` row "BOUPRESTEIS, 2-66 (line 5198)"; Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). The edition prints the heading "Buprestis — Buprestidae — Bupressedes" and the label "POISONOUS"; the label and the family name are the edition's. The chapter reads: "In the same way the bupressedes are preserved for storage. They are types of cantharides [2-65] and erucae [caterpillars] of the pine tree. Roasted a little in hot ashes, they are stored and kept in a sieve that hangs up. Their common strength [see above] is antiseptic, anti-ulcerous and heating; as a result they are mixed in medicines that cure diseases of the cornea, leprosy and wild impetigo [skin infection]. Mixed with soothing suppositories they encourage the menstrual flow. Some also have related that these cantharides help dropsy by moving the urine. Some also believe that the wings and feet of them are an antidote for those who have taken parasites in a drink."

"In the same way" refers back to 2-65 *KANTHARIDES* (line 5194), where the dried beetles are gathered "from the corn", put into an unglazed jar with a thin linen cloth tied over the mouth, and held to the fumes of very sharp warmed vinegar "until they are stifled", then threaded and stored; the most effective have "the most diversity of colours, yellowish cross streaks [in their wings], and are longbodied, full and fat". As printed in this edition, 2-65 gives no statement of strength, so the "common strength [see above]" of 2-66 points to a statement that the lines read do not contain. The buprestis is also named as a poison in two other chapters:

- *Oil of melinum* (1-55, heading line 3981, text line 3985): "It is taken as a drink against vomiting from ingesting dried beetles [2-65], bupressedes [2-66] and pinorum [1-86]."
- *Milk* (2-77 *Gala schiston*, line 5246): "New milk is good for ulcers, and inflammation caused by deadly medicines like cantharis [2-65], pityocampa [pine grub or pine caterpillar], salamander, buprestis [2-66], hyoscyamus [4-69], dorycnium [4-75], aconitum [4-77, 4-78], or ephemerum [4-85]. For this cow's milk helps best."

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, the blistering agent of the blister beetles (the Meloidae, to which the Spanish fly belongs) is cantharidin, a toxic terpenoid that irritates and blisters skin and mucosa and damages the kidneys and urinary tract when swallowed. Whether the insect the edition calls a buprestis contained cantharidin, or is a jewel beetle (Buprestidae) at all, cannot be settled from the text. Ancient names for these beetles are unsettled. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice, and a warning more than a remedy. Dioscorides records the buprestis as an ingredient in preparations for corneal disease, leprosy and impetigo, as a menstrual stimulant and as a reported diuretic, and in two other chapters names it among the poisons to be countered with oil of melinum or cow's milk. The edition labels it "POISONOUS". Beetles of this kind are dangerous to swallow or apply. The Codex gives no protocol and recommends none.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation in the chapter is the storage method: the beetles are "roasted a little in hot ashes" and kept in a hanging sieve (2-66, line 5201), preserved "in the same way" as the cantharides (vinegar fumes, linen-covered jar, threaded; 2-65). They are "mixed in medicines" for the cornea, leprosy and impetigo, and "mixed with soothing suppositories" for the menses. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Cantharidin is poisonous to humans: contact blisters skin, and swallowing a small amount of a cantharidin-containing insect can cause severe damage to the mouth, gut and kidneys and can kill. Milk does not neutralise it. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** 2-66 (line 5201): "Their common strength is antiseptic, anti-ulcerous and heating", pointing to a general statement not found in the 2-65 text as printed. No degree or humour is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What the chapters offer is a discipline of attention: gather the beetles (the cantharides are gathered "from the corn"), judge them by colour, cross-streaks and body, stifle them in vinegar fumes, roast and hang them, and know their poisons and antidotes (oil of melinum, milk). No chapter read assigns the buprestis a cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports a practice. The buprestis appears in the text as a poison first and a medicine second. A cultivator takes nothing from this entry and does not handle, eat or apply the beetle. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Toxicity:** the edition labels it "POISONOUS", and 2-65, 2-66, 1-55 and 2-77 all treat it as a poison; do not swallow or apply any blister-beetle material. Poisoning is a medical emergency; contact a poison centre.
- **Pregnancy:** the chapter says it "encourages the menstrual flow"; it is dangerous in pregnancy and must not be used to bring on a period.
- **Kidneys and urinary tract:** cantharidin-type poisons damage them; the "diuretic" report must not be tried.
- **Eyes and skin:** do not put in the eye (the "cornea" use) or on skin; it blisters.
- **Law and ethics:** insect collecting is regulated in some areas; no conservation issue is stated in the text.
