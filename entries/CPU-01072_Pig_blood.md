# Pig blood — Pig's Blood, Hog's Blood (Bryan's Ebers) / Pig blood (rGyud-bzhi)
## *Blood of the domestic pig or hog; the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary gives the pig as* Sus scrofa domestica *Brisson* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP, FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The English text on disk is C. P. Bryan's 1930 *The Papyrus Ebers*, a translation with summaries; the Egyptian words are not printed. The spine's list row is `_sources/ebers.csv` line 82, "Pig's blood", *Sus scrofa*, source field "Ebers; Wikipedia/Ebers Papyrus", a list row whose second source was not relied on. The text locus is the eye section, under "To prevent the hair growing into the eye after it has been pulled out" (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` lines 2879-2894): "Incense-ground-in-Lizard's-dung, Cow's Blood, Ass's Blood, Pig's Blood, Dog's Blood, Stag's Blood, Collyrium, Incense: Crush, rub into one in the different kinds of Blood, and place on the part whence the Hair has been pulled out so that it may not grow again." The pig's blood is thus one of five bloods in a single compound, and no remedy using pig's blood alone was found; the donkey's blood in the same compound has its own entry (III_18). Bryan lists the Hog among the animals "whose identity is not in doubt" (line 1404) and says that although the physicians "used most of his body from his teeth to his testicles", they never prescribed the hog's brain (lines 1419-1421). Other Ebers hog parts in Bryan's text are a hog's tooth, crushed and eaten in sugar-cakes for four days (line 1869), Hog's Fat in a salve for hardenings (line 2191), Hog's Bile in a douche for the vulva (line 2557), and Hog's dung among the thirty-seven ingredients of the largest prescription in the papyrus (line 3170). In a passage on a bleeding sign, the papyrus describes blood coming from the mouth or rectum as resembling "Hog's Blood when-it-is-cooked" (line 3511); that is a comparison for the patient's blood, not a remedy.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** In Clark's translation of the Explanatory Tantra ch.20, under "Blood" (the fourth of the thirteen animal medicines): "Pig blood: collects [scattered types of] poisoning and brown phlegm. Donkey blood: cures rheumatism and lymph disorders in the joints." (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 1176). Men-Tsee-Khang 2011: "Pig blood assembles poison and baekan mukpo" (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 3540). The two translations render the second disorder as "brown phlegm" (Clark) and *baekan mukpo* (Men-Tsee-Khang), which appear to be the same disorder; I did not check the Tibetan. The spine's locus is Clark line 1176; `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` has rows for pig bone, tongue and faeces (lines 338, 352, 376) but none for pig blood. the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints the pig as "Sus scrofa domestica Brisson" (line 7056). Other pig products in the same section share the pattern. Pig bone "cures brown phlegm" (Clark line 1160), pig fat "collects poison, heals psoriasis [and cures baldness]" (line 1180), pork "heals wounds and eliminates brown phlegm" (line 677), pig's stool "cures indigestion, septic disorders, infectious fevers and bile tumors" (line 1199), and the pig is among the sources of animal bile (line 880). Dioscorides's blood chapter (2-97, line 5363) names the blood of many animals and does not name the pig; the entry is therefore not tagged DMM.

### Active Components

Blood is a fluid tissue of cells (red cells, white cells, platelets) in plasma. In general, red cells carry haemoglobin, so blood is a source of iron and protein; plasma carries albumin, globulins, clotting factors, lipids, hormones and electrolytes. Pig blood is used as a food ingredient (blood sausage, blood pudding) in many cuisines and as a source of plasma proteins and haem iron in the food industry. The texts name no active component. No quantities are given here because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sources record pig's blood as one of five bloods in an Egyptian compound for a plucked eyelash, and, in the Tibetan text, as a medicine that "collects" poison and brown phlegm. The Codex has no protocol for either and offers none: raw pig blood is a recognised infection risk; the sources give no dose; the Egyptian use is at the eyelid; and the Tibetan use concerns poisoning, which is an emergency. Cooked pig blood as food is a matter of cuisine, outside this entry's sources.

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparation given is the Egyptian one, as Bryan renders it: the five bloods with collyrium and incense (the latter ground in lizard's dung), "rub into one in the different kinds of Blood", placed on the part where the hair had been pulled out. The Tibetan texts give the action only, with no method in the lines read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Raw pig blood and offal can carry hepatitis E virus, *Salmonella*, *Streptococcus suis* and parasites such as *Trichinella* (in meat) and *Toxoplasma*; cooking reduces these risks. Pig blood is a source of haem iron when cooked and eaten. No effect of pig's blood on poisoning, phlegm disorders or the regrowth of plucked eyelashes is established. The Tibetan "brown phlegm" (*baekan mukpo*) is a disease concept of that tradition and is not translated into a biomedical condition here. No clinical studies of the traditional uses were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** None is printed; Bryan's translation gives the compound by ingredient and complaint.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The translated lines print an action only ("collects [scattered types of] poisoning and brown phlegm", Clark; "assembles poison and baekan mukpo", Men-Tsee-Khang), with no taste, potency or humour assignment. ### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* The only locus for pig's blood is an eyelash remedy, one of five bloods ground with incense and collyrium. It records what was done for a complaint and does not say what the blood does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); Bryan reports no recitation with this compound, and since he summarises, that is not proof the papyrus gives none. No deity is attributed. Because Ebers identifications are modern hypotheses, "Pig's Blood" is Bryan's English rendering, and the spine row (`_sources/ebers.csv` line 82) rests on a list that cites Wikipedia as well as the papyrus. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Pig blood is one of the seven bloods in the Explanatory Tantra's animal medicines, given for "collecting" poison and brown phlegm. The verb is the tradition's own; the same verb attaches to pig fat in the neighbouring "Fat" paragraph, so the pig products share a pattern. The animal-medicine lines read name no essence-extraction (*bcud len*) use, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition; none is asserted. The spine marks the rGyud-bzhi row "id uncertain" for the animal items it lists.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do. In both traditions pig's blood is a drug for a complaint, and in Ebers it is only one of five blood ingredients. A cultivator can take from the Tibetan lines that the pig's products are listed together for "collecting" poison, which is a statement about disease and not about vitality. Pig blood cooked as food is an ordinary dish, and this entry gives no reason to eat it for cultivation.

### Contraindications

- **Infection.** Raw pig blood and offal can carry hepatitis E, *Salmonella*, *Streptococcus suis* and other pathogens; never apply raw blood to the eye or broken skin or drink it. [USER: verify specifics]
- **Poisoning.** This is an emergency requiring medical care; do not rely on an animal product.
- **Eye.** The Egyptian use is at the eyelid margin; an unsterile product there risks infection of the eye.
- **Dose unknown.** No dose is given in the sources read.
- **Religious and ethical.** Pork and pig blood are forbidden in Judaism and Islam and in some other dietary rules; use means slaughter.
