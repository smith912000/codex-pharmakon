# Dryopteris fragrans — brag.skya.ha.wo, Drakya hawo, the "minister fern" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Dryopteris fragrans, the second of three binomials Clark 1995 prints for brag.skya.ha.wo (after Corallodiscus Kingianus (Craib) Burtt and before Woodsia glabella), which the extraction row took as its key; the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary and Clark's own compounding chapter name Corallodiscus for the same drug* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under this binomial only by Clark.** No other text read for this entry names the plant. Both Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the line prints three. The extraction row (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 167) lists them as "Corallodiscus Kingianus (Craib) Burtt; Dryopteris fragrans; Woodsia glabella" and its note says that "key uses the first"; the spine row, however, carries the second, *Dryopteris fragrans*. The first name, with its printed authority, has no spine row. The binomial of this entry is therefore one of three alternatives the text prints, and the other Tibetan text and Clark's own later chapter point to the first. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1068).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within the section headed "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry reads:

"brag.skya.ha.wo: Corallodiscus Kingianus (Craib) Burtt: Dryopteris fragrans: Woodsia glabella: [has four names, for example, ming.po.bdun.gyi.sring.gchig.ma(lit. 'single sister of seven brothers') and blon.po.re.ral ('minister fern') and grows beside rocks. It has a large, thick, stiff, lobed, flat leaf and a blue flower which whitens with age. Corallodiscus has bitter and sweet tastes], neutralizes poisons, halts dysentery with fever, [heals wounds, kidney disorders and disorders of the seminal vesicle]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the four names, the habitat beside rocks, the leaf, the "blue flower which whitens with age", the sentence "Corallodiscus has bitter and sweet tastes", and the closing "heals wounds, kidney disorders and disorders of the seminal vesicle". Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the three Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the clause "neutralizes poisons, halts dysentery with fever". The commentary itself names *Corallodiscus*, and not *Dryopteris* or *Woodsia*, when it gives the taste. The commentary also describes a flower; it is a general botanical fact that ferns such as *Dryopteris* and *Woodsia* do not flower, and that *Corallodiscus* is a flowering genus, so the description sits with the first binomial and not with the second. The Tibetan name gives it a place among the ferns (*re.ral*), and the Codex does not resolve the difference. The entry gives no part used and no preparation.

*Clark 1995, the "minister fern".* Line 1084 (the entry for *ldum.bu.re.ral*, treated in I_1269) ranks three ferns: "'king fern', whilst brags.spos (Lepisorus) is the 'queen fern' and brags.skya.ha.wo (Corallodiscus) is the 'minister fern'". The gloss on line 1084 names the drug as *Corallodiscus*. In chapter 21, "The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines" (heading at line 1205), line 1212 lists "Corallodiscus kingianus" in the class of medicines that neutralize poisons. Clark's index of Latin names lists "Corallodiscus" and "Corallodiscus kingianus" (line 1573) and "Dryopteris fragrans" (line 1578). So in Clark's own chapter-21 list and in his line 1084 the drug is called *Corallodiscus*, and *Dryopteris fragrans* appears as the second name of the entry at line 1068 and in the index.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, the same Tibetan name as "Drakya hawo".* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`. In chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), line 3448 reads: "Drakya hawo is an antidote against poison and stops dysentery." The index (line 7347) lists "Drakya hawo 209, 305"; page 209 is the page of that line, and page 305 (the marker is at line 4942) is in the Glossary on Tibetan Terms, where the name was not found. The glossary's "D" block (the letter at line 5179) prints "Drakya hawo" as the eighteenth of twenty-two phonetic names (line 5192), "brag skya ha bo" as the eighteenth transliteration (line 5210), and, as the eighteenth scientific name, "Corallodiscus batalin" (line 5247, cut short in the file), with the English "Corallodiscus fem" (line 5251), which the Codex reads as "fern" with an OCR error. The pairing is by column position only. The count can be checked: "Drakpoe" stands sixteenth against *Lepisorus clathratus*, and Clark's *brag.spos* (the "queen fern") is a *Lepisorus*; "Drita sazin" stands last against *Fragaria nubicola*. The glossary prints no *Dryopteris fragrans*. The two Tibetan texts agree on the name and, in kind, on the action (poison, dysentery), and the glossary names *Corallodiscus*; the Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Dryopteris fragrans*, *brag.skya.ha.wo*, "Drakya hawo" and *Corallodiscus* found no entry on this plant. I_1225 (*Adiantum pedatum*) and I_1269 (*Drynaria propinqua*, written in this batch) quote the ferns passage (lines 1068 to 1084) in their prose and are not entries on this drug. The Dioscorides names list carries two chapters (PTERIS 4-186 and DRUOPTERIS 4-189; `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, lines 764 and 767) whose suggested identifications include *Dryopteris filix-mas*, a different species; they were not read as sources here. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Dryopteris* is a genus of ferns (Dryopteridaceae), that *Corallodiscus* is a genus of low flowering rock plants of the Gesneriaceae, and that the two are unrelated. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained, because its Latin name is not settled. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "neutralizes poisons, halts dysentery with fever" and, in Clark's commentary, "heals wounds, kidney disorders and disorders of the seminal vesicle" (Clark, line 1068), and that "is an antidote against poison and stops dysentery" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3448). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only by one of three alternatives, and they are not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary describes the leaf and flower and does not say which part is taken. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Tibetan terms (poisons, dysentery with fever, disorders of the seminal vesicle) are not modern diagnoses or measured outcomes, so no modern finding can be set beside them here. The identity of the drug is uncertain between *Corallodiscus*, *Dryopteris fragrans* and *Woodsia glabella*, which belong to different families, so a study of one plant would not bear directly on it. It is a general, well-established fact that poisoning and dysentery with fever need urgent medical care.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** "Corallodiscus has bitter and sweet tastes" (Clark, line 1068, inside the brackets), a taste given for the first binomial in the commentary. No power (hot or cool) and no post-digestive taste is printed for this drug in the lines read, and the name does not appear in the Men-Tsee-Khang taste-group lists of chapter 19.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes this drug by taste (bitter and sweet, in the commentary) and by what it does: it neutralizes poisons and halts dysentery with fever. Line 1084 gives it a rank among three ferns, "minister fern", which is a rank of names and not a grade of cultivation use. The Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same in kind ("an antidote against poison and stops dysentery"). The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this drug places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the passages cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and the link from this binomial to that framing rests on one of Clark's alternative names.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is an antidote-and-dysentery drug in the Tibetan texts, its Latin name is one of three and probably not this one, and there is no preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. The identity is uncertain among plants of different families, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Poisoning and dysentery with fever need urgent medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
