# Dichroa — 恒山 (Heng Shan; written 常山 Chang Shan in later texts); also Qi Ye (漆叶), Hu Cao

## **Dichroa febrifuga* Lour. (Hydrangeaceae), the root, the plant of the Chinese drug 常山 *chang shan*; the identification is of high confidence. In Sun's edition the Shennong paragraph for this entry is damaged (see Origin), so the classic's indications rest on Yang 1998* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

> **TOXICITY CAUTION.** This is a lower-class drug that Wu Pu's layer marks "toxic" (有毒) and that Yang's footnote describes as more often used to *provoke vomiting* than to stop it. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should take this plant.

*Heng shan* is named only by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts on disk. It is a single-text entry. The placeholder row it replaces was named "Radix Dichroae Febrifugae."

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** Sun Xingyan's edition files 恒山 in the lower classic (卷三 下经; `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 3241, 味 paragraph; name line 3237). His note under the name (line 3239) says: "the old text wrote 常山; the *Yulan* writes 恒山, which is right." The paragraph in this e-text is damaged: it reads "bitter, cold. Mainly treats injury [伤] ..." and then runs straight into the *Wu Pu* layer, so the classic's list of indications is lost here. The Shennong text proper is therefore only "bitter, cold" (味苦，寒) in Sun's text, and the indications are taken from Yang below.

*Wu Pu layer* (line 3241, continuing). "Heng shan, one name Qi Ye (lacquer leaf). Shennong, Qibo: bitter; Mr Li: very cold; Tongjun: acrid, toxic (有毒). Gathered in the second and eighth months."

*Mingyi bielu layer* (line 3243). Habitat: Yizhou and Hanzhong. The root is gathered in the eighth month and dried in the shade.

*Sun's note* (案, line 3245). He quotes the *Hou Han shu*, Biography of Hua Tuo: Tuo taught a "lacquer-leaf, 青粘 powder" (漆叶青粘散): "lacquer-leaf shavings one *dou*, 青粘 fourteen *liang*, in this proportion," and it "is said that, taken long, it expels the three worms, benefits the five viscera, lightens the body, and keeps the head from whitening." Sun cites this under the alias 漆叶 only. The passage describes a named powder in a biography; it is not Shennong text, and the sources retrieved do not show that this powder was made from this plant. It is recorded, and nothing is drawn from it.

*Drug-relations table.* The table (line 4271) names the old form: 六芝 "dislikes (恶) 常山." The next lower-class entry, 蜀漆 (name line 3249; separate monograph, not covered here), is one that Wu Pu says is "the leaf of heng shan" (蜀漆叶，一名恒山).

**Yang 1998 as second witness.** Yang (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt` line 3535) prints "Heng Shan (Radix Dichroae Febrifugae) is bitter and cold. It mainly treats cold damage cold and heat, malaria due to heat, ghost toxins, bound phlegm in the chest, and counterflow vomiting. Its other name is Hu Cao (Dependent Weed). It grows in rivers and valleys." His footnote 217 (line 3553) explains the name: "Heng Shan gave way to Chang Shan simply because it violated an imperial name taboo," and says: "Judging from the text, it seems that this medicinal is used to stop vomiting, but, as a matter of fact, it is more often prescribed to provoke vomiting," and "In modern times, this medicinal is mostly used to treat malaria." This is Yang's commentary. Yang separately prints *Shu Qi* (line 3512) as "acrid, balanced, and toxic," treating "malaria, cough and counterflow, cold and heat..." with the same Latin name.

### Active Components

*Dichroa febrifuga* is the source of the quinazolinone alkaloids febrifugine and isofebrifugine, which are known to have antimalarial activity and which are strongly emetic and toxic; a synthetic derivative (halofuginone) has been developed from febrifugine. These are well-established facts about the plant. No analytical source was retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify any further constituent before adding it.]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Toxic.**

- **What the text says it is for.** "Cold and heat" illnesses, malaria with heat, "ghost toxins," phlegm bound in the chest, and counterflow vomiting (Yang).
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no preparation, no duration. Sun's text states no indications at all (damaged).

The emetic action noted by Yang is the practical danger: the drug causes severe vomiting. No Kenetica protocol is derived from this entry, and none should be.

### Preparation & Dosing

The Shennong text gives no preparation and no dose. Wu Pu and the Mingyi layer give only harvest times (second and eighth months; root, dried in the shade; lines 3241, 3243). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any dose from this entry.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

*Dichroa febrifuga* is the plant from which the antimalarial and emetic alkaloid febrifugine was isolated, and the toxicity in the classic's own layers (Wu Pu: toxic) agrees with the plant's strongly emetic action. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SBJ (Sun 1799).** Bitter, cold (味苦，寒; line 3241). Wu Pu: Shennong, Qibo: bitter; Mr Li: very cold; Tongjun: acrid, toxic.
- **SBJ (Yang 1998).** Bitter and cold (line 3535). Yang makes no toxicity statement for this entry (he does for *Shu Qi*, line 3512).
- No organ or channel assignment is given in the classic.

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(b) Framed.* The entry stands in the lower classic, which the preface (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt` line 2935) defines as "the left envoys, mainly treating disease to correspond with earth; mostly toxic and not to be taken long," for one who "wishes to remove cold and heat evil qi, break accumulations and gatherings, and heal illness." There is no 久服 clause in the paragraph. The Hua Tuo powder passage (Sun's note, line 3245) promises "lightening the body" for a powder named after the alias 漆叶, but it comes from a later biography, not the classic, and is not used to assign this plant a status.

**Practitioner's note.** A lower-grade, toxic emetic drug. The tradition's own grading is the warning, and nothing in the record supports any cultivation use. A cultivator should not use it.

### Contraindications

- **TOXIC.** Wu Pu marks it toxic; the plant is strongly emetic. Do not ingest it.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children, the weak.** No statement in the sources retrieved. Given the toxicity, avoid. [USER: verify]
- **Malaria and fevers.** These are medical conditions; use proper care.
- **Identity.** Do not confuse with other plants called *shan* or sold under *chang shan*. [USER: verify]
