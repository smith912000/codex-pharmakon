### Active Components

Orpiment is arsenic trisulphide, As2S3, a lemon-yellow to golden mineral that forms in layers and is the "gold-coloured" arsenic of the Greek description; realgar is the red arsenic sulphide (As4S4 in formula, "arsenic disulphide" in the older translator's name) and is a different mineral. Both are toxic arsenic sources, and roasting them in air converts arsenic sulphides to the more volatile and more poisonous arsenic trioxide, which is why roasting is itself hazardous. The traditional "roasted" and "sublimated" preparations are not standardised, and the lines read do not give their composition.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. In the sources orpiment is a corrosive, depilatory and anti-parasite external remedy (Greek) and a middle-grade drug whose line also promises longevity after sublimation (Chinese). Arsenic compounds are carcinogenic poisons, and nothing here supports taking orpiment. The Codex offers no protocol, dose or preparation.

### Preparation & Dosing

Only preparations the texts give. DMM 5-121: roasted in a new ceramic jar over coals, stirred continuously until red-hot and changed in colour, cooled, finely beaten and stored; no quantity is given in the chapter. SBJ: "Protracted taking after it is sublimated" (the method of sublimation is not given in the line). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Arsenic compounds are corrosive to tissue, which is consistent with the Greek claims that they "repress abnormal growths" and "make the hair fall out"; arsenic compounds are also carcinogenic and cause chronic poisoning. Arsenic trioxide, a different compound, is a modern medicine in a specialist leukaemia setting; that does not carry over to orpiment. No clinical studies were retrieved for the traditional uses. The chapter offers the discipline of attention, and in this case a protective one: the heading says POISONOUS; the best is crusty, gold-coloured and in scales "like fish scales", unmixed, from Mysia; and there is a second, clumpier kind, from Pontus and Cappadocia. **Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: Middle Class.* Ci Huang stands under the heading "Jades and Stones: Middle Class". The preface (lines 385-390) says the middle class "mainly nurture personality and correspond to humanity. They may or may not be toxic, and [therefore,] one should weigh and ponder before putting them to their appropriate use. If one intends to control disease, supplement vacuity, and replenish exhaustion, one should base [one's efforts] on the middle class." The entry's own wording, "Protracted taking after it is sublimated may make the body light, prolong life, and prevent senility", reaches into the superior class's language and is recorded as printed, not endorsed; Yang's note says the longevity vocabulary comes from the Daoists who "created the early materia medica literature" (lines 375-378). "Make the body light" (*qing shen*) has no pharmacological equivalent and is not weight loss. The jing, qi and shen language is not printed for Ci Huang and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here for a cultivator to take. Orpiment is, in these sources, a poison with external uses, in the Chinese scheme a middle-grade drug "to weigh and ponder" before use, and in the Greek scheme a stone to be recognised by its look and handled with respect. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Orpiment and every arsenic compound are poisonous (gut, nerves, skin, liver, kidney) and carcinogenic with long exposure; roasting them releases toxic fume. Preparations sold as traditional remedies have caused arsenic poisoning. Use is banned or restricted in many places. Do not use. Ritual and demon-related claims in other entries are ritual, not therapy.
