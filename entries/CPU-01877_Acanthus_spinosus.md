# Acanthus spinosus — Akantha Arabike AKANTHA ARABIKE (3-15) and Akantha Erpekantha AKANTHA ERPEKANTHA (3-19) (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica)

## *Acanthus spinosus L., spiny bear's breeches, a large-leaved spiny herb of the Mediterranean; the identification is the English edition's, which prints "Acanthus spinosus" in the suggestions for two different chapters (3-15, labelled "Oyster Plant", and 3-19, labelled "Bears Breeches"), and is of medium confidence only, because the two chapters describe plants that differ from each other, the Arabian acantha of 3-15 being said to resemble a thistle and the garden acantha of 3-19 having broad lettuce-like leaves, so one binomial cannot be certain for both* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named by Dioscorides among the texts on disk, in two chapters, 3-15 and 3-19.** This is a single-text entry. The row was added on 13 October as an unverified placeholder from the Dioscorides name list (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, rows 421 and 425). Both rows carry the class "animal", an extraction error corrected to plant on the spine. Both chapters were read in full on 30 October, with the chapters on either side (3-14, 3-16, 3-18, 3-20) for context. A search of the other texts on disk found nothing else for this plant.

**Dioscorides, De Materia Medica (DMM), chapter 3-15, AKANTHA ARABIKE** (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading at line 6653, text at line 6654). The editor's line reads: "Acanthus spinosus — Oyster Plant". The text: "Acantha arabica seems similar in nature to the white thistle — astringent, good for excessive [menstrual] discharges of women, the throwing-up of blood and other discharges — the root being similarly effective. It grows in rough places. It is also called acanthisa, while the Romans call it spina." The "white thistle" is the plant of the chapter before it, 3-14, AKANTHA LEUKE (line 6649), which the editor glosses as "Cotton Thistle, Scotch Thistle", whose root "(taken in a drink) is good for bloodspitters, gastritis, and the abdominal cavity, and it encourages urine"; it "is laid on oedema, and a decoction of this as a mouth rinse is good for toothache"; its seed "(taken in a drink) helps convulsed children, and those bitten by snakes", and "worn as an amulet (by itself) it drives away poisonous creatures". Dioscorides reports the amulet use with "They say". Recording it is not endorsing it, and the 3-15 chapter adds nothing of its own on the point.

**Dioscorides, chapter 3-19, AKANTHA ERPEKANTHA** (heading at line 6689). The editor's line reads: "Acantha vera, [Fuchs], Acanthus sativus, Acanthus mollis [Bauhin, Linnaeus], Acanthus spinosus, — Bears Breeches", and a caption beside the chapter reads "Bears Breeches — Acanthus spinosus, Acanthus mollis". The text: "Acantha or herpacantha grows in gardens and moist rocky places. It has far broader, longer leaves than lettuce, divided like those of eruca [2-170], somewhat dark, thick and smooth. It has a smooth stalk two feet high, the thickness of a finger towards the top, surrounded all around by distances with certain longish little leaves (similar to little hives) of a hyacinth colour. From these the white seed grows out, somewhat long, yellowish, with a head similar to a thyrsus [wand]. The roots underneath are viscous, mouldy, reddish and long. Applied, they are good for burns and dislocations. Taken in a drink they encourage urine and stop discharges of the bowels. They are good for tuberculosis of the lungs, hernia, and convulsions. It is also called melamphyllon, pasderota, acanthus topiaria, mamolaria, or craepula."

The editor therefore lists the two chapters under one binomial although the Greek text separates them, and for 3-19 also lists *Acanthus mollis*. The "wild acantha" of 3-20 (CPU-01876) is a third chapter and a separate row.

### Active Components

In general terms, *Acanthus* is a genus of the family Acanthaceae, of herbs with spiny or lobed leaves, and the "viscous" root of 3-19 is of a kind that fits the family's usual mucilaginous tissues. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** In 3-15 the root, in the manner of the white thistle's root, for excessive menstrual flow, spitting of blood and "other discharges". In 3-19 the root, applied, for burns and dislocations; in a drink, to bring on urine and stop discharges of the bowels, and for tuberculosis of the lungs, hernia and convulsions.
- **What the text does not say.** Neither chapter gives a dose or a duration, and 3-15 gives no preparation.

The Codex does not translate the classic's terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend this plant.

### Preparation & Dosing

The root is applied (3-19) or taken "in a drink" (3-19, and by reference to 3-14 in 3-15). No amount, vehicle or length of use is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any dose from this entry.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Bear's breeches (*Acanthus*) are large-leaved herbs of the Mediterranean grown in gardens for their leaves and flower spikes, and their leaf form was the model for the Corinthian capital in Greek architecture. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** 3-15 prints one quality: the Arabian acantha is "astringent", by comparison with the white thistle. 3-19 prints none for the root, except that it is "viscous". No other quality is printed, and none is imported.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and neither chapter offers one. What they show is the working of a discipline of attention: 3-15 identifies a plant by its likeness to another ("similar in nature to the white thistle") and gives its ground ("rough places"); 3-19 gives the most complete description of this group: habitat (gardens and moist rocky places), leaf, stalk height, flower bracts and seed, and the colour and texture of the root. That is provenance and plant-recognition. It is not a cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here supports any cultivation use. The practical lesson of the two chapters is that a name shared by two chapters is not a guarantee that they describe one plant, and that the maker of a binomial list may have joined what the text keeps apart.
