# Cornelian Cherry — Cornelian Cherry / Cornel / Dogberry (Persian: *zoghal-akhteh*; Turkish: *kızılcık*; Greek: *krania*)

## *Cornus mas* L. | Family: Cornaceae

**Tradition Tags:** AT (Assyrian), CM (Avicenna), DMM (Dioscorides)
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry (second degree) — astringent, styptic, thickening; a "binding" fruit in Galenic-Unani reckoning
**Training Phase:** Purification

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Assyrian (AT):** The cornel (a hard-wooded *Cornus*) belongs to the tree-and-fruit vocabulary of the cuneiform materia medica of Mesopotamia, where astringent fruits were prescribed for "loosening of the bowels" and for fluxes. Cornelian cherry grows wild across Anatolia and the Zagros foothills that fringed the Assyrian world, and its intensely sour, tannin-rich drupe fits squarely the class of drying, bowel-binding fruits the tablets catalogue for diarrhoeal and haemorrhagic complaints.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** In *De Materia Medica*, Dioscorides treats the cornel (*kraneia*) among the astringent, wound-drying plants. The fruit and the decoction of the plant were held to "bind the belly" — the classic Greek indication for an astringent — and the exceptionally dense, close-grained wood (famed for spear-shafts and the shaft of the Trojan-horse tradition) was itself read as a signature of a drying, consolidating nature.

**Avicenna (CM):** In the *Qanun fi'l-Tibb*, cornelian cherry sits among the cold, dry, astringent fruits used to strengthen a weak or over-relaxed stomach, to check diarrhoea and bilious flux, and to temper hot dyscrasias. The Unani tradition that descends from Avicenna still uses the dried fruit and its syrup (*sharbat*) as a stomachic and antidiarrhoeal, and as a cooling agent in "hot" fevers.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Every tradition read the same three signatures — extreme sourness, heavy tannin astringency, and the hardest wood of any European tree — as one coherent message: this is a *binding, consolidating, drying* substance. Modern phytochemistry vindicates the reading. The pucker is iridoid and organic-acid load; the astringency is a genuine polyphenol (anthocyanin, gallotannin, flavonol) burden; and the same polyphenols that "bind" tissue also bind and inhibit the carbohydrate-digesting enzymes α-amylase and α-glucosidase. The folk signature "it tightens" maps almost cleanly onto the measured pharmacology of enzyme inhibition and antioxidant tissue-protection.

### Active Components

- **Anthocyanins** — delphinidin, cyanidin and pelargonidin 3-O-glycosides (and galactosides); the pigment of the red drupe and a principal antioxidant fraction.
- **Iridoids** — loganic acid, cornuside, loganin and secologanin-type glycosides; a chemotaxonomic hallmark of *Cornus* and a major bioactive class.
- **Flavonols and phenolic acids** — quercetin and kaempferol glycosides, gallic and ellagic acid derivatives, hydrolysable tannins.
- **Vitamin C** — cornelian cherry is notably ascorbate-rich, contributing to both flavour and antioxidant capacity.
- **Organic acids and pectin** — malic acid predominant; pectin contributing gelling and mild gut-binding behaviour.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Cornelian cherry is a **Purification-phase astringent-corrective**. Its role is to *tighten and steady* — to bind a loosened gut, to blunt the post-prandial glucose surge, and to load the tissues with anthocyanin and iridoid antioxidants during a phase focused on clearing oxidative and metabolic residue. It pairs the traditional "binding" action (diarrhoea, relaxed stomach, fluxes) with the measured metabolic action (carbohydrate-enzyme inhibition, glycation restraint). In the Kenetica frame it is the fruit reached for when the system needs consolidation rather than stimulation: a cooling, drying, sour corrective taken as syrup, decoction or dried fruit alongside heavier meals.

### Scientific Correlation

Based on articles retrieved from PubMed:

- **Dzydzan et al. (2019), *Food & Function*** — In streptozotocin-diabetic rats, extracts of red and yellow *Cornus mas* fruit (20 mg/kg for 14 days) lowered blood glucose, improved glucose tolerance, and reduced glycated haemoglobin by ~25%; the authors attribute the effect to anthocyanins, iridoids and phenolic acids attenuating hyperglycaemia and oxidative protein/lipid modification. DOI: [10.1039/c9fo00515c](https://doi.org/10.1039/c9fo00515c)
- **Brodyak et al. (2025), *Food & Function*** — Fruit extracts of several cornelian-cherry cultivars (20 mg/kg, 14 days) in streptozotocin-diabetic rats decreased blood-glucose parameters consistent with α-glucosidase inhibition, corrected glycolytic metabolites (pyruvate, L-lactate) and lactate-dehydrogenase activity, and lowered plasma oxidative-stress biomarkers. DOI: [10.1039/d4fo05426a](https://doi.org/10.1039/d4fo05426a)
- **Paun et al. (2024), *Molecules*** — *Cornus mas* fruit extracts showed strong DPPH radical-scavenging (IC₅₀ ≈ 31.8 µg/mL) and inhibited α-amylase and α-glucosidase, and stimulated insulin secretion in β-TC-6 cells under both normal and hyperglycaemic conditions. DOI: [10.3390/molecules29153595](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules29153595)
- **Dinda et al. (2016), *Journal of Ethnopharmacology*** — A systematic review of *Cornus mas* ethnomedicine, phytochemistry and pharmacology: 101 identified compounds (anthocyanins, flavonoids, iridoids predominant); fruits reported non-toxic in acute studies; cited clinical trials in type-2 diabetic and hyperlipidaemic patients showed improvement in glycaemic and lipid parameters. DOI: [10.1016/j.jep.2016.09.042](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2016.09.042)

**Evidence grade: Moderate (preclinical) / Emerging (clinical).** Consistent, mechanistically coherent rodent and in-vitro antidiabetic and antioxidant data, with early human trials reviewed but not yet replicated at scale. Traditional astringent/antidiarrhoeal use is well attested but formally under-studied.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Galenic / Unani:** Cold and dry (2nd degree); astringent (*qabid*), styptic; stomach-strengthening, bowel-binding, cooling in hot dyscrasias.
- **Ayurvedic analogue (not a classical Ayurvedic drug):** by *rasa* would read as *amla–kashaya* (sour–astringent), *shita virya* (cooling potency) — used here descriptively, not as an attested Ayurvedic citation.

### Cultivation Register

**Assyrian (AT).** The cornel belongs to the tree-and-fruit vocabulary of the cuneiform materia medica, where intensely sour, tannin-rich, drying fruits were catalogued for "loosening of the bowels" and for fluxes — the drupe fitting the class of bowel-binding fruits prescribed against diarrhoeal and haemorrhagic complaints in a system that read such illness by its causal agency and treated it by drying and binding. But the standing blocking warning is in force for every Assyrian substance identification and tablet reference, and the attestation here is one of therapeutic use for a flux, not a status assigned to the fruit for tending a person's vitality. No cultivation status is claimed. **(c) absent.**

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Dioscorides treats the cornel (*kraneia*) among the astringent, wound-drying plants, the fruit and its decoction held to "bind the belly" — the classic Greek indication for an astringent — with the exceptionally dense, close-grained wood read as a further signature of a drying, consolidating nature. He carries no subtle-body doctrine into which a cultivation status could be set; what the tradition offers a cultivator is his discipline of attention — reading the plant's signatures (the extreme sourness, the tannin pucker, the hardest wood of any European tree) as one coherent message, and gathering and assaying the fruit at its true ripeness. That is a practice of provenance and sensory judgement, not a doctrine of the vital body. **(c) absent** as to cultivation status.

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** In the *Qanun* tradition cornelian cherry sits among the cold, dry, astringent (*qabid*) fruits used to strengthen a weak or over-relaxed stomach, check diarrhoea and bilious flux, and temper hot dyscrasias — the dried fruit and its *sharbat* still carried as a stomachic and cooling antidiarrhoeal. The corpus's strongest cultivation idea is the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture, the finite vital endowment whose depletion *is* ageing; but that is a longevity frame of the whole regimen, not a status the tradition assigns to a cooling astringent fruit, and no *mufarrih* (gladdening, *ruh*-strengthening) role is claimed for it. "Strengthening the stomach" here names a digestive, humoral action on an organ, not a cultivation of the vital substrate. The fruit is attested therapeutically with no cultivation status recorded. **(b) framed.**

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator uses cornelian cherry to consolidate, not to build — a cooling, sour, drying corrective taken as syrup, decoction, or dried fruit to tighten a loosened gut, steady a relaxed stomach, and (on the measured pharmacology) blunt a post-prandial glucose surge alongside a heavy meal. It is not a longevity substance in any of the three traditions; its standing everywhere is that of a binding therapeutic reached for when the system needs steadying and set down once it is steadied. Watch the astringency in the constipated and the glucose-lowering action alongside antidiabetic drugs, and keep it to the job it does well.

### Contraindications

- **Constipation / atonic bowel:** the astringent, binding action can aggravate a sluggish or dry constipated state.
- **Hypoglycaemia risk with antidiabetic drugs:** given the demonstrated glucose-lowering action, monitor when combined with insulin or oral hypoglycaemics; dose adjustment may be needed [USER: verify with prescriber].
- **Very "cold/dry" constitutions (Unani reading):** traditionally tempered with honey or warming spices.
- **Pits:** the stone is hard and inedible — remove before decoction or consumption.
- Pregnancy/lactation: no reliable safety data at medicinal (extract) doses; culinary amounts of the fruit are traditional foods.
