# Sweet Flag / Calamus — Vacā (वचा) / Waj (واج) / Shu dag (ཤུ་དག) / Calamus aromaticus
## *Acorus calamus* L. / *Acorus americanus* Raf. / *Acorus gramineus* Aiton | Family: Acoraceae
**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, BM, DMM, SA, SS, FMT
**Energetic Classification (TCM):** ☿ Shen primary (CNS / cognitive / nervous system) — opens the orifices, clarifies the mind, dissolves phlegm misting the Heart; AChE inhibition; dose-dependent CNS paradox (low-dose clarifying, high-dose sedating) / ◉ Qi secondary (digestive carminative) — warms and arouses the Middle Burner, relieves intestinal stagnation and colic through smooth-muscle relaxation
**Training Phase:** `[REFINE]` `[RESET]`

---

> **SAFETY CLASSIFICATION — READ BEFORE USE:**
> *Acorus calamus* contains β-asarone, a documented animal carcinogen (IARC Group 2B — possible human carcinogen; DNA adduct formation confirmed in rat studies). β-asarone content varies dramatically by chemotype — from 0% in *A. americanus* (North American diploid) to up to 96% in Indian tetraploid varieties. The essential oil of high-asarone varieties is prohibited for internal use in the EU (Directive 88/388/EEC). Internal use recommendations in this entry apply exclusively to verified β-asarone-free preparations (*A. americanus*) or to low-asarone standardized extracts. Traditional aqueous decoctions of the rhizome — the historical form across all documented traditions — extract minimal essential oil content and represent a substantially different pharmacological exposure than essential oil preparations. All internal use recommendations are for short-course application (maximum 2–4 weeks) with the precautionary chemotype verification described in the Preparation section.

### Origin & Tradition

Sweet flag — the common name reflecting both the flag-iris leaf shape and the intensely aromatic sweetness of the rhizome — has one of the broadest cross-traditional footprints in the Codex. Its documented use spans six of the eight source traditions, appearing in South Asian, Greco-Roman, Arabic, Tibetan, Chinese, and Mesoamerican-adjacent pharmacopoeias with a pharmacological consistency that reflects a genuine, directly observable CNS and digestive effect. The plant's ancient cultivation history contributes to its pan-traditional presence: *Acorus calamus* was among the earliest aromatic herbs traded along the ancient spice routes of Central Asia, entering the Mediterranean basin from India and Southeast Asia by at least the 5th century BCE. In each tradition, it found application at the intersection of the cognitive and digestive systems — a pharmacological territory now coherently explained by its dual AChE inhibition and carminative mechanisms.

**Ayurvedic Tradition (SS — Sushruta Samhita):**
Vacā (वचा — the Ayurvedic name, meaning "speech" or "voice") is one of the oldest and most ceremonially significant Ayurvedic herbs, classified as a medhya rasayana — specifically, a substance that enhances intelligence and memory (medha) through rejuvenative (rasayana) action on the nervous system. The *Sushruta Samhita* and *Charaka Samhita* both document its primary application as a cognitive enhancer and voice-clarifier, with the name itself encoding the traditional belief in its specific action on speech and articulation. A remarkable traditional practice documented in Ayurvedic pediatric medicine (*Kaumara Bhritya*) involves applying a small amount of Vacā paste to the tongue of infants at the time of weaning, with the intention of "opening the voice" and stimulating early language development and cognitive capacity. This practice — which has parallels in several South Asian folk traditions — reflects an empirically observed connection between Vacā's neurological stimulation and the development of verbal intelligence.

The Ayurvedic energetic classification identifies Vacā as a Vata-pacifying herb — specifically targeting the *prana vata* (the vital breath associated with the brain and sensory organ function) and *udana vata* (the upward-moving air associated with speech, memory, and mental clarity). The *Ashtanga Hridayam* lists it in formulas for unmada (mental disorders, psychosis), apasmara (epilepsy), and as a specific nootropic agent for clarity of mind and sharpening of intellect. Vacā ghrita (Vacā-medicated ghee) is a classical preparation for cognitive enhancement and neurological support.

The Indian tetraploid chemotype of *A. calamus* — the variety most common in traditional Ayurvedic practice — has the highest β-asarone content (up to 96% of essential oil). This creates a significant modern safety concern relative to the traditional practice: traditional Ayurvedic preparations employed aqueous decoctions and ghee-based preparations that extract dramatically less essential oil than modern ethanol tinctures or essential oil preparations, and traditional use was typically in lower doses with careful titration and under practitioner supervision. The traditional Ayurvedic framework's inherent conservatism around dosing likely provided a practical margin of safety that is lost in modern self-administration of concentrated preparations.

**Greco-Roman / Dioscorides Tradition (DMM):**
Dioscorides (*De Materia Medica*, Book I, Chapter 17) catalogues sweet flag under the name *Akoron* or *Kalamoseuōdēs* (fragrant reed) — distinguishing it from the cultivated sweet flag by its wild Asian origin and superior aromatic potency. He characterizes it as warming and diuretic, and recommends it for the liver, bladder, and joints; for ruptures and convulsions; as a component of eye medicines; and as a stomachic for digestive complaints. His account of *Akoron* as a warming, aromatic, antispasmodic substance is pharmacologically coherent with the modern understanding of the rhizome's volatile sesquiterpene and phenylpropanoid content, which produce genuine smooth-muscle relaxant and carminative effects even in aqueous preparations.

Pliny the Elder (*Naturalis Historia*) notes the plant's importance in Roman perfumery and medicine, recording that the finest specimens came from India and were traded through Phoenician and Syrian merchants — confirming the ancient Silk Road commodity status of the rhizome. The *Calamus aromaticus* of Roman medical texts corresponds to the sweet flag rhizome rather than the fragrant reed (*Cymbopogon* species) with which it is sometimes confused. Roman aromatic preparations incorporated calamus as a constituent of compound medicinal perfumes and incenses — reflecting the boundary between olfactory medicine (aromatherapy in modern terminology) and internal administration that characterizes calamus use across multiple traditions.

**Arabic / Unani Tradition (AT):**
Ibn Sina (*Al-Qanun fi al-Tibb*) documents *Waj* (واج) — Arabic for sweet flag — as warming and drying in the third degree, placing it among the more vigorous warming aromatics. He prescribes it specifically for "weakness of the brain and memory impairment" (*du'f al-dimagh wa nisan al-dhikr*) — the most direct ancient statement of a nootropic application for calamus — as well as for epilepsy, flatulence, jaundice, and articular pain. His formulation of calamus preparations includes compound pills and electuaries (honey-based semisolid preparations) in which the stimulating cognitive action is moderated by the sweetness of the honey vehicle — anticipating the Anupana principle of Ayurvedic formulation. Ibn al-Baytar (13th century) elaborates on its use for memory, paralysis, and cognitive decline in the elderly.

The Arabic pharmacological tradition's classification of Waj as a "cerebral strengthener" (*muqawwi al-dimagh*) — a specific category of cognitive-supporting substances — represents the most explicit traditional articulation of calamus as a primary nootropic, as distinct from a general sedative or tonic. The mechanism now understood to underlie this: acetylcholinesterase inhibition by β-asarone (and the non-asarone constituent acoramone), increasing synaptic acetylcholine availability in the hippocampus and cortex — the identical mechanism as the approved Alzheimer's drugs donepezil and rivastigmine.

**Tibetan Medicine (FMT — rGyud bzhi):**
Shu dag (ཤུ་དག) — the Tibetan name for sweet flag — is classified in the *rGyud bzhi* as a warming aromatic with specific action on rLung (Wind) disorders and mental clarity. It appears in compound formulas for both nervous system support and digestive stagnation — the two application poles that characterize its cross-traditional use. The Tibetan application of calamus in rLung formulas is pharmacologically consonant: rLung disorders are characterized by racing thoughts, anxiety, scattered attention, insomnia, and dysregulation of the wind-energy, which corresponds broadly to the cholinergic deficit pattern that AChE inhibition addresses.

The Tibetan tradition also employs calamus in aromatic fumigation preparations — a practice that crosses into the BM (Bodily Medicine) category where aromatic smoke is used for both environmental and psychological effects. This route of administration — aromatic inhalation — is significant from a modern safety perspective: fumigation/incense applications avoid internal administration entirely, bypassing the carcinogenicity concern while potentially retaining relevant olfactory-CNS effects through the nasal mucosa-olfactory bulb pathway.

**Chinese Tradition (CM):**
Shí Chāng Pú (石菖蒲 — *Acorus gramineus* or *A. tatarinowii*) is the Chinese medicinal species — notably distinct from *A. calamus* and containing significantly lower β-asarone concentrations in most preparations. The Chinese medical literature therefore partially sidesteps the β-asarone concern by using a different species. Shí Chāng Pú is classified in the CM pharmacopoeia as a Shen-clarifying, orifice-opening herb — specifically documented to "open the nine orifices," dissolve phlegm that mists the Heart and obstructs the mind, and sharpen the senses and memory. The *Bencao Gangmu* records Shí Chāng Pú as one of the classical herbs for mental clarity and cognitive enhancement, consumed long-term by Taoist practitioners seeking mental acuity. The lower-asarone Chinese species represents the pharmacologically preferable modern analogue for internal use, as it retains meaningful AChE-inhibitory and cognitive-clarifying activity through acoramone and other constituents while having a substantially better safety profile.

**Mesoamerican / Bodily Medicine Tradition (BM):**
While *Acorus calamus* is not native to the Americas, several Mesoamerican traditions employ closely parallel aromatic reeds in ceremonial and medicinal fumigation preparations. The convergent use of aromatic reed preparations for CNS-clarifying, ritual-space-clearing, and digestive applications across pre-Columbian traditions parallels the sweet flag applications in the Old World traditions functionally, if not botanically. Following European contact, calamus was introduced to the Americas and rapidly integrated into folk medicine traditions in both North America (where Native American groups, including the Cree nation, are documented to have adopted calamus use for fatigue, pain, and as a stimulant — chewing small pieces of the fresh rhizome) and in Latin American herbal traditions. The North American usage is pharmacologically significant because *Acorus americanus* — the indigenous North American diploid species — is essentially β-asarone-free, making the Native American traditional use of fresh rhizome chewing the safest of all historical calamus preparations.

### Active Components

- **β-asarone (trans-asarone, (E)-asarone)** — primary phenylpropanoid constituent of most *A. calamus* chemotypes; CNS-stimulating at low doses (increased locomotor activity, improved learning in animal models), CNS-depressant at high doses (sedative, anesthetic); documented **acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitor** with IC50 = 3.0 μg/mL — this is the pharmacologically critical mechanism for cognitive applications; shares mechanism with FDA-approved Alzheimer's drugs donepezil (IC50 ~0.006 μg/mL — substantially more potent) and rivastigmine; additionally documented as NMDA receptor antagonist (anti-excitotoxic, anticonvulsant); also an antifungal and antibacterial agent; **CARCINOGEN in animal studies** — IARC Group 2B (possibly carcinogenic to humans); forms DNA adducts in rat liver and duodenum at doses achievable through concentrated preparations; absent in *A. americanus*; 5–10% of essential oil in European triploid; up to 96% of essential oil in Indian tetraploid; the discrepancy between traditional safety (long historical use of aqueous preparations) and modern concerns (concentrated preparations) is explained by the low essential oil yield of aqueous extraction versus modern solvent/ethanol extraction and essential oil distillation

- **α-asarone (cis-asarone)** — geometric isomer of β-asarone; found in smaller quantities in most calamus varieties; also potentially mutagenic; similar CNS profile but less extensively studied; generally co-occurs with β-asarone in chemotypes that contain it

- **Acoramone** — diterpenoid; acetylcholinesterase inhibitor; present across chemotypes including low-asarone varieties; may represent a significant contribution to cognitive activity in β-asarone-free preparations; less extensively characterized than β-asarone but pharmacologically relevant

- **Asaronaldehyde** — oxidation product of asarone; anti-inflammatory; antimicrobial; less CNS-active than the parent asarones; present in most chemotypes

- **Eugenol** — phenylpropanoid; anti-inflammatory (COX-2 inhibition); analgesic; antibacterial; also present in clove (*Syzygium aromaticum*); contributes to the carminative action

- **Methyl isoeugenol** — structural analog of eugenol; antimicrobial; carminative; present in the aromatic complex of most calamus preparations

- **Galangin** — flavonol; anti-inflammatory; neuroprotective (documented protection against glutamate-induced excitotoxicity in hippocampal neurons); antifungal; present in small quantities

- **Quercetin** — flavonol; anti-inflammatory; antioxidant; neuroprotective; contributes background anti-inflammatory action

- **Acoradin, acorenone, isocalamendiol** — sesquiterpene ketones and alcohols; antimicrobial; anti-inflammatory; contribute to the aromatic profile and antimicrobial applications; less pharmacologically characterized than the phenylpropanoids

- **Linalool** — monoterpene alcohol; anxiolytic (documented GABA-A modulation and adenosine receptor interaction); anti-inflammatory; anti-nociceptive; present in the essential oil of most calamus varieties; this compound — also prominent in lavender and coriander — may contribute to the low-dose anxiolytic/calming component of traditional calamus applications that precede the onset of the more stimulating asarone effects

- **Tannins (hydrolyzable and condensed fractions)** — astringent; antimicrobial; contribute to the anti-diarrheal application documented in the Arabic tradition; inhibit intestinal pathogen adhesion

- **Volatile fatty acids (palmitic, stearic, oleic)** — minor constituents; contribute to rhizome nutritional profile in whole-root decoctions

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Calamus occupies a carefully delimited application domain in the Kenetica pharmacopoeia — it is one of the most cross-traditionally documented cognitive herbs in the Codex, with the strongest ancient convergence on nootropic indication, but its modern safety profile requires that application be confined to β-asarone-free preparations or traditional aqueous forms, and framed primarily within the REFINE and RESET phases where the CNS-clarifying and carminative mechanisms are most directly applicable.

**1. Cognitive clarity and acetylcholine support — the primary REFINE application:**
The AChE inhibition mechanism of β-asarone (and the safer analogue acoramone) provides a mechanistically grounded cognitive-enhancement application that is particularly relevant during the REFINE phase, where mental precision, sustained attention, and technical skill execution are the performance demands. Unlike stimulant nootropics that work via monoamine enhancement (increasing dopamine/norepinephrine/adrenaline), AChE inhibition works through the cholinergic system — the neurotransmitter axis most directly associated with working memory, precision learning, attention to detail, and motor skill encoding. For practitioners in skill-intensive training phases — technical sports, martial arts, precision strength training — the cholinergic mechanism is arguably more aligned with the specific cognitive demands than central stimulation. The classical Ayurvedic medhya rasayana classification of Vacā — "sharpening of memory and intelligence" — is pharmacologically coherent with the cholinergic mechanism.

**2. The dose-dependent CNS paradox and its traditional applications:**
The documented dose-dependent bifurcation of calamus CNS action — stimulating and clarifying at low doses, sedating at higher doses — parallels the traditional differentiation between medhya (mind-sharpening) use at low-to-moderate doses and the occasional high-dose sedative or anticonvulsant application documented in Tibetan and Unani traditions. This pharmacological duality reflects the complex interaction between the multiple active constituents: β-asarone's AChE inhibition provides the low-dose cognitive stimulation; the NMDA antagonist action contributes anticonvulsant and sedative effects that predominate at higher concentrations; linalool's GABA-A-modulatory component provides background anxiolysis across both dose ranges. In Kenetica application, the low-dose window — targeting AChE inhibition with minimal NMDA antagonism — is the relevant therapeutic zone for REFINE phase cognitive support.

**3. Digestive carminative and the GI-CNS connection:**
The ◉ Qi secondary classification of calamus reflects its equally well-documented application as a warming digestive carminative. Eugenol and methyl isoeugenol relax GI smooth muscle, reducing intestinal spasm and flatulence; the warming volatile aromatics stimulate digestive secretions and peristalsis. In the RESET phase, when high training volume has disrupted the gut-brain axis (through elevated cortisol, altered gut motility, and digestive suppression from sympathetic dominance), a warming carminative that simultaneously supports CNS recovery addresses both the somatic (digestive) and neural (cognitive) dimensions of recovery. The traditional use of Vacā as the first substance given to infants — simultaneously for voice development and digestive regulation — reflects this ancient intuition about the GI-CNS connection.

**4. Aromatherapy / fumigation — the safety-preserved application:**
The traditional Tibetan and Assyrian fumigation applications of calamus — burning the rhizome as incense or in aromatic preparations — represent a route of administration that largely bypasses the carcinogenicity concern. β-asarone pyrolysis products in smoke differ chemically from the parent compound; olfactory-bulb-mediated CNS effects of aromatic volatile inhalation are documented for multiple terpenes and phenylpropanoids. For practitioners who wish to access the traditional "opening the orifices" / cognitive-space-clearing applications of calamus without internal administration, aromatic preparations using small quantities of dried rhizome — burned as incense, incorporated into steam inhalation, or diffused — represent the most historically authentic and the safest modern application.

**5. Phase placement and the safety constraint:**
Calamus is placed in REFINE and RESET phases rather than BUILD because its primary value is in cognitive precision and recovery — not in volume or anabolic accumulation. The safety constraint — β-asarone-free preparation only for internal use — means the practitioner must make an active chemotype verification decision before internal use. This is not a casual supplementation substance; it is a historically documented, pharmacologically rich herb whose modern application requires more deliberate sourcing than most entries in Volume I.

### Preparation & Dosing

> **CHEMOTYPE VERIFICATION IS MANDATORY BEFORE INTERNAL USE.** All internal use recommendations apply exclusively to verified *A. americanus* (North American diploid, β-asarone-free) or to commercially standardized low-asarone preparations confirmed to contain <0.1% β-asarone. European and Indian *A. calamus* preparations should not be used internally without laboratory-confirmed asarone analysis. The rhizome sourced in the EU is subject to Directive 88/388/EEC restrictions; commercially available "calamus essential oil" in most markets derives from high-asarone varieties and should not be used internally under any circumstances.

| Form | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Dried rhizome decoction (*A. americanus*) | 0.5–1g dried rhizome per 300mL water | Traditional form; lowest asarone exposure of all preparations; carminative and mild cognitive application |
| *A. americanus* root powder | 0.5–1g/day | In capsule; verified β-asarone-free; short courses only (2–4 weeks maximum) |
| *A. gramineus* (Chinese species) standardized extract | 250–500mg/day | Preferred modern supplement form; lower baseline asarone vs. Indian variety; most commercial "Acorus" nootropic preparations use this species |
| Aromatherapy / diffusion | Standard diffuser quantities | Dried rhizome or low-volume essential oil diffusion; CNS-clarifying olfactory application; safest route |
| Incense / fumigation | Traditional preparation | Burn small quantities of dried rhizome; smoke inhalation — traditional application; carcinogen pyrolysis products differ from oral exposure |
| Indian tetraploid essential oil (internal) | CONTRAINDICATED | 75–96% β-asarone; do not administer internally |

**Preparation notes:**
- **Aqueous decoction:** The traditional preparation method across all documented traditions minimizes essential oil extraction (hot water is a poor solvent for volatile phenylpropanoids); this represents a substantially lower β-asarone exposure than ethanol tinctures or essential oil preparations while retaining water-soluble flavonoids and tannins; in *A. americanus*, the asarone concern is eliminated and decoction extracts the acoramone and flavonoid cognitive-support fraction effectively
- **Ghee preparation (Vacā ghrita):** The traditional Ayurvedic ghee-based preparation extracts lipophilic constituents, including β-asarone (if present in the source variety); for this reason, Vacā ghrita made from Indian tetraploid root carries a substantially higher asarone exposure than aqueous preparations; if preparing Vacā ghrita for Ayurvedic cognitive applications, verify *A. americanus* sourcing

**Kenetica cycle guidance:**
- REFINE phase: *A. americanus* decoction (0.5–1g) or verified extract daily; maximum 2–4 consecutive weeks; cognitive clarity, attention, technical precision support
- RESET phase: aromatic/fumigation application primarily; digestive carminative decoction at lower dose (0.5g) if GI stagnation is a component of the recovery presentation
- BUILD phase: not indicated; calamus does not contribute meaningfully to anabolic volume accumulation and the safety constraint counsels against routine long-term use

### Scientific Correlation

**Acetylcholinesterase inhibition — the central mechanistic finding:**
Mukherjee PK et al. (2007, *Phytotherapy Research*) and multiple subsequent studies document β-asarone's AChE inhibitory activity with IC50 values in the 3.0–4.5 μg/mL range across different assay conditions — a potency approximately two orders of magnitude below donepezil (IC50 approximately 0.006 μg/mL) but within the range of pharmacologically meaningful cholinergic support at concentrations achievable with traditional preparations of high-asarone varieties. Acoramone demonstrates AChE inhibition in the same range across independently conducted assays, establishing a non-asarone cognitive mechanism of relevance for β-asarone-free preparations.

**Memory enhancement — animal evidence:**
Bhatt S et al. (2009, *Neuropharmacology*): β-asarone at 2.5 mg/kg improved spatial learning and memory consolidation in the Morris water maze in rodents — with associated increases in hippocampal acetylcholine levels measured by microdialysis. The dose used corresponds to approximately 0.2mg/kg human equivalent (applying the standard rodent-human body surface area conversion factor) — well within the range of traditional decoction doses at traditional consumption levels. Subsequent studies by Singh UP et al. (2011) confirmed memory improvement via AChE inhibition with hippocampal acetylcholine quantification, providing mechanistic validation of the behavioral findings.

**Anticonvulsant:**
Bhattacharya SK et al. (1997, *Phytomedicine*): aqueous and ethanol extracts of *A. calamus* demonstrated dose-dependent anticonvulsant effects against pentylenetetrazole and maximal electroshock-induced seizures in rodents — establishing both a GABA-potentiating and an NMDA-antagonist mechanism. The anticonvulsant effect parallels the traditional applications for epilepsy documented by Dioscorides, Ibn Sina, and the Ayurvedic tradition independently. The dual mechanism (GABA potentiation at low dose contributing to the anxiolytic/calming effect; NMDA antagonism at higher doses producing the anticonvulsant effect) illuminates the dose-dependent CNS paradox documented historically.

**Carcinogenicity — the critical safety data:**
Wiseman RW et al. (1987, *Carcinogenesis*) and Hasheminejad G and Caldwell J (1994, *Food and Chemical Toxicology*): β-asarone produced dose-dependent malignant intestinal tumors (duodenal carcinomas) in rat feeding studies at doses above 5 mg/kg/day over 26–130 weeks; DNA adduct formation was confirmed in liver and duodenal tissue by 32P-postlabeling analysis; β-asarone forms an epoxide intermediate (via CYP1A2 oxidation) that binds DNA at the N6-position of adenosine and C8-position of guanosine. IARC classified β-asarone as Group 2B (possibly carcinogenic to humans) based on sufficient evidence in animals and limited human data. Critical dose context: the rat carcinogenic dose translates to a human equivalent of approximately 0.4–0.8 mg/kg/day by body surface area correction — achievable through concentrated preparations of Indian variety root but not through traditional aqueous decoctions of any variety, and irrelevant for *A. americanus* preparations.

**Carminative and GI pharmacology:**
Gilani AH et al. (2006, *Phytotherapy Research*): spasmolytic (smooth-muscle relaxant) effects of *A. calamus* extract were documented in isolated guinea pig ileum preparations; the effect was abolished by calcium channel blockers, implicating calcium channel antagonism as the primary carminative mechanism. This establishes the pharmacological basis for the pan-traditional application of calamus for intestinal colic and flatulence.

### Contraindications

**β-asarone — carcinogenicity (THE PRIMARY SAFETY CONCERN):**
The Indian tetraploid chemotype of *A. calamus* — the most common form in traditional Ayurvedic trade and in many commercial herbal preparations sold as "calamus root" without chemotype specification — contains up to 96% β-asarone in the essential oil fraction, corresponding to β-asarone concentrations of approximately 2–9% of dried root weight. DNA adduct formation from β-asarone occurs in animal studies and is dose-dependent. Preparations of this chemotype should not be used internally in concentrated or extended-course form. The EU prohibition on calamus essential oil in food products reflects this concern. This is not a theoretical risk extrapolated from exotic exposure conditions — it is a pharmacologically documented carcinogenic mechanism at concentrations achievable through non-aqueous extraction of the Indian variety.

**Epilepsy — paradoxical seizure risk:**
The CNS-stimulating action of β-asarone at low doses can lower seizure threshold in predisposed individuals despite the anticonvulsant effect seen at higher doses in animal models. The net effect in an epileptic patient at sub-anticonvulsant doses is unpredictable. Calamus preparations should not be used in patients with active seizure disorders or epilepsy without specialist neurological oversight.

**MAOI interaction:**
The combination of calamus preparations with monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs — including phenelzine, tranylcypromine, and the herbal MAOI Banisteriopsis caapi) represents a poorly characterized but potentially significant interaction: β-asarone has documented effects on monoamine systems that could be amplified by MAO inhibition, potentially producing excessive CNS stimulation or blood pressure effects. The traditional Amazonian combination of Acorus-adjacent aromatic reeds with MAO-containing preparations in some BM contexts is not an endorsement of this combination for modern use.

**Pregnancy and lactation:** Emmenagogue activity (uterine stimulant) has been documented for *A. calamus* preparations in animal studies; combined with the carcinogenicity concern, calamus preparations of any variety should be strictly avoided during pregnancy and lactation.

**Pediatric use:** Despite the traditional Ayurvedic practice of applying Vacā to infant tongues, modern application of calamus to children is not recommended outside of specialist Ayurvedic medical practice using verified β-asarone-free *A. americanus* preparations; the carcinogenicity concern is amplified in the pediatric population given longer lifetime exposure and higher tissue sensitivity.

**Hepatic impairment:** β-asarone is metabolized by CYP1A2 to its carcinogenic epoxide; impaired hepatic function may alter this metabolism unpredictably (either reducing or increasing the epoxide intermediate). Avoid in patients with significant hepatic disease.

**Extended use:** Even with β-asarone-free preparations, calamus use beyond 4 consecutive weeks is not supported by adequate safety data. The precautionary principle applies. Cycle with herb-free intervals.
