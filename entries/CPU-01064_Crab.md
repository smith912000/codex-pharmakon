# Crab — Karkinoi (crevises, river crabs, sea crabs) / Crab (Bryan's English) / Crab, with chhu.sbur (Tibetan ant) in the Tibetan line
## *Crabs (Brachyura): the translator of Dioscorides suggests* Cancer pagurus *[Brachyura]; Bryan gives only "Crab"; Clark gives only "Crab"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP, DMM, FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** The English text on disk is C. P. Bryan's 1930 *The Papyrus Ebers*, a translation with summaries; no Egyptian word and no binomial is printed for the animal. Bryan's Chapter VII "Organic Remedies" lists the animals "whose identity is not in doubt" and "Crab" stands in the last group of that list (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt` line 1404: "Ass Hog Dog Cat Frog Tadpole Scorpion Eel Electric Eel Crab Swordfish..."; `Sources/EP_Ebers/extract/names.csv` row "Crab"). The only crab prescription found in Bryan's text is in the chapter on the hair: among the remedies for grey hairs Bryan writes that "the 'Bile-of-Many-Crabs' conjures up a messy mass, and a smelly one too", and that it was, like "Dried-Tadpoles-from-the-Canal, crushed in Oil", "applied to the outside of the head" (line 4055, in context lines 4046-4058). That is Bryan's paraphrase; the preparation is not given in detail and the original Egyptian reading is not shown. Whether other prescriptions use crab under another name cannot be told from the translation. [USER: verify]

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-12, *Karkinoi*, translator's suggestion "Cancer pagurus [Brachyura] — Crabs" (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 4964). The whole chapter: "Two spoonfuls of ashes of burnt crevises or river crabs with one spoonful of the root of gentian (taken as a drink in wine for three days together) evidently helps one bitten by a mad dog. With boiled honey they soothe cracks in the feet and perineum, as well as chilblains and diseases of the cornea. Pounded raw and taken as a drink with an ass's milk they help snakebites, and the strikes of harvest spiders and scorpions. Boiled and eaten with their broth they are good for those in consumption [wasting disease], and those who have swallowed a sea hare [2-20]. Pounded together with basil and laid out for scorpions, they kill them. Sea crabs can do the same things but they work somewhat less effectively than these."

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** In Clark's translation of the Explanatory Tantra ch.20, under the animal medicines, the line sits at the end of the "Droppings" paragraph, which then runs on into insects and small animals: "Crab: [has nine names], and chhu.sbur: Tibetan ant: Both dispel urinary retention. (Tibetan ant in particular is a mild purgative of channel disorders)" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` line 1199). The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 text gives the same in fewer words: "Crabs and water beetles cure obstruction of urine" (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 3572). The two translations pair the crab with different companions (Tibetan ant in Clark, water beetles in Men-Tsee-Khang), and I did not check which is closer to the Tibetan. The Men-Tsee-Khang index lists "Crabs 217" (line 7310). The species is not named in either text.

### Active Components

Crabs are crustaceans. In general the body is protein-rich flesh within a shell (exoskeleton) made of chitin with calcium carbonate; the flesh and the hepatopancreas ("tomalley") contain, as in other crustaceans, proteins, minerals and fats. Crustacean shell is the commercial source of chitin and chitosan. Shellfish carry well-known allergens (tropomyosin, a muscle protein). Nothing in the three texts says which part of the crab was used beyond Dioscorides's "ashes of burnt" crabs and the "broth", and Bryan's "Bile". No quantities are given because none were read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The three traditions record crab as a drug for a named complaint: mad-dog bite, cracked skin, eye and chilblain troubles, venom bites and consumption in Dioscorides; a hair remedy in Ebers; and urinary retention in the Tibetan texts. The Codex has no protocol for any of them. The mad-dog bite use in particular must not be taken as a treatment: rabies is a fatal disease that needs prompt medical care. The Tibetan use for urinary retention, an acute and dangerous condition, also calls for medical care, not a crab.

### Preparation & Dosing

Dioscorides gives the preparations with a measure for the first: "two spoonfuls of ashes of burnt crevises or river crabs" with "one spoonful of the root of gentian", taken in wine "for three days together" (2-12). Other forms given: crabs with boiled honey as a salve; crabs pounded raw and drunk with ass's milk; crabs boiled and eaten with their broth; crabs pounded with basil and laid out for scorpions. The Egyptian remedy is "Bile-of-Many-Crabs" applied to the head, with no further method in Bryan's text. The Tibetan texts give no method or dose in the lines read. No dose is given for the other uses in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General facts only. Crustacean shell is chitin and calcium carbonate; shellfish are a leading food allergen. Raw or undercooked freshwater crabs are a documented source of the lung fluke *Paragonimus* in parts of Asia, Africa and the Americas. No clinical studies of the traditional uses (rabies, consumption, urinary retention, hair colour) were retrieved for this entry, and crab ash is not an established treatment for any of them. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** 2-12 prints no quality (warming, cooling, astringent) for crabs. It does rank "sea crabs" as acting "somewhat less effectively" than river crabs.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The translated lines print an action only, "dispel urinary retention" (Clark) and "cure obstruction of urine" (Men-Tsee-Khang), with no taste or potency.

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** None is printed; the corpus is organised by complaint.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* The only crab prescription found is the hair remedy "Bile-of-Many-Crabs", which Bryan describes as a messy, smelly application to the outside of the head for grey hair. It is a remedy for a complaint, not an account of what crab does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); Bryan reports none with this remedy in the lines read, and since he summarises, that is not proof the papyrus gives none. No deity is attributed. Because Ebers identifications are modern hypotheses, the Egyptian animal behind "Crab" is Bryan's rendering.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What chapter 2-12 gives a reader is a discipline of attention in what it distinguishes: river crabs or crevises, which work well, against sea crabs, "somewhat less effectively", and the form to use for each complaint (ash in wine for a bite, boiled with broth for consumption, raw with ass's milk for venom). The chapter assigns no status beyond the therapeutic.

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* The crab is a minor entry in the Explanatory Tantra's list of animal medicines, an animal of the "droppings and small creatures" run, given for one action, dispelling urinary retention. The animal-medicine lines read (Clark lines 1147-1199) name no essence-extraction (*bcud len*) use, and no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists in this tradition; none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing to do. Across the three texts the crab is a remedy for a complaint, and two of the three lines are one-liners. A cultivator would use the chapter, if at all, to see how Dioscorides graded two kinds of crab and matched form to complaint, and then leave it on the page.

### Contraindications

- **Shellfish allergy.** Crab can cause severe allergic reactions including anaphylaxis.
- **Parasites and infection.** Raw or undercooked freshwater crab carries a risk of lung fluke (*Paragonimus*); shellfish can also carry bacterial and viral pathogens and algal toxins. [USER: verify specifics]
- **Rabies.** The Dioscorides mad-dog bite remedy is not a treatment; a bite from a possibly rabid animal needs urgent medical care.
- **Urinary retention.** An emergency; do not treat with a folk remedy.
- **Conservation.** Some crab species are overfished or protected; harvest rules vary by place.
