# Schistos stone — Schistos Lithos (σχιστὸς λίθος), "Schistus" in the haematite chapter (Dioscorides 5-145)
## *A saffron-coloured, veined, brittle stone from Spain that has "the same uses as haematite only with less strength"; the translator's suggestion is "Crystalline Laminated Rock"* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek Tradition (DMM).** The main locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-145 *SCHISTOS LITHOS* in the 2000 English translation (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 10590-10592; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv` line 918; book index "SCHISTOS LITHOS 818", line 12554, and "Schistos stone 818", line 17420; list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 871, "Schistos Lithos, , mineral, DMM V, id_uncertain yes"). The translator's heading is "SUGGESTED: Crystalline Laminated Rock."

Dioscorides' account, read in full, is:

"Schistos stone is found in Spain towards the west. That which looks like saffron in colour seems to be the best — brittle, easy to clean, compacted like amoniacum salt, with similar veins. It has the same uses as haematite only with less strength. Diluted with women's milk it fills up hollow sores, and it is very effective for hernia, prolapse of the eyes, thickness of the eyelids, and staphylomata [protrusion of the cornea]." (line 10592)

**Reading the chapter.** (1) Source: Spain, "towards the west". (2) Choice: the saffron-coloured kind "seems to be the best", with the marks "brittle, easy to clean, compacted like amoniacum salt, with similar veins". (3) Strength: "the same uses as haematite only with less strength". (4) Uses in its own voice: diluted with women's milk it "fills up hollow sores"; it is "very effective" for hernia, prolapse of the eyes, thickness of the eyelids and staphylomata. No preparation of the stone itself (burning, washing) is given in this chapter.

**The haematite chapter it refers to.** 5-144 *Aimatites Lithos* (line 10583) was read. Haematite there is "astringent, somewhat warming, and reduces the intensity of symptoms"; with honey it "wears off scars and scabs on the eyes", with women's milk it helps sore and bloodshot eyes, in a drink with wine it is given for painful urination, with pomegranate juice for women's discharges, and for spitting blood; eye salves, suppositories and touchstones are made of it (lines 10585-10589). Schistos is said to share those uses at lesser strength.

**Schistus as a counterfeit.** In the same haematite chapter (line 10589), the counterfeiters "take a piece of schistus [crystalline laminated rock], thick and round (such as those called the roots of it) and cover it in a big-bellied earthen jar containing hot ash", then rub it on a touchstone to see whether it has taken the colour of haematite. The adulteration is detected by the veins ("this has straight veins but haematites does not") and by colour (brighter, where haematite is "deeper ... like cinnabar"). So the same stone appears twice in Dioscorides: as a drug of lesser strength, and as the stock from which false haematite was made. The two chapters do not conflict; the haematite chapter is warning that a veined schistus can be heated to pass for haematite.

**Identity.** The translator's "Crystalline Laminated Rock" is a descriptive phrase (a layered rock), not a mineral name. The chapter gives colour (saffron), a vein pattern ("similar" to the veins of ammoniac salt), brittleness and a Spanish origin. It does not say what mineral it is. Whether the stone's resemblance to haematite is chemical (an iron oxide) or only in the look is not stated. The list row marks the identity uncertain. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Stated generally. The text gives no composition. A "crystalline laminated rock" is a layered rock or mineral of unspecified kind, and the passage that makes a counterfeit haematite by heating it in hot ash and judging by the colour that results suggests that it changes colour on heating, which is consistent with an iron-bearing mineral but does not show it. Haematite itself is an iron oxide. No composition is asserted for schistos.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The chapter records a stone used as a weaker form of haematite on sores and on the eye and eyelid, and for hernia and prolapse. Putting a powdered mineral in the eye or on a hernia is not supported by anything retrieved, and the sources give no protocol. No modern protocol is supplied.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparation given is dilution: "diluted with women's milk it fills up hollow sores" (line 10592). The stone is not otherwise prepared in this chapter. Haematite's preparation (burning "like Phrygian [dyer's] stone, only the wine omitted", until "mildly light and swollen up with bubbles", line 10589) is given for haematite and is not stated of schistos. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] General facts only: mineral powders in the eye are abrasive and can injure the cornea; iron oxides are used as pigments and are poorly absorbed; none is an established treatment for hernia, eyelid disorders or corneal protrusion.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM).** Schistos has no printed quality of its own. It has "the same uses as haematite only with less strength" (line 10592); haematite is printed "astringent, somewhat warming" (line 10585). That is stated of haematite and is not transferred here as schistos's quality. No hot, cold, moist or dry degree is printed for schistos.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What he offers is a discipline of attention, and schistos is a good example of it: its best kind is picked by colour, brittleness and vein pattern; and the haematite chapter teaches how it is used to counterfeit haematite and how the fraud is detected (straight veins, a brighter colour). The chapter is therapeutic and no cultivation status is assigned; no amuletic use is recorded.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here for a cultivator to take. What carries over is the habit of knowing a material's lookalikes and the test that tells them apart (veins, colour). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Eye:** mineral powders applied to the eye or eyelid risk corneal injury and infection; the conditions named (eye prolapse, staphylomata) need medical care.
- **Hernia and prolapse:** a hernia or a prolapse needs medical assessment; a mineral application is not a treatment.
- **Unidentified mineral:** the identity is unsettled; an unidentified stone should not be swallowed or applied to wounds.
- **Adulteration:** the text itself records that schistus was passed off as haematite, so the name does not guarantee what a material is.
