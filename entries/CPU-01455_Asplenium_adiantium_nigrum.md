# Black spleenwort — Asplenon (Dioscorides 3-151)

## *Asplenium adiantum-nigrum (printed "Asplenium adiantium-nigrum" by the Dioscorides translator for chapter 3-151, with Adiantum capillus-veneris on the same line)* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000). The binomials below are the translator's SUGGESTED line, not Dioscorides' own identification. **Named only by Dioscorides in this row.**

*3-151 ASPLENON* (heading at line 7824). The translator's line reads "Asplenium adiantium-nigrum, Adiantium-nigrum — Black Maidenhair Fern, Black Spleenwort, Black Oak Fern Adiantum capillus veneris, Herba capillorum-veneris — Maidenhair, Venus's Hair, Capillaire", with "see 3-121". The spelling "adiantium" is the translator's printed form. The text (from line 7828): "Asplenon has many leaves (similar to the creatures called centipedes and millipedes) growing round about out of one root. It grows on walls and shady rocks or pebble stones — stalkless, flowerless, seedless, [its leaves] cut-in like those of fern, yellowish and rough underneath, but green above. The leaves (boiled with vinegar and taken as a drink for forty days) are able to reduce the spleen, but you must also rub the spleen with the leaves pounded into small pieces with wine. It helps slow painful urination, hiccups and jaundice, and breaks stones in the bladder. It is thought to be a cause of barrenness (used alone or hung about one with the spleen of a mule) but they say that to cause barrenness it must be dug up when the night is moonless. It is also called scolopendrium, splenium, hemionion, pteryx, lonchitis, aturius, phrygia, phrygitis, or philtrodotes, while the Magi call it the blood of a weasel."

The text records the hung amulet and the moonless-night digging as things "they say" or that "it is thought", which is reporting and not endorsement.

**Identity and overlap.** Several names printed here (scolopendrium, splenium, hemionion) belong to the fern chapters nearby: 3-152 *Emionitis* prints "splenium" as its own name, and the translator gives *Asplenium scolopendrium* for 3-121 *Phyllitis* (I_1020) and 3-152. The separation of the ferns in the text is thin: stalkless, seedless, flowerless is said of several. The gloss also offers maidenhair as a second plant. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Ferns of the genus *Asplenium* are generally known as spore-bearing plants without flowers or seeds, which agrees with the text. Their chemistry was not read for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The text records a leaf decoction in vinegar for the spleen, with a poultice of the leaves, and uses for urination, hiccups, jaundice and bladder stones, and records a belief that it causes barrenness. The Codex does not recommend the plant. The fern is not marked poisonous by the translator, and no protocol is given.

### Preparation & Dosing

Leaves "boiled with vinegar and taken as a drink for forty days" for the spleen, with the leaves pounded in wine rubbed on the spleen (lines 7828-7830). No quantity is printed. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Ferns have no well-established spleen action, and the spleen is not a modern target of herbal treatment. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** 3-151 prints "yellowish and rough underneath, but green above" for the leaves. No taste, warming or cooling quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured here. What the chapter offers is the discipline of attention the cultivation spec describes: the place it grows (walls, shady rocks, pebbles), how to know it (many leaves round one root, no stalk, flower or seed, cut like fern, yellowish and rough beneath), and a forty-day course for the spleen. The chapter records amuletic and barrenness beliefs and distances itself from them ("they say"). Recording is not endorsing. It assigns no status. The identification is the translator's. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator takes from the chapter a method of identifying a plant that has no flower or seed, and the caution not to take reported beliefs for facts. No practice is given.

### Contraindications

The text records that the plant is thought to cause barrenness; do not use in pregnancy or when seeking pregnancy. No traditional use above is advice, and the forty-day course is not a recommendation.
