# Galium spurium — zangs.rtsi.wa, Zangtsi (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Galium spurium Linn., the first of three names Clark 1995 prints for zangs.rtsi.wa (then Galium aparine L. and Artemesia scoparia, white type); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints Galium aparine for "Zangtsi", with the English "Bedstraw"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT), and under this binomial only by Clark.** No other text read for this entry names this species. The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk (including the three Bhishagratna volumes) for the genus name *Galium* found two Dioscorides chapters on other species: 3-104 APARINE (line 7475, the translator's gloss *Galium aparine*, cleavers) and 4-96 GALLION (line 8893, glosses *Galium luteum* and *Galium verum*, Lady's Bedstraw or rennet). Neither is *Galium spurium*, and neither is attributed to this row. Dioscorides' line 8895 says that gallion is "called this because coagulates milk instead of rennet"; Clark's commentary below also speaks of curd in milk, and the two statements are set side by side here and not equated. Clark's preface (line 83) says that more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1107).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry follows *lug.ngal* (*Corydalis meifolia*, line 1106) and precedes *rnya.lo* (line 1108). It reads:

"zangs.rtsi.wa: Galium spurium Linn: Galium aparine L.: Artemesia scoparia (white type): [has three names. The white type has a thin, square petiole and seed which adheres to clothing and helps to produce curd when boiled in milk. It also has red pods containing small, black seeds. The black type grows in dark soft soil and has a strong smell similar to mkhan.pa (Artemesia)]. It cures jaundice and (all types of) bile disorders. [The leaf of the latter type is also said to be beneficial in sinusitis.]"

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the brackets (commentary layer) are the three names, the description of the white and the black types, and the sentence on the leaf and sinusitis. Outside them (root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the three Latin names, which are Clark's identifications, and the sentence "It cures jaundice and (all types of) bile disorders". The commentary speaks of a white type and a black type, and the third Latin name, a mugwort, goes with the black type's strong smell "similar to mkhan.pa (Artemesia)". The Codex does not say which Latin name goes with which type.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* Line 3476 reads, in part: "Zangtsi cures tripa disorders and jaundice." The index lists "Zangtsi 210, 315" (line 7558). The action agrees in kind with Clark's "jaundice and (all types of) bile disorders". The Codex does not equate *tripa* with Clark's "bile".

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary.* The "Z" block lists six phonetic names, "Zangtik Zangtsi" (line 6055), "Zema Zhati", "Zira karpo" and "Zira nagpo", with the transliterations "zangs tig zangs rtsi" (line 6060). The scientific names are "Gentianella moorcroftiana", "Galium aparine" (line 6065), "Tribulus terrestris", then "Myristica fragrans Cuminum cyminum Nigella sativa", and the English names run "Copper coloured gentian", "Bedstraw", "Small calthreps", "Nutmeg", "White Cumin", "Black cumin". *Galium aparine* stands against "Zangtsi" by position, with "Bedstraw" as its English. The glossary does not print *Galium spurium*. The two Tibetan texts agree on the Tibetan name and on the genus and differ on the species; the Codex does not choose. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** I_906 (Cleavers, *Galium aparine*) already quotes this same Clark line 1107 and the same glossary lines, and is the entry for Clark's second binomial and the glossary's species. This file is the entry for Clark's first binomial, *Galium spurium*. I_1091 (*Galium verum*) is another species, and I_1189 (*Senecio scandens*) quotes Clark's neighbouring *rgud.drus* entry, which prints *Galium boreale*. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Galium* is a genus of the madder family (Rubiaceae) of slender, square-stemmed herbs, many of which have small hooked bristles on stem and fruit that cling to clothing, and that *Artemisia* is a genus of another family (Asteraceae), so the names Clark prints point to plants of different families. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained or which part was used.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "cures jaundice and (all types of) bile disorders" (Clark, line 1107) and "cures tripa disorders and jaundice" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3476). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only by Clark's first choice among three names, and they are not Codex protocols. Jaundice is a medical matter and the Codex offers no advice for it.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify] Clark's commentary says only that the seed "helps to produce curd when boiled in milk", which describes a property of the seed in milk, not a preparation of the drug.

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Tibetan terms ("jaundice", "bile disorders", *tripa* disorders) are not measured outcomes, and the Codex does not equate them with a modern diagnosis. The identity is open between *Galium* species and a plant of another genus.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 1107) gives no taste, power or post-digestive taste for this drug; it describes the petiole, seed, pods and smell. The name does not appear in the Men-Tsee-Khang taste-group lists read for this entry (they begin at line 3204). The one action printed is the curing of jaundice and bile disorders (Clark) and of *tripa* disorders and jaundice (Men-Tsee-Khang).

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it cures jaundice and bile disorders, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same in kind. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the sentences cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and the link from this binomial to that framing rests on Clark's first choice among three.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a drug for jaundice and bile in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and its Latin name is one of three that Clark offers, with a different species in the glossary. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. The identity is open between plants of different genera, jaundice needs medical care, and no preparation or dose is given, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
