# Winged prickly ash / Zanthoxylum armatum — Tumburu (Sushruta, printed "Zanthoxylum Alatum"); gyer ma, "Guinea pepper" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011)

## *Zanthoxylum armatum* (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary; Bhishagratna's Appendix 3 prints "Zanthoxylum Alatum") | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** SS, FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Identity note.** This row was added 2026-10-09 (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP). The spellings *Zanthoxylum alatum* (Bhishagratna) and *Z. armatum* (Men-Tsee-Khang) are merged here as printed; the row has not been checked against a modern botanical authority. [USER: verify] It is a different species from CPU-00306 *Zanthoxylum bungeanum* (I_306, Sichuan pepper), which is a Chinese entry. Nothing read equates the two species. Sushruta also names *Kustumburu* (coriander) in several chapters, a different drug that is not the subject of this row.

**Sanskrit — Sushruta (SS).** The Latin name comes from Bhishagratna's Appendix 3 (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`), whose two columns the OCR prints separately. The Sanskrit run at lines 33165-33170 reads Tnda (as printed), Tulasi, *Tumburu* (line 33168), Tuttha, Tuvari. The Latin run ending at line 33196 takes in, from line 33191, "Ipomea Turpethum." (an entry for a name above the run) and continues "Morus Indica.", "Ocimum Sanctum.", "Zanthoxylum Alatum." (line 33195), "Sulphate of Copper.", "Cajanus Indica." The pairs on either side are internally consistent (Tulasi with *Ocimum sanctum*, Tuttha with "Sulphate of Copper"), so *Tumburu* sits against *Zanthoxylum alatum* in column order. The pairing holds.

The one place in the chapters where the plain name *Tumburu* occurs is Chikitsasthana ch.5 (`chikitsa/SS_Chikitsa_ch05.txt`, line 567), in the treatment of *Apatantraka* ("apoplectic convulsions" in Bhishagratna): "The patient should be made to drink a potion consisting of Tumburu, Pushkara, Hingu, Amla-vetasa, Haritaki and the three (officinal) kinds of salts, with a decoction of barley." A translator's footnote adds that Chakradatta quotes this in the colic chapter but "does not read 'Amla-vetasa' there." The plant is not given a taste, potency or other property in the chapters searched, and no other chapter use of *Tumburu* (as distinct from *Kustumburu*) was found.

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011*, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`). Under Y the glossary lists the phonetic names Yakyima, Yherma, Yhershingpa, Yugu shing, Yumo dejin, Yungwa, the transliterations gya' kyi ma, *gyer ma*, gyer shing pa, yu gu shing, yu mo mde 'byin, yung ba, and the scientific names *Chrysoplenium carnosum*, *Zanthoxylum armatum* (line 6044), *Scrophularia decomposita*, *Sambucus adnata*, *Paraquilegia anemonoides*, *Curcuma longa*, then the common names Dactylorhiza, Coriander, "Guinea pepper", Figwort, Turmeric. Read in column order, *gyer ma* sits against *Zanthoxylum armatum* and "Guinea pepper", and the next name, gyer shing pa, against *Scrophularia* and "Figwort". The pairing is by position and is not printed on one line. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995* (`Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`) corroborates the name and gives uses, although it has no separate entry for it in ch.20. Line 1066, under gyer.shing.pa, says the inferior type has a "seed similar to Zanthoxylum (gyer.ma)", so Clark pairs gyer.ma with Zanthoxylum. At line 703, in the foods section that begins at line 663 (the line 662 list gives "(1) Normal diet (chapter sixteen)"), Clark prints: "Zanthoxylum opens the mouths of the channels and increases phlegm and wind." At line 1221, in ch.21 (compounding, which opens at line 1204), Zanthoxylum is among the anthelmintics: "The category of anthelmintics contains Musk, Asafoetida, Garlic, Bengal Kino tree, Datura, Henbane, Embelia, seed of Iris ensata, Snail shell, Artemesia(nestita) ash, Daphne tangutica (tibetanum), Arisaema, Zanthoxylum and Heracleum (candicans)." Clark's text does not name the species there, so the equation with *Z. armatum* rests on the glossary.

### Active Components

*Zanthoxylum* fruits are generally known to be pungent and aromatic, with alkylamides (sanshool-type compounds) responsible for the tingling sensation, together with volatile oils. The constituents of the plants named in the two texts were not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Sushruta records one potion in which *Tumburu* is one of six named drugs. Clark records a spice that "opens the mouths of the channels and increases phlegm and wind" and a place in the anthelmintic class. These are the texts' own claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol for this plant.

### Preparation & Dosing

Sushruta, Chikitsa ch.5, line 567: a potion of *Tumburu*, Pushkara, Hingu, Amla-vetasa, Haritaki and the three salts "with a decoction of barley". No quantity is printed in the lines read. Clark's two mentions give no preparation. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Plants of the genus *Zanthoxylum* are widely used as spices and are known for the numbing, tingling sensation of their fruit. This is a general statement and no study was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SS:** no rasa, virya or vipaka is printed for *Tumburu* in the passage read.
- **FMT:** no taste or potency is printed. Clark line 703 gives the action only: it "opens the mouths of the channels and increases phlegm and wind". Clark prints these as "phlegm" and "wind" and no Tibetan terms are given in that line.

### Cultivation Register

**Sanskrit — Sushruta (SS).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The plant is claimed in a single therapeutic potion for *Apatantraka*. It is not named in any *rasayana* or *vajikarana* chapter among the passages read, and no *ojas* action is attested for it.

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Clark prints it as a dietary spice with a stated effect and as a member of the anthelmintic class. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice and not reaching the root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. Nothing read assigns this plant a place in *bcud len*. Clark's own caution is part of the record: the spice "increases phlegm and wind", which is a stated drawback and not a tonic.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the sources read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a spice in the Tibetan text, with a stated effect on phlegm and wind, and a minor ingredient of one potion in Sushruta. The abstentive answer is correct as far as practice goes: treat it as a food spice, not as a cultivation drug.

### Contraindications

No contraindication is printed in the passages read. The Tibetan text itself notes that the spice increases phlegm and wind. As a general caution, pungent spice fruits can irritate the mouth and stomach, and no medicinal use of this plant is advised here. Medical advice should be taken for convulsions, which are an emergency.
