# Grass Pea — Khesari, Chickling Vetch, Cicercula, Kesari Dal, Guaya

## *Lathyrus sativus* L. | Family: Fabaceae (Leguminosae)

**Tradition Tags:** AT, DMM, EP, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Cold and dry in the second degree (Galenic); astringent, earth-heavy pulse
**Training Phase:** Endurance / Famine-Ballast (Kenetica) — a survival ration, never a foundation stone

---

### Origin & Tradition

Grass pea is one of the oldest domesticated legumes of the Old World, a hardy blue-flowered vetch that will yield a protein-rich seed on land too dry, too flooded, or too exhausted for any other crop. That single virtue — it does not fail when everything else fails — made it both a lifesaver and a curse across four thousand years of agrarian history. It is the classic "famine pulse": eaten gratefully in good years as an occasional dish, eaten desperately and almost exclusively in bad ones, when its hidden toxin does its slow work.

**Assyrian / Mesopotamian (AT):** Cuneiform agricultural records and later Assyrian medical tablets list a chickling-type pulse among the field legumes of the alluvial plains. As a drought-tolerant crop of marginal soils it belonged to the survival economy rather than the physician's cabinet — food first, medicine only by extension.

**Ebers / Egyptian (EP):** Leguminous pulses of the *Lathyrus/Vicia* group grew across the Nile agricultural belt and appear in the broad Egyptian dietary and pharmacopoeial tradition as poultice bases and cooling foods. The Ebers material treats such pulses as nourishing but heavy — a fair intuition, given what we now know.

**Dioscorides (DMM):** In *De Materia Medica*, Dioscorides describes cultivated vetch-peas (the *lathyros/aphake* cluster) as nourishing but flatulent and hard to digest, cooked and softened before eating. The Greek agronomists already suspected that heavy, prolonged reliance on such pulses "weakened the legs" — an early, unattributed echo of what would later be named lathyrism.

**Sushruta / Ayurvedic (SS):** Classical Ayurveda knows this pulse as *khesari* (triputa). It is explicitly graded as an inferior, heavy, *vata*-aggravating dhal — permitted as famine food but flagged as harmful in excess, said to disturb the nervous channels and the lower limbs. This is one of the clearest pre-modern statements of a specific, reproducible drug hazard: the tradition correctly linked the cheap pulse to crippling of the legs.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Grass pea is the encyclopaedia's emblem of the *poisoned gift* — abundance that arrives precisely when judgement is most impaired by hunger. Its doctrinal lesson maps cleanly onto its pharmacology: the seed carries **β-ODAP**, a glutamate-mimicking excitotoxin that over-drives the very motor neurons a starving body can least afford to lose. The symbol and the molecule teach the same rule — a food that saves you in the short run can cripple you in the long run, and the crossover point is dose and duration.

### Active Components

- **β-N-oxalyl-L-α,β-diaminopropionic acid (β-ODAP, formerly β-ODAP / BOAA)** — the principal neurotoxin, a non-protein amino acid acting as an agonist at glutamatergic (AMPA/mGlu) receptors; content varies widely by variety, tissue, and environmental stress (drought, salinity, heavy metals raise it).
- **Homoarginine and other non-protein amino acids** — minor bioactive constituents.
- **Nutritionally:** high-quality protein (~25–29%), rich in lysine — the reason it persists as a crop despite the hazard.
- β-ODAP levels reported from below the ~0.15% "safe" threshold in bred low-toxin varieties up to ~0.5% in high-toxin landraces.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Within Kenetica, grass pea is filed strictly as **ballast, not fuel**. It belongs to the Endurance/Famine-Ballast phase only as a study object and a cautionary ration — a demonstration that caloric and protein sufficiency are necessary but not sufficient conditions for safety. It is never prescribed as a tonic, nootropic, or staple. Where a low-β-ODAP, properly detoxified preparation is available, it may appear as an occasional protein source; the pedagogical use is to teach the practitioner the discipline of preparation, dose ceilings, and dietary diversification.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the toxicology is well characterised. Jiao et al. review how β-ODAP — "a neuroexcitatory non-protein amino acid… responsible for human lathyrism" — accumulates in the seed and rises sharply under drought, salinity, and heavy-metal stress, precisely the famine conditions under which the crop is most eaten ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fct.2010.04.050)). Xu et al. confirm that "long-term consumption… and β-ODAP is linked to lathyrism, a degenerative motor neuron syndrome," and note that dietary deficiency in methionine and cysteine aggravates the neurotoxicity ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms18030526)). Miah et al. quantified β-ODAP across varieties, finding bred BARI lines as low as 0.086% (below the 0.15% safe threshold) versus local landraces at 0.39–0.49% — direct evidence that variety and processing, not the species alone, determine hazard ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2024.e37746)).

**Evidence grade: STRONG for the hazard.** The causal link between prolonged heavy consumption and neurolathyrism, and the identity of β-ODAP as the excitotoxin, are firmly established. No credible evidence supports any therapeutic use that would justify sustained intake.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Ayurveda (SS):** *khesari* — heavy (*guru*), *vata*-aggravating, astringent (*kashaya*) rasa, cooling *virya*; graded an inferior pulse, harmful in excess, injurious to the lower limbs. Explicitly restricted.
- **Galenic / Unani:** cold and dry; flatulent, hard to digest, "weakening to the nerves and legs" in the agronomic-medical tradition descending from Dioscorides.
- **TCM:** not a classical Chinese materia medica item; no standing qi/flavour attribution.

### Contraindications

⚠️ **SEVERE, IRREVERSIBLE NEUROLOGICAL HAZARD — READ BEFORE ANY USE.**

- **Neurolathyrism.** Prolonged, heavy consumption of grass pea (typically as a dietary staple for weeks to months, especially during famine) causes **neurolathyrism**: an irreversible **spastic paraparesis** — permanent stiffness, weakness, and paralysis of the lower limbs from upper-motor-neuron degeneration. The damage does **not** reverse when the food is stopped. Young men are historically the most affected group.
- **Mechanism:** β-ODAP is an excitotoxin acting on glutamate receptors; the toxic effect is worsened by starvation and by diets deficient in sulfur amino acids (methionine/cysteine) — i.e. exactly the conditions of famine.
- **Never a staple. Never for children, pregnant or malnourished individuals.** Safe only as an *occasional*, properly soaked/decanted or low-β-ODAP preparation within an otherwise diverse, protein-adequate diet.
- Do not present as a health food, nootropic, or endurance tonic. Any recommendation must carry this warning.
- If neurological symptoms (leg stiffness, spasticity, gait change) appear, stop immediately and seek medical care — but understand established damage is permanent.
