# Ixeris gracilis — rtsa.mkhris, Tsa tri (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Ixeris gracilis D.C., the first of three identifications Clark 1995 prints for rtsa.mkhris (the others are Cierhita macrorhiza and Sonchus sp.); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary sets a different binomial, Cicerbita macrorhiza, beside Tsa tri by column position* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. Only Clark prints *Ixeris gracilis* (lines 1082 and 1209, and the Latin-name index, line 1600). A search of Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 for "Ixeris" found nothing. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk for the genus name *Ixeris* found nothing. Dioscorides' index of Latin names (line 10888) carries the old word "cicerbita" (page 284), which is not Clark's or the glossary's *Cicerbita macrorhiza* and is not attributed to this row. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 178) the Tibetan name *rtsa.mkhris* is recorded at Clark line 1082 with the identification "Ixeris gracilis D.C.; Cierhita macrorhiza" and the note "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The key, *Ixeris gracilis*, is therefore the first name Clark prints. The extraction row lists two names; Clark's line prints three, "Ixeris gracilis D.C.: Cierhita macrorhiza: Sonchus sp." (see below). The third, a bare genus, is not in the extraction row.

**Identity doubt.** The three names are three genera of the daisy family, as the Codex understands it (Ixeris, Cierhita as printed, Sonchus); the Codex has not checked this against a flora. Clark's "Cierhita" is not a spelling the Codex can confirm; the glossary prints "Cicerbita macrorhiza" for the same Tibetan name (see below), and the Codex reads Clark's word as that name misprinted. That is a reading, not a printed fact. [USER: verify] The Codex cannot say which plant the Tibetan texts meant, and the entry stands for the Tibetan drug.

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1082).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20, within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). It reads:

"rtsa.mkhris: Ixeris gracilis D.C.: Cierhita macrorhiza: Sonchus sp.: [has two names. It has a yellow, four-petalled flower, coarse green leaf similar to that of dandelion and milky sap]. Ixeris cures bile disorders."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the number of names, the flower, the leaf and the milky sap. Outside them (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the three Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the sentence "Ixeris cures bile disorders". The word "Ixeris" in that sentence is Clark's own word for the drug. The line gives no taste, no power, no part used and no preparation.

*Clark 1995, chapter 21.* In "21. The Principles of Medicines: Compounding of Medicines" (heading at line 1205), line 1209 reads: "The class of medicines which cure bile disorders includes Chiretta, Bitter Cucumber, Kurchi bark, White Aconite, Ixeris gracilis, Pyrola incarnata, Gentiana straminea and Indian Barberry." Here the plant is named by its Latin binomial, as the fifth of eight. This line is the only other place in Clark that names it.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* The index lists "Tsatri 210,219,313" (line 7531). In chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428), line 3456 reads, in the middle of a passage: "Dhewa cures epidemic and Tsa tri cures tripa disorders." In chapter 21, "The Group and the Method of Compounding Medicine" (contents, line 229), lines 3593 to 3597 read: "Tikta, Sergyi metok, Dhungmo nyung, Bongnga karpo, Tsa tri, Yakyima, Kyiche and Kyerpa are the group of medicines that cure tripa disorders." Clark's "bile disorders" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "tripa disorders" are the two translations' wordings. Both texts place the drug among medicines for the same humour, and the Codex does not treat the two English words as one term.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "T" block).* The Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients prints its columns in separate runs. The phonetic names include "Tikta", "Tiyangku Traagkang" and "Tronbu" (lines 5897 to 5899) and "Tsa tri" (line 5904); the transliteration "rtsa mkhris" is at line 5906, the same spelling as Clark's *rtsa.mkhris*. The scientific names run (lines 5968 to 5982) "Swertia chirayta", "Dracocephalum tanguticum Dragon head", "Coptis teetordes chinensis Mamira", "Euphorbia stracheyi" with "Spurge", and "Cicerbita macrorhiza" with "Sow thistle". Counting in order, the five phonetic names Tikta, Tiyangku, Traagkang, Tronbu and Tsa tri stand against those five scientific names, so "Tsa tri" stands against *Cicerbita macrorhiza* and the English "Sow thistle". The pairing is by position, and the glossary does not print it in one row. "Tikta", the first of the five, stands against *Swertia chirayta* and "Traagkang" against the coptis, which fits Clark's chiretta entry (*tig.ta*, "Chiretta: Swertia chirata", line 974) and her gold-thread entry (*ser.po.khrag.rkang*, "Gold Thread: Coptis teetoides", line 1072). [USER: verify] The glossary therefore sets beside the Tibetan name Clark's second binomial, not her first, and does not print *Ixeris gracilis*.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Ixeris*, *rtsa.mkhris*, *Tsa tri* and *Cicerbita* found no entry on this plant. I_1223 (*Aconitum naviculare*) quotes the chapter 21 line 1209 list for another member of it. I_1275 (*Euphorbia stracheyi*, tag FMT) quotes the same "T" block and notes that "Tsa tri" with "rtsa mkhris" is Clark's *rtsa.mkhris* with "Cierhita macrorhiza" as its second binomial; it is the entry on a neighbouring name, not on this drug. I_1194 (*Sonchus arvensis*, tag DMM) and I_647 (Sow Thistle, *Sonchus oleraceus*; tags EP, AT, DMM, CM) are *Sonchus* entries from other traditions; neither cites Clark line 1082, and Clark's third name here is only the bare genus "Sonchus sp.". Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. Clark's commentary records "milky sap" and a leaf "similar to that of dandelion". It is a general fact that *Ixeris*, *Cicerbita* and *Sonchus* belong to the daisy family (Asteraceae), that members of the chicory tribe typically carry a milky latex, and that bitter sesquiterpene lactones are characteristic of that tribe. The sources read do not say which part was used.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "cures bile disorders" (Clark, line 1082), is in "the class of medicines which cure bile disorders" (Clark, line 1209), and is among the medicines that "cure tripa disorders" (Men-Tsee-Khang, lines 3456 and 3593 to 3597). These are the texts' claims in their own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and does not translate "bile disorders" or "tripa disorders" into modern diagnoses.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it cures bile disorders, and chapter 21 lists it in the class of medicines that cure them. The Men-Tsee-Khang text puts the Tibetan name among the medicines that cure tripa disorders. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes, and not in the rejuvenation material; no line read places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name, not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a drug for bile (tripa) disorders in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and the plant behind the Tibetan name is uncertain. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Bile and tripa disorders in the Tibetan texts are not modern diagnoses and need medical assessment; no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Because the identity is uncertain, a plant could not be matched to the Tibetan name from this entry in any case. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read.
