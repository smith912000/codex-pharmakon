# Donkey's lettuce — shanjar (شنجار), khass al-himar (خس الحمار), Heraklios (هرقلوس) (Avicenna, Canon Book II)

## *A prickly, lettuce-leaved herb with a red, hand-staining stem or root that the Canon names shanjar and khass al-himar ("donkey's lettuce"), and which it also equates with the Heraklios of Hunayn; the plant is not identified to a species here, the identification is ours from the Arabic descriptions, and is of low confidence, since the three Canon headings do not agree on its temperament or its parts* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

**Tradition Tags:** CM

### Origin & Tradition

> **CAUTION.** In the Canon's own words this herb brings on the menses strongly, expels the dead foetus and kills the living one. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should take this plant, least of all in pregnancy.

**Named only by Avicenna's *Canon* among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 8 November 2026 as an unverified placeholder from the Canon heading *shanjar*; the Canon headings *khass al-himar* (CPU-02736) and *Heraklios* (CPU-02761) were held as separate rows and are merged here, because the Canon itself equates them. **Avicenna's Canon (CM), three headings.**

*1. Shanjar (Canon Book II, entry *shanjar*, Arabic text line 18136).* The entry opens "هو خس الحمار" ("it is khass al-himar, donkey's lettuce"), "its kinds are many". It has a leaf like lettuce leaf, "sharp and thorny, toward black" (محدد شاك إلى السواد), and "in summer its stem reddens like blood so that it stains the hand" (ويحمر في الصيف عوده كالدم بحيث يصبغ اليد). Choice: "its leaf is the weakest thing in it" (ورقه أضعف ما فيه, line 18138). Temperament: "cold in the first degree, dry in the second" (بارد في الأولى يابس في الثانية, line 18139). The kinds are told apart by names the text prints in Arabic letters: the one called *anuqliya* (أنوقليا) is astringent with some bitterness; the one called *falusi* (فلوسي) is more astringent; the one called *anulus* (أنولوس) is more astringent and more acrid than both; "the one that has no name" (الذي لا اسم له) is near to that; and "in all of it is astringency and drying" (lines 18139 to 18141). A later line speaks of the kind called *falus* (فالوس, line 18143) and another of *yafusus* (يافسوس, line 18148). I have not matched these transliterated names to any kind in another text. [USER: verify]

Uses under this heading, as printed. "When mixed with oil and rubbed on, a vein/root/sweat" (وإذا خلط بالدهن ومرخ به عرق, line 18141); the sentence is cut off and the word عرق (*ʿirq*) is ambiguous, so no use is stated. [USER: verify] Cosmetic: "a useful liniment for vitiligo and jaundice" (طلاء نافع من البهق واليرقان, line 18142). Swellings: it is applied as a poultice with fat on scaling, and with thyme oil on the "burning" swelling (الجمرة), especially the kind called *falus* (lines 18142 to 18143). Wounds: it heals sores when used in a cerate (القيروطي, line 18144). Head: "the most useful thing for pains of the ear" (line 18144). Digestive: it helps jaundice when drunk, especially the *anuqliya* kind, and especially spleen pains; "its bark tans the stomach" (وقشره دابغ للمعدة, line 18146). Excretory: of the nameless kind, "a mithqal and a half" (مثقال ونصف) drunk with *qardamana*, hyssop or garden cress "brings out the worms and gourd seeds" (أخرج الديدان وحب القرع, lines 18146 to 18147), and the *anuqliya* is "useful for pain of the kidneys" (line 18148). Poisons: the kind called *yafusus* is "very useful against the bite of the viper", as a poultice or a drink, and the nameless kind is near to that (lines 18148 to 18149).

*2. Khass al-himar (Canon Book II, entry *khass al-himar*, Arabic text line 19135).* This is the fuller description, and it ends the identification with the sentence "وهو الشنجار" ("and it is the shanjar", line 19139). The plant has "a leaf like fine lettuce, many toward black, downy; its leaves cling to the root and are firm, holding it; the colour of its root is toward red, and it stains the hand and the ground red; it grows in good land; and it is of watery and earthy substance" (lines 19136 to 19139). The line then reads "وقد قيل فيه" ("and it has been said of it") and breaks off at a page marker; whatever was said is not in the text on disk. Choice: "the yellow is stronger, the white is watery and weak" (الأصفر أقوى والأبيض مائي ضعيف, line 19141). Temperament: "hot and dry in the beginning of the second degree" (حار يابس في أول الثانية, lines 19141 to 19142), which is not the "cold in the first, dry in the second" of the *shanjar* heading. Actions: "a cleanser and opener" (جال مفتح); the dried flower is stronger in this; the root's nature is near that of its seed, and the root is stronger, especially dry (lines 19142 to 19143). Avicenna adds "Paulus said" (قال بولس) that it has "an attracting power from the depth, so that it draws the afterbirth" (يجذب السلا, line 19143). This is the only place in these headings where he names an earlier authority.

Under the same heading: swellings, it benefits hard swellings wherever they are (line 19144); wounds, with a cerate it heals, and so does its water (line 19145); head, its juice purges the head as a snuff, and with honey it is used on *al-qalākh* (القلاخ, a word I cannot render; [USER: verify]) as a liniment (lines 19145 to 19146); eye, the dried herb cleans the trace left in the eye and the thickness of the layers (lines 19146 to 19147); digestive, it purges the liver, and the kind pickled in vinegar benefits the spleen, eaten and as a poultice (lines 19147 to 19148); excretory, "it brings on the menses strongly, and brings out the dead foetus and kills the living foetus, and it benefits hard swellings in the womb, as a pessary and as a sitting-bath in its water, and it is the strongest and most fitting of things for the menses, and the amount is one mithqal, drunk and taken as a pessary" (lines 19148 to 19150); and, with a cerate, it is used on fissures of the anus (شقاق المقعدة, line 19151).

*3. Heraklios (Canon Book II, entry *Heraklios*, Arabic text line 12319).* "It is a kind of wild garden herb. Hunayn said: it is khass al-himar, which we shall mention when we come to the letter khā" (هو جنس من البقل الدشتي. قال حنين: هو خس الحمار نذكره عند ذكرنا حرف الخاء, lines 12320 to 12321). The temperament given is "cold and moist, with some drying and a little heating, and astringency" (بارد رطب وفيه تجفيف وتسخين قليل وقبض), and under actions "in it is a moderate astringency, as they claimed" (فيه قبض معتدل فيما زعموا, lines 12321 to 12322). Here the identification is Hunayn's, and Avicenna reports it without comment.

**Identity doubts.** The Canon equates the three headings by name (line 19139, line 12320), yet the temperaments differ: cold and dry (shanjar), hot and dry (khass al-himar), cold and moist (Heraklios). The shanjar heading's own weakest part is the leaf, while the khass al-himar heading's stronger part is the root and the flower. The herb also shows two colours, a yellow and a white, in the second heading, with no equivalent in the first. The text is consistent on the leaf that resembles lettuce, the dark and downy or thorny look, and the red staining of the hand. No Latin name is given in the Canon, and none is asserted here. Modern plants called donkey's lettuce or shanjar in different regions are not one species. [USER: verify any species before it is used in the Codex.]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Dangerous in pregnancy.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Under *shanjar*: skin liniment (vitiligo, jaundice), swellings and sores, ear pain, jaundice and spleen, worms, kidney pain, viper bite. Under *khass al-himar*: hard swellings, wounds, head and eye cleansing, liver and spleen, the menses and the womb, anal fissures. Under *Heraklios*: nothing beyond a moderate astringency.
- **What the text does not say.** It gives no life-lengthening or cultivation use, and no modern equivalent of its terms.

The Codex does not translate the Canon's terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend this plant.

### Preparation & Dosing

The Canon gives three preparations and two doses, all quoted above. Preparations: mixed with oil and rubbed on (line 18141); in a cerate for sores (lines 18144 and 19145) and anal fissures (line 19151); the juice as a snuff (line 19145); pickled in vinegar for the spleen (line 19148); a poultice with fat or thyme oil (lines 18142 to 18143); a pessary and a sitting-bath in its water (lines 19149 to 19150). Doses: "a mithqal and a half" of the nameless kind with *qardamana*, hyssop or garden cress for worms (lines 18146 to 18147), and "one mithqal, drunk and as a pessary" for the menses (line 19150). The mithqal is a historical weight, and this entry converts it to no modern figure. [USER: verify] **Do not use any dose from this entry.** For *Heraklios* no preparation or dose is given.

Many plants of the lettuce tribe (Lactuceae) carry a bitter milky latex and are eaten or used as bitter herbs, and thorny, red-stemmed plants of this family and others are common in dry land. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **CM, *shanjar*.** "Cold in the first degree, dry in the second" (line 18139); the kinds are "astringent and drying" throughout (lines 18139 to 18141).
- **CM, *khass al-himar*.** "Hot and dry in the beginning of the second degree" (lines 19141 to 19142); "a cleanser and opener" (line 19142).
- **CM, *Heraklios*.** "Cold and moist, with some drying and a little heating, and astringency" (line 12321).
- The three headings disagree; none is preferred here.

### Cultivation Register

**Unani / Avicenna (CM).** *(c) Absent.* The three Canon headings treat the herb only as a drug for skin, swellings, sores, the ear and eye, the liver, spleen and kidney, worms, the menses and the womb, and poisons. They assign it no place in the maintenance of health, no life-lengthening, and no connection with the vital heat or the spirit (*ruh*). The record stops at the drug.

**Practitioner's note.** A thorny, red-stained herb that the Canon sets down under three names with three different temperaments. It has real medicinal claims in the text, among them a strong uterine action, and none for cultivation. A cultivator has nothing to take from it, and the species is not known.

### Functional–Anatomical Correlate

The Canon's three headings place this herb's action on the skin, the head and eye, the digestive organs and the womb. On the skin it is a liniment for vitiligo and jaundice, and a poultice for swellings and for sores, the latter used in a cerate. The ear is treated for pain, and the eye is cleaned of a trace left in it. Among the digestive organs the liver and spleen are named, the stomach is said to be tanned by its bark, and the kidneys are relieved of pain. The text lists worms and viper bite among its uses. For the womb and menses the Canon says it brings on menstruation strongly and expels the dead foetus, while also killing the living one, and it treats hard swellings of the womb. Its energetic description varies between headings, cold and dry in one and hot and dry in another. The identification is low confidence, so this mapping rests on the Canon's words alone. The entry is a historical record and not a recommendation.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy and the womb.** The Canon says it kills the living foetus and expels the dead one (lines 19148 to 19150). Avoid absolutely.
- **Lactation, children, the weak.** No statement in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]
- **Identity.** The species is unknown and the three headings disagree on its nature; do not collect or sell anything under these names on the strength of this entry. [USER: verify]
- **Jaundice, spleen disease, worms, viper bite, swellings.** These are medical conditions; use proper care.
