# Ox suet — STEAR BOEION (Dioscorides 2-89) / Fat-of-an-Ox, Ox fat, Bullock's fat, Cow's fat (Bryan's Ebers)
## *Kidney fat of the ox or cow, rendered and washed; Osbaldeston prints "SUGGESTED: Ox or Cow Suet"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** EP, DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

This entry is the fat of cattle taken as a material in itself: the suet that Dioscorides prepares, and the fat that the Ebers compounds name. The animal as a whole (blood, brain, horn, liver, milk, flesh) is in `III_17_Cattle.md`, which also lists the Ebers fat prescriptions in brief; they are given here with their complaints. Pig fat is in `III_37_Lard.md`.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *De Materia Medica* 2-89 *STEAR BOEION* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` lines 5309-5313, Osbaldeston and Wood 2000; `extract/names.csv` row "STEAR BOEION, 2-89 (line 5309)"). The chapter is a method of preparing, with no uses in it: "From ox or cow suet (which is near the kidneys) the skin is to be taken off, and it must be washed in sea water taken out of the ocean, then it must be put into a mortar and pounded carefully, sprinkling the seawater on it. When it is all dissolved it must be put into a ceramic jar and sea water poured into it to stand no less than twenty centimetres above it, and it must be boiled until it has lost its own smell. Afterwards for every Attic [Athenian] pound of the suet you must put in four teaspoonfuls of Tyrrhenian [Etruscan] wax. Then (having strained it and taken away the filth that lies in the bottom) it must be put into a new jar. Afterwards it is covered and is to be set out every day in the sun so that it may become white and lose its bad taste." The next chapter, 2-90 (line 5315), gives a second method for "Bulls' suet": new kidney fat washed in river water, melted with a little salt, strained into clear water, rubbed with the hands under repeated water, and boiled twice in sweet-smelling wine until "it discards all rank smell"; "It is also melted without salt to be used in some sores for which salt is not indicated. Prepared like this it is not very white." The same method serves "panthers', lions', wild boars', camels', horses', and other similar fats". Chapter 2-91 (line 5318) scents "Calves' fat as well as fat from bulls and deer" with wine and aromatics, and 2-93 (line 5339) gives a way to keep the fat of geese, poultry or calves from putrefying. The uses are in the general chapter 2-94 *STEATON DUNAMIS*, "Medicinal uses of Fats" (line 5342): "All fat is warming, softening and purifying; yet that of bulls, cows and calves is somewhat astringent." Nothing more specific to cattle fat is given there; the other animals' fats are each given their own uses.

**Egyptian (EP).** The spine's list row is `_sources/ebers.csv` line 68, "Cattle fat / tallow", *Bos taurus*, source field "Ebers; sae.saw-leipzig.de"; the second source was not relied on. Bryan's 1930 text (`Sources/EP_Ebers/text/PapyrusEbers_Unse.txt`) lists the ox among the animals "whose identity is not in doubt" (line 1392) and says of the cow that "she gave her bile, brain, blood, fat, flesh, horns, liver, milk, spleen, and tallow" (line 1429). The Egyptian words are not printed. Bryan renders the fat as "Fat-of-an-Ox", "Ox fat", "Bullock's fat" and "Cow's Fat" in different places; whether the papyrus has one word or several behind these is not stated, so the names are listed as Bryan prints them. The prescriptions found:

- *Hair pulled out of the eye.* "Fat-of-an-Ox", Olive Oil and Entrails-of-Moles, "Crush into one, put on the fire, and put in the place of the Hair" (line 2919, in the eye section, after a bloods-and-incense compound for the same purpose).
- *The wound.* "Cow's Fat or Cow's Flesh be placed on the wound in order to ripen (i.e., decompose) it" (line 2322); "To dry a wound it was plastered with Incense, Onions, and Cow Fat" (line 2371); "Roasted Cow's Fat", wax and palm-fibres in *uah*-corn as a plaster for the fourth day (line 2265); "Collyrium Cow-fat" under the heading "TO MAKE THE FLESH GROW" (line 2399).
- *The mouth.* A chewing-remedy of "Cow's Milk, Fresh Bread and Bullock's Fat" (line 3136).
- *The* met. A poultice of "Fat-of-the-Ox, Fat-of-the-Ass, Fat-of-the-Ram", dom-palm fruit, grain, poppy and sea-salt "to refresh the met" (line 3151); a liniment of "Bullock's fat, Yeast-of-Wine, Garlic, Saltpetre-from-the-South, Poppy-berries, and Oil-of-Myrrh" to make it supple, with the instruction "Do not let it dry" (line 3170); "Fat-of-the-Bullock" among the ingredients of the thirty-seven-item poultice (line 3189). Bryan notes that what the word *met* means is disputed (line 3149).
- *A mattery tumour or pus.* "Bullock's fat" with *teun*-plant, wasp's blood, sea-salt, watermelon, bean-meal and wax, cooked and used as a poultice for the *uxedu* when it has developed pus (line 3925); "Cow's fat" with wax, caraway, copper-shavings, verdigris, goose-fat and others, cooked and poulticed on a mattery tumour of the neck (line 3858).
- *Bleeding.* After a drink said to "drive out blood through his mouth or rectum", the Scribe offers instead "the following really excellent Ointment": "Ox fat, Saffron seeds, Coriander, Myrrh", crushed and applied as a poultice (line 3519).

Bryan reports no recitation with these compounds. The papyrus joins spell and drug as one intervention elsewhere, but Bryan summarises, so this is no proof that none was given. No deity is attributed here. Unrelated: a spell at line 3098 smears "tallow" without naming the animal, and is not counted.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. Suet is the hard fat from around the kidneys of cattle. In general it is almost wholly triglycerides, rich in saturated fatty acids (stearic and palmitic) with oleic acid, and contains cholesterol and traces of fat-soluble vitamins. Rendering (melting and straining) removes the connective tissue and water; washing and boiling with wine or sea water, as Dioscorides describes, removes odour and some water-soluble material.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Greek source tells how to render and whiten the fat and says that fat is warming, softening and purifying; the Egyptian compounds are plasters, ointments and liniments for wounds, pus, the eye and the *met*. The sources give no outcomes. Beef fat is a food in many cuisines and needs no protocol from this Codex; as an applied medicine it offers nothing that a plain, clean emollient does not, and raw or poorly rendered animal fat on a wound is a risk (see Contraindications).

### Preparation & Dosing

The only preparations the texts give are the ones above. Dioscorides 2-89: skin removed, washed and pounded in sea water, boiled in sea water "until it has lost its own smell", four teaspoonfuls of Etruscan wax added to each Attic pound of suet, strained, and bleached in the sun. Dioscorides 2-90: river-water washing, salt, wine boiling. Ebers: the fat is mixed into plasters and ointments with the ingredients listed under Origin; Bryan's summary gives no quantities for them in the passages read. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Beef tallow is a saturated animal fat; fats were and are used as bases for ointments and plasters, and melted fat is an occlusive. Rendering by heat reduces but does not make sterile an unwashed tissue. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Greek (DMM):** Dioscorides 2-94 (line 5342): all fat "warming, softening and purifying"; that of bulls, cows and calves "somewhat astringent". These are the qualities the text prints; no degree or humour is given.

**Egyptian (EP):** none printed. The Ebers material is organised by complaint and gives no qualities for its ingredients.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian / Ebers (EP).** *(c) Absent.* Cattle fat appears in the Ebers compounds read as a base for plasters and ointments for the eye, the wound, pus, the mouth and the *met*. That is what was done for a complaint, not what the fat does to the person's *ka*, *ba*, *akh* or *ib*. Egyptian practice treats spell and drug as one intervention (*heka*); Bryan reports no recitation with these compounds, and since he summarises, that does not show the papyrus gives none. The identity of each named substance in an indication-organised corpus is a modern hypothesis, and the different English names for the fat are Bryan's. No deity is attributed.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is supplied here. What the chapters offer is a discipline of attention, and suet shows it well: take the fat "which is near the kidneys", pull off the skin, wash it in sea water, boil it until it has lost its own smell, add wax, and bleach it in the sun until it has "lost its bad taste" (2-89, line 5309); the alternative in 2-90 washes it in river water and boils it in wine "until it discards all rank smell". The test is by smell and appearance. 2-94 gives the fat's qualities as therapeutic ones. No chapter assigns a cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing is sourced that makes suet a substance for cultivation. What the two texts offer a practitioner is a lesson in preparation: Dioscorides insists the fat be washed, boiled free of smell and judged by eye and nose before use, and the Ebers scribe uses it only as a plaster base. A cultivator who wants to eat beef fat eats it as food and takes no practice from this entry. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Hygiene and infection:** raw or poorly rendered animal fat on an open wound can introduce bacteria and spores, and the Ebers practice of ripening a wound with fat or flesh (line 2322) is not safe by any modern standard.
- **Prion disease:** bovine tissue from the brain and spinal cord can carry the agent of bovine spongiform encephalopathy; the Codex does not recommend raw or unregulated bovine tissue.
- **Diet:** beef tallow is a concentrated saturated fat; people on a lipid-lowering regimen or with cardiovascular disease should treat it as any dietary fat.
- **Allergy:** some people react to red-meat products (alpha-gal sensitisation after tick bites); topical contact is rarely the issue, ingestion can be.
- **Ethics and law:** cattle are sacred or protected in some communities and jurisdictions; slaughter and the trade in cattle products are restricted there.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** no evidence in the sources; plain food use is ordinary.
