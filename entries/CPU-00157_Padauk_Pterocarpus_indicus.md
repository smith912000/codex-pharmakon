### Origin & Tradition

*Pterocarpus indicus* is a large, fast-growing leguminous tree of the Fabaceae (subfamily Faboideae), native across maritime and mainland Southeast Asia — the Philippines (where it is the national tree, *narra*), Indonesia, Malaysia, Myanmar (Burma "padauk"), southern China, New Guinea and the wider Malesian region. It is one of several *Pterocarpus* species whose deep red-to-orange heartwood and red exudate (kino) have made the genus medicinally important across the Old World tropics. The genus name *Pterocarpus* ("winged fruit") refers to the distinctive flat, circular winged pods; the heartwood is the prized timber known in trade as Burma padauk or amboyna (the burl form).

The defining medicinal signature of the tree is its **kino** — a dark red astringent gum-resin that bleeds from cuts in the bark and trunk, drying to a brittle, ruby-coloured mass. In the doctrine-of-signatures logic that recurs across the world's red-wood and red-resin medicines (dragon's blood, true red sandalwood *Pterocarpus santalinus*, catechu *Acacia/Senegalia*), the visibly "bleeding," astringent red wood was read as a remedy that **stops bleeding, cools the blood, and binds lax tissue**. This is functional convergence rather than a single lineage: across Southeast Asian folk pharmacopoeias the wood, bark and kino of *P. indicus* are used for diarrhoea and dysentery, for mouth and throat ulceration as a gargle/wash, for skin sores, and — in widely reported folk use — for blood-sugar control (decoctions of bark and leaves taken for diabetes).

**Ayurveda / *Pterocarpus* lineage:** Ayurveda's canonical red-wood medicines are sister species — *Pterocarpus santalinus* (*rakta chandana*, red sandalwood) and *Pterocarpus marsupium* (*vijayasar/bijasar*, the classical anti-diabetic heartwood). *P. indicus* is not the Sanskrit-named drug, but it shares the genus's pterocarpan/isoflavonoid chemistry and the same use-cluster (blood-cooling, astringent, antidiabetic), so it is filed here as an Ayurveda-adjacent member of that materia-medica family. **Southeast Asian / Malay folk medicine (SBJ-adjacent):** Decoctions of bark and young leaves for diabetes, dysentery, and as a tonic; the kino as a styptic and for ulcers of the mouth and gums. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **Red-wood / *Pterocarpus* mapping:** acrid-bitter, astringent, cooling; blood-cooling, stasis-resolving, and binding — the cooling counterpart to the warming aromatic rosewoods.
- **Ayurvedic mapping (genus-adjacent):** *rakta-prasadana* (blood-clearing) and astringent (*kashaya rasa*), *pitta-* and *kapha-*reducing; the genus's *vijayasar* member is a classical *prameha* (diabetes/metabolic) drug.
- **Kenetica Qi/Jing tier:** ◉ **Qi** for the blood-cooling, astringent, stasis-resolving action, with a secondary ◈ **Jing** ascription for connective-tissue and glycaemic-metabolic support.

### Contraindications

- **Cold-deficiency / dryness patterns:** a cooling astringent can aggravate cold, dry, or atrophic presentations; not a tonic for the depleted-and-cold athlete.
- **Constipation and dry stool:** tannin astringency can worsen constipation; avoid in dry-stool patterns.
- **Iron and mineral absorption:** high tannin content can impair non-haem iron uptake; separate from iron-rich meals and supplements — relevant to athletes managing iron status.
- **Concurrent oral medications:** tannins can bind and reduce absorption of co-ingested drugs and alkaloids; space dosing.
- **Diabetes medication interaction:** the folk antidiabetic action could be additive with hypoglycaemic drugs; monitor blood glucose. [USER: verify.]
- **Pregnancy / lactation:** insufficient data; avoid medicinal internal use. [USER: verify.]
- **Sourcing / species substitution:** ensure correct identification; do not substitute for or with CITES-listed *P. santalinus*.
