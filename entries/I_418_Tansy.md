## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary Name:** Tansy, Common Tansy, Bitter Buttons, Golden Buttons  
**Synonyms:** Chrysanthemum vulgare (L.) Bernh., Pyrethrum vulgare (L.) Boiss.  
**Etymology:** *Tanacetum* from Medieval Latin *tanacetum* — possibly from Greek *athanasia* (immortality) — the dried flowers were placed with corpses to repel insects and preserve the body (athanatos = immortal). "Tansy" from the same root via Old French *tanaisie*. "Golden Buttons" describes the dense, flat-topped button-like flower heads (corymb) of disc florets only (no ray florets — distinctive; all Tanacetum may lack rays or have reduced rays vs. ray-prominent Chrysanthemum). "Bitter Buttons" is both botanical and pharmacological description.

**Botanical Description:**  
Perennial rhizomatous herb 50–150 cm. Leaves deeply pinnate, dark green, strongly and distinctively aromatic (camphor-thujone smell — sharper than most herbs; easily recognized). Stem erect, reddish-brown below. Inflorescence a flat-topped corymb of 20–200 bright yellow button-like flower heads, each 6–12 mm diameter, all-disc (ray florets absent or vestigial). Blooms July–September. Very easily naturalized; persistent once established.

**THUJONE TOXICITY CLASSIFICATION:**  
Tansy is among the highest thujone-content plants in the Asteraceae, with volatile oil containing 60–80% thujone (α + β isomers combined). This makes it significantly more toxic per unit mass than wormwood (A. absinthium) or mugwort (A. vulgaris). The FDA classifies tansy as unsafe for food use. Historical fatalities from abortifacient use documented. This entry covers pharmacological properties with extensive safety focus.

**Relationship to Feverfew:**  
Tansy and feverfew (*T. parthenium*, I-408) are sister species in genus *Tanacetum*. Key differences:
- T. vulgare: all-disc flowers (no rays); camphor/thujone scent; very high thujone; primarily anthelmintic/insecticidal
- T. parthenium: ray + disc flowers; parthenolide-dominant; migraine-specific; lower thujone

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

Tansy's convergence profile is moderate, concentrated in the Euro-Mediterranean zone, reflecting its geographic distribution and the dominance of two applications that were repeatedly and independently observed: anthelmintic activity and emmenagogue/abortifacient effects. Dioscorides' *athanasia* (immortality herb) was named for the corpse-preservation application (insect repellence via thujone volatiles), but his medicinal application focused on worm expulsion and uterine regulation.

The medieval European tradition — among the most thoroughly documented in this volume — shows tansy in Charlemagne's *Capitulare de villis* (812 CE) as a required garden plant on every imperial estate; in Hildegard von Bingen's *Physica* (c. 1150 CE) for digestive complaints; in John Gerard's *Herball* (1597) for anthelmintic use; and in British folk tradition as an Easter dish (*tansy cake* — bitter green pancakes eaten at Easter as a symbolic purification from Lenten dietary restriction). The Easter tansy cake tradition was explicitly medicinal: the bitterness purged intestinal parasites accumulated over winter, the emmenagogue effect corrected spring amenorrhea after caloric restriction, and the volatile oils provided antimicrobial action.

The insect-repellent application (rubbing tansy onto meat to prevent fly strike; hanging tansy bundles in kitchens; lining coffins with tansy) represents a preserved-insecticide use that was pharmacologically rational: thujone, camphor, and borneol in tansy volatile oil are potent contact insecticides for flies, fleas, and lice. The preservation of corpses with tansy reflects both the insect deterrence and the antimicrobial volatile oil activity.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Volatile Oil (0.5–2.5% of plant):**

- **β-Thujone** (40–60% of volatile oil): PRIMARY compound; GABA-A antagonist; uterotonic; convulsant at high doses; insecticidal
- **α-Thujone** (10–30%): More neurotoxic isomer; GABA-A antagonism at lower concentrations than β-thujone
- **Camphor** (5–20%): Counter-irritant; antimicrobial; insecticidal
- **Borneol, bornyl acetate** (5–15%): Antimicrobial; insecticidal; mild analgesic
- **Chrysanthenone, artemisia ketone**: Species-specific monoterpenes

**NOTE:** Chemotypes with dramatically different volatile oil profiles exist in T. vulgare:
- Type I (most common, toxic): β-thujone 40–70%
- Type II: camphor-dominant, low thujone — occasionally found in cultivation
- Type III: trans-chrysanthenyl acetate dominant
Clinical and toxicological data refers primarily to Type I unless specified.

**Sesquiterpene Lactones:**
- **Tatridin A and B**: Eudesmanolide-type; anti-inflammatory; significantly lower parthenolide content than T. parthenium (confirmed — no migraine efficacy from T. vulgare)
- **Vulgarin**: NF-κB inhibition; antifeedant

**Flavonoids:**
- **Axillarin, luteolin, quercetin**: Anti-inflammatory; antioxidant
- **Jaceosidin**: Anti-inflammatory polymethoxyflavone (shared with Artemisia species)

**Polyacetylenes:**
- Antimicrobial; unstable; antifungal in fresh plant

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**1. Anthelmintic (Primary Traditional Mechanism)**

Thujone's GABA-A antagonism extends to helminth and arthropod neuromuscular systems — where GABA is the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in peripheral nerve-muscle junctions. At concentrations that are sublethal to mammals, thujone produces:
- Paralytic effects in Ascaris lumbricoides (roundworm) — loss of coordinated movement → expelled by gut peristalsis
- Lethal effects in Enterobius vermicularis (pinworm) at direct intestinal contact
- Repellent effects on ectoparasites (fleas, lice) via volatile diffusion

**2. Insecticidal/Insect-Repellent**

Thujone + camphor + borneol are contact insecticides:
- House flies (Musca domestica): LD₅₀ 0.1–0.5 mg/fly for tansy extract
- Fleas (Ctenocephalides felis): Repellent and lethal at topical exposure
- Blowflies (Calliphora vomitoria): Tansy volatile oil prevents oviposition on meat (the "meat preservation" application)

**3. Uterotonic/Emmenagogue**

β-Thujone's uterotonic mechanism (same as A. vulgaris/A. absinthium thujone — see I-415, I-416): stimulates oxytocin receptor sensitivity, calcium channel activation in uterine smooth muscle → enhanced uterine contractility. At doses used historically as abortifacients, lethal thujone toxicity frequently preceded or accompanied the uterotonic effect — explaining the documented fatalities from tansy-induced abortions in 19th-century medical reports.

**4. Anti-Inflammatory (Flavonoids)**

Axillarin, luteolin, quercetin — standard flavonoid NF-κB and COX-2 inhibition at micromolar concentrations. Anti-inflammatory activity is real but secondary to the safety concerns with internal use.

**5. Antimicrobial (Volatile Oil)**

Tansy essential oil (thujone + camphor) shows broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity (MIC against gram-positive organisms 0.5–4 mg/mL; gram-negative organisms 2–8 mg/mL). Topical antifungal (Candida, dermatophytes) activity documented.

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Level C — Anthelmintic (Traditional Evidence, No Modern RCT):**

No modern RCT has been conducted on tansy anthelmintics due to the documented toxicity risk. Traditional evidence spans 2,000+ years across European traditions. Pharmacological mechanism confirmed in vitro. However, safer anthelmintic options (mebendazole, albendazole; also wormwood I-416, cloves I-XXX) make tansy an obsolete option for internal anthelmintic use.

**Level C — Topical Insect Repellent (Traditional Evidence):**

Multiple ethnobotanical validations of insect-repellent effect. Thujone and camphor confirmed insecticidal in laboratory. Topical application to animals for fly/flea control — practiced in organic farming; some efficacy data. Not FDA or HMPC registered for this use.

**Level B — Antimicrobial (In Vitro):**

Tansy essential oil: activity documented against S. aureus, Salmonella, E. coli, Candida at concentrations achievable in topical preparations. Not clinically validated in human trials.

**SAFETY DATA (Critical Context):**

Multiple documented fatalities from internal tansy use:
- Historical reports: women taking tansy for menstrual induction dying from convulsions (thujone GABA-A antagonism → tonic-clonic seizures)
- Contemporary: case reports of thujone toxicity from tansy tea
- FDA: classified as "unsafe herb" for food use (CFR 21)
- EU HMPC: No positive monograph; safety concerns preclude traditional use designation

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Easter Tansy and the Spring Purge:**

Tansy cake (sometimes called "tansy pudding" or "tansies") was eaten across England, Ireland, and parts of Scotland on Easter Sunday and the days following — the "tansy season." The dish was made from fresh tansy leaves and/or juice mixed with eggs, sugar, and cream, fried like a pancake. The Easter timing was explicitly medicinal: after 40 days of Lenten dietary restriction (reduced meat, fish, and dairy; increased legumes and grains), intestinal parasite loads were believed to increase. Tansy's anthelmintic action "purged the bowels" of winter's accumulated worms.

The green color of tansy pancakes also served symbolic Paschal purposes — green as spring renewal; the bitterness connecting to the bitter herbs of the Passover Seder (which Easter historically derived from). The culinary-medicinal integration of tansy into a religious festival represents the highest form of traditional pharmacological incorporation: the medicine becomes ritual, the ritual maintains the pharmacological practice across generations without requiring medical knowledge in the practitioner.

By the 18th century, tansy cake was falling out of fashion as the toxicity of large doses became better understood and as safer culinary alternatives emerged. The practice largely disappeared by the 19th century — one of many traditional botanical foods abandoned as food science separated medicinal from culinary and as medicine became professionalized.

**Corpse Herb:**

The connection of tansy to death and preservation is among the most persistent in the ethnobotanical record. In medieval Christian Europe, tansy was placed in grave-clothes, coffin linings, and burial shrouds — the volatile thujone and camphor oils retarding insect infestation and slowing decomposition. Church herbaria maintained tansy for this purpose. The Greek *athanasia* (immortality) as the plant's ancient name may encode the same observation: the plant that keeps death's accompaniments (insects, decay) at bay is, in a sense, the plant of "immortality."

The corpse-preservation use has a direct pharmacological logic: thujone is lethal to blowflies (which initiate decomposition via larvae), camphor retards bacterial growth, and borneol has antimicrobial effects on the anaerobic bacteria driving putrefaction. The observation that flesh preserved with tansy "lasted longer" was correct, and the religious-cultural practice encoded a genuine preservation technology.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Primary Indications (EXTERNAL/TOPICAL ONLY):**
- Insect repellent (topical — diluted essential oil; dried plant sachets; garden planting)
- Antifungal (topical — diluted essential oil for nail fungus, ringworm — with caution)
- Garden insect deterrent (companion planting — repels ants, aphids, Colorado potato beetle, squash bugs)

**INTERNAL USE: NOT RECOMMENDED**

Internal use of tansy is associated with documented fatalities and should not be recommended. This is not a herb for which any internal application can be justified given:
1. High thujone content in all common chemotypes
2. Very narrow therapeutic-to-toxic ratio
3. Available safer alternatives for all traditional indications (wormwood for digestive bitters; mebendazole for anthelmintic; feverfew for headache; safer emmenagogues)

**External/Environmental Applications Only:**

*Dried Plant Sachets (Insect Repellent):*
- Hang dried tansy bunches in closets, kitchens, pantries — repels moths, flies, ants
- Replace every 3–4 months as volatile oil evaporates
- Do NOT place where children or pets can access

*Essential Oil (Diluted — External Only):*
- Maximum 0.5% in carrier oil (thujone concentration must be controlled)
- Test for contact sensitivity — some individuals develop dermatitis
- AVOID near eyes, mucous membranes, or large surface areas (systemic absorption risk)

*Garden Companion Planting:*
- Plant around vegetable beds to repel Colorado potato beetle, aphids, and other pests
- Naturalized tansy planting provides season-long volatile deterrence
- Not suitable for organic food-proximal planting without local regulatory guidance

**ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATIONS (Internal):**

⛔ **ALL INTERNAL USE** — historical fatalities; FDA unsafe classification; EU no positive monograph

⛔ **PREGNANCY** — uterotonic, abortifacient; multiple documented fatal outcomes at doses intended for abortifacient use

⛔ **EPILEPSY** — thujone convulsant risk

⛔ **HEPATIC IMPAIRMENT** — thujone hepatotoxicity

⛔ **CHILDREN** — extreme danger; small body mass means lethal thujone doses are very small

**Historical Curiosity Note:**  
Some early 20th-century British herbals (Grieve, Culpeper reprints) still recommend tansy for internal use at doses that would now be considered dangerous. These historical recommendations should not be followed. Pharmacological knowledge of thujone toxicity has definitively superseded traditional dosing recommendations for this herb.
