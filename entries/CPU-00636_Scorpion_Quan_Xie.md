**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** 2-13 SKORPIOS CHERSAIOS, "Scorpionidae — Scorpion", the "ground scorpion" (line 4967 in `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`): "The ground scorpion — taken raw, pounded into small pieces, and so applied — is a remedy for the hurt done by it. It is also roasted for the same purpose." That is the whole chapter. The next chapter, 2-14 SKORPIOS THALASSIOS, is a different animal (the translator's gloss is "Scorpaena, Scorpaenidae — Scorpion Fish"): "The gall of the sea scorpion is good for bathing eyes, white spots on the cornea, and excessive moisture in the eyes". The land scorpion also appears as a hurt to be treated: the *mullus* (the Latin name the translation uses), cut apart raw and applied, "heals the hurt caused by the sea dragon, scorpion, and the spider" (2-24, line 5020), cut-apart house mice "are usefully applied to those touched by scorpions" (2-73, line 5233), and many plant chapters name scorpion bites as an indication (for example scorpion grass, lines 9752-9755, and lychnis seed, line 7563).

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** Scorpions are a poisonous animal in *Kalpasthana* ch.8 (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/kalpa/SS_Kalpa_ch08.txt`), and not a drug. Of treatment, the passage says: "In the case of a Scorpion bite, the affected part should not be fomented. It might, however, be fumigated with vapours of the drugs to be dealt with later on" (lines 282-285). Classification (lines 326-352): "Scorpions are divided into three classes, viz., the mild-poisoned ones (Manda-visha), those whose poison is neither mild nor strong (Madhya-visha), and the strong-poisoned ones (Maha-visha). Scorpions germinating from cow-dung or from any other rotten substances are Manda-visha. Those which germinate from (decomposed) wood or (decayed) bricks are Madhya-visha ... while those which originate from the decomposed carcase of a snake or from any other poisonous putrid organic matter are Tikshna-visha (strong-poisoned)", twelve, three and fifteen species, "making thirty species in all"; a note gives Gayadasa's count of twenty-seven (11, 3, 13). The mild-poisoned bite brings "pain ..., shivering, numbness of the limbs and a flow of blackish blood"; the middle class "a swelling of the tongue, an incapacity of deglutition and violent epileptic fits"; the keen-poisoned "pustular eruptions ... accompanied by vertigo, a burning sensation, fever and excessive discharge of black-coloured blood", and "their bite proves so rapidly fatal" (lines 353-411). Treatment (lines 413-451): a bite by a middle or strong scorpion "should be treated as a case of snake-bite to all intents and purposes"; for a mild one the part is sprinkled with *Chakra-Taila* or a tepid oil cooked with the *Vidaryadi* group, fomented with poultices of anti-venomous drugs (line 421, against the opening instruction not to foment, line 284), scratched and rubbed with powders of *Haridra*, *Saindhava*, *Trikatu* and the fruit and flower of *Shirisha*, with drinks of ghee and honey, milk with sugar and honey, or treacle; and "Fumigation with the compound made of the feathers of the tail of a cock or a peacock, Saindhava, oil and clarified butter pasted together and burnt is a speedy destroyer of scorpion-poison". In *Kalpasthana* ch.3 the venom of a scorpion, with several other creatures', is located in the body: the OCR text reads "lies in their [footnote marker] ... saliva" across a page break, and the footnote gives a variant reading (`kalpa/SS_Kalpa_ch03.txt` lines 36-40), which was not resolved. [USER: verify] In *Sutrasthana* ch.17 a pain is described as one in which the patient "as if stung by a scorpion, does not find comfort in any place" (`sutra/SS_Sutra_ch17.txt` lines 92-94). No use of the scorpion itself as a drug was found in the lines read.

### Active Components

Scorpion venom is a mixture of small neurotoxic peptides, enzymes and salts that act on nerve and muscle ion channels; the whole animal also contains proteins and fats. Venom potency varies greatly by species, and only a few species are dangerous to people. The sources here treat the scorpion as a poisoner, so the relevant composition is the venom.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. In the sources the scorpion is a source of poison and a thing to be treated (Sanskrit), a like-for-like remedy for its own sting (Greek), and an unread Assyrian reference. Nothing here supports taking or applying a scorpion. The Codex offers no protocol, dose or preparation, and gives no first-aid instruction: a scorpion sting is a medical matter.

### Preparation & Dosing

Only preparations the texts give. DMM 2-13: the scorpion pounded raw and applied to the sting, or roasted and applied. SS: no preparation of the scorpion itself; the preparations are for its bite (oil, poultices, rubbing powders, drinks, and tail-feather fumigation, as above). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

"Like cures like" applications of a scorpion to its own sting have no established mechanism. The treatment of scorpion envenomation today is supportive and, for dangerous species, antivenom; the Sanskrit instruction not to fomente the bite, and the Greek one to apply the animal, are historical. Venom peptides are a subject of laboratory research. No clinical studies were retrieved for the traditional uses. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

Scorpion stings can be dangerous, and children and people with allergy are at greatest risk. Whole-animal and venom preparations are unstandardised and may be adulterated or contaminated; they are not safe to take. Scorpions are collected from the wild, and some species are listed under international wildlife-trade rules; the status of any particular species has not been checked here. [USER: verify] Ritual and fumigation uses are ritual, not therapy.
