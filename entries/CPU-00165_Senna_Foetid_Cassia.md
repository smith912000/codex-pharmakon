### Origin & Tradition

**Identity note.** This row is marked UNCOVERED in the spine: *Cassia tora* is not the plant of the existing senna entry (`I_216_Senna_Cassia_senna.md`, *Cassia senna* / *Senna alexandrina*, tagged CM and AT), and a search of Volume I found no earlier entry for *Cassia tora*. The row name "Senna / Foetid Cassia" joins two names. Neither Tibetan text read calls the plant "senna"; Clark prints "Foetid Cassia" and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary prints "Sickle pod". The word "senna" is the spine's, and nothing read supports it. Whether the plant read as *Cassia tora* in the two Tibetan texts is the same species that modern botanists now name separately is a question the texts do not touch. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).**

*Clark 1995, Explanatory Tantra ch.20 (materia medica).* `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, line 898: "thal.ka.rdo.rje: Foetid Cassia: Cassia tora: [has four names. It has small leaves and fruits, slightly bitter taste, and dries lymph (accumulations). It has a green leaf and stem, with yellow flower and yellow wrinkled seed pod. The seed resembles the sex organ of a dog, and the hard type is superior. The common type has a thin seed pod and softer seed, with a band around it. All types] have the same uses as Cannabis (above)." The entry directly above it (line 897) reads, in part: "so.ma.ra.dza: Cannabis medica: [has thirteen names. ... The seed has sweet taste, and is slightly oily (in power) and cures wind disorders, lymph disorders, and worms. ...] It cures skin diseases and lymph conditions." Clark's cross-reference does not say whether "the same uses" means the bracketed description of Cannabis or its closing sentence, so the uses of the Foetid Cassia are stated here only as far as line 898 and the Cannabis entry together allow. Clark's extraction row is `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 55.

*Clark 1995, ch.21 (compounding).* Line 1220: "Medicines used in wind disorders include Nutmeg, raw cane sugar and various bones, whilst the class of medicines for lymph disorders consists of Sal tree resin, Foetid Cassia, Cannabis medica and concentrate of Catechu and Indian Barberry (root)." Foetid Cassia is therefore a named member of the class for lymph disorders.

### Active Components

Plants of the genus *Cassia* in the broad sense are generally known to contain anthraquinone derivatives, the chemical class of the senna laxatives. The composition of the plant the Tibetan texts name was not read, and neither text states one. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, and not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts place this seed among the medicines that "dry lymph" (Clark line 898) and treat *chhuser* disorders (Men-Tsee-Khang lines 3392, 3635 and 4276). That is the tradition's own claim for its own frame of disease. The Codex does not turn it into a protocol, and no home preparation can be derived from the sources read.

### Preparation & Dosing

Clark (lines 898 and 1220) and the Men-Tsee-Khang text (lines 3392, 3635 and 4276) give no preparation, quantity or vehicle for this plant. At Men-Tsee-Khang line 4276 it is named among supplementary ingredients "added" for *chhuser* disorder, which fixes its role in a compound but not its amount. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark line 898 prints "slightly bitter taste" for the plant and says it "dries lymph (accumulations)". No potency, post-digestive taste or secondary quality is printed for it in the lines read. The Men-Tsee-Khang lines read give actions only, and no tastes.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Foetid Cassia sits in the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica and in its class for lymph (*chhuser*) disorders, a therapeutic frame. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition pathology is at root a spiritual condition, that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching the root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. Nothing read places this plant in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or in any practice. The status is unattested.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the sources read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. In the Tibetan texts it is a bitter seed used against one family of disorders and added to compounds under a physician's direction. The honest answer for a cultivator is to do nothing with it.

### Contraindications

No safety data were read. Plants related to the senna group can act as stimulant laxatives, so this seed must not be used as a purgative or "cleanse", and anyone with abdominal pain, bowel disease, pregnancy or a child to care for should not experiment. None of the traditional uses above is advice. Seek a qualified practitioner for any real disorder, and ordinary medical care for infection or swelling.
