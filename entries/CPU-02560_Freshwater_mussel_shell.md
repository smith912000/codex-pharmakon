# Freshwater mussel shell — 马刀 *madao* (Shennong bencao jing), "horse-knife"

## *A long, blade-shaped bivalve shell, our identification (medium confidence) as a freshwater mussel of the family Unionidae, genus uncertain (Lamprotula or Cristaria are candidates); the text's own habitat lists include rivers, lakes and the sea, so a freshwater animal is not the only reading* | *(Volume III — Animal)*

**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Chinese / Shennong bencao jing (SBJ).** The *madao* shell is named only by the *Shennong bencao jing* among the texts on disk. The identification is ours. The source is Sun Xingyan's 1799 reconstruction (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_SunXingyan_1799_daizhige.txt`); the entry stands in the lower classic (*Shennong bencao jing*, Sun Xingyan ed. 1799, lower classic, entry 马刀 *madao*, same file, line 3839). The name means "horse knife", a blade-shaped thing, but the text does not describe the animal.

**The Shennong text proper** (line 3839): "Flavour pungent, slightly cold. It governs 'leaking below', red and white; cold and heat; it breaks stone-*lin* (urinary stone); it kills birds, beasts and thieving rats. It grows in ponds and marshes." (味辛，微寒。主漏下赤白，寒热，破石淋，杀禽兽、贼鼠。生池泽。) Sun adds a bracketed note that the *Taiping yulan* adds the two characters "supplements the middle" (补中) and that the Daguan edition prints this passage in black characters. The clause "kills birds, beasts and thieving rats" is a poisoning use, and the paragraph does not say how the shell is applied.

**The Wu Pu layer** (line 3841, from the *Yulan*): "One name *qige* (齐蛤). Shennong, Qi Bo and Tong Jun: salty, toxic; Bian Que: slightly cold, greatly toxic. Grows in ponds, marshes, rivers and the sea. Gather at any time." (一名齐蛤。神农、岐伯、桐君：咸，有毒；扁鹊：小寒，大毒。生池泽、江海。采无时也。) Note that this layer reports Shennong's flavour as salty and as toxic, where the paragraph above says pungent and slightly cold and does not mention toxicity. The two layers disagree, and both are given here. The Wu Pu layer is the only place where toxicity is stated.

**The Mingyi bielu layer** (line 3843): "One name *mage* (马蛤). Grows in rivers, lakes and the Eastern Sea. Gather at any time." (一名马蛤。生江湖及东海。采无时。)

**Sun Xingyan's note (案, line 3845).** Sun quotes the *Fan Zi Jiran* ("*madao* comes from Hedong"), and the *Yiwen leiju*'s quotation of the *Ben jing*: "*wenge*, with markings on the surface"; and "also *madao*, one name *ge*". He then asks: "might the old text have made it one with *wenge*?" (则岂古本与文蛤为一邪?). This is his question and not a finding. It shows that identity with the patterned clam (*wenge*) was an open point in his day.

**Preface.** The lower class is "the left-hand envoy, mainly treating disease to correspond to earth; mostly toxic, not to be taken for long" (line 2935).

### Active Components

The text names no constituent. In general terms, a bivalve shell is mostly calcium carbonate laid down with a small amount of organic matrix, and shells of freshwater mussels often have a nacreous inner layer. The soft body of a mollusc can concentrate metals and the toxins of algae from the water. Nothing more specific is stated in the texts. No analytical source was retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The classic records the shell for red and white discharge, for cold and heat and for urinary stone, and as something that kills birds, beasts and rats. The Wu Pu layer calls it toxic. The Codex does not turn these into modern indications. Urinary stone and abnormal discharge need medical care. The pest-killing clause is a record of an old toxic use and not a method.

### Preparation & Dosing

The Shennong paragraph gives no preparation and no dose. The Wu Pu and Mingyi layers say "gather at any time". No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Calcium carbonate shell powder is an antacid and calcium source; that is a general statement about the mineral, not a finding about this shell. Whether it acts on urinary stone or discharge has not been tested in sources retrieved for this entry. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **SBJ (Shennong).** The paragraph prints pungent (辛) and slightly cold (微寒). The Wu Pu layer reports, for Shennong, salty (咸) and toxic (有毒), and for Bian Que slightly cold and greatly toxic. Lower class by placement. The layers conflict, and the Codex does not choose between them.

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(b) Framed.* The entry is in the lower classic, which the preface (line 2935) describes as mainly for treating disease, mostly toxic and not for long use; the Wu Pu layer in the same entry calls the shell toxic. The paragraph has no *jiu fu* ("taken for long") clause and no life-nourishing wording. The grading itself is the warning: a drug for a defined complaint, not for long use.

**Practitioner's note.** There is nothing here for a cultivator. The classic records a lower-class shell, reported toxic by the Wu Pu layer, with no dose or course. Do nothing with it.

### Contraindications

- **Toxicity.** The Wu Pu layer reports the shell as toxic ("greatly toxic" for Bian Que). Do not take it. Shellfish bodies can also carry bacteria and algal toxins.
- **Identification.** The animal is not fixed by the text; the habitat lists include the sea and rivers, and Sun raises the possibility that the old text merged it with the patterned clam. The spine has separate entries for onycha (III_251, Dioscorides), mussel (III_289, rGyud-bzhi) and pearl (III_286); nothing from them is transferred here.
- **Conservation.** Freshwater mussels are among the most threatened animal groups in many regions; do not collect them.
- **Pregnancy, children, medicines.** The text says nothing; no use is supported. [USER: verify]
