# Parnassia palustris — Agrostis en Parnasso, the grass of Parnassus (Dioscorides 4-32)

## *Parnassia palustris, the single binomial on the translators' SUGGESTED line for 4-32 ("Grass of Parnassus"); a herb of white flower and several finger-thick roots, named for the mountain* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** The row was added because one text on disk, Dioscorides, names the plant under this chapter, and no other tradition on disk is tagged for it. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`. The chapter is *4-32. AGROSTIS EN PARNASSO*, heading at line 8288, the SUGGESTED line at line 8289 and the text at line 8290, in Book Four ("Other Herbs & Roots"). The heading means, in the translators' form, the *agrostis* (grass) "on Parnassus". The whole chapter reads:

"The grass that grows on Parnassus is more full of stems. It bears leaves similar to cissus [2-210], a white flower, and has a sweet scent, a small seed, and five or six effective roots of a finger's thickness — white, soft, strong. The juice of this (boiled with wine, as much honey, an half part of myrrh [1-77, 1-73, 4-116], pepper, and a third part of frankincense) is an excellent medicine for the eyes. It is stored in a brass box. A decoction of the roots is good for the same disorders. The seed is strongly diuretic, and stops vomits and flowing bowels. That which grows in Cilicia (which the inhabitants call cinna) inflames rude beasts if often fed on when it is moist."

The opening comparative, "more full of stems", measures the plant against something not named in the chapter. It follows 4-31 *KALAMAGROSTIS* (line 8285), a grass chapter where reed grass is said to be "bigger in every respect than gramen", but the chapter itself does not say what it compares with. The description is fuller than many: leaf compared with *cissus* (2-210), a white flower, a sweet scent, a small seed, and five or six roots described by thickness (a finger), colour (white), texture (soft) and strength. The uses are an eye medicine made from the juice, a root decoction for "the same disorders", and a seed that is "strongly diuretic" and stops vomiting and loose bowels. The last sentence records a place, Cilicia, a local name, *cinna*, and a hazard to animals: the plant there "inflames rude beasts" if they feed on it while it is moist. The text gives no deity and no amuletic use.

**The chapters around it.** 4-33 *SIDERITIS* (line 8291) follows and is a separate chapter, with a woundwort in the translators' suggestion; it is not carried over to this entry.

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for the chapter id 4-32 and for the names *parnassia*, *agrostis en parnasso* and "grass of Parnassus" found no existing entry on the same chapter. I_1074 (*Elatine paludosa*) mentions only that the translators print a plate caption "Parnassia palustris" after its own chapter (line 8354); that caption is a plate placed far from 4-32 and I_1074 is not a duplicate. ### Active Components

The composition of the plant Dioscorides describes was not read, and nothing is stated here about a particular species. As a general fact, *Parnassia* is a small marsh and meadow genus; no constituent is named in the chapter, and no composition was retrieved for it. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The chapter records an eye medicine made by boiling the juice with wine, honey, myrrh, pepper and frankincense, a root decoction for the same disorders, and a diuretic seed that stops vomiting and loose bowels. The Codex gives no protocol for any of these. Putting any preparation in the eye is a hygiene risk, and the chapter's own warning about the plant inflaming grazing animals is a reason for caution.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one compound preparation with proportions, with locus (line 8290): the juice "boiled with wine, as much honey, an half part of myrrh, pepper, and a third part of frankincense", stored in a brass box, as an eye medicine; and a decoction of the roots for the same disorders. The proportions are relative ("as much honey", "an half part", "a third part"); the amount of juice, the amount of wine, the amount of pepper and the dose or manner of use are not stated. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints a scent ("sweet"), a root quality ("white, soft, strong") and that the seed is "strongly diuretic". It prints no warming, cooling, drying or moistening quality for 4-32.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: the plant is named for its mountain, it is told from other grasses by leaf, flower, scent, seed and the number, thickness, colour and softness of its roots, a local race in Cilicia is distinguished and warned of, and the finished eye medicine is stored in a brass box. That is a provenance and a storage practice, not a subtle practice. The plant has no status in the chapter beyond that of a medicinal herb, and the text records no amuletic use. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The useful lesson is Dioscorides' method: to name a plant by its place, to describe the root as carefully as the leaf, and to note where the same plant is harmful. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

Eye medicines made at home are an infection risk and should not be put in the eye. The chapter warns that the plant growing in Cilicia inflames grazing animals when moist, which is a reminder that the plant may be harmful; the chapter's diuretic and anti-vomiting uses should not be tried.  The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
