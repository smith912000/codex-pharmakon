### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this name. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) printed under the chapter heading, not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The SUGGESTED line prints two binomials, *Erinus hispanicus* and *Erinus alpinus*, followed by the bracketed source "[Bedevian]", and then the English names "Erinus" and "Liver Balsam". The entry takes the first binomial. Whether the two are one plant in the translators' mind is not stated. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-29. ERINOS* at line 8262, SUGGESTED line at 8264, text at line 8265. The chapter stands in Book Four, "Other Herbs & Roots", and is followed by 4-30 *AGROSTIS*, a different plant. The translators' name index lists it at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`, line 609, and the consolidated extraction row is `EXTRACTION_CONSOLIDATED.csv` line 415.

The whole chapter reads: "Erinus grows by rivers and fountains and has leaves similar to those of basil yet smaller and jagged at the upper parts; with five or six little branches twenty centimeters long, white flowers, and a little black seed with an unpleasant taste. The stalk is full of liquid and so are the leaves. Two teaspoonfuls of the seed (mixed with four of honey and smeared on) stop discharges of the eyes; and the juice soothes earache (dropped in the ears with sulphur that never felt the fire and saltpetre [potassium nitrate]). It is also called ocimoides, or hydrero, while the Romans call it basil."

So the chapter has a habitat (by rivers and fountains), a description (leaves like basil but smaller and jagged toward the top, five or six branches of twenty centimetres, white flowers, small black seed, juicy stalk and leaves), two uses (the seed with honey, applied, for eye discharges; the juice, dropped in the ear with sulphur and saltpetre, for earache) and three names. The Romans' name for it, as Dioscorides prints it, is "basil".

*The name* ocimoides *and the chapter before it.* The alternative name *ocimoides* is also the heading of the chapter immediately before this one, *4-28. OKIMOEIDES* (heading at line 8253, text at line 8256). That chapter has its own SUGGESTED line (an *Acinos*, "Ocimum pilosum") and describes a plant with "leaves similar to basil" and "rough branches twenty centimetres long", whose seed decoction is used for snakebite and sciatica. The two chapters share the name *ocimoides* and the comparison with basil, and they are given different descriptions and different uses. The entry does not merge them or carry the uses of 4-28 to this plant. [USER: verify]

*The two substances named in the ear recipe.* The sulphur chapter (*5-124. THEION*, line 10477) says "The best sulphur is thought to have never felt fire and is glistening in colour, transparent, and without stones", which is the kind the earache recipe names. The saltpetre chapter (*5-130. NITRON*, line 10507) prefers nitre "smooth and rosy or white in colour, with holes through as though it were some spongy thing". Both are read here only to explain the recipe's wording and are not tagged to this entry.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records two external uses of one small riverside plant, an eye application of the seed and an ear instillation of the juice. The Codex gives no protocol. Discharge from the eyes and earache can signal infection and need medical assessment, and nothing should be dropped into an ear without a clinician's advice. The recipe also calls for sulphur and saltpetre, which are not household items to be handled casually.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives two preparations. The first is the seed: "Two teaspoonfuls of the seed (mixed with four of honey and smeared on) stop discharges of the eyes". The second is the juice of the plant, "dropped in the ears with sulphur that never felt the fire and saltpetre". The quantities of the juice, the sulphur and the saltpetre are not given, and no frequency or duration is given for either use. No dose is given in the sources retrieved for the juice. ### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. What the chapter gives toward the discipline of attention that the spec describes for this tradition is a field description and a note on provenance: where the plant grows (by rivers and fountains), how to know it (basil-like leaves, jagged toward the top, five or six branches, white flowers, small black seed of unpleasant taste), and that the ear recipe depends on a particular grade of sulphur, "that never felt the fire". That is the kind of attention to place, sensory assay and the quality of a companion ingredient that the spec names, and it is not a cultivation status. The identification is the translators'. **Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. The transferable point is a modest one: the text asks the user to know the plant by its habitat and its parts, and to name the quality of the sulphur that goes with it. The text offers nothing more.

### Contraindications

The chapter prints no warning. Eye discharge and earache need medical assessment, and ear drops of an untested preparation should not be used if the eardrum may be damaged. The identification is only the translators' suggestion, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Sulphur and saltpetre are chemicals with their own hazards. No safety information for pregnancy was read. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
