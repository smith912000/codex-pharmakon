# Papaver nudicaule — me.tog.ser.chhen, Metok serchen (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Papaver nudicaule L., the first of three binomials Clark 1995 prints for me.tog.ser.chhen (with Trollius asiaticus and Tagetes erecta L.; Clark gives no English name on the line); the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary sets Calendula officinalis and the English "Calendula, marigold" against the name "Metok serchen", so the two editions of the one text disagree on the plant* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions, and on a species name that only one of them prints.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian, Badianus, Avicenna and Sushruta material on disk for *Papaver nudicaule*, *Trollius* and the Tibetan name found nothing. Those texts do name other poppies, above all the opium poppy (*Papaver somniferum*, for example in the Ebers and Assyrian files and in Dioscorides), and a *Tagetes* in the Badianus files (*Tagetes lucida*) and in a Dioscorides index line; none of them is this drug and none is read as such. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 166) the row for *me.tog.ser.chhen* lists "Papaver nudicaule L.; Trollius asiaticus; Tagetes erecta L." with the note "more than one Latin name given; key uses the first". The key *Papaver nudicaule* is therefore the **first** of three binomials on Clark's line and not the only one; the other two are different genera in two other plant families as the Codex understands them, a buttercup-family genus and a daisy-family one (not checked against a flora). The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary does not print *Papaver nudicaule* at all. The row stands for Clark's first identification.

**Identity doubt.** The two editions give three plants in Clark and one different plant in the glossary for one Tibetan name. Clark's own preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", that "sometimes the same name refers to different plants growing in unrelated geographical locations" and (line 68) that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect". The glossary prints *Calendula officinalis* with "Calendula, marigold", and Clark's third binomial, *Tagetes erecta*, is also a marigold in English, but Clark's first is a poppy. The Codex does not choose among the four species. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1067).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (line 997). The entry follows *gyer.shing.pa* (line 1066) and is followed by *brag.skya.ha.wo* (line 1068). It reads:

"me.tog.ser.chhen: Papaver nudicaule L.: Trollius asiaticus: Tagetes erecta L.: [grows in dry soil and has a large, yellow flower, small, furry, lobed leaf, thin stalk and short petioles. It has bitter taste], unites wounds and heals necrosis of the channels."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The habitat, the description and the taste are therefore the commentary layer, and "unites wounds and heals necrosis of the channels" is outside the brackets, so it is the root-text layer as the brackets mark it. The binomials are Clark's identifications and not the root text's. The line prints no English name, no power and no preparation. Clark's name index lists *me.tog.ser.chhen* (line 1727); his Latin index lists *Papaver nudicaule* (line 1626), *Tagetes erecta* (line 1649) and *Trollius asiaticus* (line 1652).

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "M" block, lines 5471 to 5497).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. Four phonetic names are printed (lines 5471 to 5474): "Maru tse", "Men ga", "Metok lugmig" and "Metok serchen". Four transliterations follow (lines 5476 to 5479): "ma ru rtse", "sman sga", "me tok lug mig" and "me tok ser can". Four scientific names follow (lines 5481 to 5484): "Butea monosperma", "Hedychium spicatum", "Aster himalaicus" and "Calendula officinalis". The English names printed for the block (lines 5495 to 5497) are three: "Bastard teak", "Kind of ginger" and "Calendula, marigold" (the "Elecampane Elecampane" of line 5494 belongs to the rows above). Counted in order, "Metok serchen" is the fourth phonetic name, *Calendula officinalis* the fourth scientific name, and "Calendula, marigold" the last English name; the third row (*Aster himalaicus*) has no English name. The pairing is by position and is not printed in one row, but it is secure at its end: the last scientific name and the last English name agree with each other. It is treated in I_1239 for the neighbouring name "Metok lugmig". [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3448).* Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428): "Yhershingpa cures hot disorder associated with small pox. Metok Serchhen treats wounds and necrotic channels. Drakya hawo is an antidote against poison and stops dysentery." The spelling "Serchhen" differs from the glossary's "Serchen" only in the transliteration of the Tibetan sound. The printed index does not list "Metok serchen" or "Metok Serchhen" (a search of the file for "serch" found only line 3448 and the glossary line 5474). The name is not in the taste groups of chapter 19 and not in the rejuvenation chapter. The sentence gives no Latin name, no part used and no preparation.

The two editions agree in kind on the action: Clark's "unites wounds and heals necrosis of the channels" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "treats wounds and necrotic channels". The Codex reads Clark's *me.tog.ser.chhen* and the glossary's "Metok serchen" as one drug from the spelling and from that agreement; neither text says so. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Papaver nudicaule*, *me.tog.ser.chhen*, "Metok serchen", *Trollius* and *Tagetes erecta* found no entry on this drug. I_1239 (*Aster himalaicus*, tag FMT) and I_1238 (*Aster barbellatus*) print the glossary block that holds "Metok serchen" as a neighbouring name and do not give it an entry. I_958 (cempoalxochitl, *Tagetes erecta*, tag BM) is a Badianus entry on Clark's third binomial under an Aztec name and does not cite the Tibetan texts; the Codex does not read it as this drug. I_180 and I_192 (*Papaver somniferum*) are entries on the opium poppy from other traditions. The spine has no row for the glossary's *Calendula officinalis*. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Papaver* is a genus of the poppy family (Papaveraceae), that plants of that family characteristically contain isoquinoline alkaloids, and that the opium alkaloids are characteristic of *Papaver somniferum*, which is a different species. Nothing in the lines read says that *Papaver nudicaule*, or any of the other three candidate plants, contains them or that the Tibetan drug does. Clark's commentary describes a yellow-flowered, furry-leaved plant of dry ground and gives no constituent. No statement about this drug's composition is made. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a bitter yellow-flowered plant that "unites wounds and heals necrosis of the channels" (Clark, line 1067) and "treats wounds and necrotic channels" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3448). These are the texts' claims in their own terms and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose. A wound or tissue necrosis needs medical care, and no poppy-family plant should be applied or swallowed on the strength of this entry, particularly since the plant is not securely identified.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used, no quantity, no frequency and no duration. Clark's commentary describes flower, leaf, stalk and petioles but says nothing of a part taken or of external or internal use. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 1067) prints "bitter taste". No power and no post-digestive taste is printed. Men-Tsee-Khang prints no taste or power for "Metok Serchhen" in the lines read (3448, 5474), and the name is not among the taste groups of chapter 19.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes this drug by what it does: it unites wounds and heals necrosis of the channels, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same in kind. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names found it only in the lines cited above and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (chapter 23 in both editions), and Clark's materia medica names other drugs as essence-extraction ingredients (for example lines 853, 971 and 1144), none of them this one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name and not securely to this binomial.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a wound drug in the Tibetan texts, with no preparation and no dose, and the editions disagree on whether the plant is a poppy or a marigold. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Wounds and necrotic tissue need medical care. The identity is uncertain, no preparation or dose is given, and because the genus *Papaver* includes the opium poppy, which is subject to legal controls in many countries, no plant of that genus should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry; the legal status of *Papaver nudicaule* was not checked.
