# Vine-tie, diss / Ampelodesma tenax — Ampelos agria (Dioscorides 5-2); Oinanthe (5-5); Oinos oinanthinos (5-33)

## *Ampelodesma tenax* (the Dioscorides translators' suggested identification, listed together with *Vitis labrusca*, "Wild Vine") | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides.** This entry stands on one text, Dioscorides (DMM). It was added 2026-10-09 (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) because the translators' SUGGESTED lines of three chapters in Book Five name *Ampelodesma tenax*.

**Identity note.** In each of the three chapters the SUGGESTED line prints two names side by side: "Ampelodesma tenax — Vine-tie, Diss" and "*Vitis labrusca* — Wild Vine or Fox Grape". The text itself describes a wild grape vine that bears grapes, its flowers and its shoots, and does not describe a grass. *Ampelodesma tenax* is, by general knowledge, a tall Mediterranean tussock grass that does not bear grapes. The binomial therefore sits in the translators' gloss and not in anything Dioscorides says, and the row's identification is doubtful. [USER: verify] This is the principal doubt the entry records. The row has not been checked against a modern botanical authority. A "Grape" entry already exists (I_121, *Vitis vinifera*), and the plant of these chapters is Dioscorides' wild vine, not his cultivated one.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000).

*5-2 AMPELOS AGRIA* (heading at line 9771; text line 9774). SUGGESTED: "Ampelodesma tenax — Vine-tie, Diss *Vitis labrusca* — Wild Vine or Fox Grape". The whole chapter reads: "Two types of wild vine are found. The grape of one sort does not ripen, but at its flowering time it brings forth shoots or buds. The other brings to ripeness a grape that is small-grained, black and astringent. The leaves, tendrils and stalks have properties similar to the cultivated vine." The chapter is a single note on two kinds and refers the properties to the cultivated vine of the previous chapters.

*5-5 OINANTHE* (heading at line 9794; text from line 9797). SUGGESTED: "Ampelodesma tenax — Vine-tie, Diss *Vitis labrusca* — Grapes from the Wild Vine", with the translators' remark: "Oenanthe is Water Dropwort or Water Hemlock — poisonous". The text: "The fruit of the wild vine when it flowers is called oenanthe. After they have first gathered it and dried it on a sheet in the shade it must be put into a ceramic jar. The best is from Syria, Cilicia and Phoenicia. It is astringent; as a result a decoction (taken as a drink) is good for the stomach and urine, for stopping the intestines, and the spitting of blood." It continues with external uses: dried and smeared on for a queasy sour stomach; used green or dry with vinegar and *rosaceum* [1-53] "as an inhalant for headaches"; pounded with honey, saffron, rosaceum and myrrh as a poultice for bleeding wounds, new eye and mouth ulcers and gangrenous ulceration; mixed into suppositories "for repressing blood"; smeared with flour of polenta and wine for eye discharges and a burning stomach; burnt in a ceramic jar with coals "to add to eye medicines"; and with honey for hangnails, pterygium and bleeding gums. That remark is the translators', not a statement in the chapter, and it concerns the name *oenanthe*, which they note can also belong to a poisonous water plant.

*5-33 OINOS OINANTHINOS* (heading at line 9944; continuing past a page break). SUGGESTED: "Ampelodesma tenax — Vine-tie, Diss *Vitis labrusca* — Wild Vine". The text: "Vinum oenanthinum [from vine shoots or blossoms] is made as follows. Take two pounds of the dried flowers of the wild vine's new shoots, put it into nine gallons of must [grape pulp] for thirty days, then having strained it put it in jars. It is good for weaknesses of the stomach, lack of appetite, for the abdominal cavity, and for dysentery."

The index of the translation lists the binomial at three pages (742, 745 and 760), which match these three chapters (index entry at line 14647).

### Active Components

The chapters describe the flowers, fruit and shoots of a wild vine. Grape vines generally contain tannins and other polyphenols, which fits the astringency Dioscorides states. If the plant were instead the grass *Ampelodesma*, this statement would not apply. The constituents of the plant of these chapters were not read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. Dioscorides records the dried flower-fruit of the wild vine (*oenanthe*) as an astringent for the stomach, urine and bleeding, with external uses on wounds and eyes, and a steeped wine of its shoots for the stomach and dysentery. These are the text's claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol.

### Preparation & Dosing

5-5: the fruit gathered when the vine flowers, dried in the shade on a sheet and stored in a ceramic jar; best from Syria, Cilicia and Phoenicia; taken as a decoction, or applied with the honey, saffron, rosaceum and myrrh named above. 5-33: "two pounds of the dried flowers of the wild vine's new shoots" in "nine gallons of must for thirty days", then strained into jars (a wine, whose dose is not stated). No dose for the human is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

Astringent plant materials rich in tannins are generally known to tighten tissue and reduce secretions, which agrees in kind with the astringency stated. This is a general statement and no study was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** 5-5 prints "astringent". 5-2 prints "astringent" for the black grape. 5-33 prints no quality beyond the uses. No warming, cooling or other Galenic quality is printed in these chapters.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. The three chapters offer what the cultivation spec calls a discipline of attention: when to gather (when the vine flowers), how to dry (in the shade, on a sheet), how to store (in a ceramic jar), and which provenance is best (Syria, Cilicia and Phoenicia). They record no use for the person's constitution and assign no status. The binomial is a translators' gloss and the chapters describe a vine.

**Practitioner's note.** The practice the chapters teach is gathering and drying at the right season and judging by place of origin. Nothing in them gives a cultivator a practice for the person. Without a settled identification no use is recommended.

### Contraindications

The identification is doubtful, so the plant should not be sought out or used on the strength of these chapters. The translators' remark in 5-5 warns that "oenanthe" can also name a poisonous water plant, which is a reason not to treat the name as a guide to the plant. Astringent preparations are not suited to people with constipation. None of the traditional uses above is advice.
