# Grape / Vine — *Vitis vinifera* L.

**Family:** Vitaceae | **Class:** plant | **Volume:** I · Plant | **Codex id:** CPU-00120
**Attested in:** rGyud-bzhi · Shennong Bencao Jing · Sushruta · Ebers Papyrus · Assyrian tablets

---

## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

*Vitis vinifera* L. (Vitaceae), the common grapevine, is a woody, deciduous climber native to the Mediterranean basin and the Caucasus, domesticated at least 6,000–8,000 years ago. The taxonomic name was established by Linnaeus in *Species Plantarum* (1753).

**Vernacular and classical names (selected):**

| Tradition | Name | Referent |
|---|---|---|
| Sanskrit | *Draksha* (fruit/raisin); *Mridvika* (soft/ripe fruit) | Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India [1] |
| Classical Chinese | *Pútáo* (葡萄) | Shennong Bencao Jing [2] |
| Tibetan | *gNam lug me tog* (grape; Sowa Rigpa terminology varies by text) | PMC dataset [3] |
| Ancient Egyptian | *irp* (wine); grapes listed by ingredient in remedies | Ebers Papyrus [4] |
| Akkadian/Assyrian | *karānu* (wine); *inbu* (fruit/grape) | Mesopotamian cuneiform corpus [5] |

**Medicinal parts:** ripe fruit (fresh); dried fruit / raisins (*Draksha* of commerce); leaves; unfermented juice; and wine — used both as a medicine in its own right and as a solvent/vehicle for other drugs across multiple traditions.

## 2. Cross-Traditional Presence

### 2.1 rGyud-bzhi (Tibetan Medicine / Sowa Rigpa)

The *rGyud bzhi* (Four Tantras), compiled by G.yu thog Yon tan mgon po in the twelfth century CE and still the foundational reference of Sowa Rigpa, classifies materia medica within a trihumoral framework of *rlung* (wind), *mkhris pa* (bile), and *bad kan* (phlegm) [6]. A dataset drawn from the *rGyud bzhi* and from Gawé Dorjé's *Stainless Crystal Mirror of Materia Medica* records grape (*Vitis vinifera*) among Tibetan botanical remedies [3]. **Specific indications within the Four Tantras remain difficult to verify from available secondary sources; claims of precise rlung or mkhris pa applications are therefore not asserted here.** Wine is attested more broadly in Tibetan pharmaceutical preparation as a solvent and warming vehicle.

### 2.2 Shennong Bencao Jing (Classical Chinese Medicine)

The *Shennong Bencao Jing*, the oldest extant Chinese materia medica, compiled in the Eastern Han dynasty (c. 25–220 CE), records grape (*pútáo*) in its upper (*shang pin*) category — substances classified as non-toxic tonics promoting health and longevity [2]. The text states that grape "primarily treats damp impediment in the sinews and bones, augments *qi* and doubles strength, fortifies the will, makes a person robust and tolerant of hunger, and withstands wind and cold" [7]. The *Dian Nan Ben Cao* (fifteenth century) adds that grape "greatly tonifies *qi* and blood, soothes the sinews, and activates the collaterals" [7].

### 2.3 Sushruta Samhita (Ayurveda)

The *Sushruta Samhita*, the classical surgical compendium attributed to Sushruta (debated date, broadly c. third–fourth century CE in extant form), recognises *Draksha* (dried ripe grape, i.e., raisin) and *Mridvika* (the fresh soft fruit) as distinct medicinal entries [1]. The Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India confirms *Draksha* as the dried mature fruit of *Vitis vinifera* L., used in trade as raisins [1]. Therapeutically, the dried fruit is described as demulcent, cooling, sweet, laxative, and stomachic; indicated for *Trishna* (thirst), *Jvara* (fever), *Kasa* (cough), *Shvasa* (dyspnoea), *Daha* (burning sensation), *Shosha* (wasting), *Kamala* (jaundice), and *Raktapitta* (bleeding disorders) [8]. In the Ayurvedic humoral framework, *Draksha* is classified as pacifying *Vata* and *Pitta* while being acceptable in moderation for *Kapha* [1][8]. The fermented preparation *Drakshasava* (wine-based medicinal tonic) is mentioned in classical texts as a post-febrile and digestive restorative [9].

### 2.4 Ebers Papyrus (Ancient Egypt, c. 1550 BCE)

The Ebers Papyrus (Papyrus Ebers, c. 1550 BCE, likely copied from earlier compositions), containing approximately 800 prescriptions referencing some 700 plant and mineral substances, employs wine (*irp*) as one of the standard pharmaceutical vehicles — alongside beer, milk, and honey — for compounded oral medicines [4][10]. Grapes and raisins appear as direct ingredients: one remedy for diarrhoea lists figs and grapes among its components [10]. Wine served primarily as a solvent enhancing absorption and palatability of otherwise bitter botanical preparations, a role attested across many formulae in the text [4].

### 2.5 Assyrian Cuneiform Tablets (Neo-Assyrian / Mesopotamian)

The surviving Mesopotamian medical corpus comprises approximately 1,000 cuneiform tablets, with 660 from the Library of Ashurbanipal (seventh century BCE) now held in the British Museum [5][11]. In Neo-Assyrian therapeutic texts, wine (*karānu*) functions as a standard pharmaceutical solvent and vehicle: "beer or wine served as solvents for ingestion in Assyrian medical treatments" [11]. Some preparations combine herbs with wine and oils imported from adjacent regions. The library excavated from the *asipu* (healer-exorcist) practitioner's house at Aššur also preserves recipes in this form [5]. As with Egypt, grape/wine in Mesopotamia functions predominantly as a pharmaceutical medium rather than a named drug in isolation.

## 3. Convergence Analysis

Five traditions separated by geography, language, and cosmological framework independently converge on a narrow cluster of applications: *Vitis vinifera* as (i) a tonic for strength and recovery from debility or wasting; (ii) a treatment for fever, thirst, and burning sensations; (iii) a pharmaceutical solvent that improves bioavailability and palatability of other drugs; and (iv) a vehicle for post-illness restoration. The convergence of tonic and febrifuge use across Ayurveda, Chinese medicine, and Egyptian practice — independent of shared textual lineage — may reflect empirical observation of the plant's caloric density, cooling organic acids (particularly tartaric acid), and hydrating properties of ripe or dried fruit. The pharmaceutical vehicle role in Egypt and Mesopotamia parallels the fermented *Drakshasava* formulations in Ayurveda, suggesting that alcohol's solvent and preservative properties were recognised across these cultures independently.

## 4. Phytochemistry & Composition

*Vitis vinifera* parts contain a well-characterised set of bioactive constituents [12][13]:

- **Polyphenols (skin and seed):** resveratrol (a stilbenoid phytoalexin), proanthocyanidins (oligomeric: 92–95% of standardised grape seed extract), anthocyanins (in red varieties), quercetin, kaempferol, catechin, and epicatechin [12][13].
- **Organic acids (fruit):** tartaric acid is the dominant and taxonomically distinctive acid of *Vitis vinifera*; also malic, citric, and succinic acids [14].
- **Vitamins and minerals:** vitamin C, vitamin K, potassium, manganese.
- **Sugars:** glucose and fructose (in ripe fruit); concentrated in raisins.
- **Leaves:** flavonoids, tannins, hydroxycinnamic acids.

Resveratrol is biosynthesised in the skin as a phytoalexin in response to fungal stress and UV radiation [15]. Concentrations vary substantially by cultivar, climate, and processing.

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

Proposed mechanisms, drawn from in vitro and preclinical research:

1. **Nitric oxide (NO) upregulation:** Red wine polyphenols enhance endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) expression in human umbilical vein endothelial cells, increasing NO release up to 3.0-fold in laboratory studies [16]. This underpins vasodilatory effects.
2. **Antioxidant / reactive oxygen species scavenging:** Proanthocyanidins and anthocyanins reduce lipid peroxidation and protect cells from ROS-mediated damage [13].
3. **Anti-inflammatory signalling:** Resveratrol and grape polyphenols suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, CRP) in cell and animal models [17].
4. **SIRT1 / mTOR pathway modulation:** Resveratrol activates SIRT1 deacetylase in animal models, which has downstream effects on metabolism, senescence, and inflammation; this pathway has been extensively studied but remains incompletely characterised in humans [18].
5. **Anticoagulant and anti-thrombotic effects:** Polyphenols reduce platelet aggregation and thrombin activity in experimental models [19].

The bioavailability of resveratrol after oral ingestion is low in humans due to rapid phase II metabolism; this is a significant constraint on translating in vitro findings to clinical outcomes [18].

## 6. Clinical Evidence

The clinical evidence base is heterogeneous in quality. Findings are reported by compound/preparation:

**Grape seed extract (GSE) / proanthocyanidins — blood pressure:**
A meta-analysis of 16 randomised controlled trials (n = 810) published in *Medicine* (2016) found significant reductions in systolic BP (−6.077 mmHg) and diastolic BP (−2.803 mmHg) following GSE treatment; effects were larger in younger subjects, obese individuals, and those with metabolic disorders [20]. A subsequent meta-analysis of grape products published in 2021 broadly supports modest BP effects [21]. Methodological heterogeneity and small sample sizes limit conclusions.

**Grape polyphenols — inflammation:**
A systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs (*BMC Complementary Medicine and Therapies*, 2020) found that grape polyphenols significantly reduced selected inflammatory markers, though heterogeneity was high [22].

**Resveratrol — cardiovascular and metabolic:**
A 2024 systematic review in *IJMS* found that, despite extensive preclinical promise, "there is currently no conclusive clinical evidence to advocate its [resveratrol's] recommendation in any healthcare setting" [18]. A meta-analysis on coronary artery disease (PubMed 2024) noted anti-inflammatory potential but again flagged insufficient high-quality RCT data [17]. The gap between in vitro and human outcomes is substantial and well-recognised; poor bioavailability and rapid metabolism are the primary constraints.

**Assessment:** GSE for blood pressure has the strongest current human evidence base, albeit from small trials. Resveratrol's clinical story is one of compelling mechanism but unconfirmed human efficacy. Caution is warranted before making therapeutic claims based on preclinical data alone.

## 7. Phenomenological Record

Across traditions, the fresh grape and raisin are described in experiential rather than mechanistic terms:

- In Ayurveda, *Draksha* is described as producing a subjective sense of cooling, relief of burning (*daha*), and restoration of strength following illness — consistent with the sweet, moist, heavy (*guru*) qualities attributed to it [1][8].
- The *Shennong Bencao Jing* framing emphasises a felt increase in vigour, cold-resistance, and mental steadiness (*zhì*, will), pointing to an experienced tonic action [7].
- Wine in Egyptian and Mesopotamian records carries no phenomenological language for the grape itself — the emphasis is entirely on formulation outcomes (symptom resolution).
- Wine's intoxicating and altered-state dimension is noted throughout ancient commentary (see §9) but is not classified medicinally in these traditions as a sought-after therapeutic experience per se.

## 8. Practical Application

**Forms and preparations:**
- Dried raisin (*Draksha* of the API): oral, typically 10–20 g soaked in water overnight, in Ayurvedic practice [8].
- Standardised grape seed extract (GSE): oral capsule/tablet, 100–300 mg daily in clinical trials (range 100–2,000 mg) [20].
- Grape juice (unsweetened, polyphenol-rich): consumed as beverage in studies.
- Resveratrol supplement: studied at doses 150 mg–1 g/day; optimal human dose undefined.

**Cautions and interactions (sourced):**
- GSE may potentiate anticoagulant/antiplatelet drugs (warfarin, aspirin) — case reports exist; advise caution in combination [19].
- High-sugar content of raisins is relevant in diabetes management; raisins have a moderate glycaemic index.
- Resveratrol inhibits CYP3A4 and CYP2C9 in vitro; clinical significance in vivo at supplement doses is uncertain but should be noted for patients on narrow-therapeutic-window drugs [18].
- Wine as medicine carries the full spectrum of risks associated with ethanol — hepatotoxicity, addiction, cancer risk — and should not be recommended as a therapeutic vehicle in contemporary clinical practice.

## 9. Spiritual & Esoteric Layer

Wine pressed from *Vitis vinifera* occupies a singular position in the religious and symbolic imagination of the ancient world. Each tradition frames it within its own cosmological terms; the following is attributed, not synthesised:

**Greek/Roman (attributed):** Dionysus (Bacchus) was the divine patron of the vine, and wine in the Dionysian mysteries functioned as a vehicle for the god's presence — a mode of ritual communion dissolving the boundary between mortal and divine [23]. The vine's cycle (growth, harvest, fermentation, consumption) was mapped directly onto the myth of Dionysiac death and rebirth.

**Christian (attributed):** Wine at the Eucharist enacts the Last Supper: bread and wine are consecrated as body and blood of Christ, establishing the new covenant. The vine is a repeated scriptural symbol of divine relationship (*John 15:1*, "I am the true vine"). The adoption and transformation of the grapevine symbol from Mediterranean pagan tradition into Christian redemptive theology is well-documented historically [24].

**Ayurvedic/humoral (attributed):** In the tridosha framework, *Draksha* is a *rasayana* — a rejuvenating substance that nourishes the *dhatus* (bodily tissues) and supports *ojas* (vital essence), the subtlest product of digestion underlying vigour and immunity [1][8]. Wine (*madya*) is recognised separately, as a substance that in excess vitiates all three *doshas* but in minimal, medically supervised doses (as *Drakshasava*) may kindle digestive fire.

**Islamic tradition (noted):** Grape and raisin are attested in Islamic pharmacopoeia (*Tibb al-Nabawi*) as permissible tonics, while wine is prohibited as a drink but retained in some classical texts as a pharmaceutical solvent in restricted contexts — a legal-medical tension resolved differently across juristic schools. [This attestation is noted but not included in the five primary traditions of this entry.]

## 10. Functional–Anatomical Correlate

The predominant modern functional correlate of *Vitis vinifera* therapeutic use is **cardiovascular and vascular endothelial** — an alignment that also recapitulates the classical "blood and *qi*" / *raktapitta* framing of multiple traditions.

Primary systems addressed by evidence:
1. **Vascular endothelium:** NO-mediated vasodilation; blood pressure reduction (GSE, meta-analytic support) [16][20].
2. **Cardiovascular — lipid and inflammation:** Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant polyphenols with plausible but unconfirmed human benefit for atherosclerosis [17][22].
3. **Haematological:** Antiplatelet effects (preclinical) [19].
4. **Gastrointestinal (raisin):** Demulcent, mild laxative, carbohydrate-dense restorative — the most directly supported traditional use (cross-traditional agreement; modern physiology consistent) [1][8].
5. **Metabolic:** Moderate evidence for antioxidant support in metabolic syndrome; resveratrol SIRT1/mTOR effects preclinical [18].

The classical convergence on "damp impediment in sinews and bones" (Chinese), "wasting and debility" (Ayurveda, Egypt), and "recovery from fever" points to what in modern terms would be a caloric-repletion and anti-inflammatory oral intervention in catabolic states — consistent with the raisin's role as a nutrient-dense, easily digested carbohydrate source in pre-modern pharmacopoeia.
