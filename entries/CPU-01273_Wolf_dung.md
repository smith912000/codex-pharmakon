# Wolf dung — "Wolf stool" (Clark 1995); "The feces of dogs, wolves and the Tibetan snow cock" (Men-Tsee-Khang 2011); no Tibetan name is printed for it
## *The excrement of the wolf; Clark prints no Tibetan name or binomial for it, and the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary pairs "Wolf" with "Canis Lupus" in an OCR-split list* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The Explanatory Tantra (*bshad rgyud*), chapter 20, on the powers of medicines, has a section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin". It opens: "The thirteen medicines of animal origin consist of horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body. Their indications and powers are explained as follows" (Clark 1995, `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt` lines 1147-1148). Wolf dung falls under the twelfth of the thirteen kinds, "droppings", and it is named in one sentence under the sub-heading "Droppings" (line 1198). The paragraph reads, in part (line 1199): "Vulture's droppings: increase digestive heat, disperse tumors and suppurate swellings. Pig's stool: cures indigestion, septic disorders, infectious fevers and bile tumors. Human excreta: cure gall stones, neutralize poisons, reduce swellings [and subdue spirits]. Horse dung: is anthelmintic, anti-emetic, cures wind-bile combinations [and is beneficial against spirits]. Rabbit droppings: purge oedema [and are beneficial for wounds]. Dog stool: [is especially beneficial against spirits]. Wolf stool and droppings of Gong.mo: Tetragallus himalayensis: [all three] reduce swellings." Clark’s preface (line 82) explains his brackets: interpolations in square brackets are taken from the *Blue Aquamarine* and *Spreading Tree* commentaries, those in parentheses from the oral instruction of his teachers. The bracket "[all three]" is therefore a commentary reading. It most naturally counts dog stool, wolf stool and the droppings of *gong.mo*, the Himalayan snow cock, which the text glosses as *Tetragallus himalayensis*. The sentence does not say this itself, and the root-text sentence without the bracket is simply "Wolf stool and droppings of Gong.mo: ... reduce swellings." The action printed is one: it reduces swellings.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt` line 3568) says it in a different grouping: "The feces of dogs, wolves and the Tibetan snow cock reduce swelling." The two translations agree on the substance, wolf excrement, and on the single action, reducing swelling. Neither prints a Tibetan name for the wolf's dung in this line. Clark prints one for another wolf material, the stomach, *sbyang.kihi.pho.wa* (line 1170), which belongs to a different entry of this Codex.

The wolf itself is placed in the Tantra's classification of meats as one of the carnivorous animals: "Carnivorous animals include the tiger, leopard, bear (*Selenarctos thibetanus*), snow bear (*Ursus arctos*), snow leopard (*Panthera pardus*), wolf, lynx, fox, hill dog and weasel" (Clark line 672, group (e)). The Men-Tsee-Khang text lists "the tiger, leopard, black bear, Tibetan brown bear, snow leopard, wolf, lynx, fox, dhole (Tibetan wild-dog) and monkey" as "the group of carnivorous wild animals" (line 2912). Its glossary of animal names pairs "Wolf" with "Canis Lupus" (lines 7023 and 7027), but the glossary is a split two-column OCR list in which the pairing is by order and not certain. [USER: verify] The name list `_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv` has rows for wolf stomach and wolf tongue (lines 350-351) and none for wolf dung, so this attestation rests on the two sentences above and not on a list row.

*Other wolf materials, not folded in.* The wolf's stomach and tongue are separate materials with their own actions in the "Meat" sub-section of the same chapter (Clark line 1170). Outside the Tibetan texts, Sushruta (Sutrasthana ch.46, `Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt` lines 534-544) places the wolf among the *Guhashayas*, the cave-dwelling mammals, and says of the flesh of that group that it "is sweet, heavy, demulcent and strength-giving" and "subdues the deranged Vayu". That is a statement about flesh. The text read does not mention the dung, and Sushruta is not on this entry's tag line.

### Active Components

No text read names a constituent. In general terms, faeces are the undigested and indigestible part of a meal together with water, bile pigments, shed cells of the gut lining and very large numbers of gut bacteria. The composition of a carnivore's droppings depends on its recent prey. Nothing links any constituent to the action the Tantra gives, which is to reduce swellings.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts say that the stool of the wolf reduces swellings. The Codex does not recommend it. Wolf droppings can carry parasites and bacteria, including some that infect people; the wolf is a wild and in many countries protected animal; and swelling can be a sign of infection, injury, heart or kidney disease or other conditions that need assessment. The entry exists to record what the Explanatory Tantra says, and where in the animal-product list it says it.

### Preparation & Dosing

Neither translation gives a preparation, vehicle, quantity or course for wolf dung. The text does not say whether it was used fresh or dried, taken inside or applied outside, or alone or in a compound. The "medicinal horses" at the end of the chapter (Clark line 1202), raw cane sugar, crystal sugar and honey, are named as vehicles for compounded medicines in general; the text does not say that wolf dung was taken with any of them. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. The faeces of wild and domestic canids can carry intestinal parasites, such as roundworms and tapeworms whose eggs can infect people who handle or swallow them, and bacteria that cause gastroenteritis. Handling wild animal droppings without protection is an infection risk, and ingesting them is more so. Swelling has many causes and its first treatment depends on the cause. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan (FMT):** No taste, post-digestive taste or potency is printed for wolf stool; its only printed action is to reduce swellings. The Tantra's rule for meats, that all the types are sweet in taste and post-digestive taste with cool, light and coarse powers in dry-land animals (Clark line 673), is about flesh and is not carried over to droppings.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed; no cultivation status.* Wolf stool is named in the Explanatory Tantra's materia medica of animal products with a single therapeutic action (Clark line 1199). The wider frame is real but does not reach it. In Clark's account the three humors are rooted in attachment, hatred and obscuration (line 51), and these three are "the three causes" of wind, bile and phlegm disorders (line 147), so disease is at root a spiritual condition and medicine is one support. The chapter on routine behaviour asks the practitioner to refrain from the ten negative actions, the first of which is "taking life" (line 616). The rejuvenation passage in chapter 23, "Normal health" (lines 1266-1275), does not name wolf stool, and the lines read give it no *bcud len* use. The tradition has no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice, and none is asserted.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a practice for a cultivator. Wolf stool is a one-line swelling remedy in a list of thirteen kinds of animal product, and the tradition's own first precept is against taking life. The Codex does not recommend it. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Contraindications

- **Infection and hygiene:** the droppings of wild canids can carry parasite eggs and pathogens; do not collect, handle, ingest or apply them.
- **Swelling:** swelling of unknown cause, or with fever, redness, breathlessness or pain, needs medical assessment.
- **Identity:** the Tibetan text prints no name for the wolf's dung, and the identification of the animal rests on the English "wolf" and a glossary pairing that is itself uncertain.
- **Conservation and law:** wolves are protected in many countries and regions, and taking wild animals or their products is regulated or banned; do not collect or buy them.
- **Ethics:** the Tibetan source itself lists "taking life" first among the ten negative actions (Clark line 616).
