# I-519 | Yarrow (*Achillea millefolium* L.)

**Volume I — Plant-Based Substances**
**Family:** Asteraceae (tribe Anthemideae)
**Energetic Tier:** ◉ Qi (hemostatic, anti-inflammatory, diaphoretic, bitter) primary | ◈ Jing (tissue repair) secondary

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary binomial:** *Achillea millefolium* L. (1753, *Species Plantarum*) — a polyploid species complex (2x, 4x, 6x, 8x cytotypes)

**Synonyms & historical names:**
- *Achillea borealis* Bong., *A. lanulosa* Nutt. (North American entities within the complex)
- Historical: *Mille folium* (thousand leaves), *Herba militaris* (soldier's herb), *Achilleios* (Greek)

**Common names:**
- English: Yarrow, milfoil, thousand-leaf, soldier's woundwort, nosebleed plant, old man's pepper
- German: *Schafgarbe* (sheep's yarrow — sheep graze it readily), *Tausendblatt* (thousand-leaf)
- French: *Achillée millefeuille*, *Herbe à la coupure* (cut herb)
- Italian: *Achillea*, *Millefoglio*
- Spanish: *Milenrama*, *Aquilea*
- Arabic: *Batarkhān* (*Achillea* spp.)
- Chinese: 蓍草 (*shī cǎo*, "divination herb") — *A. sibirica* Ledeb. and related species used; the I Ching divination with yarrow stalks (50 stalks method) = primary Chinese cultural significance
- Japanese: *Nokogiriso* (鋸草, "saw herb")
- Sanskrit: *Gandhabhadra* (*Achillea* spp.)
- Old English: *Gearwe* (yarrow)
- Welsh: *Llysiau'r gwaed* (blood herb)

**Etymology:** *Achillea* = named for Achilles (Greek hero) who, according to Pliny, learned from Chiron the centaur to use this herb to staunch the wounds of his soldiers at Troy; *millefolium* = Latin *mille* (thousand) + *folium* (leaf) = "thousand-leaf" (the highly dissected, feathery leaves appearing as many tiny leaflets). "Yarrow" = Old English *gearwe* (origins unclear; possibly Proto-Germanic).

**The Achilles myth (pharmacological encoding):**
The naming of this plant after Achilles is one of the most explicit examples in classical botanical literature of a myth encoding pharmacological knowledge: Achilles using the herb to close wounds. *Herba militaris* (soldier's herb) and *wound wort* are independent parallel names across multiple European languages, all naming the same property (wound healing/hemostasis) from the same observation.

**Botanical authentication:**
- Erect perennial herb 20–100 cm; leaves finely divided (2–3 pinnate = "thousand-leaf" appearance; feathery grey-green texture); stems hairy; inflorescence: flat-topped corymb of small white (rarely pink/red) daisy-like capitula (ray florets 4–6, white/cream; disc florets numerous, yellow/cream); characteristic aromatic camphor-like smell when crushed; fibrous root system with underground runners (spreads vegetatively)
- Highly variable species complex; polyploidy → chemical variation; white-flowered common European form is standard pharmaceutical material; pink-flowered ornamental varieties may have different essential oil profile
- **Authentication:** finely feathery dissected grey-green leaves + flat corymb of white daisy-like heads + camphor/achilleic smell = reliable combination

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

Yarrow is the archetypal wound herb of Western and Islamic medical traditions — the plant most consistently named across multiple languages and cultures specifically for hemostasis and wound closure. The Achilles myth encodes a genuine pharmacological observation that predates the Greek mythological tradition by centuries: yarrow does measurably reduce bleeding time and promote wound healing through multiple synergistic mechanisms (tannin-mediated protein precipitation + achilleic acid platelet-aggregating proazulene precursors + anti-inflammatory sesquiterpene lactones).

The I Ching connection represents a different cultural axis: 50 dried yarrow stalks are the traditional tool for generating the hexagrams of the *Book of Changes* — each consultation requiring careful counting and sorting of the stalks in a ritual that takes approximately 15 minutes. The choice of yarrow for this purpose may encode respect for a highly familiar plant (universally present; easily harvestable; durable dry stalk) rather than any pharmacological attribution. However, Chinese medicinal use of yarrow species (*A. sibirica*, *A. alpina*) for fever and menstruation parallels the European medicinal record.

The combination of hemostatic + diaphoretic + bitter GI + anti-inflammatory + emmenagogue properties in a single widely distributed, easy-to-identify plant made yarrow the most versatile field first-aid herb in every temperate culture that had access to it — driving both pharmacological discovery and ritual significance.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Essential oil (variable by chemotype — CRITICAL):**
*Achillea millefolium* is a chemotype-rich species; EO profiles vary dramatically:
- **European chamomile-type chemotype (blue/chamazulene-rich):** azulene/chamazulene 2–40% (formed from matricine → chamazulene by steam distillation heat); anti-inflammatory; anti-allergic; blue color
- **Camphor-rich chemotype:** camphor 5–20%; antimicrobial; borneol, borneol acetate
- **1,8-Cineole chemotype:** eucalyptol-type; 1,8-cineole 10–40%; antimicrobial; expectorant
- **Total EO content:** 0.2–0.8% DW in flowers/leaves; variable by chemotype and geography
- **Achillicin and artabsin-type**: sequiterpene lactone-related volatiles contributing to bitter quality

**Sesquiterpene lactones:**
- **Achillin, achillicin**: germacranolide-type sesquiterpene lactones; bitter; anti-inflammatory (NF-κB inhibition)
- **Leucodin**: guaianolide-type; anti-inflammatory
- **Desacetylmatricarin**: related to matricin; chamazulene precursor
- **Total sesquiterpene lactones:** 0.1–0.5% DW; responsible for most CNS anti-inflammatory effects
- **Sesquiterpene lactone allergy:** contact dermatitis risk in Asteraceae-sensitive individuals (shared allergen class with chamomile, chrysanthemum, ragweed)

**Flavonoids:**
- **Apigenin**: 0.2–1.5% DW; anti-inflammatory (COX-2 inhibition); anxiolytic (GABA-A PAM — recurring across many Codex entries); antispasmodic
- **Luteolin**: 0.1–0.8% DW; NF-κB inhibition; COX-2 inhibition
- **Rutin, quercitrin, isorhamnetin**: present
- **Artemetin** (3,5,6,7,4'-pentamethoxyflavone): characteristic *Achillea* flavone; anti-inflammatory; smooth muscle relaxant

**Alkaloids:**
- **Achilleine** (betaine-type alkaloid): 0.02–0.05% DW; reduces capillary fragility; hemostatic; the original "achilleic acid" described in 19th century literature
- Stachydrine (betaine alkaloid; same class as *Leonurus* I-477 and *Stachys betonica* I-492)

**Phenolic acids:**
- **Caffeic acid derivatives** (chlorogenic acid, cynarine-related): 0.5–2% DW; antioxidant; anti-inflammatory

**Tannins:**
- **Condensed proanthocyanidins**: 2–4% DW; astringent; hemostatic; anti-diarrheal

**Proazulenes:**
- **Matricine (matricin)**: sesquiterpene lactone precursor; converted to chamazulene by heat (distillation or decoction); anti-inflammatory (5-LOX inhibitor); blue chromophore

**Phytol, terpenoids:** minor; Vitamin K-related diterpene alcohols may contribute to hemostatic effect

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Hemostatic (multi-mechanism — the defining property):**

1. **Achilleine (betaine alkaloid):** Reduces bleeding time in animal models (Hoffmann 1976); proposed mechanism: promotes platelet aggregation; reduces capillary fragility; reduces capillary dilation at wound site. Mechanism incompletely characterized at molecular level; consistent pharmacological observation.

2. **Tannin-mediated coagulation:** Condensed proanthocyanidins precipitate blood proteins (including coagulation factors and fibrinogen) at wound site → accelerate local fibrin mesh formation → mechanical hemostasis. Same mechanism as other tannin-rich wound herbs (agrimony I-502, lady's mantle I-504).

3. **Platelet activation contribution:** Chamazulene (from proazulene conversion) and flavonoids: BOTH antiplatelet (at systemic concentrations) AND platelet-activating (at local wound concentrations with different exposure dynamics). The paradox of anti-inflammatory and hemostatic properties in the same plant reflects dose-dependent and compartment-dependent pharmacology.

**Anti-inflammatory:**
- Chamazulene: 5-LOX inhibition → reduced LTB4 (leukotriene B4) production; anti-inflammatory and anti-allergic; equivalent mechanism to chamomile (I-521) with which yarrow shares this compound
- Apigenin, luteolin: COX-2 inhibition; NF-κB pathway suppression
- Achillin: NF-κB inhibition; TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 suppression
- Artemetin: smooth muscle relaxant; antispasmodic (relevant to GI and menstrual cramping)

**Diaphoretic:**
- Traditional fever management via perspiration
- EO component (camphor/borneol type) + flavonoids: mild vasodilation in skin capillaries → increased perspiration
- Consistent across European, Islamic, and North American folk traditions for feverish colds

**Bitter digestive stimulant:**
- Sesquiterpene lactones (achillin, achillicin): bitter taste receptor stimulation → vagal bile reflex → choleretic (increased bile flow); same mechanism as dandelion (I-518), gentian, artichoke
- Relevant to dyspepsia, loss of appetite, mild liver-stimulating use

**Antispasmodic:**
- Artemetin: Ca²⁺ channel antagonism → smooth muscle relaxation
- Apigenin: GABA-A modulation → central and peripheral muscle relaxation
- Relevant to dysmenorrhea, GI spasm

**Emmenagogue:**
- Traditional uterotonic at high doses; artemetin smooth muscle effect on uterine myometrium; CONTRAINDICATED in pregnancy

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Wound healing — Level C:**
- Commission E APPROVED for topical wound healing; standard wound application across all major European pharmacopoeias
- Multiple pre-RCT clinical series (German, British herbalism literature); consistent positive outcome for wound closure
- Mechanism clearly established; Level C clinical + Level D mechanistic = acceptable for traditional use monograph

**GI / dysmenorrhea — Level C:**
- Commission E APPROVED for loss of appetite, dyspeptic complaints (internal; root + herb)
- Traditional use well-documented; dysmenorrhea indication Level C (anti-inflammatory + antispasmodic mechanism supports this)
- **Afshari JT et al. 2000** (*Journal of Ethnopharmacology* 71:533-541): yarrow extract significantly inhibited *E. coli* growth and reduced UTI symptoms in pilot study; Level B

**Inflammatory bowel conditions (emerging) — Level B:**
- Potdar D et al. 2012 (*PLoS ONE* 7:e51412): *A. millefolium* extract significantly reduced intestinal inflammation markers in mouse colitis model; Level D
- Human observational data: Iranian traditional use of yarrow for ulcerative colitis; pilot clinical study (Mitić 2012) suggests benefit; not yet RCT-validated

**Fever/respiratory — Level C:**
- Traditional use across 5 traditions; diaphoretic mechanism plausible; no RCTs

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Achilles and Chiron — the wound-herb transmission:**
The myth of Achilles learning wound-herb use from Chiron the centaur (who was also teacher of Asclepius, founder of medicine) encodes a specific pharmacological inheritance. Chiron is the mythological figure who bridges animal (horse body) and human (man-mind) — an embodiment of nature-to-civilization knowledge transmission. The choice of yarrow as the specific herb in this myth, rather than any other, preserves an empirical observation in legendary form: yarrow reliably stopped battlefield bleeding. The *Iliad* (c. 8th century BCE) does not name yarrow specifically, but the Achilles-wound tradition became sufficiently established in antiquity that Pliny (*Natural History*, 77 CE) attributed the naming to this myth.

**I Ching and the sacred stalks:**
The use of 50 yarrow stalks in the *Yi Jing* (Book of Changes) divination procedure is documented from at least the Zhou dynasty (1046–256 BCE). The stalks are dried, shuffled, and divided through a complex 4-step process that generates random numbers (like dice), from which the hexagrams emerge. The choice of yarrow stalks for this ritual may reflect (a) the uniform length and straightness of dried yarrow stems — ideal as counting tools; (b) the plant's ubiquitous availability across ancient China; (c) a possible spiritual attribution (sage-like) to a plant that was simultaneously a familiar medicine. Modern I Ching practice increasingly substitutes three coins (faster), but traditional scholars maintain that the 50-stalk yarrow method generates a richer probability distribution (64 possible hexagrams with different probability weights via stalks vs equal weights via coins).

**Nosebleed plant — the dual-action paradox:**
Yarrow is called *nosebleed plant* in some English dialects (along with *staunch weed* = stops bleeding) because inserting a yarrow leaf into the nostril can INDUCE nosebleeds in some individuals (likely via mucosal irritation from EO and sesquiterpene lactone compounds) — and the same plant is used to STOP nosebleeds. This apparent contradiction is pharmacologically explicable: low-dose local irritant → mucosal vasodilation → bleeding; high-dose concentrated application + mechanical compression → tannin-mediated hemostasis. The plant was historically used for both "making a nose bleed to cure headache" (bloodletting variant) and "stopping nosebleed" (hemostasis) — contradictory folk applications unified by the single plant.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Preparations and doses:**
- **Dried herb/flower tea:** 2–4 g per 250 mL water; steep 10 minutes covered (preserve EO); 3× daily; for digestive complaints, fever (diaphoretic application), dysmenorrhea
- **Tincture (1:5, 45% ethanol):** 3–5 mL 3× daily
- **Fluid extract (1:1, 25% ethanol):** 1–2 mL 3× daily
- **Topical:** strong decoction (10 g per 250 mL, simmer 10 minutes); wound wash, sitz bath for hemorrhoids/perineal healing; fresh leaf poultice (field application — traditional)

**Commission E approved (1984):**
- Internal: loss of appetite, dyspeptic complaints (4.5 g herb/day or equivalent)
- Topical: wounds and inflamed skin; sitz baths for pelvic cramps/disorders

**EMA traditional use:** Internal: dyspeptic complaints, temporary loss of appetite, mild spasmodic GI complaints; Topical: minor wounds; Sitz baths: minor pelvic inflammatory conditions.

**CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- **Pregnancy: ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION (internal)** — emmenagogue/uterotonic at therapeutic doses; well-documented; avoid entirely during pregnancy; topical use may be acceptable (limited systemic absorption)
- **Asteraceae/Compositae allergy:** sesquiterpene lactone cross-reactivity with chamomile, chrysanthemum, mugwort, ragweed; skin test before topical use in Asteraceae-sensitive patients
- **Anticoagulant therapy:** achilleine hemostatic effect theoretically opposes anticoagulants; at standard doses, effect minor but unpredictable; monitor

**Drug interactions:**
- Warfarin: achilleine platelet-activating effect (opposing) vs flavonoid antiplatelet effect (additive) — net effect unpredictable; monitor INR
- Lithium: diuretic-like effect → possible reduced lithium clearance; monitor lithium levels
- Iron: tannin chelation; separate by 2 hours

**Combination with lady's mantle (I-504):**
Classical European phytotherapy combination for menorrhagia and cycle regulation (established in Section 8 of I-504). Synergistic mechanisms: yarrow's achilleine hemostasis + anti-inflammatory artemetin + diaphoretic (cycle-regulating) + lady's mantle ellagitannin hemostasis + uterine toning. Combined tea or tincture: equal parts dried yarrow + lady's mantle; standard traditional preparation.
