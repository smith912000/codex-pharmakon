### Origin & Tradition

Dioscorides treats Celtic nard (Greek *nardos keltike*, Latin *saliunca*) in *De Materia Medica* Book I among the aromatic nards, immediately after Indian and Syrian spikenard. He distinguishes it as a smaller, humbler root than the prized *Nardostachys jatamansi* of the Himalaya, gathered on the alpine slopes of the Ligurian Alps and the lands of the Celts and the Istrians. The plant he describes is a low cushion-forming valerian of high screes and grasslands, the whole flowering shoot and its matted rootstock being lifted, bundled, and dried for trade.

The epithet *celtica* and the Latin *saliunca* both fix the plant's geography in the Celtic Alpine world rather than the Orient. Virgil mentions *saliunca* in the *Eclogues* as a humble plant, contrasting it unfavourably with the rose — a literary token of its reputation as a poor cousin to the great Eastern nards, yet still aromatic enough to be named alongside them. Pliny the Elder, drawing on the same trade knowledge, likewise places it among the nards and notes its Alpine provenance.

Because true spikenard was a costly import, Celtic nard served the Roman and later medieval pharmacy as a closer, cheaper aromatic of the same family — a substitution that the Galenic tradition accepted on the grounds of shared warmth, bitterness, and the characteristic resinous-valerianic odour. The dried root entered compound perfumes, fumigations, and the warming digestive electuaries of the materia aromatica. In the Alpine vernacular tradition (BM) the root long retained a folk reputation as a calmative and stomachic, distilled into the "nardino" aromatic waters and bath preparations of the eastern Alps.

By analogy with its relatives the plant maps onto the Chinese conception of aromatic, Qi-moving, Shen-settling roots; the entire *Valeriana / Nardostachys* complex shares the dual signature of moving stagnation while quieting the spirit. Within Kenetica, Celtic nard is read as a minor but precise member of the nard family: an anchoring aromatic for restlessness rooted in digestive unease.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

1. **Settling of agitated Shen with a digestive root.** Celtic nard is selected where restlessness, light sleep, or nervous tension sits atop an unsettled stomach. The aromatic root both moves the stagnant Qi of the middle and draws the spirit downward, making it apt for the "racing mind with churning gut" presentation rather than for pure insomnia.

2. **Aromatic carminative for cold, sluggish digestion.** The warm, bitter, resinous profile suits a damp-cold stomach with bloating, flatulence, and poor appetite. As a milder, more available stand-in for true spikenard, it warms and moves without strong heating.

3. **Fumigation and aromatic atmosphere.** Following its classical use in incense and perfume, the dried root is burned or steamed to perfume a space and, in the traditional reading, to "clear heaviness" from a room — a settling, grounding aromatic rather than an uplifting one.

4. **Bathing and topical aromatic preparations.** The Alpine "nardino" bath tradition uses the aromatic water for general relaxation and for soothing the skin; in Kenetica this is the gentlest delivery, suited to the frail or the over-stimulated.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM / Galenic:** Warm and dry, on the order of the second degree; bitter and aromatic. Classed with the nards as a warming, drying, Qi-moving aromatic of the stomach and nerves.
- **CM (by analogy):** Aromatic root that moves stagnant Qi of the Middle Burner and settles the Shen; warming, descending. (Direct classical Chinese use is of *Nardostachys*, not *V. celtica*; the mapping is by family analogy.)
- **Kenetica tier:** ◉ Qi-anchoring aromatic, minor nard. A settling root for restlessness with digestive origin; not a primary sedative.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy and lactation:** insufficient species-specific safety data; avoid internal use. [USER: verify]
- **Sedative interaction:** as with all valerians, additive effect possible with sedatives, hypnotics, and alcohol; caution before driving.
- **Essential oil internal use:** Celtic nard oil is for inhalation and dilute topical use; do not ingest.
- **Substitution caution:** do not assume identical strength or safety to *Valeriana officinalis*; the oil and iridoid profiles differ.
