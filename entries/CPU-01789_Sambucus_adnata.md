# Sambucus adnata — Yugu shing, yu gu shing (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary by column position; Clark 1995 line 1019, "black type of elder")

## *Sambucus adnata Wall, the fourth scientific name of the "Y" block of the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary, which column order sets against "Yugu shing" (yu gu shing, no English name printed); Clark 1995 prints yu.gu.shing with five binomials, of which Sambucus adnata Wall is the last, glossed "black type of elder"* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Sushruta, Ebers and Assyrian text folders on disk for *Sambucus* found no hit. The Shennong text (Yang 1998) prints one *Sambucus* name, Lu Ying "(Flos Sambuci Japonici)" at line 3660, with Yang's note 226 (lines 3665 to 3667) saying that the identity "is not certain" and that the Latin identification "should only be regarded as provisional"; the extraction matched it to the genus only (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv`, line 190), and it is not read as this plant. The Dioscorides text prints *Sambucus nigra* and *Sambucus ebulus* in translators' SUGGESTED lines for Greek drugs (lines 9620 and 9624), which are European species and are not read as this plant. The row was added from a printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` the entry has **two** source rows. The batch locus is line 393: keyed by the **scientific name** "Sambucus adnata", locus "Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (line 6045)", with the note "Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". The second is line 138: keyed by the **Tibetan name** *yu.gu.shing*, locus "Clark 1995 (line 1019)", translator identification "Veronica longiflora; Veronica incana; Senecio dianthus Franch (white type); Cacalia quinquelobus; Sambucus adnata Wall (black type of elder)", note "key uses the first". In Clark's string *Sambucus adnata* is therefore the **fifth and last** name, not the first, and the first name of that row is *Veronica longiflora*. In the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary it is the only scientific name on its line (6045). The neighbouring names have rows or entries of their own, *Senecio dianthus* among them (I_1357, the sibling row of this batch).

**Identity doubt.** Clark prints five binomials in four genera of three families for one Tibetan name, and labels the last "black type of elder" and the third "white type". The bracketed commentary (below) distinguishes a brown, a white and a black type. Clark's preface says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" (line 83) and that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect" (line 68). The Men-Tsee-Khang glossary sets *Sambucus adnata* against "Yugu shing" and prints *Senecio dianthus* against a different Tibetan name (see I_1357), so the two editions do not divide the five Latin names in the same way. The Codex does not choose among them. The Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note (line 686) says that its botanical terms rest on the information of its own Materia Medica Department. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "Y" block).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs, and the columns do not hold the same number of entries. The phonetic names are six: "Yakyima" (line 6036), "Yherma" (6037), and, on line 6038, "Yhershingpa Yugu shing Yumo dejin Yungwa". The transliterations are "gya' kyi ma" (6040), "gyer ma" (6041) and, on line 6042, "gyer shing pa yu gu shing yu mo mde 'byin yung ba". The scientific names are six: "Chrysoplenium camosum", "Zanthoxylum armatum" and "Scrophularia decomposita" (all on line 6044), **"Sambucus adnata"** (line 6045), then "Paraquilegia anemonoides" and "Curcuma longa" (line 6046). The English names are three for the block (lines 6050 to 6052): "Guinea pepper", "Figwort" and "Turmeric" (the "Dactylorhiza" and "Coriander" of lines 6048 to 6049 belong to the rows above). Counted in order, "Yugu shing" is the fourth phonetic name, "yu gu shing" the fourth transliteration and *Sambucus adnata* the fourth scientific name. The count is checked at both ends of the block, on "Yherma" against *Zanthoxylum armatum* (where Clark's *gyer.ma* at line 1066 is paired with *Zanthoxylum*), on "Yumo dejin" against *Paraquilegia* (where Clark's *yu.mo.mde.hbyin*, line 1011, prints *Paraquilegia microphylla*) and on "Yungwa" against *Curcuma longa* and "Turmeric". No English name is printed for "Yugu shing". The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position only. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3433).* Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225; colophon at line 3583), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428): "Yugu shing joins cuts and cures hot disorder associated with poisoning." The sentence stands between "Pang gyen karpo cures laryngitis and hot disorder associated with poisoning" and "Tsed, Guthup and Dhumtak are antidotes to poison". The printed index lists "Yugu shing 207" (line 7570). The sentence gives no part used, no preparation and no taste.

*Clark 1995, materia medica run (line 1019).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry follows *spang.rgyan.dkar.po* (a gentian, line 1018) and is followed by *rtsad* (line 1020). It reads:

"yu.gu.shing: Veronica longiflora: Veronica incana: Senecio dianthus Franch (white type): Cacalia quinquelobus: Sambucus adnata Wall (black type of elder): [has nine names. The brown type is said to be dar.ya.kan, and it grows in very hot areas. The white type has a large lobed leaf, pale green on top and brown beneath. It has small, slightly funy flowers, bitter taste, cool power and subdues spirits by its pungent odor. The plains-growing black type has dandelion-like dark green leaves. Both types] heal wounds, cure fever caused by poisoning [and dispel spirits]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Almost all of the description is therefore the commentary layer: the nine names, the three types, the leaves, the taste ("bitter"), the power ("cool") and the action on spirits. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the five Latin names with Clark's "white type" and "black type" labels, which are her identifications, and "heal wounds, cure fever caused by poisoning". "Both types" is inside the brackets, although three types have been described. The text "funy" is printed so. Clark's name index lists *yu.gu.shing* (line 1749) and the Latin index lists *Sambucus adnata* (line 1641).

The two editions agree closely in kind: Clark's drug heals wounds and cures "fever caused by poisoning"; the Men-Tsee-Khang *Yugu shing* "joins cuts and cures hot disorder associated with poisoning", where "fever" and "hot disorder" are the two translators' words. The Codex reads *yu.gu.shing* and *Yugu shing* as one drug from the spelling and that agreement; neither text says so. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Sambucus adnata*, *yu.gu.shing* and *Yugu* found no entry on this plant. **I_1357** (*Senecio dianthus*, tag FMT) is the sibling row of this batch and shares Clark's line 1019. **I_1307** (*Lepidium apetalum*, *dar.ya.kan*, tag FMT) is the drug that the commentary layer of Clark's line 1019 names for the "brown type" and is a separate entry on its own passage. **I_1330** (*Peucedanum praeruptorum*, *Guthup*) and **I_1341** (*Pyrethrum tatsienense*) quote the same Men-Tsee-Khang line 3433 for the neighbouring names "Guthup" and "Ajak". **I_973** (*Zanthoxylum armatum*, tags SS, FMT) reads the same "Y" block for "Yherma". I_094 and I_686 (*Sambucus nigra*, tags DMM, CM and others) and I_637 (*Sambucus ebulus*, tags AT, CM, DMM) are entries on European elders in other traditions and are not merged. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Sambucus* is a genus of woody shrubs and small trees now placed in the moschatel family (Adoxaceae), that *Veronica*, one of the other genera Clark prints for the same Tibetan name, belongs to the plantain family (Plantaginaceae), and that *Senecio* and *Cacalia* belong to the daisy family (Asteraceae). Clark's five names therefore point to plants of at least three families, and the lines read give no composition for the Tibetan drug. Nothing is stated here about what it contains. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that will "heal wounds, cure fever caused by poisoning" (Clark, line 1019) and "joins cuts and cures hot disorder associated with poisoning" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3433). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only through the last of Clark's five names and a glossary count, and they are not Codex protocols. Poisoning, fever and open wounds need medical care, and the Codex gives no protocol and no dose for them. The "black type of elder" label is not evidence that the Tibetan drug is an elder, and the garden and hedgerow elders of Europe are not this plant.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used (the commentary layer describes leaves and flowers, and names no part as the drug), no quantity, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Tibetan terms ("fever caused by poisoning", "hot disorder associated with poisoning") are not modern diagnoses or measured outcomes, and the identity is open among five species of three families, so a study of any one *Sambucus* would not bear directly on the Tibetan drug. It is a general fact that the raw parts of some elders are not safe to eat; that is a statement about the genus and not about the Tibetan drug.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's line 1019 prints "bitter taste, cool power" for the white type, and both words are inside the brackets, so they belong to the commentary layer and not to the whole drug. It prints no post-digestive taste. The Men-Tsee-Khang lines read (3433, 6038, 6042, 6045) print no taste, power or post-digestive taste for *Yugu shing*.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it heals wounds and cures fever caused by poisoning, and in the commentary layer the white type "subdues spirits by its pungent odor" and the drug "dispel[s] spirits". That is an action against spirit-caused disorders, one of the tradition's own divisions of disease (Clark, line 128, "the division of healing diseases caused by spirits"), and it is not a cultivation status. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names (*yu.gu.shing*, *Yugu shing*, *Sambucus*) found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, colophon at line 3950), and the herb entries of Clark's chapter 20 that mention essence extraction (lines 971, 1043, 1078, 1103, 1121 and 1144) do not include it. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a wound and poison-fever drug, with a commentary-layer note on spirits, no preparation and no dose, and its Latin name is the last of five. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Poisoning, fever and wounds need medical care; the identity is open among five species of three families, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Poisoning in particular is an emergency and is not treated with a herb from a catalogue. No conservation information and no safety information for pregnancy were read. [USER: verify]
