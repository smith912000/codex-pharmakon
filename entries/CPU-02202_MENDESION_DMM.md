# Mendesion — MENDESION (Dioscorides, De Materia Medica 1-72); "Mendesium"

## *A compounded ointment, not a single plant: Dioscorides gives the recipe as balanine oil, myrrh, cassia and resin, with a little cinnamon in some versions, and the translator's heading reads "Mendesium — Resin Compound". The identification is ours: a perfumed medicinal ointment of the Egyptian type, with no single botanical source, at medium confidence. It is filed in Volume I because its ingredients are plant products and the spine row carries class plant; the ointment as such is a preparation* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk.** This is a single-text entry. The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder from an extraction of the Dioscorides chapter headings; the 30 October check pass kept it as a distinct substance and read it as a compound with no single botanical source. Whether it should eventually move to the preparations volume (Volume V) is a decision for Smith and is not made here. [USER: verify]

**Dioscorides (DMM).** The chapter is 1-72 MENDESION, in Book One, in the section headed OINTMENTS (the section heading is at `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt` line 3961; the chapter is at line 4097; the name index prints "MENDESION 75" and "Mendesium 75"). The translator's gloss line reads "SUGGESTED: Mendesium — Resin Compound". The whole chapter (lines 4097 to 4099) is: "Mendesium is made from balanine oil [1-40, 4-160], myrrh [1-77, 1-73, 4-116], cassia and resin. Some, after they have put in everything by weight, additionally put in a little cinnamon, but this is unneccessary because the things that are not pounded together do not yield their strength. It has similar properties to metopium [1-71] yet to a lower degree."

That is the full text. The bracketed numbers are the translator's cross-references to the chapters on each ingredient. The comparison is to the previous chapter, 1-71 METOPION, which Dioscorides describes as "an ointment ... prepared in Egypt which they call metopium because they mix galbanum with it" (line 4092). The text says nothing of where Mendesium is made. The name is of the form "of Mendes", the Egyptian town, but the chapter does not say so. [USER: verify]

**What the ingredient references say, for context.** 1-40 BALANINON ELAION (line 3871) is the oil of the *balanos* nut; the translator offers three alternative identifications for it, the hara nut tree (*Terminalia citrina*), the gall oak (*Quercus infectoria*) and the Egyptian balsam or zachum oil tree (*Balanites aegyptiaca*), so the base oil of the ointment cannot be named with confidence. Dioscorides says of the oil itself that "it has strength to clean spots, freckles, down on the face, and darkening cataracts and it purges the intestines. It is bad for the stomach." 1-73 STACTE, the "fat of new myrrh", is the precious, very sweet-smelling oil of myrrh (line 4100). 1-12 KASSIA and 1-13 KINAMOMON are the cassia and cinnamon chapters. "Resin" is not cross-referenced. The Codex does not attribute to Mendesium any property that the chapters on its parts give; the chapter's only claim is the comparison with metopium.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Only a comparison: it has properties "similar to metopium yet to a lower degree". Metopium itself is said to heat and burn considerably, to open the mouths of blood vessels, to draw and purge ulcers, to serve in plasters for strength, cut muscles and watery lungs, to help chills and convulsions, to encourage sweat and open the closed vessels of the uterus (lines 4092 to 4096). Mendesium would be a weaker version of those; the chapter does not list them for itself.
- **What the text does not say.** No amounts of the four ingredients ("by weight", with no weights), no method beyond pounding together, no dose, and no part of the body to apply it to.

The Codex does not translate the classic's terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend using this ointment.

### Preparation & Dosing

Dioscorides gives the recipe by ingredient only: balanine oil, myrrh, cassia and resin, "after they have put in everything by weight"; a variant adds a little cinnamon; and a method note that the ingredients must be "pounded together", because "the things that are not pounded together do not yield their strength". No weights, times or temperatures are given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. **Do not use any dose from this entry.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Myrrh, cassia and cinnamon are aromatic plant products long used in perfumery and traditional medicine, and cassia and cinnamon contain cinnamaldehyde, a known skin sensitiser. Resin-and-oil ointments are used on the skin as perfumes and carriers. The claims for metopium and for Mendesium (opening the mouths of vessels, sweat, purging ulcers) have no modern equivalents and are not translated into any. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and none is manufactured here. What the chapter does preserve, in the Dioscoridean manner, is a small discipline of attention: some makers add cinnamon "by weight", but the author says this is unnecessary and that the strength of a compound depends on pounding the parts together. That is a craft instruction about preparing an ointment, and not a cultivation claim.

**Practitioner's note.** A perfumed Egyptian-style ointment, recorded as a lesser metopium. The record is of craft interest only, and there is nothing in it for a cultivator to take up.

### Contraindications

- **Skin.** Cassia and cinnamon oils can irritate or sensitise skin; the text itself calls the related metopium heating and burning.
- **Pregnancy.** Metopium is said to open the closed vessels of the uterus (lines 4092 to 4096); avoid. The same would be assumed of the weaker Mendesium; this is an inference and not a printed warning. [USER: verify]
- **Eyes and ears.** The base oil is said to be good for dark cataracts and earache; do not instil any oil into the eye or ear on the strength of this entry.
- **Identity.** The base oil is not identifiable with confidence; resin is unspecified.
- **Ulcers, chills, convulsions, lung complaints.** These are medical conditions; use proper care.
