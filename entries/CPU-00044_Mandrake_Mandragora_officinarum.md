# Mandrake — يبروج (Yabrūj) / Μανδραγόρας (Mandragoras) / Luffah al-Barriyya
## *Mandragora officinarum L. — European Mandrake; M. autumnalis Bertol. — Autumn Mandrake*
**Tradition Tags:** AT, CM, BM, DMM, EP, SA, SS
**Energetic Classification (TCM):** ☿ Shen (extreme narcotic tier) · ◈ Jing (aphrodisiac/fertility traditional classification)
**Training Phase:** `[SPECIALIST ONLY]`

---

> ⚠️ **CRITICAL SAFETY NOTICE:** Mandrake (*Mandragora* spp.) contains the same tropane alkaloids as henbane — scopolamine, hyoscyamine, and atropine — in high concentrations particularly in the root. It was used historically as a surgical anesthetic and narcotic. **This entry is for pharmacological and historical reference only.** The root is toxic; even modest consumption causes anticholinergic toxidrome and may be fatal. The folkloric reputation is ancient and powerful; the pharmacological danger is real.

### Origin & Tradition

If there is a single plant that has generated more myth, symbolism, and medical legend than any other in Western history, it is the mandrake. Its bifurcated root — which bears a rough human-like silhouette — combined with its profound narcotic properties produced one of the most elaborated pharmacobotanical mythologies in recorded civilization. Yet beneath the legend lies genuine pharmacology: mandrake root contains the most concentrated combination of anticholinergic alkaloids in the European flora, and its medical use — from Dioscorides through Ibn Sina through the Renaissance soporific sponge — represents a continuous 2,000-year surgical tradition.

**AT — Assyrian (Anzahal — 𒀭𒍮𒀭):** The Assyrian *Anzahal* is identified by Thompson (1924) as *Mandragora* based on the therapeutic description — narcotic, soporific, used for pain requiring immobilization. It appears in Nineveh tablet formulas for extreme pain conditions, combined with other narcotic preparations. The *ashipu* (exorcist-physician) administered it ritually, with accompanying incantations that addressed the spiritual dimension of the pain while the alkaloids addressed the physical. The human-root mythology appears in Akkadian descriptions of the plant as "the plant that screams when uprooted."

**EP — Egyptian (referenced in soporific preparations):** Egyptian medicine had a sophisticated understanding of narcotic plants for surgical and pain-management purposes. The *Ebers Papyrus* references soporific preparations that modern Egyptologists have associated with mandrake or closely related Solanaceae. Egyptian artistic representations of mandrake fruit — golden, apple-shaped — appear in tomb paintings and artistic contexts from the New Kingdom period, suggesting it was a plant of cultural as well as medical significance.

**DMM — De Materia Medica (Μανδραγόρας — Mandragoras):** Dioscorides provides the most authoritative and clinically detailed ancient account of mandrake in Book IV, Chapter 75 — one of the longest entries in the text. He describes both male (*M. officinarum*) and female (*M. autumnalis*) varieties. His account of the anaesthetic wine is precise: "The root, boiled with wine to a third part, is given by the cup to those who are to be cut or cauterized, as they wish to produce anesthesia" (Osbaldeston translation). He notes that this wine "causes a sleep" from which the patient can be roused only with difficulty, and describes the dosing carefully to avoid fatality.

**CM — Canon of Medicine (يبروج — Yabrūj / Luffah al-Barriyya):** Ibn Sina provides the most systematic Islamic account. He classifies mandrake as cold in the fourth degree (maximum intensity). His discussion in Book II covers: the two species (spring/autumn); the wine preparation for surgical sleep; the antidote (vinegar + warm wine + aromatic stimulants); the topical use for pain and swelling; the aphrodisiac/fertility properties of the fruit (contested by Ibn Sina as superstition but noted as widespread belief). His description of the surgical anaesthetic procedure matches modern understanding of what the alkaloid combination would pharmacologically achieve.

**BM — Badianus Manuscript (Solanaceae relatives):** The Aztec Nahuatl tradition encountered the New World Solanaceae — primarily *Datura* species (*toloache*, *jimsonweed*) — and independently developed virtually identical medical applications: narcotic, soporific, analgesic, ritual entheogenic. The *Libellus* (1552) documents multiple Solanaceae preparations for pain, sleep induction, and altered states. This pharmacological convergence across unconnected cultures on the same plant family is among the most remarkable examples of independent medical discovery.

### The Mandrake in Biblical and Classical Literature

The cultural penetration of mandrake into mythology, religion, and literature exceeds that of any other medicinal plant:

**Genesis 30:14–16 (Hebrew Bible):** Reuben finds *dūdā'îm* (mandrakes) in the field; Rachel trades the night with Jacob to Leah in exchange for the mandrakes — the first pharmacological negotiation in recorded scripture. *Dūdā'îm* (literally "love plants") refers to mandrake's reputation as a fertility/aphrodisiac agent, consistent with the alkaloid-stimulated uterine relaxation that may have assisted conception in some contexts.

**Song of Solomon 7:13:** "The mandrakes give a smell, and at our gates are all manner of pleasant fruits" — the golden apple-scented fruit referenced in the erotic poetry context.

**Homer — Circe's Potion:** Circe's ability to transform men into animals using a herb-mixed wine is interpreted by classical scholars as a mandrake (or henbane) preparation — the confused, delirious, animal-like state produced by anticholinergic toxidrome would appear as transformation to observers.

**Screaming Mandrake Root Legend:** The belief that uprooting a mandrake causes a scream that kills the hearer — documented in Josephus (~70 CE), Dioscorides, Theophrastus, and continuous through medieval herbals — led to the elaborate ritual of using a dog tied to the root to uproot it at a distance. The legend's pharmacological basis may be the plant's high alkaloid content causing the handler to experience hallucinations (auditory and visual) simply from skin contact with the root.

**Machiavelli — La Mandragola (1518):** A satirical play using the mandrake as plot device — its narcotic properties and fertility associations exploited for comic effect; demonstrates the drug's cultural centrality in Renaissance Italy.

**Shakespeare — Othello, Romeo and Juliet, Antony and Cleopatra:** Multiple Shakespearean references to "mandragora" as a soporific agent demonstrate its continued cultural prominence in Elizabethan England.

**Modern resonance:** The "screaming mandrake" appears in J.K. Rowling's *Harry Potter and the Chamber of Secrets* (1998) — the most culturally influential modern literary continuation of a 2,500-year pharmacobotanical legend.

### Active Components

- **Scopolamine (Hyoscine)** — primary CNS-active alkaloid; amnesia, sedation, hallucination (in overdose); CNS penetration higher than hyoscyamine
- **Hyoscyamine (L-Atropine)** — antispasmodic, antisecretory, analgesic; primary peripheral effect
- **Atropine (racemic hyoscyamine)** — formed during processing; cardiac and peripheral antimuscarinic
- **Mandragorine (cuscohygrine)** — minor alkaloid contributing to total anticholinergic load
- **3α-Tigloyloxytropane** — additional tropane ester with anticholinergic properties
- **Root alkaloid content** — significantly higher than leaves; roots of *M. officinarum* contain 0.3–0.6% total alkaloids; autumn mandrake (*M. autumnalis*) may reach 0.5–1.0% in the root
- **Scopoletin** — coumarin present in all plant parts; mild anti-inflammatory, sedative at high doses
- **Withanolide-type steroids (minor fraction)** — *Mandragora* and *Withania* (ashwagandha) are both Solanaceae; mandrake carries a minor withanolide content — a distant pharmacological connection to the adaptogenic *Withania* family

**SS note (Ayurveda/Unani):** *Mandragora* is not native to the Indian subcontinent, but appears in Unani-Ayurvedic compound texts and later regional pharmacopoeias under Persian medical influence, following the CM (Avicennian) anesthetic/soporific applications.

The alkaloid ratio in *Mandragora* differs from *Hyoscyamus*: *Mandragora* has higher total alkaloid content with scopolamine dominant in some species, while roots contain more hyoscyamine. This pharmacological profile — high total alkaloid + mixed scopolamine/hyoscyamine — made the mandrake root the most potent single-plant narcotic available in the Mediterranean pharmacopoeia.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Training applications: NONE.** Mandrake has no place in training-phase pharmacology.

**Historical medical significance:** Mandrake root wine and the soporific sponge preparations containing it represent the primary general anesthetic of Western medicine from ~100 BCE through approximately 1500 CE — over 1,600 years of continuous surgical anesthesia practice. The pharmacological basis is valid: scopolamine + hyoscyamine would produce:
1. Anxiolysis and sedation (scopolamine CNS effect)
2. Anterograde amnesia (scopolamine — the patient would have no memory of the procedure)
3. Analgesia (partial, via thalamic cholinergic pathway modulation)
4. Antisecretory effect (reduced airway secretions during procedure)

The combination with opium (μ-opioid analgesia) and hemlock (flaccid paralysis via coniine — nicotinic antagonism) created a multi-mechanism balanced anesthesia logically equivalent in principle to modern balanced anesthesia (opioid + sedative + muscle relaxant).

### Preparation & Dosing

**Historical preparations (reference only):**

- **Mandragora wine** — roots (typically autumn-harvested when alkaloid concentration peaks) boiled in wine to concentrate; Dioscorides prescribes one cyathus (~45mL) for surgical sedation; equivalent to approximately 5–15mg scopolamine depending on root concentration — a high dose with significant fatality risk
- **Mandrake fruit (apple)** — the golden apple-scented fruit contains much lower alkaloid levels than the root; consumed as mild sedative/aphrodisiac; the fruit is considerably safer than the root; the "forbidden fruit" identification by some scholars
- **Topical root poultice** — for pain, tumors, joint conditions; alkaloids absorbed transdermally
- **Soporific sponge component** — root decoction combined with henbane, opium, hemlock; see I_129 (Henbane) for complete description

### Scientific Correlation

- **Alkaloid analysis:** Schimmel et al. (2001), *Journal of Ethnopharmacology*: comprehensive alkaloid profiling of both *Mandragora* species; confirmed scopolamine/hyoscyamine composition
- **Archaeological evidence:** Solms-Laubach (1871) through Lev & Amar (2002): mandrake consistently identified in ancient Egyptian and Eastern Mediterranean pharmacobotanical contexts; botanical remains and iconographic evidence from New Kingdom Egypt
- **Surgical anesthesia history:** Krizek (2012), *Acta Medica et Historica Adriatica*: review of the soporific sponge evidence across medieval surgical texts; concludes the procedure was pharmacologically viable
- **Fertility tradition:** hyoscyamine's uterine relaxant effect (antispasmodic) may have reduced uterine spasm as an indirect factor in fertility enhancement in some presentations — a pharmacological basis for the biblical fertility reputation
- **Modern toxicology:** Anticholinergic toxidrome from mandrake ingestion documented in multiple modern case reports (especially from confusion of root with parsnip or similar)
- **Anesthesia history:** Holzman RS (1998) *Anesthesiology* 89:495–513 ("The legacy of Atropos") — history of tropane-alkaloid anesthesia from *Mandragora* through modern practice; confirms Dioscorides' protocol was a pharmacologically effective surgical anesthetic
- **Alkaloid variability:** Berkov S et al. (2006) *Phytochem Anal* 17:22–32 — HPLC-MS of *Mandragora* alkaloid profiles, documenting the large inter-specimen variability that made historical dosing unreliable
- **Distilled modern application:** the one favourable benefit-risk descendant of the mandrake tradition is **pharmaceutical-grade scopolamine** (NOT mandrake preparations) — e.g. transdermal scopolamine for motion sickness, validated by Spinks AB et al. (2011) *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* CD002851; the traditional vestibular/anti-nausea mechanism delivered at a controlled dose

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Primary Tier:** ☿ Shen extreme — suppression of consciousness via cholinergic blockade; the archetypal narcotic-sedative of Western pharmacology for two millennia
**Secondary Tier (traditional attribution only):** ◈ Jing — the traditional fertility/reproductive association reflects the aphrodisiac reputation and the fruit's mild physiological effects; pharmacologically, the uterine relaxant effect may have secondary Jing relevance
**TCM Analogy:** Beyond the *Da Dú* (great toxin) category; specifically relevant to *Mei Gui Jing* (睡眠精 — the sleep essence concept) in historical Chinese medical awareness of Western drugs via Silk Road trade
**Organ-Channel Map (traditional):** Kidney-Jing (fertility association); Heart-Shen (consciousness suppression); Liver (antispasmodic, pain)
**Energetic Action:** Deep Yin-descending; extinguishes Yang; induces artificial stillness; one of the few substances that can completely silence the Three Dantians simultaneously
**Ayurvedic note:** Not significantly developed in classical SS; primarily encountered via Unani tradition

### Contraindications

Identical mechanism to henbane — see I_129 for complete anticholinergic contraindication list. Additionally:
- **Root vs. fruit distinction** — the root is far more toxic than the fruit; many historical poisonings from root confusion with edible plants (parsnip, carrot)
- **Root alkaloid variability** — alkaloid concentration varies enormously by growth conditions, harvest time, species, and plant part; safe dosing is impossible without alkaloid quantification
- **Historical fatalities** — multiple ancient and medieval accounts of fatal outcomes from miscalculated doses; Dioscorides explicitly warns that excess produces death
- **Absolute contraindication:** All the cardiac, ocular, urinary, and neurological contraindications of anticholinergic agents apply; see I_129
