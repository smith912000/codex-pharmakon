### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry: named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** This row was added on 2026-10-09 by the plant-gap run (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP). The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed on the SUGGESTED line of one chapter, and the plant is named by no other text read. The translators' list is long (see below), so the species is not decided by the text.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`.

*2-209 ANAGALLIS, KORKOROS* (heading at line 6407; SUGGESTED lines 6408 to 6411; text at line 6412 and, after a page break, line 6424, in the book on herbs with a sharp quality). The translators' lines read: "Anagallis mas [Fuchs], Anagallis arvensis [Linnaeus], Anagallis phoenecea, Anagallis repens, Lysimachia adoensis — Scarlet Pimpernel, Poor Man's Weather Glass", then "Anagallis foemina [Fuchs, Linnaeus], Anagallis coerulea — Blue Pimpernel", then the translators' warning "POISONOUS — CAUSES ANAEMIA AND DERMATITIS", then "[other usage] Korkoros, Corchoris olitorius — Corchorus". The warning is the translators' and not Dioscorides'.

The description (line 6412): "There are two kinds of anagallis, differing in the flower, for that which has an azure [blue] flower is called the female, but that of a Phoenician [red] colour is called the male. They are little shrubs spreading on the ground, with small leaves on their four-cornered little stalks, somewhat round, similar to those of helxine [4-39, 4-86], with round fruit."

The uses (lines 6412 to 6424): "They are both lessening in strength, drive away inflammation, extract [draw out] splinters or thorns that were run into the body, and repress gangrenous ulcers. The juice gargled purges the head of mucus, and poured into the nostrils it stops toothache. It is put in the opposing nostril to the sore tooth. With Attic [Athenian] honey it mends argemae [small white ulcers on the cornea] and helps moisture of the eyes. It is good (taken as a drink with wine) for those bitten by vipers, as well as for kidney and liver ailments, and for dropsy, but some say that if the anagallis which has the azure [blue] flower is applied it stops prolapse of the perineum, and that which has the Phoenician [red] flower encourages it."

The names (line 6425): "Some call it punicea, others, aeritis, aegitis, or sauritis, the Magi call it oculi sanguis, others, chelidonion, the Romans, macia, the Etruscans, masitipos, the Gauls, sapana, the Dacians, cerceraphron. The common anagallis some call corchoros, halicacabus, or zeliauros, the Magi, nycteritis, the Egyptians call it micij, the Romans, meciato, and the Africans, asirrhizi."

**Identity.** The chapter describes two kinds, blue-flowered (called "female") and red-flowered (called "male"), and says both act the same except in the one disputed use. The row's binomial *Anagallis arvensis* is attached by the translators to the red kind, with the blue kind given separately (*A. coerulea*, blue pimpernel). The text treats them as one entry. The "[other usage]" *Corchorus olitorius* is a different plant, a jute, and is listed because the heading includes the word KORKOROS. The chapter's last sentence applies a second set of names to "the common anagallis", and the text does not say how that differs from the two kinds. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Scarlet pimpernel is generally recorded as a small weed of the primrose family containing saponins and cucurbitacin-type compounds. This is a general statement about the species, and no analysis was read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The chapter records a plant used to draw out splinters, calm inflammation, repress gangrenous ulcers, clear the head and stop toothache with its juice, help the eyes with honey, and, drunk with wine, for viper bites, kidney and liver ailments and dropsy. The translators mark it POISONOUS. The Codex gives no protocol for it.

### Preparation & Dosing

The preparations in the text are: the plant applied; the juice gargled or put into the nostril (the opposite nostril to a sore tooth); the juice with Attic honey for the eye; and the plant "taken as a drink with wine" for bites, organ ailments and dropsy. No quantity, frequency or duration is printed. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] It is a general fact that scarlet pimpernel is regarded as a mildly toxic weed, and that contact with the sap can irritate the skin, which is consistent with the translators' note on dermatitis.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints that both kinds are "lessening in strength" and "drive away inflammation". It prints no warming, cooling or drying quality, and the heading of this section is therefore limited to those two phrases.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. The chapter offers a discipline of attention: two kinds that differ in flower colour, a creeping habit with four-cornered stalks, round fruit, and a long catalogue of names among the Magi, Etruscans, Gauls, Dacians, Egyptians and Africans. It records a disagreement ("some say") about a use of each kind. It assigns the plant no cultivation status.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here gives a cultivator a practice. A weed with two flower colours is recorded, with local and internal remedies attached and a translator's warning that it is poisonous. The abstentive answer is the correct one.

### Contraindications

The translators mark the plant POISONOUS and note anaemia and dermatitis. Handling can irritate the skin, and the juice put into the nose or eyes, or the plant drunk in wine, is not safe to try. Viper bite, dropsy, and kidney and liver disease need a clinician. Do not use in pregnancy. None of the uses above is advice.
