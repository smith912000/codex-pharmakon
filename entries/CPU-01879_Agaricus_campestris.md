# Agaricus campestris — Agaricon AGARICON (Dioscorides 3-1, a name the English edition lists for it); Chhatrika (Sushruta, Appendix, "Ohhatriksb" as scanned)

## *Agaricus campestris L., the field mushroom, a ground-growing edible fungus; the identification is made on two different footings: in the Sushruta Appendix Bhishagratna prints "Agaricus Campestris" against a name the scan gives as "Ohhatriksb" (Chhatrika), which is of medium confidence at species level, and in Dioscorides 3-1 the English edition lists "Agaricus campestris — Common Mushroom" only as one of several suggestions for agaricon, a drug the text describes as something quite different, so the Dioscorides tag rests on that list line and not on the description* | *(Volume IV — Fungal/Algal, shelved in Volume I)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM, SS

### Origin & Tradition

> **CAUTION.** Wild mushrooms are a leading cause of fatal plant-and-fungus poisoning, and the field mushroom has deadly look-alikes. The agaricon of Dioscorides 3-1 is, on the description, a drastic purge. This entry is a historical record only. It is not a recommendation, and nobody should gather or eat a fungus on the strength of it.

**Named by two texts among those on disk.** The row was added on 13 October as an unverified placeholder from two extraction lines. A first pass proposed merging it with CPU-01279 (*Fomes officinalis*, the larch agaric); the audit disagreed, because the field mushroom is a different species. This entry replaces the placeholder. Both loci were opened on 30 October.

**Dioscorides, De Materia Medica (DMM).** The chapter is 3-1, AGARICON, the first chapter of Book Three, `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading at line 6514. The editor's suggestions read: "Fomes officinalis, Polyphorus officinalis, Boletus purgans, Polyphorus igniarius, Boletus laricis, Ungulina officinalis — Agaric [other usage] Agaricus aurantiacus — Orange Mushroom; Agaricus campestris — Common Mushroom". The row's binomial is the third of these. The first line of the text is: "Agaricum is said to be a root similar to silphium [3-94], not thick on the outside like silphium, but all thin. Some of it is male and some female, of which the female excels, having straight veins within, but the male is round and grows the same on all sides. In taste both are the same, tasting sweet indeed at first; after dissolving it grows bitter. It grows in Agaria in the Sarmatian (country). Some say it is the root of a tree, some that it grows in the stocks of trees that are rotten, like mushrooms. It also grows in Galatia in Asia, and in Cilicia on the cedars but this is brittle and weak."

On that description the drug is a woody, bitter, tree-growing body, which the first six suggestions on the editor's list (the polypores and the larch agaric) fit, and which is not the soft, ground-growing field mushroom. The entry therefore does not claim that Dioscorides describes *Agaricus campestris*. It records that the English edition lists it, and it keeps the Dioscorides text here as the context for the list line. The properties the chapter gives: "As for the properties of it: it is astringent and warming, good for griping, indigestion, hernias, and falls from on high. Twenty grains is given with honeyed wine to those not feverish, but it is given in honey and water to the feverish. For liver ailments, asthma, jaundice, dysentery, nephritis, dysuria, womb constriction, and sickly looks a teaspoonful is given. To those with tuberculosis of the lungs it is given with passum [raisin wine]; and to the splenetic with vinegar and honey. For gastritis it is given as it is, chewed and swallowed down without moisture poured on it... Thirty grains (taken with water) stops the throwing-up of blood. ... A teaspoonful or two (taken as a drink with honey and water) purges the bowels. It is an antidote for poison taken with one teaspoonful of diluted wine; and thirty grains (taken as a drink with wine) helps the strikes and bites of snakes. Finally, it is good for all internal disorders, given according to strength and age" (lines 6518-6522). These doses belong to the agaricon of the text and are quoted with their locus; they are not doses of any field mushroom.

**Sushruta Samhita, Appendix (SS).** In the Appendix to Bhishagratna's Volume III (`Sources/SS_Sushruta/text/Bhishagratna_Vol3_djvu.txt`) the names column reads, in order, "Ohakramarda, Champaka, Ohanaka, Ohandana, OhaCageri, Oliavya, ChavikeC, Ohavyaka, Ohhatriksb, Chira-villa, Ohitraka, Chitraka-pliala, Choraka, Chora-pushpij, Chuta" (lines 31506-31534, "Ohhatriksb" at line 31522), and the identification column reads "Cassia Tora, Michelia Champaca, Cicer Arietinum, Santalum Album, Oxalis Monadelpha, Piper Chaba, Agaricus Campestris, Pongamia Glabra, Plumbago Zeylanica, See Indra-viruni, Andropogon Aciculatis, See Amra" (lines 31542-31560, "Agaricus Campestris" at line 31553). Matching the two columns by position, with the braces that join variant names, gives "Chhatrika" (scanned as "Ohhatriksb") for "Agaricus Campestris": Chavya, Chavika and Chavyaka share "Piper Chaba", and the next name takes the next identification; the following pairs (Chira-bilva with *Pongamia*, Chitraka with *Plumbago*) agree with the usual identifications. The pairing is read from a damaged two-column scan. [USER: verify against the printed page.]

The main text of the Samhita does not use the name Chhatrika in the chapters read. Its general account of mushrooms is in *Sutrasthana* ch.46, the chapter on food and drink (`sutra/SS_Sutra_ch46.txt`, lines 1847-1873): "Mushrooms are generally found to grow on stacks of straw (Palala), or are seen vegetating on the stems of bamboo (Venu) or sugar-cane (Ikshu), or as sprouting up from beneath the surface of the ground (Udbhida), or growing on a heap of decomposed cow-dung (Karisha)", with properties given by habitat, and of those "which grow on the ground (Bhumija)" that they "are heavy of digestion and do not inordinately generate the Vayu, their tastes varying according to the soil they grow on". That passage is general and names no species. A different spelling, "Chhatra" and "Atichhatra", occurs in *Sutrasthana* ch.19 (line 190, among drugs "placed on the head of the patient") and in *Chikitsasthana* ch.30 (line 145, the tonic-remedies chapter, where they are "bulbous in their origin" and "attached to a plant of the Sveta-kapoti species"). Those two are not equated here with the Appendix's Chhatrika or with any mushroom, because the Appendix does not do so in the lines read. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The sources read name no constituent. In general terms, *Agaricus campestris* is a basidiomycete mushroom, much of whose fresh weight is water, with protein, fibre and chitin in the cell wall; the genus is also known to carry small amounts of hydrazine-type compounds. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice. Do not gather or eat wild fungi from this entry.**

- **What the texts say it is for.** Dioscorides' agaricon (3-1): griping, indigestion, hernias, liver ailments, asthma, jaundice, dysentery, bleeding, bites and poisons, and as a purge. Sushruta's Appendix gives a name and an identification only.
- **What the texts do not say.** Neither text, on the lines read, assigns a use to the field mushroom itself.

The Codex does not translate the classics' terms into modern diagnoses, gives no protocol, and does not recommend this fungus.

### Preparation & Dosing

Dioscorides' agaricon is taken in honeyed wine or honey and water, or with passum, vinegar and honey, or with water or diluted wine, or chewed dry, in amounts given as "twenty grains", "thirty grains", "a teaspoonful" or "a teaspoonful or two" (3-1, lines 6518-6522). Those amounts belong to that drug. No preparation or dose is given in the Sushruta lines read. No dose is given in the sources retrieved for the field mushroom. **Do not use any dose from this entry.** [USER: verify before any numerical use.]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. *Agaricus campestris* is the field mushroom of European grassland, closely related to the cultivated button mushroom, *Agaricus bisporus*; edible species of the genus have poisonous look-alikes, among them the white *Amanita* species. The larch agaric of Dioscorides (*Fomes officinalis*) is a bracket fungus long used as a purge, so a drug named "agaricus" is not the same as a mushroom of the genus *Agaricus*. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM.** In 3-1 the agaricon is "astringent and warming", and in taste "sweet indeed at first; after dissolving it grows bitter". These qualities belong to the drug as the text describes it.
- **SS.** The Appendix gives none. The general statement in ch.46 (above) gives qualities for mushrooms by habitat, not for a named species.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(c) Absent.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and 3-1 offers none. What it offers a practitioner is a discipline of attention: the male and female kinds are told apart by their veins and shape, the places of growth are named (Agaria in Sarmatia, Galatia, Cilicia on cedars), and the dose is stated against the patient's fever, strength and age. That is a practice of identification and measure, not a cultivation status.

**Ayurveda / Sushruta (SS).** *(c) Absent.* The Appendix names and identifies; it assigns no cultivation status. The nearest names in the chapters, Chhatra and Atichhatra in the tonic chapter (Chikitsa ch.30, line 145), are said there to have "the virtue of arresting death and decay", but the Appendix does not identify them with this mushroom, and the claim is not carried over. No ojas, tejas or prana statement is made for the field mushroom.

**Practitioner's note.** A mushroom in one text's list of suggestions and in another text's glossary. Neither supports any cultivation use. A cultivator should not gather or eat wild fungi on this basis.

### Contraindications

- **Misidentification.** Deadly mushrooms resemble the field mushroom. Do not eat a wild fungus unless an expert has identified it in the hand.
- **Purge.** Dioscorides' agaricon is a purge; do not use it as one.
- **Pregnancy, lactation, children.** Dioscorides says "given according to strength and age" and nothing else. [USER: verify]
- **Identity.** The two attestations rest on a list line and a damaged scan. See above. [USER: verify]
