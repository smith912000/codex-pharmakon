# Slug — rGyud-bzhi, na.bun.bu.mo (Clark's "Slug"; Men-Tsee-Khang's "Nabun bhumo", glossed "Snail")
## *A soft-bodied mollusc; Clark prints the Tibetan na.bun.bu.mo and translates it "Slug", Men-Tsee-Khang's glossary renders the same name "Snail", and neither gives a binomial in the lines read* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The slug is entered in the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, the chapter on the powers of medicines, in Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). It stands in the section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), whose thirteen headings are "horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). The entry is in line 1202: "na.bun.bu.mo: Slug: [has eight names] and stops discharge of sinus fluid." It follows the "Droppings" heading (line 1198) and a run of insects and other small creatures (a beetle, crab, Tibetan ant, cockroach, dung and scarab beetles and the "moxa insect"); in the thirteen-heading scheme that stretch is the "whole body" group, but the heading itself is not printed in the lines read. [USER: verify] The Tibetan name appears again in Clark's index of Tibetan names (line 1710), which only repeats it.

Clark's own convention is stated in his introduction (line 83): the Tibetan name is given first, then an English name "where available", and where "any doubt at all remains regarding the identity of certain animal products the Tibetan names have been included in parentheses with the probable English identification". The slug entry has the Tibetan name in front and the English after the colon, with no binomial; that fits his note of doubt about the identity of animal products in general, but he does not say so of this entry. Clark does give a binomial for the separate entry for the snail in line 1160, "hbu.skyog: Snail: Cipangopaludina chinensis Gray", whose shell "is anthelmintic and purges oedema fluid", a different Tibetan name and a different use.

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`) has the same item in its own words: "Snails stop cerebrospinal fluid discharge" (line 3573), and its glossary of animals pairs "Nabun bhumo" with "Snail" (line 7158). So the two translations differ twice. They render the animal differently ("Slug" or "Snail"), and they render the thing that is stopped differently ("sinus fluid" or "cerebrospinal fluid"). The Tibetan wording is not in the files read, so neither difference can be settled here. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Slugs and snails are gastropod molluscs. Their bodies are mostly water and protein, and they secrete a mucus rich in glycoproteins; they can carry parasites and, because they eat plants and soil, whatever those contain. No active principle is claimed by the sources, and none specific to this entry was read.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is a single indication in a materia medica list: the slug "stops discharge of sinus fluid" (Clark) or the snail "stops cerebrospinal fluid discharge" (Men-Tsee-Khang). The Codex offers no protocol. The two readings are very different clinical matters: a watery discharge from the nose can be sinus or allergic, but a clear fluid leaking from the nose or ear after a head injury or surgery may be cerebrospinal fluid, which is a medical emergency with a risk of meningitis. The Tantra gives no preparation, dose or route in these lines.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation, route or dose is given in the sources retrieved: the Clark and Men-Tsee-Khang lines state the indication only, and say nothing of whether the animal is used fresh, dried, burnt or in a compound. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Slugs and snails are the intermediate hosts of the rat lungworm (*Angiostrongylus cantonensis*), which can cause eosinophilic meningitis in people who eat raw or undercooked snails or slugs, or produce contaminated with them. Nothing in the lines read links these animals to the treatment of any discharge. Leakage of cerebrospinal fluid is diagnosed and treated clinically, usually by imaging and surgery. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* Neither translation read gives the slug any cultivation framing. It is a one-clause indication in a run of small creatures, and the complaint is a discharge. The frame around it is the tradition's own: the project's cultivation spec records that the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) are derived from the three poisons and that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root. That point comes from the spec and not from any line about the slug. No source read attaches the slug to *bcud len* (essence extraction), and there is no formal Tibetan category of substances for subtle-body practice. Clark's remark (line 57) that "only very few animal products are used" nowadays is a note on usage, not on status.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a single indication with no preparation and no dose, the two translations disagree about both the animal and the discharge, and eating slugs or snails carries a real parasite risk. The honest use of the entry is as a record of what the Tantra says.

### Contraindications

- **Parasites:** raw or undercooked slugs and snails can carry rat lungworm and other parasites; do not eat them, and wash produce well.
- **Not a treatment for a discharge:** clear watery fluid from the nose or ear after a head injury, or with fever, stiff neck or severe headache, needs emergency assessment.
- **Hygiene:** wild-collected animals and anything they have crawled over may carry bacteria and parasites.
- **Legal and conservation status:** collecting wild molluscs may be regulated, and some species are protected; the species is not identified in the lines read. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** killing an animal for a substance with no supported benefit is hard to justify.
