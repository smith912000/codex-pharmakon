# Spiny Milk Vetch / Iklil al-Malik

## *Astragalus hamosus* L. | Family: Fabaceae

**Tradition Tags:** EP, AT, DMM, CM, SS

**Energetic Classification:** Cool, Moist (First Degree) — Demulcent, Softening, Relaxing

**Training Phase:** Restoration — for the practitioner burned dry by effort; where the tissue is irritated, the nerve raw, and force will make it worse.

---

### Origin & Tradition

Before anything else, a boundary. **This is not Huang Qi.**

*Astragalus hamosus* is a small, sprawling annual legume of the Mediterranean and Irano-Turanian dry country — a weedy pasture plant with tiny seeds and a distinctive hooked pod (*hamosus*, "hooked") that curls like a crescent or a small crown, giving it its Arabic name. *Astragalus membranaceus* (now *A. mongholicus*), the Huang Qi of Chinese medicine, is a robust perennial of northeast Asia whose sliced taproot is one of the great Qi tonics, and whose evidence base is built on **root polysaccharides** and **cycloartane saponins (astragalosides)**.

They share a genus of some 3,000 species and almost nothing else. Different continent, different life-form, different organ used (pod and aerial part versus taproot), different doctrine, different chemistry, different evidence. The Huang Qi immunomodulation literature — the polysaccharide fraction, the astragaloside IV work, the whole apparatus — **does not transfer to this plant and must not be cited for it.** This is the most consequential error available in this entry and it is made constantly, in supplement marketing and in the secondary literature, because the shared genus name is doing work it cannot do. A cross-reference is given below so the two can be read against each other; they should never be read as one.

**Unani & Arabic-Galenic Tradition (AT / DMM)**
The plant's Unani name is **إكليل الملك — *Iklil al-malik***, "the King's Crown," from the ring of hooked pods. (Note a persistent nomenclatural hazard: *Iklil al-malik* is also applied in some Arabic sources to *Melilotus officinalis*, sweet clover — a different plant with different and more dangerous chemistry, being the source of dicoumarol. **[USER: verify]** which plant is intended before acting on any Arabic-language source using this name.)

The Unani indication set is coherent and consistent across sources: *Iklil al-malik* is classed as **mulayyin** (softening/emollient) and **muhallil-i-waram** (resolvent of swelling). It is used for inflammatory swelling — applied as a poultice to hard or hot swellings, joints, and abscesses — and internally as a demulcent for irritated mucous membranes. The published pharmacognostic literature summarises the traditional profile as "emollient, demulcent, aphrodisiac, diuretic, laxative, and good for inflammation, ulcers, and leukoderma… useful in treating irritation of the mucous membranes, nervous affections, and catarrh" (Kondeva-Burdina et al., *Pharmacogn Mag*, 2014; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4103/0973-1296.139778)).

Two threads matter for our purposes. First, **anti-inflammatory and analgesic** — the plant is for hot, irritated, painful tissue. Second, **"nervous affections"** — a vague classical phrase, but it recurs, and it is the seed of the nerve-tonic reading.

**Persian / Iranian Traditional Medicine (AT)**
Iranian traditional medicine is the tradition that carries the plant's most specific and most interesting claim: *A. hamosus* appears in Iranian traditional documentation as a therapy for **a dementia whose features and symptoms resemble Alzheimer's disease** — this is the explicit ethnopharmacological premise of the neuroinflammation work cited below (Bahaeddin et al., *Appl Physiol Nutr Metab*, 2017; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1139/apnm-2017-0585)). Iranian practice also uses the **pods** specifically as a hydro-alcoholic preparation for pain and inflammatory conditions (Shojaii et al., *Iran J Pharm Res*, 2015; [PMC4277639](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4277639/)).

This is the plant's distinguishing indication. Not immunity, not Qi, not stamina — **the aging, inflamed mind**.

**Ayurvedic Practice (SS)**
The plant enters Ayurvedic and Indian Unani practice under names including *Iklil al-malik* (Unani usage in India) as a *shothahara* (anti-inflammatory) and *vedanasthapana* (analgesic) drug, used topically on swellings and internally as a mild demulcent and nervine. The Ayurvedic attestation is real but thin, and largely a Unani import into the Indian subcontinent rather than an independent classical Ayurvedic drug. **[USER: verify]** any specific *nighantu* citation before asserting one; the classical Sanskrit attestation is doubtful.

**Chinese Practice (CM) — a narrow and specific attestation**
*A. hamosus* is **not** a plant of classical Chinese materia medica. Its single documented Chinese presence is modern and formulaic: it is the principal (12 of 22 parts) ingredient of **Qiguiyin**, a hospital preparation used clinically for severe pneumonia caused by drug-resistant bacteria, combined with *Angelica sinensis*, *Lonicera sempervirens*, *Artemisia annua* and *Polygonum cuspidatum* (Ding et al., *J Ethnopharmacol*, 2021; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2021.114364)).

Handle this with care. It is entirely possible — indeed likely — that the *A. hamosus* in Qiguiyin is a **botanical misidentification in the source paper for Huang Qi (*A. mongholicus*)**, which is the plant one would expect at 12 parts in a Chinese formula of that shape, and which is the classical partner of *Angelica sinensis* in the Danggui Buxue Tang pairing that this formula visibly echoes. The paper says *hamosus*. The formula logic says *membranaceus*. **[USER: verify]** — and do not treat Qiguiyin as evidence for *A. hamosus* until it is resolved. It is reported here for completeness and flagged as suspect.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer**

The doctrine of *Iklil al-malik* is **softening**. Its Unani class name, *mulayyin*, means exactly that: it makes soft what has gone hard. Where the tamarisk binds and closes, this plant loosens and cools. Its whole gesture is the opposite of force — it is given precisely where force is the problem, where the tissue has responded to stress by hardening and heating and must be persuaded, not driven.

The crown gives the symbol. A crown is a ring of points that surrounds and protects the head without penetrating it — a boundary held at the circumference, not a pressure applied at the centre. This maps with unusual precision onto the plant's most specific indication: the aging brain under inflammatory siege, where the classical instruction is to **cool the surround**, not to stimulate the organ. The King's Crown does not sharpen the king. It defends him from the heat.

Now the chemistry, and here the doctrine does better than expected.

The **anti-inflammatory doctrine is chemically real and mechanistically specific.** The plant's flavonol glycoside RGP protects rat brain synaptosomes against 6-hydroxydopamine oxidative stress, and *A. hamosus* extract suppresses **COX-2, IL-1β and TNF-α** in amyloid-beta-injected rats while improving memory. The Unani *muhallil-i-waram* — "resolvent of swelling" — is, in a modern vocabulary, cytokine and prostaglandin suppression. When a thirteenth-century Unani physician wrote that this plant resolves hot swelling, and a 2017 Western blot shows it knocking down COX-2 and TNF-α in inflamed hippocampus, those are the same observation separated by seven centuries of vocabulary. This is one of the better doctrine-to-mechanism resolutions in the volume.

The **"nervous affections" / dementia doctrine earns a genuine, if preliminary, hearing.** This is remarkable. A vague classical phrase and a specific Iranian traditional indication pointed a modern group at an Alzheimer's model, and the model returned a positive result on both behaviour and neuroinflammatory markers. The tradition was not describing a nootropic — it was describing an anti-inflammatory acting on the brain, which is precisely what the data show. The doctrine did not merely survive testing; it **predicted the mechanism**.

The **demulcent/emollient doctrine is chemically plausible and untested.** Legume aerial parts and pods carry mucilage; a mucilage does what a demulcent is said to do, by simple physics. No data retrieved. Grade traditional.

The **aphrodisiac doctrine is unsupported and should be deflated.** It is the standard tonic-plant accretion, attached to almost every demulcent legume in the Unani corpus, and there is no evidence for it here. Discard.

### Active Components

- **Rhamnocitrin 4′-β-D-galactopyranoside (RGP)** — a flavonol glycoside (a kaempferol/rhamnocitrin derivative) isolated from *A. hamosus* leaves. This is the plant's **best-characterised single active constituent** and carries most of its individual-compound evidence (Kondeva-Burdina et al., 2014; Saleem et al., *Mol Cell Biochem*, 2013).
- **Triterpenoid saponins — azukisaponins and soyasapogenol-type.** HPTLC screening of *A. hamosus* identified **azukisaponin isomers and soyasapogenol** at Rf 0.2 (Nafti et al., *Molecules*, 2022; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27175376)). This is the chemically decisive finding of the entry: *A. hamosus* carries **oleanane-type (soyasaponin/azukisaponin) saponins — the generic legume class**, NOT the **cycloartane-type astragalosides** that define *A. membranaceus*. The two plants are not making the same molecules. The paper explicitly notes how difficult these saponins were to detect and isolate, which is part of why this plant's chemistry is thin.
- **Flavonoids (general)** — kaempferol/rhamnocitrin derivatives beyond RGP.
- **Mucilage** — presumed basis of the demulcent action. Uncharacterised.
- **Non-polar fraction constituents** — hexane and ethyl acetate fractions carried the analgesic activity in the hot-plate model, meaning the analgesic principle is **lipophilic and is not RGP or the saponins**. It has not been identified. This is an open question.

**Explicitly absent:** immunomodulatory root polysaccharides. Astragaloside IV. Formononetin/calycosin at Huang Qi levels. No evidence for any of these in *A. hamosus* was retrieved.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

*A. hamosus* is a **restoration** agent, and specifically a **cooling** restorative — which distinguishes it sharply from the warming tonics the genus name invites you to expect.

It is indicated where the practitioner has driven themselves into inflammation: joints hot and stiff, tissue irritated, mind foggy and raw, the whole system in a low-grade burn from sustained effort. The instinct at that point is to reach for a stimulant tonic. That instinct is wrong, and *Iklil al-malik* is the correction. It does not add. It takes the heat out so that the tissue can do its own repairing.

Its most distinctive Kenetica application follows the Iranian indication: the **inflamed, aging cognition** — where thinking has become effortful and unclear, and the cause is inflammatory rather than depletive. Here the plant is given not to sharpen but to cool the surround. If the fog is from emptiness rather than heat, this is the wrong plant, and Huang Qi may be the right one.

Restoration phase, then, in its strict sense: the phase where the correct action is *removal of an obstacle*, not *addition of a force*.

**Cultivator's note:** *A. hamosus* is an annual and an easy one — it is a pasture legume, not a garden specimen, and behaves like one. Germination is **temperature-dependent and this matters**: seed tested under alternating 20–35 °C achieved roughly 50% germination within the first week, while a **constant 20 °C markedly reduced germination** (Khélil et al., *Pak J Biol Sci*, 2011; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3923/pjbs.2011.693.697)). The seeds are very small and want the *diurnal swing* of open dry ground, not stable warmth. Sow shallow, outdoors, in spring, and let the day-night temperature difference do the work; a heated propagator held at constant temperature will fail this plant. Expect variability — the study found considerable germination variation between accessions. Being a legume it fixes nitrogen and asks nothing of the soil. Harvest pods at maturity (the hooks are the identification and the pods carry the best-evidenced preparation); harvest leaf for flavonoid content.

Verify identity by the hooked pod. The genus is enormous and several *Astragalus* species are **locoweeds** carrying swainsonine or selenium — misidentification in this genus is not a minor error.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the total *A. hamosus* literature is **16 papers**, of which several are taxonomy, palynology and agronomy rather than pharmacology. There are **no clinical trials**. Everything below is in vitro or rodent, mostly single-lab, mostly unreplicated. This is a **data-sparse plant** and every grade reflects that.

**Anti-inflammatory & analgesic — GRADE: PRELIMINARY (best-supported use).** Hydro-alcoholic pod extract reduced formalin-induced rat paw edema dose-dependently (P<0.05), with effects comparable to sodium salicylate — 1000 mg/kg in the acute phase, 100 and 300 mg/kg in the chronic phase. Analgesia was significant in both acetic-acid writhing and hot-plate models at 700 and 1000 mg/kg, again comparable to sodium salicylate; **hexane and ethyl acetate fractions, but not the others, carried the hot-plate activity** (Shojaii et al., *Iran J Pharm Res*, 2015; [PMC4277639](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4277639/)). The authors' own conclusion is the right one: the active components are unidentified and more work is needed. This directly and specifically corroborates the Unani *muhallil-i-waram* indication.

**Neuroinflammation & memory — GRADE: PRELIMINARY (most interesting result).** Oral *A. hamosus* extract at 100 mg/kg/day for 16 days improved memory in amyloid-beta-injected rats on both Y-maze and shuttle box, and Western blotting showed amelioration of **COX-2, IL-1β and TNF-α** (Bahaeddin et al., *Appl Physiol Nutr Metab*, 2017; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1139/apnm-2017-0585)). Single study, single lab, rodent Aβ-injection model — which is a model of amyloid toxicity, not of Alzheimer's disease, a distinction that has buried many compounds. But it was **explicitly designed to test an Iranian traditional indication and the indication held**. This deserves replication and has not received it.

**Cytoprotection / antioxidant (RGP) — GRADE: PRELIMINARY (in vitro).** Rhamnocitrin 4′-β-D-galactopyranoside protected isolated rat brain synaptosomes against 6-OH-dopamine-induced oxidative stress, and rat hepatocytes against bendamustine and cyclophosphamide toxicity, with activity **similar to silymarin** in both (Kondeva-Burdina et al., *Pharmacogn Mag*, 2014; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4103/0973-1296.139778)). Isolated-cell work. Note the flip side: a compound that alters cyclophosphamide metabolism in hepatocytes is a compound with **drug-interaction potential** — see Contraindications.

**Hepatoprotection / anticancer (RGP) — GRADE: PRELIMINARY.** RGP dose-dependently reversed DENA-induced hepatocellular carcinoma markers in Wistar rats — normalising ALT, AST, ALP, bilirubin and α-fetoprotein, restoring SOD, CAT, GPx and GST, and reducing lipid peroxidation, confirmed histopathologically (Saleem et al., *Mol Cell Biochem*, 2013; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11010-013-1792-6)). A chemically-induced carcinogenesis model. Read as antioxidant hepatoprotection, not as an anticancer claim.

**Antiproliferative (breast cancer, in vitro) — GRADE: PRELIMINARY.** *A. hamosus* extract induced apoptosis in MCF-7 cells, upregulating caspase-3, -8 and -9 and downregulating Ki-67, with greater effect in a 3D fibrin gel model than 2D culture, arresting the cycle in S and G2/M in 3D (Mahmoodi et al., *Asian Pac J Cancer Prev*, 2022; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.31557/APJCP.2022.23.2.731)). MCF-7 is the plant world's most permissive cell line; a very large fraction of plant extracts kill it. Weak evidence, reported for completeness.

**PCOS / insulin sensitisation — GRADE: PRELIMINARY.** In estradiol-valerate-induced PCOS rats, ethanolic extract reduced body weight, insulin and testosterone, increased corpora lutea and reduced cystic follicles, and upregulated **IRS1** expression (Nejati et al., *Endocr Metab Immune Disord Drug Targets*, 2022; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2174/1871530321666211110123931)). Single rodent study, no traditional indication behind it. Hypothesis-generating only.

**Qiguiyin toxicology — GRADE: NOT APPLICABLE TO THIS PLANT.** The 13-week rat study reporting no distinct toxicity for the formula (Ding et al., 2021; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2021.114364)) is a **multi-herb formula** study, and one whose *A. hamosus* attribution is suspect (above). It is **not** a safety clearance for *A. hamosus*. Note in passing that the formula at 50× clinical dose produced atrophied seminiferous tubules in 2 of 10 male rats, reversible on recovery.

**Immunomodulation — GRADE: NO EVIDENCE. This is the entry's key null.** Nothing retrieved. The chemistry actively argues against it: the plant carries generic legume azukisaponins/soyasapogenol, not the cycloartane astragalosides, and no root polysaccharide fraction is characterised. **Any immune claim made for *A. hamosus* is borrowed from *A. membranaceus* and is unsupported.**

**Aphrodisiac, diuretic, laxative, leukoderma — GRADE: TRADITIONAL, no evidence retrieved.** These appear in the traditional profile as reported in the pharmacognostic literature and nowhere else. Deflate.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

| System | Classification |
|---|---|
| Unani (*Iklil al-malik*) | Cold and moist, first degree; *mulayyin* (softening/emollient), *muhallil-i-waram* (resolvent of swelling), *musakkin-i-alam* (analgesic) |
| Persian / Iranian traditional | Cooling; nervine and anti-dementia; pods for pain and inflammation |
| Ayurvedic (Unani-derived usage) | Madhura–tikta rasa; sheeta virya; *shothahara*, *vedanasthapana*; pitta-reducing. **[USER: verify]** classical attestation |

| Kenetica | Cool, moist, softening, relaxing. Restoration-phase. Removes heat so repair can proceed; adds nothing. |

**Contrast — and hold this contrast firmly:** *A. membranaceus* (Huang Qi) is **sweet, slightly warm, tonifying, ascending, Qi-raising**. *A. hamosus* is **cool, moist, resolvent, relaxing**. On the energetics they are near-opposites. Substituting one for the other is not a species error only; it is a directional error, and it will make the wrong patient worse.

### Contraindications

- **Do not substitute for or confuse with *Astragalus membranaceus* / Huang Qi.** Different chemistry, opposite energetics, non-transferable evidence. This is the primary contraindication of the entry.
- **Do not confuse with *Melilotus officinalis*** where Arabic sources use *Iklil al-malik* ambiguously. *Melilotus* contains coumarins convertible to dicoumarol, an anticoagulant. A naming error here is a bleeding risk.
- **Genus misidentification** — *Astragalus* contains locoweeds accumulating **swainsonine** (neurotoxic) and **selenium** (toxic). Confirm the hooked pod. Do not wildcraft from unverified stands.
- **Pregnancy and lactation — avoid.** No safety data whatsoever. The PCOS work shows the plant alters testosterone and insulin in female rats; that is a reason for caution, not reassurance.
- **Cytotoxic chemotherapy — caution / likely avoid.** RGP measurably altered **bendamustine and cyclophosphamide** handling in rat hepatocytes. A plant that modulates alkylating-agent metabolism is a plant that can blunt or amplify a chemotherapy dose. Do not combine without oncological supervision.
- **Diabetes medication** — insulin-sensitising effect in a rodent model; theoretical additive hypoglycaemia. Untested.
- **Hormone-sensitive conditions** — reduced testosterone and insulin in rodents; endocrine activity is real enough to warrant caution and too poorly characterised to characterise.
- **Depletive/cold presentations — wrong plant.** This is a cooling resolvent. Given to a genuinely depleted, cold, empty practitioner it will deepen the problem. The plant it is mistaken for is the plant they need.
- **No established human dose.** Every internal dose in the literature is a rodent dose. Nothing here supports self-administration at scale.
