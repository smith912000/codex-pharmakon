### Origin & Tradition

Henna is one of the oldest continuously used cosmetic-medicines in the world — a small shrub of the arid Old-World belt (North Africa, the Near East, the Indian subcontinent) whose leaves yield the orange-red dye **lawsone**. It sits at the intersection of medicine, cosmetics and sacred ritual, staining skin, hair and nails while carrying a reputation as a cooling, astringent, protective agent. Its cultural weight in the Islamic world — as **the dye associated with the Prophet Muhammad**, who is traditionally reported to have dyed his hair and beard and to have praised henna — gives it a devotional dimension no other dye-plant shares.

**Greco-Roman / Dioscorides tradition (*kýpros*):** Dioscorides describes *kýpros* (henna) in *De Materia Medica* — the leaves as astringent and cooling, made into applications for hot swellings, mouth sores and burns, and the aromatic flowers (Egyptian *kypros* oil/ointment, "camphire" of antiquity) as a prized fragrance and emollient. Classical practice used it as a cooling, binding, wound- and sore-soothing agent.

**Egyptian tradition:** Henna use in Egypt is ancient — traces of henna-staining have been associated with mummies, and it was used cosmetically (hair, nails, skin) and as a cooling, protective application. Within the Egyptian-Arab medical continuum it served as a cooling astringent for hot skin conditions, sores and the scalp.

**Unani / Avicenna tradition (*ḥinnāʾ*):** Unani classifies henna as cold and dry, astringent (*qābiḍ*) and desiccant. It is prescribed for hot skin eruptions, burns, ulcers and boils, for cooling and strengthening hair and scalp, for cracked/inflamed feet and hands, and — with its cooling nature — for headaches from heat (applied as a paste). The flowers were valued as an aromatic. Avicenna's *Canon* treats henna as a reliable cooling astringent topical.

**Assyrian / Near-Eastern tradition:** As a native dye and cosmetic plant of the ancient Near East, henna belongs to the region's long tradition of protective, cooling body-staining and topical skin medicine — its adornment and its cooling-astringent use being inseparable.

**Ayurvedic / South-Asian tradition (*madayantika* / *mehndi*):** Ayurveda uses henna (*mehndi*) as cooling and Pitta-pacifying, applied for burning skin conditions, headaches (cooling paste on soles and palms), wounds and ulcers, and as a hair conditioner and scalp cooler; the cooling of the extremities via henna paste is a classic domestic remedy for excess heat.

**Chinese / regional tradition (*zhi jia hua*, "fingernail flower"):** Introduced to the southern Chinese and broader East/Southeast Asian sphere, henna (*zhi jia hua*, "fingernail flower," from its nail-staining) is used regionally as a cooling, blood-cooling and toxicity-resolving topical for skin sores and as a nail/skin stain.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — the stain of protection and the coolness of the blessed:** Henna's doctrine is written on the skin. To be hennaed is to be **marked, adorned and protected** — the bridal *mehndi*, the festival stain, the dyed hand held up against the evil eye. It is the medicine of the **boundary made visible**: it colours the outermost layer (skin, nail, hair — the body's edge, its interface with the world) and there it cools, binds and guards. In Kenetica henna is read as *protective sealing* — an agent that works at the surface, drawing heat out of the periphery (palms, soles, scalp, the fevered head) and laying down a cooling, astringent, staining film that both treats and marks. Its association with the Prophet layers *blessing* onto *protection*: the stain is not merely cosmetic but auspicious, a coolness carrying grace. The colour that will not wash off for weeks is the sign of a boundary that holds.

### Active Components

- **Lawsone (2-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone):** the principal dye and bioactive — the orange-red naphthoquinone responsible for staining and much of the antimicrobial, antioxidant and skin activity.
- **Polyphenols and flavonoids:** gallic acid, catechin, ellagic acid, apigetrin, quercetin (identified in leaf extracts) — antioxidant fraction.
- **Coumarins, tannins, sterols;** aromatic constituents of the flower.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Henna is the **cooling protective topical** — a surface medicine for heat and skin. Kenetica roles: (1) a **cooling, astringent application for hot skin conditions** — burns, sores, ulcers, boils, inflamed scalp and cracked extremities; (2) a **hair and scalp conditioner and cooler**, and traditional cooling paste for the fevered head, palms and soles; (3) an **antimicrobial / antifungal (notably anti-dandruff) and antioxidant** agent supported by modern extract studies. Training-phase use: Cooling and Clearing (surface heat and toxicity), Protection (the protective, sealing, marking application). **Henna in Kenetica is a topical medicine; internal use is not indicated** (see contraindications — G6PD hazard).

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed:

- Joyroy et al. (2025, *PeerJ*) characterised *Lawsonia inermis* leaf extracts, identifying **lawsone, gallic acid, catechin, ellagic acid, apigetrin and quercetin** by HPLC, and found the polar (ethanol/methanol) extracts had strong **antioxidant, antibacterial and antifungal** activity plus cytotoxicity against several cancer cell lines in vitro. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.19170)
- Lozza et al. (2019, *Scientific Reports*) showed that **henna and its pigment lawsone activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR)** and modulate skin homeostasis: lawsone influenced keratinocyte differentiation and inflammation, and a lawsone-containing cream **ameliorated skin irritation in a human acute-dermatitis model** — while, notably, also *interfering with wound-healing/tissue regeneration* in regeneration models (a context-dependent, double-edged effect on skin). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-47350-x)

**Evidence grade:** *Preclinical-to-early-translational.* Antimicrobial, antifungal and antioxidant activities are consistently demonstrated in vitro; a mechanistic human/animal study (Lozza et al.) links lawsone to AhR-mediated skin effects with both beneficial (anti-irritant) and cautionary (impaired regeneration) outcomes. Robust randomised clinical trials for specific dermatological indications were not retrieved here; the deep cosmetic-medicinal tradition is strong, but clinical evidence remains graded *promising, not yet confirmed by RCT*.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM / regional (*zhi jia hua*):** Bitter, cooling; cools Blood-heat, resolves toxicity, dispels wind — topical for skin sores.
- **Ayurvedic:** Cooling *vīrya*, Pitta-pacifying; astringent; cooling to the periphery (palms, soles, scalp).
- **Galenic / Unani:** Cold & dry (≈2nd degree); astringent (*qābiḍ*), desiccant, cooling topical.

### Contraindications

- **⛔ G6PD deficiency — serious hazard.** Lawsone can trigger oxidative haemolysis; henna (especially applied to large areas or to infants/neonates) has caused **haemolytic crisis in G6PD-deficient individuals**. Do not apply extensively to G6PD-deficient people or young infants. This is the single most important safety point for henna.
- **"Black henna" is dangerous** — so-called black henna is adulterated with **para-phenylenediamine (PPD)**, a potent contact sensitiser causing severe allergic dermatitis, scarring and lifelong sensitisation. Only natural (orange-red staining) henna should be used; avoid black henna entirely.
- Patch-test before cosmetic use; contact allergy to natural henna, though less common, occurs.
- Internal use is not recommended; the AhR data and haemolytic potential make ingestion inadvisable.
- The Lozza et al. finding that lawsone can impair tissue regeneration counsels caution applying henna to open/healing wounds — its cooling astringency suits intact hot skin better than fresh wound beds.
