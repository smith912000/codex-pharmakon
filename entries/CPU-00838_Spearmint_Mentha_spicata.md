### Origin & Tradition

Spearmint is the "gentle" mint — the culinary and medicinal mint of the ancient Mediterranean world, distinct from its sharper, menthol-heavy relative peppermint (*Mentha × piperita*), which is a much later hybrid. Where peppermint's chemistry is dominated by menthol, spearmint's essential oil is dominated by **carvone**, giving it a softer, sweeter aroma and a milder, warming-carminative rather than intensely cooling character. It is one of the oldest cultivated aromatics of the Near East and Mediterranean, present in Greek, Roman, Hebrew and Arabic culinary and medical life.

**Dioscoridean / Graeco-Roman (DMM).** The classical writers treat garden mint (*hedyosmon*, "sweet-smelling") as warming, drying and aromatic: it settles the stomach, checks vomiting and hiccup, dispels flatulence, and stimulates appetite. Dioscorides notes its warming, appetising and stomachic virtues, and — a persistent classical theme — a reputation for suppressing conception and being contrary to generation, an early hint of the reproductive/hormonal action modern research would revisit. Mint strewn in rooms and rubbed on tables was a mark of hospitality.

**Unani / Tibb (CM).** As *naʿnāʿ*, spearmint is warm and dry, a premier stomachic and carminative. Unani physicians prescribe it to strengthen a cold, weak stomach, relieve nausea, vomiting and colic, dispel wind, sweeten the breath, and lift the spirits; it is a standard ingredient of digestive and cardiotonic compounds. Its warming-aromatic profile makes it a remedy for "cold" digestive weakness.

**Ayurveda / South Asian (SS).** *Pudina* is a widely used domestic and Ayurvedic aromatic — pungent, warming, *deepana*/*pachana* (kindling digestion and clearing *ama*), carminative, and anti-nauseant. It is taken for indigestion, colic, nausea, and as a fresh cooling-yet-digestive chutney; folk use extends to freshening breath and easing headache.

**Chinese Materia Medica (TCM).** The Chinese pharmacopoeia's principal mint is *bò he* (*Mentha haplocalyx/canadensis*), acrid, cool and aromatic, entering Lung and Liver — it releases the exterior, clears heat from the head and eyes, soothes the throat, and courses constrained Liver qi. Spearmint sits in this aromatic-surface-releasing family and shares its qi-moving, tension-easing reputation.

**Folk & Magico-Traditional.** Mint is a plant of hospitality, protection, and purification across cultures — strewn on floors, hung to freshen and ward, used in cleansing washes. In the Greek myth of Minthe, the nymph is crushed and transformed into the sweet herb, tying spearmint to themes of the crushed-yet-fragrant, of sweetness released through pressure.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer — Sweetness Released Through Pressure:**
Spearmint's doctrinal signature is *aromatic release under pressure* — the plant whose fragrance appears only when the leaf is crushed. The Minthe myth encodes it exactly: the nymph trodden underfoot becomes the herb whose scent rises the more it is bruised. In Kenetica this is the teaching of the Digestive-clearing and Cooling-aromatic phases — that stagnation (of food, of qi, of feeling) is moved not by force but by *aromatic circulation*, a fragrance that penetrates and disperses what is stuck. Spearmint also carries, quietly and across millennia, an "anti-generative" reputation the ancients noted and modern endocrinology has now partly confirmed: it gently lowers androgenic drive. It is therefore the volume's emblem of **the sweetening, dispersing, de-escalating principle** — the medicine that resolves tension by circulation and softening of drive rather than by suppression. **What is stuck moves when it is warmed, sweetened, and gently pressed — not when it is forced.**

### Active Components

- **Essential oil dominated by (R)-(−)-carvone** (the defining aroma), with limonene, dihydrocarvone, and cineole — the carminative, antispasmodic, antimicrobial principle. (Contrast: peppermint is menthol-dominant.)
- **Rosmarinic acid** and other **phenolic acids/flavonoids** — antioxidant and anti-inflammatory; implicated in the antiandrogenic effect.
- **Tannins** — mild astringency.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Spearmint serves two roles in Kenetica. First, as a **warming-aromatic carminative and stomachic** for the Digestive-clearing phase: bloating, wind, colic, nausea, sluggish "cold" digestion, and the tension of a constrained gut — a gentler, sweeter alternative to menthol-heavy peppermint, better tolerated by sensitive and reflux-prone individuals.

Second, and distinctively, as a **mild anti-androgenic / hormonal-balancing tea** in the Hormonal-balancing phase — the modern, trial-supported use for lowering free testosterone in the context of androgen-excess presentations such as PCOS-associated hirsutism. This is the rare traditional "anti-generative" note that has crossed into evidence.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, Grant (2010, *Phytotherapy Research*) conducted a 30-day, two-centre randomised controlled trial of spearmint tea (twice daily) versus placebo herbal tea in 42 women with hirsutism and polycystic ovary syndrome. Free and total testosterone fell significantly in the spearmint group, LH and FSH rose, and patients' *self-reported* hirsutism (modified DQLI) improved significantly — though the objective Ferriman–Gallwey score did not change significantly over the short 30-day window, which the author attributes to the long biological lag of hair-follicle turnover. The study confirms a genuine **anti-androgen endocrine effect** while being honest that measurable cosmetic change needs longer treatment. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.2900)

**Evidence grade:** Moderate for the anti-androgen/hormonal effect — a small but properly randomised, placebo-controlled human trial with a clear biochemical endpoint, limited by small size, short duration, and lack of change in the objective clinical score. Carminative/digestive use rests on long traditional use plus general antispasmodic pharmacology of carvone-rich mint oils (strong traditional, modest formal-trial support). Claims graded and hedged accordingly.

### Cultivation Register

**Egyptian (EP).** Spearmint is tagged but the Egyptian corpus is indication-organised, and pinning a specific mint to a pharaonic prescription is a modern hypothesis, not a secure reading. No cultivation framing is attested, and no *heka* operation binds this herb to a named intervention in the sources retrieved. **(c) absent.**

**Assyrian (AT).** The blocking warning on substance identification and tablet reference is in force. Nothing in the retrieved cuneiform material assigns spearmint a role — neither an agency it opposes nor a status touching the person's vitality. **(c) absent.**

**Greek / Dioscoridean (DMM).** Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is to be invented for garden mint. What he offers a cultivator is a *discipline of attention*: recognising *hedyosmon* "the sweet-smelling" by its fragrance, judging warming from cooling by aroma and taste. He also *records* the old reputation that mint is "contrary to generation" — but recording is not endorsing, and it is a note on effect, not a cultivation regimen. **(b) framed — a therapeutic and dietetic status, no cultivation status.**

**Unani / Avicennan (CM).** As *naʿnāʿ* spearmint is a warm-and-dry stomachic that strengthens a cold, weak stomach and lifts the spirits, placing it within the economy of the *ruh* and the innate heat. But this is a therapeutic warming action; no regimen for conserving the innate heat and radical moisture is prescribed for this herb specifically, and its spirit-lifting reputation must not be inflated into a *mufarriḥ* constituent claim. **(b) framed.**

**Chinese (TCM).** The allied mint *bò he* is acrid, cool, and aromatic — it releases the exterior and courses constrained Liver qi. Categorically this is a *dispersing, surface-releasing* drug, close to the opposite of a conservational *yang sheng* tonic, and no *san pin* upper grade is attested for it. Spearmint sits in that dispersing family. Its status is therapeutic; a cultivation grade is not attested and would run against the herb's own dispersing character. **(b) framed, dropping to (c) for cultivation status.**

**Ayurvedic (SS).** *Pudina* is a *deepana*/*pachana* carminative — kindling digestion and clearing *ama*. This is therapeutic. No *rasayana* or *vajikarana* status is attested; indeed the herb's recorded anti-generative tendency runs contrary to the *vajikarana* (generative) aim. **(b) framed, dropping to (c) for cultivation status.**

**Practitioner's note.** Spearmint is a dispersing, de-escalating herb, not a tonic in any tradition's cultivation sense. A cultivator uses it for a defined job — moving a stuck, cold, windy digestion — or for the deliberate, trial-supported twice-daily antiandrogen tea. Its quiet through-line across the traditions is the *anti-generative* note, which is the categorical opposite of a generative or life-hoarding cultivation aim: this is a herb of softening and release, taken for a purpose and then set down, never a longevity drug.

### Contraindications

- **Anti-androgen action is a two-edged effect:** helpful in androgen excess, but sustained high intake is theoretically inappropriate where androgen levels must be preserved (e.g. some fertility contexts, male hormonal concerns); the ancients' "anti-generative" reputation is consistent with this. `[USER: verify]` clinical significance of casual culinary intake — trial effects used deliberate twice-daily tea.
- Reflux/GERD: mints can relax the lower oesophageal sphincter and worsen heartburn in some (less so than peppermint, but possible).
- Pregnancy/lactation: classical sources flag mint as "contrary to generation"; avoid concentrated/therapeutic doses in pregnancy — `[USER: verify]`. Culinary amounts customary.
- Essential oil is concentrated; not for undiluted ingestion, and keep away from infants' faces (carvone/menthol-type oils).
