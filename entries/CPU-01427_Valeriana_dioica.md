# Marsh Valerian / Valeriana dioica — Nardos oreine, "mountain nardus" (Dioscorides 1-8); Oinos agrias nardou, "wine of wild nard" (Dioscorides 5-69)

## *Valeriana dioica* (the Dioscorides translators' suggested identification for 1-8 and 5-69; not Dioscorides' own) | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry: named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** This row was added on 2026-10-09 by the plant-gap run (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP): the binomial *Valeriana dioica* is printed in two chapters of the English *De Materia Medica* and in no other text read for this entry. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed on a line headed SUGGESTED. It is not Dioscorides' identification, and the chapters themselves never name a valerian. The identity is therefore doubtful at the species level. [USER: verify]

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`.

*1-8 NARDOS OREINE* (heading at line 3622; SUGGESTED line 3623, "Valeriana dioica — Marsh Valerian"; text at line 3624). The whole chapter reads: "Mountain nardus (which is called thylacitis and nevis by some) grows in Cilicia and Syria. It has stalks and leaves like eryngium but smaller, yet not sharp and prickly. The two or more roots that lie underneath are black and fragrant like asphodelus, but thinner and a great deal smaller. It does not have a stalk, fruit or flower for any long time. The root is good for the same things as the Celtic nardus [1-7]." The chapter gives a habitat, a short description (eryngium-like leaves, two or more black, fragrant roots), two alternative names, and a single use by reference: the root is good for what the Celtic nardus is good for.

*1-7 NARDOS KELTIKE* (heading at line 3606; the passage on use ends at line 3621, directly above 1-8), the chapter to which 1-8 refers, carries a different translator's suggestion ("Valeriana celticus, Nardus celticus — Celtic Spikenard, Celtic Valerian") and so is not this row. What it says of use is: "It is good for the same things as the Syrian but it is more diuretic and better for stomach disorders. Taken as a drink with a decoction of wormwood [3-26] it helps inflammation of the liver, jaundice, and gas-filled intestines. In the same way it is good for the spleen, bladder and kidneys, as well as mixed with wine for bites of venomous creatures. It is used in warm compresses, liquid medicines and warming ointments." Because 1-8 says only that the root does the same as 1-7, this is the use that the text of 1-8 passes on to the mountain nardus. It is reported here as the chapter's own cross-reference and is not a use stated for 1-8 directly.

*5-69 OINOS AGRIAS NARDOU* (heading at line 10143; SUGGESTED line 10144, "Valeriana dioica — Marsh Valerian — Wine of Wild Nard"; text at line 10145), in Book Five among the medicinal wines. The whole chapter reads: "Put eight ounces of fresh root of wild nard (pounded into small pieces and sifted) into a four and a half litres of must, and leave it alone two months. It is effective for liver ailments, and frequent painful urination, as well as for an inflated stomach, and stomach disorders." The wording "wild nard" is the translators' rendering of the Greek heading, and the chapter does not say it is the same plant as 1-8. Whether 5-69 and 1-8 are one plant is the translators' judgement, as they assign the same binomial to both. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

Plants of the genus *Valeriana* are generally known to be aromatic herbs whose roots contain volatile oils and iridoid compounds (valepotriates). Whether that holds for the plant Dioscorides described was not established by anything read, and no analysis of *V. dioica* itself was opened. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The sources record a fragrant root used for liver, urinary and stomach complaints, and as an ingredient of a medicinal wine. The identification rests on a translators' suggestion, the chapter 1-8 is a few lines long, and the Codex gives no protocol for it. It is not a recommended practice.

### Preparation & Dosing

Dioscorides gives one preparation under this name: the wine of 5-69, in which "eight ounces of fresh root of wild nard" are pounded, sifted and put in "four and a half litres of must" for two months (line 10145). No dose of the finished wine is given in the lines read. The root of 1-8 is given no preparation. For the related Celtic nardus (1-7) the text prints decoction, drink with wine, warm compresses and ointments, but that is not stated for this plant. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Scientific Correlation

Valerian roots are generally known in modern herbal practice as a mild sedative preparation, but that refers to the officinal valerian and not to the plant described in the two Dioscorides chapters, and the uses recorded there (liver, urinary and stomach complaints) are different. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** no warming, cooling, drying or other quality is printed for 1-8 or 5-69. For 1-8 the root is "fragrant", and that is a sensory note and not a classification.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system and the chapters assign this plant none. What the entry offers a cultivator is a discipline of attention: where the plant grows (Cilicia and Syria), how to know it (eryngium-like leaves that are not prickly, two or more black fragrant roots, a stalk, fruit and flower that do not last), and how a wine of the fresh root is made (5-69). It records no use for the person's vital constitution. The identification with *V. dioica* is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the single text that names this plant gives a cultivator a practice with it. It is a fragrant root used as the Celtic nard was used, and the ingredient of a medicinal wine. The abstentive answer is the honest one, and any modern valerian use belongs to the officinal plant and not to what is attested here.

### Contraindications

The plant described is not securely identified, so no safety statement can rest on the Dioscorides text. Valerian preparations are generally known to cause drowsiness, and a medicinal wine contains alcohol, which is unsuitable in pregnancy, with sedatives, or when driving. Nothing above is advice. Seek a clinician before using any valerian product, particularly during pregnancy or lactation.
