# Snowcock droppings — rGyud-bzhi, "droppings of Gong.mo" (gong.mo), Tibetan snowcock
## *The droppings of the Himalayan snowcock; Clark prints "droppings of Gong.mo: Tetragallus himalayensis" (genus spelling as printed), Men-Tsee-Khang prints "the Tibetan snow cock"* | *(Volume III — Animal)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** The droppings of the snowcock are entered in the Explanatory Tantra, chapter 20, the chapter on the powers of medicines, in Clark 1995 (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`). They fall in the section "(8) Medicines of Animal Origin" (line 1147), whose thirteen headings are "horn, bone, meat, blood, bile, fat, brain, skin, nails, hair, urine, droppings and whole body" (line 1148). Under "Droppings" the entry, at line 1199, reads: "Wolf stool and droppings of Gong.mo: Tetragallus himalayensis: [all three] reduce swellings." Clark names two items here (wolf stool and the droppings of Gong.mo) but his bracket says "all three", so the third item is not named in the line read; the dog stool of the preceding sentence is one possibility, and that is an inference, not a reading. [USER: verify] The binomial is printed "Tetragallus himalayensis", which is the spelling in the file for the Himalayan snowcock (*Tetraogallus himalayensis*); it is quoted as printed. The same name appears in Clark's meat classification, where "white grouse (Tetragallus himalayensis)" stands first among the "creatures which dig with their claws" (line 672).

The Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 translation (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, line 3568) gives the item as: "The feces of dogs, wolves and the Tibetan snow cock reduce swelling." Men-Tsee-Khang thus names three items (dogs, wolves, the snowcock), which fits Clark's "all three" and suggests the dog, but the Tibetan wording is not in the files read. The two translations agree on the substance and the action: snowcock droppings reduce swelling. Men-Tsee-Khang's glossary of birds (lines 6847-6887) lists "Ghongmo" with the English "Tibetan Snowcock" and the scientific name "Tetraogallus himalayensis"; the glossary is an OCR of columns, and the pairing of the name, the English and the binomial rests on their order in the columns. [USER: verify] The Men-Tsee-Khang list of birds that dig with their claws also names the "Tibetan snowcock" (line 2899).

No preparation, no dose and no route is printed for these droppings in either translation. They are one name in a list of animal droppings, among them those of the vulture, pig, human, horse, rabbit, dog, wolf, bird, rat and pigeon (Clark line 1199; Men-Tsee-Khang line 3568).

### Active Components

Bird droppings are a mix of faecal waste and uric acid, with whatever the bird ate (for an alpine bird, plant material and some insects). Nothing in the lines read names an active principle, and none specific to this entry is claimed.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The sourced content is a single action, to reduce swellings. The Codex gives no protocol. A swelling needs clinical assessment, and wild-bird droppings carry infection risk (see Contraindications). The Tantra does not say whether the droppings are applied to the skin or taken by mouth, and no use is recommended here.

### Preparation & Dosing

No preparation, route or dose is given in the sources retrieved: Clark and Men-Tsee-Khang state the action only. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Wild-bird droppings can carry bacteria such as *Salmonella* and *Campylobacter* and fungi associated with accumulated droppings, and they are a recognised route of infection when handled or inhaled as dust. Droppings have no recognised effect on swellings. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** None is printed for the droppings. Clark prints one action ("reduce swellings") and no taste, potency or humoral action for them. The general statement on meats (Clark line 673: meats are "sweet tasting and have a sweet post-digestive taste") concerns flesh and is not applied to droppings. No Chinese classification applies: the entry is not tagged SBJ.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan / rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(c) Absent.* Neither translation gives snowcock droppings any cultivation framing; the entry is one item in a list of droppings with a single action. The frame around it is the tradition's own: the project's cultivation spec records that the three *nyes pa* (*rlung*, *mkhris pa*, *bad kan*) are derived from the three poisons and that medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root. That point comes from the spec and not from any line about this substance. No source read attaches snowcock droppings to *bcud len* (essence extraction), and there is no formal Tibetan category of substances for subtle-body practice. Clark's remark (line 57) that "only very few animal products are used" nowadays is a note on usage, not on status.

**Practitioner's note.** A cultivator does nothing with this. It is a list entry for wild-bird droppings, with one action and no preparation, and handling them carries infection risk with no supported benefit. The honest use of the entry is as a record of what the Tantra says.

### Contraindications

- **Infection:** wild-bird droppings can carry bacteria and fungal spores; do not handle, ingest or inhale dried droppings.
- **Swellings:** any new or persistent swelling needs clinical assessment; do not rely on this line.
- **Legal and conservation status:** wild birds and their collection are regulated in many countries and the identification rests on the translators' binomials. [USER: verify]
- **Ethics:** no animal needs to be harmed for droppings, but disturbing wild birds at nest and roost sites for a substance with no supported benefit is hard to justify.
