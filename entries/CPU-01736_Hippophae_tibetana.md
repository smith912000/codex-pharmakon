# Hippophae tibetana — Tarbu (star bu), "Seabuckthom" (rGyud-bzhi)

## *Hippophae tibetana, as the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints it (with the English name "Seabuckthom", an OCR form of "Seabuckthorn"); Clark 1995 prints Hippophae rhamnoides for the same Tibetan name star.bu* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); the binomial rests on one glossary line of one text, and the Tibetan name is paired to it by position.** This entry rests on one tradition's texts, in two modern editions. Only Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 prints *Hippophae tibetana* (line 5948, the row's locus). A search of Clark 1995 for "tibetana" found nothing; Clark prints *Hippophae rhamnoides* for *star.bu*. The Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. No other text read for this entry names this species: a search of the Dioscorides, Shennong (Yang 1998), Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta texts on disk for the genus name *Hippophae* found only Dioscorides chapters on *hippophaes* and *hippophaeston* (lines 9490 to 9506, translator's gloss *Hippophae rhamnoides*, Sea Buckthorn), a different species not attributed to this row. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (row 371) the entry is recorded at the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary line 5948 with the note "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". The row has one name only, so there is no first-or-later question for the key.

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (lines 5858 to 5998).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "T" block (page 314 printed at line 6000). The glossary prints its columns in separate runs. Twenty-four phonetic names are printed at lines 5860 to 5924 ("Tab seng", "Tak sha", "Tamig", "Tangkun", "Tangzil", "Tapak", then "Tarbu" at line 5878, and on to "Tukar"), with twenty-four transliterations at lines 5862 to 5926, the seventh being "star bu" (line 5881). The scientific names at lines 5928 to 5995 are twenty-four in all. Counted in order, the seventh is "Hippophae tibetana" (line 5948), and the seventh English name is "Seabuckthom" (line 5950). The count is consistent at the points where Clark's materia medica allows a check: "Thalka dorje" (eighth) stands against *Cassia tora* and "Sickle pod" (Clark, *thal.ka.rdo.rje*, line 898, *Cassia tora*) and "Tharam" (tenth) against *Plantago depressa* (Clark, *tha.ram*, line 1123). The pairing is by position, not printed in one row. [USER: verify] It is also the pairing made in I_907 (*Cassia tora*, Foetid Cassia) for the same neighbouring names. Clark's materia medica gives *star.bu* as "Sea Buckthorn: Hippophae rhamnoides", so the two Tibetan texts agree on the Tibetan name and the genus and differ on the species; the Codex does not choose.

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* "Tarbu" appears in four places. In chapter 19, line 3205 (page 186), the list of medicines with a sour taste reads "Sedu, larbu, Seyab, Kyurura, Gyashug, Dhatig, curd, buttermilk, chhang, yeast and other similar substances", where "larbu" is read here as an OCR form of "Tarbu", on the strength of the index entry "Tarbu 186" and the page. In chapter 20, lines 3393 to 3394 (page 202): "Tarbu is an expectorant, dilutes blood and controls production of phlegm." In chapter 21, line 3611 (page 221): "Chugang, Shingngar, Gundrum, Tarbu, Ruta, Gadhur, Atong, Solo karpo and Sugdra are the group of medicines that cure disorders of the lungs." Line 3614: "Seyab, Manu, Wosu, Tarbu, Upel, Sedu, Gakya and Kyurura are the group of medicines that cure hot disorder associated with baekan." The index lists "Tarbu 186,202,221" (line 7508).

*Clark 1995, the same Tibetan name (line 907).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20. The entry reads:

"star.bu: Sea Buckthorn: Hippophae rhamnoides: [has fourteen names. It is an extremely coarse, thorny bush with fruit similar to a baby bird. It has a sour taste, pierces the tongue (is very sharp), and cures lung disorders. This plant is also called ghe.wa.shing, and is of white and black types. The white type has a small, pea-sized, yellow fruit, which is extremely sour. Prepared in the form of a concentrate] it is expectorant, dissolves blood clots, disperses phlegm, [cures fevers and is beneficial in colic]."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. Inside the brackets (commentary layer) are the number of names, the description, the sour taste, the lung indication, the types and the words "cures fevers and is beneficial in colic". Outside them (root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the English and Latin names, which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and "it is expectorant, dissolves blood clots, disperses phlegm". Clark's chapter 19 puts "sea buckthorn (Hippophae rhamnoides)" in the group having sour taste (line 766). Chapter 21 puts "Hippophae rhamnoides" in the category of medicines for lung disorders (line 1213) and "Sea Buckthorn" in the category of phlegm-fever combinations (line 1215). The two texts agree that the Tibetan name is a sour, expectorant drug for lung disorders. Clark's "dissolves blood clots" and Men-Tsee-Khang's "dilutes blood" are the two translations' wordings, and the Codex does not treat them as one term.

**Overlap.** I_580 (Sea Buckthorn, *Hippophae rhamnoides*, tagged CM, TCM, SS and FMT) is the entry on the other binomial of this Tibetan name and covers *star.bu*. This entry covers the Men-Tsee-Khang binomial and is not merged with it. I_907 (*Cassia tora*, Foetid Cassia) quotes the same glossary lines for the neighbouring name "Thalka dorje" and the same positional count. I_1251 (*Chaenomeles speciosa*) and I_1252 (*Chaenomeles tibetica*) name "Tarbu" or *Hippophae* in the sour-taste lists. No entry on *Hippophae tibetana* as the subject was found. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Hippophae* is a genus of the oleaster family (Elaeagnaceae) whose fruits are sour, and that the fruits of the genus contain organic acids and vitamin C, among other compounds. Clark's commentary records a "sour taste" and fruit "similar to a baby bird" for the plant it describes. Which species or part the Men-Tsee-Khang name means is not said.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "is expectorant, dissolves blood clots, disperses phlegm" (Clark, line 907) and that "is an expectorant, dilutes blood and controls production of phlegm" (Men-Tsee-Khang, lines 3393 to 3394), and that stands in the groups of medicines for lung disorders and for hot disorder associated with baekan. These are the texts' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose, and does not advise on blood-thinning or lung disease.

### Preparation & Dosing

Clark's commentary says the fruit is "Prepared in the form of a concentrate" (line 907) and gives no method, quantity, frequency or duration. Men-Tsee-Khang prints none. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's commentary layer (line 907) prints "a sour taste" and "pierces the tongue (is very sharp)", for *star.bu*; Clark's chapter 19 puts it in the sour-taste group (line 766). Men-Tsee-Khang puts "Tarbu" (printed "larbu") in the sour-taste group (line 3205). No power or post-digestive taste is printed in the lines read.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Men-Tsee-Khang text gives this drug by what it does: it is an expectorant, dilutes blood and controls production of phlegm, and it stands in the groups for lung disorders and for hot disorder associated with baekan. Clark's entry for the same Tibetan name says the same in kind under another binomial. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this name places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the sentences cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only, and it attaches to the Tibetan name, which the Men-Tsee-Khang glossary pairs with this binomial by position only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a sour, expectorant fruit drug in the Tibetan texts, with no method of preparation and no dose. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Lung disease and any drug said to act on the blood need medical assessment, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry.
