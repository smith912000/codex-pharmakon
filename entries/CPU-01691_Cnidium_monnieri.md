# Cnidium monnieri — la.la.phud, Lalaphue (rGyud-bzhi); also She Chuang Zi in Yang's Shennong text (not yet on the tag line)

## *Cnidium monnieri, as the binomial printed in the scientific-name run of the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary against "Lalaphue", and as the second of two binomials Clark 1995 prints for la.la.phud (after Trachyspermum ammi); a Shennong entry on disk prints "Fructus Cnidii Monnieri" for She Chuang Zi* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

### Origin & Tradition

**Named by the Tibetan texts (FMT) under this tag line; the Shennong text on disk also names the binomial, so this entry does not rest on one text.** The spine row (CPU-01691) lists rGyud-bzhi only and was added as single-text. The reading below found a second text, recorded here as a finding and not as a tag: `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt` prints *Fructus Cnidii Monnieri* at line 2166 (see the Shennong paragraph below). The tag line carries FMT only, so the Cultivation Register below has an FMT paragraph and no SBJ paragraph; whether SBJ is added to the tag line is for the parent to decide. Both Tibetan texts are in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. The Tibetan name is a drug name and not a botanical one. Clark's preface (line 83) says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance", and here the line prints two. The extraction row for the glossary reads "MTK glossary scientific-name column; Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)" (`Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv`, line 307), and a second row keys Clark's line (same file, line 53) to the first binomial, *Trachyspermum ammi*. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. The row was added from the printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (lines 5435 to 5454).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients, the "L" block. The phonetic names are "Lalaphue Langthangtse / Lishi / Looh dhue dorje Lugchhung Lugmur Lugnyal Lugru mukpo Lugru serpo" (lines 5435 to 5437), the Tibetan transliterations begin "la la phud lang thang rtse / li shi / klu bdud rdo ije ..." (lines 5439 to 5441), and the scientific names begin "Cnidium monnieri" (line 5445), "Hyoscyamus niger L. Syzygium aromaticum Codonopsis thalictrifolia" (line 5446) and so on to line 5448. The English column opens with "Celery" (line 5450). Read by position, nine phonetic names pair with nine scientific names, and "Lalaphue" is first in both runs. The pairing is positional and was not printed in a single row; the English column has seven names against nine, so it cannot be matched place by place beyond its order. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text.* The name is printed as "Lalapue" in chapter 20 and "Lalaphue" in chapter 21. Line 3391 (chapter 20, ending at line 3583) reads: "Lalapue cures stomach problems and cold disorders." Lines 3621 to 3627 (chapter 21, ending at line 3689) list "Lalaphue" among the medicines that "cure cold disorders associated with baekan" ("Sedu, Phowari, Pipiling, Men ga, Tsitaka, Kakola, Sugmel, Shing tsha, Jamdre, Jitanga, Dali, Zira nagpo, Lalaphue, E mong, Subka, Che tsha, Tsabru tsha, Gya tsha, Gyam tsha, Ra tsha and Thai tsha are the group of medicines that cure cold disorders associated with baekan"). The index (line 7415) lists "Lalaphue 221". The running text gives no Latin name. That these are the glossary's "Lalaphue" rests on the spelling. [USER: verify]

*Clark 1995, the same Tibetan name (line 896).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20. The text file prints "(5) Resins" at line 879 and "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" at line 997, and no heading for a section (6); this entry stands between the two. The entry reads:

"la.la.phud: Trachyspermum amni sprague: Cnidium monnieri L.: Skt. jawani: [has three names. It has green leaves and stalk and is of three types, according to color: flowers and seeds are white, black or yellow. The seed resembles fennel and the strongest smelling seed indicates the best quality. An inferior type is found in Tibet. All types] cure stomach disorders and cold diseases."

Clark's preface (line 82) says: "Interpolations in square brackets [ ] are taken from the Blue Aquamarine commentary and Spreading Tree commentary". Inside the brackets (the commentary layer) are the three names, the three colour types, the fennel-like seed, the note on quality and the words "All types". Outside the brackets (the root-text layer, as far as Clark's brackets mark it) are the Tibetan name, the two Latin names and the Sanskrit "jawani", which are Clark's identifications and not the root text's, and the clause "cure stomach disorders and cold diseases". Clark's lead binomial is *Trachyspermum ammi*, so *Cnidium monnieri* is the second identification of two. The two Tibetan texts agree on the action (stomach, cold) and the glossary agrees on *Cnidium monnieri*.

**Chinese — Shennong Bencao Jing, a finding outside the tag line (SBJ).** `Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`, PDF page 56: the page marker `[[p56]]` is at line 2150 and the heading "Herbs: Superior Class" is printed under it at line 2151. The entry (lines 2166 to 2171) reads: "She Chuang Zi (Fmctus Cnidii Monnieri) is bitter and balanced. It mainly treats genital swelling and pain in females, and impotence and [genital] damp itch in males. It eliminates impediment qi, disinhibits the joints, and [treats] madness, epilepsy, and malign sores. Protracted taking may make the body light. Its other name is She Su (Snake Millet). Yet another name is She Mi (Snake Rice). It grows in rivers and valleys." The OCR prints "Fmctus" for Fructus. The extraction lists the row (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv`, line 88) and the Codex's list prints "Cnidium Fruit (蛇床子 Shé Chuáng Zǐ)" (`_sources/shennong.csv`, line 69); the Codex's `MASTER_INDEX.csv` (lines 704 and 706, ids 703 and 705) also lists it as Shennong-only. Whether the Tibetan *la.la.phud* and the Shennong *She Chuang Zi* are the same drug is not said by any line read, and the Codex does not equate them. This is a candidate tag addition (SBJ) and is not applied here.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Cnidium monnieri* is a plant of the carrot family (Apiaceae) whose fruits are the part generally used in the Chinese materia medica, and that the fruit is generally known to contain coumarins. Nothing here says what the Tibetan drug contained, because its Latin name is not settled between *Trachyspermum* and *Cnidium*. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a fennel-like seed drug that cures "stomach disorders and cold diseases" (Clark, line 896) or "stomach problems and cold disorders" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3391), and is grouped among the medicines for cold disorders associated with baekan (lines 3621 to 3627). The Shennong entry, which is outside the tag line, records a bitter and balanced drug for genital complaints, impediment qi, madness, epilepsy and malign sores. These are the texts' claims and not Codex protocols. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The Tibetan sources read give no preparation for this drug; Clark's commentary names the seed (and the quality of its smell) as the notable part, and no quantity, frequency or duration is given. The Shennong entry gives none either. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica classes this drug by what it cures, stomach disorders and cold diseases, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. No line read for this drug places it in *bcud len* (essence extraction) or any practice: the passages cited above were read in full and none mentions one. Its framing is therapeutic only. The Origin section records a Shennong entry for the same binomial; because the tag line carries FMT only, no Chinese paragraph is written here and no cultivation status is claimed from that entry.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a stomach and cold drug in the Tibetan texts, its Latin name is not settled in them, and a Chinese entry on disk for the same binomial is outside this entry's tag line. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. The identity is uncertain, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. Stomach disorders that persist, and the genital complaints, madness or epilepsy that the Shennong entry names, need medical care. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
