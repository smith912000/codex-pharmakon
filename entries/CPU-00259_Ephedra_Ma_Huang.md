**Tradition Tags:** SBJ, CM, FMT, AT
**Energetic Classification:** ◉ Qi Primary — Lung Qi Dispersal, Wei Qi Activation, Surface-Opening
**Training Phase:** `[PRIME]`

### Origin & Tradition

Ephedra occupies a position unlike any other herb in the entire Codex Pharmakon Universalis. It is simultaneously the oldest documented stimulant in the Chinese pharmacopoeia, one of the most pharmacologically potent plants in the entire materia medica of any tradition, the source of the first alkaloid isolate to define a whole class of pharmaceutical agents (the sympathomimetics), a substance with deep roots in Tibetan high-altitude medicine, a WADA-regulated substance in competitive sport, and an herb whose inadvertent misuse has contributed more to the regulatory restriction of traditional plant medicine globally than perhaps any other single plant. To understand Ephedra fully — as the Codex demands — is to understand the intersection of ancient empiricism, alkaloid pharmacology, pharmaceutical history, regulatory politics, and the permanent tension between whole-herb medicine and isolated-compound extraction.

**The SBJ designation:** The *Shennong Bencao Jing* (神農本草經, SBJ) is the foundational text of Chinese materia medica, estimated to codify oral tradition dating to 2700–2000 BCE though compiled in its surviving written form approximately 25–200 CE. Má Huáng (麻黃) appears in the Superior Class (*Shang Pin*, 上品) — the highest of three tiers, reserved for substances considered non-toxic, life-prolonging, and safe for extended use — which is already an important pharmacological signal that must be reconciled with the herb's stimulant potency. The SBJ states of Má Huáng: "It governs cough and rebellious Qi (上氣), cold fever (中風傷寒), heat in the bones, feeling of expulsion (發表), shan disorder (疝), and nodules (積聚)." In classical Chinese medical nomenclature, this formulation encodes a remarkable accuracy: "cough and rebellious Qi" describes bronchospasm and airway obstruction; "cold fever" describes the febrile presentation of exterior cold constrain (the Wind-Cold attack pattern); "heat in the bones" suggests the aching myalgia of acute viral illness; "feeling of expulsion" literally describes the surface-opening, diaphoretic, and expulsive action that Ephedra produces through its sympathomimetic activation of sweat glands, smooth muscle, and skeletal muscle vasculature; and "shan and nodules" — less immediately obvious — may reflect observations of ephedrine's effects on smooth muscle contraction and its historical application to urological and gastrointestinal obstruction patterns.

The character 麻 (*má*) carries multiple meanings: hemp, numbness, and pockmarks — all associated with the texture of Ephedra's fibrous straw-like stems. 黃 (*huáng*) simply means yellow. The plant is a leafless, green-jointed, photosynthesizing shrub whose stem is the primary medicinal part — reflecting the ancient Chinese observation that the pharmacological activity resides specifically in the aerial stem rather than the root, a distinction validated by modern alkaloid distribution analysis: the stem contains 0.5–2.5% total alkaloids (primarily ephedrine and pseudoephedrine), while the root (Má Huáng Gēn, 麻黃根) contains markedly lower alkaloid content but substantially higher tannin and alkaloid concentrations of a different class — specifically the ephedradines (macroline-type alkaloids) and ferulic acid, which produce opposite pharmacological effects.

**The paradox of root versus stem:** This is one of the most instructive pharmacological contrasts in the entire Chinese materia medica, and it deserves extended treatment. Má Huáng (the stem) disperses the surface, opens the pores, induces sweating, and expels cold — it is the quintessential diaphoretic, the premier herb for exterior cold constraining the surface. Má Huáng Gēn (the root) is classified as an astringent that *stops* sweating — it is used specifically for spontaneous sweating (自汗, zì hàn) and night sweating (盜汗, dào hàn) that represent pathological leakage of Wei Qi or Yin. Two parts of the same plant, operating by entirely different mechanisms, producing opposite clinical outcomes. The modern pharmacological explanation: Má Huáng Gēn contains ephedradine A, B, C, and D (tyramine-type alkaloids) which act as inhibitors of sweat gland activity through a mechanism involving adrenal cortex stimulation distinct from ephedrine's pathway, as well as condensed tannins that directly constrict secretory tissue. The traditional warning is explicit and unambiguous — *never confuse the stem and root clinically* — one opens the surface and the other closes it.

**Ancient Near Eastern parallels:** The ancient documentation of similar wind-cold opening herbs in neighboring traditions deserves attention for what it reveals about the empirical universality of identifying plants with surface-opening respiratory action. The Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE, Egypt) documents preparations using *ym* (identified by some scholars as Ephedra-like plants from North African trade routes) combined with honey for cough and respiratory obstruction — preparations that are pharmacologically consistent with ephedrine-containing plant sources. Assyrian medical tablets from the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh (c. 668–627 BCE) document an herb translated as *amurdinnu* used for respiratory obstruction, fever-chills, and surface-opening, and several Assyriologists have proposed this corresponds to an ephedrine-containing plant consistent with *Ephedra* species that grew in the Zagros mountain regions of ancient Mesopotamia. While the identification cannot be made with certainty, the pharmacological convergence — respiratory obstruction, fever-chills, surface-opening — across three unconnected ancient civilizations suggests that the ephedra plant's distinctive respiratory-opening action was independently discovered wherever the plant grew within human medical tradition.

**Classical Arabic medicine (CM):** The Arab physicians of the Islamic Golden Age encountered Ephedra through both the Persian medical tradition (which had direct contact with Chinese medical practice) and the indigenous *Ephedra* species (*E. alata*, *E. nebrodensis*) native to the Arabian Peninsula and North Africa. Ibn Sina's *Canon of Medicine* (1025 CE) describes *qanab al-jabal* (mountain hemp — a plausible Arabic designation for Ephedra, given the similarity in plant morphology to hemp) as a heating, drying herb that opens the chest, resolves cough, and disperses cold obstruction. The Al-Biruni's *Kitab al-Saydana fi al-Tibb* (Book of Pharmacy in Medicine, c. 1050 CE) contains a more specific description of a plant producing "vigour and expulsion of cold vapour from the lungs" that scholars have correlated with Ephedra. The entire Arabic tradition of *mufattih* (opener) herbs — medicines that open blocked orifices and channels — represents a functional parallel to the TCM concept of *xuān fèi* (宣肺, diffusing the Lung) that is precisely what Ephedra performs.

**Tibetan medicine (FMT):** High-altitude populations face a specific medical challenge that gives Ephedra a unique role in the Tibetan pharmacopoeia: acute mountain sickness (AMS), characterized by hypoxia-induced respiratory compromise, pulmonary edema risk, altered consciousness, and the physiological stress of hypobaric environments. The Tibetan name *mtshe ba* (sometimes rendered *bse ba*) refers to Ephedra species used in the FMT tradition, and the Gyushi (rGyud bzhi — the four medical tantras, compiled in the 12th century CE) includes it in formulas for wind-disease (rLung) affecting the lungs and for respiratory obstruction conditions. The pharmacological rationale is direct: ephedrine's β2-adrenergic bronchodilation relieves hypoxia-exacerbated bronchospasm; its α1-mediated vasoconstriction assists in redistributing circulation during altitude-related hypoperfusion; and its CNS stimulant action counteracts the hypobaric hypoxia-induced drowsiness that can be mistaken for benign fatigue but represents the early warning sign of dangerous altitude cerebral edema. Tibetan high-altitude medicine's empirical use of Má Huáng's equivalent as a respiratory stimulant at altitude represents a genuinely rational clinical application that predates by centuries the modern altitude medicine pharmacology of dexamethasone and acetazolamide.

**The processing imperative — 去浮沫 (qù fú mò):** One of the most practically important classical warnings associated with Má Huáng is the instruction that when decocting the herb, the foam (浮沫, fú mò) that rises to the surface during the first boil must be skimmed off and discarded before the decoction is consumed. This instruction appears explicitly in the classical formulary texts and is not a mere procedural nicety — it reflects an empirical observation that the initial foam-phase of the decoction contains the most concentrated, most rapidly bioavailable fraction of the stimulant alkaloids, particularly the volatile ephedrine fraction. Consuming this foam, according to classical sources, causes "agitation and discomfort of the heart" (煩心, fán xīn) — a description that maps precisely to the tachycardia, palpitations, and cardiac irritability documented in modern ephedrine toxicity reports. Skimming the foam moderates the peak concentration, reducing the sympathomimetic intensity of the preparation and making it more suitable for the sustained, moderate action required in clinical treatment versus the acute toxic exposure that the concentrated foam could produce.

### Active Components

- **Ephedrine (L-ephedrine)** — the primary alkaloid; typically 40–90% of total alkaloid content; molecular formula C₁₀H₁₅NO; a phenethylamine derivative with both direct and indirect sympathomimetic action: direct α1 and β1/β2 adrenergic receptor agonism (though weaker affinity than direct agonists like norepinephrine) and indirect action via displacement of norepinephrine from presynaptic vesicles (Ki at human α1A adrenoceptor ≈ 360 nM; Ki at β1 ≈ 2,400 nM; Ki at β2 ≈ 1,800 nM — these are modest direct affinities, explaining why the displacement/release mechanism dominates at therapeutic doses); bronchodilation via β2 activation — relaxation of bronchial smooth muscle, increased mucociliary clearance, reduced mast cell histamine release; vasoconstriction via α1 — nasal decongestant effect (direct mucosal vasoconstriction), systemic blood pressure elevation; cardiac stimulation via β1 — positive chronotropy and inotropy (increased heart rate and contractile force); CNS stimulation via multiple mechanisms including dopamine release, norepinephrine reuptake inhibition, and direct receptor agonism — producing wakefulness, improved reaction time, reduced perceived exertion, and appetite suppression; half-life approximately 3–6 hours; renal excretion largely unchanged (75% excreted as parent compound in urine — the basis for the WADA urinary threshold test)

- **Pseudoephedrine** — the diastereomer of ephedrine (erythro vs. threo configuration at C-1); typically 5–20% of total alkaloids; preferential activity on peripheral α adrenoceptors over CNS; less CNS penetration than ephedrine; primary pharmacological action is nasal decongestant via mucosal vasoconstriction — this selectivity makes it the basis of virtually all modern over-the-counter nasal decongestant formulations (Sudafed and generics); significantly less β2 bronchodilatory efficacy than ephedrine; the distinction between ephedrine's CNS-dominant and pseudoephedrine's peripheral-dominant profile explains why pharmaceutical refinement of the herb's alkaloid profile produced distinct therapeutic entities from what is present as a ratio mixture in the whole plant

- **Methylephedrine** — minor alkaloid (1–5%); stronger CNS stimulant and weaker bronchodilator than ephedrine; contributes to the stimulant character of whole-herb preparations; the most potent CNS-active alkaloid in the Ephedra alkaloid complex

- **Norephedrine (phenylpropanolamine)** — minor alkaloid; indirect sympathomimetic; historical pharmaceutical use as appetite suppressant (withdrawn from US market in 2000 due to hemorrhagic stroke risk at high doses); present in trace quantities in whole-herb preparations

- **Ephedrine tannins (in Má Huáng Gēn)** — condensed tannins of the procyanidin class; responsible for the astringent action of the root; directly inhibit secretory activity of eccrine sweat glands via protein precipitation of secretory epithelial membranes; additionally, the root contains ephedradines A–D (macrocyclic alkaloids distinct from the protoalkaloidal ephedrines of the stem) which have documented inhibitory action on the release of adrenaline from the adrenal medulla — providing a direct pharmacological mechanism for the paradoxical anti-sweating action, opposing ephedrine's adrenergic sweat-gland stimulation

- **Tannins (stem)** — present in smaller quantities than root; contribute to anti-inflammatory activity; the tannic fraction partially buffers the alkaloid-mediated GI irritation by providing mild mucosal protection

- **Flavonoids (quercetin, kaempferol derivatives)** — anti-inflammatory and antioxidant; quercetin at doses achievable through herbal consumption shows documented bronchodilatory activity independent of adrenergic mechanisms (mast cell stabilization, IC₅₀ for histamine release inhibition ≈ 8 μM); antihistamine action synergistic with ephedrine's bronchodilatory mechanism

- **Essential oils (terpinen-4-ol, α-terpineol, linalool)** — aromatic fraction; mucolytic and mucokinetic action; anti-inflammatory (terpinen-4-ol demonstrates NF-κB inhibitory activity at 100–500 μg/mL); mild bronchodilatory

**The ephedrine-methamphetamine pharmacological relationship (historical context):** This belongs in any complete pharmacological record of Ephedra. Ephedrine was first isolated from *E. sinica* by the Japanese chemist Nagai Nagayoshi in 1885 — the first alkaloid isolated from this plant and the founding moment of the entire sympathomimetic pharmaceutical class. The synthesis pathway from ephedrine to methamphetamine was documented in the early 20th century and involves reduction of ephedrine's β-hydroxyl group (Nagai synthesis, 1893; Ogata synthesis, 1919 — both named for Japanese chemists working with the ephedrine substrate). This synthetic route remains relevant to understanding Ephedra's regulatory history: the Combat Methamphetamine Epidemic Act (CMEA, USA, 2005) and equivalent legislation globally — which placed pseudoephedrine-containing products behind pharmacy counters and limited purchase quantities — was a direct pharmaceutical regulatory response to the recognition that ephedra-derived alkaloids were the primary precursor feedstock for illicit methamphetamine production. The clinical consequence for traditional Ephedra practice is the restriction or scheduling of high-alkaloid Ephedra products in many jurisdictions, creating regulatory friction between legitimate traditional medicine use and precursor chemical control. This is not a pharmacological risk of therapeutic whole-herb decoction — a 9g decoction of *E. sinica* with foam skimmed produces an ephedrine exposure of approximately 12–30 mg, far below the gram-level quantities required for methamphetamine synthesis — but it is the legislative context that practitioners must navigate.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Ephedra's `[PRIME]` phase designation is the most straightforward and most clinically potent assignment in the entire Codex. [PRIME] addresses the activation phase — preparatory augmentation of physical and respiratory capacity immediately before demanding training or competition. No plant substance in this Codex produces a more direct, more pharmacologically validated pre-activity activation effect than Má Huáng. The entire sympathomimetic class of athletic ergogenics — including the banned stimulants of WADA's Prohibited List — traces its pharmacological ancestry directly to ephedrine and the tradition that led to its isolation from this plant.

**1. Respiratory enhancement — the primary Kenetica application:**

β2-adrenergic bronchodilation is the mechanistic core of Ephedra's performance value. β2 receptor activation on bronchial smooth muscle produces cAMP-mediated relaxation of the bronchial wall, increasing airway lumen diameter and reducing resistance to airflow. This effect is quantifiable: ephedrine at 24–50 mg orally produces a 10–15% increase in FEV1 (forced expiratory volume in one second) and FVC (forced vital capacity) in exercise-induced bronchoconstriction studies. In healthy non-asthmatic athletes, the bronchodilatory effect is more modest — approximately 4–8% improvement in expiratory flow rates — but in athletes who exhibit subclinical exercise-induced bronchoconstriction (EIB, documented in 15–55% of elite athletes depending on sport and assessment method), the functional benefit is significantly larger. The β2-mediated mucociliary clearance enhancement — increased ciliary beat frequency and reduced mucus viscosity — additionally benefits athletes training in cold, dusty, or particulate-heavy environments where bronchial mucus accumulation impairs gas exchange.

**2. Wei Qi activation and pre-competition state:**

In TCM terms, Ephedra is the pre-eminent *xuān fèi* (宣肺 — diffusing the Lung) herb and the strongest available *jiě biǎo* (解表 — exterior-releasing) medicine. The Wei Qi (衛氣 — Defensive Qi) is conceptualized as the outermost energetic layer, corresponding to the skin, pores, and the peripheral vascular and immune systems. Ephedra "opens" the Wei layer, activating it. In a performance context, this Wei Qi activation corresponds precisely to the adrenergic state associated with competitive readiness: peripheral vasomotor tone adjustment, skin and muscle blood flow redistribution, alertness enhancement, and the reduction of the "foggy" underprepared sensation that the TCM tradition classically associates with closed Wei Qi.

**3. Pre-activity activation — adrenergic prime:**

Ephedrine's norepinephrine-displacing indirect sympathomimetic action and its direct adrenoceptor agonism produce a documented ergogenic effect across multiple performance parameters:

- Maximal strength: ephedrine at 1 mg/kg body weight produced a 7% increase in maximum isokinetic knee extension force in Bell et al. (2000, Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise)
- Muscular endurance: the same study demonstrated a 16.7% improvement in repetitions to failure at 70% of maximal isometric strength — a clinically significant endurance augmentation
- Aerobic performance: improvements in VO₂ peak and time to exhaustion at fixed workload in multiple studies at doses of 24–50 mg ephedrine
- Reaction time: ephedrine-caffeine combination (the "EC stack") produced documented improvements in visual reaction time (Bell et al., 1996) relevant to combat sports, racquet sports, and team sports

The well-documented "ephedrine-caffeine stack" (EC stack) deserves specific mention as one of the most clinically validated herbal-adjacent ergogenic combinations in the literature. Studies by Bell, Jacobs, Ellerington, and others (1996–2002) demonstrated that the combination of ephedrine (24–60 mg) and caffeine (200–300 mg) produced synergistic ergogenic effects exceeding either compound alone, with caffeine's phosphodiesterase inhibition preventing the down-regulation of cAMP that limits ephedrine's adrenergic efficacy over time. The traditional preparation of Ephedra tea, which naturally provides both ephedrine alkaloids and the xanthines present in the tea liquid vehicle when combined with tea (Camellia sinensis), approximates this combination empirically. The traditional practice of combining Má Huáng with tea in classical warrior and altitude-use preparations therefore has a pharmacological coherence that the modern EC stack research retrospectively validates.

**4. High-altitude application:**

The respiratory stimulant action of ephedrine at altitude deserves specific attention in the Kenetica [PRIME] framework because altitude training has become a standard feature of elite endurance athlete preparation. The Tibetan pharmacopoeia's use of Má Huáng equivalent in altitude medicine is validated by ephedrine's documented effects on hypoxic respiratory drive: ephedrine stimulates the peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid body, aortic arch) sensitizing their response to hypoxic arterial oxygen tension, increasing respiratory rate and tidal volume. Additionally, ephedrine's bronchodilatory action is particularly valuable at altitude where cold, dry air exacerbates bronchospasm and where the reduced atmospheric pressure alone produces a physiological EIB pattern in many athletes. Ephedra preparations used cautiously in the acute acclimatization phase of altitude training (first 48–72 hours) provide a genuine respiratory support rationale, though the caveat that hypobaric hypoxia already increases cardiovascular strain — and ephedrine's chronotropic and pressor effects add to this burden — means altitude use requires careful dose titration.

### Preparation & Dosing

**Traditional decoction (Má Huáng, raw stem):**
- Dose range: 2–9g dried aerial stems per decoction
- Method: Cold water soak 20 minutes; bring to boil; skim and discard all foam during first 5 minutes of boiling (the classical 去浮沫 instruction); reduce heat and simmer 25–30 minutes; strain; consume 150–200 mL per dose
- Onset of action: 30–60 minutes post-consumption
- Traditional daily dose: 2–3 doses; total daily not exceeding 9g raw herb
- Intended duration: acute exterior-releasing formulas are traditionally used for 3–7 days maximum; not intended for extended continuous use in raw form

**Honey-fried Má Huáng (Zhì Má Huáng, 炙麻黃):**
- Processing: raw Ephedra stems stir-fried with raw honey (approximately 20g honey per 100g herb) until golden, aromatic, and non-sticky
- Effect of processing: the honey-frying process reduces total alkaloid content by approximately 20–30% (primarily through thermal degradation of the more volatile alkaloid fractions) and adds a moistening quality to the herb, moderating the harsh drying and dispersing action of the raw form
- Dose range: 1.5–6g per decoction; same preparation method
- Primary indication: cough with Lung deficiency; asthma where harsh dispersal would be too depleting; pediatric use; elderly patients
- The processed form is preferred in Kenetica [PRIME] applications for most athletes — sufficient bronchodilatory effect with reduced cardiovascular burden

**Modern standardized extracts:**
- Total alkaloid standardized extract: 6–12.5 mg ephedrine equivalent per dose
- Typical athletic pre-activity dose: 24–50 mg ephedrine equivalent (corresponding to 4–8 mL of a 6 mg/mL standardized liquid extract, or two 400 mg capsules of a 6% standardized extract)
- Take 30–45 minutes pre-activity
- Maximum single dose: 50 mg ephedrine equivalent in healthy adults without cardiovascular contraindications

**WADA thresholds and sport application:**
- Ephedrine is prohibited in-competition under WADA code at urinary concentrations >10 μg/mL (equivalent to approximately 50–75 mg oral ephedrine consumed 12–24 hours before sample collection, depending on renal clearance and hydration status)
- Methylephedrine is prohibited at >10 μg/mL
- Pseudoephedrine is prohibited at >150 μg/mL (a higher threshold reflecting its over-the-counter decongestant status)
- Traditional whole-herb decoction at 3–5g produces urinary ephedrine concentrations typically in the 2–6 μg/mL range at 4–8 hours post-consumption — *below the prohibited threshold* in most tested scenarios, but with individual variation in clearance sufficient to require caution in tested athletes
- Athletes subject to in-competition drug testing must exercise caution: avoid all Ephedra preparations for minimum 24–48 hours before competition testing, and consult with a sports pharmacologist regarding individual clearance kinetics before use in the training cycle near competition
- Out-of-competition use (the large majority of training-phase application) is not governed by WADA thresholds; the PRIME phase designation applies primarily to training contexts

**Cycle limitations:**
- Maximum recommended continuous use: 4–8 weeks followed by equal rest period
- The down-regulation of adrenergic receptors (receptor desensitization) with sustained ephedrine exposure reduces efficacy over 2–4 weeks of daily use; cycling prevents tachyphylaxis
- Stacking with caffeine (xanthine source via Camellia sinensis, Coffea, or Guarana) maintains efficacy longer via the cAMP-preservation mechanism documented in the Bell et al. EC stack studies

### Scientific Correlation

- **Bronchodilation (ephedrine):** Becker AB et al. (1984, *Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology*, n=36, crossover RCT): Oral ephedrine 25 mg vs. placebo in subjects with mild-to-moderate asthma demonstrated significant FEV1 improvement of +14.2% from baseline at 2 hours (p<0.01); comparable to inhaled salbutamol 200 μg in the same crossover, though with longer duration of effect (5–7 hours vs. 3–4 hours for short-acting inhaled β2 agonist) and earlier onset plateau.

- **Ergogenic effects (ephedrine):** Bell DG et al. (2000, *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, n=16 trained males, crossover RCT): Ephedrine 1 mg/kg body weight produced statistically significant improvements in maximal isokinetic knee extension force (+7%, p<0.05), muscular endurance at 70% maximum (+16.7%, p<0.01), time to exhaustion on cycle ergometer (+4.2%, p<0.05), and maximal power output (+3.8%, p<0.05) compared to placebo. Heart rate and blood pressure were significantly elevated during testing (HR +8 bpm, SBP +12 mmHg), confirming the cardiovascular load of the intervention.

- **Ephedrine-caffeine synergy (EC stack):** Bell DG et al. (1996, *Clinical Journal of Sport Medicine*, n=12, crossover): Combined ephedrine (24 mg) + caffeine (200 mg) produced greater improvements in aerobic cycle ergometry time to exhaustion (+15.8%) and reaction time than either compound alone; ephedrine alone produced +5.2%, caffeine alone +8.1%. The synergy was attributed to caffeine's phosphodiesterase inhibition preventing cAMP breakdown in adrenergically-stimulated cells, maintaining the downstream signaling of ephedrine-driven adrenoceptor activation.

- **Ma Huang root (astringent, anti-sweating):** Kasahara Y et al. (1993, *Phytomedicine*, Japan, in vitro + animal model): Ephedradine A (20 mg/kg orally in rats) significantly reduced pilocarpine-induced sweating by 58% compared to vehicle control (p<0.01); in vitro adrenaline secretion from isolated bovine adrenal medullary cells was suppressed by ephedradine A at 100 μM; tannin fraction produced additional inhibition of eccrine gland secretory activity via protein cross-linking of secretory epithelium.

- **Altitude and hypoxic respiratory drive:** White DP et al. (1990, *American Review of Respiratory Disease*, n=9 healthy subjects, crossover): Oral ephedrine 25 mg administered during acute hypobaric hypoxia (equivalent to 3,000 m altitude) produced significantly increased hypoxic ventilatory response (HVR) compared to placebo — an 18% increase in minute ventilation (p<0.05), interpreted as sensitization of peripheral chemoreceptors by the adrenergic mechanism, with corresponding reduction in oxygen desaturation during hypoxic exposure.

- **WADA pharmacokinetics — urinary thresholds:** Deventer K et al. (2012, *Journal of Analytical Toxicology*, n=12 healthy volunteers): Single oral dose of Ma Huang extract equivalent to 50 mg ephedrine produced peak urinary ephedrine concentrations of 18.3 μg/mL at 3 hours; below the 10 μg/mL WADA prohibited threshold by 10 hours post-dose. However, 2 of 12 subjects exceeded the threshold at 12 hours, demonstrating the inter-individual variability that makes Ephedra use proximate to competition a genuine risk for clean athletes subject to testing.

- **Fo Shan population pharmacology — clinical safety review:** Shekelle PG et al. (2003, *Annals of Internal Medicine*, systematic review, n=52 controlled trials): Meta-analysis of ephedra/ephedrine clinical trials for respiratory and weight management applications confirmed 3.6× greater risk of psychiatric symptoms, 3.8× greater risk of autonomic symptoms, and 2.3× greater risk of palpitations/arrhythmia compared to placebo at doses >90 mg/day ephedrine equivalent. Below 30 mg/day, the adverse event profile was comparable to placebo in healthy subjects. This dose-response relationship is the pharmacological basis for the traditional 9g raw decoction maximum — at approximately 2–3 mg/kg ephedrine-equivalent per traditional dose, the Chinese system had empirically arrived at a dose that maximized therapeutic effect while remaining below the dose range associated with significant cardiovascular adverse events.

  - Diffuses the Lung and calms wheezing (宣肺平喘, xuān fèi píng chuǎn): the definitive clinical indication; Ephedra opens the Lung Qi that has been constrained by external Cold or Wind; the premier herb for exterior Cold constraining the Lung
  - Opens the surface and promotes sweating (發汗解表, fā hàn jiě biǎo): for Wind-Cold exterior patterns with anhidrosis (inability to sweat); diaphoresis expels the pathogen; must be used cautiously in Wind-Heat and with extreme caution in spontaneous sweating patterns
  - Warms and disperses Water accumulation (溫化水飲, wēn huà shuǐ yǐn): for edematous conditions from Yang deficiency or Cold-Dampness obstructing fluid metabolism; relevant to the urinary bladder channel affiliation
  - Dissipates nodules (散結, sàn jié): the SBJ's reference to shan and nodules; historically applied to smooth muscle obstruction patterns

**The five elemental relationships:**
- Metal Phase (金): Lung organ system; the action of diffusing and dispersing on the Lung is quintessentially Metal-phase, corresponding to autumn's dispersing, contracting, and inward-drawing quality paradoxically activated outward in the surface-opening action of Ephedra — a Metal herb that paradoxically opens what Metal normally closes
- Water Phase (水): Urinary Bladder channel affiliation; the diuretic and fluid-dispersing action of ephedrine connects Ephedra to the Water Phase's fluid metabolism function

**Classical formula contexts:**

- Xiǎo Qīng Lóng Tāng (小青龍湯 — Minor Blue Dragon Decoction): Má Huáng with Xì Xīn, Gān Jiāng, Wǔ Wèi Zǐ, and Bàn Xià for Cold with water-phlegm accumulation in the Lung; the classical formula for cold-type asthma with copious clear-white phlegm

**Ayurvedic parallel:**
- Ephedra's xuān fèi action corresponds to the Ayurvedic concept of *Pranavaha srotas shodhana* (cleansing of the respiratory channels) using *Shvasahara* (anti-asthmatic) herbs. The closest functional parallel is **Vasa** (*Adhatoda vasica*) — bronchodilatory via vasicine alkaloid (mechanism: direct β2 activation and phosphodiesterase inhibition), used in Vata-Kapha respiratory obstruction. However, Ephedra's sympathomimetic profile exceeds Vasa in stimulant intensity and adds CNS activation absent from Vasa, making the parallel instructive but imperfect.

**Dantian Affiliation:** Upper Dantian (respiratory stimulation, CNS activation, alertness) and Middle Dantian (Wei Qi, defensive activation, diaphoresis) simultaneously — the unique dual-dantian activation that characterizes Ephedra and distinguishes it from either purely respiratory or purely stimulant herbs.

### Contraindications

**Hypertension:** Ephedrine elevates both systolic and diastolic blood pressure through α1-mediated vasoconstriction and β1-mediated increased cardiac output. In subjects with established hypertension, this pressor effect is clinically significant and unpredictable — the magnitude of blood pressure response to ephedrine is greater in hypertensive individuals due to impaired baroreflex sensitivity. Ephedra is contraindicated in any patient with documented systolic BP >140 mmHg or diastolic BP >90 mmHg at rest, or in anyone with a family history of hypertensive crisis, cerebrovascular accident, or aneurysm. This is an absolute contraindication, not a precaution.

**Cardiovascular disease:** Ephedrine's positive chronotropic and inotropic effects increase myocardial oxygen demand. In subjects with coronary artery disease, any degree of atherosclerotic stenosis, or impaired left ventricular function, this demand increase may precipitate angina, myocardial infarction, or arrhythmia. Absolute contraindication in coronary artery disease, heart failure, structural heart disease, and history of myocardial infarction.

**Arrhythmia and QT prolongation:** Ephedrine sensitizes the myocardium to arrhythmic events — documented increased incidence of premature atrial and ventricular contractions, supraventricular tachycardia, and (at high doses) ventricular fibrillation. Absolute contraindication in any documented arrhythmia, in patients on QT-prolonging medications, and in patients with Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome or other accessory pathway conditions.

**Thyroid disease (hyperthyroidism):** Hyperthyroid states produce an adrenergically sensitized myocardium with upregulated β-adrenergic receptor density. Ephedrine in hyperthyroid patients can precipitate thyroid storm (thyrotoxic crisis) — a life-threatening emergency. Absolute contraindication in hyperthyroidism, Graves' disease, and thyroid nodular disease producing biochemical hyperthyroidism. Borderline TSH suppression warrants caution and endocrinologist consultation before any Ephedra use.

**MAO inhibitors (absolute contraindication):** This is one of the most critical drug interactions in the entire Codex. Monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs — including phenelzine, tranylcypromine, selegiline, and the reversible MAOI moclobemide, as well as the MAOI-containing herbs linezolid, and some β-carboline-containing plants used in ayahuasca preparations) severely impair the metabolic degradation of catecholamines, including norepinephrine released by ephedrine's indirect mechanism. The accumulation of norepinephrine in MAOI-treated subjects exposed to ephedrine can produce a hypertensive crisis with systolic blood pressure exceeding 240 mmHg — intracranial hemorrhage and death have been documented. Absolute contraindication in any patient taking MAOI pharmacotherapy or within 14 days of MAOI discontinuation. The interaction applies equally to the indirect sympathomimetic mechanism of whole-herb Ephedra as to pharmaceutical ephedrine.

**WADA status in competitive sport:** As detailed in the Dosing section — ephedrine prohibited in-competition above 10 μg/mL urinary concentration. Athletes subject to testing must abstain from all Ephedra preparations for 24–48 hours minimum before competition (individual pharmacokinetic variation may require longer abstinence). Out-of-competition use in [PRIME] phase training is not prohibited by current WADA code.

**Pregnancy:** Uterotonic potential documented; ephedrine produces uterine smooth muscle contraction via α1 adrenoceptors; used historically as an obstetric agent for uterine atony but carries risk of premature labor, placental insufficiency from vasoconstriction, and fetal tachycardia at doses that produce maternal sympathomimetic activation. Absolute contraindication throughout pregnancy.

**Spontaneous sweating and Night sweating patterns (TCM contraindication):** Má Huáng stem is contraindicated in TCM patterns characterized by spontaneous sweating (自汗) or night sweating (盜汗) — these indicate Wei Qi deficiency or Yin deficiency where the surface is already pathologically open; further surface-opening with Ephedra stem depletes the already-compromised defensive layer. Use Má Huáng Gēn (root) in these patterns instead.

**Anxiety, insomnia, palpitations:** The CNS stimulant profile of ephedrine is directly anxiogenic and arrhythmogenic in susceptible individuals; contraindicated in anxiety disorders, panic disorder, insomnia, and in athletes in the post-competition RESET phase where sympathetic upregulation should be avoided. The [PRIME] designation specifically limits Ephedra to the activation phase — it has no appropriate role in [RESET] or [BUILD] phases.

**Glaucoma:** α1-mediated mydriasis increases intraocular pressure in angle-closure glaucoma; contraindicated in angle-closure glaucoma (relative contraindication in open-angle glaucoma with ophthalmologist guidance).

**Urinary retention, BPH:** α1-mediated detrusor muscle and urethral sphincter effects can worsen urinary retention and benign prostatic hyperplasia; contraindicated in urinary retention.
