### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry: named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** This row was added on 2026-10-09 by the plant-gap run (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP). The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000) on the SUGGESTED line of one chapter. The plant is named by no other text read. The chapter describes an aromatic resin ("the oozing of an Arabic tree"), not a plant, and this entry stays with that.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`.

*1-23 KANKAMON* (heading at line 3755; SUGGESTED line 3756: "Amyris ambroisiaca, Protium icicaraba, Icica icicarabica — Gum Elemi Tree"; text at line 3757, in Book One, Aromatics). The whole chapter reads: "Cancamum is the oozing of an Arabic tree resembling myrrh [1-77, 1-73, 4-116], poisonous to the taste, which they use as a perfume. They make a perfume with it for their cloths with myrrh and styrax. It is reported to be able to make fat bodies lean — a half teaspoonful taken as a drink with water or vinegar and honey for many consecutive days. It is given to the splenetic, epileptic and asthmatic. Taken with honey and water it brings down the menstrual flow, and it quickly takes off scars in the eyes and heals their moisture diluted in wine. For gums rotten from moisture and toothache it helps as nothing else can do."

**What the text gives.** The material is a resinous exudate from an Arabian tree that "resembl[es] myrrh". It is "poisonous to the taste" and is used as a perfume for cloths, with myrrh and styrax. The medicinal uses are: leaning of fat bodies (the one dose printed), the spleen, epilepsy and asthma, bringing down the menstrual flow, eye scars and eye moisture, and rotten gums and toothache.

**Identity doubts.** (1) The translators list three binomials (*Amyris ambroisiaca*, *Protium icicaraba*, *Icica icicarabica*), the last two of which are names for American resin trees by general knowledge; the text places the tree in Arabia. The row takes the first. [USER: verify] (2) The English "Gum Elemi Tree" is the translators' gloss and elemi is a name given to resins of several different trees. The chapter does not describe a tree, only the resin and its uses. (3) The entry just before it (printed above 1-23 at the end of the preceding chapter) concerns *nascaphthum*, a bark used for perfume and inhaled for constriction of the vulva, which belongs to a different row.

### Active Components

Resins of the myrrh and incense family (Burseraceae) are generally known to contain volatile terpenes and resin acids. The material of the text was not identified, and no analysis was read. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only. The chapter records a perfume resin that is "poisonous to the taste" and is taken, in a dose of a half teaspoonful for many consecutive days, to slim the fat. It is also recorded for spleen, epilepsy, asthma, menstruation, eyes and gums. The identification is a translators' suggestion, the text itself calls the material poisonous, and the Codex gives no protocol for it.

### Preparation & Dosing

Line 3757 gives one quantity: "a half teaspoonful taken as a drink with water or vinegar and honey for many consecutive days", as a reported means of making fat bodies lean. For the other uses it says "taken with honey and water" (menstrual flow) and "diluted in wine" (eyes), with no quantity. Applied "as nothing else can do" to rotten gums and toothache. It is also used with myrrh and styrax in a perfume for cloths. The dose belongs to the text and is not a recommendation. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system. The chapter offers provenance (an Arabic tree resembling myrrh), the product (an oozing), a sensory warning ("poisonous to the taste") and its place in perfumery (with myrrh and styrax). Reports are hedged ("It is reported"). No cultivation status is assigned. The identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here gives a cultivator a practice. The resin is a perfume, and a reported slimming drink that the text itself calls poisonous to the taste. The abstentive answer is the correct one.

### Contraindications

The text calls the material "poisonous to the taste". It is not securely identified, so no safe use can be inferred. Taking an unidentified resin for many consecutive days to lose weight, or for epilepsy or asthma in place of treatment, is dangerous. The menstrual-flow use implies it is unsuitable in pregnancy. None of the uses above is advice.
