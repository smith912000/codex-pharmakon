# Sappanwood — Su Mu (蘇木), Patanga, Bakam, Brazilwood of the East

## *Caesalpinia sappan* L. (syn. *Biancaea sappan* (L.) Tod.) | Family: Fabaceae (Leguminosae)

**Tradition Tags:** CM, SBJ, SS
**Energetic Classification:** Warm-to-neutral, sweet and acrid with a salty finish — a moving, blood-quickening wood in the Galenic reckoning, opening stagnation rather than draining.
**Training Phase:** Tempering

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Shennong (TCM):** In the Chinese materia medica the heartwood is Su Mu, a blood-invigorating remedy entering the Heart, Liver and Spleen channels. Classical use is for blood stasis: traumatic injury, bruising and swelling, fixed masses, and post-partum stagnation with pain. The characteristic action is to "invigorate blood, dispel stasis, reduce swelling and stop pain" — a mover applied where blood has congealed and turned static.

**Sushruta / Ayurveda (SS):** Known as *patanga* or *pattanga*, the heartwood appears in the Ayurvedic tradition as an astringent, cooling, blood-related drug (*raktaprasadana*) used for disorders of the blood (*raktapitta*), skin conditions, and as a dye and wound remedy. It carries the same double identity there as in China — a colouring wood that is simultaneously a medicine of the blood.

**Avicenna / Unani (CM):** *Bakam* enters the Unani pharmacopoeia as an astringent, styptic and blood-purifying wood, used in dysentery and haemorrhagic complaints and prized as a red dye. The Greco-Arabic tradition inherited sappanwood through the Indian Ocean trade, where it was a major article of commerce long before the Americas gave Europe the closely related *Caesalpinia echinata* (from which the country Brazil took its name).

Sappanwood was among the great trade commodities of the medieval Indian Ocean and the Nanhai routes into China, moving as both a luxury dyestuff and a drug; its Malay-derived name *sappan* travelled with the wood itself, and the European "brazilwood" of commerce was named by analogy to it, not the reverse. The doubled value — colour and cure in one heartwood — is why it appears in so many pharmacopoeias at once.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** Sappanwood is the archetype of *the red medicine* — a heartwood that bleeds a deep crimson dye into water, and is used across traditions precisely for disorders of blood. The signature is almost too neat: a wood the colour of blood, applied to move, cool and purify the blood. This is the classical logic of the doctrine of signatures, and here it is unusually well earned. The pharmacology underneath is brazilin, a colourless compound that oxidises to the red pigment brazilein and carries genuine anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and circulatory activity — so the doctrine of "the red wood that quickens the blood" rests on a real, isolable chemistry rather than colour-association alone.

### Active Components

- **Brazilin** — the principal homoisoflavonoid; colourless in the reduced form, oxidising to the red pigment **brazilein**.
- **Sappanchalcone** — a chalcone with anti-inflammatory activity.
- **Protosappanin A** — a biphenyl-type constituent with immunomodulatory and antioxidant activity.
- **Brazilein** — the oxidised red pigment, itself bioactive and responsible for the dye colour.
- **Essential oil** and various **phenolics/flavonoids** contributing to antimicrobial action.

Brazilin's homoisoflavonoid skeleton is unusual among plant pigments and accounts for much of the wood's redox activity: the reduced (pale) and oxidised (red) forms interconvert readily, which is both why the dye shifts colour with pH and mordant and why the extract behaves as an antioxidant in assay conditions.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Su Mu is a **Tempering**-phase mover. It is indicated where the picture is *fixed, static, congested blood* — bruising and trauma that will not resolve, swellings that stay put, pain that is stabbing and localised rather than diffuse. Its role is to break stasis and reduce swelling, restoring flow to a tissue that has locked. In Kenetica it is used as a targeted resolvent for stagnation, not a general tonic, and its powerful blood-moving character is precisely why it is withheld in pregnancy (see Contraindications).

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed: Nirmal & Panichayupakaranant (2015), *Pharmaceutical Biology* — a standardised brazilin-rich extract (≈39% w/w brazilin) showed antioxidant activity (β-carotene bleaching EC50 ≈ 60.5 µg/mL), antibacterial activity stronger against Gram-positive than Gram-negative bacteria, and anti-inflammatory activity by inhibition of protein denaturation. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3109/13880209.2014.982295). This is consistent with the traditional use in swelling, infection and inflammation.

Sugiaman et al. (2024), *PeerJ* — a brazilin topical cream reduced NF-κB protein expression and downregulated the pro-inflammatory genes IL-1β, IL-6, p38 and TNF-α while promoting angiogenesis in a rat gingivitis model, offering a molecular route for the "reduce swelling, promote healing" reputation. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.17642). Vij et al. (2023), *Molecules*, reviews the sappanwood constituents (brazilin, brazilein, sappanchalcone, protosappanin A) and their reported antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and anticancer activities. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules28176247).

**Evidence grade:** Preclinical (in vitro and rodent) with a coherent anti-inflammatory/antioxidant mechanism; robust traditional use across Chinese, Ayurvedic and Unani systems. Human clinical data are limited.

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** Sweet, salty, acrid; neutral (some sources warm). Enters Heart, Liver, Spleen channels. Actions: invigorates blood, dispels stasis, reduces swelling, alleviates pain.
- **Ayurveda:** *rasa* — astringent (kashaya) and sweet; *virya* — cooling; *vipaka* — pungent; a *raktaprasadana* (blood-purifying) drug, pacifying pitta and kapha.
- **Unani:** cold and dry temperament in some readings; astringent, styptic, blood-purifying.

### Cultivation Register

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** *Bakam* enters the Unani pharmacopoeia as a cold, dry astringent, styptic and blood-purifying wood, worked for dysentery and haemorrhagic complaints and prized as a red dye. The strongest cultivation idea in the Unani corpus — the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture across the whole regimen — is a longevity frame of the constitution, not a status this tradition assigns to a cooling styptic heartwood; no *mufarrih* or cardiac-*ruh* role is claimed for *bakam* in the retrieved sources, and per the corpus discipline no *Canon* chapter is cited. Its standing here is therapeutic, not cultivational. **(b) framed:** attested in the materia medica, no cultivation status sourced.

**Chinese — Bencao (SBJ).** In the Chinese materia medica the heartwood is Su Mu 蘇木, a blood-invigorating, stasis-dispelling mover entering the Heart, Liver and Spleen channels, whose classical office is to "invigorate blood, dispel stasis, reduce swelling and stop pain" (活血祛瘀). Su Mu is not a substance of the Shennong stratum — it does not appear among the three-grade (*san pin* 三品) drugs of the *Shennong Bencao Jing* but enters the Chinese record only in the Tang materia medica (*Xinxiu Bencao*, 659 CE) and later compilations, so no upper/middle/lower grade is recorded for it in the sources retrieved. Within *yang sheng* 養生, which is conservational rather than curative, a stasis-breaking blood-mover is by its nature the opposite of a nourish-life drug: it is applied to a fixed, congested picture and then stopped. **(b) framed:** attested therapeutically, no *san pin* cultivation grade sourced for this species.

**Ayurveda (SS).** Known as *patanga* / *pattanga*, the heartwood is a *raktaprasadana* (blood-clearing) drug with astringent (*kashaya*) rasa and cooling (*shita*) virya, worked for disorders of the blood (*raktapitta*), skin conditions and wounds, and doubling as a dye. No *rasayana* (rejuvenation) or *vajikarana* (generative-vitality) assignment is recorded for *patanga* in the sources retrieved; its place is in the therapeutic and blood-dietetic register, not among the formal Sushrutan cultivation disciplines. **(b) framed.**

**Practitioner's note.** Sappanwood is a resolvent, not a tonic. Across all three traditions it is worked for a defined, congested target — stasis, bruising, haemorrhage, blood-heat, skin disease — and its whole character is to move and disperse rather than to build or conserve. Nothing in the sources makes it a substance a cultivator takes long-term to nourish life; its strong blood-moving action is precisely why it is withheld in pregnancy. Use it for the fixed complaint, at a defined dose, and then stop.

### Contraindications

**Contraindicated in pregnancy** by long traditional consensus — as a strong blood-invigorating, stasis-dispelling agent it is classed among the herbs to avoid during gestation because of the theoretical risk to the pregnancy, and this caution is explicit in the classical Chinese and Ayurvedic literature. Use cautiously in anyone with bleeding disorders or on anticoagulant/antiplatelet therapy, given its blood-moving character. Discontinue before surgery. As with any potent heartwood remedy, keep to defined courses at customary doses; high-dose or prolonged self-administration is not supported by evidence.
