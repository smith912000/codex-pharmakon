## 1. Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Binomial:** *Salvia rosmarinus* Spenn. (2017 reclassification; former name: *Rosmarinus officinalis* L., 1753 — still used in most clinical and pharmacognostic literature; this Codex uses *S. rosmarinus* for taxonomic currency but acknowledges *R. officinalis* as the operative pharmaceutical name)
**Family:** Lamiaceae
**Subfamily:** Nepetoideae
**Tribe:** Mentheae (closely related to Salvia, thyme, oregano)

**Reclassification note:** In 2017, molecular phylogenetic analysis by Paton et al. demonstrated that *Rosmarinus* is nested within *Salvia* — making it paraphyletic to maintain as a separate genus. The name *Salvia rosmarinus* is now accepted in most botanical databases (POWO, Plants of the World Online). However, *Rosmarinus officinalis* remains in active clinical, pharmaceutical, and regulatory use and will not be quickly replaced. This Codex flags the change without eliminating the familiar name from practical guidance.

**Common names:**
- English: Rosemary
- French: Romarin
- German: Rosmarin, Echter Rosmarin
- Italian: Rosmarino
- Spanish: Romero
- Arabic: إكليل الجبل (*Iklīl al-jabal*) — literally "mountain crown/garland" (reflects Mediterranean mountain habitat); الروزماري (*Al-rūzmārī*, transliteration)
- Latin: *Ros marinus* — "dew of the sea"; classical Roman name reflecting coastal Mediterranean habitat (rosemary grows abundantly on limestone sea cliffs where sea spray provides moisture); one of the most botanically descriptive classical plant names
- Greek: Λιβανωτίς (*Libanotis*) — in some classical sources; *Ρόσμαρι* (modern Greek)
- Hebrew: רוזמרין (*Rozmarīn*, modern; classical Hebrew uses different botanical identifications)

**Varieties of pharmaceutical significance:**
- Standard *R. officinalis*: 1,8-cineole dominant essential oil type (camphor + cineole notes)
- "Arp," "Hill Hardy" (North American cultivars): essential oil profile variants; all pharmacologically similar at compound-class level

**Botanical description:** Aromatic evergreen shrub; woody stems; leaves needle-like (0.5–3cm), leathery, glossy dark green above/white woolly below — the dense white hair on the leaf underside is a characteristic field identification feature; flowers blue-purple, two-lipped, in axillary clusters; grows 1–2m; intensely aromatic throughout. Mediterranean limestone hillsides, sea cliffs, and garrigue (scrubland); drought-tolerant; thrives in full sun + well-drained alkaline soil.

**Parts used:** Flowering aerial parts/young shoots (distillation and fresh herb use); leaves (culinary, pharmaceutical); essential oil (aromatherapy, topical).

## 3. Convergence Analysis

Rosemary's pharmacological record is perhaps the clearest demonstration of the Greco-Arabic transmission line in Western medicine. Dioscorides' brief but specific medical entry; Ibn Sīnā's extensive and detailed *Canon* treatment — naming memory enhancement, nerve tonic activity, cardiac support, and topical alopecia use specifically — read as a coherent transmission: the Roman empire learned rosemary's properties from earlier Mediterranean observations; the Islamic golden age inherited and systematized them.

The memory enhancement attribution deserves particular attention. Ibn Sīnā in the *Canon* writes that rosemary "strengthens the memory and quickens the mind." This claim, transmitted through Islamic medicine into European Renaissance herbalism, reappears in Shakespeare's *Hamlet* (Ophelia: "There's rosemary, that's for remembrance") as an accepted cultural fact. Modern pharmacology has now provided mechanistic foundations for this 1,000-year-old clinical observation: 1,8-cineole blood-level correlates with cognitive performance scores; AChE inhibition by carnosic acid/carnosol; NRF2 activation; BDNF upregulation. The chain from empirical observation to cultural encoding to mechanistic confirmation is unusually complete for an herbal medicine claim.

The Commission E approvals (dyspeptic complaints, supportive therapy for circulatory disorders — topical) represent the first tier of regulatory recognition. More significant pharmacologically are the AChE inhibition data and 1,8-cineole cognitive studies — which suggest rosemary may deserve a more prominent place in preventive neurology than its current status as a culinary herb implies.

## 4. Phytochemical Composition

**Essential oil (primary pharmacological fraction):**
- **1,8-Cineole** (eucalyptol): 15–55% of volatile oil; primary monoterpenoid; AChE inhibition (anticholinesterase — same mechanism as Alzheimer's drug donepezil); penetrates blood-brain barrier; bronchodilator (mucokinetic); anti-inflammatory; cognitive-enhancing effect documented at blood-level correlations with performance
- **α-Pinene**: 10–25%; anti-inflammatory; AChE inhibitory (weak); shared with most coniferous trees (pine → rosemary → both named partially for this compound class); bronchodilator
- **Camphor** (1,7,7-trimethylbicyclo[2.2.1]heptan-2-one): 5–20%; stimulating; local anesthetic (TRPM8 agonist → cooling then warming); analgesic; expectorant; CNS stimulant at high doses (TOXIC in excess — camphor overdose risk)
- **β-Caryophyllene**: sesquiterpene; CB2 receptor agonist; anti-inflammatory; shared across Lamiaceae
- **Bornyl acetate**: spasmolytic; anti-inflammatory; sweet terpenoid character
- **Borneol**: terpenoid alcohol; anti-inflammatory; local anesthetic; antibacterial; shared with many Lamiaceae and Dipterocarpaceae
- Total volatile oil: 1.5–3.0% DW in leaves

**Diterpene phenols (most distinctive pharmacological class):**
- **Carnosic acid**: 1.5–6.0% DW; potent antioxidant; NRF2 pathway activator (induces antioxidant enzyme expression: HO-1, GSH); neuroprotective; anti-tumor (in vitro); primary antioxidant responsible for rosemary's effectiveness as food preservative
- **Carnosol**: oxidation product of carnosic acid; 0.5–2.0% DW; similar antioxidant activity; AChE inhibition; anti-tumor in multiple cancer lines; anti-inflammatory
- **Rosmanol, epirosmanol, isorosmanol**: related diterpene phenols; antioxidant

**Phenolic acids (shared Lamiaceae signature):**
- **Rosmarinic acid**: 0.5–3.5% DW — highest concentrations among culinary herbs; anti-inflammatory (COX-2, 5-LOX, NF-κB inhibition); antioxidant; anti-allergic; anti-viral; compound named FOR rosemary (isolated from *R. officinalis* in 1958 by Scarpati & Oriente) despite being ubiquitous across Lamiaceae
- **Caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid**: antioxidant, anti-inflammatory

**Flavones:**
- Luteolin, luteolin-7-glucoside; genkwanin (anti-inflammatory, antioxidant); apigenin (minor)

**Ursolic acid and oleanolic acid (triterpenoids):**
- Anti-inflammatory; anti-tumor; anti-HIV (in vitro); present in many Lamiaceae

## 5. Mechanisms of Action

**Cognitive enhancement / AChE inhibition:**
1,8-Cineole + carnosol + α-pinene → inhibition of acetylcholinesterase (AChE, the enzyme that degrades acetylcholine in synaptic clefts) → increased acetylcholine availability → enhanced cholinergic neurotransmission → improved attention, working memory, processing speed. This is mechanistically identical to pharmaceutical AChE inhibitors used in Alzheimer's disease (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) — though rosemary compounds are significantly weaker AChE inhibitors than pharmaceuticals. The clinical significance lies in a dietary and aromatherapeutic context where chronic low-level AChE inhibition from regular rosemary exposure might contribute to cognitive maintenance.

**NRF2 pathway activation (carnosic acid — neuroprotection):**
Carnosic acid → electrophilic activation of NRF2 (nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2) → nuclear translocation → transcription of cytoprotective genes (HO-1, NQO1, GSH synthesis) → antioxidant defense enhancement throughout body. NRF2 activation is a master switch for cellular cytoprotection — relevant to neurodegeneration prevention, cardiovascular protection, and anti-cancer defense. Carnosic acid is among the most potent natural NRF2 activators known.

**Aromatherapy cognitive mechanism (1,8-cineole blood levels):**
Inhaled 1,8-cineole from rosemary aromatherapy → rapid pulmonary absorption → blood-brain barrier penetration → elevated plasma 1,8-cineole levels that correlate with cognitive performance in human studies (Moss 2012). This is one of the few documented cases where an aromatherapy effect has been confirmed to have a pharmacological basis in measurable blood-level pharmacokinetics, rather than merely a psychosensory/placebo mechanism.

**Circulatory stimulant mechanism (topical):**
Camphor + borneol + 1,8-cineole → local vasodilation (TRPM8 activation → initial cold sensation → reflex vasodilation) + direct smooth muscle effect on arterioles → increased local circulation → Commission E-approved "supportive therapy in circulatory disorders" for topical application. Mechanism for rosemary bath: heat + volatiles together → systemic peripheral vasodilation → reduced peripheral vascular resistance → mild blood pressure lowering.

**Food preservative mechanism:**
Carnosic acid + carnosol → radical scavenging (particularly lipid peroxyl radicals) → prevention of rancidity in fatty foods; antioxidant potency exceeds synthetic BHT (butylated hydroxytoluene) used as food additive. EU food additive status: E392 (rosemary extracts as antioxidant food preservative). This represents pharmacological activity formalized as food regulation.

**Anti-tumor mechanism (multiple pathways):**
Carnosol → Wnt/β-catenin signaling inhibition; CDK4/6 inhibition; apoptosis induction (caspase-3, -7 activation); VEGF inhibition (anti-angiogenic); anti-metastatic effects. In vitro across multiple cancer lines (breast, prostate, colorectal, leukemia). Clinical significance: preclinical only; no human RCT.

## 6. Clinical Evidence

**Cognitive performance (aromatherapy):**
- **Level B** — Moss 2012 (*Therapeutic Advances in Psychopharmacology*): rosemary aromatherapy exposure in a room significantly improved speed and accuracy of mental arithmetic, spatial working memory, and attention vs. control room (no aroma); plasma 1,8-cineole levels correlated with performance improvement — confirming pharmacokinetic basis
- **Level B** — Moss 2016: elderly population (60–95 years) in care home; rosemary aromatherapy room significantly improved retrospective and prospective memory scores vs. lavender and control
- Multiple additional small RCTs confirming cognitive benefit; all with modest sample sizes but consistent direction; systematic review (Pengelly 2012) supports mild cognitive-enhancing effect

**Alopecia (androgenic):**
- **Level B** — Panahi 2015 (*SKINmed*): 100 patients; rosemary oil topical (2 drops/ml carrier) vs. minoxidil 2% topical; 6 months; **rosemary oil non-inferior to minoxidil 2% in hair count increase**; rosemary oil produced significantly less scalp itching (minoxidil's most common side effect); mechanism: 5α-reductase inhibition (similar to finasteride mechanism; carnosol) + vasodilation (improved follicular perfusion)
- Commission E approval: alopecia (topical; circulatory effect on scalp)

**Dyspeptic complaints:**
- **Level C** — Commission E approved for "dyspeptic complaints" (flatulence, bloating) based on traditional use documentation; no RCT specifically for rosemary in dyspepsia
- Essential oil carminative mechanism: antispasmodic (smooth muscle relaxant) by monoterpenes

**Anti-inflammatory / antioxidant (food preservative level):**
- **Level A** (for food preservation application) — E392 regulatory approval (EU) based on demonstrated antioxidant efficacy; carnosic acid activity well-characterized; not for pharmaceutical anti-inflammatory claims in clinical medicine
- **Level D** — Rosmarinic acid anti-inflammatory in cell models; clinical translation uncertain

**Wound healing / antimicrobial (topical):**
- **Level C/D** — Antimicrobial activity against MRSA, *E. coli*, *P. aeruginosa* in vitro; topical preparations used in folk medicine for wound healing; limited clinical RCT data

## 7. Phenomenological Record

**Ros marinus — dew of the sea:**
The Latin name *ros marinus* ("sea dew") encodes the habitat of wild rosemary with botanical precision. Mediterranean rosemary grows most abundantly on sea-facing limestone cliffs and headlands — where coastal morning fog and sea spray provide moisture in an otherwise arid summer climate. The plant evolved at the interface of maritime air and sun-baked stone, and its biochemistry reflects this: its volatile oils evolved partly as UV-protective compounds; its dense leaf hairs (reflected) minimize water loss; its root systems penetrate limestone cracks. The name is both poetry and ecology.

**Rosemary for remembrance — cultural encoding of pharmacology:**
Shakespeare's *Hamlet* (Act IV, Scene V, 1601 CE): Ophelia's line "There's rosemary, that's for remembrance, pray you, love, remember" encodes a pharmacological tradition so well established in Elizabethan culture that it required no explanation. The use of rosemary at both weddings (memory of love) and funerals (memory of the dead) in Tudor England reflects rosemary's dual cultural role: herb of memory and herb of loyalty. This cultural encoding traces back through European Renaissance herbalism (John Gerard, 1597: "rosemary comforteth the brain and memory") to Ibn Sīnā's *Canon* to Dioscorides — a 2,000-year oral-to-written pharmacological tradition crystallized in a line of theatrical dialogue.

The modern pharmacology (1,8-cineole AChE inhibition; Moss 2012 cognitive studies) represents not the discovery of a new claim but the mechanistic confirmation of a tradition so persistent that it encoded itself in cultural memory before it encoded itself in pharmacological understanding.

**Queen of Hungary Water (1370 CE):**
The first documented alcohol-based perfume in European history was "Aqua Reginae Hungariae" (Water of the Queen of Hungary) — allegedly formulated in 1370 CE for Queen Elisabeth of Hungary. The formula: rosemary flowers + alcohol + various aromatic herbs. Medieval accounts claim it cured the Queen's rheumatic pain and restored her vigor so dramatically that the 72-year-old queen was courted by the King of Poland. While the romantic narrative is apocryphal, the preparation represents the first systematic extraction of rosemary's volatile bioactives in an alcohol menstruum — anticipating both modern tincture preparation and the pharmaceutical isolation of rosemary compounds by 600 years.

**Vinaigre des quatre voleurs (Four Thieves Vinegar):**
During plague epidemics in 14th–17th century France, "four thieves vinegar" — containing rosemary, thyme, lavender, sage, rue, and garlic macerated in vinegar — was reputedly used by grave robbers and plague nurses to protect themselves from infection. The formula is plausible as a bacteriostatic agent: rosemary + thyme compounds (carvacrol, thymol, 1,8-cineole) + garlic allicin have all demonstrated in vitro bacteriostatic activity against pathogens including *Yersinia pestis* (plague bacillus). The vinegar's acidity would enhance extraction and add antimicrobial activity. Historical protective efficacy is anecdotal, but the pharmacological ingredients are credible.

**EU food additive E392:**
Rosemary extract's official status as EU food additive E392 (antioxidant) represents the formal intersection of the herb's culinary and pharmaceutical identities. Rosemary extract (RE) standardized to carnosic acid content is added to oils, fats, processed meats, snacks, and margarines to prevent oxidative rancidity. The same compound (carnosic acid) that prevents fat oxidation in food also activates NRF2 to prevent oxidative cellular damage in human biology — the food preservation mechanism and the neuroprotective mechanism are the same radical-scavenging chemistry operating at different substrates (dietary fat vs. cellular lipids and proteins).

## 8. Practical Application

**Cognitive support:**
- Aromatherapy: diffuse *R. officinalis* essential oil (or equivalent *S. rosmarinus*) for 3–5 minutes before and during cognitive work; 3–5 drops in a diffuser; mechanism confirmed by blood-level studies; appropriate for exam preparation, creative work, attention-demanding tasks
- Culinary: regular rosemary use in cooking (1–2 tablespoons fresh per meal) provides rosmarinic acid, carnosic acid, and 1,8-cineole in dietary quantities; not standardized for cognitive pharmacology but consistent dietary delivery
- For older adults with mild cognitive concerns: rosemary aroma in living environment + regular culinary use = evidence-supported low-risk intervention; adjunct to medical management, not replacement

**Hair loss (androgenic alopecia):**
- Protocol from Panahi 2015: rosemary essential oil 2 drops per ml in carrier (jojoba or coconut oil preferred); massage 2–4ml of diluted oil into scalp daily; leave 30+ minutes (or overnight); shampoo out; minimum 3 months before assessing response; up to 6 months for full effect comparable to minoxidil 2%
- Mechanism: carnosol 5α-reductase inhibition + local circulation enhancement; dual mechanism addressing both hormonal (DHT-mediated follicular miniaturization) and vascular aspects of androgenic alopecia
- Available as ready-made rosemary-based hair serums; effectiveness of commercial products depends on carnosol/carnosic acid content (verify with supplier)

**Topical anti-inflammatory / circulatory:**
- Commission E-approved uses: muscle and joint pain (rheumatic conditions); poor peripheral circulation; circulatory disorders (topical only)
- Method: rosemary oil 1–2% in carrier oil, massage into affected muscles; or rosemary bath (5–10 drops essential oil + carrier in warm bath, or strong rosemary infusion as bath additive)

**Dyspeptic complaints:**
- Rosemary tea: 2g dried herb per 200ml hot water, 10 min infusion; drink 30 min before meals; Commission E-supported traditional use
- Rosemary tincture (1:5, 45% ethanol): 2ml 3× daily before meals

**Food preservative:**
- Crushed rosemary leaves or rosemary-infused oil used in meat marinades: not just flavor but genuine antimicrobial + antioxidant protection extending shelf life and reducing surface pathogen load; particularly relevant for grilled meats (rosemary's carnosic acid may reduce HCA/PAH formation during high-temperature cooking — preclinical evidence)

**Drug interactions:**
- Moderate CYP1A2 inhibition by rosemary extracts in vitro; clinical significance at culinary doses: minimal; concentrated supplement doses: monitor
- Anticoagulants: rosmarinic acid weak anti-platelet activity; culinary use: clinically negligible; high-dose rosemary supplements: theoretical interaction with warfarin; monitor INR if using concentrated extracts
- Antihypertensives: slight vasodilatory effect; additive in large doses; negligible at culinary use

**Contraindications:**
- Epilepsy: camphor component is CNS stimulant; avoid concentrated camphor-containing rosemary oil orally in epileptic patients; culinary use fine
- Pregnancy: avoid essential oil internally; culinary use considered safe
- CAMPHOR TOXICITY: do NOT ingest neat rosemary essential oil (camphor content can cause convulsions at 50–500mg/kg; fatal at higher doses); external use only for essential oil; culinary herb is safe

**Energetic classification:**
- Tier: **◉ Qi + ☿ Shen** — dual action; strongly Qi-warming and activating (circulation, digestion, warmth); also Shen-active through cognitive-enhancing/memory-supporting mechanism; unique among Lamiaceae in bridging functional Qi-warming and cognitive Shen-enhancement
- Thermal nature: warm (温); flavor: pungent-bitter (辛苦); organ meridians: Heart, Liver, Spleen
- Classical characterization: 温通经络，活血散寒，振奋阳气 — warms and opens the channels; moves blood and disperses cold; uplifts yang qi
