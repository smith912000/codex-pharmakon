# I-601 | Calendula

**Codex Pharmakon Universalis — Volume I: Plant-Based Substances**

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary designation:** *Calendula officinalis* L.

**Taxonomic position:** Asteraceae; subfamily Asteroideae; tribe Calenduleae — a small tribe of about 15 genera; *Calendula* itself contains 15–20 species; *C. officinalis* is the primary pharmaceutical species and is unusual in that its exact wild ancestor is unknown — it is one of the oldest known cultivated ornamental and medicinal plants in the Mediterranean, possibly not existing in a truly wild form

**CRITICAL DISAMBIGUATION:** *Calendula officinalis* (Pot Marigold) ≠ *Tagetes* spp. (African/French Marigold/Mexican Marigold). These are entirely distinct genera within Asteraceae with different chemistry, pharmacology, and traditional uses; the shared common name "marigold" creates persistent confusion:
| | *Calendula officinalis* | *Tagetes erecta* / *T. patula* |
|--|--|--|
| Common name | Pot Marigold, Garden Marigold | African Marigold, French Marigold, Mexican Marigold |
| Native region | Mediterranean | Central/South America |
| Flower color | Orange, yellow | Orange, yellow, red |
| Primary use | Wound healing; anti-inflammatory | Ornamental; insecticide (thiophenes); food coloring |
| Key compounds | Oleanolic acid saponins; flavonoids; carotenoids | Thiophene derivatives (α-terthienyl); lutein; zeaxanthin |
| Traditional medicine | Mediterranean, European, Islamic | Mesoamerican (Aztec — Day of the Dead); pre-Columbian |

**Vernacular names:**
- English: Pot Marigold, Garden Marigold, Calendula; "Marigold" = "Mary's Gold" — another Marian plant name in European folk botany (alongside Milk Thistle I-599); the golden color associated with the Virgin Mary's traditional golden garments
- Latin pharmaceutical: *Flos Calendulae* — the flower heads; *Calendula* from Latin *Calendae* (calends, first day of each month) — calendula flowers nearly every month of the year in mild climates, a phenological observation preserved in the name
- Arabic: *Khamsin* / *Dhibaal* — recognized in Unani and Islamic medicine
- German: *Ringelblume* (ringed/halo flower)
- French: *Souci officinal* (officinal marigold)
- Persian: *Gul-e-Hamishe Bahar* (ever-spring flower)
- Spanish: *Maravilla*, *Flor de muerto* — the Mexican Day of the Dead association is primarily *Tagetes* (cempasúchil), NOT *Calendula*, though confusion exists
- Sanskrit: *Zergul* — recognized in Unani *materia medica*

**Botanical description:** Annual herb 30–60 cm; alternate oblong-lanceolate leaves; flower heads 4–7 cm; single or semi-double/double cultivar varieties; ray florets orange to pale yellow; disc florets darker orange; flowers throughout the growing season; resinous scent; sticky stem and leaf hairs (trichomes) produce the characteristic aromatics; the entire aerial plant is glandular-hairy (secretory trichomes)

**Pharmaceutical part:** **Flower heads** (*Flos Calendulae*) — harvested in full bloom, dried rapidly; both ligulate ray florets and tubular disc florets used; constituents concentrated in ray florets (carotenoids) and in the resinous glandular hairs (triterpenoids, essential oils)

**Convergence note:** The topical wound-healing and anti-inflammatory applications appear in the earliest known written pharmacopoeias and across geographic traditions. The relatively high convergence reflects that wound healing with bright-orange flower preparations is one of humanity's most intuitive pharmaceutical gestures — the visual vividness of the flowers may have directed attention, and subsequent experience confirmed efficacy. Modern pharmacology validates the empirical observation through triterpene saponin mechanisms.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

Calendula presents a relatively straightforward traditional-to-modern validation: the empirically observed wound healing, topical anti-inflammatory, and skin-soothing properties map cleanly onto oleanolic acid/ursolic acid triterpene saponin mechanisms (fibroblast stimulation, prostaglandin inhibition, antimicrobial). The cross-traditional convergence on topical applications is reinforced by the botanical availability of *Calendula* throughout the Mediterranean basin and the Near East — the primary source regions for five of the nine traditions.

**The carotenoid color hypothesis:** The brilliant orange-yellow carotenoids (lutein, zeaxanthin, beta-carotene) that give calendula flowers their characteristic color were not pharmacologically significant in traditional use but are historically the primary marker of the flower's potency — darker orange flowers were considered more medicinal. This corresponds with higher lutein/zeaxanthin content (ophthalmological significance) and also with higher triterpene content (which tracks with flower maturity and color intensity).

**Medieval hospital gardens:** Calendula was grown in medieval monastery hospital gardens throughout Europe as a standard wound-care herb — part of the same tradition that included milk thistle, feverfew, and plantain. The medieval infirmarer (hospital herb keeper) would maintain calendula as a primary wound-dressing plant. This institutional continuity preserved calendula's therapeutic knowledge through the period between classical antiquity and Renaissance botanical scholarship.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Triterpene saponins (5–10% of flowers — primary wound-healing compounds):**
- **Oleanolic acid glycosides** (calendulosides A–H): oleanolic acid is a pentacyclic triterpene with documented anti-inflammatory, anti-tumor, wound-healing properties
- **Ursolic acid glycosides:** related triterpene; anti-inflammatory; anti-tumor in vitro; fibroblast stimulant
- **Faradiol monoester:** sterol ester found in calendula; anti-inflammatory in ear edema assay; the key anti-inflammatory compound in some analyses
- **Baccharis oxide:** triterpene epoxide
- **Calenduladiol:** another calendula-specific triterpene

**Flavonoids (3–5%):**
- **Isorhamnetin** and **isorhamnetin glycosides:** major; anti-inflammatory; UV-protective
- **Quercetin** and **quercetin glycosides:** antioxidant; anti-inflammatory
- **Narcissin** (=isorhamnetin-3-rutinoside): characteristic marker compound
- **Isoquercitrin:** quercetin-3-glucoside; bioavailable flavonoid form

**Carotenoids (0.2–0.5%):**
- **Lutein** (xanthophyll): highest carotenoid content; macular protective (same compound supplemented for age-related macular degeneration)
- **Zeaxanthin:** macular protective co-pigment
- **Beta-carotene:** vitamin A precursor; antioxidant
- These carotenoids accumulate in chromoplasts of the ray florets; their concentration correlates with flower color intensity

**Essential oils (0.2–0.4% of dry flowers):**
- **alpha-Cadinene** and **alpha-Cadinol**
- **Caryophyllene oxide**
- **Linalool oxide** (responsible for the characteristic calendula scent)
- **T-Muurolol**

**Polysaccharides:**
- Heteroglycans and arabinogalactans — immunostimulatory (minor; less prominent than echinacea polysaccharides)

**Resins:** 4–8% — complex mixture contributing to antimicrobial activity

**Phytosterols:** beta-sitosterol, stigmasterol, campesterol

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

### 5A. Wound Healing (Primary Clinical Mechanism)

**Fibroblast stimulation:**
- Oleanolic and ursolic acid glycosides → fibroblast migration and proliferation (scratch assay in vitro; skin wound healing in vivo)
- Collagen type I synthesis upregulation → wound tensile strength restoration
- Angiogenesis: saponins → VEGF expression in wound bed → neovascularization → improved oxygen/nutrient delivery

**Re-epithelialization:**
- Calendula preparations → keratinocyte migration stimulation → faster wound closure
- Wound contraction: calendula promotes myofibroblast activity → accelerated wound contraction

**Anti-inflammatory at wound site:**
- Faradiol monoester → potent anti-inflammatory (more potent than indomethacin in carrageenan ear edema model at equivalent topical dose)
- Isorhamnetin → COX-2 inhibition → reduced PGE₂ at wound site → controlled inflammation (preventing excessive inflammatory damage while allowing healing cytokine signal)
- Quercetin → 5-LOX inhibition → reduced leukotriene B₄ → less neutrophil recruitment → prevents wound degradation by excessive neutrophil enzymes

**Antimicrobial:**
- Calendula essential oil → membrane disruption of *S. aureus*, *E. coli*, *C. albicans* (MIC values in achievable topical concentration range)
- Saponins → detergent-like membrane disruption of bacteria and fungi
- Resin fraction → broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity

### 5B. Anti-inflammatory (Systemic)

- Oleanolic acid → IκBα stabilization → NF-κB nuclear entry inhibition → reduced TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 production
- Ursolic acid → COX-1 and COX-2 inhibition → anti-inflammatory, analgesic
- Quercetin → NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition → reduced IL-1β activation

### 5C. Radiation Dermatitis Protection

- Carotenoids → antioxidant → protection against ROS generated by ionizing radiation in skin
- Anti-inflammatory saponins → reduction of radiation-induced inflammatory cascade in skin (COX-2/PGE₂ axis)
- Wound healing mechanisms → promotion of skin repair between radiation fractions
- This mechanism complex makes calendula biologically plausible for radiation dermatitis prevention

### 5D. Lymphagogue Effect

- Minor lymphatic drainage promotion (parallel to coumarin in sweet clover I-597 but via different mechanism)
- Traditional use: calendula poultices for swollen lymph nodes; mechanism: saponin → lymphatic endothelial stimulation

### 5E. Choleretic Effects

- Oleanolic acid → hepatocyte bile acid secretion → increased bile flow
- Traditional use: calendula tea for liver and gallbladder support; mechanism plausible but clinical evidence absent

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

| Indication | Evidence Level | Key Data |
|------------|----------------|----------|
| Episiotomy wound healing | **B** | Pommert 1991 (n=100; calendula vs. petroleum jelly; faster healing, less infection); Basch 2006 review confirms wound healing data |
| Radiation dermatitis prevention | **B** | Pommert 2004 RCT (n=254 breast cancer RT patients; calendula vs. trolamine; calendula superior for acute dermatitis prevention: 41% vs. 63% Grade 2+; p<0.001) — Level A quality data; widely cited |
| Oral mucositis (radiation) | **B** | Calendula mouthwash; several pilot RCTs; comparable to chlorhexidine for Grade 1–2 mucositis; less data than for topical wound healing |
| Wound healing (general surgical) | **B** | Multiple small RCTs; consistent direction of benefit; chronic wound healing; venous leg ulcers |
| Diaper dermatitis (infants) | **B** | Calendula cream vs. petroleum jelly; Mahmoudi 2020 RCT (n=60 infants; calendula group significantly better) |
| Vaginal Candida infection | **C** | In vitro antifungal + small pilot; insufficient evidence |
| Pressure ulcers | **C** | Case series; no RCT |
| Systemic anti-inflammatory | **D** | In vitro and animal data; no clinical trial for oral use |
| Ophthalmic (eye drops) | **C** | Lutein/zeaxanthin content; eye-drop formulations in use; clinical evidence limited |

**Regulatory:**
- Commission E (Germany) approved: topical for poorly healing wounds, leg ulcers, inflammation of oral and pharyngeal mucosa, and inflammation of the skin
- EMA HMPC monograph: wound healing topical application
- Most topical preparations: 2–5% calendula extract in cream, ointment, or tincture

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**The viscosity of the resin:** Picking calendula flowers leaves a distinctively sticky, slightly bitter resin on the fingers from the trichome secretions — this stickiness is a sensory register of the terpenoid content (the same compounds responsible for wound healing). The flower is pharmacologically "giving" its medicine through touch, a tactile experience of the secretory trichomes that alerted traditional healers to the plant's wound-healing potential.

**The sun-tracking flower:** Calendula flowers track the sun (heliotropic) and close at night, opening again each morning — behavior described by medieval herbalists and inspiring folk names like "Follow-the-Sun." This was interpreted in doctrine of signatures as the plant's connection to solar/heat/anti-inflammatory principles. The modern mechanism (circadian phototropin signaling) provides a different but equally remarkable explanation.

**Mary's Gold in the hospital garden:** The monastery garden of the high medieval period was Western Europe's pharmaceutical infrastructure. Calendula — "Mary's Gold" — was grown in virtually every monastic herb garden from the 12th century onwards. The Benedictine, Dominican, and Franciscan orders maintained these gardens as part of their hospitaller mission; calendula's role in wound care was documented in their herbals, preserving pharmaceutical knowledge through what might otherwise have been a dark pharmacological age. When modern oncology researchers studied calendula for radiation dermatitis in the 1990s, they were testing a hypothesis that medieval monks had already validated empirically.

**Orange dye and cheese coloring:** Calendula has been used as a textile dye and, notably, as a natural coloring agent for butter and cheese (replacing expensive saffron). This culinary role maintained the plant in cultivation throughout Europe independently of its medicinal uses — ensuring its availability as a crop when needed medicinally.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Topical wound healing:**
- 2–5% calendula extract in cream, ointment, or gel base; apply 2–3 times daily
- For clean wounds: calendula promotes re-epithelialization; optimal after wound closure, not on open bleeding wounds
- Calendula tincture (1:5 in 70% ethanol): dilute 1:5 in sterile water for wound irrigation/compress

**Radiation dermatitis prevention:**
- Begin calendula cream application from first day of radiation; apply after each fraction; continue throughout treatment course
- Evidence-based choice: the Pommert 2004 trial is compelling (Level A quality); now recommended in many European oncology nursing protocols
- Apply immediately after radiation treatment; allow 30 minutes before applying to avoid interaction with radiation (theoretical concern, no evidence of concern in practice)

**Oral mucositis:**
- Calendula infusion as mouthwash: 2 tablespoons dried flowers per 500 mL boiling water, steep 15 min, strain; rinse 4 times daily during radiation/chemotherapy
- Calendula standardized mouthwash (commercial): per product dosing

**Diaper dermatitis:**
- Calendula cream safe for infants; preferred over petroleum jelly per Mahmoudi 2020
- Apply at each diaper change; natural choice for parents seeking gentle alternatives

**Safety:**
- Exceptional topical safety; minimal systemic absorption
- Asteraceae allergy: same cross-reactivity concern as echinacea, milk thistle, chamomile; perform patch test for highly ragweed-sensitive patients
- Oral use: safe in culinary amounts; no significant adverse events at standard tea doses
- Pregnancy: topical use widely regarded as safe; oral high-dose preparations — insufficient data; avoid concentrated extracts

**Drug interactions:**
- CNS depressants: calendula traditionally associated with sedation (weak); theoretical additive with sedative medications
- Topical interactions: none clinically significant

**Cross-reference:** I-599 Milk Thistle (Asteraceae; wound/hepatic tissue repair — different target organs, similar structural repair orientation); I-600 Echinacea (Asteraceae; immune activation vs. calendula's anti-inflammatory/healing; complementary clinical applications for infection with wound); I-602 Chamomile (Asteraceae; topical anti-inflammatory with overlapping applications — chamomile chamazulene vs. calendula faradiol monoester; both commission E approved for topical use); I-591 Aloe (topical wound healing comparison — aloe acemannan vs. calendula triterpenoids; both meta-analysis-supported for burn/wound healing; combinable)
