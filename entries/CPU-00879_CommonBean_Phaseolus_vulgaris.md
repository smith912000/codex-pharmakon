# Common Bean — Kidney Bean / Haricot / Frijol / Lūbiyā

## *Phaseolus vulgaris* L. | Family: Fabaceae

**Tradition Tags:** DMM (Dioscorides), CM (Avicenna), TCM (Chinese medicine), BM (Badianus)
**Energetic Classification:** Cool and dry in the first degree; the pod-decoction drying and diuretic (Galenic reading, applied to the old-world "bean" concept — see philological note)
**Training Phase:** Grounding / Metabolic Regulation

---

### Origin & Tradition

A philological warning must open this entry, because it governs everything that follows. *Phaseolus vulgaris* is a New World domesticate — the wild ancestor grows from Mexico to the Andes, and archaeobotany places its cultivation in the Americas millennia before any contact with the Mediterranean or Chinese worlds. It reached the Old World only through the Columbian Exchange, from the sixteenth century onward. This means that when Dioscorides, Avicenna, the Chinese classics, or the Aztec Badianus herbal name "beans," they were **not** naming the plant this entry is filed under. The classical *phaselos / phaseolus* of the Greeks and Romans, and the *lūbiyā* of the Arabic physicians, most probably denoted the cowpea (*Vigna unguiculata*) or related old-world legumes; the Chinese *dòu* is a whole family of pulses (soybean, adzuki, mung, cowpea). We therefore attach the ancient doctrines to an **old-world "bean" concept** and let the modern American species inherit that conceptual slot — a graft, not a continuity. Every classical attribution below carries this asterisk.

**Dioscorides (De Materia Medica).** In the *Materia Medica*, the legume grouped as *phaselos* is described as flatulent, of moderate nourishment, and mildly diuretic; the broths of pulses were used to loosen or bind the belly depending on preparation. The signal here is real and durable across the Greek tradition: pulses as food-medicine, sitting on the boundary between diet and drug. Attribution attaches to a *Vigna*/old-world legume, not to the American kidney bean.

**Avicenna (Canon of Medicine).** The Arabic physicians treat *lūbiyā* within the dietetics of pulses: nourishing but wind-producing, requiring correction with warming spices (cumin, pepper) to offset the cool-moist, flatulent quality. The humoral logic — that dense, cool, earthy foods breed *flatus* and phlegm unless "opened" — is the operative doctrine, and it transferred wholesale onto the New World bean when it arrived, because the sensory profile (dense, starchy, gas-producing) matched.

**Shennong / Chinese materia medica (SBJ).** The Chinese pulse tradition (the *dòu* group) assigns beans to the Spleen and Stomach, as dampness-draining, diuretic, and Spleen-supporting foods. After introduction, *Phaseolus vulgaris* (Chinese: *cài dòu* / *sì jì dòu*) was absorbed into this existing framework — again a conceptual graft, the American species filling a slot defined by old-world legumes.

**Badianus Manuscript (SBJ — the 1552 Aztec herbal).** Here, and only here, the attribution is philologically clean: the Badianus Codex is a post-Conquest Nahua document, so its *etl* (bean) genuinely is a *Phaseolus*. Mesoamerican use of bean plants — as staple sustenance and in compound remedies — is the one tradition in this entry naming the actual species.

**Doctrinal & Symbolic Layer:** The unifying doctrine across the Old World readings is "cool, dense, earthy, wind-breeding food that must be opened." Map this onto real chemistry and it is strikingly literal. The bean's flatulence is caused by α-galactoside oligosaccharides (raffinose, stachyose) that human enzymes cannot cleave, so colonic bacteria ferment them to gas — the humoral "wind" *is* microbial fermentation of indigestible sugars. The traditional correction (long soaking, hard boiling, carminative spices) genuinely reduces these oligosaccharides and eases the effect. The "cool and drying" pod-tea used as a diuretic maps onto a genuinely potassium-rich, low-sodium decoction. The doctrine is not mystical padding here; it is a pre-chemical description of a real carbohydrate-fermentation phenomenon, and the folk correction is a real mitigation. Where the doctrine over-reaches — treating the bean as a mild "cleanser" or slimming food — modern data grade it modest at best, as below.

### Active Components

- **Phaseolamin (α-amylase inhibitor, α-AI1):** a glycoprotein concentrated in white kidney bean seed; the basis of "starch-blocker" supplements. Inhibits pancreatic α-amylase, blunting the digestion of complex starch.
- **Phytohaemagglutinin (PHA):** a red-cell-agglutinating lectin, highest in raw red/kidney beans. Heat-labile — destroyed by proper boiling; the acute toxin of undercooked beans.
- **α-Galactoside oligosaccharides:** raffinose, stachyose — the flatulence factors.
- **Resistant starch and soluble fibre:** contribute to the low glycaemic index and satiety.
- **Polyphenols / flavonoids and saponins:** antioxidant fractions (higher in coloured seed coats).
- **Protein (~20–25%), lysine-rich** but methionine-limited — the classic legume amino-acid profile.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Within Kenetica the common bean sits in the **Grounding / Metabolic Regulation** phase as a food-medicine rather than an acute remedy. Two vectors matter: (1) the whole cooked bean as a low-glycaemic, satiating staple that steadies post-prandial glucose through fibre and resistant starch; (2) the standardised white-bean seed extract (phaseolamin) as a targeted α-amylase inhibitor taken before starch-heavy meals to blunt carbohydrate absorption. The traditional pod-tea (the green pod decoction, long used in European and Latin American folk practice as a diuretic and "antidiabetic" drink) is the bridge between the two — a gentle whole-plant preparation. Kenetica treats the extract as an adjunct to caloric restriction, never a substitute, in line with the honest trial data below.

### Scientific Correlation

According to PubMed, the modern "starch-blocker" claim rests on genuine but modest randomised evidence, and the honest reading deflates the marketing while vindicating a small real effect.

- A 12-week, double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT of a proprietary standardised white-kidney-bean extract (Phase 2, standardised by α-amylase-inhibitor activity), 700 or 1000 mg three times daily during calorie restriction, reported dose-dependent, statistically significant reductions in body weight, fat mass, BMI and waist/hip circumference versus placebo, and was well tolerated (Jäger et al., 2024, *Sci Rep*; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02930668) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-63443-8).
- A separate randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of a white-kidney-bean extract (2,400 mg/day before meals for 35 days) in obese subjects reported a mean loss of ~2.24 kg vs ~0.29 kg on placebo, with reduced BMI and body-fat percentage and no adverse effects (Wang et al., 2020, *Food Sci Nutr*) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/fsn3.1299).

**Evidence grade — MODEST / cautious.** Both positive trials are small, short (5–12 weeks) and, notably, industry-sponsored (Increnovo/Iovate/InQpharm; COFCO). Effect sizes are real but small and were obtained *on top of* calorie restriction — the extract is an adjunct, not a stand-alone weight-loss drug. The mechanism (pancreatic α-amylase inhibition by phaseolamin, blunting complex-carbohydrate breakdown) is biochemically coherent and directly explains the traditional pod-tea's reputation as an "antidiabetic/slimming" drink, but the honest verdict is: a small, meal-timed, starch-specific effect, easily washed out by simple sugars (which α-amylase inhibition does not touch) and by non-adherence. The classical "cooling, draining, diuretic" pod doctrine is thus **partly** vindicated (a genuine mechanism, a genuine but modest outcome) and **partly** deflated (it is not a metabolic cure).

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **TCM:** Beans (*dòu* group) — Spleen/Stomach channels; sweet, neutral-to-cool; tonify Spleen, drain dampness, promote urination. *Phaseolus vulgaris* absorbed into this frame post-introduction.
- **Ayurveda:** Common bean not classically attested (New World plant); pulses broadly *madhura/kashaya rasa* (sweet/astringent), *vata*-aggravating (wind-producing), *sheeta virya* (cooling) — applied by analogy, not by classical text. Mark: **not classically attested.**
- **Galenic / Unani:** *lūbiyā* — cool and moist, flatulent, nourishing; corrected with warming carminatives. (Attaches to old-world legume concept.)

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** Two disqualifications stack here. First, Dioscorides carries no subtle-body system to assign any substance a cultivation status — his *phaselos* is described as flatulent, of moderate nourishment and mildly diuretic, which is a dietetic-therapeutic account, not a doctrine of what the pulse does to a person's vital constitution. Second, the *phaselos* he names is almost certainly an old-world legume (a *Vigna* or relative), not the American *Phaseolus vulgaris* this entry is filed under; the classical attribution reaches this species only by the philological graft flagged at the head of the entry. What the tradition genuinely offers a cultivator is the Dioscoridean discipline of attention — judging a pulse by preparation, knowing that the broth loosens or binds the belly according to how it is cooked — a practice of provenance and sensory assay rather than a vital-body claim. Cultivation status: **(c) absent.**

**Unani — Avicenna (CM).** The Arabic physicians place *lūbiyā* squarely within the dietetics of pulses: nourishing but wind-producing, cool and moist, requiring correction with warming carminatives (cumin, pepper) to "open" the *flatus* and phlegm that dense earthy foods breed. This is a real humoral frame, and the Unani corpus does hold the strongest cultivation idea in the whole book — the conservation of innate heat and radical moisture across a lifetime — but that is a longevity principle of the whole regimen, not a status the tradition assigns to this particular cool-moist pulse, and no *mufarrih* or cardiac-*ruh* role is recorded for it. The frame is dietetic, and it attaches to the old-world legume concept, inherited by the American bean only by the same graft. **(b) framed:** a humoral-dietetic status is attested, but no cultivation status is sourced.

**Chinese — later materia medica (TCM).** The bean enters Chinese medicine through the *dòu* pulse group — assigned to the Spleen and Stomach, sweet and neutral-to-cool, tonifying the Spleen, draining dampness and promoting urination. *Phaseolus vulgaris* (*cài dòu* / *sì jì dòu*) was absorbed into this pre-existing frame only after its post-Columbian introduction, so any Chinese standing it holds is late and grafted, not classical. No *san pin* 三品 grade — the upper/middle/lower hierarchy that would mark a substance as one taken long-term to nourish life within *yang sheng* 養生 — is recorded for this species in the sources retrieved; its standing is a Spleen-and-damp dietetic one, therapeutic rather than cultivational. **(b) framed:** attested in the *dòu* frame, no *san pin* grade sourced, and post-introduction in any case.

**Nahua — Badianus (BM).** This is the one tradition in the entry that names the actual species: the Badianus manuscript of 1552 is a post-Conquest Nahua document, so its *etl* genuinely is a *Phaseolus*, used as staple sustenance and in compound remedies. But the manuscript is itself naturalistic and does **not** contain the *tonalli* / *teyolia* / *ihiyotl* model of the person; that vital-soul scheme is reconstructed from later ethnohistory (López Austin, *The Human Body and Ideology*), not read off the Badianus text, and the manuscript records the bean as food and remedy, not as something worked on a person's vital endowment. No cultivation status is attested. **(c) absent.**

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here is a cultivation substance, and the honest reason is doubled: the American bean inherits the old-world doctrines only by a conceptual graft, and even the tradition that names the true species (Nahua) records it as a staple food, not a vitality-building agent. A cultivator's work with the common bean is entirely at the level of diet and metabolism — the hard-boiled whole bean as a low-glycaemic, satiating staple, the meal-timed white-bean extract as a modest starch-blunting adjunct to caloric restriction. There is no long-term "nourish life" claim to lean on in any of the four traditions; the classical grading offers dietetic correction (soak, boil, add carminatives), not a longevity status. Treat it as food, prepared correctly, and nothing more.

### Contraindications

- **⚠️ RAW / UNDERCOOKED KIDNEY BEAN POISONING — the critical hazard.** Raw and undercooked red/white kidney beans contain phytohaemagglutinin (PHA), a lectin causing acute gastroenteritis — nausea, violent vomiting and diarrhoea within 1–3 hours. As few as four or five raw beans can trigger it; slow-cookers that never reach a true boil can *increase* toxicity by warming without destroying the lectin. Beans must be soaked and then **hard-boiled at a rolling boil for at least 10 minutes.** According to PubMed, a 2018 French military-base outbreak affected ~200 people after undercooked kidney beans in chili con carne delivered PHA above the toxic threshold (Watier-Grillot et al., 2023, *Toxins*) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/toxins15070457).
- **α-Amylase-inhibitor extracts:** may cause flatulence, bloating and loose stools (undigested starch reaching the colon). Theoretical interaction with acarbose and with anti-diabetic regimens — monitor glucose; discuss with a clinician if on hypoglycaemic drugs. Not established as safe in pregnancy/lactation at supplement doses — [USER: verify]; treat as food only in pregnancy.
- **Oligosaccharide intolerance / IBS:** whole beans aggravate FODMAP-sensitive guts.
- Isolated PHA is a laboratory mitogen/neuroanatomical tracer (its agglutinating potency is exactly why it is toxic when eaten raw) — underscoring that the hazard is intrinsic, not incidental.
