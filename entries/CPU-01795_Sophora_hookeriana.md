# Sophora hookeriana — Solgongpa, sol gong pa, "Yellow soroseris" (rGyud-bzhi, Men-Tsee-Khang glossary by column position; Clark 1995 yu.mo.srol.gong.wa, line 1011, prints "Soroseris hookeriana")

## *Sophora hookeriana, as the Men-Tsee-Khang 2011 glossary prints it on line 5830, the tenth scientific name of its "S" block, which column order sets against "Solgongpa" (sol gong pa) and the English "Yellow soroseris"; the English name and Clark 1995 (Soroseris hookeriana Stebb, line 1011) both point to the genus Soroseris, so "Sophora" may be a slip in the glossary, and the Codex keeps the name as printed* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** FMT

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by the Tibetan texts (FMT); this entry rests on one text, the rGyud-bzhi, read in two modern editions.** The editions are Clark 1995 and Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, both in `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/text/`. They are two translations of one tradition's text and are not two independent traditions. A search of the Dioscorides, Ebers, Assyrian and Sushruta text folders on disk for *Sophora* and *Soroseris* found no hit. The Shennong text (Yang 1998) prints *Sophora* once, for Ku Shen (*Sophora flavescens*, line 2761; I_1358), a different species in another tradition, and it is not read as this plant. The row was added from a printed name (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. The Codex does not state the botanical identity as certain. [USER: verify]

**Row key.** In `Sources/FMT_Tibetan/extract/names.csv` (line 364) the row is keyed by the **scientific name** "Sophora hookeriana", with the locus "Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients (line 5830)" and the note "Tibetan name not paired (OCR column split)". The line prints a single binomial, so the key is also the first and only name on its line. The Tibetan name *Solgongpa* is reached from this row by column position, as set out below. Clark's entry for the same Tibetan name has **no row** in `names.csv` (a search for "srol" and "Soroseris" found none), so the Clark passage below was found by following the name.

**Identity doubt.** This is the weakest identification in the batch, and the reasons are in the texts. The glossary prints "Sophora hookeriana" against "Solgongpa", but the English name printed beside it, in the same stretch and in the same position, is "Yellow soroseris" (line 5832), which is a name for the daisy-family genus *Soroseris* and not for a *Sophora*. Clark's entry for the matching Tibetan name (below) prints "a type of Soroseris hookeriana Stebb: Lactuca sibirica", and Clark's Latin index lists *Soroseris hookeriana* (line 1647) and no *Sophora hookeriana*. The Codex therefore reads "Sophora" in the glossary as a probable slip for "Soroseris", and it **keeps the printed name** because the spine, the row and the batch are keyed to it and because it cannot check the glossary's own source. The reading is the Codex's inference, not a statement of either text. The Men-Tsee-Khang editors' note (line 686) says that its botanical terms rest on the information of its own Materia Medica Department. Clark's preface says that "more than one Latin name is frequently given for one substance" (line 83) and that "the data given in some of these is occasionally suspect" (line 68). A *Sophora* is a legume and a *Soroseris* is a daisy; the two are not the same plant, and the drug's identity is open. [USER: verify]

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, glossary (the "S" block).* `MenTseeKhang_2011_Root_and_Explanatory_Tantra.txt`, Glossary on Medicinal Ingredients. The glossary prints its columns in separate runs, and the columns do not hold the same number of entries. The page that begins at line 5774 prints seventeen phonetic names, from "Shingngar" to "Sumchutik", of which "Solgongpa" is the tenth (line 5779: "Sok kapa Solgongpa"). The transliterations (lines 5787 to 5796) include "sol gong pa" (line 5793). The scientific names begin at line 5798: "Glycyrrhiza glabr" (so printed), "Cinnamomum zeylannicum", "Rumex nepalensis", "Rhus vemiciflua", "-", "Acorus calamus L.", "Juniperus communis", "Trigonella foenum", "Sesbania sp.", "Capsella bursa-pastoris" and **"Sophora hookeriana"** (line 5830), then "Rhodiola crenulata", "Pegaeophyton sacapiflorum", "Abelmochus manihot", "Anemone tatrasepala", "Androsace", "Phaeonychium parryoides", "-" and "Elettaria cardamomum". The English names are interleaved: "Licorice", "Cinnamon", "Field sorrel", "-", "Sweet Flag", "Juniper", "Fenugreek", "Inkberry, sesban", "Shephard's purse", **"Yellow soroseris"** (line 5832), "Himalayan stonecrop" (5836), "Musk Okra" (5842), and so on to "Cardamom". Counted in order, "Solgongpa" is the tenth phonetic name, "sol gong pa" the tenth transliteration, *Sophora hookeriana* the tenth scientific name and "Yellow soroseris" the tenth English name. The count is checked on "Sok kapa" against *Capsella bursa-pastoris* and "Shephard's purse" (ninth), on "Solo" against *Rhodiola crenulata* and "Himalayan stonecrop" (eleventh) and on "Sumchutik" against *Elettaria cardamomum* and "Cardamom" (seventeenth). The glossary does not print the pairing in one row, so it is by position only. [USER: verify]

*Men-Tsee-Khang 2011, running text (line 3432).* Chapter 20, "The Efficacy of Medicinal Substances" (contents, line 225; colophon at line 3583), within the run headed "To reveal the efficacy of herbal medicines" (line 3428): "Solgongpa heals fractured skulls and cures hot disorder associated with poisoning. Yumo dejin expels dead fetuses and foreign bodies." The sentence stands after "Jagoepoe cures disorders caused by evil spirits, poisoning and relieves contagious hot disorder". The printed index lists "Solgongpa 207" (line 7498). "Hot disorder" is the translators' word, kept as printed. The sentence gives no part used, no preparation and no taste.

*Clark 1995, the same Tibetan name (read through the name, not the binomial).* `Clark_1995_Quintessence_Tantras_Tibetan_Medicine.txt`, Explanatory Tantra chapter 20 ("The Principles of Medicines: Powers of Medicines", line 792), within "(7) Exposition of the Powers of Herbs" (heading at line 997). The entry stands in a long OCR line (line 1011) that holds many entries, after *gang.ga.chhung* and *bya.rgod.spos* and before *yu.mo.mde.hbyin*:

"yu.mo.srol.gong.wa: a type of Soroseris hookeriana Stebb: Lactuca sibirica: [grows in rocky meadows and has bluish-green leaves similar to those of dandelion. It has a hollow, bulky, combshaped flower of blue, yellow or white type. The root is extremely bitter and] heals skull fractures and fever with poisoning."

Clark's preface (line 82) says that interpolations in square brackets come from the Blue Aquamarine and Spreading Tree commentaries. The habitat, the leaves, the flower and the root are therefore the commentary layer. Outside the brackets, as Clark's marks stand, are the Tibetan name, the two Latin names, which are Clark's identifications, and "heals skull fractures and fever with poisoning". Clark's name index prints the Tibetan name as *yu.mo.srol.gang.wa* (line 1749), with "gang" where the entry has "gong".

The two editions agree closely in kind, and in order: Clark's drug "heals skull fractures and fever with poisoning", the Men-Tsee-Khang *Solgongpa* "heals fractured skulls and cures hot disorder associated with poisoning", and in each edition the next named drug is the one that expels a dead fetus (Clark's *yu.mo.mde.hbyin*, the Men-Tsee-Khang *Yumo dejin*). The Codex reads *yu.mo.srol.gong.wa* and *Solgongpa* as one drug from that agreement and from the sequence; neither text says so. The Codex's own list of rGyud-bzhi names (`_sources/rgyud-bzhi.csv`, line 257) carries "Solgongpa" with no botanical name and the identity flagged uncertain. [USER: verify]

**Overlap.** A search of the entries folder for *Sophora hookeriana*, *Soroseris*, *Lactuca sibirica*, *Solgongpa* and *srol.gong* found no entry on this drug. **I_1264** (*Delphinium brunonianum*, tag FMT) reads the same long line 1011 for the entry directly before this one (*bya.rgod.spos*). **I_1229** (*Anemone tatrasepala*, tag FMT) reads the same "S" block and lists "Sophora hookeriana" among the scientific names, and I_1306 (*Lactuca dissecta*) is a different *Lactuca* under a different Tibetan name. **I_1358** (*Sophora flavescens*, SBJ) and **I_1360** (*Sophora moorcroftiana*, FMT) are entries of the genus *Sophora* from the same batch. Nothing is merged.

### Active Components

The sources read print no constituent. It is a general fact that *Sophora* is a genus of the legume family (Fabaceae), many of whose species contain quinolizidine alkaloids, and that *Soroseris* and *Lactuca*, the genera Clark prints, belong to the daisy family (Asteraceae); the printed names therefore point to plants of two families, and these are statements about the genera and not about the Tibetan drug. The lines read give no composition for it, and nothing is stated here about what it contains. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Tibetan texts record a drug that "heals skull fractures and fever with poisoning" (Clark, line 1011) and "heals fractured skulls and cures hot disorder associated with poisoning" (Men-Tsee-Khang, line 3432). These are the texts' claims, attached to this binomial only through a glossary count, and they are not Codex protocols. A head injury, a fracture and a poisoning each need emergency medical care, and the Codex gives no protocol and no dose for them. If the glossary's "Sophora" is read as a slip for "Soroseris", the drug is not a *Sophora* at all, and anything said of *Sophora* would then not apply.

### Preparation & Dosing

The sources read give no preparation, no part used beyond the commentary-layer remark that "the root is extremely bitter" (which does not name the root as the drug), no quantity, no frequency and no duration. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **FMT:** Clark's line 1011 prints "extremely bitter" for the root, inside the brackets, so it belongs to the commentary layer. It prints no power or post-digestive taste. The Men-Tsee-Khang lines read (3432, 5779, 5793, 5830, 5832) print no taste, power or post-digestive taste for *Solgongpa*.

### Cultivation Register

**Tibetan — rGyud-bzhi (FMT).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* The Quintessence Tantra's materia medica gives this drug by what it does: it heals skull fractures and fever with poisoning, and the Men-Tsee-Khang text says the same in its own words. The cultivation spec records that in this tradition medicine is the third of four treatment branches, supporting practice without reaching its root, and that no formal category of substances for subtle-body practice exists. A search of both Tibetan texts for the drug's names (*yu.mo.srol.gong.wa*, *Solgongpa*, *Sophora hookeriana*, *Soroseris*) found it only in the lines cited above, the glossary and the indexes. It is not in the rejuvenation chapter (Men-Tsee-Khang chapter 23, colophon at line 3950), and the herb entries of Clark's chapter 20 that mention essence extraction (lines 971, 1043, 1078, 1103, 1121 and 1144) do not include it. Its framing is therapeutic only.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the lines read gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. It is a fracture and poison-fever drug with no preparation and no dose, and its Latin name is, on the texts' own evidence, probably a printing slip. The abstentive answer is the honest one.

### Contraindications

The sources read print no warning for this drug. Fractures, head injuries, fever and poisoning need medical care; the identity is open between two genera, no preparation or dose is given, and no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. It is a general fact that many *Sophora* species contain alkaloids that are toxic in excess, which is a further reason to leave the plant alone while its genus is unsettled. No conservation information and no safety information for pregnancy were read. [USER: verify]
