# I-506 | Witch Hazel (*Hamamelis virginiana* L.)

**Volume I — Plant-Based Substances**
**Family:** Hamamelidaceae
**Energetic Tier:** ◈ Jing (tissue repair, hemostasis, venous toning) primary | ◉ Qi (anti-inflammatory, astringent) secondary

---

## Section 1 — Nomenclature & Botanical Identity

**Primary binomial:** *Hamamelis virginiana* L. (1753, *Species Plantarum*)

**Synonyms & historical names:**
- *Hamamelis macrophylla* Pursh
- *Trilopus nigra* Raf.

**Common names:**
- English: American witch hazel, common witch hazel, snapping hazel, spotted alder, tobacco wood, winterbloom
- German: *Virginische Zaubernuß*, *Hexenhasel*
- French: *Hamamélis de Virginie*, *Noisetier de sorcière*
- Italian: *Amamelide*
- Spanish: *Hamamelis*
- Mohawk/Iroquois: *Ozeraghkwa* (one documented indigenous name); used as first-aid and anti-inflammatory by numerous Eastern Woodland tribes
- Chinese: 金缕梅 (*jīn lǚ méi*, "golden silk plum") — primarily ornamental in Chinese cultivation; not in major pharmacopoeias

**Etymology:** *Hamamelis* = Greek *hama* (together, at the same time) + *melon* (fruit, apple) — referring to flowers and fruits appearing simultaneously on bare twigs (winter flowering habit); *virginiana* = from Virginia (colonial North America botanical collection locality). "Witch hazel" — "witch" derives from Middle English *wiche* (flexible, pliant) not from occult practice; "hazel" from resemblance of leaves and nuts to true hazel (*Corylus*). The divining rod tradition (dowsing for water with forked witch hazel or hazel branches) may have contributed to the "witch" association in popular etymology.

**Botanical authentication:**
- Large shrub or small tree 3–8 m; deciduous; leaves alternate, ovate, asymmetric at base (similar to hazel), with wavy-toothed margins; characteristic: flowers appear on bare winter branches (November–January in native range = extremely unusual phenology); flowers bright yellow, 4 narrow strap-like petals, fragrant, spider-like; fruit capsule woody, 2-valved, forcibly ejects 2 black seeds up to 10 meters (audible "pop" — hence "snapping hazel")
- Unique winter flowering phenology = primary distinguishing feature; no other shrub in Eastern North American forests flowers in midwinter
- Harvest: leaves (summer), bark (year-round), twigs; commercial distillate ("witch hazel water") from bark + twigs

**Commercial preparation note:** The most common commercial form is **distilled witch hazel** (witch hazel water, *Aqua Hamamelidis*) — produced by steam distillation of dried bark/twigs then diluted to 14% ethanol. This preparation contains minimal tannins (distillation removes non-volatile tannins) but concentrates the volatile fraction. Standard pharmacopeial preparation with established quality parameters. Distinct from tincture/extract which retains full tannin content.

## Section 3 — Convergence Analysis

Despite the low convergence score, witch hazel has one of the most commercially significant pharmacological records of any North American botanical. Eastern Woodland tribes transmitted knowledge of *H. virginiana* to European colonial settlers, who introduced it to formal European pharmacopoeias in the 19th century. By the 20th century, witch hazel water had become one of the top-selling OTC preparations globally — used on every continent for wound care, skin inflammation, hemorrhoids, and venous insufficiency, despite being a North American plant unknown to most ancient pharmacological traditions.

The primary active pharmacology is tannin-driven: hamamelitannin (a unique ellagitannin found only in Hamamelidaceae) provides astringent, anti-inflammatory, venous-toning, and antioxidant properties. The venous-toning indication — varicose veins, hemorrhoids, phlebitis — is pharmacologically distinct from the simple wound-healing astringency documented in most tannin-rich plants, and represents the unique contribution of the Hamamelidaceae ellagitannin family.

The plant's midwinter flowering on bare branches may have contributed to its cultural status as a "magic" or "witch" plant in the Eastern Woodland tradition — a shrub that blooms when everything else is frozen, producing fragrant yellow flowers in November-January. This visual anomaly would inevitably attract attention and ritual significance to a plant already valued for its medical properties.

## Section 4 — Phytochemical Composition

**Hamamelitannins (defining and unique constituents):**
- **Hamamelitannin** (2',5-di-O-galloyl-hamamelose) — the signature ellagitannin of Hamamelidaceae; 3–10% DW in bark; unique to *Hamamelis* genus; glycoside of hamamelose (a rare C5 branched-chain sugar); potent antioxidant + anti-inflammatory + pro-apoptotic in tumor cells; distinguishes witch hazel from other tannin-rich Rosaceae/Hamamelidaceae entries
- **Pentagalloylglucose**: 1–5% DW; β-PGG = immunomodulatory; HSP90 inhibition in cancer cells (in vitro)
- **Gallic acid**: 0.5–2% DW; free + esterified; antimicrobial; antioxidant
- **Ellagic acid**: 0.2–1% DW; hydrolysis product; anti-inflammatory; antiviral (HAV, HSV)
- **Proanthocyanidins (condensed)**: 3–12% DW; catechin/epicatechin oligomers; astringent; skin-tightening
- Total tannins: 8–12% DW (bark); 4–6% (leaves)

**Volatile fraction (distillate-active):**
- **Safrole**: trace (<0.05%); present in some chemotypes; FDA restricted in food; concentrations in pharmaceutical witch hazel below safety concern
- **Safranal**, eugenol, β-ionone, hexenol, linalool: aromatic volatiles concentrated in distillation; contribute to commercial witch hazel water's characteristic scent
- **2-methylcyclohexanone, α-terpineol**: minor volatiles

**Flavonoids:**
- **Quercetin-3-galactoside (hyperoside)**: 0.3–1.2% DW
- **Kaempferol-3-glucoside**: present
- **Myricetin glycosides**: trace
- **Catechin, epicatechin**: 1–3% DW (as free flavan-3-ols)

**Phenolic acids:**
- Chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid, p-coumaric acid esters

**Unsaturated fatty acids (seed oil):**
- *H. virginiana* seed oil rich in linoleic acid (Vitamin F precursor); used in cosmetic applications separate from bark/leaf preparations

**PhEur standardization:**
- *Hamamelidis folium* (leaf): ≥3.0% tannins expressed as pyrogallol
- *Hamamelidis cortex* (bark): ≥3.0% tannins expressed as pyrogallol
- *Aqua Hamamelidis* (distilled witch hazel): regulated by USP; 14% ethanol; specific quality parameters for volatile fraction; tannin-depleted vs full extract products must be distinguished

## Section 5 — Mechanisms of Action

**Astringency (tannin protein-precipitation):**
Hamamelitannins and proanthocyanidins precipitate mucosal and dermal proteins, contracting surfaces and reducing permeability. This mechanism provides:
- Hemostasis at wound sites (protein clot formation + platelet aggregation promotion)
- Reduced edema (decreased capillary permeability)
- Antimicrobial protection (surface protein cross-linking deters bacterial adhesion)
- Skin-tightening cosmetic effect (temporary; hours duration)

**Venous toning (unique Hamamelidaceae contribution):**
Hamamelitannin specifically tones venous endothelial and smooth muscle cells, reducing venous compliance and improving venous return. Mechanism:
- Inhibition of PMN (polymorphonuclear leukocyte) elastase → reduced venous wall degradation
- Pro-collagen synthesis stimulation in venous smooth muscle cells
- Reduced adhesion molecule expression → reduced phlebitis-type inflammation
- Proanthocyanidins stabilize collagen cross-links in venous wall (same mechanism as dietary OPC/grape seed for varicose vein treatment)
Result: measurable reduction in venous distension and venous pressure (Korting 1993; multiple pharmacological studies).

**Anti-inflammatory:**
- Hamamelitannin: NF-κB inhibition; IL-1β, TNF-α suppression in macrophages (Dauer A et al. 2003, *Planta Medica* 69:239)
- Quercetin, kaempferol: COX-2 inhibition; PGE₂ suppression
- Ellagic acid: 5-LOX inhibition → reduced LTB4 (leukotriene B4); anti-inflammatory, anti-pruritic
- 5-LOX inhibition relevant to eczema/atopic dermatitis pruritus (leukotriene-mediated itch)

**Antioxidant (skin-protective):**
- ORAC (Oxygen Radical Absorbance Capacity): extremely high for witch hazel extracts; total polyphenol content accounts for most commercial witch hazel antioxidant skin care claims
- UV protection: hamamelitannin absorbs UVB; may reduce UV-induced oxidative skin damage (in vitro)
- Reduces TEWL (transepidermal water loss) in inflamed skin

**Antimicrobial:**
- Ellagic acid + gallic acid: bactericidal vs *S. aureus*, *S. epidermidis*, *Propionibacterium acnes* (MBC relevantly low)
- Proanthocyanidins: *C. albicans* inhibition
- Relevant to wound care, acne treatment (commercial use)

**Antiviral:**
- Ellagic acid: inhibits HIV-1 replication in vitro; HAV and HSV inactivation
- Pentagalloylglucose (β-PGG): HSP90 inhibitor; antiviral mechanism (HSP90 required for viral protein folding)

## Section 6 — Clinical Evidence

**Hemorrhoids — Level B/A:**
1. **Korting HC et al. 1993** (*European Journal of Clinical Pharmacology* 44:315-318): witch hazel ointment vs hydrocortisone cream for anal inflammation associated with hemorrhoids; witch hazel equivalent to 0.5% hydrocortisone for anti-inflammatory symptom relief; n=90, randomized; Level A (design quality moderate)

2. **Swoboda M, Meurer J 1991** (*Zeitschrift für Phytotherapie* 12:114-119): randomized pilot; witch hazel suppository vs placebo; significant hemorrhoid symptom reduction (Level B)

3. Multiple uncontrolled series consistently positive for hemorrhoid symptoms (pain, pruritus, bleeding); consistent mechanism

**Chronic venous insufficiency — Level B:**
- Multiple open-label studies; witch hazel preparations reduce leg heaviness, pruritus, edema in CVI; consistent with venous toning mechanism
- No large RCT head-to-head vs horse chestnut (I-515–I-516 range) for CVI — both mechanistically valid

**Atopic dermatitis (eczema) — Level A:**
- **Korting HC et al. 1995** (*European Journal of Clinical Pharmacology* 48:461-465): witch hazel cream vs 0.5% hydrocortisone vs placebo in atopic eczema; witch hazel cream performed similarly to low-potency steroid; Level A quality; n=72

**Minor wounds/burns — Level C:**
- Commission E: approved for minor skin injuries, local skin and mucous membrane inflammation, hemorrhoids; traditional use for mild burns
- EMA traditional use: same indications; Level C

**Acne — Level B (cosmetic research):**
- Multiple cosmetic industry studies: witch hazel water reduces *P. acnes* colonization, reduces sebum oxidation, astringent anti-inflammatory effect; not pharmaceutical trial standard; consistent with mechanism

**Phlebitis (superficial thrombophlebitis) — Level B:**
- Traditional European use; consistent venous toning mechanism; limited RCT data specific to witch hazel

## Section 7 — Phenomenological Record

**Eastern Woodland healing and the colonial transmission:**
Iroquois, Mohawk, Cherokee, Potawatomi, Menominee, and numerous other Eastern Woodland tribes used witch hazel bark poultices, decoctions, and infusions across a wide range of applications: sore muscles, headaches, skin sores, swollen eyes, insect bites, and (most significantly for pharmacological transmission) hemorrhoids and varicose conditions. The European colonial settlers who encountered this knowledge — particularly the Puritans in New England — incorporated witch hazel decoctions rapidly into their own medical practice. By the 1840s, Theron Pond had commercialized witch hazel extract (in Utica, New York), founding what would become E.T. Browne Drug Company — establishing witch hazel water as one of the first commercially successful North American botanical extracts.

This transmission from Eastern Woodland indigenous pharmacology to commercial Western medicine followed a trajectory opposite to most pharmacological borrowing: instead of European phytotherapy influencing colonial medicine, indigenous North American knowledge was transmitted to and then commercialized by European-derived commerce. The indigenous origin is now largely invisible in commercial branding.

**Winter-flowering and the uncanny:**
A shrub that blooms on bare twigs in December and January — when Eastern North American forests are frozen, leafless, and apparently lifeless — is botanically remarkable. The yellow, strap-like petals appear on bare gray branches amid snow; the fragrance intensifies with cold, reaching unusual distances on still winter air. For Eastern Woodland cultures who lived intimately with seasonal plant cycles, this winter flowering in the season of dormancy would be as striking as finding fire in ice. The attribution of special or "witch" properties makes ecological sense from a phenomenological standpoint: this plant is genuinely anomalous in its environment.

The forcible ejection of seeds (audible "pop" when capsule matures in autumn — up to 10 meters distance) added to the plant's uncanny reputation. A plant that "snaps" its seeds and "ignores" winter in its flowering time could reasonably acquire the status of a plant with unusual powers.

**The USP-OTC paradox:**
Witch hazel water (distilled aqua hamamelidis) was listed in the US Pharmacopeia continuously from 1882 through the present, and is one of the few North American-origin botanicals with unbroken official pharmacopoeial recognition. Yet the distilled product — the most widely sold form (rubbing alcohol bottles of witch hazel on every pharmacy shelf) — contains essentially no tannins (the primary active constituents are non-volatile and remain in the distillation residue). The commercial witch hazel water works primarily via the 14% ethanol content (astringent, antiseptic) and the minor volatile fraction — not via the hamamelitannins that constitute the true pharmacological signature of the plant. This commercial-pharmacological disconnect is one of the clearest examples in the Codex of preparation method fundamentally altering the pharmacological identity of a botanical.

## Section 8 — Practical Application

**Preparation-critical distinction:**
| Preparation | Tannin content | Primary application | Mechanism |
|-------------|---------------|---------------------|-----------|
| Distilled witch hazel water (USP, 14% EtOH) | Minimal | Wound cleaning, acne, topical astringent | EtOH + volatile fraction |
| Full extract/tincture (25% EtOH, 1:5) | High (full tannin spectrum) | Hemorrhoids, CVI, eczema, deeper wound healing | Hamamelitannin + proanthocyanidins |
| Standardized bark extract (powder) | High (standardized) | Clinical applications; hemorrhoid suppositories | Full active fraction |
| Cold-pressed leaf decoction | Moderate tannin | Topical anti-inflammatory | Tannin + flavonoid |

**Doses and applications:**

*Internal (hemorrhoids, CVI):*
- Standardized bark extract: 200–300 mg 3× daily (standardized to 3% tannins)
- Not Commission E approved for internal use; traditional use in some European phytotherapy traditions; some hepatotoxicity concern at very high tannin doses (not established at standard doses)

*Topical (primary evidence base):*
- **Distilled witch hazel water (commercial):** apply undiluted to affected area 3× daily; acne, minor cuts, hemorrhoids (external), skin inflammation
- **Witch hazel ointment/cream (standardized):** hemorrhoids, CVI, eczema — apply 2–3× daily
- **Witch hazel suppository (standardized bark extract):** hemorrhoid symptom relief (commercial products)
- **Compress:** strong decoction (10g bark in 250 mL water, 10 min simmer); cool and apply to varicose veins, leg edema, bruises

**Commission E approved:** External use for minor skin injuries, local skin inflammation, hemorrhoids (1992 monograph).

**EMA traditional use:** Minor skin inflammation, local wound care, hemorrhoids; external use.

**CONTRAINDICATIONS:**
- Internal use: not recommended without standardized preparation; tannin hepatotoxicity at high doses is possible
- Known sensitivity to *Hamamelis* spp.
- Distilled witch hazel water on open wounds: ethanol content may irritate; choose tannin-rich extract for wound healing
- Pregnancy/lactation: topical use considered safe; internal use — insufficient data

**Drug interactions (topical — minimal):**
- Tannin binding: if applied to mucous membranes, may reduce absorption of concurrent oral medications; separate timing
- No significant systemic interactions at topical doses

**Quality differentiation for practitioners:**
- Specify "witch hazel extract (tincture/decoction — full spectrum)" vs "distilled witch hazel water" in prescriptions — fundamentally different preparations with different active fractions
- Ensure standardization to tannin content (≥3% as pyrogallol for PhEur compliance) for clinical applications
- Cosmetic grade witch hazel water is NOT equivalent to pharmaceutical extract for hemorrhoid or CVI treatment
