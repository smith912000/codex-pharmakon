The chapters name the drug by the Sanskrit name only, spelled in the translation Ankota, Amkota, Amkotha, Ainkota and (in the OCR) Antkotha. A search of the name list of the Dioscorides source for *Alangium* found nothing that is this plant. **Ayurvedic — Sushruta (SS).** *The names in the chapters.* A search of the chapter files, allowing for the OCR's variants, found the drug on nine lines: Sutra 36; Chikitsa 19; Uttara 21; and Kalpa 1 (two lines), 5, 6 (two lines) and 7. A search for Kankarola found nothing. The passages were read.

*A washing decoction (Sutra 36, lines 89 to 94).* In the chapter of miscellaneous remedies for swellings, under a heading the OCR prints "ShOdhana Plasters": "A Kashaya (decoction) of Shankhini, Ankota, Sumanah, Karavira, and Suvarchchala, or of drugs belonging to the group (Ganas) known as the Aragvadadi-Varga, should be used in washing and purifying (asepsising) the contents of an ulcer, or a secreting swelling."

*A purifying oil (Chikitsa 19, lines 93 to 98).* In the chapter on hernia, hydrocele and diseases of the genitals, after a tumour has been opened, "the oil cooked with Jati, Arushkara, Ainkota and Sapta-parna should be used for the purification (disinfection) of the incidental ulcer".

*Ear (Uttara 21, lines 73 to 77).* In the chapter on ear diseases: "The four kinds of Sneha (oil, clarified-butter, lard and animal marrow) duly cooked together with the Kalka of Tanduliyaka, Amkota fruits, Ahimsra, Kendraka roots, Sarala, Deva-Daru, Lasuna (garlic), Shunthi and the scrapings of bamboo-skin and with the liquids of acid [things]". The text names the fruits.

*Poisoned food and drink (Kalpa 1, lines 274 to 283 and 388 to 397).* In the chapter on preserving food and drink from poison, for swelling of the lips, tongue and gums from a poisoned tooth-twig, the part is rubbed "with a plaster of powdered Amkotha roots or Saptachchada bark or seeds of Shirisha, pasted together with honey", and the same remedies are to be used for a poisoned tongue-cleanser or gargle. For a poisoned collyrium, an *Anjana* is prepared "with the (expressed juice of the) flower of Kapittha, Mesha-shringi, Bhallataka, Bandhuka and Amkotha separately".

*Snake-bite and rat-bite (Kalpa 5 and 6).* In Kalpa 5 the *Agada* of the leaves of Surasa, Vilva, Kapittha and Dadima, with half a part each of "black Sindhuvara, Amkotha and Gairika" in honey, is "highly efficacious especially in the case of poisoning by a Mandali-bite" (lines 460 to 468). In Kalpa 6, for a bite by a rat of the Chikkira class (headache, swelling, hiccough and vomiting), "an emetic consisting of the decoction of Jalini, Madana fruit and Amkotha should be prescribed" (lines 74 to 77), and in a further case the patient "should be made to vomit with the decoction of Jalini or with that of Shukakhya and Amkotha boiled together" (lines 196 to 198).

*An alkali (Kalpa 7, lines 7 to 20).* In the chapter on the antivenom drum, the woods of a long list of plants, among them Amkotha, are burnt to ashes, dissolved in cow's urine, filtered and boiled in the manner of preparing an alkali (the *Ksharagada*), and then powders such as Pippali-root, Tanduliyaka, Varanga and Chochaka are added.

*What the chapters do not say.* A search of the vajikarana chapter (Chikitsa 26) and the rasayana chapters (Chikitsa 27 to 30) found none of the names. No *rasa*, *virya* or *vipaka* is printed for Ankota in the passages read. The Latin identification is the appendix's. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. The Sushruta text records Ankota as an ingredient in a washing decoction for ulcers, in an oil for an ulcer after a tumour is opened, in an ear oil, as a root plaster and a flower eye-salve against poison, as an emetic in rat-bite and in an antivenom, and in an alkali. These are the text's statements about a Sanskrit name, and the Latin name the appendix attaches to it is the translator's, by position. The Codex gives no protocol and no dose.

### Preparation & Dosing

The text gives these preparations, each with its locus. A decoction of Ankota with Shankhini, Sumanah, Karavira and Suvarchchala to wash an ulcer (Sutra 36, lines 89 to 94). An oil cooked with Jati, Arushkara, Ainkota and Saptaparna (Chikitsa 19, lines 93 to 98). A fourfold sneha cooked with the paste of the fruits (Uttara 21, lines 73 to 77). A root powder in honey as a plaster (Kalpa 1, lines 278 to 280). The expressed juice of the flower as an eye-salve (Kalpa 1, lines 391 to 397). An emetic decoction with Jalini and Madana fruit (Kalpa 6, lines 75 to 77) or with Shukakhya (lines 196 to 198). Only the relative parts of the Kalpa 5 *Agada* are given (half a part of Amkotha to two parts of some of the other leaves). No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify] The Sushruta terms (the rat and snake bites, the poisoned tooth-twig, the incidental ulcer) are not modern diagnoses, so no modern finding can be set beside them. The identity rests on a positional pairing in a translator's appendix, so a study of *Alangium hexapetalum* would not bear directly on the Sushruta drug.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing here gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. In the Sushruta scheme the name is an ingredient in a physician's washes, emetics, antivenoms and alkalis. Nothing should be gathered or taken on the strength of a Latin name that the chapters do not give.

### Contraindications

The Sushruta passages read print no warning for the drug itself. Its oral uses are as an emetic (Kalpa 6), which is a physician's procedure and unsuitable for the weak, the pregnant and the very young without supervision, and in antivenoms, which describe emergencies that need medical care. The identity is open, so no plant should be gathered or used on the strength of this entry. No conservation information was read. No safety information for pregnancy was read. [USER: verify]
