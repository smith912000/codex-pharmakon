# Crude alkali (Lu Xian) — Lu Xian, "Alkali" (Yang 1998); list row 鹵鹹
## *An "alkali" of the Divine Farmer's Classic; Yang 1998 prints "Alkali" for Lu Xian and gives no composition, and his footnote 69 says the Classic treats Alkali, Salt and Halite in one passage with different indications for each* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*
**Tradition Tags:** SBJ

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** Lu Xian is printed in Yang 1998 (`Sources/SBJ_Shennong/text/Shennong_Yang_1998.txt`) on PDF page 33, in the run of "Jades and Stones: Inferior Class". The OCR of the page prints no heading; the heading is printed at the top of page 32 (line 1240) and again at the top of page 34 (line 1311), and the entry lies between them. The text (lines 1281-1287): "Lu Xian (Alkali)69 is bitter, salty, and cold. It mainly treats great fever, wasting thirst, and manic vexation. It eliminates evils and [treats] vomiting and diarrhea and gu toxins. It softens [i.e., makes elastic and flexible] the muscles and flesh. Da Yan (Sal) causes people to vomit. Rang Yan (Halitum) brightens the eyes, [relieves] eye pain, boosts the qi, fortifies the muscles and bones, and eliminates gu toxins. These are produced in lakes and swamps."

Yang's footnote 69 (lines 1297-1300 and following): "The author discusses Alkali, Salt and Halite in a single passage but gives different indications to each." The footnote then quotes Chen Cangqi (an 8th-century author; the OCR prints the name as "Chen Cang-qi") on the indications of Salt, for red eyes, ulcered canthi, headache, roundworms and other complaints. That is a later author's account of Salt and is not the Classic's text; it does not concern Lu Xian. The passage also contains two further names, Da Yan (Sal) and Rang Yan (Halitum), which this entry does not take as its own. They belong with salt, which the Codex files as its own entry (spine CPU-00298, `Volume_I_Plant_Based/entries/I_345_Salt_Sodium_chloride.md`, with the Shennong text among its traditions). Whether Lu Xian is a form of salt or a distinct substance is not settled by the text.

The list `_sources/shennong.csv` carries the row as 鹵鹹 (line 243), and `Sources/SBJ_Shennong/extract/names.csv` has the row "Lu Xian" (line 36) with the identification "Alkali". The spine's note is "low; id-uncertain", and its name "Crude alkali" with the formula Na2CO3 (crude) is the spine's own. Yang prints "Alkali" only, gives no composition, and prints no footnote doubting the gloss beyond the passage note above. That "alkali" means crude sodium carbonate in this text is a hypothesis of the spine and is not shown by the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The word "alkali" in English covers the carbonates and hydroxides of sodium and potassium, and crude natural alkali commonly means soda or natron, mainly sodium carbonate (the spine's Na2CO3), often with sodium bicarbonate, sodium chloride and sulfate mixed in. Such materials are strongly basic in water, and are used in glass, soap and cleaning. The sources read give no composition for the substance Yang translates, and the text says that it is "bitter, salty".

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only; not a recommended practice. The Classic records Lu Xian for great fever, wasting thirst and manic vexation, for vomiting, diarrhea and "gu toxins", and says that it "softens the muscles and flesh". The Codex does not endorse these claims and offers no protocol. Concentrated alkali is caustic, and nothing in the sources read supports taking this substance.

### Scientific Correlation

Sodium carbonate and bicarbonate are well-known alkaline compounds: the bicarbonate is a mild antacid, and the carbonate in strong solution is a caustic irritant. Which of these, if either, the Classic meant is not established. No medical benefit of swallowing crude alkali for the conditions the Classic names is established. ### Traditional Energetic Classification

- SBJ: "bitter, salty, and cold" (Yang line 1281). Class: Inferior (headings lines 1240 and 1311). Yang's footnote 32 (lines 849-855) explains that his rendering omits the word "flavor" and that "balanced" means neither cold nor hot; this entry is "cold". No other quality is printed.

### Cultivation Register

**Divine Farmer's Classic (SBJ).** *(a) Attested: a lower-grade drug (下品), for a short course.* Lu Xian lies between two printings of "Jades and Stones: Inferior Class" (Yang lines 1240 and 1311); the page itself prints no heading, and the entry is placed by bracketing, as Mica (Yun Mu) is in the Superior run. Yang's preface says the inferior class medicinals "mainly treat disease and correspond to earth. They are usually toxic and cannot be taken for a long time. If one intends to eliminate cold and heat and evil qi, break accumulations and gatherings, and cure disease, one should base [one's efforts] on the inferior class" (lines 391-395). Yang's own note on the classes (lines 356-366) says inferior class medicinals "specifically treat disease" and "cannot be taken in large amounts or for prolonged periods of time without developing negative side effects". The monograph prints no longevity, lightness or immortality claim, and does not itself say "toxic". The grade is a statement of the Classic as translated by Yang in 1998 and carries a cultivation meaning only in the negative.

**Practitioner's note.** A lower-grade drug in the Chinese scheme: a drug against disease, here fever and vomiting, to be taken for a defined course and stopped, and the tradition's own grading is the warning. Nothing here supports taking crude alkali. For a cultivator the entry is a record of a drug the Classic kept out of the "nourish life" class. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. ### Contraindications

Concentrated alkali burns the mouth, throat and gut. Natural crude alkali can be contaminated, and powder sold as a remedy may be a different salt. Mineral medicines are unstandardised and their sale is regulated in many places. The sodium load of crude alkali is a concern for people on salt restriction.
