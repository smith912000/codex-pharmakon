# Ampelitis (bituminous earth) — Ge Ampelitis, "Amphelitis earth", also called pharmacitis (Dioscorides 5-181)

## *A black earth from Seleucia that looks like small coals of pitch-tree wood and melts slowly when pounded with a little oil, used for the eyelids, for dyeing hair, and as a rub for vines. The translator's gloss is "Ampelite — Bituminous Earth"; the text itself gives no composition. Identification: a black bituminous or carbonaceous earth, by the translator's gloss and by the chapter's own description (black, coal-like, melts with oil); confidence medium* | *(Volume II — Mineral)*

**Tradition Tags:** DMM

### Origin & Tradition

**Single-text entry.** Ampelitis is named only by Dioscorides among the texts on disk. The locus is *De Materia Medica* 5-181, *Ge Ampelitis* (`Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading line 10766, text line 10768, book page 829 of this translation; name-index row `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/extract/names.csv`; list row `_sources/dioscorides.csv` line 906, "Ge Ampelitis", mineral). The row began on 13 October as an unverified placeholder, "ampelitis (bituminous earth)", and this entry replaces it.

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** The whole chapter, in the translator's English, reads: "Amphelitis earth (also called pharmacitis) is found in Seleucia near Syria. The black should be chosen. It is like small coals from a pitch tree, adhering like a lath [?] and glistening the same. Furthermore, it is melted slowly if a little oil is poured on it while it is pounded into small pieces. That which is white, ashy and unmeltable is considered worthless. It dissolves and cools. It is used to make eyelids beautiful, for dyeing hair, and for rubbing the vines at the time of their sprouting, for it kills worms that breed in them." The bracketed "[?]" is the translator's own query. The chapter gives: a place ("Seleucia near Syria"); a second name (pharmacitis); a rule of choice (black is chosen; white, ashy and unmeltable is worthless); a simple test (pounded with a little oil, the good kind melts slowly); two qualities ("dissolves and cools"); and three uses (making the eyelids beautiful, dyeing hair, rubbing vines when they sprout, because it kills worms in them). The translator's heading gloss is "SUGGESTED: Ampelite — Bituminous Earth". The Seleucia of the text is glossed in the translator's gazetteer (line 3527) as "Seleucia-on-Tigris in Mesopotamia" the capital of the Seleucid Empire and does not give a mine; whether Dioscorides meant that city or another is not settled by the gazetteer, which lists it only as a place name. **Position in the book.** The chapter closes the book's run of earths (5-170 *Ge*, "Earth": "All earth that falls within the scope of medicinal use is supremely useful for cooling, and for closing the pores", line 10711 onward) and comes after 5-180 (*Ge Melia*, earth from Melia). The next chapter, 5-182, is *Asbole*, soot from glass-making. The general remark of 5-170 is about earths in general and is not an attribution to this earth.

**Where the substance is not found.** A search of the other texts on disk for *ampelitis*, *pharmacitis* and "bituminous" found nothing further. The name does not appear in the Sushruta, Shennong, Ebers or Tibetan material read.

### Active Components

The chapter names no constituent. In general terms, a black earth that looks like coal and melts when worked with oil is likely a carbonaceous or bituminous material, with organic matter and clay or shale, and this agrees with the translator's gloss. ### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

**Historical record only. Not a practice.**

- **What the text says it is for.** Beautifying the eyelids, dyeing hair, and rubbing vines at sprouting time against worms.
- **What the text does not say.** No dose, no internal use, no way of preparing it beyond pounding it with oil as a test.

A black bituminous earth applied to the eyelids is a cosmetic of the ancient world, and the Codex does not recommend putting any unidentified earth near the eye or on the skin. The vine use is an agricultural note. No protocol follows from this entry.

### Preparation & Dosing

The chapter gives one operation: the earth "is melted slowly if a little oil is poured on it while it is pounded into small pieces", which is given as a mark of the good kind and also describes how it is worked. It gives no recipe for the eyelid or hair uses beyond the name of the use. ### Scientific Correlation

General, well-established facts only. Ampelite is an old mineralogical name for a black carbonaceous (and sometimes pyritic) shale; bituminous materials are mixtures of hydrocarbons and organic matter, and they can irritate skin and eyes. Dyes for hair and lids made from carbon black are a familiar cosmetic use in general. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Cultivation Register

**Greek / Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and the chapter contains none; it is not a cultivation text. What the chapter does give is a discipline of attention: where the earth comes from (Seleucia), how to choose it (the black, not the white and ashy), and a simple test of quality (whether it melts slowly under oil while being pounded). That is the frame, and no cultivation status is attested for this earth. The cosmetic and horticultural uses carry no spiritual claim.

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing for a cultivator to take. The chapter is useful only as an example of the Dioscoridean habit of choosing a material by sight and a small test, and the earth itself has no place in practice.

### Contraindications

- **Do not apply to the eye or eyelid.** The chapter's cosmetic use cannot be made safe without knowing what the earth is; bituminous materials can irritate and contaminate.
