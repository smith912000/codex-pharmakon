### Origin & Tradition

Kansu Sandwort is a small, cushion-forming alpine plant of the pink family (Caryophyllaceae), native to the high cold deserts and stony slopes of the Qinghai–Tibet plateau and the historic region of Gansu (the older romanisation "Kansu" survives in the species epithet). It grows where few medicinal plants can — at altitudes and in conditions of cold, wind, and thin air that shape both its hardy morphology and its place in the medicine of the people who live there.

It is principally a herb of Tibetan medicine (gso ba rig pa), one of the strand of plateau-adapted plants gathered into the Tibetan and adjacent Mongolian–Amdo folk pharmacopoeias (here grouped under the folk and minor-tradition tag, FMT). In that tradition its dominant role is heat-clearing: it is used to drain and cool excess heat, with particular reference to heat patterns lodged in the liver and the lung. The classical Tibetan framing speaks of clearing "fever" and "poison-heat" — categories that span what Western terms would parse as inflammatory and infectious heat conditions affecting the respiratory tract and the hepatic system.

This places Kansu Sandwort at the opposite energetic pole from the warming Zingiberaceae rhizomes that surround it in this volume: where galangal and aromatic ginger add heat and disperse cold, sandwort subtracts heat and cools. In the integrative TCM mapping used throughout this Codex, that function corresponds to a heat-clearing herb entering the Liver and Lung channels.

A clear and honest limitation must accompany this entry: rigorous, widely replicated phytochemical and pharmacological data on *Arenaria kansuensis* specifically are sparse compared with the canonical Chinese herbs. Much of what is documented sits within the Tibetan ethnomedical literature and a smaller body of modern study. This entry therefore keeps its constituent and pharmacology claims deliberately general, and flags where specifics would require verification before publication.

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Within the Kenetica framework, Kansu Sandwort fills the heat-clearing role that the warming carminatives cannot: it is reserved for athletes presenting with heat-type patterns affecting the respiratory and hepatic systems rather than the cold-stomach patterns treated by the galangals.

The relevant Kenetica presentations are those where training stress, environmental load, or acute illness produce a "heat" picture: an inflamed, hot, irritated respiratory tract; a sense of internal heat and congestion; or the hepatic-heat signs that classical traditions associate with irritability, red eyes, and a feeling of pressure under the ribs. In a plateau or high-altitude training context — the natural habitat of the herb and a setting in which the Tibetan tradition developed its use — sandwort's cooling, heat-draining action is the indicated correction when heat rather than cold dominates.

It is, by its nature, a corrective and short-course herb, used to clear an active heat pattern and then withdrawn once the heat resolves. It is not a daily tonic and not phase-anchored; it is deployed across phases only when the heat pattern appears. Given the sparse modern evidence base for this specific species, its Kenetica use should be conservative, practitioner-guided, and grounded in the traditional heat-clearing indication rather than in unverified pharmacological claims.

- **Traditional preparation:** in Tibetan and plateau folk practice, the aerial parts (and the cushion-forming herb as a whole) are typically dried and decocted, or incorporated into compound Tibetan formulas alongside other heat-clearing plateau herbs, rather than used as a high-dose single agent.

- **Compound use:** more commonly a component of multi-herb Tibetan formulas than a stand-alone remedy.

### Contraindications

- **Cold patterns:** Contraindicated in cold or Yang-deficient presentations — pallor, cold limbs, watery digestion, aversion to cold; a cooling, heat-draining herb worsens these.
- **Spleen/Stomach weakness:** cold, draining herbs can burden a weak digestion; caution where appetite is poor and stools are loose.
- **Pregnancy:** insufficient safety data for this specific species; avoid except under qualified practitioner guidance.
- **Evidence limitation:** because rigorous safety and constituent data are sparse for *Arenaria kansuensis*, use should be conservative, practitioner-supervised, and not based on extrapolation from better-studied herbs.
