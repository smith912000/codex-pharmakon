# Clematis flammula — Klematitis (Dioscorides 4-182)

## *Clematis flammula (the translators' suggestion, attributed on the line to Mabberley; "Clematis, Sweet Virgin's Bower")* | *(Volume I — Plant-Based)*
**Tradition Tags:** DMM

---

### Origin & Tradition

**Named only by Dioscorides (DMM).** No other text read for this entry names the plant under this chapter. The binomial is the suggestion of the translators (Osbaldeston and Wood 2000), printed on the chapter's SUGGESTED line with the bracketed attribution "[Mabberley]", not Dioscorides' own identification, and the Codex does not state the identity as certain. The row was added from that line (ATTESTATION_QUEUE §PLANTGAP) and has not been verified against POWO. [USER: verify] The line names one species only: "Clematis flammula [Mabberley] — Clematis, Sweet Virgin's Bower". The translators' index lists it at page 732.

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** The text is `Sources/DMM_DioscoRides/text/Dioscorides_De_Materia_Medica_2000_English.txt`, heading *4-182. KLEMATITIS* at line 9668, SUGGESTED line at 9670, text at lines 9671 and 9676 (a page break, printed page 732, falls between them).

The whole chapter reads: "Clematitis sends out somewhat reddish pliant branches, and leaves especially sharp to the taste and ulcerating. It winds around trees like smilax. A decoction of the seed of this (pounded into small pieces and taken as a drink with water or honey water) drives phlegm and bile downwards. The leaves applied remove leprosy. It is preserved in salt with lepidium [2-205] to be eaten."

So the chapter gives a description (reddish pliant branches, leaves "especially sharp to the taste and ulcerating", a plant that winds around trees "like smilax"), a seed decoction that drives phlegm and bile downwards, a leaf application that removes leprosy, and a way of preserving it in salt with lepidium "to be eaten". It prints no other names, no habitat and no season. It carries no "POISONOUS" label. The cross-reference 2-205 points to lepidium, which was not read for this entry. [USER: verify]

**Overlap with Dioscorides' earlier clematis chapters.** Book Four also holds two earlier chapters headed *KLEMATIS*. Chapter 4-7a (line 8054) is a different plant: its SUGGESTED line is "Clematis daphnoides [Fuchs, Bauhin], Vinca minor [Linnaeus] — Running Myrtle, Periwinkle", and its leaves are "similar to laurel". Chapter 4-7b *KLEMATIS ETERA* (line 8069, "another clematis") carries the label POISONOUS and a SUGGESTED line "Clematis cirrhosa — Evergreen Clematis, Clematis angustifolia — Virgin's Bower, Clematis alpina — Alpine Clematis". Its text (line 8072) describes "a vinelike branch, reddish, flexible; the leaf extremely sharp to the taste and ulcerating" that "winds around trees like smilax [4-144, 4-145]", with a seed drink that "drives phlegm and bile downward", leaves that "drive away leprosy" and the same preservation with lepidium. Chapter 4-182 therefore repeats 4-7b almost word for word, with different names on the translators' line. The two chapters are not merged here. The spine's existing entry I_071 (*Clematis vitalba*) cites "Book IV" without a chapter number. [USER: verify]

### Active Components

The constituents of the plant Dioscorides describes were not read. The translators' plant belongs to the buttercup family (Ranunculaceae), many members of which are generally known to be irritant to skin and mouth when fresh. The chapter's own words, "especially sharp to the taste and ulcerating", point the same way. [USER: verify]

### Effect & Use Case in Kenetica

Historical record only, not a recommended practice. Dioscorides records a seed decoction that drives phlegm and bile downwards, a leaf application for leprosy, and a salted preserve to eat. These are the text's claims. The Codex gives no protocol: the plant is described as ulcerating, the identification is the translators' and the same description is repeated under a chapter labelled POISONOUS.

### Preparation & Dosing

4-182 gives: a decoction of the seed, "pounded into small pieces and taken as a drink with water or honey water"; the leaves "applied"; and the plant "preserved in salt with lepidium [2-205] to be eaten". No quantity, frequency or duration is given. No dose is given in the sources retrieved. [USER: verify]

### Scientific Correlation

A seed drink that "drives phlegm and bile downwards" is a claim of purging, and a leaf application "for leprosy" is a claim about a skin condition. No study of any clematis was opened. No clinical studies were retrieved for this entry. [USER: verify]

### Traditional Energetic Classification

- **DMM:** the chapter prints "especially sharp to the taste and ulcerating" for the leaves and no warming, cooling or drying quality for 4-182.

### Cultivation Register

**Greek — Dioscorides (DMM).** *(b) Framed, not a cultivation status.* Dioscorides has no subtle-body system, and none is manufactured here. The chapter offers the discipline of attention that the cultivation spec describes for this tradition: how to know the plant (reddish pliant branches, a climber around trees), how to test it by taste, and how it was prepared. It assigns the plant no status, and the identification is the translators'. [USER: verify]

**Practitioner's note.** Nothing in the chapter gives a cultivator a practice with this plant. A sharp, ulcerating climber taken as a purgative is history and not a practice. The repetition of the description under 4-7b with a warning label is itself a reason for caution.

### Contraindications

The plant is described as sharp and ulcerating, and a near-identical chapter carries the label POISONOUS. Fresh plants of the buttercup family can blister skin and mouth, and a purgative seed drink is not safe to take unsupervised, least of all in pregnancy. The identification is the translators' suggestion, so no wild plant should be gathered or taken on the strength of this entry. None of the traditional uses above is advice. [USER: verify]
